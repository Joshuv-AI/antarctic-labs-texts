Abstract
. The (maximal) Satake compactification associated to a real reductive group G G is the closure of the symmetric space of all maximal compact subgroups of G G within the compact space of all closed subgroups of G G . We shall present three different views of a groupoid that may be associated to the Satake compactification. To begin, we shall define our Satake groupoid, as we shall call it, as a topological groupoid, and as a special case of a general construction of Omar Mohsen. Then we shall give a Lie-theoretic account of the Satake groupoid, borrowing from work of Toshio Oshima. Finally we shall identify the Satake groupoid with the purely geometric b-groupoid of the Satake compactification, using the structure of the compactification as a smooth manifold with corners. In a subsequent paper we shall use the Satake groupoid to present a new proof of Harish-Chandra’s principle, that all the tempered irreducible representations of G G may be constructed from discrete series representations using parabolic induction.



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper and its sequel
[
3
]
is to examine from the perspective of
C^{*}
-algebras and noncommutative geometry the following celebrated discovery of Harish-Chandra:

Theorem
.

Let
G
be a real reductive group. A tempered irreducible unitary representation of
G
is either square-integrable, modulo center, or embeddable into a representation that is unitarily parabolically induced from a square-integrable, modulo center, irreducible unitary representation of a Levi subgroup.

Harish-Chandra’s result played an important role in his pursuit of the Plancherel formula. In his review of Harish-Chandra’s Collected Works
[
12
]
, Robert Langlands writes that

Harish-Chandra discovered quite early on the principles which allowed him to do this [obtain an explicit Plancherel formula] …The critical notions are those of a Cartan subgroup, of a parabolic subgroup, of an induced, and of a square-integrable representation.

…The first principle is that the representations [parabolically] induced from …square-integrable [representations] suffice for the Plancherel formula …

…The second is that [a real reductive group ] has square-integrable representations if and only if there are
[compact ] Cartan subgroups …

The second principle has long been studied from a geometric perspective, starting with Parthasarathy
[
23
]
, who described the discrete series (the square-integrable representations) using Dirac operators on the symmetric space associated to a real reductive group, continuing with the work of Atiyah and Schmid
[
1
]
, and culminating in the work of Lafforgue
[
11
]
, who recovered Harish-Chandra’s classification of the discrete series using noncommutative geometry and K-theory. The theorem that we stated above is a precise version of the first principle.
Combined, the two principles paint in broad outline a picture of the tempered dual of any real reductive group.

Our approach to the theorem will proceed via the (maximal) Satake compactification of the Riemmanian symmetric space associated to a real reductive group
[
24
]
. We shall incorporate the Satake compactification into an argument involving
C^{*}
-algebras by associating to it first a groupoid, and then the
C^{*}
-algebra of that groupoid. The purpose of this paper is to describe the groupoid from three different points of view: those of topology, Lie theory and geometry. As for
C^{*}
-algebras, their relevance to Harish-Chandra’s principle is a reflection of Alain Connes’ idea that the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra of a real reductive group should be viewed as the algebra of continuous functions on the tempered dual
[
5
]
. But we shall say no more about
C^{*}
-algebras here, and instead refer the reader to the sequel,
[
3
]
.

The fastest way to present the
Satake groupoid
(as we shall call it) is to use the following simple observation of Omar Mohsen (which he has used to great effect in his own work
[
17
,
16
]
): if
G
is any group, and if
\{S\}
is any collection of subgroups of
G
that is closed under conjugation by elements of
G
, then the collection
\{C\}
of all cosets of all the subgroups in
\{S\}
carries the following structure of a groupoid over the object space
\{S\}
:

\operatorname{source}(C)=C^{-1}C,\quad\operatorname{target}(C)=CC^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{1}\circ C_{2}=C_{1}C_{2}.

Now, the Satake compactification
\mathscr{X}
of a real reductive group
G
may be defined to be the closure of the space of maximal compact subgroups of
G
within the compact space of all closed subgroups of
G
. So Mohsen’s observation immediately applies, and we obtain a locally compact Hausdorff topological groupoid, as we shall explain in Section
2
. This is our Satake groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
.

Although the above quickly characterizes the Satake compactification and the Satake groupoid, for computations it is much more convenient to construct both compactification and groupoid using Lie theory. To do so we shall follow the approach of Toshio Oshima
[
22
]
to the Satake compactification. We shall present in Section
3
a version of Oshima’s work that we hope will be broadly accessible, and in Section
4
we shall describe the Satake groupoid from the Oshima point of view.

Oshima’s construction makes it clear that the Satake compactification has finitely many
G
-orbits, which may be described using an Iwasawa decomposition
G{=}KAN
, as follows. It is well-known in Lie theory that a standard parabolic subgroup
P_{I}{=}M_{I}A_{I}N_{I}
of
G
may be asssociated to any subset
I
of the set
\Sigma
of simple restricted roots that is obtained from the given Iwasawa decomposition, and that these are the only standard parabolic subgroups; see Section
3
for more details. The
G
-orbits in
\mathscr{X}
are also in bijection with the subsets
I\subseteq\Sigma
, with the orbit
\mathscr{X}_{I}\subseteq\mathscr{X}
being of the type

\mathscr{X}_{I}\cong G\big/K_{I}A_{I}\overline{N}_{I},

where
K_{I}{=}K\cap M_{I}
, and where
\overline{N_{I}}=\theta[N_{I}]
.
The Satake compactification, viewed as a collection of closed subgroups of
G
, consists of all the conjugates in
G
of all the groups
H_{I}{=}K_{I}\overline{N}_{I}
.

The orbit
\mathscr{X}_{I}
is contained in the closure of the orbit
\mathscr{X}_{J}
if and only if
I\subseteq J
. It follows, for instance, that the orbit
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}
is open and dense in
\mathscr{X}
. In addition,
K_{\Sigma}=K
, while the group
A_{\Sigma}
is the intersection of the center of
G
with
A
, and
N_{\Sigma}
is the trivial one-element group. The orbit

\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}\cong G/KA_{\Sigma},

therefore identifies, via the map
gKA_{\Sigma}\mapsto gKg^{-1}
, with the space of maximal compact subgroups of
G
.

As for the Satake groupoid, each of the orbits
\mathscr{X}_{I}
above is a locally closed, saturated subset of
\mathscr{X}
, and also a smooth embedded submanifold, and the reduction of the Satake groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
to
\mathscr{X}_{I}
has the form

\mathscr{G}_{I}\cong G\big/K_{I}\overline{N}_{I}\underset{\,\,A_{I}}{\times}G\big/K_{I}\overline{N}_{I}

(quotient by the diagonal right action of
A_{I}
). For instance, the open and dense subgroupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\Sigma}
is

\mathscr{G}_{\Sigma}\cong G/K\underset{\,\,A_{\Sigma}}{\times}G/K.

When
G
has compact center, the group
A_{\Sigma}
is trivial, and the above is simply the pair groupoid on
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}\cong G/K
.

Oshima actually constructed a smooth, closed
G
-manifold
\mathscr{M}
into which the variety of maximal compact subgroups of
G
embeds as an open subset, while the Satake compactification embeds smoothly as a compact submanifold with corners. We shall in fact construct a Lie groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
over
\mathscr{M}
that we shall call the
Oshima groupoid
. It is a quotient of the transformation groupoid for the action of
G
on
\mathscr{M}
. The Satake compactification, viewed as a subset of
\mathscr{M}
, is a saturated subset for the Oshima groupoid, and we shall prove that the reduction of
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
to this subset is our Satake groupoid.

Now, the bounding submanifolds (of top dimension) of the Satake compactification within the Oshima space
\mathscr{M}
extend to smooth, closed hypersurfaces in
\mathscr{M}
that cross one another normally. And to any manifold, such as
\mathscr{M}
, that is equipped with a finite family of normally crossing, closed hypersufaces there is associated a Lie groupoid
[
21
,
19
]
; the construction is an elaboration of ideas from the
b
-calculus of Richard Melrose
[
14
]
(the Lie algebroid is Melrose’s b-tangent bundle). In the last section of this paper we shall present our third view of the Satake groupoid by identifing the Oshima groupoid with this geometrically-defined
b
-groupoid
.

To conclude this introduction, it is a pleasure to acknowledge with gratitude the kind assistance of Omar Mohsen, whose suggestion that his groupoid construction might be deployed in representation theory was the impetus for this work. It is a similar pleasure to acknowledge the interest of Alain Connes, who suggested to us a number of years ago that we ought to search for a groupoid that would explain Harish-Chandra’s principle.

2.
The Satake compactification and the Satake groupoid

In this section, we shall present the topological definition of the Satake compactification and groupoid of a real reductive group.

2.1.
Definition of the Satake compactification

As mentioned in the introduction, the Satake compactification that we shall study is the closure of the set of all maximal compact subgroups of
G
in the space of all closed subgroups of
G
. The latter carries a natural compact Hausdorff topology; let us recall it.

2.1.1 Definition

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
Z
be a topological space. We shall denote by
\mathscr{C}(Z)
the set of all
nonempty
closed subsets of
Z
. The
Fell topology
on
\mathscr{C}(Z)
is the topology generated by the base of open sets

V\bigl(K,\mathcal{U}\bigr)=\bigl\{\,X\in\mathscr{C}(Z):X\cap K{=}\emptyset\,\text{ and }\,X\cap U\neq\emptyset\,\,\forall U\in\mathcal{U}\,\bigr\},

where
K
ranges over all the compact subsets of
Z
, and
\mathcal{U}
ranges over all the finite families of open subsets of
Z
.

2.1.2 Theorem

(
[
6
, Thm. 1]
)
.

If
Z
is locally compact and second-countable, then the Fell topology on
\mathscr{C}(Z)
is locally compact, Hausdorff and second-countable.

2.1.3 Remark
.

In
[
6
]
, Fell includes the empty set as an element of his
\mathscr{C}(Z)
; by doing so he obtains the one-point compactification of our
\mathscr{C}(Z)
.

The following definition and lemma offer a full characterization of continuous maps into
\mathscr{C}(Z)
, analogous to the description of vector bundles via classifying maps into Grassmannians.

2.1.4 Definition
.

Let
B
and
Z
be topological spaces. A
continuous family of closed subsets of
Z
over the space
B
is a closed subset

\mathbf{E}\subseteq B\times Z

for which the projection map
\mathbf{E}\to B
is an open mapping.

2.1.5 Lemma
.

Let
B
and
Z
be locally compact topological spaces.
A function

f:B\to\mathscr{C}(Z)

is continuous if and only if the subset

{\mathbf{E}}=\{\,(b,z)\in B{\times}Z:z\in f(b)\,\}\subseteq B\times Z

is a continuous family of closed subsets in the sense of Definition
2.1.4
. ∎

From now on we shall consider the Fell topology in the case of a second-countable, locally compact Hausdorff group
G
, since this is what is of interest to us (the second countability assumption is largely unnecessary, but it streamlines some arguments).

2.1.6 Lemma

(
[
6
, Rmk. IV, p.474]
)
.

The subspace
\mathscr{S}(G)\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
of all closed subgroups of
G
is a compact metrizable subset of
\mathscr{C}(G)
.

2.1.7 Remark
.

A version of this result was proved before
[
6
]
, by Chabauty
[
4
]
, using a different but equivalent definition of the topology on
\mathscr{S}(G)
.

In the group case, the Fell topology has the following elementary continuity property:

2.1.8 Lemma
.

The maps

\displaystyle G\times\mathscr{C}(G)\to\mathscr{C}(G),\quad(g,X)\mapsto gX

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(G)\times G\to\mathscr{C}(G),\quad(X,g)\mapsto Xg

are continuous. In particular, the adjoint action of
G
on
\mathscr{S}(G)
is continuous. ∎

2.1.9 Example
.

It follows from the lemma that if
S
is a closed subgroup of
G
, then the morphism

\begin{gathered}G/S\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}(G)\\
gS\longmapsto gSg^{-1}\end{gathered}

is continuous. This also follows from Lemma
2.1.5
.

Let us now specialize to the case of interest to us in this paper.

2.1.10 Definition
.

Let
G
be a real reductive group (in Section
3.1
we shall say more about our precise assumptions on
G
; those details are not important in this section).
To be consistent with notation that we shall introduce in Section
3
, we shall denote by
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}
the set of all maximal compact subgroups of
G
.

Any two maximal compact subgroups of
G
are conjugate, so
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}
is homogeneous space of
G
, and it therefore carries a natural topology, indeed a
G
-equivariant smooth manifold structure. As we noted in Example
2.1.9
, the inclusion map of
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}
into
\mathscr{S}(G)

is continuous. It will emerge later that this map is a homeomorphism onto its image; see Section
3
.

2.1.11 Definition
.

The (maximal)
Satake compactification
of the space
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}
of maximal compact subgroups of
G
is the closure

\mathscr{X}=\overline{\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}}

in the compact Hausdorff space
\mathscr{S}(G)
.

This is not the original definition of Satake in
[
24
]
, but it is equivalent; see for instance
[
8
, Thm. 9.18]
. It is not obvious at this point that
\mathscr{X}_{\Sigma}
is an open subset of
\mathscr{X}
, but this is indeed so; see Section
3
again.

2.2.
Definition of the Satake groupoid

The Satake compactification is a conjugation-invariant family of subgroups of
G
, and so we may apply Mohsen’s groupoid construction that we described in the introduction. We recall the construction.

In general, if
\mathscr{X}
is any family of subgroups of a group
G
, closed under conjugation, then every left coset of a subgroup in
\mathscr{X}
is also a right coset of a subgroup in
\mathscr{X}
.
The
coset groupoid

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}\rightrightarrows\mathscr{X}
is the set
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
of all cosets of all subgroups in
\mathscr{X}
equipped with source and target maps

\operatorname{source}(gS)=S,\quad\operatorname{target}(Sg)=S,\qquad(g\in G,\ S\in\mathscr{X}),

and with setwise product and inverse.

2.2.1 Definition
.

Let
G
be a real reductive group. The
Satake groupoid

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}\rightrightarrows\mathscr{X}
associated to
G
is the coset groupoid of the Satake compactification.

By definition, an element of
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
is a closed subset of
G
. Thus
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
is a subset of
\mathscr{C}(G)
, and we shall be viewing it as such in this section.

The Satake groupoid shares its most basic features with the coset groupoid of any closed, conjugation-invariant subset of
\mathscr{S}(G)
, for any locally compact
G
(although for simplicity we shall focus on second-countable groups).

2.2.2 Lemma
.

Let
G
be a second-countable, locally compact Hausdorff topological group, and let
\mathscr{X}
be a closed, conjugation-invariant subset of the space
\mathscr{S}(G)
of all closed subgroups of
G
.
The coset groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
is a closed subset of
\mathscr{C}(G)
.

More precisely, if
(C_{n})
is a sequence of cosets in
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
which converges to some closed subset
C\in\mathscr{C}(G)
, then we can find some convergent sequences
g_{n}\to g\in G
and
S_{n}\to S\in\mathscr{S}(G)
such that
C_{n}=g_{n}S_{n}
for all
n
and
C=gS
.

Proof.

Suppose that some sequence
(C_{n})
in
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
converges to
C\in\mathscr{C}(G)
. Pick
g\in C
and let
V_{1}\supset V_{2}\supset\cdots
be a nested neighborhood basis for
g\in G
. Since
C\in V(\emptyset,\{V_{k}\})
(notation from Definition
2.1.1
), it follows that for every
k
there is an
N_{k}
, without loss of generality greater than
N_{k-1}
, such that
C_{n}\in V(\emptyset,\{V_{k}\})
for all
n\geq N_{k}
. Now, for every
N_{k}\leq n<N_{k+1}
, pick
g_{n}\in C_{n}\cap V_{k}
. Then
g_{n}\to g
. Since
g_{n}\in C_{n}
we can write
C_{n}=g_{n}S_{n}
for some
S_{n}\in\mathscr{X}
, and we get, thanks to Lemma
2.1.8
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}g^{-1}g_{n}S_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}g^{-1}C_{n}=g^{-1}C.

Since
\mathscr{X}
is closed, we deduce that
g^{-1}C=S
for some closed subgroup
S\in\mathscr{X}
. The result follows.
∎

2.2.3 Theorem
.

Let
G
be a second countable, locally compact Hausdorff topological group, and let
\mathscr{X}
be any closed, conjugation-invariant subset of
\mathscr{S}(G)
.
When equipped with the Fell topology it inherits from
\mathscr{C}(G)
, the coset groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
is a locally compact Hausdorff topological groupoid with open range and source maps.

Proof.

The set-wise inverse
C\mapsto C^{-1}
is Fell-continuous on
\mathscr{C}(G)
because the inverse operation is a homeomorphism from
G
to itself.

Every sequence of composable pairs of morphisms in
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
has the form

((g_{n}S_{n},S_{n}h_{n}))_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
with
g_{n},h_{n}\in G
and
S_{n}\in\mathscr{X}
.
If this sequence is convergent, then by Lemma
2.2.2
we may assume that
g_{n}\to g
,
h_{n}\to h
and
S_{n}\to S
, for some
g,h\in G
and some
S\in\mathscr{X}
. Therefore

\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}S_{n}\circ S_{n}h_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}S_{n}h_{n}=gSh=\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}S_{n}\circ\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}h_{n},

so that composition is continuous. Since the source and target maps, viewed as maps to the unit elements in
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
, are compositions of composition and inverse maps, they are continuous, too.

It remains to prove that the source and target maps are open. Since the target map is the composition of the inverse map with the source map, it suffices to prove the source map is open.
For this, we need to prove that if
V(K,\mathcal{U})
is a basic open set, as in Definition
2.1.1
(where
K
is any compact subset of
G
, and not necessarily a subgroup), then the image of the set
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}\cap V(K,\mathcal{U})

under the source map is an open set in
\mathscr{X}
.

Let
gS\in V(K,\mathcal{U})
. Then
S\in V(g^{-1}K,g^{-1}\mathcal{U})
, and moreover

S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{X}\cap V(g^{-1}K,g^{-1}\mathcal{U})\quad\Rightarrow\quad gS^{\prime}\in V(K,\mathcal{U}),

This shows that

\mathscr{X}\cap V(g^{-1}K,g^{-1}\mathcal{U})\subseteq\operatorname{source}\bigl[\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}\cap V(K,\mathcal{U})\bigr],

and therefore that
S
lies in the interior of the image of
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}\cap V(K,\mathcal{U})
under the source map, as required.
∎

3.
The Oshima space

In this section we shall present a small variation of Oshima’s construction of a smooth, closed
G
-manifold that includes the Satake compactification as a full-dimensional compact submanifold with corners. Oshima’s manifold is in fact the union of a finite set of copies of the Satake compactification, joined together along various common boundary faces.

3.1.
Background information on reductive groups and parabolic subgroups

We shall work with the real reductive groups as defined by Knapp in
[
10
, Sec.VII.2]
. This includes Harish-Chandra’s class
[
9
, Sec. 3]
, and agrees with it for linear groups
[
10
, pp. 447-449]
.

Let
G
be a real reductive group, as above, and fix the following:

(i)

A Cartan involution
\theta\colon G\to G
(we shall use the same symbol for the involution on the Lie algebra).

(ii)

A nondegenerate,
\theta
-invariant symmetric bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
that is negative-definite on the
+1
eigenspace of
\theta
and positive-definite on the
-1
eigenspace.

(iii)

An Iwasawa decomposition
G{=}KAN

[
10
, Prop. 7.31]
that is compatible with
\theta
, and its associated minimal parabolic subgroup
P_{\min}=MAN
, where
M
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{a}
in
K
.

Recall that the
standard parabolic subalgebras
of
\mathfrak{g}
for this choice of minimal parabolic subgroup are the Lie subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
that contain
\mathfrak{p}_{\min}
. They are in bijection with the subsets of the set
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})
of simple restricted roots in the following way: given
I\subseteq\Sigma
form

(3.1.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{I}

\displaystyle=\bigcap\bigl\{\,\operatorname{kernel}(\alpha):\alpha\in I\,\bigr\},

(3.1.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{I}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{I}^{\perp}\oplus\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]=0\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma},

(3.1.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}_{I}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma},

with
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
the restricted roots spaces of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})
(see for instance
[
10
, p. 455]
) and
\mathfrak{a}_{I}^{\perp}
the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
in
\mathfrak{a}
using the form in (ii) above. All of the above are Lie subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
; the algebras
\mathfrak{m}_{I}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
commute with one another; and both normalize
\mathfrak{n}_{I}
.
The corresponding standard parabolic subalgebra is

\mathfrak{p}_{I}=\mathfrak{m}_{I}\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{I}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{I}.

For all the above, see
[
10
, Sec. VII.7]
.

Now form the Lie groups

\displaystyle L_{I}

\displaystyle=\text{Centralizer of $\mathfrak{a}_{I}$ in $G$}

\displaystyle N_{I}

\displaystyle=\text{Connected Lie subgroup of $G$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{n}_{I}$}.

These are closed subgroups of
G
. Moreover
L_{I}
normalizes
N_{I}
. The product

P_{I}=L_{I}N_{I}

is also a closed subgroup of
G
; it is the
standard parabolic subgroup
associated to
I
. The group
L_{I}
decomposes as a Cartesian product of Lie groups

(3.1.4)

L_{I}=M_{I}A_{I},

where
A_{I}
is the connected Lie subgroup of
G
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
, and
M_{I}
is the mutual kernel of all Lie group morphisms from
L_{I}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\times}_{+}
; its Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{m}_{I}
. See
[
10
, Sec. VII.7]
again.

3.1.5 Theorem

(See
[
20
, Thm. 3]
or
[
10
, Prop. 7.83]
)
.

If
I
is any subset of
\Sigma
, then

P_{I}=\operatorname{Normalizer}_{G}(\mathfrak{p}_{I})=\operatorname{Normalizer}_{G}(\mathfrak{n}_{I})
.

Because of the Bruhat decomposition,
G{=}P_{\min}{\cdot}\operatorname{Normalizer}_{K}(\mathfrak{a}){\cdot}P_{\min}
, the proof is reduced to checking that if
w\in\operatorname{Normalizer}_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
, and if
w
conjugates the
P_{I}
to itself, or
N_{I}
to itself, then
w\in K_{I}=K\cap M_{I}
. Since
w
normalizes
\mathfrak{a}
, it permutes the restricted roots of
G
, and the proof may be completed by a combinatorial argument.

3.1.6 Theorem

(
[
9
, Lemma 6.2]
)
.

If
I,J\subseteq\Sigma
, and if
P_{I}
and
P_{J}
are conjugate in
G
, or
N_{I}
and
N_{J}
are conjugate in
G
, then
I=J
.

This is a result of Tits
[
25
]
, and the proof is similar: thanks to the Bruhat decomposition, it suffices to prove that if
I{\neq}J
, then no element of
N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
can conjugate
P_{I}
to
P_{J}
, or
N_{I}
into
N_{J}
. See for instance
[
2
, Prop. VI.20]
for an exposition of the combinatorial proof of this latter fact in the language of root systems (Tits uses
BN
-pairs).

3.2.
A smooth family of Lie subgroups

In this section we shall describe the main step in Oshima’s construction.

3.2.1 Definition
.

If
\Sigma
is any finite set, then we shall denote by

\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
the space of finite sequences
(t_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Sigma}
of real numbers that are indexed by the elements of
\Sigma
(or in other words, functions from
\Sigma
to
\mathbb{R}
). In addition we shall denote by
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{+}
the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
comprised of sequences that take only strictly positive values, and denote by
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}
the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
comprised of sequences that take only nonzero values.

3.2.2 Definition
.

Let
G=KAN
be a real reductive group, and let
\Sigma
be the set of simple roots associated to the given Iwasawa decomposition.

(i)

For
a\in A
and
\alpha\in\Sigma
, let

a^{\alpha}=e^{\alpha(\log(a))}
.

(ii)

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{+}
, denote by
a_{t}\in A
any element for which

a_{t}^{\alpha}=t_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\Sigma
.

When
G
has compact center, the element
a_{t}
in (ii) above is unique.
In general, when
G
does not have compact center, the ratio of any two choices lies in the center of
G
. Because of this, we may unambiguously define a subgroup
H_{t}\subseteq G
, as follows:

3.2.3 Definition
.

Let
G=KAN
be a real reductive group, and let
\Sigma
be the set of simple roots associated to the given Iwasawa decomposition.

(i)

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{+}
, we shall write
H_{t}=\Ad_{a_{t}}[K]
.

(ii)

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}
, we define
|t|
to be the sequence of absolute values
\{\,|t_{\alpha}|\,\}_{\alpha\in\Sigma}
, and then write

H_{t}=H_{|t|}
.

We aim to prove the following result:

3.2.4 Theorem
.

Let us write
\mathbf{G}=G\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
.
The closure of the subset

\{\,(h,t):h\in H_{t},\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}\,\}\subseteq\mathbf{G}

is a smooth embedded submanifold
\mathbf{H}\subseteq\mathbf{G}
, and the projection map

\mathbf{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}

is a smooth submersion whose fibers are closed Lie subgroups
H_{t}\subseteq G
.

3.2.5 Definition
.

Let
\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})
. According to elementary root system theory, we may express
\gamma
in a unique way as a combination

\gamma=\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma}n_{\gamma,\alpha}\alpha,

where each
n_{\gamma\alpha}
is a nonnegative integer. For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, we shall write

(3.2.6)

t^{2\gamma}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Sigma}t_{\alpha}^{2n_{\gamma,\alpha}}.

(To be clear we shall write

t_{\alpha}^{2n_{\gamma,\alpha}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $t_{\alpha}=0$ and $n_{\gamma,\alpha}=0$}\\
0&\text{if $t_{\alpha}=0$ and $n_{\gamma,\alpha}>0$.}\end{cases}

With this convention,
t^{2\gamma}
becomes a polynomial function of
t
.)

3.2.7 Definition
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, define a linear subspace
\mathfrak{h}_{t}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
by

\mathfrak{h}_{t}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}\bigl\{\,t^{2\gamma}X{+}\theta(X):X_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\},

where
\mathfrak{m}
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{a}
in
\mathfrak{k}
, and where
\theta
is the Cartan involution.

3.2.8 Proposition
.

Each subspace
\mathfrak{h}_{t}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. If
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}
, then
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is the Lie algebra of the group
H_{t}
in Definition
3.2.7
.

This is a consequence of the following two computations.

3.2.9 Definition
.

Define a smooth action of the Lie group
A
on the linear space
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
by

(a\cdot t)_{\alpha}=a^{\alpha}t_{\alpha}\qquad\forall\alpha\in\Sigma.

3.2.10 Lemma
.

If
a\in A
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, then

(a{\cdot}t)^{2\gamma}=a^{2\gamma}t^{2\gamma}
. ∎

3.2.11 Lemma
.

If
a\in A
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, then
\mathfrak{h}_{a\cdot t}=\Ad_{a}\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. ∎

Proof.

We calculate that

\displaystyle\Ad_{a}\bigl\{\,t^{2\alpha}X{+}\theta(X):X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,a^{\alpha}t^{2\alpha}X+a^{-\alpha}\theta(X):X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=a^{-\alpha}\cdot\bigl\{\,(a\cdot t)^{2\alpha}X+\theta(X):X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,(a\cdot t)^{2\alpha}X+\theta(X):X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\},

which, in view of the formulas for
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
and
\mathfrak{h_{a\cdot t}}
in Definition
3.2.7
, proves the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2.8
.

If
t_{\alpha}{=}\pm 1
for all
\alpha
, then
t^{2\gamma}=1
for all
\gamma
, and therefore
\mathfrak{h}_{t}=\mathfrak{k}
. If
t_{\alpha}{\neq}0
for all
\alpha
, then it follows from the Lemma
3.2.11
that
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is the Lie algebra of
H_{t}
, and so in particular is a Lie algebra. For general
t
, it follows from a limiting argument that
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is a Lie algebra, since it is clear from Definition
3.2.7
that the
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
are the fibers of a smooth vector subbundle of the constant bundle over
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
with fiber
\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

We can describe the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{h_{t}}
more explicitly as follows. We have a direct sum decomposition

(3.2.12)

\mathfrak{h}_{t}=\mathfrak{k}_{t}\oplus\theta\bigl[\mathfrak{n}_{t}\bigr],

where

\mathfrak{k}_{t}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
t^{2\gamma}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigl\{\,t^{2\gamma}X_{\gamma}{+}\theta(X_{\gamma}):X_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{n}_{t}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
t^{2\gamma}=0\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}.

If, for a given
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, we set

(3.2.13)

I=\{\,\alpha\in\Sigma:t_{\alpha}\neq 0\,\},

then

t^{2\gamma}\neq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad n_{\gamma,\alpha}=0\,\,\forall\alpha\notin I\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]=0,

where
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
is the mutual kernel of all
\alpha\in I
, as in (
3.1.1
). So we may also write

\mathfrak{k}_{t}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]=0\end{subarray}}\bigl\{\,t^{2\gamma}X_{\gamma}{+}\theta(X_{\gamma}):X_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{n}_{t}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}.

3.2.14 Proposition
.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, the Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is conjugate, via an element of
A
, to a Lie subalgebra of the form

\mathfrak{h}_{I}=\mathfrak{k}_{I}\oplus\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I}\,

where:

(i)

I=\{\,\alpha\in\Sigma:t_{\alpha}\neq 0\,\}
, as in (
3.2.13
),

(ii)

\overline{n}_{I}=\theta[\mathfrak{n}_{I}]
, with
\mathfrak{n}_{I}
as in (
3.1.3
), and

(iii)

\mathfrak{k}_{I}=\mathfrak{k}\cap\mathfrak{m}_{I}
, with
\mathfrak{m}_{I}
as in
(
3.1.2
)
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}_{t}
normalizes,
\mathfrak{n_{t}}
, so that the displayed decomposition is a semidirect product decomposition.

Proof.

According to the definitions in the statement of the proposition,

(3.2.15)

\mathfrak{k}_{I}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]=0\end{subarray}}\bigl\{\,X_{\gamma}{+}\theta(X_{\gamma}):X_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\,\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{n}_{I}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})\\
\gamma[\mathfrak{a}_{I}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma},

so that
\mathfrak{h}_{I}=\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}
for any
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
with

t^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\pm 1&\alpha\in I\\
0&\alpha\notin I.\end{cases}

But the spaces
\mathfrak{k}_{t}
and
\mathfrak{n_{t}}
satisfy

(3.2.16)

\Ad_{a}[\mathfrak{k}_{t}]=\mathfrak{k}_{a\cdot t}\quad\text{and}\quad\Ad_{a}[\mathfrak{n}_{t}]=\mathfrak{n}_{t}.

So
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is conjugate to some
\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}
, as above.
∎

Let us turn now from Lie subalgebras to Lie subgroups.

3.2.17 Definition
.

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, denote by
H_{0,t}
the connected Lie subgroup of
G
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. For
I\subseteq\Sigma
denote by
H_{0,I}
the connected Lie subgroup of
G
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{I}
.

3.2.18 Lemma
.

Each
H_{0,t}
and each
H_{0,I}
is a closed subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2.14
, it suffices to prove that
H_{0,I}
is closed, for every
I\subseteq\Sigma
. The connected Lie subgroup
K_{0,I}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}_{I}
is the connected component of the identity in the centralizer of
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
in
K
, and is therefore closed, and indeed compact. The connected Lie subgroup with Lie algebra
\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I}
is a connected Lie subgroup of the exponential group
\overline{N}
, and is therefore closed in
\overline{N}
and in
G
. The group
H_{0,I}
is the product of these two subgroups, and is therefore closed.
∎

3.2.19 Lemma
.

If
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}
, then the Lie subgroup
H_{t}\subseteq G
in Definition
3.2.3
is equal to the product

H_{t}=MH_{0,t}
,
where, as before,
M
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{a}
in
K
.

Proof.

The group
M
is included in
K
and meets each connected component of
K
; see
[
9
, Lemma 4.11]
or
[
10
, Prop. 7.33]
. Moreover it commutes with
a_{t}
, and therefore

H_{t}=\Ad_{a_{t}}[K]=M\cdot\Ad_{a_{t}}[K_{0}]=MH_{0,t},

as required.
∎

This prompts the following definition:

3.2.20 Definition
.

If
t
is any element of
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, then we shall write

H_{t}=MH_{0,t}
. In addition, for
I\subseteq\Sigma
we shall write
H_{I}=MH_{0,I}
.

The group
H_{I}
is a closed subgroup of
G
, and it decomposes as a semidirect product

H_{I}=K_{I}\ltimes\overline{N}_{I},

where
K_{I}=MK_{0,I}
(and where
K_{0,I}
is the compact, connected Lie subgroup of
G
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}_{I}
, as in the proof of Lemma
3.2.18
).

3.2.21 Theorem
.

The normalizer in
G
of the Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{I}{\subseteq}\mathfrak{g}
is
A_{I}H_{I}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{h}_{I}
is conjugate to
\mathfrak{h}_{J}
if and only if
I=J
.

We shall prove this using the following Lie algebra computation:

3.2.22 Lemma
.

If
I
is any subset of
\Sigma
, then

\{\,X\in\mathfrak{h}_{I}:\text{\rm$\ad_{X}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent}\,\}=(\mathfrak{k}_{I}\cap\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g}))\oplus\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I},

where
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})
is the center of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

Let
X=Y{+}Z\in\mathfrak{k}_{I}\oplus\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I}
and suppose that
\ad_{X}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent, say
\ad_{X}^{k}{=}0
.
If we write
\mathfrak{g}
as a direct sum of
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
-root spaces (meaning eigenspaces for the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{a}_{I}
), then the diagonal part of
\ad_{X}^{k}
for this direct sum decomposition is
\ad_{Y}^{k}
. So
\ad_{Y}^{k}{=}0
. But
\ad_{Y}
acts as a skew-symmetric operator on
\mathfrak{g}
for the inner product
\langle\underline{\,\,}\,,\underline{\,\,}\rangle=B(\underline{\,\,}\,,\theta(\underline{\,\,}))
constructed from the bilinear form in
3.1
(ii) and the Cartan involution. So in fact
\ad_{Y}{=}0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2.21
.

It follows from Lemma
3.2.22
that

\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I}=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\,\cap\,\{\,X\in\mathfrak{h}_{I}:\text{$\ad_{Z}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent}\,\}.

and therefore

\operatorname{Normalizer}_{G}(\mathfrak{h}_{I})\subseteq\operatorname{Normalizer}_{G}(\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I}).

But according to Theorem
3.1.5
,

\operatorname{Normalizer}_{G}(\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I})=\overline{P}_{I}=\theta[P_{I}].

So to prove the first assertion in the statement of the theorem it suffices to prove that
\operatorname{Normalizer}_{\overline{P}_{I}}(\mathfrak{h}_{I})=A_{I}H_{I}
.

Let
g=ma\overline{n}
be an element of
\overline{P}_{I}=M_{I}A_{I}\overline{N}_{I}
that normalizes
\mathfrak{h}_{I}
. Since
a
and
\overline{n}
normalize
{\mathfrak{h}_{I}}
, the element
m
must normalize
\mathfrak{h}_{I}
, too. Since
\mathfrak{k}_{I}=\mathfrak{m}_{I}\cap\mathfrak{h}_{I}
, the element
m
must normalize not only
\mathfrak{h}_{I}
but
\mathfrak{k}_{I}
. But
K_{I}
is a maximal compact subgroup in
M_{I}
, and the normalizer of
K_{I}
in
M_{I}
is
K_{I}
itself. So
m\in K_{I}
and therefore
g=ma\overline{n}\in A_{I}H_{I}
, as required.

The second assertion is proved in the same way: an element of
G
that conjugates
\mathfrak{h}_{I}
into
\mathfrak{h}_{J}
must conjugate
\overline{n}_{I}
into
\overline{n}_{J}
, and from here we may apply Theorem
3.1.6
.
∎

3.2.23 Remark
.

It follows from the second assertion in the theorem that
H_{I}
and
H_{J}
are conjugate if and only if
I{=}J
. Moreover the theorem also implies that if
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, then
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
and
\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}
are conjugate if and only if for every
\alpha\in\Sigma
,

(3.2.24)

t_{\alpha}\neq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad t^{\prime}_{\alpha}\neq 0,

and that
H_{t}
and
H_{t^{\prime}}
are conjugate if and only if (
3.2.24
) holds.

3.2.25 Definition
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{g}{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
be the constant vector bundle over
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
with fiber
\mathfrak{g}
, and denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}
the smooth vector subbundle of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}
whose fiber over
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
is the Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. Thus

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}=\{\,(X,t)\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}:X\in\mathfrak{h}_{t}\,\}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2.4
.

As in the statement of the theorem, denote by
\mathbf{H}\subseteq\mathbf{G}
the closure of the subset

\{\,(h,t):h\in H_{t},\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}\,\}\subseteq\mathbf{G}

We shall temporarily denote by
H^{\prime}_{t}\subseteq G
the fiber of
\mathbf{H}
over
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
. It is a closed subgroup of
G
. In fact, using the split decomposition
G=M_{\Sigma}A_{\Sigma}
, every
H^{\prime}_{t}
is a subgroup of
M_{\Sigma}
.

Our first objective is to show that
H^{\prime}_{t}
is equal to the subgroup
H_{t}
from Definition
3.2.20
.
Fix
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
. Every element of
H_{t_{0}}
may be written as a product

(3.2.26)

h=m\exp(X_{1})\cdots\exp(X_{k}),

for some
m\in M
and some elements
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathfrak{h}_{t_{0}}
. By extending the
X_{j}
to smooth sections of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}
, we find that there is a smooth section

(3.2.27)

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{R}^{\sigma}\longrightarrow\mathbf{G}\\
t\longmapsto m\exp(X_{1,t})\cdots\exp(X_{k,t})\end{gathered}

whose value at every
t
lies in
H_{t}
, and whose value at
t_{0}
is
h
. This shows that
H_{t_{0}}\subseteq H^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
.

To prove that
H^{\prime}_{t}\subseteq H_{t}
, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, observe that by continuity of the adjoint action,
H^{\prime}_{t}
certainly lies within the normalizer of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
, for every
t
. And therefore it lies within
A_{I}H_{t}
, for
I=\{\,\alpha\in\Sigma:t_{\alpha}\neq 0\,\}
, and so in
M_{\Sigma}\cap A_{I}H_{t}
.

Now, the spectrum of every element of
K
, viewed as a linear operator on the finite-dimensional complex vector space
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
via the adjoint action, lies within the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
. Since the spectrum is conjugation invariant, the same is true of any element of
H_{t}
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}_{\times}
, and since in finite dimensions the spectrum varies continuously with the operator, the same is also true of any element in any fiber
H^{\prime}_{t}
of
\mathbf{H}
. But the only elements of
M_{\Sigma}\cap A_{I}H_{t}
whose spectrum is included in the unit circle are those in the subgroup
H_{t}
. Therefore
H^{\prime}_{t}\subseteq H_{t}
, as required.

It remains to prove that
\mathbf{H}
is a smooth submanifold of
\mathbf{G}
. The exponential map

\exp\colon\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}\!\to\!\mathbf{G}

is a diffeomorphism from a neighborhood of the zero section in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}
to a neighborhood of the group-identity section in
\mathbf{G}
. It restricts to a homeomorphism from an open neighborhood of the zero section in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}
to an open neighborhood of the group-identity section in
\mathbf{H}
. So the latter neighborhood is a smooth submanifold of
\mathbf{G}
. Pointwise multiplication by the sections (
3.2.27
) then shows that any point of
\mathbf{H}
has an open neighborhood in
\mathbf{H}
that is a smooth submanifold of
\mathbf{G}
.
∎

3.2.28 Example
.

Let
G=SL(2,\mathbb{R})
.
Equip
G
with the Iwasawa decomposition for which

K=SO(2),\quad A=\left\{\,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}e^{x}&0\\
0&e^{-x}\end{smallmatrix}\right]:x\in\mathbb{R}\,\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad N=\left\{\,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&y\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]:y\in\mathbb{R}\,\right\}.

There is just one simple restricted root, which is
\alpha\colon\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x&0\\
0&-x\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mapsto 2x
. If we identify
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
with
\mathbb{R}
accordingly, then

a_{t}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}t^{1/2}&0\\
0&t^{-1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad H_{t}=\left\{\,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}c&-t^{2}s\\
s&c\end{smallmatrix}\right]:c^{2}+t^{2}s^{2}=1\,\right\}.

3.3.
Construction of the Oshima space

We first assemble the family of homogeneous spaces
\{\,G/H_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\,\}
into a single smooth manifold
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
using following well-known result:

3.3.1 Lemma

(Godement’s criterion; see for instance
[
7
, Sec. 4.9]
)
.

Let
W
be a smooth manifold and let
R\subseteq W{\times}W
be an equivalence relation. There is a smooth manifold structure on
W/R
for which the quotient map
W\to W/R
is a smooth submersion if and only if

(i)

R
is a closed subset of
W{\times}W
and a smooth embedded submanifold, and

(ii)

the projection map
R\to W
onto either factor of
W
in
W{\times}W
is a smooth submersion.

3.3.2 Definition
.

We shall denote by
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
the quotient of
\mathbf{G}=G{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
by the equivalence relation

(g_{1},t_{2})\sim(g_{2},t_{2})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad t_{1}=t_{2}\quad\&\quad g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}\in H_{t_{1}},

and we shall denote by
[g,t]\in\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
the equivalence class of
(g,t)\in\mathbf{H}
.

By Godement’s criterion, the quotient
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
carries a unique smooth manifold structure for which the quotient map from
\mathbf{G}
is a submersion. Oshima’s space is obtained from
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
by a further quotient an action of the group
A
.

Thanks to Lemma
3.2.11
,
the subgroups
H_{t}
have the following conjugation equivariance property: if
a\in A
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, then

(3.3.3)

H_{a\cdot t}=\Ad_{a}[H_{t}].

In other words, for any
a\in A
we have

(3.3.4)

(h,t)\in\mathbf{H}\quad\Rightarrow\quad(a^{-1}ha,a^{-1}\cdot t)\in\mathbf{H}.

From this it follows that the right action of
A
on
\mathbf{G}
given by

(3.3.5)

(g,t)\cdot a=(ga,a^{-1}t),\qquad(a\in A,\ [g,t]\in\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H})

descends to a smooth right action of
A
on
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
.

3.3.6 Definition
.

The
Oshima space
of the real reductive group
G
(with the chosen Iwasawa decomposition
KAN
) is the quotient space

(3.3.7)

\mathscr{M}=(\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H})/A.

We shall denote by
[\![g,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}
the class in
\mathscr{M}
determined by a point
[g,t]\in\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
.

The following two lemmas generalize the Iwasawa decomposition
NAK=G
to a fiberwise product decomposition
NA\mathbf{H}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{G}
of an open subset of
\mathbf{G}
.

3.3.8 Lemma
.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
,
H_{t}\cap AN
is the trivial subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the lemma when
H_{t}=H_{I}
, for some
I\subseteq\Sigma
.
In this case there exists
X\in\mathfrak{a}_{I}
such that, if
\overline{n}\in\overline{\mathfrak{n}}_{I}
, then

\lim_{j\to+\infty}\exp(jX)\overline{n}\exp(-jX)=e,

while of course
\exp(jX)\in A_{I}
commutes with
K_{I}
. Since
\exp(X)
conjugates
AN
into itself, it follows that

k_{I}\overline{n}_{I}\in H_{I}\cap AN\quad\Rightarrow\quad k_{I}\in AN.

Since
AN
has no nontrivial compact subgroup, it follows that

H_{I}\cap AN=\overline{N}_{I}\cap AN.

But
\overline{N}_{I}\subseteq\overline{N}
, and
\overline{N}\cap AN
is well known to be trivial (this fact is also readily proved by the method above).
∎

3.3.9 Lemma
.

The multiplication map

\displaystyle N\times A\times\mathbf{H}\longrightarrow\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle(n,a,(h,t))\longmapsto(nah,t)

is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset of
\mathbf{G}
.

Proof.

The map is injective thanks to Lemma
3.3.8
and of full rank near
n=a=h=e
thanks to the direct sum decomposition formula

\mathfrak{h}_{t}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{g},

which is clear from the definition of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. Using left and right translations in
G
, we find that it is of full rank everywhere; compare
[
10
, Lemma 6.44]
.
∎

3.3.10 Proposition
.

The map

\displaystyle A\times\mathbf{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle(a,h,t)\longmapsto(t,ha,a^{-1}\cdot t)

is a closed embedding of smooth manifolds.

Proof.

Lemma
3.3.9
shows that the map is an injective immersion and a homeomorphism onto its image. So it remains to show the image is closed.

The elements
(t^{\prime},g,t^{\prime\prime})
in the image of the map satisfy

(3.3.11)

\Ad_{g^{-1}}[\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}]=\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime\prime}},

and by continuity the same relation holds for points in the closure. This being so, it follows from Proposition
3.2.14
that if
(t^{\prime},g,t^{\prime\prime})
is a point in the closure then
for all
\alpha\in\Sigma
,

t^{\prime}_{\alpha}\neq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad t^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}\neq 0.

Let
I=\{\,\alpha\in\Sigma:t^{\prime}_{\alpha}\neq 0\,\}
. It follows from continuity that
\operatorname{sign}(t^{\prime}_{\alpha})=\operatorname{sign}(t^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in I
, and therefore
t^{\prime\prime}=a^{-1}t^{\prime}
for some
a\in A
. Let us now write

(t^{\prime},g,t^{\prime\prime})=(t^{\prime},\gamma a,a^{-1}\cdot t^{\prime}),

where
\gamma=ga^{-1}
. It follows from (
3.3.11
) that

\Ad_{a^{-1}\gamma^{-1}}[\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}]=\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime\prime}}=\mathfrak{h}_{a^{-1}t^{\prime}}=\Ad_{a^{-1}}[\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}]

and therefore
\gamma^{-1}
normalizes
\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}
. By Theorem
3.2.21
,
\gamma\in H_{t^{\prime}}A_{I}
, so
\gamma=h\alpha
for some
h\in\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}}
,
\alpha\in A_{I}
.
As a result

(t^{\prime},g,t^{\prime\prime}))=(t^{\prime},h\alpha a,a^{-1}\cdot t^{\prime})=(t^{\prime},h\alpha a,(a\alpha)^{-1}\cdot t^{\prime}),

since
A_{I}
fixes
t^{\prime}
. We have therefore proved that the image of our map is closed.
∎

We can now prove that the main theorem of this section.

3.3.12 Theorem
.

There is a unique smooth manifold structure on the Oshima space
\mathscr{M}
for which the quotient map from
\mathbf{G}
to
\mathscr{M}
is a smooth submersion.

Proof.

The space
\mathscr{M}
is the quotient of
\mathbf{G}
by the equivalence relation

\bigl\{\,\bigl((g,t),(gha,a^{-1}\cdot t)\bigr)\in\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{G}:g\in G,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma},\,\,h\in H_{t},\,\,a\in A\,\bigr\}.

It follows from Proposition
3.3.10
that this is a closed subset and an embedded submanifold, and it therefore follows from Godement’s criterion that there is a unique smooth manifold structure on
\mathscr{M}
for with the quotient map
\mathbf{G}\to\mathscr{M}
is a smooth submersion.
∎

3.3.13 Remarks
.

(i) The theorem is equivalent to the assertion that there is a unique smooth manifold structure on
\mathscr{M}
for which the quotient map from
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
to

is a smooth submersion (but it is a bit more convenient to prove the theorem as stated).

(ii) In group action language, the action of

on

is free and proper. To take this remark even further, the smooth family of Lie groups

is a Lie groupoid over

, and (

) gives an action of

on

by Lie groupoid automorphisms. The Oshima space is then the quotient of the space
\mathbf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
by a free and proper right action of the Lie groupoid
\mathbf{H}\rtimes A
.

3.3.14 Theorem

(Oshima

]
)
.

The manifold
\mathscr{M}
is compact.

Proof.

The set

(3.3.15)

\{\,[\![k,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}:k\in K,\,\,\,|t_{\alpha}|{\leq}1\,\,\forall\alpha\in\Sigma\,\}

is a compact subset of

(it is the continuous image of a compact set), while the set

Using the

) is equal to

Using the

3.4.
Orbits in the Oshima space

The Oshima space is equipped with a smooth left action of
G
by left-multiplication:

\gamma\cdot[\![g,t]\!]=[\![\gamma g,t]\!]\qquad\forall\gamma,g\in G,\,\,\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}.

There are only finitely many
G
-orbits in
\mathscr{M}
. To see this, observe that the continuous map

(3.4.1)

\begin{gathered}\mathscr{M}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}/A\\
[\![g,t]\!]\longmapsto[t]\end{gathered}

passes to a bijection from the set of
G
-orbits in
\mathscr{M}
to the set of
A
-orbits in
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
. Thus, the orbit of a point
[\![g,t]\!]
is determined by the signs of the components
t_{\alpha}
,
\sign(t_{\alpha})\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

3.4.2 Lemma
.

Let
[\![g,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}
and let
I=\{\alpha\in\Sigma:t_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
as in (
3.2.13
). Then the orbit of
[\![g,t]\!]
is
G
-equivariantly diffeomorphic to the homogeneous space
G/A_{I}H_{I}
.

Proof.

There is a point of the form
[\![e,t]\!]
in the orbit for which

t_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\pm 1&\alpha\in I\\
0&\alpha\notin I.\end{cases}

The subgroup
H_{t}
is equal to
H_{I}
. Now

g\cdot[\![e,t]\!]=[\![e,t]\!]\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists h\in H_{I},\,\,\,\exists a\in A:\,\,\,g=ha\,\,\,\&\,\,\,a^{-1}\cdot t=t.

But the condition
a^{-1}\cdot t=t
is equivalent to
a\in A_{I}
, so

g\cdot[\![e,t]\!]=[\![e,t]\!]\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad g\in A_{I}N_{I},

as required.
∎

3.4.3 Example
.

Let
G=SL(2,\mathbb{R})
,
equipped with the Iwasawa decomposition described in Example
3.2.28
. If
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
denotes the extended complex plane (
i.e
, the Riemann sphere), then the map

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{G}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\\
(g,t)\longmapsto g\cdot(it)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty},\end{gathered}

where the dot indicates action by Mobius transformations, factors as the composition of the submersion
\mathbf{G}\to\mathscr{M}
with a diffeomorphism

\begin{gathered}\mathscr{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\\
[\![g,t]\!]\longmapsto g\cdot it\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}.\end{gathered}

One sees that there are three
G
-orbits, namely the upper and lower half-planes, both diffeomorphic to
G/H_{\Sigma}=G/K
, and the extended real line
\mathbb{R}\sqcup\{\infty\}
, diffeomorphic to
G/H_{\emptyset}A_{\emptyset}=G/MA\overline{N}
.

3.5.
Group bundle over the Oshima space

It follows from the definition of equivalence relation defining the Oshima space that if
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, then

[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]=[\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!]\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Ad_{g_{1}}[H_{t_{1}}]=\Ad_{g_{2}}[H_{t_{2}}].

3.5.1 Definition
.

For
g\in G
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
we shall write

H_{[\![g,t]\!]}=\Ad_{g}[H_{t}],

which is a closed subgroup of
G
. We then write

\mathscr{H}_{\mathscr{M}}=\bigl\{\,\bigl([\![g,t]\!],\gamma\bigr)\in\mathscr{M}\times G:\gamma\in H_{[\![g,t]\!]}\,\bigr\}.

3.5.2 Theorem
.

The space
\mathscr{H}_{\mathscr{M}}
is a closed submanifold of
\mathscr{M}\times G
, and the projection map to
\mathscr{M}
is a smooth submersion.

A simple way prove the theorem is to introduce the following coordinate patches on
\mathscr{M}
:

3.5.3 Lemma
.

The smooth map

\begin{gathered}N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\longrightarrow\mathscr{M}\\
(n,t)\longmapsto[\![n,t]\!]\end{gathered}

is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathscr{M}
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
3.3.8
that the map is injective.
By composing the diffeomorphism in Lemma
3.3.9

with the self-diffeomorphism

\displaystyle N\times A\times\mathbf{H}\longrightarrow N\times A\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle(n,a,(h,t))\longmapsto(n,a,(a^{-1}ha,a^{-1}t)),

we find that
the map

(3.5.4)

\begin{gathered}N\times A\times\mathbf{H}\longrightarrow\mathbf{G}\\
(n,a,(h,t))\longmapsto(nha,a^{-1}t)\end{gathered}

is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset. The composition of this, in turn, with the submersion
\mathbf{G}\to\mathscr{M}
is therefore a submersion onto its image. Considering the commuting diagram

\textstyle{N\times A\times\mathbf{H}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
(
3.5.4
)
\textstyle{\mathbf{G}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathscr{M},}

in which the left-vertical morphism is the obvious projection map and the bottom map is the map in the statement of this lemma, we see that the bottom map is also a submersion onto its image, and hence, since

\dim(N)+\dim(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})=\dim(\mathscr{M}),

it is a diffeomorphism onto its image.
∎

Obviously we can now cover
\mathscr{M}
with open sets of the form

\{\,[\![gn,t]\!]:n\in N,t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\,\}

for varying
g\in G
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5.2
.

After pulling back
\mathscr{H}_{\mathscr{M}}
along the diffeomorphism in Lemma
3.5.3
, we obtain the space

\bigl\{\,(n,t,\gamma)\in N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times G:\gamma\in\Ad_{n}[H_{t}]\,\bigr\}\subseteq N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times G.

Under the self-diffeomorphism

\begin{gathered}N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times G\longrightarrow N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times G\\
(n,t,\gamma)\longmapsto(n,t,n^{-1}\gamma n)\end{gathered}

this is in turn mapped to the subspace

(3.5.5)

\bigl\{\,(n,t,\gamma)\in N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times G:\gamma\in H_{t}\,\bigr\}\subseteq N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\times G.

But (
3.5.5
) is evidently a closed subset and a smooth submanifold. The theorem follows from this.
∎

3.6.
Embedding the Satake compactification into the Oshima space

Let us now return to the Satake compactification
\mathscr{X}
, as defined in Section
2
.

3.6.1 Definition
.

Let
G=KAN
be a real reductive group, and let
\mathscr{M}
be the associated Oshima space. We shall write

\mathscr{M}_{+}=\{\,[\![g,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}:t_{\alpha}>0\,\,\,\forall\alpha\in\Sigma\,\},

and then denote by
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}
the closure of
\mathscr{M}_{+}
in
\mathscr{M}
.

The space
\mathscr{M}_{+}
is an open subset and a single

G/K{\cdot}A_{\Sigma}
.

3.6.2 Theorem
.

The map

(3.6.3)

\begin{gathered}\mathscr{M}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}(G)\\
[\![g,t]\!]\mapsto H_{[\![g,t]\!]}\end{gathered}

restricts to a
G
-equivariant homeomorphism from
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}
to
\mathscr{X}
.

Proof.

Let us first show that the restricted map
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}\to\mathscr{X}
is injective. According to Proposition
3.2.14
, if

H_{[\![g^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\!]}=H_{[\![g^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime\prime}]\!]}
then for every
\alpha\in\Sigma
, either
t^{\prime}_{\alpha}
and

are both zero or both strictly positive. Thus

H_{[\![g^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\!]}=H_{[\![g^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime\prime}]\!]}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\exists a\in A:a\cdot t^{\prime}=t^{\prime\prime}.

Therefore, setting
g{=}g^{\prime\prime}a
, we need only show that

H_{[\![g^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\!]}=H_{[\![g,t^{\prime}]\!]}\quad\Rightarrow\quad[\![g^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\!]=[\![g,t^{\prime}]\!].

But

H_{[\![g^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\!]}=H_{[\![g,t^{\prime}]\!]}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Ad_{g^{-1}g^{\prime}}[H_{t^{\prime}}]=H_{t^{\prime}},

and the required result therefore follows from Theorem
3.2.21
.

Next, Theorem
3.5.2
and Lemma
2.1.5
imply that the map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{S}(G)
is continuous. Since
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}
is compact, and the image of
\mathscr{M}_{+}
in
\mathscr{X}
is the dense open subset of all maximal compact subgroups, the image of
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}

is
\mathscr{X}
. Being a bijection from a compact space, the map is a homeomorphism, and
G
-equivariance is evident from Definition
3.5.1
.
∎

Theorem
3.6.2
shows that that the Satake compactification decomposes into finitely many
G
-orbits, as in Section
3.4
. To be specific, for
I\subseteq\Sigma
let
t_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
be defined by

(3.6.4)

t_{I,\alpha}=\begin{cases}1&\alpha\in I\\
0&\alpha\notin I,\end{cases}

and let

be the image under (
3.6.3
) of
[\![e,t_{I}]\!]\in\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}
.
Using (
3.4.1
) shows that

is a complete set of orbit representatives for
the Satake compactification. By Lemma

, the
G
-orbit
\mathscr{X}_{I}
of

is isomorphic to
G/A_{I}H_{I}
, and there is therefore
an isomorphism of

-sets

\mathscr{X}=\bigsqcup_{I\subseteq\Sigma}\mathscr{X}_{I}\cong\bigsqcup_{I\subseteq\Sigma}G/A_{I}H_{I}

Concerning the topology on this disjoint union, it follows from the continuity of the map (

.

4.
The Oshima groupoid

In this section we shall construct our
Oshima groupoid
,
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
, whose space of units is the Oshima space. The reduction of
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
to the Satake compactification
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}\cong\mathscr{X}
will be homeomorphic to the Satake groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
of Definition
2.2.1
, thus endowing the Satake groupoid with a smooth structure.

4.1.
Normal subgroupoids and quotients of Lie groupoids

4.1.1 Definition

(See for instance
[
13
, Sec. 2.2]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a Lie groupoid. A
normal Lie subgroupoid

of
\mathscr{G}
is a Lie subgroupoid
\mathscr{H}
with the following properties:

(i)

the inclusion
\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{G}
is a closed embedding of smooth manifolds,

(ii)

\mathscr{H}
includes every identity morphism of
\mathscr{G}
,

(iii)

\mathscr{H}
consists entirely of isotropy elements (that is, elements whose source and target are equal), and

(iv)

if
\alpha\in\mathscr{H}
and
\gamma\in\mathscr{G}
, and if
\operatorname{target}(\alpha)=\operatorname{source}(\gamma)
, then
\gamma\circ\alpha\circ\gamma^{-1}\in\mathscr{H}
.

If
\mathscr{H}
is a normal Lie subgroupoid of a Lie groupoid
\mathscr{G}
, then we may place on
\mathscr{G}
the equivalence relation

\gamma\simeq\eta\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{target}(\gamma)=\operatorname{target}(\eta)\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\gamma^{-1}\eta\in\mathcal{H}.

The set
\mathscr{G}/\mathscr{H}
of equivalence classes is a groupoid with the same object space as
\mathscr{G}
and the structure maps

\operatorname{source}([\gamma])=\operatorname{source}(\gamma),\quad\operatorname{target}([\gamma])=\operatorname{target}(\gamma)\quad\text{and}\quad[\gamma_{1}]\circ[\gamma_{2}]=[\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2}].

4.1.2 Lemma
.

If
\mathscr{H}
is a normal Lie subgroupoid of a Lie groupoid
\mathscr{G}
, then there is a unique smooth manifold structure on
\mathscr{G}/\mathscr{H}
for which the quotient map
\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{G}/\mathscr{H}
is a submersion. With this smooth manifold structure,
\mathscr{G}/\mathscr{H}
is a Lie groupoid.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Godement’s criterion, Lemma
3.3.1
.
∎

4.2.
The transformation groupoid and the Oshima groupoid

The transformation groupoid associated to the action of
G
on the Oshima space
\mathscr{M}
is of course

G\ltimes\mathscr{M}=\bigl\{\,\bigl([\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!],g,[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]\bigr)\in\mathscr{M}\times G\times\mathscr{M}:[\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!]=g{\cdot}[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]\,\bigr\},

with the structure maps

\displaystyle\operatorname{source}\bigl([\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!],g,[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]\bigr)

\displaystyle=[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]

\displaystyle\operatorname{target}\bigl([\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!],g,[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]\bigr)

\displaystyle=[\![g_{2},t_{1}]\!]

\displaystyle\bigl([\![g_{3},t_{3}]\!],g^{\prime\prime},[\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!]\bigr)\circ\bigl([\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!],g^{\prime},[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl([\![g_{3},t_{3}]\!],g^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime},[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]\bigr).

The smooth famliy of groups
\mathscr{H}_{\mathscr{M}}
in Definition
3.5.1
may be viewed as a normal Lie subgroupoid of the transformation groupoid:

4.2.1 Lemma
.

The subspace

\mathscr{H}_{\mathscr{M}}\cong\bigl\{\,\bigl([\![g,t]\!],h,[\![g,t]\!]\bigr):[\![g,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M},\,\,h\in H_{[\![g,t]\!]}\,\bigr\}\subseteq G\ltimes\mathscr{M}

is a normal Lie subgroupoid of the transformation groupoid.

Proof.

Condition (i) in Definition
4.1.1
is a consequence of Theorem
3.5.2
. Conditions (ii) and (iii) are clear, and condition (iv) follows from the conjugation relation
\Ad_{\gamma}H_{[\![g,t]\!]}=H_{\gamma{\cdot}[\![g,t]\!]}
.
∎

4.2.2 Definition
.

Let
G=KAN
be a real reductive group, and let
\mathscr{M}
be its Oshima space. The
Oshima groupoid

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
is the quotient of the transformation groupoid
G\ltimes\mathscr{M}
by the normal Lie subgroupoid
\mathscr{H}_{\mathscr{M}}
in Lemma
4.2.1
.

4.3.
Embedding the Satake groupoid into the Oshima groupoid

4.3.1 Definition
.

We shall use the notation

[\![g,g_{1},t]\!]\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}

to indicate the equivalence class of the morphism

\bigl([\![g_{2},t]\!],g,[\![g_{1},t]\!]\bigr)

in
G\ltimes\mathscr{M}
.

4.3.2 Theorem
.

The map

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}\ni[\![g,g_{1},t]\!]\longmapsto gH_{[\![{g_{1},t]\!]}}\in\mathscr{C}(G)

restricts to an isomorphism of topological groupoids from the reduction of the Oshima groupoid to
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}\subseteq\mathscr{M}
to the Satake groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
.

Proof.

A straightforward check shows that the map

\displaystyle G\ltimes\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}

\displaystyle([\![g_{2},t_{2}]\!],g,[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!])

\displaystyle\longmapsto gH_{[\![g_{1},t_{1}]\!]}

is a morphism of groupoids; it is the homeomorphism of Theorem
3.6.2
on objects. Moreover, the preimage of the object space of
\mathscr{X}
of
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
is precisely the reduction of the normal subgroupoid of Lemma
4.2.1
to
\overline{M_{+}}
. From this it follows easily that this map descends to a bijection from the reduction of
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
to
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{+}}
onto
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{X}}
.
Lemma
2.2.2
shows that we have convergence

\gamma_{i}H_{[\![g_{i},t_{i}]\!]}\to\gamma H_{[\![g,t]\!]}

in the Satake groupoid if and only if we have

H_{[\![g_{i},t_{i}]\!]}\to H_{[\![g,t]\!]},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{i}h_{i}\to\gamma h,\quad\exists h_{i}\in H_{[\![g_{i},t_{i}]\!]},\ \exists h\in H_{[\![g,t]\!]}.

By Theorem

, this is equivalent to

[\![\gamma_{i},g_{i},t_{i}]\!]\to[\![\gamma,g,t]\!]

in the Oshima groupoid
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}
.
The result follows.
∎

5.

5.1.
Local coordinates on the Oshima groupoid

We proved in Lemma
3.5.3
that the obvious map

N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\longrightarrow\bigl\{\,[\![n,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}:n\in N,\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\,\bigr\}

is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathscr{M}
. We shall now describe the reduced groupoid

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}}=\{\,\gamma\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}:\operatorname{source}(\gamma)\in\mathcal{U}\,\,\,\&\,\,\,\operatorname{target}(\gamma)\in\mathcal{U}\,\}.

Form the smooth manifold

\mathcal{W}=\bigl\{\,\bigl((n_{2},t_{2}),a,(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)\in N{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}{\times}A{\times}N{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}:t_{2}=a\cdot t_{1}\,\bigr\}.

This is a Lie groupoid with object space
N{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, and the groupoid operations

\displaystyle\operatorname{source}((n_{2},t_{2}),a,(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=(n_{1},t_{1})

\displaystyle\operatorname{target}((n_{2},t_{2}),a,(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=(n_{2},t_{2})

\displaystyle((n_{3},t_{3}),a_{2},(n_{2},t_{2})\bigr)\circ((n_{2},t_{2}),a_{1},(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl((n_{3},t_{3}),a_{2}a_{1},(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr).

It is the Cartesian product of the pair groupoid on
N
with the transformation groupoid for the action
A
on
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
in Definition
3.2.9
.

5.1.1 Theorem
.

The smooth map

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{W}\longrightarrow\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}}\\
\bigl((n_{2},t_{2}),a,(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)\longmapsto[\![n_{2}an_{1}^{-1},n_{1},t_{1}]\!]\end{gathered}

(
notation of Definition
4.3.1
)
is an isomorphism of Lie groupoids.

Proof.

Is straightforward to check that the map is a morphism of Lie groupoids.
To see that it is surjective, suppose that
[\![g,g_{1},t]\!]\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}}
. Then

[\![g_{1},t]\!]\in\mathcal{U}\quad\text{and}\quad[\![gg_{1},t]\!]\in\mathcal{U},

from which it follows that

(g_{1},t)=(n_{1}h_{1}a_{1},a_{1}^{-1}\cdot t_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad(gg_{1},t)=(n_{2}h_{2}a_{2},a_{2}^{-1}\cdot t_{2})

for some
n_{1},n_{2}\in N
,
h_{1}\in H_{t_{1}}
,
h_{2}\in H_{t_{2}}
,
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
(the identities in the display are identities in
G{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
). This implies that

[\![g,g_{1},t]\!]=[\![g,n_{1}h_{1}a_{1},t]\!]=[\![g,n_{1},t_{1}]\!]

and that
gn_{1}h_{1}a_{1}=n_{2}h_{2}a_{2}
. Write this as

g=n_{2}h_{2}a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1}n_{1}^{-1}=n_{2}an_{1}^{-1}(n_{1}a^{-1}h_{2}an_{1}^{-1})(n_{1}h_{1}^{-1}n_{1}^{-1}),

where
a=a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}
. The two terms in parentheses on the right-hand side of the display are elements of
H_{[\![n_{1},t_{1}]\!]}
, and so by definition of the Oshima groupoid,

[\![g,g_{1},t]\!]=[\![n_{2}an_{1}^{-1},n_{1},t_{1}]\!],

as required.

To prove that the map is injective, we start by noting that if

[\![n_{2}an_{1}^{-1},n_{1},t]\!]=[\![n_{2}^{\prime}a^{\prime}n_{1}^{\prime}{}^{-1},n_{1}^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\!]\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}},

then
n_{1}{=}n_{1}^{\prime}
and
t{=}t^{\prime}
by Lemma
3.5.3
, so that

[\![n_{2}an_{1}^{-1},n_{1},t]\!]=[\![n_{2}^{\prime}a^{\prime}n_{1}^{-1},n_{1},t]\!]\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}}.

It then follows from the definition of the Oshima groupoid that

n_{2}an_{1}^{-1}=n_{2}^{\prime}a^{\prime}n_{1}^{-1}(n_{1}h_{1}n_{1}^{-1})

for some
h_{1}\in H_{t_{1}}
. This means that
n_{2}a=n_{2}^{\prime}a^{\prime}h_{1}
,
and from this and Lemma
3.3.9
it follows that
n_{2}{=}n_{2}^{\prime}
and
a{=}a^{\prime}
.

It remains to prove that our smooth map is a diffeomorphism, and for this we proceed as in the proof of Lemma
3.5.3
. The relevant commutative diagram here is

\textstyle{N{\times}A{\times}N{\times}A{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(n_{2},a_{2},n_{1},a_{,}t)\mapsto([n_{2}a_{2},t],[n_{1}a_{1},t])}
\textstyle{\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}\times_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}}\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{W}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}},}

where the left and right vertical maps are

(n_{2},a_{2},n_{1},a_{1},t)\mapsto\bigl((n_{2},a_{2}\cdot t),a_{2}a_{1}^{-1},(n_{1},a_{1}\cdot t)\bigr)\\
\text{and}\quad([g_{2},t],[g_{1},t])\mapsto[\![g_{2}g_{1}^{-1},g_{1},t]\!],

respectively, and the bottom horizontal map is from the statement of our theorem. All maps other than the last-mentioned are submersions onto their images, and the left vertical map is surjective, so the bottom horizontal map is actually a submersion too. Counting dimensions, we find that it is a diffeomorphism.
∎

5.2.
Codimension-one submanifolds with normal crossings

In this subsection only,
\mathscr{M}
will denote
any
smooth manifold without boundary. The following definition is taken from
[
19
, Sec. 2.3]
, where the term
découpage
is used.

5.2.1 Definition
.

A
normal crossing structure
on
\mathscr{M}
is a finite collection of closed embeddings
\mathcal{S}{\to}\mathscr{M}
of smooth, codimension-one submanifolds without boundary such that around any point
m\in\mathscr{M}
there are local coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
centered at
m
with respect to which each
\mathcal{S}
passing through
m
is one of the coordinate hyperplanes
\{x_{j}=0\}
, with distinct
\mathcal{S}
corresponding to distinct
j
.

Monthubert
[
19
]
and Nistor, Weinsten and Xu
[
21
]
, have associated to a normal crossing structure on
\mathscr{M}
a Lie groupoid with object space
\mathscr{M}
, or rather several groupoids that differ in minor respects (they all share the same Lie algebroid). We shall consider only the following special case, and present the version of the construction that is best suited to our needs.

5.2.2 Definition
.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be a smooth manifold with normal crossing structure
\{\mathcal{S}{\to}\mathscr{M}\}
. We shall say that the normal crossing structure is
simple
if

\mathscr{M}
is connected, if each
\mathcal{S}
is connected, and if, for every
k
,
the mutual complement

\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}\,\mathscr{M}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{j}

of any set of
k
hypersurfaces in
\mathscr{M}
has precisely
2^{k}
connected components.

When
\mathscr{M}
is a smooth, connected manifold with simple normal crossing structure, we may associate to
\mathscr{M}
a groupoid
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
with object space
\mathscr{M}
as follows:

(i)

The morphism space is the set of triples
(m^{\prime},T,m)
, where

(a)

m^{\prime}
and
m
lie on the same collection
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k}
of hypersurfaces,

(b)

m^{\prime}
and
m
lie on the same side of all the hypersurfaces on which they do not lie, and

(c)

T=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is a family of orientation-preserving linear isomorphisms

T_{j}\colon N_{m}\mathcal{S}_{j}\longrightarrow N_{m^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}_{j}\qquad(j=1,\dots,k)

between fibers of the normal bundles of the hypersurfaces
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{k}
in
\mathscr{M}
. (The hypothesis of simplicity implies that each normal bundle is orientable; moreover if
T_{j}
is orientation-preserving for one orientation, then it is orientation-preserving for the other, too. So a choice of orientation is not required.)

(ii)

The source and target maps are

\operatorname{source}\colon(m^{\prime},T,m)\mapsto m\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{target}\colon(m^{\prime},T,m)\mapsto m^{\prime},

and the composition law is

(m^{\prime\prime},T^{\prime},m^{\prime})\circ(m^{\prime},T,m)=(m^{\prime\prime},T^{\prime}T,m),

where
T^{\prime}T=\{\,T^{\prime}_{1}T_{1},\dots,T^{\prime}_{k}T_{k}\,\}
.

See
[
19
, Sec. 3]
.

To understand how to equip the groupoid
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
with a Lie groupoid structure, let us consider the model case, in which
\mathscr{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and in which the hypersurfaces
\mathcal{S}\to\mathscr{M}
are the hyperplanes
\{x_{j}=0\}
for
j=1,\dots,p
. Denote by
A
the abelian group
\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
, and let
A
act on
\mathscr{M}
by coordinate-wise multiplication in the first
p
places. Then form the transformation Lie groupoid

A\ltimes\mathscr{M}=\bigl\{\,(m^{\prime},a,m)\in\mathscr{M}{\times}A{\times}\mathscr{M}:m^{\prime}=a\cdot m\,\bigr\},

in which the target and source maps are the projections on the the first and third factors, and the composition law is

(m^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime},m^{\prime})\circ(m^{\prime},a,m)=(m^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}a,m).

In the model case, the normal bundle for each
\mathcal{S}{\to}\mathscr{M}
identifies with the constant bundle with fiber
\mathbb{R}
in the obvious way, and so if
(m^{\prime},T,m)
is a morphism in
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
, then to each
T_{j}
there corresponds to a positive real number. In the model case there is therefore an isomorphism

\begin{gathered}\Gamma(\mathscr{M})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}A\ltimes\mathscr{M}\\
(m^{\prime},T,m)\longmapsto(m^{\prime},a(T),m),\end{gathered}

where

a(T)_{j}=\begin{cases}T_{j}&m_{j}=0\\
m^{\prime}_{j}/m_{j}&m_{j}\neq 0.\end{cases}

The isomorphism gives
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
a Lie groupoid structure in the model case. The general case is handled similarly, using local coordinates adapted to the embeddings
\mathcal{S}{\to}\mathscr{M}
, as in Definition
5.2.1
.

5.2.3 Remark
.

We might call the Lie groupoid
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
above the
b
-groupoid
associated to the given simple normal crossing structure on
\mathscr{M}
, because it corresponds (up to some minor variations concerning connectedness of source fibers) to the groupoid introduced by Monthubert
[
18
]
and Nistor, Weinstein and Xu
[
21
]
in the study of Melrose’s
b
-calculus
[
15
]
.
The
C^{\infty}(M)
-module of vector fields on
\mathscr{M}
that are tangent to each of the embeddings
\mathcal{S}{\to}\mathscr{M}
is finitely generated and projective, and is therefore the module of smooth sections of a vector bundle over
\mathscr{M}
, called the
b
-tangent bundle

T^{b}M
. It is equipped with a Lie bracket structure on its smooth sections and a natural morphism
T^{b}\mathscr{M}\to TM
, and these together give
T^{b}\mathscr{M}
the structure of a Lie algebroid. This is the Lie algebroid of
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
.

5.3.
Normal crossing structure on the Oshima space

We shall now return to the Oshima space
\mathscr{M}
associated to a real reductive group, as in Section
3
. For each
\alpha\in\Sigma
we may define a subset
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathscr{M}
by

S_{\alpha}=\{\,[\![g,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}:t_{\alpha}=0\,\}.

5.3.1 Lemma
.

Each
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is a smooth, closed, codimension one submanifold of
\mathscr{M}
, and the collection of closed embeddings
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}{\to}\mathscr{M}

(
\alpha\in\Sigma
)
gives
\mathscr{M}
a simple normal crossing structure.

Proof.

This is made evident by the coordinatization in Lemma
3.5.3
.
∎

5.3.2 Remark
.

Another way to see the normal crossing structure is to observe that
the finite group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\Sigma}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
in the following way: think of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
as the multiplicative group
\{\pm 1\}
, and then define

(s\cdot t)_{\alpha}=s_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}\qquad\forall\alpha\in\Sigma

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\Sigma}
action on
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
passes to compatible actions on
\mathbf{G}
,
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
and
\mathscr{M}
, all commuting with the left action of
G
. For example, the action on
\mathscr{M}
is

s\cdot[\![g,t]\!]=[\![g,st]\!]\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\Sigma},\,\,\,\forall[\![g,t]\!]\in\mathscr{M}.

The fixed point set in
\mathscr{M}
associated to any element of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\Sigma}
is automatically a smooth submanifold. The fixed point sets for the standard generators in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\Sigma}
are our hypersurface embeddings
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}{\to}\mathscr{M}
.

5.4.
Identification of the Oshima groupoid with the b-groupoid

The action of
G
on the Oshima space
\mathscr{M}
maps each
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
into iteslf, and it also maps each component of each
\mathscr{M}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
into itself. As a result the induced action on the normal bundle of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}{\to}\mathscr{M}
is orientation-preserving, and we may define a morphism of Lie groupoids

(5.4.1)

\begin{gathered}G\ltimes\mathscr{M}\longrightarrow\Gamma(\mathscr{M})\\
(m^{\prime},\gamma,m)\longmapsto(m^{\prime},[\gamma],m),\end{gathered}

where
[\gamma]
indicates the induced action of
\gamma\in G
on normal bundles (from the fibers at
m
to the fibers at
m^{\prime}
).

5.4.2 Theorem
.

The map (
5.4.1
) induces an isomorphism of Lie groupoids from
\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}

(
viewed as a quotient of the action groupoid
)
to the Lie groupoid
\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
.

Our proof of Theorem
5.4.2
will use the following technical computation.

5.4.3 Lemma
.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
and let
h\in H_{t_{0}}
. There is a smooth section of
\mathbf{H}
, which we shall write as
t\mapsto(h_{t},t)
such that
h_{t_{0}}=h
and

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t=t_{0}}h_{t}=0

for every
\alpha\in\Sigma
such that
t_{0,\alpha}=0
.

Proof.

We may write

h_{t_{0}}=k\exp(Y_{1})\cdots\exp(Y_{k})

for some
k\in M
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\in\mathfrak{h}_{t_{0}}
. We define

h_{t}=k\exp(Y_{1,t})\cdots\exp(Y_{k,t}),

where each
Y_{j,t}
has the form

Y_{j,t}=Z+\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta^{+}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}t^{2\gamma}X_{\gamma}{+}\theta(X_{\gamma})

for some
Z\in\mathfrak{m}
,
X_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
, and where
Y_{j,t_{0}}=Y_{j}
. Compare Definition
3.2.7
. Since
t^{2\gamma}
vanishes to second order in
t_{\alpha}
when
t_{\alpha}=0
,
the map
t\mapsto h_{t}
has the required properties.
∎

5.4.4 Lemma
.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
and suppose that
t_{0,\alpha}=0
for some
\alpha\in\Sigma
. Every element of
H_{t_{0}}
, viewed as a diffeomorphism from
\mathscr{M}
to itself using the action of
G
on
\mathscr{M}
, acts as the identity operator on the fiber of the normal bundle of the hypersurface
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
at the point
[\![e,t_{0}]\!]\in\mathscr{M}
.

Proof.

The fiber of the normal bundle of the hypersurface
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
at the point
[\![e,t_{0}]\!]\in\mathscr{M}
is generated by the tangent vector

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t_{0}}[\![e,t]\!]\in T_{[\![e,t_{0}]\!]}\mathscr{M}.

Given
h\in H_{t_{0}}
, if
h_{t}
is as in the previous lemma, with
h_{t_{0}}=h
, then by the Leibniz rule and the lemma,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t_{0}}[\![e,t]\!]

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t_{0}}[\![h_{t},t]\!]=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t_{0}}[\![h,t]\!]+\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t_{0}}[\![h_{t},t_{0}]\!]=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\right|_{t_{0}}h\cdot[\![e,t]\!],

which proves the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4.2
.

Recall that the Oshima groupoid may be obtained as the quotient of the transformation groupoid
G\ltimes\mathscr{M}
by the smooth family of groups
H_{[\![g,t]\!]}
over
\mathscr{M}
, viewed as a normal subgroupoid of the transformation groupoid. It follows from Lemma
5.4.4
that if
h\in H_{[\![g,t]\!]}
, and if
t_{\alpha}=0
, then
h
acts as the identity operator on the fiber of the normal bundle of the hypersurface
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
at the point
[\![e,t_{0}]\!]\in\mathscr{M}
. So the morphism of Lie groupoids

G\ltimes\mathscr{M}\longrightarrow\Gamma(\mathscr{M})

in (
5.4.1
) factors through a morphism of Lie groupoids

(5.4.5)

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}\longrightarrow\Gamma(\mathscr{M})

It remains to prove that the latter is a diffeomorphism.

For this, we will compose (
5.4.5
) with two local diffeomorphisms. Firstly, we have the Lie groupoid isomorphism

(5.4.6)

\mathcal{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}}

from Theorem
5.1.1
. Secondly, the diffeomorphism of Lemma
3.5.3
gives a Lie groupoid isomorphism

(5.4.7)

\Gamma(\mathscr{M})|_{\mathcal{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Gamma(N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}),

where the right-hand side is the
b
-groupoid of
N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
with simple normal crossing structure coming from the coordinate hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
. Composing (
5.4.6
), (
5.4.5
) and (
5.4.7
), we get the morphism of Lie groupoids
\mathcal{W}\to\Gamma(N\times\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})
given by

\bigl((n_{2},t_{2}),a,(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)\longmapsto\bigl((n_{2},t_{2}),[n_{2}an_{1}^{-1}],(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr).

Since it is evident that
n_{2}
and
n_{1}
act trivially on normal bundles for
N{\times}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
, we may also write this as

\bigl((n_{2},t_{2}),a,(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr)\longmapsto\bigl((n_{2},t_{2}),[a],(n_{1},t_{1})\bigr),

which is an isomorphism of Lie groupoids.

This proves that

\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}|_{\mathcal{U}}\cong\Gamma(\mathscr{M})|_{\mathcal{U}}.

To complete the proof, note that the morphism of Lie groupoids (
5.4.5
) is equivariant with respect to the
G
-actions

g\cdot(m^{\prime},\gamma,m)=(g\cdot m^{\prime},g\gamma g^{-1},g\cdot m),\qquad\bigl(g\in G,\ (m^{\prime},\gamma,m)\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}\bigr),

and

g\cdot(m^{\prime},T,m)=(g\cdot m^{\prime},[g]T[g^{-1}],m),\qquad\bigl(g\in G,\ (m^{\prime},T,m)\in\mathscr{G}_{\mathscr{M}}\bigr).

Since the
G
-translates of
\mathcal{U}
cover
\mathscr{M}
, the result follows.
∎
