The miR-212-3p/transferrin axis promotes bone regeneration by modulating iron homeostasis through Schwann cell Exosome-BMSCs communication
Authors: Yukun Jia, Ningdao Li, Dagang Tang, Wendan Pu, Zhiqiang Feng, Yingtao Duan, Yafei Zhu, Juan Wang, Xiaoji Luo, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional bone regeneration relies on cross talk between nerves and bone, yet the regulatory mechanisms linking Schwann cells (SCs) to osteoporotic bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (OP-BMSCs) remain unclear. This study demonstrated that SC-Exosomes (Exos) promoted the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs, and miRNA-mRNA sequencing revealed that SC-Exos, which contained the key mediator miR-212-3p, facilitated osteogenesis by suppressing the ferroptosis pathway in OP-BMSCs. miR-212-3p overexpression combined with transferrin (TF) knockdown or overexpression confirmed that miR-212-3p suppresses TF expression and validated the role of the miR-212-3p/TF axis, which maintained iron homeostasis in BMSCs, alleviated iron overload and oxidative stress, and promoted osteogenic differentiation. Furthermore, an injectable SC-Exos-loaded gelatin methacrylate hydrogel (SC-Exos/GelMA) targeting BMSCs was constructed. In vitro, SC-Exos/GelMA enabled sustained release of Exos and promoted BMSCs osteogenesis. In vivo, SC-Exos/GelMA downregulated TF expression via the miR-212-3p/TF axis, mitigated ferroptosis, restored iron homeostasis, accelerated angiogenesis/neurogenesis, and promoted bone regeneration. This study not only elucidated the role of the miR-212-3p/TF axis in the modulation of BMSCs iron homeostasis, but also constructed an injectable SC-Exos/GelMA hydrogel for bone regeneration, offering mechanistic insights and a translational therapeutic strategy for bone regeneration in clinic.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Schwann cell-derived exosomes, Ferroptosis, Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, Bone regeneration

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a major health condition worldwide characterized by decreased bone mass and the deterioration of bone microarchitecture [[1], [2], [3], [4]], which results in approximately 8.9 million osteoporosis-related fractures each year [5]. Among its various forms, glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) represents the most common type of secondary osteoporosis [6]. Patients with disrupted bone homeostasis have a significantly elevated fracture risk and an impaired capacity for bone repair and exhibit delayed healing. Although classical antiosteoporotic agents such as bisphosphonates have been widely adopted in clinical practice, their efficacy in promoting local bone defect repair remains limited, and their use is frequently associated with adverse systemic effects [7,8]. Therefore, the development of innovative and effective therapeutic strategies for osteoporosis patients is of paramount importance (see Scheme 1).

(B) Development of the delivery system: SC-Exos were loaded into an injectable GelMA hydrogel to construct a localized sustained release delivery system for the repair of bone defects in a GIOP model. (C) Mechanism of action of SC-Exos: SC-Exos deliver miR-212-3p to BMSCs, which, restores intracellular iron homeostasis and suppresses ferroptosis by specifically inhibiting TF expression, thereby promoting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and bone regeneration.

Bone is a highly innervated organ whose homeostasis is governed by the precisely regulated nerve-bone axis [9,10]. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that neural components act as the first responders after bone injury, shaping the local microenvironment to orchestrate bone regeneration [11,12]. The coordinated interplay among the neural, vascular, immune, and stem cell compartments creates a proregenerative niche that is essential for skeletal repair. In the context of osteoporotic bone healing, peripheral nerves modulate the microenvironment by releasing neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors, thereby potentiating bone regeneration [13,14]. Schwann cells (SCs) are pivotal regulators in the peripheral nervous system that are responsible for maintaining the integrity of the peripheral nervous system and innervating new bone. Mechanistically, SCs regulate the migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) through paracrine mechanisms, thereby influencing the process of bone repair [15,16]. Jones et al. [17] demonstrated that the loss of SC regulation due to dnervation led to functional defects in bone stem cells, thereby impairing bone healing. Exosomes (Exos), as important intercellular messengers of intercellular communication that transport various bioactive molecules, such as miRNAs, proteins and lipids, have shown considerable potential for application in the field of bone regeneration [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. For example, SC-Exos have been leveraged to functionalize porous Ti6Al4V scaffolds, significantly improving their bioactivity and accelerating bone repair [22]. Similarly, the infusion of SC-Exos into biomimetic periosteum markedly improved neurovascular coupling and bone regeneration [23].

However, existing research predominantly focused on the role of SC-Exos in normal physiological or acute bone injury models. It remains unclear whether the osteogenic efficacy of SC-Exos is maintained in pathological microenvironments such as GIOP, and which core functional molecules and specific signaling pathways underlie their actions. Furthermore, developing an appropriate delivery system tailored to the pathological osteoporotic microenvironment that enables sustained release of SC-Exos, thereby overcoming the limitations of their short in vivo half-life and poor targeting, remains a critical technical challenge. These gaps in our current knowledge represent a major barrier to the clinical translation of SC-Exos.

On the basis of the aforementioned research background, this study systematically investigated the role of SC-Exos in bone regeneration under GIOP conditions. First, the effects of SC-Exos on osteogenic differentiation of osteoporotic bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (OP-BMSCs) were evaluated in vitro. Subsequently, transcriptomic sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed following SC-Exos application both in vitro and in vivo to identify key signaling pathways involved in SC-Exos-mediated osteogenesis, which were then systematically validated at both levels. Through miRNA sequencing combined with bioinformatics analysis, miR-212-3p was identified as the core functional component of SC-Exos, with transferrin (TF) as its downstream target gene. The biological functions of this miRNA-target gene regulatory axis were further validated both in vitro and in vivo (Scheme). Finally, SC-Exos were loaded into a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel to establish a long-term, stable sustained-release system, and its pro-osteogenic efficacy was confirmed in a calvarial bone defect model of glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) in rats. This study provided novel theoretical insights and potential intervention targets for the clinical treatment of GIOP from the emerging perspective of the “neuro-osseous axis.”

Results

Characterization of SC-Exos

Nerves innervate nearly every region of the skeleton [12], and neuro-skeletal crosstalk plays significant roles in bone development, metabolism, and repair [9]. As highly efficient mediators of intercellular communication, exosomes have been investigated across a broad spectrum of diseases and cell types. Jones et al. [17] demonstrated that skeletal stem cells depend on paracrine signals from SCs within the bone regenerative microenvironment for effective bone healing, indicating the pivotal role of SC-Exos in skeletal repair. However, the influence of SC paracrine signaling in the treatment of osteoporosis has yet to be thoroughly investigated. In this study, primary SCs were isolated from the sciatic nerves of 4-week-old Sprague‒Dawley (SD) rats and expanded to passage 3. Exos were then harvested from the conditioned medium by differential ultracentrifugation, and their morphology, size distribution, and surface protein markers were characterized. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the isolated particles were cup shaped, uniformly spherical vesicles typical of exosomes (Fig. 1A). Western blotting verified the expression of exosomal markers, including CD9, Alix, and TSG101, all of which were significantly expressed in SC-Exos (Fig. 1B). Quantitative nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed a unimodal size distribution: 91.5% of the particles were 70–230 nm in diameter (Fig. 1C), with an absolute concentration of 5.1 × 108 particles/mL. Collectively, these data verified the successful isolation of SC-Exos.

A GIOP model was successfully established by intramuscular injection of high-dose dexamethasone (2.5 mg/kg, three times per week for 8 weeks) in SD rats. Quantitative micro-CT analysis of the bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) confirmed the successful establishment of the GIOP model (Fig. S1). OP-BMSCs were subsequently isolated from the model rats and used as the cellular model for subsequent experiments.

We next evaluated the uptake of SC-Exos by BMSCs/OP-BMSCs. SC-Exos were labeled with PKH26 and coincubated with BMSCs for 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. The uptake of SC-Exos by the BMSCs was evaluated by monitoring changes in the intracellular fluorescence. Red fluorescence signals could be detected after 3 h of coculture, and the uptake of SC-Exos increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1D).

To assess the biological impact of SC-Exos on BMSCs, BMSCs were coincubated with 1, 10, or 100 μg/mL SC-Exos. BMSCs from 4-week-old SD rats served as the normal control, whereas those from both the model and treatment groups were derived from GIOP rats. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays for semiquantitative analysis revealed the slower proliferation of BMSCs from osteoporotic rats than those from their normal counterparts; notably, exposure to a high dose of SC-Exos (100 μg/mL) markedly reversed this effect and increased their proliferation (Fig. 1E–G). Live/dead staining confirmed that the SC-Exos were biocompatible and not cytotoxic to the BMSCs (Fig. 1H). Consistent with the cell proliferation data, flow cytometric cell cycle profiling revealed pronounced G1 phase arrest of OP-BMSCs. The administration of SC-Exos markedly reduced the dwell time in the G1 phase and increased the S phase fraction of OP-BMSCs, indicating that the vesicles actively drive cell cycle re-entry and progression (Fig. 1I and S2).

Effects of SC-Exos on the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs and the angiogenic capacity of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)

To evaluate the effects of SC-Exos on the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs, we detected the mRNA and protein levels of the osteogenesis-related markers Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and osteocalcin (OCN) by reverse transcription‒quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blotting, respectively. Both the transcript and protein levels clearly increased in a dose-dependent manner, and the highest concentration of SC-Exos elicited a statistically significant increase in the expression levels of these factors compared with those in the untreated osteoporotic model group (Fig. 2A and B). Immunofluorescence staining for RUNX2 and semiquantitative analysis corroborated the above findings (Fig. 2C and S3). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining revealed that a high dose of SC-Exos markedly increased the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs (Fig. 2D and E). In summary, osteogenic differentiation is impaired in the context of osteoporosis. Notably, treatment with a high dose of SC-derived exosomes (100 μg/mL) strongly increased both the proliferative activity and osteogenic potential of BMSCs, effectively reversing the suppressive effects imposed by the osteoporotic microenvironment.

In the process of bone repair, stable neovascularization acts as the key “lifeline”, as it supports bone remodeling through the continuous delivery of nutrients, growth factors and functional cells [24,25]. Postinjury bone regeneration recapitulates embryonic skeletogenesis, which requires vascular networks to sustain BMSC activity. However, prolonged glucocorticoid exposure impairs osteoblast function and type H endothelium, suppressing intraosseous angiogenesis and promoting bone loss or osteonecrosis [26]. To verify the proangiogenic activity of SC-Exos, we challenged HUVECs, which are widely used as surrogates for endothelial progenitors, with 1 μM dexamethasone (Dex) to mimic glucocorticoid-induced injury. Dex exposure markedly downregulated the expression of the endothelial markers CD31 and CD34, whereas treatment with a high dose of SC-Exos (100 μg/mL) robustly restored their expression (Fig. 2F). Semiquantitative analysis of the immunofluorescence staining intensity corroborated these findings (Fig. 2G). We subsequently assessed the angiogenic competence of SC-Exos using a Matrigel tube formation assay. Compared with the Dex-injured group, SC-Exo treatment markedly promoted endothelial sprouting, yielding a denser network of capillary-like tubes (Fig. 2H). Quantitative analyses confirmed significant differences among the groups in terms of the number of junctions and in tube length, with high-dose SC-Exos having the strongest proangiogenic effect (Fig. 2I and J). Scratch wound and Transwell migration assays provided additional data support: SC-Exos (100 μg/mL) markedly increased the motility of endothelial cells (Fig. 2K–S4 and S5). Taken together, these findings indicate that SC-Exos not only neutralized the glucocorticoid-mediated suppression of osteogenesis but also robustly promoted angiogenesis and reestablished disrupted vascular–osteogenic coupling, suggesting new strategies with transformative potential for the treatment of osteoporosis.

Mechanism by which SC-Exos promote the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms by which SC-Exos modulate OP-BMSCs, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on samples derived from the control group (BMSCs), the model group (OP-BMSCs), and the treatment group (OP-BMSCs treated with SC-Exos). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed tight intragroup clustering with clear intergroup separation (Fig. S6). Heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct gene expression patterns across the three groups (Fig. S7). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed multiple significantly enriched pathways; notably, the ferroptosis pathway, which is closely associated with bone remodeling, was among the most prominently enriched (Fig. 3A and B). Iron is an indispensable trace element in numerous biological processes. However, dysregulated iron metabolism markedly increases the incidence of osteoporosis [27,28]. Li et al. [29] reported that blocking the ferroptosis pathway in BMSCs effectively reduced the severity of osteoporosis.

In this study, Prussian blue staining of femoral samples from osteoporotic patients revealed pronounced iron deposits in the osteoporotic group (Fig. 3C). Consistent with this finding, the expression of the ferroptosis-safeguarding enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) was markedly decreased (Fig. 3D), whereas that of TF was significantly increased (Fig. 3E). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed the parallel changes in GPX4 and TF transcription (Fig. 3F). Moreover, malondialdehyde (MDA, the end product of lipid peroxidation) and ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were substantially elevated (Fig. 3G and H). Together, these data demonstrate that osteoporotic bone exhibits clear iron metabolism dysregulation and ferroptosis activation.

BMSCs are a major source of cells in the bone marrow and play significant roles in bone homeostasis and osteogenesis. Therefore, we extracted OP-BMSCs from osteoporotic SD rats to investigate whether SC-Exos promote osteoporotic bone repair by regulating ferroptosis. In the initial phase of this study, we used CCK-8 assays to systematically evaluate the therapeutic potential of various programmed cell death inhibitors to rescue BMSC viability. The tested inhibitors included Z-VAD-FMK (a pan-caspase inhibitor that targets apoptosis), deferoxamine (DFO, an iron chelator that inhibits ferroptosis), ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor), necrostatin-1 (Nec-1, a specific necroptosis inhibitor), and 3-methyladenine (3-MA, an autophagy inhibitor). The results of the CCK-8 assays revealed that among all the treatments, only DFO and Fer-1 significantly inhibited the osteoporosis-induced decrease in OP-BMSC viability, whereas the other inhibitors had minimal protective effects (Fig. S8). The finding that two mechanistically distinct ferroptosis inhibitors—DFO, which targets iron overload, and Fer-1, which blocks lipid peroxidation—rescue cell viability strongly implicates ferroptosis as a primary mode of cell death in OP-BMSCs. We subsequently assessed oxidative stress in BMSCs by measuring the levels of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxides (LPOs). OP-BMSCs exhibited markedly elevated ROS and LPO levels, whereas treatment with SC-Exos, DFO, or Fer-1 significantly attenuated both indices (Fig. 3I and J). In addition, mitochondrial membrane potential assays revealed a pronounced decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential in OP-BMSCs, which was partially restored upon treatment with SC-Exos, DFO, or Fer-1 (Fig. 3K). FerroOrange staining revealed that SC-Exos and DFO markedly decreased the pool of labile Fe2+ in OP-BMSCs, whereas Fer-1 administration resulted in a modest but nonsignificant reduction in intracellular Fe2+ levels (Fig. 3L). TEM revealed that the mitochondria of the BMSCs in the model group exhibited typical ferroptosis-related alterations, including mitochondrial atrophy, fewer cristae, and outer membrane rupture, whereas SC-Exos, DFO, and Fer-1 partially restored the mitochondrial ultrastructure (Fig. 3M). Furthermore, MDA levels were significantly elevated in the model group, whereas SC-Exos, DFO, and Fer-1 markedly decreased MDA levels (Fig. 3N). Consistent with these findings, analysis of glutathione redox status revealed a pronounced decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio in the model group, while SC-Exos, DFO, and Fer-1 significantly reversed this change. Cotreatment with erastin significantly abolished the SC-Exo-mediated restoration of the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 3O). RT-qPCR analysis of ferroptosis-related genes revealed robust upregulation of the expression of proferroptotic mediators (TF and NCOA4) and concomitant downregulation of the expression of antiferroptotic factors (GPX4 and Nrf2) in OP-BMSCs; SC-Exos and Fer-1 largely reversed these transcriptional changes. Furthermore, erastin reactivated the ferroptotic gene expression signature that had been suppressed by SC-Exos, including the upregulation of TF and NCOA4 expression and the downregulation of GPX4 and Nrf2 expression (Fig. S9).

Collectively, these data suggest that excessive iron accumulation may lead to ferroptosis in BMSCs through the induction of oxidative stress, dysregulation of fatty acid metabolism, and activation of ferroptotic signaling. SC-Exos, DFO, and Fer-1 reestablish redox equilibrium and iron homeostasis in BMSCs by modulating multiple indices, including the GSH/GSSG ratio, Fe2+ levels, and the levels of MDA, ROS, and LPOs. Erastin-based reversal experiments confirmed that this protection is ferroptosis dependent, providing a promising therapeutic avenue for osteoporotic bone regeneration.

Screening and validation of key effector molecules in SC-Exos

Exos are nanosized extracellular vesicles that encapsulate diverse proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. Increasing evidence has revealed that microRNAs (miRNAs) act as pivotal messengers in exosome-mediated intercellular communication [30]. Exosomal miRNAs reprogram cellular behavior upon delivery, constituting a key mechanism of exosomal function.

To date, various miRNAs, including miR-3173-5p, miR-125b-5p, and miR-144-3p, have been reported to positively or negatively regulate ferroptosis [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. To identify the key miRNA effectors involved in the SC-Exo-mediated antiferroptosis activity, miRNA sequencing was performed on BMSCs before and after SC-Exo treatment.

Both the heatmap and the volcano plot revealed that the miRNA landscape differed markedly in BMSCs before and after treatment with the SC-Exos, indicating that the SC-Exos selectively transferred specific miRNAs into the BMSCs (Fig. 4A and B). On the basis of the sequencing results, miRNA mimics corresponding to highly enriched candidates were transfected into OP-BMSCs to assess their effects on osteogenic differentiation. ALP staining revealed that among these candidates, only miR-212-3p significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation (Fig. S10).

To identify the downstream target genes of miR-212-3p, we conducted an in-depth analysis of previously obtained transcriptomic sequencing data. The results revealed that genes that were differentially expressed between BMSCs and OP-BMSCs were associated with the ferroptosis pathway; however, only two genes were identified among the ferroptosis pathway-related differentially expressed genes between SC-Exo-treated OP-BMSCs and the aforementioned data (Fig. 4C). Among these genes, the ferroptosis-related gene TF was significantly upregulated in OP-BMSCs, while SC-Exo treatment markedly downregulated TF expression. Furthermore, bioinformatics prediction revealed that TF is a potential target gene of miR-212-3p (Fig. 4D). As shown in Fig. 4E, target prediction revealed a putative binding site for miR-212-3p within the 3′UTR of TF. A dual-luciferase reporter assay was subsequently performed to validate their direct interaction. The results demonstrated that compared with control cells, cells cotransfected with miR-212-3p mimics and the wild-type TF 3′UTR reporter plasmid exhibited significantly lower luciferase activity, confirming that TF is a direct target gene of miR-212-3p (Fig. 4F). To corroborate this regulatory relationship, the expression levels of miR-212-3p and TF were determined by RT-qPCR in the miR-212-3p mimic group, inhibitor group, and their respective negative control groups. The results revealed that TF expression levels were significantly negatively correlated with miR-212-3p levels (Fig. S11), confirming the negative regulatory effect of miR-212-3p on TF expression.

Regulatory roles of miR-212-3p in ferroptosis and the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs

To determine whether miR-212-3p promotes the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs by modulating ferroptosis, we first assessed cell proliferation using an EdU incorporation assay. Both SC-Exos and the miR-212-3p mimic markedly increased BMSC proliferation (Fig. 5A). Intracellular oxidative stress was then evaluated using fluorescent probes for ROS and LPOs. The miR-212-3p mimic significantly reduced the levels of ROS and LPOs (Fig. 5B and C). Flow cytometry revealed that OP-BMSCs contained elevated levels of ROS and LPOs, whereas the miR-212-3p mimic substantially reduced the levels of these molecules (Fig. 5D and E). Consistent with these findings, FerroOrange staining revealed a pronounced decrease in intracellular Fe2+ levels after miR-212-3p treatment, and quantitative Fe2+ measurements verified this result (Fig. 5F and G). Western blotting revealed that miR-212-3p suppressed the expression of the ferroptosis-related proteins TF and NCOA4, and semiquantitative analysis confirmed the significant downregulation of these proteins (Fig. 5H and S12). Furthermore, the level of MDA was substantially decreased in the SC-Exo and miR-212-3p mimic groups (Fig. 5I). Glutathione assays indicated that osteoporosis decreased the GSH level and the GSH/GSSG ratio while increasing the level of GSSG; these changes were reversed by treatment with SC-Exos or the miR-212-3p mimic (Fig. 5J). RT-qPCR confirmed the decrease in TF transcripts by miR-212-3p (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR-212-3p delivered by SC-Exos alleviates oxidative stress by inhibiting ferroptosis, thereby promoting OP-BMSC proliferation and osteogenesis under osteoporotic conditions.

To elucidate the relationship between TF and NCOA4 in the context of ferritinophagy-mediated iron dysregulation, we performed immunofluorescence staining of NCOA4 and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) in OP-BMSCs. In the osteoporotic model group, NCOA4 fluorescence intensity was markedly increased, whereas FTH1 signal was substantially diminished, which is consistent with enhanced NCOA4-associated ferritin degradation. These changes were accompanied by the previously observed upregulation of TF, collectively suggesting disruption of the iron uptake and ferritin turnover axis. Treatment with SC-Exos or the miR-212-3p mimic significantly reversed these alterations, as evidenced by reduced NCOA4 expression and restored FTH1 levels (Fig. S13). These findings indicate that miR-212-3p delivered by SC-Exos may suppress NCOA4-associated ferritinophagy and help restore ferritin homeostasis in OP-BMSCs.

Immunofluorescence staining of RUNX2 revealed a markedly stronger signal in the miR-212-3p mimic group than in any other group, and semiquantitative analysis confirmed this difference (Fig. 5L and M). Similarly, ALP and ARS staining revealed significantly greater mineral deposition in the mimic group than in the osteoporosis model group (Fig. 5N). Western blotting substantiated the pro-osteogenic effect of the miR-212-3p mimic, revealing increased RUNX2 and OCN protein levels (Fig. 5O). RT-qPCR yielded consistent results at the transcript level, with robust increases in RUNX2 and OCN mRNA levels in miR-212-3p-treated BMSCs (Fig. 5P). Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-212-3p markedly increases the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs.

To confirm that miR-212-3p is the functional mediator of the effects of SC-Exos, we used a miR-212-3p inhibitor to knockdown its expression in OP-BMSCs following exosome treatment. Transfection with the inhibitor significantly reduced miR-212-3p expression in SC-Exo-treated cells (Fig. S14A) and restored the expression of TF, which had been suppressed by SC-Exos (Fig. S14B). Furthermore, inhibition of miR-212-3p attenuated the ability of SC-Exos to reduce MDA levels (Fig. S14C), restored the GSH/GSSG ratio in OP-BMSCs (Fig. S14D), and decreased LPO (Fig. S14E) and ROS levels (Fig. S14F) and the intracellular Fe2+ concentration (Fig. S14G). Functionally, inhibition of miR-212-3p blunted the pro-osteogenic effects of SC-Exos, as evidenced by reduced RUNX2 and OCN mRNA expression (Fig. S14H and I), reduced RUNX2 and OPN immunofluorescence intensity (Fig. S14J and K), and decreased mineral deposition, as determined by ARS staining. Collectively, these results confirm that miR-212-3p is indispensable for the SC-Exos-mediated suppression of ferroptosis in OP-BMSCs and promoting their osteogenic differentiation.

miRNAs are endogenous noncoding RNAs ∼22 nucleotides in length that regulate gene expression posttranscriptionally. By base pairing with target mRNAs, they repress translation or promote mRNA decay [36,37]. miRNAs regulate ∼1/3 of human genes and key physiological processes [38]. Aberrant miRNA expression has been linked to the onset and progression of osteoporosis [39,40]. Several miRNAs have been shown to drive osteoblast differentiation by modulating key molecular targets. For instance, miR-194 facilitates osteogenesis by promoting the STAT1-mediated nuclear translocation of RUNX2 [41], whereas miR-433-3p activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through the repression of DKK1, leading to increased osteogenic differentiation [42]. To date, however, the contribution of miR-212-3p to osteoporosis remains unexplored. Recent evidence [43] has shown that miR-212-3p attenuates apoptosis in injured neurons and improves functional recovery after spinal cord injury (SCI) in rats through the suppression of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN). Notably, PTEN has been identified as a proferroptotic factor [44,45] that induces ferroptosis by increasing intracellular lipid peroxidation. In the present study, we observed a marked enrichment of miR-212-3p in BMSCs exposed to SC-Exos, suggesting that miR-212-3p may play a pivotal role in osteoporosis by modulating ferroptosis in BMSCs.

Ferritin, the primary intracellular iron storage protein, undergoes NCOA4-mediated autophagic degradation, expanding the labile iron pool and triggering ferroptosis [46,47]. In the present study, NCOA4-dependent ferritinophagy was markedly activated in the model group, resulting in accelerated ferritin turnover. Conversely, SC-Exo treatment significantly downregulated NCOA4 expression in BMSCs, indicating that SC-Exos inhibit ferritin degradation and, in parallel, mitigate cell death and lipid peroxidation.

Roles of TF in the SC-Exo-mediated regulation of ferroptosis in and osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs

To elucidate the role of TF in osteoporosis-related pathology, TF was silenced in OP-BMSCs, and multiple ferroptosis-associated parameters were monitored. Three siRNA candidates were screened and validated, and the one that demonstrated the most effective knockdown efficiency was selected for further investigation (Fig. S15). The results of the ROS and LPO assays revealed that TF knockdown markedly decreased the levels of both types of molecules compared with those in the model group (Fig. 6A–D). Consistent with this reduction in oxidative stress, intracellular ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were significantly decreased in TF-deficient cells (Fig. 6E–G), and MDA levels were likewise decreased (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, RT-qPCR and Western blotting demonstrated that silencing TF significantly reduced the expression of the ferroptosis-related protein NCOA4 (Fig. 6I and S16); these findings indicate that TF promotes ferroptosis-associated oxidative stress in OP-BMSCs.

We next investigated the effect of TF on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. RT-qPCR and Western blotting revealed significantly increased expression of RUNX2 and OCN in the TF siRNA group (Fig. 6J and S17). Immunofluorescence staining of RUNX2 likewise revealed significantly greater fluorescence intensity in the TF siRNA group than in the other groups (Fig. 6K), which was confirmed by semiquantitative analysis (Fig. 6L). In summary, these results demonstrate that the downregulation of TF markedly increased the osteogenic differentiation capacity of OP-BMSCs. Moreover, TF expression correlated positively with the ferroptotic burden and inversely with osteogenic differentiation in OP-BMSCs.

Validation of the mechanism by which SC-Exos regulate ferroptosis and osteogenic differentiation in OP-BMSCs via the miR-212-3p/TF axis

To clarify the hierarchical relationship within the miR-212-3p/TF axis, we transfected OP-BMSCs with a TF overexpression plasmid (TF-OE). We then assessed intracellular ROS, LPO, Fe2+ and MDA levels (Fig. 7A–H). Compared with those in the SC-Exo group, TF-OE markedly restored Fe2+ accumulation and LPO levels, thereby increasing oxidative stress and weakening the antiferroptotic effect of miR-212-3p. These findings demonstrate that miR-212-3p suppresses ferroptosis primarily by downregulating TF, positioning TF downstream of miR-212-3p in this pathway. Western blotting and RT-qPCR confirmed that SC-Exo treatment markedly reduced the expression of TF and NCOA4 in OP-BMSCs, whereas TF overexpression led to a pronounced increase in TF levels accompanied by the concomitant upregulation of NCOA4 (Fig. 7I and J). We next evaluated the impact of TF-OE on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Western blotting and RT-qPCR revealed that TF-OE markedly reduced the SC-Exo-induced expression of RUNX2 and OCN (Fig. 7K and L). Synthesis and characterization of the E7-Exos/GelMA hydrogel

The compound 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[poly(ethylene glycol)]-N-hydroxysuccinimide (DSPE-PEG-NHS) was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. The NHS ester readily forms covalent amide bonds with the amino groups of peptides or proteins, thereby conjugating the lipid anchor DSPE to the desired peptide/protein, and the resulting amphiphile can be incorporated into the exosomal lipid bilayer through heat-induced self-assembly and membrane relaxation (Fig. 8A). Following the protocol of Shen et al. [32], DSPE-PEG-E7 was synthesized with a 1:1 M ratio of E7 to DSPE-PEG-NHS. The conjugate then self-assembled into E7-PEG micelles in water via the solvent-displacement method. Exosomes, quantified on a protein basis, were mixed with the E7-PEG micelles at a 1:1 (w/w) ratio and incubated in a 40 °C water bath for 2 h. Finally, the E7-PEG-micelle–exosome suspension was cooled to 4 °C for subsequent use.

The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectrum of DSPE-PEG-E7 (Fig. 8B) displays three characteristic resonances: those of PEG at δ 3.6 ppm (peak 1), hydrolyzed NHS at δ 2.6 ppm (peak 2), and DSPE at δ 1.2 ppm (peak 3), with peak 2 showing the greatest integral intensity. Ultraviolet‒visible (UV‒Vis) analysis of DSPE-PEG-E7 provided additional evidence of its successful assembly (Fig. 8C). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the E7-PEG micelles exhibited the expected spherical morphology (Fig. 8D). Confocal fluorescence imaging revealed that both the E7-Exos and the native Exos were efficiently internalized by BMSCs and displayed pronounced intracellular colocalization (Fig. 8E and F). TEM also revealed protein- or micelle-like protrusions decorating the surface of the functionalized vesicles (Fig. 8G). Western blotting revealed no appreciable difference in surface protein expression between the unmodified and peptide-modified exosomes (Fig. 8H). Compared with those of the native exosomes (mean diameter ≈ 112.5 nm), the peptide-decorated particles were significantly larger (approximately 141.4 nm) (Fig. 8I). Moreover, Transwell migration assays demonstrated that the E7 peptide increased OP-BMSC motility in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S19).

Ideal scaffolds for osteoporotic bone repair must be able to load drugs and be injectable for minimally invasive delivery. Injectable hydrogels, which are administered as liquids that rapidly crosslink in situ to provide mechanical support, enable sustained therapeutic release. GelMA is widely utilized in tissue engineering and offers excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability and tunable physicochemical properties. However, pristine GelMA hydrogels exhibit a limited capacity to modulate biochemical signaling and therefore communicate poorly with resident cells. To overcome this limitation, an exosome-laden GelMA hydrogel (GelMA-Exo) was constructed. GelMA was first synthesized by reacting gelatin with methacrylic anhydride through an esterification process. To verify the photocrosslinking capability of the material, 0.1% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate and a trace amount of blue dye were added to the GelMA precursor solution. Upon irradiation for 1 min at 405 nm, the solution rapidly solidified to form a hydrogel (Fig. S20). Microscopic imaging (Fig. S21) revealed that the exosomes were uniformly distributed within the hydrogel network. We hypothesize that noncovalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, between the exosomes and the GelMA chains not only reinforce the mechanical strength of the hydrogel but also markedly decrease its swelling ratio and slow its degradation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the GelMA hydrogel features an interconnected porous architecture favorable for cell migration, proliferation, and tissue repair (Fig. 8J). Rheological measurements revealed that the cross-linked GelMA hydrogel behaves as a typical viscoelastic solid under small amplitude oscillatory shear, with the storage modulus (G′) that consistently exceeds the loss modulus (G″), indicating good solid-like stability (Fig. 8K). Stress–strain analysis demonstrated the considerable compressive resilience of the hydrogel (Fig. 8L), as the compressive modulus reached 13.5 ± 0.8 kPa at 40% strain. Because the degradation rate and swelling ratio critically influence carrier performance, we next examined cargo release and matrix breakdown. Swelling studies revealed that water uptake stabilized after approximately 24 h (Fig. 8M). In phosphate-buffered saline, the hydrogel required 25 days for complete degradation (Fig. 8N) and fluorescently labeled exosomes were still detectable after 21 days (Fig. 8O), indicating sustained release. Biocompatibility was subsequently evaluated via calcein-AM/propidium iodide live/dead staining of the BMSCs cultured within the gel. The results revealed no discernible adverse effects on cell viability or proliferation (Fig. 8P), indicating that the GelMA hydrogel was well tolerated by the BMSCs.

Exos constitute a new paradigm for intercellular communication. Nevertheless, the biomedical use of unmodified (native) exosomes faces several limitations. Exos are internalized nonspecifically by multiple cell types, which reduces the fraction delivered to the intended target cells. Chemical surface engineering can overcome this challenge by enabling cell-specific targeting [48]. One representative bone-homing strategy involves the direct insertion of amphiphilic ligands into the exosomal lipid bilayer. Furthermore, PEGylation of the nanovesicle helps the construct evade clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, prolonging its circulation half-life and thereby improving its systemic stability. Among the known BMSC-targeting motifs, the E7 peptide (EPLQLKM), discovered via phage-display screening, is among the best-characterized candidates [49]. Cellular and animal studies have confirmed that the E7 targeting peptide can home to bone-associated BMSCs both in vitro and in vivo while increasing stem cell adhesion and proliferation [50]. Accordingly, we decorated the exosomal membrane with E7 to obtain engineered exosomes that exhibit an efficient ability to target BMSCs.

GelMA hydrogels are synthesized by grafting methacrylate groups onto gelatin, imparting the resulting material with biocompatibility, enzymatic biodegradability, and tunable mechanics for tissue engineering, drug delivery, and 3D cell culture applications [51]. Standard fabrication protocols involve three steps: (1) methacrylation of gelatin, (2) dissolution of the prepolymer solution, and (3) photo- or chemically induced crosslinking. Upon UV exposure or chemical initiation, GelMA rapidly forms a stable three-dimensional network whose crosslinking density—and therefore stiffness and elasticity—can be precisely adjusted. Because the hydrogel replicates key features of the native extracellular matrix, it readily supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and lineage commitment. Moreover, its interconnected macroporous structure permits tissue infiltration and colonization throughout the construct.

Therapeutic potential of the E7-Exos/GelMA hydrogel for the repair of osteoporotic bone defects

The overarching goal of this work was to correct the iron homeostasis imbalance characteristic of osteoporosis and thereby accelerate bone regeneration. To assess the therapeutic benefit of the E7-Exos/GelMA composite, we established a critical-sized osteoporotic defect model and implanted the hydrogel directly into the lesion (Fig. 9A). Micro-CT reconstructions revealed extensive de novo bone formation at the treated sites (Fig. 9B and C); by week 12, the high-dose Exos/GelMA and, more prominently, the E7-Exos/GelMA groups had almost completely bridged the defect. In contrast, compared with the untreated group, the blank hydrogel group exhibited only negligible osteogenic enhancement, indicating that the scaffold alone contributes little to defect repair under osteoporotic conditions. Compared with the other treatments, the E7-Exos/GelMA treatment resulted in significantly greater BV/TV, BMD, Tb.Th, and Tb.N values throughout the healing period, confirming the superior capacity of E7-Exos/GelMA to restore iron homeostasis and promote robust bone regeneration in osteoporotic bone defects. Histological assessments were performed via hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining to determine the amount and maturity of the regenerated material (Fig. 9D). The hydrogel-only and low- and medium-dose Exos/GelMA groups presented sparse trabeculae with abundant fibrous tissue, indicating limited bone formation. In contrast, dense collagen and extensive new bone were produced throughout the defect in the high-dose Exos/GelMA and E7-Exos/GelMA groups.

We performed immunofluorescence staining of the defect area to assess the expression level of RUNX2, a key osteogenic transcription factor (Fig. 9E). Compared with the other groups, both the Exos/GelMA and E7-Exos/GelMA groups exhibited significantly upregulated RUNX2 expression, which was confirmed by semiquantitative analysis of the fluorescence images (Fig. S22).

The skeletal vasculature is functionally heterogeneous, and H-type vessels, the endothelial cells of which display high CD31 expression, play a pivotal role in bone metabolism by recruiting BMSCs and tightly coupling angiogenesis with osteogenesis through vessel splitting. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that CD31 levels were markedly elevated in the E7-Exos/GelMA and high-dose Exos/GelMA groups (Fig. 9F), demonstrating that both treatments exert pronounced proangiogenic effects during fracture healing. After fracture, peripheral nerves disconnect their synapses and shift into a regenerative program, where they secrete neurotransmitters and neuropeptides, such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), substance P, norepinephrine, acetylcholine, neuropeptide Y, and neurotrophic factors, that actively drive bone repair [11,52]. Emerging evidence [53] highlights CGRP as a key mediator of BMSC recruitment, osteogenic differentiation, and early revascularization. Therefore, promoting nerve regrowth and function is expected to accelerate skeletal healing. Immunofluorescence staining for CGRP revealed markedly stronger signals in the E7-Exos/GelMA and high-dose Exos/GelMA cohorts than in the other groups (Fig. 9G), indicating a pronounced increase in neurogenic activity at the defect site. To substantiate neural involvement in the repair process, immunofluorescence staining for β-III Tubulin and FGF9 was performed. β-III Tubulin, a structural protein of neuronal axons, serves as a direct histological marker of nerve fiber infiltration. Notably, β-III Tubulin-positive signals were significantly more abundant in the E7-Exos/GelMA and high-dose Exos/GelMA groups than in the remaining groups (Fig. S23), confirming active nerve fiber ingrowth into the defect area rather than merely elevated neuropeptide expression. Moreover, the expression of FGF9, which is secreted by skeletal-innervating neurons and promotes mesenchymal cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation by binding to fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) on callus-resident cells, was markedly increased in these two groups (Fig. S23). The increase in FGF9 expression suggests that regenerating nerve fibers at the defect site may directly regulate downstream osteogenic responses through paracrine FGF9/FGFR signaling, providing mechanistic evidence for the proposed neuro–osseous crosstalk. Taken together with our earlier data, these findings demonstrate that the E7-Exos/GelMA hydrogel not only augments osteogenesis and angiogenesis but also promotes peripheral nerve regeneration, providing a multifaceted stimulus for the repair of osteoporotic bone defects.

To comprehensively evaluate the biosafety of the Exos/GelMA system, a systematic in vivo safety assessment was conducted across all six experimental groups. Neither body weight trajectories nor major organ indices exhibited notable fluctuations during or after the treatment period in any group, suggesting that material implantation did not adversely affect overall physiological homeostasis (Fig. S24). At the experimental endpoint, tissue samples from major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested for histological examination. H&E staining revealed well-preserved organ architecture across all groups, with no evidence of inflammatory infiltration, cellular necrosis, or structural damage (Fig. S25), indicating that the degradation byproducts of Exos/GelMA did not elicit organ-level toxicity. Hematological and serum biochemical parameters were subsequently analyzed to investigate potential systemic responses. Routine blood indices, including red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, white blood cell count, and platelet count, remained within normal physiological ranges across all groups (Fig. S26). The levels of hepatic and renal function markers, namely, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, and urea, did not significantly differ between groups and fell within clinically normal ranges (Fig. S27). Collectively, these results demonstrate that local implantation of the Exos/GelMA system did not induce adverse systemic effects or organ toxicity, thereby supporting its favorable biosafety profile for potential clinical translation.

On the basis of the above encouraging findings, we performed RNA sequencing on bone tissue surrounding the defect sites from all three groups. Differential expression analysis revealed 4039 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the control and model groups and 3799 DEGs between the treatment and model groups, with 2932 DEGs common in both comparison groups. The four-quadrant scatter plot of DEGs revealed a significant negative correlation between the Treatment vs. Model and Model vs. Control comparisons (Fig. 10A), suggesting that SCs-Exos can significantly reverse the altered gene expression profile in the osteoporosis model, thereby playing a key regulatory role in the treatment of osteoporosis.

Hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis confirmed that TF and multiple ferroptosis-related genes were significantly upregulated under osteoporotic conditions and markedly suppressed following SC-Exo intervention (Fig. 10B), which is consistent with our in vitro findings. Accordingly, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the ferroptosis pathway was significantly enriched in both the Control vs. Model and Treatment vs. Model comparisons (Fig. 10C and D). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) provided additional evidence corroborating that ferroptosis-associated gene signatures were positively enriched in the osteoporosis model, but their enrichment was notably diminished after SC-Exo treatment (Fig. 10E and F).

Taken together, these transcriptomic data provide evidence at the global level that SC-Exos alleviate glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis by suppressing TF-mediated ferroptosis. Furthermore, in vivo RT-qPCR analysis revealed that compared with that in the control group, miR-212-3p expression in the model group was significantly decreased, whereas miR-212-3p expression was restored following SC-Exos treatment (Fig. S28).

In vivo transcriptomic profiling substantiated the pivotal contribution of the miR-212-3p/TF axis in osteoporotic bone repair. In glucocorticoid-induced osteoporotic rats, transferrin and the entire ferroptosis program were strongly activated, indicating that disrupted iron homeostasis is a key pathogenic event. Delivery of miR-212-3p via SC-Exos effectively suppressed TF expression, concomitantly reducing ferroptosis marker levels, and ultimately created a microenvironment with low oxidative stress and low iron toxicity conducive to osteogenesis. These in vivo results mirrored our earlier results in BMSCs, confirming that exosome therapy elicits consistent molecular effects from the cellular level to the whole organism.

To validate the transcriptomic sequencing results, Prussian blue staining was performed on bone tissue from each group (Fig. 11A). The results revealed significant iron accumulation in the model group that was markedly greater than that in the Exos/GelMA and E7-Exos/GelMA groups, suggesting that the composite material effectively suppressed iron overload-induced ferroptosis during bone repair.

Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining was performed on the bone tissue surrounding the defect to evaluate the expression levels of the ferroptosis-related proteins NCOA4 and TF (Fig. 11B). Compared with those in the other groups, the expression of both NCOA4 and TF was significantly lower in the Exos/GelMA and E7-Exos/GelMA groups. NCOA4 is the core receptor for ferritinophagy, and a reduction in its expression indicates the suppression of autophagic ferritin degradation, thereby limiting intracellular iron release. TF, as a downstream target of miR-212-3p, serves as the primary carrier that mediates extracellular iron uptake, and its downregulation suggests a reduction in the extracellular iron load. The synergistic downregulation of both proteins can effectively restrict the formation of a labile iron pool, attenuate ferroptotic signaling, and consequently reduce oxidative stress and iron-mediated cytotoxicity. These trends were corroborated by semiquantitative analysis of the fluorescence images (Fig. 11C). In addition, the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 11D) were highly consistent with the immunofluorescence findings, validating the above conclusions at the transcriptional level. Consistent with our in vitro findings, immunofluorescence analysis of osteoporotic bone tissues showed increased expression of TF and NCOA4 in the model group. To further characterize the ferritinophagy axis in vivo, we performed immunofluorescence staining for NCOA4, FTH1, and the autophagy marker LC3, followed by colocalization analysis. In the osteoporotic model group, both NCOA4 and LC3 signals were markedly increased, with enhanced NCOA4/LC3 colocalization, whereas FTH1 expression was substantially reduced. These findings suggest that NCOA4 is more closely associated with LC3-positive autophagy-related structures in osteoporotic bone tissues, accompanied by enhanced ferritin degradation, supporting the activation of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in vivo (Fig. S29). In contrast, SC-Exos treatment reduced NCOA4 and LC3 expression, attenuated NCOA4/LC3 colocalization, and restored FTH1 expression. These results indicate that SC-Exos may suppress NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy-associated ferritin degradation and thereby contribute to the restoration of iron homeostasis in vivo.

Discussion

OP is a degenerative skeletal disorder characterized by the progressive loss of bone mass and deterioration of the bone microarchitecture, which markedly increase the risk of fragility fractures. Despite decades of research, effective clinical management of OP remains elusive, largely because existing pharmacological regimens provide only modest benefits and are frequently accompanied by a variety of adverse effects [4,54]. GIOP, which develops after high-dose, long-term glucocorticoid exposure, is a multicellular, multifactorial process. Pharmacological concentrations of glucocorticoids drive BMSCs to commit preferentially to the adipogenic rather than the osteogenic lineage and simultaneously trigger osteoblast apoptosis, thereby depleting the osteoblast pool [55]. At the transcriptional level, glucocorticoids upregulate the expression of adipocyte-specific factors such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (C/EBPα) while downregulating the expression of the key osteogenic regulators RUNX2 and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), exacerbating the imbalance between bone formation and fat accumulation [56].

Recent investigations have focused primarily on how SC-Exos transfer enriched miRNAs to modulate BMSCs function. Wang et al. [18] demonstrated that SC-Exos promote BMSC osteogenesis by delivering let-7c-5p to regulate the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway, whereas Wang et al. [57] reported that SC-Exos attenuated ROS signaling and promoted the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through the regulation of thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) translation mediated by miR-15b-5p. Building on these findings, the present study extends this paradigm by demonstrating that SC-Exos exert their osteogenic effects through a distinct mechanism, namely, the miR-212-3p/TF axis, which specifically targets iron homeostasis in OP-BMSCs.

In the pathological microenvironment of osteoporosis, iron metabolism disorders disrupt iron homeostasis in BMSCs, impairing their antioxidant capacity and shifting their differentiation from osteogenic toward adipogenic lineages, ultimately leading to reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microstructure [[58], [59], [60], [61]]. Consistent with this finding, recent evidence has shown that the targeted regulation of iron homeostasis in BMSCs markedly promotes bone regeneration in osteoporotic models [59]. The findings of the present study align with and extend these observations: SC-Exos delivered miR-212-3p to suppress TF expression in OP-BMSCs, thereby restoring iron homeostasis, reducing oxidative stress, and promoting osteogenic differentiation. This finding is supported by the work of Zheng et al. [62], who demonstrated that BMSC-derived exosomal miR-150-3p attenuated osteoblast ferroptosis via the beta-transducin repeat-containing protein (BTRC)/Nrf2 axis, collectively highlighting exosomal miRNAs as critical regulators of iron metabolism in the osteoporotic bone environment.

Abnormal iron metabolism is recognized as an important contributor to bone loss in individuals with osteoporosis [[63], [64], [65]]. TF is an essential iron carrier protein for ferroptosis; it binds ferric iron and governs its systemic transport and cellular distribution [66]. By controlling the amount of transferrin-bound iron that enters the cytoplasm, TF serves as a molecular gatekeeper of intracellular iron homeostasis. TF orchestrates iron uptake, trafficking, and storage, directly modulating ferroptotic susceptibility. Excess iron fuels the Fenton reaction to generate •OH radicals that damage membranes, proteins, and DNA, inducing oxidative stress and ferroptosis [66,67]. TF is a pivotal regulator of iron homeostasis and consequently modulates both the onset and progression of ferroptosis. Emerging evidence [53] indicates that TF interacts with ferroptosis-related signaling cascades, including the Nrf2/Keap1 axis, which are instrumental in the execution of programmed death. In the present study, we show that silencing TF in OP-BMSCs markedly reduces intracellular iron overload, limits ferroptotic death, and ultimately increases the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs. Our findings support the idea that TF is a central driver of ferroptosis in OP-BMSCs and that TF activation and iron-dependent lipid peroxidation reinforce each other via a self-propagating loop [68]. Within the osteoporotic niche, aberrantly activated TF releases a large pool of labile Fe2+. This excess iron accelerates Fenton chemistry, allowing accumulated polyunsaturated fatty acids to react with ROS and generate substantial amounts of lipid peroxides and lipid-derived radicals. These oxidants subsequently affect cellular and organellar membranes, compromising mitochondrial integrity. The damaged mitochondria leak additional heme and ROS, increasing iron- and ROS-driven damage and thus perpetuating the vicious cycle of ferroptotic cell death. Our functional rescue data demonstrate that TF activation and unchecked lipid peroxidation constitute the two processes that drive the vicious cycle of ferroptosis in osteoporosis. By selectively upregulating or silencing miR-212-3p and TF expression, we elucidated the molecular circuitry of iron-dependent cell death, and complementary gain- and loss-of-function assays confirmed that the miR-212-3p/TF axis is a pivotal regulator of this process. These results offer new mechanistic insight into ferroptosis in the context of osteoporosis and identify practical therapeutic approaches.

Previous studies by Lu et al. [69,70] and Yang et al. [59] demonstrated that extracellular vesicles derived from endothelial progenitor cells prevented glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis by suppressing ferroptosis in osteoblasts. We described a neural regulatory mechanism by which SC-Exos promoted osteoporotic bone regeneration through restoring iron homeostasis in BMSCs. This work thus broadens our understanding of osteoporosis and highlights the potential of exosome-based therapies.

Our data also revealed that the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs is markedly compromised in the osteoporotic milieu. Low- and medium-dose SC-Exos did not offer measurable benefits, whereas the high dose significantly increased both BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation. These observations highlight the therapeutic potential of SC-Exos for osteoporosis and provide a conceptual basis for the development of exosome-based interventions. Pronounced efficacy was observed at high doses but not at low or intermediate doses, which suggests a possible dose-dependent relationship between SC-Exo delivery and therapeutic outcome along the miR-212-3p/TF axis. One plausible explanation is that sufficient intracellular accumulation of miR-212-3p may be required to achieve effective TF silencing, and below a certain concentration, the degree of TF suppression may be insufficient to meaningfully reduce ferroptosis, restore iron homeostasis, or relieve the iron overload-mediated repression of osteogenesis. However, we acknowledge that the precise dose‒response relationships among exosome concentration, intracellular miR-212-3p accumulation, TF suppression, and osteogenic output were not directly quantified in the present study. This mechanistic interpretation therefore remains speculative and warrants validation in future studies through dose-gradient miRNA quantification, TF protein expression measurements, and corresponding functional assays. Nevertheless, the dose-dependent pattern detected in our data underscores the importance of optimizing dosing regimens and rigorously evaluating long-term safety for the future clinical translation of SC-Exo-based therapies. This study confirms that SC-Exo-loaded hydrogels possess promising therapeutic potential, but their clinical translation still faces challenges associated with maintaining exosome activity in the long term and the economic feasibility of scaled-up exosome production. First, exosomes are prone to activity loss during preparation, storage, and in vivo delivery. It is also worth noting that the present study did not include a control group of directly injected free SC-Exos in vivo. This was primarily because bare exosomes administered via local injection are rapidly cleared from the tissue due to enzymatic degradation and phagocytic uptake, making it impractical to sustain therapeutically relevant concentrations throughout the experimental period; thus, such a group would lack meaningful comparability as a control. Nevertheless, this design limitation makes it difficult to fully decouple the independent contributions of the hydrogel carrier system from those of the exosomes themselves. Although short-term experiments have demonstrated the osteogenic effects of exosomes, their long-term stability and functional persistence in the complex bone microenvironment still require systematic evaluation. Future efforts should focus on developing optimized formulation processes and delivery systems, with particular emphasis on strategies to improve exosome retention and stability in vivo and ensure sustained bioactivity at the lesion site. Second, current methods for exosome preparation are inefficient, costly, and inadequate for meeting the demands of clinical-grade production. To achieve scalable translation, it is necessary to establish integrated processes that incorporate efficient purification technologies and implement stringent quality control standards. Additionally, the present study focused predominantly on osteoblast lineage commitment and did not directly assess the potential regulatory effects of SC-Exos on osteoclast activity; future studies should evaluate whether SC-Exos modulate osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption to fully characterize their role in the restoration of bone homeostasis [71].

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that SC-Exos restore iron homeostasis in BMSCs via the miR-212-3p/TF axis, thereby enabling osteoporotic bone regeneration. SC-Exos deliver miR-212-3p to suppress TF expression, normalize iron levels, and block ferroptosis in BMSCs, thereby accelerating osteogenesis in glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis models. These findings establish the miR-212-3p/TF axis as a therapeutic target and position SC-Exos/GelMA as a viable clinical strategy for targeted exosome delivery and improved bone repair.
