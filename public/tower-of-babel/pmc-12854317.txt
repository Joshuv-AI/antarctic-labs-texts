Medical student syndrome among medical and nonmedical students at Sultan Qaboos University: A comparative study of health anxiety
Authors: Abdullah Al Lawati, Zinah Alhadithy, Ghaida N. Al Abri, Ghadeer S. Al Lawati, Mohammed I. Al Azri, Tamadhir Al-Mahrouqi, Younis Al-Mufargi, Hamed Al Sinawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Medical Student Syndrome (MSS) was assessed in medical versus nonmedical students at Sultan Qaboos University.MATERIALS AND METHODS:In this cross-sectional study conducted in Muscat, Oman, 586 students (158 medical, 428 nonmedical) were selected using convenience sampling. The research was carried out between October 2024 and March 2025. Participants completed the Medical Student Syndrome Self-Explanatory Questionnaire, which assesses hypochondria and nosophobia. Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, including independent samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, Chi-square tests, and effect size calculations.RESULTS:The study found no significant differences in hypochondria and nosophobia scores between medical and nonmedical students (t = -0.991, P = 0.322, d = -0.093; t = -0.695, P = 0.487, d = -0.064, respectively). This challenges the traditional assumption that medical students exhibit greater health anxiety. Female students reported significantly higher health anxiety scores than their male counterparts across both groups (U = 7060.500, Z = −3.187, P = 0.001, r = 0.16 for hypochondria; U = 5798.000, Z = −5.011, P < 0.001, r = 0.27 for nosophobia). Additionally, health anxiety increased significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic for both medical and nonmedical students (t = -13.405, P < 0.001, d = -0.58).CONCLUSION:Nonmedical students demonstrated similar levels of health-related anxiety. Gender differences and external stressors, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, play a significant role in shaping health anxiety. As such, tailored intervention strategies and programs to address health anxiety among both medical and nonmedical students need to be implemented by psychologists and counselors in postgraduate settings.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, a long-standing assumption has been propagating around medical universities, stating that young medical students are highly susceptible to what is known as the Medical Student Syndrome (MSS).[12] This syndrome is characterized by heightened anxiety and stress about having a serious illness, a phenomenon often attributed to the extensive medical knowledge students acquire early in their training and their frequent exposure to life-threatening diseases.[3] Such exposure may amplify students’ awareness of potential symptoms, thereby increasing their anxiety regarding personal health. The concept of hypochondriasis has a long history, dating back to its initial formulation in 1671 to describe heightened anxiety about serious illness. Stress is thought to potentially affect symptom perception by heightening physical sensation through autonomic activations, leading individuals to become more attuned to their bodily condition.[4] Additionally, knowledge is believed to play a role in symptom interpretations, as it can prompt medical students to recognize the fine line between health and illness and encourage them to reconsider previously overlooked symptoms, considering their newly obtained knowledge.[56] However, in the modern Psychiatry Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), this term has been discontinued and replaced by two distinct diagnoses: somatic symptoms disorder (SSD) and illness anxiety disorder (IAD).[7] Patients with SSD are predominantly preoccupied with physical pain issues, whereas IAD patients are preoccupied with the fear of having or developing a severe, undetected medical disease.[47] Although these terms are often used interchangeably, there are distinctive characteristics between the two diagnoses. For a better understanding of the scientific terminology used in our research, the term “nosophobia” signified an unmanageable apprehension related to contracting a specific disease, whereas “hypochondria” denotes excessive worry and anxiety of having a severe disease as well as amplifying minor symptoms, even in the absence of medical evidence.[8] Furthermore, given the elevated levels of stress experienced by medical students, they subsequently exhibit a higher probability of developing anxiety and depression disorder when compared to nonmedical students.[91011] This brings us to the important question: Is medical students’ syndrome a genuine condition, and does it affect medical and nonmedical students equally?

A study conducted at the University of Sharjah in the United Arab Emirates found that the prevalence of MSS among students was 70.8%. The prevalence of MSS was higher among clinical-year students than among preclinical-year students. The study also found that 55.9% of students know MSS.[12] MSS can interfere with students’ academic performance and quality of life. Therefore, it is important to provide students with information about MSS and offer resources to help them manage the condition.[13] Although some studies report a higher prevalence of MSS among clinical-year students compared to their preclinical counterparts, other investigations find no significant difference in health anxiety levels between medical and nonmedical students. A study conducted in three universities in London focused on different groups of third-year undergraduates, including medical students, nonmedical science students, and law students. The goal was to identify participants with health concerns disproportionate to the diagnosed diseases. The results of the study showed that there were no significant differences between medical students and nonmedical students in any of the primary outcome variables. In other words, medical students did not seek medical advice for hypochondriacal health concerns more frequently than their nonmedical counterparts.[2]

In light of these mixed findings, it remains unclear whether the increased exposure to medical information genuinely exacerbates health anxiety or whether such anxiety levels are similar across different student populations. Therefore, the present study aims to determine the prevalence and clinical predictors of MSS among medical students and to compare these findings with those of nonmedical students in Oman to contribute to filling this research gap. This study is the first of its kind in Oman to systematically compare health-related anxiety between medical and nonmedical students using a structured MSS-specific tool, highlighting cultural and contextual factors often overlooked in Western-centric literature. The novelty lies in exploring MSS in an Arab university setting, during a post-pandemic period, while also analyzing gender and pandemic-related anxiety differentials. Unlike prior studies, which often focused solely on prevalence or clinical years, this research provides a multivariable analysis, including gender, mental illness, academic year, and the impact of COVID-19.

Materials and Methods

Study design and study setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among 586 students from Sultan Qaboos University (158 medical and 428 nonmedical). The Medical Student Syndrome Self-Explanatory Questionnaire was used to assess hypochondria and nosophobia. The study spanned six months, beginning with the initial concept design in October 2024. Data collection was conducted over one month in November 2024, followed by data analysis, manuscript writing, and proofreading, with the study concluding in March 2025. Statistical analyses, including independent samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests, were performed to compare group anxiety levels. Gender-based differences and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on health anxiety were also analyzed.

Study participants and sampling

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Using a convenience sampling method, this study targeted all medical students at SQU, including those in their preclinical and clinical years. This study utilized a convenience sampling method, allowing for efficient data collection from a large student population within a limited timeframe. Students who were enrolled at the medical college but were studying foundation-level courses were not considered, as they had not been exposed to medical courses yet. In addition, we targeted all nonmedical students from different colleges in the university, except the ones in their foundation year, to allow for a fair comparison.

Data collection tools and technique

Sultan Qaboos University (SQU) students were invited to participate in the study through an online Google Forms survey. The questionnaire was shared using multiple channels, including official university and college emails, social media platforms, and student communication groups. To ensure a diverse range of participants, the research team approached students in person and encouraged them to participate. To increase the response rates, reminder messages were sent through email and social media. The survey was conducted in English, as it is the primary language of instruction at SQU and is well understood by all students.

Outcome measures

Sociodemographic history

This section gathered basic sociodemographic data, including gender, age, year of study, financial situation, and the type of health care used. For nonmedical students, additional information about the field of study and parental professions was explored.

Questionnaire and structure

The Medical Student Syndrome Self-Explanatory Questionnaire was adapted from a study by Szczurek et al.,[1] with permission granted for its use. The questionnaire was in English and has been previously used in cross-cultural research across various countries, including Egypt and Poland.[13] The study description and consent form were displayed at the beginning of the form. Two questionnaire versions were available: one for medical students and one for nonmedical students.

The questionnaire was structured into multiple sections to assess different aspects of health-related anxiety. Responses were collected using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), where higher scores indicate greater levels of concern or anxiety. Additionally, the internal consistency of our study was Cronbach’s Alpha 0.79.

Operational terms

Diagnosis of mental illness by a psychiatrist

In this study, a diagnosis of mental illness refers to any psychiatric disorder formally identified by a licensed psychiatrist based on established diagnostic criteria, such as the DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition) or ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision). Participants who reported a prior diagnosis by a psychiatrist were considered to have a confirmed mental health condition.

Financial status

Financial status was assessed based on self-reported monthly household income and categorized into three levels: low, average, high average, and high income. These categories were defined according to national economic standards, and participants perceived financial sufficiency to meet personal and educational expenses.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated based on the anticipated MSS prevalence of 70% among students.[2] As such, given that the undergraduate student population at SQU is approximately 15,840 students, the targeted sample size for this study was 376 students, including medical and nonmedical students.[14] The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for the population mean, with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The targeted sample size was achieved and exceeded with 586 responses.

Ethical consideration and confidentiality

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC#3375) at Sultan Qaboos University College of Medicine and Health Sciences on September 19, 2024. The study adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration on ethical research involving human participants. Students’ information was kept in an encrypted container, accessible only to the principal investigator (PI) and coinvestigators. Participation was voluntary, and students could withdraw at any time without providing justification.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., New York, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. To compare hypochondria and nosophobia scores between medical and nonmedical students and between genders, independent samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests were applied based on data normality. Chi-square tests assessed categorical variables like gender distribution and financial status, while Spearman’s correlation explored potential links between study year and health anxiety. Additionally, paired t-tests examined changes in health anxiety before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. To provide deeper insight, effect sizes, such as Cohen’s d and rank–biserial correlation (r), were reported alongside significance levels, with P < 0.05 considered statistically meaningful.

Discussion

Our study, conducted at Sultan Qaboos University (SQU), examined the prevalence of Medical Student Syndrome (MSS) among both medical and nonmedical students, with a particular focus on hypochondria and nosophobia, as defined earlier, and pandemic-related anxiety. A key finding of our study is that, surprisingly, and contrary to popular belief, MSS was not found specifically unique to medical students, as nonmedical students exhibited similar levels of health-related anxiety. This challenges the common idea that MSS is a distinct condition solely impacting individuals undergoing medical training.[1] While medical students are frequently exposed to medical knowledge, the findings of this study indicate no significant differences in nosophobia or hypochondria scores between medical and nonmedical students. As mentioned, their levels of nosophobia were comparable to those of nonmedical students. A possible explanation for this is that while their studies expose them to the knowledge of various illnesses, they develop a more rational understanding of their sign and symptoms, prognosis, treatments, and risk factors, thus being able to differentiate life-threatening diseases from simple diagnoses, potentially mitigating excessive fear.[15]

Meanwhile, nonmedical students may experience similar levels of health-related anxiety due to widespread access to online health information, including unreliable sources. Additionally, increased exposure to disease-related knowledge does not necessarily lead to an increased belief in having illnesses (hypochondria). Medical students may become more adept at differentiating between normal bodily sensations and pathological symptoms. In contrast, nonmedical students may experience health concerns driven by personal experiences, media exposure, or general anxiety.[516] These findings align with the conceptualization of health anxiety as classified under IAD and SSD in DSM-5.[7] Similar findings were obtained in other studies. For instance, a study done in Poland concluded that medical studies are not a risk factor for the occurrence of health anxiety and hypochondriacal attitudes, but rather, the female gender and having a mental health illness play a more significant role in contributing to the development of health anxiety.[1] However, these findings contrast with some previous literature, which showed that medical students exhibit higher levels of health-related anxiety attributed to their increased exposure to disease-related information. For instance, a study by Sherif et al.[3] suggested that medical students exhibited higher health-related anxiety compared to their nonmedical counterparts, indicating a greater tendency toward MSS. Similarly, another study in Saudi Arabia showed that 78.2% of participants reported feeling symptoms or signs of the condition, with 65.6% being male.[5] Likewise, a similar study in Egypt found that 78.8% of medical students had hypochondriasis, with higher rates among females, fourth-year students, and those from rural areas, as well as a study from the University of Sharjah, which also reported a high prevalence of MSS (70.8%) among medical students.[312] This difference in prevalence could be explained by different variations in the study populations, including their culture. Cultural factors differ from population to population, and in the Arab world, this includes the stigma around mental health.[17] Our study, conducted in Oman, explored MSS in medical students within the Arab context. It adds to the literature, helps in giving insight into how health anxiety manifests across different groups in Arab countries, and helps enrich the understanding of MSS globally.

The study also examined pandemic-related anxiety between medical and nonmedical students, revealing that fear of illness increased significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to pre-pandemic levels for both groups with no significant differences, reinforcing the idea that external stressors play a significant role in shaping health fears and can be triggered by situational stressors, such as a global health crisis, rather than medical education alone.[18] Our results indicate no direct correlation between MSS symptoms and the year of study, meaning that MSS does not necessarily increase with clinical exposure. However, a slight increase in nosophobia was observed among medical students as they progressed in their studies, though the correlation was weak. This could be attributed to increased clinical exposure and awareness of disease severity but does not necessarily translate into increased hypochondria. This contrasted with the findings from the study conducted at Tanta University in Egypt, which reported that these symptoms are more common in preclinical years and decrease with clinical exposure.[16] This indicates that advancing in medical education does not directly contribute to increased health-related anxiety, suggesting that medical education alone does not necessarily increase or reduce health anxiety over time. This difference may be due to variations in curriculum structure, teaching methods, the prevalence of impostorism among students, or access to mental health support.[1920] On the contrary, the study based at the University of Sharjah found that clinical-year students were 1.75 times more likely to believe they had a disease they studied compared to preclinical students (P = 0.03).[12]

Gender-based differences highlight another important factor. This study showed that female students, regardless of their field of study, consistently exhibited higher hypochondria and nosophobia scores than male students, indicating that women are generally more likely to report health concerns, possibly due to biological, cognitive, or sociocultural influences. Similarly, the gender-based differences observed in our study align with the findings from the Polish study,[1] further emphasizing that female students consistently report higher levels of health anxiety regardless of their academic background. Universities must implement campus-wide mental health resources, including awareness campaigns and early screening for medical and nonmedical students. However, we targeted interventions for high-risk groups, such as female students and those with preexisting mental health conditions.

Limitations and Recommendation

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, because it solely constitutes self-reported data, it is subject to recall bias. We have utilized a self-reported survey because hypochondria and nosophobia, or the potential to have a psychiatric illness, are still considered sensitive topics linked with stigma and privacy concerns within Omani society. Therefore, we have used anonymous self-reporting measures to increase the participants’ privacy and confidentiality. Secondly, the study followed a cross-sectional design to determine the prevalence, but this study design limits the ability to identify causations. Thirdly, response bias might also be present, as students with an interest may be more inclined to participate in this specific study, as well as convenience sampling poses a selection bias, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to the rest of the population. Finally, an imbalance in the gender of the study participants, with a significantly higher proportion of female participants, especially among nonmedical students, may have also influenced the results.

Based on the limitations of the current study, future research should adopt longitudinal designs to assess changes in MSS over time and better establish causal relationships. Randomized or stratified sampling strategies are encouraged to enhance generalizability and reduce selection bias, along with efforts to balance gender representation. Clinician-administered assessments could complement self-reports to strengthen diagnostic validity. In addition to these methodological improvements, future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of psychological interventions, such as cognitive–behavioral therapy (CBT) or mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), in reducing health-related anxiety. Multicenter studies across Omani and international universities may reveal institutional or cultural influences on MSS. Furthermore, investigating the psychological correlates of MSS—such as general anxiety, depression, perfectionism, and burnout—as well as the role of curriculum design and early patient exposure, would enrich our understanding of this phenomenon.

Conclusion

Despite theories suggesting that medical students experience higher health anxiety than nonmedical students, this study shows comparable levels of anxiety in both groups. However, female students reported more significant health anxiety than males. These insights call for tailored intervention strategies and programs to address health anxiety among both medical and nonmedical students at SQU.
