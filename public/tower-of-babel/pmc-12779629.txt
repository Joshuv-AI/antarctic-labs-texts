Integrated persulfate/ozonation process using Fe3O4/AC catalyst for antibiotic degradation: optimization and kinetic insights
Authors: Zahra Shahbeiki, Hojjat Nadimi, Maryam Khodadadi, Yousef Dadban Shahamat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antibiotics are considered emerging contaminants due to their persistence and potential ecological risks. Among them, cefixime is frequently detected in pharmaceutical effluents and requires effective removal strategies. This study aimed to optimize catalytic ozonation for cefixime degradation using persulfate activated by magnetic activated carbon (Fe3O4/AC or MAC). The effects of pH (5–11), contact time (5–60 min), catalyst dose (0–2.5 g/L), initial cefixime concentration (10–200 mg/L), and persulfate concentration (0–70 mM) were evaluated. A central composite design (CCD) under response surface methodology (RSM) was applied to model and optimize the process. Model performance was assessed by statistical indicators. The quadratic model showed good predictive ability (R2 = 0.90). Reaction time and pH were the most significant variables influencing cefixime degradation. Under optimized conditions (pH 8, cefixime 57.5 mg/L, MAC 1.5 g/L, persulfate 1.75 mM, and 46.25 min contact time), 97% removal efficiency was achieved. The findings indicate that catalytic ozonation activated with persulfate and MAC is an effective and statistically validated process for cefixime removal from aqueous solutions. These results support its potential applicability for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Pharmaceutical residues, particularly antibiotics, are increasingly recognized as serious environmental contaminants due to their extensive and often indiscriminate use in medicine, agriculture, and industry1. Classified as contaminants of emerging concern (CECs), they reach aquatic systems mainly through hospital effluents, industrial discharges, and direct excretion in unmetabolized forms2. Reports show that 30–90% of antibiotics can be excreted unchanged, with concentrations up to 1 mg/L detected in wastewater—levels considered environmentally significant by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)3,4. The recent surge in antibiotic consumption during global health crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic has further intensified concerns, highlighting the persistent environmental footprint of these compounds5.

Among antibiotics, beta-lactams are of particular interest due to their clinical effectiveness and widespread prescription6. Cefixime (Table 1), a third-generation cephalosporin, is commonly used to treat respiratory, urinary, gastrointestinal, and dermatological infections7. Its popularity stems from broad antimicrobial activity, affordability, and accessibility8. However, cefixime is poorly metabolized in the human body, and its complex molecular structure resists biodegradation. As a result, residues persist in aquatic environments, where they may disturb ecological balance, promote antimicrobial resistance, and pose risks to public health8. In addition, interactions of cefixime with chlorine during disinfection can form toxic or carcinogenic by-products, exacerbating its environmental impact8.

Conventional wastewater treatment technologies are generally ineffective in addressing such persistent antibiotics9. Adsorption, biological processes, and coagulation–flocculation can remove some contaminants, but antibiotics often resist microbial degradation and persist in treated effluents9. Adsorption merely transfers pollutants without destroying them, and regeneration of adsorbents such as activated carbon is costly and inefficient. Biological systems are further hindered by the antimicrobial nature of the pollutants themselves10. These shortcomings highlight the urgent need for advanced treatment strategies capable of degrading antibiotics into less harmful or mineralized products rather than simply transferring or concentrating them10. Studies have demonstrated that the addition of a co-oxidant to photocatalytic processes significantly enhances the degradation efficiency of preservatives, reduces energy consumption and costs, and improves mineralization rates11,12.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) offer promising solutions for the degradation of recalcitrant pollutants, including antibiotics4,13. By generating highly reactive oxygen species such as hydroxyl and sulfate radicals, AOPs can non-selectively oxidize a wide range of contaminants14. Among these, ozone- and persulfate-based systems have received particular attention15–17. Ozone is highly reactive toward electron-rich compounds, while persulfate can be activated to produce sulfate radicals with strong and selective oxidative potential18,19. Combining ozone and persulfate (O3/PS) has been shown to enhance radical generation and improve degradation efficiency20,21. Nonetheless, ozone’s limited solubility and stability in water restrict its effectiveness, underscoring the importance of catalytic supports to accelerate radical formation and sustain treatment performance22.

Activated carbon (AC) has been widely investigated as both an adsorbent and catalyst in ozonation processes, owing to its large surface area and surface chemistry23. It facilitates pollutant adsorption and promotes ozone decomposition into reactive radicals. However, traditional AC presents drawbacks, including challenges in separation from water, losses during regeneration, and the potential release of fine particles23. To overcome these limitations, magnetic activated carbon (MAC) has been developed. By embedding magnetic particles into the carbon matrix, MAC enables easy recovery with external magnetic fields, thereby reducing secondary pollution risks and improving operational sustainability24,25.

The integration of MAC with O3/PS therefore presents a promising strategy for efficient degradation of persistent antibiotics such as cefixime. This synergistic approach leverages the adsorption capacity of MAC, the oxidative potential of ozone, and the radical-generating capacity of persulfate21,23. Importantly, MAC can act as both a catalytic support for radical generation and a recoverable material that minimizes secondary contamination23. Despite extensive research on AOPs, studies explicitly addressing cefixime and related beta-lactam antibiotics remain scarce, and the use of MAC in combination with O3/PS represents a novel and underexplored direction.

To systematically optimize such complex treatment systems, statistical tools are valuable7. RSM has been successfully applied in environmental engineering to assess parameter interactions, predict process performance, and identify optimal conditions with reduced experimental effort26. By capturing both linear and nonlinear effects, RSM offers an efficient framework for designing and improving advanced treatment processes27.

Therefore, this study investigates the degradation of cefixime in aqueous solutions using a combined O3/PS process catalyzed by magnetic activated carbon. The main objective is to evaluate the efficiency of this process through RSM based on a CCD. Specific goals include assessing the effects of key parameters such as pH, nanoparticle dosage, reaction time, initial antibiotic concentration, and ozone level on the degradation performance. In addition, the structural and surface properties of the synthesized nanoparticles were analyzed, and the reaction kinetics were examined to provide further insight into the mechanism of the combined oxidation process. This work not only contributes to understanding the synergistic effects of O3 and persulfate but also highlights the potential of this catalytic system for mitigating antibiotic contamination and associated resistance risks in water treatment.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and equipment

Cefixime (98% purity) was purchased from Daroupakhsh Pharmaceutical Company (Iran). Powdered activated carbon (AC, 99.99% C, BET surface area 907 m2/g, Sigma-Aldrich–Mina, Germany) was used as the base material. Iron salts included ferrous sulfate (FeSO4 ≥ 99.0%, ACS reagent grade), ferric chloride (FeCl3, ≥ 98.0%, ACS reagent grade), and ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, ≥ 98.0%, ACS reagent grade), all obtained from Merck (Germany). Potassium persulfate (K2S2O8, ≥ 99.0% analytical grade), concentrated nitric acid (36%), concentrated sulfuric acid (98%), sodium hydroxide (1 N), orthophosphoric acid (85%), and methanol (High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC grade)) were also supplied by Merck. PTFE syringe filters (0.45 μm) were used for sample preparation. Deionized double-distilled water was employed in all experiments.

The main instruments included an ozone generator (Sahand Co., Iran; nominal capacity 35 mg O3/min), an oxygen capsule (99.96% purity), a HACH pH meter, a Sartorius BP110S analytical balance, and a Knauer HPLC system with a UV/VIS detector. The ozone output was quantified using the standard potassium iodide method (Standard Methods, APHA, 2012)28.

Experimental methods

MAC was synthesized via a modified coprecipitation method, adapted from Selvaraj et al. 29. Powdered activated carbon (AC, 25 g) was refluxed in 36% nitric acid at 80 °C for 3 h under magnetic stirring (400 rpm) to remove impurities and introduce oxygen-containing functional groups, then washed with deionized water until neutral pH and dried at 105 °C. For magnetization, 100 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (1:4 mass ratio to AC) was dissolved in 200 mL of deionized water and mixed with pretreated AC. The suspension was sonicated (40 kHz, 150 W) for 1 h, and the pH was adjusted to ~ 10 using 1 M NaOH under stirring (500 rpm). The precipitate was filtered, dried at 105 °C, and thermally treated at 550 °C for 2 h under nitrogen flow (100 mL/min, 5 °C/min heating rate) to stabilize Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The resulting MAC was washed with deionized water using an external magnet until neutral pH, dried at 110 °C for 12 h, and stored in a desiccator (Fig. 1)29.

Fig. 1Schematic illustration of MAC synthesis.

Schematic illustration of MAC synthesis.

All ozonation experiments were performed in a laboratory-scale semi-continuous glass reactor (effective volume 250 mL) placed under a fume hood. Oxygen (99.96%) was fed at 1.5 L/min into the ozone generator (Sahand Co., Iran), producing an ozone flux of ~ 35 mg/min30. The generated ozone was introduced into the reactor through a diffuser. A 1 L gas scrubber containing 2% potassium iodide solution was used downstream to trap excess ozone (Fig. 2)30.

Catalyst characterization

The structural and surface characteristics of the synthesized MAC nanocomposite were analyzed using various instrumental techniques. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4160, Japan) was carried out at magnifications of 500× and 50,000× under an accelerating voltage of 15 kV after gold coating of the samples under vacuum conditions. Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS, INCA software) was employed in area-scan mode to determine the elemental composition of the nanocomposite. X-ray Diffraction (XRD, Philips PW3040/60, Cu Kα radiation, 2θ range 0–120°, step size 0.02°, and 1 s per step) was used to identify the crystalline structure and phase composition based on the JCPDS standard card No. 19–0629 for Fe3O4.

The point of zero charge (pHzpc) of the MAC nanocomposite was determined by the pH drift method31. Briefly, 50 mL of 0.01 M NaCl solution was adjusted to initial pH values ranging from 2.0 to 12.0 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. Then, 0.05 g of the catalyst was added to each flask, and the suspensions were equilibrated under continuous shaking for 24 h at room temperature. After filtration, the final pH of each solution was measured, and the pHzpc was identified as the point where the initial and final pH values were equal.

The specific surface area of the synthesized catalyst was determined by nitrogen adsorption at 77 K using the BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) method. The analysis was performed with a Quantachrome TPRWin v1.50 instrument under a nitrogen flow rate of 20 mL min−1 at an ambient temperature of 298 K and a pressure of 660 mmHg. The adsorption data were collected at a relative pressure (P/P0) of 0.15.

Results and discussion

Catalyst characterization

The physicochemical properties of the synthesized MAC nanocomposite was systematically investigated using SEM, XRD, EDS and pHzpc analyses.

The surface morphology of the MAC nanocomposite was characterized using SEM at magnifications of 500× and 50,000× (Fig. 3). The micrographs revealed a rough and highly porous surface structure, which is advantageous for pollutant adsorption and the distribution of catalytic active sites. This porous architecture enhances the accessibility of reactive species, such as cefixime molecules, to the catalyst surface, thereby improving the efficiency of the ozonation process. The uniform dispersion of Fe3O4 nanoparticles on the activated carbon matrix further contributes to the catalyst’s effectiveness in AOPs.

Fig. 3SEM micrographs of the MAC nanocomposite surface at (a) 500× and (b) 50,000×.

SEM micrographs of the MAC nanocomposite surface at (a) 500× and (b) 50,000×.

XRD analysis of the MAC nanocomposite (Fig. 4) showed diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 30.1°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.4°, 57.0° and 62.6°, which were assigned to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511) and (440) crystallographic planes of Fe3O4, respectively (JCPDS No. 19–0629) (Fig. 4b). In addition, a broad diffraction feature centered at 2θ ≈ 24–26° was observed and attributed to the amorphous/graphitic signal of the activated carbon support. The reference peak list shown in Fig. 4b includes the JCPDS fingerprint for Fe3O4 and indicates minor overlap with peaks from other iron oxide phases referenced in the database.

Fig. 4(a) XRD pattern of the MAC nanocomposite, (b) Identification of catalyst compounds based on XRD spectrum.

(a) XRD pattern of the MAC nanocomposite, (b) Identification of catalyst compounds based on XRD spectrum.

EDS was employed to determine the elemental composition of the MAC nanocomposite (Fig. 5). The EDS spectra identified carbon, iron, and oxygen as the primary constituents. These results confirm the successful immobilization of Fe3O4 nanoparticles on the activated carbon support, with appropriate atomic ratios that align with the expected stoichiometry of magnetite. This composition supports the catalyst’s ability to facilitate redox reactions during the ozonation process15.

Fig. 5EDS spectrum of the MAC nanocomposite with elemental composition.

EDS spectrum of the MAC nanocomposite with elemental composition.

The point of zero charge (pHzpc) of the MAC nanocomposite was determined to be 7.7, indicating that the catalyst surface is positively charged in acidic conditions (pH < 7.7) and negatively charged in alkaline conditions (pH >7.7) (Fig. 6). This property significantly influences the electrostatic interactions between the catalyst and cefixime molecules, which have a pKa of approximately 3.73, existing predominantly in anionic form at pH values above this threshold. The pHzpc value suggests that the nanocomposite is particularly effective in neutral to slightly alkaline conditions, enhancing the adsorption and subsequent degradation of cefixime during catalytic ozonation6.

The physicochemical properties of the MAC nanocomposite were further compared with those of powdered activated carbon, as summarized in Table 4. The specific surface area of the nanocomposite was 814 m2/g, slightly lower than that of powdered activated carbon (907 m2/g), likely due to the incorporation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, which occupy some pore spaces32. The Fe3O4 nanoparticle size ranged from 25 to 30 nm, and the carbon purity decreased from 99.99% in powdered activated carbon to 89.2% in the nanocomposite, reflecting the addition of iron oxide. These characteristics collectively underscore the nanocomposite’s suitability for efficient pollutant removal in advanced oxidation processes7.

The obtained BET plot exhibited a slope of 0.05 and a monolayer adsorption volume of 20.21 cm3 g−1 (STP). The total surface area of the sample was calculated as 87.98 m2, and the specific surface area was 814.64 m2 g−1, indicating a highly porous structure suitable for catalytic applications.

Statistical validation of the model

In order to validate the significance and reliability of the RSM model, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, as summarized in Table 5. The results show that catalyst concentration, reaction time, persulfate concentration, cefixime concentration and pH were statistically significant factors (p < 0.05), with catalyst dosage presenting the highest influence (F = 60.233, p = 0.00001). Interaction terms such as AB, BD and BE also exhibited significant effects (p < 0.01), indicating notable synergistic interactions between factors. The low coefficient of variation (C.V. = 1.871%) and high adequacy precision (16.518) further confirm the model’s reliability within the experimental domain. The lack-of-fit test yielded F = 5.747 and p = 0.051, which is slightly above the 0.05 threshold, suggesting the model fits the data adequately though marginally.

Comparable AOP studies have similarly demonstrated that catalyst dosage is often the dominant factor influencing degradation efficiency, since it directly controls the number of active sites and radical generation pathways27,32,33. Moreover, the significant contribution of reaction time and persulfate dosage observed in this study aligns with earlier reports on persulfate-based oxidation, where prolonged exposure and sufficient oxidant levels are essential for maximizing pollutant mineralization10,34. Moving forward, caution is advised when extending predictions beyond the studied variable ranges. Effect of operating parameters

Effect of pH

pH is a key parameter influencing chemical processes, particularly AOPs38,39. In ozonation systems, higher pH values promote the generation of hydroxyl radicals, which enhances contaminant degradation efficiency40. As shown in Fig. 8, the effect of pH was more pronounced at higher cefixime concentrations, indicating that pH strongly influences the early stages of oxidation and facilitates direct mineralization40,41. While removal efficiency increased under alkaline conditions, the relationship was not strictly linear; the trend shifted from positive at low pH values to less favorable at very high pH levels. Although the theoretical optimum values for adsorbent surface charge (pKa = 3.45; pHzpc = 7.7) suggest acidic or near-neutral conditions, several studies have demonstrated that alkaline pH enhances hydroxyl radical generation during ozonation6,42,43. Consistently, in this study, an optimum pH of 8 was obtained. These findings are in line with the report of Erim et al. (2021)44, who found pH 5.93 optimal for ZnO/Fe3O4-assisted photocatalytic degradation of cefixime under UV-A irradiation, and with Behineh et al. (2022)44, who reported 93.18% cefixime removal at pH 5.7. Other studies have also confirmed that pH strongly influences photocatalytic processes, with some reporting maximum removal in acidic conditions34 and others under alkaline conditions8, largely due to the involvement of hydroxide ions in radical formation.

Effect of initial cefixime concentration

The initial antibiotic concentration is another crucial factor affecting AOP performance. Results indicated that removal efficiency decreased as the initial cefixime concentration increased, primarily because a constant ozone dosage produces a limited amount of hydroxyl radicals6,30. At higher pollutant loadings, these radicals are consumed more rapidly by cefixime molecules and intermediate products, leaving insufficient radicals for complete degradation30. Additionally, higher pollutant concentrations increase molecular collisions among cefixime molecules, reducing their interactions with oxidizing radicals7. In this study, removal efficiency decreased from 96.10% to 79.48% as the initial cefixime concentration increased, suggesting that higher pollutant concentrations require longer contact times for effective degradation (Fig. 8a). Similar results were reported by Nazir et al. (2021)8, Zhao et al.,(2022)45 and Zhang et al.,(2021)6 who observed that lower initial pollutant concentrations yielded higher removal efficiencies because catalyst availability per pollutant molecule was greater.

Effect of catalyst concentration

Catalyst loading also significantly affected cefixime degradation. The maximum removal efficiency was achieved at a catalyst concentration of 1.5 g/L (Fig. 8). Pereira et al. (2025)24 demonstrated that the catalytic activity of activated carbon can be enhanced by MgO modification, which provides additional active sites for hydroxyl radical generation. However, excessive catalyst loading may reduce efficiency due to turbidity, light scattering, and decreased mass transfer, as also noted by Vargues et al. (2021)25. Therefore, determining the optimal catalyst dosage is essential for both process efficiency and economic feasibility. In line with these findings, Paydar et al. (2024) demonstrated the strong adsorption potential of activated carbon and zeolite for cefixime and amoxicillin46. Similarly, Cheng et al. (2025) confirmed that catalytic ozonation effectively mitigates the acute and chronic toxicity of sulfamethoxazole47, while Liu et al. (2022) highlighted that surface modification of activated carbon significantly enhances radical generation and pollutant degradation48. Collectively, these studies support the present findings that optimized catalyst loading plays a decisive role in catalytic ozonation performance. Several studies have proposed the integration of one or more co-oxidants into photocatalytic processes to enhance efficiency4,9. In a study by Sheikhmohammadi et al. (2020), the introduction of co-oxidants significantly accelerated the degradation rate of preservatives and promoted mineralization of the target pollutant, while simultaneously reducing energy consumption and operational costs by more than fivefold11.

Effect of reaction time

Reaction time is an essential operational parameter in AOPs, as it dictates the extent of oxidant–pollutant interactions26,27,45. In this study, removal efficiency increased with reaction time, particularly during the first 45 min, after which the rate of increase diminished. Figure 8d demonstrates a strong synergistic effect between reaction time and persulfate concentration, where prolonged reaction time (up to 45 min) and higher persulfate concentrations (3.0–4.0 mM) significantly enhanced cefixime degradation. The initial sharp increase can be attributed to rapid radical generation and pollutant oxidation, while the slower subsequent rate reflects the progressive depletion of cefixime and the competitive consumption of radicals by intermediates3,7. Optimizing contact time is therefore important not only for maximizing removal efficiency but also for reducing operational costs and energy consumption. Comparable results were reported by Zhang et al. (2021), who achieved high-performance cefixime degradation within 30 min of plasma treatment, underscoring the critical role of exposure duration6.

Effect of persulfate concentration

Persulfate has emerged as a promising oxidant in AOPs due to its high solubility, stability, and strong oxidative potential16,17. However, activation is required to cleave the O–O bond and generate sulfate radicals (SO4•–, E° = 2.67 V)21. In the present study, increasing persulfate dosage significantly improved cefixime degradation efficiency, confirming the strong oxidative contribution of sulfate radicals in catalytic ozonation systems. These findings are consistent with previous reports, Lin et al. demonstrated efficient removal of ofloxacin by UV-activated persulfate, Ghauch et al. reported complete chloramphenicol degradation under UV-254 activation, and Kattel et al. observed enhanced amoxicillin degradation via persulfate activation49–51. Collectively, these results highlight the synergistic role of persulfate in promoting radical-based degradation pathways for antibiotics in aqueous systems.

Kinetic study

To investigate the degradation mechanism, experiments were performed to evaluate the kinetics of cefixime removal. The results, depicted in Fig. 9, confirmed that the degradation process adheres to a pseudo-first-order kinetic model, with an excellent correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9964). The calculated apparent rate constant (k = 0.0135 min− 1) surpasses values reported for other AOPs targeting cefixime, such as UV/PMS (0.012 min− 1, Khazaei et al., 2019)52 and photocatalysis (0.015 min− 1, Zhang et al., 2020)6. This enhanced performance underscores the synergistic effect of MAC catalysts combined with persulfate and ozonation, promoting efficient radical generation and sustained degradation.

Process optimization

Using RSM with a central composite design, the optimal conditions for cefixime removal were determined as follows: pH = 8, catalyst dosage = 1.5 g/L, cefixime concentration = 57.5 mg/L, persulfate concentration = 1.75 mM, and reaction time = 46.25 min. Under these conditions, the predicted removal efficiency was 97%, while experimental validation achieved 96.1%, demonstrating the model’s accuracy and practical applicability (Figs. 8 and 10). Although the difference between R2-adj (0.831) and R2-pred (0.645) suggests some degree of model complexity, this discrepancy is likely related to the inherent nonlinearity and experimental variability of the system. The inclusion of quadratic terms helps to better represent the curvature and interaction effects in the process. Therefore, the model was considered reliable for describing the experimental domain, while acknowledging that prediction accuracy might be affected by the system’s intrinsic variability. Similar studies on antibiotic removal using RSM have reported close agreement between predicted and experimental efficiencies, confirming the reliability of CCD in process optimization7.

In comparison, standalone magnetic activated carbon required nearly twice the reaction time (~ 90 min) to attain similar removal efficiency for a structurally related cephalosporin, while persulfate activated by granular activated carbon alone needed over five times longer (~ 240 min) to degrade 80% of another antibiotic35. Catalytic ozonation using metal oxide-loaded activated carbon, although effective within ~ 50 min for TOC reduction of a sulfonamide antibiotic, showed limited catalytic advantage at neutral pH and demanded higher catalyst doses37,53.

Economic and practical considerations

From a practical standpoint, it is important to recognize that operational expenses in advanced oxidation processes are often influenced by energy demand and oxidant supply7,33. However, the relatively short reaction time and high degradation efficiency observed in this system indicate promising potential for balancing performance with cost-effectiveness. Based on recent comparative studies, the specific energy consumption (EE/O) indicator and chemical cost per unit volume are appropriate metrics for economic evaluation of AOPs. For example, published data for ozonation and hybrid AOPs show cost ranges from approximately USD 100 to USD 150 per m3 at laboratory scale59. Compared with other advanced treatments such as UV- or plasma-based processes, the proposed method shows competitive advantages in terms of efficiency and applicability60. Moreover, its added environmental benefit in mitigating antibiotic resistance highlights its broader relevance. Future efforts may focus on further economic optimization through strategies such as catalyst regeneration and coupling with sustainable ozone generation methods and detailed cost modelling including energy (kWh/m3), oxidant (USD/kg) and maintenance (USD/year) components.

Study limitations and future perspectives

This study demonstrates the high efficiency of MAC nanocomposite in persulfate-assisted catalytic ozonation; however, several limitations remain. (i) Experiments were conducted in synthetic wastewater, which may not capture the complexity of real effluents containing competing organic and inorganic species. (ii) By-products were not systematically analyzed; although complete mineralization was not the focus, future studies should employ TOC and LC-MS analyses to ensure environmental safety. (iii) Catalyst stability and reusability were not fully evaluated; regeneration studies are necessary to confirm long-term feasibility. (iv) The radical generation mechanisms proposed (Eqs. 3–7) were theoretically discussed; future work should include experimental validation through radical scavenging or EPR analyses to confirm these pathways. Addressing these aspects in future work will strengthen the applicability of this process for industrial-scale wastewater treatment.

Conclusion

This study confirmed the high efficiency of catalytic ozonation assisted by magnetized activated carbon as a persulfate activator in the removal of cefixime from aqueous solutions. RSM based on CCD identified pH, antibiotic concentration, catalyst dosage, persulfate concentration, and reaction time as significant operational parameters influencing removal performance. The optimized conditions yielded nearly complete degradation of cefixime, highlighting the potential of this process as a promising strategy for mitigating antibiotic contamination in water systems. Beyond its effectiveness, the approach demonstrates robustness and adaptability, offering valuable implications for advanced wastewater treatment. Future investigations should focus on catalyst recovery and reusability, identification of degradation by-products, and potential integration with complementary treatment technologies to enhance scalability and environmental safety.
