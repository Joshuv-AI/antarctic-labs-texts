Group 2 innate lymphocytes protect the balance between autophagy and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes during sepsis-induced cardiac injury
Authors: Kun Fang, Hong Chen, Jianhong Xie, Dongsheng Sun, Li Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroup 2 innate lymphocytes (ILC2) have an important role in orchestrating sepsis-induced immune response. However, the impact of LC2 on sepsis-induced cardiac injury is still not fully understood. This study investigated the mechanisms governing ILC2 activation within the cardiac tissue after sepsis. In vivo experiments using wild-type and IL-33 deficient mice indicated that the presence of interleukin (IL)-33, which participates in expanding and activating ILC2 cells, was correlated with higher ILC2 levels (246 ± 34 vs. 66 ± 18, p < 0.01), reduced cardiac dysfunction, and lower markers of cardiac injury. Conversely, IL-33 deficiency led to exacerbated cardiac damage. Additionally, heart ILC2 significantly increased the expression and secretion of IL-5 (2.18 ± 0.34 ng/ml vs. 1.18 ± 0.24 ng/ml, p < 0.05) and IL-13 (10.55 ± 1.13 ng/ml vs. 7.59 ± 1.13 ng/ml, p < 0.05) following sepsis, with this response being mediated by IL-33. Moreover, IL-5 deficient mice exhibited increased cardiac dysfunction and myocardial apoptosis post-sepsis (20.7 ± 4.28% vs. 29.61 ± 4.28%, p < 0.05). Furthermore, in vitro experiments involving co-cultures of ILC2 with mice cardiomyocytes after lipopolysaccharide (LPS) administration suggested that IL-5 derived from ILC2 protects cardiomyocytes from autophagy and apoptosis. These findings imply that IL-33, released in response to sepsis, induces ILC2 activation and IL-5 secretion, orchestrating the equilibrium between autophagy and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes and offering potential therapeutic avenues for mitigating sepsis-induced cardiac injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76606-4.Keywords: Group 2 innate lymphocytes, Sepsis, Autophagy, Apoptosis, Cardiac injurySubject terms: Cell biology, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Molecular medicine

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening medical emergency caused by the overwhelming response of the organism to an infection. It is a highly complex syndrome with a convoluted pathway from infection to death consisting of multiple organ dysfunction1, with the heart being one of the most commonly affected organs2. Thus, understanding the mechanisms of sepsis-induced cardiac injury and identifying the therapeutic targets may help reduce the morbidity and mortality in patients with sepsis-induced cardiac injury.

The innate immune response, which serves as the primary defense against infections, has a role in sepsis. Innate immune cells and their mediated cytokine storm are key contributors to myocardial damage in sepsis3–5; their activation has an important part in organ failure and mortality in septic patients6,7. Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), a new class of immune cells with adaptive immune functions that differentiate from T and B lymphocytes, have emerging roles in orchestrating immune response, maintaining metabolic homeostasis, and regulating tissue inflammation. ILCs are categorized into three subgroups, i.e., ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3, based on cytokine secretion patterns and transcription factor expressions. ILC2 is predominantly found in adipose-associated lymphoid tissue, lymph nodes, spleen, liver, intestine, and airways; it secretes type 2 cytokines like IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, and is primarily involved in anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair8,9. Recent studies have also discovered that ILC2 regulates the immune response during acute infection10. Survivors of sepsis exhibit higher levels of Th2 cells in the early stages of infection compared to those who succumbed. Animal studies have found that administering type 2 cytokines secreted by ILC2 reduces mortality in sepsis mice lacking functional ILC211. Another study reported that activating ILC2 in the lungs of septic mice alleviates pneumonia responses, thereby decreasing sepsis mortality12,13. While these findings suggest a protective effect of ILC2 against sepsis by improving immune dysfunction, the impact of LC2 against sepsis-induced cardiac injury is still not fully understood.

The presence of ILC2 has recently been detected in the heart14.Studies have suggested that ILC2 constitutes the primary population of ILC in mouse heart tissue (1.7%), and cardiac ILC2 exhibits a distinct phenotype concerning surface markers, transcription factors, proliferation, and cytokine production15. However, the mechanism governing heart ILC2 recruitment and the regulation of functional activity during the progression of myocardial injury following sepsis is still unclear.

This study aimed to investigate the mechanisms governing ILC2 activation within the cardiac tissue after sepsis.

Results

ILC2 increase in mouse heart post-CLP surgery

To investigate the impact of ILC2 on sepsis-induced cardiac damage, we assessed the accumulation of ILC2 in the heart following cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery. ILC2 phenotypes were identified as Lin−CD127+ST2−CD117+. The gating strategy for heart ILC2s is depicted in Figure S1. To track the accumulation of ILC2 in septic hearts, we induced sepsis through CLP or carried out sham surgery on WT mice. At 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-surgery, mice were euthanized, hearts were collected, after which single cells were prepared, and the percentage of ILC2s was analyzed using flow cytometry (Fig. 1A). Following CLP, a significantly higher number of heart Lin−CD127+ST2−CD117+ ILC2s were observed in septic hearts compared to sham-operated hearts. The levels of heart ILC2 were highest at 6 h post-CLP surgery (370 ± 40 cells). The levels returned to normal levels (compared to a sham group) by 24 h (Fig. 1B). In addition, the quantity of heart ILC2 was negatively correlated with N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels at 6 h post-CLP surgery (r = -0.47, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C). The increased numbers of heart ILC2 in post-septic hearts suggest their involvement in sepsis-induced cardiac injury.

IL-33 is required for sepsis-induced ILC2 expansion

Considering that IL-33 participates in expanding and activating ILC2 cells16–18, we measured IL-33 mRNA levels in the heart using RT-qPCR and IL-33 concentration in the plasma of CLP mice for up to 24 h. IL-33 mRNA levels in the heart (53.94 ± 2.31 vs. 0.5 ± 0.2, p < 0.01) and plasma (25.10 ± 2.02 pg/ml vs. 3.17 ± 1.15 pg/ml, p < 0.01) significantly increased, peaking at 6 h post-CLP (Fig. 2A and B).

To ascertain whether IL-33 mediates sepsis-induced ILC2 recruitment in the hearts, WT mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of recombinant IL-33 (40 µg/kg) or PBS (control) 30 min prior to CLP surgery. At 6 h post-injection, we examined both the percentage and absolute count of ILC2s in the heart. IL-33 administration increased heart ILC2 levels compared to the PBS control (238 ± 29 cells vs. 53 ± 10 cells, p < 0.01), matching the levels induced by CLP at 6 h (Fig. 2C and D).

To confirm the role of IL-33 in mediating ILC2 expansion within the heart, IL-33−/− mice underwent CLP, and heart ILC2 percentages and absolute numbers were measured 24 h post-surgery. IL-33 deficiency inhibited expansion of ILC2 in the hearts following sepsis (69 ± 18 cells vs. 247 ± 34 cells, p < 0.01). Furthermore, intraperitoneal administration of IL-33 to IL-33−/− mice reinstated the increase in ILC2 observed in the hearts after CLP (194 ± 28 cells vs. 69 ± 18 cells, p < 0.01), as observed in flow cytometry (Fig. 2E and F). These findings underscore the essential role of IL-33 in promoting the expansion of ILC2 cells in the hearts following sepsis.

ILC2 protects against sepsis-induced cardiac injury

Previous reports have suggested that ILC2 has a role in acute cardiac injury induced by myocardial infarction19,20. In this study, we further explored whether heart ILC2 is involved in protecting against sepsis-induced cardiac injury. We conducted experiments on WT and IL-33−/− mice by subjecting them to CLP and evaluating their heart function with echocardiography. Additionally, the cardiac injury was evaluated by measuring NT-proBNP, a biomarker of cardiac injury, in the plasma through chemiluminescence. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, compared to mice in the sham group, WT mice subjected to CLP exhibited reduced cardiac function, as evidenced by decreased ejection fraction (EF, 39.13 ± 5.80% vs. 59.42 ± 10.87%, p < 0.01) and fractional shortening (FS, 29.78 ± 3.31% vs. 37.87 ± 2.57%, p < 0.01), along with increased left ventricular anterior wall thickness at end-diastole (LVAW-d, 1.37 ± 0.04 vs. 0.99 ± 0.04 mm, p < 0.01) and left ventricular anterior wall thickness at end-systole (LVAW-s, 0.47 ± 0.01 vs. 0.31 ± 0.05 mm, p < 0.01). Notably, IL-33−/− septic mice demonstrated a further reduction in cardiac function compared to WT septic mice. In Fig. 3C, the levels of plasma NT-proBNP were markedly higher in WT mice following CLP compared to those observed in the sham group (7.35 ± 0.30 vs. 5.22 ± 0.22 ng/ml, p < 0.01). Similarly, IL-33−/− septic mice displayed increased NT-proBNP levels compared to WT septic mice (8.39 ± 0.26 vs. 7.35 ± 0.30 ng/ml, p < 0.05).

To ascertain a causal link between reduced ILC2 levels in the heart and diminished cardiac function in IL-33−/− mice post sepsis, we conducted in vitro co-culture experiments of ILC2 with rat cardiomyocytes in the presence and absence of LPS, with or without ILC2 neutralizing antibody CD90 or IL-33. As illustrated in Fig. 3D, co-culture with ILC2 mitigated LPS-induced cTnI in cardiomyocytes (10.05 ± 1.33 vs. 64.39 ± 2.83 pg/ml, p < 0.01), an effect mitigated by CD90 (25.65 ± 2.32 vs. 64.39 ± 2.83 pg/ml, p < 0.01), suggesting a direct protective impact of ILC2 on cardiomyocytes in sepsis. However, adding IL-33 to the culture medium did not prevent the increase of cTnI induced by LPS. These findings suggest that IL-33-induced expansion of ILC2 in the heart protects against sepsis-induced cardiac injury; yet, we also discovered that IL-33 alone did not exert this protective effect.

Characterization of cytokine expression and secretion from heart ILC2 in sepsis

Several studies have underscored the pivotal role of ILC2 as a significant cellular source of type 2 cytokines10,21,22. To characterize the type 2 cytokines released by heart ILC2 post-sepsis, IL-5 and IL-13 expression in the augmented population of heart ILC2 were quantified using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Compared to a sham surgery group, a substantial increase in IL-5-positive (IL-5+) ILC2 (8.97 ± 1.41 vs. 3.03 ± 1.21%, p < 0.01) and IL-13-positive (IL-13+) ILC2 (29.92 ± 4.03 vs. 14.79 ± 2.02%, P < 0.01) was observed at 6 h post-CLP surgery (Fig. 4A and B).

Subsequently, we assessed plasma levels of IL-5 and IL-13 up to 24 h post-CLP surgery, demonstrating a marked elevation, peaking at 6 h post-CLP surgery (IL-5: 2.49 ± 0.27 pg/ml vs. 1.11 ± 0.27 pg/ml, p < 0.05; IL-13: 10.93 ± 0.89 pg/ml vs. 6.61 ± 0.89 pg/ml, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). IL-33−/− mice showed diminished serum levels of IL-5 (1.18 ± 0.24 vs. 2.18 ± 0.34 pg/ml, p < 0.05) and IL-13 (7.59 ± 1.13 vs. 10.55 ± 1.33 pg/ml, p < 0.05), as well as reduced percentage of IL-5+ILC2 (2.27 ± 0.50% vs. 9.08 ± 3.40 pg/ml, p < 0.01) and IL-13+ ILC2 cells (10.50 ± 1.01 vs. 29.83 ± 4.03 pg/ml, p < 0.01) in both blood and heart post-CLP surgery, compared to WT septic mice (Fig. 4D and E). These findings indicate a specific pattern of cytokine expression and release from heart ILC2, which is heightened in the hearts following sepsis due to an IL-33-related mechanism.

IL-5 induces myocardial autophagy to prevent myocardial apoptosis and injury

Previous studies have emphasized the significance of the balance between autophagy and apoptosis homeostasis in cardiomyocytes as a crucial mechanism in myocardial injury23–25. To elucidate the involvement of IL-5 in regulating this balance in cardiomyocytes, we initially assessed cardiac function in WT and IL5-/- mice at 24 h post-CLP. As depicted in Fig. 5A and B, compared to WT septic mice, IL-5-/- septic mice exhibited a pronounced reduction in cardiac function, as evidenced by decreased ejection fraction (29.61 ± 7.24 vs. 39.47 ± 5.92%, p < 0.05) and fractional shortening (25 ± 5.26 vs. 29.61 ± 3.29%, p < 0.05), along with increased LVAW-d (1.51 ± 0.05 mm vs. 1.37 ± 0.04 mm, p < 0.05) and LVAW-s (0.53 ± 0.02 vs. 0.45 ± 0.01 mm, p < 0.05). Similarly, in Fig. 5C, the elevated NT-proBNP levels were further increased in IL-5-/- mice at 24 h post-CLP in contrast to WT mice (9.13 ± 0.29 vs. 7.83 ± 0.38 ng/ml, p < 0.05).

Autophagosomes were identified using transmission electron microscopy (TEM); Western blotting was then employed to evaluate autophagic activity in the hearts of septic mice. As illustrated in Fig. 5D, E and a reduced number of autophagosomes was evident in mice subjected to CLP compared to those undergoing sham surgery. Myocardial apoptosis was observed in WT or IL-5-/- mice post-CLP surgery through terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining. The results indicated a significant increase in the apoptosis index at 24 h post-CLP surgery in WT mice compared to those in the sham surgery group. Notably, this increase was more substantial in IL-5-/- septic mice compared to WT septic mice (Fig. 5H and I). Additionally, Western blot analysis of cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 5F and G) showed elevated caspase-3 activation 24 h after CLP in WT mice relative to the sham group. This activation was markedly higher in IL-5-/- septic mice, further corroborating the heightened apoptotic activity in the absence of IL-5.

Therefore, based on the data above, we concluded that IL-5 protects against CLP-induced myocardial apoptosis and injury by inducing myocardial autophagy.

ILC2-derived IL-5 protects cardiomyocytes from autophagy

To examine our hypothesis that IL-5 may mediate ILC2-dependent protection of cardiomyocytes in sepsis, we initially co-cultured ILC2 with rat cardiomyocytes in vitro with and without the addition of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (5 µg/ml), with or without neutralizing antibodies against ILC2 (CD90) or IL-5, and assessed the number of autophagosomes in cardiomyocytes. As shown in Fig. 6A and B, the number of autophagosomes decreased in cardiomyocytes co-cultured with ILC2 after 24 h of LPS treatment (2.03 ± 0.27 vs. 4.96 ± 0.85 per cell, p < 0.01). Furthermore, the reduction in autophagosome numbers following LPS treatment was significantly mitigated by CD90 (4.21 ± 0.32 vs. 4.96 ± 0.85 per cell, p < 0.01), suggesting that ILC2 protects cardiomyocytes from autophagy.

To examine whether IL-5 originates from ILC2, we co-cultured ILC2 with cardiomyocytes with LPS present in vitro, with or without ILC2 neutralizing antibody CD90 or IL-5. However, ILC2 no longer protected cardiomyocytes from autophagy. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining for light chain 3B revealed a marked reduction in autophagy in cardiomyocytes co-cultured with ILC2 post-LPS treatment compared to vehicle treatment (10.57 ± 4.89 vs. 47.96 ± 4.45%, p < 0.01), which was abolished by ILC2 neutralizing antibody CD90 (19.61 ± 3.11 vs. 10.57 ± 4.89%, p < 0.01) or IL-5 (16 ± 2 vs. 10.57 ± 4.89%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6C and D). These observations suggest that IL-5 produced by ILC2 protects cardiomyocytes from autophagy and is essential in sepsis-related cardiac injury.

Subsequently, we assessed cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that increased apoptosis of cardiomyocytes co-cultured with ILC2 after LPS treatment (12.6 ± 0.26% vs. 1.9 ± 0.8%, p < 0.01), which was abolished by ILC2 neutralizing antibody CD90 (6.02 ± 1.03% vs. 12.6 ± 0.26%, p < 0.01) or IL-5 (4.81 ± 0.92% vs. 12.6 ± 0.26%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6E and F).

In summary, these findings imply that ILC2-derived IL-5 protects cardiomyocytes from apoptosis and has a role in sepsis-related cardiac damage.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that reported how ILC2 activation induces the production and secretion of IL-5, preserving the balance between autophagy and apoptosis of cardiomyocytes. This data further clarifies the regulatory mechanisms within the innate immune response involved in myocardial protection during sepsis and may offer potential targets for preventing and treating myocardial damage following sepsis.

ILC2 lacks antigen receptors and toll-like receptors, and its activation is mainly orchestrated by inflammatory mediators such as IL-33 and IL-25. IL-33 can stimulate ILC2 to release IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, thereby inhibiting the initiation and progression of inflammation26. Moreover, IL-33 deficiency results in decreased infiltration of innate immune cells, such as neutrophils and monocytes, into the lungs, along with decreased levels of inflammatory cytokines in circulation, lung, and liver, consequently reducing lung injury12. Also, studies have demonstrated that IL-33 regulates the sepsis-induced innate immune response and participates in myocardial protection, with the quantity of ILC2 in the heart of septic mice changing significantly and correlating with the level of IL-3316,27–30. In this study, IL-33 resulted in a surge in cardiac ILC2 in mice, resembling the induction by CLP surgery. However, IL-33 deficiency prevented the expansion of ILC2 in the hearts following CLP surgery; intraperitoneal administration of IL-33 to IL-33-/- mice restored the increase in heart ILC2 after CLP. Therefore, for sepsis-induced expansion of ILC2, IL-33 is required.

The administration of a neutralizing antibody against IL-5 effectively inhibits the infiltration of neutrophils and monocytes into the lungs of septic mice. Conversely, the over-expression of IL-5 reinstated the diminished leukocyte infiltration observed in IL-33-/- mice31. Furthermore, IL-33 demonstrated the capacity to induce the amplification of IL-5-producing ILC2s, thereby fostering IL-5-dependent lung injury and vascular inflammation12,13,32. The IL-5 receptor is expressed on the surface of neutrophils and mononuclear macrophages in septic mice, with IL-5 enhancing the phagocytosis and survival of macrophages33,34. These findings were corroborated in septic patients, with higher plasma IL-5 levels found in survivors compared to non-survivors35. The therapeutic administration of IL-5 successfully increased the number of circulating neutrophils and monocytes in septic mice, ultimately reducing the mortality rate35, thereby reinforcing the protective role of IL-5 during sepsis. Our earlier investigations revealed that IL-5 inhibits cytokine levels in the plasma of septic mice, consequently mitigating myocardial damage36. In the current study, IL-5-/- septic mice exhibited a further reduction in cardiac function and increased levels of NT-proBNP compared to their WT septic counterparts. Additionally, both the antibodies against ILC2 and IL-5 significantly alleviated cardiomyocyte apoptosis induced by LPS in vitro, suggesting that ILC2-derived IL-5 mediates the protection of cardiomyocytes from apoptosis and is implicated in sepsis-associated cardiac injury.

The dysregulation between autophagy and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes is a pivotal mechanism contributing to myocardial injury37. In septic myocardium, autophagy and apoptosis are heightened, and their interplay significantly influences the onset and progression of myocardial injury during sepsis38. Previous studies39–41 have demonstrated a substantial increase in autophagy-related proteins LC3II/I and beclin1, a decrease in p62, and an enhancement in autophagy in rat cardiomyocytes treated with LPS. Concurrently, cardiomyocyte apoptosis also escalated. Treatment with the autophagy inhibitor (3-MA) further intensified cardiomyocyte apoptosis, whereas the addition of the autophagy enhancer (rapamycin) significantly reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, underscoring the protective role of autophagy against LPS-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis. A mouse septic model further confirmed that maintaining autophagy-apoptosis homeostasis can alleviate the degree of cardiac damage during sepsis37. Notably, during sepsis-associated myocardial injury, the inflammatory storm mediated by the immune response typically precedes the onset and progression of myocardial damage. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether the innate immune response, particularly the ILC2-mediated type 2 immune response, is implicated in regulating the balance between autophagy and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes. In our present study, the NT-proBNP levels of cardiomyocytes co-cultured with ILC2 induced by LPS markedly decreased, and this effect could be alleviated by the neutralizing antibody against ILC2 (CD90), suggesting that ILC2 exerts a direct protective impact on cardiomyocytes during sepsis. The percentage of IL-5+ ILC2 significantly increased in CLP mice compared to sham surgery. Similarly, the production and secretion of IL-5 significantly rose following LPS stimulation in vitro. Furthermore, cardiomyocyte apoptosis increased, and autophagy declined significantly following LPS stimulation, while both were dramatically alleviated when pretreated with antibodies against ILC2 or IL-5. These findings demonstrate that activated ILC2 modulates the balance between autophagy and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes by secreting and producing IL-5, thereby contributing to sepsis-associated cardiac injury.

The mechanism by which IL-5 regulates the equilibrium between autophagy and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes remains unclear. Liu et al.42 observed a beneficial function of ILC2 by producing IL5 to promote the growth and specialization of eosinophils and dendritic cells in mice post-myocardial infarct model. Eosinophils and dendritic cells participate in shielding cardiomyocytes from undergoing apoptosis. In addition to secreting IL-5, ILC2 secrete IL-13 and IL-9, which also have significant roles in sepsis-induced cardiac damage. Chen et al.21 found that although ILC2 constituted a small proportion, they exhibited significant growth among ST2 + leukocytes following IL-33 administration. ILC2 also exhibited the highest level of ST2 expression on their surface, indicating that ILC2 is a primary target in the heart following IL-33 administration. Further, IL-13 has an essential role in IL-33-induced cardiac protection by regulating M2 macrophage (M2ϕ) polarization.

As exogenous IL-33 elicits systemic responses, its effects are not confined to the activation of cardiac ILC2s. Furthermore, the function of IL-33 in promoting ILC2 activation in other organs and its contribution to cardiac protection via elevation of systemic serum factors remain elusive. Additionally, it is uncertain whether ILC2s activated by IL-33 from the bone marrow or spleen migrate to and gather in damaged heart tissue. Therefore, the precise pathophysiological roles of cardiac-resident ILC2s under both homeostatic and pathological conditions necessitate further elucidation.

In conclusion, we have elucidated the important role of cardiac ILC2 in sepsis-associated myocardial injury and clarified that ILC2 activation induces the production and secretion of IL-5, preserving the balance between autophagy and apoptosis of cardiomyocytes, and therefore acting as an antagonist to sepsis-induced myocardial injury. Our findings provide new ideas and intervention targets for myocardial protection during sepsis.

Methods

Mice

Male C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice aged 8–10 weeks were obtained from the Charles River Laboratory Animal Technology Corporation (Beijing, China). IL-5-deficient (IL-5-/-) mice and IL-33-deficient (IL-33-/-) mice were kindly provided by Professor Chengchao Ruan from the Department of Physiology and Pathophysiology, Fudan University, China. The mice were kept in cages with a maximum of 4–5 mice per cage and housed in an environment with a temperature of 22–25 ºC, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a light/dark cycle of 12/12 hr, periodic air changes, and ad libitum access to water and food. The experimental protocol adhered to ARRIVE guidelines and recommendations from an NIH-sponsored workshop on experimental design and reporting standards. The study followed the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals (ZJCLA) (Hangzhou, China) (protocol ZJCLA-IACUC-20100006) and conformed to the ethical principles for animal experimentation adopted.

Following established procedures, cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) procedures12 were used to induce mid-grade sepsis in the mice. In specific experiments, mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of recombinant murine interleukin (IL)-33 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) at a dose of 40 µg/kg, 30 min prior to CLP surgery.

Heart single-cell suspension preparation and flow cytometry (FACS)

Heart single-cell suspension was prepared according to established protocols. Briefly, the single-cell suspension was isolated through density gradient centrifugation with lymphocyte separation medium (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA) and pre-mixed with PBS. The cell suspension was then mixed with a staining buffer containing the following antibodies anti-mouse IL33R-BV421 (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA), anti-mouse CD127 PE (eBioscience, San Diego, California), anti-mouse Lineage cocktail PercpCY5.5 (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA), anti-mouse CD45-APC (Biolegend, San Diego, CA), and 7-aad (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA). Flow cytometry (FACS) was utilized to simultaneously detect Lin, CD45, CD127, CD117, IL33R BV421, and FVS510 expressions, enabling the identification of lymphocytes, ILCs, ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3. Specific antibodies to IL-5-PE-Cyanine 7 (eBioscience, San Diego, California), IL-9-Percp-Cy5.5 (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA), and IL-13-eFluor 450 (eBioscience, San Diego, CA) were incubated with the cells for intracellular staining, following incubation with Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (50 ng/ml), ionomycin (500 ng/ml), and brefeldin A (1 µg/ml) for 4 h. All FACS experiments were conducted on a CytoFLEX (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA), and FlowJo software was used for data analysis (version 10.8.1; FlowJo LLC, United States).

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) isolation and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mice hearts using TRIZOL reagent (Life Technologies, Pittsburgh, PA). cDNA was synthesized using iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on a Bio-Rad iQ5 real-time PCR machine. The results were normalized to 18 S values, analyzed using the comparative CT method, and shown as fold increases over controls.

Multiple mouse cytokines detection

Plasma was separated through centrifugation at 2000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C. The concentrations of IL-5, IL-13, and IL-33 in plasma or cell suspension were simultaneously determined using a mouse-premixed multi-analyte kit (R&D system, Minneapolis, MN), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Plates were read using a Luminex analyzer (X-200, Luminex, USA) within 90 min.

Analysis of myocardial injury biomarkers

The concentration of N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and cardiac tropnin I (cTnI) was determined using a chemiluminescence kit (Cloud-Clone, Wuhan, China). The absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (SpectraMax, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA),

Echocardiography

A Vevo 1100 high-frequency micro-ultrasound imaging system with a 70-MHz transducer (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) was employed to measure mouse cardiac function. Two-dimensional echocardiographic views of the mid-ventricular short axis and parasternal long axes M-mode were obtained at baseline and 24 h post-CLP. M-mode tracings at mid-papillary muscle level were recorded to measure left ventricular end-diastole and end-systole anterior wall thickness (LVAW-d and LVAW-s). The percentage of fractional shortening (% FS) and the percentage of ejection fraction (% EF) were calculated using the built-in software package.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM (TECNA-10, Philips, Holland) was used to quantify autophagosomes. Briefly, the number of autophagosomes in the myocardium was determined in each of 10 fields across three different sections of the samples (bar = 500 nm, n = 5 per group).

Terminal deoxyribonucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTPbiotin nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

In situ myocardial or cardiomyocyte apoptosis was determined using the TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay (In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit; Roche Diagnostics). Four randomly chosen fields were examined per slide at ×200 magnification. The apoptotic index is qualified as follows: apoptotic index = number of apoptotic cells/ (apoptotic cell count + number of non-apoptotic cells) *100%.

Flow cytometry

Annexin V fluorescein isothiocyanate and propidium iodide (KeyGEN BioTECH) staining were applied to assess early and late apoptosis. Phosphatidylserine on the outer leaflet of apoptotic cell membranes was analyzed. Cells were assessed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed in ice-cold 100% methanol. After rinses with PBS (5 min each), the cells were incubated with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-light chain 3B antibody (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Following washes with PBS, the samples were incubated for 60 min at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 5 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope system (Olympus Corporation).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using TRIZOL (GibcoBRL, Gaithersburg, USA) and quantified via the Bradford method. Then, 20 µg of protein was denatured by boiling at 98ºC for 5 min, then separated by tris-glycine sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) -polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against anti-light chain (LC3, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Beclin-1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), or anti-GAPDH (1:1000; Biyuntian, Wuhan, China) at 4℃ overnight. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) were then applied for 2 h at room temperature, followed by visualization using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Amersham, UK). GAPDH was used as the loading control.

ILC2 isolation and cell sorting

ILC2 were isolated and sorted as previously described12. For ILC2 sorting, the cell suspension was stained with lineage cocktail antibodies, anti-ST2 antibody (MD Biosciences, Oakdale, MN), anti-CD90.2 antibody (eBioscience, San Diego, CA), anti-KLRG1 antibody (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA), and anti-Sca-1 antibody (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA) for 30 min at 4 °C. Lin-CD90.2 + ST2 + KLRG1- cells or Lin-CD90.2 + ST2 + CD45 + cells were sorted using FACSAria (Beckman coulter, United States). The purity of ILC2p was considered when the following criteria were emt: purity > 98% and cell viability > 95%. A cell suspension of 2 × 10^5 ILC2 in 1 mL of PBS was prepared for subsequent experiments.

Co-culture of cardiomyocytes and ILC2

Neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes, obtained from Sprague-Dawley pups aged 1 to 2 days, were isolated and cultured following established procedures. The co-culture of rat cardiomyocytes and mouse ILC2 was implemented using a transwell plate with a polycarbonate membrane of 0.4 μm pore size (Corning™, Glendale, AZ, USA). ILC2, at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/ml, was cultured in the upper chamber, while cardiomyocytes were positioned in the lower insert. After 24 h of co-culture, the cell suspension was collected for the subsequent experiment.

Statistical analysis

The results are displayed as means with deviation (SD) indicated. Student’s t-test was used for both paired and unpaired data points along with analysis of variance (ANOVA). We used Scheffe’s test for multiple comparisons among groups and one-way ANOVA to assess variations between multiple groups. The Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between the ILC2 numbers and NT-proBNP. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.
