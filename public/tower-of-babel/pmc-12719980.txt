Insights into antibiotic resistance and prevalence of Stenotrophomonas maltophilia isolated from clinical samples in Iran: A systematic review
Authors: Fatemeh Sameni, Ali Dadashi, Bahareh Hajikhani, Maedeh Pourali Eshkalak, Alex van Belkum, Ali Hashemi, Azadeh Alirezaei, Masoud Dadashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfections caused by Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (S. maltophilia) are particularly difficult to treat because of their intrinsic resistance to multiple antibiotics.ObjectiveThe main aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the prevalence of infections caused by this bacterium across different provinces of Iran. Additionally, the study assessed the resistance rates of commonly prescribed antibiotics to address existing gaps in therapeutic knowledge.MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted to identify relevant original research articles published in English between 2000 and 2024, using Google Scholar, Medline (via PubMed), Embase, and Web of Science. Following screening, all studies reporting antibiotic resistance in clinical isolates of S. maltophilia from Iran were included and analyzed.ResultsA total of 1415 clinical isolates were collected from 16 regions across Iran. Most isolates showed high susceptibility to ticarcillin/clavulanic acid and minocycline (98.5 % and 98.2 %, respectively). Among the genes associated with biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance, sul1 and spgM were the most prevalent (12.4 % and 39.6 %, respectively).ConclusionGiven the low resistance rates to levofloxacin and minocycline, these antibiotics may be considered as potentially effective options for treating S. maltophilia infections in Iran. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole showed comparatively higher resistance rates, suggesting that its clinical efficacy may be declining. The rising isolation of S. maltophilia from clinical specimens in recent years, compared to the previous decade, is of both epidemiological and clinical concern, highlighting the need for ongoing surveillance and prudent antibiotic use.

Introduction

The escalation of antibiotic resistance among bacterial pathogens poses a significant threat to global public health [1]. The extensive and prolonged use of antibiotics in recent years has driven the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria, presenting a serious challenge to healthcare systems. These resistant organisms are associated with longer hospital stays, increased morbidity and mortality, and limited treatment options [2,3]. Moreover, genetic mechanisms accelerate the spread of resistance, highlighting the urgent need for effective surveillance and management in clinical practice [4].

Infections caused by Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (S. maltophilia) are particularly difficult to treat due to their intrinsic resistance to multiple antibiotics [5].

S. maltophilia is a Gram-negative bacillus first identified in 1943 from a human pleural fluid sample, originally named Bacterium bookeri. It was later reclassified as Pseudomonas maltophilia in 1961, then as Xanthomonas maltophilia in 1983, and finally assigned to the genus Stenotrophomonas in 1993. The genus name derives from Greek, meaning “narrow feeder that loves malt” [6]. Historically considered an environmental bacterium in soil and water, S. maltophilia was thought to have low pathogenic potential. Over the past few decades, however, it has emerged as a significant opportunistic pathogen in hospitals, particularly among immunocompromised patients [7]. Its ability to persist in aqueous hospital environments and colonize medical devices such as catheters and ventilators has contributed to its clinical importance [8]. The increasing recognition of S. maltophilia infections highlights not only its evolutionary transition from an environmental organism to a nosocomial pathogen, but also the challenges associated with MDR and biofilm-forming capabilities [9,10].

A major concern is the limited accuracy of microbiological diagnosis, which can lead to inappropriate treatment and potentially fatal complications [11]. Mechanisms contributing to intrinsic antibiotic resistance include multidrug efflux pumps (e.g., the AcrAB-TolC system), reduced membrane permeability, the quinolone resistance gene Smqnr, β-lactamases, and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes [12]. Globally, resistance rates of clinical isolates to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX), minocycline, and levofloxacin are 14.4 %, 9.2 %, and 1.4 %, respectively, with the highest TMP/SMX resistance reported in Asia (19.3 %) [13].

Given the growing clinical challenges and the scarcity of universally effective agents, international guidelines have sought to provide standardized recommendations for managing S. maltophilia infections.

According to the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), combination therapy with at least two active agents—such as TMP-SMX, minocycline or tigecycline, cefiderocol, or levofloxacin—is recommended, given the limited clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of any single agent [14]. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) currently defines susceptibility breakpoints only for TMP-SMX, while the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline (M100, 34th edition, 2024) expands its coverage to include TMP-SMX, levofloxacin, minocycline, and cefiderocol.

Notably, CLSI (M100-Ed34) has withdrawn breakpoints for ceftazidime—a weak inducer of the L2 β-lactamase in S. maltophilia—and revised the minocycline susceptibility breakpoint downward from ≤4 mg/L to ≤1 mg/L, a change that may significantly affect clinical interpretations [15].

Beyond intrinsic resistance mechanisms, biofilm formation represents another major obstacle in treating S. maltophilia infections. Biofilm formation further complicates treatment by providing a protective environment for bacterial communities, reducing susceptibility to both antibiotics and host immune responses [16]. Biofilm-forming S. maltophilia isolates often overexpress efflux pumps and other resistance mechanisms, enabling them to survive antimicrobial agents [17]. The interplay between strong biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance is multifaceted: biofilms restrict antibiotic penetration, induce physiological changes in bacteria, and promote the development and horizontal transfer of resistance genes [18]. Key genes influencing biofilm formation include smf-1, spgM, rmlA, and rpfF, which contribute to biofilm robustness and associated antibiotic resistance [19].

The global infection prevalence of these non-fermentative Gram-negative bacilli is estimated at 5.3 %, with the Western Pacific Region showing the highest prevalence (10.5 %) [20]. This opportunistic pathogen can cause a wide range of infections, including pneumonia, bacteremia, sepsis, meningitis, endocarditis, urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, and endophthalmitis [21].

Given the limited data on S. maltophilia infections in Iran, this systematic review aims to evaluate the prevalence of this bacterium across various provinces and assess resistance rates to commonly prescribed antibiotics. Additionally, important genes involved in antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation are discussed.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic literature review was conducted independently by two authors (MD and FS) to identify relevant original research articles published in English between 2000 and 2024, using Google Scholar, Medline (via PubMed), Embase, and Web of Science. The following keywords were used: Stenotrophomonas maltophilia OR S. maltophilia AND Iran.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All studies reporting antibiotic resistance in nosocomial clinical isolates of S. maltophilia in Iran were included. The exclusion criteria were [1]: non-human samples [2]; studies conducted in other countries [3]; review articles [4]; conference abstracts; and [5] duplicate studies. All studies were screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible papers were selected by BH and FS, and confirmed by a third reviewer (MD).

Data extraction and quality assessment

The following information was extracted from each study: first author's last name, study period, year of publication, province, number of patients with S. maltophilia, number of patients with antibiotic-resistant isolates, isolation sources, clinical wards, diagnostic methods, antibiotic resistance genes, and biofilm-forming genes. Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (AA and AD) and confirmed by a third reviewer (MD).

Results

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 159 relevant articles were initially retrieved from the four databases. After removing duplicates (n = 80), 79 unique articles remained for further evaluation. Titles and abstracts were screened, leading to the exclusion of 28 unrelated studies. Full-text assessment resulted in the removal of an additional 31 articles. The overall resistance rate to TMP-SMX was 11.2 %, with the highest frequency in Tehran (45.6 %). Conversely, resistance to levofloxacin was the lowest (1.2 %), with the highest numbers of resistant isolates reported in Razavi Khorasan (53.0 %) and Tehran (23.6 %). Most isolates showed high susceptibility to ticarcillin/clavulanic acid and minocycline (98.5 % and 98.2 %, respectively), although strains from Tehran exhibited the most frequent resistance to these antibiotics.

Prevalence of genes involved in antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation

The most prevalent antibiotic resistance genes in Iran were sul1 (176/1415, 12.4 %) and L2 β-lactamase (156/1415, 11.02 %). Among biofilm-forming isolates, the strong-biofilm phenotype predominated (249/1415, 17.5 %), followed by moderate, weak, and non-biofilm producers (14.06 %, 10.1 %, and 0.9 %, respectively). Discussion

S. maltophilia is recognized as an MDR pathogen, possessing both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms against a wide range of antibiotics [22]. In Iran, limited studies have investigated the prevalence and antibiotic resistance of this bacterium, resulting in incomplete data across all provinces. Among the 31 provinces, S. maltophilia was reported in patients from only 16, with the highest number of isolates from Tehran, Fars, and Razavi Khorasan. This distribution likely reflects the concentration of studies in Tehran, the most populous province, and the limited access to antimicrobial susceptibility testing in other regions (Fig. 3).

While the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommends multiple antibiotics for treating S. maltophilia infections, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) provides guidelines for only a limited set of agents, including TMP-SMX, minocycline, levofloxacin, chloramphenicol, ticarcillin/clavulanic acid, and cefiderocol. Notably, CLSI breakpoints for S. maltophilia have not been updated since 2019 [23].

In our review, ceftazidime resistance was 25.5 %, with the highest rates observed in Tehran and Razavi Khorasan provinces. This rate is lower than the overall resistance reported across Asia (47.0 %) [24]. The relatively lower resistance observed in our study may be partly attributed to the frequent use of disk diffusion rather than MIC-based susceptibility testing methods.

S. maltophilia exhibits natural resistance to a broad spectrum of β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems and monobactams, as well as to aminoglycosides and fosfomycin, thereby limiting the efficacy of most first-line antimicrobial agents [12,23]. Resistance to TMP-SMX was 11.2 %, reported in 66.6 % of provinces, with the highest frequency in Tehran. This rate is higher than the global average [13] and underscores the importance of appropriate TMP-SMX use and the implementation of measures to prevent the dissemination of resistant strains. In contrast, levofloxacin exhibited very low resistance (1.2 %) in Iran, considerably lower than the global resistance rate of 14.4 % [13].

However, according to the CLSI 2025 guidelines, due to the increasing antibiotic resistance observed in S. maltophilia isolates, monotherapy with levofloxacin or TMP-SMX is not recommended. Combination therapy should be considered to reduce the risk of therapeutic failure.

Notably, in our study, the sul1 gene was found with the highest frequency among S. maltophilia isolates. In a study by Morsi et al. conducted in Egypt, 37.5 % of S. maltophilia isolates were resistant to TMP/SMX, and all of them harbored the sul1 gene [25]. According to the results of the current study, the presence of resistance genes and their role in antibiotic resistance have not been reported in most studies conducted in Iran. Thus, among the 20 articles included in this review, only four mentioned the prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes. For example, of the 159 TMP/SMX-resistant isolates obtained from 10 studies, the presence of sul genes was investigated in only two.

Biofilm formation enables S. maltophilia to cause infections associated with indwelling medical devices and is linked to approximately 65 % of nosocomial infections [26]. In our study, most of the reviewed articles did not mention the biofilm-forming ability of the isolates, nor were any isolates obtained from indwelling devices. The results of the microtiter plate experiments showed that most S. maltophilia strains exhibited strong biofilm formation capability, in accordance with the findings of Duan et al. in China [27]. This finding highlights the influence of geographical variation on the prevalence of biofilm-forming genes in S. maltophilia worldwide.

Similarly, several studies have shown that S. maltophilia isolates are commonly found in blood and are more frequently isolated from male than female patients [28,29]. The incidence of bloodstream infections caused by S. maltophilia and the associated complications have significantly increased in recent years, with a mortality rate ranging from 30 % to 51 % [30]. This rise may be attributed to the excessive and frequent use of available antibiotics. Older age and the need for mechanical ventilation were identified as independent and significant risk factors that worsened the outcomes of respiratory infections [31].

Diagnosing S. maltophilia presents numerous challenges. In our study, the most common diagnostic methods were biochemical tests and culture, respectively. In contrast, a study conducted by Hafiz et al. in Saudi Arabia reported that S. maltophilia was primarily isolated in ICUs [31]. Patients in ICUs are at a high risk for nosocomial infections due to the widespread use of invasive procedures and medical devices.

One crucial aspect of this research involves examining the number of clinical S. maltophilia cases reported from 2000 to 2012 and from 2013 to 2024. The number of isolates dramatically increased during the period of 2013–2024. This trend may be explained by the fact that between 2000 and 2009, no reports of S. maltophilia resistance were published in Iran. Two hypotheses can be proposed: first, that S. maltophilia strains were not actually resistant to antibiotics during that period, which seems unlikely; and second, that increased awareness among medical personnel led to improved recognition of infections caused by this bacterium, resulting in more accurate diagnosis, effective treatment, and prevention of resistant strain dissemination in Iran.

This study has some limitations. First, studies carried out in Iran have been limited to only a few provinces, preventing the inclusion of data from all regions of the country. Second, most studies on S. maltophilia strains have not thoroughly examined biofilm formation ability, making it difficult to establish an overall correlation between biofilm severity and antibiotic resistance in Iran.

Conclusion

Based on this systematic review, S. maltophilia isolates in Iran exhibited generally low resistance rates to levofloxacin and minocycline, suggesting that these two antibiotics may serve as viable therapeutic options for the management of S. maltophilia infections. In contrast, resistance to TMP-SMX was comparatively higher, highlighting the need for cautious use of this agent and continuous national surveillance to prevent further resistance dissemination.

The high prevalence of the sul1 gene observed in Iranian isolates underscores the potential risk of widespread resistance transmission within both hospital and community settings. Moreover, as S. maltophilia was predominantly isolated from blood samples, early identification and accurate diagnosis are crucial in cases of suspected bacteremia.

Given the limited number of regional studies and the lack of data on biofilm formation and molecular mechanisms of resistance, further multicenter investigations integrating genomic and phenotypic analyses are strongly recommended to better understand the epidemiology and resistance dynamics of this emerging pathogen in Iran.
