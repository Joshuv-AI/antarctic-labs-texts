Research on drug-drug interaction prediction using capsule neural network based on self-attention mechanism
Authors: Xingxin Chen, Zhuo Wang, Zhen Miao, Bin Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMulti-drug combinations represent an effective strategy for treating complex diseases. However, due to the vast number of unknown interactions among drugs, accurately predicting drug–drug interactions (DDIs) is essential for preventing adverse drug reactions that may cause serious harm to patients. Therefore, DDI prediction plays a critical role in pharmacology.ResultsIn this paper, we propose a novel DDI prediction model that integrates a self-attention mechanism with a capsule neural network, termed ACaps-DDI. The model effectively combines chemical information from internal drug substructures with biological information from external drug targets and drug-metabolizing enzymes to predict potential drug-drug interactions.ConclusionsExperimental results on two benchmark datasets show that the ACaps-DDI model outperforms six other classification models across seven evaluation metrics, demonstrating its strong predictive performance and generalization ability. Ablation studies further confirm the effectiveness of individual components within the ACaps-DDI architecture. Finally, case studies involving three drugs (cannabidiol, torasemide, and cyclophosphamide) validate the model’s ability to predict previously unknown drug interactions. In conclusion, the ACaps-DDI model exhibits high predictive accuracy for known drugs and demonstrates promising predictive capability for unseen drugs, highlighting its practical significance for clinical research on drug interactions.

Introduction

Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) refer to the mutual effects that occur when two or more drugs are administered concurrently. The physiological outcomes of taking multiple medications can be beneficial, harmful, or negligible. Beneficial interactions enhance therapeutic efficacy or improve treatment outcomes compared to monotherapy [1], whereas harmful interactions are classified as adverse drug events (ADEs) [2, 4]. According to China’s National Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring Annual Report (2014), adverse reaction reports among patients aged 65 years and older accounted for 19.9% of all cases. Clinical data further indicate that the incidence of adverse reactions is approximately 6%-8% for individuals taking fewer than five medications, but increases sharply to around 40% for those taking six medications. Polypharmacy, commonly defined as the concurrent use of five or more drugs, has become increasingly prevalent among the elderly population [3]. Therefore, understanding DDIs to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse drug reactions has received significant clinical and research attention. Despite their clinical importance, experimental validation of DDIs remains highly challenging due to the extensive cost and time required to test the vast number of possible drug combinations. In recent years, with the advancement of computer-aided drug discovery technologies and the continuous accumulation of biomedical data, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool for predicting DDIs. Researchers collect drug information from literature and clinical reports to construct comprehensive databases that facilitate computational prediction. ML has achieved notable success in computer vision and bioinformatics [5, 6], and it shows substantial potential in DDI prediction. This computational approach effectively mitigates the limitations of traditional clinical trials (e.g., high cost, complexity, and time consumption) by enabling rapid and efficient prediction of potential drug-drug interactions using accumulated biomedical data.

A wide range of computational approaches have been developed for predicting drug-drug interactions (DDIs). Early studies primarily relied on conventional machine learning techniques. Li et al. [7] employed Bayesian networks that integrated molecular and pharmacological phenotypes to predict DDIs. Chen et al. [8] proposed the HNAI framework, which constructed DDI networks, calculated drug-drug pair similarities based on four feature types, and used five traditional classifiers—Naive Bayes, decision trees, k-nearest neighbors, logistic regression, and support vector machines—for prediction. Sridhar et al. [9] further developed a scalable probabilistic model to infer DDIs from drug similarity networks derived from known interactions. To improve representation learning, Zhang et al. [10] introduced a manifold-regularized matrix factorization method with an ensemble learning strategy that achieved superior predictive performance. Zhu et al. [11] modeled drug dependencies through a dependency network and proposed an attribute-supervised learning model for adverse DDI (ADDI) prediction. With the increasing availability of heterogeneous drug data, several studies incorporated multiple feature sources and deep learning techniques. Rohani et al. [12] integrated diverse drug information (including substructures, targets, side effects, pathways, transporters, and indications) and used neural networks to predict unknown DDIs. Deng et al. [13] introduced DDIMDL, a unified deep neural network framework that combines four types of drug features (chemical substructures, targets, enzymes, and pathways) to learn cross-modal representations of drug pairs and predict DDI-related events. Graph neural network (GNN) approaches have become increasingly prominent for DDI prediction. Feng et al. [14] applied graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to capture topological relationships within DDI networks and used deep neural networks to predict potential interactions. Wang et al. [15] proposed the MIRACLE model, which views the DDI network as a multi-view graph and employs a key-aware attention propagation mechanism to capture molecular structural information. Nyamabo et al. [16] developed SSI-DDI, a deep learning framework that directly operates on raw molecular graphs, decomposing DDI prediction into identifying pairwise substructure interactions. Kang et al. [17] further enhanced GCN-based methods by introducing a hierarchical layer-by-layer fusion rule to aggregate node embeddings, yielding more accurate link predictions. Recent work has focused on more refined drug representations. Li et al. [18] proposed a dual-view representation learning network that jointly captures local (intra-drug) and global (inter-drug) structural information. Ma et al. [19] designed a directed message-passing neural network with a substructure attention mechanism to adaptively extract informative molecular substructures, modeling DDIs as pairwise interactions between unique substructures of each drug.

Although numerous machine learning approaches have been applied to drug–drug interaction (DDI) prediction, several critical limitations persist. First, many existing methods rely primarily on the intrinsic structural information of drug molecules as input features, while overlooking the relationships between internal molecular substructures and external binding proteins, as well as the integration of heterogeneous biological data sources. In practice, drug-protein interactions exert an indirect yet significant influence on DDI prediction accuracy. Second, feature redundancy remains a major challenge. Direct concatenation of heterogeneous feature vectors often leads to dimensional inconsistencies and redundant representations, underscoring the need for more efficient and flexible strategies to integrate multimodal drug features. Third, some studies implicitly assume that incorporating a greater variety of features will inevitably enhance predictive performance. However, excessive fusion of heterogeneous information such as knowledge graphs, DDI networks, and biological annotations, can instead introduce noise and redundancy, thereby impairing the model’s ability to capture the most informative drug-related patterns.

To address the aforementioned limitations, we propose a novel framework named ACaps-DDI for drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction. The proposed approach first integrates three types of drug features and constructs similarity matrices for each drug using Gaussian similarity. The resulting drug-pair similarity vectors are then processed by a deep neural network to obtain comprehensive representations of drug pairs. Next, a self-attention mechanism is employed to assign adaptive weights to each representation, thereby enhancing both model performance and interpretability. The refined drug-pair representations are subsequently fed into a capsule neural network to perform binary classification of DDIs. The overall workflow of the proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 1, where substructure molecular graphs are derived from the DrugBank database [20]. To evaluate the effectiveness of ACaps-DDI, we compare it with six baseline models: k-nearest neighbor (KNN), logistic regression (LR), naive Bayes (NB), random forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), and DDIMDL. Materials and methods

Datasets

DrugBank [20] is a comprehensive drug database that provides detailed information on 12,151 drugs, including 3,844 FDA-approved drugs and 5,867 experimental drugs. In this study, two datasets were employed to validate the effectiveness of the proposed model: Dataset 1, constructed by Deng et al. [13], which contains a smaller number of drugs, and Dataset 2, provided by Lin et al. [21], which includes a larger set of drugs. The dataset from Deng et al. was derived from DrugBank and includes four types of features: chemical substructures, targets, pathways, and enzymes. Pathways were represented as KEGG IDs, which were mapped to the corresponding drug pathway information using the KEGG database [22]. Drug targets and enzymes were directly obtained from DrugBank. After preprocessing, the dataset contained 572 drugs, 74,528 DDI pairs, and 65 event types. Since our task focuses on binary classification, we modified this dataset by removing pathway features and converting the 65 event categories into two classes to determine whether an interaction exists between two drugs. The dataset from Lin et al. contains 1,258 drugs and 323,539 paired DDIs, with the drugs associated with 100 event categories. The same preprocessing procedure was applied, converting the 100 event categories into two binary classes. Modeling framework

Preprocessing of drug data

We constructed a balanced drug interaction dataset that includes both original positive samples and generated negative samples. Negative samples were created by randomly pairing two different drugs to form candidate drug pairs. First, any pair already identified as a positive sample was excluded. Second, we checked for duplication and applied unordered pair processing to ensure that the same drug pair did not appear in reverse order. Next, we calculated the Jaccard similarity between the molecular substructures of the two drugs. Candidate pairs with a similarity greater than or equal to the threshold value of 0.5 were discarded, while those with similarity below the threshold were retained as negative samples. This step ensures that the negative samples are not overly dissimilar from the positive samples, thereby improving the model’s ability to distinguish subtle structural differences. Duplicate pairs and those exceeding the similarity threshold were removed. The negative sample pool was then replenished iteratively until the number of negative samples matched the number of positive samples, resulting in a balanced dataset suitable for binary classification.

Drug feature extraction

The extraction and representation of drug features are crucial for model construction. Previous studies have shown that incorporating more features does not necessarily lead to better model performance [12]. Therefore, we selected three representative drug features—substructure, target, and enzyme—to provide complementary chemical and biological information. Each drug is represented as a binary feature vector, where the value of 1 or 0 indicates the presence or absence of the corresponding descriptor. According to PubChem [23], there are 881 predefined substructures, enabling each drug to be encoded as an 881-dimensional vector representing the presence or absence of specific substructure types. Similarly, there are 1,162 drug-associated targets, allowing drugs to be represented as 1,162-dimensional target feature vectors. In addition, 202 enzymes are associated with drugs, and the enzyme features are encoded as 202-dimensional binary vectors. Using this encoding scheme, each drug is represented by three feature vectors corresponding to its substructure, target, and enzyme characteristics.

The extracted feature vectors are high-dimensional and highly sparse, with most elements having a value of 0. To address this issue, we applied similarity-based feature compression to reduce sparsity and capture meaningful relationships between drugs. We compared the performance of three similarity metrics: Jaccard similarity, cosine similarity, and Gaussian similarity, and ultimately selected the Gaussian similarity metric to compute the bit vectors and construct the drug–drug similarity matrix.

For Dataset 1, we obtained three 572 × 572 drug–drug similarity matrices. Each feature of a drug is represented as a 572-dimensional vector. Therefore, each drug with three types of features is represented by a 3 × 572-dimensional vector. For Dataset 2, we obtained three 1,258 × 1,258 drug–drug similarity matrices. Each feature of a drug is represented as a 1,258-dimensional vector. Accordingly, each drug with three types of features is represented by a 3 × 1,258-dimensional vector.

Self-attention mechanisms

They have recently achieved remarkable success in various domains, including bioinformatics. Since the representations of the three features of a drug pair contribute unequally to the final DDI prediction result, we adopt a self-attention mechanism based on previous research to assign appropriate weights to each feature. Each element in the input sequence is first mapped into the Query (Q), Key (K), and Value (V) spaces through linear transformations. Capsule neural networks

Capsule neural networks [28] are designed to capture the hierarchical relationships among features and to generate representations that preserve richer semantic and structural information. Compared with conventional neural networks, capsule architectures combined with a dynamic routing algorithm can reduce the number of parameters while improving the model’s parameter efficiency. In this study, we implement a PrimaryCaps layer to extract diverse feature representations through convolutional operations and to maintain consistent vector lengths using nonlinear compression. Specifically, the PrimaryCaps layer is constructed with nine convolutional units, one input channel, one output channel, a convolutional kernel size of (9, 3), and a stride of 1. After the convolution operation, the dimensionality of each sample is transformed to (3,168, 1). A subsequent reshaping operation converts the output to a dimension of (396, 8), resulting in 396 eight-dimensional vector capsules. Finally, the capsule lengths are normalized to the range (0, 1) through a fully connected layer.

Stratified sampling was employed to ensure a consistent distribution of positive and negative samples across all folds. A learning rate scheduler was used to dynamically adjust the learning rate, starting at 0.001, and automatically reducing it when the validation loss plateaued (factor = 0.5, patience = 3). An early stopping mechanism was applied to terminate training if the validation loss did not improve for 10 consecutive rounds, restoring the best-performing model. Gradient clipping was also used to prevent gradient explosion by constraining the gradient norm within a specified threshold.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed ACaps-DDI, a new model for drug–drug interaction (DDI) prediction based on multi-source information fusion. The model effectively integrates internal molecular structures and external protein-related features through deep neural networks, a self-attention mechanism, and capsule neural networks. ACaps-DDI combines three types of drug features (substructure, target, and enzyme) and adaptively assigns importance to each feature using self-attention. The capsule network further enhances feature representation by capturing spatial and hierarchical relationships, overcoming the limitations of traditional convolutional neural networks. Extensive comparison and ablation experiments demonstrate that ACaps-DDI outperforms existing models and that each component contributes significantly to overall performance. Among the tested similarity measures, the Gaussian similarity achieved the best predictive results. In future work, we plan to incorporate additional drug-related information, such as knowledge graphs and DDI networks, to further improve prediction accuracy and enhance the model’s ability to learn from multiple data modalities.
