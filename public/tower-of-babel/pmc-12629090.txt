Advancing the well-being of staff physicians in Canada

Published: 2025

Canada’s health care systems are under strain, with 6.5 million people in Canada lacking a primary care provider,1 which increases patient complexity and volumes and contributes to physician burnout.2 Staff physicians, many of whom supervise the next generation of doctors, are carrying mounting clinical loads and administrative tasks3,4 without adequate resources, putting the quality of patient care and medical education at risk. We discuss how institutions, health systems, and regulatory bodies can support staff physicians at this critical time.

Commendable progress has recently been made in supporting the well-being of medical learners through measures such as protected time for education, limits on clinical hours worked, establishment of learner affairs offices, and safe reporting systems. Compared with residents, staff physicians have few similar safeguards. Physicians in non-teaching hospitals also face heavy clinical and administrative burdens.

Burnout among staff physicians impairs teaching effectiveness, erodes psychological safety, and undermines the clinical learning environment.5,6 It also puts patients at risk.7 Physicians experiencing burnout may lack the patience, clarity, or emotional presence required for safe, compassionate care. More broadly, strained physicians contribute to a culture of cynicism and disengagement.8,9

The important progress made to support learner well-being will be unsustainable unless it is accompanied by parallel efforts to support staff physicians, who need flexible but reasonable service expectations to recover after extended shifts or overnight call. This is not about arbitrarily capping hours or abandoning patients who require urgent care. It is about crafting evidence-informed expectations that recognize the toll of clinical fatigue.

Many of the most overwhelming tasks reported by staff physicians are administrative. Regular performance evaluations that involve long, duplicative forms, fragmented credentialling systems, and constant electronic communication demands all contribute to a sense of futility. Although faculties of medicine, hospitals, and provincial medical associations have initiated well-being programs for staff physicians that include formal well-being leads, many of these efforts remain under-resourced or siloed from the structural levers that drive burnout.

Educational and service delivery structures are increasingly misaligned. Medical schools continue to expand, which requires even more clinical placements and preceptors with the time to teach. However, hospitals and health authorities have not proportionally increased staff physician support to meet these growing educational demands. The burden is once again offloaded to staff physicians who are asked to teach more and to complete more administrative tasks, often without changes to job descriptions, protected time, or remuneration.

Lack of focus on physician well-being is not limited to academic settings. Staff physicians in nonacademic settings contend with unsafe work environments, long hours, unreasonable administrative tasks, stigma around help seeking, and few structural supports.10 Burnout among physicians is not an academic problem — it is a national health care issue. It is also not a matter of personal resilience. Physicians are seldom supported with the resources needed to enable them to consistently achieve high-quality and timely patient care. Physicians’ moral distress of being unable to provide the care they aspire to offer leads to difficulty in retaining them.

Those working within Canada’s health care systems and all their components must shift from viewing physician well-being as an individual responsibility to a system priority. Addressing the shortages of physicians and other health care providers is arguably the most important and long-lasting intervention to improve physician well-being. Unfortunately, this will take time. In the interim, specific initiatives should begin.

Medical leadership should integrate staff physician well-being into strategic planning, with designated wellness leads, sustained funding, and measurement and reporting of indicators of workplace experience and retention. Leaders should help address stigma around help seeking by modelling vulnerability, normalizing accessing support, and cultivating peer networks. Medical schools, hospitals, regulatory bodies, and health authorities should each reduce physicians’ administrative burdens by streamlining performance evaluations, coordinating university and hospital requirements, and eliminating redundant documentation so that physician time is reprioritized toward meaningful clinical and academic work. Hospital and health system leaders, together with provincial and territorial health ministries, must implement workplace well-being standards, including periodic well-being audits and metrics to evaluate staff physicians’ experiences in both academic and nonacademic settings, paired with leadership accountability.

Faculties of medicine and hospital departments should develop evidence-informed duty hour expectations for staff physicians that acknowledge the need for recovery after extended clinical service while maintaining continuity of care for patients. Models that incorporate team-based coverage and built-in redundancy will help balance flexibility with physicians’ responsibilities to patients.

Hospital human resource departments, regulatory colleges, and medical associations should create and support safe, confidential reporting pathways for staff physicians experiencing mistreatment, harassment, or systemic challenges — mirroring mechanisms available to learners — and ensure these are accessible and protected by policies of nonretaliation. Hospital administrators must meet the challenges of reduced resident availability through honest communication and adjustments to clinical models of care, rather than shifting that burden onto staff physicians.

Making staff physician well-being a priority is crucial to preserve the humanity and sustainability of the physician workforce. The time for action is now.
