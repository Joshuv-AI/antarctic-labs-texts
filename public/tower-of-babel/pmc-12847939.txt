Subpolar North Atlantic Ocean heat content drives 21st-century Arctic multi-decadal variability in CESM1 LE
Authors: Di Cai, Xianyao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArctic surface temperatures exhibit a pronounced multi-decadal oscillation superimposed on the long-term anthropogenic warming trend, yet its drivers remain poorly understood. Here we identify a critical oceanic mechanism originating in the subpolar North Atlantic. We show that variability in upper-layer (0–200 m) ocean heat content in this region precedes fluctuations in poleward ocean heat transport convergence (OHT) by approximately five years, which subsequently lead Arctic surface temperature changes by two years. Using a time-evolving radiative kernel method and PCMCI causal analysis within the Community Earth System Model Large Ensemble, we quantify the contributions to this variability. OHT directly accounts for 44.5% of Arctic temperature variability, while local feedbacks (principally albedo and lapse rate) amplify this signal, contributing an additional 53.5%. These findings underscore that this sequential oceanic process, originating in the subpolar North Atlantic and propagating into the Arctic, is critical in modulating the phase of Arctic multi-decadal temperature variability. Introduction

Superimposed on the rapid long-term warming trend, Arctic surface air temperatures (SAT) exhibit pronounced decadal to multi-decadal fluctuations. For instance, throughout the 20th century, the Arctic underwent distinct phases of climate variability, including accelerated warming from 1900 to 19401, substantial cooling and sea ice expansion from 1940 to 19702, and a renewed warming trend from 1970 to the present3. Understanding the causes of these fluctuations—whether they stem from external forcings or internal climate variability—is crucial for advancing our comprehension of the Arctic climate system. Progress in this area could enhance decadal climate predictions, potentially providing predictive skill beyond one or two years if models are initialized appropriately.

Attribution studies have identified anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions as a primary driver of the Arctic’s long-term warming trend4. However, the mechanisms governing multi-decadal temperature fluctuations remain debated. Some studies argue that external forcings, such as variations in greenhouse gases, aerosols, and explosive volcanic eruptions, explain much of the observed oscillations over the past century5–7. Others emphasize the critical role of internal climate modes, including Pacific Decadal Variability8,9 and Atlantic Multi-decadal Variability10–13.

Despite extensive research, several knowledge gaps persist. First, the initiating mechanisms remain unclear. Although local Arctic feedbacks (e.g., surface albedo, temperature, water vapor content, and cloud cover) are critical for amplifying Arctic warming14,15, the processes that initiate and phase these multi-decadal fluctuations remain poorly understood. Second, significant uncertainties persist in the mechanisms linking meridional heat transport16,17 to Arctic SAT and sea ice variability, particularly regarding the convergence of ocean heat transport (OHT)18–20. Although studies show Atlantic Water (AW) inflow directly influences regional warming and sea ice trends21, the precise pathways, relative strengths, and spatiotemporal scales of OHT’s influence remain poorly constrained. Finally, research has focused predominantly on the 20th century6. Studies examining whether the drivers and physical mechanisms of these multi-decadal fluctuations might change under the stronger anthropogenic forcing of the 21st century remain limited.

In this study, we utilize the Community Earth System Model Large Ensemble22 (CESM1 LE) to investigate the mechanisms driving 21st-century Arctic climate variability. The CESM1 LE is well-suited for this analysis (see Methods), offering a large ensemble dataset and associated radiative kernels23. While previous studies have documented its strong performance against other CMIP5 models (e.g., poleward heat transport and sea ice extent)24,25, our analysis further verifies its robust simulation of key variables relevant to this study against observations and reanalysis (see Figures S1–S3, Table S1). Specifically, we aim to: (1) characterize multi-decadal Arctic SAT fluctuations during the 21st century; (2) quantify the relative contributions of local feedbacks, the convergence of atmospheric heat transport (AHT), and OHT; and (3) identify the underlying drivers of these fluctuations. Our findings clarify the role of oceanic processes in 21st-century Arctic variability and identify a key source of predictability. This insight can be used, for example, to improve the initialization of decadal to multi-decadal prediction models, thereby enhancing their forecast skill for Arctic climate.

Results

Projected 21st century SAT trends and components of Arctic warming

The global mean warming rate projected for the 21st century is 0.46 K per decade. This warming is spatially non-uniform (Fig. 1a), with prominent features including amplified warming over the Arctic, slight cooling in the central subpolar North Atlantic, and a pronounced interhemispheric asymmetry where the Northern Hemisphere warms more than the Southern Hemisphere. Superimposed on this long-term trend, multi-decadal SAT fluctuations are evident across all latitudinal bands (Fig. 1b). At high latitudes, these temperature fluctuations tend to exhibit larger amplitudes than either hemispheric or global averages26. Specifically, within the Arctic (defined as 70°–90°N), our analysis shows positive SAT anomalies from 2000 to 2015 and again between 2055 and 2085, with negative anomalies dominating the rest of the century. Notably, there is no clear connection between the multi-decadal temperature fluctuations at the two poles, likely reflecting their different underlying drivers6. Similar patterns of variability are also evident in large ensemble projections using GFDL-CM327 (Fig. S4).

(a) Spatial distribution of SAT trends (K per decade) calculated over the period 2000–2100. (b) Linearly detrended SAT anomalies (K) for each 5-degree latitude band for the 21st century, calculated relative to the 1920–1949 reference period. All data are based on the CESM1 LE means.

The radiative kernel technique28 decomposes temperature changes into contributions from radiative and non-radiative feedbacks and is applicable to both long-term climate responses15,29,30 and short-term variations31. In this study, we applied the radiative kernel within a time-evolving framework to analyze annual Arctic SAT changes for each member of the CESM1 LE during the 21st century, focusing on variations in temperature contributions of different processes from the TOA perspective (Fig. 2). For example, over the Arctic region during 2071–2100, the total warming reaches 12.26 K, with surface albedo feedback dominating the contribution (5.77 K), followed by lapse rate feedback (3.08 K), Planck feedback, water vapor feedback, and AHT. In contrast, cloud feedback and OHT (-1.73 K) exhibit dampening effects.

Arctic SAT change (black line) during the 21st century based on the CESM1 LE ensemble means, along with the temperature contributions from individual processes, including albedo (red), lapse rate (orange), Planck (yellow), water vapor (green), cloud (grey) feedbacks, AHT (purple), and OHT (dark blue). The inset displays the trends for each process over 2000–2100.

Our analysis confirms that surface albedo and temperature (Planck and lapse rate) feedbacks are the most significant contributors to Arctic warming, consistent with previous studies examining the long-term response to quadrupled CO₂ levels in CMIP5 models29. The negative contributions from OHT—calculated as the residual convergence in the mixed-layer energy budget north of 70°N (see Methods)—and cloud feedback also corroborate earlier findings31. The net cooling from cloud feedback is consistent with enhanced shortwave reflection attributed to mixed-phase cloud microphysical changes32 and warming-induced increases in cloud water content33.

OHT contribution leads Arctic multi-decadal temperature variability

Focusing on the long-term Arctic SAT oscillations around the linear trend, we applied linear detrending to each annual time series—including Arctic SAT anomalies and temperature contributions from individual processes. Ensemble mean results (Fig. 3) show that, excluding cloud feedback, temperature contributions from individual processes exhibit substantial multi-decadal variability. Interannual fluctuations reveal that contributions from local feedbacks are closely in phase with multi-decadal Arctic SAT anomalies (Fig. 3b–e). Among these, the Planck feedback contribution shows the strongest instantaneous correlation (Fig. 3d), as expected since the Planck response represents vertically uniform warming (see Methods). Notably, the covariability between the OHT contribution and Arctic multi-decadal temperature fluctuations is pronounced (Fig. 3g), with a correlation coefficient of 0.67 (significant at the 99.99% level) and closely matched amplitude changes. The contribution from AHT, however, shows a shorter periodicity (Fig. 3h) and a weaker relationship with the multi-decadal SAT variability34.

Ensemble means of the Arctic SAT anomalies (black line) and temperature contributions from (a) all processes, (b) albedo feedback, (c) lapse rate feedback, (d) Planck feedback, (e) water vapor feedback, (f) cloud feedback, (g) OHT, and (h) AHT for the 21st century, based on the CESM1 LE means.

To further investigate these relationships, we performed the lead-lag correlations between the temperature contributions from individual processes and the detrended multi-decadal Arctic SAT fluctuations (Fig. 4). Contributions from local feedbacks—including albedo, lapse rate, Planck, and water vapor feedbacks—exhibit peak correlations at zero lag (colored lines in Fig. 4), indicating these feedbacks act as responses that amplify the existing temperature anomalies rather than initiating them. Contributions from cloud feedback and AHT do not correlate well with Arctic temperature anomalies (grey and purple lines in Fig. 4). Conversely, the OHT contribution leads Arctic multi-decadal SAT variability by approximately two years, with a correlation of 0.74 (at 99.99% significance). This significantly higher correlation suggests that changes in OHT precede and potentially linked to the initiation of Arctic temperature fluctuations on multi-decadal timescales.

Lead-lag correlations between 21st-century Arctic multi-decadal SAT anomalies and temperature contributions from individual processes. The x-axis represents the time lag in years, with negative (positive) values indicating that Arctic multi-decadal SAT anomalies lag (lead) other time series. Processes include albedo (red), lapse rate (orange), Planck (yellow), water vapor (green), cloud (grey) feedbacks, OHT (black), and AHT (purple), based on CESM1 LE means. Analyzing individual ensemble members of the CESM1 LE (Fig. S5), we found that in 70% of cases, the OHT contribution shows its strongest correlation with multi-decadal SAT variability when leading by one or two years. Specifically, the peak correlation occurs at a two-year lead in 32.5% of members, consistent with the ensemble mean (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that on decadal to multi-decadal timescales, OHT acts as a potential driver of Arctic SAT variability, while local feedbacks appear to respond to, rather than initiate, these temperature changes.

Finally, to determine whether this OHT-associated variability is an expression of internal climate dynamics or a response to external forcing, we isolated the internal variability component by removing the ensemble mean from each member and by analyzing 1701 pseudo-ensembles from the 1801-year pre-industrial control (piControl) run (see Methods). In the internal-only dataset (ensemble mean removed), the lead-lag analysis (Fig. S6) shows that the OHT contribution precedes Arctic SAT deviations by 2 years with the strongest correlation in 12.5% of cases, and by one year in 30% of cases. While some relationship between OHT and SAT exists, the correlations are generally low (e.g., correlation coefficients |r| < 0.4), statistically insignificant, and show no systematic lead time (Fig. S7). Furthermore, the pseudo-ensembles from the piControl run show no systematic OHT-SAT connection (Fig. S8a). These results suggest that the connection between OHT and multi-decadal Arctic SAT variability is not primarily a product of internal variability, but rather a feature of the forced response. This aligns with recent work by England6, proposing that the pronounced multi-decadal oscillation is itself a forced response to evolving anthropogenic aerosols and greenhouse gases.

The mechanistic distinction between forced and internal variability can be attributed to differences in their origins and temporal persistence. Anthropogenic forcing introduces a sustained radiative imbalance, driving large-scale circulation adjusments18 that generate persistent, poleward OHT anomalies35. Such forced signals demonstrate enhanced temporal coherence relative to natural variability, supporting the development of Arctic heat storage and release cycles. Conversely, internal variability is predominantly associated with OHT fluctuations through strengthening or weakening of the flow, triggered by reorganizations of upstream circulation in the North Atlantic and Nordic Seas, and by large-scale atmospheric anomalies reminiscent of the Arctic Oscillation36. Such internally driven variability is typically expressed on interannual to decadal timescales.

Subpolar North Atlantic OHC precedes OHT contribution variability

The Arctic Ocean receives heat primarily through Atlantic Water inflows, a process that plays a crucial role in the ongoing “Borealization” of the Arctic37. The magnitude of this poleward oceanic heat transport is regulated by heat convergence in the North Atlantic35,38,39 and can be decomposed into contributions from changes in ocean volume transport and temperature40,41 (Fig. S9). To investigate the upstream factors associated with the OHT variations that are linked to Arctic SAT, we focused on OHC within the subpolar North Atlantic, defined as the region between 50°–70°N.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, multi-decadal variations in the OHT contribution to Arctic temperature closely mirror OHC anomalies in the upper layers of the subpolar North Atlantic. A lead-lag correlation analysis confirms this relationship, revealing a peak correlation when subpolar North Atlantic OHC leads the OHT contribution by five years (Fig. S10). This finding suggests fluctuations in subpolar North Atlantic OHC as a robust precursor to the variability in poleward heat transport that is directly linked to Arctic temperature on multi-decadal timescales.

(a) Arctic SAT anomalies (red) and OHT contribution to Arctic temperature variability (blue), and (b) subpolar North Atlantic OHC anomalies at different depth layers: 0–200 m (red), 0–300 m (orange), 0–500 m (yellow), 0–750 m (green), 0–1,500 m (light blue), and full depth (dark blue), in ZJ (1 ZJ = 10²¹ J) for the 21st century, based on CESM1 LE means.

We then applied the same attribution analysis to this OHC-OHT relationship to distinguish between internal variability and the forced response. This connection was also found to be a feature of the forced response, not a product of internal climate dynamics. Analyses isolating internal variability confirm this: the deviation dataset (ensemble mean removed) shows no systematic lead time between the upper-layer subpolar North Atlantic OHC deviations and the OHT contribution (Fig. S11), and the piControl pseudo-ensembles show no clear connection (Fig. S8b).

Casual validation of the OHC-OHT-SAT pathway via PCMCI analysis

Previous studies largely relied on correlation to infer causal influences; however, correlation does not imply causation. A significant correlation merely indicates a statistical relationship or synchronous behavior, not a confirmed causal link42,43. Causal discovery methods are crucial for identifying robust cause-and-effect relationships and are increasingly applied in Earth system sciences42.

To move beyond simple correlation and test our proposed physical mechanisms, we apply the PCMCI (Peter and Clark Momentary Conditional Independence) causal discovery algorithm44,45. PCMCI is specifically designed for auto-correlated time series typical of climate data. It performs rigorous conditional independence tests to distinguish direct causal links from indirect correlations arising from common confounders45. We apply this analysis to three core variables: subpolar North Atlantic OHC anomalies (0–200 m), the OHT contribution to Arctic temperature variability, and Arctic SAT anomalies.

The PCMCI analysis (with a maximum lag τmax = 15 years) causally substantiates the primary driving pathway previously identified via lag-correlation (Fig. 6). The results first reveal a significant, positive causal link from the OHT contribution to Arctic temperature variability to Arctic SAT anomalies, which is statistically significant (95% confidence level) at lags of 1 and 2 years. This finding strongly validates our central hypothesis that the OHT contribution is a key driver of 21st-century Arctic SAT anomalies, leading the SAT signal by approximately 2 years (consistent with Fig. 4). Second, the analysis identifies an upstream driver: a significant positive causal link (95% confidence) from subpolar North Atlantic OHC anomalies (0–200 m) to the OHT contribution at lags of 5 and 12 years. This aligns with our earlier analysis (Fig. S10), causally confirming the subpolar OHC anomalies as a robust precursor for the OHT contribution.

Causal graph from the PCMCI analysis (τmax = 15 years) for Arctic SAT anomalies (SAT), OHT contribution to Arctic temperature variability (OHT), and subpolar North Atlantic OHC anomalies at 0–200 m (OHC). Arrows show significant (p < 0.05) causal links (orange for positive and blue for negative), with numbers denoting the time lag (τ) in years. Node color indicates the maximum autocorrelation strength of each variable.

To test the sensitivity to τmax, we reduced the maximum lag to 11 years (Fig. S12). The core driving pathways (OHC → OHT at lag 5; OHT → SAT at lags 1–2) remained robust, supporting the reliability of our results. However, the OHT → OHC feedback pathway differed significantly between settings. For τmax = 15, OHT exerted a negative causal influence on OHC at 6- and 15-year lags. Conversely, for τmax = 11, this shifted to a positive influence at 2-, 3-, and 6-year lags. This discrepancy is a direct methodological consequence of the parameter choice. The τmax = 11 setting, by definition, precludes the algorithm from detecting links beyond 11 years (e.g., the 12- and 15-year signals found with τmax = 15). This modification of the algorithm’s “visible window” alters the detected lag and sign for this specific feedback46.

In summary, the PCMCI causal analysis confirms our findings: subpolar North Atlantic OHC anomalies at 0–200 m acts as a precursor to OHT contribution to Arctic temperature variability, which in turn serves as the key trigger for Arctic SAT variability. This result provides robust, causal-mechanistic support for the hypothesis that subpolar ocean processes modulate Arctic multidecadal variability.

Discussions and conclusions

In this study, we investigated the underlying causes of multi-decadal Arctic SAT variability during the 21st century using the CESM1 LE22. By employing radiative kernel techniques28 in a time-evolving framework for the first time, we decomposed year-to-year SAT variations to quantify the contributions of poleward heat transports and local feedbacks. We further employed lead-lag correlation analysis and the PCMCI causal discovery algorithm to identify the potential mechanistic pathway: fluctuations in the upper subpolar North Atlantic OHC precede changes in poleward OHT into the Arctic. These OHT changes, in turn, act as a primary driver of multi-decadal Arctic SAT variability, and the resulting temperature signal is then amplified by local feedbacks.

Our results indicate that OHT variations, rather than local feedback processes, initiate the multi-decadal Arctic SAT fluctuations. Correlation-based analysis shows that the OHT contribution significantly leads Arctic temperature changes by approximately two years (r = 0.74, p < 0.0001). The PCMCI causal analysis provides methodological confirmation of this causal link (OHT → SAT), identifying a 1–2 year lag (95% confidence levels). This two-year lag is physically grounded in the delayed release of oceanic heat and subsequent ice-ocean interactions. AW entering the Arctic remains trapped beneath a strong halocline21, creating a subsurface heat reservoir. This heat is released through a two-stage process: first, winter convection weakens the vertical stratification, which then allows the stored heat to migrate upward toward the ice-ocean interface36,47. The resulting basal ice melt further enhances heat exchange through positive feedbacks, a process with a characteristic 1–2 year lag48 corresponding to the time required for heat anomalies to erode the halocline and affect sea ice. This finding is physically consistent with previous work; for example, Årthun et al. 49 linked a 10 TW increase in oceanic heat transport to a 145,000 km² reduction in Barents Sea ice area. Furthermore, our results show this OHT-SAT coupling is a feature of the forced response to anthropogenic emissions, not a product of internal variability, underscoring the role of large-scale ocean circulation adjustments in a changing climate.

Once this external heat enters the Arctic system, its effect on surface temperature is amplified by a suite of local positive feedbacks, including those related to albedo, lapse rate, Planck response, and water vapor. These feedbacks closely track Arctic temperature variations with minimal phase lag, enhancing the warming or cooling signals initiated by OHT. Our quantitative analysis shows that poleward OHT accounts for 44.5% of the multi-decadal SAT variability, while local feedbacks collectively explain an additional 53.5%. This dynamic underscore the critical role of OHT in modulating Arctic climate, where heat imported from regions with strong negative feedbacks triggers powerful local positive feedbacks, leading to an overall amplification of temperature change21,50.

Looking further upstream, our analysis identifies variability in the upper subpolar North Atlantic OHC as a key precursor to OHT fluctuations that reach the Arctic. We found that OHC variations in the upper 200 m of the subpolar North Atlantic lead the OHT contribution to the Arctic by approximately five years, with a significant correlation (r = 0.58, p < 0.0001). The PCMCI causal analysis provides confirmation of this positive forcing (OHC → OHT) and identifies distinct lags of 5 and 12 years (95% confidence levels). A plausible physical mechanism for this connection involves the interaction between large-scale atmospheric forcing and the regional ocean’s thermal structure. Wind stress curl patterns associated with the NAO and Icelandic Low influence basin-scale circulation, and variability in subpolar OHC modulates this wind-driven transport through Arctic gateways like the Fram Strait51,52. While our study establishes this link, the ultimate drivers of the multi-decadal OHC variability in the subpolar North Atlantic remain an area of active research. Previous studies have proposed underlying causes ranging from changes in Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)53 to variations in atmospheric aerosol loading51. Further investigation of this mechanism is beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, our work supports the idea that oceanic processes drive Arctic climate variability, including the critical role of subpolar North Atlantic dynamics54,55.

In summary, this study delineates a plausible causal chain where OHC variability in the subpolar North Atlantic drives OHT fluctuations, which in turn initiate multi-decadal changes in Arctic SAT that are subsequently amplified by local feedbacks. This mechanistic understanding has significant implications. First, the precursory signals from subpolar OHC (5-year lead) and OHT (2-year lead) could serve as valuable constraints for improving decadal-scale Arctic climate projections. Second, this pathway provides a physical basis for the accelerating “Atlantification” of the Arctic Ocean observed in recent years. However, significant uncertainties remain in future projections, particularly regarding upper-ocean stratification, vertical mixing56, and cloud feedbacks52. The divergence among CMIP6 models on whether the Eurasian Basin will experience strengthened stratification or enhanced Atlantification highlights these challenges57. Therefore, our findings underscore the urgent need for sustained observational programs targeting Arctic gateway transports and process-oriented ocean analyses to verify model fidelity and refine our understanding of Arctic climate change. Ultimately, high-latitude oceans have been identified as key regions for temperature variability on both hemispheric and global scales over interdecadal to multi-decadal timescales36,58. This recognition has important implications for climate prediction efforts on these timescales, which largely rely on oceanic adjustment mechanisms59,60.

Data and methods

Data

Observational and reanalysis datasets

To evaluate the accuracy of SAT in CESM1 LE, we compared model outputs against two in situ observational datasets and one reanalysis product. The in situ datasets include the NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies Surface Temperature version 461 (GISTEMP v4) and the Met Office Hadley Centre/Climatic Research Unit version 5.0.1.062 (HadCRUT5). Additionally, the ERA5 reanalysis63 from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) is incorporated to provide a physically constrained representation of atmospheric conditions.

For ocean diagnostics, including ocean heat transport and upper-ocean current velocities, we utilized output from the ECMWF Ocean Reanalysis System 564 (ORAS5) covering the 1979–2020 period. ORAS5 employs an eddy-permitting ocean model with a horizontal resolution of 0.25°, and vertical resolution ranging from 1 m near the surface to ~ 5 m at 50 m depth and ~ 20 m near 210 m.

Large ensembles

We employed the CESM1 LE22, which uses a consistent model configuration with approximately 1° horizontal resolution across all components. The dataset includes 40 ensemble members, covering historical forcing from 1850 to 2005, followed by the Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 8.5 scenario from 2006 to 2100. Individual members differ only by a round-off level perturbation to their 1920 initial atmospheric conditions.

For each ensemble member, we analyzed monthly mean atmospheric fields: surface pressure, surface air temperature, air temperature at multiple levels through the atmosphere, specific humidity, radiative fluxes (both all-sky and clear-sky) at the top of atmosphere (TOA), and surface energy fluxes (both all-sky and clear-sky). We also used three monthly mean ocean variables: seawater potential temperature, net surface heat flux, and mixed layer depth. To compute annual anomalies over the 21st century, we first calculated monthly anomalies relative to the 1920–1949 reference period and then average them annually31.

To characterize internal variability, we used the 1801-yr CESM1 pre-industrial control (piControl) simulation (model years 0400–2200). From this, we created a 1701-member “pseudo-ensemble” by sampling 101-year segments of Arctic SAT anomalies, OHT contributions, and subpolar North Atlantic OHC.

Additionally, we verified the robustness of 21st-century multi-decadal Arctic SAT variability using GFDL-CM3 Large Ensemble27. However, due to the absence of essential variables (e.g., radiative budget components) in the GFDL-CM3 Large Ensemble, the primary findings are derived from CESM1 LE.

Multi-decadal variability

In this study, we used two methods to isolate the multi-decadal variability in a timeseries, following the approach of England6. The first method removes the linear trend over the period 2000–2100, with the residuals referred to as “anomalies.” The second method subtracts the ensemble mean from a large ensemble of identically forced simulations, thereby eliminating the externally forced response. The remaining residuals are termed “deviations.”

The PCMCI algorithm

We employed the PCMCI (PC-Momentary Conditional Independence) algorithm44,45, a robust causal discovery method developed by Runge et al. 45 specifically for identifying causal links within large-scale, autocorrelated time-series data. The algorithm follows a two-step procedure to effectively manage high dimensionality and autocorrelation. For this study, we utilized the Python implementation available in the freely downloadable TIGRAMITE library (https://jakobrunge.github.io/tigramite/). Comprehensive algorithmic details are described in Runge et al. 44,45.

The first step utilizes the PC-stable algorithm (named for its originators, Peter Spirtes and Clark Glymour) to identify a preliminary set of potential causal drivers, or “parents” (P(Xj(t))), for each variable Xj in the network. This constraint-based algorithm iteratively performs partial correlation tests based on time-lagged (auto- and cross-) correlations. It begins with zero-order correlations and progressively adds conditioning variables (Zn), removing links where the two target variables are found to be conditionally independent given a specific set of conditions. This process efficiently narrows down the set of parent candidates.

The second step is the Momentary Conditional Independence (MCI) test, which re-evaluates the significance of all potential links identified in Step 1. For each potential link (e.g., Xi(t-τ) → Xj (t)), the MCI test calculates its partial correlation while conditioning on the entire set of parents (P(Xj(t))) discovered in the PC-stable step. Only links that remain statistically significant after this stringent conditioning are retained in the final causal network. This two-step process adheres to the causal Markov condition, which posits that a variable is statistically independent of all other variables (excluding its own effects) once conditioned on its direct causes.

The algorithm’s configuration is determined by three key parameters: tau_max, pc_alpha, and alpha_level. tau_max (τmax) specifies the maximum time lag to be considered for potential causal links; parents beyond this lag are disregarded. pc_alpha (α) sets the significance level for the conditional independence tests during the PC-stable (parent selection) step. alpha_level determines the final p-value threshold for the MCI test, which confirms the links in the final graph. In this study, α was fixed at 0.01, and alpha_level was fixed at 0.05. To assess the sensitivity of our results to the maximum lag, we explored τmax values of 11 and 15 years.
