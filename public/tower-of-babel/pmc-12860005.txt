Strengthening community engagement in pandemic preparedness: insights from COVID-19 response in Sidama Region, Ethiopia
Authors: Aschalew Abayneh, Sileshi Demelash Sasie, Selamawit Mengesha, Mark Spigt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the critical importance of community engagement in managing disease outbreaks. In Ethiopia, efforts to control the pandemic were hampered by a limited understanding of the disease, especially in rural areas. While early studies provided some insights, there remains a significant gap in comprehensive data on community involvement throughout all phases of outbreak management. This study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, practices, and community participation in pandemic preparedness and response, emphasizing that informed and engaged communities are key to early detection, effective intervention, and overall outbreak control. Learning from the COVID-19 experience, it is essential to understand how communities can be better equipped to respond to future public health threats.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out between October and December 2022 across eight rural districts in the Sidama region of Ethiopia. Using multistage sampling, a total of 593 community members were selected and interviewed with a semi-structured questionnaire. Data were entered into EpiData version 4.6 and analyzed in SPSS version 25 using descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with knowledge, attitudes, practices (KAP), and community participation.ResultsThe majority of participants, 76% (95% CI: 72.5–79.3), demonstrated sufficient knowledge about COVID-19 transmission and prevention, yet only 58% (95% CI: 54.0–61.9) showed positive attitudes, and 28% (95% CI: 24.5–31.7) reported practicing good preventive behaviors. Participation in emergency response activities, such as mask-wearing, handwashing, and symptom identification, was relatively low at 35% (95% CI: 31.2–39.0). Education level, marital status, and risk perception were significant predictors of KAP and participation levels. Individuals with primary (AOR = 5.06, 95% CI: 2.81–9.11) or secondary education (AOR = 4.84, 95% CI: 2.64–8.87) had greater odds of possessing adequate knowledge compared to those without education. Unmarried participants were less likely to exhibit positive attitudes (AOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.35–0.96), and perceiving low personal risk was associated with lower preventive practices (AOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.21–0.48) and participation in response activities (AOR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.12–0.30).ConclusionWhile knowledge of COVID-19 was relatively high, attitudes were only moderate and preventive practices remained low. The finding that only 35% of the population actively participated in COVID-19 response efforts highlights a critical gap in community engagement during health crises. From an emergency management perspective, this calls for more effective strategies to translate awareness into proactive involvement. The Ministry of Health and WHO must focus on trust-building, communication, and empowering local leaders to mobilize communities. Tailored, community-driven models and culturally appropriate messaging are essential to ensure grassroots resilience and preparedness in future pandemics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25921-0.Keywords: Community engagement, COVID-19, Ethiopia, Outbreak preparedness, Public health emergency response, Rural areas

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly affected health systems, societies, and economies worldwide [1, 2]. Especially in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia facing a disproportionate burden. As of March 2022, despite various measures, Ethiopia reported over 470,000 confirmed cases and more than 7,500 deaths, illustrating the virus’s severe impact [3]. Despite efforts to curb community transmission, challenges persist due to gaps in public understanding of disease dynamics and preventive measures. This shortfall in awareness hinders efforts to achieve the behavioral changes necessary for controlling the virus’s spread. Active community engagement is essential in managing crises like COVID-19, as it empowers communities to adopt preventive practices. Without a solid foundation of knowledge, it becomes difficult to achieve the behavioral changes necessary to halt the spread of the virus. The community’s response is an essential factor in controlling any outbreak, and understanding this response can offer valuable insights for future crises [4, 5]. Without sufficient awareness of how the virus spreads and the measures needed to prevent it, achieving the necessary behavior changes to disrupt transmission remains a significant challenge [6, 7]. Studies such as Reuben et al. [8] assessment of 589 individuals in North-Central Nigeria during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 highlight this need. They found that although 85.5% of participants understood COVID-19 transmission and prevention, only 59.3% practiced effective preventive measures, and just 47.8% held positive attitudes toward them. Similarly, Gebretsadik et al. [9] Examined 550 patients attending COVID-19 screening centers in Northeast Ethiopia, finding that while 82.5% demonstrated strong knowledge of COVID-19, adherence to preventive practices was lower at 65.3%. In addition, Mustafa et al. [10] reviewed Ethiopia’s preparedness plans and response strategies, noting that community engagement was identified as a key element.

These district-level studies offer initial insights but lack the national scope necessary to inform comprehensive, localized strategies aligned with WHO standards. Although the Ethiopian Ministry of Health created national response plans emphasizing multi-sectoral coordination and community participation, evidence on community involvement in outbreak responses remains limited and geographically restricted. Broader research is necessary to evaluate community participation in managing outbreaks across all phases, especially in rural areas. This study seeks to fill these critical gaps by assessing community engagement in multiple rural districts, contributing to a more community-centered approach to pandemic preparedness and management in Ethiopia, in line with WHO guidelines that prioritize communities as key stakeholders throughout epidemic responses.

Ethiopia presents a unique setting for understanding community engagement in public health emergencies, offering valuable insights for global health preparedness. As a predominantly rural, low-income country, Ethiopia faces distinct challenges in health access, socio-economic vulnerability, and infrastructure limitations [11]. Additionally, the country has extensive experience managing outbreaks, from cholera to yellow fever, which may shape community perceptions of government health initiatives. In regions accustomed to severe health crises, such as the Sidama Region, it is essential to examine whether COVID-19 was viewed as similarly critical or met with less urgency. Previous research has shown that community engagement is essential for effective outbreak management; however, in Ethiopia, studies have largely been limited to specific districts and focused on individual knowledge and attitudes rather than broader community participation [8, 9]. Beyond knowledge, attitudes, and practices, community participation in emergency response activities such as case reporting, health education, risk communication, and supply distribution is a critical but underexplored dimension of pandemic preparedness in Ethiopia. This study therefore examined both KAP and participation to provide a more comprehensive understanding of community engagement during the COVID-19 response.

To meet WHO standards, more comprehensive research was needed to assess population participation across all phases of the outbreak, particularly in rural settings [12]. This study aimed to address these critical evidence gaps by evaluating community participation in multiple rural districts. The findings are intended to enhance Ethiopia’s capacity to implement truly community-centered pandemic preparedness and management, in accordance with global guidelines that prioritize engaging communities as key stakeholders throughout epidemics. This study aims to address a significant gap in current understanding by investigating awareness, attitudes, practices, and involvement in pandemic response activities in Sidama. This study will provide empirically grounded insights into how rural communities engage with public health initiatives. This evidence is crucial for WHO, the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute as they seek to develop more resilient, community-centered response frameworks. The findings from this research can support more effective allocation of resources and improve multi-sectoral coordination, ultimately strengthening Ethiopia’s and other similar settings’ preparedness for future public health emergencies [13, 14]. Therefore, study was conducted after the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, at a time when transmission had declined, and the disease was no longer classified as a public health emergency of international concern. The intent, therefore, is not to evaluate ongoing COVID-19 control, but rather to learn from community experiences during the pandemic to strengthen preparedness for future outbreaks.

To meet WHO standards, more comprehensive research was needed to assess community participation across all phases of outbreak management, particularly in rural settings where health system capacity is limited [12]. This study was therefore undertaken to address these evidence gaps by examining awareness, attitudes, practices, and participation in pandemic response activities across multiple rural districts in Sidama. Although the COVID-19 pandemic has since subsided and is no longer classified as a public health emergency of international concern, the experience remains instructive. The intent of this study is not to evaluate ongoing COVID-19 control, but to derive lessons that can inform preparedness for future epidemics and pandemics. The findings offer empirically grounded insights into how rural communities engage with public health initiatives, which are essential for WHO, the Ministry of Health, and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute in strengthening community-centered preparedness frameworks. This evidence can support more effective resource allocation and improved coordination across sectors, ultimately contributing to more resilient and responsive public health systems in Ethiopia and similar settings [13, 14].

Methods

Context

Ethiopia has struggled to control the spread of COVID-19 since the pandemic’s onset in early 2020. By October 2022, when this study was conducted, the country had reported over 470,000 confirmed cases and more than 7,500 related deaths, highlighting the severe toll of the virus. Despite the Ethiopian Ministry of Health’s collaboration with partners to develop national response plans emphasizing community engagement and multi-sectoral coordination, case rates remained particularly high in rural areas, with national surveillance data showing that over 60% of confirmed infections during 2021 were reported outside major urban centers [15]. To curb ongoing transmission and reduce severe outcomes, efforts were made to raise awareness about transmission methods, symptoms, preventive measures, and available health resources through outreach campaigns utilizing multimedia platforms and on-the-ground activities. However, the effectiveness of these efforts and the extent of information dissemination and participation in COVID-19 control measures had not been systematically evaluated, particularly among vulnerable rural populations. This study aimed to fill these critical knowledge gaps.

Study design, area and period

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from October to December 2022 to assess knowledge, attitudes, practices (KAP), and participation of rural populations in pandemic preparedness and COVID-19 response activities. The cross-sectional design was chosen because it allows for the collection of data at a single point in time, enabling the estimation of prevalence and the identification of factors associated with KAP and community participation. This design was particularly appropriate in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, as it provided an efficient and timely approach to capturing information from a wide sample of households during an ongoing public health crisis.

Study area

The study was carried out in the Sidama Regional State of Ethiopia, one of the country’s most recently established regional states, located in the southern part of the country. Sidama is characterized by a predominantly rural population, with agriculture—especially coffee production—serving as the main economic activity. The region has limited healthcare infrastructure compared to urban centers, making it particularly vulnerable during public health emergencies such as COVID-19.

Within Sidama, eight rural districts were purposively selected for this study: Aleta-Wendo, Bensa, Aleta-Chuko, Dale, Huko, Ciri, Loka Abaya, and Dara. These districts were chosen because they are among the most populous in the region and had previously reported above-average caseloads of COVID-19, making them priority sites for evaluating community engagement and preparedness. Each district is further divided into kebeles (the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia, equivalent to wards or neighborhoods), which formed the basis for the sampling strategy. This setting provided a diverse yet representative picture of rural Sidama, allowing the study to capture the realities of pandemic response and preparedness in communities with limited resources, high population density in some areas, and significant reliance on community health structures.

Study population

The study population was derived from a sampling frame that included all registered households in the enumerated districts, based on the most recent national census or national ID registration data. This frame provided a comprehensive list of residential addresses in the study area, along with basic household demographic information, such as the number of residents and their ages. A random starting point was selected for each district, and then every second household was systematically chosen along predetermined routes or zones mapped out within each district. Fieldworkers visited the selected households and obtained a complete list of all usual residents from the head of the household or another informant. All household members aged 18 years or older were eligible for inclusion. Fieldworkers then approached each eligible individual, explained the study, and requested verbal informed consent for an interview. Those who consented formed the final study population from which primary survey data was collected.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined to be 605, calculated using the single population proportion formula, with adjustments for a design effect of 1.5, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and an additional 5% to account for potential non-response.

Sampling procedure

A multistage stratified random sampling technique was applied, with proportional allocation by district, random selection of kebeles, and systematic random sampling of households. From each selected household, one eligible individual was randomly chosen for interview. In Stage 1, the calculated sample size of 605 was proportionally allocated across the eight districts according to their total population size. In Stage 2, two kebeles (the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia) were randomly selected from each district. In Stage 3, the number of households to be sampled from each selected kebele was determined proportionally based on the total number of households. Systematic random sampling was then employed using the household registers to identify the required number of households within each kebele. Finally, one randomly selected eligible individual from each identified household was interviewed.

Data collection materials

The questionnaire was adapted from validated KAP instruments and additional questions were added to capture local practices and perceptions. The draft tool was pre-tested in one non-sample district with 35 community members. Feedback was used to refine wording, ensure cultural appropriateness, and adjust response options before formal data collection. The knowledge assessment questionnaire for this study was adapted from tools used in previous studies on COVID-19 knowledge as several studies have developed and validated questionnaires to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding COVID-19. These instruments were designed for various populations, including nursing students in Korea [17], adults in Malaysia [18], and the general French population [19]. The questionnaires typically covered topics such as transmission, symptoms, risk factors, and preventive measures. Similarly, Dolnicar et al. [20]employed a closely related 14-item multiple-choice questionnaire in Austria, further supporting the effectiveness of this approach in accurately assessing COVID-19 knowledge. The researchers of the current study adapted questions from these validated instruments, ensuring cultural relevance and comprehensibility for the local Ethiopian population through expert review.

Attitudes were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale [21] ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree,’ in response to seven statements evaluating attitudes toward preventive behaviors and the government’s response. Scores were summed, and if the distribution was skewed, the median was used to differentiate between positive and negative attitudes. If the distribution was normal, the mean was used for this differentiation [21] Practices were evaluated through questions on adherence to personal protective measures, with response options of ‘always,’ ‘sometimes,’ ‘rarely,’ or ‘never.’ Responses were summed, and if the distribution was skewed, the median was used to classify adherence levels as high or low. If the distribution was normal, the mean was used for categorization [22]. Knowledge was measured with 12 questions (score range 0–12). Due to skewed distribution, the median score (≥ 7) was used as the cut-off for adequate knowledge. Attitudes were assessed with 15 Likert-scale items (score range 0–15). Because the distribution was approximately normal, the mean score (≥ 9) was used as the threshold for positive attitudes. Practices were measured with 10 questions on preventive behaviors (score range 0–10). As the distribution was skewed, the median score (≥ 6) was applied to classify adherence as good or poor.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was carried out using a pre-tested, interviewer-administered structured questionnaire. Twelve data collectors, each holding at least a BSc in public health, were recruited and received two days of training on research ethics, questionnaire administration, and informed consent procedures. The data collection took place concurrently across the eight selected districts from October to December 2022, with supervisory support. Participation was assessed using a 13-item questionnaire covering involvement in case reporting, risk communication, supply distribution, and other community response activities. Each item was rated on a 3-point scale (‘high,’ ‘moderate,’ or ‘low’).

Data quality assurance

Before fieldwork began, all data collection staff received comprehensive training focused on research ethics, informed consent, survey administration skills, and data confidentiality. The draft tools were pre-tested on a small sample to assess comprehension, logic, and response scales, with feedback used to refine the questionnaire. During data collection, supervisors conducted daily reviews of completed paper surveys for completeness and logical consistency, addressing any issues on the spot to enhance data quality. Completed questionnaires were checked daily for completeness and consistency before data entry.

Security protocols included the secure storage and transport of paper forms. Electronic data entry clerks then input the survey data into software, with supervisors conducting periodic spot-checks in the field. Throughout training, piloting, data entry, cleaning, and storage, strict adherence to ethical research practices and data validity was maintained, ensuring the collection of reliable quantitative information to meet the study’s objectives. These quality control measures were implemented to optimize the reliability and integrity of the survey findings.

Data management and analysis

All quantitative data were double-entered into EpiData version 4.6 (EpiData Association, Denmark) [23]to ensure accuracy. Range and consistency checks were performed to validate correct data capture. The validated dataset was then exported to SPSS version 25 [24]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. KAP scores were categorized based on predefined cut-offs: knowledge (≥ 7/12), attitudes (≥ 9/15), and practices (≥ 6/10). Bivariate logistic regression was performed, and variables with p < 0.25 were entered into the multivariable model.

Participation was measured using a 13-item scale that assessed involvement in community emergency response activities, including case reporting, risk communication, and supply distribution. Each item was rated on a 3-point scale (‘high,’ ‘moderate,’ or ‘low’). Item scores were summed, and the overall distribution was assessed for normality. Where the distribution was skewed, a median split was used to categorize participation as good or poor [25]; where normally distributed, the mean was applied [26].

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) scores were categorized based on predefined cut-offs. Knowledge was measured with 12 items (score range 0–12) and classified as adequate if the score was ≥ 7, based on the median cut-off. Attitudes were measured with 15 Likert-scale items (score range 0–15) and categorized as positive if the score was ≥ 9, using the mean cut-off. Practices were measured with 10 items (score range 0–10) and classified as good if the score was ≥ 6, based on the median cut-off. The main dependent variable was community participation in Public Health Emergency Management (PHEM) activities, assessed using a 13-item scale and dichotomized into good versus poor participation.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio-demographic and outcome variables. Bivariate logistic regression was performed to explore associations between explanatory and outcome variables. Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. A backward elimination strategy was applied to build the final model. Model adequacy was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Correlation analyses were conducted between knowledge, attitude, and practice scores using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Correlation strength was interpreted using standard thresholds: r < 0.30 = low correlation, r = 0.30–0.50 = moderate correlation, and r > 0.50 = high correlation.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board of St. Paulo’s Millennium Medical College approved this study (reference number PM 23/71, dated 26/7/2022). Participants provided written informed consent after being informed about the study procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality, and their right to participate voluntarily or withdraw at any time. Identifying information was removed and replaced with codes to protect privacy. Hardcopy data was securely stored in locked cabinets, while electronic files were encrypted, and password protected. Data was reported anonymously in aggregate form. There were no risks associated with participation, and individuals had the option to skip questions or withdraw at any time. The study aimed to contribute to improved programs and policies, with potential benefits outweighing any minimal risks. The study adhered to ethical standards to ensure the privacy, dignity, and welfare of participants, in accordance with relevant guidelines.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Out of 605 individuals approached, 593 participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 98%. The average age of the participants was 35.37 years with a standard deviation of 10.84. The majority of participants were male (n = 342; 58%), and more than half (385 = 64.92%) lived in rural areas. In terms of educational status, 193 participants (32.60%) had no formal education, while 229 (38.68%) had attained secondary or higher education (Table 1).

Participant’s knowledge about COVID-19

Overall, 76% (n = 451; 95% CI: 72.5–79.3) of participants had adequate knowledge, while 24% (n = 142; 95% CI: 20.7–27.5) had inadequate knowledge. About 221 participants (37.39%) were aware that asymptomatic COVID-19 patients cannot spread the virus, while 372 (62.61%) were unaware of this fact. Around 210 participants (35.53%) knew that the incubation period could last up to 14 days, whereas 383 (64.47%) did not. The majority of participants, 80.71%, were knowledgeable about preventive measures such as avoiding crowds and public transport, but 119 (19.29%) lacked this knowledge. Over three-fourths, or 76.14%, understood the risk factors, such as chronic diseases, while 117 (23.86%) were unaware of these risks. Nearly 70% of participants were familiar with typical COVID-19 symptoms, though 183 (30.17%) did not recognize common symptoms like fever and cough (Table 2).

Attitude towards COVID-19 preventive measures

Positive attitudes towards COVID-19 prevention were observed in 58% (n = 344; 95% CI: 54.0–61.9), while 42% (n = 249; 95% CI: 38.1–46.0) expressed negative attitudes. The majority of participants, 483 (81.86%), believe that social distancing and handwashing can prevent COVID-19. Approximately three-quarters of the participants, 447 (76%), believe that COVID-19 vaccination can prevent the infection. Additionally, half of the participants, 310 (52.54%), have confidence in the preventive measures implemented by the government (Table 3).

Practice on COVID-19 prevention and control

Good preventive practices were reported by 28% (n = 166; 95% CI: 24.5–31.7), while 72% (n = 427; 95% CI: 68.3–75.5) demonstrated poor practices. Around 219 participants (37%) regularly wash or sanitize their hands. Around 214 participants (36.21%) wear a facemask when experiencing flu-like symptoms. A majority of 372 participants (63.16%) use non-conventional remedies, such as honey, garlic, ginger, and lime, when they have flu-like symptoms (Table 4).

Community participation in PHEM activities

Participation in PHEM activities was categorized as good in 35% (n = 208; 95% CI: 31.2–39.0) and poor in 65% (n = 385; 95% CI: 61.0–68.8). Nearly 252 participants (42.64%) reported that a reporting system is in place within their community for suspected COVID-19 cases. Overall, 35.34% of participants demonstrated good community participation.

Factors associated with knowledge about COVID-19

Key factors associated with COVID-19 knowledge among participants included education level, alcohol use, and risk perception. Participants with primary education had significantly higher odds of possessing adequate knowledge compared to those with no education (AOR 5.06, 95% CI 2.81–9.11). Similarly, those with secondary or higher education were nearly five times more likely to have adequate knowledge compared to those with no education (AOR 4.84, 95% CI 2.64–8.87). Alcohol consumption was also associated with more than three times greater odds of having adequate knowledge compared to non-drinkers (AOR 3.49, 95% CI 1.26–9.61). Additionally, participants who perceived a low personal risk of COVID-19 had an 89% reduced likelihood of having adequate knowledge compared to those who perceived a high risk (AOR 0.11, 95% CI 0.06–0.20). Therefore, achieving at least a primary education, alcohol use, and perceiving a higher vulnerability were important determinants of better COVID-19 understanding according to this study (Table 5).

Factors associated with attitude about COVID-19

The study identified several factors significantly associated with attitudes toward COVID-19. Education level emerged as a key influencer, with participants who had primary education being over 2.5 times more likely to hold favorable attitudes compared to those with no education (AOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.67–4.39). Those with secondary education had approximately 3.4 times higher odds of positive attitudes compared to those with no education (AOR 3.38, 95% CI 2.02–5.65).

Marital status also impacted attitudes, with unmarried participants showing about a 42% reduced likelihood of favorable attitudes compared to married participants (AOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.35–0.96). Thus, higher educational attainment was associated with more positive attitudes, while being unmarried was marginally linked to less favorable attitudes, according to the results (Table 6).

Factor associated with COVID-19 prevention practice

The study highlighted several key factors associated with COVID-19 prevention practices. Risk perception played a significant role, with those perceiving a low personal risk of the virus being 68% less likely to engage in preventive behaviors compared to those with a high-risk perception (AOR 0.32, CI 0.21–0.48). Additionally, a history of prior COVID-19 infection was linked to a more than 2.8 times greater likelihood of adopting preventive measures compared to those with no infection history (AOR 2.81, CI 1.76–4.47) (Table 7).

Discussion

The study revealed that while three-quarters of participants possessed adequate knowledge of COVID-19 transmission, symptoms, risk factors, and prevention measures, only 58% held favorable attitudes toward these practices, and actual engagement in prevention behaviors was even lower at around 28%. Fewer than 40% reported involvement in local emergency response activities, with higher education levels and risk perception positively associated with better knowledge, attitudes, and participation. However, over 60% of participants did not consistently practice key preventive behaviors such as hand hygiene or wearing masks when symptomatic. Socioeconomic factors, particularly education level and marital status, significantly influenced both preventive behaviors and community involvement in COVID-19 response efforts. These findings have broader implications beyond COVID-19 and are applicable to other highly contagious diseases like influenza, tuberculosis, and measles, where knowledge of transmission and prevention is often widespread, but translating that knowledge into consistent preventive behaviors remains a challenge. Similar factors such as education and risk perception influence responses to these diseases, and the low engagement in prevention behaviors observed mirrors patterns from other outbreaks, where large portions of the population fail to adopt critical practices such as vaccination, isolation, or personal hygiene measures despite awareness. Socioeconomic disparities further limit access to preventive resources and community participation, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to enhance both knowledge and behavior change. Addressing these gaps is essential for strengthening public health efforts not only in the context of COVID-19 but also in managing future outbreaks of other highly contagious diseases.

The observation that knowledge does not always translate into behavior aligns with existing public health literature. A WHO working paper highlighted a common trend where awareness during crises rises quickly, but preventive actions often lag due to socioeconomic barriers [27]. This Ethiopian study identifies specific contextual factors that either enable or hinder the adoption of preventive behaviors. On a national level, Ethiopia’s Health Sector Transformation Plan acknowledges the importance of addressing the “social determinants of practice” to achieve the desired outcomes [28]. Insights from this community-level analysis can inform the development of multisectoral policies and programs that empower populations to protect their health. Another key finding from our data was that unmarried participants were significantly less likely to have positive attitudes compared to married individuals. In rural Kenya, a study found that while communities strongly adhered to COVID-19 containment measures, older adults—especially those unmarried or with lower income—experienced a decline in overall well-being, suggesting that social support buffers vulnerability [29].

Globally, public health experts emphasize that COVID-19 communication must go beyond merely conveying biomedical information by also addressing the sociocultural factors that shape community decisions and behaviors [30]. As highlighted by The Lancet, effectively controlling pandemics requires public health measures that tackle the “social drivers of transmission” by influencing how people interact “ [31]. The findings of this study support the conclusions of large-scale reviews, which demonstrate that both cognitive and emotional factors play a role in guiding preventive health practices [32]. Specifically, the impact of education level and risk perception on knowledge, attitudes, and participation observed in this study aligns with evidence that perceptions, alongside information, significantly influence preventive behaviors [32]. This supports ongoing calls for health promotion models in the region to integrate psychological and structural determinants into their programs, fostering sustainable behavioral and social change over the long term [33, 34]. Our results further showed that low risk perception was strongly associated with poor preventive practice, echoing evidence from SARS and MERS epidemics where perceived susceptibility was the most reliable predictor of protective behaviors [35, 36]. This highlights the importance of embedding risk communication strategies that emphasize personal vulnerability to encourage protective action.

The study uncovered a gap between sufficient COVID-19 awareness and low civil participation in rural Sidama, reflecting similar challenges observed elsewhere in sustaining community engagement beyond outbreak periods [34, 37]. A review published in Development and Change emphasized that social capital and trust in public institutions play a crucial role in mediating grassroots involvement in health security efforts [38]. The findings from this study suggest the need for pilot interventions aimed at strengthening the relationships within Ethiopia’s public health system and building the foundations for long-term cross-sector coordination and community resilience [39]. Regular collaboration among multiple stakeholders could improve coordination, while empowering local leadership and maintaining ongoing prevention dialogue could strengthen resilience. Recognizing that engagement is crucial to national health security, insights gained from implementation can help refine and optimize emergency preparedness and response efforts. Notably, only 35% of participants in this study reported active participation in COVID-19 emergency response activities. This mirrors findings from the West African Ebola epidemic, where lack of trust limited community involvement, but when local leaders were engaged, participation significantly improved [40, 41]. Strengthening trust and community ownership should therefore be central to Ethiopia’s preparedness strategy.

Strengths

This study had several notable strengths. It utilized a rigorous multistage cluster sampling method to obtain a representative sample, enhancing the generalizability of the findings to the target population. The sample size was carefully calculated to ensure sufficient statistical power for analysis. By gathering primary data across multiple rural districts in the Sidama region, the study was able to capture a wide range of perspectives from the local population. Rigorous data quality controls were upheld through thorough training of data collectors, pre-testing of tools, and regular evaluation of completed questionnaires.

Limitations

However, the study also had some limitations. The cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish temporal relationships between factors. Additionally, the data was self-reported, making it susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. Since the study was conducted in a single region, the findings may not be generalizable to the entire country, or populations outside Ethiopia. There is also a potential for under-reporting of sensitive practices, such as social interactions, due to social acceptability biases.

Policy recommendation

Community engagement and participation in COVID-19 prevention needs to be strengthened. Localized awareness campaigns involving grassroots stakeholders should be conducted regularly. This will improve knowledge retention and foster a sense of community ownership. Multi-sectoral coordination between different actors should also be scaled up to facilitate a comprehensive response. Regular coordination meetings will allow for roles to be clarified and messaging to be standardized.

Access to healthcare in rural areas needs to be expanded. Outreach programs can boost testing, vaccination, and medical advice in remote villages. Partnering with local leaders and women’s groups to conduct mobile screening camps will especially help vulnerable communities with low access. Behavior change communication also needs to be tailored to address prevailing attitudes. Involving influential figures like religious leaders and teachers can help alter risk perceptions and social norms. Disease surveillance networks and case reporting should likewise be strengthened through trained community volunteers and grassroots committees.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study underscores that while COVID-19 awareness in rural Sidama was moderately high, there is a significant need to strengthen participatory prevention practices to effectively reduce community transmission. Policy efforts should prioritize overcoming socio-economic barriers to behavior change through context-specific, multisectoral strategies that actively involve communities. Ongoing monitoring and implementation research can help refine and optimize localized responses and epidemic preparedness over time. By fostering grassroots participation and ownership throughout all phases of outbreak management, Ethiopia can develop resilient public health systems capable of addressing current and future health emergencies.
