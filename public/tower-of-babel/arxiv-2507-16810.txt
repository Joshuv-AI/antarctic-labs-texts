The inverse initial data problem for anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations via Legendre time reduction method
Cong B. Van, Thuy T. Le, Loc H. Nguyen

Abstract
We consider the inverse initial data problem for the compressible anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations, where the goal is to reconstruct the initial velocity field from lateral boundary observations. This problem arises in applications where direct measurements of internal fluid states are unavailable. We introduce a novel computational framework based on Legendre time reduction, which projects the velocity field onto an exponentially weighted Legendre basis in time. This transformation reduces the original time-dependent inverse problem to a coupled, time-independent elliptic system. The resulting reduced model is solved iteratively using a Picard iteration and a stabilized least-squares formulation under noisy boundary data. Numerical experiments in two dimensions confirm that the method accurately and robustly reconstructs initial velocity fields, even in the presence of significant measurement noise and complex anisotropic structures. This approach offers a flexible and computationally tractable alternative for inverse modeling in fluid dynamics with anisotropic media.

inverse initial data problem; anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations; Legendre time reduction.

Introduction

Let
d\geq 2
denote the spatial dimension, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. We consider a fourth-order tensor field
\boldsymbol{\mu}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
representing the viscosity property of the medium,
which satisfies the coercivity: There exists a constant
\Lambda>0
such that for all matrices
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{d}\mu_{ijkl}\,\xi_{ij}\,\xi_{kl}\geq\Lambda|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}.

Let

Consider a fluid velocity field
\mathbf{u}:\Omega\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
governed by the following initial value problem for the compressible anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}=-\nabla p+{\rm div}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\mathbf{u}\right),&(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=0&(\mathbf{x},t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{U}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\mathbf{U}_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the initial velocity field and

is the fluid pressure.
Our focus is on the following inverse problem:

Problem 1

(Inverse initial data problem)
.

Given the lateral boundary observation

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\quad\mbox{for all }(\mathbf{x},t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.2)

reconstruct the initial condition
\mathbf{U}_{0}
.

The inverse initial data problem for anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations holds substantial practical importance in a variety of scientific and engineering domains, including seismology, aerospace and automotive engineering, hydraulic systems, oceanography, and atmospheric modeling
[
23
,
32
,
35
,
36
]

In these contexts, direct access to the initial velocity field is often infeasible, while accurate reconstruction of this field is essential for initializing predictive simulations of fluid dynamics. Recovering the initial state from lateral boundary measurements thus constitutes a critical and timely inverse problem.
From a theoretical standpoint, this problem presents significant challenges. The well-posedness of the forward anisotropic Navier–Stokes system remains unresolved in general settings, particularly in three dimensions and without strong regularity assumptions. For the inverse problem, the absence of Carleman estimates or other analytical tools applicable to fully anisotropic fourth-order tensors makes it difficult to establish uniqueness or stability. As a result, many foundational theoretical questions remain open in this setting.

In this work, we focus exclusively on the computational aspect of the problem. We do not attempt to address the underlying analytical difficulties but instead develop a numerical framework based on time-dimensional reduction and iterative regularization. The proposed method achieves stable and accurate reconstructions in practice, providing a viable computational approach for a class of inverse problems that currently lack rigorous theoretical guarantees.

While the primary focus of this paper is on an inverse problem associated with the anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations, it is essential first to understand the status of the corresponding forward problem. The well-posedness and regularity of the forward model form the foundation upon which the theoretical analysis and numerical treatment of inverse problems are built. Therefore, we briefly review relevant results on the forward problem.
While the global existence and regularity of solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations remain one of the most challenging open problems in mathematical fluid dynamics, substantial progress has been made in specific settings. Foundational analytical frameworks have been developed for various formulations of the Navier–Stokes system, particularly in the compressible and stationary cases

Further developments in the well-posedness theory include the treatment of compressible stationary Navier–Stokes equations with small data, where uniqueness and differentiability were proven in

[

Inverse problems for the Navier–Stokes equations have been extensively studied to determine unknown coefficients or initial data from boundary or interior measurements. In
[

, a numerical approximation for inverse problems was developed using a Lagrange–Galerkin method that effectively addresses drag reduction and velocity identification. A regularized Gauss–Newton method for shape reconstruction in two-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations was derived in

Our strategy for solving the inverse problem associated with the Navier–Stokes equations stated in Problem
1
is grounded in the use of the Legendre time-reduction technique. Specifically, we approximate the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
in the time variable by truncating its Fourier series with respect to an exponentially weighted Legendre basis of the weighted space
L_{e^{-2t}}^{2}(0,T)
. This basis, recently introduced in
[
34
]
for inverse initial data problems in elasticity, and further utilized in
[
22
]
for Maxwell’s system, offers notable advantages over classical bases such as the trigonometric system and the Legendre polynomial without the exponential weight. In particular, the exponential-weighted Legendre polynomials possess two desirable properties: non-vanishing derivatives and favorable convergence behavior of the truncated series applied to time derivatives of the velocity field.
By applying this time-reduction procedure, we reformulate the original time-dependent inverse problem into a sequence of time-independent systems, where the principal operator takes the form
{\rm div}(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\cdot)
. We refer to this reformulated system as the time-dimensional reduction model. Leveraging this structure, we then employ a Picard iteration to solve this model. The obtained solution directly yields an approximate solution to the original inverse problem, thereby enabling a globally convergent reconstruction scheme.

2
The Legendre polynomial-exponential basis

The Legendre polynomial-exponential basis, first introduced in
[
34
]
, is fundamental to the time-dimensional reduction method employed in this study. This basis integrates the spectral characteristics of classical Legendre polynomials with an exponential weight, providing a structured approximation framework in the space of exponentially weighted square-integrable functions.
For clarity and completeness, we briefly revisit this basis below.
Let
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
denote the Legendre polynomials on
(-1,1)
, defined by Rodrigues’ formula:

P_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(x^{2}-1)^{n}.

To define an orthonormal basis on
(0,T)
, we introduce the affine transformation
x=\frac{2t}{T}-1
, and define the rescaled polynomials

Q_{n}(t):=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{T}}P_{n}\left(\frac{2t}{T}-1\right)\quad\mbox{for }t\in(0,T).

The set
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
forms an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(0,T)
.
We then define the Legendre polynomial-exponential basis by:

\Psi_{n}(t):=e^{t}Q_{n}(t)\quad\mbox{for }t\in(0,T).

The system
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
forms an orthonormal basis of the exponentially weighted space

L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T):=\left\{u\in L^{2}(0,T)\,\middle|\,\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}|u(t)|^{2}dt<\infty\right\},

with the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}:=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}u(t)v(t)\,dt.

Proposition 1
.

The Legendre polynomial-exponential basis functions
\Psi_{n}
, for
n\geq 0
, satisfy the following regularity and spectral properties:

1.

(Smoothness and non-vanishing derivatives) For each
n\geq 0
, the function
\Psi_{n}
is infinitely differentiable on
(0,T)
, and none of its derivatives of any order vanishes identically on this interval.

2.

(Spectral coefficient decay) For every integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
k
and
T
, such that for all
u\in H^{k}(0,T)
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{2k}\left|\langle u,\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right|^{2}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{k}(0,T)}^{2}.

(2.1)

3.

(Derivative norm growth) There exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
T
, such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{3/2},\quad\text{and}\quad\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{7/2}.

(2.2)

This proposition was proved in Proposition 2.1, Lemma 2.1, and Lemma 2.2 in
[
34
]
. We do not repeat its proof here.
Let
N
be a positive integer. We introduce three key operators associated with this basis.

•

Fourier Coefficient Operator
(
\mathbb{F}^{N}
): For
u\in L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}\left((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\right)
, we define the vector of its first
N+1
coefficients by:

\mathbb{F}^{N}[u](\mathbf{x}):=\begin{bmatrix}u_{0}(\mathbf{x})&u_{1}(\mathbf{x})&\cdots&u_{N}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where

u_{n}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}u(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{n}(t)dt.

•

Expansion Operator
(
\mathbb{S}^{N}
): Given the coefficient vector

\mathbf{U}=(u_{0},\ldots,u_{N})^{\top}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times(N+1)},

the space-time expansion is defined by

\mathbb{S}^{N}[\mathbf{U}](\mathbf{x},t):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}u_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}(t),

•

Projection Operator
(
\mathbb{P}^{N}
): The projection of
u\in L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
onto the subspace
\mathbb{V}^{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{\Psi_{0},\ldots,\Psi_{N}\}
is:

\mathbb{P}^{N}[u](\mathbf{x},t):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}u(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{n}(t)dt\right)\Psi_{n}(t).

(2.3)

These operators satisfy the identity:

\mathbb{S}^{N}[\mathbb{F}^{N}[u]]=\mathbb{P}^{N}[u],

and Parseval’s identity implies:

\|\mathbb{P}^{N}[u]\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}=\|\mathbf{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times(N+1)}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})}.

(2.4)

Remark 1
.

For any integer
N\geq 1
, the operators
\mathbb{F}^{N}
,
\mathbb{S}^{N}
, and
\mathbb{P}^{N}
admit natural restrictions to vector-valued functions in the space
L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}\left((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega)^{d}\right)
for any
p\geq 1
. In this setting, the Parseval-type inequality in (
2.4
) continues to hold with
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
replaced by
H^{p}(\Omega)^{d}
. Explicitly, we have

\|\mathbb{P}^{N}[u]\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})}=\|\mathbf{U}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)^{d\times(N+1)}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})}.

, resulting in:

\sum_{n=0}^{N}s_{mn}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{mnl}\left(\mathbf{u}_{l}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla p_{m}(\mathbf{x})+{\rm div}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right),

(3.15)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
, where the coupling coefficients are defined by

s_{mn}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(t)\Psi_{m}(t)\,dt,\quad a_{mnl}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\Psi_{l}(t)\Psi_{n}(t)\Psi_{m}(t)\,dt,

and

p_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}p(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)dt.

The corresponding Cauchy boundary conditions for each mode
\mathbf{u}_{m}
follow from (
1.2
) and (
3.12
):

\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\textbf{0}\quad\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)\,dt,

(3.16)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega
.
Equations (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) define a coupled boundary value problem for the vector of coefficients
\mathbb{F}^{N}[\mathbf{u}]
. We refer to this as the
time-dimensional reduction model
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}s_{mn}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{mnl}\left(\mathbf{u}_{l}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla p_{m}+{\rm div}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\textbf{0},&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}_{m}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)\,dt,&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.17)

for
m=0,1,\dots,N
.

Solving the system (
3.17
) yields the vector of Fourier modes
\mathbb{F}^{N}[\mathbf{u}]
, which can be used to reconstruct an approximation of the original space-time solution
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
via the expansion
\mathbb{P}^{N}[\mathbf{u}]=\mathbb{S}^{N}[\mathbb{F}^{N}[\mathbf{u}]]
. According to the approximation result in (
3.13
), this reconstruction provides a full space-time representation of
\mathbf{u}
, thereby transforming the inverse problem (Problem
1
) into the computational task of solving the reduced model (
3.17
).
Despite this reduction, solving the system (
3.17
) remains highly nontrivial due to the presence of the nonlinear convective term

\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{mnl}\left(\mathbf{u}_{l}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x}),\quad m\in\{0,1,\dots,N\},

(3.18)

making (
3.17
) a coupled nonlinear system.
We will apply a conventional Picard iteration to overcome this difficulty, see Section
4.2
.

Remark 3
.

The time-dimensional reduction model (
3.17
) is an approximate representation of the original system, stemming from the truncation of the infinite Fourier expansion in (
3.13
). Despite this, we adopt the working assumption that

\mathbf{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{0}&\mathbf{u}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

satisfies the reduced model. This assumption is justified by the fact that the truncation error diminishes as the cutoff parameter
N
increases, with the error terms in the left-hand sides of (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) vanishing in the limit as
N\to\infty
, see Theorem
3.5
. Consequently, the reduced model becomes an increasingly accurate surrogate for the full dynamics. Moreover, from a computational standpoint, the assumption is natural: practical implementations inherently involve a finite
N
, making the exact infinite-dimensional formulation intractable.

Remark 4
.

The uniqueness of the time-reduction model (
3.17
) remains an open problem due to the complexity introduced by the anisotropic fourth-order tensor
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the presence of the nonlinear convection term given in (
3.18
). A well-established approach in the literature for establishing uniqueness in inverse problems is the use of Carleman estimates. However, deriving a Carleman estimate in the fully anisotropic setting is highly nontrivial and currently lacks a general theoretical framework. In contrast, if the problem is restricted to the isotropic case, Carleman estimates become more tractable; see, for instance,
[
9
,
11
]
. Under such simplifications, the uniqueness of the time-reduction model (
3.17
) could then be rigorously analyzed.
Therefore, we reserve a detailed investigation of uniqueness and stability results based on Carleman estimates for the isotropic case as a direction for future research.

4
Numerical study

In this section, we describe a semi-implicit finite difference scheme to generate simulated data, and the Picard quasi-reversibility method employed to solve the time-reduced model (
3.17
), thereby addressing Problem
1
. We also present several numerical examples to illustrate the performance of the proposed approach.

4.1
Data generation

We present numerical simulations in the two-dimensional setting (
d=2
) for simplicity. The fourth-order viscosity tensor
\boldsymbol{\mu}
used in our simulations is specified via its flattened representation:

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\rm flat}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1111}&\mu_{1112}&\mu_{1121}&\mu_{1122}\\
\mu_{1211}&\mu_{1212}&\mu_{1221}&\mu_{1222}\\
\mu_{2111}&\mu_{2112}&\mu_{2121}&\mu_{2122}\\
\mu_{2211}&\mu_{2212}&\mu_{2221}&\mu_{2222}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\tfrac{1}{16}&\tfrac{1}{16}&\tfrac{3}{8}\\
\tfrac{1}{16}&\tfrac{1}{4}&\tfrac{1}{4}&0\\
\tfrac{1}{16}&\tfrac{1}{4}&\tfrac{1}{4}&0\\
\tfrac{3}{8}&0&0&\tfrac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

This tensor does not correspond to the isotropic case. In isotropic linear viscoelasticity, the fourth-order viscosity tensor has the structure

\mu_{ijkl}^{\rm iso}=\alpha\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\theta\,(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}),

where
\alpha
and
\theta
are scalar constants. In such a case, certain components, such as
\mu_{1211}^{\rm iso}
, are identically zero. However, for the tensor defined above, we have
\mu_{1211}=\tfrac{1}{16}\neq 0
, thereby violating the isotropic form. This confirms that the chosen
\boldsymbol{\mu}
represents an anisotropic medium. For simplicity, in this numerical section, we choose
\nabla p=0
as the pressure gradient does not influence the formulation of our reconstruction method.

We employ a semi-implicit finite difference scheme to solve the forward problem numerically. The computational domain
\Omega=(-1,1)^{2}
is discretized using a uniform
41\times 41
Cartesian grid, corresponding to
\Delta x=\Delta y=0.05
. The temporal interval
[0,T]
with
T=1
is uniformly partitioned into 700 time steps
\{t^{1},t^{2},\dots,t^{700}\}
, with the step size
\Delta t\approx 0.00143
.

For the time discretization, we denote
\mathbf{u}(x,y,t^{n+1})
by
\mathbf{u}^{n}
and adopt a semi-implicit scheme in which the time derivative is approximated by
\frac{\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}}{\Delta t}.
The diffusion term involving the fourth-order viscosity operator
{\rm div}(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\mathbf{u})
is treated implicitly at time
t^{n+1}
, while the nonlinear convection term
(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}
is computed explicitly using the known velocity field
\mathbf{u}^{n}
from the previous time step. This leads to a linear system for
\mathbf{u}^{n+1}
at each time step, which is solved using a sparse direct solver. Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed at each step. This implicit-explicit like approach improves stability while keeping the computational complexity manageable.

After obtaining the numerical solution
\mathbf{u}^{*}
to the forward problem using the semi-implicit finite difference method described above, we extract the associated Neumann boundary data
\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}^{*}
by computing the normal derivative of the velocity field along the boundary
\partial\Omega
. This exact boundary flux is then used to generate synthetic measurement data by introducing multiplicative noise. Specifically, we define the noisy observations
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}^{*}(1+\delta\,\mathcal{R})
, where
\mathbf{f}^{*}=\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}^{*}
,
\delta=10\%
is the noise level, and
\mathcal{R}
is a uniformly distributed random field with values in
[-1,1]
. This formulation reflects realistic measurement errors and serves to validate the robustness of the proposed inversion algorithm under noisy boundary conditions.

4.2
A Picard algorithm to solve the time-reduction model

We define the admissible set

H:=\left\{\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times(N+1)}\ \middle|\ \mathbf{U}|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbf{0}\right\},

which contains the solution of the reduced system in (
3.17
). Let the initial guess of the true solution be denoted by

{\bf U}^{(0)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{0}&\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Assuming

{\bf U}^{(k)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{0}&\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

is known for some
k\geq 0
, we define the next iterate

{\bf U}^{(k+1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{(k+1)}_{0}&\mathbf{u}^{(k+1)}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}^{(k+1)}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

as the solution to the following boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}s_{mn}\mathbf{u}_{n}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{mnl}\left(\mathbf{u}_{l}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{u}_{n}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\\
\hskip 142.26378pt=-\nabla p_{m}+{\rm div}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\mathbf{u}_{m}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{x})\right),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
\mathbf{u}_{m}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0},&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}_{m}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}_{m}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(4.1)

for each
m=0,1,\dots,N
.
This iteration scheme is inspired by the classical Picard method. In general, the convergence of the sequence
\{{\bf U}^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 0}
depends on the proximity of the initial guess
{\bf U}^{(0)}
to the true solution of (
3.17
). However, we do not pursue this local convergence analysis, as such accurate initial guesses are typically unavailable in practice. Instead, we demonstrate numerically that the iteration converges even when the initial guess is chosen as
{\bf U}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
.

The next step is to solve the over-determined problem (
4.1
). By over-determined, we mean the presence of both Dirichlet and Neumann information of
{\bf U}^{(k+1)}
in this problem. We propose to use the quasi-reversibility method, first introduced in
[
17
]
. That means the solution
{\bf U}^{(k+1)}
is the minimizer of a least-squares functional involving a Tikhonov regularization term

J^{\epsilon,N}_{{\bf U}^{(k)}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\Bigg[\int_{\Omega}\Big|{\rm div}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\varphi_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}s_{mn}\varphi_{n}(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{mnl}\left(\mathbf{u}_{l}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\right)\mathbf{v}_{n}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\\
-\nabla p_{m}(\mathbf{x})\Big|^{2}d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}|\partial_{\nu}\varphi_{m}-\mathbf{f}_{m}|^{2}d\sigma(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon\|\varphi_{m}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\Bigg].

(4.2)

The existence and uniqueness of the minimizer of
J^{\epsilon,N}_{{\bf U}^{(k)}}
are guaranteed by the strict convexity of the functional and the fact that its domain
H
is a Hilbert space.

We summarize the Picard iteration and quasi-reversibility method to solve (
3.17
) and hence Problem
1
in Algorithm
1
.

Remark 5
.

While our numerical experiments indicate that the iterative sequence
\{\mathbf{U}^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 0}
converges reliably to the target solution when initialized from zero, we do not provide a theoretical justification of this convergence. The main difficulty lies in the lack of an appropriate Carleman estimate tailored to the anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations in the time-reduced framework. Developing such an estimate is highly nontrivial due to the complex structure of the fourth-order viscosity tensor. In the isotropic setting, where the viscosity tensor assumes a simpler form, one could potentially employ Carleman weight functions to construct and analyze the convergence of an analogous iterative scheme using existing Carleman estimates
[
9
,
11
]
. However, since the focus of this work is on anisotropic media, we defer the theoretical convergence analysis for the isotropic case to future research.

5
Concluding Remarks

This work presents a new computational approach to the inverse initial data problem for the anisotropic Navier–Stokes equations by leveraging a Legendre-based time reduction technique. By projecting the velocity field onto an exponentially weighted Legendre basis, we reformulated the original time-dependent problem into a sequence of time-independent elliptic systems, making the problem more tractable both analytically and numerically. This dimensional reduction not only simplifies the temporal complexity of the inverse problem but also enables stable recovery of initial velocity fields from partial and noisy boundary measurements.

The numerical results confirm that the proposed method yields accurate and stable reconstructions across diverse test cases, including those with high-contrast and geometrically complex initial conditions. Importantly, the technique exhibits robustness against significant levels of noise, demonstrating its practical potential for real-world applications where direct access to initial states is infeasible.

An important and challenging open question is whether the sequence
\{{\bf U}^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 0}
, defined in Section
4.2
, converges to the true solution of the time-reduced model (
3.17
). A positive resolution of this question would mark a significant contribution to the theory of inverse problems. One powerful approach to establishing convergence involves incorporating Carleman weight functions into the cost functional
J^{\epsilon,N}_{{\bf U}^{(k)}}
and utilizing Carleman estimates. However, appropriate Carleman estimates tailored for the anisotropic setting are not currently available.

If we instead restrict our attention to the isotropic case, specifically, the Lamé system, then existing Carleman estimates become applicable, see e.g.,
[
9
,
11
]
. In this context, some recently developed techniques, called the Carleman convexification
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
18
]
, the Carlaman contraction principle
[
19
,
20
,
26
]
and the Carleman–Newton method
[
1
,
21
]
can be extended to construct a globally convergent sequence
\{{\bf U}^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 0}
that approximates the true solution to the time-reduction model (
3.17
). In this case, both analytical and numerical global solvability of Problem
1
are achievable. Extending these ideas to the anisotropic setting forms a central direction for our future research.
