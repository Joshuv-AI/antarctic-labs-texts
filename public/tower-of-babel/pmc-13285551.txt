TAX1BP1 targets STING1 via microautophagy and Golgiphagy to limit inflammatory signaling
Authors: Sujit Suklabaidya, Suchitra Mohanty, Edward W Harhaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe CGAS-STING1 pathway plays a key role in detecting cytosolic DNA and initiating immune responses. Excessive STING1 activation can lead to aberrant inflammation and autoinflammatory diseases; therefore, the STING1 degradation pathway is tightly regulated by several negative regulatory mechanisms. In our recent study, we show that the selective autophagy receptor TAX1BP1 functions as a negative regulator of STING1 signaling. TAX1BP1 promotes the degradation of activated STING1 through microautophagy by facilitating the interaction of STING1 with the ESCRT-0 protein HGS, and selective autophagy of the Golgi apparatus in a process known as Golgiphagy. In TAX1BP1-deficient macrophages, STING1 aggregates accumulate at the trans-Golgi network, leading to stronger antiviral and inflammatory responses. Innate immune signaling pathways require precise control to ensure effective host defense against pathogens while avoiding excessive inflammation. The CGAS-STING1 pathway senses DNA from viral genomes and other microbes to initiate innate immune responses. The CGAS-STING1 pathway can also be triggered by self-DNA that accumulates in the cytoplasm from damaged mitochondria or DNA damage. Upon sensing of DNA, CGAS generates the second messenger 2’3’-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), which binds to and activates STING1. Activated STING1 oligomerizes and translocates from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi apparatus, where it recruits and activates the kinase TBK1 at the trans-Golgi. TBK1 then phosphorylates the transcription factor IRF3, resulting in the production of type I interferons (IFN) and proinflammatory cytokines. While this pathway is essential for acute inflammatory responses to pathogens with DNA genomes, sustained STING1 activation may lead to autoimmune and autoinflammatory diseases. Therefore, controlled regulation of STING1 signaling is imperative in preventing excessive immune responses. Previous studies showed that activated STING1 traffics to post-Golgi vesicles and is targeted for lysosomal degradation by endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent microautophagy whereby STING1-containing vesicles are directly encapsulated by lysosomes. However, the molecular mechanism linking STING1 trafficking to its degradation, and the role of autophagic receptors in controlling STING1 turnover were not fully understood.

Autophagy depends on cargo receptors that recognize ubiquitinated substrates and deliver them to phagophores for lysosomal degradation. The selective autophagy receptor SQSTM1/p62 was previously described as a negative regulator of the CGAS-STING1 pathway by targeting STING1 for degradation. TAX1BP1 is a selective autophagy receptor that cooperates with SQSTM1 in various cellular functions, including regulating inflammatory signaling and clearing protein aggregates. TAX1BP1 harbors a zinc finger domain that binds to polyubiquitin chains and LC3-interacting regions that promote binding to mammalian Atg8-family members, enabling it to connect ubiquitinated cargo to autophagic membranes. Although its roles in selective autophagy and immune signaling are established, it was unclear whether TAX1BP1 directly regulates STING1 signaling.

In our study, we discovered a previously unknown role for TAX1BP1 in limiting STING1 signaling through a Golgi-specific autophagic pathway [1]. We used genetic and biochemical approaches to show that a loss of TAX1BP1 leads to prolonged STING1 activation and heightened production of type I IFN and proinflammatory cytokines when stimulated by CGAS or STING1 agonists. TAX1BP1-deficient macrophages displayed enhanced phosphorylation of TBK1, IRF3 and IKK, indicating that TAX1BP1 serves as a critical brake on STING1 signaling. Moreover, tax1bp1fl/fl Lyz2/LysM-Cre mice exhibit elevated levels of type I IFN and proinflammatory cytokines upon in vivo administration of the STING agonist diABZI, and decreased viral loads in the draining lymph node and liver when infected with the double-stranded DNA orthopoxvirus ectromelia virus (ECTV). Mechanistically, we observed that activating the STING1 pathway is accompanied by dramatic changes in the Golgi apparatus, consisting of swelling and fragmentation. After stimulation, STING1 accumulates at trans-Golgi membranes, which can serve as platforms that sustain downstream signaling prior to their engulfment by phagophores (Figure 1). Interestingly, TAX1BP1 localizes with STING1-associated Golgi membranes for clearance through autophagic degradation. Furthermore, TAX1BP1 functions as a selective autophagy receptor for microautophagy together with SQSTM1 and cooperates with components of the ESCRT machinery to promote the degradation of activated STING1. In TAX1BP1-deficient macrophages, fragmented Golgi accumulate, creating a platform for sustained STING1 signaling. These findings suggest that TAX1BP1 acts as an adaptor between STING1-associated Golgi membranes and the autophagic machinery for degradation of fragmented Golgi in a process known as Golgiphagy (Figure 1).

As a consequence of STING1 activation, the Golgi becomes swollen and fragmented, which in turn triggers Golgiphagy. The selective autophagy receptor TAX1BP1 is required for STING1-induced Golgiphagy, promoting the degradation of a subset of activated STING1 molecules and thereby attenuating STING1 signaling through a negative feedback mechanism. In addition to mediating Golgiphagy, TAX1BP1 also facilitates ESCRT-dependent degradation of STING1. Golgiphagy has been described in the context of cellular stress and quality control of organelles; however, its role in innate immune signaling has been largely unexplored. The Golgi apparatus is crucial for STING1 activation and plays an active role in regulating the duration of STING1-mediated signaling. By degradation of STING1-containing Golgi membranes through TAX1BP1 and SQSTM1-mediated Golgiphagy, STING1 activation is effectively coupled with its signaling termination after initiating an immune response. Our findings uncover a new regulatory process in which the autophagic degradation of STING1-associated Golgi membranes serves as a negative feedback mechanism to terminate STING1 signaling independent of microautophagy or macroautophagy, but requiring STING1, TAX1BP1 and SQSTM1. These findings suggest that organelle quality control pathways significantly influence innate immune responses. Therefore, selective autophagy pathways such as Golgiphagy not only govern intracellular homeostasis, but also act as dynamic regulators of signaling networks by controlling the availability of membrane platforms that support signal transduction.

In summary, TAX1BP1 functions as a key negative regulator of STING1 signaling through selective autophagy of the Golgi apparatus. By linking STING1-associated Golgi fragments to autophagic machinery, TAX1BP1 promotes Golgiphagy and thus limits the duration and magnitude of innate immune responses. These findings reveal a new link between organelle turnover and immune regulation and offer insights into how cells maintain immune balance and homeostasis after activating the CGAS-STING1 pathway.
