Projective Representations, Bogomolov Multiplier, and Their Applications in Physics
Ryohei Kobayashi, Haruki Watanabe

Abstract
We present a pedagogical review of projective representations of finite groups and their physical applications in quantum many-body systems. Some of our physical results are new. We begin with a self-contained introduction to projective representations, highlighting the role of group cohomology, representation theory, and classification of irreducible projective representations. We then focus on a special subset of cohomology classes, known as the Bogomolov multiplier, which consists of cocycles that are symmetric on commuting pairs but remain nontrivial in group cohomology. Such cocycles have important physical implications: they characterize (1+1)D SPT phases that cannot be detected by string order parameters and give rise, upon gauging, to distinct gapped phases with completely broken non-invertible Rep ⁡ ( G ) \mathrm{Rep}(G) symmetry. We construct explicit lattice models for these phases and demonstrate how they are distinguished by the fusion rules of local order parameters. We show that a pair of completely broken Rep ⁡ ( G ) \mathrm{Rep}(G) SSB phases host nontrivial interface modes at their domain walls. As an example, we construct a lattice model where the ground state degeneracy on a ring increases from 32 without interfaces to 56 with interfaces.



I
Introduction

One of the most fundamental tools for describing symmetries in physics is the representation theory of groups.
In quantum mechanics, in particular, the transformation rules for quantum states often take the form of projective representations.
For instance, an electron possesses an internal spin-
1/2
degree of freedom, corresponding to a two-dimensional representation of the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
symmetry; however, its faithful symmetry action must be viewed as a projective representation of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
, which is realized, for example, through spatial rotation symmetry.
Formally, a projective representation is defined as a map
U\colon G\to U(d)
satisfying

\displaystyle U(g)U(g^{\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime})U(gg^{\prime}),

(1)

for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
, where
\alpha(g,g^{\prime})\in U(1)
is a phase factor.

This mathematical structure is far from merely abstract; it plays an essential role in various branches of modern physics.
In one spatial dimension, for example, symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases are classified by the projective representations of their internal symmetry group
PhysRevB.83.035107
;
PhysRevB.85.075125
.
In crystalline solids, electronic states are represented by Bloch functions in momentum space, and the projective representations of the little group at each
\bm{k}
-point determine the degeneracies and topological properties of energy bands.
This information can be systematically extracted through symmetry indicators
SI
and topological quantum chemistry
TQC
— frameworks widely employed in high-throughput searches for topological insulators and semimetals.
Moreover, the projective representation underlies modern, discrete‐symmetry versions of the Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorem. If the on-site degrees of freedom within a single unit cell furnish a non-trivial projective representation of the global symmetry group — so that no one-dimensional linear representation exists in the corresponding cohomology class — then a gapped, symmetry-preserving, and non-degenerate ground state is impossible. The system must either exhibit ground-state degeneracy or support gapless excitations.
This extends the original argument for half-odd-integer spin chains.

PhysRevLett.119.127202
;
OgataTasaki
;
PhysRevX.8.011040
;
Cheng2016translational
;
Cheng2023LSM
.

Despite their importance, detailed and accessible expositions of the mathematics underlying projective representations remain limited.
Classical references on group theory in physics, such as Refs.
Tinkham
;
bradley2009mathematical
, typically discuss this topic only briefly, while mathematically oriented texts e.g. Refs.
Curtis
;
Haggarty
;

may present a high barrier for many physics-trained readers.
The purpose of this note is thus to provide an elementary and self-contained introduction to projective representations of
finite
groups, beginning from fundamental definitions.

Throughout this note, we assume
G
to be a finite group and set
U(1)\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z|=1\}
.
As a recurring example, we frequently refer to the simplest nontrivial case,

\displaystyle G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},

(2)

which is isomorphic to the group generated by
\pi
-rotations about the
x
,
y
, and
z
axes, and thus commonly emerges as an actual symmetry in physics.
For this group, there exist four distinct one-dimensional ordinary (linear) irreducible representations, while there is only a single two-dimensional irreducible projective representation.
In fact, when the group
G
is
Abelian
, the number of inequivalent irreducible projective representations is always smaller than the number of ordinary (linear) irreducible representations.

The situation significantly changes for a
non‑Abelian
group. There it is possible that the number of linear irreducible representations matches the number of the projective ones. This happens when the projective representation corresponds to a
2
‑cocycle

\alpha(g,g^{\prime})=\alpha(g^{\prime},g)

Such projective representations correspond to elements of the Bogomolov multiplier

Moravec
;
Davydov2014

The second aim of this note is to study the role of the Bogomolov multiplier in topological phases of many-body systems. Some of the results presented in this note are known, while some are new.
We first review the properties of symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases in (1+1)D characterized by a cocycle in the Bogomolov multiplier. Specifically, the edge states of the (1+1)D SPT phase transform under the projective representation characterized by a nontrivial Bogomolov multiplier. In particular, while a large class of (1+1)D SPT phases are characterized by non-local order parameters known as string order parameters, we will see that the SPT phases associated with the Bogomolov multiplier cannot be detected by string order parameters.
This observation was first made in Ref.

We then study the implications of Bogomolov multipliers in (1+1)D gapped phases with non-invertible

We investigate how to distinguish and characterize the two phases in (1+1)D with fully broken

•

II
Projective Representations

In this section, we summarize the definitions and basic results on projective representations.

II.1
Properties of 2-Cocycles

II.1.1
Group cohomology

A map
\alpha\colon G\times G\to U(1)
is called a
2-cocycle
if it satisfies the
cocycle condition

\displaystyle\alpha(g,g^{\prime})\alpha(gg^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime})\alpha(g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime})

(4)

for all
g,g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}\in G
.
The set of all such maps is denoted
Z^{2}(G,U(1))
.
The trivial 2-cocycle
\alpha^{(0)}
is defined as
\alpha^{(0)}(g,g^{\prime})\equiv 1
for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
.

A map
\beta\colon G\to U(1)
is called a
1-cochain
. Using a 1-cochain
\beta
, we define a map
\delta\beta\colon G\times G\to U(1)
by

\displaystyle\delta\beta(g,g^{\prime})\coloneqq\beta(g)\beta(g^{\prime})/\beta(gg^{\prime}).

(5)

This map
\delta\beta
is called a
2-coboundary
; it also satisfies the cocycle condition (
4
) and thus belongs to
Z^{2}(G,U(1))
.
The set of all 2-coboundaries is denoted by
B^{2}(G,U(1))
.

Two
2
-cocycles
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
are said to be
equivalent
, written
\alpha^{\prime}\sim\alpha
, if there exists a 1-cochain
\beta
such that

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(g,g^{\prime})=\delta\beta(g,g^{\prime})\alpha(g,g^{\prime}).

(6)

The equivalence class containing
\alpha
is denoted by
[\alpha]
.
The set of equivalence classes of 2-cocycles under this relation forms an abelian group called the
second group cohomology
of
G
with coefficients in
U(1)
, defined by

\displaystyle H^{2}(G,U(1))\coloneqq Z^{2}(G,U(1))/B^{2}(G,U(1)),

(7)

which always forms an abelian group.

Example 1
.

For
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, group elements can be represented as
g=(n,m)
with
n,m=0,1
, and the group multiplication is defined by
(n,m)(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})=(n+n^{\prime},m+m^{\prime})\mod 2
.
In this case, we have
H^{2}(G,U(1))\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and a representative nontrivial 2-cocycle not equivalent to
\alpha^{(0)}
is given by

\displaystyle\alpha(g,g^{\prime})\coloneqq(-1)^{nm^{\prime}}.

(8)

Lemma 1
.

For an element
x\in G
, define
\alpha^{\prime}(g,g^{\prime})\coloneqq\alpha(xgx^{-1},xg^{\prime}x^{-1})
. Then,
\alpha^{\prime}\sim\alpha
.

Proof.

Define a 1-cochain by
\beta(g)\coloneqq\alpha(xgx^{-1},x)/\alpha(x,g)
. Using the cocycle condition (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\beta(g,g^{\prime})\alpha(g,g^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta(g)\beta(g^{\prime})}{\beta(gg^{\prime})}\alpha(g,g^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(xgx^{-1},x)\alpha(xg^{\prime}x^{-1},x)[\alpha(x,gg^{\prime})\alpha(g,g^{\prime})]}{\alpha(x,g)\alpha(x,g^{\prime})\alpha(xgg^{\prime}x^{-1},x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{[\alpha(xgx^{-1},x)\alpha(xg,g^{\prime})]\alpha(xg^{\prime}x^{-1},x)}{\alpha(x,g^{\prime})\alpha(xgg^{\prime}x^{-1},x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{[\alpha(xgx^{-1},xg^{\prime})\alpha(xg^{\prime}x^{-1},x)]}{\alpha(xgg^{\prime}x^{-1},x)}

\displaystyle=\alpha(xgx^{-1},xg^{\prime}x^{-1})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}(g,g^{\prime}),

(9)

thus verifying (
6
).
∎

II.1.2
\alpha
-regularity of conjugacy classes

We now review some basic facts about conjugacy classes.
The orbit of an element
g\in G
under conjugation
g\mapsto xgx^{-1}
is called the
conjugacy class
of
g
, denoted by

\displaystyle C(g)\coloneqq\{xgx^{-1}\mid x\in G\}.

(10)

The set of all conjugacy classes of
G

is denoted by

\displaystyle C(G):=\{C(g)\mid g\in G\}.

(11)

The set of elements commuting with
g
is called the
centralizer group
of
g
and denoted by

\displaystyle Z(g)\coloneqq\{h\in G\mid hg=gh\}.

(12)

The set of elements commuting with all elements of
G
is called the

of
G
and denoted by

\displaystyle Z(G)\coloneqq\{h\in G\mid hg=gh,\;\forall g\}.

(13)

For a subgroup
H
of
G
, the set of all left cosets
gH
is denoted by
G/H
.

Theorem 1
.

The map

\displaystyle\phi\colon G/Z(g)\longrightarrow C(g),\qquad\phi(xZ(g))\coloneqq xgx^{-1},

(14)

is a bijection.
Consequently, the following relation holds among the orders of these groups:

\displaystyle|Z(g)||C(g)|=|G|.

(15)

Proof.

If
xZ(g)=yZ(g)
then
y^{-1}x\in Z(g)
, so
y^{-1}xg=gy^{-1}x
and hence
xgx^{-1}=ygy^{-1}
.
Thus the value of
\phi(xZ(g))
does not depend on the representative of the coset. First assume
\phi(xZ(g))=\phi(yZ(g))
, i.e.
xgx^{-1}=ygy^{-1}
.
Rewriting gives
y^{-1}xg=gy^{-1}x
, so
y^{-1}x\in Z(g)
and therefore
xZ(g)=yZ(g)
. Hence
\phi
is injective. Conversely, for any
h\in C(g)
there exists
x\in G
with
h=xgx^{-1}
by definition of the conjugacy class, and then
\phi(xZ(g))=h
. Hence
\phi
is surjective.
∎

We now introduce the notion of
\alpha
-regularity, a key concept in classifying projective representations. An element

is called
\alpha
-regular
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\alpha(g,h)=\alpha(h,g)

(16)

for all
h\in Z(g)

Clearly,
\alpha
-regularity is invariant under conjugation; that is,

Lemma 2
.

If

is
\alpha
-regular, then every conjugate
xgx^{-1}\in C(g)
is also
\alpha
-regular.

Proof.

An element of
Z(xgx^{-1})
can be written
xhx^{-1}
with

.
By Lemma
1
,

\displaystyle\alpha(xgx^{-1},xhx^{-1})=\frac{\beta(g)\beta(h)}{\beta(gh)}\alpha(g,h).

(17)

Since

is
\alpha
-regular, the right-hand side is symmetric under
g\leftrightarrow h
, and the claim follows.
∎

We denote by
C^{(\alpha)}(G)
the set of
\alpha
-conjugacy classes of
G
, and write
|C^{(\alpha)}(G)|
for the number of such classes.

Lemma 3
.

For
g\in G
, define a map
\varphi_{g}\colon Z(g)\to U(1)
by

\displaystyle\varphi_{g}(h)\coloneqq\frac{\alpha(g,h)}{\alpha(h,g)}.

(18)

Then
\varphi_{g}
is a group homomorphism.

Proof.

Cocycle condition (
4
) immediately yields
\varphi_{g}(hh^{\prime})=\varphi_{g}(h)\varphi_{g}(h^{\prime})
for
h,h^{\prime}\in Z(g)
.
∎

Theorem 2

(Number of
\alpha
-regular conjugacy classes)
.

The number of
\alpha
-regular conjugacy classes

is given by

\displaystyle|C^{(\alpha)}(G)|=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}\frac{\alpha(g,g^{\prime})}{\alpha(g^{\prime},g)}\delta_{gg^{\prime},g^{\prime}g}.

(19)

Here,
\delta_{x,y}=1
if
x=y
and
0
otherwise.
For the trivial cocycle
\alpha^{(0)}
, this reduces to the total number of conjugacy classes

\displaystyle|C(G)|=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}\delta_{gg^{\prime},g^{\prime}g}.

(20)

Proof.

By Lemma
3
the map
\varphi_{g}
gives a one-dimensional representation of

.
If
g
is
\alpha
-regular,
\varphi_{g}
is trivial and

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in Z(g)}\varphi_{g}(h)=|Z(g)|.

(21)

If
g
is
not

\alpha
-regular, character orthogonality implies the sum vanishes.
Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in Z(g)}\frac{\alpha(g,h)}{\alpha(h,g)}=\begin{cases}|Z(g)|&(\text{If $g$ is $\alpha$-regular})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(22)

Using
|Z(g)|=|Z(g_{0})|=|G|/|C(g_{0})|
for
g\in C(g_{0})
from (

) and summing over each conjugacy class yields Eq. (

).
∎

It follows immediately from Eq. (

) that

iff

-regular.

Example 2
.

For the Abelian group

II.2
Projective Representations

II.2.1
Multipliers and Projective Representations

A 2-cocycle
\alpha\colon G\times G\to U(1)
satisfying the normalization condition

\displaystyle\alpha(e,g)=\alpha(g,e)=1

(24)

for all
g\in G
is called a
multiplier system
of
G
.
From the cocycle condition, we see that for all
g\in G
,
\alpha(g,e)=\alpha(e,g)=\alpha(e,e)
holds. Thus, by defining a constant 1-cochain
\beta(g)\equiv 1/\alpha(e,e)
, the adjusted cocycle

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(g,g^{\prime})\coloneqq\delta\beta(g,g^{\prime})\alpha(g,g^{\prime})=\frac{\alpha(g,g^{\prime})}{\alpha(e,e)}

(25)

becomes a multiplier system.

Given a multiplier system
\alpha
, a map
U\colon G\to U(d)
satisfying

\displaystyle U(g)U(g^{\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime})U(gg^{\prime})

(26)

for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
is called a
projective representation
of
G
associated with
\alpha
. Ordinary (linear) representations correspond to the trivial multiplier system
\alpha=\alpha^{(0)}
. The dimension
d
of the matrices is called the
dimension of the representation
, and
\chi_{U}(g)\coloneqq\mathrm{tr},U(g)
is called the
character
.

Example 3
.

For
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, a projective representation associated with the multiplier
\alpha(g,g^{\prime})=(-1)^{nm^{\prime}}
is given by

\displaystyle U(0,0)=\sigma_{0},\quad U(1,0)=\sigma_{1},\quad U(0,1)=\sigma_{2},\quad U(1,1)=-i\sigma_{3},

(27)

where
\sigma_{\mu}
are the Pauli matrices. This representation has dimension
2
, and its character is
2
for the element
(0,0)
and
0
for all other elements.

Theorem 3
.

Let
U
be a projective representation of
G
associated with
\alpha
. If an element
g\in G
is not
\alpha
-regular, then

\displaystyle\chi_{U}(g)=0.

(28)

Proof.

For
h\in Z(g)
,

\displaystyle U(g)U(h)=\alpha(g,h)U(gh),\quad U(h)U(g)=\alpha(h,g)U(hg).

(29)

Since
gh=hg
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha(g,h)U(h)U(g)U(h)^{-1}=\alpha(h,g)U(g).

(30)

Taking the trace, we obtain the result when
\alpha(g,h)\neq\alpha(h,g)
.
∎

When an element
g\in G
is
\alpha
-regular, it is possible to have
\chi_{U}(g)\neq 0
. However, note that in projective representations, even if elements
g
and
g^{\prime}
belong to the same conjugacy class, it
does not necessarily
hold that
\chi_{U}(g^{\prime})=\chi_{U}(g)
. Indeed, if
g^{\prime}=xgx^{-1}
, then

\displaystyle\chi_{U}(g)=\frac{\alpha(x,g)\alpha(xg,x^{-1})}{\alpha(x,x^{-1})}\chi_{U}(g^{\prime}),

(31)

and thus a discrepancy by a
U(1)
factor arises.

Theorem 4
.

If there exists a projective representation of dimension
d
, then
\alpha^{d}\sim\alpha^{(0)}
. In particular, if there is a one-dimensional projective representation, then
\alpha\sim\alpha^{(0)}
.

Proof.

Taking the determinant of both sides of the defining relation for a projective representation,

\displaystyle U(g)U(g^{\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime})U(gg^{\prime}),

(32)

we have

\displaystyle\alpha(g,g^{\prime})^{d}=\frac{\det U(g)\det U(g^{\prime})}{\det U(gg^{\prime})}.

(33)

Thus, by defining a 1-cochain
\beta(g)=\det U(g)\in U(1)
, we see
\alpha(g,g^{\prime})^{d}=\delta\beta(g,g^{\prime})
, proving the claim.
∎

As we will see later, a regular projective representation, whose dimension is
|G|
, always exists for any multiplier system
\alpha
. Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha^{|G|}\sim\alpha^{(0)}.

(34)

II.2.2
Schur’s Lemma

A projective representation

(with a fixed multiplier

) is

,

of equal multiplier such that, in a common

\displaystyle U(g)=\begin{pmatrix}V(g)&0\\
0&W(g)\end{pmatrix}\qquad(\forall g\in G).

(35)

Otherwise

is
irreducible
.
By definition every one-dimensional representation is irreducible.
Irreducible representations will be denoted

with dimension

.

Let

and

be irreducible projective representations associated with the same multiplier system

. A matrix

satisfying

\displaystyle U_{A}(g)M=MU_{B}(g)

for all

is either a zero matrix or an invertible square matrix with dimension

. Moreover, if

, then

must be proportional to the identity matrix.

Theorem 5

(Orthogonality Relations for Representations)
.

Let

and

be irreducible projective representations of

. Then the following relation holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}[U_{A}(g)]_{ij}[U_{B}(g)]_{kl}^{*}=\frac{|G|}{d_{A}}\delta_{AB}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}.

(37)

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary

II.3

II.3.1
Group Algebra and Projective Representations

To obtain further information about irreducible representations, we introduce the group algebra. Fixing a multiplier system
\alpha
, consider an
|G|
-dimensional vector space over complex numbers. Suppose the linearly independent basis vectors of this vector space are given by
\{|g\rangle\}_{g\in G}
indexed by elements
g\in G
. Define the multiplication on this vector space by

\displaystyle|g\rangle\cdot|g^{\prime}\rangle\coloneqq\alpha(g,g^{\prime})|gg^{\prime}\rangle.

(45)

The identity element is
|e\rangle
, satisfying
|e\rangle\cdot|g\rangle=|g\rangle\cdot|e\rangle=|g\rangle
. This algebra is called the
\alpha
-twisted group algebra
.

The operation of left multiplication by
|g\rangle
, denoted by

, is a linear map on this vector space:

\displaystyle L_{g}\Big(\sum_{g^{\prime\prime}\in G}|g^{\prime\prime}\rangle c_{g^{\prime\prime}}\Big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{g^{\prime\prime}\in G}|g\rangle\cdot|g^{\prime\prime}\rangle c_{g^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g^{\prime\prime}\in G}\alpha(g,g^{\prime\prime})|gg^{\prime\prime}\rangle c_{g^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g^{\prime}\in G}|g^{\prime}\rangle\sum_{g^{\prime\prime}\in G}[U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)]_{g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}}c_{g^{\prime\prime}}

(46)

Here, we define a
|G|
-dimensional matrix

with components given by

\displaystyle[U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)]_{g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}}=\delta_{g^{\prime},gg^{\prime\prime}}\alpha(g,g^{\prime\prime})

(47)

The matrix

satisfies

\displaystyle[U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)^{\dagger}U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)]_{g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}}=\delta_{g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}}

(48)

thus it is unitary. Moreover, it fulfills

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g^{\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime})U_{\mathrm{reg}}(gg^{\prime}),

(49)

making it a projective representation associated with the multiplier system
\alpha
. This representation is called the

. Its character is given by

\displaystyle\chi_{U_{\mathrm{reg}}}(g)=|G|\delta_{g,e}.

(50)

Theorem 7

(Sum of Squares of Dimensions)
.

Let
U_{A}
(
A=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(\alpha)}
) be irreducible projective representations associated with
\alpha
, and let their dimensions be

. Then, the following relation holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{A=1}^{N_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(\alpha)}}d_{A}^{2}=|G|.

(51)

Proof.

Interpreting the orthogonality relation for representations (

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{A=1}^{N_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(\alpha)}}d_{A}^{2}\leq|G|.

On the other hand, using the orthogonality of characters, if the regular projective representation contains an irreducible representation

, it does so exactly
d_{A}
times. Therefore, we have

, and taking the trace yields

\displaystyle\chi_{U_{\mathrm{reg}}}(g)=\sum_{A}d_{A}\chi_{U_{A}}(g).

(53)

From (

gives

\displaystyle\sum_{A}d_{A}^{2}=|G|,

which achieves the upper bound of (

times in the regular projective representation.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For

\displaystyle|\Psi_{g}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{x\in G}\frac{1}{\alpha(x,x^{-1})}|x\rangle\cdot|g\rangle\cdot|x^{-1}\rangle=\sum_{x\in G}\frac{\alpha(x,g)\alpha(xg,x^{-1})}{\alpha(x,x^{-1})}|xgx^{-1}\rangle.

(56)

Then, for all

\displaystyle|g^{\prime}\rangle\cdot|\Psi_{g}\rangle=|\Psi_{g}\rangle\cdot|g^{\prime}\rangle.

(57)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle|g^{\prime}\rangle\cdot|\Psi_{g}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in G}\frac{\alpha(g^{\prime},x)}{\alpha(x,x^{-1})}|g^{\prime}x\rangle\cdot|g\rangle\cdot|x^{-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in G}\frac{\alpha(g^{\prime},g^{\prime}{}^{-1}y)}{\alpha(g^{\prime}{}^{-1}y,y^{-1}g^{\prime})}|y\rangle\cdot|g\rangle\cdot|y^{-1}g^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in G}\frac{\alpha(g^{\prime},g^{\prime}{}^{-1}y)}{\alpha(g^{\prime}{}^{-1}y,y^{-1}g^{\prime})\alpha(y^{-1},g^{\prime})}|y\rangle\cdot|g\rangle\cdot|y^{-1}\rangle\cdot|g^{\prime}\rangle

The second equality follows by the substitution

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(g^{\prime},g^{\prime}{}^{-1}y)}{\alpha(g^{\prime}{}^{-1}y,y^{-1}g^{\prime})\alpha(y^{-1},g^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{\alpha(y,y^{-1})}.

(59)

∎

Applying (

II.3.3
Method to Determine Irreducible Representations

When the order of group

First, generate a random

\displaystyle H\coloneqq\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)H_{0}U_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)^{\dagger}.

Then, similarly to (

Thus,

II.3.4
Central Extension

Let
A
be any Abelian group, and consider a 2-cocycle
\omega\in Z^{2}(G,A)
taking values in
A
.
Consider the direct product set
A\times G
with elements denoted as
(a,g)
, where
a\in A
and

. Define the multiplication on this set by

\displaystyle(a,g)(a^{\prime},g^{\prime})=(aa^{\prime}\omega(g,g^{\prime}),gg^{\prime}).

(78)

This defines a group

called the

of

. In this construction,

\displaystyle 1\rightarrow A\xrightarrow{i:a\mapsto(a,e)}\hat{G}\xrightarrow{\pi:(a,g)\mapsto g}G\rightarrow 1

(79)

forms a short exact sequence, with

.

In fact, the projective representation of

is understood as

of a certain central extension

. Let us consider a projective representation characterized by a 2-cocycle

. As shown in (

. Then, take

and

to define the finite group

.

Now consider the linear representation

of

. Define

\displaystyle U(g)U(g^{\prime})=\hat{U}(1,g)\hat{U}(1,g^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\alpha(g,g^{\prime}),gg^{\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime})\hat{U}(1,gg^{\prime})=\alpha(g,g^{\prime})U(gg^{\prime}).

Thus,

. Consequently, some results established previously, such as the orthogonality relations for characters and representations, follow immediately from known results for linear representations.
Moreover, the condition that the projective representation is trivial

III
Bogomolov Multiplier

We now discuss examples where every element

is
\alpha
-regular, but
\alpha

Let

;

;

and is denoted

for any Abelian groups.

Historically, Mangold asserted that if every

is

-regular, then
\alpha
is a 2-coboundary (meaning

is trivial for any finite group)
Higgs1989projective
. However, as demonstrated in Ref.

, a counterexample exists.
Moravec
Moravec
exhaustively investigated groups with
|G|\leq 64
, finding that the smallest order for which

is nontrivial is
|G|=64
, and among 267 groups of order
|G|=64
(excluding isomorphic cases), nine groups have nontrivial

.

If
[\alpha]\in B(G)
, the following relations must all hold:

•

Every conjugacy class of

is an
\alpha
-regular conjugacy class. Denote the number of these classes by
|C^{(\alpha)}(G)|=|C(G)|
.

•

Let
d_{A}
be the dimension of irreducible projective representations

of
G
associated with
\alpha
. The number of distinct irreducible projective representations,

Physical Applications of Bogomolov Multiplier

IV.1
Review: Absence of String Order Parameters in (1+1)D SPT Phases

An SPT phase cannot be characterized by local order parameters. Instead, in many cases a (1+1)D
G
SPT phase is characterized through a non-local order parameter called a
string order parameter

Pollmann2010spectrum
;
Turner2011onedimensionalphase
;
PerezGarcia2008string
;
Haegeman2012orderparameter
. Suppose that the
G
symmetry acts onsite. The idea is to consider a symmetry operator
U_{g}
with
g\in G
supported on an open region
\Sigma
of length
l
, with boundaries labeled
L
and
R
.
The string order parameter is defined as the ground state expectation value of the operator in the form of

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{g}(\Sigma,V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)})=\bra{\text{GS}}V_{g}^{(L)}U_{g}(\Sigma)V_{g}^{(R)}\ket{\text{GS}}~,

(106)

where
V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)}
are local unitaries supported at the ends of
\Sigma
. In general, there exists a choice of local operators
V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)}
such that the above expectation value remains nonzero in the limit of
l\to\infty.

The operator
V_{g}^{(L)}U_{g}(\Sigma)V_{g}^{(R)}
creates a pair of
g\in G
symmetry defects when acting on a ground state.
In principle, there is freedom in the choice of terminations
V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)}
, which leads to different choices of symmetry defects for a given symmetry operator
U_{g}
on a closed 1d chain. The above choice of
V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)}
, which yields a non-vanishing string order parameter, corresponds to a specific, preferred choice of a
g
symmetry defect, such that the defect does not create excitations.

With the above choices of
V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)}
, a large class of (1+1)D
G
SPT phases can be characterized by the electric charge under
Z(g)
carried by the symmetry defects
V_{g}^{(L)},V_{g}^{(R)}
,

\displaystyle U_{h}V_{g}^{(L)}U_{h}^{\dagger}=\varphi_{g}(h)V_{g}^{(L)}~,\quad U_{h}V_{g}^{(R)}U_{h}^{\dagger}=\varphi_{g}(h)^{*}V_{g}^{(R)}~.

(107)

When a (1+1)D SPT phase is characterized by
\alpha\in H^{2}(G,U(1))
, an electric charge carried by
V_{g}^{(L)}
(or
V_{g}^{(R)\dagger}
) under
Z(g)
is given by
\varphi_{g}\in\text{Rep}(Z(g))
introduced in (
18
);
\varphi_{g}(h)=\alpha(g,h)/\alpha(h,g)

Pollmann2012detection
.

Let us consider an example of a string order parameter in a (1+1)D SPT phase with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.
The model is given by a cluster state with a qubit at each site,

\displaystyle H_{\text{SPT}}=-\sum_{j}Z_{j-1}X_{j}Z_{j+1}~,

(108)

with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(\text{even})}:\prod_{j\in\text{even}}X_{j},\quad\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(\text{odd})}:\prod_{j\in\text{odd}}X_{j}~.

(109)

This SPT Hamiltonian is obtained by applying a finite-depth circuit called an SPT entangler, on a trivial symmetric product state;

\displaystyle H_{\text{SPT}}=U_{CZ}H_{0}U^{\dagger}_{CZ}~,

(110)

with

\displaystyle H_{0}=-\sum_{j}X_{j}~,\quad U_{CZ}=\prod_{j}CZ_{j,j+1}~,

(111)

where
CZ
is a Controlled-
Z
operator.
The string order parameters of
H_{\text{SPT}}
for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry are simply obtained by products of local stabilizer Hamiltonians, which take the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{even}}=Z^{(L_{\text{odd}})}\left(\prod_{j\in\Sigma_{\text{even}}}X_{j}\right)Z^{(R_{\text{odd}})}~,\quad\mathcal{S}_{\text{odd}}=Z^{(L_{\text{even}})}\left(\prod_{j\in\Sigma_{\text{odd}}}X_{j}\right)Z^{(R_{\text{even}})}~.

(112)

These operators leave the ground state invariant.
The string order parameter
\mathcal{S}_{\text{even}}
has the
Z
operator at the odd site
L_{\text{odd}}
, and therefore carries electric charge under
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\text{odd}}
, and vice versa.

IV.1.1
(1+1)D SPT phase for Bogomolov multiplier

When a (1+1)D
G
SPT phase
is characterized by a cocycle
\alpha
belonging to the Bogomolov multiplier
B(G)
, the string order parameter fails to detect it. This is because every
g\in G
is
\alpha
-regular, implying that
\varphi_{g}
is trivial; consequently, the symmetry defects
V_{L},V_{R}
do not carry an electric charge. The absence of a string order parameter was first pointed out in Ref.
Pollmann2012detection
using the symmetry group given in Sec.
III.1
.

We now explicitly construct a lattice model for the SPT phase with
\alpha\in B(G)
. We use the choice of
G
discussed in Sec.
III.1
.
Let us consider a 1d chain with
|G|
dimensional local Hilbert space on each site.
The local Hilbert space at each site is spanned by basis states
\ket{g}
labeled by group elements
g\in G
.
We define the operators

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{X}_{g}\ket{h}=\ket{gh}~,\quad\overleftarrow{X}_{g}\ket{h}=\ket{hg^{-1}}~.

(113)

A trivial gapped phase with
G
symmetry is realized by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{0}=-\sum_{j}\sum_{g\in G}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}~,

(114)

with onsite
G
symmetry

\displaystyle U_{g}=\prod_{j}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}~.

(115)

As in the case of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
cluster state, the SPT phase is obtained by conjugating the trivial Hamiltonian
H_{0}
by an SPT entangler. The SPT entangler for
\alpha\in B(G)
is given by the unitary

\displaystyle U_{CZ}=\prod_{j}CZ^{j,j}_{a,b}CZ^{j,j+1}_{a,b}~,

(116)

where each unitary
CZ_{j,j}^{a,b},CZ_{j,j+1}^{a,b}
are Controlled-Z-type operators

\displaystyle CZ_{j,j}^{a,b}\ket{g_{j}}=(-1)^{n_{a}(g_{j})n_{b}(g_{j})}\ket{g_{j}}~,\quad CZ_{j,j+1}^{a,b}\ket{g_{j},g_{j+1}}=(-1)^{n_{a}(g_{j})n_{b}(g_{j+1})}\ket{g_{j},g_{j+1}}~,

(117)

and

\displaystyle Z^{(j)}_{a}\ket{g_{j}}=(-1)^{n_{a}(g_{j})}\ket{g_{j}}~,\quad Z^{(j)}_{b}\ket{g_{j}}=(-1)^{n_{b}(g_{j})}\ket{g_{j}}~,

(118)

where
a,b
label the generators of
G
defined in (
81
), and
n_{a}(g),n_{b}(g)
are mod 2 numbers that count the number of
a,b
appearing in the word representation of
g\in G
using the generators
a,b,c_{1},c_{2},x,y_{1},y_{2}
(see e.g., (
85
)).

The SPT Hamiltonian is then given by

\displaystyle H_{\text{SPT}}=U_{CZ}H_{0}U_{CZ}^{\dagger}=-\sum_{j}\sum_{g\in G}(Z_{a}^{(j-1)})^{n_{b}(g)}(Z_{b}^{(j)})^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}(Z_{a}^{(j)})^{n_{b}(g)}(Z_{b}^{(j+1)})^{n_{a}(g)}~,

(119)

which again has the onsite
G
symmetry
U_{g}
.
The string order parameter is obtained by conjugating the symmetry operator
U_{g}
on a region
\Sigma
with the SPT entangler
U_{CZ}
.
This results in a string order parameter localized at the right end of

U_{g}(\Sigma)
,

\displaystyle V_{g}^{(R)}=(Z_{a}^{(R-1)})^{n_{b}(g)}(Z_{b}^{(R)})^{n_{a}(g)}~.

(120)

The electric charge of
V_{g}^{(R)}
under
h\in Z(g)
is given by
\alpha(g,h)/\alpha(h,g)
, with
\alpha(g,h)=(-1)^{n_{a}(g)n_{b}(h)}\in B(G)
introduced in (
87
). Since
\alpha
belongs to the Bogomolov multiplier, this electric charge is trivial.
The string order parameters thus do not carry any nontrivial electric charge under the symmetry.

Although the string order parameters carry only trivial charges,
H_{\text{SPT}}
realizes a nontrivial SPT phase with protected edge modes.
To demonstrate this explicitly, we introduce a boundary to the SPT Hamiltonian while preserving the
G
symmetry,

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{\text{(open)}}_{\text{SPT}}=&-\sum_{2\leq j\leq L-1}\sum_{g\in G}(Z_{a}^{(j-1)})^{n_{b}(g)}(Z_{b}^{(j)})^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}(Z_{a}^{(j)})^{n_{b}(g)}(Z_{b}^{(j+1)})^{n_{a}(g)}~\\
&-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G\\
n_{b}(g)=0\end{subarray}}(Z_{b}^{(1)})^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(1)}(Z_{b}^{(2)})^{n_{a}(g)}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G\\
n_{a}(g)=0\end{subarray}}(Z_{a}^{(L-1)})^{n_{b}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(L)}(Z_{a}^{(L)})^{n_{b}(g)}~,\end{split}

(121)

where
the last two terms are edge Hamiltonians that commute with the bulk Hamiltonian.
We can find a set of boundary operators that commute with the Hamiltonian, and thus act within the ground state Hilbert space,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta^{(L)}_{g}&:=(Z_{a}^{(1)})^{n_{b}(g)}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(1)}(Z_{b}^{(2)})^{n_{a}(g)}~,\\
\eta^{(R)}_{g}&:=(Z_{a}^{(L-1)})^{n_{b}(g)}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(L)}(Z_{b}^{(L)})^{n_{a}(g)}~,\end{split}

(122)

where
\eta_{g}^{(L)},\eta_{g}^{(R)}
correspond to left and right edge modes.
These operators follow a projective representation of
G

\displaystyle\eta^{(L)}_{g}\eta^{(L)}_{h}=\eta^{(L)}_{gh}\alpha(g,h)~,\quad\eta^{(R)}_{g}\eta^{(R)}_{h}=\eta^{(R)}_{gh}\alpha(g,h)~,

(123)

with
\alpha(g,h)=(-1)^{n_{a}(g)n_{b}(h)}\in B(G)
.
This implies the presence of nontrivial edge modes that carry a projective representation of
G
associated with the cocycle
\alpha\in B(G)
.
Indeed, the ground state Hilbert space of
H^{\text{(open)}}_{\text{SPT}}
has a structure of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{(L)}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{(R)}
, where
\eta_{g}^{(L/R)}
acts on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{(L/R)}
by an irreducible projective representation, leading to 4-fold ground state degeneracy.

IV.2.1
Maximally
\text{Rep}(G)

Let us consider a (1+1)D
G

\alpha\in H^{2}(G,U(1))
. We gauge the
G
symmetry of the SPT phase, resulting in a gapped phase with
\text{Rep}(G)

If the cocycle
\alpha
belongs to the Bogomolov multiplier
B(G)

\text{Rep}(G)
symmetry is maximally broken
kobayashi2025soft

\text{Rep}(G)
symmetry operators act faithfully on the ground state Hilbert space, and the ground states have maximal degeneracy among all possible gapped phases. In Sec.
IV.4
we discuss the classification of
\text{Rep}(G)
maximally broken phase using symmetry TQFT.

Let us describe explicit lattice models for distinct maximally
\text{Rep}(G)
broken phases.
The models are obtained by gauging the
G
symmetry of either the trivial or SPT Hamiltonian in (
114
), (
119
). The
G

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{X}^{(j)}_{g}\mapsto\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}~,\quad Z_{a}^{(j)}Z_{a}^{(j+1)}\mapsto Z_{a}^{(j+1)}~,\quad Z_{b}^{(j)}Z_{b}^{(j+1)}\mapsto Z_{b}^{(j+1)}~.

(124)

Applying this KW transformation to Eqs. (
114
) and (
119
) yields the following Hamiltonians:

\displaystyle H^{(0)}_{\text{SSB}}=-\sum_{j}\sum_{g\in G}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}~,

(125)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{(\alpha)}_{\text{SSB}}&=-\sum_{j}\sum_{g\in G}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}(Z_{a}^{(j)})^{n_{b}(g)}(Z_{b}^{(j+1)})^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}~.\end{split}

(126)

These models have a non-invertible
\text{Rep}(G)
symmetry given by a matrix product operator (MPO) defined as follows.
Let
\Gamma\in\text{Rep}(G)
be an irreducible representation of
G
, with dimension
d_{\Gamma}
. Then we define a local MPO with bond dimension
d_{\Gamma}
as
fechisin2023noninvertible

\displaystyle Z_{\Gamma}=\sum_{g\in G}\Gamma(g)\otimes|g\rangle\langle g|~,

(127)

where
\Gamma(g)
is a representation matrix acting on the bond Hilbert space. See Fig.
1
.

The corresponding symmetry operator is then given by the MPO formed by
Z_{\Gamma}
,

\displaystyle W_{\Gamma}=\text{tr}\left[\prod_{j}Z^{(j)}_{\Gamma}\right]=\sum_{\{g_{j}\}}\text{tr}(\Gamma(g_{1}\dots g_{L}))\ket{g_{1}\dots g_{L}}\bra{g_{1}\dots g_{L}}~,

(128)

where the last expression is for a periodic chain with length
L
. This operator indeed satisfies the
\text{Rep}(G)

\displaystyle W_{\Gamma}W_{\Gamma^{\prime}}=W_{\Gamma\otimes\Gamma^{\prime}}=N_{\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}}^{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}W_{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}~,

(129)

where
N_{\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}}^{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}
is the fusion multiplicity of irreducible representations.

The symmetry operator commutes with the local Hamiltonian of
H^{(0)}_{\text{SSB}}

\displaystyle W_{\Gamma}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}=\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}W_{\Gamma}~,

(130)

which can be seen by

\displaystyle\begin{split}W_{\Gamma}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}\ket{h_{1},\dots h_{j},h_{j+1},\dots h_{L}}&=W_{\Gamma}\ket{h_{1},\dots h_{j}g^{-1},gh_{j+1},\dots h_{L}}\\
&=\text{tr}(\Gamma(h_{1}\dots h_{L}))\ket{h_{1},\dots h_{j}g^{-1},gh_{j+1},\dots h_{L}}\\
&=\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j+1)}W_{\Gamma}\ket{h_{1},\dots h_{j},h_{j+1},\dots h_{L}}~.\end{split}

(131)

The symmetry
W_{\Gamma}
also commutes with local Hamiltonians of
H^{(\alpha)}_{\text{SSB}}
.

IV.2.2
Ground states and symmetry actions

Let us study the ground states of the above
\text{Rep}(G)
SSB phases and the action of the symmetry operator on them.
Among several possible approaches, we adopt the following method, which can be readily generalized to more involved cases discussed later. We transform the SSB Hamiltonians into the short-range entangled (SRE) ones by a sequential circuit
Chen2024sequential
, which is a linear depth circuit of local unitaries. Since its depth scales linearly with the system size instead of constant, it can map long-range entangled SSB states into SRE states, and a local operator into a nonlocal one.

To describe the sequential circuit, let us introduce the following Controlled-
\overrightarrow{X}
operator

\displaystyle(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}\ket{g_{i},g_{j}}=\ket{g_{i},g_{i}g_{j}}~.

(132)

This operator acts on the local terms of the Hamiltonian
H_{\text{SSB}}^{(0)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(i)}(C\overrightarrow{X})^{\dagger}_{i,j}&=\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(i)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}~,\\
(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(i)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}(C\overrightarrow{X})^{\dagger}_{i,j}&=\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(i)}~,\\
(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}(C\overrightarrow{X})^{\dagger}_{i,j}&=\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}~.\end{split}

(133)

In a periodic chain with length
L
, let us consider the following sequential circuit

\displaystyle U_{CX}=(C\overrightarrow{X})_{L-1,L}\dots(C\overrightarrow{X})_{2,3}(C\overrightarrow{X})_{1,2}~.

(134)

By using the transformation rule of local Hamiltonians, one can see that
H_{\text{SSB}}^{(0)}
is transformed as

\displaystyle U_{CX}H_{\text{SSB}}^{(0)}U_{CX}^{\dagger}=-\sum_{g\in G}(\overleftarrow{X}^{(L)}_{g}\overrightarrow{X}^{(L)}_{g})\prod_{j=1}^{L-1}\overrightarrow{X}^{(j)}_{g}-\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\sum_{g\in G}\overleftarrow{X}^{(j)}_{g}~,

(135)

where the local Hamiltonian
\overleftarrow{X}_{g}^{(L)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(1)}
is transformed into the first nonlocal term, while the other Hamiltonians are transformed into onsite operators. Since the transformed Hamiltonian stabilizes a trivial product state in the bulk, the ground state after the sequential transformation becomes a product state in the form of

\displaystyle U_{CX}\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{L-1}\left(\sum_{g_{j}\in G}\ket{g_{j}}\right)\otimes\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}}_{L}~,

(136)

where
\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}}_{L}
is a local state at the site
L
which minimizes the Hamiltonian
\sum_{g\in G}(\overleftarrow{X}^{(L)}_{g}\overrightarrow{X}^{(L)}_{g})
.
Such a local ground state is labeled by a conjugacy class
C\in C(G)
. For
C\in C(G)
, it is given by

\displaystyle\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}(C)}_{L}=\sum_{g\in C}\ket{g}~.

(137)

Therefore the ground state degeneracy of
H_{\text{SSB}}^{(0)}
is given by
|C(G)|=32
. We therefore label the ground states of
H^{(0)}_{\text{SSB}}
by a conjugacy class
\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}(C)}
.

The symmetry operator is mapped into an onsite operator at the site
L
,

\displaystyle U_{CX}W_{\Gamma}U_{CX}^{\dagger}=\sum_{g_{L}}\text{tr}(\Gamma(g_{L}))\ket{g_{L}}\bra{g_{L}}~.

(138)

Therefore, the symmetry action on each ground state is given by a group character

\displaystyle W_{\Gamma}\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}(C)}=\chi_{\Gamma}(C)\ket{\text{GS}^{(0)}(C)}~.

(139)

This generalizes the symmetric cat state of SSB phases with finite invertible symmetries.

The same sequential circuit can be used to trivialize the other SSB Hamiltonian
H^{(\alpha)}_{\text{SSB}}
associated with the nontrivial element of Bogomolov multiplier
\alpha\in B(G)
. The circuit transforms the local Hamiltonians in the following manner:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}(Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g})^{(i)}(C\overrightarrow{X})^{\dagger}_{i,j}&=(Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g})^{(i)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}^{(j)}~,\\
(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}(\overleftarrow{X}_{g}Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(i)}(Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)}\overrightarrow{X}_{g})^{(j)}(C\overrightarrow{X})^{\dagger}_{i,j}&=(Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)}\overleftarrow{X}_{g}Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(i)}(Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)})^{(j)}~,\\
(C\overrightarrow{X})_{i,j}(\overleftarrow{X}_{g}Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(j)}(C\overrightarrow{X})^{\dagger}_{i,j}&=(Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(i)}(\overleftarrow{X}_{g}Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(j)}~.\\
\end{split}

(140)

By the sequential circuit
U_{CX}
followed by a constant-depth circuit

\displaystyle U_{CZ}=\prod_{j=1}^{L-1}(CZ)^{a,b}_{j,j}(CZ)^{a,b}_{j,j+1}~,

(141)

the Hamiltonian
H_{\text{SSB}}^{(\alpha)}
is transformed into

\displaystyle U_{CZ}U_{CX}H_{\text{SSB}}^{(\alpha)}U_{CX}^{\dagger}U_{CZ}^{\dagger}=-\sum_{g\in G}(\overleftarrow{X}_{g}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)}Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(L)}\prod_{j=1}^{L-1}\overrightarrow{X}^{(j)}_{g}-\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\sum_{g\in G}\overleftarrow{X}^{(j)}_{g}~.

(142)

The bulk Hamiltonian again becomes onsite after the sequential transformation, so the ground state is again expressed as a product state

\displaystyle U_{CZ}U_{CX}\ket{\text{GS}^{(\alpha)}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{L-1}\left(\sum_{g_{j}\in G}\ket{g_{j}}\right)\otimes\ket{\text{GS}^{(\alpha)}}_{L}~,

(143)

where
\ket{\text{GS}^{(\alpha)}}_{L}
is a ground state of a single site Hamiltonian
\sum_{g\in G}(\overleftarrow{X}_{g}\overrightarrow{X}_{g}Z_{b}^{n_{a}(g)}Z_{a}^{n_{b}(g)})^{(L)}
.

To count the number of ground states

IV.4.1
Review: Soft symmetry of (2+1)D topological order

In this section, we discuss the role of the Bogomolov multiplier in (2+1)D topological order.
An element of group cohomology
\alpha\in H^{2}(G,U(1))
generally corresponds to an ordinary (0-form) global symmetry of (2+1)D

gauge theory, dubbed a
gauged SPT
symmetry
Barkeshli2023codimension
;
Barkeshli:2022edm
;
Barkeshli:2023bta
;
Hsin:2019fhf
;
Yoshida2016generalized
. This can be understood as follows.
Let us consider a (2+1)D trivial gapped phase with
G
symmetry. Let us take a 1d submanifold of this 2d trivial phase, and place a (1+1)D
G
SPT phase specified by
\alpha\in H^{2}(G,U(1))
along this 1d locus. This can be done by acting the SPT entangler supported on the 1d submanifold. We then gauge the
G
symmetry of the whole 2d system. After gauging the symmetry, the system becomes a (2+1)D topological order given by the
G
gauge theory, and the decoration of (1+1)D SPT phase becomes an insertion of a symmetry defect in the
G
gauge theory. This construction explains how the element of
H^{2}(G,U(1))
defines a symmetry of
G
gauge theory in (2+1)D. See Fig.
3
.

The anyons in the
G
gauge theory in (2+1)D are labeled by a pair
([g],\pi)
, where
[g]\in C(G)
and
\pi\in\text{Rep}(Z(g))
is an irreducible (linear) representation of
Z(g)
.
The first label corresponds to the magnetic flux, and the second corresponds to the electric charge of
Z(g)
attached to it. The gauged SPT symmetry acts on the labels of anyons by

\displaystyle([g],\pi)\to([g],\pi\times\varphi_{g})

(174)

with
\varphi_{g}(h)=\alpha(g,h)/\alpha(h,g)
.
That is, the symmetry shifts the electric charge attached to the magnetic flux.

An example of such a gauged SPT symmetry is found in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory in (2+1)D, which is two copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
toric codes. Let us consider the nontrivial element
\alpha\in H^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},U(1))
. The corresponding gauged SPT operator permutes the anyon as

\displaystyle m_{1}\to m_{1}e_{2},\quad m_{2}\to m_{2}e_{1},\quad e_{1}\to e_{1},\quad e_{2}\to e_{2}~.

(175)

Let us now consider an element
\alpha\in B(G)
of the Bogomolov multiplier. This leads to a global symmetry of (2+1)D
G
gauge theory with the following exotic properties:

•

The symmetry does not permute labels of anyons
Davydov2014
. This is because every
g\in G
is
\alpha
-regular, so
\varphi_{g}
becomes trivial for any
g\in G
, meaning that the anyon permutation in (
174
) becomes trivial.
Therefore, the symmetry acts trivially on the ground state Hilbert space on a 2d torus.

•

The symmetry fractionalization is also trivial, i.e., the anyons do not carry fractional charges under the symmetry.

•

Nevertheless, the symmetry is still faithful; it acts on a ground state Hilbert space of higher genus surface by a nontrivial operator. While the symmetry do not permute the labels of anyons, the symmetry acts on the fusion vertices of the anyons, where a pair of anyons fuses into a single one at the junction.

The global symmetry of (2+1)D topological order with the above properties is referred to as a
soft symmetry
