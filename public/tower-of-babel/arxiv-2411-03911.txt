The crucial discovery of thermonuclear X-ray bursts: never throw away old data! Publication type: Article Type
Erik Kuulkers*

Abstract
The detection of Type I X-ray bursts is attributed to those seen by the Astronomical Netherlands Satellite (ANS) in September 1975 from the globular cluster NGC 6624 containing the X-ray source 4U 1820 − - 303. I revisit these X-ray bursts, by re-analysing data from the Soft X-ray Experiment (SXX) onboard ANS, which were stored on microfiche. Earlier accounts of X-ray bursts had been reported; the first Type I X-ray burst recorded is the one observed by Vela 5B from Cen X-4 in July 1969.

Introduction

Canonical Type I X-ray bursts
(
Hoffman \BOthers. \APACyear1978
)
result from thermonuclear shell flashes on a neutron star, which is caused by the ignition of either He and/or H-rich material supplied by a binary companion star (
Hansen \BBA van Horn \APACyear1975
;
Woosley \BBA Taam \APACyear1976
;
Maraschi \BBA Cavaliere \APACyear1977
;
Lamb \BBA Lamb \APACyear1978
)
1
1

1

For reviews on Type I X-ray bursts, see, e.g., (
W\BPBIH\BPBIG. Lewin \BOthers. \APACyear1993
;
Strohmayer \BBA Bildsten \APACyear2006
;
Galloway \BBA Keek \APACyear2021
.
. These bursts generally appear as short transient events wherein the X-ray intensity rises rapidly on a time scale of seconds, and decays in an exponential fashion back to the pre-burst level. The decay lasts almost always longer than the rise. Burst durations range from several seconds up to half an hour.
The energy spectra during these events can be fitted with a black-body model with temperatures of the order of
\sim
1 keV
with an emission size of about 10 km, consistent with a neutron star
(
Swank \BOthers. \APACyear1978
)
.
Generally, the burst energy spectra harden during the rise and peak, and soften during the decay.

2
Astronomical Netherlands Satellite: ANS

2.1
ANS instrumentation

The first extra-Solar source discovered in X-rays was Scorpius X-1, during a rocket flight in 1962
(
Giacconi \BOthers. \APACyear1962
)
.
Starting with the first dedicated X-ray astronomy satellite, Uhuru
(
Giacconi \BOthers. \APACyear1971
, e.g., )
, many satellites in the 1970’s with X-ray detectors were mostly scanning satellites.
The Astronomical Netherlands Satellite (ANS), launched on 30 August 1974, was the first 3-axis stabilized X-ray satellite, and so could point to sources for longer stretches of time compared to previous satellites
(
Bloemendal \BBA Kramer \APACyear1973
)
. ANS was active from 1974–1976.

ANS had three instruments onboard:

•

Soft X-ray Experiment
(
Brinkman \BOthers. \APACyear1974
;
den Boggende \BBA Lafleur \APACyear1975
, SXX; )
. It consisted of a grazing-incidence paraboloidal-mirror with
a proportional counter in the focal plane covering the energy range around 0.25 keV and a proportional counter
sensitive at 0.3–0.5 keV and 1–7 keV. The time resolution ranged from 0.125 s (1 energy band) to 16 s (7 energy channels)

•

Hard X-ray Experiment
(
Gursky \BOthers. \APACyear1975
, HXX; )
. It consisted of a large area detector (LAD)
unit for measuring 1–30 keV X-rays, and a Bragg-crystal spectrograph tuned for detection of the silicon lines.

•

UV-telescope sensitive at 150–330 nm
(
van Duinen \BOthers. \APACyear1975
)
.

2.2
ANS observations of 4U 1820
-
303

During a rocket flight in 1974 the black-hole X-ray binary Cygnus X-1 showed msec bursts
(
Rothschild \BOthers. \APACyear1974
)
.
At the time,
Bahcall \BBA Ostriker \APACyear1975
suggested that the globular cluster NGC 6624 contained a central black hole of mass
\sim
100–1000 M
⊙
based on the soft X-ray spectrum and variability.
It was postulated that if a Solar-mass black hole shows msec bursts, a super-massive black hole of
\sim
1000 M
⊙
might show second-long bursts (see
Heise \APACyear2010
).
It was thus decided to spend time with ANS on observing NGC 6624, which contained the X-ray source 4U 1820
-
303, detected earlier with Uhuru
(
Giacconi \BOthers. \APACyear1974
)
.
4U 1820
-
303 is nowadays known as a low-mass X-ray binary at a distance of about 8 kpc with an an orbital period of only 11.4 min.
It contains a 0.06–-0.07 M
⊙
helium white dwarf with a neutron star (see, e.g.,
Yu \BOthers. \APACyear2024
, and references therein).

Various ANS observations towards NGC 6624/4U 1820
-
30 were done in September 1974, and March and September 1975.
During observations on 28 September 1975 (see Figs. 2 and 3), two X-ray bursts were detected
(
Grindlay \BBA Heise \APACyear1975
;
Grindlay \BOthers. \APACyear1976
)
.
Each displayed a rapid rise in flux (
<
1 s) by a factor of 20–30 followed by a
\sim
8 s exponential decay.
A fit to the HXX burst data with a thermal spectrum revealed a black-body temperature of
kT=5(+3,-2)
keV at the peak of the burst, while during the decay it became
kT=20(\pm 8)
keV.
Adopting a distance of 10 kpc, the X-ray luminosity increased from
\sim
3
\times
10
37
erg s
-1
to
\sim
1
\times
10
39
erg s
-1
. The total burst energy was
\sim
2
\times
10
39
erg
(
Grindlay \BOthers. \APACyear1976
)
.

The decay portion and progressive spectral hardening of the ANS bursts from NGC 6624 had been interpreted as a result of scattering of a brief primary burst of relatively
soft X-rays by a surrounding hot-plasma cloud (
Grindlay \BBA Gursky \APACyear1976\APACexlab\BCnt1
;
Canizares \APACyear1976
;
Grindlay \APACyear1978
). The X-rays would originate from the vicinity of a supermassive 1000 M
⊙
black hole,
and thus confirming the supermassive black-hole hypothesis in NGC 6624. Other explanations were also put forward (see, e.g.,
Lewin \BBA Joss \APACyear1981
, and references therein).

However, it was soon realized that these X-ray bursts are actually due to thermonuclear runaways on a neutron star (see Sect. 1).
The occurrence of X-ray bursts in 4U 1820
-
303 depends on the state of the binary: X-ray bursts cease when its X-ray intensity transits from the so-called low to the hard state

(
Clark \BOthers. \APACyear1977
)
2

X-ray states correspond to different accretion regimes, i.e., the accretion rate is thought to increase from low to high state.
.
Indeed, as judged from the Vela 5B (3–12 keV) data
(
Priedhorsky \BBA Terrell \APACyear1984
)
, the September 1975 ANS observations were taken during a low state.

The X-ray satellite SAS-3 was launched in May 1975. After the report of the events seen by ANS, bursts of X-rays, recurrent at nearly equal intervals,
were detected in data obtained during an observation of 4U 1820
-
303 on 17–19 May 1975.
The properties were similar to that seen by ANS. The average X-ray intensity of 4U 1820
-
303 during these observations was also low during the X-ray bursts
(
Clark \BOthers. \APACyear1976
)
.

If the bursts seen by ANS are indeed Type I X-ray bursts, the apparent increase of
kT
seems to be a problem.
The expected cooling during decay of a Type I X-ray burst is difficult to prove with the SXX data, because of lack of statistics.
The X-ray bursts may need a reanalysis; the instrument response info is still available in paper form (e.g.,
den Boggende \APACyear1979
).
Also, a comparison with those bursts seen later on with more sensitive instruments is warranted.

The SXX data has been preserved on 157 microfiches; each microfiche contains basically 208 pages of ASCII data.
During November 1975 to February 1976, 1800 tapes with the ANS data of all earlier passages were re-processed, to profit from the improvements in the processing software; these were labeled ”re-run” data
(
Lamers \APACyear2016
)
.
At the end of 2023 all microfiches were scanned and OCR-ed; see Fig.

for an example. The OCR-ed results of the relevant data were manually inspected and corrected.
To date, the data from the HXX are not anymore available.

Are those seen by ANS the first X-ray bursts?

Bursts with timescales of seconds were discovered using data from two Vela-5 spy satellites designed to detect nuclear tests (Klebesadel et al. 1973); we now know that of most these are related to gamma-ray bursts (GRBs).
However,
Belian \BOthers. \APACyear1972
had reported a burst in X-rays on 7 July 1969 with Vela 5B coming from the X-ray transient Cen X-4, with a duration of about 10 min.
It showed a fast rise and a power-law decay. The hardness ratio (6–12 keV over 3–6 keV) softened during the decay.
The burst emission could be modeled with a Planckian spectrum, with
kT\simeq 2.6
keV at the peak and
kT\simeq 1.3
keV during the decay, consistent with the observed softening.
At the peak, the burst reached a flux which was 60 times brighter than that seen from Crab (!), corresponding to a luminosity of about 2
\times
10
38
erg s
-1
(at the source distance of 1.2 kpc;
Kuulkers \BOthers. \APACyear2009
).
The total energy release was about 5
\times
10
39
erg (at 1.2 kpc; Matsuoka et al. 1980). The X-ray burst was interpreted by Belian et al. (1972) as an accretion event.
However, although longer than the typical Type I X-ray burst, it resembles the so-called intermediate-duration Type I X-ray bursts (see, e.g.,
Alizai \BOthers. \APACyear2023
, and references therein).

A subset of the Vela-5 bursts were concentrated in the region of the Norma constellation
(
Belian \BOthers. \APACyear1976
)
; they probably originated from the X-ray bursters 4U 1608
-
52 and/or from 4U 1636
-
536
(
Lewin \BBA Joss \APACyear1981
)
.
Re-analysis of Uhuru data between 1970 and 1973 revealed four burst events (in December 1971 and May 1972) which also originated from the same region in Norma (
Grindlay \BBA Gursky \APACyear1976\APACexlab\BCnt3
;
Grindlay \BBA Gursky \APACyear1976\APACexlab\BCnt2
).
No enhanced emission was seen during Uhuru transits either before or after the events.
Two of the Uhuru events show a rise time of
<
2 s, spectral hardening with time, and peak intensity comparable to
that of the Crab. For the other two events no evidence for a spectral change during the burst was observed.

It was soon found out that comparable short bursts like those seen by ANS had been reported from observations by the Russian Kosmos 428 satellite in June 1971 (
Babushkina\BCBL \BOthers. \APACyear1975a
;
Babushkina\BCBL \BOthers. \APACyear1975b
).
While these bursts were very similar to the ANS X-ray bursts in their time
profile and spectral hardening, their much higher (factor 10
-
100) energy flux and
detected energies originally suggested a closer relationship to GRBs
(
Grindlay \APACyear1976
)
.
Indeed, the X-ray instrument on-board Kosmos 428 was sensitive between 40–190 keV. Since Type I X-ray bursts reach temperatures up to
kT\sim 3
keV, they will not show up typically above about 30 keV (see, e.g.,
Kuulkers \BOthers. \APACyear2010
).
They are nowadays not believed to be of cosmic origin, but may, e.g., be caused by the Earth’s magnetosphere
Lewin \APACyear1977

An X-ray burst with duration of 15 s was detected on 19 September 1972 by Uhuru, coming from the globular cluster NGC 1851
(
Jones \BBA Forman \APACyear1976
)
, which contains the known X-ray burster MX 0513
-

The X-ray intensity of the source was relatively constant, although the spectrum was observed to harden significantly.

Two (possibly four) soft X-ray events were observed by the Apollo 15 (August 1971) and Apollo 16 (April 1972) X-ray spectrometers during the trans-Earth coast (
Gorenstein \BOthers. \APACyear1974
). Their origin is not clear, but one event may be an X-ray burst (see
Kuulkers \BOthers. \APACyear2009

Note that a GRB was observed by the gamma-ray and X-ray spectrometers on Apollo 16 as well as by Vela 6A on 27 April 1972
(
Metzger \BOthers. \APACyear1974
;
Trombka \BOthers. \APACyear1974
)
.

Based on the literature, the event seen by Vela 5B in July 1969
(
Belian \BOthers. \APACyear1972
)
, is the earliest record of a (Type I) X-ray burst.

4
Conclusions

The 1970’s data are (not only) of historical value; old X-ray data can still be
a rich resource many decades after. They enable us to compare results with those of much later
measurements and interpret them in light of what we know nowadays. The data from the 1970’s extend the long-term baseline for characterizing state change and burst behaviour in sources like 4U 1820
-
303.

The first Type I X-ray burst ever observed is the one from Cen X-4, recorded on 7 July 1969 by the Vela 5B satellite.

Last but not least: never throw away old data; (legacy) archives are important!
