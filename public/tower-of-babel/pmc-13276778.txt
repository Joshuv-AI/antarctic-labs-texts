A case of complete renal pedicle avulsion with deceptively stable hemodynamics: Critical diagnostic pitfall of misdiagnosis as ectopic kidney
Authors: Xinji Yang, Herong Zhu, Xiaofei Hou, Ke Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a 42-year-old female with AAST Grade V renal trauma from blunt crush injury, who presented with hemodynamic stability despite surgically confirmed complete renal pedicle avulsion. Initial CT mimicked congenital ectopic kidney, risking misdiagnosis. The patient underwent emergency nephrectomy with favorable outcome. We elucidate the pathophysiology of this paradoxical phenomenon, and highlight its diagnostic pitfalls and management principles.

Introduction

Renal trauma is a life-threatening clinical emergency caused by blunt or penetrating mechanisms, with injury severity classified by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) Renal Injury Scale (Grades I-V).1 The 2018 update of this scale further standardized the classification system to optimize severity assessment and clinical management of renal trauma.1 Grade V injuries represent the most severe category, including main renal pedicle avulsion, devascularized kidney, and shattered kidney, and are associated with significant morbidity and mortality.2

Complete renal pedicle avulsion is an extremely severe subtype of Grade V renal injury. Given that renal blood flow reaches approximately 600-700 mL/min/1.73 m,2,3 complete transection of the renal pedicle is traditionally believed to inevitably cause rapid massive hemorrhage, hemorrhagic shock, and high mortality within a short timeframe. Consequently, survivors of renal pedicle avulsion are rare, with few cases reported in the literature. However, emerging clinical observations have identified a paradoxical and easily overlooked phenomenon: patients with radiologically and surgically confirmed complete renal pedicle avulsion may present with stable systemic hemodynamics upon admission, a presentation that deviates drastically from classical pathophysiological expectations.4 This deceptive stability often leads to delayed diagnosis and misjudgment of injury severity, posing a major threat to patient survival.

Meanwhile, traumatically displaced kidney secondary to renal pedicle avulsion may present with ectopic renal location on imaging, which is highly susceptible to misdiagnosis as congenital pelvic ectopic kidney, especially in hemodynamically stable patients without typical severe trauma manifestations. Herein, we report a case of complete renal pedicle avulsion with sustained hemodynamic stability, which was initially suspected to be pelvic ectopic kidney on imaging. We systematically analyze the underlying mechanisms of this clinical paradox, summarize key differential diagnostic points, and highlight critical lessons for diagnosis and operative decision-making.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old woman presented to the general surgery emergency department 4 hours after sustaining a mechanical crush injury to her left abdomen. The patient complained of left lower abdominal tenderness and back pain. Of note, despite sustaining a high-energy crush injury with subsequent surgically confirmed complete renal pedicle avulsion, the patient remained hemodynamically stable on presentation, with a blood pressure of 102/61 mmHg, heart rate of 84 bpm, and oxygen saturation of 98%. Neurological examination was intact, and physical examination only revealed left flank tenderness without visible external trauma. Laboratory investigations revealed moderate anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 7.8 g/dL.

Initial abdominopelvic CT demonstrated an ectopically located left kidney in the lower abdomen, along with a retroperitoneal hematoma (Fig. 1). Subsequent rib CT identified bilateral multiple rib fractures. The patient was urgently transferred to the urology department, where a provisional diagnosis of AAST Grade V renal trauma with renal pedicle avulsion was made, although pelvic ectopic kidney could not be excluded. Intraoperatively, a resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) was placed by the endovascular surgery team as a prophylactic measure for potential uncontrolled hemorrhage. A midline laparotomy was performed, extending from the xiphoid process to 5 cm below the umbilicus, with a left transverse extension toward the anterior axillary line. Exploration revealed a large retroperitoneal hematoma surrounding a displaced left kidney. The renal pedicle was completely avulsed, and the renal artery exhibited a spiral-type trifurcation rupture with localized thrombosis. After nephrectomy and evacuation of approximately 800 mL of clotted blood, the REBOA balloon was removed. The procedure lasted 218 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 100 mL and a total transfusion volume of 1200 mL. Histopathological examination confirmed traumatic renal rupture. The patient recovered well and was transferred to the thoracic surgery department on postoperative day 8 for further management of rib fractures.

Discussion

This case highlights two critical challenges of complete renal pedicle avulsion: paradoxical hemodynamic stability despite a traditionally assumed fatal injury, and the high risk of misdiagnosing the traumatically displaced kidney as congenital pelvic ectopic kidney. Three synergistic pathophysiological mechanisms explain the transient hemostasis that permits this deceptive stability. First, blunt trauma triggers sympathetic activation and local myogenic reflexes, inducing profound vasospasm that markedly reduces or temporarily occludes the ruptured vessel lumen. The spiral trifurcation rupture observed here amplifies this effect, as the torn arterial ends retract and constrict.5,6 Second, full-thickness pedicle avulsion exposes subendothelial collagen and tissue factor, rapidly activating the coagulation cascade and platelet aggregation to form a stable thrombus at the transected stump, which was directly confirmed intraoperatively. Third, the confined retroperitoneal space allows accumulating blood to generate a tamponade effect, raising local tissue pressure to systemic systolic levels and compressing the torn vascular ends. Collectively, vasospasm, local thrombosis, and retroperitoneal tamponade create a fragile “hemostatic window” that can abruptly fail if vasospasm resolves, thrombus dislodges, or the hematoma is disrupted.4

This hemodynamic stability creates a dangerous diagnostic pitfall: the displaced kidney may be mistaken for a congenital pelvic ectopic kidney, delaying life-saving intervention. Congenital pelvic ectopic kidneys arise from disrupted embryonic ascent, typically lack an acute trauma history, and demonstrate preserved perfusion on contrast-enhanced imaging.7 In contrast, traumatic displacement from pedicle avulsion presents with a clear high-energy trauma history, and CTA reveals renal vessel discontinuity, absent distal perfusion, and a perirenal/retroperitoneal hematoma. Therefore, for any patient with an ectopically located kidney on post-traumatic imaging, even with completely normal vital signs, urgent contrast-enhanced CT with CTA is mandatory to exclude pedicle avulsion.8 Relying on hemodynamic stability as a marker of injury severity can be fatal in this setting, as life-threatening vascular injury may be occult.9

In this case, REBOA was prophylactically placed but not utilized, as the same mechanisms that preserved hemodynamic stability had already temporarily controlled bleeding. REBOA is a minimally invasive technique for non-compressible torso hemorrhage, bridging the time to definitive surgical control.10 Current EAST guidelines support its use as an alternative to resuscitative thoracotomy in traumatic cardiac arrest from subdiaphragmatic hemorrhage and in selected high-risk patients.11,12 This case prompts reconsideration of routine prophylactic REBOA in hemodynamically stable renal pedicle avulsion: preoperative placement may not be essential and could delay definitive renal pedicle control, though this requires validation in larger series. Nevertheless, multidisciplinary preparation for sudden massive hemorrhage, including standby REBOA capability, remains mandatory.

Current EAU guidelines recommend nonoperative management (NOM) for most Grade I-IV injuries, and emerging evidence supports NOM for selected hemodynamically stable Grade IV-V injuries. Grade IV injuries can be substratified into low-risk IVa and high-risk IVb, with IVb carrying a 25-fold higher odds of intervention.13,14 Systematic review data indicate that first-line NOM for stable Grade IV-V injuries achieves high renal preservation with comparable complication rates to open surgical exploration (OSE).15 However, complete renal pedicle avulsion remains an absolute contraindication for NOM and mandates emergent surgery, regardless of hemodynamic stability. The rationale is twofold: transient hemostasis is inherently unpredictable and can fail catastrophically, and complete devascularization causes irreversible renal infarction, with only 2.8% renal function preservation reported for conservatively managed Grade V pedicle injuries.16,17 Renal vascular embolization is also contraindicated in complete pedicle avulsion.18 In this case, emergent laparotomy with nephrectomy and pedicle ligation was performed without intraoperative or postoperative catastrophic hemorrhage, confirming the safety and necessity of this approach.

Conclusions

This case establishes that complete renal pedicle avulsion may present with deceptively stable hemodynamics due to a transient hemostatic triad of vasospasm, local thrombosis, and retroperitoneal tamponade. Simultaneously, the traumatically displaced kidney can be misdiagnosed as congenital pelvic ectopic kidney, a critical pitfall that delays intervention. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for pedicle injury in all high-energy blunt abdominal or flank trauma, even with normal vital signs, and urgent CTA is mandatory to differentiate traumatic displacement from congenital ectopia. While NOM is appropriate for selected stable high-grade injuries, complete pedicle avulsion demands emergent surgical exploration irrespective of hemodynamic status due to the risk of sudden fatal hemorrhage and irreversible renal function loss. The role of prophylactic REBOA in this specific population remains to be defined, and routine preoperative placement may not be necessary, though multidisciplinary hemorrhage preparedness is essential. Survival ultimately hinges on recognizing the limitations of hemodynamic stability as a triage marker and executing rapid surgical control before irreversible collapse.
