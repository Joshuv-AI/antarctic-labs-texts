Genetic ALS—Progressive Sleep Defects Precede the Onset of Clinical Disease
Authors: Christina Lang, Simon J. Guillot, Antje Knehr, Patrick Weydt, Johannes Dorst, Hayrettin Tumani, Albert C. Ludolph, Luc Dupuis, Matei Bolborea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe onset of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is preceded by non‐motor signs detectable many years before motor symptom onset. We recently showed that early ALS patients and presymptomatic carriers of ALS‐associated gene mutations display altered sleep macroarchitecture, characterised by increased wakefulness and decreased non‐REM sleep, together with altered microarchitecture, particularly reduced slow oscillations and sleep spindles. Here we aimed at determining whether sleep alterations are progressive in premanifest carriers of ALS‐associated gene mutations.MethodsThis longitudinal observational cohort study included presymptomatic first‐degree relatives of ALS patients carrying pathogenic gene mutations (n = 18) and noncarrier family members (n = 3). Sleep macroarchitecture and sleep microarchitecture were assessed using standardised polysomnography and electroencephalographic analyses at baseline and in a follow‐up examination after 2.3 ± 1.5 years.ResultsIn all carriers, sleep alterations were more severe at follow‐up compared to the initial examination, for both macro‐ and microarchitecture. The progression of sleep alterations was seemingly independent of proximity to motor onset, whether evaluated by increased neurofilament levels or phenoconversion during the study period.ConclusionsSleep macro‐ and microarchitectural abnormalities progressively worsen during the premanifest phase of ALS. These findings suggest that polysomnography‐derived sleep measures and their neurochemical correlates may serve as sensitive, noninvasive surrogate biomarkers of disease progression in clinically silent ALS. This could support disease prevention, for example, by the antisense oligonucleotide tofersen in patients carrying SOD1 mutations.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is the most common adult‐onset motor neuron disease, resulting from combined degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons and leading to progressive paralysis and premature death, typically three to four years after the onset of motor symptoms. Onset usually occurs between 60 and 70 years of age [1, 2] and most cases are sporadic, without a known family history. Between 5 and 10% of ALS cases are caused by mutations in more than 40 distinct genes, usually through autosomal dominant inheritance. In Europe, the most frequent mutations are found in C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS genes [1, 2].

In ALS gene carriers, definite motor symptom onset is usually preceded by a prodromal period that may include mild motor impairment. During this prodromal period, an increase in circulating neurofilament levels, a reliable biomarker of axonal injury, has been reported [3, 4]. Before this highly variable prodromal period, presymptomatic gene carriers do not show any motor impairment either clinically or through biomarker evidence [4]. However, many studies indicate that these individuals already exhibit non‐motor signs, such as weight loss [5] or cognitive impairment [6], many years before motor symptom onset, during the premanifest period of the disease. Whether these early non‐motor signs worsen as individuals approach disease onset remains unclear, largely due to the lack of longitudinal studies in presymptomatic gene carriers.

We recently demonstrated that sleep is impaired in ALS, both in the clinically manifest period and in premanifest gene carriers. Specifically, prominent defects in sleep macroarchitecture were observed and characterised by increased wake and decreased deep sleep (NREM2/3) [7]. These defects were present in symptomatic patients in the absence of detectable respiratory insufficiency. They were accompanied by marked defects in microarchitecture, particularly decreased slow oscillations and sleep spindles [8]. Importantly, similar impairments were observed in presymptomatic gene carriers many years before manifest disease. These sleep alterations correlated with cognitive scores [7], and both macro‐ and microarchitectural changes were also observed in multiple mouse models of familial ALS [7, 8].

A major question arising from these initial studies is whether sleep defects—macro‐ or microarchitecture—remain stable as individuals approach disease onset, or whether they worsen, suggesting an association with rampant disease progression. This could make a prediction of disease onset possible. In this study, we performed longitudinal follow‐up polysomnography in 18 presymptomatic ALS gene carriers and in three family members with negative genetic testing previously included in our initial studies.

Methods and Materials

Design

Longitudinal prospective observational study.

Setting and Participants

All participants in this study are first‐degree relatives of fALS patients with an ALS‐causing mutation confirmed by genetic testing who were seen at the outpatient clinic for motor neuron diseases or in the neurology department of Ulm University Hospital between 2019 and 2024. This cohort has been previously described [7, 8]. Relatives of these patients were informed of the opportunity to participate in the study. Inclusion criteria were: Age 18 years or older and being a first‐degree relative of a confirmed fALS case. The exclusion criterion was the presence of a neurodegenerative disease or other serious illness, as well as a relationship to another study participant; further, an Apnea‐Hypopnoe‐Index (AHI) greater than 20 per hour or a Periodic‐Limb‐Movement‐Index greater than 20 per hour in the baseline polysomnography led to the exclusion of the participant. Study participants did not undergo genetic testing at the time of inclusion in the study. Genetic testing was performed purely for scientific purposes and for later classification. Participants who wished to undergo official predictive genetic testing had to do so outside the study, after the first study visit, in accordance with the provisions of the German Genetic Diagnosis Act.

Medical history and current medication use were documented as part of the clinical assessment. Individuals with relevant medical or psychiatric conditions or medication use that, in the opinion of the investigators, could substantially interfere with sleep assessment or study participation were not included. Phenoconversion was defined as the occurrence of clinically evident ALS‐related signs or symptoms in a previously presymptomatic gene carrier, supported by electromyographic findings. Biomarker abnormalities, including NfL levels, were not used to define phenoconversion.

Ethical standards

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised version) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Ulm University (approval number: 68/19).

Electroencephalography in Subjects

All participants underwent two visits, each including a one‐night full polysomnography according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines [9], with monitoring of various physiological parameters, including electroencephalogram (EEG), surface electromyogram (EMG), electrooculogram (EOG), electrocardiogram (ECG), respiratory effort and flow, pulse, and oxygen saturation. EEG electrodes were placed according to the international 10–20 system. The following electrodes were used in each subject: Fz, C3, C4, Cz, P3, P4, Pz, O1, O2, A1, and A2. The sampling rate was 512 Hz in each case. A “lights off” marker was placed in each recording to indicate when the participant turned off the lights and attempted to sleep. Participants were free to choose a start time between 8:00 p.m. and midnight. The measurement ended at 6:00 a.m. Two volunteers underwent an additional third polysomnography under the same protocol.

EEG Preprocessing

Analyses were performed using available Python packages (Python Software Foundation. Python Language Reference, version 3.12. Available at http://www.python.org) relying on the MNE package. EEG preprocessing followed established protocols [7, 8]. Recordings were first de‐identified using the open‐source Prerau Lab EDF De‐identification Tool (Version 1.0; 2023) in Python (Prerau Lab EDF De‐identification Tool retrieved from https://sleepeeg.org/edf‐de‐identification‐tool), concealing mutation and visit number prior to further analysis. Signals were then notch‐filtered to remove the 50 Hz power line. Independent component analysis was applied to remove residual artefacts, which were then excluded upon visual inspection while being blinded to genotype and visit number, based on standard criteria (ocular topography, muscle‐related high‐frequency power), as described [7, 8].

Analysis of Sleep Macroarchitecture

Analyses focused on the sensorimotor cortices (C3 and C4), which are known to be impaired at disease onset, as well as nearby interhemispheric sulci (Fz, Cz and Pz). Sleep staging was performed on a 6‐h window with 30‐s epochs, starting at lights off, using the YASA deep learning algorithm (v0.6.5) [10] and spectral analysis. Time in bed and total sleep time were calculated across the full recording. Automated sleep staging, hypnograms, and spectrograms were generated using Welch's method as described [7, 8]. Sleep pressure was quantified using the area under the curve (AUC) of delta power (0.5–4 Hz) of the first hour of the 6‐h window, computed using Simpson's rule. REM efficiency was calculated as the ratio of theta power (4–8 Hz) to delta power (0.5–4 Hz) during REM epochs. Sleep staging and analysis followed AASM guidelines [9].

Analyses of Sleep Microarchitectural Events

Analyses were performed as in our previous study [8]. Briefly, sleep spindles, slow oscillations, and K‐complexes were analysed across all electrodes, with outliers removed using an isolation forest algorithm [8]. K‐complex analysis was restricted to the sensorimotor cortex (C3), which is known to be impaired in ALS, using the MNE and SciPy packages [8]. Sleep spindle density was defined as the number of sleep spindles per minute of NREM2 sleep. Slow oscillation density was defined as the number of slow oscillations per minute of NREM2 sleep. K‐complex density was defined as the number of K‐complexes per minute of NREM2 sleep. All analyses were performed in accordance with AASM guidelines [9].

Measurement of Neurofilament Light Chain Levels

Blood samples were collected by venous sampling. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min, and the extracted serum was aliquoted and frozen within 30 min at –80°C. Serum samples were stored in polypropylene tubes. For NF‐L quantification in serum, the microfluidic Ella platform (Bio‐Techne, Minneapolis, USA) was used. The analyses were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions [11].

Statistical Analysis

Prior to any statistical analysis, normality and homoscedasticity were tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Bartlett's test, respectively. Follow‐up analyses were performed using paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon‐Mann–Whitney rank‐sum tests when normality was not met.

For longitudinal analyses, each sleep variable was modelled using a linear mixed‐effects framework, with visit (visit 1 vs. visit 2) and interval between visits as fixed effects, and case treated as a random factor to account for repeated measures. Analyses were performed separately for the C9orf72 group, SOD1 group, other mutations, and the full cohort.

In all cases, p value were adjusted using FDR‐BKY correction. Data are presented as violin plots with each value displayed as a data point and expressed as medians ± interquartile range. Visualisations were generated using Seaborn and Matplotlib packages. Results were deemed significant at adjusted p value < 0.05, and only corrected pvalues (adj. p value) are reported.

Results

In the original baseline study, 61 participants were examined, including 35 gene carriers and 26 non‐carriers from the family. From this cohort, 15 participants were excluded from the present longitudinal analysis based on the predefined exclusion criteria. Of the remaining eligible participants, 18 gene carriers and 3 non‐carrier controls could be re‐invited and underwent follow‐up assessment.

We also included n = 3 non‐carrier individuals who were negative for gene testing. All volunteers had an initial polysomnography in a previous study [7, 8]. The second visit occurred 2.3 ± 1.5 years after the first visit (range: 0.3–5.2 years). Two individuals underwent a third polysomnography, including one who phenoconverted to clinically manifest ALS. Progressive Changes in Sleep Macroarchitecture

Polysomnography results were analysed using the YASA deep learning algorithm [10], over a 6‐h window following lights off. Hypnograms of two representative premanifest gene carriers (one C9orf72 and one SOD1) at visit 1 and visit 2 are shown in Figure 1, with quantitative results in Figure 2 and Table 2. There was a systematic increase in the percentage of wake (Figure 2A) across all 18 subjects, while rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (Figure 2B) and non‐rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep (Figure 2C) showed heterogeneous variations across volunteers. Interestingly, when NREM stage was subdivided into NREM1, 2, and 3, we observed a clear increase in NREM1 (light sleep) at the expense of NREM3 (deep sleep) (Figure 2D–F). Consistent with a worsening of the initial sleep phenotype, an augmentation in sleep onset latency (Figure 2G), a reduction in sleep efficiency (Figure 2H), a decline in sleep maintenance efficiency (Figure 2I), a decrease in REM efficiency (Figure 2J), an increase in sleep pressure (Figure 2K), and an increase in sleep fragmentation (Figure 2L) were observed. The direction of these changes was maintained in the two individuals who underwent a third polysomnography (Figure S1). There were no significant changes in the non‐carrier group (Figure S2). When stratified for genotypes, we observed no major differences across ALS‐causing mutations, although these analyses remained exploratory in non‐C9orf72 carriers due to limited sample size (Figures S3–S5). There were no major changes in medication that could underlie the observed effects (Table 3). Thus, the alteration in sleep stages observed in presymptomatic ALS gene carriers worsens over time.

Premanifest Alterations in Sleep Microarchitecture

Altered sleep is also reflected in alterations in sleep‐related electroencephalogram (EEG) hallmarks, including sleep spindles, slow oscillations, and K‐complexes, that are critical for sleep functions. Using the polysomnography recordings, we observed a significant decrease in sleep spindle density (Figure 3A), slow wave oscillation density (Figure 3B), and K‐complex density (Figure 3C). Here again, the direction of the changes in sleep microarchitecture was consistent in the two individuals who underwent a third visit (Figure 6) and in non‐carriers (Figure 7). Similar to macro‐architectural alterations, the observed directionality of change in microarchitecture was consistently observed when carriers were stratified across genotypes (Figures 8–10).

Sleep Alterations and Their Independence From Motor Symptom Onset

One C9orf72 gene carrier phenoconverted to clinically manifest ALS between the second and third visit (indicated by the red dot and solid red line in Figures 2 and 3 and Supporting Information). No specific divergence from the general sleep alteration was observed in this patient. To assess whether proximity to onset was related to worsening of sleep defects, we measured NF‐L levels across visits. Three subjects showed pathologically high neurofilament levels (defined as a z‐Score > 1.0 at follow‐up), suggestive of proximity to disease onset [3, 12]. The four subjects close to disease onset (three with pathological NF‐L, one phenoconverter) did not show obvious distinct patterns in the worsening or extent of sleep alterations as compared to other subjects with normal NF‐L levels (Figure 4A–C).

Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to assess longitudinal intra‐individual changes in sleep architecture in gene carriers. In our previous study, we demonstrated that sleep patterns, including macrostructural and microstructural sleep architecture, differ between gene carriers and non‐gene carriers. The present study extends these findings by focusing on longitudinal sleep changes within gene carriers over time. Here, we performed longitudinal follow‐up polysomnography in presymptomatic ALS gene carriers and in family members with negative genetic testing. We observed a systematic progression of both macro‐ and microarchitectural sleep alterations. However, this progression seemingly was independent of proximity to motor onset evaluated by presently used measures such as increased neurofilament levels or phenoconversion during the study. These results suggest that polysomnography could serve as an objective measurement of disease progression in clinically silent ALS.

The identification of frequent ALS‐associated gene mutations has enabled the investigation of premanifest signs in individuals at high risk of developing ALS. To date, most studies in presymptomatic ALS gene carriers have been cross‐sectional in nature (for review, see [13]). It is well established that presymptomatic gene carriers show imaging abnormalities; on this topic, longitudinal studies are available mostly for C9orf72. C9orf72 gene carriers show atrophy in several subcortical regions—particularly the thalamus [14, 15]—as well as white‐matter atrophy in the cervical spinal cord. Additional abnormalities include disrupted network activity [16] and white matter atrophy in the spinal cord [17]. Corticospinal abnormalities appear subtle but show slight progression over 18 months of follow‐up [17]. Longitudinal studies demonstrated that subcortical atrophy, especially thalamic atrophy, is progressive and already significant by the third decade of life in both C9orf72 gene carriers [18, 19, 20]. Our observation of progressive sleep deficits may therefore be related to this ongoing subcortical atrophy—particularly thalamic degeneration—in C9orf72 gene carriers, as such changes are already present at the ages represented in our cohort. It is important to note that the sleep alterations observed in ALS are therefore probably independent of developmental alterations, which were recently considered critical in C9orf72 ALS‐FTD [21, 22], since they are seemingly progressive over the few years of our study.

The relatively small sample size represents a limitation of the present study. This is mainly attributable to the longitudinal design and the high effort associated with repeated polysomnographic assessments, which require participants to attend the hospital for overnight recordings. This is particularly challenging in presymptomatic gene carriers who are otherwise healthy, often working in regular jobs, and may have family and other everyday responsibilities. These factors make recruitment and retention difficult and limit the feasibility of substantially larger longitudinal polysomnography cohorts. This may have introduced selection bias, as individuals willing and able to undergo repeated in‐hospital assessments may not fully represent the broader population of gene carriers.

Psychiatric symptoms, psychological distress, and medication may influence sleep architecture and therefore represent potential confounders in the present cohort. Although relevant medical history and medication use were documented during clinical assessment, psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety or mood disturbances were not systematically assessed using standardised questionnaires. Due to the small sample size and exploratory nature of the study, these variables were not included as covariates in the statistical models. This should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, we cannot completely rule out that the progression observed in gene carriers is related to ageing. Indeed, it is already well known that sleep micro‐ and macroarchitecture change with ageing, with a particular reduction in deep sleep and a reduction in the density of sleep spindles and slow waves [23, 24]. However, our analyses show a tendency towards an increased proportion of REM sleep in gene carriers over time, which is inconsistent with a purely age‐related process, and is consistent with the increased proportion of REM sleep that we detected in ALS patients [7]. In addition, the physiological effects of ageing on sleep manifest over decades; a change of this extent in such a short period of time is unusual and is likely to be related to a disease or premature ageing. Indeed, we did not observe significant alterations in sleep patterns in the 3 non‐carrier individuals included. However, future studies should include larger populations to disentangle the age effect from genetic background effects.

Sleep parameters are additionally influenced by inter‐individual variability and methodological factors. Although established normative data from healthy populations provide useful context for parameters such as sleep onset latency, sleep maintenance, REM sleep measures, and sleep efficiency, direct comparison with such values is limited in the present cohort. This is due to the wide age range, variable intervals between assessments, small sample size, and the use of single‐night polysomnography at each assessment. The latter is particularly relevant because the potential first‐night effect may influence sleep continuity, sleep onset latency, and REM sleep parameters, thereby limiting direct comparability with normative values of physiological sleep.

Presymptomatic gene carriers also show discrete alterations in cognitive function, although neuropsychological performance is generally within the normal range before symptom onset in C9orf72 carriers [20]. The reported phenotypes principally involve deficits in verbal fluency [25, 26]. In addition to cognitive signs, subtle oculomotor impairments [26] have also been demonstrated, as well as metabolic and sleep abnormalities [7, 27, 28, 29], but their evolution as individuals approach disease onset remains poorly characterised. Our study did not allow us to relate the progression of cognitive or metabolic dysfunction to sleep alterations, largely because these signs remain subtle at this stage and would require longer follow‐up and larger sample sizes. This is consistent with the broader ALS literature, in which most available data remain cross‐sectional [13]. The definition of mild motor impairment (MMI) is relatively recent [4, 30], and further longitudinal studies are needed to improve understanding of early disease trajectories.

In our study, we did not find an obvious relationship between the progression of sleep abnormalities and phenoconversion. In addition, the recently proposed Miami Framework highlights multiple dimensions of the premanifest ALS period—including frontotemporal and extrapyramidal domains [31], and it remains to be determined whether sleep‐related changes are linked to these additional phenotypic dimensions.

Overall, our study provides the first evidence that EEG‐related abnormalities progress during the premanifest phase of ALS. These changes might be the basis of neurochemical abnormalities, such as changes in the orexin or MCH system, which can be quantified and finally used as predictive tools [7]. These findings also emphasise the need for multimodal characterisation of at‐risk individuals to clarify how sleep alterations and their neurochemical consequences relate to disease onset and progression.
