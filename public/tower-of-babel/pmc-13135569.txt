Assessing PHILOS plate as an alternative fixation method for pediatric femoral neck fractures: a biomechanical comparison with cannulated screws
Authors: Bo Shiun Lim, Yu-Po Wang, Shang-Ta Chung, Chi-Hsiu Wang, Shu-Wei Chang, Ting-Ming Wang, Chih-Kai Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric femoral neck fractures require stable fixation to avoid complications. It remains unclear whether fixation with the Proximal Humeral Internal Locking System (PHILOS) can serve as an alternative to cannulated screw fixation. The purpose of this study was to compare the biomechanical properties of PHILOS and cannulated screws for stabilizing unstable pediatric femoral neck fractures using a synthetic bone model.Materials and methodsTwelve fourth-generation synthetic composite femurs were randomly assigned to screw fixation (Group S) or PHILOS fixation (Group P) (n = 6 each). A standardized vertically oriented Delbet type II osteotomy was created in all specimens. Group S was fixed with three 6.5-mm cannulated screws, whereas Group P received a PHILOS plate with 3.5-mm locking screws. Each specimen underwent a standardized loading protocol using a universal testing machine. Axial stiffness, cyclic displacement, ultimate failure load, and failure modes were recorded and statistically compared between groups.ResultsNo statistically significant difference was found in axial stiffness between Group P (746 ± 300 N/mm) and Group S (753 ± 256 N/mm) (p = 1.000). Displacement after cyclic loading was significantly greater in Group P (1.42 ± 0.3 mm) compared with Group S (0.57 ± 0.2 mm) (p = 0.004). The ultimate failure load was higher in Group S (2378 ± 513 N) than it was in Group P (1652 ± 206 N) (p = 0.025). Upon reaching ultimate load, all specimens in both groups failed at the femoral head region due to femoral head broken.ConclusionsThe adult PHILOS plate with 3.5-mm locking screws demonstrated inferior biomechanical stability compared with three 6.5-mm cannulated screws in this synthetic composite femur model.Level of evidenceControlled laboratory study.Keywords: Pediatric femoral neck fracture, Screw fixation, Proximal humeral internal locking system, Biomechanics

Introduction

Pediatric femoral neck fractures are rare, accounting for only 0.3–0.5% of all pediatric fractures [1–3], but their clinical significance should not be underestimated. These injuries usually result from high-energy trauma, such as traffic accidents or falls [4–6], with up to 20–50% of patients developing long-term complications, including avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head, coxa valga, proximal femoral physeal arrest, or nonunion [2, 3]. Clinically, the Delbet, Pauwels, and the AO classifications are widely used to guide treatment strategies and predict prognosis of pediatric femoral neck fractures [7–10]. The Delbet classification divides pediatric femoral neck fractures into four types based on the fracture location: transepiphyseal (type I, AO: 31-E1.1), transcervical (type II, AO: 31-M3.1 I), cervicotrochanteric (type III, AO:31-M3.1 II), and intertrochanteric (type IV, AO: 31-M3.1 III) [7]. Type I fractures carry the highest risk of AVN, whereas type II fractures are the most common [8]. In contrast, the Pauwels classification is determined by the inclination of the fracture line relative to the horizontal plane: type I (< 30°), type II (30–50°), and type III (> 50°), suggesting that a steeper angle indicates greater shear forces and reduced mechanical stability [9].

It is widely accepted that treatment of pediatric femoral neck fractures should achieve anatomic reduction and stable fixation while minimizing physeal injury through physeal-sparing techniques [3, 11]. Despite this, there are currently no established treatment guidelines for the optimal management of these fractures [3]. In clinical practice, cannulated screws and pediatric proximal femoral locking plates (e.g., the locking compression pediatric hip plate) are the most commonly used fixation methods, whereas the use of Kirschner wires has become much less common [3, 8, 11–13]. A recent study has further indicated that pediatric proximal femoral locking plates feature superior biomechanical properties compared with cannulated screws fixation, owing to their fixed-angle design and locking mechanism [14]. Moreover, Haider et al. reported that pediatric proximal femoral locking plates more effectively preserved the anatomic articulotrochanteric distance, while cannulated screws fixation was associated with a higher incidence of reduction loss and postoperative coxa vara [12].

However, due to limited availability and higher cost, pediatric proximal femoral locking plates could not be widely used in clinical practice, with cannulated screws remaining the most commonly used fixation method, particularly in hospitals with limited resources or in rural regions [8, 12, 15–17]. To address this limitation, some surgeons have suggested using the adult Proximal Humeral Internal Locking System (PHILOS) plate as an alternative to pediatric proximal femoral locking plates [15]. Gurnea et al. reported three cases of pediatric femoral neck fractures successfully treated with PHILOS fixation, all of which achieved complete union without complications, suggesting a readily available fixation option [15]. However, current evidence is limited to a few case reports with no supporting biomechanical studies nor clinical comparative study. Therefore, it remains unclear whether PHILOS fixation provides superior stability compared with cannulated screws fixation in pediatric femoral neck fractures.

To fill the gap in the current literature, this study aimed to evaluate the biomechanical properties of PHILOS fixation and cannulated screws fixation using a synthetic bone model, with the goal of providing biomechanical evidence to inform surgical decision-making in pediatric femoral neck fractures. We hypothesized that PHILOS fixation would provide superior biomechanical properties compared with cannulated screws fixation in unstable pediatric femoral neck fractures.

Methods

Material and specimen preparation

A total of 12 synthetic composite femurs (absolute™ 4th Gen., 17 PCF Solid Foam Cancellous, Small; Sawbones, Vashon, WA, USA) were used in this study; a smaller specimen size with an overall femur length of 375 mm and a canal diameter of 9.5 mm was selected, which corresponds to the anatomical scale of an adolescent patient approximately 11 years of age [18]. Synthetic bone models were used instead of cadaveric specimens because pediatric cadaver availability is limited and associated with ethical concerns. These composite femoral models are engineered for biomechanical testing, exhibiting structural and material properties that closely mimic those of human bone. Moreover, they provide geometric standardization, consistent mechanical properties, and convenient handling without requiring freezing or controlled environmental conditions.

The specimens were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 each): (1) the cannulated screw fixation group (Group S) and (2) the adult proximal humeral locking plate (PHILOS) group (Group P). The proximal 15 cm of each femur was resected and embedded distally in a 2-inch polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tube using industrial cement to ensure rigid stability during subsequent mechanical testing. The fracture model was made in accordance with the previous study [14]. Before creating the femoral neck fracture, all specimens underwent predrilling to ensure an accurate anatomical reduction after osteotomy. To maintain consistent implant positioning, predrilling was guided by a custom-made drilling jig in Group S, whereas in Group P, predrilling was guided by the locking plate. Next, an oblique osteotomy was performed across the midportion of the femoral neck in all specimens at an angle of 80° to the horizontal plane using a wire saw, creating a standardized Delbet type II fracture model.

In Group S, three 3.0-mm Kirschner wires were inserted in a parallel inverted triangular configuration under fluoroscopic guidance. Each pilot hole was subsequently reamed with a 4.9-mm cannulated drill and tapped with a 6.5-mm tap before screw insertion. Three 6.5-mm cannulated screws (Asnis III Cannulated Screw System, Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) were inserted along the predrilled holes to achieve anatomic reduction, each 65 mm long (Fig. 1A). To ensure a physeal-sparing fixation, screw placement and length were determined according to prior clinical experience.

The fluoroscopy images of unstable Delbet II femoral neck fractures fixed with A 6.5-mm cannulated screws and B a 3.5-mm Proximal Humeral Internal Locking System (PHILOS)

In Group P, reduction was achieved using a 3.5-mm Proximal Humeral Internal Locking System (PHILOS, Synthes, Oberdorf, Switzerland), positioned at the same distance below the greater trochanter in all specimens to ensure consistent placement. Three 3.5 × 65 mm proximal locking screws were inserted along the predrilled holes into the femoral neck region (Fig. 1B). The screw configuration and length were determined based on clinical experience to replicate a physeal-sparing fixation, with identical screw lengths used in both groups to ensure consistency.

Biomechanical testing

After fixation, specimens were mounted on a universal testing machine (AG-X; Shimadzu Corp., Tokyo, Japan) for biomechanical testing. The PVC-embedded distal ends were fixed in a custom-made jig, which was adjusted to achieve a valgus angle of 7° between the femoral shaft axis and the direction of axial compression force, replicating the physiological loading angle of the hip during double-leg stance [19] (Fig. 2). The fixation method and testing angle were determined based on previous biomechanical studies on femoral neck fractures [14, 20–22]. A 5000 N load cell was used for continuous load monitoring throughout the tests, and the load-displacement curve was recorded simultaneously during cyclic loading using Trapezium X software (version 1.00; Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan).

The PVC-embedded distal ends of the femurs were fixed in a custom-made jig, which was adjusted to achieve a valgus angle of 7° between the femoral shaft axis and the direction of axial compression force, replicating the physiological loading angle of the hip during double-leg stance

After the setup was completed, each specimen was tested following the biomechanical testing protocol based on previous studies [14, 20, 21]. First, a preload of 200 N was applied for 120 s at a crosshead speed of 2 mm/min, and the load was subsequently increased up to 600 N at the same crosshead speed. After pre-tensioning, cyclic loading between 200 and 1,000 N was applied to each specimen at a frequency of 1 Hz for 1,000 cycles. This loading range was selected to represent light repetitive activities during the rehabilitation phase, considering that forces transmitted through the femoral neck can reach 3–5 times body weight during activities such as jogging [23]. During this phase, axial stiffness was calculated from the slope of the linear elastic region of the load-displacement curve. Next, the cyclic displacement of each specimen after 1000 loading cycles was measured and compared between groups. Finally, a load-to-failure test was performed at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min until catastrophic failure occurred. Both the ultimate failure load and failure mode of each specimen were recorded. During mechanical testing, all load and displacement data were recorded using Trapezium X software (version 1.00; Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

An a priori power calculation was conducted using G*Power software, version 3.1.9.7 (Düsseldorf, Germany), using pilot data obtained from six specimens that had been randomly divided into two groups. The analysis assumed an α level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80. Based on the mean and standard deviation of ultimate failure load measured in the pilot groups, an effect size of 1.85 was derived. The calculation indicated that at least 12 specimens, 6 in each group, would be sufficient to provide adequate statistical power.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons of non-parametric parameters between groups, including axial stiffness, cyclic displacement, and ultimate failure load, were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 12 synthetic composite femurs were randomly assigned into two groups (n = 6 per group), including Group S and Group P. All specimens completed cyclic loading and load-to-failure tests. The summary of biomechanical results for both groups is presented in Table 1, while the comparative results of axial stiffness, cyclic displacement, and ultimate failure load between the two groups are illustrated in Fig. 3. No statistically significant difference was found in axial stiffness between Group P (746 ± 300 N/mm) and Group S (753 ± 256 N/mm) during cyclic loading (p = 1.000). The displacement after cyclic loading was significantly greater in Group P (1.42 ± 0.3 mm) compared with that in Group S (0.57 ± 0.2 mm) (p = 0.004), indicating a less stable construct in Group P. Finally, all specimens were subsequently loaded to failure. Group S (2378 ± 513 N) demonstrated a significantly higher ultimate failure load than Group P (1652 ± 206 N) (p = 0.025). Failure mode was recorded after reaching the ultimate load. In both groups, all specimens failed at the femoral head region due to femoral head broken (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The major finding of this study was that PHILOS plates demonstrated inferior biomechanical properties compared with cannulated screws, as evidenced by greater cyclic displacement and a lower ultimate failure load. Although previous studies have shown that pediatric proximal femoral locking plates provide superior biomechanical properties compared with cannulated screws [12, 14], they are not always available in clinical setting, especially for hospitals in resource-restricted regions [8, 12, 15–17]. As a result, surgeons have considered using PHILOS as an alternative fixation method [15]. However, whether PHILOS offers any biomechanical advantage over cannulated screws in pediatric femoral neck fractures has remained unclear. This study aimed to fill this gap using a synthetic pediatric femur model. Surprisingly, our results contradicted our initial hypothesis, which showed that PHILOS was biomechanically weaker than cannulated screws.

Previous studies have shown that pediatric proximal femoral locking plates outperform cannulated screws both in biomechanical performance [14] and in preserving the anatomic articulotrochanteric distance clinically [12]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that PHILOS fixation would also outperform cannulated screws due to its similar fixed-angle design and locking mechanism. However, our results showed the opposite. One possible explanation is the substantial difference in screw diameter between PHILOS fixation (three proximal locking screws with 3.5-mm diameters) and cannulated screw fixation (three cannulated screws with 6.5-mm diameters). Previous biomechanical studies have consistently demonstrated that screws with a larger diameter provide greater thread–bone contact area and improved fixation stability [24–27]. Leggon et al. demonstrated that 6.5-mm screws had significantly higher pull-out strength than 3.5-mm screws in a canine femur model [24]. In vertebral cancellous bone, Wittenberg et al. found that larger-diameter screws achieved superior pull-out strength [27], and Viezens et al. reported a 36–45% increase in fatigue load and construct stiffness with increased screw diameter [26]. Similarly, Lin et al. showed that 7.3-mm screws produced less displacement than 4.5-mm screws in vertical femoral neck fractures, indicating superior mechanical stability [25]. These findings suggest that the smaller 3.5-mm screws used in PHILOS may compromise mechanical stability. Although PHILOS employs a fixed-angle, locking-plate design similar to pediatric proximal femoral locking plates, the smaller screw diameter may offset its theoretical mechanical advantages.

Beyond biomechanical performance, cost-effectiveness is another important consideration when selecting implants, both in clinical practice and in terms of public health. Cannulated screws are widely available in clinical settings, and their cost is relatively low (approximately USD 135). In contrast, PHILOS is considered an off-label implant for pediatric femoral neck fractures and incurs greater medical expenses (approximately USD 2,200 in our country and up to USD 3,500 in other countries [28]). From an overall treatment perspective, the pediatric proximal femoral locking plate remains the ideal choice for managing pediatric femoral neck fractures due to its superior biomechanical properties compared with cannulated screws [14]. However, when this implant is unavailable, cannulated screws may represent a more practical and cost-effective option than the off-label use of the PHILOS plate, while still providing adequate biomechanical strength.

To evaluate the consistency of our biomechanical model, we compared our findings with those of prior studies using similar synthetic femur setups. Hsiao et al. reported an axial stiffness of 547 ± 93 N/mm, a cyclic displacement of 0.88 ± 0.40 mm, and an ultimate failure load of 2036 ± 256 N in Delbet type II fracture models fixed with three 6.5-mm cannulated screws in an inverted triangular configuration [14]. Using a similar testing protocol, Kuan et al. reported an axial stiffness of 856.5 ± 83.1 N/mm, a cyclic displacement of 0.41 ± 0.14 mm, and an ultimate failure load of 2612.7 ± 172.9 N in medium-sized synthetic femurs fixed with cannulated screws [20]. These values fall within a similar range to those observed in our cannulated screws group (axial stiffness = 753 ± 256 N/mm, cyclic displacement = 0.57 ± 0.2 mm, ultimate failure load = 2378 ± 513 N), supporting the reproducibility of our biomechanical setup and loading protocol. This consistency suggests that the inferior performance of PHILOS is unlikely to be explained by methodological factors alone and instead reflects a true biomechanical disadvantage.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although cadaveric specimens better simulate in vivo behavior, pediatric femurs are difficult to obtain due to ethical and availability constraints. Therefore, small-sized synthetic composite femurs were used instead. Their uniform geometry and consistent material properties help minimize variability in mechanical behavior between specimens. The use of small-sized synthetic composite femurs in simulating pediatirc femoral neck fracture has been utilized before [14]. Second, this study evaluated only the immediate postoperative mechanical stability, the long-term effects of fracture healing could not be examined. Third, this study focused only on a standardized Delbet type II fracture pattern, which may not represent the full spectrum of clinical scenarios. Fourth, given that axial compression represents the majority of physiological loads acting on the femoral neck during normal activities [23], only axial loading was applied in our biomechanical tests. Since torsional and anterior–posterior bending forces were not evaluated, the failure modes observed in this study may differ from those encountered clinically. Lastly, biomechanical performance may not always translate directly to clinical outcomes. Further clinical studies are needed to clarify whether the inferior biomechanical performance of PHILOS observed in this study corresponds to real-world outcomes.

Conclusions

The adult PHILOS plate with 3.5-mm locking screws demonstrated inferior biomechanical stability compared with three 6.5-mm cannulated screws in this synthetic composite femur model.
