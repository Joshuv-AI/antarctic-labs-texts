Effect of ultrasound treatment on kidney bean protein isolate-oat β-glucan glycation conjugates: The structure, digestibility and emulsifying properties
Authors: Zhigang Sun, Chaojiang Dong, Yue Gu, Yiying Jin, Fangxiao Lou, Tianfu Cheng, Lingling Zhang, Zengwang Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractConjugates of kidney bean protein isolate (KBPI) and oat β-glucan (OG) were successfully prepared via ultrasonic-assisted Maillard reaction (wet heating). The effects of ultrasonic power density (9.62–29.37 W/cm2) on its structural characteristics, in vitro digestive behavior, and emulsifying properties were investigated. The results demonstrated that ultrasonic treatment significantly enhanced the covalent conjugation between KBPI and OG. Specifically, the ultrasonically modified conjugate (UHKBPI-OG3) prepared at 22.42 W/cm2 exhibited higher grafting degree of 23.53 %. Appropriate ultrasonic treatment reduced the average particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of conjugates, as well as increasing the absolute value of their ζ-potential. Circular dichroism (CD) analysis revealed that ultrasound-assisted wet heating induced conformational changes in KBPI, showing a significant reduction in α-helix content accompanied by an increase in random coil structure. This structural transition suggests enhanced molecular flexibility, which is favorable for achieving higher glycosylation efficiency. Ultrasonic treatment enhanced the emulsifying properties of UHKBPI-OG3, increasing emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) by 20.75 % and 15.65 %, respectively, compared to wet heating. Concurrent reductions in interfacial tension, apparent viscosity, and droplet size further contributed to improved emulsion stability. Furthermore, UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate (24.22 W/cm2) exhibited the lowest protein digestibility. These findings demonstrate that optimized ultrasonic power density enhances KBPI-OG covalent conjugation. The improved functional properties of conjugates, achieved through modulated protein-polysaccharide interactions, provide theoretical support for developing novel emulsion stabilizers.Keywords: Ultrasonic power density, Kidney bean protein isolate, Wet heating, Structure, Digestibility, Emulsifying properties

Introduction

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) has identified white kidney beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) as a strategic crop for addressing protein-energy malnutrition, particularly in resource-limited agricultural systems due to their high adaptability and nutritional density [1]. Beyond their macronutrient value, these legumes contain bioactive components including triterpenoid saponins and α-amylase inhibitors, which demonstrate clinically validated hypocholesterolemic and hypoglycemic effects through modulation of lipid absorption and starch digestion [2]. The primary components of kidney bean protein are legumin and albumin [3]. However, the compact nature of legumin tends to result in decreased molecular flexibility, resulting in limited emulsifying ability [4,5]. Therefore, there is an urgent necessity to develop green and efficient modification technologies for kidney bean protein isolate (KBPI) to increase its value and economy for food industry applications.

In recent years, researchers have been optimizing the properties of legume proteins through multidimensional technological means, including physical modulation, chemical modification and enzymatic treatment, promoting their applications in dairy-based foods [6]. In particular, the chemical modification technique based on the Millard reaction by non-enzymatic glycosylation of reducing sugars with proteins, can be carried out efficiently under mild aqueous conditions (low temperature and no catalyst required), which is both functional and environmentally friendly [7]. The conjugates generated by this reaction can improve the interfacial adsorption behaviour to significantly reduce the interfacial tension between droplets, resulting in more stable emulsions [8]. Furthermore, the polysaccharide increases the number of hydrophilic groups on the surface of proteins, which effectively improves the dispersion of proteins in aqueous systems [9]. Dual mechanism modified proteins show excellent emulsifying activity and stability in complex food matrices [3,10]. Although the Maillard reaction has the significant advantage of enhancing protein emulsifying properties, its industrial application is hindered by prolonged reaction times (multiple hours for wet heating and several days for dry heating) and inconsistent product quality. These factors substantially increase production cost [11]. At the same time, the glycosylation process involves complex non-enzymatic cascades that lead to product heterogeneity (e.g. generation of late glycosylated end products) and difficulty in controlling reaction kinetics, resulting in a direct impact on product quality consistency and process controllability [12]. In addition, recent studies have shown that prolonged high temperature heating can also causes proteins in solution to denature and aggregate, which has a negative effect on glycosylation [13]. Therefore, it is necessary to apply new technologies to improve the Maillard reaction and increase the application performance of proteins.

Ultrasonic treatment, as a green non-thermal physical modification technology, modulates the conformational alteration and aggregation state distribution of conventional proteins through the synergistic effects of cavitation, shear stress, and microfluidic effects [14]. The mechanism involves disruption of secondary bonds (hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions), induction of secondary/tertiary structural rearrangements, exposure of reactive groups and optimization of multi-scale structural homogeneity to significantly enhance the functional properties of the protein [[15], [16], [17]]. In addition, ultrasound is also used as a pretreatment to increase the glycosylation reaction rate and to modulate the dispersion of aggregates to improve the solubility of the system [18]. Recent studies indicate that this enhanced solubility resulting from ultrasonic treatment also contributes to improved stability of the complex within colloidal and emulsion components [19]. Ma et al. [20] reported that ultrasound-assisted glycosylation reaction can conjugates enhance the emulsifying activity of soybean protein-pectin complexes, suggesting its potential application in the regulation of protein-polysaccharide covalent bond.

Oat β-glucan (OG) is a non-starch polysaccharide formed by the linkage of monomeric D-glucopyranose units through β-(1 → 3) and β-(1 → 4) glycosidic linkages [21]. OG has attracted great attention in the food and health sectors due to its unique biological activity alongside functional properties. OG's biological activity, including cholesterol metabolism modulation and immune-boosting effects [22], combines with its technological advantages of high emulsifying stability and water retention to synergistically enhance food product texture [10]. From the point of view of molecular structure, the β-(1 → 3) glycosidic linkage in the backbone of OG imparts special conformational flexibility to the molecular chain, so that the active hydroxyl group are regularly and densely distributed [15]. This characteristic structural configuration confers functional versatility by simultaneously ensuring molecular integrity and promoting effective interfacial engagement, thereby establishing fundamental structure–activity relationships. Currently, most studies focus on the role of ultrasound in improving the functional properties of conventional proteins (emulsification and hydrophobicity, etc.) of traditional proteins (e.g. soybean proteins). However, the mechanism underlying ultrasound-induced conformational changes in KBPI and its coupling with OG glycosylation remains systematically unexplored. Furthermore, structural and functional characterization of conjugates, along with in vitro digestibility studies, are relatively scarce.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of different ultrasonic power densities (9.62 W/cm2, 18.21 W/cm2, 24.22 W/cm2 and 29.37 W/cm2, at 28 Hz) on the structural, physicochemical and functional properties of KBPI-OG conjugates. The interactions and binding mechanisms were investigated through multi-dimensional structural characterization (intrinsic fluorescence spectra, surface hydrophobicity (H0), Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR), Circular Dichroism (CD)), basic physicochemical properties (particle size, ζ-potential, Polydispersity index (PDI)), and morphology (transmission electron microscopy). Emulsifying properties and digestion characteristics were assessed by comprehensive characterization (emulsion mean particle size, Emulsifying Activity Index (EAI), Emulsion Stability Index (ESI), confocal laser scanning microscopy) and in vitro protein digestibility. This work established an experimental foundation for developing novel conjugates as functional food ingredients.

Materials and methods

Materials

Kidney beans were supplied by Douyuan Agricultural Products Co., Ltd (Jining, Shandong, China). OG (purity: 90 %) was purchased from Kanghe Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Xi'an, Shaanxi, China). Sunflower oil (Golden Dragon Fish) was acquired from Kerry Agrifood Co., Ltd. (Yingkou, Liaoning, China). Pepsin and trypsin were purchased from Sigma Co., Ltd. (St. Louis, MO, USA). All the chemical reagents used in this work were analytical grade.

Preparation of KBPI

The preparation of KBPI was based on the method of Guo et al. [23] with some slight improvements. Firstly, kidney beans were peeled and ground to powder, and then sieved through a fine 80 mesh sieve. The kidney bean powder mixed with N-hexane in a ratio of 1:4 (w/v) and stirred at room temperature for 4 h. The above procedure was repeated 3 times to obtain defatted kidney bean powder.

The defatted kidney bean powder was then mixed with deionized water in a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The pH of the dispersion was adjusted to 8.0 with sodium hydroxide (2.0 mol/L). The dispersion was a centrifuged at 7500 × g for 15 min using a high speed refrigerated benchtop centrifuge (Centrifuge 5810R, Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). After that, the pH of the dispersion was adjusted to 4.5 with hydrochloric acid (2.0 mol/L) and the precipitate was collected after centrifugation (7500 × g, 15 min, 4 ℃). The precipitates were washed three times with deionized water and then centrifuged, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 with sodium hydroxide (2.0 mol/L). Finally, the resulting dispersion was freeze-dried by freeze-drying machine (pilot10-15EP, Bo Medical Kang Instrument Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). The freeze-dried powder was stored at −80 ℃ for subsequent experiments. The protein content of the KBPI was measured at 85.05 % using a Dumas nitrogen determination apparatus (rapid MAXN exceed, Elemental Analysis Systems, Inc., Langenselbold, Germany).

Preparation of the KBPI-OG conjugates by ultrasound technology

KBPI (10 mg/mL) was completely dispersed with OG (20 mg/mL) in a buffer solution of PBS (0.01 mol/L, pH = 7.0) and hydrated overnight at 4 °C. KBPI was mixed with OG in a ratio of 1:1 (v/v) and stirred continuously for 20 min at room temperature to form the non-covalent complex MKBPI-OG. After then, the MKBPI-OG was treated with an ultrasonic device (SCIENTZ-IID, Ningbo Xinzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China) equipped with circulating cooling water. The experimental conditions were as follows: ultrasound power densities of 9.62 W/cm2, 18.21 W/cm2, 24.22 W/cm2, and 29.37 W/cm2, frequency of 28 k Hz, temperature of 80°C, and reaction time of 20 min. At the end of the reaction, the products were transferred to an ice-water bath to stop the reaction from continuing. Subsequently, the sample solutions were dialyzed to remove salt ions at 4 °C for 48 h. Finally, MKBPI-OG mixture and KBPI-OG conjugates were freeze-dried, and then stored at −80°C for further analysis. The conjugates were named according to different ultrasonic power densities as UHKBPI-OG1 (9.62 W/cm2), UHKBPI-OG2 (18.21 W/cm2), UHKBPI-OG3 (24.22 W/cm2), and UHKBPI-OG4 (29.37 W/cm2). The conjugate prepared by the wet heating without ultrasound treatment was named as HKBPI-OG.

Characterization of KBPI-OG conjugates

Degree of grafting (DG)

The grafting degree of the KBPI-OG conjugates were determined by the O-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) method [24], with minor adjustments. Prior to measurement, OPA solvent (4 mL) and KBPI-OG conjugate solution (1 mg/mL, 200 μL) were mixed using a vortex mixer (IAKT18102, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and then incubated in a 35 ℃ water bath for 2 min. The absorbance valued at 340 nm were measured using a spectrophotometer (EVOLUTION201, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). OPA solvent was used as background blank. Determination of intrinsic fluorescence spectra

The intrinsic fluorescence spectra of the samples were determined according to the method described by Qu et al. [25]. The freeze-dried samples were dissolved in PBS (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.0) buffer solution to prepare a dispersion at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The intrinsic fluorescence spectra of the sample dispersions were measured by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7100, Hitachi Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) with the excitation wavelength of 280 nm, scanning wavelength range 290–450 nm and scanning speed of 240 nm/min.

Determination of H0

The H0 of samples was determined by 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) probe method [20]. Lyophilised samples were dissolved to a concentration of 1 mg/mL and mixed with ANS solution (8.0 mmol/L, pH 7.4) in a volume ratio of 400:1(v/v). Subsequently, the solutions were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. Finally, the H0 of the sample solutions was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7100, Hitachi Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). The excitation and emission wavelengths were 390 nm and 200–600 nm, respectively.

Size of the particle

The samples were dissolved in deionized water to prepare a solution with a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL according to the method described by Zhou et al. [24]. Particle size, ζ-potential and PDI were recorded at room temperature using a nanoparticle size and ζ-potential analyser (NANO ZS90, Malvern Instruments Co., Ltd, Malvern, UK). Under refractive index matching conditions: 1.467 for the dispersed phase and 1.330 for the matrix phase, particle size distributions were analyzed using average particle size (nm) data.

Determination of FT-IR

FT-IR of freeze-dried samples was analyzed using the method previously expressed by Ma et al. [20], with some modifications. Firstly, appropriate amounts of freeze-dried samples and potassium bromide powder were weighed and mixed by uniform grinding in an agate mortar. Then, thin tablets were produced by a powder tablet press (FW-5 Tianjin Topu Instrument Co., Ltd, Qingdao, Shandong, China). Finally, an FT-IR instrument (Nicolet is50 Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was used in the wave number range from 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans.

CD

The method previously described by Chen et al. [12] was used to determine the secondary structure of the samples. Freeze-dried samples were diluted with deionized water to a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL solution. Thereafter, the samples were placed in a 1 mm cuvette, and multiple sample solutions were measured using a circular dichroism spectrometer (Chirascan, Applied Photophysics Ltd, Surrey, UK). Instrument parameters were set to a scan range of 190–260 nm and a scan bandwidth of 0.1 nm with a response time of 1 s. The average of three scans was fitted and analyzed by the Chirascan software.

SDS-PAGE

Freeze-dried samples were reconstituted in deionized water to a concentration of 2 mg/mL solution and then thoroughly mixed with the protein loading dye. The mixture was heated in boiling water for 5 min and then loaded into a gel tank. Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V and 120 V for concentrated and separated gels, respectively. The gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250 solution for 2 h followed by destaining with a solution containing 10 % glacial acetic acid and 45 % methanol. Molecular weights of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture and KBPI-OG conjugates were calculated using Marker (11–180 kDa) as a reference [26].

Translucent electron Microscope (TEM)

The microstructure of the sample solution was observed using TEM (H-7650, Hitachi Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, 5 μL (0.01 mg/mL) of the sample solution was pipetted onto a 200 mesh copper grid. The copper grid was placed in the negative stain (uranyl acetate) for negative staining and then dried. Finally, the TEM was performed to make observations and take photographs.

Determination of interfacial tension and contact angle

Application of the test method previously reported by Chen et al. [27] with some modifications. Applied an appropriate amount of emulsion to a clean slide to form a homogeneous film approximately 0.1 cm in diameter. Using a high-precision syringe, a drop of water was applied to the surface of the film and at the moment of contact between the drop of water and the film, the data were recorded by an optical contact angle 3D morphology analyzer (Theta Flow 3D, Biolin Technology Co., Ltd, Gothenburg, Sweden). The droplet profile was solved numerically and the Laplace-Young equation was fitted to determine the interfacial tension.

Characterization of emulsion

Preparation of emulsions

Freeze-dried samples were prepared to a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL using PBS (0.01 mol/L, pH 7) buffer solution at room temperature and stored overnight at 4 °C. The sample solution and sunflower oil (3:1, v/v) were homogenized using an emulsifying homogeniser (Ultra-Turrax T25, IKA Group Inc., Staufen, Germany) at a speed of 10,000 × g for 3 min.

Determination of emulsion particle size

The particle size of the emulsion was measured using a laser particle size analyzer (SYNC, Microtrac Instrument Co., Ltd, USA), and the particle size was expressed as the average particle size.

Determination of confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM)

The microstructure of the prepared sample emulsions was observed under a 40 × objective using a CLSM (FV3000, Olympus Optical Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Mixed Nile Red and Nile Blue staining solution (1 mg/mL) with the sample in a 1:3 (v/v) ratio and stain for 15 min. The microstructure of the emulsion was analyzed by setting the excitation wavelength to 488 nm and 633 nm, respectively [24].

Determination of apparent viscosity

The apparent viscosity of the emulsions was determined by increasing the shear rate from 0.01 s−1 to 100 s−1 at 25°C using a rheometer (HAAKE MARS Ⅲ, Thermo Scientific, USA). Shear rate versus apparent viscosity relationship recorded and analyzed [28].

EAI and ESI

Application of the method described by Liu et al. [29] for the analysis of the emulsifying properties. Pipetted 50 µL of the emulsion from the bottom of the centrifuge tube into 5 mL (0.1 % SDS) of the solution and measured the absorbance value A0 at 500 nm. Testing of protein digestibility in vitro

Gastric digestion

The gastric digestion test was performed referring to the method recommended by Qu et al. [30] with minor additions. Pepsin (25000 U/mL) was mixed with the sample solution in a ratio of 1:50 (w/w) a pH of 1.5. The dispersion was incubated at 37 °C for 120 min using a water bath oscillator (ZHSY-50WV, Shanghai Zhichu Instrument Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China), and the pepsin was inactivated by boiling in a water bath for 10 min. The gastric digestion was collected and the protein content was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method.

Intestinal digestion

Intestinal digestion test in accordance with the report by Zhou et al. [31]. A portion of the pepsin hydrolysate was taken and the pH of the solution was adjusted to 7.5 with sodium hydroxide (2.0 mol/L). Trypsin (40000 U/mL) was mixed with the sample solution in a ratio of 1:50 (w/w). The dispersion was incubated for 120 min at 37 °C with shaking. After the reaction is complete, the trypsin was inactivated by boiling in a water bath for 10 min. The intestinal digestion products were obtained by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The digestion supernatant was collected and then the protein content was measured by BCA.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were replicated three times and the data were presented as average ± SD. p-value was less than 0.05, indicating a significant difference between the sample data. Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS22 and all graphics were drawn using Origin 2022.

Results and discussion

Degree of grafting (DG)

The grafting degree of KBPI-OG conjugates prepared via wet heating (HKBPI-OG) and ultrasonic-assisted wet heating (9.62–29.37 W/cm2, UHKBPI-OG) is presented in Fig. 1A, demonstrating significantly higher DG values for UHKBPI-OG conjugates compared to HKBPI-OG (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, the DG values of the UHKBPI-OG conjugates showed a trend of increasing initially followed by a decrease with increasing ultrasonic power density. The UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate prepared at 24.22 W/cm2 ultrasound demonstrated the highest DG value of 23.53 %, showing significant differences (p < 0.05) compared with conjugates produced at other power densities. This indicated that appropriate ultrasound can generate a certain amount of shear force and free radicals, which facilitate changes in the structure of KBPI and OG and expose more amino groups and carbonyl species, thereby triggering the Maillard reaction [30]. Concurrently, localized thermal effects from cavitation lowered the reaction energy barriers and enhanced collision frequency between KBPI and OG molecules, synergistically increasing the grafting degree of conjugates [32]. When ultrasonic power density increased to 29.37 W/cm2, excessive ultrasound-induced KBPI denaturation, aggregation and free radical-mediated oxidative damage [12], resulting in a decrease in the DG value of the UHKBPI-OG4 conjugate.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy reflects changes in the protein tertiary structure during conjugation, specifically characterising alterations in the local polarity around tryptophan (Trp) residues [13]. The changes in the intrinsic fluorescence spectra of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, and KBPI-OG conjugates are illustrated in Fig. 1B. At 280 nm excitation, untreated KBPI exhibited maximum fluorescence intensity at 336 nm. However, in the physical mixture of KBPI and OG (MKBPI-OG), the maximum emission wavelength (λmax) did not shift, but its fluorescence intensity decreased. This decrease was mainly attributed to transient weak hydrogen bonding between OG and KBPI. Compared with the MKBPI-OG, the HKBPI-OG conjugate (wet heating) exhibited a slight red shift in λmax and a decrease in fluorescence intensity. This phenomenon was attributed to conformational changes in KBPI induced by the Maillard reaction during OG conjugation, which exposed the Trp residues to a polar microenvironment (causing the red shift). Simultaneously, steric hindrance from conjugated OG groups shielded Trp from photophysical processes, leading to reduced fluorescence intensity [33]. The fluorescence spectra of ultrasound-induced conjugates showed regular changes: λmax gradually red-shifted and the fluorescence intensity decreased continuously. At an ultrasonic power density of 24.22 W/cm2, λmax shifted to 338 nm and fluorescence intensity reached its lowest value. This can be explained by the synergistic effect of ultrasonic cavitation and glycosylation. The shear force and microjet generated by ultrasonic cavitation contributed to the unfolding of the KBPI structure, thereby exposing Trp residues from a non-polar environment to a more polar one [20]. Meanwhile, the glycosylation reaction dissociates KBPI aggregates, and the shielding effect of long-chain polysaccharides further quenches fluorescence intensity, collectively enhancing KBPI's functional properties [27]. In addition, When ultrasonic power density reached 29.37 W/cm2, λmax exhibited blue shift, indicating reduced polarity in the local micro-environment of amino acid residues [27]. Under excessive ultrasonic power density, protein–protein interactions (hydrophobic forces and hydrogen bonds) dominated, promoting aggregate formation while weakening polysaccharide binding [34].

H0 analysis

The H0 values of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG conjugates (ultrasound-assisted wet heating) are shown in Fig. 1C. Compared with untreated KBPI, the H0 value of the MKBPI-OG mixture decreased slightly. This may be due to the adsorption of OG molecules on the KBPI surface through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, with its long-chain structure masking some hydrophobic groups. At the same time, hydroxyl groups of OG form a hydration layer on KBPI, thereby partially shielding hydrophobic groups from solvent access and contributing to reduced overall H0 [35]. The H0 value of the HKBPI-OG conjugate higher than those of untreated KBPI. This resulted from glycosylation induced localized structural dissociation/rearrangement of KBPI, which exposed more hydrophobic groups [36]. In addition, it showed that the H0 of UHKBPI-OG conjugates increased and then decreased with the increase of ultrasonic power density. Among them, the UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate exhibited the highest H0 at 24.22 W/cm2 ultrasound. This might be attributed to ultrasonic cavitation mechanically dissociating KBPI aggregates to release hydrophobic groups [20], combined with glycosylation induced peptide chain extension preventing the refolding of hydrophobic regions [29]. These effects synergistically enhanced H0. Under excessive ultrasonic power density (29.37 W/cm2) could cause over cross-linking of KBPI molecules. This process results in the formation of complex three dimensional network structures, which limit the exposure of hydrophobic groups and reduce H0 [33].

Particle size, PDI and ζ-potential analysis

The aggregation behavior of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixtures, KBPI-OG conjugates prepared by wet heating and ultrasound-assisted wet heating was explored by determining the mean particle size, PDI and ζ-potential, as shown in Fig. 2(A–C). The average particle size was 1455.7 nm for untreated KBPI, along with a higher PDI value of 0.7953 and an absolute value of low ζ-potential of 22.00 mV, indicating that KBPI molecules were less stable and prone to aggregation. In the MKBPI-OG physical mixture sample, the OG bound to KBPI through hydrogen bonding and weak hydrophobic interactions. This inhibits the formation of protein aggregates, thereby reducing the average particle size to 1380.7 nm and increasing the absolute ζ-potential value. Compared with the MKBPI-OG, the mean particle size and PDI of the HKBPI-OG conjugate (wet heating) were decreased significantly (p < 0.05), and the absolute value of the ζ-potential was enhanced, but not significantly different (p > 0.05). These results imply that the Maillard reaction could dissociate some of the protein aggregates and form surface charged conjugates, thereby inhibiting further aggregation of proteins [37]. The mean particle size and PDI of UHKBPI-OG conjugates showed a decreasing and then increasing trend with increasing ultrasonic power density. The UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate prepared at 24.22 W/cm2 ultrasound had a smaller particle size. These data suggest that low ultrasound power densities fail to disrupt the KBPI molecular bonding forces (hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions), resulting in insufficient unfolding of its conformation. Whereas excessive power densities promote hydrophobic group exposure and cross-linking, leading to aggregate formation. Meanwhile, ultrasonic power density significantly affected the ζ-potential of UHKBPI-OG conjugates. The UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate (24.22 W/cm2) exhibited the highest absolute value of ζ-potential of 27.15 mV, ascribed to ultrasound-Maillard reaction synergism: Ultrasonic cavitation unfolded the KBPI conformation to expose charged groups (–COO−/–NH3+), while OG covalently linked via the Maillard reaction inhibited KBPI refolding through steric hindrance. These effects collectively maintained high charge density, leading to increased ζ-potential absolute value [38]. Nevertheless, when the power density reached 29.37 W/cm2, mechanical forces disrupted both the KBPI hydrogen bonding network and the KBPI-OG covalent bonds, triggering conformational collapse and OG dissociation. This resulted in burial of charged groups and disintegration of the electrostatic barrier, reducing the absolute ζ-potential value.

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy enables characterization of protein secondary structures and protein–sugar interactions at the molecular level. The FT-IR of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG (ultrasound-assisted wet heating) was exhibited in Fig. 2D. The FT-IR of KBPI in the amide I band (1655 cm−1) mainly reflects the C=O stretching, the amide II band (1544 cm−1) contains of N–H bending and C-N stretching [18,39]. The addition of OG induced the weakening of the absorption peaks in the FT-IR amide I and amide II bands of MKBPI-OG, suggesting that the KBPI surface was covered by OG molecules, resulting in part of the N–H being unable to interact with the FT-IR spectrum.

HKBPI-OG and UHKBPI-OG conjugates showed the enhanced absorption peaks in both amide I and amide II bands compared with MKBPI-OG, indicating that covalent binding affects the N–H bending and the C═O stretching [40]. However, the enhancement of absorption peaks in the amide I and amide II bands were more pronounced in the UHKBPI-OG conjugates treated at different ultrasonic power densities compared with HKBPI-OG. This change was attributed to the proteins being more stretched after ultrasound treatment, resulting in the formation of more Schiff bases (C═N), pyrazines (C-N) and Amadori compounds (C═O) [41,42]. In addition, the broadening of the peaks and the increase in absorption intensity of all conjugates in the amide A band (3100–3667 cm−1) wavenumber range were attributed to the increase in the stretching of the intermolecular hydrogen bonds N–H and O–H after glycosylation [43]. The peak absorption wavenumber of UHKBPI-OG3 (24.22 W/cm2) was increased from 3292 cm−1 to 3312 cm−1 compared with untreated KBPI. This result indicated that ultrasonic treatment increases the N–H bond content, reduced the N–H bond length to ═N-H, and correspondingly improved the wavenumber of the stretching vibration. HKBPI-OG and UHKBPI-OG conjugates showed new absorption peak at 1027 cm−1, this phenomenon can be attributed to the vibration of the C-O-C glycosidic bond. However, the absorption peak of the conjugate was stronger than that of MKBPI-OG, indicating the presence of glycosylation [39]. In addition, the intensity of the absorption peak at 1080 cm−1 (C-O stretching) in the ultrasound treated UHKBPI-OG conjugates increased significantly, suggesting that ultrasound increased the exposure of glycosylation sites or promoted the formation of more stable covalent cross-links in the conjugates [44].

CD analysis

The CD spectra (190–260 nm) directly reflect conformational changes in the secondary structure of proteins [30]. Both wet heating and ultrasound-assisted wet heating significantly altered the secondary structure of KBPI, as evidenced by shifts in peak positions and intensity changes within the 190–260 nm range, as illustrated in Fig. 2E. The percentages of α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn and random coil for all samples are shown in Table 1. Among them, the secondary structure composition of untreated KBPI comprised α-helix (10.2 %), β-sheet (48.3 %), β-turn (14.9 %) and random coil (26.6 %). In the MKBPI-OG physical mixture sample, OG had no effect on the secondary structure of KBPI. Compared with untreated KBPI, the KBPI-OG conjugates prepared by wet heating or ultrasound treatment showed decreased α-helix content and increased random coil content. Notably, UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate prepared at 24.22 W/cm2 ultrasound exhibited the most significant secondary structural changes. Specifically, their α-helix content decreased to 6.6 %, while that of random coil increased to 30.8 % (p < 0.05). In comparison, the effect of OG on the structural transformation of KBPI was reduced at 29.37 W/cm2 ultrasound. Collectively, these results suggested that optimal ultrasound-induced cavitation effectively destabilizes KBPI's conformation to enhance structural flexibility, while preventing irreversible protein aggregation [35]. The transition from ordered to disordered secondary structure not only improves functional properties but also exposes more reaction sites, greatly facilitating the glycosylation process [45]. The CD results demonstrated that glycosylation significantly altered the secondary structure of KBPI, consistent with the FT-IR analysis. Crucially, ultrasound induced greater structural unfolding in KBPI, thus facilitating its glycosylation with OG. This explains the higher DG values achieved through ultrasonication compared to wet heating.

SDS-PAGE analysis

SDS-PAGE of the untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG (ultrasound-assisted wet heating) was displayed in Fig. 2F. KBPI subunit bands at 32 kDa, 25 kDa, 22 kDa, and 17 kDa were considered to be lectin (legumin) [46,47]. A subunit band with an approximate molecular weight of 46 kDa corresponds to 7S vicilin, consistent with the 41–47 kDa range reported by S. Mundi et al. [46]. The MKBPI-OG mixture prepared by simple physical mixing (lane 2) exhibited an intensified band at ∼ 75 kDa. This might be due to the presence of trace protein impurities in the OG reagent, as OG itself did not change the electrophoretic mobility or banding pattern of native KBPI. The characteristic KBPI bands with molecular weights in the range of 11–17 kDa and 35 kDa were significantly weaker in the conjugates (lanes 3–7) compared with lanes 1 and 2. Concurrently, high molecular weight bands at the top of the gel (lanes 3–7) become darker and new bands appeared. This suggests glycosylation-induced formation of high-Mw conjugates. Notably, the KBPI-OG conjugates exhibited new bands at ∼ 61 kDa and ∼ 76 kDa. This could be due to the formation of dimers or trimers through intermolecular disulfide bonds or hydrophobic interactions [48]. Compared with HKBPI-OG (lane 3), at an ultrasonic power density of 24.22 W/cm2, the colour of the new bands was observed darker in lane 6, suggesting that proper sonication could promote the formation of glycosylation products. In contrast, low ultrasonic power densities (9.62 and 18.21 W/cm2) were insufficient to induce KBPI unfolding, consistent with CD data. However, excessive ultrasonic power density (29.37 W/cm2) induced degradation and/or conformational changes in KBPI, thereby reducing grafting efficiency, as corroborated by DG analysis.

TEM analysis

The morphology of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG physical mixture, HKBPI-OG and UHKBPI-OG was examined by TEM, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Untreated KBPI (Fig. 3A) exhibited dispersed globular structures, whereas the MKBPI-OG mixture (Fig. 3B) formed amorphous aggregates upon polysaccharide addition. This aggregation presumably stemmed from intermolecular force imbalance between OG and KBPI, leading to aggregate formation [49]. In contrast, the HKBPI-OG conjugate (wet heating, Fig. 3C) displayed curved short-chain structures. This may be attributed to long-chain OG disrupting the KBPI globular structure through covalent binding, exposing charged groups and thereby increasing surface charge [36]. Simultaneously, the flexible OG chains cross-linked multiple KBPI molecules via hydroxyl groups, forming curved continuous chains [50]. Enhanced electrostatic repulsion and steric hindrance synergistically inhibited aggregation, ‌ resulting in dispersed chain-like aggregates‌. When the ultrasonic power density was increased from 9.62 W/cm2 to 24.22 W/cm2 (Fig. 3D–F), irregularly shaped micelles exhibited increased branching and significantly elongated chain lengths. This occurred because ultrasound-induced mechanical effects promoted covalent bond formation between OG and KBPI, thereby enhancing grafting degree and molecular chain rigidity. As the DG increased, ultrasonic cavitation likely disrupted hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic interactions within micellar cores, causing structural loosening. Simultaneously, OG molecules aggregated on KBPI surfaces, forming stretched conformations that synergistically increased micellar chain dimensions in both length and width [51,52]. As ultrasonic power density continued to increase, the micelles exhibited size reduction tendencies (Fig. 3G). This might have occurred because ultrasound energy exceeded the micellar core's stability threshold, causing loosening or disintegration of the originally compact aggregated structure.

Interfacial tension

The interfacial tension changes for all samples over adsorption time are illustrated in Fig. 4A. The results demonstrate that interfacial tension decreased rapidly within the initial 200 s for all samples, indicating rapid adsorption capability at the oil–water interface. From 200 s until the end of the test, interfacial tension gradually declined and stabilized, suggesting progressive interfacial adsorption approaching saturation without subsequent changes. All glycosylated conjugates exhibited significantly greater interfacial tension reduction compared to the MKBPI-OG physical mixture. This enhancement originates from OG's capacity to increase KBPI hydrophilicity, which facilitates aqueous phase interaction while optimizing amphiphilic balance for achieving more ordered interfacial molecular arrangements. Furthermore, the spatial hindrance effect promotes formation of a denser interfacial adsorption layer. These combined mechanisms enable glycosylated conjugates to demonstrate superior performance to the MKBPI-OG physical mixture samples in reducing oil–water interfacial tension [53]. The variation of ultrasonic power density had a significant effect on the interfacial tension. It was found that the interfacial tension of UHKBPI-OG3 prepared at an ultrasonic power density of 24.22 W/cm2 was lower than that of all other groups. This may be related to the particle size (Fig. 2A) and H0 (Fig. 1C) of conjugate. ‌The smaller particle size and higher H0 allowed UHKBPI-OG3 to adsorb at the oil–water interface without having to overcome higher energy barrier, thereby reducing the interfacial tension [54].

Interfacial tension (A) and contact angle (B) of KBPI, MKBPI-OG, HKBPI-OG and UHKBPI-OG. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

Contact angle

The wetting behaviour of conjugates at the emulsion interface can be quantified using the contact angle (θ), which was a parameter closely related to emulsion stability. As shown in Fig. 4B, the contact angles (θ) of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG (ultrasound-assisted wet heating) particles were below 90°, indicating their hydrophilic nature. This property facilitates their migration to oil–water interfaces, thereby stabilizing oil-in-water emulsions. Compared with the KBPI sample (θ = 59.85°), the θ values of the conjugates subjected to decreased significantly. At an ultrasonic power density of 24.22 W/cm2, UHKBPI-OG3 exhibited the minimal contact angle of 43.13°. This reduction was primarily attributed to the optimal grafting degree that enhanced surface hydrophilicity, combined with accelerated interfacial diffusion due to smaller particle size [27]. The contact angle of UHKBPI-OG4 increased to 51.29° at an ultrasonic power density of 29.37 W/cm2. This was because ultrasonic cavitation induces conformational changes in KBPI that typically enhance wettability under moderate conditions. However, excessive ultrasonic power density triggers irreversible KBPI aggregation, thereby reducing wettability and emulsion stability.

Characterization of oil-in-water emulsion

3.11.1. Mean particle size of emulsion

Droplet size was one of the most important indexes for the emulsion stability. The mean particle size of all sample emulsions are presented in Fig. 5. The mean droplet size of KBPI emulsion was about 53.71 μm. However, both the MKBPI-OG mixture and HKBPI-OG conjugate exhibited smaller mean particle sizes than the KBPI emulsion, with HKBPI-OG showing a larger size reduction (p < 0.05). This was attributed to the faster formation of a viscoelastic film by the HKBPI-OG conjugate, which resulted in higher mechanical strength than that of the intermolecular interaction in the MKBPI-OG mixture, thereby inhibiting droplet aggregation [55]. Conjugates (UHKBPI-OG1-4) prepared by ultrasound-assisted wet heating exhibited smaller mean particle sizes compared with HKBPI-OG. In particular, at an ultrasonic power density of 24.22 W/cm2, the particle size of the UHKBPI-OG3 emulsion significantly decreased to 22.82 μm (p < 0.05). This evidence indicated that the appropriate ultrasonic power density effectively induced KBPI structural unfolding, thereby exposing hydrophobic domains, while OG glycosylation simultaneously enhanced steric hindrance and amphiphilicity, consistent with the observed ζ-potential variation and contact angle reduction. This dual mechanism synergistically improved interfacial adsorption efficiency, effectively suppressing droplet coalescence, resulting in a reduction in emulsion droplet size [35,56]. As ultrasonic power density increases, the emulsified particle size increases. This phenomenon likely occurs because excessive ultrasound induces disordered aggregation of proteins and conjugates at the oil–water interface, impairing their ability to effectively reduce interfacial tension and consequently enlarging emulsion droplets.

CLSM analysis

The microstructure and distribution of the sample emulsions were observed using CLSM, as depicted in Fig. 6. As expected, the KBPI-stabilized emulsion exhibited larger oil droplets with evident flocculation (Fig. 6A), indicating ineffective adsorption of KBPI at the oil–water interface. In contrast, the emulsion stabilized by MKBPI-OG physical mixture (Fig. 6B) showed reduced flocculation and smaller droplet sizes, yet insufficient interfacial interactions between KBPI and OG molecules led to incomplete inhibition of droplet coalescence [37]. Compared with MKBPI-OG, emulsions of both HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG (9.62 W/cm2 and 18.32 W/cm2) exhibited a further reduction in oil droplet size, but showed bridging flocculation (Fig. 6C–E). These observations were attributed to partial unfolding of KBPI conformation under single wet heating or low-power ultrasound-assisted wet heating, resulting in incomplete glycosylation. Consequently, the steric hindrance effect of OG was insufficient to prevent flocculation. When ultrasonic power density increased to 24.22 W/cm2, the UHKBPI-OG3 conjugate emulsion formed uniformly distributed smaller oil droplets (Fig. 6F). This suggested that optimised ultrasound synergized with the glycosylation reaction to alter the KBPI conformation appropriately, thereby enhancing the emulsion's stabilising capacity [35]. These effects might be primarily related to the higher hydrophobicity of UHKBPI-OG3, which allowed more KBPI adsorption at the interface and reduced the free energy required for new interface formation [57]. Furthermore, the high grafting efficiency of UHKBPI-OG3 not only promoted overlayer formation but also enhanced the interfacial viscoelastic response [33]. Increasing the ultrasonic power density to 29.37 W/cm2 caused the UHKBPI-OG4 conjugate emulsion to flocculate again, leading to a significant increase in droplet size (Fig. 6G). Excessive ultrasonic power density likely induced KBPI‌ irreversible aggregation, resulting in its insufficient adsorption at the oil droplet interface [53].

Apparent viscosity

Apparent viscosity reflects intermolecular forces and fluidity within a liquid, and describes its flow properties. As shown in Fig. 7A, the apparent viscosities of KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, and KBPI-OG conjugate emulsions decreased with increasing shear rate. When the shear rate reached 20 s−1, the apparent viscosities of the emulsions tended to stabilize, indicating a shear-thinning phenomenon characteristic of pseudoplastic fluids [58]. At the same shear rate, the apparent viscosities of the conjugate emulsions were found to be significantly higher than that of the KBPI. Whereas the MKBPI-OG physical mixture showed no significant difference from KBPI. ‌ The apparent viscosity enhancement in conjugates likely stemmed from hydroxyl groups introduced by glycosylation, which strengthened hydration layer interactions with KBPI through covalent bonding. In contrast, the MKBPI-OG mixture maintained similar apparent viscosity to KBPI because OG interacted with KBPI only through transient hydrogen bonds, which were insufficient to modify protein conformation or interfacial stability [59]. The apparent viscosities of conjugates prepared with different ultrasonic power densities were higher than those of HKBPI-OG. Under the ultrasonic treatment at 24.22 W/cm2, the UHKBPI-OG3 emulsion developed enhanced mechanical shear effects among ultrasonically treated groups, attributed to its maximal DG value. This led to improved particle dispersion and stabilized emulsion structure, thereby increasing apparent viscosity [33].

EAI/ESI

The emulsifying properties of protein-polysaccharide conjugates are typically evaluated by EAI and ESI [27,60]. EAI and ESI of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG (ultrasound-assisted wet heating) were presented in Fig. 7B. Compared with untreated KBPI, both ESI and EAI values of the MKBPI-OG mixture were significantly increased (p < 0.05), indicating that: (1) The amphiphilic structure of KBPI reduced interfacial tension [37]. (2) OG reinforced the interfacial film through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, which collectively inhibited droplet aggregation [59]. Furthermore, covalent grafting of OG onto KBPI provided more stable interfacial anchoring, resulting in significantly enhanced ESI and EAI values. Especially at an ultrasound power density of 24.22 W/cm2, ESI and EAI increased by 20.75 % and 15.65 %, respectively (p < 0.05), compared to HKBPI-OG. These results demonstrated that ultrasonic cavitation induced structural transformation in KBPI from an ordered to disordered random coil conformation. This unfolding exposed buried hydrophobic groups and reactive sites, thereby enhancing OG grafting efficiency (Fig. 1A). The increased molecular flexibility weakened intramolecular interactions, ‌leading to decreased average particle size (Fig. 2A) and PDI (Fig. 2B), while promoting surface charge redistribution to elevate absolute ζ-potential (Fig. 2C). Collectively, these changes stabilized the oil–water interfacial film through the combined synergistic effects of electrostatic repulsion and steric hindrance [12], thereby improving ESI and EAI values. When the ultrasound power density reached 29.37 W/cm2, both EAI and ESI values decreased significantly, likely resulting from ultrasound-induced excessive denaturation/aggregation of KBPI and oxidative damage to critical functional groups (e.g., −SH and –NH2) [14]. These structural changes led to increased H0 (Fig. 1C) and a substantial decrease in apparent viscosity (Fig. 7A), ultimately impairing interfacial tension (Fig. 4A) and reducing emulsion stabilization.

Protein digestibility analysis

The protein digestibility of untreated KBPI, MKBPI-OG mixture, HKBPI-OG (wet heating) and UHKBPI-OG (ultrasound-assisted wet heating) during simulated gastrointestinal digestion is depicted in Fig. 8. The results indicated that the protein digestibility values of untreated KBPI and MKBPI-OG were significantly higher than those of the conjugates at all digestion times. The incremental order of protein digestibility was KBPI > MKBPI-OG > HKBPI-OG > UHKBPI-OG. This reduced digestibility could be attributed to the OG chains shielding the enzymatic cleavage sites of KBPI in a dense cross-linked network, while the sterically hindered glycosylated groups impeded the binding of proteases to peptide chains, thereby compromising the efficiency of enzymatic cleavage [61,62]. The protein digestibility at the intestinal stage increased by 16.98 %, 16.44 %, 11.63 %, and 13.70 % for UHKBPI-OG1 (43.88 %), UHKBPI-OG2 (41.65 %), UHKBPI-OG3 (38.72 %), and UHKBPI-OG4 (39.60 %), respectively, compared with the gastric digestion stage. After entering the intestinal digestion, trypsin will continue to hydrolyse the gastric digestion products, and the peptides are further dissociated. The results of the previous experiments show that ultrasound can further open up the structure of the protein, exposing more sites for enzymatic degradation. This allows trypsin to bind to the substrate more quickly and efficiently and to act as a catalyst [14].

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that KBPI-OG conjugates prepared via ultrasonic-assisted moist heat treatment exhibit higher grafting degrees. Ultrasonic cavitation induces unfolding of the KBPI structure, thereby providing additional reactive sites for covalent bond formation with OG. At 24.22 W/cm2 ultrasonic power density, the UHKBPI-OG3 showed the highest grafting degree with increased H0 and reduced particle size. Fluorescence spectra indicated tertiary structure loosening, evidenced by intensity weakening and red shift. UHKBPI-OG3 emulsion also displayed optimal emulsifying properties, including homogeneous microstructure and better emulsifying properties. Additionally, reduced digestibility demonstrated resistance of the cross-linked structure to enzymatic degradation. This approach provides a theoretical basis for designing high-performance food emulsifiers. Our subsequent research will delve into the omics information of covalent complexes within the digestive systems of organisms.
