Can ChatGPT Take My Call? evaluating AI in gynecologic oncology telephone triage
Authors: Ann Marie Mercier, Sharonne Holtzman, Aashna Saini, Lisa J Mordell, Andrew Vallejo, Kristen Zeligs, Stephanie Williams, Caitlin Carr, Stephanie V Blank
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ChatGPT matched fellow’s triage decisions for most patient calls.•When AI management differed, it favored patient safety with conservative over-triage.•Physicians rated AI-generated responses as highly accurate, clear, and useful.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, ChatGPT, Telephone triage, Clinical decision support

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly being integrated into medical practice, from applications spanning diagnostic support, risk stratification, clinical decision making, and workflow optimization. In particular, ChatGPT has gained widespread adoption given its open source access and classification as a large language model capable of processing large amounts of information and generating human-like text for a wide range of users.

Patients and clinicians increasingly turn to ChatGPT for health-related information. Several studies have explored ChatGPT’s performance in Obstetrics and Gynecology (OBGYN), particularly its ability to answer common medical questions. Antelo et al. (2025) evaluated ChatGPT’s responses to common pregnancy questions and found inconsistency, with misinformation present in 27% of responses. (Antelo et al., 2025) Within the field of gynecologic oncology (GO), Patel et al. (2024) reported that ChatGPT provided accurate and comprehensive responses in 83% of common genetic questions related to gynecologic malignancies. (Patel et al., 2024) Similarly, Hermann et al. (2023) demonstrated accurate responses regarding cervical cancer prevention, survivorship, and quality of life topics, but identified less accurate responses related to cervical cancer diagnosis and management strategies. (Hermann et al., 2023).

Despite growing interest, there remains limited published evidence assessing ChatGPT’s performance in real-world GO clinical scenarios. In this study, we evaluated ChatGPT’s ability to triage and respond to GO patient telephone encounters received by on-call GO fellows. The primary objective was to assess AI’s accuracy in determining clinical urgency and providing appropriate guidance, and to directly compare its recommendations with those of on-call GO fellows.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional, single-institution study. Thirty real-world patient questions derived from telephone encounters managed by on-call GO fellows between December 1, 2024 and July 31, 2025 were included (Table 1). Clinical scenarios encompassed post-operative concerns, gynecologic symptoms, chemotherapy side effects and other oncologic issues. For each encounter, the documented clinical management plan was developed by the on-call fellow in conjunction with the attending gynecologic oncologist.

Gynecologic Oncology Patient Questions.

This table lists the 30 patient telephone questions used as clinical vignettes to assess AI-generated triage responses. Questions entered into ChatGPT-4o platform with the following prompt: “I am a Gyn Onc fellow on call. A patient called who is (insert above question). Should I: 1) send this patient to the emergency department, 2) schedule urgently in clinic this week, 3) stay home with at-home intervention, or 4) advise routine follow-up?

To minimize bias from prior interactions, each question was entered into a fresh ChatGPT-4o session. All prompts were generated by a single investigator (medical professional) to ensure consistency. Each vignette began with the following standardized prompt: “I am a gynecologic oncology fellow on-call. A patient called who is___” and concluded with an identical triage question offering four response options: “Should I: 1) send this patient to the emergency department, 2) schedule urgently in clinic this week, 3) stay home with at-home intervention, or 4) advise routine follow-up?”.

Four gynecologic oncologists independently evaluated each response across five domains: accuracy, comprehensiveness, clarity, relevance, and applicability using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor; 3 = acceptable; 5 = very good). The “acceptable” threshold reflects the minimum standard of clinical reasonableness.

Reviewers also assessed each response for the presence of misinformation (yes / no). Vignette-level mean scores were calculated for each domain by averaging across reviewers. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare mean domain scores against a predefined acceptability threshold of 3.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.. The prevalence of misinformation was reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Mean reviewer scores for each vignette were grouped by clinical category (post-operative concerns, gynecologic symptoms, chemotherapy related, or other oncologic issues) and compared across domains using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Agreement between ChatGPT recommendations and documented provider management assessed with Cohen’s kappa. Patterns of misclassification (over-triage and under-triage) were summarized descriptively. Inter-reviewer variability was evaluated using the Friedman test based on vignette-level scores. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted in R (version ≥ 4.0) and Python (version ≥ 3.10).

Results

Mean domain scores with corresponding standard deviation (SD) and p-value for AI’s responses are presented in Fig. 1. Scores were as follows: accuracy 4.30 (SD 0.84, p < 0.0001), comprehensiveness 4.37 (SD 0.76, p < 0.0001), clarity 4.43 (SD 0.77, p < 0.0001), relevance 4.80 (SD 0.48, p < 0.0001), applicability 4.73 (SD 0.58, p < 0.0001), and overall 4.53 (SD 0.65, p < 0.0001). Across all domains, physician ratings of AI generated responses exceeded the predefined acceptable threshold of 3.0 (p < 0.0001).

Across vignette clinical categories, mean overall scores ranged from 4.38 to 4.62, on Likert scale 1.0 to 5.0. Chemotherapy-related vignettes achieved the highest overall mean score 4.62 (SD 0.20). Other categorized vignettes had the lowest numerical mean (4.38, SD 0.50), while gynecologic symptom management and post-operative care vignettes scored similarly (4.56, SD 0.22 and 4.53, SD 0.40, respectively). Misinformation was identified in four clinical scenarios (3.3%; 95% CI, 0.6–16.7%) including three post-operative care scenarios and one gynecologic symptom management.

Agreement between AI recommended triage and fellows’ documented clinical management was high (κ = 0.87). Exact clinical management agreement occurred in 26 of 30 cases (86.7%). Discrepant recommendations were observed in four cases: one chemotherapy related, one gynecologic symptom management, and two post-operative care. Compared with fellow decision making, AI over-triaged three patients (10.0%) by recommending urgent clinic follow up within one week. In one case (3.3%), AI under-triaged the scenario by recommending home management rather than urgent clinic evaluation as advised by the on-call fellow. In all 10 cases where the fellow’s documented recommendation advised emergency department evaluation, AI issued the same recommendation (Table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that an AI agent can generate accurate and comprehensible responses to GO patient concerns while also appropriately triaging after-hours clinical scenarios. Across all vignette types, mean physician ratings exceeded 4.0 on a 5-point Likert scale, reflecting high-quality AI responses across domains of accuracy, comprehensiveness, clarity, relevance, and clinical applicability. Physician ratings surpassed the predefined acceptability threshold of 3.0, supporting that AI-generated responses were clinically reasonable and within the standard of care; however, this does not imply equivalence to trainee-level performance. Although chemotherapy-related vignettes consistently demonstrated the highest means, these differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that AI remained consistent across a diverse range of GO patient questions. Importantly, physician review identified minimal misinformation within AI responses. AI generated triage recommendations demonstrated high concordance with fellow management, including complete agreement in emergent scenarios and conservative over-triage in the few discordant cases. Together, these findings support the safety of AI-assisted triage for home-based patient concerns within the context studied.

Several prior studies have examined the role of AI in GO, particularly in the context of treatment planning and clinical decision making, with mixed results. Rios-Doria et al. (2023) evaluated ChatGPT’s responses to tumor board cases and found concordance with the institutional multidisciplinary recommendations in only 46% of cases. (Rios-Doria, 2024) Similarly, Reicher et al. (2025) reported that AI-generated recommendations for complex GO case management were often cumbersome, whereas responses to more fundamental clinical questions − such as screening and genetic risk assessment − were generally accurate. (Reicher et al., 2025) These findings highlight the limitations of applying AI to nuanced oncologic decision making. In contrast, our study focused on a narrower, well-defined clinical task: after-hours triage of GO patient telephone calls. By constraining AI responses to predefined triage options aligned with real-world fellow decision-making, we were able to directly compare AI recommendations to clinical management. This structured prompting approach may account for the higher concordance observed in our study, particularly in urgent scenarios where ChatGPT demonstrated complete agreement with emergency department referral decisions.

Our results are also consistent with emerging literature evaluating AI performance in acute and time-sensitive clinical contexts. Kim et al. (2025) demonstrated that ChatGPT-generated responses to emergency department GO scenarios − including differential diagnosis, initial work-up, and management − were rated more favorably by physician reviewers than human generated responses. (Kim, 2025) Beyond the field of gynecologic oncology, Ayers et al. (2023) found that AI-generated replies to patient medical questions on social media were rated as higher quality, more empathetic, and more comprehensive than physician responses. (Ayers et al., 2023) Collectively, these studies suggest that AI may be particularly well suited for structured, time-sensitive clinical interactions, such as patient triage and symptom assessment.

This study has several notable strengths. It utilized real-world patient scenarios, incorporated independent evaluation by multiple gynecologic oncologists, and compared AI recommendations directly with documented clinical decisions rather than hypothetical standards. Additionally, the inclusion of diverse clinical presentations enhances the relevance of our findings to reflect routine on-call practice.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. The modest sample size limits generalizability, and the single-institution design may reflect institution specific practice patterns. Furthermore, while this study evaluated triage accuracy and concordance, it did not assess downstream clinical outcomes or patient-centered measures. Future work should include larger, multicenter cohorts, prospective implementation, and evaluation of patient outcomes to better define the clinical impact of AI-assisted triage workflows.

Overall, the clinical implications of these findings are substantial. As AI tools become increasingly integrated into healthcare systems, the focus must shift from whether they should be used to how they can be implemented safely and effectively. Our data suggest that ChatGPT may serve as a safe adjunct for after-hours triage as a decision support tool, not to replace fellows, but to provide a layer of structured reasoning during overnight triage. AI may help reduce on-call cognitive burden for trainees as overnight call requires rapid decision-making under fatigue, and having a tool that quickly organizes differential diagnoses, risk factors, etc. may help reduce mental burden.

In conclusion, AI-assisted triage has the potential to enhance night and weekend coverage in GO when deployed with appropriate clinical overnight. These findings support further investigation into the role of AI as an adjunctive tool to support on-call personnel and optimize triage workflows in GO practice.
