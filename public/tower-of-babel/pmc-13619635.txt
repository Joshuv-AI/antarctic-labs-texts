Reflective AI use and student engagement in AI-supported programming: technology acceptance, programming self-efficacy, and behavioral trace evidence
Authors: Sheng Wang, Jiabi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAI coding assistants are increasingly used in programming education, but willingness to use them does not show whether students evaluate or learn from AI suggestions. Grounded in technology acceptance, self-regulated learning, and self-efficacy theory, this study examined reflective AI use and student engagement.MethodsThe Reflective AI Acceptance Integration Framework (RAIAF) organized three independent public datasets: a survey structural equation model (N = 131), double human coding of 66 nonblank Critical Engagement responses from a Copilot field study, and complementary programming-behavior prediction using DTA logs (N = 1, 423; 40% calendar-window sample N = 1, 122). The DTA dataset did not measure AI use and was used only to test whether recovery-related programming traces added predictive information.ResultsIn the MLR survey model, technology acceptance predicted reflective use (β = 0.694, p < 0.001) and engagement (β = 0.694, p < 0.001); reflective use predicted programming self-efficacy (β = 0.519, p < 0.001) and engagement (β = 0.273, p = 0.034). None of the hypothesized indirect effects was significant. Initial text-coder agreement was limited (linear-weighted κ = 0.421; ordinal α = 0.495), and consensus-coded reflective use did not significantly predict productive engagement (b = 0.149, p = 0.202). In DTA, adding recovery features increased held-out final-success Macro-F1 from 0.725 to 0.743, but average gains in repeated and 30%–50% window analyses were negligible. Activity-defined engagement was already captured by overlapping activity indicators.DiscussionThe findings distinguish acceptance from reflective use but do not establish a general mediation mechanism. Reflective use was associated with self-efficacy and engagement in the survey, whereas its text and behavioral evidence was exploratory, small, and context dependent.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is changing the psychological conditions under which students learn programming. In AI-supported programming environments, learners can receive code suggestions, debugging hints, explanations, and automated feedback while they are solving tasks. These functions may reduce frustration and help novice programmers continue when they encounter syntax errors, failed test cases, or unclear requirements. However, programming learning is not only the production of working code. Students must also monitor the quality of external feedback, judge whether an AI suggestion is appropriate, revise errors, and transfer what they have learned to new problems. The central educational-psychological issue is therefore not simply whether AI tools are accepted, but whether students engage with AI-generated support in reflective and learning-oriented ways.

This problem is timely because generative AI changes the relation between help seeking, feedback use, and learner agency. A student may accept an AI programming assistant because it is convenient, but still copy suggestions passively. Another student may compare a suggestion with personal reasoning, identify a flaw, revise the code, and use the feedback to build confidence for later tasks. These two learners may appear similar if only tool acceptance or use frequency is measured, but they differ psychologically in metacognitive monitoring, feedback interpretation, and self-regulated learning. The present study focuses on this difference by examining how students' acceptance of AI tools, reflective AI use, programming self-efficacy, and engagement are related.

Several research traditions inform this question. Technology acceptance research explains why learners are willing to use digital tools, especially through perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Student-engagement research suggests that learning involvement includes behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions (Fredricks et al., 2004). Self-regulated learning and metacognition research explains how learners plan, monitor, and adjust strategies when using feedback (Zimmerman, 2002; Pintrich, 2000; Winne and Hadwin, 1998). Programming self-efficacy research further shows that confidence in programming capability is related to persistence and achievement in difficult coding tasks (Bandura, 1997; Ramalingam and Wiedenbeck, 1998). Recent work on generative AI and computing education has examined ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, code generation, and automated feedback (Kasneci et al., 2023; Milano et al., 2023; Thorp, 2023; Noy and Zhang, 2023; Doshi and Hauser, 2024; Denny et al., 2024; Raihan et al., 2025). Together, these studies suggest a key limitation: positive attitudes toward AI tools do not reveal whether students check, revise, and internalize AI-generated suggestions.

This limitation motivates the present study. We define reflective AI use as students' active evaluation, explanation, correction, and revision of AI-supported feedback. Reflective AI use is not treated as mere frequency of tool use; rather, it is a psychologically meaningful form of feedback engagement. We also focus on programming self-efficacy, which captures students' perceived capability to complete programming tasks. The main task of this paper is to examine whether technology acceptance is associated with reflective AI use, whether reflective AI use is associated with programming self-efficacy, and whether these constructs help explain engagement in AI-supported programming. A secondary task is to examine whether reflection-related behaviors can be observed in text responses and programming traces.

To address these tasks, we organize the analyses through the Reflective AI Acceptance Integration Framework (RAIAF). As shown in Figure 1, RAIAF integrates three complementary sources of evidence. First, a survey-based structural model tests the relationships among technology acceptance, reflective use, programming self-efficacy, and learning engagement. Second, a Copilot field-study text analysis explores whether reflective use can be observed from students' open-ended Critical Engagement responses. Third, a DTA programming-log analysis constructs early-window behavioral indicators and predicts later learning outcomes. This design is intentionally transparent and conservative. It does not assume that all psychological paths must be significant. Instead, it asks where the proposed psychological mechanism is supported, where it is weak, and how far behavioral trace evidence can extend the interpretation.

The main contributions of this study are three-fold.

First, we provide an educational-psychological account of AI-supported programming that distinguishes technology acceptance from reflective AI use. Instead of treating acceptance as the final explanation of engagement, the study examines how acceptance is related to reflective feedback engagement, programming self-efficacy, and learning engagement. The survey results support strong paths from technology acceptance to reflective use and engagement, and from reflective use to programming self-efficacy, while the hypothesized indirect effects are not statistically significant. This offers a more nuanced account of AI-supported programming engagement.

Second, we use a multi-source evidence design that combines survey modeling, text-based reflective-use coding, and programming-trace prediction. The Copilot field-study analysis explores reflective use from Critical Engagement texts, while the DTA analysis models early programming behavior from log data. This design helps bridge psychological constructs and behavioral evidence, while also making clear where the evidence is strong, weak, or task-dependent.

Third, we test whether reflection-related behavioral features add predictive information for final task completion. In the DTA experiment, the held-out improvement is small and the average gain across repeated and window-sensitivity analyses is negligible. The DTA branch therefore supplies complementary programming-behavior evidence and is not a validation of reflective AI use.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on technology acceptance, programming self-efficacy, reflective learning, generative AI in programming education, and behavioral trace analytics. Section 3 presents the analytical design, including the survey model, text-based reflective-use coding, and programming-log feature construction. Section 4 describes the datasets, experimental settings, comparisons, ablation study, and further analyses. Section 5 concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.

Related work

Technology acceptance, self-efficacy, and engagement in AI-supported programming

Prior work on the problem addressed in this study can be grouped into four categories: technology acceptance, student engagement, programming self-efficacy, and generative AI in computing education. The first category originates from the Technology Acceptance Model, which explains system use through perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989). Later, UTAUT integrated several acceptance theories and highlighted performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions as major determinants of technology use (Venkatesh et al., 2003). These models provide a strong basis for studying students' acceptance of AI-supported programming tools, but they were not designed to explain whether students use AI suggestions reflectively. The second category concerns student engagement. A widely used view treats engagement as a multidimensional construct that includes behavioral, emotional, and cognitive components (Fredricks et al., 2004), and self-regulated learning research further explains how students plan, monitor, and adjust learning strategies (Zimmerman, 2002; Pintrich, 2000; Winne and Hadwin, 1998). The third category concerns programming self-efficacy. Grounded in self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1997), early programming research developed and validated programming-specific self-efficacy measures for novice programmers (Ramalingam and Wiedenbeck, 1998). This work shows that students' confidence in programming is educationally meaningful, although its role may change when AI tools provide immediate code suggestions. The fourth category concerns AI and LLMs in education. Broader AI-in-education reviews have described intelligent tutoring, adaptive support, and data-informed instruction as major application areas (Holmes et al., 2019; Luckin et al., 2016; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019; Ouyang and Jiao, 2021). Recent LLM reviews further describe both the promise and risk of generative AI for learning, including personalized support, feedback generation, over-reliance, academic integrity concerns, and the need for AI literacy (Kasneci et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026). High-profile discussions in Nature and Science venues have also emphasized that generative AI can reshape higher education, authorship norms, productivity, and creative work, while raising reliability and diversity concerns (Milano et al., 2023; Thorp, 2023; Noy and Zhang, 2023; Doshi and Hauser, 2024). In computing education, recent studies and reviews have examined ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, and other code-generation tools as supports for programming tasks, while also noting concerns about foundational understanding, critical thinking, and assessment validity (Denny et al., 2024; Prather et al., 2023; Becker et al., 2023; Raihan et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Nathaniel et al., 2025). Empirical Copilot studies further show that AI coding assistants may influence productivity, behavior, and understanding, but the effects are context-dependent and often based on small or task-specific samples (Shihab et al., 2025; Aziz, 2026).

Different from studies that treat acceptance or usage as the main endpoint, the present work asks whether acceptance is connected to reflective use, programming self-efficacy, and engagement in AI-supported programming. The study also avoids assuming that all theoretical paths must be significant. Instead, it tests the psychological paths with survey data, explores text-based reflection in a Copilot field-study dataset, and examines whether related behaviors appear in programming traces. This design keeps the motivation consistent across the paper: accepting AI is not the same as engaging with AI-generated suggestions in a reflective and learning-oriented way.

Reflective AI use, feedback engagement, and behavioral trace evidence

The present paper is also related to three lines of work: reflective learning in programming, behavioral trace evidence, and interpretable educational prediction. First, reflection, feedback use, and self-regulation have long been regarded as important for learning complex skills (Zimmerman, 2002; Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick, 2006; Shute, 2008). Prior work on adaptive learning and self-regulated learning has shown that monitoring, feedback interpretation, and strategy adjustment are central to productive learning (Azevedo et al., 2009; Zimmerman, 2002; Pintrich, 2000). In programming education, recent work has designed tools that allow learners to replay coding processes and reflect on their strategies, errors, and improvements (Xie et al., 2023). Such studies show that reflection can make invisible problem-solving processes more visible. However, most reflection-oriented work focuses on designed interventions or qualitative learning processes, rather than linking reflective feedback engagement to AI acceptance and behavioral traces. Second, learning analytics and educational data mining have used clickstream, submission, activity, and progress features to predict student outcomes (Long and Siemens, 2011; Romero and Ventura, 2010; Baker and Yacef, 2009; Gasevic et al., 2015; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). In programming courses, submission logs are especially valuable because they capture attempts, errors, correction patterns, and task completion. For example, explainable prediction studies have used programming-submission features and SHAP-style explanations to interpret student performance models (Hoq et al., 2023). The DTA dataset and related work provide a large-scale source of automatically collected programming solutions and learning-process data from a (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, United States) programming course (Demidova et al., 2023). Third, explainability has become increasingly important in educational prediction, drawing from broader interpretable machine-learning work as well as educational data mining (Ribeiro et al., 2016; Lundberg and Lee, 2017; Miller, 2019; Rudin, 2019). Recent EDM and learning-analytics work emphasizes that predictive models should not only be accurate, but also interpretable and useful for instructional decision-making (Khosravi et al., 2022; Gunasekara and Saarela, 2024; Islam et al., 2025). These studies provide important methodological foundations, but many still rely mainly on generic activity indicators, and fewer explicitly test whether theoretically grounded reflective behaviors add value beyond basic engagement features.

The present work differs from this literature by using behavioral trace evidence as a cautious complement to psychological analysis rather than as a replacement or validation of psychological measurement. It constructs baseline activity, recovery-related, and persistence-related feature groups and tests their incremental prediction through controlled ablation. The feature names are theory-informed descriptions of log patterns, not measurements of latent reflective AI use or self-efficacy.

Methods

Overview of the analytical design

We organize the study through a Reflective AI Acceptance Integration Framework, abbreviated as RAIAF. RAIAF is an evidence-integration design rather than a new machine-learning architecture or a claim that the three datasets measure the same process. The datasets contain different participants and contexts and are not pooled. The survey and Copilot modules concern AI-supported programming, whereas the DTA module contains programming behavior without a direct measure of AI use.

RAIAF consists of three modules. The first module estimates the relationships among technology acceptance (TA), reflective use (RU), programming self-efficacy (PSE), and engagement (ENG). The second module explores reflective use through open-text responses in the Copilot field-study dataset. The third module transforms DTA programming logs into early-window behavioral trace features and predicts later learning outcomes as complementary evidence about recovery and persistence in programming, not as validation or replication of reflective AI use.

Methodological rationale

The rationale of RAIAF is three-fold. First, survey modeling tests whether the proposed psychological mechanism is supported at the construct level. Second, text-based coding checks whether reflective use can be observed beyond closed-ended survey items. Third, log-based prediction asks whether recovery-related programming behaviors provide incremental predictive information. Because DTA does not measure AI use, the third module cannot confirm the first two.

The method is also conservative. It does not claim that all indirect effects are significant. It reports when a hypothesized path is not supported. It also treats self-efficacy-related behavioral indicators as approximations, not as direct replacements for psychological self-efficacy scales. This distinction is important for maintaining construct validity.

Experiments

Experimental overview

The experiments were designed to answer three questions. First, does the survey-based psychological model support the proposed links among technology acceptance, reflective use, programming self-efficacy, and engagement? Second, can text-based reflective use in the Copilot field-study data provide exploratory evidence of reflection in AI-supported programming? Third, do recovery and persistence indicators add early predictive information in DTA programming logs?

The experiments combine latent-variable modeling, exploratory text coding, and complementary behavioral trace prediction. Only the first two modules directly address AI-supported programming.

Datasets

Dataset A: AI-supported programming survey

Dataset A was an AI-supported programming learning survey dataset from Mendeley Data (Tang et al., 2026). It contained N = 131 valid responses. Four constructs were used in the main analysis: technology acceptance (TA), reflective use (RU), programming self-efficacy (PSE), and learning engagement (ENG). TA was measured with six items, RU with three items, PSE with four items, and ENG with nine items.

The internal consistency of the item sets was high. Cronbach's alpha was 0.938 for TA, 0.951 for RU, 0.962 for PSE, and 0.957 for ENG. The parcel-based latent SEM had CFI = 0.951, TLI = 0.934, RMSEA = 0.082, and SRMR = 0.029. The model-fit results are summarized in Table 1. However, the item-level MLR model showed weak incremental fit (CFI = 0.889; TLI = 0.874), so parcel-model results are not presented as resolving all measurement-model concerns. An item-level WLSMV sensitivity model for the ordered responses produced CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.067, and SRMR = 0.039, but it yielded negative observed-variable variances and a non-positive-definite parameter covariance matrix; this inadmissible solution was not used for inference.

Dataset B: copilot field-study text evidence

Dataset B was the GitHub Copilot field-study dataset from Mendeley Data (Aziz, 2026). The student-level file contained N = 114 records, of which 66 contained nonblank Critical Engagement text and 48 were blank. Two human coders independently coded the 66 nonblank responses into RUItext scores ranging from 0 to 3. Before adjudication, the coders agreed exactly on 38 of 66 responses (57.6%); 95.5% of ratings differed by no more than one category. Linear-weighted Cohen's κ was 0.421, and Krippendorff's ordinal α was 0.495. The 28 disagreements were resolved by consensus after reliability was calculated. The 48 blank responses remained missing and were excluded from text inference. Among the 66 coded texts, the consensus distribution was 8 cases coded as 0, 38 as 1, 16 as 2, and 4 as 3.

The mean technology acceptance score in Dataset B was 4.730, with a standard deviation of 0.255 on a five-point scale. Of the 114 students, 33 (28.9%) had the maximum score of 5 and 93 (81.6%) scored at least 4.5. This concentration at the upper end constitutes a ceiling effect and sharply restricts between-student variance. The mean RUItext score among the 66 nonblank responses was 1.242.

The reliability estimates describe the two coders' independent ratings before discussion. Consensus scores were used only to form the final analysis variable and were not used to recalculate or inflate intercoder reliability.

Dataset C: DTA programming behavioral traces

Dataset C was the DTA programming-log dataset, a public programming-submission dataset generated by a Digital Teaching Assistant environment (Demidova et al., 2023). It does not contain a measure of AI-assistant use. The full student-level sample contained N = 1, 423 students, and the 40% course-calendar window contained early activity for N = 1, 122 students. Features were extracted before one common cutoff defined from the course-wide log range.

Two prediction targets were used. The first target, final_success_all_11, indicated whether a student solved all 11 programming tasks. The second target, renamed activity_defined_engagement, was constructed from the full-period activity score. This score was the mean of standardized total attempts, active days, active weeks, unique tasks attempted, and regularity. Students at or above the 67th percentile were assigned the activity-defined outcome. Because the label derives from activity intensity and regularity, it is not interpreted as the broader psychological construct of engagement.

Experimental settings

The survey analysis was conducted in R 4.6.1 with lavaan 0.7–2. The main parcel-based latent SEM was estimated with robust maximum likelihood (MLR), treating the five-point Likert responses and their predefined parcel means as approximately continuous (Rhemtulla et al., 2012). Direct paths are reported with MLR robust standard errors, two-sided p values, and 95% robust Wald confidence intervals. Indirect and total effects were re-estimated with maximum likelihood and 5,000 case-resampling bootstrap draws; percentile intervals, rather than normal-approximation bootstrap p values, determine indirect-effect significance. All 22 modeled item responses were complete, so the specified full-information maximum-likelihood missing-data option did not change the effective sample.

No a priori power analysis was possible because the public secondary dataset fixed the sample at N = 131. SEM sample requirements depend on loading strength, model complexity, missingness, and effect size rather than a universal ratio (Wolf et al., 2013). With four constructs and a three-coefficient serial indirect effect, this sample was considered adequate only for exploratory estimation of large component paths and likely underpowered for small mediation effects (Fritz and MacKinnon, 2007). The lower-complexity parcel model was therefore paired with transparent item-level and scale-score sensitivity models; it was not used to claim that N = 131 validates the full measurement structure.

The Copilot text analysis was exploratory. Consensus-based coding formed a four-level RUItext score for the 66 nonblank responses; the 48 blank responses remained missing. Complete-case regressions tested whether technology acceptance predicted RUItext and whether RUItext predicted productive engagement and project score. The text-based indirect effect used 5,000 case-resampling bootstrap draws and percentile confidence intervals.

For DTA prediction, labels were constructed from the full course period, whereas predictors were restricted to the common early calendar window. A stratified 70% development/30% held-out split was created once with seed 20260523. Median imputation and standardization were implemented inside each scikit-learn pipeline and fitted only on the relevant training fold. The held-out observations were never used for preprocessing, learner selection, hyperparameter selection, or feature-configuration selection.

Within the 70% development set, five-fold stratified grid search compared elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, and histogram-based gradient boosting using ROC-AUC. The logistic grid used C∈{0.1, 1, 3} and l1_ratio∈{0, 0.5, 1}; random forest used n_estimators∈{200, 500}, max_depth∈{3, 5}, and min_samples_leaf∈{2, 5}; histogram-based gradient boosting used learning_rate∈{0.03, 0.1}, max_iter∈{100, 200}, and max_leaf_nodes∈{15, 31}. The selected pipeline was refitted on the complete development set and evaluated once on the untouched test set. A repeated five-fold analysis with the locked learner and hyperparameters was used only as a descriptive stability analysis, not as a second independent test. The 30%, 40%, and 50% calendar-window sensitivity analysis used the same locked specifications. Python analyses used scikit-learn with seed 20260523 in a CPU environment.

Analytical comparisons

Comparison with existing methods

The comparison was organized around theoretically interpretable baseline models. At the survey level, the technology-acceptance-only path served as a baseline explanation of engagement. At the behavioral level, the activity-only trace model served as the main baseline, and augmented configurations added recovery and persistence indicators.

Because no established public benchmark defines the same survey constructs, Copilot text labels, and DTA outcome targets together, we used two complementary comparison strategies. First, the survey and text models were compared against theoretically simpler paths, such as technology-acceptance-only and activity-only explanations. Second, the DTA experiment compared 24 learner-feature configurations formed by three candidate learners and four feature configurations across two prediction targets. Ablation study

The ablation study evaluates the contribution of each feature group. The Baseline model uses activity and progress features. Baseline + Reflective is retained as the configuration name in result files but adds recovery-related variables; it is not a measured reflection construct. Baseline + SE-related adds behavioral persistence approximations, and the Full Model combines the unique variables.

For final-success prediction, Baseline + Reflective showed a 0.018 held-out Macro-F1 difference. The original repeated analysis showed a mean difference of 0.006, whereas the calendar-window rerun under the current locked pipeline showed a 40% difference of 0.001. This sensitivity to the evaluation run, together with the very small Brier differences, indicates statistical rather than practical importance. No intervention benefit or educationally meaningful gain is inferred.

The SE-related behavioral indicator group provided weaker evidence. For final success, it had similar ROC-AUC and slightly higher PR-AUC than Baseline in the held-out split, but Macro-F1 and Brier score were worse. These mixed differences do not support a stable advantage.

For activity-defined engagement prediction, none of the augmented feature sets materially improved the activity-only Baseline. Repeated cross-validation produced almost identical ROC-AUC values across configurations. The complete repeated cross-validation results are reported in Table 11. This pattern reflects the label definition: baseline features already represent the same activity domain. The target is retained only as a positive activity benchmark and boundary test, not as an independent test of psychological engagement.

Experimental analysis

Generalization analysis

Robustness was examined in three ways. First, the survey model was compared across item, parcel-latent, parcel-scale, and ordered-item sensitivity specifications. Second, the text analysis used a separate Copilot context and excluded blank responses. Third, DTA prediction used an untouched test set, a descriptive repeated analysis, and 30%–50% course-calendar windows. These checks assess sensitivity within modules; they do not establish participant-level or contextual generalization across the independent datasets.

The survey direct paths TA → RU, RU → PSE, TA → ENG, and RU → ENG were consistent with the theoretical rationale, but the indirect effects were not significant. In the Copilot analysis, neither the TA → RUItext path nor the text-based indirect effect was significant. DTA recovery features produced a small held-out difference and negligible average window-sensitivity differences. RAIAF is therefore a cautious organization of complementary evidence, not a universal mediation model.

Permutation importance was used to describe the behavioral trace models. For activity-defined engagement prediction, the most important features were early active days, early active weeks, and early unique tasks solved, reflecting the construction of the activity-based outcome. The second design parameter is the activity-defined engagement threshold. The 67th percentile avoids an overly rare positive class, but its choice is conventional and the resulting label overlaps with activity predictors.

The third design choice is feature fusion. Recovery-related features produced slightly larger held-out differences than the SE-related group for final success, but neither augmented group showed a stable practical advantage. The Full Model did not consistently outperform simpler configurations.

The fourth design choice is the model family. RAIAF is not a neural-backbone method. It uses conventional classifiers to keep the behavioral trace pipeline interpretable. Therefore, parameter analysis focuses on early-window size, label construction, and feature-group design rather than neural backbone comparison.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the survey sample was fixed at N = 131 and was likely underpowered for small indirect effects. Parceling substantially improved fit over the item-level MLR model and can conceal item-level misspecification. Although parcels followed predefined subdimensions and their loadings were strong, item-level CFI and TLI were weak, TA–ENG HTMT exceeded 0.85, and the item-level WLSMV sensitivity solution was inadmissible. The parcel results therefore require replication with a prospectively powered sample and a validated measurement model. Second, none of the indirect effects was significant. The contribution is limited to selected direct associations, including TA → RU, RU → PSE, TA → ENG, and RU → ENG, rather than a serial mediation process.

Third, the Copilot text analysis remains exploratory despite the addition of independent double human coding. Exact agreement was 57.6%, linear-weighted Cohen's κ was 0.421, and Krippendorff's ordinal α was 0.495. These estimates indicate limited initial agreement, especially at the boundaries between adjacent categories, and do not justify treating RUItext as a fully validated scale. Disagreements were resolved by consensus for analysis, but consensus does not replace the initial reliability evidence. In addition, coding was conducted from Chinese translations of the English responses; although programming tokens were preserved, translation may have reduced some semantic nuance. Future studies should refine and pilot the category boundaries before full coding and validate the measure in larger multilingual samples. Fourth, the Copilot technology-acceptance measure shows a specific ceiling effect: 28.9% of participants received the maximum score and 81.6% scored at least 4.5 on the five-point scale. This restricted variance likely attenuated associations involving technology acceptance and may have contributed to the non-significant text-based paths.

Fifth, DTA does not measure AI use, reflection, or self-efficacy directly; its recovery and persistence variables are behavioral approximations from a different programming context. Sixth, the activity-defined engagement label overlaps with baseline predictors and does not provide an independent psychological criterion. Seventh, the held-out predictive differences are small, and the window-sensitivity differences are negligible; no practical intervention benefit has been demonstrated.

Finally, the study is based on secondary datasets. Although this design improves reproducibility, it limits control over task design, measurement timing, and intervention conditions. The DTA trace dataset is especially useful for examining programming behavior, but it should not be interpreted as a direct observation of AI-assistant use. Future research should collect longitudinal data that combine real-time AI interactions, validated self-efficacy scales, process-level reflection measures, and experimental or quasi-experimental reflection prompts.

Conclusions

This study examined reflective AI use as an educational-psychological issue in AI-supported programming. The survey and Copilot modules addressed AI-supported contexts, while the independent DTA module supplied complementary programming-behavior evidence only. In the survey, technology acceptance predicted reflective use and engagement, and reflective use predicted programming self-efficacy and engagement. The indirect effects were not significant, and the text results did not replicate the proposed paths. The proposed mechanism was therefore only partially supported.

The behavioral trace results were more limited. Recovery-related features produced a small held-out difference for final completion, but repeated and calendar-window analyses showed negligible average gains. For activity-defined engagement, baseline activity features were already strong because the label used overlapping activity indicators. These results do not demonstrate practical educational benefit or validate reflective AI use.

Future work should extend this study in three directions. First, longitudinal data should be collected to examine how students' acceptance, reflection, and self-efficacy change over time. Second, future studies should include richer AI interaction logs, such as prompts, generated code, revision traces, and feedback responses. Third, text-based reflective-use coding should be improved through pilot calibration, clearer boundary rules, independent multilingual coding, and stronger intercoder reliability. Experimental or quasi-experimental designs could also test whether reflection prompts embedded in AI programming tools can strengthen students' reflective use and improve later learning outcomes.

Overall, this study shows that AI-supported programming learning should not be evaluated only by whether students accept or frequently use AI tools. A more useful perspective is to examine how students respond to AI support, whether they revise and learn from feedback, and when such behaviors improve learning outcomes. RAIAF provides a transparent and reproducible step toward connecting educational-psychological constructs with behavioral trace evidence.
