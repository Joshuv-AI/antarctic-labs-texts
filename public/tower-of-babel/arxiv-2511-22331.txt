On the Condition Number Dependency in Bilevel Optimization
Lesi Chen, Jingzhao Zhang

Abstract
Bilevel optimization minimizes an objective function, defined by an upper-level problem whose feasible region is the solution of a lower-level problem. We study the oracle complexity of finding an ϵ \epsilon -stationary point with first-order methods when the upper-level problem is nonconvex and the lower-level problem is strongly convex. Recent works (Ji et al., ICML 2021; Arbel and Mairal, ICLR 2022; Chen el al., JMLR 2025) achieve a 𝒪 ~ ​ ( κ 4 ​ ϵ − 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\kappa^{4}\epsilon^{-2}) upper bound that is near-optimal in ϵ \epsilon . However, the optimal dependency on the condition number κ \kappa is unknown. In this work, we establish a new Ω ⁡ ( κ 2 ​ ϵ − 2 ) \Omega(\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{-2}) lower bound and 𝒪 ~ ​ ( κ 7 / 2 ​ ϵ − 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\kappa^{7/2}\epsilon^{-2}) upper bound for this problem, establishing the first provable gap between bilevel problems and minimax problems in this setup. Our lower bounds can be extended to various settings, including high-order smooth functions, stochastic oracles, and convex hyper-objectives: (1) For second-order and arbitrarily smooth problems, we show Ω ( κ y 13 / 4 ϵ − 12 / 7 ) \Omega(\kappa_{y}^{13/4}\epsilon^{-12/7}) and Ω ( κ 17 / 10 ϵ − 8 / 5 ) \Omega(\kappa^{17/10}\epsilon^{-8/5}) lower bounds, respectively. (2) For convex-strongly-convex problems, we improve the previously best lower bound (Ji and Liang, JMLR 2022) from Ω ⁡ ( κ / ϵ ) \Omega(\kappa/\sqrt{\epsilon}) to Ω ⁡ ( κ 5 / 4 / ϵ ) \Omega(\kappa^{5/4}/\sqrt{\epsilon}) . (3) For smooth stochastic problems, we show an Ω ⁡ ( κ 4 ​ ϵ − 4 ) \Omega(\kappa^{4}\epsilon^{-4}) lower bound.

Introduction

We study the first-order oracle complexity for solving the bilevel optimization

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}}F({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})),\quad{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})=\arg\min_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}).

(1)

The goal is to minimize the hyper-objective
F({\bm{x}})
, which is implicitly defined by two explicit objectives
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
and
g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
and the
\arg\min
operator in
g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
with respect to
{\bm{y}}
.
This formulation originates from the two-player general-sum Stackelberg game
[
57
]
, and reflects the sequential nature of the decision process of two players
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
. Bilevel optimization of this form got extensive attention in the machine learning community due to its wide applications, such as meta-learning
[
54
]
, hyper-parameter tuning
[
8
,
19
,
46
]
, generative adversarial networks
[
22
]
, and reinforcement learning
[
33
,
63
,
25
]
.

To ensure that Problem (
1
) is well-defined, we follow
[
21
,
28
,
25
]
to study the standard nonconvex-strongly-convex (NC-SC) setting, where the lower-level function
g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is strongly convex in
{\bm{y}}
while the upper-level function
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
can be nonconvex.
In NC-SC bilevel problems, a first-order method applied to the
hyper-objective
F({\bm{x}})
acts as a second-order method on
g

because

\displaystyle\nabla F({\bm{x}})=\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}}))-\nabla_{xy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}}))[\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}}))]^{-1}\nabla_{y}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})).

(2)

In large-scale problems, directly inverting the Hessian matrix
\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is very expensive. There are mainly two lines of work to address this issue.
The first line studies algorithms based on Hessian-vector product (HVP) oracles. Among them, the best-known
theoretical guarantees
[
28
,
4
]
show that they
can find an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
F({\bm{x}})
with
{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{4}\epsilon^{-2})
and
{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{9}\epsilon^{-4})
first-order oracle complexity for smooth deterministic and stochastic problems, respectively,
where
\bar{\kappa}_{y}
is the global condition number for the problem (see Definition
2.2
). In addition,

59
showed an improved upper bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{13/4}\epsilon^{-7/4})
for second-order smooth deterministic problems.

Since HVP oracles are more expensive than gradient oracles,
another line of work

[
44
,
42
,
43
,
41
,
35
,
55
,
45
,
53
]

directly uses gradients to optimize
F(x)
by solving the penalty function:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+\lambda(g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})),

(3)

where
\lambda=\Omega(\epsilon^{-1})
is a large penalty coefficient.
Among them,

35

developed the first fully first-order method that can find an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
F({\bm{x}})
with
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}({\rm poly}(\bar{\kappa}_{y})\epsilon^{-3})
first-order oracles for smooth deterministic problems.
In a follow-up work,
13
improved the complexity to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{4}\epsilon^{-2})
, which achieves the near-optimal dependency on
\epsilon
. This result was also extended to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{15/4}\epsilon^{-7/4})
for second-order smooth deterministic problems
[
13
]
, and to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{11}\epsilon^{-6})
for smooth stochastic problems
[
34
,
13
]
.

Although both HVP-based and gradient-based methods can achieve (near)-optimal
\epsilon^{-2}
rates for NC-SC bilevel problems, their upper bounds have high
\bar{\kappa}_{y}
dependencies compared with
NC-SC minimax problems, which are special bilevel problems with
f=-g
. For instance,

40
proposed an algorithm with a
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}}\epsilon^{-2})
complexity for smooth NC-SC
deterministic minimax problems, where
\kappa_{y}\leq\bar{\kappa}_{y}
is the condition number of the lower-level problem (see Definition
2.2
). Under the same setting,

37
,
66
proved a nearly matching lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}}\epsilon^{-2})
.
However, the state-of-art methods
for deterministic smooth NC-SC bilevel problems requires an
{\mathcal{O}}(\bar{\kappa}_{y}^{4}\epsilon^{-2})
complexity.
Given this, we wonder
whether NC-SC bilevel problems are provably more challenging than NS-SC minimax problems measured by the condition number dependency.

1.1
Additional Related Work

In addition to the works discussed above, we further review the closely related work on lower bounds, including lower bounds for minimization, minimax, and bilevel problems.

Lower bounds for minimization problems

In the textbooks,
48
,
49
established tight lower bounds for convex optimization using chain-like quadratic functions. Recently,
11
,
12
further abstracted the above idea into the concept of zero-chain as a generic approach for proving lower bounds. By carefully constructing zero-chains,
11
proved the tight
\Omega(\epsilon^{-p/(p+1)})
lower bound for

p
th-order methods to find
\epsilon
-stationary points of
p
th-order smooth nonconvex functions. In the same series of work,
12

proved
\Omega(\epsilon^{-12/7})
and
\Omega(\epsilon^{-8/5})
lower bounds for
first-order methods to optimize second-order smooth and arbitrarily smooth nonconvex functions, respectively.
Many advancements were made following these seminal works.
6
proved the tight
\Omega(\epsilon^{-4})
and
\Omega(\epsilon^{-3})
lower bounds for nonconvex stochastic first-order optimization under the general expectation and the mean-squared smooth setting, respectively.

18
,
39
proved the tight
\Omega(n+\sqrt{n}\epsilon^{-2})
lower bound for nonconvex finite-sum first-order optimization;
5
proved
the tight
\Omega(\epsilon^{-7/2})
and
\Omega(\epsilon^{-3})
lower bounds for first-order stochastic nonconvex optimization on second-order smooth objectives, and
high-order stochastic nonconvex optimization on arbitrarily smooth objectives, respectively.

Lower bounds for minimax problems

Inspired by the lower bounds in minimization problems, researchers also proved tight lower bounds for (first-order) minimax optimization under many setups.

47
gave tight
\Omega(\epsilon^{-1})
lower bound for convex-concave minimax problems.
52
,
65
proved the tight

\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}/\epsilon})
and

\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{\kappa_{x}\kappa_{y}})

lower bounds for C-SC and SC-SC problems, respectively.

24
extended those results to the finite-sum setting.

37
,
66
proved the tight
\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}}\epsilon^{-2})
lower bound for nonconvex-strongly-convex (NC-SC) problems.
Their results have also been extended to

\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}}\epsilon^{-12/7})
,
\Omega(n+\sqrt{n\kappa_{y}}\epsilon^{-2})
, and

\Omega(\kappa_{y}^{1/3}\epsilon^{-4})
for
second-order smooth problems
[
58
]
,
finite-sum problems
[
66
]
, and
general stochastic problems
[
37
]
, respectively.

Lower bounds for bilevel problems

Although the lower bounds for both minimization and minimax problems have been extensively studied, we find there are only a few works on the lower complexity bounds of bilevel optimization, except for the following ones. In their novel work,

27
proved lower bounds for (S)C-SC bilevel problems instead of the more common NC-SC problems.
The established the lower bound of
\tilde{\Omega}(\min\{\kappa_{y},\epsilon^{-3/2}\}/\sqrt{\epsilon})
and

\tilde{\Omega}(\kappa_{y}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}})

for C-SC and SC-SC setups, respectively.

34
established tight upper and lower bounds for
r
-
{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})
-aware optimization,
where the oracle only gives
r
-estimates of
{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})
along with
r
-locally reliable gradients. This setting recovers stochastic bilevel optimization if
r=+\infty
, but requires
r={\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
, which is different from the setting of bilevel optimization with standard oracles.

14
considered bilevel problems without lower-level strong convexity, and constructed a hard instance such that any first-order algorithm will get stuck at
{\bm{x}}_{0}
within finite iterations.
17
proved a lower bound for finite-sum NC-SC problems, but the
\Omega(n+\sqrt{n}\epsilon^{-2})
lower bound misses the condition number dependency and is subsumed by the
\Omega(n+\sqrt{n\kappa_{y}}\epsilon^{-2})
lower bound from NC-SC minimax optimization
[
66
]
.

Notations

For a vector
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
we let
x_{i}
be its
i
th coordinate; define its support by
{\rm supp}({\bm{x}})=\{i\mid{\bm{x}}_{i}\neq 0\}
and its progress by
{\rm prog}=\max\{i\geq 0\mid{\bm{x}}_{i}\neq 0\}
. We use
\|\,\cdot\|
to denote both the Euclidean norm of a vector and the operator norm of a matrix/tensor.
Given an event
A
, we let
{\bm{1}}_{A}
denote its indicator function. We let
{\bm{e}}_{i}
be the vector with its
i
th element being
1
and all the other elements being
0
.
Given functions
f,g:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
, we use
f={\mathcal{O}}(g)
to denote there exists a numerical constant
C<+\infty

such that
f(x)\leq Cg(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
f=\Omega(g)
to denote
g={\mathcal{O}}(f)
, and
f=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(g)
to denote
f={\mathcal{O}}(g\max\{1,\log g\})
. We also denote
f\asymp g
when
f={\mathcal{O}}(g)
and

both holds.

2
Problem Setup

In this section, we introduce the bilevel function class considered in this paper, the algorithm class to which our lower bound applies, and the complexity measurement of the algorithms.

2.1
Function Class

Our main focus is the general high-order smooth nonconvex-strongly-convex (NC-SC) bilevel problems as follows, where the case
p=1
is introduced by
21
, and the case
p=2
is recently introduced by
26
.

Definition 2.1

(
p
th-order smooth NC-SC problem)
.

Given
p\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
,
L_{0},\cdots,L_{p+1}>0
,
\Delta>0
, and
\mu_{y}\in(0,L_{1}]
,
we use
{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{nc-sc}}(L_{0},\cdots,L_{p+1},\mu_{y},\Delta)
to denote the set of all bilevel problems
(f,g)
, where the upper-level function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and lower-level function
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
satisfy the following assumptions:

1.

f
have
L_{q}
-Lipschitz continuous
q
th-order derivative for all
q=1,\cdots,p
; and
g
have
L_{q}
-Lipschitz continuous
q
th-order derivative for all
q=1,\cdots,p+1
, that is, for every
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{q}f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})-\nabla^{q}f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{q}\|({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})-({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})\|,\quad q=0,\cdots,p~;

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{q}g({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})-\nabla^{q}g({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{q}\|({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})-({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})\|,\quad q=1,\cdots,p+1.

2.

For any fixed
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
,
g
is
\mu_{y}
-strongly convex in
{\bm{y}}
, that is, for any
{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}
,

\displaystyle g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{2})\geq g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{1})+\langle\nabla_{y}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{1}),{\bm{y}}_{2}-{\bm{y}}_{1}\rangle+\frac{\mu_{y}}{2}\|{\bm{y}}_{1}-{\bm{y}}_{2}\|^{2}.

3.

The hyper-objective
F({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}}))
in Eq. (
1
) is lower bounded and

\displaystyle F({\bm{0}})-\inf_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}}F({\bm{x}})\leq\Delta.

For these problems, we define the lower-level and global condition numbers
\kappa_{y}
and
\bar{\kappa}_{y}

as follows. By definition, we have
\kappa_{y}\leq\bar{\kappa}_{y}
.

Definition 2.2
.

For the
p
th-order smooth NC-SC function class in Definition
2.1
, we define
\kappa_{y}:=L_{1}/\mu_{y}
as the condition number of
g
and
\bar{\kappa}_{y}:=\bar{L}/\mu_{y}
as the global condition number of the bilevel problem, where
\bar{L}=\max_{q=0,\cdots,p+1}L_{q}
.

In our lower bound analyses, we construct bilevel problems with quadratic lower-level functions, which are formally defined in Definition
2.3
below.
Note that for this subclass, we do not make assumptions on the boundedness of
L_{0}
since both the lower and upper bounds do not depend on it.

Definition 2.3

(Quadratic lower-level problem)
.

For
p\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
,
L_{1},\cdots,L_{p}>0
,
\mu_{y}\in(0,L_{1}]
, and
\Delta>0
, we denote

{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{nc-scq}}(L_{1},\cdots,L_{p},\mu_{y},\Delta)\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{nc-sc}}(+\infty,L_{1},\cdots,L_{p},0,\mu_{y},\Delta)

as the subset of NC-SC problems, where the lower-level function takes the form of

\displaystyle g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{J}}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{b}}^{\top}{\bm{y}},

(4)

where
\mu_{y}{\bm{I}}_{d_{y}}\preceq{\bm{H}}\preceq L_{1}{\bm{I}}_{d_{y}}
and
-L_{1}{\bm{I}}_{d_{y}}\preceq{\bm{J}}\preceq L_{1}{\bm{I}}_{d_{y}}
.

From a complexity theory perspective, it is natural to consider quadratic functions as optimizing convex quadratics is usually as hard as general convex functions
[
49
, Theorem 2.1.13]
. Therefore,
understanding the complexity of quadratic problems is essential to tackle more complex setups.
From a practical perspective, this problem class already covers many useful applications in reinforcement learning, meta-learning, robust optimization, and graph neural network training, as detailed in Appendix
A
.

2.2
Algorithm Class

Following the seminal framework proposed by
48
, we model the algorithmic complexity by the number of oracle accesses, where first-order oracles reveal the gradient information at the query points, defined as follows.

Definition 2.4

(First-order oracle)
.

Let
f
and
g
be two continuous differentiable functions. We define the first-order oracle
for the bilevel problem
(f,g)

as the mapping
{\mathbb{O}}^{\rm fo}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=(\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\nabla_{y}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
.

We then define the (deterministic) first-order algorithms as follows, which
generalizes the algorithm classes defined
in
[
1
,
5
,
11
,
6
]

from a single objective to two objectives.
Our definition
encompasses almost all existing first-order algorithms for bilevel optimization
[
41
,
55
,
35
,
45
]
.

Definition 2.5

(First-order algorithm)
.

A first-order algorithm
A
consists of a sequence of measurable mappings
\{\texttt{A}^{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\texttt{A}^{t}
takes in the first
t-1
oracle responses to product the
t
-th query
{\bm{w}}^{t}=({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t})
, such that
{\bm{w}}^{0}=({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}})
and

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{t}=\texttt{A}^{t}\left({\mathbb{O}}^{\rm fo}({\bm{w}}^{0}),\cdots,{\mathbb{O}}^{\rm fo}({\bm{w}}^{t-1})\right),\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}.

(5)

We denote
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\rm fo}}
as the set of all deterministic algorithms that follow protocol (
5
).

In addition to first-order algorithms, all of our lower bounds also apply to the
following algorithm class in Definition
2.7
, which includes many standard HVP-based algorithms
[
21
,
28
,
25
]
.
These algorithms introduce an additional variable
{\bm{v}}
that tracks
-(\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))^{-1}\nabla_{y}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
via gradient updates on the quadratic function
\frac{1}{2}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{v}}-\nabla_{y}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})^{\top}{\bm{v}}
and then use the approximate hyper-gradient
\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+\nabla_{xy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{v}}
to update
{\bm{x}}
. Formally, we define the deterministic HVP oracle and HVP-based methods as follows.

Definition 2.6

(HVP oracle)
.

Let
f
be differentiable and
g
be twice differentiable. We define the HVP oracle for the bilevel problem
(f,g)
as the mapping

\displaystyle{\mathbb{O}}^{\rm hvp}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{v}})=(\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\nabla_{y}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\nabla_{xy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{v}},\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{v}}).

Definition 2.7

(HVP-based algorithm)
.

An HVP-based algorithm
A
consists of a sequence of measurable mappings
\{\texttt{A}^{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\texttt{A}^{t}
takes in the first
t-1
oracle responses to product the
t
-th query
{\bm{w}}^{t}=({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t},{\bm{v}}^{t})
, such that
{\bm{w}}^{0}=({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}},{\bm{0}})
and

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{t}=\texttt{A}^{t}\left({\mathbb{O}}^{\rm hvp}({\bm{w}}^{0}),\cdots,{\mathbb{O}}^{\rm hvp}({\bm{w}}^{t-1})\right),\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}.

(6)

We denote
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\rm hvp}}
as the set of all deterministic algorithms that follow protocol (
5
).

Definition
2.7
subsumes the hyper-gradient-based algorithm class previously defined in
[
27
, Definition 2]
.
Since the HVP and gradient oracles for quadratic functions are equivalent, proving lower bounds for first-order algorithms on lower-level quadratic problems implies that the same lower bound applies to HVP-based algorithms. Formally, we have the following statement.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
g
is a quadratic function of the form (
4
), then

{\mathcal{A}}^{{\rm hvp}}={\mathcal{A}}^{\rm fo}
.

Proof.

If
g
is a quadratic function of the form (
4
), then we have that
\nabla_{y}g({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}})={\bm{b}}
,
\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})={\bm{H}}
, and
\nabla_{xy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})={\bm{J}}
. Therefore,
it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\nabla_{yy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{v}}={\bm{H}}{\bm{v}}=\nabla_{y}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}})+\nabla_{y}g({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}}),\\
\nabla_{xy}^{2}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{v}}={\bm{J}}{\bm{v}}=\nabla_{x}g({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}}).\end{cases}\end{split}

(7)

Then,
for any
sequence
\{({\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{y}}^{0},{\bm{v}}^{0}),\cdots,({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t},{\bm{v}}^{t})\}
generated by an
HVP-based algorithm that satisfies Eq. (
6
), we know from the above equation that the expanded sequence
\{({\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{y}}^{0}),({\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{v}}^{0}),\cdots,({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t}),({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{v}}^{t})\}
satisfies Eq. (
5
), which means that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hvp}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm fo}
.

Conversely
, for any sequence
\{({\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{y}}^{0}),\cdots,({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t})\}
generated by a first-order algorithm that satisfies Eq. (
5
), we also
know from Eq. (
7
) that the expanded sequence
\{({\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{y}}^{0},{\bm{y}}^{0}),\cdots,({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t},{\bm{v}}^{t})\}
satisfies Eq. (
6
), which means that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm fo}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hvp}
.

∎

2.3
Complexity Measure

The ultimate goal of bilevel optimization is to optimize the hyper-objective
F({\bm{x}})
implicitly defined in Eq. (
1
). Given that
F({\bm{x}})
is nonconvex in general, finding its approximate minima requires exponential complexity in the worst case
[
48
]
. Hence, we follow
[
11
,
12
,
6
]
to measure the complexity of an algorithm by the number of oracle calls to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point.

Definition 2.8
.

We call
a point
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
if

\displaystyle\|\nabla F({\bm{x}})\|\leq\epsilon.

Definition 2.9
.

Given a bilevel function class
{\mathcal{F}}
and the associated first-order oracle
{\mathbb{O}}
, we define the worst-case complexity of an algorithm class
{\mathcal{A}}
to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point as

\displaystyle{\rm Compl}_{\epsilon}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{O}})=\sup_{(f,g)\in{\mathcal{F}}}\inf_{\texttt{A}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\inf\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid\mathbb{E}\|\nabla F({\bm{x}}^{t})\|\leq\epsilon\},

where
\{{\bm{z}}^{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
{\bm{z}}^{t}=({\bm{x}}^{t},{\bm{y}}^{t})
is the sequence generated by
A
running on
(f,g)
.

3
Lower Bounds

In this section, we establish new lower bounds for bilevel optimization. We begin in Section
3.1
by introducing a generic framework for deriving these lower bounds. We then apply this framework to obtain lower bounds across various setups. Specifically, Section
3.2
presents lower bounds for deterministic nonconvex-strongly-convex (NC-SC) and (S)C-SC problems under different orders of smoothness. Section
3.3

uses a slightly different construction to prove lower bounds for
randomized and stochastic algorithms in NC-SC problems.

3.1
Construction Overview

We construct hard instances with chain-like structures to prove optimization lower bounds.
Such functions are known as zero-chains, which are
formally introduced by
11
as an abstraction of the idea in
[
49
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Zero-chain)
.

A function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{T}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a (first-order) zero-chain if

\displaystyle{\rm supp}({\bm{x}})\subseteq\{1,\cdots,t-1\}~~\Longrightarrow~~{\rm supp}(\nabla f({\bm{x}}))\subseteq\{1,\cdots,t\},\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}.

We say a coordinate is activated if it becomes non-zero from zero. As shown in
[
7
,
11
]
, if
f({\bm{x}})
is a zero-chain, then for any deterministic algorithm
A
, one can adversarially rotate the
f({\bm{x}})
such that
A
running on the rotated function can only activate its coordinates one by one.
This leads to a generic recipe for proving lower bounds: Construct a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{T}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

that meets two criteria: (1) it is a zero-chain, and (2) a point
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
cannot be an
\epsilon
-solution unless its
T
-th coordinate is activated.

We apply this framework to prove lower bounds for bilevel problems, with the intention of maximizing the chain length
T

while ensuring that the constructed instance remains
in the function class of interest.
To achieve this goal, we
make novel changes to the constructions in (near)-optimal NC-SC minimax lower bounds

[
37
,
58
]
, amplifying their

\kappa_{y}
dependency by leveraging the bilevel structure.

Figure 1:
(a) The construction for NC-SC minimax lower bound
[
37
,
58
]
. (b) The construction for our NC-SC bilevel lower bound. An orange node or arrow represents a coordinate or connection in the upper-level, while the black represent the ones in the lower-level. A bold node indicates that a nonconvex regularization is added on the corresponding coordinate.

First, we recap the constructions for NC-SC minimax problems in
[
37
,
58
]
.
Let
\bar{f}_{\nu,r}^{\rm nc}:{\mathbb{R}}^{T}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be the NC zero-chain
[
12
]
for minimization problems (see Definition
3.2
).

37

{\bm{x}},{\bm{z}}
as the minimization (upper-level) variables and
{\bm{y}}
as the maximization (lower-level) variable, and constructed functions such that
(1) every progress from
z_{i}
to
x_{i}

requires traversing a lower-level strongly-convex sub-chain with length
K=\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}})
; (2)
the hyper-objective

has similar
properties to
\bar{f}^{\rm nc}_{\nu,r}({\bm{x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{T}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
.
Such a construction leads to a zero-chain with a total length
KT

The analysis in
[
12

indicates that the largest possible value of

that ensures

and

under first- and second-order smoothness, which leads to the lower bounds of

and

[

In this work, we modify the previous construction in NC-SC minimax optimization as follows (also illustrated in Figure
1
):

1.

We move
{\bm{z}}
from the upper to the lower level, and let the lower-level connection in
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{z}}
lead to
{\bm{z}}^{*}({\bm{x}})=K^{2}{\bm{x}}

f({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}},{\bm{y}})
fixed, we can
still have the freedom to manipulate the lower-level function
g({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}},{\bm{y}})
.

2.

We carefully design the connections of
{\bm{x}},{\bm{z}},{\bm{y}}
(the triangles in Figure

instead of
{\bm{x}}

with a careful modification in both

and

by a factor of

Our hard instance characterizes two sources of condition number dependencies in the complexity of bilevel problems:
First, we use the variable
{\bm{y}}
to couple sub-chains into the zero-chain in
{\bm{x}}

\kappa_{y}
dependency from the computation of

Using this hard instance, we can prove a set of lower bounds for NC-SC bilevel problems.
If we let

3.2.1

Lemma 3.1

(

The functions
\Upsilon_{r}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{f}^{\text{nc-sc}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}},{\bm{y}})&:=\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{2}\left(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}-1\right)^{2}+\nu\sum_{i=1}^{T}\Upsilon_{r}\left(\frac{z_{i}}{\sqrt{K}}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}h({\bm{z}},{\bm{y}})},\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{\rm sc}(x_{1},x_{2},{\bm{y}})&:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}(y_{i}-y_{i+1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2K^{2}}\|{\bm{y}}\|^{2}-\left(x_{1}y_{1}-x_{2}y_{K}\right)\\

.

The hard instance

1.

3.3

4.1
Algorithm Overview

The main challenge of designing fully first-order method for bilevel optimization is that the algorithm only has access to first-order oracles of

To address this issue, we follow

\nabla F

\lambda

Lemma 4.1

Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we provide lower bounds and accelerated fully first-order algorithms in NC-SC bilevel optimization. For deterministic first-order smooth problems, we prove an
\Omega(\kappa_{y}^{2}\epsilon^{-2})

Let

n

Example A.1

Given
T

i

({\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm tr},{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\rm tr})
and a testing set
({\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm test},{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\rm test})

{\bm{G}}_{i}^{\rm test}=\left({\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm test}\right)^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm test}

{\bm{G}}_{i}^{\rm tr}=\left({\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm tr}\right)^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm tr}

We consider the meta-learning for linear regression

\displaystyle\quad\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm tr}{\bm{y}}_{i}^{*}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\rm tr}\|^{2},

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\quad{\bm{y}}_{i}^{*}({\bm{x}})=\arg\min_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm test}{\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\rm test}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}\|^{2}.

In this example, the hyper-objective
\varphi({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}}))
is (strongly) convex.

Proof.

The lower-level solution has a closed form

\displaystyle\quad{\bm{y}}_{i}^{*}({\bm{x}})=({\bm{G}}_{i}^{\rm test}+\lambda{\bm{I}}_{d})^{-1}\left(\lambda{\bm{x}}+\left({\bm{A}}_{i}^{\rm test}\right)^{\top}{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\rm test}\right).

Plugging the above equation into the upper-level function gives the closed form of
\varphi({\bm{x}})
, which is a convex quadratic function because since its Hessian matrix is positive (semi)definite:

\displaystyle\quad{\bm{H}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\lambda^{2}({\bm{G}}_{i}^{\rm test}+\lambda{\bm{I}}_{d})^{-1}{\bm{G}}_{i}^{\rm tr}({\bm{G}}_{i}^{\rm test}+\lambda{\bm{I}})^{-1}\succeq{\bm{O}}_{d}.

.
∎

A.2
Nonconvex-Strongly-Convex Examples

The NC-SC bilevel problems have many applications in the actor-critic method
[
33
]
, which is a class of important algorithms in reinforcement learning. For this algorithm class, there is an actor in the upper level that optimizes a (typically nonconvex) cost function to select a good policy, and a critic in the lower level that evaluates by estimating the Q-function. The Q-function can be calculated based on the Bellman equation, which corresponds to solving a strongly convex function in the basic linear Markov decision process (MDP) setup.

Formally, we define the MDP as a tuple with five elements
(S,A,\gamma,P,R)
, where
S
is the state space,
A
is the action space,
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor of the rewards,
P
defines the transition kernel such that
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
denotes the probability of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
after taking action
a

in state
s
, and
R
defines the reward function such that
R(s,a)
denotes the immediate reward received after taking action
a
in state
s
.
Let
Q^{\pi}
denote the Q-function for policy
\pi
.
Under the linear setup,
Q^{\pi}
is assumed to have a linear representation
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\langle\theta^{\pi},\phi(s,a)\rangle
, where
\theta^{\pi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the weight vector of parameters and
\phi(s,a)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the
feature vector for the state-action pair
(s,a)
. Then we can show that the induced bilevel optimization problem is nonconvex-strongly-convex with a quadratic lower-level problem.

Example A.4

As in the proof of NC-SC lower bound, our construction of
{\bm{P}}={\rm diag}({\bm{P}}_{x},{\bm{P}}_{z},{\bm{P}}_{y})
ensures that
any first-order deterministic algorithm with
t\leq n^{\prime}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}^{t}_{i}=0,\quad\forall i=\lfloor T/2\rfloor+1,\cdots,T,

where
n^{\prime}=\lfloor T/2\rfloor(K+1)-K=\Omega(KT)
. This further gives the following lower bound of the hyper-gradient norm:

\displaystyle\|\nabla F({\bm{P}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{t})\|\geq\mu_{x}\|{\bm{P}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{t}-{\bm{x}}^{*}\|\geq\mu_{x}\|{\bm{P}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{t}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu_{x}\left(\|{\bm{P}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{t}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{*}\|-\|\tilde{\bm{x}}^{*}-\hat{\bm{x}}^{*}\|\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu_{x}\left(\frac{\beta q^{\lfloor T/2\rfloor+1}(1-q^{T-\lfloor T/2\rfloor})}{(1-q)K^{3/2}}-\frac{\beta q^{T+1}}{\alpha K^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{x}Dq^{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}}{2}\left(1-\left(1+\frac{1-q}{\alpha K^{3/2}}\right)q^{T-\lfloor T/2\rfloor}\right)\geq\frac{\mu_{x}Dq^{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}}{8}.

In the above, the first inequality uses the
\mu_{x}
-strong convexity of
F({\bm{x}})
, the second last inequality uses
\hat{x}_{i}^{*}=\beta q^{i}/K^{3/2}
and Eq. (
34
), and the last one uses the condition of
T
in Eq. (
35
).
To let
\|\nabla F({\bm{x}}^{t})\|>\epsilon
we require that

\displaystyle T\leq 2\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{x}D}{8\epsilon}\right)\Big/\ln(q^{-1}).

(36)

Recall that
K=\Theta(\sqrt{\kappa_{y}})
and
\alpha^{-1}=\Theta\left(\kappa_{y}^{3/2}\kappa_{x}\right)
. We can derive that

\displaystyle\ln(q^{-1})\geq\frac{q}{1-q}=\frac{2+\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+4\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+4\alpha}-\alpha}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+4\alpha}-\alpha}-1\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-1,

(37)

where the last inequality uses
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
.
If the accuracy
\epsilon
satisfies that
\epsilon\lesssim\alpha\mu_{x}D
, or equivalent to
\epsilon\lesssim\mu_{x}D/(\kappa_{x}\kappa_{y})
, then we know there exists
T
that jointly fulfills
Eq. (
35
) and (
36
). Now,
we let

be the smallest value that fulfills Eq. (
36
), and by Eq. (
37
), we have

T=\Omega(\kappa_{y}^{3/4}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}}\ln(\mu_{x}D/\epsilon))
, which implies the final lower bound of

\displaystyle\Omega(KT)=\Omega\left(\kappa_{y}^{5/4}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}}\ln(\mu_{x}D/\epsilon)\right).

∎
