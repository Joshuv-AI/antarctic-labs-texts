Qualitative evaluation of the Mentor Mothers’ programme in Nigerian military hospitals: the path to meeting PMTCT targets
Authors: Josephine Moshe Ibu, Tracy Mbuba Ibu, Akon Emmanuel Ndiok, Kator David Igbudu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) of HIV remains a global public health priority aimed at eliminating paediatric HIV infections and improving maternal health outcomes. The WHO advocates a comprehensive four-pronged approach: primary prevention of HIV among women of reproductive age, prevention of unintended pregnancies among women living with HIV, prevention of HIV transmission from mothers to infants and provision of treatment, care and support to women living with HIV, their infants and families. Despite significant progress in expanding PMTCT services in low- and middle-income countries, implementation challenges persist, especially in military hospital settings. Nigerian military hospitals, like others in sub-Saharan Africa, operate dual service systems for military and civilian populations, often straining available resources. Although well-organised, hierarchical command structures, frequent staff redeployments and competing operational priorities disrupt service continuity and integration with maternal and child healthcare. Consequently, programme delivery and follow-up remain inconsistent. This study explored factors that facilitate or hinder implementation of the Mentor Mothers (MMs) programme in military health facilities.MethodsA descriptive exploratory qualitative design was employed to examine PMTCT implementation through the MMs programme in three military hospitals in Abuja. 18 participants were purposively selected, including three physicians, 3 PMTCT focal nurses, 6 MMs and 6 patient participants enrolled in PMTCT care. Data were gathered through six key informant interviews and two focus group discussions. Thematic analysis (TA) was used to identify patterns and relationships within the data.FindingsFacilitators of effective PMTCT implementation included strong peer support networks, improved confidence among MMs, ongoing training and supervision, effective facility leadership and positive nurse–client relationships. Key challenges identified were fear of stigma leading to non-disclosure, limited male partner involvement, cultural barriers and intermittent gaps in resources and programme monitoring.ConclusionThe MMs programme enhances PMTCT adherence and psychosocial support in Nigerian military hospitals. Achieving sustained impact requires stigma reduction, male partner engagement, strengthened supervision and institutionalised mentorship to advance Nigeria’s elimination of mother-to-child transmission goals.Keywords: HIV, Community Health, Health Services Accessibility, Public Health Practice, Qualitative Research

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Nigeria has one of the highest HIV/AIDS burdens globally, with significant rates of new paediatric HIV infections and faces challenges in preventing mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) of HIV. This necessitates innovative approaches to improve PMTCT outcomes. The Mentor Mothers (MMs) programme entails using mothers living with HIV to provide peer support, education and counselling to other mothers diagnosed with HIV to improve PMTCT outcomes. This programme has been implemented in Nigerian military hospitals as part of broader efforts to enhance PMTCT services.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study contributes to the body of knowledge by offering context-specific insights into the facilitators and barriers affecting the implementation and sustainability of the MMs programme within Nigerian military health facilities. It identifies key enablers such as enhanced self-efficacy among MMs, strong peer support systems, effective leadership, ongoing training and positive staff–patient interactions, all of which support the success of the programme. Additionally, it sheds light on persistent challenges, including stigma-related non-disclosure of HIV status, limited male partner involvement and cultural norms that hinder engagement with PMTCT services. By focusing on military hospitals, a setting often under-represented in PMTCT research, this study provides rare and practical evidence that can guide the adaptation, scaling and sustainability of peer-led HIV interventions in similar contexts.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

The new findings imply that there is still a challenge on how the PMTCT impact can be achieved especially at community level. There is a need to invest in a country-specific responsibility to provide an integral link between communities and health facilities, using innovative strategies to support measures that address how the limiting factors identified could be mitigated to achieve PMTCT targets.

Background

Despite Nigeria expanding prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) services among pregnant women and integrating them into antenatal care (ANC), significant gaps remain, especially in the northern part and among the poor. These include low male partner involvement, ongoing stigma, poor retention in care and limited access in rural areas. The purpose of the Nigerian Department of Defence (DoD) Mentor Mothers (MMs) programme is to improve retention in care and adherence to scheduled visits among pregnant women living with HIV and their infants. The programme supports the operationalisation of the existing DoD policies on the PMTCT of HIV, which are designed to address the majority of paediatric HIV infections that occur through vertical transmission. To this end, the Nigeria DoD MM programme provides structured peer support and community-based interventions to strengthen PMTCT service delivery.

Recent studies have affirmed that MMs (women living with HIV) who have successfully navigated PMTCT services are instrumental in improving care retention, adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) and early infant diagnosis (EID) outcomes through targeted psychosocial and experiential support.1 These peer supporters are trained to provide education, encourage appointment attendance and offer emotional support, thereby addressing both behavioural and structural barriers to PMTCT success.2 The MM model aligns with global best practices (such as ‘Community Health Workers (CHWs) and Lay Cadres’, ‘Male Partner Engagement’, ‘Community ART Groups and Mother–Baby Pairing Models’, ‘Stigma Reduction and Community Sensitization’, ‘Client Feedback and Co-creation’, for differentiated service delivery (DSD) and community engagement to enhance maternal and child health outcomes in high-burden HIV settings. In alignment with the National Agency for the Control of AIDS’s National HIV & AIDS Strategic Plan 2023–2027, these global best practices in DSD and community engagement have been prioritised to enhance maternal and child health outcomes, particularly in the context of PMTCT. This strategic framework outlines Nigeria’s latest policy direction and national response priorities for HIV/AIDS from 2023 to 2027, focusing on prevention, treatment, care and impact mitigation. It is more aligned with current implementation practices, including the role of MMs, DSD and integration with broader health systems. Data from the Nigerian Military HIV Research Programme, (which pioneered the MM strategy in military hospitals), show that MM activities were initiated in the first quarter of 2014 and became fully operational by the second quarter of 2015 in Nigeria DoD health facilities. This intervention was necessitated by the persistently high burden of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) of HIV within the military population and its surrounding communities. Despite such efforts, recent studies continue to underscore the challenges in retention across the PMTCT cascade. Evidence from Nigeria and similar settings reveals that while initial uptake of services may be high, significant drop-offs occur due to structural, individual and sociocultural barriers at each stage—from HIV testing and treatment initiation to infant diagnosis, follow-up and retesting.3 Adherence to clinical advice and ART regimens is often undermined by stigma, fear of disclosure, lack of partner support and facility-level inefficiencies,4 reinforcing the need for context-specific, community-driven interventions like the MM programme to enhance engagement and continuity of care.

Central to tackling these challenges in the military hospitals is the utilisation of effective peer support through the MMs programme. The DoD MM intervention therefore served to complement the human resource workforce in HIV care to achieve PMTCT targets using mothers who themselves had benefited from PMTCT in the military health facilities.5 Because of the significance of the MM programme for PMTCT, this study explored the intervening conditions that facilitate or hinder the implementation of the MMs programme in the DoD health facilities.6

Methods

Conceptual framework

The socioecological model (SEM) was used in this study as a guiding framework7 to examine the multilayered factors influencing the implementation and effectiveness of the DoD MMs programme in Nigerian military health facilities. At the individual level, barriers such as limited HIV knowledge, low self-efficacy, fear of disclosure and poor adherence to ART were identified, which the MMs helped to address through peer support and counselling. Interpersonally, lack of partner support and family influence emerged as critical factors affecting women’s consistent engagement with PMTCT services, while the MM programme served to reinforce support where it was lacking. At the community level, stigma, discrimination and cultural beliefs regarding HIV and infant feeding practices were seen to hinder service uptake, with MMs playing a key role in normalising care and reducing stigma. Institutional factors such as the availability of trained personnel, integration of services and leadership support within military hospitals were identified as enablers of programme success. At the policy level, the alignment of the MM initiative with national HIV/AIDS strategies and military health protocols provided a strong foundation for its implementation. The SEM thus enabled a comprehensive understanding of the facilitators and barriers at each level of influence, highlighting where targeted interventions could enhance the programme’s impact.

Research design

A descriptive exploratory qualitative design was employed in this study using key informants’ interviews and two focus group discussions. This was to ensure a representative sample and adequately explore the facilitating factors and hindrances to the MM programme in the military health facilities. Data were analysed using TA. The manuscript has been developed in accordance with the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research.8

Research setting

The study was conducted in three military hospitals in Abuja, namely the Guards Brigade Medical Centre, the Nigeria Airforce Base Hospital and the DHQ Medical Centre. There are 22 medical facilities under the DoD located across Nigeria, with the Army maintaining 12 hospitals, the Navy having 4 hospitals and the Air Force 6 hospitals spanning primary, secondary and tertiary level services. Although they all provide services for PMTCT of HIV, not all of them have engaged the services of MMs, which is why only the facilities using MMs were selected for this study. The choice of the selected hospitals in Abuja was strategic - Abuja is the administrative headquarters of the Nigerian military medical corps where the MM programme was initiated in 2014. The selected hospitals are all located within military barracks in Abuja and represent the three branches of the Nigerian Armed Forces - Army, Navy and Air Force.

The facility type or size was considered based on its capacity to conform to international best practices.9 The determination that the selected hospitals met international ‘best practices’ was informed by their adherence to key WHO standards9 and the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief site-level monitoring indicators.10 These include the availability of PMTCT services, trained health personnel, integration of HIV testing into ANC and consistent provision of ART as well as structured peer support integration, continuous MM training, confidentiality safeguards and stigma reduction strategies.

Study population

A maximum variation sampling approach11 was considered in selecting the participants from one health facility each of the Army, Air Force and the Defence Headquarters medical facilities in Abuja that provided the MMs programme. The study population included nurses, doctors, pregnant women living with HIV (enrolled in the PMTCT programme) and MMs who themselves had benefited from PMTCT in the military health facilities.12

Sample

A sampling framework was developed based on the relationships between the participants as team players in the DoD MM programme. 18 participants comprised of 3 doctors, 3 nurses (1 each from the selected hospitals), 6 MMs and 6 pregnant women living with HIV (2 each from the selected hospitals), enrolled in the PMTCT programme were approached face to face and recruited for this study. This was to ensure diverse perspectives and data richness, and to gather perspectives on resource allocation, supervision and policy adherence. The sample, however, was selected from this population based on specific inclusion criteria such as stage in the PMTCT cascade, willingness to participate and availability during the study period. None dropped out of the study.

These participants met the characteristics suited for the study as they were engaged either at the level of the MM/PMTCT provision or consumers of the programme, and their willingness to participate in the research. Eligibility criteria were that data would be obtained only from the military healthcare facilities that offer the MMs programme and from participants who have a direct contact with/participation in the programme for a minimum of 6 months (for the patients), and a minimum of 1 year (for the doctors and nurses and the MMs). The MMs provided first-hand insights on implementation, challenges and their lived experiences, contributing to clinical outcomes, adherence and retention in care. PMTCT focal nurses were responsible for coordinating PMTCT activities and supervising MMs. Physicians provided clinical services, oversight and were directly involved in HIV/PMTCT care. The patients were enrolled in PMTCT services, ideally supported by an MM and validated the role and impact of MMs from the beneficiaries’ point of view.

Data collection

Data were collected using semistructured interview and focus group discussion guides. They were developed by JMI, after a literature review13 (see onlinesupplemental files 1 2). These instruments were designed to elicit participants’ experiences, perceptions and roles in the implementation of PMTCT and the MM support framework. The interviews were held at the various hospitals’ conference rooms at the participants’ convenience. Key informant interviews were conducted with doctors and nurses, while focus group discussions were held with the MMs and pregnant women living with HIV, enrolled in the PMTCT programme. Interview sessions lasted between 30 and 45 minutes each, were conducted in English language and audio recorded with the participants’ permission. English is the predominant language in Nigeria, and most of the women accessing PMTCT care in these settings have a basic understanding of it. However, for participants with limited proficiency, communication was occasionally facilitated using simplified or broken (pidgin) English with the assistance of trained interpreters fluent in both English and the local dialect of the affected participant(s) to ensure understanding and clarity.

Despite the heterogeneous predetermined sample size, data saturation was observed among the different cohorts of participants as they all had the same (good) knowledge about the DoD MM program. The researcher employed reflexivity throughout the research process. JMI, who was a nurse anaesthetist had interacted severally with clients living with HIV and was undertaking a doctoral programme at the time. However, her interaction and knowledge did not interfere with the process of data collection or influence the report of findings that emerged from the study in any way. The development of the interview guides was informed by the WHO health systems responsiveness tool,14 the15 key informants survey tool, and the health systems performance assessment tool16.

Data analysis

NVivo V.12 was used to manage data. Data were read many times to ensure that the researchers were familiar with the identified concepts. This allowed for a proper understanding of the meanings the participants ascribed to their constructs, then transcribed verbatim and analysed using the TA technique17 18 . Similarities and differences within the constructs were identified which were then aggregated to extract themes and subthemes from the data set. This was achieved by discussing the codes with an independent analyst to arrive at a consensus on the code names. Saturation was monitored by comparing new data against existing themes. When subsequent interviews no longer produced new codes or themes, saturation was considered reached. Through an iterative process, the TA technique exemplified the link between subthemes and the key themes and how these impacted on the DoD MM programme. The findings were summarised under each theme in a narrative fashion using pertinent literature to confirm and/or challenge the research findings.

Results

A total of 18 participants took part in the study, comprising 6 healthcare providers, 6 MMs and 6 patient participants enrolled in the PMTCT programme. The distribution of participants by category is illustrated in figure 1, which shows an equal representation (33.3%) across the three participant groups. The healthcare providers included medical officers and PMTCT focal nurses with professional experience ranging from 4 to 22 years, and between 4 and 13 years of involvement in PMTCT-related services.

Distribution of participants by category. This pie chart illustrates the proportional representation of three key stakeholder groups involved in the MM (peer mentor) intervention in Nigerian military hospitals, with each group contributing equally: Patient participants (33.3%)- women aged 26–34 years, on antiretroviral therapy for 6 months to 4 years, including newly diagnosed pregnant women and mothers in long-term care; peer support workers (33.3%) representing MMs with 2–5 years’ experience in PMTCT and healthcare providers - medical officers and PMTCT focal nurses (33.3%) with 4 - 22 years of professional experience, and 4–13 years in PMTCT service.

The MMs, who served as peer support workers, had between 2 and 5 years of experience in providing psychosocial support and adherence counselling to PMTCT clients. All had undergone formal training in PMTCT service delivery. The patient participants were women aged between 26 and 34 years, all enrolled in PMTCT care. Their duration on ART ranged from 6 months to 4 years, with varying family contexts—some newly diagnosed and pregnant, while others had one or two children and had been in care for several years. This diverse mix of participants provided rich insights into PMTCT service implementation and the MM support framework across different levels of engagement.

The findings from the study were organised and summarised into key thematic areas, as presented below:

Peer support, improved self-efficacy and motivation

Support from MMs was generally perceived by PMTCT enrollees as more significant than healthcare staff support. The awareness and the realisation that they possessed the ability to alter the risk of vertical transmission of the virus enhanced adherence to PMTCT protocols.19 For example, MMs who had been involved with the PMTCT protocols consistently reported that it had given them increased confidence and empowerment; citing practical beneficial outcomes such as healthy babies, healthy mothers and improved ability to negotiate for family planning. Similarly, the enrollees (patients) reported that knowledge and improved self-efficacy and motivation gained from the MMs was a consequence of their close relationship with those experiencing a similar experience in a positive manner. MMs were perceived by the patients and some healthcare staff as being better able to communicate with patients about the PMTCT protocols, mainly because their personal experience was felt to increase their credibility, leading to high rates of retention and compliance to clinical care among patients:

To be sincere, no mentor mothers, no PMTCT, and no EID. Without mentor mothers, all these things I mentioned will not work. We devote ourselves to making sure our work is well done. Since I started, none of my children has turned positive. … And most of the husbands trust me… because I am working with the knowledge I have. (Mentor Mother)

During the lecture, she advised me a lot and I was very happy. …. (Patient)

The complementary role of the MMs significantly enhances the delivery of PMTCT services by offering consistent, experience-based peer support to women living with HIV across the continuum of care. Drawing from their own lived experiences with HIV and successful PMTCT navigation, MMs are uniquely positioned to build trust with clients, address fears and misconceptions and provide emotional reassurance. They assist women in understanding the importance of timely antenatal visits, adherence to ART, safe infant feeding practices and EID. This support extends beyond the clinical setting into the community, where MMs often conduct follow-ups and help women overcome barriers such as stigma, disclosure challenges and partner non-involvement. Their role bridges the gap between patients and formal health workers, encouraging women to remain engaged in PMTCT services and adhere to recommended guidelines. As one of the beneficiaries explains…

the encouragement and information they give to one is necessary because a lot of people just feel that because you are positive your child is positive too. (Patient)

Leadership

Leadership was one of the key performance indicators that facilitated the implementation of the MM programme in the military hospitals. Leadership has been portrayed as the conduct of an individual when overseeing the activities within an enterprise toward a shared goal.20 The critical feature of the leadership role, therefore, involves guiding group activities and adapting to change.21 Effective leadership by PMTCT care professionals becomes vital in modern healthcare settings. This is against the backdrop of the continuous drive to improve the quality of healthcare provision in an environment where demands and need for increased efficiency and productivity thrive22 and was determined by asking the stakeholders to specifically describe what has enabled the successful implementation of the programme in the DoD:

The leadership has been there whenever I seek approval to do some things…they allow me to do that either through guided, quicker reviews of my proposals and my requests. (Doctor)

Skills acquisition and specific training

Findings revealed that the programme staff, particularly the MMs, acquired a diverse range of skills through multiple channels. A key source of their competence was their personal lived experiences with HIV, which provided them with deep empathy, practical insight and credibility when supporting other women navigating PMTCT services. In addition to this experiential knowledge, they benefited from formal training on HIV and PMTCT protocols, where they learnt about the clinical aspects of prevention, maternal and infant care and antiretroviral adherence. Their capacity was further enhanced through regular review meetings, which served as platforms for peer learning, feedback and performance reflection. They also attended workshops and seminars provided by implementing partners such as United States Agency for International Development (USAID), the Institute of Human Virology Nigeria and the Nigerian Ministry of Defence Health Implementation Programme. These trainings focused on areas such as HIV counselling, documentation and reporting, psychosocial support and confidentiality in service delivery. They were also regularly updated with periodic information on evolving guidelines, emerging best practices and new innovations in HIV care. Moreover, many MMs and programme staff developed data literacy skills, enabling them to track client outcomes, complete documentation accurately and contribute to the monitoring and evaluation of programme performance. This combination of personal experience and structured capacity-building positioned the staff to deliver more effective, empathetic and data-informed PMTCT support…

I have been going to Top Rank Hotel for training and retraining like PMTCT training, adherence counselling training, drug reaction training and so far, so good. (Mentor Mother)

We teach them to facilitate peer support within mother support group members where they discus coping skills and promotion of positive living. (Doctor)

Hindrances to the Mentor Mothers’ programme

Structural and operational challenges

Despite the considerable and well-coordinated efforts by the Nigerian DoD, many participants identified numerous barriers hindering the effective implementation and facilitation of the MM programme for PMTCT. These challenges included limited funding, understaffing, concerns around job security and wages, inadequate resources and complex power dynamics compounded by competing internal and external demands on service delivery.

MMs and some healthcare staff expressed that insufficient resources, particularly funding and staffing, as well as time constraints, were major obstacles that limited the health system’s responsiveness to patient needs. Several participants also described the healthcare facilities as inadequate, which impeded their ability to deliver accessible and comfortable care. Staff shared feelings of frustration when practical limitations prevented them from offering additional, inclusive or discreet services. Furthermore, both internal and external constraints such as cultural and religious beliefs, leadership structures and organisational policies were seen to influence the DoD’s capacity to meet population needs effectively. Overall, participants noted that due to the scale and structure of service delivery, particularly at the larger health facilities, this added complexities in responding promptly and effectively to patient needs at the point of care. This was reflected in their perceptions:

It is a Nigerian problem and not peculiar to the Ministry of Defense or military hospitals…our Teaching Hospitals settings are also not encouraging. There’s manpower shortage and doctors and nurses get fatigued. (PMTCT Doctor)

The non-availability of space is a problem. I just find a convenient place because there is no office for a mentor mother. (Mentor Mother)

Challenges related to HIV status non-disclosure and men’s role in PMTCT

Non-disclosure of HIV status to partners was a major barrier hindering the effective implementation of the MMs programme, particularly at the family level. Women who had not disclosed their HIV status often expressed deep fears of rejection, domestic violence, blame or abandonment, leading them to conceal their HIV diagnosis. In some instances, women reported hiding their medications,23 avoiding taking ART in the presence of their partners or lying about clinic visits—practices that ultimately disrupted adherence to PMTCT protocols. For example, one participant shared that she stored her medication inside a multivitamin container and only took it when her spouse was away, fearing that disclosure would result in her being sent back to her parents.

Some of them would tell you they have not confided in their husband. Disclosure has been a big problem. (PMTC focal person)

Another participant admitted that her partner could refuse to accompany her to the clinic once he suspected she might be HIV positive, reinforcing her decision to remain silent.

On the positive side, the women acknowledged that MMs played a vital role in facilitating disclosure by providing emotional support, building trust and even mediating conversations between couples. One participant recounted how an MM accompanied her during a disclosure meeting with her husband, which resulted in increased support and shared responsibility in attending clinic visits. Women who successfully disclosed their status often reported improved treatment adherence, increased male involvement in PMTCT and a sense of relief from no longer living in secrecy. Nevertheless, persistent fear, cultural norms and lack of disclosure counselling continued to limit open communication, thus affecting the full benefits of PMTCT care.

…my husband is not aware. I just told the mentor mother that she should not let him know for now. (Patient)

The non-involvement of men in PMTCT care was also an issue of great concern for some of the participants. Most of them reported low rates of men’s involvement in PMTCT protocols. Therefore, they advocated for men’s involvement in PMTCT and MMs programmes. Some of the reasons given by participants for low male engagement with the programme were conflicting working hours and patients’ non-disclosure issues. However, patients who had disclosed their status cited examples of significant advantageous male behaviour change in areas such as family planning, taking advice from MMs, HIV testing and condom use:

…the first thing they told me was to bring my husband here. So, we did the counselling together. (Patient)

In the hospital where I was before, once you get to 4 months and you have not brought your husband to the clinic, they will stop attending to you until you bring your husband… that was what saved me because I did not know my husband was positive. I would never have known. I would have had my baby and put him at risk. Getting the men involved is helpful. (Patient)

A salient point that emerged from the findings was the challenge many women faced in disclosing their HIV status and specifically their HIV medication to their spouses. This issue was deeply rooted in the fear of stigma and rejection, highlighting the pervasive sense of shame associated with being identified as an HIV-positive mother, particularly outside the supportive environment of the hospital.24 For the majority of the participants, taking antiretroviral medication at home meant confronting not just the physical realities of treatment, but also the emotional weight of secrecy. As a result, secrecy became a coping mechanism, with several women admitting to hiding their medications, skipping doses when their partners were around or storing pills in concealed places. This behaviour, while protective in intent, often posed a risk to treatment adherence and overall health outcomes. The burden of having to manage their illness privately, without the emotional or practical support of a partner, added psychological strain to an already vulnerable situation. These findings underscore the urgent need for partner-inclusive HIV education, disclosure counselling and stigma-reduction interventions, particularly within military and conservative community settings where gender norms and confidentiality concerns are heightened:

Some of them do not like disclosing to their partners and that hinders our counselling the partner. So how do we manage their drugs? I met one yesterday and asked, ‘how are you taking your drug’? She said she stores it in a drug sachet and tells her husband it is her antenatal drug. (PMTCT focal person)

Cultural and community behavioural norms

A few of the MMs and patients reported that some cultural, traditional practices and beliefs prevent adherence to PMTCT protocols, citing stigmatisation, witchcraft, traditional birth options, traditional medicines and religious factors as barriers that hinder the implementation of the programme…

Because they feel that people who know them will recognize them, especially when the people involved live in the same community with them; they would not want to be seen when coming to access their drugs rather they will go to another hospital to assess their care due to stigmatization. (PMTC focal person)

The mentality of the women inside the barracks is different from that of the civilian women so I think they really need to work on the women in this community. I have seen a case of one woman whose 3 children are positive today because she didn’t want to go to the hospital. So, I think the women in this area really need to be educated on HIV very well. (Patient)

Although most of the PMTCT providers reported low rates of stigma and discrimination associated with HIV/AIDS in the health facilities, the patients felt otherwise and stressed the need for raising community awareness about HIV. They felt that stigma was, for the most part, associated with lack of awareness and knowledge about HIV, stating that stigma and discrimination were more prevalent within extended family and the community (barracks) than in the facilities. While patients greatly valued the significant role MMs played in reducing stigma both within communities and healthcare facilities, the findings of this study also revealed that elements within the healthcare system including some health workers were still perceived as sources of stigma and discrimination. The doctors and PMTCT focal nurses admitted that certain routine practices, such as designated clinic days, separate pharmacies for PMTCT clients and medication refill procedures, along with their own attitudes, might have been misinterpreted by patients as stigmatising. Although none of the MMs were identified by either patients or staff as contributing to stigma or discrimination, some patients did recount negative experiences with laboratory personnel, which they interpreted as discriminatory and stigmatising.

…I wanted to give my urine sample to the person that asked me to go and urinate, I just said ‘madam’ and she just shouted, ‘I beg, I beg, you want to infect all of us here’? And I felt bad. (Patient)

This study further revealed that cultural beliefs and communal behavioural norms continue to pose significant threats to the success of the PMTCT programme, particularly at the community level. These deeply ingrained attitudes, such as associating HIV with moral failure, discouraging open conversations about the disease and promoting silence around sexual and reproductive health, create an environment where stigma flourishes and misinformation thrives. Such norms not only discourage women from accessing services but also isolate those who are already enrolled in the programme, limiting their ability to adhere to treatment or disclose their status. In some cases, the fear of community backlash or being labelled as promiscuous25 has led women to discontinue care or avoid health facilities altogether, thus endangering both maternal and infant health. As a result, many participants strongly advocated for intensified HIV education and awareness campaigns targeted at community members, including men, religious leaders and traditional gatekeepers. They emphasised the importance of using culturally sensitive approaches to dispel myths, reduce stigma and promote a more supportive environment that encourages uptake and continuity of PMTCT services:

There are so many women that have this virus but they don’t want to come out, they keep giving birth to HIV positive children because of the feeling that ‘I don’t want my neighbor to see and hear about me and gossip’. I have seen a case of one woman whose 3 children are positive today because she didn’t want to go to the hospital… the women in this area really need to be educated on HIV very well. (Patient)

These incidences, as illustrated above, typically occur when stigma and discrimination become overwhelming barriers to the success of HIV care and treatment programmes. When women fear being judged, ostracised or even abandoned by their families or communities due to their HIV status, they may withdraw from formal healthcare systems altogether. In such contexts, the perceived risk of social exposure often outweighs the perceived benefits of medical care, especially in settings where confidentiality is not guaranteed. This climate of fear and mistrust can drive some women to seek alternatives that they believe offer greater privacy and emotional safety, most notably traditional birth attendants (TBAs). As reported by several participants, TBAs were often viewed as more discreet, less judgmental and more accessible within the community, providing what some patients interpreted as ‘better security’. However, while TBAs may offer emotional comfort, reliance on them instead of trained health professionals poses serious risks to both mother and child, particularly in the context of HIV and PMTCT. This highlights the urgent need for community-based stigma-reduction efforts and integration of culturally trusted figures into formal HIV care pathways.

Because they feel that people who know them will recognize them, especially when they come to access their drug, they would go to another hospital to assess their care for fear of stigmatization. (PMTCT focal nurse)

Discussion

As confirmed by study participants, the Nigerian DoD MMs intervention has played a pivotal role in enhancing PMTCT service delivery across military health facilities. The programme was widely credited with improving maternal retention in care, adherence to ART, uptake of EID and promoting male involvement in PMTCT services. These findings align with recent evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of peer-led interventions in increasing ART adherence and maternal–infant health outcomes.26 Skills acquisition and specific training were foundational to the success of the (MMs) programme, particularly within the structured environment of military hospitals. The programme equips MMs who are often women living with HIV (with lived experiences) with the knowledge, counselling skills and confidence necessary to support peers navigating the PMTCT cascade. Targeted training in areas such as data management, ART adherence counselling, EID, confidentiality and stigma reduction enables MMs to function not only as peer supporters but also as competent extensions of the clinical team. These competencies enhance their ability to deliver consistent and empathetic support to women enrolled in PMTCT care, improving client retention, treatment adherence and uptake of critical services.

Effective leadership within military health facilities further strengthens the implementation of the MM programme by providing structure, accountability and support. Strong leadership fosters an enabling environment for MMs to thrive, ensuring their integration into the broader healthcare team, encouraging collaboration with clinical staff and facilitating continuous supervision and capacity building. The DoD MM programme leadership recognises the value of the MM programme, and allocates resources, advocates for its sustainability and ensures alignment with national HIV prevention goals. In the military hospitals, where hierarchical systems are pronounced, committed leadership played a pivotal role in normalising peer-led interventions and in bridging the gap between professional healthcare providers and community-based support, ultimately accelerating progress towards achieving PMTCT targets.

However, despite the reported success of the MM strategy, many MMs described their roles as demanding, primarily due to the ongoing burden of HIV-related stigma, discrimination and societal non-acceptance within their communities. These challenges, and their own psychosocial well-being, often affected both their ability to operate effectively, underscoring the need for stronger community sensitisation and structural support. The study therefore highlights several practical insights to further improve PMTCT outcomes within the DoD health facilities, including formal integration of MMs into health teams, psychosocial support for peer workers and expanded antistigma community interventions.

Again, this study echoes the arguments from the social ecological framework,7 27 whereby hindrances to successful PMTCT delivery are shaped by a range of interrelated factors, including individual-level behaviours, peer and family influences, community context and broader sociocultural influences. Recent studies reaffirm that ‘community context’ encompasses prevailing social norms, attitudes and practices such as stigma, discrimination and gender inequality which continue to pose significant barriers to women’s ability to access and adhere to HIV treatment within PMTCT programmes.28 Stigma, in particular, remains a prominent community-level factor that discourages disclosure, limits social support and reduces uptake of antenatal HIV services.4 29 Additionally, religious beliefs and traditional practices still influence infant feeding decisions, often leading to deviations from PMTCT guidelines and compromising the effectiveness of prevention strategies.30 These findings highlight the continued relevance of adopting multilevel, context-sensitive interventions that address sociocultural dynamics in order to improve maternal and child health outcomes in PMTCT programmes.

Individual-level barriers to effective PMTCT engagement include limited self-efficacy, low perceived risk of HIV transmission and inadequate understanding of treatment.6 Recent studies have shown that despite high general awareness of HIV/AIDS across many Nigerian communities, knowledge related to specific prevention and treatment practices including timely administration of antiretrovirals such as Nevirapine remains insufficient, often contributing to poor adherence and suboptimal outcomes.31 Additionally, misinformation or lack of health literacy continues to hinder readiness to use available free HIV counselling and testing services. It becomes necessary to enhance existing educational strategies by expanding outreach, incorporating context-specific content and adopting participatory learning approaches to improve understanding and engagement with PMTCT services.32

Peer and family influences are also critical in shaping PMTCT involvement, with evidence indicating that lack of partner support significantly decreases women’s likelihood of sustained engagement in care.33 34 Gender norms further complicate this dynamic by limiting women’s ability to disclose their status or involve their partners in antenatal and PMTCT-related care, ultimately reinforcing barriers to adherence and continuity.35 36 These findings reinforce the need for context-sensitive interventions that empower women, engage male partners and enhance community-level education to improve PMTCT service uptake and retention.

HIV status disclosure to male partners is a critical component for optimal outcomes in PMTCT;1 however, the web of secrecy and silence demonstrated by some participants in this study is pervasive in the HIV environment despite the many awareness campaigns and engagement with the community.37 Relatedly, the fear of stigma, divorce and intimate partner violence discourages women from disclosing to male partners, undermining partner support, adherence to HIV care and increasing the risk of MTCT.38 While some may receive love and support from the people they tell, others may not be that accepted.39 Thus, non-disclosure makes it more difficult for a woman to adhere to PMTCT protocols as compared with those whose partners are aware of the spouse’s status and that Nevirapine is being administered to an infant.28 In the context of PMTCT care, male partner involvement implies men accompanying spouses to ANC care clinics, undertaking an HIV test as stipulated in the ANC routine and providing financial and psychological support to the spouse. Thus, the nature of men’s involvement could be described as positive when the men commit totally (in every aspect of PMTCT care emotionally, financially and practically) in the PMTCT interventions not only financially, but also by way of reminding the spouse of clinical schedules, medication and provision of needed resources. This commitment is crucial for shaping effective policies and interventions that enhance treatment adherence, care retention, EID and overall PMTCT outcomes.40 41 It also ensures compliance which is significant in advancing HIV education, facilitates disclosure, reduces stigma and discrimination of people living with HIV.42

The findings of this study also reveal notable divergences between the perspectives of healthcare providers (doctors and nurses) and MMs regarding the implementation of the PMTCT programme within Nigerian military health facilities. These variations underscore the complex interplay between institutional, interpersonal and sociocultural factors influencing programme delivery and outcomes. First, while doctors and nurses primarily identified clinical and system-level barriers such as insufficient staffing, delays in service delivery and inconsistent data documentation as key challenges to PMTCT effectiveness, MMs emphasised individual and community-level obstacles. These included fear of HIV disclosure, stigma within families and communities and lack of partner support. Such divergences reflect the different vantage points each group occupies within the healthcare system: health professionals engage patients through structured service delivery, while MMs interact more intimately with clients’ lived experiences in both clinical and community settings.

Second, differences were observed in how each group perceived the effectiveness and value of the MM programme. Healthcare providers often described MMs as valuable but supplementary to formal care, focusing on their role in patient tracing, adherence encouragement and health education. In contrast, MMs viewed themselves as central to PMTCT success, offering not only information but also emotional and psychosocial support through shared lived experiences, which they believe fosters trust and sustained engagement in care. This aligns with findings from studies by Carbone et al,43 who report that peer-led interventions are often perceived by beneficiaries as more accessible and relatable than professional care alone.

Another divergence relates to prioritised solutions for improving PMTCT outcomes. Health workers tended to recommend enhancements in logistics, increased human resources and improved training as critical areas for intervention. MMs, however, emphasised the need for greater community engagement, improved male involvement and formal recognition of their roles, including consistent remuneration and inclusion in decision-making processes—recommendations echoed in previous studies on community health interventions in sub-Saharan Africa.44

Furthermore, in understanding patient motivation and behaviour, healthcare professionals sometimes attributed missed appointments or non-adherence to poor health literacy or deliberate non-compliance. In contrast, MMs contextualised such behaviours within broader social vulnerabilities, including fear of stigma, emotional trauma and misinformation. These observations mirror findings by Ndambo et al,45 who argue that a community-informed lens is essential to understanding health-seeking behaviour in HIV care.

Finally, the divergent perceptions regarding role recognition were striking. While health professionals viewed the MM programme as supportive but external to clinical responsibilities, MMs expressed concerns about being undervalued and excluded from formal health system structures, despite their pivotal role in improving patient retention and adherence. This points to a need for better integration of lay health workers within formal healthcare teams, a recommendation also emphasised by WHO46 in its guidance on DSD. These divergent perspectives underscore the importance of fostering collaboration and mutual understanding among stakeholders at all levels of service delivery. Integrating insights from both professional and peer-led cadres can lead to more holistic, context-sensitive approaches to PMTCT, ultimately strengthening outcomes for mothers and their infants.

Limitations

This study adopted a qualitative research design, where the purpose is not statistical generalisation but analytical or theoretical generalisation. In qualitative inquiry, the emphasis lies in the depth, diversity and richness of data, rather than numerical representativeness. The 18 participants included in this study—comprising physicians, PMTCT focal nurses, MMs and pregnant women living with HIV - were purposively selected to represent key stakeholders across the PMTCT cascade within DoD health facilities. This multilevel inclusion ensured triangulation of perspectives, which is consistent with the principles of qualitative rigour as outlined by, 47 and .48 The sample size achieved data saturation, as no new themes emerged after the final interviews, indicating adequacy for qualitative exploration.

However, due to the highly regimented and intelligence-restricted structure of the Nigerian Military, the interviewer (JMI) faced limited access to their health facilities and participants, contributing to the small sample size, leading to under-representation of key policy and implementation stakeholders, thereby hindering detailed responses from some participants. To ease access, a knowledgeable research assistant from one of the facilities was engaged. The military’s stringent non-disclosure policy on HIV-related issues, coupled with the scarcity of relevant data and literature on the implementation of the MMs programme for PMTCT within their hospitals, limited the breadth and generalisability of the study’s findings. Furthermore, language barriers occasionally required the use of interpreters fluent in English, pidgin English and local dialects to ensure effective communication during interviews.

Nonetheless, several strengths enhance the credibility and value of the study. First, the use of multiple qualitative data sources - key informant interviews and focus group discussions - allowed for data triangulation, strengthening the depth and validity of insights. Second, the inclusion of diverse participant categories (healthcare providers, MMs and women enrolled in PMTCT care) provided a broad and balanced range of perspectives. Third, the study team ensured that participants were not excluded based on their language proficiency, and language was not used as a selection criterion. Instead, efforts were made to ensure inclusivity and equitable participation across linguistic backgrounds. This approach helped mitigate selection bias, making sure that meaning was preserved during translation and transcription by adopting a back-translation approach and cross-checking transcripts for accuracy. This enhanced the credibility and transferability of the findings. The study was conducted in military health facilities actively implementing the MM programme, an under-researched context, which offers unique and practical insights into real-world implementation. Measures were also taken to ensure rigour, including the use of field notes and observation of verbal and non-verbal cues during interviews. Findings, which were member-checked for accuracy, revealed critical implementation gaps based on participants’ direct accounts. As a country with a high HIV burden, Nigeria still faces significant challenges in achieving elimination of MTCT (eMTCT) of HIV. The MM programme remains a promising strategy for reaching national eMTCT targets.

Conclusion

The findings from this study have shown that the programme has not been without some challenges. There is a great need, therefore, to expand access to information about HIV and ensure that these are accessible to all groups, particularly those vulnerable to HIV.49 Although the Nigerian DoD MM programme was mostly reported by all the participants as beneficial to improving retention and adherence to clinical care, the challenge still remains as to how quality and impact can be achieved at community level. The need to invest in a country-specific responsibility to provide an integral link between communities and health facilities, using innovative strategies such as the need for enhanced training and capacity-building among MMs not necessarily due to gaps in formal education, but rather to ensure they are equipped with the specific skills, competencies and up-to-date knowledge required to function effectively within the complex institutional and clinical settings of military hospitals. This distinction is critical, as it reinforces the importance of continuous professional development in strengthening peer-led interventions and improving PMTCT outcomes, independent of educational background.
