The quantity and severity of adverse drug reactions experienced by patients with multi-drug resistant tuberculosis in the Ugu District
Authors: Serisha Ramasir, Frasia Oosthuizen, Varsha Bangalee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMDR-TB treatment has a history of being associated with a lengthy regimen accompanied by numerous adverse drug reactions (ADRs). This notion results from the aminoglycoside-based regimen and its association with severe and long-term ADRs such as ototoxicity, renal impairment, and optic neuritis. The introduction of a shorter regimen offered a more convenient and safer regimen. Data on the safety of the short bedaquiline regimen (SBR) has rarely been explored since the widespread use of bedaquiline was recommended in 2018. This is especially important for a country like South Africa, where the factors influencing therapy outcomes are vast and multifactorial. The method employed in this study included an investigation of patient clinical charts and documentation of noted ADRs. This was deemed the most appropriate method because medical officers or doctors did not complete other data sources such as ADR reports. All patients from a three-year period diagnosed with MDR-TB were included in the study. The most documented ADRs were thrombocytopenia, hypothyroidism, prolonged QTc, gastrointestinal disturbances, and rash. Three of the 5 most common ADRs were categorized as severe. There were no ADR reports submitted for escalation to relevant authorities. New drugs to the MDR-TB regimen, bedaquiline, and linezolid were primarily responsible for most ADRs. SBR appears to be associated with more severe ADRs than initially anticipated or documented. The focus previously was on QTc prolongation. The cost, including financial, human and emotional on treating the ADRs should also be factored in when comparing to previous regimens. Based on current data, increased monitoring of baseline results and management time of ADRs is imperative in ensuring a better response to therapy. Established pharmacovigilance systems are necessary to ensure the continued safety of drugs, irrespective of the disease state or known safety profile of the drug.Keywords: Adverse Drug Reactions, Short Bedaquiline Regimen, Safety, Severity, Multi-Drug Resistant Tuberculosis

Background

Pharmacovigilance is an important aspect of pharmacy that ensures access to safe as well as efficacious medication. The pharmacovigilance program in South Africa’s public health care sector is typically associated with a non-functional aspect of pharmacy. Historically, most pharmacovigilance activities that provided reports on medication safety were generated from active systems in special programs such as HIV and TB. The reliance on passive reporting systems has proven less effective in identifying and producing new information on medication safety [1]. The monitoring of patients while on treatment is important when using new drugs. The impact of pharmacogenomics on patient outcomes, drug-drug interactions, drug-food interactions, and establishing rare adverse drug reactions (ADRs) is mainly established post the clinical trial phase. The failure to establish functional reporting systems results in an underestimation of the medication's safety profile.

An active reporting system is imperative in programs such as multi-drug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) where there are constant changes in the treatment protocol such as new medications being used, bedaquiline, delamanid and pretomanid, and the repurposing of older medication, clofazimine, and levofloxacin. Furthermore, the use of combination therapy which is the goal of treatment in tuberculosis (TB), increases the potential of ADRs occurring due to overlapping toxicities. The grading and quantification of the ADRs play a vital role in establishing the safety profile of the regimen holistically.

The End TB strategy was established by the WHO to reduce the global impact of TB within a set timeframe. One of the major goals of the End TB strategy is to provide novel, effective, and safe approaches to managing MDR-TB [2]. The development of new regimens is a major part of this strategy in response to high rates of drug-resistant tuberculosis.

Multi-drug resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is a growing threat to the health of South Africans. South Africa (SA) accounted for 35% of all diagnosed cases of MDR-TB in the African Region in 2022 and 3% of cases globally. Among the 30 high burden TB countries, SA is among the 14 countries with the highest burden of TB, TB/HIV and multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB). There have been different causes that have led to increased drug resistance, including insufficient healthcare infrastructure, prescription of the wrong treatment (either wrong dose or treatment length), poor quality drugs or drug unavailability, poor adherence to therapy, or MDR-TB re-infection, among others [3]. The high rates of HIV co-infection exacerbate this situation. The standardized regimen approach is used in the management of patients with a positive diagnosis of MDR-TB. The regimen used before 2018, the aminoglycoside-based regimen, was associated with poor outcomes and numerous ADRs. This regimen was associated with severe irreversible ADRs. The well-documented ADRs included ototoxicity, optic neuritis, peripheral neuropathy, gastro-intestinal disturbances and drug-induced liver injury. Although the reporting of these ADRs was poor the widespread impact of these ADRs on positive patient outcomes prompted the need for a novel regimen.

The direction of the WHO strategies has been focussed on novel drug development in the field of MDR-TB. There had not been any new drugs to treat TB in the last 40 years [4]. In 2018, the introduction of bedaquiline and delamanid saw the introduction of novel anti-TB drugs [5]. The repurposing of clofazimine and linezolid together with the new drug bedaquiline, resulted in the development of a standardized regimen to treat MDR-TB termed the short bedaquiline regimen (SBR). This regimen comprises of bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine, levofloxacin, ethionamide, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol. The escalating levels of MDR-TB and the emerging health crisis resulted in the FDA approving the use of bedaquiline under accelerated approval provisions. This decision was based on results showing that bedaquiline used in combination therapy reduced the time to sputum culture conversion and increased the number of patients with sputum culture conversion. A major recommendation was that active pharmacovigilance systems be implemented to monitor the safety of bedaquiline. The safety concerns that arose from the phase 2 trial were QT prolongation and unexplained mortality associated with the use of bedaquiline. This reiterated the importance of established pharmacovigilance systems [5].

The long-term and rare ADRs would only be established through long-term monitoring and active reporting of suspected ADRs. These suspected ADRs should be submitted for a causality assessment to confirm that the ADR is a result of drug exposure and not an external factor. Clinic trial data might not detect all ADRs due to the controlled nature of data collection. The influence of external factors such as co-morbidity, socio-economic factors, and drug interactions will not be accounted for during ADRs. These may only be found in post-clinical trial conditions.

Comprehensive post-marketing pharmacovigilance is required for the detection, investigation and management of ADRs that were not discovered during clinical. Effective pharmacovigilance should take into account trends in use, as well as the occurrence of ADRs, enabling more effective advice to be given to those prescribing medicines and should ensure better standards of safety and efficacy. In response to this, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched active drug safety monitoring (aDSM) [6].

aDSM is a pharmacovigilance initiative for monitoring the safety of drugs used in MDR-TB. The framework ensures the safety of patients who receive MDR-TB treatment with new medicines (e.g., bedaquiline, delamanid), repurposed medicines (e.g., linezolid, clofazimine), or new regimens, which don't have a well-established safety profile [7]. The aim is to identify any new potential "signals" and any new emerging safety concerns. This initiative was launched in 2018 in the Ugu and Ethekwini. New ADR forms were introduced. These were meant to make reporting easier, quicker, and more user-friendly in an attempt to improve the reporting. Despite this active monitoring system, the compliance to ADR reporting was minimal. In the rural Ugu district, ADR reporting remained poor.

Murchison Hospital is located on the southern coast of KwaZulu Natal. The hospital is a 300-bed facility. In addition to generalized medical and maternity services, the hospital offers MDR-TB services. This hospital is the central hospital for MDR-TB services for the Ugu district. The hospital provides inpatient and outpatient services through the combined efforts of a multi-disciplinary team. In addition to diagnosis and treatment of MDR-TB, occupational health and social services are available to the patients. The MDR-TB unit is fully equipped with resources to assist patients in achieving positive patient outcomes. The impact of ADR on patient outcomes is well understood as evident from previously used aminoglycoside-based MDR-TB regimen. The counselling of patients is an attempt to ensure all patients understand what ADRs can be expected during treatment. aDSM training was conducted with nurses, doctors, and pharmacists at the hospital. All ADRs should be documented in patient files and an ADR report form must be completed and sent to the pharmacy. In addition, they should also be recorded in the electronic TB drug register (EDR). This may provide insight into the prevalent ADRs and a more targeted approach in counselling patients. In addition to this, the literature on the ADR profile of the SBR is found to be lacking as all available information focuses on the safety profile of bedaquiline and not as a regimen. The safety profile of the SBR is important due to the different drugs in the regimen with overlapping toxicities. This study aims to provide insight into this aspect. There is a direct link between a high incidence of ADRs and patients’ adherence to treatment [8]. When patients experience ADRs that are debilitating and impact their daily functioning, they lose confidence in the treatment plan and medical officers. This occurs when patients associate the treatment and specifically the medication with their ill health. This notion then prompts patients to disengage from care [9]. It is therefore important to establish the regimen's safety to ensure adherence and subsequent treatment efficacy.

Methodology

The study was conducted at Murchison Hospital in the Ugu district. This hospital is the main site of MDR-TB initiations and management in the district. All MDR-TB patients that were treated at the hospital between January 2019 and December 2021 were included in the study. Patient files were used as the primary data source. The secondary data source was the EDR. Ethical approval was obtained from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BREC) at University of KwaZulu Natal (UKZN) (BREC/00002065/2020). Gatekeeper approval was also obtained from the research site, Murchison Hospital. Relevant authority was also granted by the National Department of Health (NDoH). There was no direct patient or public involvement in the study therefore individual patient consent was not a requirement as per the BREC committee.

The aim of the study was to quantify the patients who experienced ADRs and quantify the number of ADRs. The study also identified the types of ADRs and commented on the causality through conducting Naranjo assessments. ADR reports should be completed for all ADRs documented in medical notes and submitted to the pharmacy department for documentation and submission to the relevant department. The severity of the ADRs was classified according Hartwig’s Severity Assessment Scale [10]. They were classified as follows (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Respective participant data were entered and analysed using statistical software (SPSS version 28). Descriptive statistics were used to determine the prevalence of ADRs and the frequencies of different categories of ADRs. The factors associated with ADRs were determined using univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses and presented as odds ratios (ORs) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The level of statistical significance was at the p-value of < 0.05.

There were 147 patients who experienced at least one ADR during the course of their treatment. None of these ADRs have been documented in the form of ADR reports nor documented in the EDRWeb. All identified ADRs were noted by doctors and nurses in patient files. A total of 496 ADRs were experienced in this cohort of patients. The univariate analysis found a significant association between ADRs and co-morbidities (odds ratio (OR) 1.84, 95% CI 1.2–2.9) and alcohol use (OR = 2.15, 95% CI 1.4–3.4). There was no significant association with other factors.

There was no ADR documentation in the form of ADR reports on EDR or documentation forwarded to the pharmacy department for escalation to the relevant departments. The only documented ADRs were established through medicine clinical notes. A Naranjo causality assessment is a standard tool appearing on all suspected ADR forms [11]. This was used to establish causality (see Annexure A). This was to be conducted on all ADR report forms completed by the doctors or nurses however no ADR forms were submitted therefore no causality assessments were completed. Based on the information that was available in clinical notes, the information that was extracted on causality is represented in Table 3 [11]. The majority of the ADRs experienced were allocated to the “probable” category with 9 definite ADRs. The ADRs that were included in this category included abnormal ECG (n = 4), thrombocytopaenia (n = 2), and drug induced liver injury (DILI) (n = 3). The implicated drugs were bedaquiline for abnormal ECG, linezolid for thrombocytopaenia and linezolid and pyrazinamide in DILI.

Discussion

The expedited approval of bedaquiline and its subsequent use in MDR-TB regimens occurred as a result of the increasing infections with MDR-TB and a reduced number of effective drugs to manage the rapidly developing resistance to the available treatment. The side effects associated with bedaquiline were documented to be increased mortality, nausea and prolonged QTc [12]. The standardized regimen that was used in phase two clinicals trial did not correspond with the background regimen used in the current guidelines in terms of the composition of drugs making up the background regimen [13]. The main reported ADR from this trial was QTc prolongation. A higher death rate was found in the bedaquiline group (3% versus 2%). This study found a mortality rate of 14.4%. This was higher than the results of the STREAM trial [14]. This may be attributed to several confounding factors such as the various co-morbidities, complications of the TB infection or late presentation to care. There were 57.7% of patients who were HIV co-infected and 67.3% of those who demised were HIV co-infected.

The primary ADRs reflected in this sample included thrombocytopenia, hypothyroidism, prolonged QTc, gastrointestinal disturbances, and rash. The ADR profile of this sample reflects a different picture compared to the cohort in the STREAM trial. The common factor was QTc prolongation. The drug implicated according to the causality assessment was bedaquiline. The other drugs that could potentially be implicated included clofazimine and levofloxacin [15]. The rechallenge approach which involves a phased in approach of drugs was often used to establish causality. The suspected drug was stopped and the patient was monitored to establish if there was improvement. If there was improvement then the drug was re-introduced and continued, if there are no further complications. If there was no clinical improvement on stopping the suspected drug then it may be concluded that another drug is the causative agent in the ADR. This was also used to ensure that patients did not receive sub-optimal therapy. The main drug implicated in thrombocytopenia was linezolid. The other causes of low haemoglobin were ruled out through baseline investigations. Hypothyroidism was attributed to ethionamide, and linezolid was implicated in GI disturbances. The overlapping ADRs created a barrier to establishing causality. The rechallenge approach assisted in this regard.

The 2019 revision to the National Guidelines for the Management of drug-resistant TB in South Africa found adjustments made to linezolid use and removing high doses of isoniazid and pyridoxine from the regimen. Previously, linezolid was issued until the second line probe assay (LPA) results were received. If XDR-TB or pre-XDR-TB was excluded then linezolid was stopped. The 2019 revision mentioned that linezolid should be issued for 2 months irrespective of the results of the second line LPA. This change and increased exposure to linezolid, a drug known to be associated with myelosuppression, may have resulted in an increased number of patients who experienced thrombocytopenia. The use of drugs like zidovudine in the ARV regimen in HIV co-infected patients may potentiate the occurrence of low haemoglobin levels due to overlapping toxicities. Although current guidelines caution against the concomitant use of linezolid and zidovudine, there is a need for an active system to monitor this or even consider adjusting the ARV regimen. The implications and increased cost of managing a patient experiencing thrombocytopenia or low haemoglobin must be considered against the benefit of using linezolid routinely in MDR-TB regimen. There were 8 deaths in patients who experienced myelosuppression secondary to linezolid. The deaths may not have been directly caused by the ADR. Further investigation into these deaths is required.

The most commonly known ADR associated with the SBR is the QT prolongation [16]. In this cohort it appears as the third most common ADR. Although there is a high incidence of QT prolongation compared to other ADRs it was not found to be a main problem afflicting patients in this cohort. This may be attributed to closer monitoring of patients in this regard. The majority of patients who experienced QT prolongation recovered following temporary interruption of either bedaquiline, clofazimine or levofloxacin. There was one death in a patient who experienced QT prolongation, however, the death may not be attributed to the ADR alone owing to the fact that the patient did have additional co-morbidities and presented for treatment at a stage of advanced TB infection. Further investigation would be required to establish the cause of mortality.

Hypothyroidism was an ADR that had not been highlighted in the SBR before this cohort. The drugs used in MDR-TB associated with hypothyroidism include ethionamide and para-aminosalicylic acid (PAS) [17]. There were 70 cases of hypothyroidism with 72% of these cases occurring within the intensive phase of treatment. Ethionamide or PAS is used in the intensive phase of treatment. In the interim clinical guideline and the 2019 review, it was recommended that ethionamide be removed completely from the SBR, however, this implementation of the recommendation was only seen in 2020. Prior to this, all MDR-TB patients were treated using ethionamide in the regimen unless an inhA mutation was present in which case ethionamide was excluded. The monitoring of baseline and monthly tests enabled the detection of this ADR as it was not symptomatically apparent. A systematic review conducted by Tola et al. found to concur with the results of this study. It concluded that hypothyroidism secondary to ethionamide use is not uncommon as previous literature suggests (Tola et al., 2019). There should be closer and continued monitoring going forward in regimens that include ethionamide and PAS to ensure early detection and treatment.

Contrary to previous literature that highlighted the safety profile of bedaquiline, this study has highlighted an increased number of ADRs associated with the SBR. There were 64 ADRs that were classified as mild, 292 as moderate, and 140 as severe. The SBR was originally marketed as a safer regimen in the management of patients with MDR-TB. It is possible that due to the expedited approval of the drug and constituents of the background regimen, many important and significant ADRs may have been missed and therefore the safety profile was underestimated. This study highlights important ADRs in the regimen not previously noted. The drugs mainly implicated in the ADRs include bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine and ethionamide.

The study further highlighted gaps in an effective and functional pharmacovigilance program. The reporting of ADRs as per the official documentation is found to be lacking. There was only one ADR report submitted to pharmacy. The form was further not completed correctly despite the recent revision to ensure ease of reporting. Vital baseline information such as age, current regimen, and gender were all omitted in the report. The outcome was also omitted. This form was for a patient who experienced linezolid-induced thrombocytopenia. The MDR-TB clinical patient chart allows for easy documentation ranging from minor level 1–3 severity to severe, life-threatening level 4–7 on the severity scale. The monitoring of ADRs in programmatic conditions such as MDR-TB and ART programs is especially important considering that these guidelines are continually updated to ensure access to effective, safe, and cost-effective treatment. Proper documentation of ADRs is imperative in adding to the body of knowledge on the safety profile of the SBR. This study is important as it identifies ADRs and potentially adds knowledge to the existing safety profile of the SBR. Furthermore, there are very limited studies on the safety and ADR of the SBR [18].

The Naranjo causality scale was not calculated for any ADR experienced by patients. This highlights a major issue regarding pharmacovigilance activities. As highlighted previously the documentation of ADRs is a consistent issue unless an active reporting system is used. Where passive systems are in place there is little to no response on ADRs. In addition to this there is often incomplete documentation where reporting is concerned. The lack of defined roles and responsibilities potentiates the issue. Although the pharmacovigilance program may be managed via the pharmacy department, the key role players such as nurses and doctors are integral in reporting identified ADRs. In 2018, King Dinuzulu Hospital held a training to introduce the new ADR forms specifically for the purpose of MDR-TB reporting. This document was intended to enhance and encourage reporting however, it failed to achieve its goal in this setting. The reasons may be established through consultation with medical personnel but ultimately the lack of functional systems at both the institutional and provincial levels is the root cause.

The importance of studies such as this also highlighted that many safety studies focus on severe ADRs and very rarely on ADRs that are mild but occur on a large scale. These may not directly impact patents care but it does impact outcomes such as loss to follow. ADRs such as skin rashes and GI disturbances impact patients’ quality of life which may not be life-threatening but severe enough to cause disruption to treatment. Through the assessment of the incidence of these, we can take corrective and possibly preventative steps to minimize the impact of these ADRs.

Conclusion

SBR is an effective regimen in the treatment of MDR-TB. This study has not mimicked the safety profile as reflected in previous studies. There is a high incidence of severe ADRs were experienced by patients. These side effects however are reversible through a stopping causative drug. The three most implicated drugs include linezolid, bedaquiline and ethionamide. The study mainly highlights the need and importance of established pharmacovigilance systems. This is essential in ensuring ADRs are detected reported and managed effectively. More data on the ADR profile in different settings is recommended to establish if the results of this study may be replicated.

Limitations

The data obtained was from patient records therefore the information that was documented was done so for the purposes of patient care and not research. As a result, information on the possible causality of the ADR may be missed. The causality assessment should be conducted by the clinician who has discovered and reported the ADR to ensure accuracy of the report. This also made establishing the severity of the ADR subjective as vital information pertaining to the ADR may have not been recorded. Information on establishing the outcome following the ADR was also difficult as this was often not documented.
