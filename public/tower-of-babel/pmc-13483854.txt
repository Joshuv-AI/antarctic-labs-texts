Minimum acceptable diet and its associated factors among children aged 6-23months in rural households of South Ethiopia
Authors: Kassahun Fikadu, Abinet Teshome, Zeleke Hailemariam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal nutrition throughout the first 1,000 days is critical for a child’s development, but malnutrition remains a major issue in Ethiopia. The Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD) is an important indication of nutritional quality in children aged 6–23 months, although its prevalence and determinants vary significantly across the country. The purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence of MAD and its related characteristics in Southern Ethiopia’s rural Gamo Zone, to guide targeted local interventions.MethodsFrom July to September 2023, a community-based, cross-sectional study was undertaken in three rural areas with 1,479 mother-child (6–23 months) couples. Households were selected using a multistage sampling procedure. Data were gathered using a pre-tested, structured questionnaire on the KoboCollect app and analyzed with SPSS version 25. We employed bivariate and multivariable logistic regression to discover characteristics linked to MAD, with statistical significance established at p-value < 0.05.ResultsAmong children who had started complementary feeding, 41.1% (n = 595; 95% CI: 38.6, 43.7) received a MAD. Several factors were found in the multivariable analysis to be strongly connected with MAD achievement. These included the child’s age (AOR = 1.52 for 12–17 months and AOR = 1.45 for 18–23 months), having a working father (AOR = 1.6), family food security (AOR = 2.11), mother postnatal care attendance (AOR = 2.16), and possessing a television or radio (AOR = 1.67).ConclusionLess than half of the children in the study fulfilled the minimum requirement for an appropriate diet, indicating that optimal feeding practices were not followed. Interventions should prioritize strengthening postnatal care counseling, increasing household food security, and utilizing media channels for health education. Promoting father engagement and providing assistance to younger infants (6–11 months) is critical for enhancing child nutrition outcomes.Keywords: Associate factors, Infant and young child feeding (IYCF) complementary feeding, Minimum acceptable diet (MAD), Rural districts, South Ethiopia

Introduction

Optimal nutrition during early childhood is fundamental to a child’s holistic development, shaping their cognitive, social, and physical well-being for a lifetime [1]. The first 1,000 days, from conception to a child’s second birthday, represent an unparalleled window of opportunity for establishing a foundation for lifelong health [2]. Nutritional deficits during this critical period can lead to lasting consequences, including poor neurodevelopment, lower educational attainment, and reduced economic productivity in adulthood [1, 3]. Children under two years old are disproportionately vulnerable to malnutrition, a public health crisis that is especially severe in Ethiopia, where it contributes to more than half of all infant and child deaths [4].

The World Health Organization uses the Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD) as a critical indicator to assess the quality of nutrition for children aged 6 to 23 months [5]. MAD is a composite measure assessing whether complementary feeding meets minimum standards for both dietary diversity and meal frequency over the previous day [6]. Minimum Dietary Diversity (MDD) requires consumption from at least five of eight specified food groups, while Minimum Meal Frequency (MMF) relates to the number of meals and snacks a child receives [7]. This indicator is vital for tracking progress and targeting interventions to prevent nutritional deficiencies.

Across Ethiopia, studies reveal significant disparities in MAD prevalence. For example, some regional studies have reported rates as high as 74.6% [8, 9], while others found rates as low as 12.6% [9]. This lower figure mirrors the national average of just 13% reported in the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey, highlighting a widespread challenge [4]. This variability points to the strong influence of local socioeconomic, cultural, and programmatic factors.

Research has consistently identified several key determinants of achieving a minimum acceptable diet. These factors include the child’s age, maternal education level, exposure to health information via media, consistent antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, and giving birth in a health facility [8–10]; health system factors such as postnatal check-ups and access to healthcare; and child-related factors like the child’s age [10]. Nurses and midwives are central to improving these outcomes [11]. Through targeted counseling during ANC, delivery, and postnatal visits, they can significantly enhance maternal knowledge and caregiving practices related to nutrition [12].

Despite this evidence, there is still a dearth of information on the exact status and causes of MAD inside the particular setting of Gamo Zone, Ethiopia. Studies have been carried out elsewhere in the South Ethiopia [13, 14], however the clear regional variation in MAD incidence emphasizes the necessity of local knowledge. Local food systems, agro-ecology, cultural ideas, and economic circumstances interact complicatedly to influence feeding patterns that may differ greatly even between adjacent areas. Therefore, in order to develop successful, focused public health policies, region-specific information for Gamo Zone is absolutely vital. Therefore, this study seeks to evaluate the frequency of the minimum acceptable diet and find its related variables among children 6–23 months in Gamo Zone. The results are meant to give the Gamo Zone health officials and frontline personnel the exact data they have to have to create effective, local programs to fight child malnutrition.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based, cross-sectional study was conducted between July 15 and September 15, 2023, in three randomly selected rural districts—Arba Minch Zuria, Chencha, and Mirab Abaya—within the Gamo Zone of Southern Ethiopia. Together, these three districts have 307,849 inhabitants in general population. Local health registry data indicated that within these areas, there were roughly 51,273 target population (eligible mother-child pairs). This region is characterized by a mix of highland and lowland topographies.

Population and sampling

Source population

All mothers with children aged 6–23 months residing in the rural districts of Gamo Zone.

Study population

All mothers with children aged 6–23 months living in the randomly selected rural districts (or clusters) of Gamo Zone during the data collection period.

Study unit

A mother-child pair (where the child is aged 6–23 months) from the selected households who meets the eligibility criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Mothers/caregivers with children aged 6–23 months who had been permanent residents of the selected districts for at least six months prior to the study.

Exclusion criteria

Mothers/caregivers who were temporary residents or had lived in the study area for less than six months.

Sampling technique

A multi-stage stratified sampling technique was employed, involving three districts. The three study districts (Arba Minch Zuria, Chencha, and Mirab Abaya) were randomly selected from the Gamo Zone. A proportional number of kebeles (the smallest administrative units) were randomly selected from each district. Further, households with eligible mother-child pairs were identified from the family folders maintained by local Health Extension Workers. A simple random sampling method was then used to select the final participating households from this list, with the sample size allocated proportionally to the population of each selected kebele details are mentioned in Table 1and elsewhere [16].

Operational definitions

Minimum acceptable diet (MAD)

it is a composite indicator for children aged 6–23 months. It was assessed based on the 24-hour dietary recall and defined as meeting both the Minimum Dietary Diversity (MDD) and Minimum Meal Frequency (MMF) criteria, according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines.

Minimum dietary diversity (MDD)

Defined as the consumption of foods from at least five of the eight recommended food groups [15].

Minimum meal frequency (MMF)

Defined as receiving solid, semi-solid, or soft foods (including snacks) at least twice a day for breastfed infants 6–8 months, three times a day for breastfed children 9–23 months, and four times a day for non-breastfed children 6–23 months [15].

Complementary feeding knowledge

The knowledge of complementary feeding was assessed using five yes/no questions (rated 1/0). A total score was calculated, and scores ≥ the mean were considered as high knowledge.

Women’s attitude on complementary feeding

Each item included a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree, 5 = strongly disagree). Responses to ‘Strongly agree’ or ‘Agree’ were coded 1; all other responses were coded 0. Scores exceeding the mean showed a favorable attitude.

Household food security

This was assessed using the Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES) [18], this has eight items. For items that answered ‘No’ were assigned a code of 0, while those that replied ‘Yes’ were assigned a code of 1. Similarly, an individual was considered Food Secure if their responses to all initial questions were either ‘No’ or ‘Yes and details are mentioned elsewhere [16, 19].

Data collection tool

A structured, pre-tested questionnaire was developed by adapting validated tools from previous studies and the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS). The questionnaire was translated from English into the local languages (Gamo Gofa and Amharic) and then back-translated to ensure accuracy. Data were collected electronically using the KoboCollect application on tablets.

Pre-testing

The questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% of the sample size in a neighboring district with similar socio-demographic characteristics. Based on the pre-test, necessary revisions were made to improve clarity and flow.

Data quality assurance

Data collectors with prior experience in health surveys were recruited and received intensive training on the study protocol, ethical considerations, and the use of the data collection application. Field supervisors monitored the data collection process daily, checking for completeness and consistency. Regular review meetings were held to address any challenges encountered in the field.

Data management and analysis

Data were exported from the KoboCollect server to SPSS version 25 for cleaning and analysis. For, descriptive Statistics frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants and the prevalence of key variables, including MAD, MDD, and MMF. Further, bivariate logistic regression was first performed to assess the association between each independent variable and the MAD outcome. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were first examined for inclusion. Variables with biological plausibility and persistent relationships with Minimum Acceptable Diet in prior research were included in the multivariable model, regardless of their bivariate p-value. Before modeling, multicollinearity was tested using variance inflation factors (VIF); no variable had a VIF more than 10. A complete-case analysis was conducted, as less than 5% of records had missing data on any variable. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess the final model’s goodness of fit (p > 0.05). Due to tiny primary sampling units (kebeles) and low intra-class correlation, sample size was calculated using a design effect only. The regression analysis did not explicitly account for the multistage design. This is acknowledged as a limitation. Also, Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 1,479 mother-child pairs participated (response rate 94.6% compared to target sample of 1,564). Of these, 1,447 children had begun supplemental feeding and were involved in the Minimum Acceptable Diet study. The mean age of the mothers were 27.31(SD = 5.78) years. Of 1,479 study participants, 780(52.7%) of the mothers were 25–34 years age, and 900(60.9%) of the mothers were protestants. 556 (36.6%) of mothers and 481(32.5%) of husbands had attended primary education respectively. Two-third of mothers 1145(77.4%) were housewives and 825 (55.8%) of husbands were farmers. Majority of the households 1232(83.3%) had improved source of water for drinking and food preparation. The most common water sources for drinking and food preparation were pipe (95.2%), river (10.7%), protected spring (4.6%), and protected dug (4.2%) (Table 2).

Factors associated with minimum acceptable diet

Children whose fathers are employed are approximately 1.6 times (AOR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.13, 2.27) more likely to receive MAD than those whose fathers are farmers. Mothers who received postnatal care were approximately twice as likely (AOR = 2.16, 95% CI: 1.68, 2.77) to offer Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD) than those who did not receive such care. Households with food security were 2.1 times more likely (AOR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.53, 2.93) to offer minimally acceptable dietary (MAD) options. Children in the age groups of 12–17 and 18–23 months were around 1.5 times more likely to receive MAD, with adjusted odds ratios of 1.52 (95% confidence interval: 1.15, 2.00) and 1.45 (95% confidence interval: 1.11, 1.91), respectively, when compared to children aged 6–11 months. Households that owned a TV or radio had 1.7 times greater likelihood (Adjusted Odds Ratio = 1.66, 95% Confidence Interval: 1.29, 2. 15) of offering Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, 41.1% (n = 595; 95% CI: 38.6, 43.7) of children aged 6–23 months who had begun supplemental feeding received a Minimum Acceptable Diet. In our study setting, the practice of providing a minimum acceptable diet for children aged 6–23 months was in line with a study conducted in Ethiopia 36.5% [20], Mongolia (43.8%) [21], and in Latin America and the Caribbean (43.7%) [22].

However, the current study’s MAD is higher than a study conducted in North East Ethiopia 16.7% [23]. For this study, data-collection period (dry season), ongoing food insecurity (the location is one of the sites for long-term development initiatives under the Government of Ethiopia’s Productive Safety Net Program (PSNP)), potential disturbances due to conflict, and consistently low consumption of eggs and meat products might explain the difference. Tulla District Sidama Ethiopia 14.5% [24]. The primary attributable difference for the Tulla district study can be due to several reasons. Initially, the research concentrated on undernourished children participating in outpatient therapy programs, while the other research encompassed the wider community beginning complementary feeding. Secondly, more than 50% of the mothers involved in the study lacked formal education, which influenced their capacity to offer Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD). Third, numerous mothers indicated that their limited income restricted their capacity to provide a variety of meals, an aspect not mentioned in the other research. Finally, the consumption of nutrient-rich foods was notably minimal, as only a small number of children included essential food groups such as meat, eggs, or fruits and vegetables in their diets. This lack of variety adversely affected dietary diversity and, consequently, the Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD). It is also higher than other studies conducted in Sidama Aleta Wendo 13.8% [25]. The key reasons explaining its Lower MAD may be because only 14.7% (95% CI: 11.74–17.72) of children met MDD – a core component of MAD. Without achieving MDD, meeting MAD is impossible, and this severe shortfall directly constrains the overall prevalence. Further, low household wealth – a powerful barrier, limited food preparation training, low maternal education and its downstream effects, this barrier appears more pronounced in Aleta Wondo than in the current study population. Likewise, the current study is higher than a study conducted in Goncha district, northwest Ethiopia (12.6%) [9]. This can be due to chronically food‑insecure rural setting as the study was conducted in a district “known to experience chronic food insecurity due to variable rainfall pattern” where “the population especially who are resided in lowland Kebeles are users of government safety net program”. In addition, nearly two‑thirds (65.8%) of mothers in the Goncha had no formal education versus 21.9% in our study, which may partly explain the difference.

The current prevalence is lower than a study conducted in Addis Ababa [8], Eastern Ethiopia [26], South Ethiopia [14], North West Ethiopia, East Africa and India [27, 28]. The differences might be due to the residence of the participants, whereby most of the participants in the current study are from rural villages, while the participants in the study conducted in Addis Ababa were urban dwellers. Other factors such as homogeneity of the population and other sociocultural and economic difference could have played a role in the differences of the outcome.

Overall, the literature review demonstrates that, whereas southern Ethiopia has some general agro-ecological commonalities, MAD prevalence is very localized. Our findings differ significantly from those of neighboring districts such as Tulla and Aleta Wondo, demonstrating that community-level factors—such as specific programmatic interventions, variations in local food market access, and the baseline wealth of the sampled clusters—frequently outweigh generalized regional trends. This emphasizes the vital importance of using localized, district-specific data to guide public health action, rather than extrapolating regional averages.

After conducting multivariable logistic regression age of the child, PNC visit, and occupation of the husband, having TV/Radio and household food security were found to have a statistically significant association with Minimum Acceptable Diet.

Children with employed fathers were 1.6 times more likely to have optimal Minimum Acceptable Diet. This is consistent with a study conducted in Aleta Wondo district [25], Eastern Ethiopia [26], Northwest Ethiopia [9]. This finding aligns with studies [29, 30], suggesting that stable employment may enhance household resources and health literacy.

Children with 12 to 17 months and 18–23 months were 1.5 and 1.45 times more likely to have optimal Minimum Acceptable Diet respectively. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Nepal [31] and Mongolia [32], Tulla district South Ethiopia [24], Eastern Ethiopia [26] but is in contrary to a finding of a research in Pakistan [33], whereby children from 6 to 11 months of age had high odds of receiving Minimum Acceptable Diet. The reasons for this finding could be that parents may perceive young infants as unable to digest diverse foods [27], leading to delayed introduction of complementary foods and lower dietary diversity in early months [28, 34]. Mothers that follow postnatal care at health care institutions were 2 times likely to have children with Minimum Acceptable Diet. In addition to detecting health complications after delivery, postnatal care can be used to relay information on child feeding, which in turn increase the nutritional status of the children.

Children living in households with TV or Radio were 1.7 times more likely to receive Minimum Acceptable Diet compared with children of households with no media access. This finding is supported by a research finding of secondary data analysis from countries in east Africa [27] and Eastern and Northwest Ethiopia [9, 26]. This finding can be linked to the fact that mothers living in households with Television and Radio have an access to regular and updated information about child feeding, which increase the probability of the children receiving the variety of diets in an optimum frequency.

Household food security was also associated with minimum acceptable diet, as children living in food secured households are 2 times more likely to receive minimum acceptable diet compared to those living in food insecure households. This finding is in line with a study conducted in Semien Bench district, Ethiopia [35], South Ethiopia [14], in which children from food insecure households were more likely to suffer from undernutrition and its consequences. This finding can be explained by the fact that households that are food secure have an access to varies type of complementary foods and can manage to serve the appropriate number of meals for their children.

Variables such as ANC visit, place of delivery, age of the mothers, educational status of the mother and the father were found to have no statistically significant association with Minimum Acceptable Diet.

Although it addresses with a large sample size (1,479 participants) in rural settings in Ethiopia, the findings should be interpreted in light of the following limitations. Dietary data were collected using a 24-hour recall, which is subject to recall bias and may not reflect usual dietary intake. Social desirability bias may have led mothers to over report desirable feeding practices. The study was conducted in three districts of Gamo Zone and may not be representative of all rural areas in Ethiopia. Finally, the exclusion of severely ill children at the time of data collection may have slightly underestimated the true prevalence of inadequate feeding.

Clinical and Public Health Implications.

The finding that only 41.1% of children received a minimum acceptable diet highlights a substantial gap in optimal infant and young child feeding practices in this rural Ethiopian setting. The strong associations with modifiable factors—postnatal care attendance, household food security, media access, and paternal employment—point to clear intervention targets. First, strengthening postnatal care services to include dedicated, practical counseling on complementary feeding could significantly improve MAD. Currently, only half of mothers receive such counseling; integrating hands‑on demonstrations and follow‑up visits into routine PNC could bridge this gap. Second, interventions addressing household food insecurity—such as linking nutrition programs with agricultural support (home gardening, small livestock) and safety nets—are essential. The two‑fold higher odds of MAD among food‑secure households underscore that nutrition education alone is insufficient without concurrent food access. Third, the role of media exposure suggests that expanding nutrition messaging through radio, community screenings, and mobile platforms could reach fathers and other household decision‑makers, thereby creating a supportive environment for optimal feeding. Fourth, the cross-sectional design prevents causal linkages from being deduced. Fifth, the regression models did not explicitly account for multistage sampling, which may slightly understate standard errors; nonetheless, the sample size and minimal cluster-level heterogeneity minimize this worry. Finally, the lower MAD among infants aged 6–11 months calls for targeted messaging to counter beliefs that young infants cannot digest diverse foods. Antenatal and early postnatal contacts are ideal opportunities to introduce these concepts before poor practices become entrenched. These findings support a multi‑sectoral approach involving the health system, agriculture, and social protection sectors to improve child nutrition in similar low‑resource settings.

Conclusion

Nearly three-fifths (58.9%) of children did not reach the Minimum Acceptable Diet, whereas 41.1% achieved. Higher age of the child, being food secured, having TV/Radio in the household, attending post-natal care and a father being employed were found to be the predictors for Minimum Acceptable Diet.
