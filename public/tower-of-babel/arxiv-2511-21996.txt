Pressure-robust optimally convergent H ⁡ ( div ) H(\mathrm{div}) finite element method without the commuting diagram property for the steady Oseen equations Note: It is supported by National Natural Science Foundation of China (11771257)
Jin Zhang, Xiaowei Liu

Abstract
This work develops a convergence theory for H ⁡ ( div ) H(\mathrm{div}) -conforming finite element methods applied to the steady Oseen problem, focusing on cases where the exact finite element complex holds while the commuting diagram property may fail. The proposed method incorporates vorticity stabilization to ensure optimal-order convergence of the velocity error, especially for convection-dominated cases. As a crucial component of the analysis, exact de Rham and finite element complexes provide a framework whose utility includes establishing velocity error estimates independent of the discrete inf-sup constant. As a representative example, Stenberg finite elements demonstrate the framework’s validity and offer several computational advantages: pressure robustness, fewer degrees of freedom than classical RT or BDM elements due to vertex continuity, and convergence without requiring the commuting diagram property. Moreover, the proposed methodology is applicable to a class of finite element pairs that violate the commuting diagram property, thereby offering new possibilities for efficient discretizations of incompressible fluid problems, particularly in high Reynolds number regimes.

Introduction

Classical inf-sup stable mixed finite element methods for incompressible flow problems typically relax the divergence constraint, leading to velocity errors that depend on pressure approximation. This results in a loss of pressure robustness
[
27
]
, meaning the numerical scheme fails to preserve key invariance properties of the continuous problem. Such non-pressure-robust behavior can introduce significant inaccuracies, as discussed in
[
27
]
and
[
32
]
.
The development and analysis of pressure-robust schemes has been an active field of research, which encompasses the following directions: (1) constructing divergence-free finite element pairs, e.g., the Scott–Vogelius element
[
37
,
36
]
on barycentric-refined meshes and the Guzmán–Neilan element
[
18
,
17
]
;
(2) modifying the numerical scheme, such as Linke’s approach
[
31
]
; and
(3) employing postprocessing strategies, as discussed in
[
26
, Remark 4.102]
.
For the first popular strategy,
H(\text{div})
-conforming methods have emerged as particularly attractive, since they enable the design of divergence-free finite element pairs on general meshes with greater flexibility than
H^{1}
-conforming methods.
For example, whereas
H^{1}
-based Scott-Vogelius elements necessitate either specific mesh geometries or high polynomial degrees on general triangulations
[
37
,
41
,
19
]
,
H(\text{div})
-conforming methods provide enhanced flexibility and robustness.

Traditional
H(\text{div})
-conforming finite elements, such as Raviart-Thomas (RT) elements
[
33
,
34
]
and Brezzi-Douglas-Marini (BDM) elements
[
9
,
8
]
, are widely used for incompressible flow simulations
[
30
,
20
,
35
,
22
,
21
,
5
]
due to their satisfaction of the commuting diagram property and optimal approximation error estimates. In contrast, elements that fail to satisfy the commuting diagram property often exhibit velocity errors inversely proportional to the discrete inf-sup constant—unlike those that satisfy the property. This dependency becomes critical when the inf-sup constant degenerates (e.g., under anisotropic mesh refinement), as it leads to unbounded error growth and loss of numerical stability.
Certain modern
H(\text{div})
-conforming elements, such as Stenberg elements
[
39
]
, while not satisfying the commuting diagram property, offer notable advantages: inf-sup stability, exactly divergence-free solutions, and a substantial reduction in degrees of freedom relative to RT or BDM elements, achieved through their vertex continuity constraints. Nevertheless, elements of Stenberg type have seen limited adoption in incompressible flow simulations, where robust control of velocity errors is essential, despite these advantageous features.
The present study seeks to address this gap by establishing a convergence theory for
H(\text{div})
-conforming methods in the context of the Oseen problem, with particular emphasis on scenarios where the exact finite element complex holds but the commuting diagram property fails.

A key challenge in
H(\text{div})
-conforming methods is the lack of
H^{1}
-conformity, necessitating discontinuous Galerkin (DG) techniques for the discretization of diffusive and convective terms. Within the DG framework, upwinding is commonly employed to stabilize convection-dominated flows
[
14
]
. For upwind-based RT and BDM elements of order
k
, the orthogonal properties of RT elements enable proof of
k+1/2
-order convergence for the velocity field in the
L^{2}
-norm
[
5
,
21
,
22
]
. Analogous results have also been established for
H^{1}
-conforming methods including Scott-Vogelius (SV) elements
[
1
]
. Unfortunately, extending these convergence results to
H(\text{div})
-conforming methods that violate the commuting diagram property, such as Stenberg elements, remains an open problem.
To address this, we propose a stabilization approach that combines vorticity stabilization and upwinding, guaranteeing both numerical and theoretical

k+1/2
-order convergence while preserving pressure robustness. This stabilization strategy draws inspiration from existing DG and convection stabilization techniques but is specifically adapted to
H(\text{div})
-conforming settings.

This work focuses on the Oseen problems, encompassing but not limiting to convection-dominated cases. An
H(\text{div})
-conforming finite element method, stabilized with upwinding and vorticity stabilization, is developed to solve the problems. The analysis establishes pressure-robust
L^{2}
-norm convergence of order
k+1/2
for the velocity approximation, leveraging exact de Rham complexes and finite element complexes even when the commuting diagram property is violated. Beyond its application to Stenberg elements, the proposed methodology offers a general framework for analyzing a broader class of finite element pairs that lack the commuting diagram property, thereby expanding the range of efficient discretizations for fluid problems. Additionally, this work extends the concept of vorticity stabilization from
H^{1}
-conforming methods to
H(\text{div})
-conforming ones, opening new avenues for robust and accurate simulations of incompressible flows, particularly in high Reynolds number regimes.

2
Oseen equations and finite element methods

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\in\{2,3\}
) be a bounded, polyhedral, contractible domain with Lipschitz
continuous boundary
\partial\Omega
. We consider the following
Oseen equations, for simplicity of presentation
with homogeneous Dirichlet data: find the velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
and the pressure
p
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

where
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+c\boldsymbol{u}
,
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscosity,
\boldsymbol{b}
is the advection velocity,
c
is a given scalar function and
\boldsymbol{f}
is the external body force.
The Oseen-type problem arises as a fundamental auxiliary formulation in numerous numerical schemes for solving the Navier–Stokes equations (see
[
26
, Remark 5.1]
). In computational practice, this linearized problem appears in two primary contexts: For steady-state simulations, the Picard iteration method generates a sequence of Oseen problems where the reaction coefficient vanishes
c\equiv 0
and the convection term employs the velocity field from the preceding iteration as the advective velocity
\mathbf{b}
. In time-dependent simulations utilizing semi-implicit temporal discretization, each time step requires solving an Oseen problem where
\mathbf{u}
represents the unknown velocity at the new time level,
\mathbf{b}
corresponds to the explicitly treated advective velocity (typically extrapolated from previous time steps), and the reaction coefficient
c=1/\Delta t
emerges naturally from the time-stepping scheme.
Focusing primarily (though not exclusively) on the high-Reynolds-number regime, and motivated by
[
26
, Remark 5.3]
, we impose the following condition:

c(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})\geq r_{0}>0,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,

(2)

where
r_{0}
is a strictly positive constant.

Let
V:=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}

and
Q:=L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then the weak form of (
1
) reads as follows: Find
\boldsymbol{u}\in V
and
p\in Q
such that

\displaystyle\nu\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v},p)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{V^{\prime}\times V}\quad

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{v}\in V,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{u},q)

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\forall q\in Q,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=

\displaystyle(\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{v}),\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=((\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=

\displaystyle(c\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\big),\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v},p)=-(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v},p).

For vector fields we have
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,d\mathbf{x}
, while for matrix-valued functions
(\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{v})=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{v}\,d\mathbf{x}
denotes the Frobenius inner product.
The term
\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{V^{\prime}\times V}
denotes the duality pairing between the dual space
V^{\prime}
and
V
for
\mathbf{f}\in V^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{v}\in V
. Under condition (
2
) along with appropriate regularity assumptions on
\boldsymbol{b}
and
c
, the existence and uniqueness of a solution
(\mathbf{u},p)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
of (
3
) is guaranteed, as proved in
[
6
,
16
,
26
]
.

Next, we introduce the weakly divergence-free space

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{div}}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in V:\ (\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v},q)=0\quad\forall q\in Q\}.

This space consists of
H^{1}
-vector fields with zero divergence in the weak sense and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Our theoretical framework is based on potential functions of weakly divergence-free velocity fields and their corresponding divergence-free finite element approximations. This leads us to consider the following natural function spaces that characterize the kernel of the divergence operator:

\displaystyle Z:=

\displaystyle\left\{z\in H^{1}(\Omega):\;\mathrm{curl}z\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{2}\right\},\quad\text{if $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

\displaystyle Z:=

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{z}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{3}:\;\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{z}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{3}\right\},\quad\text{if $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}$}.

This space consists of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
(or
H^{1}(\Omega)^{3}
in 3D) whose curl lies in the subspace
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
The connection between these spaces and divergence-free fields is made precise by the following fundamental result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\in\{2,3\}
) be a bounded, Lipschitz domain. For any
\boldsymbol{u}\in H^{r}(\Omega)^{d}
with
r\geq 1
satisfying
\mathrm{div}\boldsymbol{u}=0
, there exists a potential
\boldsymbol{z}
(a scalar when
d=2
, or a vector when
d=3
) with components in
H^{r+1}(\Omega)
such that

\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{u}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(4)

Moreover, the following stability estimate holds

\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{r+1}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{r}

where C > 0 is independent of
\boldsymbol{u}
.

Proof.

The existence of such
\boldsymbol{z}
follows from the generalized Bogovskii operator theory
[
13
, Theorem 4.9(b)]
, utilizing the vanishing cohomology of contractible domains.
∎

Remark 1
.

Since
\nabla\cdot V\subset Q
, the weakly divergence-free condition implies pointwise divergence-free almost everywhere in
\Omega
. Thus, we may equivalentlly characterize
V_{\mathrm{div}}
as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{div}}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in V:\;\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\}.

Theorem
1
yields the fundamental relationship between the potential space and divergence-free space:

\mathrm{curl}Z=V_{\mathrm{div}}.

(5)

2.1
Finite element spaces and de Rham complexes

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}
be a family of shape-regular meshes of
\Omega
consisting of triangles (
d=2
) or tetrahedra (
d=3
). For each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote its diameter by
h_{K}
and define the mesh size
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}
. The set of all facets in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, which is partitioned into: the subset
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}
of interior facets and the subset
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}_{h}
of boundary facets
F\subset\partial\Omega
.
For each facet
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we fix a unit normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{F}
, chosen arbitrarily for interior facets and taken as the outward unit normal
\mathbf{n}
for boundary facets. Let
\varphi
be a piecewise continuous (scalar-, vector- or tensor-valued) function. For any facet
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in F
, we define:

[\varphi]_{F}(\boldsymbol{x})\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\lim\limits_{t\to 0+0}(\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}-t\boldsymbol{n}_{F})-\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}+t\boldsymbol{n}_{F}))\quad&&\text{if $F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}$},\\
&\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\quad&&\text{if $F\in\mathcal{F}^{\partial}_{h}$},\end{aligned}\right.

and

\{\varphi\}_{F}(\boldsymbol{x})\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\lim\limits_{t\to 0+0}\frac{1}{2}(\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}-t\boldsymbol{n}_{F})+\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}+t\boldsymbol{n}_{F}))\quad&&\text{if $F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}$},\\
&\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\quad&&\text{if $F\in\mathcal{F}^{\partial}_{h}$}.\end{aligned}\right.

The operators act componentwise
for vector- and tensor-valued functions. The facet subscript
F
is often omitted when clear from context. Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
denote the
L^{2}
-inner product on facet
F
, defined by

\langle v,w\rangle_{F}:=\int_{F}vw\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\forall v,w\in L^{2}(F).

Additionally, we define the following broken inner products:

(v,w)_{h}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(v,w)_{K},\quad\langle v,w\rangle_{\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}}\langle v,w\rangle_{F},\quad\langle v,w\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\langle v,w\rangle_{F}

(6)

with associated norms
\|\cdot\|_{h}
,
\|\cdot\|_{h,\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}}
,
\|\cdot\|_{h,\mathcal{F}_{h}}
, respectively.

The finite element spaces employed in this work are deeply connected to two classical de Rham complexes, which serve as a cornerstone for both theoretical analysis and the development of stable numerical methods for partial differential equations
[
4
,
3
,
2
]
. For a two-dimensional domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the first complex
[
16
, §1.3 ]
is given by

0\hookrightarrow H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\xlongrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{curl}}}\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\mathrm{div},\Omega)\xlongrightarrow{\mathrm{div}}L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to 0,

(7)

while for a three-dimensional domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the second complex
[
12
, §4.2]
takes the form

0\hookrightarrow H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\xlongrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{grad}}}\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)\xlongrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{curl}}}\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\mathrm{div},\Omega)\xlongrightarrow{\mathrm{div}}L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to 0.

(8)

Here, the function spaces are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\mathrm{div},\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}:\;\;\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathrm{div},\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathrm{div},\Omega):\;\;\mathbf{v}|_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{3}:\;\;\mathbf{curl}\mathbf{v}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{3}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega):\;\;\mathbf{v}|_{\partial\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\},

where
d=2
or 3 denotes the spatial dimension and
\boldsymbol{n}
is the unit normal vector of
\partial\Omega
.
A sequence being a complex means that the composition of any two consecutive mappings vanishes. The complex is exact when the range of each map coincides precisely with the kernel of the subsequent one. By virtue of the domain
\Omega
being contractible, the de Rham complexes (
7
) (for
d=2
) and (
8
) (for
d=3
) are both exact, as established in
[
16
,
3
]
.

We begin by defining the finite element space for velocity:

\displaystyle V_{h}=\{

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{H}(\mathrm{div},\Omega):\;\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(K),\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h};\;\;\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}.

Here, the local space
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(K)
is a set of vector-valued piecewise polynomials satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)^{d}\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(K)
and
\mathbb{P}_{k+1}(K)^{d}\not\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(K)
. To maintain the generality of the theoretical framework presented in Section
3
, we do not impose further explicit constraints on the definition of
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(K)
.
The finite element space
V_{h}
satisfies the conforming inclusion
V_{h}\subset H_{0}(\text{div},\Omega)
, while typically failing to satisfy
V_{h}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
. This construction encompasses classical elements including RT and BDM elements, as well as more recent variants like Stenberg elements. The finite element space for pressure is denoted by
Q_{h}
and satisfies
Q_{h}\subset L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Existing convergence analyses for incompressible flows employing
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming elements—particularly RT and BDM elements—universally rely on the commuting diagram property. One important consequence of this property is that the velocity error estimates become independent of the discrete inf-sup constant. However, modern elements like the efficient Stenberg variant lack this property. We therefore develop a convergence theory based on exact finite element complexes, extending the framework to these elements.

Assumption 1
.

The pair

forms discrete finite element subcomplexes as follows:

\text{{2D case}}:\quad 0\hookrightarrow Z_{h}\xlongrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{curl}}}V_{h}\xlongrightarrow{\mathrm{div}}Q_{h}\to 0

and

\text{{3D case}}:\quad 0\hookrightarrow W_{h}\xlongrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{grad}}}Z_{h}\xlongrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{curl}}}V_{h}\xlongrightarrow{\mathrm{div}}Q_{h}\to 0.

(10)

These discrete complexes satisfy the following fundamental properties:

1.

They provide conforming discretizations of the continuous complexes (
7
) and (
8
), respectively, with the following inclusions:

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle\subset L^{2}_{0}(\Omega),

\displaystyle V_{h}

\displaystyle\subset\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathrm{div};\Omega),

and

\begin{cases}Z_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)&\text{for }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},\\

(11)

2.

The discrete complexes are exact.

The local approximation space

Remark 3

The exactness

The inclusion (

Lemma 1

Assume
\boldsymbol{z}\in Z\cap H^{k+2}(\Omega)
. For every multi-index
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following estimation holds:

\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in Z_{h}}\|\partial^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})\|_{h}\leq Ch^{k+2-|\alpha|}|\boldsymbol{z}|_{k+2,\Omega}\quad\text{for $|\alpha|\leq k+1$}.

Remark 4
.

For incompressible flows, the commuting diagram property has been a standard requirement for
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming elements in numerous classical works
[
5
,
35
,
30
,
22
]
.
This property theoretically ensures that the velocity field error is independent of the discrete inf-sup stability constant.

Assumption
1
replaces this classical requirement with the exactness of the discrete complex
(V_{h},Q_{h})
. Although exactness follows from the commuting diagram property, the reverse implication fails in general. This work thereby broadens the scope of viable discrete spaces while contributing new theoretical insights to mixed finite element analysis.

2.3
Stabilized
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming finite element method

The stabilized
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming finite element method of (
1
) reads as follows:
Find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} A(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})=&\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\\
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})=&0\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},\end{aligned}\right.

(15)

where
A(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}):=\nu\mathcal{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
and
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\delta_{0}(\tau\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{f},\mathrm{curl}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{h}
. The components of
A(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
are presented in the remainder of this subsection.

Remark 7
.

From Remark
3
, one has
\mathrm{div}V_{h}\subset Q_{h}
and the second equation in (
15
) implies

\displaystyle V_{h,\mathrm{div}}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}:\;\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\}.

Note that
V_{h}\not\subset V
, which implies

V_{h,\mathrm{div}}\not\subset V_{\mathrm{div}}
.

In order to introduce
A(\cdot,\cdot)
, we first define the space

V(h)=V_{h}+\left[V\cap H^{3}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\right].

For the discretisation of the diffusion term, we employ the standard symmetric interior penalty form
\mathcal{D}_{h}:\;V(h)\times V_{h}\to\mathbb{R}

[
14
]
. The form
\mathcal{D}_{h}
is defined
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}):=

\displaystyle(\nabla\boldsymbol{w},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{h}-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\left(\langle\{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\}\boldsymbol{n}_{F},[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\rangle_{F}+\langle[\boldsymbol{w}],\{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\}\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\rangle_{F}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\frac{\sigma}{h_{F}}\langle[\boldsymbol{w}],[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\rangle_{F},

where
[\nabla\boldsymbol{w}]_{ij}=\frac{\partial w_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
and
h_{F}
is the diameter of the face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
. The penalty parameter
\sigma
is a positive constant independent with
\nu
and
h
and has to be chosen sufficiently large to ensure the coercivity of
\mathcal{D}_{h}
.

Remark 8
.

The finite element method enforces the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
\mathbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbf{0}
through a hybrid strategy. Taking the Stenberg finite element as an example, the method combines: (1) strong imposition in the discrete space
V_{h}
by setting
\mathbf{u}_{h}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
at all vertices
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega
and enforcing the normal flux condition
\int_{F}\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,q\,\mathrm{d}s=0
for all
q\in\mathbb{P}_{k-1}(F)
on each boundary face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
; (2) weak imposition of the remaining boundary conditions through the numerical scheme.

The reaction term
\mathcal{R}:\;V(h)\times V_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
and the pressure-velocity coupling
\mathcal{P}:\;V(h)\times Q\to\mathbb{R}
remains unchanged:

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(c\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\quad\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h}):=-(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})_{h}.

For the (linearised) inertia term, we introduce the convection term
\mathcal{C}_{h}:~V(h)\times V_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
with upwinding and the vorticity stabilitzation
S:\;V(h)\times V_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}):=

\displaystyle((\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{h}-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{i}}\langle(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F})[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}],\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\gamma^{c}_{F}\langle|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\rangle_{F},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=

\displaystyle\delta_{0}\left\{(\tau\mathrm{curl}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\mathrm{curl}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{h}+\langle h^{2}[(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}],[(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}]\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}\right\},

where the broken scalar products are defined in (
6
) and
\delta_{0}>0
is a constant will be specified later. The weighting factor
\gamma^{c}_{F}
is given by

\gamma^{c}_{F}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\quad&&\text{ if $F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}$},\\
&\frac{1-\mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})}{2}\quad&&\text{if $F\in\mathcal{F}^{\partial}_{h}$},\end{aligned}\right.

and the stabilization parameter

\tau|_{K}=\tau_{K}
(also see
[
1
]
) is defined as

\tau_{K}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}h_{K}}{\nu}\right\}\frac{h^{3}_{K}}{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}}.

Remark 9
.

For
H^{1}
-conforming divergence-free inf-sup stable finite element pairs, the vorticity stabilization introduced in
[
1
]
differs from classical SUPG methods
[
25
]
in its stabilization target. While standard SUPG stabilizes the convection-dominated term
(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}
directly, vorticity stabilization specifically acts on the divergence-free term
\mathrm{curl}((\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u})
.
Unlike conventional SUPG stabilization, which produces pressure-dependent velocity solutions through its inclusion of pressure terms in the stabilization operator, the vorticity stabilization, by design, eliminates this coupling and rigorously maintains pressure-robust velocity approximations.

Remark 10
.

The term
\mathcal{C}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})
represents the standard upwind discretization of the convection term. In classical
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming finite element methods, particularly BDM and RT elements, conventional upwinding schemes are consistently employed to stabilize the convection-dominated terms
[
5
,
35
,
30
,
22
]
.

The vorticity stabilization
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})
is introduced to ensure that the velocity approximation error achieves an order of
k+1/2
in the
L^{2}
norm while preserving pressure robustness. If
\mathcal{C}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})
is defined as

\big((\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}\big)_{h}-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{i}}\big\langle(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{F})[\mathbf{u}_{h}],\{\mathbf{v}_{h}\}\big\rangle_{F}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}}\gamma^{c}_{F}\langle|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\rangle_{F},

(16)

which corresponds to a central flux discretization,
numerical experiments reveal certain instabilities.
Previous studies
[
28
]
have shown that in convection-dominated regimes, nonconforming finite element methods require not only SUPG-type stabilization but also additional jump penalization to maintain stability, as standalone SUPG stabilization may be insufficient.

To conduct the analysis, we introduce a norm that is mesh-dependent, as follows:

\displaystyle\vvvert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\vvvert^{2}:=

\displaystyle\|\nu^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{h}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sigma\nu h_{F}^{-1}\langle[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\rangle_{F}

(17)

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\langle|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\rangle_{F}+\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle+r_{0}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{0}.

For the subsequent well-posedness and convergence analysis of numerical solutions, we require both continuous and discrete forms of the trace theorem. The following local trace inequality
[
40
]
is fundamental: there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, every facet
F\subset\partial K
, and every function
v\in H^{1}(K)
,

|v|_{0,F}\leq C\left(h^{-1/2}_{K}|v|_{0,K}+h^{1/2}_{K}|v|_{1,K}\right).

(18)

Additionally, we employ the discrete trace inequality (see
[
14
, Remark 1.47]
), which holds particularly because
\mathbf{V}_{k}(K)
consists of vector-valued piecewise polynomials: For any
\mathbf{v}_{h}\in V_{h}
and any element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the following trace inequality holds :

|\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{0,\partial K}\leq Ch^{-1/2}_{K}|\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{0,K}.

(19)

3
Wellposedness of the finite element method and convergence

To establish the well-posedness and convergence analysis for (
15
), we require two fundamental mathematical ingredients. First, we recall the following coercivity result:

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\sigma>0
is sufficiently large. Then, the bilinear form
A(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive on

V_{h}
with respect to the energy norm
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert
, i.e.,

A(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\geq\frac{1}{2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\vvvert^{2}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}.

(20)

Proof.

The proof follows from
[
14
, Lemma 4.59]
.
∎

Second, the following discrete inf-sup condition for the finite element pair
V_{h}/Q_{h}
is also needed.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a constant
\beta^{h}_{is}>0
, independent of the mesh size
h
and the viscosity
\nu
, such that

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h}\|q_{h}\|_{0}}=\beta_{is}^{h},

(21)

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\vvvert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\vvvert\|q_{h}\|_{0}}\geq C(\nu+\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}h+1)^{-1/2}\beta_{is}^{h},

(22)

where the mesh-dependent norm is defined as

\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h}^{2}=\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{h}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{-1}_{F}\|[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\|^{2}_{0,F}.

According to
[
7
, (1.3)]
, for all
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}
, we have the following stability estimate:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{0}\leq

\displaystyle C\left[\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{h}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}}h^{-d}_{F}\left(\int_{F}[\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}+\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h}^{2}.

Combining this with inverse inequalities, we obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\vvvert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\vvvert^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C(\nu+\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}h+\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{K}h^{-2}_{K}(\nu^{2}h^{-2}_{K}+\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}+1)+1)\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h}^{2},

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\nu+\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}h+(\nu+h+h/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty})+1)\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h}^{2}

and

\displaystyle\vvvert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\vvvert\leq C(\nu+\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{0,\infty}h+1)^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h},

(23)

where
C
is a positive constant independent of
h
and
\nu
.
Furthermore, if the discrete inf-sup condition (
21
) holds, then the equivalence (
23
) immediately implies the validity of (
22
).

Lemma 3
.

For the specific choice of finite element pair
V_{h}/Q_{h}=\mathrm{Stenberg}_{k}/\mathrm{DG}_{k-1}
, where
k\geq 2
in 2D or
k\geq 3
in 3D, the stability conditions in Assumption
2
are satisfied.

Proof.

The detailed proof can be found in the Appendix.
∎

The well-posedness of the discrete problem (
15
) follows from two key ingredients: the coercivity property established in Lemma
2
and the discrete inf-sup condition (
22
). A complete analysis of this mixed formulation can be found in
[

Moreover, the numerical scheme (

\left\{\begin{aligned} A(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p-p_{h})=&0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\\
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})=&0\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

(24)

3.1
An error estimate for the velocity

Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in Z_{h}
be arbitrary and set
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}:=\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in V_{h,\mathrm{div}}
.
Define

\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\eta}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}.

where

\boldsymbol{\eta}:=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}=\mathrm{curl}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})
from Theorem
1
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{w}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
.

Remark 11
.

Based on the exactness of the finite element subcomplex (Assumption
1
), i.e.,
\mathrm{curl}Z_{h}=V_{h,\mathrm{div}}
, we establish a relationship between the approximation errors
\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}
for
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in V_{h,\mathrm{div}}
and
\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}
for any
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in Z_{h}
. The latter is more amenable to analysis due to the simpler structure of
Z_{h}
.

Let
z
be sufficiently regular. We define the norm:

\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\ast}:=\vvvert\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{z}\vvvert^{2}+(\nu+h)\sum_{s=0}^{3}h^{2s-4}\|D^{s}\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{h},

where
D^{s}\boldsymbol{z}
denotes the derivative tensor
(\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{z})_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=s}
, that is, gradient for
s=1
, Hessian matrix for
s=2
, etc.
Our analysis begins with a semi-robust estimate of the convection and reaction terms.

Lemma 4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
\nu
such that

|\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\vvvert,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}.

Proof.

Integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\}\cdot[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}]\mathrm{d}s-(\boldsymbol{\eta},(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})_{h}

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\gamma^{c}_{F}\langle|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|[\boldsymbol{\eta}],[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}]\rangle_{F}+((c-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{1}+\mathscr{C}_{2}+\mathscr{C}_{3}+\mathscr{C}_{4}.

We now bound each term on the right-hand side of (
25
).
By the trace inequality (
18
), for any facet
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}

\|[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\|^{2}_{0,F}+\|\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\}\|^{2}_{0,F}\leq C\sum_{K\in K_{F}}\left(h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|^{2}_{0,K}+h\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)

(26)

where
K_{F}:=\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:\,F\subset\partial K\}
.
Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathscr{C}_{1}|+|\mathscr{C}_{3}|\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}(\|[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\|_{0,F}+\|\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\}\|_{0,F})\;\|\sqrt{|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}]\|_{0,F}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|^{2}_{0}+h\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{0}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert,

and

|\mathscr{C}_{4}|=((c-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{0}\;\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{0}\leq Ch^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert.

(28)

We now proceed to estimate the most challenging term
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. We reformulate
\mathcal{C}_{2}
through integration by parts:

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{2}=

\displaystyle-(\boldsymbol{\eta},(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})_{h}=-(\mathrm{curl}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})_{h}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{i}}\int_{F}[\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}]\cdot\{(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\int_{F}\{\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\}\cdot[(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{F}]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h},\mathrm{curl}((\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}))_{h}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{2,a}+\mathscr{C}_{2,b}+\mathscr{C}_{2,c}.

When
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, direct calculation yields

[\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}]\cdot\{(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\}=-[\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}]\times\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\cdot\{(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\}\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}.

Combining this identity with the regularity results from (
13
), we conclude that

\mathscr{C}_{2,a}=0.

(30)

An application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, together with the substitution
\eta\to\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}
in (
26
), leads to

\displaystyle|\mathscr{C}_{2,b}|=\left|\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\int_{F}\{\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\}\cdot[(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{F}]\mathrm{d}s\right|

(31)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{-2}\|\{\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\}\|^{2}_{0,F}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{2}\|[(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{F}]\|^{2}_{0,F}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{-1}\left(h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|^{2}_{0}+h\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})\|^{2}_{h}\right)^{1/2}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert.

For every element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the inverse inequality yields

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{curl}(\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}-c\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\|_{0,K}\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{-1}_{K}(h^{-1}_{K}\nu\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{0,K}+\|c\|_{0,\infty,K}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\|_{0,K})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\nu^{1/2}h^{-2}+h^{-1})\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert_{K}.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathscr{C}_{2,c}|=|(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h},\mathrm{curl}((\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}))_{h}|

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h},\mathrm{curl}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}))_{h}+(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h},\mathrm{curl}(\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}-c\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}))_{h}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{K}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{K}\|\mathrm{curl}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{0,K}\|\mathrm{curl}(\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}-c\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(h^{-3/2}+\nu^{1/2}h^{-2}\right)\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{0}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert.

Substituting the bounds from (
30
)—(
32
) into (
29
), we derive the following estimate:

|\mathscr{C}_{2}|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\;\vvvert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\vvvert.

(33)

Corollary 1
.

In addition to the assumptions of Theorem

, suppose that

\boldsymbol{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\cap H^{k+1}(\Omega)^{d}
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0

and

, such that

\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\vvvert\leq Ch^{k}(h^{1/2}+\nu^{1/2})\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{k+1}.

Remark 12
.

The velocity error is pressure-robust, independent of pressure approximation. In the construction of pressure-robust finite elements,
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming elements— particularly RT and BDM elements—have been widely adopted (see
[
6
,
29
,
30
,
35
,
5
,
22
]
). The popularity of these elements stems largely from their satisfaction of the commuting diagram property, which facilitates straightforward derivation of pressure-robust optimal error estimates for the velocity field. In contrast, another
H(\mathrm{div})
–conforming element—the Stenberg element—though featuring fewer degrees of freedom, has received comparatively less attention due to its failure to satisfy the commuting diagram property. Leveraging the property that Stenberg elements form an exact finite element subcomplex, this work establishes for the first time pressure-robust optimal velocity error estimates, thereby paving the way for further investigation of these elements.

Remark 13
.

When
\nu\leq Ch
, the velocity error attains the optimal convergence order of
k+\frac{1}{2}
in convection-dominated regimes. This result is consistent with the sharpness bounds established in prior works: for RT and BDM elements in
[
5
]
, and for Scott-Vogelius element in
[
1
]
. For
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming finite element methods of convection-dominated Oseen problems, particularly when using RT and BDM elements, the
k+1/2
-order convergence of the velocity field using
k
-th order elements relies crucially on three key factors: the commuting diagram property, the special relationship between BDM and RT elements, and the orthogonal properties of the RT interpolation operator. In contrast, for other
H(\mathrm{div})
elements such as Stenberg elements which generally lack these properties, the analogous analytical techniques become inapplicable for achieving such convergence rates. In this work, we establish the
k+\frac{1}{2}
-order convergence through two key components: exact finite element subcomplexes and vorticity-based stabilization. This approach is not limited to specific element choices and can be naturally extended to a wide range of finite element pairs.

3.2
An error estimate for the pressure

Denote by
\pi_{h}:L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\rightarrow Q_{h}
the
L^{2}(\Omega)
orthogonal projection onto
Q_{h}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
solve (
1
) and

(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
solve (
15
). Let
\boldsymbol{z}
satisfy (
4
). Suppose Assumption
1
and
2
hold. Then the following error estimate holds: For any
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in Z_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}=\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in V_{h,\mathrm{div}}
,

\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}\leq C\left(\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\vvvert+\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\vvvert+(h^{1/2}+\nu h^{-1/2})\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}\right).

Proof.

From (
21
), for
\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\in Q_{h}
, we can find

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\in V_{h}
satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}=\pi_{h}p-p_{h},\quad\text{since $\mathrm{div}V_{h}\subset Q_{h}$},

(35)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|_{1,h}\leq(\beta^{h}_{is})^{-1}\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

Combining this with the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality
[
7
, (1.3)]
, we derive

\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|^{2}_{0}\leq C\left(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|^{2}_{h}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{-1}_{F}\|[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\|^{2}_{F}\right)\leq C\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|^{2}_{0}.

(36)

Let
\boldsymbol{e}:=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
. Then from (
35
) and (
24
), one has

\displaystyle\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|^{2}_{0}=

\displaystyle(\mathrm{div}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h},\pi_{h}p-p_{h})=\underbrace{(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h},\pi_{h}p-p)}_{=0,\;\text{since $\mathrm{div}V_{h}\subset Q_{h}$ }}-A(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\nu\mathcal{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})+\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})+\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})).

We now analyze each term on the right-hand side in detail.

First, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality along with (
35
) gives

\displaystyle(\nabla\boldsymbol{e},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})_{h}\leq

\displaystyle\|\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\|_{h}\;\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|_{h}\leq C\nu^{-1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\;\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\frac{\sigma}{h_{F}}\langle[\boldsymbol{e}],[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sigma^{2}h_{F}^{-1}\langle[\boldsymbol{e}],[\boldsymbol{e}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h_{F}^{-1}\langle[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\nu^{-1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

Furthermore, for any
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in Z_{h}
with
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}=\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, trace inequality (
18
), triangle inequality and inverse inequality imply

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\langle\{\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\}\boldsymbol{n}_{F},[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}\leq C\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h_{F}\langle\{\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\},\{\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\}\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h_{F}^{-1}\langle[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h_{F}\sum_{K\in K_{F}}\left(h^{-1}_{K}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\|_{0,K}^{2}+h_{K}\|D^{2}\boldsymbol{e}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{h}+h^{2}\|D^{2}(\boldsymbol{u}-\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})\|^{2}_{h}+h^{2}\|D^{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\|^{2}_{h}\right)^{1/2}\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{h}+h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|^{2}_{*}+\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\|^{2}_{h}\right)^{1/2}\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\nu^{-1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert+h^{-1/2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}+\nu^{-1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\vvvert\right)\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0},

where
K_{F}
has been defined in (
26
). Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the discrete trace inequality (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\langle[\boldsymbol{e}],\{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\}\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\rangle_{F}\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h_{F}^{-1}\langle[\boldsymbol{e}],[\boldsymbol{e}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h_{F}\langle\{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\},\{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\}\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\nu^{-1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|_{h}\leq C\nu^{-1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

Thus, we conclude

\nu|\mathcal{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})|\leq C(\nu^{1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert+\nu^{1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\vvvert+\nu h^{-1/2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*})\;\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

(38)

Integration by parts leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\{\boldsymbol{e}\}\cdot[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\mathrm{d}s-(\boldsymbol{e},(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})_{h}

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\gamma^{c}_{F}\langle|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|[\boldsymbol{e}],[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}+((c-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{1}+\mathscr{P}_{2}+\mathscr{P}_{3}+\mathscr{P}_{4}.

We now estimate each term individually.
By the trace inequality (
18
), triangle inequality and inverse inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\langle\{\boldsymbol{e}\},\{\boldsymbol{e}\}\rangle_{F}\leq C\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(h^{-1}_{K}\|\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{0,K}+h_{K}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{0,K})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{0}+h\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}-\mathrm{curl}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})\|^{2}_{h}+h\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|^{2}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{0}+\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*}^{2}+h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|^{2}_{0}\right).

Then Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and stability estimate (
35
) yield

\displaystyle|\mathscr{P}_{1}|\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\langle\{\boldsymbol{e}\},\{\boldsymbol{e}\}\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{-1}_{F}\langle[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert^{2}+h\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|^{2}_{*}+\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\vvvert^{2})^{1/2}\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

From Hölder’s inequality and the stability estimate (
35
), we deduce

\displaystyle|\mathscr{P}_{3}|\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\gamma^{c}_{F}\langle|\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}|[\boldsymbol{e}],[\boldsymbol{e}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{-1}_{F}\langle|[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}],[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}]\rangle_{F}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{1/2}\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

Additionally, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, along with stability estimates (
35
) and (
36
), gives

\displaystyle|\mathscr{P}_{2}|+|\mathscr{P}_{4}|\leq

\displaystyle C\|\boldsymbol{e}\|_{0}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|_{h}+C\|\boldsymbol{e}\|_{0}\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|_{0}\leq C\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

Combining these estimates, we arrive at

|\mathcal{C}_{h}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})|\leq C(\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert+\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\vvvert+h^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\|_{*})\;\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

(39)

Finally, applying the inverse inequality, discrete trace inequality (
19
), and stability estimates (
35
) and (
36
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})\leq

\displaystyle C(h^{3}\|\mathrm{curl}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})\|^{2}_{h}+h^{2}\|(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|^{2}_{h,\mathcal{F}_{h}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\nu^{2}h^{-1}+h)\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|^{2}_{h}+Ch\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h}\|^{2}_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\nu^{2}h^{-1}+h)\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}^{2}.

Thus, we have

\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})\leq\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{e})^{1/2}\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{h})^{1/2}\leq C\vvvert\boldsymbol{e}\vvvert\;(\nu h^{-1/2}+h^{1/2})\|\pi_{h}p-p_{h}\|_{0}.

(40)

Substituting (
38
)—(
40
) into (
37
) completes the proof.
∎

Remark 14
.

Theorem

\vvvert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\vvvert

3

Remark 15
.

When employing the Stenberg space for velocity discretization paired with a pressure space of degree

Proof of Lemma
3

To prove

\inf\limits_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,h}\|q_{h}\|_{0}}=\beta_{is}^{h}>0,

(43)

we will
employ the macro-element analysis technique introduced in
[
38
]
. First, we introduce some notations.
Let
M=K_{1}\cup K_{2}
be an arbitrary macroelement consisting of two adjacent elements
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
sharing a common edge (in 2D) or face (in 3D). The space
Q_{h}
admits the orthogonal decomposition:

Q_{h}=\bigoplus_{M}Q_{h}(M)\oplus\bar{Q}_{h}

where

\displaystyle Q_{h}(M)=\{q_{h}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega):\;q_{h}|_{K_{i}}\in P_{k-1}(K_{i}),\;i=1,2;\;q_{h}=0\;\text{on $\Omega-M$}\},

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{h}=\left\{q_{h}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega):\;q_{h}|_{M}\in P_{0}(M)\right\}.

Define the local velocity space on
M
as:

V_{h}(M)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{v}\in H_{0}(\mathrm{div},M):\;\boldsymbol{v}|_{K_{i}}\in\left(P_{k}(K_{i})\right)^{d},\;i=1,2;\\
&\text{all degrees of freedom on $\partial M$ vanish};\;\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\;\text{on $\Omega-M$}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Then, the stability (
43
) can be established through verification of two key conditions: first, the uniform inf-sup stability of the pair
V_{h}(M)/Q_{h}(M)
with respect to
M
, and second, the inf-sup stability of the pair
V_{h}/\bar{Q}_{h}
.

A.1
(a) Uniform inf-sup stability for
V_{h}(M)/Q_{h}(M)

Given the quasi-uniformity of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the finite element pair
V_{h}(M)/Q_{h}(M)
is uniformly inf-sup stable with respect to

M
if and only if the following macroelement condition (cf.
[
38
]
,
[
26
, §3.5.4]
) holds :

N_{M}:=\left\{q\in Q_{h}(M):(\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v},q)_{M}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{v}\in V_{h}(M)\right\}=\{0\}.

(44)

Let
q|_{K_{i}}=\sum_{|\beta|=0}^{k-1}c_{\beta}^{i}\mathbf{x}^{\beta}
.
For each
i=1,2
and multi-index
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
, we construct
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\alpha}\in V_{h}(M)
whose support is contained in
K_{i}
and which satisfies

\int_{K_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=1,

as its sole non-zero degree of freedom; cf.
[
39
, (2.9), (2.10), (2.12)–(2.16)]
.
Then, integration by parts yields:

0=(\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\alpha},q)_{M}=-\sum_{|\beta|=0}^{k-1}c_{\beta}^{i}\int_{K_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{x}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=-c_{\alpha}^{i}.

Thus,
c_{\alpha}^{i}=0
for
|\alpha|\geq 1
, implying
q|_{K_{i}}=c_{i}
(constant on each
K_{i}
). Further, for any
\mathbf{v}\in V_{h}(M)
,

0=(\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v},q)_{M}=(c_{1}-c_{2})\int_{F}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{F}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad F=K_{1}\cap K_{2}.

Select
\mathbf{v}\in V_{h}(M)
such that
\int_{F}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{F}\,\mathrm{d}s=1
. This forces
c_{1}=c_{2}
, so
q|_{M}
is globally constant. Since
q\in L^{2}_{0}(M)
, we conclude
q=0
. Thus the macroelement condition (
44
) holds.

A.2
(b) Inf-sup stability for
V_{h}/\bar{Q}_{h}

The inf-sup stability of the pair
V_{h}/\bar{Q}_{h}
holds if and only if there exists a Fortin operator
\pi:V\to V_{h}
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot(\pi\mathbf{v}),\bar{q}_{h})=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v},\bar{q}_{h}),

\displaystyle\forall\bar{q}_{h}\in\bar{Q}_{h},

(45)

\displaystyle\|\pi\mathbf{v}\|_{1,h}\leq C\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1},

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{v}\in V.

To ensure the first condition in (
45
), we impose the flux continuity requirement:

\int_{T}(\boldsymbol{v}-\pi\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\mathrm{d}s=0,

(46)

where
T=M\cap M^{\prime}
denotes the interface between adjacent macroelements
M
and
M^{\prime}
.
For the stability of the Fortin operator, we prescribe the vertex-based averaging:

(\pi\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{|S_{i}|}\int_{S_{i}}\mathbf{v},

(47)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are vertices of
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
All remaining degrees of freedom for
\pi\boldsymbol{v}
follow the conventional definition of Stenberg elements
[
39
]
, with the modification that the degree of freedom corresponding to (2.15) in
[
39
]
must be expressed in integral form. This completes the full specification of the operator
\pi\mathbf{v}
, which satisfies the first equation in (
45
).

The stability estimate
\|\pi v\|_{1,h}\leq C|v|_{1}
follows from the definition of the projection operator
\pi
combined with the Poincaré inequality.

We focus on the two-dimensional case. Let
\hat{K}
be the reference element with vertices
\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{2}
and edges
\hat{e}_{0},\hat{e}_{1},\hat{e}_{2}
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{i}
denotes the unit outer normal vector to the edge
\hat{e}_{i}
. Consider a mesh element
K=\mathscr{F}(\hat{K})
obtained through an affine mapping
\mathscr{F}
, with corresponding vertices
\boldsymbol{V}_{i}=\mathscr{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i})
and edges
e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}
. The unit outer normal vector to
e_{i}\subset\partial K
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{n}^{i}
.

The dual basis
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})
is defined with respect to the degrees of freedom
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
on the reference element
\hat{K}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i}),\quad i=0,1,2,

(48)

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{e}_{i}}\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{i}\,\hat{\phi}_{j}^{i}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{s},\quad i=0,1,2,\quad\hat{\phi}_{j}^{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{k-2}(\hat{e}_{i}),

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{K}}\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{k-2}(\hat{K}),

where
\{\hat{\phi}_{j}^{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\}
forms a basis for the polynomial space
\mathbb{P}_{k-2}(\hat{e}_{i})
, and
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\}
constitutes a basis for the first
kind Nédélec space
\mathcal{N}_{k-2}(\hat{K})
, whose definition is referred to
[
6
, (2.3.37)]
. Similarly, the dual basis
\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})
on the physical element
K
corresponds to the degrees of freedom
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{V}_{i}),\quad i=0,1,2,

(49)

\displaystyle\int_{e_{i}}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{i}\,\phi_{j}^{i}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad i=0,1,2,\quad\phi_{j}^{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{k-2}(e_{i}),

\displaystyle\int_{K}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\quad\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{k-2}(K),

with the basis functions transforming according to:

\displaystyle\phi_{j}^{i}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}_{j}^{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\quad\text{where }\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathscr{F}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(50)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}).

Assume

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),

(51)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})=

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\boldsymbol{y},

where

\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{V}_{0}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{V}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{V}_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{e}_{0,0}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{e}_{2,k-2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{C}_{0}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots)^{T}

is the vector-valued function consisting of the dual basis functions
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
is the coordinate vector of
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

From (
50
) and (
51
), we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{V}_{i})=\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i})\quad\forall i,

(52)

\displaystyle\int_{e_{i}}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{i}\;\phi_{j}^{i}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\hat{e}_{i}}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{i}\;\hat{\phi}_{j}^{i}\mathrm{d}\hat{s}\quad\forall i,j,

\displaystyle\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\hat{K}}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\mathrm{d}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\forall i.

Then
we have

\boldsymbol{y}=B_{K}\boldsymbol{b},

(53)

where

B_{K}=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{I}\end{matrix}\right),

and

\boldsymbol{b}=\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{V}_{0}),\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}),\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2}),\int_{e_{0}}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{0}\;\phi^{0}_{0}\mathrm{d}s,\ldots,\int_{e_{2}}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{2}\;\phi^{2}_{k-2}\mathrm{d}s,\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\ldots\right)^{T}.

The operator
\pi
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\pi\boldsymbol{v}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\frac{1}{|S_{i}|}\int_{S_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\;\boldsymbol{\omega}^{V}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})

(54)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\int_{e_{i}}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{i})\phi_{j}^{i}\mathrm{d}s\;\boldsymbol{\omega}^{e}_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{i}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\;\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}^{C}(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
S_{i}
is the union of mesh elements containing the vertex
V_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{V}_{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{e}_{i,j},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}^{C}
represent the dual basis functions corresponding to the degrees of freedom specified in (
49
).

Now we need to prove the following stability: for
m=0,1

\|D^{m}\pi\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,K}\leq C\left(h^{-m}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(K)}+h^{1-m}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(K)}\right),

(55)

where
S(K)
is the set of the influence elements of
K
, i.e.,

S(K)=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{I}(K)}T,\quad\mathcal{I}(K):=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:\;T\cap K\neq\emptyset\}.

From Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the continuous trace inequality (
18
) and (
50
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|S_{i}|}\int_{S_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\right|\leq Ch^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(K)},

(56)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{e_{i}}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\underline{\boldsymbol{n}}^{i})\phi_{j}\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,e_{i}}\|\phi_{j}\|_{0,e_{i}}\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,K}+h\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,K}\right),

\displaystyle\left|\int_{K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,K}\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\|_{0,K}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,K}.

From (
53
) and (
51
), one has

\displaystyle\|D^{m}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{V}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{0,K}\leq Ch^{1-m},

(57)

\displaystyle\|D^{m}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{e}_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{0,K}+\|D^{m}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{C}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{0,K}\leq Ch^{-m}.

From (
54
), (
56
) and (
57
), we can easily obtain (
55
).

Define

\boldsymbol{c}_{K}=\frac{1}{|S(K)|}\int_{S(K)}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

and note

\int_{S(K)}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{K})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0.

From (
55
) and the Poincaré inequality
[
26
, Lemma C.3]
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla\pi\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,K}=\|\nabla(\pi(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{K}))\|_{0,K}

(58)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(h^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{K}\|_{0,S(K)}+\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{K})\|_{0,S(K)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(K)}.

We now analyze the term
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}h^{-1}_{F}\|[\pi\boldsymbol{v}]\|^{2}_{0,F}
. The definition of the auxiliary set
S(F)
depends on the face type: for interior faces
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{i}\ni F=K\cap K^{\prime}
between adjacent elements
K
and
K^{\prime}
, we set
S(F)=S(K)\cup S(K^{\prime})
, while for boundary faces
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
, we define
S(F)=S(K)
. The mean value of
\boldsymbol{v}
over
S(F)
, denoted by
\boldsymbol{c}_{F}
, is given by

\boldsymbol{c}_{F}=\frac{1}{|S(F)|}\int_{S(F)}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

This definition immediately yields the zero-mean property:

\int_{S(F)}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0.

For any
F\in\mathcal{F}^{i}_{h}
, discrete trace inequality
[
14
, Lemma 1.46]
, (
55
) and the Poincaré inequality
[
26
, Lemma C.3]
yield

\displaystyle h^{-1}_{F}\|[\pi\boldsymbol{v}]\|^{2}_{0,F}=h^{-1}_{F}\|[\pi\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F}]\|^{2}_{0,F}=h^{-1}_{F}\|[\pi(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})]\|^{2}_{0,F}

(59)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{-2}\left(\|\pi(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})\|^{2}_{0,K}+\|\pi(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})\|^{2}_{0,K^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(h^{-2}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F}\|_{0,S(K)}^{2}+\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})\|_{0,S(K)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+C\left(h^{-2}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F}\|_{0,S(K^{\prime})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})\|_{0,S(K^{\prime})}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(h^{-2}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F}\|_{0,S(F)}^{2}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(F)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(F)}^{2}.

For any
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\partial}_{h}
, , the following estimate holds:

|c_{F}|\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(F)}.

This result follows immediately from the vanishing trace condition
\boldsymbol{v}|_{F}=\boldsymbol{0}
and the Poincaré inequality
[
26
, Theorem A.36 & Remark A.37]
. Furthermore, by combining this estimate with techniques similar to those used in establishing inequality (
59
), we can derive the following bound

\displaystyle h^{-1}_{F}\|[\pi\boldsymbol{v}]\|^{2}_{0,F}\leq C\left(h^{-1}_{F}\|\pi(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F})\|^{2}_{0,F}+|\boldsymbol{c}_{F}|^{2}\right)\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,S(F)}^{2}.

(60)

Gathering the estimates from (
58
), (
59
) and (
60
) and summing over all elements
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and all facets
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we establish the stability inequality in (
45
).
