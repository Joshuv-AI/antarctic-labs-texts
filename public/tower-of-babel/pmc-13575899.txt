Reconsidering First-Line Therapy for Clarithromycin-Resistant Helicobacter pylori in Korea: The Role of Bismuth-Augmented Triple Therapy
Authors: Jae Kak Park, Cheal Wung Huh
Published: 2026

Helicobacter pylori has a seroprevalence of approximately 51% in the Republic of Korea.1 As a Group 1 carcinogen, it is causally linked to chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, and gastric adenocarcinoma.2 In Korea, where gastric cancer remains among the most frequent malignancies,3 H. pylori eradication is a cornerstone of prevention. However, rising clarithromycin resistance has driven nationwide eradication rates of standard triple therapy down to approximately 70%.4 Dual-priming oligonucleotide polymerase chain reaction (DPO-PCR) targeting 23S rRNA point mutations has therefore been increasingly adopted to enable tailored therapy, and the 2020 Korean guidelines recommend either proton pump inhibitor (PPI)–amoxicillin–metronidazole (PAM) or PPI–bismuth–metronidazole–tetracycline (PBMT) for clarithromycin-resistant strains.5 Neither option is ideal: PAM suffers from suboptimal efficacy because of frequent co-resistance to metronidazole (approximately 29.5%),6 while PBMT carries greater gastrointestinal toxicity and consumes tetracycline—an agent many clinicians prefer to preserve as a salvage option given its persistently low resistance rate in Korea.

In this issue of Gut and Liver, Koh et al.7 report a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial evaluating whether simply adding bismuth to the 14-day PAM regimen (PAM-B; PAM plus tripotassium dicitratobismuthate) can overcome the limitations of both guideline-endorsed options in DPO-PCR–confirmed clarithromycin-resistant infection. The authors should be commended for designing and executing a coordinated multicenter trial in this challenging clinical setting, where high-quality randomized evidence has been notably scarce. A total of 198 patients across seven southeastern Korean tertiary centers were randomized 1:1:1 to PAM, PAM-B, or PBMT for 14 days. In the intention-to-treat analysis, PAM-B achieved an eradication rate of 84.7%, significantly higher than PAM (68.2%) and numerically comparable to PBMT (81.8%), with the per-protocol contrast even more pronounced (96.5% vs 75.4% vs 94.6%). Notably, nausea and vomiting were markedly less frequent with PAM-B than with PBMT (6.8% vs 25.0%). However, several caveats temper the strength of these findings: recruitment was limited to southeastern Korea; the comparison between PAM-B and PBMT was not pre-specified as a formal non-inferiority analysis, and the apparent equivalence should therefore be interpreted as suggestive rather than confirmatory; unmeasured co-resistance to metronidazole may have contributed to residual failures across all three arms;6 the absence of blinding and trial registration may have biased reporting of subjective outcomes and limits methodological transparency.

Against this backdrop, by simply adding bismuth to a 14-day PAM regimen, the authors restored eradication efficacy to levels exceeding the accepted 80% intention-to-treat and 90% per-protocol thresholds, while avoiding tetracycline altogether and reducing nausea and vomiting. These findings are broadly consistent with the Maastricht VI/Florence and 2024 American College of Gastroenterology guidelines, which favor bismuth-containing regimens in regions of high clarithromycin resistance,8,9 and with a recent Korean nationwide registry showing that bismuth quadruple regimens remain effective despite a metronidazole resistance rate of 29.5%.6 PAM-B is therefore a credible first-line option in patients identified by DPO-PCR as harboring clarithromycin-resistant infection, although its broader applicability will depend on the continued availability and uptake of molecular resistance testing in routine practice—a prerequisite that is not yet uniformly met across Korean institutions. Looking ahead, large-scale, nationwide, prospectively registered trials will be required to define the optimal place of PAM-B in tailored eradication algorithms, particularly in head-to-head comparisons against PBMT and against potassium-competitive acid blocker–based regimens, which have shown enhanced efficacy in clarithromycin-resistant infection in recent meta-analyses.10
