Characterization of groupoid categories in terms of its category of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -sets
J. Miguel Calderón, Alberto G. Raggi-Cárdenas, Itzel Rosas, Ramón H. Ruiz-Medina

Abstract
A 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -set is a functor from the category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} to the category of finite sets and functions. The category of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -sets, 𝒞 − set \mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}} , is defined as the category whose objects are 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -sets, and whose morphisms are natural transformations between them. In this document we provide some concise characterizations of groupoids in terms of their category of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -sets. Keywords: Groupoid, 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -set, indecomposable object, Burnside ring. MSC 2020: 18A25, 18B40, 19A22.



1
Introduction

Group actions are a fundamental tool in understanding algebraic structures, with applications in symmetry, combinatorics, and representation theory. Classical results –such as Burnside’s lemma and the study of the Burnside ring
[
tD69
]
– illustrate how actions encode essential information about finite groups. This framework also appears in diverse areas, including geometric invariants and permutation representations
[
DF04
]
. However, as mathematical structures increase in complexity, so does the need for generalizations that preserve their underlying categorical nature.

Category theory provides a natural setting for such generalizations. By formalizing structure-preserving transformations, categories extend the concept of groups: morphisms replace group elements, and composition generalizes multiplication
[
ML78
]
. This shift in perspective has proven useful in various areas, including algebraic topology, homological algebra, and even theoretical computer science
[
Sim11
]
. In particular, the transition from groups to categories invites a parallel extension of actions: just as groups act on sets, categories can “act” via functors to sets, providing a broader framework for analyzing structure.

In this work, we explore this idea by generalizing group actions to category actions, building on the approach introduced by P. Webb
[
Web23
]
in the context of biset functors. We focus instead on the internal properties of category actions themselves. In this setting, we consider
\mathcal{C}
-sets –functors from a category
\mathcal{C}
to
\operatorname*{set}
– as a natural extension of the notion of
G
-sets. These functors can be thought of as category actions. As shown in
[
Ros23
]
, this framework enables richer descriptions of certain categorical structures –particularly groupoids– through the behavior of their associated
\mathcal{C}
-sets. Here, we provide a characterization of groupoids in terms of their categories of
\mathcal{C}
-sets, bridging combinatorial and categorical viewpoints.

The main objective of this article is to prove that, if
\mathcal{C}
is a category of finite type –that is, the number of indecomposable
\mathcal{C}
-sets, up to isomorphism, is finite– and, in addition,
\mathcal{C}
is finite and connected, then the following theorem holds.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite, connected category. Then the following statements are equivalent:

i)

\mathcal{C}
is semisimple.

ii)

\mathcal{C}
is of finite type.

iii)

\mathcal{C}
is a groupoid.

iv)

For all
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\operatorname*{set}
, all object
x\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and all element
a\in\Omega(x)
, we have:

\mathcal{C}^{\Omega}\cdot a=\mathcal{C}_{w}^{\Omega}\cdot a.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout this work, we consider finite categories –those whose classes of objects and morphisms are finite sets.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. A
\mathcal{C}
-set
is a covariant functor from the category
\mathcal{C}
to the category
\operatorname*{set}
of finite sets and functions.

For notational convenience, rather than writing
\Omega(\alpha)(u)
to denote the image of an element
u\in\Omega(x)
under the induced map, we abbreviate it as
\alpha u
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\operatorname*{set}
is
finite
if, for all
x\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, the set
\Omega(x)
is finite. The
\mathcal{C}
-sets form a category
\mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}}
, whose objects are finite
\mathcal{C}
-sets and whose morphisms are natural transformations between them.

Note that when
\mathcal{C}
is the delooping of a group
G
(that is, a category with a single object whose morphisms are the elements of the group), the category of
\mathcal{C}
-sets is isomorphic to the category of
G
-sets.

Example 3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the following quiver:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}=}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

A

-set

\Omega=(X,Y,f)
where
X=\Omega(x)
,
Y=\Omega(y)
and
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is such that
f=\Omega(\alpha)
.

Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}
-set. We say that
\Omega_{1}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-subset
of
\Omega

\Omega_{1}
is a subfunctor of
\Omega

Proposition 4

(
[

]

Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}
-set.
\Omega_{1}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-subset of
\Omega
if and only if

\Omega_{1}(x)\subseteq\Omega(x)

for every object
x

\mathcal{C}
and, for every
\alpha\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(x,y\right)

\Omega(\alpha)\left(\Omega_{1}(x)\right)\subseteq\Omega_{1}(y).

In some texts, this proposition is taken as the definition of a
\mathcal{C}
-subset, as in the case in Definition 2.2 of
[

]

Given two
\mathcal{C}
-sets
\Omega_{1}

\Omega_{1}\sqcup\Omega_{2}
to be the
\mathcal{C}
-set defined at each object

(\Omega_{1}\sqcup\Omega_{2})(x):=\Omega_{1}(x)\sqcup\Omega_{2}(x)

and on morphisms
\alpha:x\longrightarrow y

(\Omega_{1}\sqcup\Omega_{2})(\alpha):=\Omega_{1}(\alpha)\sqcup\Omega_{2}(\alpha)

sends each element of
\Omega_{i}(x)
to the corresponding one in
\Omega_{i}(y)
.

We say that a
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega
is
non-empty
if exists
c\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
such that
\Omega(c)\neq\emptyset
. We will denote the empty
\mathcal{C}
-set as
\varnothing
.

Definition 5
.

A non-empty
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega
is said to be
indecomposable
if it cannot be expressed properly as a disjoint union, that is, if
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
are
\mathcal{C}
-sets such that
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\sqcup\Omega_{2}
, then
\Omega_{1}=\varnothing
or
\Omega_{2}=\varnothing
.

As with
G
-sets, any
\mathcal{C}
-set can be decomposed as a disjoint union of indecomposable sets, as shown in the next result:

Theorem 6

(
[
Web23
]
, Proposition 2.3, (2))
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. Up to order, every non-empty finite
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega
has a unique decomposition

\Omega=\Omega_{1}\sqcup\Omega_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_{n}

where each
\Omega_{i}
is indecomposable.

Definition 9
.

Let
\Omega:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\operatorname*{set}
be a
\mathcal{C}
-set and let

from

to

is a sequence of morphisms
\alpha=\alpha_{n}\cdots\alpha_{1}
of the form
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y_{2}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y_{n}=y}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y_{i-1}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y_{i}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}

If
\alpha

Let

\left(\mathcal{C}_{w}^{\Omega}\cdot u\right)(y)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightsquigarrow y\\
\alpha\end{subarray}}\alpha u

.

We have a characterization of indecomposable
\mathcal{C}

Theorem 10

(
[
Ros23
]
, Teorema 1.28)
.

Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}
-set. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

\Omega
is an indecomposable
\mathcal{C}
-set;

2.

For every object
x
of
\mathcal{C}
and every element
u\in\Omega(x)
,
\mathcal{C}_{w}^{\Omega}\cdot u=\Omega
;

3.

There exists an object
x
of
\mathcal{C}
and an element
u\in\Omega(x)
such that
\mathcal{C}_{w}^{\Omega}\cdot u=\Omega
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
connected
if, for every
x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, there exists a walk between
x
and
y
. Equivalently, a category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be connected if it cannot be expressed as
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{1}\sqcup\mathcal{C}^{2}
, where
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
are non-empty full subcategories of

such that no morphism start in one and finish in the other one.

.

Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}

x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{0}

\mathcal{C}

Remark 12
.

Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}
-set, and let
x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
and

. We have that
\mathcal{C}^{\Omega}\cdot u
is a
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}_{w}^{\Omega}\cdot u

Note that the equality does not always hold. We now present an example where it fails:

Example 13
.

Consider the category
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}=}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}

Definition 14
.

A non-empty
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega
is said to be
simple
if it has no subfunctors apart from the whole functor and the empty functor.

Example 15
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the category defined in Example

\Omega_{n}
be the indecomposable
\mathcal{D}
-sets as defined therein. Note that

A category
\mathcal{C}
is

if every
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

Note that every simple
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

As in the case of indecomposable sets, we have a characterization of simple
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}
-sets:

Theorem 16
.

Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}
-set. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

\Omega
is a simple
\mathcal{C}
-set;

2.

For every object
x
of
\mathcal{C}
and every element
u\in\Omega(x)

\mathcal{C}^{\Omega}\cdot u=\Omega
.

Definition 17
.

We define the
Burnside ring
of the category
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
B(\mathcal{C})
, as the Grothendieck group of the category
\mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}}
with respect to the disjoint union of
\mathcal{C}
-sets.

We have a product operation in
\mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}}
: given two
\mathcal{C}
-sets
\Omega,\Psi
, we define
(\Omega\times\Psi)(x)=\Omega(x)\times\Psi(x)
for every object
x
of
\mathcal{C}
, and
(\Omega\times\Psi)(\alpha)=(\Omega(\alpha),\Psi(\alpha))
for every morphism
\alpha
of
\mathcal{C}
. With this definition, the multiplication in
B(C)
is given by
\times
on the generators.

As in the case of finite groups, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 18

(
[
Web23
]
, Proposition 2.9)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. Its Burnside ring
B(\mathcal{C})
is a commutative ring with identity, and with
\mathbb{Z}
-basis the set of isomorphism classes of the indecomposable
\mathcal{C}
-sets.

While bisets can themselves be seen as a generalization of
G

[

[

Definition 19
.

Given categories
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, we define a
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
-biset
to be a functor
\Omega:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}^{op}\longrightarrow\operatorname*{set}
, that is, a
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}^{op}
-set. We will denote it by
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\Omega}_{\mathcal{D}}
.

Remark 20
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-set
\Omega
can be regarded as a
(\mathcal{C},\mathbf{1})
-biset, where
\mathbf{1}
is the category with a single object and a single morphism. We denote such a
\mathcal{C}
-set as
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\Omega}
and, when the category is clear from context, simply by
\Omega
.

Given a
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
-biset
\Omega
, morphisms
\alpha:x\longrightarrow x_{1}
in
\mathcal{C}
and
\beta:y_{1}\longrightarrow y
in
\mathcal{D}
, and an element
u\in\Omega(x,y)
, we get elements

\displaystyle\alpha u:=\Omega(\alpha,1_{y})(u)\in\Omega(x_{1},y),

\displaystyle u\beta:=\Omega(1_{x},\beta)(u)\in\Omega(x,y_{1}).

If
\alpha
and
\beta
are walks, we denote the image of
u
under the corresponding walk using the same notation as for morphisms.

Example 21
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is the delooping of a finite group
G
and
\mathcal{D}
is the delooping of a finite group
H
, a
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
-biset is a
(G,H)
-biset.

We can form disjoint unions of bisets, but there is also a notion of
product
or
composition
, which is described below.

Definition 22
.

Given a
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
-biset
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\Omega}_{\mathcal{D}}
and a
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E})
-biset
{}_{\mathcal{D}}{\Psi}_{\mathcal{E}}
we build a
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E})
-biset
\Omega\times_{\mathcal{D}}\Psi
by the formula, for
x\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
and
z\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
,

(\Omega\times_{\mathcal{D}}\Psi)(x,z)=\left(\bigsqcup_{y\in\mathcal{D}_{0}}\Omega(x,y)\times\Psi(y,z)\right)/\sim,

where
\sim
is defined as follows.
Let
(u,b)\in\Omega(x,y)\times\Psi(y,z)
and
(a,v)\in\Omega(x,y^{\prime})\times\Psi(y^{\prime},z)
, we say that
(u,b)\sim(a,v)
if and only if there exists a walk
\beta:y\rightsquigarrow y^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{D}
such that
u\in a\beta
and
v\in\beta b
.

Given categories
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{E}
, and functors
F:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}
and
G:\mathcal{D}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}
we obtain a
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
-biset that we denote
{}_{\mathcal{C}^{F}}{\mathcal{E}}_{{}^{G}\mathcal{D}}
. In objects
x
of
\mathcal{C}
and
y
of
\mathcal{D}
this biset is defined by

{}_{\mathcal{C}^{F}}{\mathcal{E}}_{{}^{G}\mathcal{D}}(x,y)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(G(y),F(x)\right).

For example, if we take
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{E}
and
F=G=1_{\mathcal{C}}
we get the biset
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{C}}
defined in objects as
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(y,x\right)
.

As in biset theory for finite groups, if
\mathcal{D}
is a subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
and we consider the inclusion functor
\iota:\mathcal{D}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
, then the biset
{}_{\mathcal{D}^{\iota}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{C}}={}_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{C}}
encodes the restriction operation, and the biset
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{{}^{\iota}\mathcal{D}}={}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{D}}
encodes the induction operation.

3
Proof of the characterization theorem

This section presents the main result of this work: a characterization of groupoids in terms of their simple and indecomposable
\mathcal{C}
-sets. The proof relies on a sequence of technical propositions that will be used to establish the main theorem. In this section, we will work only with connected categories, since the Burnside ring of a disconnected category can be seen as the product of the Burnside rings of its connected components.

Theorem 23
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a subcategory of a category
\mathcal{C}
, and let
{}_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
{}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{D}}
be the restriction and induction operations defined previously. Consider the functors

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}-{\operatorname*{set}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r}

given as follows:

•

Let
\Psi\in(\mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}})_{0}
, then
r(\Psi)={}_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{\mathcal{C}}{\Psi}
,

•

Let
\Omega\in(\mathcal{D}-{\operatorname*{set}})_{0}
, then
i(\Omega)={}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{D}}\times_{\mathcal{D}}{\Omega}
.

Then
r
and
i
are adjoint functors.

Proof.

We give the explicit isomorphisms:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}-{\operatorname*{set}}}\left(i(\Omega),\Psi\right)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}-{\operatorname*{set}}}\left(\Omega,r(\Psi)\right)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

Let
x\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
y\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
be objects, and let
\zeta:i(\Omega)\longrightarrow\Psi
and
\theta:\Omega\longrightarrow r(\Psi)
be morphisms in the corresponding categories.

For
\alpha
: note that we can see
r(\Psi)
as
\Psi
, but restricted to the objects in
\mathcal{D}
only. Then
\alpha(\zeta)_{x}:\Omega(x)\longrightarrow r(\Psi)(x)=\Psi(x)
, so if
a\in\Omega(x)
,

\alpha(\zeta)_{x}(a)=\zeta_{x}([1_{x},a]).

For
\beta
: recall that
i(\Omega)={}_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{D}}\times_{\mathcal{D}}{\Omega}
. Then
\beta(\theta)_{y}:i(\Omega)(y)\longrightarrow\Psi(y)
, so if
[f,b]\in i(\Omega)(y)
,

\beta(\theta)_{y}([f,b])=\Psi(f)(\theta_{z}(b)),

with
z\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
such that
b\in\Omega(z)
.

Note that both
\alpha
and
\beta
are well-defined and are inverses of each other.
∎

Corollary 24
.

There exists, for every
\mathcal{D}
-set
\Omega
, with
\mathcal{D}
a subcategory of a category
\mathcal{C}
, a natural transformation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta:\Omega}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ri(\Omega)}

such that for every
y\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
\eta_{y}(a)=[1_{y},a]
.

Proof.

This natural transformation is the unit of the adjunction given in Theorem
23
.
∎

Lemma 25
.

Let
x
and
y
be objects of a category
\mathcal{C}
, and let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}=x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

be a subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\alpha
is not an isomorphism in
\mathcal{C}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\alpha
is not a retraction in
\mathcal{C}
. Define the walk
\beta:=(\alpha^{op}\alpha)^{t}
, for some
t\geq 1
. Then
1_{x}\cdot\beta=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose that
1_{x}\cdot\beta\neq\emptyset
. Without loss of generality, this implies that
1_{x}\cdot(\alpha^{op}\alpha)\neq\emptyset
, so we take an element
l\in 1_{x}\cdot(\alpha^{op}\alpha)=(\alpha^{op}\alpha)^{*}(1_{x})=\alpha^{*}((\alpha^{op})^{*}(1_{x}))
. Since
(\alpha^{op})^{*}(1_{x})
is a nonempty set, there exists
\lambda\in(\alpha^{op})^{*}(1_{x})
, so that
\alpha^{*}(\lambda)=\lambda\alpha=1_{x}
, which contradicts the fact that
\alpha
is not a retraction.
∎

Lemma 26
.

Let
x
and
y
be objects of a category
\mathcal{C}
, and let
\alpha:x\longrightarrow y
be a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
such that it has no left inverse. Consider the subcategory
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}=x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
of
\mathcal{C}
, and the
\mathcal{D}
-sets
\Omega_{n}
defined as in Example
7
or Example
8
. Then there exists a natural transformation from
\Omega_{n}
to
ri(\Omega_{n})
, which is injective on each component.

Proof.

Corollary
24
gives us the existence of this natural transformation which, indeed, is the unit of the adjunction given in Theorem
23
. We call it
\eta
. It is enough to prove that
\eta_{x}
is injective.

Assume that
\eta_{x}(a)=\eta_{x}(b)
, which means that
[1_{x},a]=[1_{x},b]
. By the definition of the equivalence relation in the biset composition, there exists a walk
\beta
in
\mathcal{D}
from
x
to
x
such that
1_{x}\in 1_{x}\cdot\beta
and
b\in\beta\cdot a
.

Given the structure of the category
\mathcal{D}
, there are only two possibilities for the walk
\beta
: either
\beta
is the identity morphism or it is of the form
(\alpha^{op}\alpha)^{t}
for some
t\geq 1
. Suppose
\beta
is not the identity; then, by Lemma
25
,
\beta
must be one of the walks described therein, and in particular,
1_{x}\cdot\beta=\emptyset
, which is a contradiction.

Therefore,
\beta
must be the identity morphism. In this case,
b\in\beta\cdot a
implies
b=\beta\cdot a=\mathrm{id}\cdot a=a
. Hence,
\eta_{x}
is injective.
∎

We now proceed with the proof of the Theorem
1
.

Proof.

i)\implies ii)
As
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}
-sets, up to isomorphism. As in a semisimple category any
\mathcal{C}

ii)\implies iii)

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is of finite type. Consider two objects

and a morphism

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}=x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
and let
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},...,\Psi_{k}
be all the indecomposable
\mathcal{C}
-sets, and consider
\Omega_{n}
the
\mathcal{D}
-sets defined for the quiver in the statements above (Example
7
or Example

\Omega_{n}

It also satisfies that

\Omega_{n}\cong ri(\Omega_{n})|_{\mathcal{D}}\cong\left(\Psi_{1}|_{\mathcal{D}}\right)^{a_{1}}\sqcup\left(\Psi_{2}|_{\mathcal{D}}\right)^{a_{2}}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\left(\Psi_{k}|_{\mathcal{D}}\right)^{a_{k}}.

As each

To verify this point, it is enough to show that for every

Corollary 27
.

Given a category
\mathcal{C}
, its Burnside ring has finite rank if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is a groupoid.

This follows directly from the definition of the Burnside ring and the fact that the set of the isomorphism classes of the indecomposable objects forms a basis for the ring.

This work indicates that the Burnside ring of a finite category with infinite rank remains largely unexplored, with several fundamental questions still open. Among these are: What is the structure of its spectrum? What are the idempotent elements of the Burnside ring? How can simple
\mathcal{C}
-sets be characterized? It is also natural to ask whether the Burnside ring can characterize the category
\mathcal{C}
in the general (non-groupoid) case. These and other questions point to a promising direction for further research.
