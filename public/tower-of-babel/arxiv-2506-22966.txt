Detection of coordinated fleet vehicles in route choice urban games. Part I. Inverse fleet assignment theory.
Grzegorz Jamróz ∗, Rafał Kucharski

Abstract




Abstract:
Detection of collectively routing fleets of vehicles in future urban systems may become important for the management of traffic, as such routing may destabilize urban networks leading to deterioration of driving conditions. Accordingly, in this paper we discuss the question whether it is possible to determine the flow of fleet vehicles on all routes given the fleet size and behaviour as well as the combined total flow of fleet and non-fleet vehicles on every route. We prove that the answer to this Inverse Fleet Assignment Problem is ’yes’ for myopic fleet strategies which are more ’selfish’ than ’altruistic’, and ’no’ otherwise, under mild assumptions on route/link performance functions. To reach these conclusions we introduce the forward fleet assignment operator and study its properties, proving that it is invertible for ’bad’ objectives of fleet controllers. We also discuss the challenges of implementing myopic fleet routing in the real world and compare it to Stackelberg and Nash routing. Finally, we show that optimal Stackelberg fleet routing could involve highly variable mixed strategies in some scenarios, which would likely cause chaos in the traffic network.

Keywords:
coordinated fleets of vehicles, collective routing, inverse problems, detection, route assignment, autonomous vehicles, Stackelberg and Nash equilibrium

Year 2025: Technological advances of recent years have enabled the introduction of self-driving cars

Year 2045: As the time has been passing by, some cities have started discovering that even though the technology has enabled autonomous driving indistinguishable from human driving and the road infrastructure and number of vehicles is unchanged, the driving conditions have deteriorated. What has gone wrong? Is this a possible future scenario? Can it be prevented?

In
[
25
]
it was shown that coordinated routing strategies applied by a fleet of CAVs, which otherwise drive exactly as human drivers, may, for certain plausible myopic fleet objectives, result in deterioration of driving conditions across the system or for the human drivers (HDVs), while the travel times of CAVs are improved. Looking for ways to address this undesirable outcome, in this paper we set out to assess the capabilities a city may require to detect fleets of collectively routing vehicles (CRVs), which may be formed of coordinated fleets of CAVs or subscribers of route guidance systems, including systems such as Google Maps, whose inner workings are undisclosed, however many drivers unconditionally follow their instructions.
To this end, we introduce and solve the inverse fleet assignment problem, which consists in identifying the flows of fleet vehicles on routes/links based on measurement of total flows. We defer the discussion of detection of individual drivers to
[
26
]
. To fix the ideas, as mentioned above, in the mixed HDV-CRV traffic system we can interpret the CRVs as:

•

Commercial guidance system
, where a given fraction of drivers is subscribed to a routing system and follows its guidance exactly. The guidance is based on a predefined strategy/behaviour.

•

Fleet of CAVs
, consisting of a given share of CAVs which select routes according to a pattern dictated by a centralized commercial fleet controller, which applies a given collective strategy, termed fleet behaviour.

In both cases, the identities of drivers of CRVs are unknown to the city. The city is assumed, however, to know the number of participants and the collective strategy adopted by CRVs.

Note that in contrast to scenarios considered in the literature previously

not

\displaystyle T^{HDV}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}q^{HDV}_{r}t_{r}\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\right),

\displaystyle T^{CRV}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}q^{CRV}_{r}t_{r}\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\right).

•

i)

The inverse fleet assignment problem

Here we state the inverse fleet assignment problem in the continuous setting, with flows allowed to be real-valued, which is a reasonable approximation in large systems. We discuss the discrete version in Section
3.3
.

Problem 1

(Inverse fleet assignment problem, continuous)
.

Suppose the total flow in the system on a given day is given by
\mathbb{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{R})
and the coefficients
\lambda^{HDV},\lambda^{CRV}
of the fleet strategy in (
1
) as well as fleet size
q^{CRV}
are known. For which
\lambda^{CRV},\lambda^{HDV},t_{r}
is it possible to determine the flow
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
? Is the mapping
\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
(Lipschitz) continuous? Equivalently, is the inverse mapping
\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
(Lipschitz) continuous?

Before embarking on the solution of Problem
1
we provide some examples which show that for certain fleet behaviours the inverse may not be
unique.

Example 2

(Social fleet)
.

Let
\mathbb{q^{SO}}
be the system optimum for a given total number of vehicles. Suppose that
0<q^{CRV}<|\mathbb{q^{SO}}|
. Then for any
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
such that
\mathbb{0}\leq\mathbb{q^{HDV}}\leq\mathbb{q^{SO}}
a social fleet assigns vehicles so as to achieve system optimum, i.e. by
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}:=\mathbb{q^{SO}}-\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
. Therefore, the mapping
\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
is not single-valued at
\mathbb{q}=\mathbb{q^{SO}}
, as viable distinct
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
can arise from multiple
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
.

Example 3

(Altruistic fleet)
.

Suppose the fleet’s objective is to minimize human drivers’ travel time. Let there be three independent routes and
\mathbb{q}=(30,30,40)
. If
q^{CRV}=30
then for
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}=(30,0,40)
necessarily
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}=(0,30,0)
as the fleet aims not to increase human drivers’ travel time. Similarly, for
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}=(0,30,40)
we obtain
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}=(30,0,0)
. Consequently, the mapping
\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
may be multi-valued for altruistic fleets.

Examples
2
and
3
demonstrate that determining the assignment of fleet vehicles based on fleet size and objective fails for social and altruistic fleets. Theorem
18
shows that for rational objectives given by (
1
) these are essentially the only cases when the mapping
\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
may not be single-valued, compare Fig.
1
.

Background

There is a vast literature on autonomous driving. Nevertheless, the coexistence of (fleets of) autonomous vehicles and human drivers has been studied typically from the point of view of microscopic driving, see e.g.
[
19
,
16
]
or evolutionary game theory
[
5
]
. The game-theoretical route choice behaviour in mixed systems involving various players has been addressed e.g. in
[
54
,
22
]
. The detection of cooperating fleets of vehicles has been studied for car following and platooning in
[
13
]
. However, it seems that the identification of group players has not been addressed in the route choice context.

Inverse problems, see e.g.
[
21
]
, have been known in science for a long time, and first rigorous solutions date back to the early 20th century when Hermann Weyl
[
50
]
deduced the asymptotic growth of eigenvalues of the Laplace operator from the area of the domain, which later gave rise to the celebrated ’Can one hear the shape of a drum?’ problem
[
28
]
.

Solving inverse problems allows one to deduce the cause (e.g. initial condition, parameters) given the observation. For instance, observation of the gravitational or electric field may allow one to deduce the distribution of mass or electric charge and ’inverting’ the diffraction image or signals emitted by protons are the basis of spectroscopic methods in physics and chemistry, e.g.
[
52
]
or tomography methods in medicine, e.g.
[
35
]
. Many inverse problems, however, are ill-posed, e.g. the unstable inverse heat equation, in which the initial temperature distribution is to be deduced from observation of this temperature at a later point in time, or indeed the shape of a drum which is in general not uniquely determined by the set of the eigenvalues of the Laplace operator
[
20
]
.

The most important inverse problem in transportation engineering is the OD demand matrix estimation from observed link flows, see e.g.
[
4
,
8
,
31
,
45
]
. In this approach the observer is provided with link counts (congestion levels) from detectors and tries to estimate the OD demand matrix, typically under the assumption of user equilibrium. Our setting bears some resemblance to it in the way that we begin with link flows. However, we do not estimate the OD demand; in fact the crux of the argument is presented on a single Origin-Destination pair. What we instead strive to estimate are the flows of fleet vehicles under known total flows on routes/links, and
known
OD demand of CRVs and HDVs. Another stream of research is related to inferring the network state from transportation data
[
53
]
, such as the shortest path from some observations
[
7
]
.
We are not aware of any literature on inverse problems of fleet assignment in a setting similar to ours, see e.g.
[
56
]
for a different context of drayage, since, the problem being futuristic enough, it has not been considered in classical transportation settings. Therefore, the results presented in this paper are, to the authors knowledge, the first of a kind and open a new area of research.

Convex optimization methods in transportation are studied in detail e.g. in
[
6
]
where also standard algorithms, such as Frank-Wolfe constrained optimization
[
17
]
for determining user equilibrium or system optimum, which result in convex functionals, are discussed. However, for more elaborate assignment objectives, efficient algorithms seem to be unexplored. It is the more so for the inverse problems, see however
[
12
]
for a recent survey of inverse optimization methods and
[
23
]
for the inverse combinatorial problems such as the inverse shortest path problem, where the goal is to find costs of arcs, closest possible to the a priori estimates, such that the observed paths selected by travelers are the shortest.

Finally, let us mention the more general Inverse Optimal Control
[
30
]
and Inverse Reinforcement Learning,
[
3
,
15
,
36
]
, which typically focus on recovering the reward function, rather than initial state/parameters, from observed sequential/continuous behaviour and are more suited to problems with abundant data, e.g.
[
11
]
, in contrast to the static limited-data one-off inverse problem considered in this paper.

Fleet assignment theory

In this section we discuss the basic ideas related to myopic fleet assignment and introduce the fleet assignment operators. We study their properties, providing multiple examples to facilitate understanding. We remark that the notions related to networks, which we utilize, such as route flow, link flow, link-route incidence matrix, route-flow to link-flow conversion as well as system-optimal (yet not with general objectives) optimization in both route-flow and link-flow formulation are standard in transportation theory, see e.g.
[
9
,
6
]
.
We also note that here we use the term ’operator’ to represent a mapping between two spaces, not to be confused with the fleet operator/controller who owns/controls the fleet and prescribes its behaviour. In the end, however, the fleet operator/controller may indeed use the image of the fleet assignment operator to assign the flows to routes. For clarity, in the following we avoid using the term ’fleet operator’ in favour of ’fleet controller’ and ’(fleet) assignment operator’ is understood as a mapping between vector spaces.

Fleet objective function

Reformulating (
1
) we define the fleet objective function as

F(\mathbb{h},\mathbb{f})=(\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{h}+\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{f})\cdot\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{h}+\mathbb{f})

(2)

which, for a given HDV flow
\mathbb{h}\in[0,\infty)^{R}
, assigns to every fleet assignment
\mathbb{f}\in[0,\infty)^{R}
a generalized cost
F(\mathbb{h},\mathbb{f})
. In (
2
)

\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}):=(t_{1}(\mathbb{q}),\dots,t_{R}(\mathbb{q}))^{T}

is the R-vector of travel times
and
\mathbb{f}\cdot\mathbb{t}
and
\mathbb{h}\cdot\mathbb{t}
are dot products.

Remark 4
.

Continuity properties of
F
are the same as continuity properties of
\mathbb{t}
. For instance, if
\mathbb{t}
is Lipschitz continuous then so is
F(h,\cdot)
.

Fleet Assignment Operators

Definition 5

(Route Fleet Assignment Operator)
.

For given
\lambda^{HDV},\lambda^{CRV}
and fleet size
q^{CRV}
we define the
Route Fleet Assignment Operator

G:[0,\infty)^{R}\to 2^{[0,\infty)^{R}}
by

G\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}\right)=\arg\min_{\mathbb{f}}\left\{F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f}):|\mathbb{f}|=q^{CRV}\right\}.

The Route Fleet Assignment Operator
G
assigns to every
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
the set of minimizers of the fleet objective function. Using
G
, we define the operator
H
, which maps the HDV flow to the total flow by

H:=Id+G,

i.e.

H\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}\right)=\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+G\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}\right).

Definition 6
.

Let
I\in\{0,1\}^{R\times A}
be the
link-route incidence matrix
defined by
I^{r\alpha}=1
if link
\alpha
belongs to route
r
and
I^{r\alpha}=0
otherwise.

Definition 7
.

Let
\Lambda:[0,\infty)^{R}\to[0,\infty)^{A}
be the route-flow to link-flow conversion operator defined by

\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}I^{r\alpha}q_{r}.

Definition 8

(Link Fleet Assignment Operator)
.

Suppose the delay functions
t_{r}
on every available route
r
are given as sums of costs on links, i.e.
t_{r}(\mathbb{q})=\sum_{\alpha}I^{r\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}(\Lambda(\mathbb{q}))
where
\tau_{\alpha}
are differentiable link-delay functions (e.g. BPR functions). Consider the link objective function

\Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=(\lambda^{HDV}\boldsymbol{\eta}+\lambda^{CRV}\boldsymbol{\phi})\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\phi}).

For given
\lambda^{HDV},\lambda^{CRV}
and fleet size
q^{CRV}
we define the
Link Fleet Assignment Operator

\Gamma:[0,\infty)^{A}\to 2^{[0,\infty)^{A}}
by

\Gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\eta^{HDV}}\right)=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left\{\Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta^{HDV}},\boldsymbol{\phi}):\exists\mathbb{f}:|\mathbb{f}|=q^{CRV}\mbox{ and }\boldsymbol{\phi}=\Lambda(\mathbb{f})\right\}.

The Link Fleet Assignment Operator identifies the set of optimal fleet link flows given the HDV link flow. This link flow may potentially originate from various fleet route flows.

Proposition 9

(Convexity of domain)
.

i)

The set
\{\mathbb{q}\in[0,\infty)^{R}:|\mathbb{q}|=q^{CRV}\}
is convex.

ii)

The set
\{\mathbb{a}\in[0,\infty)^{A}:\exists\mathbb{f}:|\mathbb{f}|=q^{CRV}\mbox{ and }\mathbb{a}=\Lambda(\mathbb{f})\}
is convex.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{a}=\Lambda(\mathbb{f})
,
\mathbb{\tilde{a}}=\Lambda(\tilde{\mathbb{f}})
and
|\mathbb{f}|=|\mathbb{\tilde{f}}|=q^{CRV}
then for every
c\in[0,1]
we have

c\mathbb{a}+(1-c)\mathbb{\tilde{a}}=c\Lambda(\mathbb{f})+(1-c)\Lambda(\mathbb{\tilde{f}})=\Lambda(c\mathbb{f}+(1-c)\mathbb{\tilde{f}}),

where we used the linearity of operator
\Lambda
. We conclude by noting that

|c\mathbb{f}+(1-c)\mathbb{\tilde{f}}|=c|\mathbb{f}|+(1-c)|\mathbb{\tilde{f}}|=q^{CRV},

which proves both i) and ii).
∎

Proposition 10

(Properties of Fleet Assignment Operators)
.

Assume that the route/link delay functions
t_{r},\tau_{\alpha}
are continuous, increasing and convex. Then

i)

G(\mathbb{q^{HDV}})
is non-empty for every
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
.

ii)

\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\eta^{HDV}})
is non-empty for every
\boldsymbol{\eta^{HDV}}
.

iii)

If

contains one element then the corresponding

is a one-element set.

Proof.

i) and ii) are an immediate consequence of compactness of the set

Proposition 12

If the routes are linearly dependent, i.e. the link-route incidence matrix is not of maximal rank, then the Route Fleet Assignment Operator

for every
l
. Suppose that
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\in G(\mathbb{q^{HDV}})
has positive entries. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}+\varepsilon\mathbb{a}
has positive entries and it yields the same link flow as
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
.
∎

Next we study the continuity of the forward fleet assignment operators while the continuity of the inverse is established in Theorem
22
. We restrict ourselves to the set
|\mathbb{q^{HDV}}|=const
. We begin by showing an example of lack of continuity resulting in ’switching’ the local minimum.

Example 13
.

Let there be two equivalent independent routes. For the malicious strategy and
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}=(25,25)
we have
G(\mathbb{q^{HDV}})=\{(50,0),(0,50)\}
, i.e. there are two minimizers. However, for
\mathbb{q^{HDV,\varepsilon}}=(25+\varepsilon,25-\varepsilon)
, where
\varepsilon>0
, the unique minimizer is
G(\mathbb{q^{HDV,\varepsilon}})=\{(50,0)\}
. On the contrary, for
\varepsilon<0
we have
G(\mathbb{q^{HDV,\varepsilon}})=\{(0,50)\}
. Consequently,
G
is discontinuous at
(25,25)
.

Definition 14
.

We say that
\mathbb{f}
is a strict local minimizer of
F
if

F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{\tilde{f}})>F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f})

for
\|\mathbb{\tilde{f}}-\mathbb{f}\|
small enough, where
\|\cdot\|
is any finite-dimentional norm (e.g. maximum).

Proposition 15

(Continuity of fleet assignment operator)
.

Let
\mathbb{f}
be a strict local minimizer of
F
and let
\mathbb{q^{HDV,n}}
be a sequence of HDV flows satisfying
|\mathbb{q^{HDV,n}}|=|\mathbb{q^{HDV}}|
and
\mathbb{q^{HDV,n}}\to\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
. Then

G^{loc}\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV,n}}\right)\to G^{loc}\left(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}\right)=\{\mathbb{f}\}

as
n\to\infty
, where
G^{loc}
is the restriction of operator
G
to a small neighbourhood of
\mathbb{f}
.

Proof.

Let
K\ni\mathbb{f}
be a small closed neighbourhood such that
F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{\tilde{f}})>F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f})
for
\mathbb{\tilde{f}}\in K.

Suppose
\mathbb{f_{n}}\in G^{loc,K}(\mathbb{q^{HDV,n}})
. As
K
is compact there exists a subsequence
\mathbb{f_{n_{k}}}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\mathbb{{f_{0}}}\in K
. Suppose
\mathbb{f_{0}}\neq\mathbb{f}
. Then,
\delta=F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f_{0}})-F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f})
is strictly positive by the fact that
\mathbb{f}
is a strict local minimizer and by continuity of
F
we have, for
k
large enough,

F(\mathbb{q^{HDV,n_{k}}},\mathbb{f_{n_{k}}})>F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f_{0}})-\frac{\delta}{2}=F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f})+\frac{\delta}{2}

on the one hand and

F(\mathbb{q^{HDV,n_{k}}},\mathbb{f})<F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f})+\frac{\delta}{2}

on the other. Hence

F(\mathbb{q^{HDV,n_{k}}},\mathbb{f})<F(\mathbb{q^{HDV,n_{k}}},\mathbb{f_{n_{k}}}),

which contradicts the fact that
\mathbb{f_{n_{k}}}
is a minimizer.
∎

Corollary 16
.

Strict local minimizers are stable. This fails to hold if we drop the assumption of strictness. Globally, if there is more than one strict minimizer for which the objective values coincide then ’switching’ of minimizer can occur, see Example
13
. Nevertheless, if there is only one strict local minimizer which is the case for strictly convex objectives, the fleet assignment operator
G
is globally continuous as the set
K
in the proof of Proposition
15
can be taken as the whole feasible set.

’Real-world’ assignment by fleet controller

Real-world assignment of fleet vehicles is a discrete rather than continuous optimization problem. Below, we discuss the relation of the two approaches. A
’continuous’ real-world fleet controller
would likely:

•

Assign the vehicles according to the unique minimizer in the case of
strictly convex
objective function (e.g. selfish, social), which can be identified by Frank-Wolfe or similar algorithms.

•

Assign the vehicles using one of the finite number of local minimizers in the case of
strictly concave
objective function (e.g. malicious). The minimizers can be found by inspecting the corners of the feasible set and can exhibit the same (by chance) or different objective values. In the latter case one may assume (although it is not necessary for further exposition) that the best local minimizer, i.e. the global minimizer is selected.

•

Assign the vehicles using: brute force (in the case of small systems), metaheuristics
[
18
]
or other algorithms in the case of
non-convex and non-concave objectives
. The identified assignment will typically be a local minimum (perhaps non-strict) of the objective function, which is what we assume in this paper.

A real-life
’discrete’ fleet controller
could:

•

Take (one of) the identified ’continuous’ local minimizers and round up the flows. This approach is reasonable when the objective function is Lipschitz continuous with a not-too-large Lipschitz constant. Then the assignment will result in near optimal objective (whatever the optimal discrete objective may be).

•

Find the actual discrete minimum over the discrete space. This approach results potentially in a better objective function value but is computationally very expensive and possibly prohibitive.

In this paper we assume that the fleet operator/controller uses the first approach as a computationally reasonable alternative, leaving the other option to further research. The first approach allows us to assume that the actual discrete flow used is close to a continuous minimizer, see Section
3.3
. On the other hand, for the second approach (fleet controller using global discrete minimizers) we note that

•

The discrete global minimizer(s) does not necessarily lie close to the global continuous minimizer even in the case of strictly convex objective function, unless the objective is uniformly convex.

•

The discrete global minimizers(s) does not have to lie close to any of the continuous local minimizers, even in the case of strictly concave objectives.

•

Even if the continuous minimizer is unique, the discrete minimizer does not have to be unique.

Finally, we note that even if the fleet controller/operator assigns vehicles using a local (and not global) minimizer, the inverse theory still can be applied, see Remark
21
.

Inverse fleet assignment theorem and related results

In this section we formulate and prove the main technical results of this paper, i.e. the theorem on inverse fleet assignment along with other auxiliary results.

Inverse fleet assignment theorem

Definition 17
.

A square real matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is
positive definite
if for every real vector
\mathbb{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\mathbb{v}\neq 0
we have
\mathbb{v}^{T}M\mathbb{v}>0
.

Note that in Definition
17
we do not necessarily assume that the matrix
M
is symmetric. As, however,
\mathbb{v}^{T}M\mathbb{v}=\mathbb{v}^{T}M^{T}\mathbb{v}
for any square matrix
M
, we find that the value of the quadratic form depends only on the symmetric part of
M
, i.e.
\mathbb{v}^{T}M\mathbb{v}=\mathbb{v}^{T}((M+M^{T})/2)\mathbb{v}
.

Theorem 18

(Inverse of fleet assignment)
.

Let
\mathbb{t}=(t_{1}(\mathbb{q}),t_{2}(\mathbb{q})),\dots,t_{R}(\mathbb{q}))^{T}
be continuously differentiable delay functions (travel times) of routes
1,\dots,R
, dependent on the flow of all vehicles
\mathbb{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{R})
and let
q^{CRV}
be the total flow of fleet vehicles (i.e. fleet size).
If
\lambda^{HDV}<\lambda^{CRV}
then for every
\mathbb{q}
such that the gradient matrix
\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q})
is positive definite there exists at most one
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
such that
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\in G(\mathbb{q-q^{CRV}})
. In other words, for
\lambda^{HDV}<\lambda^{CRV}
and positive definite
\mathbb{\nabla t(q)}
there exists at most one couple
(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{q^{CRV}})
such that
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}}=\mathbb{q}
and
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
optimizes the fleet objective, which is furthermore equivalent to
H^{-1}(\mathbb{q})
being an at most one-element set.

Proof.

The optimization by fleet can be expressed as

\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbb{f}}\left\{F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{f}):|\mathbb{f}|=q^{CRV}\right\}.

(3)

Now, given a total vehicle assignment
\mathbb{q}
, fleet size
q^{CRV}
and fleet strategy we will show that inclusion

\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbb{f}}\left\{F(\mathbb{q}-\mathbb{q^{CRV}},\mathbb{f}):|\mathbb{f}|=q^{CRV}\right\}

(4)

has at most one solution
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
, i.e.
\mathbb{q}
can arise from at most one couple
(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{q^{CRV}})
conditional on fleet strategy and size.

Suppose first that
\arg\min_{\mathbb{f}}\left\{F(\mathbb{q}-\mathbb{f^{*}},\mathbb{f}):|\mathbb{f}|=q^{CRV}\right\}=\mathbb{f^{*}}
. Then for every feasible direction
\mathbb{g}
, i.e.
\mathbb{g}
such that
\mathbb{f}^{*}+\delta\mathbb{g}
has non-negative entries for
\delta
small enough (viable flow) and
|\mathbb{g}|=0
(so that
|\mathbb{f}^{*}+\delta\mathbb{g}|=q^{CRV}
) we have

\frac{d^{+}}{d\delta}F(\mathbb{q}-\mathbb{f^{*}},\mathbb{f^{*}}+\delta\mathbb{g})|_{\delta=0}\geq 0.

Computing the derivative we obtain

\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g}\cdot\mathbb{t(q)}+\left(\lambda^{HDV}(\mathbb{\mathbb{q}-\mathbb{f^{*}}})+\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{f^{*}}\right)\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{g}})\geq 0,

where
\mathbb{\nabla t}
is the gradient matrix and
\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{g}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\partial_{r}\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}))g^{r}
is the derivative of
\mathbb{t}
in direction
\mathbb{g}
evaluated at
\mathbb{q}
. Rearranging we obtain

\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g}\cdot\mathbb{t(q)}+\left((\lambda^{CRV}-\lambda^{HDV})\mathbb{f^{*}}+\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q}\right)\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{g}})\geq 0.

(5)

Suppose now that
\mathbb{\tilde{f}}=\mathbb{f^{*}}+\mathbb{g}
is another solution of (
4
). Then the directional derivative of
F
at
\mathbb{\tilde{f}}
in a feasible direction
\mathbb{\tilde{g}}
is non-negative and hence

\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{\tilde{g}}\cdot\mathbb{t(q)}+\left((\lambda^{CRV}-\lambda^{HDV})\mathbb{\tilde{f}}+\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q}\right)\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{\tilde{g}}})\geq 0.

(6)

Setting
\mathbb{\tilde{g}}=-\mathbb{g}
and adding (
5
) and (
6
) we obtain

(\lambda^{CRV}-\lambda^{HDV})(\mathbb{f^{*}}-\mathbb{\tilde{f}})\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{g}})\geq 0.

Noting that
\mathbb{g}=\mathbb{\tilde{f}}-\mathbb{f^{*}}
is a feasible direction in (
5
) and
\mathbb{\tilde{g}}=-\mathbb{g}
is feasible in (
6
) we obtain

-(\lambda^{CRV}-\lambda^{HDV})\mathbb{g}\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{g}})\geq 0.

(7)

Consequently, if
\mathbb{\nabla t}(\mathbb{q})
is positive definite and
\lambda^{CRV}>\lambda^{HDV}
, the solution of (
4
), if it exists, is unique.

∎

Corollary 19
.

Theorem
18
implies that for every fleet strategy, which is more (own group) ’selfish’ than ’altruistic’ (i.e. e.g. for selfish, malicious, disruptive) the fleet assignment can be recovered from the assignment of all vehicles and the size of the fleet. On the other hand, e.g. for social (
\lambda^{HDV}=\lambda^{CRV}=1
) or altruistic (
\lambda^{HDV}=1
,
\lambda^{CRV}=0
) this is not possible.

Remark 20
.

In Theorem
18
it suffices to assume that
\mathbb{\nabla t(q)}
is positive definite only with respect to feasible directions, i.e.
\mathbb{g}\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}){\mathbb{g}})>0
for every
\mathbb{g}\neq\mathbb{0}
such that
|\mathbb{g}|=\mathbb{0}
.

Remark 21
.

Theorem
18
assumes that the fleet controller is able to assign the flows on routes such that the result minimizes the objective function, i.e. for given (expected by the fleet controller) HDV assignment
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
it can identify at least one
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
such that (
3
) is satisfied. The proof of the theorem, however, requires only that
\mathbb{f}^{*}
and
\mathbb{\tilde{f}}
be
local
minimizers. Consequently, the theorem still holds true for fleet controllers which use algorithms that generate assignments minimizing the objective locally. Such algorithms (e.g. gradient methods) are easily available. In contrast, global optimization techniques often use metaheuristics
[
18
]
or approximation algorithms
[
51
]
and typically their convergence to the global minimum cannot be guaranteed yet they often find a local minimum. For instance, for the malicious strategy (with concave objective function), among assignments which put all fleet vehicles on a route with non-zero HDV flow there may be multiple local minimizers. If the fleet uses an algorithm that selects a route to which it assigns all vehicles resulting in a local (yet not necessarily global) minimization, this assignment can still be detected.

Theorem 22

(Continuity of the inverse fleet assignment operator)
.

Let
\mathbb{t}
be twice continuously differentiable,
\nabla\mathbb{t}
be positive definite and
\lambda^{HDV}<\lambda^{CRV}
. Fix the total flow
q
. Then the inverse fleet assignment operators
I-H^{-1}:\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
as well as
H^{-1}:\mathbb{q}\mapsto\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
are continuous.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbb{f}^{*}\in(I-H^{-1})\mathbb{q}^{*}
and
\mathbb{f}^{\#}\in(I-H^{-1})\mathbb{q}^{\#}
sastisfy
|\mathbb{f}^{*}|=|\mathbb{f}^{\#}|=q^{CRV}
for
|\mathbb{q}^{*}|=|\mathbb{q}^{\#}|=q
. Then, denoting
L:=\lambda^{CRV}-\lambda^{HDV}
we obtain, by
(
5
),

\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g^{*}}\cdot\mathbb{t(q^{*})}+\left(L\mathbb{f^{*}}+\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q^{*}}\right)\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q^{*}}){\mathbb{g^{*}}})\geq 0

for every feasible direction
\mathbb{g}^{*}
. Rearranging this inequality and writing a similar one for
\mathbb{q}^{\#}
and feasible direction
\mathbb{g}^{\#}
we obtain

\begin{cases}-L\mathbb{f}^{*}\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})\mathbb{g^{*}})&\leq\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g^{*}}\cdot\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})+\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q}^{*}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})\mathbb{g^{*}}\\
-L\mathbb{f}^{\#}\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})\mathbb{g^{\#}})&\leq\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g^{\#}}\cdot\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})+\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q}^{\#}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})\mathbb{g^{\#}}\end{cases}

Setting
\mathbb{g}:=-\mathbb{g}^{\#}=\mathbb{g}^{*}=\mathbb{f}^{\#}-\mathbb{f}^{*}
and adding the above inequalities we obtain

\displaystyle L\mathbb{g}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})\mathbb{g}+L\mathbb{f}^{\#}\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})-\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*}))\mathbb{g}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g}(\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})-\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#}))

\displaystyle+\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q}^{*}(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})-\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#}))\mathbb{g}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{HDV}(\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#})\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})\mathbb{g}

with the five terms denoted by
A,B,C,D,E
and the inequality assuming the form
A+B\leq C+D+E
. Assuming that the delay function is twice continuously differentiable, we may thus estimate

\displaystyle L\rho(\nabla\mathbb{t})\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L\mathbb{g}^{*}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})\mathbb{g^{*}}=A,

\displaystyle|B|=|L\mathbb{f}^{\#}\cdot(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})-\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*}))\mathbb{g}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L\|\mathbb{f}^{\#}\|_{2}\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}\|\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#}\|_{2}\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2},

\displaystyle|C|=|\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{g}(\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})-\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#}))|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\lambda^{CRV}|\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2}\|\nabla\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}\|\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#}\|_{2},

\displaystyle|D|=|\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{q}^{*}(\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{*})-\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#}))\mathbb{g}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\lambda^{HDV}|\|\mathbb{q}^{*}\|_{2}\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}\|\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#}\|_{2}\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2},

\displaystyle|E|=|\lambda^{HDV}(\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#})\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q}^{\#})\mathbb{g}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\lambda^{HDV}|\|\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#}\|_{2}\|\nabla\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2},

where

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{|\mathbb{q}|=q}\|\nabla\mathbb{t(\mathbb{q})}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{|\mathbb{q}|=q}\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t(\mathbb{q})}\|_{2},

\|v\|_{2}=(\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2})^{1/2}
is the euclidean norm of a vector and
\|\nabla\mathbb{t(\mathbb{q})}\|_{2}
,
\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t(\mathbb{q})}\|_{2}
are the second (spectral) norms of operators
\nabla\mathbb{t(\mathbb{q})}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t(\mathbb{q})}
, respectively. Furthermore,
\rho(\nabla\mathbb{t})
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q})
minimized over the compact set
|\mathbb{q}|=q
. Hence,

L\rho(\nabla\mathbb{t})\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left[(L\|\mathbb{f}^{\#}\|_{2}+|\lambda^{HDV}|\|\mathbb{q}^{*}\|_{2})\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}+(|\lambda^{CRV}|+|\lambda^{HDV}|)\|\nabla\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}\right]\|\mathbb{q}^{*}-\mathbb{q}^{\#}\|_{2}\|\mathbb{g}\|_{2}

and, consequently,

\|\mathbb{f^{*}}-\mathbb{f}^{\#}\|_{2}\leq\frac{K}{(\lambda^{CRV}-\lambda^{HDV})\rho(\nabla\mathbb{t})}\|\mathbb{q^{*}}-\mathbb{q^{\#}}\|_{2}

(8)

for some constant
K
dependent on
\|\nabla\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}
,
\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{t}\|_{2,\infty}
,
q^{CRV}
,
q
and number of routes
R
.

∎

Remark 23
.

The inverse operator may be continuous even though the forward operator is discontinuous, as is the case for the malicious strategy, see Example
13
.

Next, we demonstrate that if the route travel times are sums of link travel times given by BPR functions then
\mathbb{\nabla t}
is essentially always positive definite. Let us begin with two definitions, which slightly generalize Definition
8
to systems where link-additivity holds only on certain routes and not necessarily globally.

Definition 24
.

We say that a travel system is
link additive
with respect to routes
1,\dots,R
if the route delay functions from the travel time vector
\mathbb{t}=(t_{1}(\mathbb{q}),t_{2}(\mathbb{q})),\dots,t_{R}(\mathbb{q}))^{T}
can be expressed as

t_{r}(\mathbb{q})=\sum_{\alpha}I^{r\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}\left(\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha}\right),

where
\tau_{\alpha}
are continuously differentiable link-delay functions, i.e. route travel times are sums of link travel times on links making up a route and every link travel time depends only on the total flow on this link and includes background traffic.

Definition 25
.

Routes
r_{1},\dots,r_{R}
in a link-additive system are
linearly dependent
if there exists a vector
\mathbb{v}\neq\mathbb{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{R}
such that
\sum_{r=1}^{R}v_{r}r_{r}=0
, which means that for every link
\alpha
in the network
\sum_{r=1}^{R}v_{r}I^{r\alpha}=0.

Proposition 26
.

In a link-additive system:

i)

The objective function (
2
) can be expressed as

F(\mathbb{h},\mathbb{f})=\sum_{\alpha}\left(\lambda^{HDV}h_{\alpha}+\lambda^{CRV}f_{\alpha}\right)\tau_{\alpha}(h_{\alpha}+f_{\alpha})

(9)

where
h_{\alpha}=\Lambda(\mathbb{h})_{\alpha},f_{\alpha}=\Lambda(\mathbb{f})_{\alpha}
are the link flows corresponding to route flows
\mathbb{h},\mathbb{f}
, respectively.

ii)

For a given route flow of all vehicles
\mathbb{q}
if
\mathbb{q^{CRV}},\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{CRV}}
give rise to the same fleet link flows then the corresponding
\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{HDV}}
also result in the same HDV link flows

iii)

If route assignments
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
and
\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{HDV}}
result in the same link flows and
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
,
\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{CRV}}
result in the same link flows then
F(\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{q^{CRV}})=F(\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{HDV}},\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{CRV}})
, i.e. the objective function (
2
) is invariant to replacing HDV and CRV route flows by different route flows which result in the same link flows.

Proof.

i) In a link-additive system we have

\displaystyle F(\mathbb{h},\mathbb{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda^{HDV}\mathbb{h}+\lambda^{CRV}\mathbb{f})\cdot\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{h}+\mathbb{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\lambda^{HDV}{h_{r}}+\lambda^{CRV}{f_{r}}\right)t_{r}(\mathbb{h}+\mathbb{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\lambda^{HDV}{h_{r}}+\lambda^{CRV}{f_{r}}\right)\sum_{\alpha}I^{r\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}(\Lambda(\mathbb{h}+\mathbb{f})_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\lambda^{HDV}{h_{r}}+\lambda^{CRV}{f_{r}}\right)I^{r\alpha}\right)\tau_{\alpha}(\Lambda(\mathbb{h}+\mathbb{f})_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\left(\lambda^{HDV}h_{\alpha}+\lambda^{CRV}f_{\alpha}\right)\tau_{\alpha}(h_{\alpha}+f_{\alpha})

ii) is a simple consequence of the fact that
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}=\mathbb{q}-\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
and linearity of the route-flow to link-flow conversion operator
\Lambda
, which implies
\Lambda(\mathbb{q^{HDV}})=\Lambda(\mathbb{q})-\Lambda(\mathbb{q^{CRV}})
.

iii) is a direct consequence of i) as the objective function (
2
) can, in link-additive systems be expressed as a function dependent on link flows only, (
9
).
∎

Proposition 27

(Gradient of route travel times is positive semi-definite in link additive systems)
.

Let
\mathbb{t}=(t_{1}(\mathbb{q}),t_{2}(\mathbb{q})),\dots,t_{R}(\mathbb{q}))^{T}
be the travel times on routes
1,\dots,R
in a link additive travel system. If
\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}>0
for every link
\alpha
, i.e. link delay functions are strictly increasing, then

i)

The gradient matrix
\nabla\mathbb{t}
is positive semi-definite.

ii)

The gradient matrix
\nabla\mathbb{t}
is positive definite iff the routes
1,\dots R
are linearly independent.

Proof.

i) Denoting
e_{ij}
the
R\times R
matrix with entry
(i,j)
equal
1
and all other entries equal
0
we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbb{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\frac{\partial t_{i}}{\partial q_{j}}e_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\frac{\partial\sum_{\alpha}I^{r_{i}\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}(\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha})}{\partial q_{j}}e_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}I^{r_{i}\alpha}\frac{\partial\tau_{\alpha}(\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha})}{\partial q_{j}}e_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}I^{r_{i}\alpha}I^{r_{j}\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}\left(\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha})\right)e_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}\left(\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha})\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}I^{r_{i}\alpha}I^{r_{j}\alpha}e_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}d_{\alpha}M_{\alpha},

where we denoted
d_{a}:=\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\Lambda(\mathbb{q})_{\alpha})
and
M_{\alpha}:=\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}I^{r_{i}\alpha}I^{r_{j}\alpha}e_{ij}
.
Now, every
M_{\alpha}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite with one eigenvalue equal
N_{\alpha}
(number of routes containing
\alpha
) and
R-1
null eigenvalues. Hence,
\nabla\mathbb{t}
is positive semi-definite as a linear combination, with non-negative coefficients, of positive semi-definite matrices.

ii) Suppose first that routes
1,\dots,R
are linearly dependent. Then there exists a vector
\mathbb{v}\neq\mathbb{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{R}
such that for every link
\alpha
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{R}v_{i}I^{r_{i}\alpha}=0
. Consequently
M^{\alpha}\mathbb{v}=\mathbb{0}
for every link
\alpha
and hence
\mathbb{v}
is a null vector of
\mathbb{\nabla t}
which implies
\mathbb{v}^{T}\mathbb{\nabla t}(\mathbb{q})\mathbb{v}=0
.
On the contrary, if routes
1,\dots,R
are linearly independent then for every
\mathbb{v}\neq\mathbb{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{R}
there exists a link
\alpha_{0}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{R}v_{i}I^{r_{i}\alpha_{0}}\neq 0
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{v}^{T}\mathbb{\nabla t}(\mathbb{q})\mathbb{v}=\sum_{\alpha}d_{\alpha}\mathbb{v}^{T}M_{\alpha}\mathbb{v}=\sum_{\alpha\neq\alpha_{0}}d_{\alpha}\mathbb{v}^{T}M_{\alpha}\mathbb{v}+d_{\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{v}^{T}M_{\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{v}

is positive as
\sum_{\alpha\neq\alpha_{0}}d_{\alpha}\mathbb{v}^{T}M_{\alpha}\mathbb{v}
is non-negative and
\mathbb{v}^{T}M_{\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{v}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{R}v_{i}I^{r_{i}\alpha_{0}}\right)^{2}>0
.

∎

As a corollary, we have the following generalization of Theorem
18
, which holds in the case of linearly dependent routes.

Theorem 28

(Inverse of fleet assignment for linearly dependent routes)
.

Let
\mathbb{t}=(t_{1}(\mathbb{q}),t_{2}(\mathbb{q})),\dots,t_{R}(\mathbb{q}))^{T}
be continuously differentiable delay functions (travel times) of routes
1,\dots,R
in a link-additive system.
If
\lambda^{HDV}<\lambda^{CRV}
then for every
\mathbb{q}
such that the gradient matrix
\nabla\mathbb{t}(\mathbb{q})
is positive definite
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\in G(\mathbb{q-q^{CRV}})
and
\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{CRV}}\in G(\mathbb{q-\tilde{q}^{CRV}})
implies
\mathbb{q^{CRV}},\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{CRV}}
give rise to the same fleet link flows. Consequently, for
\lambda^{HDV}<\lambda^{CRV}
total route flow
\mathbb{q}
(or equivalently total link flow) uniquely determines
CRV
link flow optimizing the fleet objective.

Proof.

Repeating the proof of Theorem
18
we let
\mathbb{f^{*}}
,
\mathbb{\tilde{f}}
to be two solutions of (
4
) resulting in different fleet link flows (note that if routes are linearly independent then different route flows
always
result in different link flows). Then
\mathbb{g}=\mathbb{\tilde{f}}-\mathbb{f^{*}}
is a route flow (with entries which may be negative) whose link flows (again with perhaps negative entries) do not all vanish. Consequently, as in the proof of Proposition
27
, there exists a link
\alpha_{0}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{R}g_{i}I^{r_{i}\alpha_{0}}\neq 0
and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}^{T}\mathbb{\nabla t}(\mathbb{q})\mathbb{g}=\sum_{\alpha}d_{\alpha}\mathbb{g}^{T}M_{\alpha}\mathbb{g}=\sum_{\alpha\neq\alpha_{0}}d_{\alpha}\mathbb{g}^{T}M_{\alpha}\mathbb{g}+d_{\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{g}^{T}M_{\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{g}>0

which contradicts (
7
). Hence,
\mathbb{q^{CRV}},\mathbb{\tilde{q}^{CRV}}
give rise to the same fleet link flows. We note that by Proposition
26
different solutions with the same link flows imply the same value of the objective function.

∎

In the case of link-additive systems, in which
every route
is available and optimized by CRVs, we can reformulate the inverse problem in terms of link-flows using (

), which results in an alternative, simplified proof of the inverse fleet assignment theorem. We state the result in a more general form, allowing the link flows to be weakly interdependent with proof which follows the same lines as the proof of Theorem

Definition 29

(Dependent link-additive systems)
.

Let
\mathbb{a}=(a_{\alpha})
be the vector of link flows in a travel system. We say that this travel system is
dependent link-additive
if for every route
r
from Origin to Destination we have

t_{r}=\sum_{\alpha}I^{r\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{a}\right),

where
\tau_{\alpha}
are continuously differentiable link-delay functions.

Remark 30
.

Note that Definition
29
generalizes Definition
24
as it assumes that link delay functions
\tau_{\alpha}
depend on the whole vector of link flows
\mathbb{a}
and not only on the link flow on the given link
\mathbb{a}_{\alpha}
.

Theorem 31

(Inverse of fleet assignment for dependent link-additive systems)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\tau}(\mathbb{a})=(\tau_{\alpha})(\mathbb{a})^{T}
be the vector of continuously differentiable link delay functions dependent on the vector
\mathbb{a}=(a_{\alpha})^{T}
of link flows. Let
q^{CRV}
be the total flow of fleet vehicles (i.e. fleet size). If
\lambda^{HDV}<\lambda^{CRV}
then for any realisable (i.e. resulting from at least one route flow) link flow
\mathbb{a}
such that the gradient matrix
\nabla\boldsymbol{\tau}(\mathbb{a})
is positive definite there exists at most one realisable
\mathbb{a^{CRV}}
such that
\mathbb{a^{CRV}}\leq\mathbb{a}
and

\mathbb{a^{CRV}}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{a}-\mathbb{a^{CRV}})
, where
\Gamma
is the link assignment operator defined in Defintion
8
.

Proof.

The proof is a repetition of the proof of Theorem
18
with the route flows replaced by link flows, i.e.
(\mathbb{q},\mathbb{q^{HDV}},\mathbb{q^{CRV}})\to(\mathbb{a},\mathbb{a^{HDV}},\mathbb{a^{CRV}})
, the route delay functions
\mathbb{t}
replaced by link delay functions
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and the objective function
F
replaced by the objective funtion
\Phi
. One only needs to verify that for realisable link flows
\mathbb{\tilde{a}}
and
\mathbb{a^{*}}
the direction
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=\mathbb{\tilde{a}}-\mathbb{a^{*}}
is feasible for
\mathbb{a^{*}}
, which indeed follows by convexity of realisable link flows, Proposition
9
.
∎

Corollary 32

If the link delay functions are independent (e.g. given by the BPR functions) then
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{\alpha}(a_{\alpha}))
and hence

\nabla\boldsymbol{\tau}
is a diagonal matrix with entries

.

The positive-definitedness of

.

Remark 34
.

The default setting is to consider all the routes between one OD pair. Then the CRVs ’compete’ against HDVs from the same OD pair. However, we might also consider a restriction to only a subset of available routes (e.g. lorries using only selected routes). Then the HDVs having
exactly the same
route choice set are considered as included in the objective function. All other vehicles (perhaps those with a larger choice set) are included as background traffic.

Remark 35
.

Comparison of Fig.
1
and Fig.

reveals that there is a certain mismatch between the ease of optimization by the fleet and uniqueness of inverse. Namely, there exist cases (below we list only those with
\lambda^{CRV}\geq 0
) for which

•

Fleet optimizes a convex objective and the inverse is unique (e.g. selfish),

•

Fleet optimizes a convex objective and the inverse is non-unique (e.g. social, altruistic),

•

Fleet optimizes a concave objective and the inverse is unique (e.g. malicious),

•

Fleet optimizes an objective which is neither convex nor concave on the whole domain and the inverse is unique (e.g. disruptive).

(Inverse of fleet assignment for multiple OD pairs)

Inverse fleet assignment in the discrete case

The discrete case in inverse fleet assignment is more difficult than the continuous one as the standard tools of differential calculus are unavailable. Moreover, as set out in Section
2.3
the discrete fleet assignment according to a prescribed objective
(\lambda^{CRV},\lambda^{HDV})
is not straightforward. Assuming, however, as discussed in Section
2.3
that the fleet controller’s assignment consists in 1) identifying a local minimum (minima) 2) selecting a local minimum and 3) Arbitrary rounding-up flows to integer values, we have the following result.

Proposition 40
.

Assume the following pipeline:

1.

Fleet controller knows the (integer) HDV flow
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}

2.

Fleet controller identifies a continuous local minimizer
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
of the objective function.

3.

Fleet controller rounds up the flows obtaining an integer CRV flow
\mathbb{q^{CRV,integer}}
which is close to
\mathbb{q^{CRV}}
.

4.

City obtains the total integer flow
\mathbb{q}=\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV,integer}}
yet it a priori knows neither
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
nor
\mathbb{q^{CRV,integer}}
.

5.

City finds a flow
\mathbb{{q^{city}}}
which lies in the image of the (continuous) fleet assignment operator
H
such that
\|{\mathbb{q^{city}}}-\mathbb{q}\|
is minimized.

6.

Using the inverse assignment theory (Theorem
18
or Theorem
31
or Theorem
28
) city identifies the unique continuous HDV flow
\mathbb{q^{HDV,city}}
such that
H(\mathbb{q^{HDV,city}})={\mathbb{q^{city}}}
.

Then
\mathbb{q^{HDV,city}}
is close to
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{q^{HDV,city}}-\mathbb{q^{HDV}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|H^{-1}(\mathbb{q^{city}})-H^{-1}(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mbox{Lip}(H^{-1})\|\mathbb{q^{city}}-(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mbox{Lip}(H^{-1})\left(\|\mathbb{q^{city}}-\mathbb{q}\|+\|\mathbb{q}-(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}})\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mbox{Lip}(H^{-1})\|\mathbb{q^{CRV,integer}}-\mathbb{q^{CRV}}\|

where we used the fact that

\|\mathbb{q^{city}}-\mathbb{q}\|=\min_{\mathbb{r}}\|\mathbb{r}-\mathbb{q}\|\leq\|(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}})-\mathbb{q}\|
as
(\mathbb{q^{HDV}}+\mathbb{q^{CRV}})
belongs to the image of
H
and the Lipschitz constant
\mbox{Lip}(H^{-1})
is bounded by Theorem
22
.
∎

Example 41

(Nonuniqueness of the discrete inverse assignment)
.

Let there be two equivalent routes with total flow
\mathbb{q}=(50,50)
and convex delay functions. Let the fleet apply the selfish strategy. For
q^{CRV}=19
we have the unique
\mathbb{q}^{CRV}=(9.5,9.5)
for the continuous inverse assignment. However, in the discrete case there are two solutions
\mathbb{q}^{CRV}=(9,10)
and
\mathbb{q}^{CRV}=(10,9)
.

Example 42
.

As above but for the malicious strategy. The malicious strategy (be it continuous or discrete) always assigns all the vehicles to one route as concave functionals are minimized in the corners of a polygonal domain. Therefore, for an integer
\mathbb{q}
the discrete inverse and the continous inverse coincide.

Conclusions, Open Problems and Future directions

Fleets of cooperating vehicles such as CAVs, once they start operating in our cities on a full scale might blend in with the surroundings and become indistinguishable from other vehicles. The collective routing of these vehicles, however, might increase human drivers’ or overall travel times and cause congestion or chaos.
Detection of cooperating fleet vehicles might prove rather difficult in the real world.
Nevertheless, in this paper we showed that the first step of the process, i.e. identification of flows of collectively routing vehicles is theoretically achievable for fleet behaviours which are more selfish than altruistic – a range which covers most standard fleet objectives. The future work
[
26
]
will encompass discussion of methods of detection of individual collectively routing vehicles and efficient algorithms necesary to achieve this goal.

Some other issues that we left open are:

i)

Proving the continuity of the inverse fleet assignment operator with respect to parameters.

ii)

Fully solving the discrete inverse problem.

iii)

Characterizing the image of the forward fleet assignment operator, i.e. which total assignments
\mathbb{q}
can arise when the size of fleet is given by
q^{CRV}
?

iv)

Proposing efficient exact and heuristic algorithms of fleet assignment in nonstandard (non-convex) cases.

v)

Addressing the inverse fleet assignment problem with multiple fleets or fleets applying more elaborate objectives which are not linear combinations of HDV and CRV such as multiplicative objectives or multi-day objectives based on total payoff over the considered period, see also Appendix
A
for Stackelberg routing.

vi)

Considering more general transportation settings such as varying departure times of agents, stochasticity and uncertainty of fleet assignment, more realistic delay functions or simulations in open source virtual environments such as SUMO
[
33
]
with fleets agents applying machine learning to optimize their routing,
[
1
,
2
]
etc.

Appendix A
Relation to Stackelberg and Nash routing

In this section we compare the Stackelberg and Nash routing to the myopic routing used in this paper. In
Stackelberg
routing, the atomic player (leader), being able to
predict
the equilibrium behaviour of the non-atomic players (followers) given its own assignment, routes the vehicles so as to optimize its objective once the equilibrium is reached. More formally, we have the following definition, compare
[
37
,
10
]
.

Definition 46

(Stackelberg equilibrium)
.

Stackelberg equilibrium
[
42
]
is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium of the sequential game where the leader first commits to a given strategy and then the follower makes a choice
knowing
the commitment of the leader.

In our setting of mixed fleet – human driver system a Stackelberg equilibrium occurs when

i)

the fleet operator, being able to predict the induced
equilibrium
flows resulting from human driver assignment, chooses its own assignment such that its objective is optimized
provided
the human drivers indeed assign themselves according to the respective equilibrium.

ii)

the human drivers assign themselves according to (Wardrop, Stochastic etc.) User Equilibrium,
knowing
the assignment of the fleet.

In
Nash
routing, on the other hand, the fleet controller computes and applies every day the flows originating from a Nash equilibrium of the
simultaneous
game, where neither the fleet nor the HDVs (treated as a group) can improve by unilaterally changing their assignment. To be able to apply this kind of routing, the fleet controller requires, similarly as for Stackelberg, an ability to predict the equilibrium flows of HDVs.

In contrast, the
myopically
routing fleet controller considered in this paper

•

First predicts the
non-equilibrium
flows of HDVs on a given day based on behavioural models of HDVs and observation of previous HDV assignments. The way this prediction works does not play any role in this paper, however, as long as it is precise.

•

Then it selects an assignment which optimizes the one-day objective given the predicted HDV assignment.

•

HDVs indeed behave according to the model used by the fleet controller.

Comparison of myopic and Stackelberg/Nash routing

Comparing the myopic, Stackelberg and Nash routing we find that, Table
1
,

•

The myopic routing can be applied without precise knowledge of (often non-unique) equilibrium human behaviour and requires only a day-to-day human behaviour prediction model, which could potentially be very simple (e.g. use the flows from the day before in settings when the flows do not vary much day-to-day). On the other hand, for Stackelberg, an equilibrium human behaviour model is necessary to compute the HDV assignment for every possible assignment of the fleet. The same applies to Nash routing.

•

Even if the optimal Stackelberg fleet routing can be computed and it is a pure strategy, there may exist many (Wardrop, Stochastic) User Equilibria with the given fleet flow. This may happen even when there is no fleet at all when Wardrop equilibria are non-unique.
If the fleet controller selects the Stackelberg flows and sticks to them, the HDV flows may converge to any of these subgame Nash equilibria. In this context we note that:

–

The time-scale of this convergence is not clear
[
24
]
.

–

The Stackelberg routing eventual efficiency is conditional on the
initial human flows
. In contrast to standard Stackelberg games setting, where the follower(s) are supposed to
choose
the equilibrium behaviour, in favour or not of the leader, leading to Strong/Weak or other Stackelberg equilibria, in our setting the followers only
converge
to the equilibrium flow and have little control over which flow this convergence will end up with.

•

Similarly, for Nash routing the eventual efficiency depends on whether HDV flows converge to the Nash equilibrium flows desired by the fleet operator.

•

The myopic routing in simple settings often converges to a state where the flows of both the fleet and HDVs stabilize, which resembles a Nash Equilibrium (see
[
25
]
). Indeed, the fleet controller predicts that the Human Driver assignment will not change (much) the next day, and this indeed happens, and it routes the same flows the next day. This, however, is often distinct from Stackelberg routing which may be more efficient for the fleet controller, however is
not
the best response to equilibrium HDV behaviour (see e.g.
[
55
]
).

•

The myopic routing is (can be) always based on pure strategies as the fleet controller ’moves’ second and there is no added value of applying a mixed strategy even when the payoffs of two or more strategies coincide. In contrast, for Stackelberg and Nash routing mixed strategies are often indispensable.

•

The Stackelberg routing may not be more efficient than myopic routing even if the HDV flows eventually converge to the most favourable equilibrium, as this routing disregards the transient periods which the controller can still take advantage of, see Remark
49
. The same applies to Nash routing.

•

The optimal Stackelberg routing and myopic routing are optimization problems and so they always exist. On the other, under the reasonable practical assumption of only pure HDV strategies the Nash equilibrium and consequently Nash routing (even for mixed fleet strategies) may not exist, see Example
47
.

•

It is NP-hard to compute exactly the Stackelberg Equilibrium and simple heuristic algorithms are not very efficient
[
27
,
37
]
. Nevertheless for ’convex’ players efficient algorithms for solving this bilevel problem based on solving variational inequalities are well-known
[
54
,
44
]
, which however do not scale well, see
[
32
]
for recent approaches.

•

If HDVs cannot be treated as a single player then Stackelberg leadership is not necessarily beneficial
[
46
]
.

Based on the above, we conclude that myopic routing, even though in many cases it is likely not optimal long-term, is a simple to implement greedy strategy which maximizes the immediate reward and we consider it as a baseline for studying different behaviours of fleet controllers. Whether (and when) the Stackelberg routing is better on average than myopic routing is an open question. Finally, we note that if the fleet controller was to use the Stackelberg routing, which was known to the city, the problem of identifying fleet flows would cease to be an inverse problem and would amount to
computing
the Stackelberg equilibrium and no total flows of vehicles would be needed, as is in the case of myopic routing setting considered in this paper.

Example: Stackelberg equilibria for malicious fleet controller

In this section we show the differences between different kinds of routing on an example of malicious fleet controller. We note that equilibrium outcomes for standard ’convex’ players, i.e. system optimal player (corresponding to social fleet in our setting), Cournot-Nash player (corresponding to selfish fleet in our setting) and User Equlibrium player (corresponding to HDVs in our setting) are discussed in detail in
[
54
]
, along with algorithms computing the respective equilibria, see also
[
43
]
for previous results in UE - System Optimal player setting.

Example 47

(Optimal Stackelberg routing is mixed, Nash routing does not exist)
.

Let the system consist of two independent routes with the same convex delay functions
t_{1}=t_{2}
,
q^{HDV}
human drivers minimizing their travel time (i.e. tending to Wardrop User Equilibrium) and
q^{CRV}
fleet vehicles. Suppose for simplicity that
q^{CRV}=q^{HDV}
. All Stackelberg equilibria are of the form

\mathbb{q^{CRV}}=\begin{cases}(q^{CRV},0)\mbox{ with probability }p,\\
(0,q^{CRV})\mbox{ with probability }1-p,\end{cases}

(10)

and
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}
the corresponding unique induced user-equilibrated HDV routing, where
p\in(0,1)
. It may happen that the unique equilibrium is obtained for
p=0.5

and
q=(q^{HDV}/2,q^{HDV}/2)
, or there can be two solutions. Consequently, optimal Stackelberg malicious fleet routing is stochastic and causes massive oscillations of travel times on routes. On the other hand, considering Nash equilibria, we note that any best response (i.e. in fact myopic) fleet routing puts all fleet vehicles on one route. This implies that a Nash equilibrium with pure strategy HDV routing (which we assume) does not exist as
(q^{CRV},0)
induces best HDV response
(0,q^{HDV})
which in turn induces best fleet response
(0,q^{CRV})
and so on. Consequently, the fleet cannot use Nash routing as it does not exist.

We first note that for
p=1
the corresponding induced HDV assignment is
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}=(0,q^{HDV})
. In comparison, it can be shown that for
p=0.5
the corresponding
\mathbb{q^{HDV}}=(q^{HDV}/2,q^{HDV}/2)
incurs higher average travel times for HDVs by convexity of
t_{1},t_{2}
. Therefore, (
10
) with
p=1
or
p=0
(by symmetry) is not an optimal Stackelberg routing. Otherwise, the claim of Example
47
is a consequence of the following more general result.

Proposition 48
.

For the malicious fleet of size
q^{CRV}
, aiming to maximize total HDVs travel time, and a system with two routes and HDVs minimizing their individual travel times, the optimal Stackelberg routing is supported on strategies
(q^{CRV},0)
and
(0,q^{CRV})
.
