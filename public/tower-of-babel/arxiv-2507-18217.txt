Dissipation functions and Brownian oscillators
M. Colangeli, L. Rondoni, P. Vozza

Abstract
We develop a general framework for response theory in diffusion processes governed by Fokker-Planck equations, based on the notion of the Dissipation Function. Using the analytically solvable Brownian oscillator model, we derive exact response formulae for both overdamped and underdamped dynamics of harmonically bound Brownian particles. We also demonstrate that for certain observables and under suitable time scaling, the operations of model reduction and response formula extraction commute.



Keywords:—
Response theory; Fokker-Planck equation; overdamped limits; Underdamped Langevin dynamics.

The search for non-perturbative approaches to response theory, such as the method based on the transient time correlation function developed in
[

, gained renewed momentum with the introduction of the
Dissipation Function
, defined in
[

,

]
as the energy dissipation rate that satisfies the fluctuation relation.
This concept enabled the unification, within a consistent theoretical framework, of the diverse methodologies employed in nonequilibrium molecular dynamics. The Fluctuation Theorem, systematized in
[

]
, provides a rigorous probabilistic description of entropy production in small, driven systems and applies to time-reversible dynamics subjected to constant external forces. Extensions to periodically driven systems were subsequently developed in
[

,

,

. The mathematical framework of response theory based on the Dissipation Function was further formalized in
[

[

]
, as well as to systems exhibiting collective behavior and phase transitions, such as Kuramoto-type models
[
1
]
, where standard linear response theory typically breaks down.

In this work, we extend this formalism to stochastic diffusion processes with additive noise. We demonstrate that many structural features originally derived for deterministic dynamics can be systematically transferred and adapted to stochastic systems governed by Fokker–Planck equations
[
48
,
20
]
.

We further derive explicit response formulae for both overdamped and underdamped Langevin dynamics. Importantly, these formulae are
exact
, in the sense that they are not limited to the linear response regime and remain valid for finite perturbation amplitudes.

Focusing on the analytically tractable case of harmonic Brownian oscillators, we illustrate the applicability of the exact response theory, that becomes particularly interesting in complex situations. We then examine the commutativity of two operations: the derivation of response expressions and the overdamped limit, which corresponds to a perfect separation of time scales between fast and slow variables. This commutativity for selected observables reflects a structural symmetry of the response formalism based on the Dissipation Function and may also have practical implications. In particular, for systems with complex particle dynamics, it may be more tractable to probe the regime of perfect time scale separation at the level of observable averages, rather than by resolving the full stochastic dynamics of individual constituents.

2
Dissipation Function in the Fokker–Planck Equation

In this section, we analyze the role of the Dissipation Function in the evolution of probability densities governed by the Fokker–Planck equation.
We begin with the stochastic differential equation (SDE) for a variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
written in the Itô’s form:

dx(t)=A(x)\,dt+\sigma(x)\,dW(t)\,,\quad x(0)=x_{0},

(2.1)

where
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, and
W(t)
is a standard Wiener process in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The generator
L
of the process acts on state functions
O:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

L\ O(x)=A(x)\cdot\nabla O(x)+D(x):\left(\nabla\nabla O(x)\right),

(2.2)

where
D(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma(x)\sigma(x)^{T}
is the diffusion matrix, and
\nabla\nabla O
denotes the Hessian matrix of
O
.

The
L^{2}
-adjoint of the generator
L
, known as the
Fokker–Planck operator
, acts on probability densities
f(x,t)
according to:

L^{*}f(x,t)=\nabla\cdot\left[-A(x)p(x,t)+\nabla\cdot\left(D(x)f(x,t)\right)\right]\,.

(2.3)

The corresponding forward Kolmogorov equation (or Fokker–Planck equation) for
f(x,t)
reads:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(x,t)

\displaystyle=L^{*}f(x,t)\;\,,\quad\text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),

(2.4a)

\displaystyle f(x,0)

\displaystyle=f_{0}(x)\qquad,\quad\text{on}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{t=0\}.

(2.4b)

Using the shorthand notation
f_{t}(x)=f(x,t)
, Eq. (
2.4a
) can be rewritten as:

\partial_{t}f_{t}(x)=\Omega^{f_{t}}(x)\,f_{t}(x)\,,

(2.5)

where the state function
\Omega^{f_{t}}(x):=(L^{*}f_{t}(x))/f_{t}(x)
, called
Dissipation Function
1
1

1

We purposely adopt, here, the same terminology used in the deterministic setting
[
24
]
.
, is given explicitly by:

\displaystyle\Omega^{f_{t}}(x)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot A(x)-A(x)\cdot\nabla\log f_{t}(x)+\nabla\nabla:D(x)

\displaystyle\quad+2\,(\nabla\cdot D(x))\cdot\nabla\log f_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\quad+D(x):\left[\nabla\nabla\log f_{t}(x)+\nabla\log f_{t}(x)\otimes\nabla\log f_{t}(x)\right],

(2.6)

see Appendix
A
for details.
Finally, we define the expectation of the state function
O(x)
at time
s\geq 0
as:

\langle O\rangle_{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}O(x)\,f_{s}(x)\,dx.

(2.7)

Exploiting the formal solution of Eq. (
2.4a
), we find:

\langle O\rangle_{s}=\int O(x)\left(e^{sL^{*}}p_{0}(x)\right)\,dx=\int\left(e^{sL}O(x)\right)p_{0}(x)\,dx.

The time derivative of
\langle O\rangle_{s}
reads:

\frac{d}{ds}\langle O\rangle_{s}=\int\left(\frac{d}{ds}e^{sL}O(x)\right)f_{0}(x)\,dx=\int(Le^{sL}O(x))f_{0}(x)\,dx,

which, using the adjoint operator, becomes:

\frac{d}{ds}\langle O\rangle_{s}=\int\left(e^{sL}O(x)\right)\left(L^{*}f_{0}(x)\right)\,dx.

Finally, from (
2.5
), we find:

\frac{d}{ds}\langle O\rangle_{s}=\int\left(e^{sL}O(x)\right)\Omega^{f_{0}}(x)f_{0}(x)\,dx=\langle O(x_{s})\Omega^{f_{0}}(x_{0})\rangle_{0}.

(2.8)

By integrating both sides of (
2.8
) from 0 to
t
, we finally obtain the Exact Response Formula:

\langle O\rangle_{t}-\langle O\rangle_{0}=\int_{0}^{t}\langle O(x_{s})\Omega^{f_{0}}(x_{0})\rangle_{0}\,ds.

(2.9)

We note that the average appearing under the integral in Eq. (
2.9
) is taken with respect to the initial density
f_{0}(x)
. To establish a connection with response theory, it is helpful to further characterize
f_{0}(x)
introducing an alternative process, called the
reference dynamics
and defined for times
t\in(-\infty,0)
, which admits
f_{0}(x)
as its invariant probability density. The dynamics governed by Eq. (
2.1
) can be interpreted as the result of applying a perturbation to the reference dynamics at time
t=0
, and therefore will be referred to as the
perturbed dynamics
.

We also observe that if in Eq. (
2.9
) one sets
O(x)=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the normalization of the probability density
f_{s}(x)
, for
s\geq 0
, implies
\langle\Omega^{f_{0}}\rangle_{0}=0
.

3
From the underdamped to the overdamped Langevin dynamics

We let
x=(q,p)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and consider the underdamped Langevin equation describing a Brownian particle subjected to an external potential
U(q)

[
41
,
37
]
:

\displaystyle dq(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{m}\,dt,

(3.10a)

\displaystyle dp(t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{q}U(q)\,dt-\gamma~p\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma m\beta^{-1}}\,dW(t).

(3.10b)

We briefly outline the formal steps leading to the overdamped limit of Eqs. (
3.10
), following the standard derivation described in
[
37
,
14
]
.
We introduce
\epsilon>0
and in Eqs. (
3.10
) we rescale
\gamma\mapsto\gamma/\varepsilon
and
t\mapsto t/\varepsilon
, which yields

\displaystyle dq^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon m}\,dt,

(3.11a)

\displaystyle dp^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{q}U(q^{\varepsilon})\,dt-\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon^{2}}p^{\varepsilon}\,dt+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2\gamma m\beta^{-1}}\,dW(t),

(3.11b)

where we have exploited the scaling property of the Wiener process
W(t/\varepsilon)=1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\ W(t)
.
From (
3.11a
) and (
3.11b
), we obtain:

dq^{\varepsilon}=\frac{p^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon m}\,dt=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma m}dp^{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\gamma m}\nabla_{q}U(q^{\varepsilon})\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\beta\gamma}}\,dW(t).

Taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, we arrive at the overdamped Langevin dynamics
[
37
]

dq=-\frac{1}{\gamma m}\nabla_{q}U(q)\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\beta\gamma}}\,dW(t).

(3.12)

In the sequel we will focus on Brownian oscillators, namely Brownian particles bound by a harmonic potential
U(q)=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}q^{2}
, and evaluate Eq. (
2.9
) for both the overderdamped dynamics (
3.12
) (in Sec.
4
) and its underdamped counterpart (
3.10
) (in Sec.
5
).

4
Exact response for the overdamped Langevin dynamics

The application of the response theory introduced in Sec.
2
to Eq. (
3.12
) begins with the definition of the reference dynamics:

dq(t)=-\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}q}{\gamma}\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\beta_{0}\gamma}}\,dW(t),\quad t\leq 0,

(4.13)

which switches, at time
t=0^{+}
, into the perturbed one, defined as follows:

dq(t)=-\frac{\omega^{2}q}{\gamma}\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\beta\gamma}}\,dW(t)\;,\quad t>0.

(4.14)

Note that both parameters
\omega_{0}\rightarrow\omega
and
\beta_{0}\rightarrow\beta
have changed as a result of the perturbation.
The Fokker-Planck equation corresponding to Eq. (
4.14
), known as Smoluchowski equation
[
5
]
, reads

\frac{\partial f_{t}(q)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\left[\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma}q\ f_{t}(q)+\frac{1}{m\beta\gamma}\frac{\partial}{\partial q}f_{t}(q)\right],

(4.15)

and is supplied with the initial datum

f_{0}(q)=\sqrt{\frac{m\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}m\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}q^{2}\right)}.

(4.16)

Equation (
4.15
) can be cast in the form

\frac{\partial f_{t}(q)}{\partial t}=\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{t}}f_{t},

(4.17)

where the subscripts in
\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{t}}
highlight the two parameters that characterize the perturbation.
A straightforward computation (see Eq. (
A.5
) in Appendix
A
) leads to the following expression of the Dissipation Function
\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}
characterizing the overdamped dynamics (
4.14
):

\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q)=A_{0}+B_{0}q^{2}\;,

(4.18)

where
A_{0}=(\beta\omega^{2}-\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2})/(\beta\gamma)
and
B_{0}=-A_{0}/\langle q^{2}\rangle_{0}
, with
\langle q^{2}\rangle_{0}=(\beta_{0}m\omega_{0}^{2})^{-1}
.
Formula (
4.18
) reveals that the initial distribution (
4.16
) is effectively invariant for the reference dynamics (
4.13
), because it holds

\Omega_{\omega_{0},\beta_{0}}^{f_{0}}(q)=0.

(4.19)

Moreover, we also point out that
\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}
vanishes, more generally, if the condition
\beta\omega^{2}=\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}
is satisfied. This follows from the fact that the invariant densities of the reference and the perturbed dynamics are both Gaussian distributions:

f_{0}(q)=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{\beta_{0}m\omega_{0}^{2}}\right),\quad f_{\infty}(q)=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{\beta m\omega^{2}}\right).

Under the condition
\beta\omega^{2}=\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}
, the variances of these distributions coincide (and so do also the respective means, which both vanish), implying
f_{0}(q)\equiv f_{\infty}(q)
.
For the Brownian oscillator model described by Eq. (
4.14
), the expression of the conditional probability density
p(r,t|q,0)
can be made explicit:

p(r,t|q,0)=\sqrt{\frac{\beta m\omega^{2}}{2\pi\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\omega^{2}}{\gamma}t}\right)}}\exp{\left[-\frac{\beta m\omega^{2}\left(r-q\,e^{-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma}t}\right)^{2}}{2\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\omega^{2}}{\gamma}t}\right)}\right]}.

(4.20)

We compute, first, the exact response formula for the observable
O(q)\equiv q^{2}
.
In this case Eq. (
2.9
) reads

\langle q^{2}\rangle_{t}=\langle q^{2}\rangle_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\langle q_{s}^{2}\,\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q_{0})\rangle_{0}\,ds.

(4.21)

From Eq. (
4.20
), we obtain the expression

\langle q^{2}\rangle_{t}=\frac{\beta\omega^{2}e^{-2\kappa t}+\left(1-e^{-2\kappa t}\right)\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}}{m\beta_{0}\beta\omega_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}},

(4.22)

where
\kappa=\omega^{2}/\gamma
denotes a characteristic relaxation frequency.
We point out that formula (
4.22
) possesses the proper asymptotic behavior: it reduces to
\langle q^{2}\rangle_{0}
at
t=0
while it recovers the expression
\langle q^{2}\rangle_{\infty}=(\beta m\omega^{2})^{-1}
in the long time limit.
We turn, then, to the analysis of the response formula for the state function
O(q)\equiv\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q)
.
In this case formula (
2.9
) reads:

\langle\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}\rangle_{t}=\langle\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}\rangle_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q_{s})\,\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q_{0})\rangle_{0}\,ds.

(4.23)

We first verify that it correctly holds

\langle\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}\rangle_{0}=A_{0}+B_{0}\langle q^{2}\rangle_{0}=0,

(4.24)

whereas

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\langle\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q_{s})\,\Omega^{f_{0}}(q_{0})\rangle_{0}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{0}\int_{0}^{s}\langle q_{s}^{2},\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q_{0})\rangle_{0}ds

(4.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{0}(1-e^{-2\kappa t})(\langle q^{2}\rangle_{\infty}-\langle q^{2}\rangle_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\beta\omega^{2}-\beta_{0}\omega_{0})^{2}}{\beta^{2}\gamma\omega^{2}}(1-e^{-2\kappa t}).

5
Exact response theory for the underdamped Langevin dynamics

We now apply the formalism of Sec.
2
in the set-up of the underdamped dynamics (
3.10
). We start, as in Sec.
4
, with the definition of the reference dynamics for
t\leq 0
, which takes the form:

\displaystyle dq(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{m}\,dt,

(5.26a)

\displaystyle dp(t)

\displaystyle=-m\omega_{0}^{2}q\,dt-\gamma~p\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma m\beta_{0}^{-1}}\,dW(t).

(5.26b)

At time
t=0^{+}
the joint perturbation
\omega_{0}\rightarrow\omega
and
\beta_{0}\rightarrow\beta
makes the reference dynamics turn into the perturbed one, defined for
t>0
as follows:

\displaystyle dq(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{m}\,dt,

(5.27a)

\displaystyle dp(t)

\displaystyle=-m\omega^{2}q\,dt-\gamma~p\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma m\beta^{-1}}\,dW(t).

(5.27b)

The Fokker-Planck equation describing, for
t>0
, the evolution of the probability density
f_{t}(q,p)
(an instance of the Kramers equation
[
5
]
) reads

\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial t}=-\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial q}+m\omega_{0}^{2}q\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial p}+\gamma f_{t}+\gamma p\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial p}+\frac{\gamma m}{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{t}}{\partial p^{2}},

(5.28)

and is supplied with the initial datum

f_{0}(q,p)=\frac{\beta_{0}\omega_{0}}{2\pi}e^{-\frac{\beta_{0}}{2}\left(m\omega_{0}^{2}q^{2}+\frac{p^{2}}{m}\right)}.

(5.29)

The dissipation function
\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}
derived from (
4.15
) corresponds to the state function

\Omega_{\omega,\beta}^{f_{0}}(q,p)=A_{1}p^{2}+B_{1}qp+C_{1},

(5.30)

where
A_{1}=-\frac{\gamma\beta_{0}}{m}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{0}}{\beta}\right)
,
B_{1}=\beta_{0}(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})
and
C_{1}=-A_{1}\langle p^{2}\rangle_{0}
, with
\langle p^{2}\rangle_{0}=m/\beta_{0}
.
We note that
f_{0}(q,p)
in (
5.29
) is invariant for the reference dynamics (
5.26
), for
\Omega_{\omega_{0},\beta_{0}}^{f_{0}}=0
holds.
Furthermore, letting
x=(q,p)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Eq. (
5.28
) can be rewritten in the form

\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial t}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\bigg[-A_{i,j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(x_{j}f_{t})+D_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{t}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\bigg],

(5.31)

where the matrices
A
and
D
have the following form:

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&&1/m\\
-m\omega^{2}&&-\gamma\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}0&&0\\
0&&\gamma/\beta\end{pmatrix}.

(5.32)

In the large friction regime
\gamma>2\omega
that is considered here, the two eigenvalues of the matrix
A
are real,
-\lambda_{\pm}<0
, with
\lambda_{\pm}=(\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4\omega^{2}})/2
. The time scale separation, in this model, is quantified by
\Delta=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}=\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4\omega^{2}}
.

Proceeding as in Sec.
4
, let us consider, first, the exact response formula for the observable
O(q,p)\equiv q^{2}
.

The evaluation of time correlation functions for the underdamped dynamics (
5.27
)
\langle O(x_{s})\Omega_{\omega\beta}^{f_{0}}(x_{0})\rangle_{0}
can be carried out by knowledge of the conditional probability density
p(y,t|x,0)
, which in this case reads

p(y,t|x,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma(t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(y-e^{At}x\right)\Sigma^{-1}(t)\left(y-e^{At}x\right)\right),

(5.33)

where

\Sigma(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{As}\ D\ e^{A^{T}s}ds

is the covariance matrix. We point out that the stationary covariance matrix
\bar{\Sigma}:=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\Sigma(t)
satisfies the Lyapunov equation.

A\bar{\Sigma}+\bar{\Sigma}A^{T}=-D,

which is an instance of the Fluctuation-Dissipation relation
[
41
,
37
,
14
]
.
Using Eq. (
B.6e
) in Appendix
B
, we find that, for the state function
O(q,p)\equiv q^{2}
, formula (
2.9
) evaluates to:

\displaystyle\langle q^{2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{0}m\omega_{0}^{2}}+\frac{e^{-2t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}}{m\beta\beta_{0}\lambda_{-}(\lambda_{-}-\lambda_{+})^{2}\lambda_{+}(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\omega_{0}^{2}}\times

(5.34)

\displaystyle\left[e^{2t\lambda_{-}}\lambda_{-}(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\left(\beta\lambda_{-}\omega^{2}-(\beta_{0}\gamma+\beta(-\gamma+\lambda_{-}))\omega_{0}^{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{2t\lambda_{+}}\lambda_{+}(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\left(\beta\lambda_{+}\omega^{2}-(\beta_{0}\gamma+\beta(-\gamma+\lambda_{+}))\omega_{0}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2e^{t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}\lambda_{-}\lambda_{+}\left(\beta(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\omega^{2}-(2\beta_{0}\gamma+\beta(-2\gamma+\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+}))\omega_{0}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+e^{2t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}(\lambda_{-}-\lambda_{+})^{2}\left(-\beta(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\omega^{2}+(\beta_{0}\gamma+\beta(-\gamma+\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+}))\omega_{0}^{2}\right)\right].

More insight on the formula (
5.34
) is gained by inspecting the large-
\gamma
regime. To this aim we adopt in (
5.34
) the scaling previously introduced in Sec.
2
, i.e.
\gamma\rightarrow\gamma/\epsilon
,
t\rightarrow t/\epsilon
. The following asymptotics holds

\lambda_{+}=\frac{\gamma}{\epsilon}+O(\epsilon)\quad,\quad\lambda_{-}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma}\epsilon+o(\epsilon).

(5.35)

Note that the time scale separation
\Delta\sim\epsilon^{-1}
diverges as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, which corresponds to the regime of perfect time scale separation.
Inserting the expressions (
5.35
) in (
5.34
) and then taking the overdamped limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, one obtains

\langle q^{2}\rangle_{t}=\frac{\beta\omega^{2}e^{-2\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma}t}+\left(1-e^{-2\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma}t}\right)\beta_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}}{m\beta_{0}\beta\omega_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}},

(5.36)

which recovers formula (
4.22
).
This result highlights a key feature of the response theory developed in Sec.
2
: for certain observables
O(q,p)
, the two operations of taking the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and deriving the response formula (
2.9
) commute.

We now turn our attention to the state function
O(q,p)=p^{2}
. In this context, the momentum
p(t)
is regarded as a
fast
variable, while the rescaling
t\rightarrow t/\epsilon
corresponds to observing the evolution of the system on a
slow
time scale
[
26
]
. On such a scale, as
\epsilon
decreases, the quantity
\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t}
is expected to thermalize increasingly rapidly toward its equilibrium value
m/\beta
, as dictated by the equipartition theorem. This behavior is fully confirmed by our calculations, which, using Eq. (
B.6d
), show that

\displaystyle\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{0}m}\Bigg(1+\frac{e^{-2t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}(\beta-\beta_{0})\gamma}{\beta(\lambda_{-}-\lambda_{+})^{2}(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}\Big[-e^{2t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}(\lambda_{-}-\lambda_{+})^{2}

(5.37)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 4e^{t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}\lambda_{-}\lambda_{+}+(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})\big(e^{2t\lambda_{+}}\lambda_{-}+e^{2t\lambda_{-}}\lambda_{+}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-2t(\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{+})}(e^{t\lambda_{-}}-e^{t\lambda_{+}})^{2}\omega^{2}(\omega-\omega_{0})(\omega+\omega_{0})}{(\lambda_{-}-\lambda_{+})^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}}\Bigg).

At
t=0
formula (
5.37
) correctly reproduces
\langle p^{2}\rangle_{0}=m/\beta_{0}
.
Letting
\gamma\rightarrow\gamma/\epsilon
,
t\rightarrow t/\epsilon
, using (
5.35
), and finally passing to the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we find

\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t}=\frac{m}{\beta}\quad,\quad t>0,

(5.38)

which confirms that, after the rescaling, the quantity
\langle p^{2}\rangle_{t}
undergoes an instantaneous thermalization.

As a next step, we consider the state function
O(q,p)=qp

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have extended the response theory based on Dissipation Functions, originally developed in the context of deterministic dissipative dynamical systems
[
24
]
, to stochastic processes with additive noise governed by a Fokker–Planck equation. A key feature of our derivation is twofold: (i) the response formulae are conveniently expressed in terms of the initial probability density
[
24
]
; and (ii) these formulae remain valid for perturbations of arbitrary magnitude, and are therefore not restricted to the conventional linear response regime.

Focusing on the analytically tractable case of particles confined in a harmonic potential, we have demonstrated a notable property of the formalism: for specific observables, the operations of deriving the response formulae and taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, known as the overdamped limit, commute. Moreover, the theory correctly captures the average behavior of certain state functions, such as the Dissipation Function associated with the underdamped dynamics, which exhibits instantaneous relaxation to its equilibrium value in the overdamped limit; see Eq. (
5.42
). While this behavior is consistent with model reduction procedures such as adiabatic elimination, which rely on a large separation of time scales
[
20
,
46
]
, it does not reproduce the behavior of the average Dissipation Function obtained for the overdamped dynamics; see Eq. (
4.25
). This discrepancy arises from the fact that the two Dissipation Functions depend on different sets of variables, namely fast variables in the underdamped case and slow variables in the overdamped dynamics, see Eqs. (
5.30
) and (
4.18
), respectively. As a result, their averages exhibit distinct behavior in the overdamped limit.

Although such discrepancies are often encountered in the study of anomalous transport phenomena
[
29
]
, they can more fundamentally be traced back to the distinct time scales governing the decay of correlation functions, as explicitly derived for the Brownian oscillator model in Secs.
4
and
5
.

Looking ahead, the framework developed in this work sets the stage for applying response theory to high-dimensional stochastic systems, where model reduction techniques may assist in identifying a relevant subset of resolved variables, typically those evolving on slow time scales and governing the macroscopic behavior of the system.

2.5

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(A(x)f_{t}(x))

L^{*}f=-(\nabla\cdot A(x))f_{t}(x)-A(x)\cdot\nabla f_{t}(x)+\nabla\nabla:(D(x)f_{t}(x))\;,
