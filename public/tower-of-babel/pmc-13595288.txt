Diagnosis and Management of Patients With Suspected Left-Sided Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis According to Cardiac FDG-PET CT Results
Authors: Juan Caceres, Mark Edwards, Sarah Kurz, Barbara C S Hamilton, Chaitanya Madamanchi, Venkatesh Murthy, G Michael Deeb, Andrew W Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite advances in echocardiography, the definitive diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) remains difficult. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (chemical name 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluro-D-glucose) positron emission tomography (PET) shows promise with improved sensitivity. Our study explored the effects of PET findings on the diagnosis of PVE and patient management decisions.MethodBetween 2016 and 2022, 140 patients underwent PET to evaluate suspected left-sided PVE, resulting in 93 patients with positive PET (+PET) and 47 patients with negative PET. Patients were classified as definite, possible, or rejected PVE according to the 2023 Duke-International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases guidelines, initially excluding and then including PET results. We evaluated the association between PET results and clinical outcomes such as duration of antibiotics, performance of cardiac surgery within 3 months, and long-term mortality. A qualitative analysis of medical decision-making was performed.ResultsDemographics were similar between groups. patients with +PET had higher C-reactive protein levels (9.8 vs 3.1 mg/L, P = .001) and were more likely to have positive blood culture and echocardiographic findings. The addition of PET significantly increased the diagnosis of definite endocarditis (46%–63%, P < .001). A +PET was positively associated with ongoing use of antibiotics after 3 months (odds ratio [OR] 3.93, P = .002) and surgical management (OR 5.99, P = .008). Based on qualitative review of medical decision-making, PET influenced management in 88 patients (63%), supporting longer duration of antibiotics in 22%, surgical management in 8.6%, and shorter duration of antibiotics in 11% of patients.ConclusionsPositron emission tomography results, when incorporated into multidisciplinary discussion, aided in a more definitive diagnosis of PVE and influenced management in the majority of patients.

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is a complex disease that accounts for up to a third of endocarditis cases. Its incidence is 4.1 and 5.2 per 1000 person-years for surgical and transcatheter valves, respectively [1, 2]. Compared to native valve endocarditis, echocardiography (Echo) has reduced sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing PVE due to acoustic artifacts and prosthetic shadowing [3, 4]. While the modified Duke criteria have been the basis for diagnosing prosthetic valve endocarditis, recent studies have shown reduced sensitivity compared to other criteria incorporating 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (chemical name 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose) (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) [5, 6]. As such, the International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases (ISCVID) has recommended the incorporation of abnormal uptake by PET as a major criterion for the diagnosis of infectious endocarditis [7]. A recent study showed that the inclusion of PET into the diagnostic criteria yielded a sensitivity of 84% for suspected left-sided PVE compared to 57% for the modified Duke criteria alone, though there was reduced specificity (71% compared to 96%, respectively) [5]. Other studies have also demonstrated the value of PET in reclassifying and improving the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis [6, 8, 9].

Although there is a growing body of literature showing the improved diagnostic accuracy of PET for the evaluation of suspected PVE, there are limited data on how PET findings affect patient management decisions. Many unanswered questions exist regarding how to incorporate the PET scan results into optimal patient management. Common clinical dilemmas in patients undergoing PET evaluation for suspected PVE are as follows: (1) How should patients with negative Echo and abnormal PET evaluation be managed? (2) What degree of FDG uptake should be considered diagnostic of PVE? and (3) In patients without surgical indications identified by Echo, should an abnormal PET support the surgical management of PVE?

At our institution, PET has played an increasing role in diagnosing patients with suspected PVE. Patients with suspected or confirmed PVE are managed with guidance from a multidisciplinary endocarditis team (MET), with expert representation from infectious diseases, cardiac surgery, cardiology, radiology, and nuclear cardiology, among others. The purpose of this study is to provide a descriptive analysis of the management of patients with suspected left-sided PVE based on PET results, with a particular focus on how the results of PET influence patient management under the direction of a MET.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective, single-center study including admitted patients ≥18 years of age with a prior history of aortic or mitral valve replacement or repair who underwent a PET for suspected left-sided PVE between April 2016 and December 2022. Sixteen patients included in this analysis had their index hospitalization prior to the formal implementation of the MET in 2018. A review of patient records indicates that all 16 patients underwent multidisciplinary evaluation. Patients were included if the PET indication included the following terms: endocarditis, bacteremia, infection, or fever. Patients were excluded for the following: cardiac surgery within 3 months prior to PET; presence of a left ventricular assist device (LVAD); or lack of inpatient admission associated with the PET scan. Patients underwent PET under the direction of consulting teams (Infectious Diseases, Cardiology, and/or Cardiac Surgery) or after a formal discussion by the MET. In general, indications for PET were (1) high index of suspicion for PVE with negative echocardiographic findings, (2) abnormal echocardiographic evaluation of prosthetic valves that was indeterminate for infectious vs noninfectious etiology (eg, flail prosthetic leaflet due to degenerative vs infectious causes); (3) determining the extent of infection in patients with multiple prosthetic valves, devices, and/or grafts; and, less commonly, (4) further evaluation of clinically suspected PVE in patients unable to undergo transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). Indications (1) and (2) above are supported by the 2023 European Society of Cardiology endocarditis guidelines as Class I indications [10]. Patients with suspected right-sided PVE were not included in this analysis to avoid additional heterogeneity in treatment approaches between left- and right-sided endocarditis. Based on formal physician interpretation and MET review, PET and Echo results were defined as positive, negative, or indeterminate. For indeterminate PET results, independent blinded review by 2 expert readers (C. M. and V. M.) was performed for 12 cases that were initially identified as indeterminate but subsequently adjudicated as either negative (6) or positive (6); this final determination was used as the result of the PET in our analyses. For our analyses, indeterminate Echo results were categorized as negative. All PET scans were performed at our institution, utilizing a cardiac-specific field of view. Patient preparation consisted of a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet the entire day prior to the scan and an AtkinsTM shake on the morning of the PET scan. Images were obtained using a Siemens Biograph mCT (Knoxville, TN). Inadequate adherence to the dietary preparation can be easily identified by the presence of significant myocardial FDG uptake [11]; if this significantly affected study interpretation, the study was repeated, and the results of the diagnostic PET study are reported. Image interpretation was performed by Level III nuclear cardiology readers. Of note, there are no specific diagnostic criteria for PVE by FDG-PET. In general, abnormal PET scans are characterized as having FDG uptake involving the prosthetic material with SUVmax greater than the background SUVmean of the left ventricular blood pool, with intense, focal/multifocal, or heterogeneous FDG uptake patterns supporting abnormal FDG uptake [7]. When FDG uptake was present, final interpretation of abnormal (positive) PET took into account patient history/prior imaging findings, type of prosthesis, intensity and distribution of FDG uptake, duration of time from cardiac surgery, and presence/absence of BioGlue according to surgical reports (rarely used at our institution) to make the final determination of negative or positive for PVE.

Echocardiography studies included transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) or TEE studies based on availability for each patient. To determine the diagnostic effect of PET, the 2023 Duke-ISCVID Criteria excluding PET (hereafter termed “2023 Duke-ISCVID [excluding PET] Criteria”) were initially applied to each patient to characterize patients into definite, possible, or rejected PVE. Patients were then categorized using the full 2023 Duke-ISCVID Criteria (including PET) (hereafter termed “2023 Duke-ISCVID Criteria”) [7]. Differences in the classification between these 2 criteria were assessed and summarized in Figure 1. For the final determination of PVE, the 2023 Duke-ISCVID guidelines were used [7]. Demographic, diagnostic, and outcome data points were obtained from the medical record through chart review, facilitated by the Electronic Medical Record Search Engine tool provided by our institution [12]. Patients were followed up to the final data acquisition date of 4 June 2024. Death data was supplemented using the Michigan Death Index and our institution's data tools. The University of Michigan Institutional Review Board approved the study with a waiver of patient consent due to the study's observational nature.

Univariate analyses were described using median values (interquartile ranges of 25%–75%) for continuous variables with nonparametric distribution and as a number with a corresponding percent for categorical variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ² tests with Pearson statistics for variables with expected values greater than 5. Otherwise, Fisher's exact test was used. A Kruskal–Wallis significance test was used for continuous variables. The positive predictive value of a PET for diagnosing endocarditis was calculated with confidence intervals per Mercaldo et al using MedCalc software [13, 14]. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to predict the odds of surgery within 3 months and antibiotic duration based on PET results, controlling for Echo results, microbiologic criteria (blood cultures and laboratory tests as described by the 2023 Duke-ISCVID Criteria), history of infectious endocarditis, age (continuous variable), and sex. A marginal effects analysis was also performed. Survival duration was measured from admission date until death or censoring on 4 June 2024. Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to evaluate the 5-year survival of each group based on index admission date, and comparisons between groups were made using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox hazard regression was performed for overall survival, adjusting for PET results, Echo results, microbiologic criteria, history of infectious endocarditis, age, sex, and surgical management. The proportionality assumption for this model was valid. Statistical analyses were performed using the STATA (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) software package.

RESULTS

During the study period, 140 consecutive patients underwent PET to evaluate suspected left-sided PVE. There were 93 patients with positive PET (+PET) scans. Of the 47 patients with negative scans, 32 had no cardiac FDG uptake, 8 had mild or nonspecific uptake, 1 had pacemaker uptake, 3 had aortic graft uptake, 2 had native valve uptake, and 1 had native aorta uptake. There were no differences in coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, or history of infectious endocarditis between groups. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed in all patients, while TEE was performed in 115 patients (82%). The reasons for not proceeding with TEE included medical contraindication to TEE (n = 20), patient refusal/TEE not within goals of care (n = 3), and abnormal prosthetic valve mass identified by TTE, and PET was the preferred test for distinguishing between infectious and noninfectious etiologies (n = 2). Past surgical history was also similar between groups (Table 1). Patients with a +PET had higher C-reactive protein levels (9.8 vs 3.1 mg/L, P = .001) and were more likely to meet major blood culture and imaging criteria based on the 2023 Duke-ISCVID (excluding PET) Criteria. A total of 89 (64%) patients had a negative echocardiographic evaluation (−Echo), of whom 42 (45%) patients had a +PET (Supplementary Table 1). Of the 42 patients with +Echo/+PET, the indications for PET were as follows: (1) evaluation of other prosthetic material, such as aortic grafts or cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) (n = 26), (2) confirmation that echocardiographic abnormalities were representative of infection (n = 8), (3) PET performed prior to TEE due to medical instability/transient contraindications for TEE (n = 6), and (4) initial echocardiographic evaluation negative but subsequently positive after PET (n = 2). Patients with +PET were more likely to receive a diagnosis of definite endocarditis than negative PET (−PET) (87% vs 15%) and less likely to have possible endocarditis (13% vs 45%, respectively) (Table 1). Positron emission tomography was performed at a median of 6 days after hospital admission (interquartile range [IQR], 5–10), which was similar for the −PET and +PET groups (Table 1). For patients treated with surgery (n = 40), a +PET scan had a positive predictive value of 92% (95% CI 86%–96%) for surgical/pathological findings consistent with endocarditis; definite endocarditis by the 2023 Duke-ISCVID (excluding PET) Criteria had a positive predictive value of 95% (95% CI 90–98%) (Supplementary Table 2). The addition of PET imaging to the 2023 Duke-ISCVID Criteria significantly increased the diagnosis of definite endocarditis (46%–63%, P < .001), with 24 (17%) patients being reclassified from possible to definite endocarditis (Figure 1).

All patients had an infectious disease consultant involved in their care. However, patients with +PET had higher rates of cardiac surgery consultation (82% vs 40%, P < .001). A total of 40 patients underwent surgical management. Surgery was performed at a median of 16 days from hospital admission (IQR, 10–25 days). Patients with +PET were more often treated with surgery than patients with −PET (40% vs 6.4%, P < .001; Table 1). When the analysis was restricted to patients with possible PVE by the 2023 Duke-ISCVID (excluding PET) Criteria, there were a small number of patients treated with surgical management, without significant differences between the +PET and −PET groups (1 [4.8%] vs 2 [6.9%], P = .75) (Supplementary Table 3). Only 12 (24%) patients with −Echo/+PET underwent surgical management compared to 25 (60%) patients with +Echo/+PET (Supplementary Table 1).

Patients with +PET were more likely to be treated with antibiotics for at least 6 weeks (85% vs −PET: 53%, P < .001) and more likely to be treated with long-term suppressive antibiotics (53% vs 21%, respectively, P < .001). For patients with possible PVE by the 2023 ISCVID (excluding PET) Criteria, there was a trend toward more frequent treatment with long-term suppressive antibiotics with +PET vs −PET, though not statistically significant (62% vs 38%, P = .09). A similar proportion of patients with −Echo/+PET and +Echo/+PET were treated with at least 6 weeks of antibiotics (42 [82%] vs 37 [88%], respectively) and long-term suppressive antibiotics (28 [55%] vs 21 [50%], respectively).

Based on a qualitative analysis of medical decision-making by the MET, the results from PET influenced patient management in the majority of cases (88 patients, 63%). Positron emission tomography results influenced the duration of antibiotics in 52 (37%) patients. Thirty-one patients were treated for a longer duration of antibiotics related to a +PET result; this included the decision to complete a full 6-week course for the treatment of PVE or to continue longer-term antibiotics for at least 3 months. A −PET result supported the decision for shorter courses of antibiotics in 15 patients, deemed not to need the full 6-week course. Twelve patients had a +PET result that influenced the decision to proceed with surgical management (Table 1; Supplementary Table 4).

When controlling for age, sex, and other 2023 Duke-ISCVID (excluding PET) Criteria, multivariate analysis revealed that a positive PET was negatively associated with shorter antibiotic courses (less than 6 weeks) (OR 0.27, P = .004) and positively associated with the ongoing use of antibiotics after 3 months (OR 3.93 P = .002) and surgical management (OR 5.99, P = .008). On marginal effects analysis, there was a marginal decrease in the probability of short-term antibiotics with the addition of a +Echo or +PET. There was a marginal increase in the probability of surgery within 3 months with the addition of a +Echo or +PET, especially when these studies were both positive (Table 2).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly higher survival probability in patients with +PET imaging after 5 years (52%; 95% CI 37%–65% vs 22%; CI 6.8%–42%, log-rank = 0.02) (Figure 2). We developed 2 multivariable Cox regression models for long-term survival—one additionally controlling for surgical management. Our model demonstrated that, when controlling for surgery, a +PET was not associated with improved long-term survival. Surgical management was associated with a lower risk of long-term mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 0.21, 95% CI 0.08–0.55), and a positive echocardiogram was associated with an increased risk of mortality (HR 2.00, 95% CI 1.05–3.81) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we provide a relatively large single-center experience using PET for evaluating patients with suspected PVE under the guidance of a MET. Our analysis suggests that PET results influenced clinical management decisions in the majority of cases. Patients with a +PET were more frequently treated with at least 6 weeks of antibiotics compared to those with −PET results (85% vs 53%, P < .001) and were more frequently treated with long-term suppressive antibiotics (at least 3 months) (53% vs 21%, P < .001).

An analysis of medical decision-making by the MET indicated that a +PET led to a recommendation for a longer course of antibiotics in 31 (33%) patients, and −PET supported a shorter duration of antibiotics in 15 (11%) patients. Although a higher proportion of patients with +PET underwent cardiac surgery, this was unlikely to be driven primarily by the results of PET. There was a significantly higher proportion of patients with +Echo/+PET treated with surgery than patients with −Echo/+PET (60% vs 24%). Furthermore, patients with possible endocarditis by the 2023 Duke-ISCVID (excluding PET) Criteria were unlikely to undergo surgery even with +PET results (2 of 29 [6.9%]). For the 12 patients in whom +PET influenced the decision for surgical management, 10 patients had evidence of PVE, and 9 patients had a significantly more burden of infection than identified on Echo; however, 2 patients had false-positive PET scan results without evidence of prosthetic valve infection at the time of surgery. For other patients with surgical indications established by clinical or echocardiographic findings, the PET scan's results helped clarify the extent of infection.

Positron emission tomography has recently been incorporated into the updated 2023 Duke-ISCVID guidelines, with abnormal FDG uptake representing a major criterion for the diagnosis of endocarditis [7]. Prior studies have demonstrated an improved sensitivity for the diagnosis of PVE, though this does come at the cost of reduced specificity [15, 16]. Since a re-do operation is a significant event for a patient, often involving high risk, there needs to be a high degree of clinical certainty when recommending surgical management of PVE. The decision to proceed with surgical management of PVE is complex, even in persons with definite PVE. Echocardiography plays a prominent role in identifying patients requiring urgent surgery due to significant valve dysfunction or paravalvular complications. However, surgical management of established PVE should be considered, even in those with indeterminate or −Echo [10]. Patients with a normal Echo and abnormal PET evaluation represent a clinical conundrum. In our cohort, the majority of these patients were treated with a full course of antibiotics of at least 6 weeks’ duration (82%), oftentimes with long-term suppressive antibiotics (55%), but rarely underwent surgical management (24%).

The findings of our study replicate prior studies that have demonstrated improved sensitivity and diagnostic certainty in patients being evaluated for suspected PVE [15]. While PET imaging should not replace Echo, it may be particularly helpful for cases in which the diagnosis or extent of infection is not clear. In our study, patients with a final diagnosis of definite endocarditis had a +Echo only 49% of the time (43 of 88) compared to 92% of patients with a +PET (81 of 88). Adding PET findings to the diagnostic criteria increased the proportion of patients with definite endocarditis from 46% to 63%. Our findings correlate with a multicenter study by Duval et al, which noted a modification in the classification of disease in 24.3% of patients with suspected PVE due to the incorporation of whole-body PET [17]. Prior studies have demonstrated a worse prognosis in patients with +PET results [18]. Our study showed no difference in adjusted overall survival according to PET results after controlling for patients undergoing surgical management, implying that source control is essential for the management of PVE. Differences in our findings compared to prior studies may also be due to differences in the clinical endpoint; San et al used a composite endpoint involving multiple events compared to our study reporting all-cause mortality alone [18]. Prior studies evaluating the effect of PET on patient management for suspected PVE are limited. In the study by Duval et al evaluating whole-body PET for patients with suspected endocarditis, only half of the patients had prosthetic valves (n = 70), and it was not reported whether prosthetic valves were left sided or right sided, which could affect surgical decision-making [17]. Our study expands upon the prior data utilizing a larger cohort of patients with suspected left-sided PVE. Prior studies have not discussed details of patient management, so it is possible that the relatively high rates of suppressive antibiotics used in our +PET group helped mitigate differences in survival previously seen. While patients with abnormal PET imaging may be sicker, they may also be treated more aggressively based on multidisciplinary discussion.

This study has several notable limitations. Nonstandardized interpretation of PET by nonblinded readers is a potential limitation. Distinguishing infection from sterile inflammation by FDG uptake patterns is a particular diagnostic challenge. There is an absence of formal guidelines that provide specific criteria for diagnosing PVE in patients with FDG uptake by PET. The 2023 Duke-ISCVID Criteria provide general guidance for abnormal uptake patterns [7] but further guidance is limited to expert opinion [19, 20]. Notably, prior studies evaluating the use of PET for evaluating patients with suspected PVE have used different diagnostic criteria for abnormal FDG uptake, reflecting the complexity of PET interpretation in this context [16–18]. Since our study was not specifically evaluating the diagnostic performance of PET, we relied on PET interpretation by expert nuclear cardiology readers to determine the effect on patient management. Differences in diagnostic interpretation may exist between our institution and others. Our study represents consecutive patients undergoing PET for evaluation of suspected left-sided PVE. The need for PET was determined by treating clinicians; therefore, there is the potential for selection bias in this study; however, patients included in this study represent a real-world use of PET in the evaluation of suspected PVE. The retrospective nature of this study is a limitation, especially regarding the qualitative analysis of medical decision-making according to PET, which was not always clearly defined by chart review. Different readers determined the result for each PET and echocardiogram and can thus have variability in interpretation. The inclusion of patients with indeterminate Echo results into the −Echo group does create some challenges in interpretation; the rationale for grouping these patients is based on the anticipated management of patients with indeterminate results being more similar to those with negative results; for example, an indeterminate result would be unlikely to result in supporting the need for surgery or an increased duration of antibiotics. Finally, the Cox regression model accounting for surgical management is subject to immortal time bias, since patients undergoing surgery must have survived to the date of their operation, which could have been up to 3 months from hospital admission. This may contribute to inflated benefits of surgical management in this model; however, surgery was performed at a median of 16 days (IQR 10–25 days), so the effect of immortal time bias on long-term outcomes is likely limited.

CONCLUSION

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (chemical name 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluro-D-glucose) positron emission tomography is a valuable diagnostic tool in patients with suspected prosthetic valve endocarditis. It can allow for a more definitive diagnosis of a condition that is notoriously challenging to diagnose appropriately. There is a paucity of data regarding how incorporating PET into routine diagnosis affects patient management. In this study, under the guidance of a MET, results from PET often aided in a more definitive diagnosis of PVE and, in turn, influenced management in the majority of patients, most commonly influencing the duration of antibiotic treatment. Further investigation is necessary to understand how PET may identify patients with suspected PVE who may be better served with surgical management.
