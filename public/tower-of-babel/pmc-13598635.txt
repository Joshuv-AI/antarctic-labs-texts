Response to Methodological Concerns Regarding the Correlation Analysis and Clinical Interpretation of Intracellular Mycophenolic Acid Measurement in Kidney Transplant Recipients [Response to Letter]
Authors: Na Yang, Yuqing Zhao, Shiyu Liu, Yifan Zhou, Xinyue Ji, Tianqi Zhang, Runbin Sun, Huaijun Zhu, Yao Du, Min Wang
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We thank Guo et al for their interest in our article and for recognizing the analytical contribution of our LC-MS/MS method for intracellular mycophenolic acid (MPA) and mycophenolic acid glucuronide (MPAG). Their correspondence raises important points concerning the statistical treatment of repeated observations, the interpretation of partial exposure, clinical translation, and assay comparability. We welcome the opportunity to clarify the intended scope of our work.

Before addressing each point individually, we note that several of the concerns raised, regarding gender-based assessment using AUC0-t, the absence of clinical outcome validation, and the use of different analytical platforms, were explicitly acknowledged as limitations in the Discussion and Conclusion sections of our original manuscript. We appreciate the opportunity to reiterate and further elaborate on these points, and we have taken this correspondence as an occasion to perform an additional mixed-effects analysis that strengthens the statistical basis of our interpretation. We would respectfully suggest that a full consideration of the manuscript, particularly the Discussion and Conclusion sections, would have clarified that these limitations were already recognized and discussed in detail. Several of the concerns raised appear to restate points that we had already explicitly identified, rather than raising new issues.

Below, we provide a point-by-point response to the concerns raised.

Plasma–PBMC Correlation Analysis

We thank Guo et al for raising this important statistical consideration. We acknowledge that the pooled Spearman correlation analysis in Figure 6, which included 139 samples from 40 recipients, does not explicitly account for within-subject dependence arising from repeated measurements. This initial analysis was intended as an exploratory, descriptive assessment of rank association rather than a definitive inferential estimate of a within-person effect, and we acknowledge that the conventional p values should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, we note that Spearman rank correlation has been previously utilized in MPA pharmacokinetic studies for exploratory concentration-concentration comparisons.1

To address this concern directly, we performed an additional analysis using a Gamma generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with patient-specific random intercepts. A log-link function was specified because MPA concentrations exhibited substantial right skewness and heteroscedasticity, which violated the assumptions of a conventional Gaussian linear mixed model. The re-analysis confirmed a statistically significant positive association between plasma and intracellular MPA concentrations. However, plasma MPA concentrations explained only a limited proportion of the variability in intracellular MPA exposure, with marginal R2 values of 0.28 for MMF and 0.25 for EC-MPS. These findings are consistent with our original interpretation that plasma MPA concentrations alone have limited predictive ability for intracellular exposure.

Even setting aside the mixed-model re-analysis, the dependence structure primarily affects the uncertainty attached to the pooled estimate and prevents a clean separation of within-subject from between-subject association; it does not by itself erase the observed ordering and dispersion of the paired concentrations. Accordingly, our principal interpretation was based on the modest magnitude of the pooled correlation coefficients (Rs = 0.396 for EC-MPS and Rs = 0.480 for MMF) together with the pronounced scatter in Figure 6, rather than on statistical significance alone.

We agree that future confirmatory studies should explicitly separate within-recipient and between-recipient components using approaches such as repeated-measures correlation or within-between modeling.

Sparse Sampling and Interpretation of AUC0-t

We thank Guo et al for this important observation. We agree that MMF may exhibit a secondary plasma MPA peak at approximately 6 - 8 h after dosing, which is generally attributed to enterohepatic recirculation. This phenomenon was explicitly acknowledged in our Introduction: “a secondary concentration peak emerges at approximately 6 - 8 h post-dose in some patients, which is attributed to enterohepatic recirculation”. Accordingly, we recognize that the later enterohepatic recirculation phase is not captured by the sampling interval used in our study.

However, we believe that the comment may benefit from clarification regarding the purpose for which AUC0-t was used in our study. AUC0-t was not intended to estimate total systemic MPA exposure. Rather, it was used as a predefined, model-independent measure of exposure within the observed sampling interval for an exploratory assessment of gender-related pharmacokinetic differences. We did not use AUC0-t for direct comparison between MMF and EC-MPS, because the two treatment groups were assessed using different clinical sampling schedules. Thus, the incompleteness of the sampling interval does not invalidate the intended use or interpretation of this endpoint within the defined study context.

It is important to note that the limited sampling strategies (LSS) are widely used for plasma MPA therapeutic drug monitoring. The selection of the time points for this limited sampling strategy is based on previous experimental data through correlation analysis with full PK profiling (AUC0-12h for MMF).2 This LSS provides an effective and cost-efficient approach for estimation of the full MPA AUC in renal transplant patients.

PBMC specimens were collected in parallel with the corresponding clinical plasma samples at the same time points. Although validated LSSs are available for estimating plasma MPA exposure, to our knowledge, validated LSS approaches for estimating intracellular MPA and MPAG exposure have not yet been established. We therefore used the non-compartmental linear trapezoidal method to obtain a model-independent estimate of exposure over the predefined sampling interval. This approach was selected to provide a straightforward and transparent exploratory assessment of early intracellular exposure and concentration - time characteristics without making assumptions required by a pharmacokinetic model or an unvalidated intracellular LSS model. These considerations were already addressed in the original manuscript, as clearly stated in the Discussion.

Clinical Interpretation and Exposure–Response Evidence

We thank Guo et al for this comment. We would like to clarify that the primary objective of our study was to develop and validate a sensitive LC-MS/MS analytical platform for the simultaneous quantification of intracellular MPA and MPAG, and to provide an initial pharmacokinetic characterization of these intracellular analytes in kidney transplant recipients. The phrase “may facilitate the optimization of individualized immunosuppressive dosing regimens” was therefore intended to indicate a potential future application of this analytical capability, rather than an established clinical benefit demonstrated in the present study.

We acknowledge that prospective exposure - response studies are necessary to establish clinically meaningful intracellular MPA concentration thresholds, and that the absence of such data is a limitation. This was explicitly acknowledged in the Discussion and Conclusion section of our original manuscript, where we noted that the optimal intracellular MPA concentration threshold balancing immunosuppressive efficacy and toxicity remains undefined, and that prospective clinical trials are required to further characterize the associations between intracellular MPA concentrations and clinical efficacy and toxicity outcomes.

However, the absence of an established exposure-response relationship does not diminish the importance of characterizing intracellular MPA exposure. Since MPA exerts its immunosuppressive effects primarily within lymphocytes, intracellular drug concentrations may better reflect pharmacologically relevant target-site exposure than plasma concentrations alone. In this regard, we would respectfully note that the study by Chen et al1 cited in the correspondence actually aligns with our own observations rather than contradicting them. The poor plasma-intracellular correlation reported by Chen et al is consistent with our findings, and this concordance supports, rather than undermines, the rationale for investigating intracellular exposure. Chen et al also noted that intracellular MPA monitoring may serve as a potential biomarker for infection monitoring in kidney transplant recipients. Indeed, this well-documented discordance between plasma and intracellular MPA concentrations was precisely the rationale that motivated the present study, but the point may not have been fully appreciated in the correspondence. If plasma concentrations reliably predicted intracellular drug levels, direct measurement of intracellular MPA would be less necessary.

We agree that clinical implementation of intracellular MPA monitoring requires further validation through prospective studies incorporating efficacy and toxicity outcomes, and we have clarified the intended scope of our conclusions accordingly.

Plasma Immunoassay versus PBMC LC-MS/MS

We thank Guo et al for raising this methodological consideration. We would respectfully observe, however, that the use of different analytical platforms for plasma and PBMC samples was not only acknowledged but explicitly discussed in the Discussion section of our original manuscript. Reiterating a limitation that we have already identified and discussed does not raise a new concern, though we are happy to elaborate further below.

The choice of platforms was driven by both clinical and analytical constraints. Plasma MPA concentrations were measured using a commercially available immunoassay (Siemens Syva Emit® 2000 on a Viva-E automated analyzer), which is widely used in routine clinical TDM because it provides rapid, standardized results for clinical decision-making. In contrast, intracellular MPA and MPAG quantification requires a highly sensitive analytical method, and LC-MS/MS remains the reference approach for intracellular drug measurement. Re-analysis of all plasma samples by LC-MS/MS was not feasible because the clinical specimens were limited residual samples obtained from kidney transplant recipients. Given that both methods have undergone method validation and reliably capture concentration-time trends, they remain suitable for correlation-based analyses of plasma and intracellular exposure.

In summary, we agree that the clustered sampling design warrants caution in interpreting conventional p values, and we have addressed this by performing an additional Gamma generalized linear mixed-effects analysis that confirms our original interpretation. We also acknowledge the limitation of sparse sampling, recognize that clinical outcomes remain to be established, and agree that assay-specific bias should be considered. We respectfully note, however, that these latter three points were already explicitly acknowledged as limitations in the Discussion and Conclusion of our original manuscript. With the clarifications above and the additional mixed-effects analysis, the study remains a method-development and cellular-PK investigation that provides a validated tool for prospective individualized MPA dosing studies.
