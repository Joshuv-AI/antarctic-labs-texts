A Real-Space Formulation of the Zak Phase via Weyl m m -Functions
Habib Ammari, Clemens Thalhammer

Abstract
. We establish a new, real-space formula for the Zak phase for one dimensional periodic Jacobi operators in terms of the Weyl m + m_{+} -function that does not rely on Floquet-Bloch theory. This novel representation highlights the dependence of the Zak phase on boundary terms. Moreover, we show how to recover the classical quantisation of the Zak phase for periodic Jacobi operators with inversion symmetric fundamental cells.



Keywords.
Jacobi operators, Zak phase, Berry phase, Weyl
m
-functions, Bulk-Boundary Correspondence

AMS Subject classifications.
47B36, 47A10, 81Q10

1.
Introduction

Since the foundational work of Zak
[
20
]
, the Zak phase has emerged as a central concept in the study of topological properties of one-dimensional quantum systems. It captures the geometric phase accumulated by a Bloch wave function across the Brillouin zone and provides a direct link between the bulk properties of a material and its edge phenomena, a principle often referred to as the bulk-boundary correspondence. While the Zak phase can, in general, take any value in
[0,2\pi)
, it becomes quantised in systems with certain symmetries, such as inversion or chiral symmetry
[
6
,
20
]
, leading to robust predictions for edge states in topological insulators.

Beyond its role in topological physics, the Zak phase also naturally connects to the spectral theory of discrete operators. In particular, Weyl
m
-functions arise in the analysis of Jacobi operators and encode detailed spectral information, including the density of states and the location of spectral gaps
[
17
,
7
]
. This spectral perspective provides a bridge between the abstract mathematical framework and physically observable quantities such as edge modes and transport properties.

In this work, we exploit this connection to provide a novel formula for the Zak phase of discrete tight-binding operators in terms of Weyl
m
-functions, continuing the trend
[
3
,
2
,
10
]
of rephrasing results from mathematical physics in the language of dynamical systems. This approach not only offers a new computational tool but also sheds light on the dependence of the Zak phase on edge conditions and boundary choices, a feature that is often implicit in traditional formulations.

The paper is organised as follows. In section 2, we introduce the necessary mathematical framework, including the relevant concepts from the spectral theory of Jacobi operators and a brief overview of Weyl
m
-functions. Building on this foundation, section 3 states the main result and provides a brief discussion of its implications, with particular emphasis on the dependence of the Zak phase on edge conditions. In section 4, we demonstrate the consistency of our formulation with the classical Zak phase and illustrate how it naturally leads to the quantisation of the Zak phase for periodic structures whose fundamental cells possess inversion symmetry. Finally, section 5 is devoted to a rigorous proof of the main result.

2.
Preliminaries

The main object of interest in this paper is the Jacobi operators
\mathcal{J}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by

\mathcal{J}\phi(n)={\bm{a}^{(n-1)}}\phi(n-1)+{\bm{b}^{(n)}}\phi(n)+{\bm{a}^{(n)}}\phi(n+1),

where
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
and
{\bm{a}^{(n)}}>0,{\bm{b}^{(n)}}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

For a thorough introduction, we refer the reader to
[
7
,
17
]
. We may also define the semi-infinite Jacobi operators
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
as

(2.1)

\mathcal{J}_{+}\phi(n)=\begin{cases}&{\bm{b}^{(1)}}\phi(1)+{\bm{a}^{(1)}}\phi(2),\quad n=1,\\
&{\bm{a}^{(n-1)}}\phi(n-1)+{\bm{b}^{(n)}}\phi(n)+{\bm{a}^{(n)}}\phi(n+1),\quad n\geq 2,\end{cases}

and

(2.2)

\mathcal{J}_{-}\phi(n)=\begin{cases}&{\bm{b}^{(0)}}\phi(0)+{\bm{a}^{(-1)}}\phi(-1),\quad n=0,\\
&{\bm{a}^{(n-1)}}\phi(n-1)+{\bm{b}^{(n)}}\phi(n)+{\bm{a}^{(n)}}\phi(n+1),\quad n\leq-1.\end{cases}

Note that
\mathcal{J}_{+}
is defined by (
2.1
) for
n\in\mathbb{N}
with the convention that
\phi(0)=0
.

Suppose that
\lambda\in\rho(\mathcal{J})
, where
\rho(\mathcal{J})
denotes the resolvent set of
\mathcal{J}
. Then there exist two formal solutions
u_{\pm}(\lambda,\cdot)
to

\mathcal{J}u_{\pm}=\lambda u_{\pm},

that are square-summable respectively at
\pm\infty
. The function
u_{+}
is often referred to as the stable direction, whereas
u_{-}
is the unstable direction. Then we may define the Weyl
m
-functions
m_{\pm}
for
\lambda\in\rho(\mathcal{J})
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
as

(2.3)

m_{+}(\lambda,n_{0})=-\frac{u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0}+1)}{{\bm{a}^{(n_{0})}}u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0})}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{-}(\lambda,n_{0})=-\frac{u_{-}(\lambda,n_{0})}{{\bm{a}^{(n_{0})}}u_{-}(\lambda,n_{0}+1)}.

Let
e_{1}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
be defined by
e_{1}(n)=1
if
n=1
and
e_{1}(n)=0
otherwise. Let

\mathcal{J}_{\pm,n_{0}}=((S^{*})^{n_{0}}\mathcal{J}S^{n_{0}})_{\pm}

with
S
being the shift operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
given by
S\phi(n)=\phi(n+1)
and let
S^{*}
be the
\ell^{2}
-adjoint of
S
.

The Weyl
m
-functions may alternatively be equivalently defined by

(2.4)

m_{+}(\lambda,n_{0})=\langle(\mathcal{J}_{+,n_{0}}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad m_{-}(z,n_{0})=\langle(\mathcal{J}_{-,n_{0}}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle

with the convention that the scalar product is sesquilinear in its second variable, or by

(2.5)

m_{+}(\lambda,n_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-\lambda}d\mu_{+}(n_{0},z)\quad\text{and}\quad m_{-}(z,n_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-\lambda}d\mu_{-}(n_{0},z)

with
d\mu_{\pm}(n_{0},\cdot)
being the spectral measure associated with
\mathcal{J_{\pm}}
and
n_{0}
. That is,
m_{\pm}
is the Borel transform of the measure
\mu_{\pm}(n_{0},\cdot)
. Consequently,
m_{\pm}
is also a Herglotz function
[
7
,
17
]
. Hence, the limit

(2.6)

N_{\pm}(\lambda,n_{0})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow+0}m_{\pm}(\lambda+i\varepsilon,n_{0})

exist almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}
and
d\mu_{\pm,n_{0},acc}(z)=N_{\pm}(z,n_{0})d\mu
.
Weyl
m
-functions also satisfy the following property:

(2.7)

m_{+}(z,i)=\frac{1}{{\bm{b}^{(i)}}-z-{\bm{a}^{(i+1)}}^{2}m_{+}(z,i+1)}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{-}(z,i)=\frac{1}{{\bm{b}^{(i)}}-z-{\bm{a}^{(i-1)}}^{2}m_{-}(z,i-1)},

which we will use later on. In what follows, we will often omit the index of
m_{\pm}
and instead define
m_{\pm}(\lambda)=m_{\pm}(\lambda,0).

Particular attention will be devoted to Jacobi operators with periodic coefficients. We therefore briefly devote a moment to settle the notation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}\in\{\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z}\}
and let
\mathcal{J}_{(+)}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{X})
be a Jacobi operator with diagonal and off-diagonal elements
{\bm{b}^{(i)}}
and
{\bm{a}^{(i)}}
. We say that
\mathcal{J}
is
p
-periodic if there exists some
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.8)

{\bm{a}^{(i+p)}}={\bm{a}^{(i)}}\quad{\bm{b}^{(i+p)}}={\bm{b}^{(i)}},\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{X.}

The Weyl
m
-functions of periodic Jacobi operators are particularly well behaved. Due to the property (
2.7
), they can be represented as solutions to a quadratic equation of the form

(2.9)

a(\lambda)m_{\pm}(\lambda)^{2}+b(\lambda)m_{\pm}(\lambda)+c(\lambda)=0.

By the implicit function theorem,
m_{\pm}
is thus locally analytic, and its poles are a subset of the roots of the coefficient
a(z)
. Therefore, the limit

(2.10)

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}m_{\pm}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)

exists for almost every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the above implies that the spectral density is also locally analytic.

Two remarks are now in order.

Remark 2.2
.

Alternatively, equation (
2.9
) can also be easily derived using the transfer matrix of the system; see, e.g.,
[
7
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

The zeros of the leading coefficient in equation (
2.9
) actually yield the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the half-line operator. For periodic operators, it is known that these eigenvalues are either within gaps or at the boundary of the band gaps, which is why the absolutely continuous part of the spectral density is analytic in the interior of bands and has at most
(p-1)
roots.

The
p
-periodic Jacobi operators are special in the sense that they can be understood as Laurent or Toeplitz operators, for which there is a vast amount of literature available; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
18
,
1
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be a
p
-periodic Jacobi operator. Then there exists some continuous function
f
from
\mathbb{C}
into
\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
, called the symbol of the operators
T(f)
or
L(f)
that are respectively the associated Toeplitz and Laurent operators to
\mathcal{J}
, such that
\mathcal{J}=L(f)
if
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathcal{J}_{+}=T(f)
if
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{N}
.

In particular, it is not difficult to see that the symbol
f
is equal to the Floquet-Bloch transform of the operator
L(f)
. In the physics literature, the symbol
f
is thus often referred to as a Bloch Hamiltonian. The formal solutions to
(\mathcal{J}-\lambda)u=0
can be readily constructed knowing that
(k,v)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
satisfy

(2.11)

(f(k)-\lambda)v=0.

Then we can verify that

(2.12)

u=(\dots,e^{-ik}v,v,e^{ik}v,\dots)^{\top}

is a formal solution of
(\mathcal{J}-\lambda)u=0
. Here, the superscript
\top
denotes the transpose.

Assuming the operator
\mathcal{J}
to be self adjoint and
p
-periodic, it is a standard result that there exist
\lambda_{1}^{-}\leq\lambda_{1}^{+}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{p}^{-}\leq\lambda_{p}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{J})
of
\mathcal{J}
is given by

\sigma(\mathcal{J})=\bigcup_{i}\,(\lambda_{i}^{-},\lambda_{i}^{+}).

Fix some
1\leq n\leq p
, we say that the
n_{0}
th
band is isolated if

(2.13)

\lambda_{n-1}^{+}<\lambda_{n}^{-}<\lambda_{n}^{+}<\lambda_{n+1}^{-}.

From standard perturbation theory, it then follows that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}(k)
in that band is an analytic function of the Bloch momentum
k
and so are the eigenfunctions
u_{n}(k)

[
12
,
13
]
. Thus, we may define the Zak phase
[
19
,
15
,
20
]
as

(2.14)

\gamma_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{A}_{n}(k)dk,

where

(2.15)

\mathcal{A}_{n}(k)=-i\langle u_{n}(k),\partial_{k}u_{n}(k)\rangle

is the Berry connection. It is well known that the Zak phase is quantised under certain symmetry assumptions such as inversion symmetry.

3.
Main Result

The main result of this paper is the following real space formulation of the
Zak phase.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{J_{+}}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
be a self-adjoint periodic Jacobi operator. Assume that the
n
th
band is isolated.
If we define the Berry connection as

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}_{n}(k)=-i\langle u_{n}(k),\partial_{k}u_{n}(k)\rangle,

then it holds that

(3.2)

\mathcal{A}_{n}(k)=\frac{\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(k)}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda+i0))}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda+i0))}-\frac{1}{2}.

In particular, if we denote by
\lambda_{n,\min},\lambda_{n,\max}
the lower and upper band edges, then the following formula for the Zak phase holds:

(3.3)

\gamma_{n}=\int_{\lambda_{n,\min}}^{\lambda_{n,\max}}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda_{n}(k)+i0)}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda_{n}(k)+i0)}d\lambda-\pi.

We will postpone the proof of Theorem
3.1
to section
5
in favour of a brief discussion.

Equation (
3.3
) provides many insights into the nature of the Zak phase, first and foremost it is worth emphasising that equation (
3.3
) is entirely formulated in real space, with the only remaining artifact of the translation invariance being the existence of spectral bands. Though such real space representations already exist in the form of trace formulas
[
14
,
11
,
15
]
, the link to Weyl
m
-functions is new to the best of our knowledge. This is noteworthy, as the Weyl
m
-functions are a spectral quantity, whereas usually the Zak phase is thought to be expressible in terms of phases.

Moreover, its appearance is consistent with the fact that the Zak phase is not a bulk property
[
9
,
8
,
4
,
16
,
15
]
. Instead, its value depends on the choice of the fundamental cell in the definition of
m_{\pm}
.

The Weyl
m
-functions are also related to surface impedance. Surface impedance is commonly defined as the ratio of the field to the flux at some energy (or frequency)
\lambda
. Inside a bandgap, this takes the following form:

(3.4)

Z_{R}(\lambda)=\frac{u_{+}(0,\lambda)}{{\bm{a}^{(0)}}(u_{+}(0,\lambda)-u_{+}(1,\lambda))}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{L}(\lambda)=\frac{-u_{-}(0,\lambda)}{{\bm{a}^{(0)}}(u_{-}(0,\lambda)-u_{-}(1,\lambda))}.

We can rewrite the above in terms of Weyl
m
-functions to obtain

(3.5)

Z_{R}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{{\bm{a}^{(0)}}+{\bm{a}^{(0)}}^{2}m_{+}(\lambda)}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{L}(\lambda)=\frac{{\bm{a}^{(0)}}m_{-}(\lambda)}{{\bm{a}^{(0)}}+{\bm{a}^{(0)}}^{2}m_{-}(\lambda)}.

Since the Weyl
m
-functions are real valued in the bandgaps, only the real parts of the Weyl
m
-functions remain in the above formula, providing a link to the Zak phase through equation (
3.3
).

4.
Three Examples

4.1.
Su–Schrieffer–Heeger Model

The Bloch Hamiltonian of the Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model takes the form

(4.1)

\displaystyle f(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}e^{-ik}+\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{1}\\
t_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
t_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}e^{ik}.

A straightforward diagonalisation shows that the eigenvalues are

(4.2)

\lambda(k)=\pm\lvert t_{1}+t_{2}e^{-ik}\rvert,

and we may choose the corresponding normalised eigenvectors as

(4.3)

u_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-e^{i\phi_{k}}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

where the phase
\phi_{k}
is defined through the complex argument of

t_{1}+t_{2}e^{-ik}
. Differentiation then gives the Berry connection,

(4.4)

\mathcal{A}(k)=-i\langle u(k),\,\partial_{k}u(k)\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{k}\phi(k),

which when integrated over the Brillouin zone leads to the well–known expression
for the Zak phase,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\phi(0)-\phi(2\pi)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\lvert t_{1}\rvert<\lvert t_{2}\rvert,\\
\pi,&\lvert t_{1}\rvert>\lvert t_{2}\rvert.\end{cases}

This yields the familiar distinction between the topologically
trivial and non-trivial phases of the SSH chain.

To compute the Weyl
m
-functions, we first determine the stable direction associated with

\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)
. For this, it suffices to identify a kernel element of

f(k+i\varepsilon)-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)
, since the decaying direction can then be constructed
straightforwardly as in section
2
. A direct computation shows that the Weyl
m_{+}
-function is given by

(4.6)

m_{+}\bigl(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)\bigr)=-\frac{(t_{1}+t_{2}e^{-ik+\varepsilon})\,e^{ik-\varepsilon}}{t_{2}\,\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)}.

Taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain

(4.7)

m_{+}\bigl(\lambda(k)\bigr)=-\frac{e^{i(\phi(\lambda)+k)}}{t_{2}}.

Differentiating this expression with respect to
k
yields

(4.8)

\frac{\Re\bigl(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda(k))\bigr)}{\Im\bigl(m_{+}(\lambda(k))\bigr)}=\phi^{\prime}(\lambda(k))\lambda^{\prime}(k)+1.

Hence, our alternative expression for the Zak phase becomes

(4.9)

\widetilde{\gamma}=\phi(\lambda(\pi))-\phi(\lambda(0)).

Thus, this formulation is fully consistent with the standard approach and reproduces the
usual Zak phase of the SSH model.

4.2.
Rice-Mele Model

The Rice–Mele model is a one-dimensional tight-binding model that extends the
SSH model by including a staggered on-site potential.
Its Hamiltonian reads

(4.10)

\displaystyle H(k)=\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}e^{-ik}+\begin{pmatrix}\Delta&t_{1}\\
t_{1}&-\Delta\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
t_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}e^{ik},

where
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
denote the intracell and intercell hopping
amplitudes, respectively, and
\pm\Delta
is the staggered on-site
potential. In contrast to the SSH model, the presence of a nonzero

\Delta
breaks both the inversion and chiral symmetry. Let
k\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
and suppose that
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}
solves

(4.11)

H(k)\mathbf{v}=\lambda(k)\mathbf{v}.

Some algebra then readily yields

(4.12)

\frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}}=\frac{\Delta+\lambda}{(t_{1}+t_{2}e^{ik})}.

Thus, we obtain an explicit expression for the Weyl-
m_{+}
function

(4.13)

m_{+}(\lambda(k))=-\frac{\Delta+\lambda}{(t_{1}+t_{2}e^{ik})}\frac{e^{ik}}{t_{2}}.

Thanks to this explicit form, it becomes feasible to accurately evaluate equation (
3.3
) numerically. In Figure
1
, we see excellent agreement between the numerical implementation of the new formula and a discrete implementation of the Zak phase as done, e.g., in
[
19
]
.

3.3
) and a standard implementation for various parameters. The grey lines mark where the Berry phase becomes degenerate due to equal or vanishing hopping terms.

4.3.
Mirror Symmetric Fundamental Cells

It has long been known that inversion symmetric fundamental cells in one-dimensional media lead to a quantisation of the Zak phase
[
20
]
. This result is usually derived through parity arguments for Wannier functions
[
20
]
or Bloch eigenfunctions
[
6
]
. In this subsection, we show, using only real-space symmetry arguments, how quantisation can be recovered based on Theorem
3.1
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{J_{+}}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
be a
p
-periodic Jacobi operator. We say that
\mathcal{J_{+}}
has an inversion/mirror symmetric fundamental cell if the following holds:

(4.14)

{\bm{b}^{(i)}}={\bm{b}^{(p-i+1)}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{a}^{(i)}}={\bm{a}^{(p-i)}}\quad\text{ for }i=1,\dots,p.

As our goal is to recover the quantisation of the Zak phase using only equation (
3.3
), we have to understand how symmetry affects the Weyl
m
-functions. This is the purpose of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{J_{+}}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
be a
p
-periodic Jacobi operator with an inversion symmetric fundamental cell. Suppose that the
n
th
band is isolated. Then
\lvert m_{+}(\lambda+i0)\rvert=\lvert{\bm{a}^{(0)}}\rvert^{-1}
is constant along that band.

The finding of this lemma is highlighted in Figure
2
, where, among other things, we see that the absolute value of the Weyl
m
-function is indeed constant for a symmetric trimer setup. In the proof of the above lemma, we will make use of yet another property of periodic Jacobi operators: reflectionless-ness. For our purposes, the following definition of reflectionless is sufficient, though stronger characterisations exist
[
17
]
.

Definition 4.3
.

A Jacobi operator
H
is called reflectionless if, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda\in\sigma(H)
, the following holds:

(4.15)

u_{+}(\lambda+i0,n_{0})=\overline{u_{-}(\lambda+i0,n_{0})}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\lambda=0
. Let
\mathcal{J}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the associated periodic Jacobi operator, where the fundamental cells are arranged so that a fundamental cell begins at
1
. Define the operator

W:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}),e_{n}\rightarrow e_{-n+1},

where
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the standard basis. If we denote by
u_{\pm}
the solution that is in
\ell^{2}
as
\pm n\rightarrow\infty
, then we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}Wu_{+})_{n}

\displaystyle={\bm{a}^{(n-1)}}u_{+}(-n+2)+{\bm{b}^{(n)}}u_{+}(-n+1)+{\bm{a}^{(n)}}u_{+}(-n)

\displaystyle={\bm{a}^{(-n+1)}}u_{+}(-n+2)+{\bm{b}^{(-n+1)}}u_{+}(-n+1)+{\bm{a}^{(-n)}}u_{+}(-n)

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore,
Wu
is also a solution of
\mathcal{J}Wu=0
and is in
\ell^{2}
as
n\rightarrow-\infty
. This implies that
Wu_{+}
must be a multiple of
u_{-}
, that is,
u_{+}(\lambda,n)=u_{-}(\lambda,-n+1)
. Because
\mathcal{J}
is also reflectionless, we have
u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0})=\overline{u_{-}(\lambda,n_{0})}
. Therefore, we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle m(\lambda,n_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0}+1)}{{\bm{a}^{(n_{0})}}u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0})}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0}+1)}{{\bm{a}^{(n_{0})}}\overline{u_{-}(\lambda,n_{0})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0}+1)}{{\bm{a}^{(n_{0})}}\overline{u_{+}(\lambda,n_{0}+1)}},

from which the claim follows.
∎

We write the Weyl
m
-function in polar form as

(4.18)

m_{+}(\lambda)=r(\lambda)e^{i\phi(\lambda)}.

Substituting this representation into the integrand of our new formula
(
3.3
), we obtain the following relation:

(4.19)

\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda))}{|Im(m_{+}(\lambda))}=\frac{r^{\prime}(\lambda)}{r(\lambda)}\cot\phi(\lambda)-\phi^{\prime}(\lambda).

For
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{J})
, the boundary value

r^{\prime}(\lambda+i0)
vanishes according to Lemma
4.2
. As a consequence,
the first term does not contribute to the integral, and the expression
for the Zak phase simplifies. We are left with

(4.20)

\gamma=-\int_{\lambda_{\min}}^{\lambda_{\max}}\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\phi(\lambda_{\min})-\phi(\lambda_{\max}).

Finally, since the Weyl
m
-function is real-valued in the spectral gaps,
the phases at the band edges satisfy

\phi(\lambda_{\min}),\ \phi(\lambda_{\max})\in\{0,\pi\}.

It follows immediately that the Zak phase can only take values in integer
multiples of
\pi
, and is therefore quantised.

5.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

The main idea of the proof is that we can use analyticity of the eigenvectors to extend the Berry connection into the complex plane. Once this is done, the proof consists of three main steps: First, we express the Berry connection in terms of the resolvent of
\mathcal{J}_{+}
. Second, the resolvent is expressed using spectral integrals to rewrite the expression in terms of
m_{+}
. Lastly, the limit as one approaches the real axis is taken.
Thus, we begin by defining

\mathcal{A}_{n}(k+i\varepsilon)=-i\langle u_{n}(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u_{n}(k+i\varepsilon)\rangle.

As
\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)\notin\sigma(\mathcal{J)}
, there exists a solution
u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
to
\mathcal{J}u=\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)
that is square-summable as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. From section
2
, we know that, up to phase and normalisation,
u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)
is determined by

(5.1)

u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)=(J_{+}-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))^{-1}e_{1}.

Crucially, the first
k
entries of
u_{+}
when defined as in (
5.1
) form a solution of

(5.2)

f(k+i\varepsilon)u(k+i\varepsilon)=\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)u(k+i\varepsilon),

where
f
is the symbol associated with
\mathcal{J}
. By understanding
u_{+}
as a function of
\lambda
, (
5.1
) enforces a periodic phase, after normalising we can use
u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)
defined by equation (
5.1
) to compute the Berry connection. By viewing
\mathcal{J}_{+}
as a Toeplitz operator, it can be shown that
u_{+}
takes the form

(5.3)

u_{+}=c\left(u(k+i\varepsilon),e^{ik-\varepsilon}u(k+i\varepsilon),e^{2ik-2\varepsilon}u(k+i\varepsilon),\dots\right)^{\top},

where
u(k+i\varepsilon)
is a normalised solution of (
5.2
) whose phase is in agreement with that of
u_{+}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a normalisation constant. We have

(5.4)

\lVert u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rVert^{2}=c^{2}\frac{1}{1-e^{2\varepsilon}},

which yields

c=\lVert u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rVert\sqrt{1-e^{-2\varepsilon}}.

Furthermore, we want to express
\partial_{k}u
through
\partial_{k}u_{+}
. To this end, note, on the one hand, that
\partial_{k}u_{+}
solves

(5.5)

(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)=\lambda^{\prime}(k+i\varepsilon)u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon),

that is,

(5.6)

\partial_{k}u_{+}=\lambda^{\prime}(k+i\varepsilon)(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))^{-2}e_{1}.

On the other hand, we can also use the quasi-periodicity of
u_{+}
to gain some more knowledge about the entries of
\partial_{k}u_{+}
. Since

(5.7)

u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon,n+k)=e^{ik-\varepsilon}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon,n),

we find that

(5.8)

\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon,n+k)=e^{ik-\varepsilon}\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon,n)+ie^{ik-\varepsilon}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon,n).

This then yields

(5.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=c\left(\left(\partial_{k}u(k+i\varepsilon),e^{ik-\varepsilon}\partial_{k}u(k+i\varepsilon),e^{2ik-2\varepsilon}\partial_{k}u(k+i\varepsilon),\dots\right)^{\top}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+i\left(0,e^{ik-\varepsilon}u(k+i\varepsilon),2e^{2ik-2\varepsilon}u(k+i\varepsilon),\dots\right)^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle+c^{\prime}\left(u(k+i\varepsilon),e^{ik-\varepsilon}u(k+i\varepsilon),e^{2ik-2\varepsilon}u(k+i\varepsilon),\dots\right)^{\top}.

Combining equations (
5.3
) and (
5.9
), we obtain

(5.10)

\displaystyle\langle u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rangle

\displaystyle=c^{2}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\varepsilon}}\langle u(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}(u+i\varepsilon)\rangle-ic^{2}\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon}}{(1-e^{-2\varepsilon})^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{cc^{\prime}}{1-e^{-2\varepsilon}},

where we have used the fact that
u(k+i\varepsilon)
is normalised. Rearranging (
5.10
) and using (
5.5
) yields

(5.11)

\displaystyle\langle u(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u(kc+i\varepsilon)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lVert u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rVert^{2}}\langle u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rangle+i\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon}}{(1-e^{-2\varepsilon})^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\varepsilon}}\frac{\partial_{k}\lVert u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rVert}{\lVert u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rVert}.

Next, we want to use equations (
5.1
) and (
5.6
) as well as results from section
2
to express the Berry connection in terms of spectral integrals. To this end, we first note that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\langle u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda^{\prime}}(k+i\varepsilon)\langle(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))^{-1}e_{1},(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))^{-2}e_{1},\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda^{\prime}}(k+i\varepsilon)\langle(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\overline{\lambda}(k+i\varepsilon))^{-2}(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle.

At this point, we introduce the following integrals which will become useful later on:

(5.13)

\displaystyle I_{0}(\alpha,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(z-\alpha)}{(z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2}}d\mu,

\displaystyle I_{1}(\alpha,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\eta}{(z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2}}d\mu,

\displaystyle I_{2}(\alpha,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\eta^{3}}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}d\mu,

\displaystyle I_{3}(\alpha,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\eta^{2}(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}d\mu,

\displaystyle I_{4}(\alpha,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(z-\alpha)^{2}-\eta^{2}}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}d\mu.

Here,
d\mu
is the spectral measure associated with
\mathcal{J}_{+}
and
e_{1}
. Through functional calculus, we can express various quantities related to
u_{+}
using these integrals
[
7
,
17
]
:

(5.14)

\displaystyle I_{1}(\Re(\lambda),\Im(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Im(\lambda)\lVert(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{1}\rVert^{2},

\displaystyle I_{2}(\Re(\lambda),\Im(\lambda))

\displaystyle=-\Im(\lambda)^{2}\Im\left(\langle(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\overline{\lambda})^{-2}(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle_{+}\right),

\displaystyle I_{3}(\Re(\lambda),\Im(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Im(\lambda)^{2}\Re\left(\langle\mathcal{J}_{+}-\overline{\lambda})^{-2}(\mathcal{J}_{+}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{1},e_{1}\rangle_{+}\right),

\displaystyle I_{4}(\Re(\lambda),\Im(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda)).

In particular, it holds that

(5.15)

\displaystyle\langle u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u_{+}(k+i\varepsilon)\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda^{\prime}}(k+i\varepsilon)\Im(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))^{-2}\left(I_{3}(\Re(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)),\Im(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)))\right.

\displaystyle\left.-iI_{2}(\Re(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)),\Im(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)))\right).

In the following, we will suppress the arguments of the integrals for better readability. Next, we must take care of
\partial_{k}\lVert u_{+}\rVert
. Writing
\alpha=\Re(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))
and
\eta=\Im(\lambda(k+i\varepsilon))
and using equations (
5.14
), we find that

(5.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\lVert u_{+}\lVert

\displaystyle=\partial_{k}\sqrt{\eta^{-1}I_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{+}\rVert^{-1}\partial_{k}(\eta^{-1}I_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{+}\rVert^{-1}\left(-\eta^{-2}\eta^{\prime}I_{1}+\eta^{-1}\partial_{k}I_{1}\right).

Upon exchanging integration and differentiation, we find that

(5.17)

\partial_{k}I_{1}=\eta^{\prime}I_{4}+2\eta^{-1}\alpha^{\prime}I_{3}.

Substituting this into (
5.16
), we have

(5.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\lVert u_{+}\lVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{+}\rVert^{-1}\left(-\eta^{-2}\eta^{\prime}I_{1}+2\eta^{-2}\alpha^{\prime}I_{3}+\eta^{\prime}\eta^{-1}I_{4})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{+}\rVert\left(-\eta^{\prime}\eta^{-1}+2\eta^{-1}\alpha^{\prime}\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}}+\eta^{\prime}\frac{I_{4}}{I_{1}})\right).

Combining equations (
5.11
), (
5.15
), and (
5.18
) yields

(5.19)

\displaystyle\langle u(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}(u+i\varepsilon)\rangle

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{\prime}-i\eta^{\prime})\eta^{-1}\left(\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}}-i\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}}\right)+i\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon}}{(1-e^{-2\varepsilon})^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{\eta}-\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\eta}\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}}-\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}\frac{I_{4}}{I_{1}}

\displaystyle=i\left(\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon}}{(1-e^{-2\varepsilon})^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\eta}+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\frac{I_{4}}{I_{1}}-\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{\eta}\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}}\right),

where we have used the identity

(5.20)

\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\frac{I_{4}}{I_{1}}.

Assume now that
k\notin\{0,2\pi\}
. Here,
\lambda(\cdot)
is analytic
[
13
]
and we thus expand

(5.21)

\lambda(k+i\varepsilon)=\lambda(k)+i\varepsilon\lambda^{\prime}(k)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Combining the above expansion with the fact that
\lambda(k)
and its derivatives are real together with equation (
5.19
), we obtain the following asymptotics:

(5.22)

\displaystyle\langle u(k+i\varepsilon),\partial_{k}u(k+i\varepsilon)\rangle_{d}

\displaystyle=i\left(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(k)\varepsilon}{2}\frac{I_{4}}{I_{1}}\right)-\frac{\lambda^{\prime\prime}(k)}{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=i\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}{2}\frac{I_{4}}{I_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{\prime\prime}(k)}{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

To obtain the result of Theorem
3.1
, we need to deal with the term containing
I_{3}
. First, we note that the measure
\mu
can be decomposed as

(5.23)

\mu=\mu_{ac}+\mu_{pp},

where the measure
\mu_{ac}
has its support contained in the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{J}_{+}
and
\mu_{pp}
is a point measure whose support is constrained to the point spectrum of
\mathcal{J}_{+}
, which in turn is a subset of the resolvent set of
\mathcal{J}
. In particular, we can show that

(5.24)

d\mu_{ac}=N(\lambda)d\lambda,

where
N(\cdot)
is the spectral density associated to
\mathcal{J}_{+}
.
Let
\delta>0
be small enough that
[\lambda(k)-\delta,\lambda(k)+\delta]
lies in the interior of
\sigma(\mathcal{J})
. We have

(5.25)

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\eta^{2}(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\eta^{2}(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}N(z)dz+\eta^{2}\sum w_{i}\frac{(z_{i}-\alpha)^{2}-\eta^{2}}{((z_{i}-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}},

where
z_{i},w_{i}
are the enumeration of the point spectra and the associated weight. Because
\alpha=\lambda(k)
lies in a spectral band,
\lvert z_{i}-\alpha\rvert>0
for all
i
. Therefore, the sum in equation (
5.25
) clearly vanishes as
\eta\rightarrow 0
, which is the case when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. We now split the remaining integral as follows:

(5.26)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\eta^{2}(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}N(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda(k)-\delta}^{\lambda(k)+\delta}\frac{\eta^{2}(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}N(z)dz

\displaystyle+\eta^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[\lambda(k)-\delta,\lambda(k)+\delta]}\frac{(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}N(z).

Because
\frac{(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}
is bounded for
\delta
small enough and
N(\cdot)
is integrable, the second integral also tends to 0. Through a change of variables, we now obtain the following identity:

(5.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda(k)-\delta}^{\lambda(k)+\delta}\frac{\eta^{2}(z-\alpha)}{((z-\alpha)^{2}+\eta^{2})^{2}}N(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha-\delta)}^{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha+\delta)}\frac{y}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}N(\eta y+\alpha)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha-\delta)}^{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha+\delta)}\frac{y}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}N(\alpha)dy

\displaystyle+\int_{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha-\delta)}^{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha+\delta)}\int_{0}^{y}\frac{y}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}N^{\prime}(\eta x+\alpha)dxdy.

As
\varepsilon
tends to 0, the first integral on the right-hand side vanishes due to the symmetry. We proceed with the second integral as follows:

(5.28)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\lvert\int_{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha-\delta)}^{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha+\delta)}\int_{0}^{y}\frac{y}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}N^{\prime}(\eta x+\alpha)dxdy\rvert\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\qquad\displaystyle\leq\int_{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha-\delta)}^{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha+\delta)}\lvert\int_{0}^{y}\frac{y}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}N^{\prime}(\eta x+\alpha)dx\rvert dy\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\qquad\displaystyle\leq\int_{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha-\delta)}^{\eta^{-1}(\lambda(k)-\alpha+\delta)}\frac{\eta y^{2}}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}\sup_{x\in[\lambda(k)-\delta,\lambda(k)+\delta]}\lvert N^{\prime}(x)\rvert dy.\end{array}

As
N(\cdot)
is analytic away from the band edges and
\frac{y^{2}}{(y^{2}+1)^{2}}
is integrable, this integral also vanishes in the limit. Using the following dispersion relation in one dimension
[
13
]
:

(5.29)

\Delta(\lambda(k))=2\cos(k),

we obtain

(5.30)

\lambda^{\prime}(k)=\frac{\sin(k)}{2\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda(k))}.

This implies that
\lambda^{\prime}
vanishes only at the band edges. We therefore obtain

(5.31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(k)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{A}(k+i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda(k)+i0)}{\operatorname{sign}(\lambda^{\prime}(k))\Im(m_{+}(\lambda(k)+i0))}-\frac{1}{2}.

This proves the first part of Theorem
3.1
. All that remains is a change of variables:

(5.32)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathcal{A}(k)dk

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda(k)+i0))}{\operatorname{sign}(\lambda^{\prime}(k))\Im(m_{+}(\lambda(k)+i0))}-\frac{1}{2}dk

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sign}(\lambda(\pi)-\lambda(0))\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda(k)+i0))}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda(k)+i0))}dk

\displaystyle-\operatorname{sign}(\lambda(\pi)-\lambda(0))\int_{-\pi}^{0}\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(k)}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda(k)+i0))}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda(k)+i0))}dk-\pi

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sign}(\lambda(\pi)-\lambda(0))\int_{\lambda(0)}^{\lambda(\pi)}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda+i0))}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda+i0))}d\lambda

\displaystyle-\operatorname{sign}(\lambda(\pi)-\lambda(0))\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{-\lambda^{\prime}(-k)}{2}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda(-k)+i0))}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda(-k)+i0))}dk-\pi

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\min}}^{\lambda_{\max}}\frac{\Re(m_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda+i0))}{\Im(m_{+}(\lambda+i0))}d\lambda-\pi,

which concludes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

6.
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we established a real-space representation of the Zak phase for periodic Jacobi operators by expressing the Berry connection in terms of the half-line Weyl
m
-function. This leads to an explicit spectral formula for the Zak phase that depends only on boundary values of the resolvent and does not require Floquet-Bloch theory.

To highlight consistency with the classical formulation of Zak phase, we studied the SSH model analytically and the Rice-Mele model numerically. Moreover, we showed that the classical quantisation of structures with inversion symmetric fundamental cells can be recovered from equation (
3.3
) without resorting to momentum-space based arguments.

The present work raises some unanswered questions. Perhaps the most important from a conceptual point of view is the precise interpretation of (
3.3
) as a geometric or topological phase.
