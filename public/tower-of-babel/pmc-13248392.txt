Relationship between severe tooth wear and temporomandibular disorders: a cross-sectional study using propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Han Qin, Hui Miao, Pei Wang, Jinru Li, Yifan Liu, Xiaohua Chen, Dongliang Sun, Hongxu Yang, Shaoxiong Guo, Shibin Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe relationship between severe tooth wear and temporomandibular disorders (TMD) remains unclear in literature. The objective of this study was to retrospectively investigate the relationship between severe tooth wear and TMD by using the method of propensity score matching (PSM).Methods1911 TMD patients were enrolled in the study. After tooth wear index (TWI) assessment screening, 1557 TMD patients were divided into the severe tooth wear group (case group) and the mild tooth wear group (control group). Then propensity score-matching (PSM), Chi-square tests and logistic regression were used to compare the prevalence of TMD symptoms between the cases and matched controls.ResultsIn 1911 TMD patients, only 81 patients (4.24%) suffered from severe tooth wear. After PSM (74 cases vs. 141 controls), the prevalence of pain (P = 0.307), joint noise (P = 0.515), restricted mouth opening (P = 0.791) and condylar bony degeneration (P = 0.747), was not different significantly between the severe tooth wear group and the mild tooth wear group. Logistic regression analysis showed that older age (OR = 1.046, P = 0.001) may be the risk factors for TMJ condylar bony degeneration in TMD patients, but severe tooth wear (OR = 0.822, P = 0.525), sex (OR = 1.912, P = 0.061), and disease duration (OR = 1.001, P = 0.804) showed no significantly higher risk of it.ConclusionsIn this clinical cohort, the observed prevalence of severe tooth wear among TMD patients was only 4.24%. Within this TMD cohort, severe tooth wear was not significantly associated with TMD symptoms or TMJ condylar bony degeneration.Keywords: Temporomandibular disorders, Severe tooth wear, Cross sectional cohort study, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) encompasses a group of pathologies affecting the temporomandibular joint, masticatory muscles, and related structures. Its core clinical manifestations include orofacial pain, articular sounds, and restricted mandibular function [1–2]. Tooth wear is considered as a visible sign of physiological functional wear and a rather rapid onset in bruxism. It is probably seen more as a result of functional disturbance in the masticatory system [3]. Tooth wear alters the existing occlusal plane introducing deflective occlusal interferences. Occlusion is treated not only as the ratio of contact between teeth, but also as a dynamic, morphological and functional relation, presenting a great influence on chewing, swallowing and speech [4]. Occlusal/skeletal factors, such as crossbite, overjet and centric relation (CR) to maximum intercuspation (MI) discrepancy greater than 2 mm, could be considered as occlusal risk factors for TMD [3].

The correlation between TMD and tooth wear is of serious concern to both patients and the dental professionals. Studies of mandible samples from Aboriginal population or prehistoric human population indicate that tooth wear is associated with TMJ remodeling or degenerative changes [5–8], whereas no significant correlation between the degree of tooth wear and the severity of degenerative joint disease was found in mandibles from a contemporary sample [9]. Costa et al. reported a high incidence of teeth with wear facets, observed in patients with TMD dysfunction [10]. However, Pullinger and Seligman found it was difficult to differentiate TMD patients from nonpatients based on the severity of tooth wear and hence doubted the etiologic role of dental attrition in TMD [11–12]. The above-mentioned inconsistent conclusion may be due to the mandible sample from different eras, different study design and unparallel patient groups.

Propensity score matching (PSM) is a statistical method that can be used to make causal inference when there are more confounding factors involved in non-randomized controlled studies [13]. In the analysis of observational clinical studies, due to various reasons, there are many biases and confounding variables. PSM can effectively reduce the impact of these biases and confounding variables, so as to make a more reasonable comparison between the observation group and the control group [14].

Given the inconsistent findings in the literature and the methodological limitations of previous cross-sectional studies, the purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between severe tooth wear and TMJ degenerative disorders, as well as TMD symptoms, using a robust PSM approach. Furthermore, we aimed to investigate the precise prevalence of severe tooth wear in a large cohort of TMD patients and to differentially examine its association with specific TMD symptoms (pain, joint noise, limited mouth opening, and condylar degeneration). To the best of our knowledge, it is the first study to apply PSM in this context, thereby minimizing confounding bias and providing more reliable evidence on the relationship between severe tooth wear and TMD. We hypothesized that, among TMD patients, those with severe tooth wear would exhibit more severe symptoms or condylar bony degeneration, based on previous studies reporting associations between dental attrition and TMJ remodeling or degenerative changes in anthropological samples [5–8].

Methods

Study participants

Patients with TMD symptom admitted to the TMD Clinic in School of Stomatology, the Fourth Military Medical University from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2021 were recruited in the present study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) All participants met the DC/TMD diagnostic criteria for TMD [15]; (2) complete medical records; (3) preserved dentition model from oral scanning. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age under 18 or over 65; (2) TMJ ankylosis; (3) Inflammatory diseases of TMJ (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, viral infections, etc.); (4) TMD treatment history; (5) ever accepted occlusal equilibration; (6) abnormal teeth development; (7) Patients with obvious signs of dental erosion, characterized by smooth, cup-shaped concavities on occlusal or lingual surfaces of teeth typically associated with acid exposure; (8) Edentulous patients. A total of 1911 patients with TMD were enrolled in the present study, all of whom underwent routine dental and TMD clinical examinations, as well as TMJ regional CBCT imaging (Fig. 1). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all the participants. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Fourth Military Medical University (No. KQ-YJ-2023-085), and all procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of tooth wear

The assessment of tooth wear was performed by a single examiner who was calibrated prior to the study. The calibration process involved a training session on the tooth wear index (TWI) criteria using a set of 30 dentition models not included in the study sample. To assess ​intra-examiner reliability, the examiner re-evaluated 30 randomly selected models from the study sample at a two-week interval. The intra-examiner reliability, assessed using weighted Kappa statistics, was excellent (κ = 0.89). The degree of tooth wear was assessed according to Smith and Knight tooth wear index (TWI) [16]. The gradings were as follows:

The inclusion criteria for the severe tooth wear group (the case group) was that all natural teeth had moderate to severe tooth wear (TWI ≥ 2) [17], among which ≥ 2 teeth had severe tooth wear (TWI ≥ 3), while dentition with teeth of grade 0 and 1 TWI were defined as mild tooth wear and included in the mild tooth wear group (the control group) [18]. Patients who did not meet the above inclusion criteria were excluded. After TWI screening, 1557 patients were left.

Assessment of CBCT(Cone Beam Computed Tomography)

During seated scanning, patients were instructed to stand or sit in the CBCT device (HiRes3D, LargeV Instrument Corp., Ltd, Beijing China), and rest their heads against the head fixation device to ensure that the skull remains stationary throughout the scanning process. The scanning conditions were 100 kV, 4 mA, 0.25 mm thickness. The scanning plane was parallel to the orbital ear plane, and the scanning range was from the top of the TMJ fossa in the middle cranial fossa to the plane of the sigmoid notch of the mandible. The main manifestations of TMJ condylar degeneration included: (1) Hyperplasia of bone (hyperplasia of bone cortex, which can form osteophytes); (2) Osteosclerosis (increased bone tissue density); (3) Bone resorption (erosion of bone cortex, decrease in density); (4) Bone wear or wear down (condyle flattening and shortening); (5) Cystic change [19, 20]. Both unilateral and bilateral TMJ with the above abnormal image manifestations were recorded as positive. Condylar development problems such as condylar dysplasia, condylar bifurcation deformity, developmental depression and developmental asymmetry were not included in the statistics. CBCT images were evaluated independently by two senior oral radiologists who were calibrated to the specific diagnostic criteria for condylar bony degeneration. Prior to the study, both radiologists participated in a calibration session. Inter-examiner reliability was assessed by having both radiologists independently evaluate 50 randomly selected CBCT scans not included in the final study sample. The inter-examiner reliability between the two radiologists, evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for absolute agreement, was excellent (ICC = 0.91). In case of disagreement during the formal evaluation phase, a third senior oral radiologist was consulted to reach a consensus (Fig. 2).

Propensity score matching

To control for potential confounders, 81 cases were matched with controls from a pool of 1,911 subjects. Propensity scores were generated using a logistic regression model that included sex, age, disease duration (patient-reported duration of TMD chief symptoms), maximum mouth opening, self-reported bruxism, self-reported chewing-side preference, a history of chronic TMD (symptoms persisting > 3 months), and a history of post-orthodontic TMD onset (with an orthodontic treatment history). Matching was performed using a 1:2 nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit propensity score. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD). This approach successfully matched 74 cases with 141 controls from an initial pool of 1,911 subjects, achieving adequate covariate balance (all standardized differences < 0.1 after matching). This process yielded a matched cohort of 74 cases and 141 controls (total n = 215, Fig. 1). All subsequent analyses comparing TMD symptoms between severe and mild tooth wear groups were conducted on this PSM-matched cohort. Logistic regression was further performed on the matched cohort to evaluate the association between severe tooth wear and condylar degeneration.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using PASS software (version 15.0), indicating a minimum of 74 subjects per group was required to achieve 80% power with a two-sided α of 0.05 and an odds ratio (OR) of 2.0.

Balance in covariates after PSM was assessed using standardized differences, with a value < 0.1 indicating good balance. Data management and preliminary descriptive statistics were performed using IBM SPSS (version 26.0). All subsequent inferential analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2), including propensity score matching (performed with the MatchIt package). Age (normally distributed) was compared using the independent t-test, while disease duration (non-normally distributed) was compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The association between covariates and condylar degeneration was analyzed using logistic regression. In addition, logistic regression was applied to the matched cohort to assess the relationship between severe tooth wear and condylar degeneration after adjusting for age, sex, and duration. Comparisons of categorical variables (TMD symptoms) between the severe and mild tooth wear groups in the PSM-matched cohort were conducted using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics and prevalence of symptoms before PSM

The general characteristics and prevalence of TMD symptoms of patients before PSM were shown in Table 1. In all the 1911 participants before TWI screening, the number of patients with severe tooth wear was only 81 (4.24%). There was no significant difference in sex ratio between the two groups (P = 0.503). The age of the severe tooth wear group was significantly older than that of the mild tooth wear group (P < 0.001). The duration of TMD was shorter in the severe tooth wear group than that in the mild tooth wear group (P = 0.041). In terms of TMD symptoms, patients with mild tooth wear were more likely to have joint noise (P = 0.010), while pain (P = 0.004) and mouth restriction (P = 0.014) symptoms were more likely to occur in patients with severe tooth wear. The proportion of TMJ condylar bony degeneration was no significantly different between the two groups (P = 0.112).

Analysis of risk factors for TMJ condylar bony degeneration

Logistic regression analysis in the matched cohort revealed that age remained a significant factor (OR = 1.046, P = 0.001), while sex and disease duration were no longer significant (P = 0.061 and P = 0.804, respectively). Severe tooth wear was not associated with condylar bony degeneration (OR = 0.822, P = 0.525), which was consistent with the findings from the overall cohort (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study contributes to the existing literature about the relationship between TMD and severe tooth wear in a special way. Firstly, by employing PSM, we minimized confounders and provided more credible evidence. Secondly, the strikingly low prevalence of severe tooth wear among TMD patients, and no significant difference in TMD symptoms between two matched groups (severe teeth wear and mild teeth wear) challenged the conventional wisdom that severe tooth wear may be related to the development of TMD.

Traditional views usually consider tooth wear as an indicator of cumulative parafunctional activity, thus suggesting a relationship with TMD. Yadav S reported that muscle tenderness and pain had a significant relation to tooth wear [21]. Dao TT et al. even found that bruxism patients who reported orofacial pain experienced worse pain than those with myofascial pain [22]. However, this study showed a very low prevalence of severe tooth wear among TMD patients (4.24%). It is suggested that severe tooth wear may not be a common feature, an accompanying phenomena or a direct consequence of TMD. Regarding symptoms, the pre-matching data showed that patients with severe tooth wear had a higher incidence of pain and mouth opening limitation, while joint noise was more common in the mild tooth wear group. However, after controlling confounders via PSM, the prevalence rates of pain, joint noise, mouth opening limitation, and condylar degeneration showed no significant differences between the two matched groups. This indicates that the initially observed symptom differences may be partially attributed to some confounders, such as age, sex or psychological factors, etc. Pain may be related to muscle fatigue and spasm caused by bruxism, as well as asymmetric masticatory muscle activity due to altered occlusal relationships [22, 23].

Severe tooth wear affects not only the teeth themselves, but also the entire stomatognathic system. TMJ is a joint that connects the masticatory muscles to the upper and lower jaws, and maintains the functions of chewing, swallowing, speech and even facial expression. Its inner condyle-disc position relationship changes with the occlusal vertical distance. Many scholars believe that the posterior displacement of the condyle during severe tooth wear may cause damage to the inner wall of the joint cavity or compression of nerves and blood vessels in the soft tissues behind it, resulting in TMD. It is even suggested that tooth wear can be used as a predictor of TMJ disc displacement [24]. Studies of mandible samples from Aboriginal population or prehistoric human population indicate that tooth wear is associated with TMJ remodeling or degenerative changes [5–8]. However, after PSM, the results of this study showed that severe tooth wear does not seem to have harmful effect on the degenerative bony changes of TMJ condyle. This may be due to the long-term process of severe tooth wear. The continuous changes and progressive load on the joint surface may make the joint tissue be more tolerant and then easy to establish a new equilibrium state. In 1994, Mehta N et al. proposed the weak chain theory of diseases in the stomatognathic system, believing that the stomatognathic system is a chain composed of teeth, periodontal tissue, masticatory muscle and TMJ [25]. When the stomatognathic system is overloaded, the weakest link in the chain will break first, so that the rest link of the system will be protected and continue to adapt [25]. In patients with severe tooth wear, abnormal or harmful load of stomatognathic system may be released through the loss of teeth hard tissue, thus protecting periodontal tissue, masticatory muscle and TMJ. Therefore, there is no close relationship between severe tooth wear and TMD or condylar bony degeneration.

Similarly, several references in the literature indicate that the effect of bruxism on the stomatognathic system differ depending on the nature of bruxism [26, 27]. Patients who grind their teeth are more likely to present tooth wear rather than TMD, whereas patients who clench their teeth, present no dental wear but are more at risk of developing TMD due to the joint overload. These observations could explain, at least in part, the absence of a relationship between severe tooth wear (which is rather a consequence of grinding) and TMD (which are rather a consequence of clenching).

The identical symptom profiles before and after PSM confirm the robustness of the matching procedure rather than a lack of statistical power. This study has several limitations. Firstly, severe tooth wear accounted for only 4.24% of the 1,911 TMD patients, which may limit statistical power and the generalizability of the comparison. Secondly, the present study did not include a non-TMD control group, so the findings cannot be interpreted as evidence regarding whether severe tooth wear is related to the occurrence of TMD itself. Thirdly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Other limitations include not differentiating the pattern of wear across dental quadrants. Previous studies have shown that the reduction of occlusal vertical height in different quadrants may be related to the occurrence and development of TMD [24, 28]. When the severe tooth wear group was included in this study, the dentition was evaluated as a whole, and the wear degree in different quadrants was not distinguished. Pain was assessed as a single composite variable without distinguishing between masticatory muscle pain and TMJ pain, which may have different associations with tooth wear. In addition, the bony degeneration of TMJ involves the destruction of TMJ surface, which is characterized by subchondral osteosclerosis, osteophytes, pseudocysts and erosion, etc. [29]. These changes are usually observed in the condyle, but may also involve other locations such as the articular fossa [30, 31], while only the condylar changes were observed in this study.

Conclusions

Within the studied cohort of patients seeking care for TMD, severe tooth wear was uncommon. After rigorously controlling for multiple confounders via PSM, severe tooth wear was not associated with a higher prevalence of TMD symptoms (including pain, joint noise, limited mouth opening) or condylar bony degeneration.
