Occurrence of Infusion Associated Reactions and Antidrug Antibodies in Enzyme Replacement Therapy for Fabry Disease and the Effect of Preventive Measures
Authors: Maud Janssens, Sanne J van der Veen, Laura van Dussen, André B P van Kuilenburg, Marion M M G Brands, Mirjam Langeveld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMale patients with the classical phenotype of Fabry disease (FD) are at risk of developing inhibiting antidrug antibodies (iADAs) against recombinant α‐galactosidase‐A, administered in the form of enzyme replacement therapy (ERT). The presence of ADAs is linked to infusion‐associated reactions (IARs) and reduced ERT effectiveness. This study evaluates the incidence of iADAs and IARs in ERT treated classical male FD patients, their interrelatedness and the effect of an adjusted ERT initiation protocol, aimed at inducing immune tolerance. In this monocenter cohort study, a retrospective analysis was performed to evaluate the occurrence of iADAs and IARs. Prospectively, a stepwise dose escalation ERT initiation protocol was evaluated in classical male FD patients with a calculated high risk of iADA formation and compared to the standard initiation protocol. Retrospectively (n = 52), the proportion of patients experiencing IARs was higher in patients that developed iADAs (20/27 seropositive patients, 0/19 seronegative, p < 0.001). Higher iADA titers were associated with persisting iADA titers (p = 0.004). Prospectively (n = 17), none of the patients in the intervention group (stepwise dose escalation) experienced IARs (0/8), compared to half of the patients in the control group (5/9, p = 0.029). The proportion of patients developing iADAs was not different between the two groups (respectively, 4/8 and 6/9, p = 0.52). To conclude, iADA development is associated with the occurrence of IARs. The stepwise dose escalation at ERT initiation used in the current study in a single cohort seems to prevent IARs in male patients with classical FD, but does not seem to prevent iADA development.Keywords: antidrug antibodies, enzyme replacement therapy, Fabry disease, immune tolerance induction, infusion‐associated reactions, lysosomal storage disorder

Introduction

Fabry disease (FD) (OMIM number 301500) is a rare lysosomal storage disorder, caused by pathogenic variants in the galactosidase alpha gene (GLA gene, OMIM number 300664). GLA is located on the X‐chromosome (Xq22.1) and encodes the enzyme alpha‐galactosidase A (αGal A). Absent or reduced activity of αGal A results in the accumulation of glycosphingolipids, particularly globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and its derivatives, in various cell types [1].

FD is a progressive disease with involvement of multiple organ systems. Male patients with classical FD are most severely affected due to the (almost) complete deficiency of αGal A. These patients generally present with characteristic FD symptoms: acroparesthesia, cornea verticillata and angiokeratoma. Long‐term manifestations include cardiovascular symptoms such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart failure, arrythmia and myocardial infarction, progressive renal insufficiency and neurovascular symptoms such as a stroke [2].

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is currently the most widely used therapy for FD and consists of biweekly infusions with recombinant αGal A. Three forms of ERT are approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA): agalsidase‐alfa (Replagal, Takeda, 2001), agalsidase‐beta (Fabrazyme, Sanofi, 2001), and a second generation ERT pegunigalsidase‐alfa (Elfabrio, Chiesi, 2023) [3, 4, 5]. Several studies have demonstrated that ERT can prevent or delay progression of some disease manifestation, in particular renal disease [6].

One downside of ERT is the development of antidrug antibodies (ADAs). Male patients with the classical phenotype of FD lack endogenous αGal A. Their immune system does not recognize the recombinant enzyme as an endogenous protein, posing them at risk for ADA development [7, 8, 9]. The presence of ADAs is linked to decreased effectiveness of ERT and to the occurrence of infusion‐associated reactions (IARs) [8]. The decreased effectiveness of ERT is caused by the inhibition of recombinant αGal A activity by ADAs (iADAs) and inhibited uptake of recombinant αGal A into target cells [8, 10, 11]. Exact numbers on the proportion of patients developing iADAs are difficult to establish and vary from 35% to 74% in male FD patients, due to the heterogeneity of analyzed cohorts (heterogeneity in sex, phenotype, and therapy form) [9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In general, FD patients develop iADAs in the first 3–6 months after start of ERT [8, 13, 15, 16]. The loss of effectiveness of ERT is characterized by a reduced biochemical response (less reduction in plasma globotriaosylsphingosine (lysoGb3) levels) and a reduced clinical response (accelerated decline in renal function in ERT inhibition positive patients compared to ERT inhibition negative patients) [9, 10].

In addition to the decreased ERT effectiveness, ADAs are associated with the occurrence of IARs [8]. Patients generally experience their first IARs within the first 6 months after start of ERT and in most patients IARs re‐occur during following infusions [13, 16]. IARs can vary from mild skin rash to severe anaphylactic shock [17, 18]. To minimize IARs, antihistamines, corticosteroids, and slower infusion rates can be applied. Using this approach, most patients can continue treatment, though the burden of the reactions and the use of medication to prevent or treat them is considerable.

As both the occurrence of IARs and the reduced effectiveness of ERT are linked to the presence of iADAs, it is important to prevent the development of iADAs. Especially because iADA development seems irreversible and attempts to tolerize high titer iADA‐positive FD patients have not been successful [12, 14]. In hemophilia, in which ADA formation was a frequent problem in severely affected patients with very low factor VIII levels, treatment initiation protocols with frequent administration of low dose factor VIII have successfully induced immune tolerance [19]. Desensitization protocols have been developed in lysosomal storage disorders for patients experiencing IARs [20], however, these protocols did not include preventive measures. We therefore hypothesized that the preventive approach used in hemophilia might also work in FD. The objective of this study is to evaluate the association between iADAs and IARs in male FD patients with the classical phenotype and to evaluate if a stepwise dose escalation protocol for male FD patients with the classical phenotype, who are at high risk of developing iADAs, leads to a reduction in iADA formation and to a decrease of IARs.

Methods

Patient Population

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration, as revised in 2013. Data were collected at the Dutch FD center (Amsterdam UMC, University of Amsterdam, the Netherlands) after obtaining written informed consent for the Dutch FD registry from each patient (ethics approval code: AMC2014_192). This registry contains data of all Dutch FD patients from 1999 onwards who consented to their data being used for scientific studies. Diagnosis of FD was confirmed by a combination of DNA analysis, enzymatic assay, and phenotypic features [2, 21]. Classical FD in male patients was defined as a residual enzyme activity of ≤ 5% of the mean reference, a plasma lysoGb3 level > 50 nmol/L, and the presence of at least one characteristic symptom: childhood onset typical acroparesthesia, cornea verticillata, and/or angiokeratomas [2]. All male patients with confirmed FD who did not meet the criteria for classic FD are categorized as having nonclassical FD. Female patients in this study were not categorized by phenotype.

Retrospective Analysis: Association Between iADA Development an Occurrence of IARs

Longitudinal data of the patients that started ERT (agalsidase‐alfa and agalsidase‐beta) were collected up to December 31, 2024. The analysis was split in three groups: male FD patients with the classical phenotype that started ERT before June 2020, male FD patients with the nonclassical phenotype that started ERT up to December 31, 2024, and female FD patients that started ERT up to December 31, 2024 (Supporting Information S1A).

Prospective Analysis: ERT Initiation Protocols

From June 2020 onwards a stepwise dose escalation protocol was applied as a safety protocol to start agalsidase‐beta in treatment naïve male patients with classical FD with a high risk of developing iADAs as determined using a risk calculator [22] (see below for details). Treatment naïve classical male FD patients that started agalsidase‐beta between 2014 and 2020 with a high risk of developing iADAs (retrospectively determined) were included in the control group and followed the standard ERT initiation protocol (these patients were also included in the retrospective analysis, Supporting Information S1B). Data of patients were included if they started agalsidase‐beta after 2014, at which time the use of electronic medical records was introduced at the Amsterdam UMC, enabling reliable data capture. From 2014 onwards standard practice for the treatment of FD was treatment with agalsidase‐beta 1 mg/kg/eow without the use of premedication before the occurrence of IARs. Only patients who were at high risk to develop iADAs were included in the control group to enable comparison to the intervention group which also included high risk patients only. The protocols were evaluated based on observational data gathered prospectively up to June 30, 2025. All patients signed consent for use of their data (ethics approval code: AMC2014_192).

Risk Stratification

The risk of developing iADAs was estimated using the risk prediction model developed by our group [22], based on the following parameters: mutation type (nonsense/frameshift or missense/other), the intended treatment (agalsidase‐alfa, agalsidase‐beta) and the untreated plasma lysoGb3. The final prediction model was validated internally using 10‐fold cross validations as described in the original publication. High risk for developing iADAs was defined as > 46% chance for developing iADAs (low risk of developing iADAs < 46%, AUC 0.76, accuracy 0.73, specificity 0.68, sensitivity 0.78, Supporting Information S2).

Stepwise Dose Escalation Protocol (Intervention Protocol)

In our stepwise dose escalation protocol, both the dose of agalsidase‐beta (in the first 30 weeks, weekly infusions) and the infusion rate (weeks 30–49, infusions every other week (eow)) were gradually increased over time (Table 1A and Supporting Information S3 and S4). Patients received their first infusion in the hospital, as well as every infusion that had an increased dose or infusion rate. The remaining infusions were administered at home by a home care nurse (when no IARs had occurred during the in‐hospital infusions). Based on the half‐life time of agalsidase‐beta (t 1/2 = 45–100 min [4]), it would be preferable to have an even higher frequency than one infusion a week at start of the stepwise dose escalation protocol. However, it was not possible to exceed the maximum approved and reimbursed dose of agalsidase‐beta (1.0 mg/kg/eow), which means that for example daily administration would have resulted in such a low dose that it would not provide relevant antigen exposure.

Standard Initiation Protocol (Standard Protocol)

Patients were treated with the full dose (agalsidase‐beta 1 mg/kg eow) from the first infusion onwards, gradually increasing the infusion rate (Table 1B). The first 13 infusions were administered in the hospital; if no IARs had occurred, ERT could be continued at home, administered by the patients themselves or a home nurse. Not all patients starting this ERT initiation protocol will reach the final infusion rate due to the occurrence of IARs (lower infusion rate during following infusions), and if IARs occurred, this meant they had to come to the hospital for several infusions before continuing with administration at home by a home nurse if this was deemed safe by the treatment team. Self‐administration would not be possible in these cases.

Infusion‐Associated Reactions (IARs)

IARs were defined as signs or symptoms experienced during or shortly after (< 24 h) infusion with recombinant enzyme. In the analyses, no distinction was made between the different types of IARs. If there was doubt whether or not described complaints or symptoms should be considered an IAR, individual cases were discussed by the first and last author. Patients experiencing IARs were treated according to the Amsterdam UMC protocol (Supporting Information S5) [23].

Statistical Analysis

For statistical analysis and visualization, RStudio version 4.4.3 and Prism GraphPad 10.2.0 were used. For descriptive analysis, the median (M) with the range was used, based on the normality of the data. To compare proportions the Chi‐square test or the Fisher's exact test was used. To compare medians, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used. To evaluate the effectiveness of the stepwise dose escalation protocol a survival curve analysis was performed on the presence of iADAs and the occurrence of IARs. A p < 0.05 was defined as significant.

Laboratory Measurements

Both plasma lysoGb3 and iADA titers were measured at the Amsterdam UMC, location AMC. From May 2024 onwards plasma lysoGb3 was analyzed by mass spectrometry using 13C6 labeled lysoGb3. From August 2015 until May 2024 plasma lysoGb3 was analyzed by mass spectrometry using glycine labeled lysoGb3 as internal standard [24, 25, 26]. Before 2015, plasma lysoGb3 was measured on the basis of tandem mass spectrometry with isotope‐labeled lysoGb3 as internal standard. Results from all three assays are comparable and can be used interchangeably [2]. iADA titers (neutralizing ADAs) were analyzed as described by Linthorst et al. [8]. The method is based on the measurement of enzymatic activity of recombinant α‐Gal A in vitro, when different dilutions of patient plasma are added. Patients were considered to be seropositive for iADAs against ERT when the titer exceeded six on at least one occasion [8]. The seropositive patients were split into tolerized, defined as having an iADA titer < 6 at the two most recent assessments (after having an iADA titer > 6 in previous assessments) and nontolerized (titer continuously > 6).

Results

Retrospective Analysis

Classical Male FD Patients

For the retrospective analysis of the association of iADA development and occurrence of IARs, data were analyzed from classical male patients who have been under follow‐up at any period between 1999 (introduction of ERT) and December 2024 (data collection closure). Of the 73 male patients with classical FD identified, 65 of these patients started ERT, of whom 52 started before June 2020. The median age at start of ERT was 26.6 years (range 5.3–58.5 years) in this cohort of 52 patients. Twelve patients died during follow‐up, with a median age of 56 years at time of death (range of 26–66 years). The median untreated plasma lysoGb3 was 103.7 nmol/L (range 52.7–150.3 nmol/L). Sixteen patients started treatment with agalsidase‐alfa (31%), while 36 patients received agalsidase‐beta as the first form of ERT (69%). Twenty‐three patients switched to another form of ERT or to trial medication during the treatment period (44%). The course of treatment for each individual patient is visualized in Figure 1. Due to a shortage of agalsidase‐beta, a substantial number of patients were temporarily switched from agalsidase‐beta to agalsidase‐alfa around 2010. Three patients moved abroad and we lack information regarding their current treatment status.

Development of iADAs

In this cohort of 52 male patients with classical FD, the iADA titer was at least measured once in 48 patients (Supporting Information S1B). The median number of times the iADA titer was measured during ERT was 9 with a range from 1 to 22 (in seropositive patients: median 10 [range 5–22], in seronegative patients median 6.5 [range 1–13]). 20/48 patients (42%) remained seronegative for iADAs throughout the entire period they were on ERT. Twenty‐eight patients (58%) were categorized as seropositive. The median time until testing positive for iADAs after start of ERT was 3.4 months (range 2.3–79.5 months). Eighteen of the 28 seropositive patients (64%) developed iADAs within the first 6 months after initiation of ERT and 22 (79%) within the first year. However, the exact moment of iADA development is difficult to establish because it depends on the moment iADA testing was performed, which was earlier in patients experiencing IARs (first standard measurement 12 months after start ERT in absence of infusion reactions).

Seven of the 28 seropositive patients (25%) eventually achieved an iADA titer of < 6 and were defined as tolerized. In addition, three patients exhibited a clear declining trend in iADA titers. However, for the majority of seropositive patients (18/28 patients, 64%), seroconversion was not reversible. Of the seven patients who tolerized, only one patient had a maximum iADA titer higher than 100. In the patients who remained seropositive, 16 of the 18 patients (89%) had a maximum iADA titer over 100. A lower maximum iADA titer is associated with tolerization (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.004, Dunn‐test (Bonferroni) tolerized group vs. not tolerized group: p = 0.0015, Figure 2).

Infusion Associated Reactions

Information about the occurrence of IARs was available for 49 patients of this cohort (Supporting Information S1B). Twenty‐one patients experienced at least one IAR (43%) and almost all of them had recurrent IARs (17/20, information on recurrence missing for one patient). The median time until the first experienced IAR after the initiation of ERT was 2.3 months (0.4–63.9 months). As shown in Figure 1, most patients experienced the first IAR within the first 6 months after start of ERT (15/21, 71%). Depending on the severity of IARs, an appropriate approach to treat or prevent IARs during subsequent infusions was implemented. Premedication was started in 18 patients and consisted of antihistamines (1/18 patients, 5%), corticosteroids (6/18 patients, 33%) or a combination of both (11/18 patients, 58%). Despite the premedication, most patients still experienced recurrent IARs during following infusions (16/17, information on recurrence is missing for one patient that received premedication). All patients were able to continue ERT infusions, while using premedication there is a significant burden for the patients of the continuous need to use antihistamines and corticosteroids, the ongoing infusion reactions and the prolonged infusion duration.

The Association Between the Development of iADAs and the Occurrence of IARs

Of the 52 patients, six patients were excluded from this analysis because of missing values (absent information about the occurrence of IARs and/or missing iADA titers, Supporting Information S1B). Of the 20 patients who experienced IARs, 20 had developed iADAs (100%, Figure 3A). In contrast, in patients never experiencing IARs, only 27% had developed iADAs (7/26 patients). The proportion of patients experiencing IARs is significantly higher in patients that developed iADAs (Fisher's exact test, p < 0.001). The mean iADA titer per patient in seropositive patients was not significantly different between the patients that did and did not experience an IAR (median mean iADA titer per patient of 50 and 24, respectively, Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.53, visualization in Figure 3B).

Nonclassical Male FD Patients

Up to December 31, 2024, we identified 58 identified male FD patients with the nonclassical disease phenotype, of which 15 were started on ERT (seven with agalsidase‐alfa and eight with agalsidase‐beta). One of these 15 patients has experienced an IAR once during his 28th infusion (ERT initiation with agalsidase‐beta). The patient received corticosteroids as premedication during following infusions (unknown whether infusion rate was decreased and if premedication was phased out again). Unfortunately, no information is available about the iADA status of this patient. None of the nonclassical male FD patients with known iADA status developed iADAs during their treatment period with ERT (no iADAs 11/15 patients, unknown 4/15 patients).

Female FD Patients

Up to December 31, 2024, 194 female FD patients were identified, of whom 90 started ERT. Three female FD patients experienced IARs (initial therapy agalsidase‐beta), which were all mild reactions. Two patients had recurrent IARs (first reaction 7 and 8 months after ERT initiation) and one patient experienced only one IAR (almost a year after start of ERT). In all three patients, the infusion rate was lowered during the following infusions. Two patients received pretreatment with antihistamines and corticosteroids, and one patient was prescribed antihistamines only. None of the three patients developed iADAs. In 50 female patients, the iADA titer was measured after start of ERT. Only one patient had an iADA titer > 6 (namely 7) at one timepoint, which was < 6 on follow‐up measurements and is therefore considered not relevant.

Stepwise Dose Escalation Protocol at Initiation of Agalsidase Beta

Starting from June 2020, the risk of developing iADAs was determined for all male patients with the classical phenotype of FD who were about to start treatment with agalsidase‐beta (n = 13). Eleven of these patients were classified as being at high risk of developing iADAs (risk > 46%), and they followed the intervention protocol with stepwise dose escalation of agalsidase‐beta dose and adjustment of infusion rate, as described in Section 2. Three patients were excluded from analysis because of a short follow up time after start of treatment. In the control group, nine patients were included (see Section 2 for further details). At the time patients received their first infusion with agalsidase‐beta, age, untreated plasma lysoGb3 and the risk to develop iADAs did not differ significantly between intervention and control group (Table 2A). The follow up duration (as a result of the study design) was significantly shorter in patients in the intervention group (p < 0.001, data were included up to June 30, 2025). The mutation (type) and the untreated plasma lysoGb3 of the individual patients can be found in Supporting Information S6.

The Development of iADAs

The proportion of patients developing iADAs was not significantly different between the intervention group (4/8, 50%) and the control group (6/9, 67%) (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.52, Table 2A). In seropositive patients only, there was no difference in the highest iADA titer measured (intervention group median iADA titer 145 and control group median 48.5, Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.26, Table 2B). The Kaplan–Meier curve (Figure 4A) shows that all seropositive patients (both groups) developed iADAs within the first year after start of treatment with agalsidase‐beta (log‐rank test, p = 0.41). The median time until the development of iADAs after start of ERT did not differ between the intervention group and control group (median time 2.9 and 2.8 months, respectively, Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.57).

Survival curve analysis stepwise dose escalation protocol vs standard initiation protocol. (A) Development of iADAs (Log Rank test, p = 0.41). (B) Occurrence of IARs (Log Rank test, p = 0.015).

The Occurrence of IARs

None of the patients in the intervention group experienced IARs, compared to half of the patients in the control group (respectively, 0/8 and 5/9, Fisher's exact test: p = 0.029, Table 2B, log‐rank test: p = 0.015, Figure 4B). All first IARs were observed within the first 6 months after ERT initiation (median time 1.39 months, range 1.03–2.35 months).

Low Risk Patients

Three patients that started with ERT (agalsidase‐beta) after 2014 were considered low risk of developing iADAs (calculated risk 40%, 24%, and 37%) and followed the control protocol (standard initiation protocol). One patient experienced multiple IARs and developed iADAs. The second patient developed a peak iADA titer level of 7, which is only just above the cutoff of seropositivity (seropositive is defined as iADA titer > 6). The third patient experienced atypical IARs (stomach ache, headache, pain on the chest) and developed iADAs.

In Figure 5, the relationship between the iADA titers and the plasma lysoGb3 values during treatment per included patient is visualized for both groups. A reduction in plasma lysoGb3 to below 20 nmol/L is considered a significant biochemical response to treatment [27]. In the intervention group (Figure 5A) four patients reached this level. These four patients were seronegative (Patients 2, 6, 7, and 8). One patient (Patient 5) did develop iADAs; however, the last assessed iADA titer was < 6. This patient achieved a treated lysoGb3 of 24.2 nmol/L. In the three other patients (Patients 1, 3, and 4), the maximum iADA titer was > 100 and they reached a treated lysoGb3 of 35.8, 35.1, and 40.7 nmol/L. In the control group (Figure 5B) three patients remained seronegative and six patients developed iADAs, of which three patients tolerized. Only one patient had iADA titers higher than 100 (Patient 7). All nine patients, independent from iADA titer, reached a treated lysoGb3 lower than 20 nmol/L.

Discussion

In this study, the stepwise dose escalation protocol at ERT initiation does not seem to prevent the development of iADAs against agalsidase‐beta in classical male FD patients. However, none of the patients that followed the stepwise dose escalation protocol experienced IARs, compared to half using the standard ERT initiation approach.

In general, iADA development is irreversible and only a small number of patients tolerize [8]. In the current study, higher iADA titers (> 100 using this study's assay) are associated with a lower chance to tolerize. Furthermore, based on the longitudinal data of 17 patients, we observed that most patients with iADA titers levels above 100 had a reduced biochemical response to ERT. This is in line with earlier observations on the relationship between the presence of high iADA titers and a less robust reduction in plasma lysoGb3 levels in response to ERT [10, 28, 29]. Higher untreated plasma lysoGb3 levels are associated with worse clinical outcomes [30]. Examples are worse renal outcomes (steeper eGFR decline and faster increase in albuminuria), cardiac outcomes (faster increase in left ventricular mass, left atrial volume index and higher relative wall thickness on echocardiography), and a faster increase of the Fazekas score on MRI brain [31]. Therefore, the goal is to reach a treated plasma lysoGb3 as low as possible. However, the exact magnitude of the impact of iADAs on ERT effectiveness is difficult to determine as FD is a very slowly progressive disorder and many other factors, such as age and disease stage at ERT initiation, additional cardiovascular risk factors, and type of ERT influence therapeutic response [6, 27].

Besides the reduced ERT effectiveness, the presence of iADAs is also associated with the occurrence of IARs and this relationship seems to be independent of the iADA titer level. In our cohort, almost half of the male FD patients with classical FD (standard ERT initiation protocol) experienced IARs. All patients that experienced IARs were iADA positive, whereas not all patients with iADAs experienced IARs. Thus, the presence of circulating iADAs in itself is not enough to trigger an IAR. Limgala et al. [32] hypothesized that crosstalk between immune cells can result in an IgE independent mast cell activation causing IARs in FD patients. We hypothesize that the threshold for mast cell activation is higher than the threshold for the induction of iADAs. By reducing the antigen load and gradually increasing it in the stepwise dose escalation protocol, we appear to attenuate mast cell activation. However, this modulation might not be sufficient to prevent the iADA development, suggesting that the level of antigen exposure achieved is adequate to drive iADA formation, but remains below the threshold required to trigger mast cell mediated IARs. A possible explanation for the lack of effect on iADA development is the relatively long time between infusions (at least 7 days), which might be too long in view of the half‐life time of agalsidase‐beta (t 1/2 = 45–100 min) [4]. It is hypothesized that a tolerance state lasts only 3–4 half‐lives of a drug [33]. Consequently, in FD patients, after cessation of agalsidase‐beta administration, the tolerance state would be lost within a day. Data on patients that will start treatment with pegunigalsidase‐alfa which has a half‐life of ~53–134 h [5], will give insights in the potential for tolerance induction with longer circulating antigens.

Since the protocol consists of a slow buildup of ERT dose, full dose is not reached until 25 weeks of treatment, while the control group is treated with the full agalsidase‐beta dose from the start. FD is a very slowly progressive disorder with clinical complication development spanning years to decades [34, 35] and it is unlikely that this delay in reaching the full agalsidase‐beta dose has significant impact on the expected biochemical response and the long term clinical outcomes. The difference in follow up time between the intervention and control group is not an explanation for the lack of effect of stepwise dose escalation in preventing iADA development, since in general patients develop both iADAs and IARs within the first year after ERT initiation. Another point that should be mentioned is that the prediction model was applied to a subset of the patients included in our analysis, some of whom were also a part of the training dataset of the prediction model of Van der Veen et al. [22]. However, the purpose of our analysis was not to evaluate the model's performance, but to obtain the predicted risk for these specific patients. Therefore, the overlap of these datasets does not introduce bias.

Based on our study, the stepwise dose escalation protocol used in this study does not seem to be a solution for the problem of iADA formation and the associated decreased ERT efficacy. New approaches to prevent iADAs are needed. Trials with immunosuppressives administered before ERT initiation, in particular those using Bortezumib in Pompe's disease, have shown that tolerance induction for several years might be feasible [36, 37]. Given the significant burden and risks for side effects, this approach might be considered primarily in patients at risk of developing high titers (e.g., > 100) of iADAs in FD, since high iADA titer levels appear to interfere substantially with ERT response. Thus, better ways to predict risk of high iADA titer development in male patients with classical FD are needed.

Until a method to prevent iADA formation is found, stepwise dose escalation might be useful to prevent the occurrence of IARs. The protocol is associated with fewer patients experiencing IARs, however the current study was conducted in one cohort only and the findings should be confirmed by studying more patients, preferably also a second cohort. Since two of the three classical male FD patients with a calculated low risk for iADA development went on to develop IARs in the current study, one might consider using the stepwise dose escalation protocol in all classical male FD patients. For each individual patient, the potential benefits (fewer IARs, preventing premedication use, prolonged infusion duration, and extra hospital visits, and enabling unassisted home infusions) and disadvantages (higher number of infusions in the first 30 weeks of treatment, longer duration till full dose is reached) of the stepwise dose escalation protocol can be discussed and weighed in a shared decision‐making process. As the occurrence of IARs is very low in male patients with the nonclassical phenotype and in female FD patients, using stepwise dose escalation in these groups is not warranted. Our continued research efforts are aimed at establishing the effect of iADAs and of other factors on the therapeutical response to ERT and ways to induce tolerance to prevent the development of iADA against recombinant enzymes in FD.
