Abstract
. We prove interior Hölder regularity and Harnack inequalities for viscosity solutions of a class of degenerate fully nonlinear pseudo- p \displaystyle p -Laplacian equations in nondivergence form. Our main approach is an adaptation of the sliding paraboloid method with anisotropic functions tailored to the coordinatewise degeneracy.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study interior Hölder regularity and Harnack inequalities for viscosity solutions of the following degenerate pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian inequalities in nondivergence form:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\lambda,\Lambda}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}\leq f(x),\\
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\lambda,\Lambda}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)+\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}\geq f(x),\end{cases}\quad\text{in }B_{1},

where
\displaystyle p\geq 0
,
\displaystyle 0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
,
\displaystyle f\in L^{n}(B_{1})
and
\displaystyle\left[|z_{i}|^{p/2}\right]=\operatorname{diag}(|z_{i}|^{p/2})
is the diagonal matrix with entries
\displaystyle|z_{i}|^{p/2}
on the diagonal.

The following are examples that satisfy the aforementioned inequalities.

(1)

the degenerate pseudo-
\displaystyle(p+2)
-Laplacian equation.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{p+2}u(x)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i}\partial_{i}(|\partial_{i}u|^{p+1}\partial_{i}u)=\sum_{i}|\partial_{i}u|^{p}\partial_{ii}u=f(x).

(2)

For some symmetric matrix function
\displaystyle(a_{ij}(x))
with eigenvalues in
\displaystyle[\lambda,\Lambda]
and some
\displaystyle|b_{i}(x)|\leq\Lambda
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}(x)|\partial_{i}u|^{p/2}|\partial_{j}u|^{p/2}\partial_{ij}u+\sum_{i}b_{i}(x)\partial_{i}u=f(x).

The pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian equation has long been a central theme in the analysis of partial differential equations and has been the subject of extensive research.
In the divergence form, the pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian arises as the Euler–Lagrange equation corresponding to the anisotropic functional:

\displaystyle\displaystyle w\rightarrow\int\sum_{i}|D_{i}w|^{p}.

The Lipschitz regularity of weak solutions of pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian was established by Bousquet, Brasco, Leone, and Verde
[
6
]
via Moser’s iteration.
For further results of higher regularity of pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian, see
[
4
,
5
,
21
,
8
,
7
]
.
In contrast, the basic regularity results such as Hölder continuity and Harnack inequalities follow from standard methods for the
\displaystyle p
-laplace equation (see Chapter 6 and 7 of
[
17
]
) due to the equivalence of the two integrands

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i}|D_{i}u|^{p}\approx|Du|^{p}.

In the nondivergence setting, Lipschitz regularity for viscosity solutions of pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian was established by Demengel
[
16
]
based on the Ishii-Lions method, and Birindelli and Demengel
[
3
]
proved existence and Lipschitz regularity for a broader class of equations including the pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Pucci’s operator, which is the motivation for the inequalities (
1.1
).
However, the basic regularity theory in nondivergence case does not appear to be standard and differs from that of the divergence case.
The reason is that we do not have the equivalence for the two nondivergence forms:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{p+2}u=\sum_{i}|D_{i}u|^{p}D_{ii}u\not\approx|Du|^{p}\sum_{i}D_{ii}u\approx\Delta_{p+2}u.

For the
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian, the equation is uniformly elliptic where
\displaystyle|Du|>c
for some
\displaystyle c>0
.
Therefore, one can apply the results of Imbert and Silvestre
[
18
]
or Mooney
[
22
]
, who established Hölder regularity and Harnack inequalities for equations which hold only where the gradient is large.

On the other hand, the pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian exhibits coordinatewise degeneracy: it loses ellipticity whenever a partial derivative vanishes, that is, at points where
\displaystyle D_{j}u=0
for some
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
This degeneracy is unbounded and occurs on a set much larger than
\displaystyle\{|Du|=0\}
, so the preceding results do not apply directly.
More precisely, if
\displaystyle D_{j}u(x)=0
for some
\displaystyle j
, the second derivative
\displaystyle D_{jj}u(x)
may become unbounded; in contrast, if there exists a direction
\displaystyle i
with
\displaystyle D_{i}u(x)\neq 0
, then
\displaystyle D_{ii}u(x)
remains controlled and no loss of regularity occurs along the
\displaystyle i
-th direction.
Consequently, discarding data at points where
\displaystyle D_{j}u(x)=0
also neglects the information carried by nondegenerate directions
\displaystyle i
with
\displaystyle D_{i}u(x)\neq 0
, and this prevents us from establishing regularity.
Therefore, it is essential to exploit the information from nondegenerate directions at points of degeneracy.

Despite these difficulties caused by strong anisotropy and coordinatewise degeneracy, we develop the basic regularity of a class of degenerate pseudo-
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian in nondivergence form.
The first main result is Hölder regularity with
\displaystyle L^{n}
data in the right-hand side.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in C(B_{1})
satisfy (
1.1
) in the viscosity sense with
\displaystyle p\geq 0
and
\displaystyle f\in C\cap L^{n}(B_{1})

Then
\displaystyle u\in C^{\alpha}(B_{1/2})
for some
\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha(n,\lambda,\Lambda,p)>0
with the estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{n}(B_{1})}^{\frac{1}{1+p}}\right),

where
\displaystyle C=C(n,\lambda,\Lambda,p)>0
.

We present the second main result, an Harnack inequality with
\displaystyle L^{n}
data in the right-hand side.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in C(B_{1})
be nonnegative and satisfy (
1.1
) in the viscosity sense with
\displaystyle p\geq 0
and
\displaystyle f\in C\cap L^{n}(B_{1})
.
Then
\displaystyle u\in C^{\alpha}(B_{1/2})
for some
\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha(n,\lambda,\Lambda,p)>0
with the estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{B_{1/2}}u\leq C\left(\inf_{B_{1/2}}u+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{n}(B_{1})}^{\frac{1}{1+p}}\right),

where
\displaystyle C=C(n,\lambda,\Lambda,p)>0
.

We now outline the main ideas and novelties of our proof.
Our primary tool is the sliding paraboloid method, originally introduced by Cabre
[
11
]
and developed by Savin
[
23
]
.
We slide the paraboloid from below or above until it touches the graph of a solution
\displaystyle u
under consideration.
Then the touching points have some good properties and by applying the area formula, the measure of touching points can be estimated in terms of the measure of the corresponding vertex points.
This approach has been successfully employed to prove the Krylov-Safanov results and
\displaystyle W^{2,\delta}
regularity results for elliptic and parabolic equations.
See also
[
20
,
22
,
1
,
26
,
25
,
14
,
13
,
19
]
.
For degenerate equations, by choosing a suitable functions such as cusps
\displaystyle|x|^{1/2}
in
[
18
,
2
,
9
]
, or other power functions
\displaystyle|x|^{1+\alpha}
in
[
10
,
24
]
instead of the standard paraboloid
\displaystyle|x|^{2}
, it is possible to handle the degeneracy of the equations.
In our setting, we take the following anisotropic function as our ‘paraboloid’:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varphi(x)=-\frac{1+p}{2+p}\left(|x_{1}|^{1+\frac{1}{1+p}}+\cdots+|x_{n}|^{1+\frac{1}{1+p}}\right),

to overcome the degeneracy of the equations.
The motivation of the choice of
\displaystyle\varphi
is that it satisfies the following identity:
\displaystyle-\left[|D_{i}\varphi|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}\varphi\left[|D_{i}\varphi|^{p/2}\right]=I_{n}
.
It should be noted, however, that
\displaystyle\varphi(x)
fails to be
\displaystyle C^{2}
on the set
\displaystyle\bigcup\{z_{i}=0\}
, which causes additional difficulties in the proof.

To explain this in more detail, we slide a paraboloid
\displaystyle\varphi(z-y)
with vertex
\displaystyle y
from below until it touches
\displaystyle u
at
\displaystyle x
.
Since
\displaystyle Du(x)=D\varphi(x-y)
, we have

y_{i}=x_{i}+|D_{i}u|^{p}D_{i}u(x).

for each
\displaystyle i=\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
Therefore, by differentiating with respect to
\displaystyle x
and taking determinants, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\det(D_{x}y)=\det(I+(p+1)\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]).

From the fact
\displaystyle D^{2}u(x)\geq D^{2}\varphi(x-y)
and using the equalities (
1.1
), we deduce that
\displaystyle\det(D_{x}y)\leq C(1+|f(x)|^{n})
, and then apply area formula to get the desired result.
Note that this argument can be applied where
\displaystyle D^{2}\varphi(x-y)
is well-defined.

If the inequalities degenerate at a point
\displaystyle x
, for example only on
\displaystyle n
-th direction, say
\displaystyle D_{n}u(x)=0
and
\displaystyle D_{i}u(x)\neq 0
for
\displaystyle i\neq n
, then we have
\displaystyle x_{n}-y_{n}=0
which causes
\displaystyle D_{nn}\varphi(x-y)
to blow up.
In this case, we discard
\displaystyle n
-th direction and carry out the argument in the
remaining nondegenerate directions by restricting the argument to the
\displaystyle(n-1)
-dimensional slice
\displaystyle H=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:z_{n}=y_{n}\}
.
Observe that the restricted solution
\displaystyle u_{\{n\}}
also touches the restricted paraboloid
\displaystyle\varphi_{\{n\}}(x-y)
at
\displaystyle x
and the restricted map
\displaystyle x\rightarrow y
on
\displaystyle H
is well-defined since
\displaystyle x_{n}=y_{n}
.
Moreover, the restricted
\displaystyle\varphi_{\{n\}}(x-y)
as a function on the
\displaystyle(n-1)
-dimensional space
\displaystyle H
is smooth at
\displaystyle x
since
\displaystyle x_{i}-y_{i}\neq 0
for
\displaystyle i\neq n
.
Also, putting
\displaystyle D_{n}u(x)=0
, the inequalities (
1.1
) in
\displaystyle n
dimensions reduce to the same form of the inequalities in
\displaystyle(n-1)
dimensions.
Hence, by repeating the above argument with the dimension reduced from
\displaystyle n
to
\displaystyle(n-1)
and then applying the Fubini’s theorem to sum over the slices, we obtain the desired result, Lemma
3.1
.

We next construct a barrier function, which is needed to establish the doubling property, Lemma
4.1
.
By taking the standard barrier function
\displaystyle\Phi(x)=|x|^{-a}
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle a>0
, one can verify that it is a subsolution in the sense that
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}\Phi|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}\Phi\left[|D_{i}\Phi|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|D\Phi|^{p+1}>1
.
If we consider
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
data of
\displaystyle f
with
\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<1
, then the doubling property follows directly from the comparison principle.
However, this approach fails when only
\displaystyle L^{n}
data for
\displaystyle f
are available.
Instead, we slide the barrier and use the area formula in order to measure the set of touching points by the set of vertex points as in Lemma
3.1
.
Our selection of the barrier function is the following anisotropic function

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(x)=|\varphi(x)|^{-a}=\left(|x_{1}|^{1+\frac{1}{1+p}}+\cdots+|x_{n}|^{1+\frac{1}{1+p}}\right)^{-a},

for sufficiently large
\displaystyle a>0
.
The reason for using
\displaystyle|\varphi(x)|
instead of
\displaystyle|x|
is that it matches with the degeneracy of the equation.
We also note that
\displaystyle\Phi(x)
fails to be
\displaystyle C^{2}
on the set
\displaystyle\bigcup\{z_{i}=0\}
, so that a similar restriction argument at degenerate points, as in Lemma
3.1
, is required.
Once Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
4.1
is proved, the proofs of Hölder regularity and Harnack inequalities follow by standard arguments (see
[
12
]
or
[
18
]
).

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we introduce the notation and preliminaries.
In Section
3
we prove the measure estimate, Lemma
3.1
.
In Section
4
we prove the doubling property, Lemma
4.1
, and complete our proof of the main theorems.

\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

3.
Measure estimate

Now we prove the following measure estimate lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
\displaystyle p\geq 0
, there exist
\displaystyle\delta<1
,
\displaystyle\mu<1
and
\displaystyle M>1
depending on
\displaystyle n,\lambda,\Lambda
and
\displaystyle p
such that if
\displaystyle u\in C(Q_{1})
satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}u\geq 0\text{ in }Q_{1},\\
\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}\leq f(x)\text{ in }Q_{1},\\
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{n}(Q_{1})}\leq\mu\\
|\{u>M\}\cap Q_{1}|\geq(1-\delta)|Q_{1}|,\end{cases}

then
\displaystyle u>1
in
\displaystyle Q_{1/4n}
.

Proof.

By considering the inf convolution of
\displaystyle u
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)=\inf_{y\in\overline{Q_{1}}}\left(u(y)+\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\epsilon}\right),

we may assume that
\displaystyle u
is semiconcave and twice differentiable almost everywhere (See
[
12
,
18
]
).
We argue by a contradiction assuming there exists a point
\displaystyle x_{0}\in Q_{1/4n}
such that
\displaystyle u(x_{0})\leq 1
.

We define the vertex set
\displaystyle V=\{u>M\}\cap Q_{1/4n}
for some
\displaystyle M>1
to be chosen later, and slide the paraboloid with vertex in
\displaystyle V
from below until it first touches the graph of
\displaystyle u
.
Our choice of the ‘paraboloid’ is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varphi(x)=-K\frac{1+p}{2+p}\left(|x_{1}|^{1+\frac{1}{1+p}}+\cdots+|x_{n}|^{1+\frac{1}{1+p}}\right)

for some
\displaystyle K>1
to be determined later.
Note that
\displaystyle\varphi
is
\displaystyle C^{1}
, but not
\displaystyle C^{2}
in
\displaystyle n
hyperplanes
\displaystyle\{z_{i}=0\}
for
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
Explicitly, we define the touching point set as

\displaystyle\displaystyle T=\{x\in\overline{Q_{1}}:\exists y\in V\text{ such that }u(x)-\varphi(x-y)=\min_{z\in\overline{Q_{1}}}u(z)-\varphi(z-y)\}.

Note that for
\displaystyle y\in V\subset Q_{1/4n}
and
\displaystyle z\in\partial Q_{1}
, we have
\displaystyle u(z)-\varphi(z-y)\geq K\frac{1+p}{2+p}\left(1-\frac{1}{4n}\right)^{2}
since
\displaystyle u\geq 0
.
On the other hand, we obtain
\displaystyle u(x_{0})-\varphi(x_{0}-y)\leq 1+K\frac{1+p}{2+p}n\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)<K\frac{1+p}{2+p}\left(1-\frac{1}{4n}\right)^{2}=:M
by choosing a large
\displaystyle K>1
.
Hence, the minimum cannot be attained on the boundary
\displaystyle\partial Q_{1}
and thus
\displaystyle T\subset Q_{1}
.
Moreover, it follows that
\displaystyle T\subset\{u<M\}\cap Q_{1}
.

By the differentiability of
\displaystyle u
, for each
\displaystyle x\in T
there is a unique vertex point
\displaystyle y\in V
such that
\displaystyle\varphi(z-y)
touches
\displaystyle u
from below at
\displaystyle x
.
We therefore define the mapping
\displaystyle m:T\rightarrow V
which associates each touching point
\displaystyle x
with its corresponding vertex
\displaystyle y
.
Moreover, we get

(3.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{i}u(x)=D_{i}\varphi(x-y)=-K|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{\frac{1}{1+p}}\frac{x_{i}-y_{i}}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{2}u(x)\geq D^{2}\varphi(x-y)=-\frac{K}{1+p}\left[|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\frac{p}{p+1}}\right].

Observe that
\displaystyle D^{2}\varphi(x-y)
blows up when
\displaystyle x-y\in\bigcup\{z_{i}=0\}
.
By (
3.1
) we can write the map
\displaystyle m
explicitly as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle y_{i}=x_{i}+\frac{1}{K^{1+p}}|D_{i}u|^{p}D_{i}u(x).

We want to prove that
\displaystyle Du
is Lipschitz in
\displaystyle T
in order to apply the area formula to the map
\displaystyle m:x\mapsto y
as in
[
18
]
.
However, at critical points where
\displaystyle|x_{i}-y_{i}|=0
for some
\displaystyle i
, the Hessian
\displaystyle D^{2}\varphi(x-y)
blows up, making it difficult to prove the Lipschitz continuity and the inequality (
3.2
) also cannot be used.
In order to deal with this difficulty, we decompose the touching point set according to whether each coordinate direction is degenerate or non-degenerate.
For
\displaystyle I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
and
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, we define

(3.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}T^{\epsilon}_{I}&=\{x\in T:|x_{i}-y_{i}|>\epsilon\ \text{ for }\ i\in I,\ \ |x_{j}-y_{j}|=0\ \text{ for }\ j\notin I\},\\
T_{I}&=\{x\in T:|x_{i}-y_{i}|>0\ \text{ for }\ i\in I,\ \ |x_{j}-y_{j}|=0\ \text{ for }\ j\notin I\},\end{split}

where
\displaystyle y=m(x)
and define
\displaystyle V^{\epsilon}_{I}=m(T^{\epsilon}_{I})
and
\displaystyle V_{I}=m(T_{I})
.
Then for
\displaystyle 0<\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}
, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}T^{\epsilon_{2}}_{I}\subset T^{\epsilon_{1}}_{I},\quad T_{I}=\bigcup_{\epsilon>0}T^{\epsilon}_{I},\quad T=\bigsqcup_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}T_{I},\\
V^{\epsilon_{2}}_{I}\subset V^{\epsilon_{1}}_{I},\quad V_{I}=\bigcup_{\epsilon>0}V^{\epsilon}_{I},\quad V=\bigcup_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}V_{I}.\end{split}

Our goal is to prove
\displaystyle|V_{I}|\leq C(|T_{I}|+\mu^{n})
for each
\displaystyle I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
by applying the area formula.

Case 1 :
\displaystyle I=\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

For simplicity, we denote
\displaystyle T_{\{1,\cdots,n\}}=T_{n}
and
\displaystyle V_{\{1,\cdots,n\}}=V_{n}
.
Let
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
be a small number.
For
\displaystyle x\in T_{n}^{\epsilon}
and
\displaystyle y=m(x)
, there exists a paraboloid
\displaystyle\varphi(z-y)+C_{0}
that touches
\displaystyle u
from below at

4.
barrier function and doubling property

In this section, we prove the doubling property
by using a barrier function.
For brevity, for
\displaystyle x=(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle b>0
, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\displaystyle|x|_{b}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{b}\in\mathbb{R},\quad x^{b}:=(|x_{i}|^{b-1}x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This convention will be used throughout this section.
Note that
\displaystyle D(|x|_{b})=bx^{b-1}

\displaystyle D(x^{b})=b\left[|x_{i}|^{b-1}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{ab}|x|_{b}^{-a}=\frac{1}{ab}\left(|x_{1}|^{b}+\cdots+|x_{n}|^{b}\right)^{-a},

\displaystyle a>1
is large enough and
\displaystyle b=1+\frac{1}{1+p}

The choice of
\displaystyle b
is related to the ‘paraboloid’

introduced in Section
3

Observe that
\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-|x|_{b}^{-(a+1)}x^{b-1},

\displaystyle\displaystyle=b(a+1)|x|_{b}^{-(a+2)}(x^{b-1}\otimes x^{b-1})-(b-1)|x|_{b}^{-(a+1)}\left[|x_{i}|^{b-2}\right].

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \ -\left.(b-1)\left[|x_{i}|^{b-2+(b-1)p}\right]\right)

\displaystyle x
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{x}y

\displaystyle\displaystyle=I-D((D\Phi)^{-1})(Du(x))\cdot D^{2}u(x).

Note that
\displaystyle D((D\Phi)^{-1})\circ D\Phi(z)=(D^{2}\Phi)^{-1}(z)
by the inverse function theorem.
By (
4.3
), we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle D((D\Phi)^{-1})(Du(x))=D((D\Phi)^{-1})\circ D\Phi(x-y)=(D^{2}\Phi)^{-1}(x-y),

and so

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{x}y

\displaystyle\displaystyle=I-(D^{2}\Phi)^{-1}(x-y)\cdot D^{2}u(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(D^{2}\Phi)^{-1}(x-y)\cdot(D^{2}\Phi(x-y)-D^{2}u(x))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=D((D\Phi)^{-1})(Du(x))\cdot(D^{2}\Phi(x-y)-D^{2}u(x)).

Recall that
\displaystyle D^{2}\Phi(x-y)
is well defined since
\displaystyle x\in T^{\epsilon}_{n}
.
By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle(D\Phi)^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-K^{\frac{1}{ab+1}}|x|^{-\frac{a+1}{ab+1}}_{\frac{b}{b-1}}x^{\frac{1}{b-1}}=-K^{\frac{1}{ab+1}}|x|^{-\frac{a+1}{ab+1}}_{2+p}x^{1+p},

\displaystyle\displaystyle D((D\Phi)^{-1})(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-K^{\frac{1}{ab+1}}|x|^{-\frac{a+1}{ab+1}}_{2+p}\left[|x_{i}|^{p/2}\right]B(x)\left[|x_{i}|^{p/2}\right],

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle B(x)=\left(-\frac{a+1}{ab+1}(2+p)\frac{x^{1+p/2}}{|x|^{1/2}_{2+p}}\otimes\frac{x^{1+p/2}}{|x|^{1/2}_{2+p}}+(1+p)I_{n}\right).

Note that
\displaystyle B(x)
is a bounded matrix in the sense that it satisfies
\displaystyle|B_{ij}(x)|\leq C(a,p)
for any
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which implies
\displaystyle|\det(B(x))|\leq C
.
Using
\displaystyle|Du(x)|>c
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\det(D_{x}y)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=C|Du|^{-n\frac{a+1}{ab+1}}_{2+p}\left|\det\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]B(x)\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right](D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}\Phi(x-y))\right)\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq C\left|\det\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right](D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}\Phi(x-y))\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)\right|=:C|\det(A)|.

Note that by (
4.4
),
\displaystyle A=\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right](D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}\Phi(x-y))\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]
is a nonnegative definite matrix.
Furthermore, using (
4.3
) and (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u(x)\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\left(D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}\Phi(x-y)\right)\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}\Phi|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}\Phi(x-y)\left[|D_{i}\Phi|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|D\Phi|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\lambda\operatorname{tr}(A).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\det(D_{x}y)|=C|\det(A)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A)}{n}\right)^{n}\leq C|f(x)|^{n}.

Applying the area theorem to the mapping
\displaystyle m:T^{\epsilon}_{n}\rightarrow V^{\epsilon}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|V_{n}^{\epsilon}|=\int_{T^{\epsilon}_{n}}|\det D_{x}y|\ dx\leq\int_{T^{\epsilon}_{n}}Cf(x)^{n}\ dx\leq C\mu^{n}.

By letting
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we obtain
\displaystyle|V_{n}|\leq C\mu^{n}
.

Case 2 :
\displaystyle I=\emptyset
.
By the same argument as in Lemma
3.1
,
\displaystyle V_{\emptyset}=T_{\emptyset}=\emptyset
.

Case 3 :
\displaystyle\emptyset\subsetneq I\subsetneq\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

Let
\displaystyle k=|I|
,
\displaystyle J=\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus I
, and let
\displaystyle(a_{j})_{j\in J}\in Q^{n-k}_{1/8n}
be
\displaystyle|J|
numbers satisfying
\displaystyle a_{j}\in(-1/8n,1/8n)
.
We define the
\displaystyle k
-dimensional slice
\displaystyle H(a_{j})=\bigcap_{j\in J}\{z_{j}=a_{j}\}
, and restrict the functions
\displaystyle u,\Phi,m
and the sets
\displaystyle T^{\epsilon}_{I},V^{\epsilon}_{I}
on
\displaystyle H(a_{j})\cap Q_{1/8n}^{n}
, denoting them by the same notations as in Lemma
3.1
.
Note that if
\displaystyle x\in T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})
, then
\displaystyle y=m(x)\in V^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})
since
\displaystyle x_{j}=y_{j}=a_{j}
for
\displaystyle j\in J
.
Thus, the restricted map
\displaystyle m_{(a_{j})}:T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})\rightarrow V^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})
is well defined.
Moreover, the restricted barrier
\displaystyle\Phi_{(a_{j})}(z-y)
touches
\displaystyle u_{(a_{j})}
from below at
\displaystyle z=x
, by which

\displaystyle\displaystyle Du_{(a_{j})}(x)=D\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\quad D^{2}u_{(a_{j})}(x)\geq D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)\in S(k).

Since
\displaystyle\Phi_{(a_{j})}(z-y)
is smooth at
\displaystyle z=x
and
\displaystyle u
is semiconcave, it follows by the same argument as in Lemma
3.1
that
\displaystyle Du_{(a_{j})}
and
\displaystyle m_{(a_{j})}
are Lipschitz continuous in
\displaystyle T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x-(D\Phi_{(a_{j})})^{-1}(Du_{(a_{j})}(x)).

Differentiating the above equation with respect to
\displaystyle x
and using the same argument as in
Case 1
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{x}y=D((D\Phi_{(a_{j})})^{-1})(Du_{(a_{j})}(x))\cdot(D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)-D^{2}u_{(a_{j})}(x)).

Recall that
\displaystyle D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)
is well defined since
\displaystyle x\in T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})
.
Note that we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle D((D\Phi_{(a_{j})})^{-1})(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-K^{\frac{1}{ab+1}}|x|^{-\frac{a+1}{ab+1}}_{2+p}\left[|x_{i}|^{p/2}\right]B(x)\left[|x_{i}|^{p/2}\right],

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle B(x)=\left(-\frac{a+1}{ab+1}(2+p)\frac{x^{1+p/2}}{|x|^{1/2}_{2+p}}\otimes\frac{x^{1+p/2}}{|x|^{1/2}_{2+p}}+(1+p)I_{k}\right),

which satisfies
\displaystyle|\det(B(x))|\leq C
.
Using
\displaystyle|Du(x)|>c
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\det(D_{x}y)|\leq C\left|\det\left(\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right](D^{2}u_{(a_{j})}(x)-D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y))\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\right)\right|=:C|\det(A)|

with
\displaystyle A=\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\left(D^{2}u_{(a_{j})}(x)-D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)\right)\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\geq 0
.
Moreover, by the same calculation as in (
4.1
) replacing
\displaystyle n
by
\displaystyle k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}\Phi_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}\left[|D_{i}\Phi_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|D\Phi_{(a_{j})}|^{p+1}>1.

Recalling that
\displaystyle|D_{j}u(x)|=0
for
\displaystyle j\in J
, we find by (
3
) that

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{(a_{j})}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u(x)\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u_{(a_{j})}(x)\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du_{(a_{j})}|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\left(D^{2}u_{(a_{j})}(x)-D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)\right)\left[|D_{i}u_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}\Phi_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}\Phi_{(a_{j})}(x-y)\left[|D_{i}\Phi_{(a_{j})}|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|D\Phi_{(a_{j})}|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\lambda\operatorname{tr}(A).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\det(D_{x}y)|=C|\det(A)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A)}{k}\right)^{k}\leq C|f_{(a_{j})}(x)|^{k}.

Therefore, by Fubini’s theorem and the area formula applied to
\displaystyle m_{(a_{j})}:T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})\rightarrow V^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|V^{\epsilon}_{I}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{1/8n}^{n-k}}|V_{I}^{\epsilon}(a_{j})|\ da_{J}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{1/8n}^{n-k}}\int_{T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})}|\det D_{x}y|\ dx_{I}da_{J}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q_{1/8n}^{n-k}}\int_{T^{\epsilon}_{I}(a_{j})}C|f_{(a_{j})}(x)|^{k}\ dx_{I}da_{J}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{T^{\epsilon}_{I}}C|f(x)|^{k}\ dx\leq C\mu^{n}.

Letting
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain
\displaystyle|V_{I}|\leq C\mu^{n}
.

Conclusion.

Therefore, we have
\displaystyle|V_{I}|\leq C\mu^{n}
for every
\displaystyle I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
Using (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|Q_{1/8n}|=|V|\leq\sum_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}|V^{\epsilon}_{I}|\leq C2^{n}\mu^{n}.

This leads to a contradiction if we choose
\displaystyle\mu>0
sufficiently small, depending on
\displaystyle n,\lambda,\Lambda,p
.
∎

By combining the two lemmas above, Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
4.1
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.1
.

For
\displaystyle p\geq 0
, there exist
\displaystyle\delta<1
,
\displaystyle\mu<1
and
\displaystyle M>1
such that for any
\displaystyle k\geq 1,r\leq 1
and
\displaystyle u\in C(Q_{6nr})
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}u\geq 0\text{ in }Q_{6nr},\\
\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}\leq f(x)\text{ in }Q_{6nr},\\
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{n}(Q_{6nr})}\leq\mu,\\
|\{u>kM\}\cap Q_{r}|\geq(1-\delta)|Q_{r}|,\end{cases}

then
\displaystyle u>k
in
\displaystyle Q_{3r}
.

Proof.

Write
\displaystyle M=M_{1}M_{2}
where
\displaystyle M_{1}
is the constant in Lemma
3.1
and
\displaystyle M_{2}
is the constant in Lemma
4.1
.
Then by Remark
2.1
,
\displaystyle v(x)=\frac{u(rx)}{kM_{2}}\in C(Q_{6n})
satisfies all the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
, and so
\displaystyle v>1
in
\displaystyle Q_{1/4}
, which implies
\displaystyle u_{r}(x)=\frac{u(rx)}{k}>M_{2}
.
Applying Lemma
4.1
to
\displaystyle u_{r}
, we obtain
\displaystyle u>k
in
\displaystyle Q_{3r}
.
∎

Using the above corollary and Calderón-Zygmund cube decomposition (Lemma
2.1
), we have the following
\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}
estimate (See
[

Theorem 4.1
.

For
\displaystyle p\geq 0
, there exist
\displaystyle\delta<1
,
\displaystyle\epsilon<1
and
\displaystyle\mu<1
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in C(Q_{6nr})
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}u\geq 0\text{ in }Q_{6n},\\
\mathcal{M}^{-}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p/2}\right]\right)-\Lambda|Du|^{p+1}\leq f(x)\text{ in }Q_{6n},\\
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{n}(Q_{6n})}\leq\mu,\\
\inf_{Q_{3}}u\leq 1,\end{cases}

then for any
\displaystyle t>0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\{u>t\}\cap Q_{1}|<Ct^{-\epsilon}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the following inequality

(4.6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\{u>M^{i}\}\cap Q_{1}|<C(1-\delta)^{i}.

Let
\displaystyle E_{i}=\{u>M^{i}\}\cap Q_{1}
.
By
\displaystyle E_{i}\subset E_{1}
and Corollary
4.1
with
\displaystyle k=r=1
, we have
\displaystyle|E_{i}|\leq|E_{1}|\leq(1-\delta)|Q_{1}|
.
Moreover, for any dyadic cube
\displaystyle Q=Q_{r}(x_{0})
satisfying
\displaystyle|E_{i}\cap Q|>\delta|Q|
, by applying Corollary
4.1
with
\displaystyle k=M^{i-1}
, we obtain
\displaystyle\tilde{Q}\subset Q_{3r}(x_{0})\subset E_{i-1}
where
\displaystyle\tilde{Q}
is the precedessor of
\displaystyle Q
.
Therefore, by Lemma
2.1
, we have
\displaystyle|E_{i}|\leq(1-\delta)|E_{i-1}|
, which implies (
4.6
) and finishes the proof.
∎
