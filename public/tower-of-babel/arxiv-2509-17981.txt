An adaptive Hermite spectral method for the Boltzmann equation Thanks: August 10, 2026.
Sihong Shao, Yanli Wang, Jie Wu

Abstract
We propose an adaptive Hermite spectral method for the three-dimensional velocity space of the Boltzmann equation guided by a newly developed frequency indicator. For the homogeneous problem, the indicator is defined by the contribution of high-order coefficients in the spectral expansion. For the non-homogeneous problem, a Fourier-Hermite scheme is employed, with the corresponding frequency indicator formulated based on distributions across the entire spatial domain. The adaptive Hermite method includes scaling and p p -adaptive techniques to dynamically adjust the scaling factor and expansion order according to the indicator. Numerical experiments cover both homogeneous and non-homogeneous problems in up to three spatial dimensions. Results demonstrate that the scaling adaptive method substantially reduces L 2 L^{2} errors at negligible computational cost, and the p p -adaptive method achieves time savings of up to 74 % 74\% .

Hermite spectral method, adaptive method, Boltzmann equation, high-dimensional numerical method, unbounded domain

Introduction

The Boltzmann equation, as a fundamental governing equation in kinetic theory, has wide-ranging applications in rarefied gas dynamics
[
2
,
9
]
as well as in related fields such as semiconductor transport
[
1
]
. However, its inherent high dimensionality and quadratic collision model pose significant challenges for numerical simulation. Historically, a variety of numerical schemes have been proposed, including both stochastic and deterministic approaches. Among the stochastic methods, the most representative one is the direct simulation Monte Carlo method
[
2
]
, which has low complexity but usually suffers from stochastic fluctuations. The deterministic methods
[
10
]
, including the discrete velocity models
[
12
]
, Fourier spectral methods
[
19
,
20
]
, Hermite spectral method
[
26
,
15
]
, mapped Chebyshev spectral method
[
14
]
, and so on, often face high complexity in high-dimensional simulations. Existing deterministic results for three-dimensional problems remain limited
[
9
]
. In this work, we develop a scale-
p
-adaptive Hermite method for the Boltzmann equation that reduces error as well as computational cost, thereby enabling the efficient simulation of fully three-dimensional problems.

Adaptive scaling techniques for the Hermite spectral method have been studied for a long time. A strategy for selecting the scaling factor based on the support of the target function is proposed in
[
24
]
, which inspires the subsequent works
[
22
,
13
]
. Recently, an adaptive spectral method, incorporating moving, scaling, and
p
-adaptive techniques, has been proposed for problems on unbounded domains in
[
27
,
28
,
7
]
. Specifically, a frequency indicator measuring the proportion of the high-order coefficients in the spectral expansion has been introduced for the scaling and
p
-adaptive techniques
[
27
,
28
]
, with successful applications to various problems
[
8
]
. This progress inspires our work.

When the Hermite spectral method is applied to the velocity space of the Boltzmann equation, the distribution function is approximated directly in the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The method is naturally conservative and has spectral accuracy. However, a major limitation lies in the high computational cost of evaluating the collision operator, especially when simulating the non-homogeneous problem with large Knudsen numbers. In such regimes, the distribution function is far from local equilibrium, requiring a large expansion order in the Hermite method
[
15
]
and thus leading to substantial computational expense.

Another issue of the Hermite spectral method is that its numerical performance strongly depends on the choice of the expansion center
[
4
]
, particularly the scaling factor. Appropriate choices could reduce the required expansion order, whereas unreasonable choices may even lose convergence. In existing studies, the determination of the expansion center usually depends on prior knowledge or physical properties of the specific problem. For example, the local temperature is chosen as the scaling factor in
[
5
]
. The scaling factor in the Hermite method is fixed as a manually chosen constant
[
26
,
15
]
. An alternative approach based on the highest moment is proposed in
[
4
]
, where the steady-state shock structure problem with high Mach number is studied with convergence results reported. Additionally, the physically dependent scaling factor is also employed in
[
11
]
to enhance convergence when applying the Hermite spectral method to Vlasov equations. Meanwhile, discussion of the adaptive expansion order in kinetic equations remains insufficient. A
p
-adaptive Hermite spectral method is proposed in
[
25
]
for the Vlasov equation, where the Hermite expansion order is adjusted based on the value of the highest order coefficient.

Inspired by the recent works
[
27
,
28
]
, we propose an adaptive Hermite spectral method for the Boltzmann equation to adaptively adjust the expansion order and scaling factor. For homogeneous problems, a frequency indicator is defined based on the high-order Hermite expansion coefficients. The
p
- and scaling adaptive methods are brought up by adjusting the expansion order and scaling factor according to the frequency indicator. A projection algorithm is constructed to realize the transform between Hermite expansions with different scaling centers, which maintains the conservation of the method. Then, a Fourier-Hermite scheme is employed for the non-homogeneous problem. Corresponding frequency indicator and adaptive methods are also constructed by taking the distributions on all collocation points into account. The proposed framework selects scaling factors more effectively, thereby improving the efficiency of the Hermite spectral method.

In the numerical experiments, three spatially homogeneous problems are first considered. The results confirm that the scaling adaptive method accurately captures the evolution of the distribution while significantly reducing the
L^{2}
error.
A mixed-Gaussian problem in which the non-adaptive Hermite method fails to converge is also examined, whereas the proposed scale-
p
-adaptive method achieves spectral convergence and reduces computational time by approximately half. Subsequently, spatially non-homogeneous problems are investigated, including two one-dimensional problems, the two-dimensional Taylor-Green vortex problem, and the three-dimensional Hexa-Gaussian perturbation problem. The reduction of error and the saving in computational time demonstrate the high efficiency of this adaptive method.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The basic properties of the Boltzmann equation, the discretization of the microscopic velocity space, and the approximation of the quadratic collision model are first introduced in Sec.
2
. The
p
- and scaling adaptive method for the homogeneous problem is presented in Sec.
3
, and the adaptive Hermite method for the non-homogeneous problem is proposed in Sec.
4
. Several numerical experiments are displayed in Sec.
5
to validate this adaptive Hermite spectral method. This paper concludes with some future works and details in Sec.
6
and supplementary materials.

2
Preliminaries

This section begins with a brief introduction to the dimensionless Boltzmann equation. Then, the discretization of the microscopic velocity space in the framework of the Hermite spectral method is presented, followed by the detailed approximation of the Boltzmann collision term.

2.1
Boltzmann equation

In kinetic theory, the state of a large number of particles is described by a distribution function
f(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})
defined on a six-dimensional phase space, where
t
represents time,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
represent the spatial position and the microscopic velocity, respectively. The Boltzmann equation governs the time evolution of
f
by

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\bm{v}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Q[f,f],\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\bm{x}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(1)

where
\varepsilon
is the Knudsen number. The Boltzmann collision term
Q
accounts for binary collisions between particles:

Q[f,f](\bm{v})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}[f(\bm{v}_{*}^{\prime})f(\bm{v}^{\prime})-f(\bm{v}_{*})f(\bm{v})]B(|\bm{g}|,\chi)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{*}.

(2)

In this context,
\bm{g}=\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{*}
is the relative velocity, and
\chi=\arccos\left(\bm{g}\cdot\bm{\omega}/|\bm{g}|\right)
is the scattering angle. The unit vector
\bm{\omega}
denotes the direction of the post-collisional relative velocity. The post-collisional velocities
\bm{v}^{\prime}
and
\bm{v}_{*}^{\prime}
can be expressed as

\bm{v}^{\prime}=(\bm{v}+\bm{v}_{*}+|\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{*}|\bm{\omega})/2,\qquad\bm{v}_{*}^{\prime}=(\bm{v}+\bm{v}_{*}-|\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{*}|\bm{\omega})/2.

(3)

These expressions are derived from the microscopic conservation laws of momentum and energy. The specific form of the collision kernel
B(|\bm{g}|,\chi)
depends on the collision model under consideration. In this work, we will focus on the inverse power law (IPL) model
[
2
]
, where the inter-particle force is inversely proportional to the
\eta
-th power of their distance. The kernel then takes the following form:

B(|\bm{g}|,\chi)=C_{\rm IPL}|\bm{g}|^{\frac{\eta-5}{\eta-1}}\frac{W_{0}}{\sin\chi}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}W_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\chi}\right|,\hskip 18.49988pt\eta>3,

(4)

where
C_{\rm IPL}
is a constant given by nondimensionalization
[
15
]
and
W_{0}
is a positive impact parameter independent of
|\bm{g}|
. Detailed relation between
W_{0}
and
\chi
can be found in literatures
[
2
,
15
]
. Within the IPL model, cases with
\eta>5
are referred to as “hard potentials”, while those with
\eta<5
are called “soft potentials”. When
\eta=5
, the kernel
B
becomes independent of
|\bm{g}|
; this special case is known as the “Maxwell molecules” model.

The collision operator
Q
preserves mass, momentum, and energy as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(\bm{v})Q[f,f]\mathrm{d}\bm{v}=0,\qquad\phi(\bm{v})=1,\bm{v},|\bm{v}|^{2}/2.

(5)

Another important property of the collision operator
Q
is Boltzmann’s
H
-theorem, which states that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q[f,f]\ln f\mathrm{d}\bm{v}\leqslant 0.

(6)

The equality holds if and only if
f
reaches the equilibrium named Maxwellian

\mathcal{M}(\bm{v})=\frac{\rho}{(2\pi\theta)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}}{2\theta}\right),

(7)

with the dimensionless macroscopic density
\rho
, velocity
\bm{u}
and temperature
\theta

\displaystyle\rho(t,\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\quad\bm{u}(t,\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bm{v}f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\quad\theta(t,\bm{x})=\frac{1}{3\rho}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}f\mathrm{d}\bm{v}.

(8)

In addition, other macroscopic variables of interest can be derived from
f
. Let
\bm{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
and
\bm{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
, we define the stress tensor and heat flux as

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}(t,\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left((v_{i}-u_{i})(v_{j}-u_{j})-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{3}|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}\right)f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\qquad i,j=1,2,3,

(9)

\displaystyle q_{i}(t,\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(v_{i}-u_{i})|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}f\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\qquad i=1,2,3.

(10)

2.2
Discretization of the microscopic velocity space

In this section, we introduce the discretization of the microscopic velocity space in the framework of the Hermite spectral method. As a generalization of the Hermite spectral method for the Boltzmann operator
[
26
]
, the scaled asymmetrically-weighted Hermite functions are adopted as the basis. The one-dimensional form reads

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(v)=\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{(2\pi)^{1/4}\sqrt{2^{k}k!}}H_{k}\left(\frac{\beta v}{\sqrt{2}}\right){\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta^{2}v^{2}}{2}},\qquad k\geqslant 0,

(11)

where
\beta>0
is a scaling factor. Here,
H_{k}
denotes Hermite polynomials given by Rodrigues’ formula:

H_{k}(v)=(-1)^{k}{\rm e}^{v^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}v^{k}}\left[{\rm e}^{-v^{2}}\right].

(12)

It is evident that
H_{k}
shares the same odd-even parity with
k
, so does
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}
. We also define the scaled Hermite polynomial by dropping the exponential factor from
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}
:

H_{k}^{\beta}(v)=\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{(2\pi)^{1/4}\sqrt{2^{k}k!}}H_{k}\left(\frac{\beta v}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\qquad k\geqslant 0.

(13)

The three-dimensional basis functions are constructed via the tensor product:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v})=\mathcal{H}_{k_{1}}^{\beta}(v_{1})\mathcal{H}_{k_{2}}^{\beta}(v_{2})\mathcal{H}_{k_{3}}^{\beta}(v_{3}),\qquad H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v})=H_{k_{1}}^{\beta}(v_{1})H_{k_{2}}^{\beta}(v_{2})H_{k_{3}}^{\beta}(v_{3}),

(14)

where
k=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
is a multi-index in three dimensions. It also satisfies the orthogonality:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{l}^{\beta}(\bm{v})\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}=\delta_{k_{1}l_{1}}\delta_{k_{2}l_{2}}\delta_{k_{3}l_{3}}.

(15)

where
\delta_{kl}
is the Kronecker Delta symbol. This framework leads naturally to a Petrov-Galerkin method. The distribution function
f
is approximated by the finite expansion

f(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{v})\approx f_{N}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{v})=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,\bm{x})\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-{\bm{\zeta}}),

(16)

where
N
denotes the expansion order,
{\bm{\zeta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the moving center, and
|k|=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}

[
26
,
15
]
. The number of terms is
(N+1)(N+2)(N+3)/6=\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. The coefficients are computed as

\hat{f}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})f(\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\qquad|k|\geqslant 0.

(17)

Macroscopic physical variables can be derived from the expansion coefficients of
f
as

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{4}}\hat{f}_{{0}}^{\beta}}{\beta^{\frac{3}{2}}},\quad u_{i}=\frac{\hat{f}_{{e}_{i}}^{\beta}}{\beta\hat{f}_{{0}}^{\beta}},\quad\theta=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left[\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\hat{f}_{2{e}_{i}}^{\beta}}{\hat{f}_{{0}}^{\beta}}+1\right)-u_{i}^{2}\right],

(18)

where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
refers to the multi-index vector with
1
in the
i
-th position and
0
elsewhere. For example,
e_{1}=(1,0,0)
.

For the basis function parameters,
{\bm{\zeta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents a moving center, and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a scaling factor that significantly influences the convergence of the truncated series. The parameters
{\bm{\zeta}}
,
\beta
, and the expansion order
N
play a critical role in the accuracy of the approximation to
f
in (
16
). Classically, there are several strategies for choosing
{\bm{\zeta}}
and
\beta
. One common approach sets them according to physical variables by
{\bm{\zeta}}=\bm{u}
, and
\beta=1/\sqrt{\theta}

[
26
]
. Alternatively, they may be chosen as global constants based on the physical properties of the problem
[
15
,
11
]
. However, these choices may be inadequate for distribution functions with significant spatial or temporal variation, potentially resulting in poor or even divergent spectral approximations.

In this work, we propose an adaptive method that dynamically adjusts the expansion parameters
\beta
and
N
based on the evolution of
f
, aiming for improvements in approximation quality. This is achieved by monitoring frequency indicators, which will be introduced in the following sections. As for the moving center
{\bm{\zeta}}
, we follow the method proposed in
[
15
]
, where a globally uniform value is adopted. The development of an adaptive strategy for
{\bm{\zeta}}
is left for future work.

Before introducing the adaptive method, we first present the approximation of the IPL collision model within the Hermite spectral framework in the following section.

2.3
Approximation of the collision operator

Due to the complex form of the quadratic collision term (
2
), the computational cost of approximating it is extremely high. The evaluation of the Hermite expansion coefficients of the collision operator under the IPL model is provided in
[
26
]
, and its generalization to the adaptive Hermite spectral method is rather straightforward. For simplicity, only a sketch of the approximation of the collision operator is given in this section. Detailed derivations and discussions are left to the supplementary material
A
.

Consider approximating the collision term using a truncated series:

\displaystyle Q[f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}](\bm{v})\approx Q_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(\bm{v})=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta}),

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})Q[f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}](\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\quad 0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N.

(20)

The coefficients can be calculated by the following combined model

\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{|i|=0}^{N_{0}}\sum_{|j|=0}^{N_{0}}A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}\hat{f}_{i}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\hat{f}_{j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},&0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N_{0},\\
\nu_{N_{0}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}-\hat{f}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\right),&N_{0}<|k|\leqslant N.\end{cases}

(21)

Here,
N_{0}
is a given constant order for the quadratic collision operator.
\left\{A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}\right\}
is a precomputed tensor.
\nu_{N_{0}}
denotes the spectral radius of the discrete linearized collision operator
[
26
]
.
\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}
denotes the expansion coefficients of the local Maxwellian. The complexity will be
\mathcal{O}(N^{9})
if
N\leqslant N_{0}
, and
\mathcal{O}(N_{0}^{9}+N^{3})
if
N>N_{0}
.

It is known that by appropriately scaling the Hermite basis functions, one can achieve equal or better accuracy with a smaller expansion order
N
. In this way, a significant saving in computation time can be realized when
N<N_{0}
. This is precisely the goal of the adaptive method to be introduced in the next section.

3
Adaptive Hermite spectral method for the homogeneous problem

In this section, the adaptive Hermite method based on the idea presented in
[
27
,
28
]
is studied for the homogeneous setting. The moving center
{\bm{\zeta}}
is fixed as the macroscopic velocity in the homogeneous problem, and it will be omitted temporarily in this section. The Petrov-Galerkin method for the homogeneous Boltzmann equation yields the following ODE system

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{Q}_{k}^{\beta},\qquad 0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N.

(22)

which can be discretized by a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method.

To construct the indicator for both the scaling and
p
-adaptive methods, we adopt a similar approach from
[
28
]
, where a frequency indicator is defined to measure the proportion of high-order terms in the truncated expansion. In general, this indicator has been shown to be closely related to the lower bound of interpolation error. Following this thought, an indicator applicable to both
p
-adaptivity and scaling is constructed. In the framework of the Hermite discretization (
16
), the desired indicator should measure the contribution of high-order terms in the entire expansion. It is therefore defined as

\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](t)\coloneq\left(\frac{\sum\limits_{|k|=N-\bar{N}+1}^{N}\left(\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta}(t)\right)^{2}}{\sum\limits_{|k|=0}^{N}\left(\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta}(t)\right)^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

(23)

with
\bar{N}=\max\left\{\lfloor N/3\rfloor,2\right\}
. That is, the contribution of the highest
\bar{N}
-th order expansion coefficients is monitored.

In the following, the scaling and
p
-adaptive algorithms are developed in sequence. These are applied after each time step to maintain the indicator at a prescribed level by tuning the scaling factor
\beta
and expansion order
N
. The projection algorithm for scaling adjustment is also introduced.

3.1
The
p
-adaptive algorithm

In this section, the detailed
p
-adaptive algorithm is introduced using the indicator (
23
). Based on the spectral convergence behavior with respect to the expansion order
N
, an increasing value of
\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}]
indicates that the current order
N
is insufficient and should be increased. In contrast, a small value of
\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}]
suggests that the expansion order can be decreased by discarding the highest-order expansion coefficients without significantly compromising accuracy, thus reducing computational complexity.

In particular, for the spatially homogeneous Boltzmann equation, the distribution function
f
converges to the Maxwellian (
7
) as time evolves. Consequently, with a suitable scaling factor such as
\beta=1/\sqrt{\theta}
, only a few terms are needed in the Hermite expansion (
16
) to approximate
f
accurately when it is near equilibrium. Therefore, we can expect the expansion order
N
to decrease gradually under the
p
-adaptive algorithm, leading to a substantial reduction in computational cost.

The detailed algorithm is presented in Alg.

. Several key components of the algorithm are summarized below:

•

At the start of the simulation, a reference indicator value is initialized using the initial distribution:

\mathcal{F}^{(p)}=\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](0).

(24)

The goal of the

•

In the algorithm,
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}_{h}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}_{l}
are the two thresholds primarily determined by the reference value. At time

, if the indicator
\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](t^{n})

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(p)}
, the expansion order

will be increased by

to reduce the indicator. The newly added coefficients are set to zero. Conversely, if the current indicator is below the lower threshold
\mathcal{F}_{l}^{(p)}
, the expansion order
N
will be considered for reduction by
\Delta N
. Here,
\Delta N
denotes the adaptive step size. Additionally, whenever the expansion order
N
is modified, the reference value
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}
is considered updated to the current indicator. The procedure for determining
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}_{h}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}_{l}
, as well as for updating
{\mathcal{F}^{(p)}}
, will be described in detail later.

•

and
N_{\min}
denote the maximum and minimum order allowed.If
N

N_{\max}

, the reference indicator value
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}

•

{\mathcal{F}^{(p)}}
to become too small. For instance, when

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(p)}

\mathcal{F}_{l}^{(p)}

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(p)}=\max\left(\eta_{h}^{(p)}\mathcal{F}^{(p)},\mathcal{F}_{h,0}\right),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{l}^{(p)}=\max\left(\eta_{l}^{(p)}\mathcal{F}^{(p)},\mathcal{F}_{l,0}\right),

where
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}>0

\mathcal{F}_{l,0}>0
are two constant parameters used to prevent the thresholds from becoming too small. Parameters
\eta_{h}^{(p)}>1
and
\eta_{l}^{(p)}<1
determine the threshold and the sensitivity of the algorithm. Note that the algorithm becomes more sensitive as
\eta_{l}^{(p)}
and
\eta_{h}^{(p)}
are set closer to
1

{\mathcal{F}^{(p)}}

{\mathcal{F}^{(p)}}
is updated as

{\mathcal{F}^{(p)}}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](t^{n}),\qquad{\rm if}~\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](t^{n})\geqslant\eta_{0}^{(p)}{\mathcal{F}^{(p)}},

(26)

where
\eta_{0}^{(p)}
is a small positive constant.

In the simulation, parameters should be selected based on the specific problem. Here, we provide a representative configuration. The adaptive step size is set to
\Delta N=1
. The lower bound of the expansion order is
N_{\min}=2
, which ensures the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy. The upper bound,
N_{\max}=24
, is determined by computational resources and the scale of the problem. The three indicator ratios are set as
\eta_{0}^{(p)}=0.01
,
\eta_{l}^{(p)}=0.3
, and
\eta_{h}^{(p)}=1.5
. The constant threshold values are set as
\mathcal{F}_{l,0}=10^{-9}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h,0}=10^{-6}
. These parameters collectively govern the update of the reference indicator and the determination of the thresholds.

3.2
The scaling adaptive algorithm

When the distribution function
f
is close to Maxwellian, the optimal scaling factor typically approaches
1/\sqrt{\theta}
. However, in certain situations, such as the early stages of the homogeneous case or in non-homogeneous cases near the kinetic regime,
f
can be far from equilibrium. In these cases, our adaptive scaling algorithm may yield a more appropriate scaling factor. Compared to previous works
[
26
]
, where the scaling factor is fixed as a constant, the following adaptive algorithm can better capture the evolutionary behavior of the numerical solution by adaptively adjusting the scaling factor.

Before presenting the adaptive scaling algorithm, we introduce a conservative projection operator
\mathcal{T}^{\beta\rightarrow\beta^{\prime}}
. This operator rescales
f_{N}^{\beta}
from the current
\beta
to a new
\beta^{\prime}
. The relevant polynomial and function spaces are defined as follows:

P_{N}\coloneq{\rm Span}\{H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}),0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N\},\qquad V_{N}^{\beta}\coloneq{\rm Span}\{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}),0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N\}.

(27)

Thus, the projection operator
\mathcal{T}^{\beta\rightarrow\beta^{\prime}}
, or the orthogonal projection from
V_{N}^{\beta}
to
V_{N}^{\beta^{\prime}}
, is defined as

\mathcal{T}^{\beta\rightarrow\beta^{\prime}}[f_{N}^{\beta}](\bm{v})\coloneq\sum_{|l|=0}^{N}\hat{f}_{l}^{\beta^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{l}^{\beta^{\prime}}(\bm{v}),

(28)

where the new coefficients are defined as

\hat{f}_{l}^{\beta^{\prime}}\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{l}^{\beta^{\prime}}(\bm{v})f_{N}^{\beta}(\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{l}^{\beta^{\prime}}(\bm{v})\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}.

(29)

The detailed implementation of it will be presented later.

Similar to the previous
p
-adaptive algorithm, the scaling adaptive algorithm aims to maintain the indicator at a low level by adjusting the scaling factor. In the finite expansion (
16
), the scaling factor
\beta
determines the approximating function space
V_{N}^{\beta}
, which directly affects the error. This algorithm also utilizes the same indicator (
23
), where a smaller value typically corresponds to a better approximation.

However, unlike in the
p
-adaptive algorithm, it is not straightforward to determine whether increasing or decreasing
\beta
will reduce the indicator based solely on the current value of
\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}]
. Therefore, a bidirectional line search method is employed to optimize
\beta
. The detailed procedure is presented in Alg.
2
, with several points listed below:

•

The mesh of candidate
\beta
values can be constructed in various ways. In this work, we restrict
\beta
to an exponential mesh, defined using a constant parameter
q<1
, which determines the resolution of the mesh:

S=\{\beta=q^{m}\mid m\in\mathbb{Z},~\beta_{\min}\leqslant q^{m}\leqslant\beta_{\max}\}.

(30)

Here,
\beta_{\min}
and
\beta_{\max}
denote the lower and upper bounds of
\beta
, respectively.

•

The initial scaling factor
\beta
is determined based on the initial distribution function
f(0,\bm{v})
, which is assumed to have an analytical form. We compute the adaptive Hermite expansion of
f(0,\bm{v})
using all
\beta\in S
, and the value of
\beta
with the smallest indicator is selected as the initial scaling factor:

\beta_{0}=\argmin_{\beta\in S}\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](0).

(31)

The reference value is then initialized as

\mathcal{F}^{(s)}=\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta_{0}}](0).

(32)

•

In the algorithm,

\mathcal{F}_{l}^{(s)}=\eta_{l}^{(s)}\mathcal{F}^{(s)},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(s)}=\eta_{h}^{(s)}\mathcal{F}^{(s)}

(33)

represent two thresholds determined by the reference value, where
\eta_{l}^{(s)}<1
and
\eta_{h}^{(s)}>1
are two parameters analogous to those in the
p
-adaptive algorithm. At time
t^{n}
, if the indicator
\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](t^{n})
falls outside the reference range
[\mathcal{F}_{l}^{(s)},\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(s)}]
, the algorithm attempts both decreasing and increasing the scaling factor
\beta
. The two new factors are chosen as

\beta_{\rm de}=\beta q,\qquad\beta_{\rm in}=\beta/q.

(34)

Accordingly, the new coefficients are computed by transforming
f_{N}^{\beta}
to the new scaling factors
\beta_{\rm de}
and
\beta_{\rm in}
. Among the three scaling factors
\beta_{\rm de},\beta
, and
\beta_{\rm in}
, the one that yields the minimum indicator value is selected as the new
\beta
, provided it does not reach the boundary of the mesh
S
:

\beta\leftarrow\argmin_{\beta^{\prime}\in\{\beta_{\rm de},\beta,\beta_{\rm in}\}}\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta^{\prime}}].

(35)

The procedure defined by (
34
) and (
35
) terminates when a local minimum of
\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}]
is reached. Finally, if
\beta
is modified during this procedure, the reference
\mathcal{F}^{(s)}
will be updated

\mathcal{F}^{(s)}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}[f_{N}^{\beta}](t^{n}).

(36)

To summarize the parameters, the lower and upper bounds of the scaling factor are
\beta_{\min}=0.15
and
\beta_{\max}=2
, respectively. We set
q=0.9995
to achieve a fine grid for
\beta
. The indicator ratios are chosen as
\eta_{l}^{(s)}=0.9995
, and
\eta_{h}^{(s)}=1.0005
.

Implementation of the projection operator

In this part, the implementation of the operator
{\mathcal{T}}^{\beta\rightarrow\beta^{\prime}}
in (
28
) is presented. Similar algorithms can be found in the literature
[
15
,
18
,
16
]
, though a different approach is proposed here. To compute the new coefficients, we define the integral

T_{l,k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{l}^{\beta^{\prime}}(v)\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(v)\mathrm{d}v,

(37)

which possesses the properties stated in Prop.
1

Proposition 1
.

Let
T_{l,k}=0
for
l<0
or
k<0
, then it holds that

\displaystyle T_{l,k}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad l<k\;{\rm or}\;2\nmid l+k,

(38)

\displaystyle T_{l,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{k+1}{l+1}}T_{l+1,k+1},\qquad T_{l,l}=\left(\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}\right)^{l+\frac{1}{2}},\qquad k,l\geqslant 0,

(39)

\displaystyle T_{l,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}\sqrt{\frac{k+1}{l}}T_{l-1,k+1}+\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}\sqrt{\frac{k}{l}}T_{l-1,k-1}-\sqrt{\frac{l-1}{l}}T_{l-2,k},\quad l\geqslant 1,\;k\geqslant 0,

(40)

\displaystyle T_{l,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{l(k+1)}}{l-k}\left(\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta}-\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}}\right)T_{l-1,k+1},\qquad l\geqslant 1,\;k\geqslant 0,\;l\neq k.

(41)

The proof is provided in the supplementary material
B
.
T_{l,k}
can be calculated using Hermite-Gauss quadrature, which results in a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
for all
0\leqslant l,k\leqslant N
. However, thanks to the properties established above, an algorithm with reduced complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
can be devised for computing all
T_{l,k}
. Specifically,
T_{l,k}
for
0\leqslant l,k\leqslant N
forms an
(N+1)\times(N+1)
lower triangular matrix with only about a quarter of the entries being nonzero. Therefore, we first evaluate the main diagonal entries
T_{l,l}
for
0\leqslant l\leqslant N
, and then apply (
41
) recursively to obtain the lower triangular entries.

With these
T_{l,k}
, the coefficients
\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta^{\prime}}
can be rewritten as

\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta^{\prime}}=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{f}_{k}^{\beta}T_{l_{1},k_{1}}T_{l_{2},k_{2}}T_{l_{3},k_{3}},

(42)

which are computed through the following process:

\displaystyle g^{(1)}_{(l_{1},k_{2},k_{3})}=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{l_{1}}\hat{f}_{(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})}^{\beta}T_{l_{1},k_{1}},\qquad 0\leqslant l_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}\leqslant N,

(43)

\displaystyle g^{(2)}_{(l_{1},l_{2},k_{3})}=\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{l_{2}}g^{(1)}_{(l_{1},k_{2},k_{3})}T_{l_{2},k_{2}},\qquad 0\leqslant l_{1}+l_{2}+k_{3}\leqslant N,

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{(l_{1},l_{2},l_{3})}^{\beta^{\prime}}=\sum_{k_{3}=0}^{l_{3}}g^{(2)}_{(l_{1},l_{2},k_{3})}T_{l_{3},k_{3}},\qquad 0\leqslant l_{1}+l_{2}+l_{3}\leqslant N.

The computational complexity of (
43
) is
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
. Thus, we have achieved an exact realization of the projection with overall complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
.

Remark 2
.

The projection operator
{\mathcal{T}}^{\beta\rightarrow\beta^{\prime}}
satisfies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(\bm{v})f_{N}^{\beta}(\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(\bm{v})\mathcal{T}^{\beta\rightarrow\beta^{\prime}}[f_{N}^{\beta}](\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\qquad\forall\phi\in P_{N}.

(44)

This ensures the conservation of all macroscopic variables, including density, macroscopic velocity, and energy.

Although only the scaling transformation has been presented above, a generalized algorithm that simultaneously performs moving and scaling can be derived in a similar manner. In this case, the projection mapping a finite expansion centered at
[{\bm{\zeta}},\beta]
to a new one centered at
[{\bm{\zeta}}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}]
can be calculated.

So far, the adaptive algorithms for the homogeneous problem have been presented. The discussion of the adaptive algorithms for the non-homogeneous Boltzmann equation will be introduced in the next section.

4
The non-homogeneous problem

In this section, the adaptive Hermite spectral method is extended to the non-homogeneous Boltzmann equation. This work first considers the scenario with periodic boundary conditions, and extensions to other boundary conditions will be left for future work. For spatial discretization, the Fourier spectral method is employed, and the semi-discrete form of the non-homogeneous problem is presented in Sec.
4.1
. A new frequency indicator is introduced in Sec.
4.2
. Finally, the complete adaptive scheme is summarized in Sec.
4.3
.

4.1
Semi-discrete scheme

This subsection introduces the discretization of both spatial and microscopic velocity spaces for the non-homogeneous Boltzmann equation, using the Fourier and adaptive Hermite spectral methods, respectively. For simplicity, the spatially one-dimensional case is presented. Generalization to three dimensions is straightforward.

Let the spatial domain be
\Omega=[0,L]
. Under periodic boundary conditions, spatial discretization is performed using a Fourier basis. Assuming an even number
M
as the expansion order in space, the basis functions are given by

E_{l}(x)\coloneq{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}lx\frac{2\pi}{L}},\qquad x\in\Omega,\qquad|l|\leqslant M/2.

(45)

The associated collocation points are
x_{j}=j\frac{L}{M}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,M-1
. Following the expansion in (
16
), the distribution function
f
is approximated as

f(t,x,\bm{v})\approx f_{N,M}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,x,\bm{v})=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,x)\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta}),

(46)

with coefficients defined by

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,x)\coloneq\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)E_{l}(x),

(47)

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)\coloneq\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}E_{l}(x)H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-{\bm{\zeta}})f(t,x,\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v}\mathrm{d}x,\qquad|l|\leqslant M/2.

For the non-homogeneous problem, the local macroscopic variables can still be computed using (
18
). Moreover, at the collision points
x_{j},j=0,\cdots,M-1
, the coefficients
\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,x)
are approximated as

\hat{f}_{k,j}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t):=\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,x_{j})=\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)E_{l}(x_{j}).

(48)

In the adaptive method, the coefficients
\hat{f}_{k,j}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)
, rather than
\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)
, are used to evolve the numerical solution due to their compatibility with the homogeneous formulation. Thanks to the Fast Fourier transform (FFT), the computational cost of converting between
\hat{f}_{k,j}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)
and
\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)
is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3}M\log M)
.

With the expansion (
46
), the convection term is approximated by

v_{1}\frac{\partial f_{N,M}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}}{\partial x}(t,x_{j},\bm{v})=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{h}_{k,j}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta}),

(49)

with

\hat{h}_{k,j}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}=\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}l}{L}\left(\frac{\sqrt{k_{1}}}{\beta}\hat{g}_{k-e_{1},l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}+\zeta_{1}\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{1}+1}}{\beta}\hat{g}_{k+e_{d},l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}\right)E_{l}(x_{j}).

(50)

A detailed derivation is provided in the supplementary material
C
. The total complexity of evaluating the convection term is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3}M\log M)
. Additionally, the approximation of the collision operator follows the same approach described in Sec.
2.3
, that is, (
21
) is applied at each spatial collocation point
x_{j}
. Consequently, the semi-discrete form of the Boltzmann equation can be written as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{f}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+\hat{h}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)=\hat{Q}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t),\quad 0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N,\;0\leqslant j<M,

(51)

where
\hat{h}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}
and
\hat{Q}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}
are given in (
50
) and Sec.
2.3
, respectively. In the simulation, a third-order Runge-Kutta method
[
23
]
is utilized for time discretization. The time step length is chosen based on the CFL condition, which will be specified in Sec.
5
.

This concludes the discretization of the Boltzmann equation using the Fourier-Hermite framework. The adaptive method is presented in the subsequent sections.

4.2
Frequency indicator for non-homogeneous problem

Following the approach in Sec.
3
, a similar indicator to (
23
) is constructed for the non-homogeneous case. With the spectral expansion (
46
) and the collocation points (
48
), the frequency indicator is defined as

\mathcal{F}[f_{N,M}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}](t)\coloneq\left(\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{|k|=N-\bar{N}+1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\left(\hat{f}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)\right)^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\left(\hat{f}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)\right)^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

(52)

where
\bar{N}=\max\left\{\lfloor N/3\rfloor,2\right\}
. This indicator, denoted by
\mathcal{F}[f_{N,M}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}]
, monitors the contribution of the highest-order
\bar{N}
coefficients across the entire spatial expansion. In this way, the indicator defined by (
52
) serves the same purpose as (
23
).

In this work, the Fourier spectral method is utilized to discretize the spatial domain as a preliminary approach, enabling spectral accuracy in approximating the convection term. This choice avoids obscuring the effects of adaptive techniques in the microscopic velocity space. The indicator defined in (
52
) can also be applied to other numerical schemes for the Boltzmann equation, such as the finite volume method, etc.

Remark 3
.

A more natural way to design the indicator involves the coefficients
\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)
, as in

\mathcal{F}[f_{N,M}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}](t)\sim\left(\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{|k|=N-M+1}^{N}\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\left|\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)\right|^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\left|\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)\right|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.

(53)

Since the coefficients at spatial points are updated during the numerical simulation,
\hat{f}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)
is used in (
52
) instead of
\hat{g}_{k,l}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t)
. Moreover, by Parseval’s formula

\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\left|\hat{g}_{k,l}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{\Omega}\left(\hat{f}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t,x)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\left(\hat{f}_{k,j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(t)\right)^{2},

(54)

where the rectangular integration rule is utilized for the last approximation, which is exact for
E_{l}(x)
with
|l|<M

[
21
]
. Due to the rapid decay of Fourier coefficients, the quadrature error is negligible in practice, often reaching machine precision. As a result, (
53
) is practically equivalent to (
52
).

6
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, an adaptive method is proposed in the framework of the Hermite spectral method for the Boltzmann equation. A
p
- and a scaling adaptive method are firstly presented for the homogeneous problems, where a frequency indicator is constructed based on the expansion coefficients for both adaptive methods.

Subsequently, the frequency indicator is extended to the non-homogeneous problem where the Fourier spectral method is utilized for the spatial discretization. Then, a combined adaptive Hermite spectral method composed of the scaling and
p
-adaptive method is proposed to solve the Boltzmann equation. Finally, the high efficiency of this adaptive method is validated by three homogeneous and four non-homogeneous cases, respectively. For future work, we aim to extend the applications of this adaptive Hermite method to other problems with shock or Maxwell boundary conditions.

Appendix A
Detailed approximation of the collision operator

Here, a detailed version of Sec.
2.3
is presented. Following the approach in
[
26
]
, we first approximate the collision term using a truncated series:

\displaystyle Q[f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}](\bm{v})\approx Q_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}(\bm{v})=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N}\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta}),

(65)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})Q[f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}](\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\quad 0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N.

(66)

Substituting the expansion (
16
) into the bilinear form (
2
) yields a relation between the coefficients of the collision term
Q[f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}]
and distribution
f_{N}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}

\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}=\sum_{|i|=0}^{N}\sum_{|j|=0}^{N}A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}\hat{f}_{i}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\hat{f}_{j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},\qquad 0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N,

(67)

where

\displaystyle A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\beta}(\bm{v}_{*}^{\prime}-\bm{\zeta})\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\beta}(\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{\zeta})-\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\beta}(\bm{v}_{*}-\bm{\zeta})\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})]

(68)

\displaystyle B(|\bm{g}|,\chi)H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{*}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}.

Note that the moving center
\bm{\zeta}
on the right-hand side of (
68
) can be eliminated. The ninth-order tensor
A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}
, involving an eight-dimensional integral, plays a critical role in evaluating the collision term. To avoid redundant computation, it is necessary to factor out the scaling parameter
\beta
from (
68
). Observe that

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v})=\beta^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{1}(\beta\bm{v}),\qquad H_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v})=\beta^{\frac{3}{2}}H_{k}^{1}(\beta\bm{v}).

(69)

Furthermore, the IPL model (
4
) gives

B\left(\left|\frac{\bm{g}}{\beta}\right|,\chi\right)=\beta^{-\frac{\eta-5}{\eta-1}}B(|\bm{g}|,\chi).

(70)

After a change of variables, we obtain from (
68
) that

\displaystyle A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\beta^{3}[\mathcal{H}_{i}^{1}(\bm{v}_{*}^{\prime})\mathcal{H}_{j}^{1}(\bm{v}^{\prime})-\mathcal{H}_{i}^{1}(\bm{v}_{*})\mathcal{H}_{j}^{1}(\bm{v})]\beta^{-\frac{\eta-5}{\eta-1}}B(|\bm{g}|,\chi)\,

(71)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\beta^{\frac{3}{2}}H_{k}^{1}(\bm{v})\,\beta^{-6}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{*}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}

(72)

\displaystyle=\beta^{-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\eta-5}{\eta-1}}A^{i,j;1}_{k}.

(73)

Using this relation, we can precompute and store the tensor
\left\{A^{i,j;1}_{k}\right\}
. During computation, this tensor is scaled to
\left\{A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}\right\}
according to the current value of
\beta
, as adjusted by the adaptive algorithm. The details of computing the tensor
\left\{A^{i,j;1}_{k}\right\}
are beyond the scope of this work. Interested readers are referred to
[
26
]
.

The computational complexity of evaluating all coefficients
\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}
in (
67
) is of
\mathcal{O}(N^{9})
. In some cases, a large number of
N
is required to achieve sufficient approximation accuracy. The resulting time and memory demands could exceed the capabilities of a single machine. To address this, the combined collision model introduced in
[
26
,
15
]
is adopted here to help mitigate the high computational cost associated with large
N
.

Specifically, a moderate constant order
N_{0}
is introduced. The core idea is to apply the full quadratic collision operator only to the coefficients
\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}
with
0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N_{0}
. For the remaining terms, a BGK-type operator is utilized. The resulting combined model is given by

\hat{Q}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{|i|=0}^{N_{0}}\sum_{|j|=0}^{N_{0}}A^{i,j;\beta}_{k}\hat{f}_{i}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\hat{f}_{j}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta},&0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N_{0},\\
\nu_{N_{0}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{k}-\hat{f}_{k}^{\bm{\zeta},\beta}\right),&N_{0}<|k|\leqslant N.\end{cases}

(74)

Here, the constant
\nu_{N_{0}}
denotes the spectral radius of the discrete linearized collision operator. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
26
]
. The Hermite coefficients of the local Maxwellian, denoted by
\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
for
N_{0}<|k|\leqslant N
, can be calculated recursively in
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
operations. To conclude, if
N\leqslant N_{0}
, the full collision model (
67
) is adopted with complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{9})
. Otherwise, if
N>N_{0}
, the combined model (
21
) is adopted and the complexity is reduced to a more feasible
\mathcal{O}(N_{0}^{9}+N^{3})
.

Despite this simplification, accurately evaluating the collision term remains computationally expensive. However, it is known that by appropriately selecting the Hermite basis functions, one can achieve equal or better accuracy with a smaller expansion order
N
. We emphasize that a significant reduction in computation time can be realized when
N<N_{0}
. This is precisely the goal of the adaptive technique introduced in the next section.

Proofs of Eqs. (

–

) are as follows.

•

Simplifying this gives (

Derivation of advection term

To compute the advection term, we need the following relation

v_{1}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})=\frac{\sqrt{k_{1}+1}}{\beta}\mathcal{H}_{k+e_{1}}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})+\zeta_{1}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta})+\frac{\sqrt{k_{1}}}{\beta}\mathcal{H}_{k-e_{1}}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta}).

\displaystyle v_{1}f_{N,M}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}(t,x_{j},\bm{v})=\sum_{|k|=0}^{N+1}\hat{F}_{k}(t,x_{j})\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\beta}(\bm{v}-\bm{\zeta}),

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{k_{1}}}{\beta}\hat{f}_{k-e_{1}}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}+\zeta_{1}\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{1}+1}}{\beta}\hat{f}_{k+e_{1}}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta},\quad 0\leqslant|k|\leqslant N+1,

with
\hat{f}_{k}^{{\bm{\zeta}},\beta}\coloneq 0
for

\hat{F}_{k}(t,\cdot)\in{\rm Span}\left\{E_{l}\mid-M/2\leqslant l\leqslant M/2\right\}.

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{k}(t,x_{j})=\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\hat{G}_{k,l}(t)E_{l}(x_{j}),

Then we have

\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{k}}{\partial x}(t,x_{j})=\sum_{l=-M/2}^{M/2}\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}l}{L}\hat{G}_{k,l}(t)E_{l}(x_{j}).

which is the same as (
49
) and (
50
).
