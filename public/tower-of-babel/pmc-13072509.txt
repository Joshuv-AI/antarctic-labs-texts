Mortality among patients diagnosed with stroke presenting to remote Emergency Department in Bhutan – a retrospective study
Authors: Pelden Wangchuk, Phuntsho Norbu, Ugyen Rinzin, Tashi Wangchuk, Yeshey Dorjey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a leading cause of disability and mortality globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Outcomes remain poor in remote, resource limited settings due to delayed presentation, limited health literacy, and constrained emergency care systems. Evidence on stroke outcomes from such settings is limited. This study aimed to determine stroke-related mortality and identify factors associated with mortality among patients presenting to a remote Emergency Department (ED) in Bhutan.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted from August 1, 2018 to July 31, 2021 at the ED of the Eastern Regional Referral Hospital (ERRH), in Bhutan. All newly diagnosed stroke patients were included. Demographic, clinical, and outcome data were collected using a standardized data collection form. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.ResultsAmong 104 stroke patients, 50% (n = 52) were male, and the mean ± SD age was 65.2 ± 16.1 years. Ischemic stroke accounted for 57.7% (n = 60) of cases, while 42.3% (n = 44) had hemorrhagic stroke. Overall, in-hospital mortality was 16.3% (n = 17). Most patients were illiterate (92.3%, n = 96) and farmers (91.3%, n = 95). Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, present in 76.9% (n = 80), followed by diabetes mellitus (16.3%, n = 17). Nearly half of hypertensive patients were untreated, and only 64.7% (n = 11) of diabetic patients were receiving treatment. Among modifiable risk factors, alcohol use was recorded in 35.6% (n = 37), followed by tobacco use in 3.8% (n = 4). Although 84.6% (n = 88) of patients arrived by ambulance, no patients presented within the 4.5-hour thrombolytic window. Delayed presentation was noted in all stroke types, with the median time from symptom onset to hospital presentation of 24 h (IQR 17–76). None of the studied factor reach statistical significance for predicting mortality, likely due to small sample size.ConclusionStroke-related mortality in our setting was high at 16.3%. Despite ischemic stroke being predominant subtype, no patients presented within the thrombolytic window. These findings highlight the need for public health initiatives to improve stroke recognition, timely hospital presentation, and access to acute care interventions in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Stroke, Mortality, LMIC, Bhutan

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death among non–communicable diseases and the third leading cause of combined death and disability worldwide [1]. Evidence from high-income countries demonstrates a declining trend in stroke-related mortality, largely attributable to advances in prevention, early recognition and organized acute stroke care [2, 3]. In contrast, stroke-related mortality and disability remain disproportionately high in low-and middle income countries (LMICs) [4]. This disparity is likely driven by the rising prevalence of stroke risk factors alongside substantial gaps in timely access to evidence-based acute stroke care in LMICs [5, 6].

Acute stroke management is extremely time-sensitive; each minute of untreated ischemic stroke results in the loss of approximately 1.9 million neurons [7, 8]. Recognizing this urgency, the American Heart Association (AHA) emphasizes a coordinated chain of survival for stroke to improve survival and functional outcomes [9]. This chain involves early symptoms recognition by patients and families, rapid activation of emergency medical services (EMS), timely transport to hospitals capable of neuroimaging and reperfusion therapies (thrombolysis or thrombectomy), and access to early rehabilitation services.

An effective stroke system of care requires the seamless and timely execution of each component of this chain. However, LMICs faces substantial challenges at both the patient and health-system levels. Limited awareness of stoke warning signs, sociocultural beliefs, delayed care-seeking behavior, inadequate transportation systems, and poor community support contribute to delays in hospital presentation and secondary prevention. At the health system level, barriers include limited access to reliable neuroimaging, shortages of trained stroke specialists, low awareness of early rehabilitation among health care providers, and restricted access to long term and chronic stroke care services [10].

Bhutan faces many of these challenges, compounded by unique geographic and systemic factors. Initial stroke symptoms may not always be recognized as medical emergencies, contributing to delays in hospital presentation. Evidence from other Asian and low-resource settings suggests that poor symptom awareness and sociocultural practices, including reliance on traditional healers, are important contributors to pre-hospital delay in stroke care [11–13]. Bhutan’s mountainous terrain and remote communities, despite road access, substantially prolong transport times to ERRH with CT capability, resulting in delayed neuroimaging. However, its impact on the frequent loss of the therapeutic window for timely stroke reperfusion remains underexplored. Although Bhutan has five computed tomography (CT) scanners serving a population of approximately 0.79 million, the dispersed population and difficult geography limit timely access to these services.

During the study period, there were no neurologists and no dedicated stroke centers in the country. Stroke care was physician-led in three referral hospitals and two district hospital with CT capability. Only one MRI scanner was available nationwide, located at national referral hospital.

Given these contextual challenges and the absence of prior studies assessing stroke mortality in our study setting, the primary objective was to describe the characteristics and outcomes of stroke patients presenting to a remote ED in Bhutan. Secondary objective was to explore potential factors associated with mortality. Understanding stroke outcomes in a setting with no neurologists and limited stroke infrastructure may provide valuable insights to inform health-system strengthening and guide the development of pragmatic stroke care models in similar resource-limited settings.

Method

Study design

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study using routinely collected clinical data over a three-year period, from 1 August 2018 to 31 July 2021.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the ED of ERRH, a remote regional referral hospital in Bhutan. Bhutan’s public healthcare system comprises one national referral hospital equipped with both CT and MRI facilities, two regional referral hospitals (ERRH and Central Regional Referral Hospital), each with CT capability, and two district hospitals with CT scanners, amounting to a total of five CT scanners nationwide. During the study period, there were no neurologists and no dedicated stroke centers in the country.

ERRH is a 150-bed hospital providing services through 13 clinical departments, including emergency medicine, internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, gynecology, orthopedics, otorhinolaryngology, ophthalmology, dentistry, radiology, pathology, pharmacology and physiotherapy. The ED serves as both the primary point of emergency care for the Mongar region and the referral center for six district hospitals in eastern Bhutan. It provides emergency services to patients of all age groups and functions as the entry point for both direct presentations and inter-facility referrals.

At the time of the study, the ED was staffed by one emergency physician, five general medical officers, six emergency medical responder and fifteen nurses. All patients with suspected acute stroke whether presenting directly or referred from peripheral hospitals, were initially assessed and managed in the ED. Acute stroke care included clinical evaluation and non-contrast CT imaging which was available on site, and MRI facilities were not available at ERRH.

Following initial assessment and stabilization, patients with confirmed stroke were admitted to the medical ward for further management and post stroke care. Although, Bhutan lacks formal stroke centers, physician-led stroke care was provided in three regional referral hospitals including ERRH and two district hospitals. Designated beds within the medical ward were allocated for stroke patients. Critically ill patients requiring advance monitoring or organ support were managed in the intensive care unit (ICU), which was overseen by medical specialist in the absence of trained intensivist. Physiotherapy services were available for both inpatients and outpatients as part of post stroke rehabilitation.

Study population

The study population included patients presenting directly from the Mongar region as well as those referred from peripheral districts hospital to the ED of ERRH. At the time of the study, ERRH provided CT services and stroke care support to six district hospitals, which were staffed by general doctors and without CT capability, and to seventeen primary health care units within Mongar district, which were staffed by health assistants (equivalent to nurse practitioners). The primary health care centers provide first aid and refer patients to higher-level facilities as needed.

Inclusion criteria

All patients presenting to the ED with a first ever episode of acute stroke during the study period were included. Recurrent stroke patients were excluded because recurrent strokes are generally associated with higher mortality and poorer outcomes compared to first ever-strokes, which could overestimate mortality outcomes in our study population.

Sample size and sampling method

A retrospective census sampling method was used, whereby all eligible stroke cases within study period were included to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from of ED records, inpatient ward files, and ICU chart, where applicable. Information regarding admission to the ward or ICU and patient survival status were obtained from hospital medical records. Data collection was conducted using a paper-based case record form, which was developed based on literature searches and the objectives of the study. Site clearance was sought from ERRH hospital administration, and ethical clearance was obtained from Institutional research Board (IRB) of Khesar Gyalpo university of medical Sciences of Bhutan (KGUMSB).

Study variables

Collected variables included demographic characteristics (sex, age, marital status, education, and occupation); clinical presentation (loss of consciousness, seizure, facial weakness, limb weakness, speech disturbance, vomiting, urinary incontinence, headache, and mode of transport); comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, valvular heart disease, transient ischemic attack, and coronary arterial diseases); modifiable risk factors such as tobacco use and alcohol use; imaging findings (hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke); management details (airway management, ICU care, and referred out); hospitalization duration; and outcomes (died or survived). Mortality was recorded as overall in-hospital mortality, including deaths occurring in the ED, wards and ICU. Data were entered in SPSS version 23.

Data analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 23; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariable and multivariable regression analysis were performed to identify factors associated with in hospital mortality among patient with acute stroke. Associations were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 104 stroke patients were admitted to the ED of ERRH from 1 August 2018 to 31 July 2021. The mean age of patients was 65.2 ± 16.1 years (range 16–93 years). Overall, 16.3% (n = 17) of patients died, while the remaining patients survived. Stroke was equally distributed between males and females. The majority of the patients were married (93.3%, n = 97), illiterate (92.3%, n = 96), and farmers (91.3%, n = 95) by occupation (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

As shown in multiple response Table 2, hypertension was the most common comorbidity, present in 76.9% (n = 80) of patients, followed by diabetes mellitus in 16.3% (n = 17). Transient ischemic attack was noted in 4.8% (n = 5) of patients. Among the 80 patients with hypertension, 47.5% (n = 38) were not on medications. Among patients with diabetes, 64.7% (n = 11) were on medication. Regarding modifiable risk factors, alcohol use was noted in 35.6%, (n = 37), while tobacco use was present in 3.8% (n = 4). Among the presenting symptoms of stroke, more than 83% (n = 86) had limb weakness, and over half of them had speech disturbance (55.8%, n = 58). Approximately 84% (n = 88) of stroke patients were brought to the hospital by ambulance. The duration of hospital admission was similar between the groups (p > 0.05).

Time lapse and duration of hospitalization

The median time from symptom onset to hospital presentation was 24 h in both stroke types, with IQR of 20–48 h for hemorrhagic stroke and 16–72 h for ischemic stroke. The median duration of hospital stay was slightly longer in hemorrhagic stroke at 9 days (IQR, 5–20) compared to 7 days (IQR, 4–16) for ischemic stroke; however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.256). No patients presented to the hospital within the 4.5-hour thrombolytic window.

Discussion

In this three-year retrospective study, 104 new stroke patients were admitted to our emergency department (ED), with an overall in-hospital mortality of 16.3% (n = 17). This rate was higher than that reported in other regions. A study from India reported a mortality rate of 7.9% [14], while Australia data showed a 30 day mortality of 12% following hospital admission [15]. Although unknown case mix and case severity limits the direct comparability, difference is stroke care systems may partly explain this disparity. The studies from India and Australia were conducted in tertiary care centers with specialist-led stroke services, including dedicated stroke units or multidisciplinary stroke teams. In contrast, stroke care in Bhutan is physician-led, with no neurologist or stroke specialists and no designated stroke centers. Stroke patients are accommodated in designated ward beds and these systemic gaps may have contributed to the higher mortality observed in our cohort.

Patient education and stroke literacy significantly influences the stroke chain of survival. In our cohort, the majority of patients were farmers (91.3%) with limited formal education, likely contributing to delays in symptoms recognition. Despite high utilization of Emergency Medical Services (EMS), the median time from symptoms onset to hospital arrival was prolonged at 24 h (IQR, 17–76). Delays may be further exacerbated by a rigid, non-centralized referral pathway, as peripheral hospitals must secure individual case acceptance from higher-level hospital before dispatching an ambulance, process that is time-consuming. The absence of a central stroke command center to coordinate immediate activation and transfer to neuroimaging capable center remains a critical barrier. Establishing a streamlined communication protocols and a centralized dispatch system is essential to reduce pre-hospital delays.

Consequently, none of our patients presented within the 4.5 h thrombolytic window, highlighting a critical gap in acute stroke care. In contrast, studies from other Asian countries report 9–37% of patients presenting within thrombolytic window [16, 17]. This disparity reflects low public stroke literacy, inadequate pre-hospital transport systems, and systemic lack of prioritization for stroke services. The absence of a single neurologist in the entire country underscores a significant gap in human resource planning and clinical coordination. Addressing these barriers ranging from community education to specialized neurological workforce development is urgent to improve stroke outcomes in Bhutan.

In our study population, a substantial proportion of patients with hypertension were not receiving antihypertensive therapy, with 48.8% (n = 39) untreated, and only 64.7% (n = 11) of patients with diabetics mellitus were on medication. This finding warrants further study to identify barriers to treatment, including limited access to care, medication availability, and patient attitudes toward long-term therapy. Addressing these gaps is essential for improving stroke risk factor control.

The high proportion of untreated hypertension may partly explain the relatively large burden of hemorrhagic stroke observed in our setting (42.3%, n = 44). Globally, ischemic stroke remains the predominant subtype, while hemorrhagic stroke accounts for approximately 12–38% of the cases. HICs report a higher proportion of ischemic stroke, whereas hemorrhagic stroke is more common in LMICs [18–20]. The hemorrhagic stroke with raised intracranial pressure requires neurosurgical intervention [21], however, neurologists are unavailable nationwide and neurosurgical services are not available at our hospital. Patients requiring neurosurgical intervention must be referred to the national referral hospital or to centers in India, resulting in delays to definitive care, poorer outcomes, and substantial financial burden.

There is a clear need for context-appropriate optimization of stroke care across the continuum, including primary prevention through patient education and risk-factor management, strengthening acute stroke services, and improving access to rehabilitation. These interventions should be tailored to varying levels of population literacy in Bhutan, with the long term goal of reducing stroke incidence and improving outcomes.

Despite the availability of five CT scanners nationwide, Bhutan’s mountainous terrain and widely dispersed population pose significant barriers to rapid hospital access. In addition to limited public awareness of stroke symptoms, geographic challenges and infrastructural constraints likely contributed to prolonged pre-hospital delays, a pattern consistent with reports from other LMIC setting [22–24]. The absence of thrombolysis-eligible presentations in our cohort warrants further investigation to delineate patient-related, pre-hospital, and system-level contributors to delay.

In the analysis of mortality predictors, no factors reach statistical significance, likely due to the small sample size. A nationwide, multicenter study involving all CT-capable hospitals and a larger patient population is therefore required to more robustly identify factors associated with stroke mortality in Bhutan.

Conclusion

In-hospital stroke mortality in Bhutan was high (16.3%) with significant delays from symptom onset to hospital presentation. No patients arrived within the 4.5-hour thrombolytic window, highlighting gaps in public stroke literacy, pre-hospital transport and acute stroke services. The absence of neurologist and limited neurosurgical services further exacerbate poor outcomes. A coordinated national stroke strategy in urgently needed to improve public education, streamline pre-hospital transport, ensure timely access to neuroimaging, and strengthen acute stroke care to reduce stroke-related mortality.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First due to its retrospective design, we were unable to systemically assess stroke severity using the National Institute of Health Stroke scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Inclusion of NIHSS and GCS would have allowed more accurate stratification of stroke severity and clearer interpretation of its association with outcomes. Second, data on pre-morbid functional status and functional outcomes at discharge or following treatment, as measured by the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS), were unavailable. Consequently, outcomes were limited to in-hospital mortality, and functional recovery among survivors could not be evaluated. Stratification of survivors by mRS would have provided a meaningful assessment of post stroke disability and quality of survival. Third, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and may increase the risk of type II error, thereby reducing the precision of estimates and limiting generalizability of the findings and comparability with larger studies.

In addition, neuroimaging characteristics, including CT findings such as stroke subtype, lesion size, and hemorrhagic transformation, were not consistently available and therefore could not be included in the analysis. The time of symptoms onset and timing of imaging would have been important metrics of stroke care; however, due to the retrospective nature of the study, these data were frequently not documented in clinical records and therefore couldn’t be included in the analysis, limiting comparability with other studies where such variables are routinely reported. These imaging variables may have influences both mortality and survivor outcomes. Furthermore, key pre-hospital factors, such as reasons for delayed presentation, duration of transportation, and referral pathways were not captured. Given the remote and geographical challenging setting, these factors are likely to play a critical role in delayed access to case and subsequent outcomes. Taken together, these limitations may have contributed to variations in observed mortality and restrict the ability to fully elucidate determinants of stroke outcomes in this settings.
