Estimating the burden of anaemia in women of reproductive age attributable to wildfire-sourced fine particulate matter: a multicentre cross-sectional study in low- and middle-income countries
Authors: An Xiaofei, Ning Kang, Pengfei Li, Mingkun Tong, Fangzhou Li, Tong Jiahui, Xu Huaiyue, Xue Tao, Hao Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change has been assumed as a key contributor to global undernutrition. One possible channel is that global warming increases air pollution exposure by causing more and more wildfires. This study examined the effect of wildfire-sourced particulate matter (PM)2.5 exposure on anaemia in women of reproductive age (WRA), a dominating form of undernutrition across the low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). We collected haemoglobin measurements of 1 549 972 WRA from 85 Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) in 45 LMICs between 2004 and 2021. Wildfire-sourced PM2.5 exposure was estimated through a synthesis of ground observations, satellite remote sensing measurements and the Goddard Earth Observing System with Chemistry (GEOS-Chem) model simulations. The effects on the haemoglobin levels, as well as the odds of anaemia, were estimated by the mixed-effects regression models. Finally, population-attributable fraction or number was calculated to assess the burden of wildfire-sourced anaemia among WRA, by extrapolating the regression model result to a study domain of 125 LMICs. In the fully adjusted model, a 1 μg/m³ increase in wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was associated with a 0.1263 g/L reduction in the haemoglobin levels of women (95% CI −0.1540 to –0.0987) and a 1.3% increase in odds of anaemia (ORs 1.013, 95% CI 1.010 to 1.016). The non-linear analysis showed a monotonically increasing exposure-response relationship, which suggested a saturated effect for an exposure concentration >5.85 μg/m³. Across the study domain of 125 LMICs, wildfire-sourced PM2.5 contributed to WRA anaemia of 35.9 million (95% CI 30.1 to 42.1) in 2000, rising to 52.8 (95% CI 44.3 to 61.9) in 2019. The numbers accounted for 8.81% (95% CI 7.38% to 10.32%) and 9.54% (95% CI 7.99% to 11.17%) of all anaemic WRA, respectively. To meet the global 2030 target of reducing 50% WRA anaemia, warranted are climate mitigations and adaptations against wildfire smoke exposure.

Introduction

Anaemia in women of reproductive age (WRA) has become a pressing global public health challenge.1 2 According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study in 2021, 31.2% of women worldwide suffer from anaemia, with a prevalence rate of 33.7%. The years lived with disability attributable to anaemia reached 926.8 per 100 000 women.2 Anaemia in WRA increases the likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preterm birth, low birth weight and maternal mortality, and is also associated with increased risk of stroke,3 cardiovascular diseases,4 dementia5 and elevated all-cause mortality.6 In contrast to high-income countries, the prevalence of anaemia among WRA is significantly elevated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), due to limited economic resources,7 inadequate health systems,8 9 a high burden of chronic and infectious diseases10 and adverse environmental and climatic conditions.11 12 For example, countries such as Nigeria, India and Gabon reported anaemia prevalence rates among WRA exceeding 50% in 2019, while high-income countries such as Australia, the UK and Germany reported rates below 12%.2 The primary aetiologies of anaemia include nutritional components (such as iron deficiency),13 non-nutritional factors (such as chronic inflammation) and genetic predispositions,14 but a number of cases remained unexplained. Therefore, comprehensive research into the determinants of anaemia in women from LMICs is essential to develop effective interventions, reduce the burden of anaemia and achieve the global target of a 50% reduction in anaemia among WRA by 2030.

Recent studies suggest that particulate matter (PM)2.5 may be a potential risk factor for anaemia.15 A multi-ethnic population cohort study conducted in southwestern China found that long-term exposure to PM2.5 increased anaemia by 14% (ORs 1.14, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.28).16 Similarly, a study in sub-Saharan Africa found that each 1 μg/m³ increase in lifetime cumulative PM2.5 was associated with a 1.1% increase of anaemia (β=0.011, 95% CI 0.008 to 0.015).17 Reducing PM2.5 concentrations could play a critical role in reducing the burden of anaemia in LMICs. Research from India suggests that a reduction in PM2.5 to 5 μg/m³ could result in a decline in the prevalence of anaemia in WRA from 53.0% to 34.6%.18 The health effects of PM2.5 vary depending on the source. The WHO 2021 Global Air Quality Guidelines emphasised the necessity of understanding the health effects of PM2.5 from different sources.

Wildfires are a significant source of PM2.5 emissions,19 defined as unplanned fires resulting from natural processes or human activities that affect vegetation in wildland areas.20 From 2016 to 2019, the global average excess PM2.5 concentration due to wildfires reached 3.2 μg/m³. LMICs are particularly affected, with significant wildfire-sourced PM2.5 contributions: 61% in Laos, 45% in the Democratic Republic of Congo and 44% in Sierra Leone, all relative to their annual average PM2.5 concentrations.21 22 Environmental factors such as high temperatures and wind can facilitate the long-range transport of wildfire-sourced PM2.5, affecting areas up to 1000 km away,20 which could lead to environmental pollution and increased risk of disease and mortality.23 In addition, PM2.5 emitted from wildfires is more toxic than that from urban sources, mainly due to its different chemical composition and smaller particle size. Other adverse factors associated with wildfires, such as extreme heat and oxidising gases,24 further increase the toxicity of PM2.5 to human health.25 26

Presently, the majority of research on the health effects of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 has focused on mortality rates27 28 and respiratory diseases,2932 while there is a scarcity of studies on the effects of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 on anaemia.33 Considering the rising frequency, intensity and duration of wildfires worldwide due to climate change and land use change,34 it is crucial to investigate the effects of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 on anaemia in WRA, especially in LMICs.35

This multicentre cross-sectional study collected data from WRA residing in 45 LMICs and estimated the association between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and anaemia. Furthermore, an exposure-response association for wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was derived and subsequently applied to analyse the distribution and trends of the wildfire-sourced PM2.5 attributable anaemia burden among WRA in LMICs, as well as to identify the most heavily impacted countries and regions . This study provides empirical evidence linking wildfire-sourced PM2.5 exposure to anaemia risk among WRA. The findings contribute to the growing body of literature on environmental health impacts in LMICs and may inform future public health strategies for anaemia prevention within environmental governance frameworks.

Methods

Study population

Population data from LMICs were collected and organised using the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) programme (https://dhsprogram.com/).36 37 The DHS programme is a global project aiming to collect information on population, health, nutrition and living standards in LMICs.38 Funded by the United States Agency for International Development and executed by ICF International, the DHS programme has conducted over 400 surveys, encompassing a population exceeding 5 billion and covering >90 LMICs since 1984. It stands out as the largest and most representative survey initiative globally.39

The DHS programme employs a two-stage methodology for the collection of data. In the initial stage of the process, primary sampling units (PSUs), which are typically census blocks, are randomly selected in proportion to their size based on geographic region and urban-rural classification. In the second stage, 25–30 households are randomly selected within each PSU, thereby ensuring that every household has an equal chance of being sampled.40 In order to enhance the representativeness of the data at the population level within each stratum, the DHS assigns weights to households and individuals based on sampling probabilities and response rates. Geographical locations of respondents are recorded using Global Positioning System coordinates. The initial sample size for the research was 3 789 701. After excluding individuals with missing exposure and outcome data, as well as those lacking survey locations or with a sampling weight of zero, 1 549 972 WRA from 85 DHS surveys conducted from 2004 to 2021 across 45 LMICs were included in the analysis (online supplemental supporting figure 1). Exposure assessment

The concentration of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was estimated through a synthesis of ground observations, satellite remote sensing measurements and the GEOS-Chem model simulations, as previously described in detail.20 In brief, the methodology entails the application of an inverse distance weighting spatial interpolation technique to enhance the precision of the daily programme data, encompassing both total and wildfire-sourced components, obtained from GEOS-Chem to a high spatial resolution of 0.25°×0.25°. The refined data are then correlated with gridded meteorological data and ground monitoring stations based on their latitude and longitude, thereby creating a training dataset for model prediction. We trained a random forest model to forecast PM2.5 levels recorded at different monitoring sites. Lastly, the trained model was used in conjunction with global seamless prediction data to estimate daily total PM2.5 concentrations for each 0.25°×0.25° grid across various countries. The monthly average wildfire-sourced PM2.5 concentrations for global grids were calculated over the period from 2000 to 2019.41 Model accuracy was evaluated through independent validations20: (1) against major documented wildfire events and (2) via external comparison with an independent network of PurpleAir sensors not used in training. The estimated concentrations showed moderate agreement with PurpleAir measurements (R2=0.51), substantially outperforming the raw GEOS-Chem simulations (R2=0.18), underscoring the improvement achieved by the machine learning framework.

We assessed individual exposure to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 by spatially correlating PM2.5 concentrations with the geographically anonymised coordinates of participants. For each respondent, we extracted the 6-month average PM2.5 concentration preceding haemoglobin measurement using a buffered averaging approach centred on their displaced coordinates. Last, to control for environmental confounders, we retrieved temperature data from the Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications.42 The methods for data matching and the calculation of monthly average temperatures mirrored those applied to the wildfire-sourced PM2.5.

Outcome

According to WHO, anaemia in WRA is defined as haemoglobin <120 g/L for non-pregnant women and <110 g/L for pregnant women. Data regarding anaemia in women were gathered through the evaluation of haemoglobin levels in blood samples collected via fingertip capillary methods. No adjustments are made when the altitude is below 1000 m. Because significant physiological changes affecting haemoglobin concentrations are more pronounced at higher altitudes. At elevations below 1000 m, the partial pressure of oxygen remains sufficiently high, and the body’s compensatory mechanisms that increase haemoglobin levels are minimal or negligible. Additionally, the DHS adjusted women’s haemoglobin concentrations based on their smoking habits. Specifically, no adjustments are made for women who smoke fewer than 10 cigarettes per day. For women who smoke between 10 and 19 cigarettes daily, or for whom smoking levels are uncertain, or who use non-cigarette tobacco products, haemoglobin levels are decreased by 3 g/L. For women smoking between 20 and 39 cigarettes per day, the reduction is 5 g/L; if they smoke 40 cigarettes or more daily, the reduction is 7 g/L.43 The final haemoglobin concentrations for women were computed after these adjustments.

Statistical analyses

The associations between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and the prevalence of anaemia, as well as haemoglobin concentrations among WRA, were estimated by the mixed-effects regression models. Given the heterogeneity among different surveys related to economic factors, testing protocols and operational differences, we incorporated various surveys from different years and regions as fixed effects in our regression models. Additionally, to further control for heterogeneity, we included PSUs as random effects and assigned weights to each participant to adjust for sampling errors. Consistent with prior evidence44 and guided by a directed acyclic graph (online supplemental supporting figure 2), the following covariates were included to mitigate confounding: (1) time and location variables, included survey’s year and month, the hemisphere and longitude; (2) environmental variables including temperature and PM2.5 concentrations not related to wildfires; (3) lifestyle and habit factors specifically for reproductive-aged women, including smoking status, menopause status, residence duration and body mass index (BMI) (underweight: BMI ≤18.5 kg/m²; normal: 18.5 kg/m²<BMI≤25 kg/m²; overweight: 25 kg/m²<BMI≤30 kg/m²; obese: BMI>30 kg/m²); (4) female variables, including age, age at first birth and pregnancy intervals; (6) household-related factors, including wealth index and number of children. The missing values of covariates (online supplemental supporting table 6) were randomly imputed by chained equations using the R package mice. The fully adjusted model was designated as the primary model for subsequent analyses.

Non-linear exposure-response relationships between wildfire-sourced particulate matter (PM)2.5 and (A) anaemia risk (OR (95% CI)) and (B) haemoglobin concentration (β (g/L)). Solid lines represent predicted values from adjusted models; shaded bands indicate 95% CIs.

To investigate whether age, education level, employment status, pregnancy status, smoking habits, BMI, household wealth index and type of cooking energy affect the association between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and anaemia in WRA, we performed a stratified analysis on these variables. Furthermore, we also included interaction terms between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and each variable in the model to examine their joint effects. The significance of the interactions was evaluated using the Wald test.

In our exposure-response analysis, we applied natural spline functions with 3 df to represent the concentration of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and to explore its potential nonlinear relationship with both anaemia and haemoglobin levels in WRA.

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to ascertain the robustness of the model and results. Given the possibility of a lagged effect of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 on anaemia among WRA, we employed multiple exposure time windows in the primary model, including averages for the periods of 0–3 months, 0–6 months, 0–9 months, 0–12 months, 0–15 months, 0–18 months, 0–21 months, 0–24 months, 0–27 months, 0–30 months, 0–33 months and 0–36 months prior to the surveys. The exposure windows for the remaining environmental covariates were maintained in accordance with those of wildfire-sourced PM2.5, with comparable adjustments. Moreover, to reduce the impact of potential biases associated with smaller sample sizes, the primary analysis was limited to countries that had conducted at least two surveys.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The sample comprises 1 549 972 WRA from 27 African countries, 10 Asian countries, three European countries, three North American countries, one Oceanian country and one South American country. The average age of the participants was 29.77 (±9.78) years, with 739 054 women diagnosed with anaemia, resulting in an overall prevalence rate of 47.68%. The women’s average monthly exposure to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was 3.17 (±3.85) µg/m³, while anaemic women had an exposure level of 2.74 (±3.46) µg/m³. The anaemic women exhibited higher proportions of individuals who were pregnant (6.16%), unemployed (57.33%), with a BMI classified as underweight (20.63%) or normal (60.09%) and not in menopause (96.65%) compared with the non-anaemic group. Detailed information regarding the exposure distribution of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and the sample is available in figure 1 and table 1.

Association between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and anaemia in WRA

In fully adjusted models, a 1 µg/m³ increase in wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was associated with a 1.013 increase in the odds of anaemia (95% CI 1.010 to 1.016) and a decrease in haemoglobin concentration of 0.1263 g/L (95% CI −0.1540 to –0.0987) (online supplemental supporting table 2). The stratified analysis revealed correlations between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and anaemia, as well as haemoglobin levels among women across nearly all subgroups (online supplemental supporting figures 3 and 4). The risk of anaemia attributable to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 exposure was nearly uniform among WRA across different age groups. In overweight women, a 1 µg/m³ increase in wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was associated with an OR of 1.014 (95% CI 1.007 to 1.021) for anaemia, which was higher than in other groups. In contrast, no statistically significant association was observed between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and either anaemia prevalence or haemoglobin levels in women with obesity. Additionally, WRA with higher family wealth index faced a greater risk of anaemia from wildfire-sourced PM2.5 than those from lower wealth index, while no significant associations were found in women with the lowest wealth index.

The non-linear analysis showed a monotonically increasing exposure-response relationship, which suggested a saturated effect for an exposure concentration >5.85 μg/m³ (figure 2A and B). The odds for anaemia exhibited a rapid increase with rising wildfire PM2.5 concentrations <5.85 µg/m³, but reached a plateau above this threshold. Concurrently, the correlation between wildfire PM2.5 and alterations in haemoglobin levels exhibited a notable decline at lower concentrations, which became increasingly subtle at higher levels.

Disease burden of WRA anaemia attributable to wildfire-sourced PM2.5

Across the study domain of 125 LMICs, estimating burden of anaemia attributable to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 among WRA in 125 LMICs rose from 35 921 188 in 2000 (95% CI 30 074 870 to 42 069 725) to 52 845 947 in 2019 (95% CI 44 251 813 to 61 872 965). The numbers accounted for 8.81% (95% CI 7.38% to 10.32%) and 9.54% (95% CI 7.99% to 11.17%) of all anaemic WRA, respectively. Either absolute or relative burden demonstrated a notably growing trend from 2000 to 2019. The burden was particularly severe in regions such as South Asia, Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. The 10 countries most affected by wildfire-sourced PM2.5 anaemia among WRA were India, China, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Indonesia, Myanmar, Brazil and Vietnam (figure 3, online supplemental supporting table 4).

Figure 4 and online supplemental supporting table 5 depict changes in population-weighted wildfire-sourced PM2.5 concentrations, the corresponding changes in anaemia burden and the burden attributable to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 from 2000 to 2019 among WRA in 125 LMICs. From 2000 to 2019, the total population-weighted concentration of wildfire PM2.5 increased by 2.887 µg/m³. Most of these countries saw significant increases in both population-weighted PM2.5 levels and women’s anaemia burden in 2019, particularly in India, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Nigeria and Pakistan, where the increase attributed to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 surpassed one million cases. Conversely, 18.4% of LMICs reported a decline in the burden of anaemia attributable to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 by 2019 compared with 2000. For instance, in Myanmar, the Philippines, Turkey and the Republic of the Congo, the anaemia burden was reduced by 276 300, 37 319, 25 443 and 17 323, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

Two sensitivity analyses showed our findings’ robustness (online supplemental supporting table 3). The first involved substituting the average monthly concentration of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 for the 6 months preceding the survey with concentrations derived from varying exposure time windows. The findings revealed a generally positive correlation between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and anaemia prevalence across most exposure time windows, with a few exceptions (online supplemental supporting figure 5). Specifically, the time window exhibiting the greatest effect size was from 0 to 9 months prior to the survey, where each 1 µg/m³ increase in wildfire-sourced PM2.5 concentration was associated with an OR of 1.026 for anaemia (95% CI 1.021 to 1.030). In the second analysis, we focused on countries that had conducted two or more surveys. We found that anaemia odds increased by a factor of 1.017 (95% CI 1.014 to 1.021) per 1 µg/m³ wildfire PM2.5 increment. The results of both sensitivity analyses remained consistent with the primary findings of this study, suggesting that our results are robust.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale cross-sectional study exploring the impact of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 on anaemia among WRA in LMICs. Our findings indicated that for 1 μg/m³ increase in wildfire-sourced PM2.5, the OR for anaemia risk was 1.013 (95% CI 1.010 to 1.016). The non-linear exposure-response curve revealed a saturated effect for an exposure concentration >5.85 μg/m³. Moreover, burden of disease research showed that in 2019, wildfire-sourced PM2.5 exposure was responsible for anaemia in 52 845 947 (95% CI 44 251 813 to 61 872 965) WRA across 125 LMICs. The numbers accounted for 9.54% (95% CI 7.99% to 11.17%) of all anaemic WRA.

Presently, there is limited research investigating the relationship between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and anaemia. Our findings lend support to the conclusions regarding the health effects of PM2.5 from various sources that have been reached in other studies. For instance, Elbarbary et al48 discovered that an increase of 1 IQR (26.1 μg/m³) in PM2.5 corresponds to an 11% increase in odds of anaemia in elderly individuals (OR 1.11; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.16). Similarly, Morales-Ancajima et al49 analysed a cohort of 139 368 children under 5 years old in Peru, reporting a significant association between elevated outdoor PM2.5 levels and reduced haemoglobin (−0.3 g/L; 95% CI −0.5 to –0.2) along with an increased incidence of severe anaemia (OR 1.18; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.27). Another study involving children across 36 LMICs indicated that for every 10 μg/m³/year increase in PM2.5, the OR for anaemia in children was 1.10 (95% CI 1.09 to 1.11), with an average haemoglobin concentration drop of 0.75 g/L (95% CI 0.81 to 0.68).50 A study in sub-Saharan Africa similarly indicated a 1.1% increase in odds of anaemia for each 1 μg/m³ increment in lifetime cumulative PM2.5 exposure (95% CI 0.008 to 0.015).17 The inconsistency of results may be attributed to differences in exposure selection and the characteristics of the study populations. The chemical composition, level of human toxicity and patterns of exposure of PM2.5 vary depending on the source.25 Compared with the general population, reproductive-aged women in LMICs may be less resilient to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 due to socio-economic and environmental factors.

Currently, there is a lack of research focusing on the exposure-response curve of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 in relation to anaemia. Nonetheless, several studies have investigated the association between PM2.5 of general origins and anaemia. A research study in Xi’an, China, identified an ‘M’-shaped exposure-response curve that links PM2.5 to anaemia prevalence in women.51 Additionally, two other studies documented a significant positive correlation between PM2.5 and the rise in anaemia cases among children and the elderly.30 31 Our findings showed a monotonic exposure-response relationship, which suggested a saturated effect for an exposure concentration >5.85 μg/m³. This indicates the necessity of acknowledging the health effects of low levels of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 on WRA. More research is required to investigate the variance in the relationship between wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and PM2.5 from general sources regarding anaemia in women.

There are notable variations among countries regarding the burden of anaemia in WRA attributed to wildfire-sourced PM2.5. In 2019, 91.6% of LMICs reported a higher burden of anaemia compared with 2000. For instance, from 2000 to 2019, the number of WRA with anaemia due to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 increased by 5 122 299 in India and 912 099 in Bangladesh, with both countries showing a consistent annual rise in their PAF. These countries are in urgent need of strategies to combat the negative effects of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 on anaemia among WRA. Conversely, certain LMICs, such as Myanmar, have shown a downward trend in the attributable burden of anaemia; however, their increase in the PAF indicates that wildfire-sourced PM2.5 still poses an increased risk of anaemia in WRA. A comprehensive analysis of data from 125 LMICs indicates a notably growing trend of either absolute or relative burden from 2000 to 2019. In summary, wildfire-sourced PM2.5 is a major factor contributing to anaemia. Countries should strengthen their monitoring and management of wildfire-sourced PM2.5 and improve health policies to mitigate its impact on women’s health.

PM2.5 exposure induces anaemia through systemic inflammation and iron dysregulation.52 Inhalation triggers pulmonary inflammation that progresses systemically,53 elevating pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-10. IL-6 upregulates hepcidin to impair intestinal iron absorption, promote macrophage iron sequestration and disrupt iron recycling—potentially exacerbated by copollutants—while IL-10 further restricts iron availability by enhancing macrophage retention and ferritin expression.54 55 Concurrently, systemic inflammation suppresses erythropoiesis through inhibited erythroid precursor differentiation, erythropoietin resistance and reduced erythropoietin production.56 PM2.5-induced oxidative stress also directly damages erythrocytes, collectively forming a multifactorial cascade.57

This study also has several limitations. First, our assessment of exposure was based on the atmospheric distribution of wildfire-sourced PM2.5, rather than on the specific exposure levels of individuals.58 We assumed that exposure levels within each grid unit were uniform, which fails to account for individual differences, such as daily behaviours (eg, reducing outdoor activities or wearing masks during periods of elevated PM2.5 concentrations), variations in indoor and outdoor exposure and the effects of population migration on our findings.34 Our exposure assessment is subject to spatial uncertainty due to the displacement of DHS cluster coordinates (0–10 km randomisation), a protocol implemented to ensure respondent confidentiality. This geographic obfuscation introduces spatial misalignment when matching wildfire-sourced PM2.5 data, potentially biasing exposure estimates. Furthermore, our analysis did not account for synergistic effects between wildfire PM2.5 and co-emitted pollutants—notably ozone generated through photochemical reactions during combustion events. This omission may lead to underestimation of the total haematological health burden attributable to wildfire emissions. Third, the use of point-of-care devices for capillary haemoglobin measurement introduces potential measurement error, which is inherent to DHS survey protocols but may marginally bias anaemia prevalence estimates. Additionally, due to the inherent limitations of the DHS programme, covariates including household conditions, reproductive behaviours and preferences, behavioural habits and primary cooking energy sources—were collected via questionnaires within the framework of a cross-sectional research design. Finally, this study did not take into account the potential impacts of factors such as women’s history of miscarriages, trimester, antepartum and postpartum haemorrhage, genetic diseases, dietary intake, health conditions, socioeconomic status, levels of industrialisation and access to healthcare facilities and services on the results.

In summary, we found that long-term exposure to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 was associated with a significantly increased risk for anaemia and a decreased haemoglobin level in WRA, emphasising the potential health risks posed by wildfire-sourced pollution. For most LMICs, the burden of anaemia in WRA attributable to wildfire-sourced PM2.5 is increasing. It is important to develop and implement effective policy measures to reduce wildfire occurrences and mitigate their environmental consequences. These initiatives will help protect women’s health and contribute to the achievement of the goal of a 50% reduction in anaemia among WRA by 2030.
