Fermentation of Dendrobium officinale juice using lactic acid bacteria and evaluation of its antioxidant and structural properties
Authors: Sanabil Yaqoob, Aysha Imtiaz, Kanza Aziz Awan, Hiba Naveed, Ahmad Faraz, Waleed Sultan, Qing Shen, Chunlai Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of mono- and co-culture lactic acid bacteria (LAB) fermentation on the antioxidant capacity, structural properties and aroma of Dendrobium officinale juice. Fermentation was performed using Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (LP), Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (LPC) and Lactobacillus helveticus (LH) individually and in binary combinations (LP-LPC, LP-LH and LPC-LH). An integrated analytical approach, including total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical scavenging assays, Headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled to gas chromatography–mass spectrometry HS-SPME-GC/MS, electronic nose (E-nose), ATR-FTIR, X-ray diffraction (XRD) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), was applied to evaluate fermentation-induced modifications. LAB fermentation significantly enhanced antioxidant properties in a specie-dependent manner (p < 0.05). LPC-LH co-culture (D6) exhibited the highest TPC (492.38 mg GAE/mL) and TFC (545.78 mg QE/mL), representing nearly five-fold and four-fold increases, respectively, Compared with the control. Furthermore, D6 showed the strongest DPPH (72.58%) and ABTS (84.44%) radical scavenging activities. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis revealed substantial volatile reconfiguration, characterized by increased organic acids, glycerol and nitrogen-containing metabolites, particularly in co-culture systems. Electronic Nose PCA explained 94.45% of total variance, clearly distinguishing treatments (p < 0.05). Structural analyses demonstrated significant protein conformational changes and increased semi-crystallinity in mixed cultures, while Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM) confirmed extensive matrix disruption and enhanced porosity. The investigation underscores the potential of LAB, particularly in co-culture systems, to optimize the nutritional properties of D. officinale juice, paving the way for its development as a functional fermented beverage.Keywords: Lactic acid bacteria fermentation, dendrobium officinale juice, Antioxidant activity, Microstructural analysis, ATR-FTIR, X-ray diffraction, Functional beverage development

Introduction

The rise in chronic illnesses such as diabetes, gastrointestinal diseases, cardiovascular disease and oxidative stress-related conditions worldwide highlights the urgent need for safe, sustainable and treatment approaches (Munir et al., 2024; Naveed et al., 2024). Although the pharmacotherapies focus only on medicine, however their limitations e.g. adverse side effects, long-term dependency or high costs have driven an increased interest in the plant-based disease management (Nagarajan et al., 2025). Functional and medicinal plants have consequently become focus point of research aimed at managing disease and promoting health due to their diverse bioactive compounds and long history of human use (Dar, Shahnawaz, Ahanger, & Majid, 2023). Among these botanicals, Dendrobium officinale, a herb of the Orchidaceae family occupies a distinguished place in traditional Chinese medicine, where it is considered as a health-preserving plant (Fengxi Yang et al., 2023). Studies have shown that it contains certain biological active compounds such as polysaccharides, alkaloids, flavonoids and phenolic compounds. Collectively, these compounds contribute to a broad nutraceutical and functional spectrum including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, hypoglycemic and gastroprotective impacts (Bao et al., 2024). Most of these biological activities have been described for extracts and juices prepared from the fresh or dried stems of D. officinale, exhibiting the principal edible and medicinal part of the plant (Pang et al., 2026; X. Xu et al., 2025). Thus, the physiological effects discussed in this study denote specifically to stem-derived preparations unless stated otherwise.

The stems of D. officinale are specifically rich in water-soluble polysaccharides, which comprises about 20–45% of the dry weight and are recognized as the major bioactive components. Additionally, the stems include phenolic compounds, alkaloids, flavonoids, amino acids and soluble sugars that contribute to their nutritional as well as functional properties collectively (Zhang et al., 2023). Fresh stem juice commonly reveals high soluble solids content because of the abundance of polysaccharides and soluble carbohydrates, which may provide suitable substrates for LAB fermentation (G. Yu et al., 2023).

Recently, Wanlin Liu et al. (2023) explored the effects of microbial fermentation on D. officinale using headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry HS-SPME-GC–MS and LC-MS approaches and demonstrated considerable modifications in volatile as well as in non-volatile metabolites. Nevertheless, that study primarily aimed at global metabolomic changes and did not comparatively evaluate effects of the specific strain of different LAB monocultures and co-cultures on antioxidant properties, protein structural modifications, color attributes, crystallinity and microstructural traits. In contrast, the present study systematically investigates the impact of selected LAB species in both monoculture and binary co-culture systems using an combined analytical platform comprising antioxidant assays, GC–MS, ATR-FTIR, XRD, CLSM and SEM analyses. This broad approach provides deeper understandings of synergistic interactions among LAB species and their influence on the structural and physicochemical characteristics of fermented D. officinale juice.

The present research aims to study the effects of single- and dual-strain lactic acid bacterial fermentation on the bioactive composition and antioxidant capacity of D. officinale juice. In contrast to previous studies that primarily focused on either metabolomic profiling or single-strain fermentation effects, this work incorporate both monoculture and co-culture LAB systems to systematically compare their functional as well as structural effects. Moreover, this study distinctively merge multiple complementary analytical techniques (ATR-FTIR, XRD, CLSM, GC–MS and electronic nose analysis) to simultaneously assess fermentation-induced alterations at chemical, microstructural, structural and aroma levels. This integrated multifaceted method enables a more comprehensive understanding of how microbial interactions impact both transformation of bioactive compound and matrix restructuring. Furthermore, the study forms relationship between aroma changes, structural modifications and antioxidant functionality, providing novel insights into collaborative effects of LAB co-cultures on plant-based fermentation systems. In general, this research communicates an important gap in understanding strain-specific and synergistic mechanisms of fermentation in D. officinale juice and supports its formulation as a functional fermented beverage.

Materials and methods section

Procurement of raw material

Fresh stems of D. officinale were acquired from local markets of Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China. The stem was selected because it signifies the principal edible and medicinal portion of the plant and comprises of the highest percentage of bioactive polysaccharides. After washing with distilled water thoroughly to eradicate impurities, the stems were cut into small pieces. For preparation of juice, fresh D. officinale stems were mixed with distilled water at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v; 100 g fresh stems: 1000 mL distilled water) and standardized using a laboratory blender till a uniform slurry was attained. The homogenate was filtered using sterile muslin cloth subsequently to obtain the juice, which was then subjected to fermentation trials as shown in Fig. 1. All juice preparation procedures were performed under aseptic settings to ensure reproducibility of experiments and to minimize microbial contamination. Distinct combinations of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Lactobacillus helveticus purchased from SynBio Tech in Beijing, China were cultivated on MRS medium at 37 °C and kept at 4 °C (Table 1). All of the solvents or compounds used were analytical grade.

Fermentation process and experimental conditions of Dendrobium officinale juice

For the beginning of fermentation process, probiotic species e.g. LP (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum), LPC (Lacticaseibacillus paracasei), LH (Lactobacillus helveticus), LP-LPC (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei), LP-LH (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Lactobacillus helveticus) and LPC-LH (Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Lactobacillus helveticus) were individually cultured in MRS broth within an HZQ-F160 incubator until reaching the logarithmic growth phase of bacterial concentration of 107–108 CFU/mL at initial time of fermentation before its addition into D. officinale juice. Following activation, the cultures were centrifuged using a Ruijiang RJ-TDL-50 A centrifuge (Ruijiang Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Wuxi, China) at 4000 ×g for 10 min at 25 °C. The bacterial cells were rinsed in a sterile 0.1% NaCl solution after the supernatant was removed. After that, the suspensions were utilized as fermentation starter cultures. The juice was pasteurized for 30 min at 70 °C before fermentation (with pH of range 3.2–4) (Yaqoob et al., 2024). The conditions for fermentation (2% inoculum (v/v), 37 °C, 48 h and 60 rpm) were selected based on previous findings involving lactic acid fermentation of plant-based beverages such as mulberry juice, which reported well-organized microbial growth and improved functional properties under similar environments. Though the composition of sugar of D. officinale differs from that of fruit matrices, these uniform conditions were opted to facilitate comparison among LAB species and to assess their relative fermentation performance in same experimental settings.

Phenolic extraction of Dendrobium officinale juice

Free phenolic compounds have been extracted using the technique of Dou, Chen, Fu, and Liu (2022), with minor modifications. The sample was mixed with 20 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol, weighing 1.0 g. Following that, the mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at 4000 ×g. The resultant supernatant was collected, lyophilized and preserved as the undigested phenolic fraction for further examination.

Total phenolic contents (TPC)

The TPC was quantified following the Folin-Ciocalteu method as mentioned by Chen, Chen, Xiao, and Fu (2018). For the reaction mixture, 200 μ L of sample extract, 1 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, 3 mL of 20% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution and 12 mL of distilled water were mixed together. Following a vigorous vortex, the solution was incubated in a water bath at 70 °C for 10 min. Using a SpectraMax i3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), absorbance measurements were made at 765 nm. Using a gallic acid calibration curve (0–500 μ g/mL), the data were quantified and reported as milligrams per liter of sample (mg /L).

Total flavonoid contents (TFC)

TFC was measured from the sample using the procedures out lined by Chen et al. (2018). 100 μL of distilled water, 10 μL of 5% sodium nitrite solution along with samples were mixed together and allowed to stand for 5 min. Following that, 15 μL of 10% aluminum chloride and 50 μL of sodium hydroxide and distilled water were added. Utilizing the SpectraMax i3 spectrophotometer from Mo lecular Devices, Silicon Valley, CA, USA, absorbance measurements were performed at 510 nm. Quercetin was used as a reference to determine TFC and the results were reported as milligrams per liter (mg /L) of sample.

Determination of DPPH scavenging activity

The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl free radical scavenging activity of the sample was assessed using the technique outlined by Ramirez, Zambrano, Sepúlveda, Kennelly, and Simirgiotis (2015). 120 μL of the sample was added to 4.2 mL of a 0.1 Mm, DPPH solution made in methanol (MeOH). After that, the mixture was left at room temperature in the dark for 1.5 h. A UV–Vis spectrophotometer was used to determine the absorbance at 517 nm. A blank sample devoid of DPPH was utilized. Determination of ABTS scavenging activity

The ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical cation scavenging activity was performed in line with the procedure described by Aregbe et al. (2025). Briefly, ABTS reaction solution (by mixing 7 mM ABTS with 4.95 mM potassium persulfate (1:1, v/v)) was prepared and stored at 25 °C in the dark for 16 h in juice samples. The mixture was further diluted with methanol to achieve an absorbance value of 0.731 at 734 nm. Total sugar content and reducing sugars

The total sugar content of the samples was determined using the phenol–sulfuric acid method as described by Lam, Dinh, and Dang-Bao (2021). Briefly, 100 μL of the dry ethanolic extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of distilled water and 0.5 mL of 5% phenol solution. Subsequently, 2.5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) was carefully added to the mixture. After thorough mixing, the solution was allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured at 490 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Quantification was performed using a standard calibration curve of pure d-glucose and results were expressed as milligrams of glucose equivalents per milliliter of sample (mg GE/mL). Reducing sugar content was determined as described by Aregbe et al. (2025) using 3,5-dinitrosalicylic (DNS) acid method. Briefly, 0.5 mL of DNS reagent was mixed with 0.5 mL of the SBCB, boiled in test tubes for 5 min and allowed to cool to room temperature. The absorbance was read at 540 nm using a UV spectrophotometer.

Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) of Dendrobium officinale juice

Volatile compounds in Dendrobium officinale juice were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS: Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), following the method of Wanlin Liu et al. (2023) with modifications. Briefly, 1 g of juice was transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial, mixed with saturated sodium chloride and sealed with a TFE silicone septum (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Volatile extraction was performed using a 65 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Supelco, USA) at 60 °C for 20 min. After extraction, the fiber was thermally desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min in the injection port of a Thermo Trace 1300 GC–MS system equipped with a DB-Wax capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The GC oven program was set as follows: initial temperature 40 °C, ramped to 250 °C at 5 °C/min. The injector and detector temperatures were 250 °C and 280 °C, respectively. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV, with the ion source, quadrupole and transfer line maintained at 150 °C, 150 °C and 280 °C, respectively. Spectra were acquired in the m/z range 30–350 with a scan rate of 1 scan/s. Volatile components were tentatively identified by comparing their mass spectra and linear retention indices (LRIs) with entries in the MWGC spectral library (MetWare/Maiwei gas chromatography spectral library; Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). A minimum spectral similarity/match-quality threshold of 70% was applied for tentative identification. Components were considered putatively identified when both spectral similarity and LRI agreement were acceptable. Reference standards were not used for confirmation; therefore, the reported compound identities should be regarded as tentative rather than confirmed. Moreover, the HS-SPME-GC–MS without chemical derivatization has limited suitability for the reliable analysis of highly polar and non-volatile organic acids, the detection of formic acid and oxalic acid in the present research was treated as tentative library-based identification rather than definitive quantitative determination. These components were annotated based on mass-spectral matching and linear retention-index comparison and were therefore not interpreted as absolute concentrations. Nonanoic acid, being comparatively more amenable to headspace analysis, was considered more suitable for detection by the applied HS-SPME-GC–MS procedure.

Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) and secondary structure of Dendrobium officinale juice

Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted to investigate the functional groups present in juice using a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an ATR accessory. Spectral data were collected in the range of 4000–700 cm1, following the method described by Rehman et al. (2025). Secondary structural elements were specifically analyzed within the amide I region (1600–1700 cm1). To extract compositional and structural information, spectral deconvolution and curve fitting were performed using PeakFit™ Version 4.0 and OMNIC™ software (Version 32). Amide I band deconvolution (1700–1600 cm−1) was carried out after baseline correction using a rubber-band method. Peak fitting was done by using Gaussian-Lorentzian mixed functions with second-derivative analysis to evaluate positions of initial peak. Curve fitting was performed using Origin software (OriginLab, USA) and the goodness of fit was estimated by R2 values.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of Dendrobium officinale juice

The crystalline structure of the sample was characterized using X-ray diffraction on a Rigaku AXS SmartLab diffractometer, following the method of Xie et al. (2023). The analysis was conducted at 40 kV and 40 mA using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15405 nm). The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 3° to 50° with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 10° per min. The relative crystalline was determined by calculating the ratio of the integrated area under the crystalline peaks to the total diffraction area, offering insights into the structural order within the samples. Freeze-drying was applied in order to preserve the natural structural characteristics of the constituents of juice while eliminating water, thus enabling reliable evaluation of crystallinity and structural rearrangements brought about by fermentation.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) of Dendrobium officinale juice

Confocal laser scanning microscopy was employed to visualize microbial and particulate structures in Dendrobium officinale juice, following the protocol of Cardona and Iriart (2013) with modifications. Juice samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane to collect microorganisms and particulates, which were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (phosphate-buffered saline) for 15 min at room temperature. The fixed material was pelleted by centrifugation, washed with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin BSA in PBS for 30 min. Samples were subsequently incubated with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated antibody or lectin probe (dilution 1:100–1:200 in blocking buffer) in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. After three PBS washes, the stained pellet was mounted on glass slides with an anti-fade medium and covered with a coverslip. Imaging was performed using a CLSM system equipped with a 488 nm argon laser for excitation and an emission detector set at 510–530 nm. Acquisition settings, including laser power, pinhole size (1 airy unit) and detector gain, were optimized to minimize background and photobleaching. Treatments from the three independent fermentation batches were prepared and imaged, with each sample was determined in triplicates. For CLSM analysis, polysaccharide compositions were conceptualized by the use of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated concanavalin A (FITC-ConA) lectin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to specifically stain residues of α-mannopyranosyl and α-glucopyranosyl. Samples were examined under excitation/emission wavelengths of about 488 nm/520 nm.

Electronic nose analysis of Dendrobium officinale juice

Volatile compound analysis was performed using a PEN3 electronic nose (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Germany) following the protocol of S. Yu et al. (2022). Samples (10 mL) were equilibrated in 50 mL sealed vials before analysis. Operating parameters included: 120 s measurement time, 150 s sensor purge, 5 s pre- incubation, with carrier and injection gas flows of 400 and 200 mL/min, respectively. The metal oxide semiconductor MOS sensor array enabled volatile organic compounds VOC fingerprinting and discrimination.

Data analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) using SPSS software. For fermentation experiments, three independent fermentation batches were prepared independently and treated as biological replicates. Treatments from each independent batch were subjected to three replicate analytical measurements, which were considered technical replicates. For statistical analysis, the independent fermentation batches (n = 3) constituted the experimental replicates, while technical replicates were used to obtain the mean value for each batch. Findings were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to determine substantial differences across the samples at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Antioxidant activity of Dendrobium officinale juice

All antioxidant potential were obtained from three independently prepared fermentation batches (biological replicates; n = 3) and each batch was analyzed in triplicate (technical replicates). The mean of the three technical measurements was used to represent each independent fermentation batch for statistical analysis. Statistical evaluations among treatments were accomplished using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD test at a significance level of p < 0.05. The antioxidant analysis of D. officinale juice depicted that lactic acid bacterial fermentation notably increased antioxidant properties in a specie-dependent manner. The non-fermented control (D0) showcased the lowest total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC) and radical scavenging activities DPPH and ABTS, suggesting limited free radical-quenching capability in its indigenous form. All fermented treatments showed increased phenolic as well as flavonoid concentrations and enhanced antioxidant activity as compared to D0. These findings confirm that fermentation functions as an effective bioprocessing approach for improving the functional and nutraceutical attributes of D. officinale juice. Among the single-strain fermentations, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (D1) produced greater enhancement in TPC and antioxidant activity than Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (D2) and Lactobacillus helveticus (D3). However, the most pronounced improvements were observed in mixed-culture fermentations, highlighting the importance of microbial interactions. The combination of L. paracasei and L. helveticus (D6) resulted in the highest TPC (492.38 mg/L) and TFC (545.78 mg /L), representing approximately five-fold and four-fold increases respectively, Compared withthe non-fermented control. Among treatments, LPC-LH (D6) displayed the maximum TPC (492.38 mg /L) and TFC (545.78 mg /L) and these values were remarkably higher than those of all other treatments in accordance to Tukey's HSD test. The overall antioxidant activity (D6 > D5 > D4 > D1 > D2 > D3 > D0) consistently paralleled phenolic and flavonoid concentrations, demonstrating a clear functional equivalence between polyphenol enrichment and antioxidant capacity.

The improvement of antioxidant parameters subsequent to fermentation can be attributed to numerous biochemical transformations which occurs during lactic acid bacterial fermentation. Despite the fact that enzymatic activities were not quantified directly in the present research, prior investigations have verified that LAB species often possess β-glucosidase, esterase and other hydrolytic enzymes capable of modifications in plant phenolics (Sarıtaş et al., 2024). Consequently, the mechanisms proposed should be considered as credible explanations rather than direct indication. Moreover, fermentation may obstruct structures of plant cell wall, facilitating the release of intracellular phenolics that are otherwise embedded within the cell matrix, as reported by Paventi et al. (2025). The higher phenolic and flavonoid contents observed in the fermented samples may be associated with fermentation-induced release or transformation of bound phenolics. The stronger response was noted in the co-culture treatments, particularly D6, may further exhibits improved metabolic interactions between the species and greater transformation of substrate compounds. However, the specific enzymatic mechanisms responsible for these alterations were not directly investigated in the present research. The similar increasing trend reported between TPC, TFC, DPPH and ABTS further indicate that phenolic and flavonoid compounds contributed substantially to the antioxidant potential of fermented D. officinale juice.

Similar increasing trend were observed between TPC and DPPH, TPC and ABTS, TFC and DPPH and TFC and ABTS, demonstrating that phenolic and flavonoid constituents were key contributors to the antioxidant potential of fermented D. officinale juice. These results further support the observed comparable rise in concentration of polyphenols and radical scavenging capacity among the treatments. These findings are compatible to previous findings showing that lactic acid bacterial fermentation boosts phenolic and flavonoid contents in plant-derived matrices and thereby augments antioxidant capacity. Sarıtaş et al. (2024) reported that fermentation with L. plantarum significantly raised total phenolic content and improved radical scavenging activities of DPPH and ABTS in fruit substrates via enzymatic biotransformation of glycosylated phenolics. Similarly, Alharbi (2026) established that LAB fermentation of herbal infusions improved concentrations of phenolics and supported antioxidant properties, underlining contributions of strain-specific and synergistic enzymes.

The antioxidant potential observed following fermentation were consistent with the modifications in phenolic and flavonoid contents, with the co-culture sample showing comparatively greater improvements. These results indicate that fermentation altered the antioxidant assay of D. officinale juice in a treatment-dependent manner. However, the improved antioxidant activity perceived in fermented samples may not be completely due to phenolic compounds. The FTIR results showed substantial changes in secondary structures of protein, specifically in mixed-culture treatments, signifying protein unfolding and possible hydrolysis during fermentation process. Although proteolytic activity and peptide profiles were not directly observed in the present study, previous literature have showcased that lactic acid bacterial fermentation can generate low-molecular-weight bioactive peptides having strong radical scavenging characteristics. Such peptides may donate protons or electrons in order to neutralize free radicals and thus play role in DPPH and ABTS scavenging activities. Consequently, the increase in antioxidant capacity observed in D4-D6, especially in D6, is likely the outcome of a collective contribution of phenolic compounds and fermentation-derived antioxidant peptides instead of phenolics alone. This explanation is reinforced by reports presenting that the proteolytic systems of lactic acid bacteria hydrolyze proteins into peptides with antioxidant potential during the process of fermentation (Kieliszek et al., 2021; Waśko et al., 2012). Subsequently, the synergistic development of antioxidant activity in co-culture treatments may moderately reflect improved peptide generation in addition to heightened phenolic bioavailability. Nonetheless, further studies relating proteolytic activity assays and peptide characterization are essential to confirm this hypothesis. Hence, the combined outcomes and supporting study show controlled lactic acid bacterial fermentation to be an effective strategy for the enhancement of antioxidant potential of D. officinale juice, as mentioned in Fig. 2 (A).

GC–MS analysis of Dendrobium officinale juice

The GC–MS analysis revealed a pronounced, specie-dependent biotransformation of the volatile and semi-volatile metabolite profile of Dendrobium officinale juice following lactic acid bacterial fermentation Fig. 2 (C). When compared with the non-fermented control (D0), which reflected the intrinsic volatile fingerprint of the fresh plant matrix, all fermented treatments (D1-D6) exhibited substantial compositional shifts. These shifts were characterized by the reduction of certain native constituents and quantitative amplification of microbial-derived metabolites. Moreover, the transformation pattern was not uniform across treatments; rather, it followed a hierarchical trend influenced by the complexity of the starter culture. Co-culture fermentations (D4-D6) consistently produced broader and more intensified chemical modifications than single-strain fermentations (D1-D3), suggesting synergistic metabolic interactions. Formic acid and oxalic acid were tentatively annotated in the GC–MS findings; moreover, because these highly polar/non-volatile acids were analyzed without derivatization, their detection should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive quantitative evidence. Additionally, these components were not used as primary evidence for treatment-related alterations in organic-acid concentration.

A predominantly noteworthy feature of fermented samples was the presence of nitrogen-containing heterocycles and amines, involving pyridine and piperidine derivatives, which were almost absent in the control. The development of these composites is likely related to the extensive proteolytic activity of lactic acid bacteria, particularly L. helveticus, which is well documented for its strong protease and peptidase systems. Proteolysis releases free amino acids that later undergo further microbial catabolism, producing a various collection of volatile nitrogenous metabolites. Although proteolytic activity was not examined directly in the present research, the distinct accumulation of nitrogen-containing components in fermented treatments, particularly in D6, indirectly proposes heightened amino acid turnover during co-culture fermentation. Pyridine and piperidine derivatives may derive from the degradation of amino acids such as lysine, ornithine and proline through progressive decarboxylation, deamination and cyclization reactions. Piperidine, for example, is generally obtained from lysine catabolism, while pyridine derivatives may arise from secondary reactions including amino carbonyl intermediates formed during microbial metabolism. The evidently increased abundance of these metabolites in D4-D6 compared with D1-D3 depicts that mixed cultures promoted deeper amino acid catabolism, presumably by metabolic complementarity and cross-feeding interconnections among species. The higher presence of nitrogen-containing compounds in the co-culture treatments (D4-D6), specifically D6, also displayed a positive relation with the greater degree of protein structural modification detected by ATR-FTIR. Although proteolytic activity and peptide concentrations were not directly assessed, the concurrent rise in nitrogenous metabolites together with decrease in α-helix and β-sheet structures and enhancement in random coil and β-turn conformations explains improved protein degradation during the process of fermentation. Hence, the GC–MS and FTIR results jointly deliver indirect indication that mixed-culture fermentation encouraged more substantial proteolysis and consequent amino acid catabolism.

Aldehydes such as nicotinaldehyde exhibited consistent rise across fermented treatments, with the highest percentage (13%) recorded in D6. Aldehydes and ketones perceived in fermented samples may develop from degradation pathways of amino acid. Branched-chain amino acids such as leucine, isoleucine and valine are known precursors of various aldehydes and ketones through transamination accompanied by decarboxylation and reduction/oxidation reactions. Aromatic amino acids like phenylalanine and tyrosine may also accord to the formation of volatile aldehyde through Strecker-type degradation. Though no direct extents of transamination, deamination or Strecker reactions were executed in this research, the considerable collection of aldehydes and ketones in co-culture treatments explains increased amino acid metabolism under mixed-strain fermentation conditions. Ketonic compounds, including octahydro-indanone, exhibited noteworthy enrichment particularly in D4-D6. The better accumulation of these metabolites in mixed cultures than that in monocultures further demonstrate that specific strain combinations, specifically LPC-LH (D6), has greater metabolic capacity for amino acid and precursor bioconversion. Thus, the assessed metabolite profile appears to be the result of synergistic interactions among species rather than that from the activity of individual cultures. The noticeable enhancement in glycerol and quercitol is most likely related with the coordinated carbohydrate-metabolizing enzyme systems of the lactic acid bacteria as compared to a single enzyme. Accumulation of glycerol can be result of the activity of glycerol-3-phosphate phosphatase and related glycerol metabolic enzymes following the utilization of carbohydrate, while the improved quercitol content is probably related to glycosidases and carbohydrate-hydrolysing enzymes that release cyclitol-related components from the matrix of plant. Since enzyme activities were not directly measured in this research, these mechanisms should be observed as biologically supported interpretations rather than experimentally confirmed pathways. The highest levels noted in D6 further support improved metabolic cooperation among the mixed bacterial cultures.

The most pronounced divergence from the control profile was observed in the increase of carboxylic acids and their derivatives, which became dominant chemical classes following fermentation. Organic acids such as nonynoic acid (31% in D6) and oxalic acid (13% in D6) were minor constituents in D0 but became quantitatively significant post-fermentation. This sharp rise reflects intensive lactic acid bacterial metabolism, involving carbohydrate fermentation, lipid transformation and organic acid production. A particularly notable feature of fermented samples was the emergence of nitrogen-containing heterocycles and amines, including pyridine and piperidine derivatives, which were nearly absent in the control. Their formation indicates active microbial transformation of endogenous amino acids and alkaloids through transamination, deamination, decarboxylation and Strecker-type degradation reactions. The increasing abundance of these compounds in co-culture treatments, especially D6, suggests deeper amino acid metabolism when multiple species are present. Additionally, the consistent appearance of sulfur-containing compounds exclusively in fermented samples further underscores the expanded metabolic capacity of LAB consortia to generate diverse volatile metabolites from plant substrates.

Comparative evaluation of treatment groups highlights distinct quantitative and qualitative differences between single-strain and co-culture fermentations. While single species (D1-D3) clearly differentiated the control sample by increasing acids, polyols and selected nitrogenous compounds, co-culture treatments (D4-D6) exhibited amplified metabolic intensity and broader chemical diversification. In particular, D6 consistently displayed peak levels of glycerol, major organic acids and nitrogenous metabolites, positioning it as the most metabolically active system. This pattern strongly suggests cross-feeding interactions, where metabolites produced by one strain serve as substrates for another, thereby extending metabolic pathways and generating more complex end products. The fermented volatile profile, characterized by elevated organic acids, polyols and nitrogenous heterocycles aligns with established metabolic outputs of lactic acid bacteria while simultaneously expanding the chemical landscape of D. officinale. Previous studies of (Alkay et al., 2024); Fan Yang et al. (2023) have identified organic acids and polyols as inherent bioactive components of the plant; however, the present findings demonstrate that fermentation significantly amplifies these constituents.

These compositional shifts not only reflect intensified microbial metabolism but may also contribute to enhanced bioactivity and improved functional potential. Similar findings were observed in the study of Wanlin Liu et al. (2023), which used HS-SPME-GC–MS and LC-MS to show that microbial fermentation of D. officinale significantly alters volatile and non-volatile metabolomes, producing new microbial metabolites and increasing acids and alcohols after fermentation. This directly supports the observation of specie-dependentvolatile emergence and acidification. Xing et al. (2025) reported that LAB fermentation of fruit matrices increases organic acids, alcohols and esters and identified key volatile markers associated with improved antioxidant properties. Lee et al. (2023) demonstrated via UPLC-QTOF-MS and GC–MS that LAB fermentation can raise levels of metabolites such as indole-3-lactic acid and 3-phenyllactic acid which correlate with antimicrobial and antioxidant activity; these metabolites and their functional relationship are consistent with the bioactive volatile pool we observe in co-culture treatments. A contrasting perspective was studied by Fan Yang et al. (2023), in which a significant decreases in some original phenolic compounds during fermentation despite the formation of new phenolic metabolites, illustrating that fermentation can both generate bioactive metabolites and deplete specific native compounds. This nuance helps explain why some native volatiles decline in the data even as overall antioxidant-related metabolites increase.

Color characteristics of Dendrobium officinale juice

Color parameters (L*, a*, and b*and ΔE) were determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD multiple-comparison test (p < 0.05). The color attributes of D. officinale juice were markedly influenced by lactic acid bacterial fermentation, as illustrated in the radar plot, Fig. 2 (B). The plot provides a comprehensive comparison among the non-fermented control and the fermented treatments. A clear outward expansion of the plotted areas is observed for all fermented samples (D1-D6) Compared with D0, exhibiting that fermentation modified the chromatic profile, with substantial differences observed among treatments (ANOVA, p < 0.05). Notably, the co-culture treatments (D4-D6) generally exhibited larger polygonal areas than the monocultures (D1-D3), suggesting a stronger overall impact on color development, likely attributable to synergistic microbial interactions. Regarding lightness (L*), all fermented treatments (D1-D6) showed a distinct outward shift relative to D0, indicating enhanced brightness following fermentation. Among the treatments, the co-culture D6 (LPC-LH) exhibited the most pronounced expansion along the L* axis, followed by D4 and D5, whereas the monocultures (D1-D3) demonstrated moderate but consistent increases. This trend suggests that fermentation improved matrix clarification and light reflectance, possibly through enzymatic degradation of cell wall polysaccharides and redistribution of chromophore compounds. Similar increases in L* after lactic acid fermentation have been reported by Yi et al. (2023), who observed enhanced brightness in fermented plant substrates due to structural disintegration and improved optical diffusion. In terms of redness (a), all fermented samples exhibited greater chromatic intensity than D0 with a more substantial outward expansion observed in the co-culture treatments (D4-D6) compared with the monocultures (D1-D3). Among them, D6 displayed the more enhancement than others as this pattern indicates intensified red tonal characteristics following fermentation, likely due to increased release or transformation of phenolic pigments under acidic and enzymatically active conditions. Kumar et al. (2025) demonstrated that lactic acid fermentation enhances phenolic bioavailability through microbial enzymatic hydrolysis, thereby intensifying chromatic parameters such as a. Similarly, Mohammed et al. (2023) reported increased redness in fermented plant extracts as a consequence of improved pigment liberation and stabilization. The stronger expansion observed in D4-D6 suggests that combined species may have exerted complementary enzymatic effects, resulting in greater pigment transformation compared to single-strain fermentations. A comparable trend was observed for yellowness (b), where D1-D6 showed a noticeable outward shift relative to D0 with co-culture treatments again exhibiting the most pronounced enhancement. The intensified b values in D4-D6 indicate strengthened yellow tonal components, which may be associated with improved extractability of carotenoid-related compounds or the formation of stable pigment derivatives during fermentation. Wenjing Liu et al. (2025) reported that co-fermentation systems produced greater chromatic shifts in plant-based beverages than monocultures, attributing these changes to synergistic microbial metabolism and enhanced pigment modification. The concurrent increases in both a and b in D4-D6 therefore reflect an overall intensification of warm color tones in the fermented juice. The total color difference (ΔE) demonstrates the cumulative effect of fermentation on visual perception. All fermented treatments (D1-D6) exhibited a substantial outward displacement Compared with D0, indicating clearly perceptible color changes. The most pronounced deviation was observed in D6, followed by other co-culture treatments (D4 and D5), while the monocultures showed comparatively smaller yet evident deviations. Although some studies report decreased lightness after fermentation due to pigment polymerization and non-enzymatic browning Dong et al. (2023), the present findings indicate the opposite trend. The consistent outward shifts in L* for D1-D6 suggest that, in Dendrobium officinale juice, pigment liberation and matrix clarification predominated over browning reactions. This highlights the substrate-specific nature of fermentation-induced color changes and confirms that LAB fermentation in this system enhanced brightness rather than causing darkening. The color data suggest that the D. officinale juice contains a low concentration of pigments or retains a stable extract, resulting in a light-yellow appearance. A limitation of the present research is that fermentation performance was not directly observed through viable cell counts, pH or titratable acidity measurements. Although considerable metabolite transformations and physicochemical alterations showed active microbial fermentation, the absence of these standard fermentation parameters confines direct confirmation of LAB growth kinetics and acidification patterns. Future literature should combine microbial enumeration and acidification measurements to broadly characterize the process of fermentation.

Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) and secondary structure of Dendrobium officinale juice

Differences in secondary-structure proportions attained from FTIR deconvolution were estimated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD test (p < 0.05). The FTIR spectra was used to examine the structural modifications in Dendrobium officinale juice subjected to different fermentation samples (D0-D6). As illustrated in Fig. 3 (A), the FTIR image of all samples presented characteristic absorption bands in the regions of 3500–3000 cm−1 (O—H stretching of polysaccharides and water), 2920–2850 cm−1 (C—H stretching), 1700–1500 cm−1 (amide I and II regions associated with protein secondary structure) and 1200–900 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching and glycosidic linkages of polysaccharides). Noticeable spectral differences were noted between the non-fermented treatment (D0) and fermented groups (D1-D6), particularly in the amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1), exhibiting protein structural alterations induced by microbial activity. Fermentation with single species (D1, D2, D3) manifested moderate shifts in band intensity and peak positions, while mixed-strain fermentations (D4-D6) reflected in more pronounced changes, suggesting synergistic enzymatic hydrolysis and protein-polysaccharide interactions. Deconvolution of the amide I band revealed changes in the proportions of α-helix (1651–1660 cm−1), β-sheet (1600–1639 cm−1), random coil (1640–1650 cm−1) and β-turn (1661–1700 cm−1) structures, Fig. 3 (B). The non-fermented sample (D0) revealed a higher proportion of β-sheet and α-helix, reflecting a more ordered protein structure. Fermentation with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (D1) and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (D2) led to a slight decrease in α-helix content accompanied by an increase in β-turns and random coils, implying partial unfolding of protein chains. The treatment with Lactobacillus helveticus (D3) showed similar but less intense modifications. In comparison to the mixed cultures (D4-D6) depicted a more pronounced decrease in α-helix and β-sheet content with a substantial increase in β-turn and random coil structures, particularly in D6 (LPC-LH), demonstrating that combined microbial action caused stronger proteolytic effects and structural rearrangements. The observed reduction in ordered secondary structures (α-helices and β-sheets) and associated enhancement in disordered structures (random coils and β-turns) may be related to several physicochemical changes occurring during fermentation. A major factor is the reduction in pH as a result of production of lactic acid. As fermentation advances, the accumulation of organic acids decreases the environmental pH, leading to protonation of amino acid side chains and disruption of electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and salt bridges that alleviate native protein conformations. Resultantly, proteins become partially unfolded, leading to a conversion from highly ordered structures to more flexible and unorganized conformations. Additionally, the production of lactic acid and other metabolites changes the ionic strength of the fermentation medium. Disparities in ionic strength may weaken intramolecular interactions and lower stability of protein, thereby promoting structural rearrangements and augmented exposure of hydrophobic and reactive groups. Similar fermentation-induced protein unfolding has been described in different plant-based substrates fermented with lactic acid bacteria.

(A). ATR–FTIR spectra of non-fermented and lactic acid bacteria-fermented Dendrobium officinale juice treatments (B) Amide I band deconvolution and secondary structure distribution of Dendrobium officinale juice proteins in non-fermented control and LAB-fermented samples.

The treatment with L. helveticus (D3) exhibited comparable but less intense modifications. Comparatively, mixed cultures (D4-D6) showed more distinct reduction in α-helix and β-sheet content with a considerable increase in β-turn and random coil structures, mainly in D6 (LPC-LH), representing wide structural rearrangements. The decrease in content of α-helix in D6 was statistically of importance compared with D0 (p < 0.05). These secondary structure transitions imitate the breakdown of natural protein configurations and possible introduction of hydrophilic groups, which can impact the functional as well as sensory properties of fermented D. officinale juice. Proteolytic activity was not directly enumerated in the present literature, however the previous reports have described that lactic acid bacteria possess sophisticated cell-envelope proteinases and intracellular peptidase systems capable of hydrolyzing food proteins into peptides and amino acids. According to Kieliszek et al. (2021), LAB-derived proteases showed broad substrate specificity and considerably contribute to protein degradation and generation of bioactive peptide during fermentation. Correspondingly, the proteinase purified from L. rhamnosus OXY was revealed to efficiently hydrolyze protein substrates, leading to marked structural modifications and peptide release (Waśko et al., 2012). Therefore, the pronounced decrease in ordered α-helix and β-sheet structures in combination with the associated increase in random coil and β-turn conformations observed in D4-D6, particularly D6, may probably be attributed to the action of LAB proteolytic systems and fermentation-induced acidification rather than a single mechanism. The more significant structural modifications examined in mixed-culture fermentations further indicate synergistic interactions among species, leading to enhanced substrate utilization and greater protein conformational rearrangements. Such structural unfolding may also describe the CLSM observations, where disorganization of dense matrix structures and redistribution of fluorescent compounds were apparent in co-culture treatments. However, since protease activities were not measured directly, these mechanistic understandings should be considered credible explanations supported by literature rather than definitive evidence.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The XRD patterns presented in Fig. 4 were attained from freeze-dried D. officinale juice powders prepared from non-fermented and fermented samples, allowing assessment of fermentation-induced alterations in the structural organization of the solid matrix. The non-fermented sample (D0) exhibited a relatively low and broad diffraction pattern with no sharp peaks, reflecting its predominantly amorphous structure. After fermentation with single species (D1-D3), a noticeable increase in diffraction intensity was noted, particularly in the range of 10–30°, accompanied by the appearance of weak peaks, suggesting partial structural rearrangements and the formation of ordered regions within the matrix. Among the single species, Lactobacillus helveticus (D3) observed the most pronounced alterations, with a distinct peak near 20°, suggesting the development of semi-crystalline domains likely due to enzymatic activity and acidification during fermentation. Mixed-strain fermentations (D4-D6) reflected in more significant modifications compared to single-strain treatments. All mixed treatments presented sharper and more intense peaks between 15° and 30°, indicating enhanced molecular ordering and partial crystallization of biopolymers. The LPC-LH treatment (D6) revealed the highest intensity and most distinct diffraction peaks, showing the strongest degree of structural rearrangement and semi-crystalline formation. This enhanced crystallinity in mixed-strain fermentations can be attributed to synergistic enzymatic hydrolysis of polysaccharides and proteins, leading to the breakdown of complex amorphous structures and subsequent reorganization into more ordered arrangements. Taken together, the XRD image demonstrate that fermentation alters the structural organization of D. officinale juice, with mixed-strain fermentations exerting the greatest impact on crystallinity. These structural transformations may influence the physicochemical stability and functional characteristics of the fermented juice, such as water retention, texture and bioactive moieties release.

XRD patterns of non-fermented and LAB-fermented Dendrobium officinale juice.

The outcomes of the current research are in agreement with Li et al. (2022) investigated that lactic acid bacteria fermentation of rice starch substantially improved crystallinity, as observed by sharper XRD peaks, due to enzymatic hydrolysis followed by re-aggregation of amylose and amylopectin chains into ordered regions. Similarly, Y.-n. Xu et al. (2020) found that fermentation of Dendrobium officinale polysaccharides with Lactobacillus plantarum increased the degree of crystallinity, which they attributed to the breakdown of complex polysaccharides into shorter chains that could realign into semi-crystalline domains. These coherent results support the interpretation that the synergistic activity of mixed microbial species in the current study enhanced enzymatic degradation and molecular reorganization, leading to the formation of more crystalline structures, as evidenced by the sharper peaks in D4-D6. However, not all researches report an increase in crystallinity following fermentation. Zhao et al. (2019), reported that fermentation of wheat bran with Lactobacillus species actually decreased crystallinity due to extensive degradation of ordered cellulose regions and disruption of hydrogen bonds, which prevented the reformation of crystalline domains. This inconsistent finding highlights that the effect of fermentation on crystallinity is highly dependent on the substrate composition and the enzymatic profile of the microorganisms involved. In polysaccharide-rich systems with limited cellulose, such as D. officinale juice, partial hydrolysis likely facilitates molecular realignment rather than complete structural breakdown, explaining the increase in crystallinity observed in this study. In summary these outcomes suggest that mixed-strain fermentation enhances molecular ordering in D. officinale juice, which may improve its structural stability, modify texture and influence functional properties relevant to food applications.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy of juice

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) demonstrated qualitative differences in the spatial organization and fluorescence distribution between the non-fermented control (D0) and fermented Dendrobium officinale juice samples (D0-D6), as illustrated in Fig. 5. The control treatment (D0) exhibits a relatively uniform and compact distribution of fluorescently labelled particulate structures. Following fermentation, differences in the organization and distribution of fluorescent signals were noted across the samples. Among the single-strain groups, LP (D1) revealed a relatively loosened arrangement of fluorescently labelled structures, whereas LPC (D2) exhibited greater redistribution of fluorescence with more separated particulate features. D3 presenting localized changes in fluorescence distribution while retaining relatively aggregated structures. The mixed-strain samples (D4-D6) demonstrates more pronounced qualitative modifications in fluorescence distribution and particulate organization than the single-strain treatments. D4 showed greater separation of fluorescently labelled structures, while D5 showed more dispersed fluorescent features. D6 displayed the most pronounced qualitative alteration, characterized by highly dispersed particulate features and redistribution of fluorescence within the observed field. These observations indicate that co-culture fermentation was associated with greater changes in the spatial organization of fluorescently labelled structures compared with monoculture fermentation. Moreover, these changes are qualitative and should not be interpreted as quantitative evidence of porosity, matrix disruption or polysaccharide degradation.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images of non-fermented and lactic acid bacteria-fermented Dendrobium officinale juice showing microstructural changes. Panels A-G represent control, LP, LPC, LH, LP-LPC, LP-LH and LPC-LH treatments, respectively.

The microstructural changes observed in the fermented samples are consistent with the expected effects of lactic acid bacteria on plant-based substrates. The qualitative differences in fluorescence distribution and particulate organization noted after fermentation may be associated with fermentation-induced alterations in the organization of protein-associated and other particulate components. The greater alterations observed in D4-D6 are consistent with the possibility of stronger structural reorganization under co-culture conditions; however, CLSM alone cannot establish the occurrence or extent of enzymatic hydrolysis of specific polysaccharides or proteins. Therefore, these changes are interpreted as qualitative changes in spatial organization rather than direct evidence of matrix degradation or increased porosity. These qualitative observations are broadly consistent with prior research of LAB-fermented plant matrices, which have reported modifications in particulate organization, structural loosening and redistribution of fluorescent signals following fermentation. These findings align with the study of Yaqoob et al. (2024) on LAB-fermented mulberry juice, which reported significant microstructural loosening and increased exposure of intracellular compartments due to synergistic enzymatic activity of mixed species. Their CLSM images similarly showed that co-cultures produced more extensive structural disintegration Compared withmonocultures, supporting the stronger breakdown observed in samples D4-D6. A second consistent finding comes from Wanlin Liu et al. (2023), who investigated D. officinale juice fermentation using mixed yeast-LAB cultures and documented pronounced microstructural degradation, increased porosity and enhanced fluorescence in CLSM images. They attributed these changes to combined enzymatic actions, including glycosidase and protease activities mirroring the extensive breakdown observed in sample Do, the most disrupted among all treatments.

However, a divergent trend is seen in the work of Wu et al. (2023), who showed that fermentation of Dendrobium officinale polysaccharides using Lactobacillus species resulted primarily in molecular-level degradation without causing substantial microstructural collapse under CLSM imaging. Their results indicated only mild loosening of the matrix, rather than the extensive fragmentation observed in our co-fermented samples. The discrepancy may stem from differences in substrate type (purified polysaccharides vs. whole juice), microbial species, fermentation duration or concentration of hydrolytic enzymes. Whole juice systems, such as in the present study, contain complex polysaccharide-protein networks that may be more susceptible to synergistic enzymatic breakdown during co-fermentation, explaining the more severe microstructural alterations in samples D4-D6. Thus, CLSM results demonstrate that fermentation, especially involving mixed LAB cultures, markedly improves structural degradation of D. officinale juice, promoting the release of bioactive moieties and potentially improving bioavailability. These observations propose that LAB fermentation induced modifications in the organization of protein-containing particulate structures, possibly because of acidification and microbial enzymatic activities during fermentation process. Since no polysaccharide-specific fluorescent probe was utilized and quantitative image analysis was not performed thus, the CLSM results should be interpreted qualitatively. Thus, terms like “porosity” and “matrix disruption” have been avoided, and the experimented fluorescence differences are considered only as indicative of changes in the spatial distribution of protein-associated structures. Given the observed structural modifications (XRD and CLSM) and the improved antioxidant activity following lactic acid bacterial fermentation, further research should aim at evaluating the in vitro bio accessibility and bioavailability of phenolic contents in fermented D. officinale juice. Furthermore, the storage stability (shelf-life behavior, antioxidant retention and microbial stability collectively under refrigerated and room-temperature conditions) should be investigated systematically to determine feasibility of the product. These studies will be crucial to validate the long-term functional quality and commercial potential of fermented juice as in functional beverages.

Electronic nose analysis

The electronic nose (E-nose) analysis was utilized to evaluate the alterations in the volatile aroma of Dendrobium officinale juice subjected to different fermentation sample codes. The three-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) plot clearly distinguished the samples based on their fermentation types, illustrating 63.94, 19.92 and 10.59% of the total variance by PC1, PC2 and PC3, respectively, thus accounting for 94.45% of the total changes in the dataset as shown in Fig. 6. The non-fermented control (D0) was positioned separately from the fermented treatments, demonstrating that fermentation markedly altered the volatiles composition of the juice. Distinct clustering patterns were noted for juices fermented with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (D1), Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (D2) and Lactobacillus helveticus (D3), exhibiting specie-dependenteffects on aroma compound development. The co-fermented treatments Lactiplantibacillus plantarum + Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (D4), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum + Lactobacillus helveticus (D5) and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei + Lactobacillus helveticus (D6) occupied intermediate or unique positions in the PCA space, implying potential combined effect or complementary interactions between bacterial species during volatile compound formation. The PEN3 electronic nose comprises of ten metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors with different selectivities toward volatile compounds. Precisely, W1C is primarily sensitive to aromatic compounds, W5S to nitrogen oxides, W3C to ammonia and aromatic compounds, W6S to hydrogen, W5C to short-chain alkanes and aromatic compounds, W1S to methane and broad-range hydrocarbons, W1W to sulfur-containing compounds and terpenes, W2S to alcohols as well as to partially aromatic compounds, W2W to sulfur-containing organic compounds and W3S to long-chain alkanes. Consequently, differences in sensor response intensities reflect alterations in the corresponding volatile classes during fermentation. Notably, the D5 (LP-LH) and D6 (LPC-LH) codes revealed the most pronounced shifts along PC1 and PC2, presenting a greater modification of the aroma, possibly associated with increased ester and alcohol production. The radar plot further supported these present outcomes, illustrating modifications in sensor response intensities among all samples. The control treatment Do indicate relatively low sensor responses, reflecting its mild aroma characteristics. On the contrary, fermentation with L. paracasei (D2) and L. plantarum (D1) lead to stronger responses of W1W, W2W and W5S, showing increased formation of sulfur-containing volatiles and nitrogen-related components. Increased responses of W2S and W3S further depicts enhanced development of alcohols and hydrocarbon-derived volatiles, consistent with the GC–MS results. The Lactobacillus helveticus treatment (D3) manifested moderate responses, portraying the formation of specific aroma-active compounds, though less intense than those produced by D2. Among the co-fermented samples, D5 (LP-LH) and D6 (LPC-LH) reflecting the highest sensor responses across most channels, consistent with their PCA distribution, showing the generation of complex volatile profiles with improved aromatic characteristics. The D4 (LP-LPC) treatment demonstrated intermediate behavior, suggesting balanced volatile production. Overall, the E-nose data confirmed that fermentation substantially diversified the volatile composition of Dendrobium officinale juice, with the most profound effects noted in dual-strain fermentations involving Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Lactobacillus helveticus (D6). Sensor responses differed significantly among treatments (p < 0.05). These findings emphasize the potential of controlled lactic acid bacterial fermentation to enhance and modulate the sensory quality of Dendrobium officinale juice through strain-specific and synergistic metabolic activities. The present investigation aligns well with earlier researches on LAB-fermented fruit juices, which highlight the ability of specific lactic acid bacterial species to enrich aroma complexity through the biotransformation of precursors into aromatic metabolites. Liang, Deng, Hu, Zhao, and Meng (2022) elucidated that apple juice fermented by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei demonstrated a significant increase in acids, alcohols and ketones, accompanied by a reduction in aldehydes and esters, leading to enhanced aroma intensity and complexity. This pattern closely parallels the present study, where both D1 and D2 fermentations produced higher sensor responses in W1W, W2W and W5S, presenting a richer volatile composition than the non-fermented juice. Likewise, another study by Wang, Zhang, and Lei (2021) on pear juice fermented with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus casei and Lactobacillus helveticus in monoculture and binary mixtures found that co-fermentation improved the formation of alcohols, esters and acids while decreasing aldehyde and ketone levels, yielding a more pleasant aroma and overall sensory profiling. This corroborates the present outcomes where co-fermented samples (D4-D6) revealed higher overall E-nose responses and distinct PCA clustering, suggesting combined effects among species in generating a more desirable aromatic profile. However, Jiang et al. (2025) reported the effect of LAB fermentation on heat-sterilized tomato juice and observed that while fermentation enhanced some volatiles, it also produced undesirable sulfur-containing components such as dimethyl sulfide, leading to off-odors. In contrast, Dendrobium officinale juice fermented under the conditions in this study did not exhibit signs of off-flavor development, suggesting that substrate composition and strain compatibility play crucial roles in shaping the sensory outcomes of LAB fermentation. Accordingly, the coherent findings with previous studies confirm that lactic acid bacterial fermentation, particularly through dual-strain combinations, can effectively improve the aroma complexity and sensory appeal of Dendrobium officinale juice. Simultaneously, the divergent tomato juice study underscores the importance of selecting appropriate microbial species and substrates to achieve desirable aromatic attributes without the generation of unwanted volatiles.

The present outcomes has several methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Fermentation performance was not directly characterized by viable starter culture counts at inoculation and at the end of fermentation, pH or titratable acidity; therefore, the growth kinetics and acidification profile of the LAB cultures could not be directly established. Additionally, the fermentation conditions were not optimized for D. officinale juice with respect to inoculum level, fermentation duration and temperature. The CLSM images were qualitative because no polysaccharide-specific fluorescent probe or quantitative image analysis was utilized; therefore, the images cannot be used to quantitatively establish porosity, matrix disruption, particle dimensions or specific polysaccharide degradation. Likewise, the proteolytic activity and peptide profiles were not directly measured, so the contribution of fermentation-derived peptides to antioxidant assay remains a proposed mechanism rather than an experimentally confirmed pathway. Future researches should incorporate these measurements and optimize fermentation conditions to provide stronger mechanistic and process-level validation.

Conclusion

This study reveals that lactic acid bacterial fermentation altered the antioxidant, physicochemical, structural and volatile profile of Dendrobium officinale juice in a specie-dependent manner. Fermented samples exhibit higher total phenolic, flavonoid contents and enhances DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities compared with the non-fermented control, with the LPC-LH co-culture (D6) presenting the highest values. GC–MS analysis demonstrates pronounced changes in the volatile and semi-volatile metabolite profiles after fermentation, particularly in co-culture treatments, which showed greater accumulation of organic acids, polyols and nitrogen-containing metabolites. Electronic-nose analysis further differentiated the fermented treatments from the control and suggests distinct aroma profiles among the fermentation. ATR-FTIR and XRD analyses indicated fermentation-associated changes in protein conformation and crystalline characteristics, while CLSM revealed qualitative changes in the spatial distribution and organization of fluorescently labelled particulate structures. In a nutshell, the findings indicate that LAB fermentation, particularly the LPC-LH co-culture, can significantly modify the functional, structural and aroma-related characteristics of D. officinale juice and may provide a basis for further development of fermented D. officinale beverages.
