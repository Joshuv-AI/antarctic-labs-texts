Acute retinal necrosis: Report 1—Epidemiology, clinical profile, and misdiagnosis from the largest Indian case series
Authors: Swati Priyadarshini, Parthopratim Dutta Majumder, Arshee Ahmed, Suchitra Pradeep, Amala Elizabeth George, Vinita Rao, Jyotirmay Biswas, Sudha K Ganesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To describe the clinical profile of a large cohort of patients with acute retinal necrosis (ARN) in a tertiary eyecare institute from India.Methods:Retrospective analysis of all patients with ARN, visiting the uveitis clinic of a tertiary eye institute between 2010 and 2022.Result:The study included 144 eyes of 119 patients with ARN (mean age 37.6 ± 15.8 years); 68.6% were males. A history of chickenpox was present in 12.6% and viral encephalitis in 9.2%. The common presenting symptom was blurred vision (94.9%), followed by redness (28.8%), pain (24.6%), and floaters (15.3%). Misdiagnosis occurred in 36.9% of cases. At presentation, 79.0% had unilateral disease, while 21.0% were bilateral, with many showing sequential involvement after a mean interval of 5.9 years. Anterior uveitis was documented in 30.5% of eyes, vitritis grade ≥ II in 70.8%, and all four quadrants of retinitis in 47.8%. Retinal vasculitis and optic nerve involvement were noted in 84.0% and 39.6%, respectively. Polymerase chain reaction detected herpes simplex virus and varicella-zoster virus with equal frequency (28.6% each); dual infection was found in 5.0%. Most patients received intravenous acyclovir induction followed by oral antivirals, with valacyclovir used in 80.7%. The mean treatment duration was 5 months. Follow-up averaged 2 years, during which the visual acuity declined from 1.58 to 1.89 logMAR. Major complications included cataract (79.2%), retinal detachment (72.5%), hypotony (48.6%), and optic atrophy (25%).Conclusion:ARN remains a devastating viral retinitis which is frequently underrecognized in its early stages.

Acute retinal necrosis (ARN) is a rare, rapidly progressive inflammatory condition of the eye characterized by full-thickness necrotizing retinitis, often presenting as panuveitis.[1] It is primarily caused by herpes family viruses, most commonly varicella-zoster virus (VZV), followed by herpes simplex virus types 1 and 2 (HSV-1, HSV-2). Less frequently, cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) involvement has been reported.[2] ARN typically affects otherwise healthy, immunocompetent individuals but can also occur in immunocompromised patients. Though ARN presents as a unilateral disease initially, bilateral involvement can occur after a variable period of time.

Clinically, patients present with acute vision loss, eye redness, photophobia, floaters, and sometimes ocular pain. On examination, intense vitritis, peripheral retinal whitening with patchy areas of necrosis, retinal arteritis (occlusive vasculitis), and anterior chamber inflammation are characteristic.[1] If untreated, ARN can rapidly progress circumferentially, potentially leading to retinal detachment and blindness.[3] Early diagnosis and antiviral therapy are critical to improve visual outcomes and prevent involvement of the fellow eye. However, visual outcomes in the majority of the cases are generally poor with high risks of complications, with most patients experiencing vision worse than 20/200 despite treatment.[13] This study aims to evaluate the demographics and clinical profile of patients with ARN diagnosed at a tertiary care center in South India. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the largest case series of ARN reported from India.

Method

This was a retrospective observational study of patients diagnosed with ARN between 2010 and 2022. The study was conducted at Sankara Nethralaya, a tertiary eye care center in South India. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the hospital and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

ARN was diagnosed based on clinical examination using the American Uveitis Society (AUS) criteria. Cases with a minimum follow-up of 2 months were included, while those with incomplete documentations were excluded. Additionally, any atypical retinitis that did not conform to the clinical description suggested by the AUS criteria, including posterior pole retinitis, was excluded from the analysis. Patients with ARN who were receiving therapeutic immunosuppression or had an underlying immunosuppressive condition, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, were excluded. This exclusion was undertaken to capture the classic features of the disease without dilution by atypical presentations related to immunosuppression. This approach emphasizes ARN as a syndrome occurring in otherwise immunocompetent adults, in whom herpesviruses can cause aggressive disease despite intact immunity. For each patient, information was retrieved from medical records regarding demographic details (such as age and gender), clinical presentation, disease course and outcome, ocular involvement, etiology, and associated systemic findings, and was entered into a computerized database. In our hospital, after a detailed medical history is taken, each patient undergoes a complete ophthalmic examination, including assessment of best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus examination. These details were reviewed and entered into the database. Where available, fundus diagrams or photographs were assessed, and the extent of retinitis was noted based on the number of quadrants involved. Details of prior diagnoses, treatment, or systemic illness were also recorded.

Laboratory investigations included serology for syphilis and HIV in all patients. Additional tests such as toxoplasma serology, Mantoux test, and other investigations were performed when clinically indicated or at the discretion of the treating physician. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from aqueous aspirates or vitreous samples (when obtained during surgical intervention) was carried out. For bilateral cases, only one eye was selected for such intraocular fluid analysis. For each patient, the PCR result from a single eye was considered representative and was analyzed on a per-patient basis, rather than per eye. However, due to high costs, these tests were not always feasible given patient financial constraints. In cases where PCR was negative, management of ARN was guided by the response to the treatment.

Antiviral treatment consisted of induction therapy with intravenous or oral antivirals, followed by maintenance therapy. Intravenous acyclovir (10 mg/kg, 8-hourly) was administered, followed by oral antiviral therapy. Intravitreal ganciclovir (2 mg/0.1 ml) was given as needed, with frequency determined by treatment response, severity, and clinician discretion. Oral corticosteroids (1 mg/kg/day) were initiated at least 3–4 days after starting antiviral therapy and tapered weekly. Prophylactic laser photocoagulation surrounding the posterior margin of necrotic retina was not a standardized protocol at our center and was applied only in selected patients. The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) values for statistical analysis and presented as logMAR and Snellen equivalents. Qualitative and quantitative variables were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests.

Results

The index study included 144 eyes of 119 patients with ARN [Table 1]. The mean age of the patients was 37.57 ± 15.8 years (range: 5–75 years). Of these, 81 (68.6%) were males and 38 (31.9%) were females. Fifteen patients (12.6%) had a history of chickenpox, and 11 (9.3%) had a history of viral encephalitis. Ten patients (8.4%) gave history of nonspecific viral fever, and three patients (2.5%) presented with a preceding history of trauma. The presenting complaints were blurred vision (94.9%), redness (28.8%), pain (24.6%), and floaters (15.3%).

Forty-four patients (36.9%) were initially misdiagnosed with conditions other than ARN. These included retinal vasculitis (8 patients, 18.2%), panuveitis (6, 13.6%), intermediate uveitis (7, 15.9%), papillitis (6, 13.6%), exudative retinal detachment (3, 6.8%), endogenous endophthalmitis (3, 6.8%), and toxoplasmosis (4, 9.1%). Additionally, six patients were diagnosed with anterior uveitis and one with episcleritis. In these seven cases, fundus examination was not performed, and the diagnosis was made solely on the basis of anterior segment inflammation. Two such patients were treated elsewhere with intravenous methylprednisolone. At the time of presentation to us, 19 patients were on oral valacyclovir 1 gram thrice a day with a working diagnosis of ARN.

A total of 94 (79.0%) patients had unilateral involvement. Among the 25 (21.0%) bilateral cases, 10 showed simultaneous involvement at presentation, while the remaining developed sequential involvement after an average latency period of 5.85 years (range: 2–10 years). Forty-four eyes (30.5%) had anterior uveitis; however, many of our patients were on topical corticosteroid when they presented to us. Vitritis of grade II or more was documented in 102 eyes (70.8%), while all eyes had vitreous inflammation. Details on the extent of retinitis were available for 111 eyes. Of these, 19 eyes (17.1%) had involvement of one quadrant, 31 eyes (27.9%) had two quadrants, 8 eyes (7.2%) had three quadrants, and 53 eyes (47.8%) had all four quadrants affected. Retinal vasculitis was documented in 121 eyes (84.0%), while optic nerve involvement was observed in 57 eyes (39.6%).

PCR for HSV, VZV, and CMV genomes was performed on ocular fluids in 94 (78.9%) patients: aqueous specimens from 74 (62.1%) cases and vitreous specimens from 20 (16.9%) cases. In nine patients, PCR results from aqueous aspirates were further confirmed using vitreous samples obtained during surgery; thus, both aqueous and vitreous PCR analyses were performed in 7.6% of cases. Among PCR-positive cases, HSV and VZV were isolated with equal frequency, each in 34 patients (28.6%). In 18 cases (17.6%), no viral DNA was detected. Dual positivity was observed in 6 patients (5.0%) for both HSV and VZV. HSV with CMV was detected in 1 case (0.8%), and VZV with CMV in 1 case (0.8%). In PCR-negative cases, treatment was continued as conventional antiviral therapy. In cases with dual positivity with CMV as one of the organisms, systemic acyclovir was continued with intravitreal ganciclovir injections.

The median time from symptom onset to antiviral treatment initiation was 15 days (range: 1–120 days).

Initial induction therapy with intravenous acyclovir was administered in 74 patients (62.2%) cases and subsequently shifted oral antiviral drugs. Of the patients receiving oral antiviral therapy, 96 (80.7%) were treated with valacyclovir alone (1 gram three times a day), 12 (10.1%) with acyclovir (800 mg four times a day) alone, and 7 (5.9%) received both agents at different times during their treatment. The main reason for the shift from oral valacyclovir to oral acyclovir was financial constraints associated with valacyclovir therapy. None of the patients developed any renal abnormalities secondary to acyclovir therapy. The average duration of antiviral therapy was 5.04 ± 3.05 months (range: 2–24 months). Oral corticosteroids were administered in all cases. Only 16 eyes received intravitreal injections of ganciclovir. Prophylactic laser barrage was performed in 22 eyes (15.5%).

The median follow-up duration was 735 days (range: 60 days–7.5 years). The mean visual acuity at presentation was 1.58 ± 1.30 logMAR, which declined to 1.89 ± 1.44 logMAR at final follow-up. Complications encountered were cataract (114 eyes, 79.2%), ocular hypertension (30 eyes, 21.6%), retinal detachment (103 eyes, 72.5%), cystoid macular edema (CME) (14 eyes, 9.7%), hypotony (70 eyes, 48.6%), epiretinal membrane (25 eyes, 17.4%), and optic atrophy (36 eyes, 25%).

Discussion

The present study provides several important insights into the clinical characteristics of patients with ARN from India. First, representing the largest case series reported from India to date, this study delineates clinical features that have been sparsely described in the existing literature. Table 2 summarizes and compares previously published Indian case series on ARN. Among the five available studies, three primarily focused on outcomes of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment secondary to ARN, thereby limiting their ability to comprehensively characterize the overall clinical spectrum of the disease. In contrast, the present study additionally highlights referral patterns, diagnostic inaccuracies, and associated risk factors in a large cohort, areas that have not been systematically addressed previously.[456] Second, this study is among the few large case series in the literature to exclude patients with underlying immunosuppression. Consequently, the cohort reflects the clinical characteristics of ARN occurring in immunocompetent individuals, underscoring the aggressive nature of the disease despite intact immunity. Third, PCR data in Indian patients with ARN have not been widely reported in large cohorts. Even prior Indian studies evaluating surgical outcomes of retinal detachment secondary to ARN did not provide detailed virological confirmation or details.[456] In this context, the present study offers comprehensive PCR-based etiological data and, to our knowledge, is the first report from India to document dual viral genome positivity in patients with ARN.

The current study recorded the initial diagnoses assigned to patients with ARN before presentation to our institute, revealing a high rate of misdiagnosis. Since acute retinal necrosis typically begins in the peripheral retina with sparing of the posterior pole in the early stages, patients often present with minimal ocular symptoms initially, and the disease may be overlooked if a thorough dilated fundus examination is not performed. In such situations, ARN may be mistaken for acute anterior uveitis or other clinical entities, as has also been reported in the literature. In our cohort, about one in three patients were misdiagnosed before referral to our tertiary eye care center. Notably, two patients were inappropriately treated with pulse corticosteroids under the assumption of non-infectious uveitis. Zhu et al.[9] reported an immunocompetent male with ARN initially diagnosed as Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease (VKH), which led to corticosteroid initiation without antivirals and subsequent disease progression. The clinical overlap included panuveitis, serous retinal detachment, and vasculitis, as well as OCT features frequently seen in VKH. Only deterioration, careful clinical review, and PCR confirmation identified ARN as the cause. ARN can mimic Behçet’s disease, sarcoidosis, lupus retinal vasculitis, and even toxoplasmic retinochoroiditis, given shared findings such as occlusive vasculitis, panuveitis, necrotizing retinitis, and disc edema.[9] We attribute this high rate of misdiagnosis in our series to several factors: lack of awareness of ARN, omission of dilated fundus examination in cases of anterior uveitis (six patients were diagnosed as anterior uveitis and one as episcleritis), and possible limitations in the skill of indirect ophthalmoscopy among many clinicians.

Demographic features such as mean age and gender predilection in our study were consistent with reports from other studies in India and worldwide.[45678,] In the present study, the youngest patient with ARN was 5 years old. Although many previous Indian studies have not explicitly reported age ranges, Roy et al.[7] documented the youngest affected patient in their series as 6 years of age. Additionally, Mahajan et al.[10] reported a case of unilateral ARN in a pediatric patient. Other pediatric cases reported from India include a 5-year-old child with extensive peripheral necrotizing retinitis following recovery from SARS-CoV-2 infection, and a 6-year-old child who developed exudative retinal detachment complicating ARN.[1112]

A history of previous herpetic infection has been reported in patients with ARN. In a study from the United Kingdom, 55.6% of patients with ARN had a history of herpetic infection.[13] The risk of ARN following herpetic encephalitis has been estimated to be 3.7–11%.[14] In the index study, history of chickenpox was elicited in only 12.6% of patients. In a recent case series from India, 22% reported a previous history of chickenpox.[8] Being a retrospective analysis, it is not impossible that a prior episode of varicella (chickenpox) may often be missed or not elicited in the medical history of patients diagnosed with ARN, possibly due to subclinical or unrecognized infections in childhood.

ARN most commonly presents unilaterally, but bilateral involvement can occur in about 3–35% of cases. VZV or HSV is believed to propagate to the other eye by axonal transport along the optic nerves and through the optic chiasm, allowing the virus to spread from the initially affected retina to the contralateral eye.[15] The second eye (fellow eye) can become affected either simultaneously or sequentially, often within the first 6 weeks to several months after the initial eye is involved.[15] The index study recorded 21% bilateral cases, and among them, 10 patients simultaneous involvement.

One of the major limitations of the present study was that PCR testing was performed in only 78.9% of patients. PCR remains an important microbiological investigation in the management of ARN and ideally should be performed on either aqueous or vitreous samples, with the latter being more sensitive.[13] In another study by Nasir et al.,[6] only 45.4% of patients underwent PCR analysis of ocular fluids, despite all patients undergoing surgery for retinal detachment secondary to ARN. Except for one case series comprising 18 patients, none of the studies included PCR testing in all patients, likely reflecting the high cost of the investigation and the associated socioeconomic burden on patients.[8] However, as in this case series, PCR may fail to yield results; in our cohort, 17.6% of cases showed no detectable viral DNA. The possible reasons for negative results include insufficient DNA levels in the early stages of disease (especially in aqueous aspirates),[16] prior antiviral treatment, nucleic acid degradation due to improper sampling or transportation, the presence of amplification inhibitors in the reaction system, or mutations at primer binding sites.[17] However, other studies from India showed a similar observation on PCR negativity. Nasir et al.[6] reported a PCR positivity of only 32.5% in their cohort of ARN patients. Additionally, ARN secondary to other viral etiologies, such as Epstein–Barr virus, cannot be entirely excluded as the present study was unable to perform PCR testing for these pathogens due to nonavailability and logistical constraints. However, as outlined in the methodology, in cases with negative PCR results, the diagnosis and management of ARN were guided by the clinical response to treatment. Resolution of retinitis with conventional antiviral therapy in these patients suggests a higher likelihood of false-negative PCR results for HSV or VZV, rather than involvement of alternative viral pathogens.

Our study also reported instances of dual viral positivity: Six patients tested positive for both HSV and VZV, one for HSV with CMV, and one for VZV with CMV. Although extremely rare, such dual positivity in ARN has been reported in the literature.[18192021]

Blumenkranz et al.[22] demonstrated that after starting intravenous acyclovir for ARN, regression of retinal lesions was first observed on average 3.9 days after initiation of therapy, with complete resolution requiring an average of 32.5 days. Notably, no new lesions or optic nerve involvement developed more than 48 hours after the start of therapy, although lesion progression within the first 48 hours could still occur.[22] Palay et al.[23] reported that the rate of bilateral involvement in ARN dropped significantly with systemic antiviral treatment from 75.3% to 35.1%. As a protocol, though our institute advocates intravenous antiviral treatment for all cases of ARN, this is not always possible as reflected in our study.

Many of our patients were started already on oral valacyclovir (1 gram three times a day), and some of the patients declined hospital admission for intravenous treatment. High-dose oral valacyclovir (2 g three times daily) is clinically equivalent to intravenous acyclovir for ARN, with comparable visual outcomes, retinal detachment rates, and vision loss.[24] While acyclovir reaches peak plasma levels faster than oral valacyclovir, overall systemic exposure (area under curve) is similar.[25] Nineteen patients were on oral valacyclovir 1 gram thrice a day with a working diagnosis of ARN at the time of presentation. Studies have shown that valacyclovir 2 g thrice a day is pharmacokinetically equivalent to intravenous acyclovir and clinically effective, whereas 1 g thrice a day a day is also effective but results in slightly lower plasma levels.[2627] A small section of the patients with ARN had to shift to acyclovir because of the higher cost of therapy with valacyclovir in our study.

Being a retrospective study, the index study has several limitations. Some patients could not be included as they had only a single visit and were subsequently lost to follow-up. Similarly, some patients presented with advanced complications such as phthisis, following suboptimal management elsewhere. Given our role as a tertiary care hospital, a referral bias is inevitable, with the possibility that more severe cases of ARN were overrepresented. Despite its limitations, the present study highlights a clear pattern of ARN in an Indian cohort. In addition to outlining the demographic characteristics of affected patients, it provides important clinical insights into this sight-threatening condition. Notably, this is the first study from India to systematically document the spectrum of initial misdiagnoses, underscoring the diagnostic challenges and the consequent delays in initiating appropriate antiviral therapy. Our findings also emphasize that PCR, although invaluable, can yield negative results in a subset of cases, while dual viral positivity is not as uncommon as previously thought. Collectively, these observations reinforce that ARN remains a devastating viral retinitis which is frequently underrecognized in its early stages. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion, perform careful dilated fundus examinations in all suspected cases, and initiate antiviral therapy promptly to preserve vision.
