The Wishart–Rosenzweig–Porter random matrix ensemble
Victor Delapalme, Leticia F. Cugliandolo, Grégory Schehr, Marco Tarzia, Davide Venturelli

Abstract
In recent years the Rosenzweig–Porter (RP) ensemble, obtained by adding a diagonal matrix with independent and identically distributed elements to a Gaussian random matrix, has been widely used as a minimal model for the emergence of fractal eigenstates in complex many-body systems. A key open question concerns the robustness of its phase diagram when the assumption of independent and uncorrelated entries is relaxed — an assumption that simplifies its analysis, but is generally violated in realistic quantum systems. In this work, we take a first step in this direction by considering a deformed Wishart (rather than Gaussian) random matrix, which we dub the “Wishart–RP” ensemble. Using perturbation theory, as well as the cavity and replica methods and the Dyson Brownian motion approach, we characterize its phase diagram and localization properties. Remarkably, we show that the level compressibility, which quantifies spectral correlations in the fractal phase, coincides with that of the Gaussian RP model, thereby extending the universality conjectured in [SciPost Phys. 14 , 110 (2023)] beyond the fully uncorrelated setting. We confirm our results with numerical tests.

The Wishart–Rosenzweig–Porter ensemble

The WRP ensemble is defined as a sum of two
N\times N
random matrices
A
and
B
:

\textbf{H}=\textbf{A}+\nu\textbf{B}\,,

(2)

where
\nu
is a constant of

\mathcal{O}(1)
.
Here,
A
is a random diagonal matrix
A_{ij}=a_{i}\delta_{ij}
, where the
a_{i}
’s are
N
independent and identically distributed random variables with probability density
p_{a}
(we assume
\langle a\rangle=0
, and
p_{a}(0)>0
).
In Eq. (
2
),
B
is a Wishart matrix:

\textbf{B}=M^{-\gamma}\,\textbf{WW}^{T}\,,

(3)

where
W
is an
N\times M
rectangular matrix with i.i.d. Gaussian distributed entries, i.e.

\rho_{W}[\textbf{W}]=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{NM/2}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{M}e^{-W_{i\ell}^{2}/2}\,,

(4)

such that
\langle W_{i\ell}\rangle=0
and
\langle W_{i\ell}^{2}\rangle=1
.
We define

c=N/M\leq 1
,
and
we restrict ourselves to the case in which
N
and
M
are of the same order, i.e.
c
is of

\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The elements of the matrix
B
are given by

\displaystyle B_{ij}

\displaystyle=M^{-\gamma}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}W_{i\ell}W_{j\ell}\,,

(5)

hence one has

\langle B_{ii}\rangle=M^{1-\gamma},\qquad\langle B_{ij}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle B_{ij}^{2}\rangle=M^{1-2\gamma}\quad\text{for }i\neq j\,.

(6)

Note that the matrix
B
in Eq. (
5
) is positive definite, i.e. with all positive eigenvalues.
In the large-
N
limit, the eigenvalues of
B
are distributed according to the celebrated Marčenko–Pastur law
[
1
,
2
]
.
Since
\langle\mathrm{Tr}\,\textbf{B}\rangle\propto NM^{1-\gamma}\propto N^{2-\gamma}
,
it follows that the eigenvalues of
B
(and, consequently, the support of the Marčenko–Pastur distribution) scale as
N^{1-\gamma}
. In particular, they grow with
N
for
\gamma<1
, decrease with
N
for
\gamma>1
, and remain of

\mathcal{O}(1)
for
\gamma=1
.

At first sight, the ensemble (
2
) appears similar to the model introduced in Ref.
[
50
]
, where the matrix
\mathbf{B}
is likewise constructed as a sum of independent projectors. However, there is an important distinction between the two cases: in Ref.
[
50
]
, the matrix
\mathbf{B}
is defined so that its energy levels are independent random variables, and therefore follow the Poisson statistics. In contrast, in our setting,
\mathbf{B}
is rotationally invariant, and its energy levels exhibit the universal correlations of random matrix theory at all scales. Although the phase diagrams of the two models share qualitative similarities (see Sec.
3.1
below), this distinction results in fundamentally different spectral correlations. In this sense, the WRP model studied here is more closely related to the standard GRP ensemble than to the model of Ref.
[
50
]
.

Nonetheless, the WRP ensemble also differs from the standard GRP ensemble in a key aspect: the entries of
\mathbf{B}
are statistically dependent. In particular, from Eq. (
5
) it is straightforward to show that, albeit the connected two-point correlations vanish, the correlations between
n
-tuples of matrix elements with repeated (but different) indices are given by

\langle B_{i_{1}i_{2}}B_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots B_{i_{n}i_{1}}\rangle=M^{\,1-n\gamma}\,.

(7)

Note that, since
M=N/c
, the parameter
c
has the effect of tuning these correlations (at fixed
N
), the maximally correlated case corresponding to the
c\to 0
limit.

Perturbation theory: Mott’s criteria for localization and ergodicity

The simplest and most intuitive way to analyze the phase diagram of the model is through first and second-order perturbation theory for the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of
H
in
Eq.

2
, which yield the so-called
Mott’s criteria
for localization and ergodicity
[
85
]
.
These have been successfully applied to the GRP model and its generalizations (see e.g. Ref.
[
45
]
for a detailed explanation).

In the limit where the off-diagonal matrix
B
is absent, all eigenvectors
|\psi_{i}\rangle
are trivially localized on a single site, i.e.
|\psi_{i}\rangle=|i\rangle
(where
|i\rangle
is the position basis), with corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}=a_{i}
. The first criterion, known as
Mott’s criterion for localization
, states that Anderson localization around a single matrix index occurs when the average level spacing
\Delta=1/(N\rho)
is much larger than the tunneling amplitude between different indices. The second criterion, known as
Mott’s criterion for ergodicity
, provides a sufficient condition for ergodicity. The idea is to estimate the average escape rate
\Gamma
of a particle localized on a given site using Fermi’s Golden Rule, and to compare it to the spread of energy levels. When the average spreading width
\Gamma
is much larger than the
energy bandwidth,
the different indices are fully hybridized: starting from a given site, the wave packet spreads to any other site with the same energy on a timescale of
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

In equations, the Mott’s criterion for localization reads:
\langle|H_{ij}|\rangle\ll\Delta
, where
\Delta=1/(N\rho)
is the average gap,
\rho
being the average spectral density at the considered value of the energy.
Since for
\gamma>1
the eigenvalues of the matrix
B
are of order
N^{1-\gamma}
and they all vanish in the large-
N
limit, the average density of states
is asymptotically given by the probability distribution of the entries of the matrix
A
, namely
\rho(\lambda)=p_{a}(\lambda)
.
For
\gamma<1
, instead, the entries of
A
are much smaller than the eigenvalues of
B
in the large-
N
limit, and the average DoS is given by a Marčenko–Pastur distribution with support growing as
N^{1-\gamma}
. We thus obtain that the average level spacing is

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta=1/(Np_{a}(\lambda))=1/(Mc\,p_{a}(\lambda)),&{\rm for}\;\;\;\gamma>1\,,\\[5.0pt]
\Delta\propto N^{-\gamma},&{\rm for}\;\;\;\gamma<1\,.\end{array}\right.

Since the spectral properties of the model do not depend on the specific energy at which they are probed, in the following, without loss of generality, we focus on the center of the energy band, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}E_{0}=0+\mathcal{O}(N^{1-\gamma}),&\qquad{\rm for}\;\;\;\gamma>1\,,\\[5.0pt]
E_{0}\propto N^{1-\gamma},&\qquad{\rm for}\;\;\;\gamma<1\,.\end{array}\right.

From the definition of the model one immediately obtains

\langle|H_{ij}|\rangle=\nu M^{-\gamma}\left\langle\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}W_{i\ell}W_{j\ell}\right|\right\rangle=\nu\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}M^{1/2-\gamma}\,.

(20)

The Mott’s criterion then states that Anderson localization (i.e. absolute convergence of the perturbative expansion) close to the eigenvectors of
A
occurs provided that

\langle|H_{ij}|\rangle=\nu\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}M^{1/2-\gamma}\ll\dfrac{1}{cp_{a}(0)M}\ \ \ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \ \ \gamma>3/2\,.

(21)

Applying second-order perturbation theory to the eigenvalues and the Fermi golden rule, we now compute the average bandwidth
\Gamma
that corresponds to the energy window within which hybridization occurs:

\Gamma=2\pi p_{a}(0)\left\langle\sum_{j}H_{ij}^{2}\right\rangle=2\pi p_{a}(0)N\nu^{2}M^{1-2\gamma}=2\pi p_{a}(0)\nu^{2}cM^{2-2\gamma}\,.

(22)

The quantity
\hbar\Gamma
can be interpreted as the bandwidth that can be reached in a time of
\mathcal{O}(1)

from a given site
i
, and is often called the Thouless energy
E_{T}
. This implies that the eigenvectors within this energy window are hybridized by the
Wishart perturbation.

For
1<\gamma<3/2
, such energy band decreases with the system size as
E_{T}\propto N^{2-2\gamma}
, but is still much larger than the mean level spacing
\Delta\propto N^{-1}
. This entails that the system is not Anderson localized; nevertheless,
E_{T}
remains much smaller than the total bandwidth, which is of
\mathcal{O}(1)
. This signifies that the particle can only explore a subextensive portion of the total Hilbert space.

The Anderson localization transition occurs when
E_{T}
becomes smaller than the mean level spacing, i.e. for
\gamma>3/2
. This implies that the average escape time from site
i
, defined as
\Delta t\equiv\hbar/E_{T}
, grows at least linearly with
N
, and thus the eigenfunctions remain localized on
\mathcal{O}(1)
sites. In contrast, the transition to the fully delocalized phase takes place when
E_{T}
becomes of the order of the total bandwidth, i.e. for
\gamma\leq 1
. For
\gamma<1
one has that
E_{T}
is much larger than the total bandwidth
N^{1-\gamma}
. Hence, starting at site
i
, a wave packet can reach any other site in a time of
\mathcal{O}(1)
, corresponding to full delocalization.

In the intermediate phase,
1<\gamma<3/2
, the support set of the eigenvectors (i.e. the number of sites hybridized by the perturbation) is given by the ratio between the width of the hybridized energy window and the average gap between adjacent energy levels. It therefore scales as

\frac{E_{T}}{\Delta}\sim N^{D},\qquad D=3-2\gamma.

(23)

The partially extended but fractal eigenstates are thus linear combinations of
N^{D}
localized states associated with nearby energy levels. The wave-function of one of these eigenstates can thus be represented as
[
61
,
35
,
42
]

|\psi\rangle\approx\sum_{i:|a_{i}|<E_{T}}c_{i}\,N^{-D/2}|i\rangle\,,

(24)

with
c_{i}
being a Gaussian random variable with zero mean and variance
1
. These eigenstates give rise to the so-called
mini-bands
in the local spectrum (see Fig.
2
). The Thouless energy thus corresponds to the energy window within which Wigner–Dyson-like spectral correlations (and in particular level repulsion) establish.

All the moments
I_{q}
of the wave-function coefficients (the so-called generalized inverse participation ratios, IPRs) behave as

I_{q}=\sum_{i}|\langle i|\psi\rangle|^{2{q}}\propto N^{D_{q}(1-{q})}\,,

(25)

which defines
the fractal dimensions
D_{q}
.
From
Eq.

24
, we thus deduce that the
D_{q}

are degenerate and equal to
D
for all positive integer
{q}
(see Eq. (
35
) below for a more precise computation).
Hence, similarly
to the GRP model, the intermediate phase of the WRP ensemble is fractal but not multifractal
[
31
,
45
,
50
]
(in which case
D_{q}
would actually vary with
q
). As discussed above in Sec.
1
, the emergence of such a fractal phase is particularly relevant in many contexts.

In summary, the phase diagram of the WRP ensemble is schematically shown in Fig.
1
and contains three phases: fully delocalized for
\gamma<1
, Anderson localized for
\gamma>3/2
, and fractal for
1<\gamma<3/2
. The fractal dimensions (for
{q}\geq 1
) are given by

D_{q}=D=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\quad\textrm{for~}\gamma<1\,,\\
3-2\gamma&\quad\textrm{for~}1<\gamma<3/2\,,\\
0&\quad\textrm{for~}\gamma>3/2\,.\end{array}\right.

(26)

3.1
Spectrum of fractal dimensions

In this Section we derive the full multifractal spectrum of eigenstate amplitudes using standard perturbation theory.
To this end, we follow closely the
perturbative calculation done in Ref.
[
31
]
for the GRP model, by adapting it to the WRP ensemble.
We denote by
w_{ij}=|\psi_{i}(j)|^{2}
the squared amplitude of the
i
-th eigenvector on site
j
, with
\psi_{i}(j)=\langle j|\psi_{i}\rangle
.
The first-order correction to the eigenvectors of
A
reads

|\psi_{i}\rangle=|i\rangle+\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{H_{ij}}{a_{i}-a_{j}}\,|j\rangle.

(27)

Consequently, for
j\neq i
, the amplitude is

w_{ij}=\frac{H_{ij}^{2}}{(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}}.

(28)

The convergence of the perturbative series is ensured for
\gamma>3/2
, where eigenstates are fully localized.
For
1<\gamma<3/2
, convergence still holds due to the random signs of both
H_{ij}
and
\Delta_{ij}=a_{i}-a_{j}
, as in the RP ensemble.

Equation (
28
) shows that
w_{ij}
results from the product of two independent random factors:

x_{ij}=H_{ij}^{2}
, and
y_{ij}=\Delta_{ij}^{-2}
.
The first is the square of a Gaussian variable with variance
\langle H_{ij}^{2}\rangle=\nu^{2}N^{1-2\gamma}
, while the second has a heavy-tailed distribution
P(y_{ij})\sim y_{ij}^{-3/2}
and a typical value of
\order{1}
.
Hence,
w_{ij}
inherits a power-law tail with exponent
3/2
and a typical scale
w_{\mathrm{typ}}\sim N^{1-2\gamma}
.
We can thus represent its probability density as

P(w_{ij})=\frac{1}{w_{\mathrm{typ}}}\,P_{\mathrm{reg}}\!\left(\frac{w_{ij}}{w_{\mathrm{typ}}}\right)+C\,\frac{\Theta(w_{ij}>N^{1-2\gamma})}{N^{\gamma-1/2}\,w_{ij}^{3/2}},

(29)

where
P_{\mathrm{reg}}
denotes the regular part, and
C
is fixed by normalization.
Imposing the normalization condition
\sum_{j}|\psi_{i}(j)|^{2}=1\Leftrightarrow\langle w_{ij}\rangle=N^{-1}
introduces an upper cutoff
w_{\max}
on the singular tail, obtained from

N\langle w_{ij}\rangle\!\sim\!N^{2-2\gamma}+\frac{C}{N^{\gamma-3/2}}\!\int_{N^{1-2\gamma}}^{w_{\max}}\!dw_{ij}\,w_{ij}^{-1/2}=1,

(30)

which yields
w_{\max}\sim N^{2(\gamma-3/2)}
.
Because amplitudes cannot exceed unity, this expression holds only for
\gamma<3/2
; when
\gamma>3/2
, in the localized phase, one must set
w_{\max}=1
.
To restore proper normalization in the localized regime, an additional delta peak at
w_{ij}=1
must be included:

\hat{P}(w_{ij})=P(w_{ij})+A\,\delta(w_{ij}-1),\qquad\text{for }\gamma>3/2,

(31)

with
A=N^{-1}
, yielding the dominant contribution from the fully localized site.

(a)
(b)

Figure 5:
(a) Multifractal spectrum
f(\alpha)
, Eq. (
33
), and (b) fractal dimensions
D_{q}
, Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
),
for several values of
\gamma
spanning the three regimes.

Next, we define the function
f(\alpha)
(known as the spectrum of fractal dimensions)
in such a way that
\sim N^{f(\alpha)}
nodes have wavefunction amplitudes

|\psi_{i}(j)|^{2}\sim N^{-\alpha}
.
For
1<\gamma<3/2
, the integration of the tail of
P(w_{ij})
gives

N^{f(\alpha)}=\frac{C}{N^{\gamma-3/2}}\!\int_{N^{-\alpha}}^{N^{2(\gamma-3/2)}}\!dw_{ij}\,w_{ij}^{-3/2}\sim N^{\alpha/2+3/2-\gamma},

(32)

valid for

\alpha
in between

\alpha_{\min}=2(3/2-\gamma)
and
\alpha_{\max}=2\gamma-1
.
Thus,

f(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{3}{2}-\gamma,\qquad(\alpha_{\min}<\alpha<\alpha_{\max}).

(33)

For
\gamma>3/2
, the minimum exponent becomes
\alpha_{\min}=0
, and the triangular shape of
f(\alpha)
at the transition,
f(\alpha)=\alpha/2
, coincides with that of the Anderson model on the Bethe lattice
[
86
,
87
]
.

Alternatively, one may compute directly the moments of the wave-function intensities,

N\langle|\psi_{i}(j)|^{2q}\rangle\propto N^{-\tau_{q}}
, using

\langle w_{ij}^{q}\rangle\sim N^{q(1-2\gamma)}+\frac{C}{N^{\gamma-1/2}}\!\int_{N^{1-2\gamma}}^{N^{2(\gamma-3/2)}}\!dw_{ij}\,w_{ij}^{q-3/2}.

(34)

For
q<1/2
, the integral is dominated by typical amplitudes, leading to
\tau_{q}=q(2\gamma-1)-1
,
while for
q>1/2
large fluctuations dominate, yielding
\tau_{q}=(q-1)(3-2\gamma)
.
The corresponding generalized fractal dimensions,
D_{q}=\tau_{q}/(q-1)
, are then

D_{q}=\begin{cases}3-2\gamma,&q>1/2,\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{1-q(2\gamma-1)}{1-q},&q<1/2.\end{cases}

(35)

In the localized regime (
\gamma>3/2
), a similar computation gives:

D_{q}=\begin{cases}0,&q>1/(2\gamma-1),\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{1-q(2\gamma-1)}{1-q},&q<1/(2\gamma-1).\end{cases}

(36)

The full set of
f(\alpha)
and
D_{q}
curves is displayed in Fig.
5
.

For
\gamma>3/2
, the distribution
\hat{P}(w_{ij})
in Eq. (
31
) produces a delta peak at
w_{ij}=1
, resulting in a singular contribution
N^{-1}\delta(w_{ij}-1)
.
The corresponding multifractal spectrum exhibits a spike at
\alpha=0
, see Fig.
5
(a).
Although such a non-convex
f(\alpha)
cannot be represented as a Legendre transform, the scaling exponents
\tau_{q}
extracted from Eq. (
36
) match those of the convex envelope of
f(\alpha)
,
namely
f(\alpha)=\alpha/(2\gamma-1)
for
0\leq\alpha\leq 2\gamma-1
.
An analogous triangular shape with slope smaller than
1/2
is also observed in the localized regime on random regular graphs
[
86
]
.

4
The cavity method

In this Section, we apply the cavity method to determine the diagonal elements of the resolvent matrix of our model (
2
). This approach not only enables us to re-derive the phase diagram
shown in Fig.
1

beyond perturbation theory, but also provides access to more complex spectral observables, such as the two-point correlations of the energy levels and the spectral compressibility.

The basic idea of this approach is to obtain a
self-consistent relation for the resolvent matrix
\textbf{G}=(\lambda_{\epsilon}\mathbb{1}-\textbf{H})^{-1}
of
H
, which becomes asymptotically exact in the large-
N
limit. Here
\lambda_{\epsilon}=\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon
,
\lambda
being the real energy at which we probe the spectral properties of
H
, and
\epsilon
being an imaginary regulator that will be sent to zero at the end of the calculation (after taking the
N\to\infty
limit).
To set the stage, let
us assume that we know the resolvent of an
(N-1)\times(N-1)
matrix

\textbf{H}^{(1)}
, and let us add an extra row and an extra column (say row
1
and column
1
), thereby obtaining an
N\times N
matrix that we denote
H
.
Using the standard formula of matrix inversion one immediately deduces

\left[G_{11}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\right]^{-1}=\frac{\textrm{minor}(\lambda_{\epsilon}\mathbb{1}-\textbf{H})_{11}}{\det(\lambda_\epsilon\mathbb{1} - \textbf{H})}\,.

(37)

We now use the Schur complement formula (also known as the block matrix inversion formula) to expand the determinant in the denominator in terms of the minor along the first row. After simple algebra one immediately obtains

\left[G_{11}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\right]^{-1}=\lambda_{\epsilon}-H_{11}-\sum_{i,j=2}^{N}H_{1i}G^{(1)}_{ij}(\lambda_{\epsilon})H_{j1}\,,

(38)

where the superscript of
G^{(1)}_{ij}
indicates the element of the resolvent of the
(N-1)\times(N-1)
matrix

\textbf{H}^{(1)}
in the absence of the first row and column, with indices
i,j
going from
2
to
N
.

This relation is
general
and
exact
, without any assumption on the elements
H_{ij}
. However, in order to close these equations and obtain useful relations for the diagonal terms, one must introduce certain approximations. The standard approach consists in neglecting the contributions of the off-diagonal terms
\sum_{i\neq j=2}^{N}H_{1i}G^{(1)}_{ij}(\lambda_{\epsilon})H_{j1}
, which leads to a closed set of equations for the diagonal elements. Yet, applying this approximation to the Wishart case (even to the pure Wishart ensemble, without the matrix
\mathbf{A}
) yields an incorrect self-consistent relation — that even fails to reproduce the correct Marčenko–Pastur distribution for the average DoS. As discussed in Ref.
[
2
]
, a different treatment is required for the Wishart ensemble. By substituting
H_{1i}
and
H_{j1}
with their explicit definitions from Eqs. (
2
) and (
5
), and separating the resulting double sum over
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
into contributions with equal and distinct indices, we obtain:

\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{M}W_{1\ell}W_{i\ell}W_{1\ell^{\prime}}W_{j\ell^{\prime}}=\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}W_{1\ell}^{2}W_{i\ell}W_{j\ell}+\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\sum_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}}^{M}W_{1\ell}W_{i\ell}W_{1\ell^{\prime}}W_{j\ell^{\prime}}\,.

(39)

In this expression,
W_{1\ell}^{2}
is a random variable with mean
1
and variance
2
, while
W_{1\ell}W_{1\ell^{\prime}}
are Gaussian random variables with zero mean and unit variance. As discussed in Ref.
[
2
]
, for a fixed realization of the elements of
\mathbf{H}^{(1)}
, the left-hand side of the equation above converges in the thermodynamic limit to its average,
\nu M^{-\gamma}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}W_{i\ell}W_{j\ell}=\nu B_{ij}
. We thus obtain, to the leading order,

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\sum_{i,j=2}^{N}G^{(1)}_{ij}\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{M}W_{1\ell}W_{i\ell}W_{1\ell^{\prime}}W_{j\ell^{\prime}}\simeq\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\sum_{i,j=2}^{N}G^{(1)}_{ij}\left(\nu B_{ij}+O(M^{1-\gamma})\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{M^{\gamma}}\textrm{Tr}\left[\textbf{G}^{(1)}\nu\textbf{B}^{(1)}\right]+O(M^{2(1-\gamma)})\,.

The trace in the expression above can be rewritten as (we omit the superscript
(1)
to simplify the notation):

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}\left[(\lambda_{\epsilon}\mathbb{1}-\textbf{A}-\nu\textbf{B})^{-1}(\nu\textbf{B}+\textbf{A}-\lambda_{\epsilon}\mathbb{1}-\textbf{A}+\lambda_{\epsilon}\mathbb{1})\right]=\textrm{Tr}\left[-\mathbb{1}+(\lambda_{\epsilon}\mathbb{1}-\textbf{A})\textbf{G}\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=-(N-1)+\sum_{i=2}^{N}(\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{i})G_{ii}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\,.

In the large-
N
limit, the resolvent of the
N\times N
matrix converges to the resolvent of the
(N-1)\times(N-1)
matrix
(up to corrections of
\mathcal{O}(1/N
)). Furthermore
H_{11}=a_{1}+\nu M^{-\gamma}\sum_{l}W_{1\ell}^{2}=a_{1}+\nu M^{1-\gamma}+\mathcal{O}(M^{1/2-\gamma})
. Neglecting all terms smaller than
M^{1-\gamma}
, one finally obtains the cavity equations for the diagonal elements of the resolvent matrix:

G_{11}^{-1}(\lambda_{\epsilon})=\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{1}+\nu M^{1-\gamma}\left[c-1-\frac{c}{N}\sum_{i}(\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{i})G_{ii}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\right]\,.

(42)

This equation is asymptotically exact for the WRP ensemble in the large-
N
limit.
In the following, we will use it to extract information on the density of states.

4.1
Density of states, local density of states, and phase diagram

First, the knowledge of the diagonal elements of the resolvent matrix immediately yields the spectral density (
8
),

\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\imaginary\left[\Tr\textbf{G}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\right]\,.

(43)

For
\gamma<1
the eigenvalues of
H
scale as
M^{1-\gamma}\gg 1
, and the support of the DoS grows with
N
. Indeed, upon neglecting the subleading
a_{i}
terms, defining
\lambda=M^{1-\gamma}\tilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon}
with
{\rm Re}(\tilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon})\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, and introducing
g(\lambda_{\epsilon})=1/N\sum_{i}G_{ii}(\lambda_{\epsilon})
, in the large-
N
limit one obtains
[
2
]

\frac{1}{g(\tilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon})}=\tilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon}+\nu(c-1)-\nu c\tilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon}g(\tilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon})\,,

(44)

from which the Marčencko–Pastur distribution is immediately recovered from
Eq.

43
.

In the regime
\gamma>1
, we have that
M^{1-\gamma}\ll 1
. In this case, it is thus convenient to introduce the small parameter

\eta\equiv\nu M^{1-\gamma}\ll 1,

(45)

and the (rescaled) self-energies as

G_{ii}(\lambda_{\epsilon})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{i}+\eta\,\Sigma_{i}(\lambda_{\epsilon})}\,,

(46)

where the
\Sigma_{i}
’s are of
\mathcal{O}(1)
(and should not be confused with a summation symbol over
i
).
The imaginary part of
G_{ii}
gives the local density of states (LDoS), representing the contribution of site
i
to the total density of states:

\rho_{i}(\lambda)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}|\psi_{\alpha}(i)|^{2}\delta(\lambda-\lambda_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\imaginary G_{ii}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\,,

(47)

so that
\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}(\lambda)
(compare with
Eq.

8
).
Physically,
\rho_{i}(\lambda)
corresponds to the inverse lifetime of a particle created at site
i
with energy
\lambda
, and provides the order parameter distribution function for Anderson localization.

As shown self-consistently below, the self-energy
\Sigma_{i}
turns out to be independent of the index
i
at leading order for
\gamma>1
. Plugging the expression (
46
) into the cavity equation (
42
) and expanding the terms proportional to
\eta
, one deduces the following self-consistent equation for the self-energy:

\Sigma(\lambda_{\epsilon})=\frac{\eta\,c\,\Sigma(\lambda_{\epsilon})}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{i}}-1+\mathcal{O}(M^{2(1-\gamma)})\,,

(48)

which immediately yields

\Sigma(\lambda_{\epsilon})\simeq-\left(1-\dfrac{\eta\,c}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{i}}\right)^{-1}\,.

(49)

In the large-
N
limit the average of the self-energy over the probability distribution of the diagonal elements can be conveniently rewritten in a more compact form using the definition of the Stieltjes transform of
p_{a}

\mathcal{G}_{a}(\lambda_{\epsilon})=\int\mathrm{d}a\;\frac{p_{a}(a)}{\lambda_{\epsilon}-a}\,.

(50)

From Eq. (
49
) and the definition above, for large
N
one finds

\Sigma(\lambda_{\epsilon})\approx-\frac{1}{1-\eta\,c\,{\cal G}_{a}(\lambda_{\epsilon})}\,,

(51)

and from Eq. (
46
) one obtains

g(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{ii}(\lambda)={\cal G}_{a}(\lambda+\eta\Sigma)={\cal G}_{a}\left(\lambda-\frac{\eta}{1-\eta\,c\,{\cal G}_{a}(\lambda)}\right)\,.

(52)

Finally, the average DoS in the large-
N
limit can be written as

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm Im}\,{\cal G}_{a}\left(\lambda-\frac{\eta}{1-\eta\,c\,{\cal G}_{a}(\lambda)}\right)\,.

(53)

Hence, for
\gamma>1
, the average DoS of the WRP ensemble coincides with the probability distribution of the diagonal entries,
\rho(\lambda)=p_{a}(\lambda)
, up to subleading corrections of
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
.
In fact, Eq. (
53
) follows directly from the Zee formula
[
83
]
,
which expresses the resolvent (i.e. the Stieltjes transform) of the sum of two independent random matrices in terms of their individual resolvents. The same result will be re-derived in Sec.
5.1
using the replica method. We will also return to the finite-
N
corrections to the average DoS of the WRP ensemble in the different regimes.

This result shows that the cavity approach captures the transition of the average DoS taking place at
\gamma=1
: for
\gamma<1
,
H
is dominated by
B
, and the average DoS follows, to leading order, the spectral density of
B
(i.e. the Marčenko–Pastur law), up to subleading corrections. Conversely, for
\gamma>1
,
A
dominates, and the leading contribution to the average DoS is given by the distribution of the diagonal entries, again up to subleading corrections.

We now show that the cavity approach, and in particular Eqs. (
46
) and (
49
), also allow one to obtain the second phase transition, occurring at
\gamma=3/2
, between the intermediate fractal phase and the Anderson-localized one. To this end, we return to Eq. (
49
) and introduce the real and imaginary parts of the self-energy as
\Sigma=\sigma-\mathrm{i}\,\hat{\sigma}
(recall that
\lambda_{\epsilon}=\lambda-\mathrm{i}\epsilon
). For
\gamma>1
, expanding to leading order in
\eta\ll 1
, one obtains

\displaystyle\sigma(\lambda)

\displaystyle\simeq-1-\frac{\eta\,c}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{1}{\lambda-a_{i}}+o(\eta)\,,

(54)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\eta\,c}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{\epsilon}{(\lambda-a_{i})^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}+o(\eta)\,.

Note that we have neglected the imaginary regulator
\epsilon
with respect to the imaginary part of the self-energy, since the natural scale of the former is of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
, while the latter is of order
\eta\propto N^{1-\gamma}\gg N^{-1}
.
As discussed in Sec.
3
, localization occurs when the corrections to the Green’s function due to the imaginary part of the self-energy (i.e. the Thouless energy) are smaller than the mean level spacing. From Eq. (
54
) one finds

\hat{\sigma}(\lambda)\simeq\eta\,c\,\pi\,p(\lambda)\,.

(55)

From Eq. (
46
), one immediately obtains the corrections to the imaginary part of the Green’s function due to the perturbation, yielding the Thouless energy
E_{T}=\eta\hat{\sigma}(\lambda)=\nu^{2}c\pi p_{a}(\lambda)M^{2-2\gamma}
. Anderson localization is then realized when this Thouless energy is smaller than the average gap
1/[Np_{a}(\lambda)]
, i.e. when

E_{T}=\nu^{2}c\pi M^{2-2\gamma}p_{a}(\lambda)\ll\frac{1}{Np_{a}(\lambda)}\,,

(56)

which reproduces the condition
\gamma>3/2
obtained from the Fermi golden rule, Eq. (
22
), and Mott’s criterion, Eq. (
20
). The transition to the fully delocalized regime occurs when the Thouless energy exceeds the total bandwidth, i.e. when
\gamma<1
.

In conclusion, the cavity method allows one to re-derive the phase diagram discussed in Sec.
3
and schematically presented in Fig.
1
, in a more rigorous and controlled way.

4.2
Density-density correlations and level compressibility in the intermediate regime

We now apply the cavity approach to compute the two-point density-density correlation function in the intermediate fractal regime,
1<\gamma<3/2
, which constitutes one of the main focuses of this work. The two-point function is defined as
\langle\rho(\omega_{1})\rho(\omega_{2})\rangle_{c}
. From Eqs. (
46
), (
47
) and (
54
) we first obtain
[
68
]

\displaystyle\langle\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\omega_{1})\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\omega_{2})\rangle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left\langle\rho_{i}(\omega_{1})\rho_{j}(\omega_{2})\right\rangle\,,

(57)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(\omega)=\frac{\eta\hat{\sigma}(\omega)}{(\omega-a_{i}+\eta\sigma(\omega))^{2}+(\eta\hat{\sigma}(\omega))^{2}}\,,

where
\sigma(\omega)
and
\hat{\sigma}(\omega)
are given in Eq. (
54
). It is convenient to introduce the discrete version of the Stieltjes transform (
50
) of
p_{a}
at finite
N
as

\displaystyle S(\lambda_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\epsilon}-a_{i}}=s(\lambda_{\epsilon})+{\rm i}\hat{s}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\,,

(58)

\displaystyle\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=-1-\eta cs(\omega)\,,\;\quad\hat{\sigma}(\omega)=\eta c\hat{s}(\omega)\,.

In the intermediate phase, for energy separations of the order of the mean level spacing,
\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}\sim\Delta\propto N^{-1}
, the level statistics is governed by the universal GOE ensemble. (This regime actually lies outside the range of validity of the approximations underlying the cavity approach.) For energy separations of order
\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, i.e. much larger than the width of the mini-bands in the local spectrum, the levels are instead expected to be uncorrelated.

The physically relevant regime in the fractal phase is the crossover between RMT behavior at small energy separation and Poisson statistics at large separations, which is expected to occur on the scale of the Thouless energy. For this reason, we consider energies
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}

separated by intervals of the order of the Thouless energy. For convenience (although this is not strictly necessary), we shift the interval by
\eta
, which slightly simplifies the formulas below. We thus set

\omega_{1}=\eta-x\,\eta^{2}\,,\qquad\omega_{2}=\eta+x\,\eta^{2}\,,\qquad x=\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

(59)

On these energy scales, one can approximate

s(\omega_{j})
and
\hat{s}(\omega_{j})
(with
j=1
or 2) as independent of
x
at the leading order,
namely

s(\omega_{j})\simeq s(\eta+o(\eta))\approx s(\eta)\,,\qquad\hat{s}(\omega_{j})\simeq\hat{s}(\eta+o(\eta))\approx\hat{s}(\eta)\approx\pi\big[p_{a}(0)+\eta p^{\prime}(0)\big]\,.

(60)

Within this approximation, the
\rho_{i}
’s become random variables that depend only on the set of all
\{a_{i}\}
’s, and are therefore uncorrelated. One thus obtains

\langle\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\omega_{1})\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\omega_{2})\rangle_{c}\simeq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\rho_{i}(\omega_{1})\rho_{i}(\omega_{2})\right\rangle_{c}\,=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}N}\left\langle\rho_{i}(\omega_{1})\rho_{i}(\omega_{2})\right\rangle_{c},

(61)

where the local density of states
\rho_{i}(\omega)
is given by the second line of Eq. (
57
),
and the remaining average on the r.h.s. of
Eq.

61
is intended over
p_{a}(a)
.
Note that
\langle\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\omega_{1})\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\omega_{2})\rangle_{c}
actually becomes
i
-independent after averaging over
p_{a}(a)
.
This approximation is only valid on the scale of the Thouless energy, and breaks down at the scale of the average spectral gap
1/N
.

The expression above of the density-density correlator allows one to compute the level compressibility
\chi(E)
, using the following relation between the two-point density-density correlation function and the second cumulant of the number of eigenvalues in a given energy window
(see e.g. App. F in Ref.
[
61
]
):

\kappa_{2}(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})=N\int_{\omega_{1}}^{\omega_{2}}{\rm d}\tilde{\omega}_{1}\int_{\omega_{1}}^{\omega_{2}}{\rm d}\tilde{\omega}_{2}\;\langle\rho(\tilde{\omega}_{1})\rho(\tilde{\omega}_{2})\rangle_{c}\,.

(62)

Introducing the energy shift
x
as in
Eq.

59
, using Eqs. (
57
) and (
61
), and performing the integral over
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{2}
(with the change of variable
\tilde{\omega}_{1}=\eta+x\eta^{2}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{2}=\eta-x\eta^{2}
) before the one over the
a_{i}
’s, one finally obtains

\displaystyle\pi^{2}\kappa_{2}(x)=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}a\,p_{a}(a)\left[\arctan\left(\frac{\eta^{2}x+a+\eta^{2}s(\eta)}{\eta^{2}c\hat{s}(\eta)}\right)-\arctan\left(\frac{a-\eta^{2}x+\eta^{2}s(\eta)}{\eta^{2}c\hat{s}(\eta)}\right)\right]^{2}

(63)

\displaystyle-\left\{\int{\rm d}a\,p_{a}(a)\left[\arctan\left(\frac{\eta^{2}x+a+\eta^{2}s(\eta)}{\eta^{2}c\hat{s}(\eta)}\right)-\arctan\left(\frac{a-\eta^{2}x+\eta^{2}s(\eta)}{\eta^{2}c\hat{s}(\eta)}\right)\right]\right\}^{2}\,.

Since the difference
of the two atan’s is non-zero only in a small interval centered around zero and of width
\eta^{2}
, one can perform the change of variable
a=\eta^{2}\tilde{a}

and replace
p_{a}(\eta^{2}\tilde{a})
by
p_{a}(0)
in the integrals above.
The disconnected part of
\kappa_{2}
, corresponding to the integral in the second line, gives
\kappa_{1}^{2}(x)=[2\eta^{2}p_{a}(0)(x+s(\eta))]^{2}
.
The term in the second line is thus of
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{4})
, and can be neglected with respect to the term in the first line, which is of
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
.
For a symmetric distribution
p_{a}
, the real part of the Stieltjes transform (
50
) computed at energy
\eta
is of
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
, and can be also neglected at the leading order. Furthermore, it is convenient to change again variable as
\tilde{a}\to c\hat{s}\tilde{a}
. Since
\hat{s}\approx\pi p_{a}(0)
, we have:

\kappa_{2}(x)\approx\frac{\eta^{2}p_{a}^{2}(0)c}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}\tilde{a}\,\left[\arctan\left(\frac{x}{c\pi p_{a}(0)}+\tilde{a}\right)-\arctan\left(\tilde{a}-\frac{x}{c\pi p_{a}(0)}\right)\right]^{2}\\
\,.

(64)

Plugging this result into Eq. (
10
) and dividing by the first cumulant
\kappa_{1}(x)\simeq 2\eta^{2}p_{a}(0)x
, one finally obtains the spectral compressibility on the scale of the Thouless energy:

\chi(x)\approx\frac{c\,p_{a}(0)}{2\pi x}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}\tilde{a}\,\left[\arctan\left(\frac{x}{c\pi p_{a}(0)}+\tilde{a}\right)-\arctan\left(\tilde{a}-\frac{x}{c\pi p_{a}(0)}\right)\right]^{2}\\
\,.

(65)

Using the property (see e.g. 1.625 in
[
88
]
)

\arctan(a)-\arctan(b)=\arctan(\frac{a-b}{1+ab})\,,

(66)

upon calling
y\equiv\frac{x}{c\pi p_{a}(0)}
in Eq. (
65
) and changing variables in the integral as
u=\tilde{a}\sqrt{1+y^{2}}
, we obtain

\chi\left(y=\frac{\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}}{2\pi cp_{a}(0)\eta}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{1+y^{2}}}{\pi^{2}y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{u}\left\{\arctan\left[\frac{2y}{u^{2}(1+y^{2})+1-y^{2}}\right]\right\}^{2}\,,

(67)

where we stress that we have chosen the branch
\arctan(z)\in[0,\pi]
. Upon integrating by parts and performing some algebra
[
89
,
88
]
, the integral over
u
can be computed explicitly to give Eq. (
13
), which is exactly the same universal scaling function that some of us found for the GRP ensemble (see Eqs. (126) and (127) of Ref.
[
61
]
).
Alternatively (and equivalently), in
A
we provide a derivation of the scaling function (
13
) starting from
Eq.

65
.
In particular, the asymptotics of the function
\chi(y)
can be checked to give

\chi(y)\simeq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{y}{\pi}\,,&\qquad y\ll 1\,,\\[5.0pt]
1-\dfrac{2(1+\ln y)}{\pi y}\,,&\qquad y\gg 1\,,\end{array}\right.

(68)

showing that
\chi(y)
interpolates between Wigner–Dyson statistics at low energy, and Poisson statistics at higher energy.
This function is plotted as a dot-dashed line in
Figs.

3
and
4
.

5
The replica method

In this Section we derive the average density of states reported in Eq. (
53
) using the replica method
[
90
]
.
We also apply the replica strategy to calculate the level compressibility
given in Eq. (
13
). In passing, we derive in Sec.
5.2
the full counting statistics of the WRP model.

5.1
Density of states

We report here the main steps of the derivation, while we defer its details to
B
.
Using the Edwards–Jones formula
[
91
]
, we
first
express the average density of states as

\rho(\lambda)=-\frac{2}{N\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{Im}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\langle\ln\;\mathcal{Z}(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon)\rangle,

(69)

where we
introduced
the partition function

\mathcal{Z}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\differential[N]{r}\;\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}{\bm{r}}^{\;T}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-\textbf{H})\bm{r}}.

(70)

The average of the logarithm
in Eq. (
69
)
can be expressed using the replica trick as

\langle\ln\mathcal{Z}(\lambda)\rangle=\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{n}\ln\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda)\rangle.

(71)

Using standard techniques, one can then recast the average as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda)\rangle=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\phi\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\mathcal{D}\psi\mathcal{D}\hat{\psi}\text{ }\exp\left\{-\sqrt{NM}\mathcal{S}_{n}[\phi,\hat{\phi},\psi,\hat{\psi};\lambda]\right\}\,,

(72)

with the action
\mathcal{S}_{n}[\phi,\hat{\phi},\psi,\hat{\psi};\lambda]
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}[\phi,\hat{\phi},\psi,\hat{\psi};\lambda]=\frac{{\rm i}}{\sqrt{c}}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\phi(\vec{u})+{\rm i}\sqrt{c}\,\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\psi(\vec{r})

\displaystyle\quad-\sqrt{c}\;\ln\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\varphi_{a}(-\vec{r}^{\,2}/2)\;\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda\vec{r}^{\,2}+i\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\;\ln\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\right]+\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sqrt{c}\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\text{ }\phi(\vec{u})\psi(\vec{r})\left(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2},

(73)

where
\eta=\nu M^{(1-\gamma)}
and
\varphi_{a}
is the characteristic function of
p_{a}
, i.e.

\varphi_{a}(y)=\int\;\mathrm{d}a\;p_{a}(a)\;\mathrm{e}^{-{\rm i}ay}\,.

(74)

(Here and in
B
, we denote by
\bm{r}
a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and by
\vec{r}
a vector in the replica space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.)
Next, by using saddle-point evaluation, we get that
\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda)\rangle\approx\exp\left(-\sqrt{NM}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\rm sp}\right)
, where
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\rm sp}
denotes the action evaluated at the saddle point. Combining this with Eqs. (
69
) and (
71
), we finally obtain the density of states as

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\imaginary G(\lambda_{\epsilon})+\mathcal{O}(1/N)\,,

(75)

where
G(\lambda)\propto\lim_{n\to 0}\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\rm sp}

(which actually coincides with the resolvent associated to the WRP model, compare with
Eq.

43
)
satisfies the
self-consistent equation

\displaystyle G(\lambda)={\rm i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\;\;\varphi_{a}(-z)\exp\left[-{\rm i}z\left(\lambda-\frac{\eta}{1-c\,\eta\,G(\lambda)}\right)\right]\,.

(76)

Equivalently, upon rewriting the characteristic function as
in
Eq.

74
,
switching the order of the two integrals, computing the integral over
z
first and then the one over
a
, we obtain

G(\lambda)=\mathcal{G}_{a}\left(\lambda-\frac{\eta}{1-c\,\eta\,G(\lambda)}\right),

(77)

where
\mathcal{G}_{a}
is the Stieltjes transform of
p_{a}
, defined as
in
Eq.

50
.
This is consistent with the result found with the cavity method in Eq. (
53
), since
G(\lambda)=\mathcal{G}_{a}(\lambda)+\mathcal{O}(\eta)
. We
also note that this result is equivalent to the Zee formula
[
83
]
, which allows one to compute the resolvent
G_{1+2}
of the sum of two mutually free random matrices, given their respective resolvents
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Indeed, the Zee formula can be written as a self-consistent equation (see e.g. Appendix C in
[
61
]
)

G_{1+2}(z)=G_{1}\bigl(z-R_{2}(G_{1+2}(z))\bigr),

(78)

in terms of the R-transform of the second matrix, i.e.
R_{2}
. In our case, the R-transform of Wishart matrices is

R_{W}(z)=1/(1-cz)

[
2
]
. Now, using the fact that rescaling a random matrix by a factor
\eta
scales its R-transform as
R_{\eta W}(z)=\eta R_{W}(\eta z)
, and plugging this into Eq. (
78
), we obtain again Eq. (
77
).

(b)

(centered at

and of width

), the Stieltjes transform is known in closed form:
\mathcal{G}_{Cauchy}(\lambda)=1/(\lambda-\mu\pm{\rm i}\omega)
, where the

branches correspond to

or

5.2
Full counting statistics

Here, we apply the replica method to derive the level compressibility given in Eq. (
13
).
To this end, we follow the procedure introduced in Refs.
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
, and more recently applied in Ref.
[
61
]
to the case of the GRP model. Accordingly, we first obtain the cumulant generating function of the number of eigenvalues in a given interval, see Eq. (
9
), in the large-
N
limit — this quantity is also known as full counting statistics. From this expression, the level compressibility can then be retrieved using Eq. (
10
). Here, we will mainly focus on the intermediate phase (
1<\gamma<3/2
), and on intervals of the scale of the Thouless energy.

The number of eigenvalues in an interval delimited by
\alpha
and
\beta
is
given by

I_{N}[\alpha,\beta]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\Theta(\beta-x_{i})-\Theta(\alpha-x_{i})\right]\,,

(79)

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside distribution.
Using the standard identity

\Theta(-x)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[\ln(x+{\rm i}\varepsilon)-\ln(x-{\rm i}\varepsilon)\right]

(80)

from complex analysis,
one can show that

I_{N}[\alpha,\beta]=-\frac{1}{\pi{\rm i}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\ln\left[\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\beta-{\rm i}\varepsilon)\mathcal{Z}(\alpha+{\rm i}\varepsilon)}{\mathcal{Z}(\beta+{\rm i}\varepsilon)\mathcal{Z}(\alpha-{\rm i}\varepsilon)}\right],

(81)

where
\mathcal{Z}
is the partition function appearing in the calculation
of the spectral density in Eq. (
70
)
(note that,
for
\mathcal{Z}(\alpha+{\rm i}\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{Z}(\beta+{\rm i}\varepsilon)
, one actually has to
compute
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathrm{d}^{N}r\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\bm{r}^{T}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-\textbf{H})\bm{r}}
instead of
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathrm{d}^{N}r\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\bm{r}^{T}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-\textbf{H})\bm{r}}
to ensure convergence
of the integral). Now, assuming that one
can exchange the limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
and the logarithm, one writes the cumulant generating
function of the random variable
I_{N}[\alpha,\beta]
as

\mathcal{F}_{[\alpha,\beta]}(s)\equiv\ln\langle\mathrm{e}^{-sI_{N}[\alpha,\beta]}\rangle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\;\ln\;\langle\left[\mathcal{Z}(\beta_{\varepsilon}^{*})\mathcal{Z}(\alpha_{\varepsilon})\right]^{{\rm i}s/\pi}\left[\mathcal{Z}(\beta_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{Z}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{*})\right]^{-{\rm i}s/\pi}\rangle\,.

(82)

Once again,
in the spirit of the replica method, we first calculate

Q_{[\alpha,\beta]}(n_{\pm})=\langle\left[\mathcal{Z}(\beta_{\varepsilon}^{*})\mathcal{Z}(\alpha_{\varepsilon})\right]^{n_{+}}\left[\mathcal{Z}(\beta_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{Z}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{*})\right]^{n_{-}}\rangle\,,

(83)

where
n_{\pm}
are two independent integers. Next, we perform
the analytic continuation of
n_{\pm}
to the imaginary axis to
express
\mathcal{F}_{[\alpha,\beta]}(s)
in the form

\mathcal{F}_{[\alpha,\beta]}(s)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\;\ln\;\lim_{n_{\pm}\to\pm{\rm i}s/\pi}\;Q_{[\alpha,\beta]}(n_{\pm})\,.

(84)

Following steps similar to those used in the calculation of the density of states, we obtain (see
B
)

\displaystyle Q_{[\alpha,\beta]}(n_{\pm})\propto\int\mathcal{D}\phi\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\mathcal{D}\psi\mathcal{D}\hat{\psi}\text{ }\exp\left\{-\sqrt{NM}\mathcal{S}_{n_{\pm}}[\phi,\hat{\phi},\psi,\hat{\psi};\hat{\Lambda}]\right\},

(85)

with the action given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n_{\pm}}[\phi,\hat{\phi},\psi,\hat{\psi};\hat{\Lambda}]=\frac{{\rm i}}{\sqrt{c}}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\phi(\vec{u})+{\rm i}\sqrt{c}\;\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\psi(\vec{r})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sqrt{c}\;\ln\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)\;\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\vec{r}\hat{\Lambda}\vec{r}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\;\ln\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\right]+\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sqrt{c}\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\text{ }\phi(\vec{u})\psi(\vec{r})\left(\vec{u}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)^{2},

(86)

and where we introduced the block matrices

\hat{\Lambda}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dmat{
\alpha_\epsilon\mathbb{1}_{n_+} \!\!\!\!,
-\beta_\epsilon^* \mathbb{1}_{n_+} \!\!\!\!,
\beta_\epsilon\mathbb{1}_{n_-} \!\!\!\!, -\alpha_\epsilon^* \mathbb{1}_{n_-} }\endlx@physics@matrix),\qquad\hat{L}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dmat{
\mathbb{1}_{n_+}\!\!\!\!, - \mathbb{1}_{n_+} \!\!\!\!, \mathbb{1}_{n_-} \!\!\!\!, - \mathbb{1}_{n_-} }\endlx@physics@matrix).

(87)

Next, by using the saddle-point approximation, one can obtain a re-parametrisation of the action in terms of two matrices
\hat{K}
and
\hat{C}^{-1}
(see
B
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n_{\pm}}[\hat{K},\hat{C}^{-1};\hat{\Lambda}]=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{c}\ln\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a\;p_{a}(a)\;\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\left(\hat{C}^{-1}-{\rm i}a\hat{L}\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c}}\Tr\log\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+{\rm i}q\eta\hat{K}\right)-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sqrt{c}\eta\;\Tr\left[\hat{K}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+{\rm i}q\eta\hat{K}\right)^{-1}\right],

(88)

where

\hat{C}^{-1}
and
\hat{K}

have to satisfy the self-consistent relations

\hat{C}^{-1}={\rm i}\hat{\Lambda}+{\rm i}\eta\hat{L}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+{\rm i}q\eta\hat{K}\right)^{-1}\hat{L},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{K}=-{\rm i}\hat{L}\mathcal{G}_{a}\left(\left({\rm i}\hat{L}\hat{C}\right)^{-1}\right),

(89)

where

\mathcal{G}_{a}
was given in
Eq.

50
.
We now look for a block diagonal solution of Eq. (
89
), i.e. of the form

\hat{K}\equiv\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dmat{k_{\alpha} \mathbb{1}_{n_+} \!\!\!\!,
\bar{k}_{\beta} \mathbb{1}_{n_+} \!\!\!\!,
k_{\beta} \mathbb{1}_{n_-} \!\!\!\!, \bar{k}_{\alpha} \mathbb{1}_{n_-}}\endlx@physics@matrix),\;\hat{C}^{-1}\equiv\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dmat{\Delta^{-1}_{\alpha}\mathbb{1}_{n_+}\!\!\!\!, \bar{\Delta}^{-1}_{\beta} \mathbb{1}_{n_+} \!\!\!\!, \Delta^{-1}_{\beta} \mathbb{1}_{n_-} \!\!\!\!, \bar{\Delta}^{-1}_{\alpha} \mathbb{1}_{n_-} }\endlx@physics@matrix).

(90)

This Ansatz can then be inserted in Eq. (
5.2
) to derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n_{\pm}}=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{c}\ln\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a\;p_{a}(a)\;\mathrm{\mathrm{}}e^{-\frac{n_{+}}{2}\ln\left[\left(\Delta^{-1}_{\alpha}-{\rm i}a\right)\left(\bar{\Delta}^{-1}_{\beta}+{\rm i}a\right)\right]-\frac{n_{-}}{2}\ln\left[\left(\Delta^{-1}_{\beta}-{\rm i}a\right)\left(\bar{\Delta}^{-1}_{\alpha}+{\rm i}a\right)\right]}\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c}}\left\{n_{+}\ln\left[(1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\alpha})(1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\beta})\right]+n_{-}\ln\left[(1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\beta})(1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\alpha})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sqrt{c}\eta\left[n_{+}\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}}{1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\alpha}}+\frac{\bar{k}_{\beta}}{1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\beta}}\right)+n_{-}\left(\frac{k_{\beta}}{1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\beta}}+\frac{\bar{k}_{\alpha}}{1+i\eta c\bar{k}_{\alpha}}\right)\right].

(91)

Now, taking the limit
n_{\pm}\to\pm{\rm i}s/\pi
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\pm\frac{{\rm i}s}{\pi}}=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{c}\,\ln\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a\,p_{a}(a)\,\exp\left(-\frac{{\rm i}s}{2\pi}\ln\left[\frac{\Bigr(\Delta^{-1}_{\alpha}-{\rm i}a\Bigl)\left(\bar{\Delta}^{-1}_{\beta}+{\rm i}a\right)}{\left(\Delta^{-1}_{\beta}-{\rm i}a\right)\left(\bar{\Delta}^{-1}_{\alpha}+{\rm i}a\right)}\right]\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{{\rm i}s}{2\pi\sqrt{c}}\ln\left[\frac{(1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\alpha})(1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\beta})}{(1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\beta})(1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\alpha})}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta s}{2\pi\sqrt{c}}\left[\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}}{1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\alpha}}+\frac{\bar{k}_{\beta}}{1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\beta}}\right)-\left(\frac{k_{\beta}}{1+{\rm i}\eta ck_{\beta}}+\frac{\bar{k}_{\alpha}}{1+{\rm i}\eta c\bar{k}_{\alpha}}\right)\right].

(92)

This finally allows us to obtain the cumulant generating function

\mathcal{F}_{[\alpha,\beta]}(s)
, which follows from Eqs. (
84
) and (
85
) as

\mathcal{F}_{[\alpha,\beta]}(s)=\sqrt{NM}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\;\mathcal{S}_{\pm{\rm i}s/\pi}\;+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\gamma}).

(93)

This concludes the replica calculation. To get the moments of the number of eigenvalues in an interval
I_{N}[\alpha,\beta]
, one then proceeds as follows:

(i)

The self-consistent equations (
89
) can be solved numerically to find the 8 elements of the block matrices
\hat{K}
and
\hat{C}^{-1}
in Eq. (
90
). Note that these are actually 8 nonlinear equations, which can be solved with minimal numerical effort for any reasonable choice of
p_{a}

(ii)

6
Dyson Brownian motion

In this Section we
derive the phase diagram of the
WRP
model
using
yet another
approach, similar to
the one
first introduced in Ref.
[
96
,
97
]
, and later
applied to
the GRP model in Ref.
[
33
]
.
The idea is to
interpret
Eq.

2
as a matrix-valued stochastic process, for which
A
represents the initial condition, and
B
a perturbation
which is turned on at
the fictitious “time”
t=0
;
the matrix
H
is eventually recovered at the final time
t=T
.
Under the effect of this perturbation, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors themselves become stochastic processes, and
inspecting their behavior
can give us information on the
limiting eigenvalue density, and the statistics of the phase (localized, delocalized or fractal).

We start by
setting up
the problem. We study the matrix
\textbf{H}(t)=\textbf{A}+\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}
, where
\textbf{X}(t)
is a
N\times M
matrix-valued Brownian motion, and
A
is the same diagonal random matrix as in Eq. (
2
).
At each time step, we can write the evolution of
\textbf{X}(t)
as
\textbf{X}(t+\mathrm{d}t)=\textbf{X}(t)+\textbf{g}(t)
, with
\textbf{g}(t)
being an i.i.d. uncorrelated
N\times M
Gaussian noise with zero mean and variance proportional to
\mathrm{d}t
, i.e.
\langle g_{ik}(t)\rangle=0
and
\langle g_{ik}(t)g_{jl}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
The stochastic evolution equation of
\textbf{H}(t)
then follows as

\displaystyle\textbf{H}(t+\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=\textbf{A}+\textbf{X}(t+\mathrm{d}t)\textbf{X}(t+\mathrm{d}t)^{T}

(97)

\displaystyle=\textbf{A}+\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}+\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}+\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}+\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}=\textbf{H}(t)+\delta\textbf{H}(t),

where in the last line we defined
\delta\textbf{H}(t)=\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}+\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}+\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}
. Requiring that, at time
T
,
\textbf{H}(t=T)=\textbf{A}+\nu M^{-\gamma}\textbf{W}\textbf{W}^{T}
, and by using that
\sum_{l=1}^{M}X_{il}X_{jl}\sim M\,t
, we get that the stopping time of this process
must be given by
T=\nu M^{-\gamma}
. Now, applying perturbation theory up to second order, one can derive the following stochastic differential equations for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(t)
and the components of the associated eigenvectors
\braket{n|\psi_{i}(t)}\equiv\psi_{i}(n;t)
of
\textbf{H}(t)
, starting from the initial condition
\lambda_{i}(t=0)=a_{i}
and
\psi_{i}(n;\,t=0)=\delta_{in}
(we omitted the time dependencies for clarity, and the details of the derivation are given in
C
):

\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sigma^{2}\left[M+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}-\langle a\rangle_{i}-\langle a\rangle_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\right]+2\sigma\sqrt{\lambda_{i}-\langle a\rangle_{i}}\;\zeta_{i},

(98)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{i}(n)}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\;\sigma^{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\langle a\rangle_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}-\sum_{l\neq i}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{l}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\psi_{i}(n)\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}-\langle a\rangle_{i}-\langle a\rangle_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}+\sigma\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\zeta_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}.

(99)

Here,
\zeta_{i}
is
a delta-correlated Gaussian white noise,
while
\zeta_{ij}

is a correlated Gaussian noise with zero mean and variance

\displaystyle\langle\zeta_{ij}(t)\zeta_{kl}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\Bigl[(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad-\bigl(\delta_{ik}\langle a\rangle_{jl}+\delta_{il}\langle a\rangle_{jk}+\delta_{jk}\langle a\rangle_{il}+\delta_{jl}\langle a\rangle_{ik}\bigr)\Bigr].

(100)

We also defined

\displaystyle\langle a\rangle_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\textbf{A}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}=\sum_{n}a_{n}|\psi_{i}(n;t)|^{2}\,,

(101)

\displaystyle\langle a\rangle_{ik}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\textbf{A}\ket{\psi_{k}(t)}=\sum_{n}a_{n}\psi_{i}(n;t)\psi_{k}(n;t)\,,

(102)

which implies that, in this model, eigenvalues are coupled to eigenvectors (this was not the case in the GRP model — see Eqs. (12) and (13) in Ref.
[
33
]
).
Moreover, one can note that
setting
A
to
0

correctly renders
the Dyson Brownian motion equation for the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a pure Wishart process
[
98
]
.
Although in general
these equations are complicated to solve,
here we will merely be concerned with
their short-time
behavior,
because the stopping time
T\sim N^{-\gamma}
is vanishingly small for all values of
\gamma

of interest.

We begin by considering the evolution of the eigenvalues in
Eq.

98
.
At short times, only the first term in Eq. (
98
)
gives a relevant contribution,
i.e.
\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}\simeq\sigma^{2}M
,
because
\lambda_{i}(t=0)=a_{i}=\expectationvalue{a}_{i}(t=0)
.
Thus, after a time
T\sim N^{-\gamma}\ll 1
, the eigenvalues have moved by

\delta\lambda_{i}(T)=\lambda_{i}(t)-a_{i}\sim\sigma^{2}MT\sim N^{1-\gamma}.

(103)

This means that for
\gamma>1
the shift of the eigenvalues is vanishingly small, and thus they
remain
close to
their initial configuration
\lambda_{i}(t=0)=a_{i}
:
the spectral density is then given by
p_{a}
, as expected.
Conversely,
for
\gamma<1
,
a_{i}=\mathcal{O}(1)
are negligible
with respect to
the shift of
\mathcal{O}(N^{1-\gamma})
, and thus the eigenvalues essentially follow the evolution of a pure Wishart process, whose stationary distribution is known to be the Marčenko–Pastur distribution
[
98
]
.

For the eigenvectors, one can
perform
a similar analysis. At short times, for
n\neq i
, the only non-zero term in Eq. (
6
) is the last one:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{i}(n)}{\mathrm{d}t}\underset{t\ll 1}{\approx}\sigma\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{jn}\frac{\zeta_{ij}}{a_{i}-a_{j}}=\sigma\frac{\zeta_{in}}{a_{i}-a_{n}},

(104)

and Eq. (
6
) simplifies to
\langle\zeta_{ij}(t)\zeta_{kl}(t^{\prime})\rangle\underset{t\ll 1}{\approx}\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;\bigl[\lambda_{i}(t)+\lambda_{j}(t)-a_{i}-a_{j}\bigr]\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\bigr)
.
The variance of the components of the eigenvectors at time
T
is then given by

\displaystyle\Big\langle\big|\psi_{i}(n;T)\big|^{2}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma}{a_{i}-a_{n}}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\big\langle\zeta_{in}(t)\,\zeta_{in}(t^{\prime})\big\rangle\,

(105)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(a_{i}-a_{n})^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t\;\big(\lambda_{i}(t)+\lambda_{n}(t)-a_{i}-a_{n}\big)\,.

Now, using
\lambda_{i}(t)-a_{i}\approx\sigma^{2}Mt
, and the fact that
the mean level spacing scales as
a_{i}-a_{n}\sim 1/N
, we obtain that after a time
T\sim N^{-\gamma}

\Bigl\langle\Bigl|\psi_{i}(n;T)\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr\rangle\sim N^{2}\times MT^{2}\sim N^{3}T^{2}\sim N^{3-2\gamma}.

(106)

Therefore, the eigenvectors are delocalized whenever
\gamma<3/2
, and are localized otherwise.

7
Conclusions

In this paper we introduced a new variant of the Rosenzweig–Porter model, which we called the Wishart–Rosenzweig–Porter ensemble. This model is defined as the sum of a diagonal matrix and a Wishart matrix, as in
Eqs.

2
and
3
. We provided a comprehensive analysis of its properties in the limit of large matrix size, combining several complementary analytical approaches. In particular, we
characterized
the phase diagram of the model using perturbation theory (Sec.
3
), the cavity method (Sec.
4
), the replica formalism (Sec.
5
), and a Dyson Brownian motion approach (Sec.
6
).

The WRP ensemble displays two distinct transitions (see
Fig.

1
): a transition in the spectral density at
\gamma=1
, and a localization transition in the eigenvector statistics at
\gamma=3/2
. In the intermediate regime (
1<\gamma<3/2
), the eigenvectors are neither fully localized nor extended, but instead occupy a fractal support of size
N^{D}
, with fractal dimension
D=3-2\gamma<1
, which is thus much smaller than
N
.

We also analyzed the spectral correlations through the full counting statistics and the level compressibility defined in Eq. (
10
). The key result of our work is that, in the intermediate phase (
1<\gamma<3/2
), for energy scales between the mean level spacing and the Thouless energy (
1/N\ll E_{T}\sim N^{2-2\gamma}\ll 1
), the level compressibility follows the same scaling function as in other RP-type models (see Eq. (
13
)). This agreement, observed both analytically and numerically, strongly supports the hypothesis that spectral correlations on this scale are (super)universal. While their behavior at smaller and larger energy scales depends on the specific form of the underlying matrix distributions, the intermediate-scale correlations appear to be independent of any microscopic detail.

This finding motivates further numerical investigations of the crossover function (
10
) in realistic many-body disordered quantum systems exhibiting (multi)fractal phases, to test whether the same universal behavior persists in those contexts as well.
The most natural framework to begin this investigation is provided, in our view, by the quantum random energy model
[
22
,
25
,
23
,
24
,
53
]
.

Finally, several open questions emerge from our study.
First,
Wishart matrices often describe covariance data where the number of samples greatly exceeds the number of variables
[
76
,
75
]
; this motivates extending the WRP ensemble to the regime
M\ll N
, e.g.
M\sim N^{\gamma^{\prime}}
with
\gamma^{\prime}<1
.
Second,
both GOE and Wishart ensembles share the key feature of having a spectral density of the form
\exp[-\mathrm{Tr}\,V(M)]
, leading to Haar-distributed eigenvectors. This common structure may underlie the observed universality and suggests possible extensions to generic matrix models with potentials
V(M)
.
Third, having found that
correlations between the entries of
\mathbf{B}
preserve the conjectured universality of the level compressibility, it would be interesting to explore what happens when correlations are also introduced among the entries of
\mathbf{A}

[
72
,
73
]
. Another direction is to study spectral correlations in models with explicitly multifractal eigenstates, for example by considering sums of several random matrices with distinct fractal dimension spectra.
Finally, it would be highly desirable to develop a more systematic framework to study spectral correlations, possibly using tools from free probability theory, which would constitute a
promising avenue for future research.

Appendix A
Scaling function of the level compressibility

In this Appendix, we provide the details of the computation
that leads to the closed-form scaling function (
13
), assumed by the level compressibility
\chi(E)
in the vicinity of the Thouless energy
E_{T}
.
To this end, we analyze
the integral that enters the definition of
\chi(x)
given in Eq. (
65

F_{\chi}(b)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}\tilde{a}\left[\arctan\left(b+\tilde{a}\right)-\arctan\left(\tilde{a}-b\right)\right]^{2},

(107)

such that
\chi(x)\approx\frac{c\,p_{a}(0)}{2\pi x}F_{\chi}(x/(c\pi p_{a}(0)))
. We first notice that
F_{\chi}(0)=0
and compute its derivative
F_{\chi}^{\prime}(b)
, which reads

F_{\chi}^{\prime}(b)=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}\tilde{a}\left(\frac{1}{1+(\tilde{a}-b)^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+(\tilde{a}+b)^{2}}\right)\left({\rm atan}(\tilde{a}+b)-{\rm atan}(\tilde{a}-b)\right)\;.

(108)

By using the symmetry of the interval of integration together with
{\rm atan}(-x)=-{\rm atan}(x)
, the derivative
F_{\chi}^{\prime}(b)
can be simply re-written as

F_{\chi}^{\prime}(b)=4\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}u\frac{{\rm atan}(u)}{1+(u-2b)^{2}}\;.

(109)

To proceed, it is convenient to use the integral representation

\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}k\,\rme^{{\rm i}kx-|k|}\;.

(110)

We then insert this integral representation into Eq. (
109
)
and use the identity

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}u\,{\rm atan}(u)\,\rme^{{\rm i}ku}=\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{k}\,\rme^{-|k|}

(111)

(which can be shown using integration by parts), to compute the integral over
u
in Eq. (
109
). This leads to

F_{\chi}^{\prime}(b)=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\rm dk}{k}\sin(2 k \, b)\rme^{-2|k|}=4\pi{\rm atan}(b)\;,

(112)

which can be easily
proven by taking a derivative with respect to

112

F_{\chi}(b)=\int_{0}^{b}{\rm d}x\,{\rm atan}(x)=b\,{\rm atan}(b)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+b^2)\;.

(113)

Using
\chi(x)\approx\frac{c\,p_{a}(0)}{2\pi x}F_{\chi}(x/(c\pi p_{a}(0)))

The condition of minimisation of the action,
namely

\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}_{n}}{\delta\psi}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}_{n}}{\delta\phi}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}_{n}}{\delta\hat{\psi}}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}_{n}}{\delta\hat{\phi}}=0,

(120)

yields the following four saddle-point equations:

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\phi(\vec{u})\left(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}\,,

(121)

\displaystyle\psi(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{a}(-\vec{r}^{\,2}/2)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda\vec{r}^{\,2}+{{\rm i}}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right]}{\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}{\,{}^{\prime}}\varphi_{a}(-\vec{r}{\,{}^{\prime}}^{2}/2)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda\vec{r}{\,{}^{\prime}}^{2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r}{\,{}^{\prime}})\right]}\,,

(122)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}c\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\psi(\vec{r})\left(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}\,,

(123)

\displaystyle\phi(\vec{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\right]}{\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}{\,{}^{\prime}}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}{\,{}^{\prime}}^{2}+{\rm i}\hat{\phi}(\vec{u}{\,{}^{\prime}})\right]}\,,

(124)

where we recall the definition of
\eta=\nu M^{1-\gamma}
. Now we note that, according to the Edwards–Jones formula (
69
), the spectral density can be recovered as

\rho(\lambda)=-\frac{2}{N\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{Im}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{n}\ln\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda)\rangle.

(125)

Using the saddle-point construction and
Eq.

72
,
we can express
\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda)\rangle

as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda)\rangle\simeq\exp\left\{-\sqrt{NM}\mathcal{S}_{n}[\phi^{*},\hat{\phi}^{*},\psi^{*},\hat{\psi}^{*};\lambda]\right\},

(126)

where the apex superscript “star” (introduced here for clarity, but omitted hereafter) indicates that these fields are solutions of the saddle-point equations (
121
)–(
124
).
In particular, we note that there is only one term in the action (
5.1
) in which
\lambda
appears explicitly, and it only involves the field
\hat{\psi}
. This implies that, in order to derive the spectral density, we only need to find the solution for
\hat{\psi}
— indeed, contributions coming from the implicit derivatives of the other fields with respect to
\lambda
(calculated according the standard chain rule) vanish by construction at the saddle point due to
Eq.

120
(see Ref.
[
61
]
).
Thus, our
strategy will be to find a self-consistent equation for
\hat{\psi}
,
which can be achieved as follows:

(i)

First, we insert Eq. (
123
) into Eq. (
124
) to eliminate
\hat{\phi}

and get an expression for
\phi
as a functional of
\psi
:

\phi(\vec{u})=\frac{1}{z_{\phi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}-{\rm i}\frac{c\eta}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\psi(\vec{r})\left(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(127)

where
z_{\phi}=\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{2}-{\rm i}\frac{c\eta}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\psi(\vec{r})\left(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}\right]
.

(ii)

Second, we insert Eq. (
122
) into Eq. (
127
), to get an expression for

\phi
as a functional of
\hat{\psi}
:

\phi(\vec{u})=\frac{1}{z_{\phi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}-{\rm i}\frac{c\eta}{2z_{\psi}}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{\vec{r}^{\,2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda\vec{r}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})}\left(\vec{u}^{\prime}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}\right],

(128)

where
z_{\psi}=\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\varphi_{a}(-\vec{r}^{\,2}/2)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda\vec{r}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right]
.

(iii)

Finally, we insert Eq. (
128
) into Eq. (
121
), and deduce the self-consistency equation for

\hat{\psi}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})=-\frac{\eta}{2z_{\psi}}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\vec{u}^{\,2}}{2}-\frac{{\rm i}c\eta}{2z_{\psi}}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{\vec{r}^{\,2}}{2}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}\lambda\vec{r}^{\,2}}{2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right]\left(\vec{u}^{\prime}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}}\left(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r}\right)^{2}\,.

(129)

In the following,
we shall try to find a solution of

Eq.

129

in the form of a rotationally invariant Ansatz, i.e.
\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})=\hat{\psi}(r)
, which only depends on the norm of
\vec{r}

in replica space.
Using the identity
\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}\;(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{r})^{2}=\frac{(ur)^{2}}{n}\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}
,
where
\differential{\Omega_n}
is the differential of the
n
-dimensional solid angle in spherical coordinates,
we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(r)=-r^{2}\frac{\eta\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}}{2nz_{\psi}}\int\mathrm{d}u\;u^{n+1}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}-\frac{{\rm i}c\eta\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}}{2nz_{\phi}}u^{2}\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}r^{\prime\,n+1}\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{r^{\prime\,2}}{2}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}\lambda r^{\prime\,2}}{2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(r^{\prime})\right]}.

(130)

This expression can then be simplified by defining

J(r;\lambda)=\varphi_{a}(-r^{2}/2)\;\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda r^{2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(r)\right]\,,

(131)

with
J^{\prime}(r;\lambda)=\partial_{r}J(r;\lambda)
. Rewriting
z_{\psi}=\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}\int\mathrm{d}r\;r^{n-1}\;J(r;\lambda)
and integrating by parts
z_{\psi}=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}}{n}\int\mathrm{d}r\;r^{n}\;J^{\prime}(r;\lambda)
, we obtain

\hat{\psi}(r)=-r^{2}\frac{\eta\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}}{2nz_{\phi}}\int\mathrm{d}u\;u^{n+1}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+{\rm i}\frac{c\eta}{2}u^{2}\frac{\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\;r^{\prime n+1}J(r^{\prime};\lambda)}{\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}r^{\prime n}J^{\prime}(r^{\prime};\lambda)}\right]\,.

(132)

This expression can be further simplified by defining

F_{n}(u;\lambda)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+{\rm i}\frac{c\eta}{2}u^{2}\frac{\int\mathrm{d}r\;r^{n+1}J(r;\lambda)}{\int\mathrm{d}r\;r^{n}J^{\prime}(r;\lambda)}\right]\,,

(133)

and
F_{n}^{\prime}(u;\lambda)=\partial_{u}F_{n}(u;\lambda)
. Again, rewriting
z_{\phi}=\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}\int\mathrm{d}u\;u^{n-1}\;F_{n}(u;\lambda)
and integrating by parts
z_{\phi}=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{n}}{n}\int\mathrm{d}u\;u^{n}\;F_{n}^{\prime}(u;\lambda)
we have

\hat{\psi}(r)=\frac{\eta}{2}r^{2}\;\frac{\int\mathrm{d}u\;u^{n+1}F_{n}(u;\lambda)}{\int\mathrm{d}u\;u^{n}F_{n}^{\prime}(u;\lambda)}\,.

(134)

We can now take the limit
n\rightarrow 0
, and arrive at

\hat{\psi}(r)=\frac{\eta}{2}\;r^{2}\;\frac{\int\mathrm{d}u\;u\,F_{0}(u;\lambda)}{\int\mathrm{d}u\;F_{0}^{\prime}(u;\lambda)}.

(135)

Let us now go back to the Edwards–Jones formula and the replica trick in

Eqs.

69
and
71
,
which allow us to compute the spectral density as

\displaystyle\rho(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi N}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\imaginary\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{\ln\langle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\lambda_{\epsilon})\rangle}{n}=\frac{2}{\pi N}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\imaginary\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{NM}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\rm sp}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{c}}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\imaginary\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{-{\rm i}\sqrt{c}}{2}\frac{1}{n}\frac{\int\mathrm{d}r\;r^{n+1}J(r;\lambda_{\epsilon})}{\int\mathrm{d}r\;r^{n-1}J(r;\lambda_{\epsilon})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\imaginary\mathrm{i}\frac{\int\differential{r}rJ(r;\lambda_{\epsilon})}{\int\mathrm{d}r\;J^{\prime}(r;\lambda_{\epsilon})}\equiv\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\imaginary G(\lambda_{\epsilon})\,,

(136)

where we have identified the resolvent

G(\lambda)\equiv\;{\rm i}\;\frac{\int\mathrm{d}r\;rJ(r;\lambda)}{\int\mathrm{d}r\;J^{\prime}(r;\lambda)}.

(137)

To
obtain a closed equation for
the resolvent, we first rewrite

Eq.

133
as

F_{0}(u;\lambda)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\frac{c\,\eta}{2}u^{2}G(\lambda)\right]\,.

(138)

From this expression, if we assume that
G(\lambda)
has a negative real part on the support of
\rho
(to be checked a posteriori), then we have that
F_{0}(u\to\infty;\lambda)=0
and
F_{0}(u\to 0;\lambda)=1
. This allows us to simplify the denominator of Eq. (
135
), and then by applying the change of variables
y=u^{2}/2
we find

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}r^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\;u\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\frac{c\,\eta}{2}u^{2}G(\lambda)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}r^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;\exp\left[-y(1-c\eta G(\lambda))\right]=\frac{\eta}{2}r^{2}\frac{1}{1-c\,\eta G(\lambda)}.

(139)

Next, since
G(\lambda)
has a positive imaginary part,
from
Eqs.

139
and
131
we deduce that

J(r\to\infty;\lambda)=0
and
J(r\to 0;\lambda)=1
. These limits let us simplify the denominator of Eq. (
137
),
so that by applying the change of variables
z=r^{2}/2

and inserting
Eq.

131
we get

\displaystyle G(\lambda)

\displaystyle={\rm i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\;r\;\varphi_{a}(-r^{2}/2)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda r^{2}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(r)\right]

\displaystyle={\rm i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\;r\;\varphi_{a}(-r^{2}/2)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\lambda r^{2}+\frac{{\rm i}}{2}r^{2}\frac{\eta}{1-c\,\eta G(\lambda)}\right].

(140)

Finally, upon changing variables as
z=r^{2}/2
we recover Eq. (
76
).

B.2
Full counting statistics and level compressibility

To obtain the action (
86

we follow very similar steps to the ones presented in the previous Section for the density of states:
we start from
Eq.

83
, we perform the Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation,
we average over the Gaussian measure and we insert the fields as in
Eqs.

121
,
122
,
123
and
124
to obtain

Eq.

85
.

Now, to evaluate the action (
86
) at the saddle point, we first write the saddle-point equations for the fields, which read

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\phi(\vec{u})M(\vec{u},\vec{r})\,,

(141)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}c\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\psi(\vec{r})M(\vec{u},\vec{r})\,,

(142)

\displaystyle\psi(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\psi}}\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\vec{r}\hat{\Lambda}\vec{r}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right]\,,

\displaystyle\phi(\vec{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\phi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\right]\,,

(144)

where we introduced

.
Inserting the first two equations back into Eq. (

), the action at the saddle point simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n_{\pm}}[\phi,\psi;\hat{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{c}\ln Z_{\psi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\ln Z_{\phi}-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sqrt{c}\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\text{ }\phi(\vec{u})\psi(\vec{r})M(\vec{u},\vec{r}),

(145)

where

\displaystyle Z_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\vec{r}\hat{\Lambda}\vec{r}+{\rm i}\hat{\psi}(\vec{r})\right],

(146)

\displaystyle Z_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}+{\rm i}\hat{\phi}(\vec{u})\right]\,.

On the other hand, by inserting the first two saddle-point equations into the last two, one can eliminate
the dependence on the conjugated fields
\hat{\psi}
and
\hat{\phi}
, and
obtain
a set of two equations for
\psi
and
\phi
only:

\displaystyle\psi(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\psi}}\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\vec{r}\hat{\Lambda}\vec{r}-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\;\phi(\vec{u})M(\vec{u},\vec{r})\right]\,,

(147)

\displaystyle\phi(\vec{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\phi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}^{\,2}-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}c\eta\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\psi(\vec{r})M(\vec{u},\vec{r})\right]\,.

(148)

To make progress, we now introduce
an
n\times n
matrix
\hat{K}
such that

\vec{u}\hat{K}\vec{u}\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\psi(\vec{r})M(\vec{u},\vec{r}),

(149)

which allows us to rewrite

Eqs.

147
and
148
as

\displaystyle\phi(\vec{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\phi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+iq\eta\hat{K}\right)\vec{u}\right],

(150)

\displaystyle\psi(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\psi}}\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)\exp\left\{-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\vec{r}\left[\hat{\Lambda}+\eta\hat{L}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+iq\eta\hat{K}\right)^{-1}\hat{L}\right]\vec{r}\right\}\,.

(151)

Inserting
Eq.

151
into
Eq.

149
then
gives the following self-consistent equation for

:

\displaystyle\vec{u}\hat{K}\vec{u}=\frac{1}{Z_{\psi}}\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\;\;\varphi_{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}\hat{L}\vec{r}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\vec{r}\left[\hat{\Lambda}+\eta\hat{L}\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+iq\eta\hat{K}\right)^{-1}\hat{L}\right]\vec{r}}\;M(\vec{u},\vec{r}),

which is actually equivalent to Eq. (
89

\hat{C}

Using

150
and
151
,
we can now express

and

in Eq. (

)
as

\displaystyle Z_{\phi}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{n/2}\times\left[\det\left(\hat{\mathbb{1}}+iq\eta\hat{K}\right)\right]^{-1/2},

(153)

\displaystyle Z_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a\;p_{a}(a)\;\left[\det\left(\hat{C}^{-1}-ia\hat{L}\right)\right]^{-1/2}.

(154)

Furthermore, the interaction term in the action (

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\text{ }\phi(\vec{u})\psi(\vec{r})M(\vec{u},\vec{r})=\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\text{ }\phi(\vec{u})\int\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\text{ }\psi(\vec{r})M(\vec{u},\vec{r})=\int\mathrm{d}\vec{u}\text{ }\phi(\vec{u})\;\vec{u}\hat{K}\vec{u}

Appendix C
Details of the Dyson Brownian motion calculation

Here we provide details of the derivations presented in Sec.
6
.

C.1
First-order perturbation theory for the eigenvalues

We begin by detailing the steps leading from
Eq.

97
to the evolution
equation (
98
) for the eigenvalues.
To this end, we resort to
perturbation theory. At first order, the latter
tells us that the perturbation
\delta\textbf{H}(t)
shifts the eigenvalues of
\textbf{H}(t)
by
\delta^{(1)}\lambda_{i}=\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}
, where
\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}
is the
i
-th eigenvector of
\textbf{H}(t)
at time
t
.

First, let us study the effect of the term
\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}
in
\delta\textbf{H}(t)
on the eigenvalues:

\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}=\sum_{n,m}\sum_{l}\psi_{i}(n;t)g_{nl}(t)X_{ml}(t)\psi_{i}(m;t)\equiv\xi_{i}(t),

(156)

where we introduced the random variable
\xi_{i}(t)
. As this random variable is itself a sum of random variables of finite variance, by virtue of the central limit theorem we expect it to be Gaussian distributed in the large-
N
limit. Adopting the Itô convention, its average is given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{i}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\Bigl\langle\sum_{n,m}\sum_{l}\psi_{i}(n;t)g_{nl}(t)X_{ml}(t)\psi_{i}(m;t)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\sum_{l}\psi_{i}(n;t)\langle g_{nl}(t)\rangle X_{ml}(t)\psi_{i}(m;t)=0,

(157)

while its variance reads

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\Bigl\langle\sum_{n,m,n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\psi_{i}(n;t)g_{nl}(t)X_{ml}(t)\psi_{i}(m;t)\psi_{i}(n^{\prime};t^{\prime})g_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})X_{m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\psi_{i}(m^{\prime};t^{\prime})\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m,n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\psi_{i}(n;t)\psi_{i}(m;t)\psi_{i}(n^{\prime};t^{\prime})\psi_{i}(m^{\prime};t^{\prime})\langle g_{nl}(t)g_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle X_{ml}(t)X_{m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\sum_{n,m,n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\psi_{i}(n;t)\psi_{i}(m;t)\psi_{i}(n^{\prime};t^{\prime})\psi_{i}(m^{\prime};t^{\prime})X_{ml}(t)X_{m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\delta_{nl}\delta_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\sum_{n,m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l}\psi_{i}(n;t)\psi_{i}(m)(t)\psi_{i}(n;t)\psi_{i}(m^{\prime};t)X_{ml}(t)X_{m^{\prime}l}(t).

(158)

We now note that, because of the normalization of the eigenvectors
\sum_{n}\psi_{j}(n;t)\psi_{i}(n;t)=\braket{i(t)|j(t)}=\delta_{ij}
, the variance can be further simplified as

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta_{ij}\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l}\psi_{i}(m;t)\psi_{i}(m^{\prime};t)X_{ml}(t)X_{m^{\prime}l}(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta_{ij}\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta_{ij}\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\textbf{H}(t)-\textbf{A}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta_{ij}\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\braket{a}_{i}(t)\right),

(159)

where
\braket{a}_{i}(t)
was given in
Eq.

101
.

Next, following similar steps as in Eq. (
156
),
one can show that the contribution of
\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}
is the same as the one of
\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}
. Finally, the contribution of the term
\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}
yields

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{i}(t)}\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\sum_{l}\psi_{i}(n;t)g_{nl}(t)g_{ml}(t)\psi_{i}(m;t)

\displaystyle=M\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2}).

(160)

C.2
Second-order perturbation theory for the eigenvalues

The second-order correction to the eigenvalues is given by

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{|\bra{\psi_{j}(t)}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

(161)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{|\bra{\psi_{j}(t)}\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}+\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\biggl[|\bra{\psi_{j}(t)}\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}|^{2}+|\bra{\psi_{j}(t)}\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+2\bra{\psi_{j}(t)}\textbf{g}(t)\textbf{X}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}\bra{\psi_{j}(t)}\textbf{X}(t)\textbf{g}(t)^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}(t)}\biggr]\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2}).

In the second line, the second term is the same as the first one upon switching
i\leftrightarrow j
in the numerator, while the term in the last line will give a
\delta_{ij}
upon averaging, thus giving no contribution to the sum over
j\neq i
.
Retaining only the terms up to order
\mathrm{d}t
and henceforth, unless otherwise stated, omitting the time-dependence at
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\Bigg[\sum_{n,m,n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\psi_{j}(n)g_{nl}X_{ml}\psi_{i}(m)\psi_{j}(n^{\prime})g_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}X_{m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\psi_{i}(m^{\prime})+\bigl(i\leftrightarrow j\bigr)\Bigg]\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\left[\sum_{n,m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l}\psi_{j}(n)X_{ml}\psi_{i}(m)\psi_{j}(n)X_{m^{\prime}l}\psi_{i}(m^{\prime})+\bigl(i\leftrightarrow j\bigr)\right]\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,m^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{l}X_{ml}\psi_{i}(m)X_{m^{\prime}l}\psi_{i}(m^{\prime})+\bigl(i\leftrightarrow j\bigr)\Bigg]\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\bra{\psi_{i}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}+\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{j}}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\bra{\psi_{i}}\textbf{H}-\textbf{A}\ket{\psi_{i}}+\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{H}-\textbf{A}\ket{\psi_{j}}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}-\braket{a}_{i}-\braket{a}_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}.

(162)

Gathering all the contributions in Eqs. (
156
), (
160
) and (
162
), we finally get Eq. (
98
).

C.3
First-order perturbation theory for the eigenvectors

We now detail the steps leading from
Eq.

97
to the evolution
equation (
6
) for the eigenvectors.
The correction to the
n
-th component of the
i
-th eigenvector at first order reads

\displaystyle\delta^{(1)}\psi_{i}(n)=\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{g}\textbf{X}^{T}+\textbf{X}\textbf{g}^{T}+\textbf{gg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}.

(163)

Let us then focus on the first two terms,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{g}\textbf{X}^{T}+\textbf{Xg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\equiv\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\xi_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}},

(164)

where we defined the random variable
\xi_{ij}\equiv\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{gX}^{T}+\textbf{Xg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}
. Again,
since this
is a sum of random variables with finite variance,
we expect that in the large-
N
limit it will converge to a Gaussian random variable of mean value

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{ij}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\sum_{l}\psi_{j}(n;t)\langle g_{nl}(t)\rangle X_{ml}(t)\psi_{i}(m;t)+\bigl(i\leftrightarrow j\bigr)=0,

(165)

and variance

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{ij}(t)\xi_{kl}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\Bigl\langle\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{gX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{l}}\textbf{gX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{k}}+\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{gX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{l}}\textbf{Xg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{k}}+\bigl(i\leftrightarrow j\bigr)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;\left[\delta_{jl}(\lambda_{i}\delta_{ik}-\langle a\rangle_{ik})+\delta_{jk}(\lambda_{i}\delta_{il}-\langle a\rangle_{il})+\bigl(i\leftrightarrow j\bigr)\right]

(166)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\;\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;\Bigl[(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\bigr)-\bigl(\delta_{ik}\langle a\rangle_{jl}+\delta_{il}\langle a\rangle_{jk}+\delta_{jk}\langle a\rangle_{il}+\delta_{jl}\langle a\rangle_{ik}\bigr)\Bigr],

where

\braket{a}_{ik}(t)
was given in
Eq.

102
.

We finally consider the contribution of
\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{g}\textbf{g}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}
, finding

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{j}}\textbf{gg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}=\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l}\psi_{j}(m)\psi_{i}(m^{\prime})g_{ml}g_{m^{\prime}l}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

(167)

\displaystyle=M\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\sum_{m}\psi_{j}(m)\psi_{i}(m)}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2})=M\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j\neq i}\psi_{j}(n)\frac{\braket{\psi_{i}|\psi_{j}}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}=0.

C.4
Second-order perturbation theory for the eigenvectors

The second-order calculation for the eigenvectors has three contributions:

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}\psi_{i}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{l\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{k}}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{l}}\bra{\psi_{l}}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{l})}

(168)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{k}}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\psi_{i}(n)\sum_{k\neq i}\frac{|\bra{\psi_{k}}\delta\textbf{H}(t)\ket{\psi_{i}}|^{2}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}.

The first one is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{l\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{k}}\textbf{g}\textbf{X}^{T}+\textbf{X}\textbf{g}^{T}\ket{\psi_{l}}\bra{\psi_{l}}\textbf{g}\textbf{X}^{T}+\textbf{X}\textbf{g}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{l})}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq i\\
l\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{kl}\bra{\psi_{l}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}+\delta_{ik}\bra{\psi_{l}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{l}}+\delta_{il}\bra{\psi_{k}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{l}}+\delta_{ll}\bra{\psi_{k}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{[\psi_{k}(n)]^{-1}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{l})}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\lambda_{i}\delta_{ik}-\braket{a}_{ik}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{l\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\lambda_{i}\delta_{ik}-\braket{a}_{ik}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{l})}

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\braket{a}_{ik}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}-\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\braket{a}_{ik}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})}\sum_{l\neq i}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{l})}\,.

(169)

The second contribution
reads

\displaystyle-\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\bra{\psi_{k}}\textbf{gX}^{T}+\textbf{Xg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}\textbf{gX}^{T}+\textbf{Xg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2})

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{2\delta_{ik}\bra{\psi_{i}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}+2\delta_{ii}\bra{\psi_{k}}\textbf{XX}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}

\displaystyle=-2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\lambda_{i}\delta_{ik}-\braket{a}_{ik}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}=2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}t\sum_{k\neq i}\psi_{k}(n)\frac{\braket{a}_{ik}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}.

(170)

Finally, the last term in
Eq.

168
can be simplified using

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq i}\frac{|\bra{\psi_{k}}\textbf{gX}^{T}+\textbf{Xg}^{T}\ket{\psi_{i}}|^{2}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}=\sum_{k\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{k}-\braket{a}_{i}-\braket{a}_{k}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}.

(171)

Gathering all the contributions in Eqs. (
164
), (
C.4
), (
C.4
) and (
171
),
we finally
obtain Eq. (
6
).
The noise variance in Eq. (
6
) follows instead from
Eq.

166
.
