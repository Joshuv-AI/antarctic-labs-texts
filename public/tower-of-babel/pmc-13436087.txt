Longitudinal Outcomes and Prognostic Risk of Normalized Airflow Obstruction in a Korean General Population Cohort
Authors: Yun Seok Kim, Yong Suk Jo, Chin Kook Rhee, John S Kim, Yet Hong Khor, Joon Young Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSome individuals with airflow obstruction later show normalized spirometric values, but the clinical implications of this phenomenon in the general population are unclear.MethodsUsing data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), we examined the characteristics and prognosis of individuals with normalized airflow obstruction (NO), defined as FEV1/FVC <0.7 at baseline that normalized to ≥0.7 during follow-up.ResultsBetween 2000 and 2006, 4,807 participants were classified as NO (n=242), fixed obstruction (FO; persistent FEV1/FVC <0.7; n=277), or normal (FEV1/FVC ≥0.7 throughout; n=4,288). Over eight years of follow-up (2007–2014), the NO group contained more men, current smokers, and individuals with lower body mass index than the normal group and showed intermediate lung function between FO and normal groups. Annual FEV1 decline was similar across groups (−48.8 mL/year normal; −48.3 mL/year NO; −45.8 mL/year FO). However, by the end of the 12-year follow-up period, 37.2% of participants in the NO group developed airflow obstruction, with a 4.22-fold higher risk compared with the normal group (95% CI 3.26–5.46). Findings were consistent in a sensitivity analysis excluding individuals with excessive lung function variability. (HR 7.41, 95% CI 5.45–10.07).DiscussionThese findings indicate that NO may represent a distinct and dynamic spirometric phenotype associated with increased risk of future airflow obstruction and highlight the need for early identification and preventive strategies in at-risk populations.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, COPD, KoGES cohort, cohort studies, spirometry, normalized airflow obstruction

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a respiratory condition characterized by structural abnormalities in the airways and/or alveoli, resulting in permanent airflow obstruction that can lead to hypoxia and poor ventilation.1 COPD is the third leading cause of death worldwide, placing considerable social and financial burdens on healthcare systems.2,3

Recent research has increasingly focused on the early detection and management of COPD, which may have the biggest impact on reducing the clinical and socioeconomic burden driven by this disease.4–7 Studies have shown that early diagnosis through case-finding strategies reduces healthcare utilization for respiratory illnesses and improves symptom control.8,9 Additionally, several studies have emphasized the importance of early indicators, such as preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) and pre-COPD, in predicting future airflow obstruction.10–12 Patients with pre-COPD have been found to experience a more rapid annual decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) relative to individuals with COPD, PRISm, or normal lung function.13–16

Current guidelines require forced spirometry to confirm COPD, with a post-bronchodilator FEV1 to forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio of < 0.7.1 This criterion indicates irreversible airflow obstruction. However, in clinical practice, some patients initially diagnosed with COPD based on a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio of < 0.7 exhibit normalization during follow-up. Our previous research identified such patients within a COPD cohort and found that their annual rate of decline in FEV1 was slower and their health-related quality of life better than those with persistent obstruction, although exacerbation rates were similar.17 However, that study was limited by a short follow-up period and small sample size, with the significance of the clinical characteristics of patients with normalized airflow obstruction (NO) in the general population remains unknown.

NO should be distinguished from established concepts such as PRISm, pre-COPD, reversible airflow obstruction, and spirometric variability. Unlike PRISm or pre-COPD, NO refers to a longitudinal spirometric pattern in which an initially reduced FEV1/FVC ratio subsequently normalizes during follow-up. This normalization may reflect multiple possibilities, including measurement variability, reversible airway disease, asthma-COPD overlap, changes in effort or technique, regression to the mean, smoking cessation, or true modification of lung-function trajectory.

In this study, we investigated clinical outcomes, including the risk of future airflow obstruction and the rate of lung function decline, among individuals with normal lung function, NO, and fixed airflow obstruction (FO) in a large population-based cohort. We hypothesized that NO would represent a distinct disease entity and be associated with an increased risk of future airflow obstruction relative to normal lung function.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was based on the Ansan-Ansung cohort, a large-scale prospective study designed to investigate factors associated with chronic diseases in both urban (Ansan) and rural (Ansung) regions. The cohort is part of the Korea Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), which examines chronic diseases in Korea and is supported by the National Genome Research Institute. Detailed information regarding the study design and protocol has previously been published.18 Participants aged 40 to 69 years were enrolled between 2001 and 2002, with baseline measurements taken during that period. Biennial examinations were conducted, and we analyzed data up to the seventh biennial survey (2013–2014). Individuals with a prior diagnosis of asthma were excluded.

Data Collection and Definitions

Demographic data were collected using the KoGES questionnaire, administered by trained investigators. Smoking history, monthly income, and comorbidities were obtained via self-reported questionnaires. For patients with dyspnea, the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score was used for classification. Chronic bronchitis was defined as a cough with sputum production lasting at least 3 months per year for 2 consecutive years.19,20

Lung function tests were performed during each biennial examination using a VMAX2130 spirometer (Sensormedics Corporation, Yorba, CA, USA) in accordance with the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society guidelines.21 Spirometry was conducted by trained personnel following the standardized KoGES protocol. Post-bronchodilator testing was not performed in this cohort. Post-bronchodilator testing was not performed in this cohort. FO was defined as persistent airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC < 0.7) during the last two follow-ups of the baseline period (Figure S1). NO was defined as airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC <0.7) documented in at least one of the first two baseline assessments, followed by normalization to FEV1/FVC ≥0.7 at the third baseline assessment. Participants with an FEV1/FVC ratio <0.7 at the first baseline assessment and ≥0.7 at the third baseline assessment were classified as NO even when FEV1/FVC data at the second baseline assessment were missing, because both airflow obstruction and subsequent normalization were observed during the baseline classification period.17,22 The normal group was comprised of participants with an FEV1/FVC ratio of > 0.7 for all assessments during the baseline period. Participants who had an FEV1/FVC ratio ≥0.7 during the second baseline assessment but developed airflow obstruction during the third baseline assessment were excluded because they did not satisfy the predefined criteria for either the NO group or the FO group. These individuals may have represented newly developed airflow obstruction rather than normalization of a pre-existing obstructive pattern. Excluding these individuals minimized potential misclassification between study groups.

Baseline data were collected during the first to third observation periods (2001–2006), representing to year 0 (2001–2002), year 2 (2003–2004), and year 4 (2005–2006). These data defined the normal, NO, and FO groups. The follow-up period (2007–2014) included year 6 (2007–2008), year 8 (2009–2010), year 10 (2011–2012), and year 12 (2013–2014), during which lung function and airflow obstruction outcomes were tracked.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.2 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Differences among the three groups were assessed using the chi-squared test for categorical variables and analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables.

Longitudinal analyses were performed to assess annual changes in lung function, including FEV1, FVC, FEF25-75%, and FEV1/FVC, over the 8-year follow-up period among the groups. These analyses used linear mixed models with random intercepts and slopes for each participant. The models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, and smoking status. An unstructured covariance matrix was specified for the random effects. All available repeated spirometry measurements were included in the models, allowing participants with unequal numbers of observations to contribute to the analysis under a missing-at-random assumption. Missing FEV1/FVC data were assessed separately for the baseline classification period and the longitudinal follow-up period. The availability of FEV1/FVC data at each visit was summarized overall and by baseline spirometric group in Supplementary Table S1, and the baseline FEV1/FVC patterns among participants classified as NO are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Survival analysis was conducted to evaluate the difference in time to first airflow obstruction between the normal and NO groups. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was used to identify covariates associated with the occurrence of airflow obstruction, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking history, and baseline FEV1 (L).

Sensitivity Analyses

We performed a sensitivity analysis to exclude patients with a high probability of asthma by removing those with excessive variability in lung function. Excessive variability was defined as a change in FEV1 of more than 12% and 200 mL between visits.23

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committees and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments, as well as relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In total, 4,807 participants were included in the study (Figure S1). During the baseline period, there were 277 individuals in the FO group, 242 individuals in the NO group, and 4,288 individuals in the normal group (Table 1). The proportion of male patients was higher in the FO (85.9%) and NO (63.2%) groups, and the majority of the NO group resided in urban areas (80.6%). In all three groups, asymptomatic individuals were more common than symptomatic individuals; however, chronic bronchitis was most prevalent in the FO group (4.1%). Compared with the normal group, both the NO and FO groups had lower BMI values (23.9 ± 2.7 and 23.9 ± 2.9 kg/m2, respectively). Regarding smoking status, current smokers were the most prevalent in the FO group (53.4%), less common in NO group (37.6%), and least common in the normal group (20.6%). Pulmonary function tests showed that FEV1%-predicted was highest in the normal group, followed by the NO group and then the FO group; FEF25-75% followed a similar pattern.

Lung Function Trajectories

The annual rate of change in lung function significantly differed among all groups (Figure 1). The annual rates of decline in FEV1 were −48.8 mL in the normal group, −48.3 mL in the NO group, and −45.8 mL in the FO group (FO vs NO, p = 0.264; FO vs normal, p = 0.054; NO vs normal, p = 0.001). The annual rates of decline in FVC were −36.1 mL in the normal group, −35.8 mL in the NO group, and −43.0 mL in the FO group (FO vs NO, p = 0.009; FO vs normal, p < 0.001; NO vs normal, p = 0.913). The annual rates of decline in the FEV1/FVC ratio were −0.4 in the normal group, −0.6 in the NO group, and −0.5 in the FO group (FO vs NO, p = 0.008; FO vs normal, p = 0.002; NO vs normal, p < 0.001). The annual rates of decline in FEF25-75% were −87.5 mL in the normal group, −87.9 mL in the NO group, and −44.0 mL in the FO group (FO vs NO, p < 0.001; FO vs normal, p < 0.001; NO vs normal, p = 0.921).

Analysis of Airflow Obstruction Occurrence

The NO group exhibited a significantly higher occurrence of airflow obstruction compared with the normal group (Figure 2A and Table S4). At the final follow-up, airflow obstruction had occurred in 37.2% of the NO group and 5.9% of the normal group. The hazard ratio for future airflow obstruction in the NO group was 4.22 compared with the normal group (95% confidence interval [CI], 3.26–5.46; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Yearly Transition of Airflow Obstruction

In the NO group, 28.9% (70/242) developed obstruction by the 12th year, whereas in the normal group, 5.4% (231/4,288) of participants developed airflow obstruction by the same point (Figure 3). By the 12th year of follow-up, 7.5% of the normal group and 47.6% of the NO group had developed airflow obstruction (Figure S3A). If airflow obstruction was present in the previous year, there was a significant tendency for increased obstruction in the current year across all groups (Figure S3B).

Sensitivity Analysis

To minimize the influence of asthma, an additional analysis excluding individuals with excessive variability was conducted. During the baseline period, there were 263 individuals in the FO group, 90 individuals in the NO group, and 3,988 individuals in the normal group. The overall baseline characteristics were similar across the groups (Table S3). Lung function trajectories showed a more pronounced decline in the NO group (Figure S2). The annual rates of decline in FEV1 were −42.3 mL in the normal group, −53.0 mL in the NO group, and −46.4 mL in the FO group (FO vs NO, p = 0.027; FO vs normal, p = 0.010; NO vs normal, p < 0.001). The annual rates of decline in the FEV1/FVC ratio were −0.4 in the normal group, −0.7 in the NO group, and −0.5 in the FO group (FO vs NO, p < 0.001; FO vs normal, p < 0.001; NO vs normal, p < 0.001). The annual rates of decline in FEF25-75% were −87.0 mL in the normal group, −86.9 mL in the NO group, and −44.3 mL in the FO group (FO vs NO, p < 0.001; FO vs normal, p < 0.001; NO vs normal, p = 0.995). The median time to first airflow obstruction was earlier in the NO group after excluding individuals with excessive variability than in the overall NO group in the primary analysis. (Figure 2B). At the final follow-up, airflow obstruction had occurred in 65.6% of the NO group and 6.0% of the normal group. The hazard ratio for future airflow obstruction in the NO group was 7.41 compared with the normal group in the multivariate analysis (p < 0.001; 95% CI, 5.45–10.07) (Table 2). In the group excluding patients with excessive variability, 48.9% (44/90) of the NO group developed airflow obstruction (Figures S4 and S5).

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical significance of NO compared with the normal and FO groups in a general population cohort. The NO group was younger and had a lower smoking prevalence than the FO group, but had lower BMI, higher age, and higher smoking prevalence compared to the normal group. Although the rate of lung function decline was more rapid in the NO group than in the FO group, the difference was not statistically significant. However, this trend became more pronounced and statistically significant after excluding subjects with suspected asthma. At the final follow-up, 40% of the NO group had developed airflow obstruction, and they were over four times more likely than the normal group to develop future obstruction. The yearly transition of airflow obstruction status revealed that the normal group tended to maintain a normal status, the FO group consistently exhibited obstruction, and the NO group showed more variability. Collectively, our findings suggest that patients with NO have a high probability of developing future airflow obstruction.

The mechanisms underlying NO are likely heterogeneous. In some individuals, normalization of airflow obstruction may reflect transient or partially reversible factors, such as airway inflammation, mucus burden, variable airway tone, smoking cessation, or improved spirometric effort and technique.24 These mechanisms differ from fixed airflow obstruction, which is more likely to reflect persistent structural changes, including airway remodeling, small airway narrowing, and emphysematous destruction.1 Therefore, NO should not be interpreted as simple resolution of COPD, but rather as a dynamic spirometric phenotype that may include both reversible functional changes and early structural airway disease. Because post-bronchodilator spirometry, computed tomography, and inflammatory biomarkers were unavailable, the exact mechanisms of NO could not be directly determined in this study.

In our previous study, which analyzed the Korea COPD Subgroup Study cohort—a nationwide multicenter COPD cohort in South Korea—14.2% of patients who initially had FO transitioned to NO during follow-up.17 Forty percent of the NO group who completed follow-up developed airflow obstruction. The NO group had better symptom scores, improved mental health, and a higher FEV1 compared with patients with FO, although there was no difference in the annual exacerbation rate. Similar results were observed in studies from the US based COPDGene study, Netherlands, Denmark, and the UK.14,24–27 Although patients with normalized airflow obstruction may not meet the current spirometric definition of COPD, they appear to constitute a distinct subgroup with an increased risk of future airflow obstruction.

In this study, the NO group exhibited a highly dynamic state, a trend that became even more pronounced when individuals with excessive variability were excluded. While the FO and normal groups showed a change of < 10% in their airflow obstruction status based on the previous year’s status, more than 20% of the NO group experienced airflow obstruction; this figure increased to > 35% in the sensitivity analysis. This variability in the NO group, characterized by frequent transitions to airflow obstruction, is comparable to the hypervariable state observed in PRISm during previous studies.24,25 In a study by Wijnant et al (24), 15.7% of individuals with PRISm transitioned to normal spirometry, whereas 49.4% progressed to COPD. These findings support the hypothesis that NO represents a distinct category and may be a transitional state between normal lung function and airflow obstruction, similar to PRISm. Considering the dynamic nature of NO, early detection and management are crucial for individuals with this condition.

In our study, the rate of decline in FEV1 was more pronounced in the NO group than in the normal and COPD groups, particularly in the subset excluding those with excessive variability. According to Lange et al,28 half of the individuals who develop COPD do so due to a rapid decline in FEV1 over time, as observed in three longitudinal cohorts. These rapid decliners have been identified as at-risk individuals for future airflow obstruction.29 Our findings indicate that patients with NO may represent a subset of rapid decliners who are at increased risk of future airflow obstruction. Furthermore, a previous study showed that individuals with a rapid decline in FEV1/FVC had a two-fold higher covariate-adjusted incidence rate of airflow obstruction and an increased risk of all-cause mortality.30 Given that the NO group in our study exhibited the fastest decline in FEV1/FVC, they may share characteristics with rapid FEV1/FVC decliners. Although the overall analysis showed only a small absolute difference in annual FEV1 decline, the analysis excluding individuals with excessive lung-function variability demonstrated more pronounced declines in FEV1 and FEV1/FVC in the NO group. This finding supports the interpretation that NO may represent a clinically relevant phenotype associated with progressive airflow limitation, although the smaller sample size requires cautious interpretation. Continuous monitoring of the NO group is necessary, even when the FEV1/FVC ratio becomes normalized.

This study has three major strengths. Notably, this study provides population-based evidence on lung function trajectories and future airflow obstruction among individuals with NO in a large prospective cohort. The study confirmed that NO is a significant risk factor for future airflow obstruction. Second, the cohort used in this study has been well-validated for lung function trajectories.31 Third, repeated spirometric measurements over an 8-year follow-up period allowed us to evaluate longitudinal lung function trajectories and changes in airflow obstruction status.

This study also has several limitations. First, post-bronchodilator lung function data were not available. However, longitudinal studies have shown that serial measurements of pre-bronchodilator FEV1 can be used to effectively evaluate patients with COPD.32 Additionally, numerous studies have used pre-bronchodilator values as a viable alternative to post-bronchodilator measurements.26,33–36 Because post-bronchodilator spirometry was not available in this cohort, COPD could not be confirmed according to GOLD diagnostic criteria. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting longitudinal patterns of pre-bronchodilator airflow obstruction rather than confirmed COPD diagnosis. Second, asthma was not completely excluded. Although we excluded patients with a history of asthma based on patient interviews, we did not have data on other asthma markers such as post-bronchodilator lung function, blood eosinophil count, or fractional exhaled nitric oxide. Therefore, we could not entirely rule out asthma. However, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with excessive variability, which is highly specific to asthma, to strengthen our findings.23 Third, this study did not include data on computed tomography findings or the diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide, which can precede airflow obstruction. Fourth, information regarding COPD-specific treatment was not available in the KoGES cohort. Therefore, we could not determine whether normalization of airflow obstruction was related to treatment responsiveness, natural variability in lung function, or other factors. Future studies incorporating treatment information are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying normalized airflow obstruction. Fifth, some individuals classified as NO may have achieved pseudo-normalization due to random variability or regression to the mean. This possibility is inherent to longitudinal spirometric studies and may have resulted in misclassification of a subset of participants. Sixth, because this study was conducted within a community-based cohort in Korea, there are concerns about the generalizability of the findings. Future validation in other large cohorts will be necessary to confirm these results. Seventh, although the overall cohort was large, the NO subgroup was relatively small, which may have limited the precision of subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in independent cohorts. Eighth, missing spirometry data may have introduced selection bias. Although we summarized the availability of FEV1/FVC data at each visit and used linear mixed models with all available repeated measurements for longitudinal lung-function trajectories, non-random missingness and informative dropout cannot be fully excluded. In particular, some NO participants had missing FEV1/FVC data at the second baseline assessment, which limited characterization of the intermediate spirometric trajectory. In addition, the sensitivity analysis excluding individuals with excessive lung-function variability reduced the NO group from 242 to 90 participants. Therefore, the increased hazard ratio observed in this analysis should be interpreted cautiously with regard to statistical precision and magnitude. Nineth, we did not apply formal adjustment for multiple comparisons in the longitudinal lung-function analyses. Therefore, p-values from pairwise comparisons, particularly marginal findings, should be interpreted cautiously because of the potential risk of type I error. Finally, although the 8-year follow-up allowed us to evaluate longitudinal spirometric trajectories and future airflow obstruction, it was insufficient to fully assess long-term clinical outcomes such as mortality, exacerbations, imaging progression, or clinically confirmed COPD progression. Longer-term validation studies incorporating these outcomes, imaging biomarkers, and post-bronchodilator spirometry are needed to clarify the long-term clinical significance of normalized airflow obstruction.

In conclusion, we investigated the longitudinal outcomes of lung function and airflow obstruction status among individuals in the normal, NO, and FO groups using a large, regional cohort from the general population. The NO group showed a faster decline in lung function trajectories than the FO group. By the end of the 12-year follow-up period, 37.2% of participants in the NO group had developed airflow obstruction, with a 4.22-fold higher likelihood of progression to airflow obstruction relative to the normal group. The NO group exhibited a highly dynamic transition in their airflow obstruction status. Based on these findings, individuals with NO may represent an at-risk subgroup characterized by a dynamic spirometric trajectory and an increased likelihood of future airflow obstruction. Even when spirometry normalizes, these individuals may benefit from continued surveillance and periodic lung function assessment. Future studies are needed to identify biomarkers and imaging features associated with progression in individuals with NO and to determine whether preventive strategies can reduce the risk of future airflow obstruction.

Data Sharing Statement

The datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to legal and security restrictions imposed by the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), which is a government-managed national cohort. According to KoGES policy, data access is restricted to authorized researchers within approved secure facilities, and the data cannot be shared or transferred outside these systems. Therefore, the datasets used in this analysis cannot be made publicly available.
