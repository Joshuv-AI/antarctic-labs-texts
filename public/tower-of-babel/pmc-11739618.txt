Does methylene blue improve outcomes in patients with post-cardiac surgery vasoplegic syndrome?
Authors: Michael Z L Zhu, Jackson Scullion, Andrie Stroebel, Cheng He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVESA best evidence topic was written according to a structured protocol described in ICVTS. The question addressed was ‘In patients with vasoplegic syndrome following cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, does adjunctive methylene blue improve outcomes including reduced mortality, morbidity and vasopressor requirements?’METHODSOvid Medline was searched using a reported search algorithm. Articles that represented the best evidence to answer the clinical question were selected, tabulated and discussed.RESULTSSeven articles were selected: 4 randomized controlled trials and 3 observational studies. Studies were single centre with sample sizes ranging from 30 to 120 patients. Two studies reported a significant reduction in mortality among patients who received adjunctive methylene blue for post-cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) vasoplegic syndrome compared with placebo or standard therapy. Four studies reported improved haemodynamic stability and reduced vasopressor requirements following methylene blue. No studies reported methylene blue-related adverse events or worse outcomes compared to controls or standard therapy. There was significant heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of the timing and dosage of methylene blue therapy as well as the definition of vasoplegic syndrome.CONCLUSIONSThe summation of the best available evidence, which was limited to small clinical trials and observational studies, suggests that the adjunctive administration of methylene blue for refractory post-CPB vasoplegic syndrome is safe and may provide benefits in terms of improved haemodynamic stability, reduction in vasopressor requirements and reduced mortality. Questions surrounding the optimal timing of methylene blue administration, as prophylactic, adjunctive, or rescue therapy for vasoplegic syndrome, require further investigation.

INTRODUCTION

A best evidence topic review was written according to a structured protocol. This is fully described in ICVTS [1].

THREE-PART QUESTION

In ‘patients with vasoplegic syndrome following cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, does adjunctive methylene blue improve outcomes, particularly mortality, morbidity, vasopressor requirements?’

CLINICAL SCENARIO

A 65-year-old diabetic man presents in acute pulmonary oedema and cardiogenic shock with presumed delayed presentation myocardial infarction. Coronary angiogram showed severe triple vessel disease with a culprit left circumflex, which was stented. Echocardiogram showed severe mitral regurgitation with papillary muscle rupture and left ventricular ejection fraction of 35%. The patient underwent emergency coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and mitral valve replacement. Moderate doses of norepinephrine were required on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) to maintain blood pressure. After a prolonged period of reperfusion, the patient was weaned from CPB on low-dose inotropes but escalating doses of norepinephrine and vasopressin. Methylene blue (MB) infusion (2 mg/kg over 60 min) was administered as adjunctive therapy for refractory vasoplegia. Over the next 10 h, vasopressors were gradually weaned and the patient was extubated uneventfully. You search the literature for whether adjunctive MB, an inhibitor of nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, improves outcomes in patients with post-cardiac surgery vasoplegic syndrome (VS).

SEARCH STRATEGY

The Ovid Medline Database was searched on 25 May 2024 using the following search terms:

[Methylene blue OR Methylthioninium chloride] AND [Cardiac surgery OR Cardiothoracic surgery OR Cardiovascular surgery OR Cardiopulmonary bypass] AND [Vasopleg* or Vasodil* or Hypoten* or Shock*]. The search included dates from 1946 (Ovid Medline default) to 24 May 2024. The reference lists of relevant articles were also screened.

SEARCH OUTCOME

A total of 119 articles were found using the reported search strategy. Conference abstracts and studies with <10 patients in the intervention group were excluded. Seven articles [4 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 3 observational studies] were identified as providing the best evidence to answer the question (Table 1). Our search also identified one meta-analysis [2], which incorporated pooled data from the 4 already included RCTs and 1 additional observational study. To avoid repetition and due to concerns regarding significant heterogeneity between the pooled studies, a decision was made to exclude the meta-analysis from the main review. Instead, this meta-analysis, by Perdhana et al. [2], is tabulated and discussed in Supplementary material 1.

RESULTS

Levin et al. [3] randomized patients to receive MB (1.5 mg/kg) versus placebo for postoperative VS. From 638 patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery, 56 patients (8.8%, 56/638) met study criteria for VS and were included. Randomization and subsequent administration of MB (N = 28) or placebo (N = 28) occurred after the diagnosis of postoperative VS. Among patients who received MB, VS resolved within 2 h of drug administration with no instances of rebound vasoplegia during subsequent observation, out to 96 h. In contrast, VS remained prevalent at 24 h (MB: 0/28, 0%, C: 21/28, 75%; P < 0.001) and 48 h (MB: 0/28, 0%, C: 8/28, 28.6%; P = 0.002) in the placebo group. There were no deaths or instances of renal, respiratory, multiorgan failure, or sepsis in the MB group. In contrast, patients randomized to placebo had 21.4% (6/28) mortality, 14.3% (4/28) renal and respiratory failure and 25% (7/28) multiorgan failure. Seven (7/28, 25%) patients in the placebo group developed sepsis with 6 of these patients dying from sepsis or multiorgan failure—a rather concerning and unexplained finding. No adverse events relating to MB or placebo were reported. The study concluded that MB reduces mortality and morbidity in patients with post-cardiac surgery VS.

Ozal et al. [4] conducted an RCT to investigate prophylactic MB (2 mg/kg), administered 1 h before surgery. One hundred elective CABG patients at risk for VS, due to being on preoperative angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), calcium channel blockers and/or intravenous heparin, were included with 50 receiving MB. Significantly fewer patients in the MB group required inotropic support to wean from CPB (7/50, 14% vs 24/50, 48%; P < 0.001), or experienced intraoperative or early postoperative VS (0/50, 0% vs 13/50, 26%; P < 0.001). Length of stay in ICU was also halved (1.2 vs 2.1 days; P < 0.001). No adverse events were reported, although the authors highlight several known risks associated with MB given at higher doses including arrhythmia, increased pulmonary vascular resistance, systemic vasoconstriction leading to impaired end-organ perfusion and gas exchange impairment. The study concluded that, in patients undergoing CABG, prophylactic MB significantly reduces the incidence and severity of VS in the intraoperative and early postoperative periods.

Maslow et al. [5] compared MB (3 mg/kg), administered at commencement of CPB, to saline in an RCT of 30 patients receiving preoperative ACEI. The study focused only on the intraoperative period and no postoperative data was reported. In patients who received MB, there was a higher percentage increase of systemic vascular resistance (SVR) (10 min post-drug: 21.4% vs −7.9%; P < 0.005; 60 min post-drug: 12.4% vs 2.4%; P > 0.05), lower vasopressor requirements (10 min post-drug: 0% vs 80%; P < 0.005; 60 min post-drug: 7% vs 60%; P < 0.01) and lower lactate levels (10 min post-drug: 0.84 vs 1.30 mmol/l; P < 0.01; 60 min post-drug: 1.29 vs 1.85 mmol/l; P < 0.05). No adverse events relating to MB were reported. It is worth noting that Maslow et al. [5] and Ozal et al. [4] specifically selected patients considered at increased risk of VS by recruiting patients on preoperative ACEIs, calcium channel blockers and/or intravenous heparin therapy. Thus, interpretation of the findings from these 2 trials should be made with these factors in mind.

Cho et al. [6] conducted an RCT involving 42 patients who underwent cardiac surgery for infective endocarditis. Patients were randomized to receive either a single prophylactic dose of MB (2 mg/kg) before CPB (N = 21), or saline control (N = 21). Two patients (9.5%, 2/21) in the control group developed postoperative vasoplegia, were treated with MB, and subsequently excluded from analysis after randomization. Perioperative vasopressor requirements and haemodynamic parameters did not significantly differ between the MB and control (C) groups. There were no major differences in major morbidity endpoints [MB: 28.6% (6/21), C: 47.4%, 9/19; P = 0.22], and ICU stay [MB: (3.8 ± 2.0), C: (4.7 ± 4.9) days; P = 0.43]. The authors concluded that prophylactic MB did not reduce rates of perioperative mortality, morbidity or vasopressor requirement in patients undergoing valve surgery for infective endocarditis. Importantly, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution because it did not follow an intention-to-treat protocol and nearly 10% of the control group were excluded from analysis after randomization. These methodological flaws introduce significant bias and type-1 error.

Mehaffey et al. [7] compared early (intraoperative; 40.7%, 48/118) and late (ICU; 59.3%, 70/118) administration of MB in a retrospective cohort of 118 patients. When averaged across all patients, within the first 12 h after MB administration, both mean arterial pressure (MAP) and SVR increased by around 10%, while norepinephrine requirements were approximately halved. Early administration of MB was associated with lower rates of 30-day mortality and renal failure [early: 10.4% (5/48), late: 28.6% (20/70); P = 0.018 for both]. No difference was observed in hospital length of stay (early: 12, late: 12 days; P = 0.45) or major morbidity endpoints, which included permanent stroke, renal failure, reoperation, mediastinitis and prolonged ventilation [early: 35.4% (17/48), late: 52.9%, (37/70); P = 0.062]. On multivariate logistic regression analysis, patients who received early MB had significantly less risk-adjusted major adverse events (odds ratio = 0.35; P = 0.037). The study concluded that early administration of MB improves survival and reduces the risk of major adverse events in patients with CPB-induced VS.

Saha et al. [8] studied the effect of MB on haemodynamics and outcomes in patients with VS after left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation. Twenty-seven LVAD patients were included in the MB group (15 received MB intraoperatively, 12 postoperatively) and 41 patients were designated to a propensity-matched control group. There was no difference in norepinephrine requirement when comparing the control and intervention groups (C: 0.10 (0.07–0.13) vs MB: 0.16 (0.1–0.21) μg/kg/min; P = 0.07). However, patients who received intraoperative MB had significantly lower requirements than those who received postoperative MB (early: 0.10 (0.07–0.16) vs late: 0.18 (0.15–0.22) μg/kg/min; P = 0.008). There was no difference in rates of death, renal failure, need for additional mechanical circulatory support, or ICU length of stay. The authors concluded that MB did not improve outcomes in patients with vasoplegia after LVAD implantation.

Kofler et al. [9] retrospectively studied 1022 patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB who developed VS, of whom 221 received intraoperative MB. Among them, 63 patients received MB (2 mg/kg) within 15 min from the onset of VS. The authors specifically aimed to evaluate the effect of early intraoperative administration of MB and thus the remaining 158 patients who received MB after 15 min from the onset of VS were not further analysed. Using propensity-score matching, 60 patients who received early MB were matched and compared to a control cohort who received standard therapy. After MB administration, MAP was significantly higher compared to the control group during the first 60 min, while norepinephrine and vasopressin requirement was significantly lower 30 min after MB administration and remained lower for the next 90 min compared with the control group. Patients who received MB required less overall norepinephrine (MB: 7.4 ± 3.3 vs C: 9.7 ± 6.7 mg; p = 0.018) and vasopressin (MB: 6.1 ± 5.1 vs C: 11 ± 13.4 International Units (IU); P = 0.003). Mean duration of mechanical ventilation (MB: 203 ± 338 vs C: 195 ± 275 h; P = 0.92) and ICU stay were similar (MB: 16 ± 21 vs C: 20 ± 37 days; P = 0.47). Survival at 90 days was also similar [MB: 81.7% (49/60) vs C: 80% (48/60); P = 0.27]. The authors concluded that early intraoperative administration of MB in cardiac surgery patients with refractory VS improved haemodynamic stability and provided an additive effect to standard vasopressor therapy within the first hour after MB; however, this did not lead to improved survival outcomes. In terms of study limitations, it must be highlighted that the omission of the 158 patients who received MB outside of the early 15-min window introduces sampling bias and is also a missed opportunity to compare the outcomes of early versus late administration of MB.

CLINICAL BOTTOM LINE

The documented use of MB for refractory VS after cardiac surgery dates to the late 1990s. Despite this, published evidence remains limited to only a few small-scale, single-centre clinical trials and observational studies. The comparison of these studies are further complicated by differences in terms of dosage and timing of MB administration as well as varying definitions for post-cardiac surgery vasoplegia. Nevertheless, the summation of available evidence, suggests that MB can be safely administered in cardiac surgery patients with or at risk of VS. MB appears to temporarily augment haemodynamics and reduce vasopressor requirements and may, in some cases, improve key outcomes including shorten ICU stay and reduce organ failure and mortality. Notably, the 2024 EACTS/EACTA/EBCP guidelines on CPB in adult cardiac surgery has classified the use of MB in alpha-1 agonist-refractory VS with a class IIa, level of evidence B recommendation. These guidelines also recognize hydroxocobalamin as potentially effective for refractory vasoplegia (class IIb, level of evidence C) although, at the time of writing, the available evidence appears less robust than that for MB [10]. Questions relating to the optimal timing of MB administration (preoperatively/prophylactically, intraoperatively/early, postoperatively/late or as rescue therapy) and the utility of repeat doses require further investigation.
