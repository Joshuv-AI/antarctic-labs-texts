Pilonidal granuloma formation after an incision and drainage procedure is associated with retained hair within the sinus – A case series
Authors: Helene Nepomuceno, Claire Abrajano, Bill Chiu
Published: 2024

Background

Pilonidal disease is an acquired condition of the gluteal cleft and sacrococcygeal region, characterized by sinus and abscess formation in association with midline openings or pits that entrap hair and debris [1]. The pathophysiology of pilonidal disease is thought to involve an inflammatory foreign body response from the entry of hair and other debris. Karydakis described three factors that play a role in hair entry underneath the skin surface: loose hair, force (friction that leads to hair insertion), and skin vulnerability [2]. The depth of the natal cleft is associated with an anaerobic environment with moisture and pressure that can lead to increased hair insertion [3].

Pilonidal disease presents on a spectrum of acute and chronic disease, from inactive pilonidal sinuses to active infections [4]. About 50 % of patients present with an acute infection [5]. Physical examination may demonstrate erythema and induration with or without fluctuance or active drainage. An abscess cavity will usually communicate with characteristic midline pits and be contaminated with hair and debris. These midline pores are the primary openings and are the source of the pilonidal disease. After several acute abscesses, a chronic draining sinus may develop via subcutaneous epithelialized tracts. These secondary sinuses typically open cephalad to the right or left aspect of the gluteal cleft and may be characterized by heaping granulation tissue and/or intermittent drainage [[6], [7], [8]].

While these sinuses and granulomas have been described in the literature as findings in more complex pilonidal disease, their pathophysiology is not well-characterized, and the evolution of this finding has not been previously reported. In this case presentation, we describe two patients with pilonidal disease who developed a secondary sinus/granuloma in the course of the disease. We hypothesize that the secondary sinus or granuloma is the result of a chronic nonhealing wound, and the wound is nonhealing due to presence of foreign body within the cavity.

Case presentation 1

A 16 year-old male with a history of sacral dimple developed pain, swelling, and drainage at the gluteal cleft, consistent with pilonidal abscess (Fig. 1A). An incision was made at the top of the gluteal cleft to the right of midline over the area of swelling, and the pus was drained. Despite managing the wound with Sitz baths and a course of antibiotics, the incision epithelialized into a draining secondary sinus (Fig. 1B) that developed a raised painful granuloma (Fig. 1C). He underwent excision of the pilonidal pits and granuloma. Case presentation 2

A 15 year-old male presented to our surgical clinic after an incision was made at the top right of the gluteal cleft to drain a pilonidal abscess. Although the swelling at the gluteal cleft has resolved, a granuloma was formed at the previous incision site with recurrent drainage (Fig. 2A). He underwent excision of pilonidal pits and granuloma, and moderate amount of hair was evacuated from the pilonidal sinus. Discussion

Pilonidal disease presents on a spectrum of varying symptoms, acuity, and severity. We report two cases of patients who developed chronic symptoms with the development of a granuloma and draining secondary sinus. In both cases, the granuloma formed after the incision and drainage of an acute abscess that failed to heal completely. Both patients were managed with a pit-picking and granuloma excision procedure. Key to both procedures was the removal of a considerable amount of hair which presumably led to the failure of the wound to heal. This implicates that when a wound from a previous incision and drainage procedure fails to heal, the surgeon should proceed with a pilonidal excision procedure expeditiously. The role of hair is central to pilonidal disease pathophysiology and is a known factor in foreign body granuloma formation – a cutaneous inflammatory response to endogenous or exogenous material in the dermis that is not broken down readily by macrophages [9,10]. Interdigital pilonidal sinuses of the hand with and without granuloma have also been described in barbers, where embedded hair fragments played a central role [11,12].

Many pilonidal patients present with such a granuloma or secondary sinus at the gluteal cleft, but there has been no documentation of how this forms or the natural history of this development. We now describe the first report of the evolution of this persistent nonhealing granuloma in sacrococcygeal pilonidal disease. Karydakis proposed a theory of hair entry and exit through the formation of primary and secondary fistulae [13]. Hairs are able to propel themselves into the skin through their tough root [14]. Once one hair inserts successfully, other hairs can insert more easily. Foreign body tissue reaction and infection follow and the primary sinus of pilonidal disease forms. Primary sinuses are portals of continuous entrance of hair, and secondary fistulae are portals of continuous exit of hair as well as abscess drainage [7]. These secondary openings were noted to be most often located cephalad and to the patient's left side “due to the local tissue architecture” [2]. The direction of the hair follicle determines the direction of the tract, propelling toward the hair root; thus the majority of sinus tracts extend cephalad and additional tracts may branch laterally [7].

The timing of granuloma development would support the above mechanism in the cases we have described. For both, a persistent nonhealing wound with granuloma occurred within days after initial incision and drainage procedure. Presumably, the incision and drainage facilitates the body's natural response to expel the hair along its course of exit, although this claim is speculative and warrants further research. In our patients, the course of drainage was cephalad and to the right, though previous reports show most secondary sinuses drain to the left [15]. To our knowledge, the reason for favoring drainage to the left has not been defined and may be an area for further investigation. The presence of a secondary sinus or granuloma may indicate a higher severity of disease or a higher probability of recurrence: Liang et al. showed that patients with a granuloma at initial presentation can have recurrent drainage from the previous granuloma site after excising the granuloma [8]. Based on these cases, we advocate that if there is persistent drainage from a pilonidal wound, there may be hair within the cavity. Surgical intervention to treat this nonhealing wound needs to eliminate the hair within the cavity, be it through minimally invasive approach or wide excision.

Conclusion

Pilonidal disease with an associated granuloma or secondary sinus is a chronic response whereby the body may try to expel a foreign body in the form of retained hair within the cavity. Research on this foreign body reaction can further elucidate the mechanism. Our cases demonstrated the formation and evolution of pilonidal granuloma or secondary sinus after initial incision and drainage of a pilonidal abscess. Recurrent drainage with the development of a secondary sinus or granuloma requires excision of the granuloma, wound debridement, and removal of hair.
