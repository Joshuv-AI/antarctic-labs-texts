Safety of the extension of use of galacto‐oligosaccharides as a novel food pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2015/2283
Authors: EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), Dominique Turck, Torsten Bohn, Montaña Cámara, Jacqueline Castenmiller, Stefaan De Henauw, Ángeles Jos, Alexandre Maciuk, Inge Mangelsdorf, Breige McNulty, Androniki Naska, Kristina Pentieva, Alfonso Siani, Frank Thies, Margarita Aguilera‐Gómez, Thomas Frenzel, Harry J McArdle, Peter Moldeus, Monika Neuhäuser‐Berthold, Josef Rudolf Schlatter, Henk van Loveren, Emanuela Turla, Domenico Azzollini, Karen Ildico Hirsch‐Ernst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, the EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA) was asked to deliver an opinion on the extension of use of galacto‐oligosaccharides (GOS) as a novel food (NF) pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2015/2283. The NF is produced from milk‐derived lactose using β‐galactosidases. The applicant proposed to extend the use of GOS to several food categories (non‐alcoholic beverages, confectionery, chewing gum, cocoa and chocolate‐based products and protein products). The target population is the general population. GOS is already authorised and included in the Union list of NFs for use in several foods, including infant formula, and in food supplements. The Panel estimated that the proposed extension of use would result in a highest 95th percentile intake of GOS from all food uses, of up to 48.5 g/day in adults. As compared to the exposure to GOS from the currently authorised food uses (at the highest P95), the proposed extension of use is expected to result in an increase in intake of GOS of up to 17%. The information provided on the proposed use levels and anticipated intake does not raise safety concerns. The Panel concludes that the proposed extension of use of GOS is safe under the proposed conditions of use.Keywords: extension of use, galacto‐oligosaccharides, GOS, novel food

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

The Union list set out in the Annex to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2470 1 includes galacto‐oligosaccharide as an authorised novel food. Pursuant to Article 5 of Regulation (EC). No 258/97, 2 galacto‐oligosaccharide has been authorised to be placed on the market as a novel food to be used in a number of foods, including infant formula and follow‐on formula as defined in Regulation (EU) No 609/2013. 3

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/684 4 amended the conditions of use of the novel food galacto‐oligosaccharide. In particular, the use of galacto‐oligosaccharide has been extended to additional foods namely, dairy confectionery, cheese and processed cheese, butter and spreadable fats.

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/1027 5 amended the specifications of the novel food galacto‐oligosaccharide. In particular, galactose has been removed from the specifications.

On 21 March 2025, the company ‘FrieslandCampina Ingredients’ submitted a request to the European Commission in accordance with Article 10 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 to request a change in the conditions of use of the novel food galacto‐oligosaccharide. The current application proposes to extend the current conditions of use of galacto‐oligosaccharide to include non‐alcoholic beverages, confectionery, chewing gum, cocoa and chocolate‐based products and protein products.

The applicant has requested data protection under Article 26 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 for data in support of this request.

In accordance with Article 10(3) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, the European Commission requests the European Food Safety Authority to provide a scientific opinion on a change in the conditions of use of the novel food galacto‐oligosaccharides.

In this opinion, the European Food Safety Authority should also document whether and to what extent the requirements of Article 26(2)(c) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 are fulfilled regarding the data for which the applicant is requesting data protection.

Additional information

On 17 December 2020, EFSA adopted an opinion on a change in the conditions of use of GOS in food supplements (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021a). The panel concluded that the NF was safe under the proposed extension of use.

On 14 September 2021, EFSA adopted an opinion on the extension of use of GOS as an ingredient in other food categories (dairy confectionery, cheese and processed cheese, butter and spreads) (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021b). The panel concluded that the NF was safe under the proposed extension of use.

On 28 February 2022, EFSA adopted an opinion on a change in the conditions of use in food for special medical purposes (EFSA NDA Panel, 2022). The panel concluded that the NF was safe under the proposed conditions of use.

DATA AND METHODOLOGIES

Data

The safety assessment of this NF is based on data supplied in the application and additional data identified by the panel.

Administrative and scientific requirements for NF applications referred to in Article 10 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 are listed in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2469. 6

A common and structured format on the presentation of NF applications is described in the EFSA guidance on the preparation and presentation of an NF application (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021c). As indicated in this guidance, it is the duty of the applicant to provide all of the available (proprietary, confidential and published) scientific data (including both data in favour and not in favour) that are pertinent to the safety of the NF.

The applicant has submitted a confidential and a non‐confidential version of a dossier following the ‘EFSA guidelines on the preparation and presentation of a NF application’ (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021c) and the ‘Administrative guidance for the preparation of applications on novel foods pursuant to Article 10 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283’ (EFSA, 2021).

In accordance with Art. 38 of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 7 and taking into account the protection of confidential information and of personal data in accordance with Articles 39–39e of the same Regulation, and of the Decision of EFSA's Executive Director laying down practical arrangements concerning transparency and confidentiality, 8 the non‐confidential version of the dossier has been published on Open.EFSA. 9

According to Art. 32c(2) of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 and to the Decision of EFSA's Executive Director laying down the practical arrangements on pre‐submission phase and public consultations, EFSA carried out a public consultation on the non‐confidential version of the technical dossier from 16 September 2025 to 7 October 2025 for which no comments were received.

This NF application includes a request for protection of proprietary data in accordance with Article 26 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283. The data requested by the applicant to be protected consist of the certificates of analysis (Annex A in the compositional data).

Methodologies

The assessment follows the methodology set out in the EFSA guidance on NF applications (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021c) and the principles described in the relevant existing guidance documents from the EFSA Scientific Committee. The legal provisions for the assessment are laid down in Article 11 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 and in Article 7 of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2469.

This assessment concerns only the risks that might be associated with the consumption of the NF under the proposed conditions of use and is not an assessment of the efficacy of the NF with regard to any claimed benefit.

In the context of this opinion, EFSA's definition of dietary fibre (i.e. non‐digestible carbohydrates plus lignin; EFSA NDA Panel, 2010) does not reflect the additional requirement of having a beneficial physiological effect demonstrated by generally accepted scientific evidence laid down in Annex I of Regulation (EC) 1169/2011 10 for:

edible carbohydrate polymers which have been obtained from food raw material by physical, enzymatic or chemical means, and

edible carbohydrate polymers which have been obtained from food raw material by physical, enzymatic or chemical means, and

It is out of the scope of this opinion to establish whether the fraction of non‐digestible carbohydrates present in the NF meets the legal definition of dietary fibre in the EU or not.

ASSESSMENT

Introduction

This opinion addressed the applicant's request to extend the use of the NF as a food ingredient in additional food categories (non‐alcoholic beverages, confectionery, chewing gum, cocoa and chocolate products and protein products).

Identity of the NF

The NF, which is the subject of this request for extension of use, is mainly composed of galacto‐oligosaccharides (GOS). GOS are oligosaccharides containing 2–9 galactosyl units linked to a terminal glucose via β‐glycosidic bonds such as β (1 → 2), β (1 → 3), β (1 → 4) or β (1 → 6). GOS are considered dietary fibre, in accordance with the definition that encompasses non‐digestible carbohydrates (EFSA NDA Panel, 2010).

The NF is produced from milk‐derived lactose by an enzymatic process using β‐galactosidases from microorganisms as specified in the Union list. The applicant stated that no changes have been introduced in the production process as defined in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2470. 11

History of use of the NF and/or of its source

History of use of the source

As previously reported by EFSA (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021a, 2021b, 2022), GOS are naturally occurring in milk‐based products. Fermented milk products (such as yoghurts) can contain a significant amount of GOS due to the enzymatic activity of microbial β‐galactosidases on lactose (Toba et al., 1982; Villaluenga‐Martinez et al., 2008). Lactose‐free milks can also contain GOS as a result of lactose hydrolysis (Ruiz‐Matute et al., 2012).

History of use of the NF

GOS have been authorised 13 to be placed on the EU market as NF to be used in a number of foods (e.g. milk drinks, yoghurt, dairy desserts, cereals, cheese), food supplements, baby foods, infant formula and follow‐on formula.

As previously reported by EFSA (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021a, 2021b, 2022), GOS are permitted for use in foods, food supplements, infant formulae in several countries (e.g. Australia, New Zealand, the United States).

The NF is also authorised for use in food supplements, at a maximum daily intake of 16.2 g of GOS, for individuals aged 3 years and above. The exposure to GOS from the combined intake of the NF from the uses as ingredient (new and authorised uses at the highest P95) and food supplements ranges from 55 g GOS per day to 65 g GOS per day. The Panel considers that it is very unlikely that individuals consuming high amounts of GOS in foods would simultaneously consume food supplements containing the NF. Furthermore, high intakes of fermentable fibres such as GOS may induce gastrointestinal symptoms which limit their acceptability and intake (see Section 3.7).

Nutritional information

GOS are a type of dietary fibre. The term dietary fibre collectively describes non‐digestible carbohydrates that are resistant to hydrolysis and absorption in the small intestine and enter the colon substantially unmodified. Non‐starch polysaccharides, the main constituents of dietary fibre, are heterogeneous in their chemical composition and physicochemical properties (e.g. solubility, viscosity, fermentability), characteristics that determine their functional (e.g. laxation) and undesirable (e.g. bloating, flatulence, borborygmi/rumbling) gastrointestinal (GI) effects (EFSA NDA Panel, 2010).

Supplemental intakes of dietary fibre (i.e. added to foods or consumed as food supplements) may lead to undesirable GI symptoms, but the amount of supplemental fibre at which these effects occur highly depends on the type of fibre (e.g. the extent to which it is fermented in the colon), the pattern of consumption (e.g. as a single dose or throughout the day), and host factors (e.g. diet, intestinal microbiota composition, genetics and health status), with high interindividual variability. Inulin‐type fructans (ITFs) and GOS, as fermentable fibres, can induce flatulence and bloating. Supplemental intakes of GOS of 20 g/day have been shown to be well tolerated by healthy adults in human intervention studies (Mysonhimer & Holscher, 2022). Tolerance to supplemental fermentable fibre has been shown to increase over time, whereas the persistence of undesirable GI effects limits acceptability, and thus intake (Dinleyici et al., 2025; Holscher et al., 2022).

No tolerable upper intake level (UL) for dietary fibre, or for a specific type of fibre, has been set. Adequate intakes (AI) for dietary fibre in mixed diets, but not for specific fibre types, have been established for all population groups (except infants) based on bowel function (i.e. laxation) (EFSA NDA Panel, 2010). Average intakes of dietary fibre in most adult populations and in half of the child population in Europe are below the AI for the respective age group (EFSA NDA Panel, 2022).

It is estimated that a breastfed infant would consume between 4 and 12 g/day of non‐digestible oligosaccharides during the first half year of life. The addition of non‐digestible oligosaccharides to infant formula and follow‐on formula is regulated as in Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2016/127. 16

The panel does not expect that the proposed new uses of this non‐digestible oligosaccharide are nutritionally disadvantageous.

Toxicological information

The applicant conducted a comprehensive literature search (dated: January 2025) to identify new studies (published after the EFSA safety assessment in 2021a) on GOS. The full search strategy, databases and results were provided. No new genotoxicity, toxicity (subchronic, chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity), reproductive and developmental toxicity studies were identified.

Human data

The literature search provided by the applicant identified human intervention studies with GOS which were published since the last EFSA opinion (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021a).

Two studies were conducted in infants. Preterm infants were administered GOS via infant formula (GOS supplemented formula not characterised) for up to 28 days, and no adverse events were reported (Utomo et al., 2021). In healthy term infants, the administration of a short‐chain GOS and long‐chain FOS mix (9:1 ratio) at 0.4 g/100 mL of infant formula for 3 months and at 0.03 g/g of infant formula for 11 months was well tolerated (Bellaiche et al., 2023; Piloquet et al., 2024).

The applicant identified four human intervention studies in adults, two of which were not considered further: one study (Schaafsma et al., 2022) was available only as an abstract, while the other (Buhaș et al., 2023) used a GOS/FOS/XOS mixture in combination with a mixture of five bacterial strains as compared to no intervention, so that the design did not allow drawing conclusions about the effects of GOS alone on the endpoints measured. The characteristics of the remaining two studies identified by the applicant (Angima et al., 2025; Azcarate‐Peril et al., 2017) and of two additional studies identified by EFSA (Looijesteijn et al., 2024; Schoemaker et al., 2022) are briefly described below.

These four intervention studies investigated the effects of GOS supplementation in adult populations. Azcarate‐Peril et al. (2017) conducted a randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial in which 85 lactose‐intolerant subjects were randomised (2:1 ratio) to receive either increasing doses of purified GOS (RP‐G28, > 95% purity) from 1.5 to 15 g/day over 36 days, or placebo, followed by a 30‐day dairy reintroduction period. Schoemaker et al. (2022) randomised 132 adults (18–59 years) with self‐reported constipation to consume either 5.5 g (n = 45) or 11 g (n = 44) of GOS daily, or maltodextrin (n = 43; control), for 3 weeks. Looijesteijn et al. (2024) examined the effects of daily supplementation with 1.3 g (n = 44) or 2.0 g GOS (n = 44) for 3 weeks in 88 healthy women (42–70 years), following a 3‐week control period. GOS were consumed as a single daily dose in these three studies. Angima et al. (2025) provided 14 lactose‐intolerant adults with either 5.2 g of GOS powder twice daily (n = 7), resulting in a total daily intake of 7.6 g GOS and 2.2 g lactose, or 1 g lactose (n = 7, control) for 3 weeks, followed by a 3‐week washout period. In these four studies, GOS supplementation was generally well tolerated with no serious adverse events reported, though mild and transient gastrointestinal symptoms such as bloating were occasionally noted.

The panel notes that these studies in adults have tested daily doses of GOS below the anticipated intakes of the NF under the proposed uses and use levels, and that no studies in children have become available. The panel also notes that human intervention studies using higher supplemental daily doses of GOS have shown good tolerance in adults at 20 g/day (Section 3.7). No adverse effects other than undesirable GI symptoms have been reported at higher doses.

DISCUSSION

The NF is mainly composed of GOS and it is produced from milk‐derived lactose by an enzymatic process. GOS are considered dietary fibre, in accordance with the definition that encompasses non‐digestible carbohydrates.

GOS is authorised as NF to be used in a number of foods, food supplements and infant formula and follow‐on formula. This opinion addresses the applicant's request to extend the use of the NF as a food ingredient in additional food categories (non‐alcoholic beverages, confectionery, chewing gum, cocoa and chocolate products and protein products). As compared to the exposure to GOS from the currently authorised food uses (at the highest P95), the proposed extension of use is expected to result in an increase in intake of GOS of up to 17%.

No UL for dietary fibre, or for a specific type of fibre, has been set (EFSA NDA Panel, 2010) and, apart from undesirable gastrointestinal effects (e.g. bloating, flatulence, borborygmi/rumbling), which in general may be related to high intakes of fermentable fibre, no other adverse effects have been reported. The occurrence of such gastrointestinal symptoms related to the intake of fermentable fibre differs among individuals and may limit its intake.

The other constituents present in the NF (e.g. lactose, glucose) are normal components of the habitual diet.

The panel considers that the proposed extension of the use of NF, mainly composed of galacto‐oligosaccharides, in additional food categories does not raise safety concerns.

CONCLUSION

The panel concludes that the NF, mainly composed of galacto‐oligosaccharides, is safe under the proposed extension of use.
