The association between the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) and postoperative outcomes after hip fracture in older adults: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Kangzu Peng, Yimin Chen, Yufeng Ge, Yixiao Chen, Qingnan Sun, Gang Liu, Xinbao Wu, Minghui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe GNRI is an objective nutritional status screening tool. Introduction

In 2019, the global prevalence of hip fractures among individuals aged 55 years and older was reported at 1191.39 per 100,000 population, a figure that is expected to have risen with the accelerating trend of global aging [1, 2]. Hip fracture is a common and severe injury in older adults, especially among individuals with osteoporosis. Notably, more than half of male patients with hip fractures and 39.2% of female patients were either deceased or institutionalized within two years post-fracture, imposing a considerable psychological burden on patients or their families and a substantial economic burden on national healthcare systems [3].

Growing evidence indicates that malnutrition is an independent predictor of 12-month mortality and postoperative complications, including delirium and delayed mobility recovery [4–6]. Importantly, timely nutritional interventions have been shown to significantly improve clinical outcomes [7]. As the initial step in nutritional care pathways, nutritional screening plays a pivotal role in identifying individuals at risk, thereby enabling appropriate assessment and intervention. Screening tools should be rapid, user-friendly, and feasible for use by healthcare workers with varying levels of training [8]. To date, several tools for screening the risk of protein–energy malnutrition have been recommended in various clinical guidelines, including Mini Nutrition Assessment (MNA) [9], Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) [10], Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 (NRS 2002) [11] and Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) [12]. However, there is no universally accepted gold standard for screening nutritional risk in older adults. Furthermore, these tools vary in applicability across settings—some are better suited for community-dwelling individuals than for hospitalized patients. Certain tools are overly subjective, others are complex, and many require biochemical indicators that may be difficult to obtain in routine clinical practice.

The GNRI, developed by Bouillanne [13], is a simplified tool for perioperative nutritional screening based on preoperative serum albumin (Alb), height and body weight. It has been validated as a reliable predictor of postoperative mortality and infectious complications—such as sepsis and pneumonia—in geriatric trauma patients [14]. Moreover, the GNRI has been investigated across a wide range of clinical populations, including those with diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and stroke [15–19]. It has also demonstrated significant correlation with the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria, a consensus-based diagnostic framework endorsed by four major international nutrition societies [20]. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that serum albumin, a key component of the GNRI, reflects not only nutritional status but also inflammatory burden and acute physiological stress especially in the setting of acute trauma such as hip fracture [21]. Therefore, in the context of acute hip fracture, the GNRI may be more appropriately interpreted as a clinically useful tool for risk stratification, rather than a pure nutritional screening measure.

Despite its simplicity and broad applicability, only several studies have examined the association between GNRI and short-term outcomes after hip fracture [14, 22–25]. Evidence from prospective studies exploring the prognostic value of the GNRI in predicting mortality, recovery of pre-fracture mobility and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) simultaneously in Chinese older hip fracture patients with long-term follow-up remains limited.

The present study aimed to investigate the association between admission GNRI and long-term postoperative outcomes—including mortality, functional status, and HRQoL among older patients undergoing surgical treatment for hip fractures.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data were derived from the Geriatric Hip Fracture Database, which was established prospectively between November 2018 and November 2019, concurrent with the implementation of a co-managed orthogeriatric care model at Beijing Jishuitan Hospital in China. The database was originally developed as part of a prospective observational study investigating the impact of the co-managed orthogeriatric care model on outcomes of older patients with hip fractures in China (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03184896, Registration Date (First Posted): 2017-06-14), which had received approval from the Biomedical Ethics Committee at Beijing Jishuitan Hospital (201807-11). The investigators performed routine follow-up at 1 month, 3 months, 12 months, 3 years and 5 years postoperatively through telephone interviews or outpatient reviews. Data were collected using a tablet-based Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system and the completeness and consistency of data were routinely checked by trained research staff. The database has been used by numerous orthopedic researchers [26–30].

Study design and populations

We performed a post-hoc analysis of our previous prospective observational study, which examined the impact of a co-managed orthogeriatric care model for hip fractures on older patients in China [31]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Our study screened a total of 1092 patients aged 65 and older who had received surgery for hip fracture within 3 weeks after injury from November 2018 to November 2019. The clinical approach has been previously described. Patients who fulfilled the specified exclusion criteria would be excluded during the screening process: [1] those who failed to obtain laboratory examination data to calculate GNRI [2] those with pathological fractures or periprosthetic fractures and [3] those with terminal malignancies. According to the exclusion criteria, 145 patients were ruled out. A total of 947 eligible patients were divided into four groups based on GNRI quartiles at admission (Q1 with GNRI ≤ 96; Q2 with 96 < GNRI ≤ 101; Q3 with 101 < GNRI ≤ 104; Q4 with GNRI > 104).

General information

A comprehensive set of demographic and perioperative variables was prospectively collected. Demographic information included age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), smoking and alcohol habits, educational attainment, and living situation (e.g., living alone or being homebound). Education was categorized into five levels, ranging from no formal education to university degree or higher.

Baseline comorbidities were systematically recorded, including hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, depression, Parkinson’s disease as well as cognitive and visual impairments. Cardiac function was evaluated by preoperative echocardiography to assess left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was used to quantify overall health status.

Perioperative data included fracture classification, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA), anesthesia type, surgical method, time to surgery (TTS), length of hospital stay (LOS), and receipt of postoperative physiotherapy. Laboratory parameters obtained at admission encompassed blood routine and blood biochemical tests, such as calcium and hemoglobin. Surgical treatment was determined based on fracture characteristics and categorized as either internal fixation or arthroplasty.

Nutritional status

The GNRI was calculated using the formula: GNRI = [1.489 × Albumin (g/L)] + [41.7 × (current body weight/ideal weight)]. Ideal body weight was determined using the Lorentz equations: for men, ideal weight = H − 100 - [(H − 50)/4]; for women, ideal weight = H − 100 - [(H − 150)/2.5], where H represents height in centimeters and ideal weight in kilograms. For older adults, due to the challenges in accurately measuring true standing height, we employed knee height (KH) as a proxy for estimating actual height, which means for men, H = (2.02 × KH) - (0.04 × age) + 64.19; for women, H = (1.83 × KH) - (0.24 × age) + 84.88.

Outcome

The primary outcome was 3-year all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included recovery of pre-fracture functional status, assessed using the Fracture Mobility Score (FMS), and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), assessed using EQ-5D utility scores at each follow-up time point.

HRQoL was measured using the EQ-5D-5 L instrument [32], which evaluates five health domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each domain is scored on a five-point scale, ranging from no problems to extreme problems. Responses were converted into a utility score using a validated value set specific to the Chinese population [33].

Functional mobility was assessed by the Fracture Mobility Score (FMS), which is utilized by the National Hip Fracture Database (NHFD), the Scottish Hip Fracture Audit and the Alters Trauma Register DGU and recommended by Fragility Fracture Network. It has been validated against the Parker Mobility Score [34]. The FMS categorizes mobility into five levels: [1] freely mobile without aids; [2] mobile outdoors with one aid; [3] mobile outdoors with two aids or a frame; [4] some indoor mobility but never goes outside without help; [5] no functional mobility (non-ambulatory) [35].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on data distribution. Baseline tables were intended to describe the study population rather than to define the set of confounders for adjusted analyses. Covariates included in multivariable models were selected based on a prior clinical relevance and existing literatures, supplemented by univariable analyses to identify additional potential confounders. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between GNRI and 3-year all-cause mortality, both with and without adjustment for potential confounders. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to illustrate differences in survival across GNRI quartiles. Logistic regression analyses were employed to compare the recovery of mobility at each follow-up time point among groups, again with and without adjustment for confounding variables. To assess differences in EQ-5D utility scores across GNRI quartiles over time, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied. Additionally, multivariable linear regression models, adjusting for all relevant covariates, were used to evaluate the relationship between baseline GNRI and EQ-5D utility scores at each individual follow-up. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the discriminative ability of GNRI for predicting 3-year mortality. The optimal cutoff value was identified using the Youden index.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.R-project.org). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Population and baseline characteristics

In this study, a total of 947 eligible patients who underwent surgery for hip fracture were included for analysis. (Fig. 1.) Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 80 ± 7.8 years and 28% of them were men. Compared with the Q1 group, patients in Q2–Q4 were generally younger and had higher body mass index (BMI). They were more likely to suffer femoral neck fractures (FNFs) and to be freely mobile without walking aids before injuries, but less likely to be housebound or cognitively impaired. In addition, Q2–Q4 patients had a higher prevalence of diabetes and were less likely to receive internal fixation. Conversely, patients in Q1 exhibited higher ASA scores, more severe inflammatory responses, and lower levels of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and hemoglobin, indicating a poorer overall physiological condition.

Associations between GNRI and over-all survival

At the end of the follow-up, 193 (20%) patients had died, and mortality rates were significantly different between the four groups (39% [92/238] in Q1; 22% [53/236] in Q2; 14% [33/235] in Q3; 6.3% [15/238] in Q4; respectively). Kaplan–Meier curves showed a lower survival probability in Q1 compared to Q2, Q3, and Q4 (Fig. 2 log-rank test p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Model 2, progressively lower mortality risks were observed with increasing quartiles relative to Q1. The adjusted HRs (95% CI) were 0.71 (0.48–1.05) for Q2, 0.48 (0.30–0.79) for Q3 and 0.26 (0.14–0.50) for Q4. Consistently, compared with participants with lower GNRI (Quartile 1, ≤ 96), those with higher GNRI (Quartile 2–4, > 96) were associated with a significantly lower mortality risk (HR = 0.58, 95%CI 0.40–0.85, p = 0.005). (Table 2.) Multivariable-adjusted RCS suggested a linear association between admission GNRI and 3-year mortality (P for non-linearity > 0.05), indicating that higher GNRI values were associated with a continuous decline in mortality risk. (Fig. 3.)

Predictive value of GNRI for 3-year mortality and survival analysis

ROC analysis of the GNRI identified an optimal cutoff value of 98 for 3-year mortality, with a sensitivity of 0.617, specificity of 0.714 and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.726 (95% CI 0.69–0.77, p < 0.001). (Supplement Fig. 1A) The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed a significantly lower survival probability in patients with a GNRI of 98 or lower (Lower group) compared with those with a GNRI above 98 (Higher group) (p < 0.001). (Supplement Fig. 1B) Consistently, patients in the higher GNRI group (defined by the dichotomized cutoff) were associated with a significantly lower risk of mortality compared with those in the lower GNRI group (HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.32–0.68, p < 0.001). (Table 2.)

Subgroup analysis and robustness analyses

We conducted subgroup analyses by stratifying the data according to various factors, including age, gender, BMI, inflammation, hemoglobin, smoking habits, pre-fracture functional status, diabetes, delayed LOS and time to surgery. (Fig. 4.) No significant interaction was observed between GNRI and these variables with respect to 3-year all-cause mortality. (all P for interaction > 0.05) As a sensitivity analysis, we further conducted propensity score matching (PSM) followed by robust cox proportional hazards regression in Model 3, which showed that higher GNRI remained associated with a lower risk of mortality, both in the Q1 vs. Q2–4 comparison (HR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.34–0.91, p = 0.019) and in the dichotomized GNRI analysis (HR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.36–0.85, p = 0.007). Secondary Outcome

Patients in higher GNRI quartiles showed a tendency toward higher odds of regaining pre-fracture mobility at both 1-year and 3-year follow-ups. In the fully adjusted Model 2, individuals in Q3 (OR = 1.86, 95% CI 1.15–3.03, P = 0.012) and Q4 (OR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.03–2.83, P = 0.037) were more likely to recover to their pre-injury functional status at 1 year. However, this association persisted only in Q4 (OR = 1.77, 95% CI 1.02–3.07, P = 0.042; Table 3.) at 3-year follow-up. The EQ-5D utility demonstrated a consistent increase during the initial three follow-up periods. (Fig. 5A) Patients in Q1 consistently exhibited lower EQ-5D utility values at the 30-day, 120-day and 3-year follow-up intervals compared with those in Q3 and Q4. Moreover, solely at the 1-year follow-up, patients’ EQ-5D utility values in Q1 were lower than any other group (Fig. 4A, Supplement Table 1) In multivariable linear regression analyses, admission GNRI was positively associated with EQ-5D scores at all follow-up assessments. (Fig. 5B)

Discussion

This study demonstrates a consistent and clinically relevant association between admission GNRI and 3-year all-cause mortality as well as long-term functional outcomes among older patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. Higher GNRI values were associated with a lower risk of 3-year mortality, better recovery of pre-fracture mobility, and higher health-related quality of life (HRQoL) scores. Specifically, patients in higher GNRI quartiles had greater 3-year survival probabilities and were more likely to regain pre-injury mobility during long-term follow-up. Additionally, individuals with lower GNRI values exhibited significantly poorer quality of life across nearly all follow-up intervals. A consistent positive association was observed between admission GNRI and EQ-5D utility scores at all four follow-up time points. These findings suggest that admission GNRI may serve as a practical risk stratification indicator for long-term outcomes in older patients with hip fractures.

Malnutrition is prevalent among older adults, affecting nearly half of the geriatric population [36]. It is consistently associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including increased risks of delirium, mortality, institutionalization, impaired mobility, and delayed wound healing [5, 6, 33, 37, 38]. In the context of hip fracture, previous studies have reported associations between GNRI and short-term postoperative mortality as well as functional outcomes. However, data simultaneously addressing long-term survival, recovery of pre-injury functional status, and health-related quality of life remain unclear.

Several studies have validated GNRI as a predictor of short-term postoperative mortality. In a retrospective cohort study including 1040 patients, Funahashi et al. [22] reported that a GNRI score below 75.4 at admission was significantly associated with a higher risk of 30-day postoperative mortality (OR = 22.99, 95%CI 7.55–78.05; P < 0.001) compared to those with a higher GNRI score. Kotera et al. [23] further demonstrated that a GNRI < 82 (indicating severe nutritional risk) was associated with a significantly elevated 180-day mortality rate (14.4%) compared to patients with normal nutritional status (GNRI ≥ 99; 0.8%). In their studies, GNRI showed an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.811 for 30-day mortality and 0.74 for predicting 180-day mortality, respectively, suggesting moderate to good predictive performance [22, 23]. Similar findings were confirmed in a study using propensity score-matched cohorts [39]. Conversely, a separate PSM-based study suggested that GNRI may not be significantly associated with postoperative survival in older hip fracture patients [25]. This discrepancy may be partly attributable to differences in study design, sample size, follow-up duration and statistical analysis. Nonetheless, a recent meta-analysis concluded that patients with low GNRI had a markedly higher risk of postoperative mortality than those with high GNRI (OR: 3.12 95%CI: 1.47, 6.61 I2 = 87% p = 0.003) [40]. While these prior investigations focused on short-term endpoints, our prospective cohort extended the follow-up duration to 3 years, offering new insights into the long-term prognostic value of GNRI. Our study identified an optimal GNRI cut-off value of 98 for predicting 3-year mortality; however, the corresponding sensitivity and specificity indicate only moderate discriminative performance. This suggests that GNRI should not be interpreted as a stand-alone decision-making threshold, but rather as a screening or risk stratification tool to help identify older patients with hip fracture who are at higher risk of adverse outcomes. However, GNRI is simple, inexpensive and readily available at admission, relying solely on routinely collected anthropometric and laboratory parameters. These features make GNRI a pragmatic tool for early risk assessment in real-world clinical settings. Subgroup and robustness analyses in the present study were conducted in an exploratory way to examine the consistency of the observed associations between GNRI and 3-year mortality. Although no significant interaction was observed across key patient subgroups, the interaction findings should be interpreted with caution.

Regaining pre-injury function is generally considered indicative of a favorable prognosis. Although definitions of functional outcomes vary across studies, our findings regarding the association between GNRI and postoperative functional recovery are broadly consistent with previous research. For instance, Hao et al. [24] reported that vitamin D deficiency (< 12 ng/mL) was significantly associated with impaired mobility, while Inoue et al. [41] observed an association between GNRI and postoperative walking speed during acute hospitalization phase, despite no significant relationship with motor-FIM scores. Similarly, Yokoyama et al. [42] demonstrated that poor nutritional status (GNRI < 92) was associated with adverse postoperative functional outcomes, including inability to walk at discharge and a higher prevalence of walking disability at 6 months. In a cohort of 155 hip fracture patients with comorbid heart failure, Tamamura et al. [43] further suggested that malnutrition (GNRI < 92) may further impaired in-hospital functional recovery, suggesting an additive negative effect. However, these prior studies primarily focused on short-term functional endpoints and exhibited substantial heterogeneity in outcome definitions, timing of assessment and study populations. In contrast, our study extends existing evidence by showing that GNRI-defined poor nutritional status (Q1) is associated with lower ambulatory ability at both 1-year and 3-year follow-up, suggesting that the association between GNRI and impaired mobility may persist beyond the early postoperative period, contributing to sustained functional limitations.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) refers to an individual’s perceived well-being in relation to physical, psychological, and social domains, as influenced by illness or injury, medical interventions, aging, and sociocultural changes. It provides a more comprehensive assessment of overall health status than clinical indicators alone. A previous study using the EQ-5D instrument to evaluate QoL in older patients with hip fractures found that HRQoL did not return to pre-injury levels even after treatment [44]. In addition, patients receiving multidisciplinary nutritional care have been reported to experience smaller short-term declines in EQ-5D index scores compared with controls (P = 0.004) [45]. While several studies have examined the effects of surgical strategies on EQ-5D outcomes [46, 47], few have specifically explored the association between preoperative nutritional status as measured by the GNRI and HRQoL. Chen et al. [26] found that patients with a preoperative PNI > 46.8 exhibited significantly higher EQ-5D utility scores at 30 days postoperatively. However, this difference did not persist at later follow-up points. In our study, patients at risk of malnutrition, as indicated by lower GNRI values, were consistently associated with lower EQ-5D utility scores across all follow-up intervals. This discrepancy may be partly attributable to differences between GNRI and PNI in what they capture clinically. Although statistically significant, the observed differences were modest in magnitude, and their clinical relevance should be interpreted with caution. These findings suggest that admission GNRI may provide useful information for long-term risk stratification of HRQoL after hip fracture surgery in older adults.

The underlying mechanism of the association between GNRI and long-term mortality, postoperative mobility and HRQoL remain incompletely understood. GNRI integrates serum albumin and the ratio of actual to ideal body weight and is therefore thought to reflect protein reserve, systemic inflammatory burden and overall energy balance. Hypoalbuminemia may represent not only inadequate nutritional intake but negative acute phase response, reflecting underlying inflammation and physiological stress [21]. Such inflammatory states have been associated with increased vulnerability to postoperative complications, which could plausibly contribute to higher mortality risk and poorer long-term HRQoL [48, 49]. In addition, insufficient protein and caloric intake may contribute to loss of muscle mass and strength, impaired bone healing and accelerated sarcopenia, thereby potentially delaying functional recovery after hip fracture [50–52].

This study has some limitations and thus the results may be regarded as preliminary and require further validation. First, this study represents a post-hoc analysis of a prospective observational cohort, which inherently limits causal inference. Although multivariable adjustment, propensity score matching, and subgroup analyses were performed to address confounding, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered as causal effects. Second, the possibility of chance findings due to multiple testing cannot be excluded, and the absence of statistically significant interactions may reflect limited statistical power rather than true effect homogeneity. Third, nutritional status was assessed only at admission, and postoperative changes or the effects of nutritional interventions were not captured, potentially underestimating the dynamic relationship between nutrition and long-term outcomes. Finally, as this was a single-center study conducted in a Chinese population, the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings and ethnic groups may be limited.

Conclusion

In conclusion, admission GNRI appears to be associated with long-term mortality, functional recovery, and health-related quality of life in older patients with hip fractures. As a simple index derived from routinely available clinical parameters, GNRI may provide useful information for early risk stratification in this population. Although its discriminative performance is moderate, incorporation of GNRI into perioperative screening could potentially help identify patients at higher risk who may benefit from closer monitoring, individualized postoperative care, and targeted rehabilitation strategies. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate its clinical utility and to determine how GNRI may be optimally integrated into comprehensive geriatric fracture care pathways.
