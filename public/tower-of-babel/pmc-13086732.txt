Interactions between bacterial microbiota and protozoan parasites: an intimate relationship
Authors: Raquel Aguilar-Rosales, Alma Reyna Escalona-Montaño, Ana Pamela Gómez-García, Daniel Andrés Sánchez-Almaraz, Martha Legorreta-Herrera, Omar Fernández-Rivera, María Magdalena Aguirre-Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interactions between parasites and the gut microbiota play a key role in immune responses and susceptibility to zoonotic diseases; however, many aspects of how protozoan infections alter microbial diversity and how these changes influence parasite pathogenicity and host defense remain poorly understood. This review integrates recenst studies using murine models of the Muroidea superfamily, highlighting patterns in microbial diversity, functional interactions, and potential immunometabolic mechanisms underlying these relationships. Both consistent findings and discrepancies among studies are identified, reflecting differences in parasite species, experimental models, and infection conditions. Unlike previous work, this review emphasizes the link between microbiota alterations, infection progression, and host responses. Finally, these findings are integrated to provide an interpretive framework that advances our understanding of how the microbiota modulates infection dynamics and identifies key knowledge gaps that warrant further investigation.

Introduction

The term microbiota refers to the community of microorganisms (viruses, bacteria, fungi, parasites, and archaea) that inhabit a specific niche—not only within the human body but also in animals, plants, soil, and aquatic environments. This community includes commensal, opportunistic, and transient microorganisms that play essential roles within their environment. The microbiome encompasses the entirety of these microorganisms along with their genetic material and the metabolites they produce directly or indirectly, or that participate in their production (Marchesi and Ravel 2015). Distinguishing between these concepts is important, as analyses based solely on microbial taxonomic composition may not fully explain host–microorganism interactions. Functional metabolic activities encompassed within the microbiome are increasingly recognized as key determinants of immune modulation and disease outcomes, particularly in the context of parasitic infections.

In humans, the most extensively studied microbiota is bacterial, particularly that of the gastrointestinal tract, followed by the oral cavity, the genitourinary, the respiratory tracts, and the skin. The human gut microbiota comprises approximately 100 trillion microorganisms, predominantly belonging to three phyla: Bacillota (~ 49%), Bacteroidota (~ 38%), and Actinobacteria (~ 5%), along with smaller contributions from other phyla such as Proteobacteria (~ 5%) (Peng et al. 2025). In mice, the gut microbiota mainly includes Bacteroidota (45.77%), Bacillota (44.14%), and Tenericutes (3.12%) (Yang et al. 2019). Findings in mice show that these models are particularly useful for studying how the microbiota interacts with diet, infections, and treatments. Their microbiota is relatively stable and can be modified under controlled laboratory conditions, allowing for the analysis of specific changes and their effects. Although it is important to consider the particularities and limitations of the murine model, its potential to provide valuable insights into microbiota function and dynamics remains undeniable.

Symbiosis refers to direct interactions between organisms of different species that may be beneficial, as in mutualism, or harmful, as in parasitism (Rózsa and Garay 2023). In the host–microbiota relationship, eubiosis describes a balanced state dominated by beneficial commensal microorganisms that support host health (Eckburg et al., n.d.), whereas dysbiosis involves unfavorable alterations in microbial composition and function, including the loss of dominant commensals and the expansion of opportunistic or pathogenic taxa (Gevers et al. 2014). Therefore, understanding microbiota–host interactions requires not only describing microbial composition but also assessing their physiological and clinical consequences.

Intestinal eubiosis is crucial for the development and regulation of the immune system as well as for maintaining host homeostasis. Additionally, the gut microbiota plays a key role in digestion by regulating the metabolism of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) through the fermentation of complex carbohydrates (J.M 2006). SCFAs have been shown to modulate adaptive immunity by influencing the differentiation and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs), T helper cells (Th), and B cells, promoting a balance between inflammatory and tolerogenic responses (Park et al. 2015). They also affect innate immunity by regulating the activity of macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), as well as cytokines production. These effects—mediated by mechanisms such as the activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPRs), including GPR41, GPR43, and GPR109A, and the inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs)—contribute to the maintenance of immune homeostasis and protection against inflammation and autoimmune diseases (Kaisar et al. 2017; Zou et al. 2021). Collectively, these findings underscore the existence of a dynamic balance among the microbiota, host physiology, and diet, which influences the initial colonization, development, and stability of the intestinal ecosystem.

In addition SCFAs, the gut microbiota generates various metabolites that play crucial physiological roles for the host and can indirectly affect parasites. Among these, secondary bile acids result from the transformation of primary bile acids synthesized by the liver; these compounds interact with receptors such as FXR and TGR5 to regulate lipid metabolism, epithelial barrier function, and the differentiation of immune cells, including Treg populations, thus contributing to the maintenance of intestinal and systemic immune homeostasis (Ohno 2021; Yoon et al. 2023). Furthermore, certain tryptophan-derived metabolites (such as indoles and aryl hydrocarbon receptor [AhR] activators) are produced by gut bacteria from dietary amino acids and modulate immune responses while promoting intestinal mucosal integrity through AhR activation in epithelial and innate lymphoid cells, contributing to tolerance and defense against pathogens (Wang et al. 2023).

Similarly, other relevant metabolites are polyamines (such as putrescine, spermidine, and spermine), which participate in cell proliferation, tissue repair, and the regulation of inflammation through effects on macrophages and lymphocytes, influencing both host responses and the availability of ecological niches that can favor or limit parasitic colonization (Yoon et al. 2023).

Various intestinal bacteria possess biosynthetic pathways capable of producing vitamins, which can be absorbed in the intestine and contribute to the host’s nutritional and functional balance. These vitamins act as coenzymes in fundamental metabolic reactions, modulating energy production, immune function, and immune cell differentiation, and in some cases, influencing the microbial ecology itself by favoring commensal species and limiting pathogens (Hossain et al. 2022; Uebanso et al. 2020). Of relevance to parasitic infections, alterations in the gut microbiota—such as those observed during Giardia duodenalis colonization—may reduce the presence of bacterial genes responsible for cobalamin (vitamin B12) biosynthesis, potentially affecting nutrient availability and the host immune response (Guetterman et al. 2022; Mejia et al. 2020).

The relationship between the microbiota and parasites is complex, as parasites establish prolonged interactions with the host and its microbial community, impacting the intestinal and systemic environment in ways that favor their survival and modulate the host’s immune response. These bidirectional interactions can alter the composition, diversity, and function of the intestinal microbiota, influence homeostasis, and shape the host’s response to infections and antiparasitic treatments (Ramírez et al. 2025).

The intestinal microbiota plays a crucial role in the dynamics of intracellular parasitic infections, influencing immune responses and inflammatory processes. Intracellular protozoa can evade host immunity, invade, and replicate within host cells, while competing with gut microorganisms for essential nutrients. These interactions affect both the parasite’s ability to establish and maintain infection and the composition and abundance of the host microbiota (Fadaee et al. 2025; Li et al. 2023). Investigating these relationships in murine models provides valuable insights into disease mechanisms and supports the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

This review addresses intestinal protozoa and hemoparasites of clinical and epidemiological importance, such as Leishmania spp., Toxoplasma gondii, Trypanosoma cruzi, Entamoeba histolytica, as well as Blastocystis spp. and Giardia duodenalis, selected for their prevalence in humans and animals, their relevance in human diseases, and evidence that their infections are associated with specific changes in the microbiota and the host’s immune responses.

Although interactions between the microbiota and parasites have been previously described, it remains unclear how parasitic infections alter microbial diversity and function, and how these changes, in turn, influence parasite pathogenicity and host responses. This review aims to (1) compile recent evidence on the impact of parasitic infections on the gut microbiota in murine models and (2) discuss the implications of these interactions for the development of preventive and therapeutic strategies. To this end, findings from experimental studies and murine databases are integrated to provide a comprehensive view of the microbiota as a modulator of parasitic infection.

Based on this, several bioinformatic resources have been developed to integrate data from multiple studies and compile microbiome analysis results from animal models and parasitic systems. One of the most notable examples is the Murine Microbiome Database (MMDB), a resource specifically focused on murine microbiotas. Initially, the MMDB integrated data from 14 projects, encompassing more than 4,700 species and 1,700 microbial genera derived mainly from fecal samples of healthy mice; its most recent version includes data from 17 projects and a total of 762 samples. One of its most consistent findings is the recurrent presence of members of the Muribaculaceae family as common constituents of the murine intestinal microbiota (Yang et al. 2019). By integrating data from diverse intestinal and fecal samples across multiple studies, the MMDB facilitates cross-study comparisons and the identification of consistent microbial composition patterns, enabling analyses of how factors such as diet, environment, mouse strain, and infection influence microbiota structure and function. Nevertheless, the MMDB remains limited by incomplete coverage of species, sample types, and experimental conditions, underscoring the need to expand and diversify available datasets to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the murine microbiota (Yang et al. 2019).

Another landmark initiative is the Parasite Microbiome Project (PMP), a collaborative effort launched in 2017 to standardize microbiome analysis procedures in parasites and their hosts. The PMP proposes a two-phase approach that includes the integration of data within a centralized framework and the development of experimental models to investigate causal relationships between parasite virulence, disease outcomes, and microbiome composition. Although the PMP does not constitute a traditional database, it has played a critical role in establishing open-access protocols and promoting large-scale comparative analyses across studies and model systems. However, its current coverage of functional interactions and parasite-associated microbial data remains limited, highlighting the need to expand and diversify datasets to strengthen comparative and mechanistic research in this field (Dheilly et al. 2014).

Accordingly, this review is structured to examine how bidirectional interactions between protozoan parasites, the gut microbiota, and host immune responses shape infection outcomes, emphasizing shared mechanisms and model-dependent differences across murine systems.

Bacterial microbiota in models of the superfamily Muroidea

Mice

The murine gut microbiota is dominated by Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Tenericutes (Yang et al. 2019) (Fig. 1). Bacillota are widely distributed along the gastrointestinal tract and are functionally diverse, contributing to butyrate production, stress resistance, and symbiotic host interactions (Van-Wehle and Vital 2024). Within this phylum, Oscillibacter spp. are enriched in the cecum, where their metabolic flexibility allows adaptation to substrates derived from digestion and microbial metabolism (Chung et al. 2020; Jin et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2019). Bacteroidota predominate across intestinal regions due to their capacity to degrade polysaccharides and adapt to gut conditions, while Proteobacteria contribute to redox modulation that supports anaerobic communities (Gryaznova et al. 2022; Vilchez-Vargas et al. 2022). Rats

Rats are widely used to study the relationship between intestinal dysbiosis and disease development. (Li et al. 2017)Their gut microbiota is dominated by Bacteroidota, followed by Bacillota and Proteobacteria. Low-abundance phyla—including Actinobacteria, Saccharibacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Tenericutes—may act as modulators of the ecosystem. Notably, Saccharibacteria (formerly TM7) are obligate epibionts living on host bacteria and are associated with dysbiosis in humans (Chipashvili et al. 2021; Čoklo et al. 2020; Ferrario et al. 2017). Rare phyla such as Verrucomicrobia, Spirochaetae, Fusobacteria, and Elusimicrobia, despite low prevalence (≤ 0.1%), may contribute to microbial balance. Lactate-producing bacteria, including Lactobacillus and Turicibacter, dominate the stomach and small intestine, regulate luminal pH, stimulate mucus and antimicrobial peptide production, and modulate overall microbiota composition (Dicksved et al. 2012; Lynch et al. 2023; Rastogi and Singh 2022).These functional properties may influence host metabolism and susceptibility to pathogens (Čoklo et al. 2020; Ferrario et al. 2017; Lynch et al. 2023). Core large-intestine genera, such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, produce SCFAs, contributing to pH regulation, immune modulation, and epithelial barrier maintenance(Čoklo et al. 2020; Ferrario et al. 2017; X. Gu et al. 2022; D. Li et al. 2017).

Hamsters

The Syrian hamster’s gut microbiota comprises Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Tenericutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Spirochaetae, and Deferribacteres, closely resembling that of humans and mice. Clostridium spp. is highly prevalent in the intestinal environment (Gu et al. 2015). Unlike mice, hamsters sustain prolonged infections due to limited immune control, producing pathophysiological alterations and lesions resembling those of natural hosts. This makes them particularly useful for studying host–protozoan interactions, such as visceral Leishmania infections (Jiménez-Antón et al. 2019). Their microbiota composition and prolonged pathogen exposure allow realistic analyses of microbial contributions to infection dynamics and host immune responses.

Relationship between the microbiota and intestinal protozoa

Within this framework, intestinal protozoa represent a relevant model to examine how microbiota-driven immune modulation influences infection susceptibility and disease progression.

The intestinal microbiota plays a central role in shaping mucosal immune responses and inflammatory processes through its effects on innate and adaptive immune cells, the production of bioactive metabolites, and the maintenance of epithelial barrier integrity. These mechanisms collectively influence host susceptibility to and the progression of infections caused by intestinal protozoa, including Giardia spp., Entamoeba histolytica, and Cryptosporidium spp., by modulating the magnitude and quality of the host immune response (Partida-Rodríguez et al. 2017).

Certain intestinal protozoa can further modulate host immune pathways by interfering with antigen presentation and T-cell activation, allowing them to establish infection within specific cellular or mucosal niches without complete immune evasion (Ulusan Bagci and Caner 2022). In parallel, protozoa–microbiota interactions involve competition for nutrients in the intestinal lumen and mucosa, as well as parasite-induced modifications of the local microenvironment, which can either facilitate or restrict parasite colonization while reshaping the composition and abundance of commensal bacterial communities (Horrocks et al. 2023).

Together, these bidirectional interactions between intestinal protozoa, the gut microbiota, and host immunity provide a mechanistic framework for understanding disease pathogenesis and inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies against protozoal intestinal infections (Grondin et al. 2024).

Microbiota associated with intracellular parasite infections

Leishmania spp.

Leishmania spp. are obligate intracellular protozoa that infect cells of the mononuclear phagocytic system and cause leishmaniasis, a disease with diverse clinical manifestations depending on the infecting species. Transmission occurs through the bite of infected sandflies of the genera Phlebotomus and Lutzomyia (Alvar et al. 2012; Arenas et al. 2017). In murine models, research has primarily focused on cutaneous and visceral forms of the disease (Abadías-Granado et al. 2021; Costa et al. 2023).

Although a limited number of studies have explored potential interactions between Leishmania infection and the intestinal microbiota, the available evidence remains scarce and inconsistent, particularly in rodent models. Consequently, the role of the gut microbiota in modulating Leishmania infection outcomes cannot yet be conclusively established. (Fig. 2)

Additional studies indicate that enzymatic factors such as SOD3 can suppress early cellular immune responses during parasitic infections, highlighting the complex regulation of host defenses by both microbial and host-derived mediators (Li et al. 2024).

Trypanosoma cruzi

Trypanosoma cruzi is a flagellated protozoan with a complex life cycle involving triatomine insect vectors and mammalian hosts, and is the etiological agent of Chagas disease. Transmission occurs mainly through contact with infected feces of triatomine insects, although oral, congenital, transfusional, and transplant-associated routes have also been described. (Castañeda et al. 2023; Killets et al. 2025). In murine models, infection is characterized by an acute inflammatory phase followed by chronic persistence.

Microbiota and immune responses during T. cruzi infection

Evidence linking T. cruzi infection to alterations in the gut microbiota remains scarce. A single study in BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice reported changes in microbial diversity and shifts in dominant taxa 16 days post-infection, suggesting that infection may influence microbiota composition (Castañeda et al. 2023) (Fig. 5). Experimental evidence nevertheless indicates that microbiota composition can modulate immune responses during T. cruzi infection. Germ-free mice monoassociated with specific bacterial species showed differential immune polarization, with most bacteria promoting a protective Th1 response and nitric oxide production, whereas Enterococcus faecalis induced IL-10 production and a more permissive immune profile (Duarte et al. 2005). During acute infection, Th1 responses—particularly IFN-γ–mediated macrophage activation—are essential for parasite control, whereas Th2-biased responses are associated with increased susceptibility.

Overall, although current evidence is limited, available studies suggest that the gut microbiota may influence immune polarization and disease outcome during T. cruzi infection. Further research is required to clarify whether microbiota alterations are a cause or consequence of infection and to evaluate their potential as therapeutic targets.

Toxoplasma gondii

Toxoplasmosis is a parasitic zoonosis caused by Toxoplasma gondii, an obligate intracellular protozoan of the coccidian type. This parasite exhibits two main phases in its life cycle: a sexual phase, which occurs in the intestinal epithelium of cats, and an asexual phase, which takes place extraintestinally in intermediate hosts such as mammals and birds. Its life cycle includes five developmental stages: tachyzoite, bradyzoite, oocyst, cyst, and sporozoite (Halonen and Weiss 2013).

T. gondii infection is primarily acquired through the ingestion of food or water contaminated with oocysts excreted by infected cats, or through the consumption of raw or undercooked meat containing tissue cysts (Áurea Pereira 2002; Montoya and Liesenfeld 2004). Cats and other felids are the main sources of oocyst production—an environmentally resistant form of the parasite found in the feces of infected animals. Once ingested, these oocysts release tachyzoites. Bradyzoites, on the other hand, are mainly located in tissue cysts within the muscles and brain of the intermediate host. Tachyzoites are the most actively replicating form of the parasite, rapidly disseminating throughout the host organism and capable of transmitting infection to another host—for instance, through laboratory accidents or blood transfusions (Halonen and Weiss 2013).

Although many mice infection models involve systemic (e.g., intraperitoneal) inoculation rather than direct intestinal exposure, substantial evidence indicates that systemic inflammatory mediators can dysregulate gut barrier integrity and thereby modulate the intestinal microbiota. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β have been shown to directly increase intestinal epithelial permeability by altering tight junction dynamics via myosin light chain–dependent mechanisms, leading to disassembly of tight junction proteins and increased paracellular flux across the epithelium (e.g., TNF-α and IFN-γ both potentiate barrier disruption) (Meyer et al. 2023). In clinical contexts, elevated systemic cytokines including IL-6 and IFN-γ correlate with compromised gut barrier markers (e.g., zonulin) and dysbiotic microbial signatures, suggesting that systemic inflammation and barrier loss can drive microbiota alterations even in the absence of direct intestinal infection (Souza et al. 2025). These findings reinforce the notion that systemic immune responses induced by parasites could indirectly reshape the gut microbial ecosystem and underscore the importance of oral infection models for capturing direct parasite–microbiota interactions rather than changes secondary to systemic inflammation.

Therefore, while these studies provide relevant contextual information, they do not allow direct conclusions regarding intestinal microbiota–T. gondii interactions, underscoring the importance of oral infection models for evaluating biologically meaningful host–microbiota–parasite relationships.

Microbiota and immune response in T. gondii Infection

In contrast to systemic infection models, mechanistic insights into the interaction between T. gondii, intestinal immunity, and the gut microbiota largely derive from oral infection studies, which allow direct parasite–epithelium–microbiota interactions. In this context, interleukin-22 (IL-22) has emerged as a key regulator of intestinal homeostasis during T. gondii infection. IL-22 promotes mucin expression and induces antimicrobial peptides such as Reg3β and Reg3γ via STAT3 signaling, thereby contributing to epithelial regeneration and microbiota containment (Couturier-Maillard et al. 2018).

Using orally infected IL-22-deficient C57BL/6 and CB/J mice, Couturier-Maillard et al. (2018) demonstrated that reduced expression of Reg3β and Reg3γ led to marked alterations in microbiota composition and exacerbated Th1-driven intestinal inflammation. These findings provide direct evidence that IL-22-dependent antimicrobial pathways play a central role in limiting inflammation-associated dysbiosis during intestinal toxoplasmosis. Importantly, these immune-mediated effects on the microbiota cannot be directly compared with observations from intraperitoneal infection models, underscoring the need to interpret microbiota changes within the specific biological framework of each experimental approach, as interactions between the gut microbiota and host immune system are highly context-dependent and shaped by disease and experimental conditions (Feng et al. 2025). Entamoeba histolytica

Entamoeba histolytica, the causative agent of human amebiasis, is not only a classical gastrointestinal pathogen but also interacts significantly with the host gut microbiota. Accumulating evidence indicates that the composition of the gut microbiota can influence both the expression of parasite virulence and the host’s susceptibility or resistance to infection. Clinical studies show that patients with amebiasis exhibit differences in gut microbiota structure and diversity compared to healthy individuals, characterized by an increased abundance of potentially pathogenic genera and a reduction in beneficial commensal bacteria, suggesting a close association between E. histolytica infection and intestinal dysbiosis (Iyer et al. 2023). Experimental studies have also demonstrated that E. histolytica can phagocytize specific intestinal bacteria, and that the presence of certain bacterial populations can influence the parasite’s response to oxidative stress, with potential consequences for its survival and pathogenic behavior during infection (Ankri 2021; Guillén, 2023; Varet et al. 2018).

Influence of the microbiota and immune response in amebiasis

Colonization of mice with commensal Clostridia or segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) confers protection against experimental amebiasis. In these mice, bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) produced significantly higher levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2). Interleukin-23 (IL-23) also plays a key role, as it promotes interleukin-17 (IL-17) production and neutrophil activation—both essential for immunity against E. histolytica. Transferring BMDCs from SFB-colonized mice provided protection against infection with this parasite (Burgess et al. 2020; Burgess and Petri 2016).

Serum amyloid A (SAA), a host damage-associated molecular pattern, was elevated in the serum of SFB-colonized mice compared to those lacking this commensal. Treating BMDCs with SAA partially reproduced the increase in IL-23 observed in BMDCs from SFB-colonized mice. These findings suggest that trained immunity may be a mechanism through which bacterial members of the intestinal microbiota influence the host response to amebiasis (Burgess et al. 2016).

Both commensal and pathogenic intestinal microbes can modulate the host immune system, enhancing mucosal immune responses against E. histolytica infection. Colonization with Clostridia or SFB not only alters dendritic cell function but also boosts specific immune pathways such as IL-23/IL-17, which are critical for defense against E. histolytica. This suggests that some commensals do more than compete with pathogens—they can train the immune system, strengthening mucosal immunity and reducing susceptibility to infection (Burgess et al. 2020; Burgess and Petri 2016).

The interaction between E. histolytica and the gut microbiota significantly influences pathogenesis and the host immune response. Studies in murine models have shown that colonization of the gut with specific commensal bacteria, such as segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB), confers protection against amebiasis by increasing IL-17A levels and enhancing the activity of dendritic cells and neutrophils—mechanisms that promote a more effective immune response against the parasite (e.g., increased IL-23 production by dendritic cells and activation of effector cells) (Burgess and Petri 2016). Additionally, the microbiota influences the secretion of Muc2 mucin and IL-25, key components of innate immunity that help protect the colonic mucosa and limit tissue invasion by E. histolytica. Dysbiosis and reduction of these molecules are associated with increased susceptibility to amebic colitis (Watanabe et al. 2017). Moreover, the parasite’s interaction with intestinal mucus and commensal bacteria can alter barrier structure and trigger pro-inflammatory responses mediated by cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, contributing to both defense and tissue damage in symptomatic amebiasis (Leon-Coria et al. 2020). These findings indicate that the microbiota not only participates in resistance or susceptibility to infection but also modulates key immune and functional pathways during the host–parasite interaction.

Giardia duodenalis

Giardia duodenalis is an intestinal protozoan with a global distribution that can cause infections ranging from asymptomatic carriage to chronic diarrhea and malabsorption. Its life cycle includes a trophozoite stage responsible for colonizing the small intestine and a resistant cyst stage that enables fecal–oral transmission (Adam 2021).

Influence of the microbiota and immune response in giardiasis

During Giardia duodenalis infection, increasing evidence indicates that the parasite is associated with significant alterations in the gut microbial ecosystem, although available data remain limited and highly context dependent. Experimental infection of C57BL/6 J mice has shown marked dysbiosis along the gastrointestinal tract, characterized by an expansion of aerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria—particularly Proteobacteria—and a concomitant reduction in obligate anaerobes such as Clostridia and Lactobacillaceae (Barash et al. 2017). These changes have been linked to the fermentative metabolism of G. duodenalis, which increases luminal oxygen availability and disrupts redox balance, thereby favoring oxygen-tolerant taxa at the expense of key anaerobic commensals involved in short-chain fatty acid production and vitamin biosynthesis. Several studies in murine models have demonstrated that G. duodenalis infection induces significant alterations in the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota. In particular, a decrease in Bacteroidota has been observed in the upper small intestine, while the relative abundance of Bacillota appears to remain relatively stable, albeit with reduced diversity (Barash et al. 2017; Fekete et al. 2021). Furthermore, Giardia infection is associated with the enrichment of bacterial families such as Comamonadaceae, Moraxellaceae, Flavobacteriales, and Rhodocyclaceae, as well as a decrease in Melainabacteria, with these effects varying according to the intestinal region and prior antibiotic use (Barash et al. 2017). Collectively, these findings suggest that Giardia modulates the gut microbiota in a niche- and ecosystem state–dependent manner.

Clinical and translational studies support this association, reporting altered microbial diversity and reduced abundance of beneficial taxa in individuals with giardiasis, although the direction and magnitude of these changes vary with host age, nutritional status, and infection severity (Allain et al. 2018; Beatty et al. 2017). Unlike many enteric pathogens, G. duodenalis does not induce robust intestinal inflammation; instead, it subtly reshapes the intestinal microenvironment by competing for nutrients such as arginine and modulating immune pathways including IL-17-mediated responses, which are important for parasite clearance (Dann et al. 2015; Fekete et al. 2021). Together, these findings suggest that giardiasis is associated with a functional reorganization of the gut ecosystem rather than overt inflammatory damage, while also highlighting the need for more systematic and longitudinal studies to define the precise role of the microbiota in susceptibility, persistence, and clinical outcomes of Giardia infection (Maertens et al. 2021).

In addition to compositional changes in the gut microbiota during Giardia infection, experimental studies show that these alterations are related to host-specific immune mechanisms. In murine models, Giardia infection induces a robust Th17 response with a marked increase in IL-17A, which promotes IgA secretion into the intestinal lumen and enhances parasite clearance and antimicrobial defense (Dann et al. 2015). Th17 cell differentiation and IL-17A production are modulated by the resident microbiota. In germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice, the Th17 response is compromised and the infection is prolonged, highlighting the role of commensal communities in training mucosal immunity (Maertens et al. 2021). Beyond cytokine regulation, Giardia-induced dysbiosis is also associated with metabolic alterations, such as changes in redox balance and fermentative metabolism, which can affect nutrient absorption, epithelial integrity, and contribute to long-term sequelae such as post-infection irritable bowel syndrome (Fink & Singer 2017).

G. duodenalis–induced dysbiosis not only alters gut microbial composition but also has important immunological consequences. Microbiota transfer studies have shown that mice receiving Giardia-associated microbiota develop an exacerbated pro-inflammatory response, characterized by increased production of IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Beatty et al. 2017) These results indicate that microbiota modifications driven by parasitic infection can act as key mediators of intestinal inflammation, indirectly amplifying the host immune response.

Blastocystis spp.

Blastocystis spp. is a unicellular intestinal parasite classified within the Stramenopiles (Heterokonta), a group that mainly includes free-living flagellated or ciliated microorganisms. Transmission occurs through the fecal–oral route. Infection with Blastocystis spp. has been variably associated with gastrointestinal disorders, including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), although its clinical significance remains controversial. In addition to the classically described vacuolar, granular, and amoeboid forms, other morphological variants—such as avacuolar and multivacuolar forms—have also been described (Jeremiah and Parija 2013; Yason et al. 2019).

Influence of the microbiota and immune response in Blastocystis spp. infection

Blastocystis spp. modulates both the intestinal microbiota and the host immune response, with effects that vary markedly depending on the subtype. In C57BL/6 and Rag1⁻/⁻ mice, experimental colonization with Blastocystis ST4 has been shown to induce protective immune responses without causing histological damage. The presence of the parasite promotes the expansion of IL-5– and IL-13–producing T helper 2 (Th2) cells, as well as the induction of IL-10–secreting regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby fostering an anti-inflammatory environment and maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis. At the microbiota level, colonization with ST4 increases the abundance of Clostridia vadin BB60 and Lachnospiraceae NK4A136, while reducing potentially pro-inflammatory genera such as Odoribacter and Paraprevotella (Deng et al. 2022) (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Subtype-specific effects of Blastocystis on gut microbiota composition and host immune responses. a ST4 b ST7. In contrast, infection with the pathogenic subtype ST7 has been linked to opposite effects, including the activation of pro-inflammatory responses, a decrease in commensal bacteria, and histological lesions compromising mucosal integrity. In C57BL/6 mice, acute infection with ST7-B or ST7-H reduced beneficial populations such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, while promoting the proliferation of Escherichia coli and inducing oxidative stress within the microbiota. At the tissue level, ulcerations and loss of colonic architecture were observed, indicating a pronounced pro-inflammatory response (Yason et al. 2019).

These findings confirm that the impact of Blastocystis spp. on intestinal health depends on the specific subtype. ST4 promotes an anti-inflammatory intestinal environment and the growth of beneficial commensal bacteria, whereas ST7 induces microbiota imbalance and oxidative stress associated with tissue damage. This demonstrates that the parasite’s ability to modulate both immunity and microbial composition can tip the balance between intestinal homeostasis and pathology. Understanding these subtype-specific differences provides valuable insight into how certain parasites may contribute to gut health, while others predispose the host to inflammation and dysbiosis (Yason et al. 2019)(Fig. 10).

Studies on Blastocystis subtypes show that the impact of this parasite on intestinal ecology and host response depends on the specific subtype. Pathogenic strains such as ST7 can alter beneficial bacterial populations (e.g., Bifidobacterium longum and Lactobacillus spp.) and increase taxa such as Escherichia coli, probably through oxidative stress and direct competition with commensal bacteria, contributing to dysbiosis and epithelial damage in vivo (Yason et al. 2019). In contrast, subtypes such as ST4 appear to interact protectively with commensals and the intestinal barrier; colonization with ST4 maintains epithelial integrity and reduces the growth of potentially pathogenic bacteria such as Bacteroides vulgatus, modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and bacterial stress responses (Deng and Tan 2022) These subtype-dependent effects suggest that Blastocystis influences the host’s immune and metabolic pathways not only by altering microbial composition but also by reshaping intestinal ecosystem functionality and its interaction with mucosal immunity.

Methodological limitations and interpretive challenges in microbiota and parasite studies

Despite growing interest in the gut microbiota as a modulator of parasitic infection outcomes, several methodological limitations compromise the interpretability and comparability of studies. A key concern is the lack of baseline microbiota characterization before infection, which makes it difficult to separate infection-induced changes from pre-existing host and environmental effects. Baseline variation can be influenced by animal facility conditions, supplier, diet, and host genetics—factors that shape gut microbiota composition in rodent models and affect phenotypic outcomes in disease studies (Franklin and Ericsson 2017; Zhang et al. 2021). Another common practice is the use of germ-free or antibiotic-treated animals to infer causal roles of microbial communities. While these approaches allow mechanistic insights, they also introduce confounding factors, such as altered immune development and microbial ecology, complicating interpretation of how specific taxa or functions influence host-parasite interactions. Furthermore, many studies are conducted with small sample sizes and heterogeneous experimental designs (including differences in infection route, parasite dose, sampling time points, and anatomical sites), contributing to inconsistent or contradictory microbial profiles across studies (Hansen et al. 2014).

Finally, the predominant reliance on 16S rRNA gene sequencing limits taxonomic resolution and functional inference, particularly when comparing datasets generated using different regions, platforms, or bioinformatics pipelines. Comparative studies have shown that shotgun metagenomics recovers more taxa, identifies rare microorganisms underrepresented in 16S studies, and enables direct characterization of genomic functions rather than inferring them from taxonomic markers (Durazzi et al. 2021). While shotgun metagenomics provides higher taxonomic and functional resolution, it remains less common in parasitology studies and is more costly for many experimental designs (La Reau et al. 2023).

Shared immunological and metabolic functions in parasitic infections

The reviewed studies show that, despite the diversity of intracellular and luminal parasites, there are shared functional mechanisms in the interaction between microbiota, metabolism, and immune response. In various murine models, the presence SCFAs producing bacteria, such as members of the Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Muribaculaceae families, is consistently associated with resistance to infection and regulation of the inflammatory response, favoring regulatory T cell differentiation and controlled macrophage activation (Abdugheni et al. 2022; Burgess and Petri 2016; Lopes et al. 2021) Furthermore, increases in Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia often coincide with phases of inflammation or intestinal stress induced by various parasites, reflecting a shared functional adjustment of the microbiota in response to infection (Lv et al. 2022; Meng et al. 2023).

Similarly, the microbiota consistently modulates immune polarization across different infections, enhancing protective Th1 responses in infections such as T. cruzi and T. gondii, and regulating the production of key cytokines such as IL-17, IL-22, and IFN-γ in the case of Leishmania and Entamoeba (Castañeda et al. 2023; Couturier-Maillard et al. 2018; Naik et al. 2015). In addition to microbial-driven effects, host-intrinsic regulatory mechanisms also shape early immune responses to parasites. Notably, Li et al. (2024) demonstrated that extracellular superoxide dismutase (SOD3) suppresses early cellular immune activation during parasitic infection by limiting macrophage activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. These findings highlight that conserved immune regulatory pathways, acting in parallel with microbiota-derived signals, contribute to shared patterns of susceptibility and immune control across parasitic infections (Li et al. 2024).

It is worth noting that microbiota-mediated effects on host immunity and infection outcomes can be strongly influenced by the genetic background of the mouse strain. For instance, C57BL/6 mice often mount protective Th1 or regulatory responses associated with maintenance of beneficial microbial communities, whereas susceptible or immunodeficient strains such as BALB/c or Rag1⁻/⁻ mice may exhibit dysbiosis, exaggerated pro-inflammatory responses, or impaired control of the parasite (Abdugheni et al. 2022; Couturier-Maillard et al. 2018; Naik et al. 2015). These findings suggest that host genetics modulates both the magnitude and quality of immune-microbiota interactions, and underscore the need to consider strain-specific effects when interpreting murine models of parasitic infections.

Finally, although the abundance of specific species may vary between models and parasites, the findings highlight that the microbiota acts as an active modulator of intestinal homeostasis, immune responses, and metabolism, demonstrating the existence of conserved mechanisms common to different infections that could be leveraged in preventive or therapeutic strategies. However, broader coverage of microbial species and their functions is needed to confirm these patterns and better understand their relevance in different biological contexts.

Translational relevance and limitations of rodent microbiota models

Rodent models have been indispensable for analyzing host-microbiota interactions under controlled conditions. In gut microbiota research, murine systems allow for experimental perturbations—such as genetic inactivation, controlled microbial colonization, and environmental interventions—that help assess causality in microbiota-host interactions. These approaches have been useful for identifying shared qualitative patterns in microbial composition—such as the dominant presence of the phyla Bacillota and Bacteroidota in both mice and humans—and for studying conserved microbial functions, such as the production of SCFAs which modulate immune and metabolic responses in both hosts (e.g., the presence of shared core bacterial communities across species). (Krych et al. 2013; Nagpal et al. 2018).

However, translating rodent findings to humans requires caution. Comparative analyses reveal that while many bacterial genera are found in both mice and humans at the taxonomic level, relative abundances and functional repertoires often differ substantially, with limited overlap at the species level, leading to differences in entire microbial communities. (Kieser et al. 2022) Furthermore, standard laboratory mice are maintained under controlled conditions that do not capture the environmental and microbial exposures experienced by humans, which could limit the ecological validity of microbiota alterations observed in experimental settings. These limitations underscore the importance of considering complementary studies in humans, humanized models, or approaches that compare human and mouse microbial communities to strengthen the translational relevance of the results (Wos-Oxley et al. 2012).

Probiotics in parasitic infections

Several experimental studies have explored the potential of specific probiotic strains to modulate the dynamics of intestinal parasitic infections. In animal models of giardiasis, administration of Bifidobacterium longum 51A significantly reduced the parasite load, suggesting both a prophylactic and therapeutic effect (Fonseca et al. 2019) Similarly, in murine models, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) has shown the ability to modulate the mucosal immune response during Giardia infection, promoting increased secretory IgA and CD4⁺ cell activation, indicating a relevant role in infection control (Goyal & Shukla 2013). Additional studies have indicated that certain strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium can increase the production of intestinal mucins and strengthen the epithelial barrier, factors that contribute to the host’s defense against parasites such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium (Travers et al. 2011). However, the available evidence comes mostly from in vivo and in vitro models, and probiotic efficacy depends on multiple variables, including strain, dose, duration of treatment, and host immune status. Some probiotics have shown contradictory effects in Cryptosporidium infections, possibly due to modifications in the intestinal environment that may favor parasite proliferation under certain conditions (Raheem et al. 2021).Overall, these findings underline the potential of probiotics as complementary tools in the management of parasitic infections, but also highlight the need for well-designed, controlled clinical studies to establish their safety, efficacy, and applicability in humans.

Conclusion

Despite advances in the study of host–microbiota interactions, significant gaps remain in our understanding of the mechanisms by which parasitic infections—both intracellular and luminal—alter the composition and function of the gut microbiota. Available evidence indicates that these infections are associated with dynamic microbial changes driven by immune modulation, competition for nutrients, and alterations in the intestinal metabolic environment.

Experimental and observational studies further support these concepts. In murine models, infection with L. infantum has been shown to induce gut microbiota dysbiosis and immune alterations associated with visceral leishmaniasis pathogenesis (Xiao et al. 2025). Similarly, T. gondii infection reduces bacterial diversity and profoundly reshapes microbiota structure, reflecting direct parasite–microbiota interactions (Wang et al. 2025). In humans, the presence of intestinal protozoa such as Entamoeba, Blastocystis, and Giardia has been associated with shifts in bacterial abundance and diversity, suggesting that these parasites can modulate immune and metabolic homeostasis. Collectively, these interactions between parasites, microbiota, and the immune system may influence disease progression, treatment efficacy, and the restoration of microbial balance following infection (Beyhan & Yıldız, 2023).

Interpretation of these findings is, however, constrained by substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, the frequent lack of baseline microbiota data, and a strong reliance on animal models. Although these models have been essential for identifying specific immunological and metabolic pathways, they do not always capture the complexity of parasitic infections in natural human settings. Species-specific differences, inter-individual microbiota variability, and environmental influences further limit direct translation to human health. Future research should therefore prioritize longitudinal observational studies, investigations in endemic populations, and integrative experimental designs to better define how the microbiota contributes to susceptibility, persistence, and clinical outcomes of parasitic infections within appropriate ethical and epidemiological frameworks.
