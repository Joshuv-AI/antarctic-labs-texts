A Broad-Spectrum Copper Oxidase Synthesizes Fungal Peptidomelanin
Authors: Shravanthi Srivatsan, Samayeta Pramanik, Pushya Pradeep, Senjuti Sarkar, Sindhu Priya Chandrappa, Riley Gall, Anmol Singh, Bhavana Chamraj, Bhavya Kasala, Upama Majumder, T. Bhavana Meenakshi, Likhit Reddy, Vivek Suranse, Varshaa Arer, Judy Jays, Dipshikha Chakravortty, Deepesh Nagarajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peptidomelanin is a fungal biopolymer secreted during the germination of Aspergillus niger melanoliber spores. Peptidomelanin is composed of an insoluble core polymer derived from L-DOPA, surrounded and solubilized by short, heterogeneous peptide chains. In this work, we report the identification and biochemical characterization of BroSCO (gene ID: ABHI18_005222), a broad spectrum copper oxidase responsible for synthesizing peptidomelanin. Our model of BroSCO possesses a type 3 dicopper center housed in a large, solvent accessible catalytic center that can accommodate substrates possessing a range of sizes. BroSCO polymerizes L-DOPA into melanin along the Raper-Mason pathway. BroSCO oxidizes thiol groups from cysteine and cysteinylated peptides, allowing them to copolymerize with L-DOPA, forming peptidomelanin in the latter case. Our identification of BroSCO as the enzyme responsible for synthesizing peptidomelanin lead to the characterization of a previously unknown biochemical pathway, expanding our understanding of fungal biochemistry and melanogenesis.

Introduction

Melanin is dark brown to black pigment synthesized by all kingdoms of life. It is a large, heterogeneous, amorphous biopolymer composed primarily of phenolic and indolic subunits. Melanin can broadly be classified into three major types: eumelanin, pheomelanin, and allomelanin, each differing in their biosynthetic precursors and structural motifs.

Eumelanin is synthesized from the oxidative polymerization of L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA), leading to a cross-linked polymer composed of different ratios of 5,6-dihydroxyindole (DHI) and 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA) monomers. The substrates and intermediates produced during eumelanin synthesis were elucidated by Raper and Mason.

Pheomelanin, in contrast, incorporates cysteine into its backbone and is characterized by benzothiazine units, resulting in a sulfur-containing polymer possessing a distinct orange-red coloration. Pheomelanin is biochemically synthesized from L-DOPA copolymerized with the amino acid cysteine to form a range of cysteinyl-DOPA isomers (2-S-,5-S-, 6-S, and 2-S-5-S-cysteinyldopa), with 5-S-cysteinyldopa predominating. Cysteinyldopa isomers form predominantly benzothiazine intermediates, with minor contributions from benzothiazole and isoquinoline intermediates. , These intermediates ultimately polymerize into pheomelanin.

Allomelanins are nitrogen-free melanins often found in fungi. Rather than polymerizing from L-DOPA, they are synthesized from nitrogen-free precursors such as catechols or 1,8-dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN). Pyomelanin, a minor type of melanin, is produced extracellularly through the autoxidative polymerization of secreted homogentisic acid and gentisic acid. It is notable for its solubility in water.

Melanin plays a multifaceted role in biological systems, offering protection against ultraviolet and ionizing radiation, aiding in thermoregulation, protecting against reactive oxygen species (ROS), metal ion chelation , and enhancing microbial pathogenicity and resistance to antimicrobial agents. , Melanin may even transduce energy: capturing electromagnetic radiation to produce useful energy in radiotropic fungi. Despite its widespread occurrence, its precise chemical structure has not been elucidated, largely due to its inherent amorphous nature, heterogeneity, and generally poor solubility in aqueous environments.

In our previous work, we described the discovery and biochemical characterization of peptidomelanin. Peptidomelanin is a dark, water-soluble pigment that is secreted into the surrounding medium by germinating spores of A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) (Figure A). Peptidomelanin consists of a core polymer derived from L-DOPA, surrounded and solubilized by a corona of short heterogeneous peptides (Figure B). Peptidomelanin possessed the ability to chelate large quantities of heavy metals including lead, mercury, and uranium (as uranyl).

(A) Peptidomelanin (4 mg/mL, in deionized water/Milli-Q) observed through a quartz cuvette with a path length of 1 mm. Peptidomelanin is intensely dark and water-soluble. (B) Our model of the biochemical composition of peptidomelanin. All results depicted here are supported by experimental data as described in our previous work. In this work, we expand on one result, showing evidence that cysteine’bridges’ also link peptide chains to the core polymer derived from L-DOPA.

Tyrosinases are a class of enzymes (EC 1.14.18.1) responsible for synthesizing melanin. Tyrosinases are oxidases containing a type 3 dicopper center, ,, and are found across all kingdoms of life. Tyrosinases are required to catalyze the first two rate-limiting steps in melanin biosynthesis: the hydroxylation of monophenols (l-tyrosine) to o-quinones, and the oxidation of o-diphenols to o-quinones (catechol oxidase/diphenolase). In fungi, tyrosinases are responsible for pigmentation of the cell wall, conidia, and fruiting bodies. Structurally, the enzyme class contains an active site composed of two copper ions each coordinated by three histidine residues, existing in multiple redox states (met-, deoxy-, oxy-, deact- tyrosinase) that influence its catalytic properties.

In this work, we describe BroSCO: a broad-spectrum copper oxidase that synthesizes peptidomelanin. We identified BroSCO in the annotated genome of A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366). BroSCO possessed no orthologs in the genome of the A. niger type strain (MTCC 281) that does not secrete peptidomelanin. Our model of BroSCO contains a large, solvent accessible type 3 dicopper center capable of accommodating sterically bulky substrates. BroSCO catalyzes the oxidation of thiol groups from cysteine and from a cysteinylated peptide CGGPGDP, allowing them to copolymerize with L-DOPA, and forming peptidomelanin in the latter case. The identification of BroSCO as the enzyme responsible for peptidomelanin synthesis has led to the characterization of a previously undiscovered biochemical pathway. This work therefore expands our understanding of fungal biochemistry and melanogenesis.

Results

Here we describe the identification and characterization of BroSCO: a broad spectrum copper oxidase from the genome of A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366): the strain that secretes peptidomelanin. We had previously sequenced and annotated the genome of A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366). This whole-genome sequence is available in GenBank under the accession number JBECZT000000000.1. The sequence data is available in NCBI-SRA under the accession number SRR31522722. We obtained an annotated whole genome sequence of a type strain of A. niger (MTCC 281/ATCC 9029/NRRL3) from MycoCosm. , We found 10 and 8 genes annotated as tyrosinases in the A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) and A. niger (MTCC 281) genomes, respectively. We constructed a maximum likelihood-based phylogenetic reconstruction of all 18 tyrosinases using both nucleotide (Figure A) and protein (Figure B) sequences. We observed that every tyrosinase from A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) possessed one close ortholog from A. niger (MTCC 281). This was true for all genes except for gene ID ABHI18_005222 from A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) that possesses no close ortholog. We also created a matrix comparing all pairwise clustalW alignment scores of individual tyrosinase genes from A. niger (MTCC 281) and A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) (Supporting Information Table S1, dimensions: 8 × 10). From Table S1, gene ID ABHI18_005222 possessed a maximum of 30%/36% (gene/protein) similarity to any tyrosinase gene from A. niger (MTCC 281), confirming our findings. As A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) is the only identified strain that secretes peptidomelanin, it must possess novel tyrosinases not found in the A. niger type strain (MTCC 281). This made gene ID ABHI18_005222 a strong potential candidate enzyme for peptidomelanin synthesis. Peptidomelanin consists of an insoluble core polymer derived from L-DOPA, solubilized by short, covalently linked peptide chains. We hypothesized that tyrosine and/or cysteine residues within short peptides could act as “anchors”, linking the peptide chain to the core polymer. In our previous work, we showed evidence for the copolymerization of dansylated L-DOPA (analogous to tyrosine possessing a peptide extension) into melanin. In this work, we explored the possibility of polymerizing peptidomelanin using cysteine anchors from cysteinylated peptides. This reaction was catalyzed by gene ID ABHI18_005222 that we have named BroSCO: broad-spectrum copper oxidase, for reasons described later in the text.

During pheomelanin biosynthesis, cysteine is oxidized forming a thiolate anion that spontaneously reacts with L-DOPA forming cysteinylDOPA. , Oxidation is pH dependent, and occurs spontaneously at an alkaline pH. However, oxidation of a thiol group at a physiologically relevant neutral pH requires a catalyst.

Previous studies have shown the importance of “gatekeeper” residues for governing the substrate specificity of tyrosinase and catechol oxidase, both containing type 3 dicopper centers. We therefore hypothesized that an enzyme containing a type 3 dicopper center coordinated in a large, solvent accessible catalytic center, lacking any such gatekeeper residues, would possess broad substrate specificity. Such an enzyme could catalyze the oxidation of cysteine and cysteinylated peptides at a physiologically relevant pH (pH 7.0), in addition to catalyzing the oxidation of L-DOPA to form the core polymer. While cysteine is a small molecule, a short cysteinylated peptide would present a bulky substrate that would require a large, solvent accessible cavity for access to the catalytic center.

We used AlphaFold2 to model all 10 fungal tyrosinases from A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366). Gene IDs ABHI18_007048, ABHI18_012284 and ABHI18_012562 could not be modeled. All 7 successfully modeled tyrosinases possessed histidine-rich catalytic centers (Data set S1: tyrosinases_alphafold.zip). Naccess was used to determine the solvent accessibility of each histidine residue in each catalytic center (Figure A). We observed that BroSCO (gene ID ABHI18_005222) possessed the most solvent accessible catalytic center (median solvent accessibility = 4.86%) of all the tyrosinases modeled (Figure B). Further, BroSCO possessed a significantly more solvent accessible catalytic center when compared to mushroom tyrosinase (PDB ID 5M6B, Figure C), representing a model fungal tyrosinase (median solvent accessibility = 1.21%, p = 2e-15, Student’s t test). A dicopper center was modeled into the AlphaFold2 model of BroSCO using Autodock Vina (Data set S1: BroSCO_Cu_autodock.zip/BroSCO_Cu_autodock.pse).

Alphafold2 could not predict a structure for the N-terminal region of BroSCO (MAILEQSLILLVAVLSLEVAAVPH, residues 1–24). This region was truncated and replaced with a 6XHIS tag. The gene encoding BroSCO was chemically synthesized and cloned into a pET22b­(+) plasmid for expression in E. coli (BL21). Due to the absence of a predicted structure at the N-terminal region, we made no attempts to synthesize and characterize untruncated BroSCO. BroSCO was found to be insoluble, and was only detected in the pellet fraction of the cell sonicate (Figure A). We resolubilized BroSCO in 8 M urea and subjected it to “watering out” using 2-fold volumes of deionized water (Milli-Q) as described in the methods section. Purified BroSCO was dialyzed against 100 mM tris at pH 7.0. A large fraction of BroSCO precipitated out (Figure B, BroSCO pellet). A smaller fraction remained in the supernatant and appeared to form a dimer (Figure B, resolubilized BroSCO). Dithiothreitol (DTT)-reduced BroSCO appears monomeric (Figure C), indicating that BroSCO dimerization requires disulfide bond formation. An illustrated protocol for preparing BroSCO is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). Immunoblotting using mouse anti-6XHIS antibodies (Figure E,F) confirms that the purified protein is BroSCO, which was designed with an N-terminal 6XHIS tag.

Since BroSCO is a fungal enzyme, there exists the possibility that fungal post-translational modifications are required to solubilize the enzyme in its native environment. We used MusiteDeep to predict the post-translational modifications possible along the sequence of untruncated BroSCO. MusiteDeep predicted 8 high-confidence post-translational modifications, specifically phosphorylation and glycosylation at PTMscores >0.5. As expected, all residues predicted with high confidence to host post-transational modifications were found on the surface of our Alphafold2 model of BroSCO (Figure S2), which helped computationally validate the Alphafold2 structural model. No post-translational modifications were predicted with high confidence on the BroSCO N-terminal disordered region, justifying its truncation on the synthesized gene. All MusiteDeep predictions are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S2).

Copper concentration estimation using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), coupled with protein concentration estimation using the nanodrop technique, was used to calculate the Cu:BroSCO stoichiometric ratio. This ratio was found to be 2.3 ± 0.5:1 (Figure D), in good agreement with the expected 2:1 ratio for a protein containing a single type 3 dicopper center. ICP-MS raw data, nanodrop protein concentration readings, and stoichiometric calculations are provided in the Supporting Information (Data Set S2, Text S5).

BroSCO synthesizes peptidomelanin in vitro via the copolymerization of cysteinylated peptides with a core polymer derived from L-DOPA. Reactions A1-A3 (Figure ) depict the oxidative polymerization of L-DOPA to form melanin in the absence of catalyst (A1), in the presence of mushroom tyrosinase (A2) and in the presence of BroSCO (A3). All melanin polymers were dialyzed against deionized water (Milli-Q) and dried into powder form. The melanin polymers’ elemental composition was determined using scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), as described in the methods section. In all cases, the resulting SEM-EDS spectra indicated only trace amounts of sulfur. This is expected as L-DOPA does not possess any elemental sulfur in its molecular structure.

BroSCO copolymerizes L-DOPA with cysteinylated peptides to synthesize peptidomelanin in vitro. Reactions A1-A3: L-DOPA can oxidatively polymerize into melanin (A1) or can be catalyzed by mushroom tyrosinase (A2) or BroSCO (A3). Melanin polymerized from L-DOPA alone will possess a negligible sulfur content, as sulfur is not present in L-DOPA. Reactions B1–B3: L-DOPA can oxidatively polymerize to melanin in the presence of methionine (B1), or can be catalyzed by mushroom tyrosianse (B2) or BroSCO (B3). L-DOPA does not copolymerize with methionine, and therefore the resulting melanin polymer possesses negligible sulfur content. Reactions C1–C3: Cysteine inhibits the polymerization of L-DOPA in the absence of a catalyst (C1). Cysteine creates a reducing environment that prevents the autoxidative polymerization of L-DOPA. The addition of mushroom tyrosinase (C2) or BroSCO (C3) catalyzes the synthesis of pheomelanin (cysteinylated melanin). Reactions D1-D3: A cysteinylated peptide (CGGPGDP) inhibits L-DOPA polymerization in the absence of a catalyst (D1). Mushroom tyrosinase (D2), due to its buried catalytic center (Figure A,C) cannot catalyze this reaction. BroSCO, due to its large, solvent accessible catalytic center (Figure A,B) can catalyze the copolymerization of L-DOPA and CGGPGDP, creating synthetic peptidomelanin.

Reactions B1–B3 (Figure ) depict the oxidative polymerization of L-DOPA to form melanin in the presence of methionine. Melanin forms in the absence of catalyst (B1), in the presence of mushroom tyrosinase (B2), and in the presence of BroSCO (B3). Methionine contains an inert thioether (−CH2–S-CH3) group that will not copolymerize with melanin (negligible SEM-EDS sulfur signal).

L-DOPA cannot oxidatively polymerize into melanin in the presence of reducing agents. Likewise, we have observed that L-DOPA cannot oxidatively polymerize in the presence of thiol (SH−) groups from cysteine or dithiothreitol (DTT) (Supplementary Figure S3). Thiol groups create a reducing environment that prevents the oxidation of L-DOPA to form melanin. The presence of an oxidizing agent (KMnO4) or catalyst (described below) allows for the polymerization of L-DOPA in the presence of cysteine.

Reactions C1–C3 (Figure ) depict the oxidative polymerization of L-DOPA in the presence of cysteine. As expected, L-DOPA cannot polymerize in the presence of cysteine in the absence of a catalyst (C1). Mushroom tyrosinase can catalyze the oxidation of L-DOPA and its copolymerization with cysteine (C2). Cysteine’s thiol group contains a sulfur atom, allowing cys-L-DOPA copolymerization to be tracked via the SEM-EDS sulfur signal. The melanin polymer produced by reaction C2 contained 3 ± 0.7% sulfur, corresponding to 11.3 ± 2.5% cysteine. Likewise, BroSCO (C3) can catalyze the oxidation of L-DOPA and its copolymerization with cysteine. The melanin polymer produced by reaction C3 contained 6.8 ± 3.4% sulfur, corresponding to 26.0 ± 12.7% cysteine. BroSCO’s ability to incorporate a greater percentage of cysteine into the copolymer compared to mushroom tyrosinase (C3 vs C2, p = 3e-3, Student’s t test) may be due to its larger, solvent accessible catalytic center (Figure B).

The sulfur content of L-DOPA polymerized in the presence of cysteine is significantly greater than the sulfur content of L-DOPA polymerized in the presence of methionine (Figure ), for both mushroom tyrosinase (C2 vs B2, p = 4e-7, Student’s t test) and BroSCO (C3 vs B3, p = 8e-5, Student’s t test). This indicates that the thiol (−SH) group from cysteine is essential for copolymerization, while the thioether (−CH2–S-CH3) group is nonreactive in this context. Both cysteine and methionine contain primary amine (−NH2) and carboxyl (−COOH) groups, indicating that these groups are not responsible for copolymerization.

Reactions D1-D3 (Figure ) depict the oxidative polymerization of L-DOPA in the presence of a cysteinylated peptide (CGGPGDP). Peptide GPGDP had previously been designed to bind to lithium ions, ,, We placed an N-terminal cysteine as the anchor residue, followed by a glycine residue as a spacer, providing peptide GPGDP access to the surrounding solvent. Peptide CGGPGDP does not copolymerize with L-DOPA in the absence of catalyst (D1), or in the presence of mushroom tyrosinase (D2). Due to the presence of a large, solvent accessible catalytic center, BroSCO is able to accommodate bulky substrates such as cysteinylated peptides (CGGPGDP). BroSCO therefore catalyzes the copolymerization of peptide CGGPGDP to L-DOPA (D3), creating synthetic peptidomelanin (CGGPGDP-melanin). CGGPGDP-melanin contained 1.1 ± 0.3% sulfur, corresponding to 20.1 ± 4.7% peptide content. The sulfur content of CGGPGDP-melanin was significantly higher (D3 vs B3, p = 2e-6, Student’s t test) than L-DOPA polymerized in the presence of methionine, once again showing that a thiol group (from N-terminal cysteine) is required for BroSCO-catalyzed peptidomelanin copolymerization. It should be noted that a 1:1.25 L-DOPA:CGGPGDP mass ratio corresponds to a 1:0.25 L-DOPA:cysteine mass ratio. A lower L-DOPA:cysteine ratio was needed while polymerizing CGGPGDP-melanin in order to maintain the ∼ 20% peptide content found in natural peptidomelanin, and consequently resulted in a lower sulfur content. SEM-EDS raw data for reactions A1-D3 is provided in the Supporting Information (Data Set S2).

Reactions F1–F4 (Figure S4) were performed in order to determine the spectrum of activity of BroSCO. BroSCO was able to copolymerize L-DOPA with thioglycolic acid (Figure S4, Reaction F2) and with dithiothreitol (Figure S4, Reaction F4). We have also experimentally demonstrated the ability of BroSCO to copolymerize L-DOPA with cysteine (Reaction C3), the cysteinylated peptide CGGPGDP (Reaction D3). These results confirm the ability of BroSCO to copolymerize L-DOPA with a wide range of substrates containing thiol functional groups, justifying the enzyme’s ″Broad Spectrum″ designation. SEM-EDS raw data for Reactions F2 and F4 is provided in the Supporting Information (Data Set S2).

Melanin is an amorphous polymer. Synthetic L-DOPA melanin (Reaction A3) forms a continuous smear on an SDS-PAGE gel, as every melanin molecule possesses a unique molecular weight (Figure A). Peptidomelanin, like melanin, is an amorphous polymer. Both natural peptidomelanin and BroSCO-synthesized CGGPGDP-melanin display continuous smears on an SDS-PAGE gel (Figure A). However, the majority of particles in both polymers migrate below 135 kDa, indicating a smaller polymeric size compared to L-DOPA melanin, possibly as a consequence of peptide-L-DOPA copolymerization. Additionally, the incorporation of short peptides (CGGPGDP) that will impart solubility and a net negative charge (−2, from C-terminal and aspartate) may also help CGGPGDP-melanin migrate faster through an electric field.

Natural peptidomelanin possesses a peptide content of 22.98 ± 1.84%. We had previously employed acid-hydrolysis using 6 N HCl to hydrolyze peptide bonds in a sample of natural peptidomelanin, freeing individual amino acids from the copolymer. The amino acid composition was quantified using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The peptide composition of natural peptidomelanin was then obtained from the total amino acid composition after acid-hydrolysis and the mass of natural peptidomelanin before acid hydrolysis. CGGPGDP-peptidomelanin contains a peptide composition of 20.1 ± 4.7% (Figure B). We calculated this value from the SEM-EDS sulfur signal (reaction D3, Figure ), which can directly be converted into peptide content using a conversion factor of: CGGPGDP MW/sulfur MW. Despite natural peptidomelanin and CGGPDGP melanin differing in their origin, both contain a peptide content of ∼ 20%.

The FTIR spectrum of CGGPGDP-melanin closely resembles that of natural peptidomelanin (Figure C). Both exhibit a broad dip between 3500 and 3100 cm–1, which can be attributed to O–H or N–H stretching vibrations, along with a sharp dip between 1800 and 1600 cm–1 corresponding to C–C or C = O stretching.

CGGPGDP-melanin was designed from a GPGDP parent peptide previously reported to chelate lithium ions, ,, Preliminary experiments show that CGGPGDP-melanin has the ability to bind to lithium at Li concentrations ≥ 100 ppm (Figure S5A). The Li:CGGPGDP-melanin binding stoichiometric ratio, at the highest Li concentration tested, was found to be 5.0 ± 0.1 mg/g (Figure S5B, Text S6, Data set S2:Li_ICP-MS.pdf). As the scope of this study was limited to characterizing BroSCO’s ability to synthesize peptidomelanin, we will fully explore the ability of synthetic peptidomelanin variants, including CGGPGDP-melanin, to chelate metals in a subsequent report.

We have established that BroSCO can catalyze the copolymerization of cysteinylated peptides with L-DOPA forming peptidomelanin (Figures and ). We have also established that BroSCO can catalyze the copolymerization of thiolated compounds with L-DOPA, forming peptidomelanin analogues (Figure S4). It is therefore prudent to determine whether BroSCO also possesses tyrosinase activity: the ability to convert L-DOPA into indolic compounds along the Raper-Mason pathway. , The ability of BroSCO to synthesize L-DOPA melanin would eliminate the biological need for a separate enzyme to synthesize the melanin core polymer of peptidomelanin.

Using dansyl chloride as a molecular probe for primary amines, we have established that BroSCO polymerizes L-DOPA into indolic melanin (Figure ). Under our reaction conditions, dansyl chloride will selectively react with primary amines, while remaining nonreactive toward secondary amines (Figure A). Initially, we polymerized L-DOPA using BroSCO, creating L-DOPA melanin (Reaction G1). The same batch of L-DOPA melanin was dansylated, yielding dansylated L-DOPA melanin (Reaction G2). Both polymers (products of Reactions G1 and G2), along with monomeric L-DOPA (Substance G0) were subjected to SEM-EDS in order to determine their nitrogen and sulfur content. All three substances possessed a nitrogen content of ∼ 6–7% (Figure B), showing that most of the nitrogen originating from the primary amine of L-DOPA was retained after polymerization. As expected, dansylation increased the sulfur content of dansylated, L-DOPA melanin (Reaction G2, Figure C). Our previous work (Kolipakala et al. Text S2, Table S2, Figure S15), supported by external studies, confirms that the dansylation reaction efficiency for primary amines in amino acids is ∼ 100%. Therefore, there will exist a 1:1 correspondence between sulfur atoms originating from dansyl, and nitrogen atoms originating from dansylated, primary amines. SEM-EDS raw data for Substance G0, Reactions G1 and G2 is provided in the Supporting Information (Data Set S2).

Together, the nitrogen and sulfur content of dansylated L-DOPA melanin (Reaction G2) could be used to calculate the polymer’s molar ratio of nitrogen:sulfur (Figure D), where we excluded nitrogen originating from dansyl. This ratio was found to be 14.0 ± 8.4:1. From the aforementioned 1:1 primary amine:dansyl correspondence, the ratio of nitrogen forming secondary:primary amines is 13.0 ± 8.4:1. In other words, only 7.1% of the nitrogen content found in dansylated L-DOPA melanin (Reaction G2) remains as primary amines. Following the Raper-Mason pathway, the remaining 92.9% of nitrogen would have undergone cyclization to form undansylatable secondary amines within indolic monomers. We performed Orbitrap mass spectrometry experiments on L-DOPA polymerized using BroSCO (Reaction H1). We detected chemical entities possessing molecular weights in good agreement with the molecular weights of intermediates and products along the Raper-Mason pathway. Chemical entities with molecular weights in good agreement with the theoretical molecular weights of L-DOPA, dopaquinone, leucodopachrome, dopachrome, dihydroxyindole carboxylic acid (DHICA), and dihydroxy indole (DHI) were detected (Figure ). Discussion

In this work, we have described the identification and characterization of BroSCO: a broad spectrum copper oxidase. The AlphaFold2 model of BroSCO possessed a large, solvent accessible type 3 dicopper center (Figure A,B). Using ICP-MS, we determined the Cu:BroSCO stoichiometric ratio to be 2.3 ± 0.5:1, in close agreement with the expected stoichiometric ratio of 2:1, supporting the designation of BroSCO as a “copper”-containing oxidase. BroSCO catalyzes the oxidative copolymerization of cysteine and L-DOPA (Figure , reaction C3), the oxidative copolymerization of a cysteinylated peptide CGGPGDP and L-DOPA (Figure , reaction D3), and the oxidative copolymerization of thiolated compounds with L-DOPA (Figure S4), thus supporting the designation “broad spectrum oxidase”.

Both mushroom tyrosinase (Figure , reaction C2) and BroSCO (Figure , reaction C3) can oxidatively copolymerize cysteine with L-DOPA, forming pheomelanin. Mushroom tyrosinase is well-established as a catalyst for this reaction. It stands to reason that BroSCO copolymerizes cysteine with L-DOPA using the same set of reactions elucidated for the pheomelanin biosynthetic pathway.

Peptide CGGPGDP possesses an N-terminal cysteine that allowed for its BroSCO-catalyzed copolymerization into synthetic peptidomelanin. Unlike mushroom tyrosinase, the large, solvent accessible catalytic site of BroSCO allows it to accommodate bulky substrates like cysteinylated peptides. The peptide chain linked to cysteine’s carboxyl group should not interfere with the formation of benzothiazine intermediates. These benzothiazine intermediates should then copolymerize with the core polymer derived from L-DOPA (formed via the Raper-Mason pathway) to form peptidomelanin.

Additionally, BroSCO can copolymerize thiolated small molecules (thioglycolic acid and dithiothreitol) with L-DOPA (Figure S4). These observations confirm that BroSCO possesses a broad spectrum of catalytic activity, and should therefore be able to copolymerize a wide range of thiolated substrates with L-DOPA, without the formation of benzothiazine intermediates. We therefore infer that a thioether bond (similar to the one present in cysteinyldopa) is sufficient for copolymer formation. Future work will confirm whether BroSCO can copolymerize C-terminal cysteinylated peptides with L-DOPA, without the need for benzothiazine intermediate formation.

Based on the results of our BroSCO-catalyzed oxidative copolymerization experiments (Figure ), and based on established melanogenic pathways, we propose a biosynthetic pathway for the polymerization of peptidomelanin (Figure ). Both mushroom tyrosinase and BroSCO possess type 3 dicopper centers (Figure B,C), with BroSCO also possessing the expected Cu:BroSCO stoichiometric ratio (2.3 ± 0.5:1). BroSCO polymerizes L-DOPA into indolic melanin along the Raper-Mason pathway , as demonstrated using dansylation experiments (Figure ) and Orbitrap mass spectrometry (Figure ). Therefore, during sporogenesis, BroSCO will be able to synthesize 5,6-Dihydroxyindole (DHI) and 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA) from L-DOPA. These monomers are required to form the core polymer of peptidomelanin.

Our proposed peptidomelanin biosynthetic pathway involving BroSCO. BroSCO incorporates cysteinylated peptides into a core polymer derived from L-DOPA, forming peptidomelanin. It is therefore conceivable that the peptidomelanin biosynthetic pathway is analogous to the well-characterized pheomelanin biosynthetic pathway, using a similar set of reactions involving cysteinylated peptides as substrate in place of cysteine. Cysteinyldopa exists as several isomers: 2-S-,5-S-, 6-S-, and 2-S-5-S-cysteinyldopa. For simplicity, we have only depicted the major isomer (5-S-cysteinyldopa) in our reaction pathway. We have also depicted 5,6-Dihydroxyindole (DHI) and not 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA) in our reaction pathway for simplicity.

Peptidomelanin is found only in the spores of A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366), and is only secreted during sporulation. During sporogenesis, we hypothesize that excess cytoplasmic proteins within spore mother cells are proteolyzed to form short peptides, some of which may contain cysteine. BroSCO could then incorporate these cysteinylated peptide fragments into the growing peptidomelanin polymer. This hypothesis would represent the simplest source of peptides for peptidomelanin, but will require further experimentation to validate.

The identification and characterization of BroSCO as the enzyme responsible for peptidomelanin synthesis, to the best of our knowledge, has previously not been reported in the literature. Future work will involve investigating the evolutionary origins of BroSCO. We are currently sequencing recently isolated peptidomelanin secreting strains in order to understand their mechanisms of peptidomelanin synthesis, and to search for possible BroSCO homologues.

Applications for BroSCO include biochemically engineering peptidomelanin variants. We are interested in incorporating peptide chains possessing amino-acid compositions tailored for chelating heavy metals with high affinity and specificity. BroSCO’s insolubility is potentially a result of its fungal origin. E. coli may lack the ability to incorporate post-translational modifications required for correct folding (Figure S2, Table S2). Expressing BroSCO in a fungal host (Komagataella pastoris, formerly Pichia pastoris) may result in a soluble enzyme. We hope to report the in vitro and in vivo synthesis of engineered peptidomelain using soluble BroSCO in a future communication.

Methods

Sequence Data Set Preparation and Phylogenetics

The nucleotide data set was prepared by retrieving tyrosinase sequences from the genomes of A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) and A. niger type strain (MTCC 281). The protein data set was made by translating the nucleotide sequences in MEGA7. The mushroom tyrosinase outgroup sequences (nucleotideGQ354802.1 and proteinACU29458.1) were retrieved from the NCBI database. Both data sets were manually curated to check for stop codons and subsequently aligned with MUSCLE. A maximum likelihood approach was implemented to reconstruct the nucleotide and protein phylogeny using IQTree. The best partition scheme for the nucleotide data (subset into each codon position) and the optimal model for sequence evolution was determined using Model Finder, followed by tree inference in IQTree. Similarly, the best model for amino acid sequence evolution was determined and a phylogenetic tree was constructed. The node support for the topology was estimated with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates using the nearest neighbor interchange (NNI) heuristic for tree optimization. The final trees were visualized using FigTree. Additionally, uncorrected p-distances (pairwise) were calculated for the nucleotide and protein sequences using MEGA7.

Alphafold2 Modeling of A. Niger Melanoliber Tyrosinases

AlphaFold2 was run as a standalone program on a device with the following specifications: AMD Threadripper 3970x, 256GB DDR4 3 GHz, NVIDIA RTX 3080 10 GB. The parameters used to run alphafold are as follows: Commit dbe2a438ebfc6289f9602- 92f15dbf421a05e563d from https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphafold; maximum template date: 2024–05–10; full databases; PDB date: 2024–05–09. The AlphaFold2 models of all tyrosinases, including BroSCO, are provided in the Supporting Information (Data Set S1).

Modeling a Dicopper Center in the brosco Catalytic Center

Molecular docking was performed using Autodock 4.2.6, using the vina force field (Autodock Vina. PDBQT files were prepared for the protein (BroSCO) and ligand (dicopper). The ligand was obtained from PubChem (PubChem ID: 4628300). The grid box was developed with the Autodocking program using the grid creation wizard interface. The grid box was created with reference to the predetermined residues (His-84, His-92, His-101, His-218, His-222, His-244, His-245). A site-specific study focused on the enzyme’s catalytic center (center: x = 3.5 Å, y = −0.97 Å, z = −1.5 Å; position of x = 20 Å, y = 40 Å, z = 20 Å) using the Kollman charges (−17.4) to correctly describe the electrostatics into which the ligand was placed. Vina was run using an interface for commands. BroSCO possessing a modeled dicopper center is provided in the Supporting Information (Data Set S1:).

Gene Synthesis and Cloning

We truncated the BroSCO N-terminal region (MAILEQSLILLVAVLSLEVAAVPH, residues 1–24) predicted to be disordered by AlphaFold2, replacing it with a 6XHIS tag. No attempts were made to synthesize or express untruncated BroSCO. Truncated BroSCO was used for all in vitro experiments, and will simply be referred to as BroSCO. Sequences for both the original and truncated variants of BroSCO are provided in the Supporting Information (Text S1, S2). Gene synthesis and cloning was performed by GenScript Biotech. BroSCO was synthesized and cloned into the pET-22b­(+) vector between the NdeI and XhoI restriction sites.

Peptide Synthesis

Peptide CGGPGDP (50 mg) was synthesized by GenScript Biotech with a purity of 88.1%. QC reports are included in the Supporting Information (Data Set S2).

Preparation and Purification of brosco

Stock

pET-22b­(+) cloned with BroSCO was transformed into E. coli (BL21) and stored as a 1 mL 25% glycerol stock at −80 ◦C.

Starter Culture

A small portion of this glycerol stock was inoculated into 50 mL of LB broth supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and incubated at 37 ◦C/48h/180 rpm to form the starter culture.

Preparatory Culture

A 10 mL aliquot of this starter culture was transferred to 5 separate flasks, each containing 50 mL of LB broth supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 1 mM Isopropyl-beta-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), for a total culture volume of 300 mL. The cultures were incubated at 37 ◦C/12 h/180 rpm to induce BroSCO production.

Protein Extraction

300 mL of preparatory culture was transferred to 6 × 50 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 7800 rpm/15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the individual pellets washed twice with 40 mL of 100 mM tris at pH 7.0 each time. The washed pellets were pooled and collectively resuspended in 20 mL of 100 mM tris at pH 8.0. This suspension was sonicated for 20 min (5 s pulse on, 2 s pulse off, 50% power rate, Prog@Probe: 1@4) using an ultrasonicator (Labman, PRO650). The sonicate was centrifuged at 7800 rpm/15 min. BroSCO was found to be insoluble and was only present in the pellet (Figure A). The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was dissolved in 10 mL of 8 M urea while heating in a water bath at 100 ◦C/1 h (P8MU).

Protein Purification

We developed a technique we term “watering out” to purify BroSCO from other proteins in the P8MU extract. Let the volume of P8MU be x. We added x volume of deionized water (Milli-Q) to P8MU, creating P4MU with volume 2x and a urea concentration of 4 M. We centrifuged P4MU at 7800 rpm/15 min to obtain a pellet (P4MU-pellet) and supernatant (P4MU-supernatant). P4MU-pellet was retained and stored at 4 ◦C. P4MU-supernatant was further diluted with 2x volume of deionized water (Milli-Q) to create P2MU with a volume of 4x and a urea concentration of 2 M. We centrifuged P2MU at 7800 rpm/15 min to obtain a pellet (P2MU-pellet) and supernatant (P2MU-supernatant). P2MU-pellet was retained and stored at 4 ◦C. P2MU-supernatant was further diluted with 4x volume of deionized water (Milli-Q) to create P1MU with a volume of 8x and a urea concentration of 1 M. We centrifuged P1MU at 7800 rpm/15 min to obtain a pellet (P1MU-pellet) and supernatant (P1MU-supernatant). P1MU-pellet and P1MU-supernatant were retained and stored at 4 ◦C. P4MU-pellet, P2MU-pellet, P1MU-pellet, and P1MU-supernatant were run on an SDS-PAGE gel to determine the amount and purity of BroSCO (∼35 kDa).

BroSCO typically precipitates out in pellet-P4MU and/or pellet-P2MU, with slight variations for every protein preparation. Pellet(s) containing BroSCO were pooled and resuspended in 10 mL of 8 M urea while heating in a water bath at 100 ◦C/1 h (P8MU_f). P8MU_f was first dialyzed against 1L of 100 mM tris (pH 7.0) + 1 mM CuSO4.5H2O across a 3 kDa membrane for 24 h to replenish the dicopper catalytic center. P8MU_f was then dialyzed twice against 2 × 1 L of 100 mM tris (pH 7.0) across a 3 kDa dialysis membrane for 2 × 24 h to remove excess urea and copper. After dialysis, P8MU_f was centrifuged at 7800 rpm/15 min to obtain a pellet (Figure B: BroSCO pellet) and supernatant (Figure B: Resolubilized BroSCO). The supernatant was used for all further experiments.

This protocol is illustrated in the Supporting Information (Figure S1).

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE) Experiments

SDS-PAGE experiments were performed using the running (resolving) gel, stacking gel and running buffer composition as described in our previous work (Kolipakala et. al, Table 2).

Before loading onto an SDS-PAGE gel, BroSCO samples were prepared under nonreducing (Figure A,B), and dithiothreitol (DTT)-reducing environments (Figure C,E,F).

To prepare BroSCO under a nonreducing environment, 100 μL of the protein sample +10 μL of 10% SDS was boiled at 100 ◦C until a final volume of 20 μL was obtained. To this volume, we added 10 μL of loading dye containing 0.2 M tris-HCl, 277 mM SDS, 6 mM bromophenol blue, and 4.3 M glycerol.

To this volume, we added 10 μL of loading dye containing 0.2 M tris-HCl, 277 mM SDS, 6 mM bromophenol blue, and 4.3 M glycerol.

All SDS-PAGE gels were run, stained, and destained using protocols described in our previous work (Kolipakala et. al.)

ICP-MS analysis to determine Lithium concentrations in the parts per billion (ppb) range was carried out by Eurofins India using a PerkinElmer 350X instrument. 50 mL samples of Li-bound CGGPGDP-melanin treated in 20% nitric acid were sent for analysis. The instrument was configured specifically for elemental lithium detection. Data acquisition and interpretation were performed using Syngistix software (version 4.0, PerkinElmer).

Orbitrap Mass Spectrometry

A 10 mg/mL stock of L-DOPA in deionized water (Milli-Q) was prepared. 400 μL of this stock was mixed with 100 μL of 0.1 mg/mL BroSCO (prepared in 100 mM tris at pH 7.0) and incubated at 37 ◦C for 24 h to enzymatically synthesize melanin. The reaction was quenched by adding 12 N HCl, to a final concentration of 0.5%. The reaction mixture was passed through a centrifugal concentrator with a 3 kDa molecular weight cutoff. This reaction mixture (Reaction H1) was analyzed using Orbitrap mass spectrometry. This reaction mixture was expected to contain L-DOPA substrate and other intermediates along the Raper-Mason pathway.

Orbitrap mass spectrometry was performed at the Liquid Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (LCMS) Facility at the Indian Institute of Science (Bangalore). An Orbitrap Fusion (Tribrid Series) instrument was used. Our sample (Reaction H1) was subjected to ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) using a Vanquish UHPLC system, and a Hypersil Gold column (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The column possessed a length of 100 mm, internal diameter of 2.1 mm, and a particle size of 1.9 μm. The mobile phase contained two solvents. Solvent A: 100% water +0.1% formic acid, and solvent B: 100% acetonitrile +0.1% formic acid. Retention time (RT) and MS1 data was provided for all chemical entities observed.

Nanodrop Protein Concentration Estimation

Ten μL of BroSCO dialyzed (using a membrane with a 3 kDa cutoff) against 100 mM tris at pH 7.0 was loaded into a NanoDrop 2000c UV–vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Protein concentration was estimated using absorbance at 280 nm. Readings were collected after buffer subtraction using dialyzate.

ClustalW Alignment

All gene and protein alignment scores (based on substitution matrix and gap penalties) were generated using ClustalW (version 2.1, https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw). We used the following pairwise alignment parameters (slow/accurate): gap open penalty = 10, gap extension penalty = 0.1, weight matrix = BLOSUM. We performed 1 vs 1 pairwise alignments for all tyrosinase genes and proteins annotated on the genomes of A. niger (MTCC 281) against A. niger melanoliber (MTCC 13366) and reported the alignment scores in Table S1.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

All data were processed, analyzed, and visualized using the R programming language (4.1.2 (2021–11–01) – “Bird Hippie”). All plots in Figures A–B-D were generated using using R. For all reactions, statistical significance testing was performed using Student’s t test, using the inbuilt function t.test (). For substances/reactions G0-G2 (Figure B), statistical significance testing was performed using the two-tailed one-sample Student’s t test, comparing our experimental elemental composition against a single theoretical value, using the inbuilt function t.test­().
