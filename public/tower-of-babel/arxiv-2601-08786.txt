Simple repair policies and decompositions for semi-coherent systems with simultaneous failures
Guido Lagos, Jorge Navarro, Héctor Olivero

Abstract
We consider semi-coherent binary systems that are subject to simultaneous failures of its components. These are systems whose components can be either working or failed; the system can also be working or failed depending on the state of the components; and repairing a component cannot cause the system to fail. We consider that one or more components can fail simultaneously, allowing us to model external shocks and disasters. For this, we use the Lévy-frailty Marshall-Olkin (LFMO) multivariate distribution to model the failure times of the components. We aim to answer in which states of the system we should repair the components. This is a challenging question, as the number of repair policies grows super-exponentially in the number of components. To tackle this, we propose a simple family of repair policies, which we call r r -out-of- n n :R repair policies , where one repairs all failed components when the system fails or when there are r r or more failed components. Our main contribution is that we derive exact and simple expressions for key performance-evaluation quantities of the system operating under our proposed repair policies. That is, we give explicit expressions for the mean time-to-failure of the system, mean time-to-repair, probability of system-failure before repair, and component- and system-repair rate. We also give expressions for the expected cost and long-term average cost, when there are components’ and system repair cost. The only relevant parameters involved in the derived expressions are the structural signature of the system, and the Laplace exponent associated to the LFMO distribution.



1
Introduction

In this paper we develop exact expressions for a class of repair policies when a binary system is subject to simultaneous failures of its components.
We do this for
binary systems
with
semi-coherent structure
, that is, systems whose components can be either working or failed, and where repairing (respectively, breaking down) a component cannot break down (respectively, repair) the system; see Figure
1
for a basic example.
To make the model more realistic, we assume that one or more components can fail simultaneously, for example, due to an external shock that takes down several components at once.
We model this using the
Lévy-frailty Marshall-Olkin
(LFMO) distribution for the times of failure of components.
This is a Markovian model that considers a degradation process common to all components, and an individual tolerance to degradation for each component.
In this setting, we consider repair policies where we either repair all failed components, or continue operating with the system as-is.

The main question we tackle is
in which states of the system should we repair the failed components?
This is a particularly challenging question, as there is an
exponential
explosion of complexity with the number
n
of components of the system: as we illustrate in the basic example in Figure
1
, when the system has
n
components, there are
2^{n}
states of the components of the system,
3^{n}-2^{n}
possible transitions due to simultaneous failures, and
2^{2^{n}}
possible repair policies

In rigor, there are
2^{2^{n}-1-N_{\text{sys-failed}}}
policies, where
N_{\text{sys-failed}}
is the number of states where the system is failed; since we cannot repair components when none has failed, and it makes no sense to continue working with a system that has already failed, i.e.,
2^{n}-1-N_{\text{sys-failed}}
is the number of states where there are failed components but the system continues working.
.

To tackle this challenge, we consider a family of simple repair policies, that we call

The main result of this paper is that we show explicit expressions for key performance evaluation quantities of the system operating under our proposed repair policies. That is, we give explicit expressions for the mean time-to-failure of the system, mean time-to-repair, probability of system-failure before repair, component- and system-repair rate, to name a few. We also give explicit formulas for the long-term mean cost when there is a cost for repairing components, and repairing a failed system incurs an additional system-repair cost due to operational interruption.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first result of this type for semi-coherent systems and for simultaneous failures.

Main contributions

The main contributions of this work are the following:

1.

We propose simple repair policies, that we call
r
-out-of-
n
:R repair policies, and show explicit mathematical formulas for key performance indicators (e.g., mean time-to-failure, probability of failure before repair, etc.) of general semi-coherent systems operating under these policies, when it is subject to simultaneous failures of its components. We do this using the LFMO distribution for the failure times of the components. Our formulas involve only the structural signature of the system and the Laplace exponent of the underlying Lévy subordinator.
As far as the authors know, this is the first time that explicit expressions have been derived for general semi-coherent systems with simultaneous failures.

2.

As particular cases, we also obtain explicit expressions in two cases of interest in the literature: the case of
k
-out-of-
n
:F systems with LFMO distributed failure times of its components (see Proposition
2.1
); and the case of general semi-coherent systems with iid exponentially distributed failure times of its components (see Corollary
3.1
).

3.

We define the
process signature
of the system as the probability distribution of the number of failed components when the system fails. We derive explicit expressions in the setting of semi-coherent systems with LFMO distributed components’ lifetimes. We do this wheren there is no repair policy (see Proposition
2.3
) and when an
r
-out-of-
n
:R policy is in operation (see Theorem
3.1
).

4.

From a methodology perspective, our analysis extends the classical decomposition result of Samaniego
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, to decompose further quantities of interest, such as the number of failed components, the times of repair, and costs; see Section
3.2
.
This is an important development, as the Samaniego result is a mainstay in the probabilistic analysis of system reliability, which, in part, has greatly motivated the study of systems’ signatures.
In addition, our results are heavily based on the exchangeability property of the LFMO distribution, so our work opens new research venues to more general exchangeable distributions.

1.1
Literature review

The formal study of binary reliability systems started in the late 1950s, see
[
4
,
5
]
, with its study driven by studying structure functions, redundancy, and properties such as monotonicity and coherency; see e.g.
[
6
]
and the recent survey
[
7
]
. An important development was the definition and study of the signature of a system in
[
1
]
, which allows to analyze mean failure times, aging properties, and residual lifetimes, see the survey
[
8
]
, and more recently in
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, to approximate the behavior of either large times or for large systems.

For the modeling of the simultaneous failures of components, we use the Lévy-frailty Marshall-Olkin distribution, originally proposed as a copula in
[
12
]
and later extended to a multivariate distribution with exchangeable components in
[
13
]
. It is a particular case of the Marshall-Olkin distribution, originally proposed in the late 1960s in
[
14
]
, which is a classic model for simultaneous failures in reliability modeling. Other multivariate distributions used to model simultaneous failures are multivariate additive processes
[
15
]
, shock models
[
16
]
, and especially copulas, see
[
17
]
and references therein.

The literature on mathematical modeling of repairable systems is considerable. Some approaches in the literature are age-based policies, where the average cost is optimized based on the age of the system, see
[
18
]
and references therein; also conditioned-based maintenance (CBM) where there is a stochastic process modeling the degradation of the system and a maintenance is programmed based on the state of the degradation, see
[
19
]
; and so-called opportunistic maintenance, where system failures are used to replace other components that are operative but have a high probability of failure, see
[
20
]
. Other classical approaches are imperfect repairs, where a repair does not restore components or the system to be “as good as new” condition.
See
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
and the survey
[
24
]
for overviews of maintenance topics in Reliability Theory.

Deriving expressions for the mean cost rate to optimize it is a central theme in Reliability Theory; however, there are no works proposing formulas for these quantities in a general setting of monotonous or coherent systems with simultaneous failures. Indeed, the following works have explored cost rate policies and expressions, but for specific coherent structures such as series, parallel,
k
-out-of-
n
:F, and related systems. The paper
[
25
]
considers age-based policies and derives conditions for general coherent systems under discrete iid components’ lifetimes; however, they derive explicit expressions only for
k
-out-of-
n
and linear consecutive-
k
-out-of-
n
:F systems. Analogously,
[
18
]
considers series and parallel systems with dependent components, modeled using general copulas, and
[
26
]
considers parallel systems with dependent exchangeable distribution of components’ lifetimes.
See the references therein for further works on replacement policies.
However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are no works in the literature proposing the simple policies we present in this work, nor deriving general formulas for semi-coherent systems with simultaneous failures, as we do here.

2.1
Lifetimes of components

Throughout this work, we consider a system with
n>1
components, where each component can be in a working or failed state. For each component
i=1,\ldots,n
, denote by
T_{i}
the random time at which it fails. We want to model that several components can fail simultaneously, say because of degradation shocks that hit the system, and for that we focus on the joint distribution of the random vector of lifetimes
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. A classical model for this is the following Marshall-Olkin (exponential) distribution, originally proposed in
[
14
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Marshall-Olkin distribution)
.

A random vector
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to have a
Marshall-Olkin
(MO) distribution if

\displaystyle T_{i}=\min_{V\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\,:\,i\in V}X_{V},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

where, for all
V\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
,
X_{V}
is an exponential random variable with parameter
\lambda_{V}\geq 0
, and is independent of the other random variables.

The random variables
X_{V}
represent the time of arrival of a shock that simultaneously hits all components in the set
V
. Hence, (
1
) specifies that the time of failure of a component is the first arrival time of any of the shocks that hit it. It is known that the resulting distribution for
\mathbf{T}
has a multidimensional version of the memoryless property of exponential random variables, see e.g.
[
27
, Section 3.1]
, and thus induces a Markovian structure on the failure times. However, a considerable drawback of the MO distribution is its
parametric complexity
— we need to specify the value of the parameters
\lambda_{V}\geq 0
for all
V\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
,
V\neq\emptyset
, i.e., a total of
2^{n}-1
parameters.

A particular subfamily of the MO family is the one of
exchangeable
Marshall-Olkin (eMO) distributions, that hold when
\lambda_{U}=\lambda_{V}
whenever
|U|=|V|
, i.e., they have the same cardinal; and that, indeed, induce a random vector
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
with exchangeable components.
A further subfamily of the eMO distributions is the following, proposed in
[
12
,
28
]
.

Some important properties of the LFMO distribution are the following.
First, marginally, the failure times of the components

\mathbb{E}[T_{i}]=1/\Psi(1)

Second, as argued in

\displaystyle\lambda^{(n)}_{V}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{|V|-1}\binom{|V|-1}{i}(-1)^{i}\left(\Psi(n-|V|+i+1)-\Psi(n-|V|+i)\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=n-|V|}^{n}(-1)^{j-n+|V|+1}\binom{|V|}{n-j}\Psi(j).

(4)

Note that
\lambda^{(n)}_{V}
only depends on

. Another important property is that an LFMO distributed random vector

has a so-called

(EXC) distribution, meaning that for all permutation

(T_{1},\dots,T_{n})

In fact it is

Also, the joint reliability function of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{1}>t_{1},\dots,T_{n}>t_{n})=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\sum_{\emptyset\neq V\subset\{1,\dots,n\}}\lambda^{(n)}_{V}\max_{i\in V}t_{i}\right),

\Psi(0)=0

[

More important for reliability modeling, the LFMO distribution has low parametric complexity: the only parameters needed are the number
n
of components of the system and the Lévy subordinator process
\mathbf{L}
, which in turn is fully characterized by its Laplace exponent function
\Psi(x)
, see
[
29

Even more, the only information of the LFMO distribution that is needed for the results in this paper is the values of
\Psi(1)
, …,
\Psi(n)

Some examples of Lévy subordinator
\mathbf{L}
are the following.
The most basic one is choosing
L_{t}=\mu t
for some
\mu>0
.
In this case we obtain that the components’ lifetimes are iid exponential random variables with rate
\mu
.
Another important class is the family of compound Poisson processes with non-negative drift
\mu\geq 0
, rate
\lambda>0
and some non-negative jumps distribution, say
\mathcal{J}
; i.e., when the degradation process is
L_{t}=\mu t+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{t}}J_{j}
for all
t>0
, where
\mathbf{N}
is a Poisson process with rate
\lambda>0
and
J_{j}
are iid non-negative random variables with distribution
\mathcal{J}
.
In that case, the Laplace exponent function is
\Psi(x)=\mu x+\lambda(1-\mathbb{E}[e^{-xJ_{1}}])
. This case can be informally described as there being a steady degradation making that, nominally, each component fails after an expected time of
1/\mu
; however, on average every
1/\lambda
time units there is a degradation shock that can make several components fail at once, and where each surviving component fails with probability
p:=1-\mathbb{E}[e^{-J_{1}}]
. In fact, for
1\leq l\leq k\leq n
, if there are
k

J_{j}
kills
l

\binom{k}{l}p^{l}(1-p)^{k-l}
.
We note that compound Poisson processes are referred to as
finite activity
processes, as the number of jumps in any finite time interval is finite almost surely.
In contrast, some classical examples of
infinite activity
subordinators (i.e., almost surely infinite jumps on any finite time interval) are Gamma processes, whose Laplace exponent is
\Psi(x)=\beta\log(1+x/\eta)
for parameters
\beta,\eta>0
; inverse Gaussian processes, with
\Psi(x)=\beta(\sqrt{2x+\eta^{2}}-\eta)
for
\beta,\eta>0
; and stable subordinators for
\alpha
in
(0,1)
, with
\Psi(x)=x^{\alpha}
.
Importantly, even though infinite activity subordinators cannot be simulated without bias due to the discrete nature of computers, the only information we need to compute our results are the values of
\Psi(1)
, …,
\Psi(n)
.
See
[
27
, Annex A.2]
for a brief summary and properties of subordinators.

Order statistics of the LFMO distribution

An element of the LFMO distribution that is crucial for our work is the order statistics of the components’ failure times
T_{1}
, …,
T_{n}
, denoted by
T_{1:n}
,
T_{2:n}
, …,
T_{n:n}
. That is,
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\}=\{T_{1:n},\ldots,T_{n:n}\}
and
T_{1:n}\leq T_{2:n}\leq\ldots\leq T_{n:n}
.
From the reliability perspective,
T_{k:n}
is the time of failure of the
k
-th component that fails.
From
[
9

we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{k:n}>t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\binom{i-1}{n-k}(-1)^{i-n+k-1}e^{-t\Psi(i)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}T_{k:n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\binom{i-1}{n-k}(-1)^{i-n+k-1}\frac{1}{\Psi(i)}

(5)

for
1\leq k\leq n
. See
[
9
,
10
]
for further results on the approximation of lower-, middle- and upper-order statistics of the LFMO distribution.

In particular, note that using the latter notation,
T_{1:n}
is the failure time of a series system with
n
components whose lifetimes are LFMO distributed. In this case, the reliability function is

\mathbb{P}(T_{1:n}>t)=\exp\left(-\Psi(n)t\right)

In general, the series system of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(t)=k\right)=\sum_{i=n-k}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\binom{i}{n-k}(-1)^{i-n+k}e^{-t\Psi(i)}.

Alternatively,
\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k:n}>t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(N(t)<k\right)
and
\mathbb{P}\left(N(t)=k\right)=\mathbb{P}(T_{k+1:n}>t)-\mathbb{P}(T_{k:n}>t)
with
T_{n+1:n}=+\infty
.
In fact,
\mathbf{N}
is a continuous-time Markov chain, due to the memoryless and Markov properties, respectively, of the triggers
\varepsilon_{i}
and the Lévy subordinator
\mathbf{L}
.
It also holds that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}N(t)=n
.

An important fact, that we use profusely in our results, is that there is a “dual” relation between the ordered failure times
T_{k:n}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, and the number
N(T_{k:n})
of failed components at these times. Indeed, it holds that
\{N(T_{r:n})<k\}=\{T_{r:n}<T_{k:n}\}
,
\{N(T_{r:n})\geq k\}=\{T_{r:n}\geq T_{k:n}\}
and
\{N(T_{r:n})=k\}=\{T_{r:n}=T_{k:n}<T_{k+1:n}\}
.
The results (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
) below can be viewed as probabilities exploiting this connection.

We also consider the associated discrete-time Markov chain
(N^{disc}_{i}\,:\,i=0,1,\ldots)
that counts the number of failed components after the
i
-th time of failure of components, i.e.,
N^{disc}_{i}=N(\tau_{i})
where
\tau_{0}:=0
and
\tau_{i+1}:=\inf\{t>\tau_{i}:\Delta N(t):=N(t)-N(t^{-})>0\}
for all

, with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
.
Note that
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\ldots
and
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
, …correspond to the times at which

components fail, because several components can fail simultaneously. For instance, in the example shown in Figure

we see that
\tau_{1}=T_{1}=T_{3}<\tau_{2}=T_{2}
, so

,

and

.
It holds that

is a discrete-time Markov chain over the state space

, and the chain is absorbed at state

The following lemma establishes several key results and quantities that we use in our results.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider a system with
n
components where the components’ lifetimes follow a LFMO distribution with Laplace exponent function
\Psi
. Recall the definition (
3
) for the rates
\lambda^{(l)}_{k}
, for any
1\leq k\leq l\leq n
.

1.

For the continuous-time Markov chain
\mathbf{N}=(N(t)\,:\,t\geq 0)
of number of failed components at each time, the rate of the transition from having
i
to
j
failed components is

\binom{n-i}{j-i}\lambda_{j-i}^{(n-i)}=\sum_{k=i}^{j}(-1)^{j-k+1}\frac{\binom{n}{j}\binom{j}{k}\binom{k}{i}}{\binom{n}{i}}\Psi(n-k)

for
0\leq i<j\leq n
and zero otherwise.
That is, for
i<j
, given that the system with
n
components has
i
of them failed, the time until the next arrival of any shock that simultaneously hits
j-i
of the remaining
n-i
working components, is distributed exponential with rate
\binom{n-i}{j-i}\lambda_{j-i}^{(n-i)}
.

2.

For the discrete-time Markov chain
\mathbf{N^{disc}}=(N^{disc}_{i}\,:\,i=0,1,\ldots)
of number of failed components after the
i
-th time of failure of components, the probability
P_{i,j}
of making a (one-step) transition from having
i
to
j
failed components (i.e., given that the system with
n
components has
i
of them failed, next arrives a shock that hits
j-i
of the working components) is

\displaystyle P_{i,j}=

\displaystyle\binom{n-i}{j-i}\frac{\lambda^{(n-i)}_{j-i}}{\Psi(n-i)}=\sum_{k=i}^{j}(-1)^{j-k+1}\frac{\binom{n}{j}\binom{j}{k}\binom{k}{i}}{\binom{n}{i}}\frac{\Psi(n-k)}{\Psi(n-i)}

(6)

for
0\leq i<j\leq n
and zero otherwise.

3.

For
0\leq i<j\leq k\leq n
, denote by
F_{[0,i],\,j}
(respectively,
F_{[0,i],\,[j,k]}
) the probability that the discrete-time chain
\mathbf{N^{disc}}
of number of failed components, goes from having zero to
i
failed components in one or more steps, and then jumps
in one step
from the set
\{0,\ldots,i\}
into the state
j
(respectively, into the set
\{j,\ldots,k\}
).
It holds that
F_{[0,i],\,j}
it satisfies the following recursive formula:

\displaystyle F_{[0,i],\,j}=\begin{cases}P_{0,j}&\text{for $i=0$ and $j>i$ }\\
F_{[0,i-1],\,j}+F_{[0,i-1],\,i}\,P_{i,j}&\text{for $i\geq 1$ and $j>i$}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(7)

and
F_{[0,i],\,[j,k]}=\sum_{l=j}^{k}F_{[0,i],\,l}
.

4.

Denote by
F_{i,\,j}
the probability that the chain
\mathbf{N^{disc}}
goes from having zero to
i
failed components in one or more steps, and then jumps,
in one step
, from
i
to
j
failed components. It holds that
F_{i,\,j}=\sum_{l=0}^{i}(P^{l})_{0,i}P_{i,j}
for
0\leq i<j\leq n
and 0 otherwise, where
(P^{l})_{0,i}
is the component
(0,i)
of the matrix
P^{l}
, and the matrix
P=(P_{i,j}:i,j=0,\ldots,n)
has the probabilities
P_{i,j}
defined in (
6
). Also,
F_{[0,i],\,j}=\sum_{l_{1}=0}^{i}F_{l_{1},\,j}
and
F_{[0,i],\,[j,k]}=\sum_{l_{1}=0}^{i}\sum_{l_{2}=j}^{k}F_{l_{1},\,l_{2}}
.

5.

For
1\leq r,k\leq n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}<T_{k:n})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F_{[0,r-1],\,[r,k-1]}&\text{for }r<k\\
0&\text{for }r\geq k\end{cases}

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}=T_{k:n})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F_{[0,r-1],\,[k,n]}&\text{for }r<k\\
1&\text{for }r=k\end{cases}

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{r:n})=k\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F_{[0,r-1],\,k}&\text{for }r\leq k\\
0&\text{for }r>k.\end{cases}

(10)

In the rest of the paper, our results will be mostly expressed using the probabilities
\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}<T_{k:n})
,
\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}=T_{k:n})
and
\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{r:n})=k\right)

shows that these reduce to the terms

and

(see Part 5.), and also gives two ways of computing the latter: a recursive formula in Part 3., and a power matrix formula in Part 4.
Also note that, from the computational perspective, the only information of the LFMO distribution needed to compute the probabilities in Lemma

are the values of
\Psi(1)
, …,
\Psi(n)
.

We also note that Parts 1. and 2. of Lemma
2.1
were already observed in
[

in giving an efficient simulation algorithm for the failure times of the components. The latter is, in essence, that if there are
i

; then sample the new number of failed components

in

Spoiler of simple repair policies

To give a glimpse of the main result of this paper, Theorem

Its proof is direct from Theorem

Proposition 2.1
.

Consider a
k

n
:F system. Assume that its components’ lifetimes follow an LFMO distribution. Further assume that we can repair (instantaneously) all the failed components, at cost
c_{cmp}(j)
for repairing

if the system has also failed. Lastly, consider a repair policy in which all failed components are instantly repaired when the system fails, or when
r

1.

The probability
p^{(r)}

2.

Letting

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{C[0,t]}{t}=\frac{p^{(r)}c_{sys}+\sum_{j=k\wedge r}^{n}F_{[0,(k\wedge r)-1],\,j}\,c_{cmp}(j)}{\mathbb{E}\left[T_{k\wedge r:n}\right]}\quad\text{a.s.,}

and
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{k\wedge r:n}\right]/p^{(r)}
.

4.

The rate at which repairs, system failures, and components’ failures occur, are

System structure

We consider a
binary system
with
n

\mathbf{x}

\{0,1\}^{n}

We assume that there is a deterministic so-called

Definition 2.3

(Monotone, semi-coherent and coherent systems)
.

Consider a system with
n>1

\Phi:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}

1.

The system is

2.

The system is

\Phi(\mathbf{1})=1

3.

The component

\mathbf{x}\rvert_{x_{i}=y}
denotes the state
\mathbf{x}
but with
x_{i}
replaced by
y
.

4.

The system
has no irrelevant components
iff none of its components is irrelevant.

5.

The system is
coherent
iff it is monotone and has no irrelevant components.

Note that a semi-coherent system is just a non-trivial monotone system, in the sense that it excludes the two trivial cases of
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=0
for all
\mathbf{x}

3
Cost of simple repair policies

In this section we give our main result for the long-term mean cost of simple repair policies. For that, we consider the following cost and repair structure.

Assumptions

1.

The repair of
j
failed components costs
c_{cmp}(j)
.

2.

The repair of a failed system costs
c_{sys}
plus the cost of repairing all failed components.

3.

Any failure of a component or the system is detected instantaneously.

4.

Both types of repairs, i.e., of the system and of components, are performed instantly.

5.

At time
t=0
the system starts with all its components working.

In other words, for example, the
preventive
repair of a system that is still working but has two failed components, costs
c_{cmp}(2)
; however, the
corrective
repair of a system that failed and has three failed components, costs
c_{sys}+c_{cmp}(3)
.
In this way, the system repair cost
c_{sys}
models the additional loss in productivity of the system.

We will consider the following simple repair policies that do not discriminate on which components —either critical or not, in whichever sense— have failed, and only use the number of failed components.

Definition 3.1
.

For a system with
n>1
components, and for any
r
in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we define the
r
-out-of-
n
:R repair policy
as the one where all failed components, and the system itself if failed, is repaired in either of the following cases:

1.

when
r
or more components fail;

2.

when the system fails.

In a system operating under an
r
-out-of-
n
:R repair policy, denote by
T_{fail}^{(r)}
and
T_{rep}^{(r)}
the first time of system failure and repair, respectively.

Note that two events can happen at the first repair time
T_{rep}^{(r)}
: either the system failed, in which case it holds that
T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}
; or the system could have continued working with the failed components, in which case
T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}
.
Hence —recalling that
N(t)
is the number of failed components at time
t
—, at the repair time
T_{rep}^{(r)}
the cost incurred is
c_{cmp}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)}))
, plus
c_{sys}
if the system also failed, i.e.,
c_{cmp}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)}))+c_{sys}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right\}}
.

The following is the main result of this paper. Its proof is deferred to Section
5
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider a semi-coherent system with
n>1
components and signature
\mathbf{s}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and assume that its components’ lifetimes follow a LFMO distribution with Laplace exponent function
\Psi
. Assume that the system operates under an
r
-out-of-
n
:R repair policy, for a given
r
in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Let
T_{fail}^{(r)}
and
T_{rep}^{(r)}
be as in Definition
3.1
.
Denote by
N(t)
the number of failed components at time
t>0
, and
C[0,t]
the total cost incurred by operating the system during the time interval
[0,t]
.
Then the following results hold.

1.

Denoting by
p^{(r)}:=\mathbb{P}\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)
the probability that the first repair is due to a system failure, it holds that

\displaystyle p^{(r)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k\wedge r:n}=T_{k:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}s_{k}+\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}s_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{r:n}=T_{k:n}\right),

(16)

\displaystyle 1-p^{(r)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k\wedge r:n}<T_{k:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}s_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{r:n}<T_{k:n}\right).

(17)

where
\mathbb{P}\left(T_{r:n}=T_{k:n}\right)
and
\mathbb{P}\left(T_{r:n}<T_{k:n}\right)
are computed as shown in (
9
) and (
8
), respectively.

2.

The probability that at the time of repair there are exactly
j
failed components, for
1\leq j\leq n
, is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j\mathbf{1}_{\left\{j<r\right\}}+n\mathbf{1}_{\left\{j\geq r\right\}}}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right),

(18)

where the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right)
is computed as shown in (
10
).

3.

When
p^{(r)}>0
, for
j=1,\ \ldots,\ n
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\left|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{fail}^{(r)})=j\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right);

(20)

and for
j=r,\,\ldots,\,n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\left|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-p^{(r)}}\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r:n}\right)=j\right)

(21)

where
\sum_{k=n+1}^{n}
is zero, per usual convention; while
\mathbb{P}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)})=0
for
j<r
.

4.

When
p^{(r)}>0
the system will eventually fail, i.e.,
T_{fail}^{(r)}<+\infty
almost surely, and we have that the long-term mean cost of the system operating under this repair policy is

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{C[0,t]}{t}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{fail}^{(r)}]}{\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]}{\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}}\qquad\text{a.s.,}

(22)

where the mean costs
\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{fail}^{(r)}]
and
\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]
, and mean times
\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}
and
\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}
, satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{fail}^{(r)}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]=c_{sys}+\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right)

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}=\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\left[T_{k\wedge r:n}\right]

(24)

where
p^{(r)}
,
\mathbb{P}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j)
and
\mathbb{E}[T_{k\wedge r:n}]
are computed in (
16
), (
18
) and (
5
), respectively.

5.

When
p^{(r)}=0
the system never fails, i.e.,
T_{fail}^{(r)}=+\infty
almost surely, and it holds that
\lim_{t\to\infty}C[0,t]/t=\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]/\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}
, where
\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right)
and also
\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\left[T_{k\wedge r:n}\right]
.

We remark that, as a direct corollary, the rate of occurrence of system failures, repairs, and components’ failures, are (respectively)

and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right)/\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}

Also, the previous result holds for any

We also note that it is not unusual that

Finally, we note that (

Corollary of i.i.d. components.

A direct corollary of Theorem

Corollary 3.1

1.

2.

The expected time and cost, respectively, until the first repair, when starting with all components working, is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]

\displaystyle=\quad\ \ \sum_{k=1}^{r}s_{k}(c_{sys}+c_{cmp}(k))+\ \left(\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}s_{k}\right)c_{cmp}(r)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{r}s_{k}\left(\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}\right)\quad+\ \left(\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}s_{k}\right)\left(\sum_{i=n-r+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}\right)\right].

\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}=\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}/p^{(r)}

. We group the proofs according to each section.

5.1
Proofs regarding the LFMO distribution

Proof of Lemma
2.1
in Section
2.1
.

To prove 1., we start by noting that the LFMO distribution can alternatively be defined as the MO distribution in (
1
) with the rates
\lambda^{(n)}_{|V|}
in (
3
); see e.g.
[
27
, (3.3), p. 103]
.
Hence, by the memoryless property of exponential random variables, when there are
n-i
components alive, the time until the arrival of the next shock that takes down a given subset of
j-i
of these working components is distributed exponential with rate
\lambda^{(n-i)}_{j-i}
.
Furthermore, there are
\binom{n-i}{j-i}
of these possible subsets of
j-i
components of the
n-i
working ones, so the time until the arrival of any of these
\binom{n-i}{j-i}
shocks is
\binom{n-i}{j-i}\lambda^{(n-i)}_{j-i}
. We conclude by noting that
\binom{n-i}{j-i}\lambda_{j-i}^{(n-i)}=\sum_{k=i}^{j}(-1)^{j-k+1}\binom{n}{j}\binom{j}{k}\binom{k}{i}\Psi(n-k)/\binom{n}{i}
for
0\leq i<j\leq n
, by (
4
).

To prove 2., it is sufficient to note that, by basic properties of Markov chains,
P_{i,j}
is equal to the rate
\binom{n-i}{j-i}\lambda^{(n-i)}_{j-i}
from Part 1., divided by the sum of all the outgoing rates from state
i
.
Defining
\Delta\Psi(m):=\Psi(m+1)-\Psi(m)
, it holds that the latter sum of outgoing rates is

\displaystyle\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\binom{n-i}{k-i}\lambda^{(n-i)}_{k-i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-i}\binom{n-i}{k}\lambda^{(n-i)}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-i}\binom{n-i}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\binom{k-1}{l}(-1)^{l}\Delta\Psi(n-i-k+l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-i}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{n-i}{k}\binom{k}{l}(-1)^{l+1}\Psi(n-i-k+l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{n-i}\Psi(n-i-p)\sum_{q=\max(p,1)}^{n-i}(-1)^{q-p-1}\binom{n-i}{q}\binom{q}{p}

\displaystyle=\Psi(n-i)\sum_{q=1}^{n-i}(-1)^{q-1}\binom{n-i}{q}-\sum_{p=1}^{n-i}\Psi(n-i-p)\binom{n-i}{p}(1-1)^{n-i-p}

\displaystyle=\Psi(n-i)-0,

which proves (
6
).

For 3., (
7
) comes from Part 2. and basic properties of Markov chains, conditioning on the jump that takes the chain into the state
j
or the set
\{j,\ldots,k\}
.

To prove 4., for the formula for
F_{i,\,j}
, note that due to the basic properties of Markov chains and the process starting from zero,
F_{i,\,j}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(P^{l})_{0,i}P_{i,j}
. It follows that the only possible values for
l
are
l=0,\ldots,i
, since for the chain
N^{disc}
all possible transitions are from
i
to
j
with
i<j
.

For 5., for
r<k
, the event
\{T_{r:n}<T_{k:n}\}
corresponds to the cases when the chain
N^{disc}
jumps from the set
\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
to the set
\{r,\ldots,k-1\}
before entering the set
\{k,\ldots,n\}
.
Noting the definition of
F_{[0,i],\,j}
in 2., the probability
\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}<T_{k:n})
is
\sum_{j=r}^{k-1}F_{[0,r-1],\,j}=F_{[0,r-1],\,[r,k-1]}
.
Similarly, the probability
\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}=T_{k:n})
consists of the cases where the chain
N^{disc}
jumps from the set
\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
directly to the set
\{k,\ldots,n\}
, and this corresponds to the probability
\sum_{j=k}^{n}F_{[0,r-1],\,j}=F_{[0,r-1],\,[k,n]}
. Also, trivially,
\mathbb{P}(T_{r:n}<T_{k:n})=0
for
r\geq k
and
\mathbb{P}(T_{k:n}=T_{k:n})=1
.

Similarly, for (
10
), the event
\{N(T_{k:n})=j\}
for
j\geq k
corresponds to the case when the discrete chain of failed components
N^{disc}
jumps from the set
\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
to
j
, and this corresponds to
F_{[0,k-1],\,j}
. Lastly, the case
j<k
clearly has a nil probability.
∎

5.2
Proofs regarding system structure

Proof of Proposition
2.2
in Section
2.3
.

First, out of the
n!
sequences of
n
components, the number of sequences where the system continues to work right after the
k
-th failure is
n!\overline{S}_{k}=k!(n-k)!\ \#\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\lvert\mathbf{x}\rvert=n-k,\ \Phi(\mathbf{x})=1\}
.
Indeed, for any state
\mathbf{x}
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
k
failed components (there are
\#\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\lvert\mathbf{x}\rvert=n-k,\ \Phi(\mathbf{x})=1\}
of these states), there are
k!
ways of sequencing first the failed components, and then
(n-k)!
ways of sequencing the working ones. Due to the monotonicity of the system, if
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=1
then in each of these sequences the system is working right after the
k
-th failure.
This proves the second statement of Proposition
2.2
.
For the first statement, again due to the monotonicity of the system, the sequence of sets
\{\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\lvert\mathbf{x}\rvert=n-k,\ \Phi(\mathbf{x})=1\}:\ k=1,\ldots,n\}
is decreasing (nested) as
k
grows. Hence,
n!(\overline{S}_{k-1}-\overline{S}_{k})
is the number of sequences that fail at the
k
-th failure.
∎

5.3
Proofs regarding semi-coherent systems with LFMO distribution

Proof of Lemma
2.2
in Section
2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector of triggers of the Definition
2
of the LFMO distribution, and denote by
\sigma(\mathbf{\varepsilon})
the permutation of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
that makes
\varepsilon_{\sigma(1)}<\ldots<\varepsilon_{\sigma(n)}
. This permutation is well defined almost surely, as the triggers are i.i.d. exponentially distributed, so with probability one there are no ties between the triggers, and there is a unique permutation that does this. Denote also

\Sigma^{\phi,k}:=\{\text{permutation }\sigma\text{ of }\{1,\ldots,n\}:\phi(\mathbf{0}_{\sigma,1:l})=1\ \forall l=1,\ldots,k-1\text{ and }\phi(\mathbf{0}_{\sigma,1:l})=0\ \forall l=k,\ldots,n\}
,
where the vector
\mathbf{0}_{\sigma,1:l}
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
is

(\mathbf{0}_{\sigma,1:l})_{\sigma(i)}=\begin{cases}0&i=1,\ldots,l\\
1&i=l+1,\ldots,n\end{cases}

That is,
\Sigma^{\phi,k}
is the set of sequences (seen as permutations) where, when we turn off the components of the system sequentially one by one following the order of the permutation, the first system failure occurs when we turn off the
k
-th component.
In this way, the sets
\Sigma^{\phi,1},\ldots,\Sigma^{\phi,n}
forms a partition of the set of all permutations of
n
elements, and
\sigma(\mathbf{\varepsilon})
is in one (and only one) of these sets.
Next, note that
T_{fail}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k:n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\sigma(\mathbf{\varepsilon})\in\Sigma^{\phi,k}\right\}}
holds almost surely, since when
\sigma(\mathbf{\varepsilon})
is in
\Sigma^{\phi,k}
the
k
-th trigger is the one that makes the system fail. It follows that
\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\varepsilon)\in\Sigma^{\phi,k})=\#\Sigma^{\phi,k}/n!=s_{k}
since the permutation
\sigma(\varepsilon)
is distributed uniformly over the set of permutations of
n
elements (because the triggers
\varepsilon_{i}
are iid) and
s_{k}
is the proportion of permutations that, when there are no simultaneous failures, the system fails at the
k
-th failure. With this, the vector
(T_{fail},\ T_{1:n},\ \ldots,\ T_{n:n})
is almost surely equal to
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(T_{k:n},\ T_{1:n},\ \ldots,\ T_{n:n})\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\sigma(\mathbf{\varepsilon})\in\Sigma^{\phi,k}\right\}}
which is equal in distribution to
(T_{k:n},\ T_{1:n},\ \ldots,\ T_{n:n})
with probability
s_{k}
, for
k=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

5.4
Proof of the main theorem

Proof of Theorem
3.1
in Section
3
.

For the proof, we repeatedly use the strong Markov property and claim that once the system reaches its first system failure time or its first repair time, since all components are completely repaired, the system behaves stochastically as a brand new system.

In the following, we formalize the latter argument.
Let
(\mathbf{\chi}(t):t\geq 0)
be the
component status
process at each time instant, i.e.,
\mathbf{\chi}(t)\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\chi_{i}(t)=1
iff component
i
is working at time
t
.
Similarly, let
(\chi_{sys}(t):=\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}(t)):t\geq 0)
be the
system status
process at each time instant; i.e.,
\chi_{sys}(t)=1
iff the system is working (
\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}(t))=1
) at time
t
.
Also, when operating under an
r
-out-of-
n
:R repair policy, denote by
T_{rep}^{(r)}[1]
,
T_{rep}^{(r)}[2]
, …and
T_{fail}^{(r)}[1]
,
T_{fail}^{(r)}[2]
, …the sequences of, respectively, times of repairs and of system failure.
Also, define
T_{rep}^{(r)}[0]:=0
and
\varepsilon_{i}[0]:=E_{i,0}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
E_{i,m}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
and
m\geq 0
, are iid standard exponential random variables.
Then inductively define for
m=0,1,\ldots

\displaystyle\chi_{i}[m](t)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{1}_{\left\{L_{t}\geq\varepsilon_{i}[m]\right\}}\qquad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle\chi_{sys}[m](t)

\displaystyle:=\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}[m](t))

\displaystyle T_{rep}^{(r)}[m+1]

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>T_{rep}^{(r)}[m]\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\chi_{i}[m](t))\geq r\ \text{or}\ \chi_{sys}[m](t)=0\right\}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}[m+1]

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}L_{T_{rep}^{(r)}[m+1]}+E_{i,m+1}&\text{if }\chi_{i}[m]\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}[m+1]\right)=0\\
\varepsilon_{i}[m]&\text{otherwise, for all }i=1,\ldots,n\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{\chi}[m](t)=(\chi_{1}[m](t),\ \ldots,\ \chi_{n}[m](t))
.
Similarly,
T_{fail}^{(r)}[0]:=0
and for
m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle T_{fail}^{(r)}[m+1]:=\min\left\{T_{rep}^{(r)}[l]\ :\ l\geq 1,\ T_{rep}^{(r)}[l]>T_{fail}^{(r)}[m]\text{ and }\qquad\qquad\qquad\right.

\displaystyle\left.\chi_{sys}[l-1]\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}[l]\right)=0\right\}.

In this way,
\chi_{i}(t):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\chi_{i}[m](t)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{T_{rep}^{(r)}[m]\leq t<T_{rep}^{(r)}[m+1]\right\}}
is the status of component
i
at time
t
, and

\chi_{sys}(t):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\chi_{sys}[m](t)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{T_{rep}^{(r)}[m]\leq t<T_{rep}^{(r)}[m+1]\right\}}

is the corresponding system status.

Note that, by construction, the times
T_{rep}^{(r)}[m]
,
m\geq 1
, and
T_{fail}^{(r)}[m]
,
m\geq 1
, are sequences of stopping times with respect to the filtration induced by the process
\left((\mathbf{\chi}(t),\,\chi_{sys}(t)):t\geq 0\right)
— in fact, by the sub-filtration induced by the process
\left((\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\chi_{i}(t)),\,\chi_{sys}(t)):t\geq 0\right)
of number of failed components and system status.
It follows that the component status process
(\mathbf{\chi}(t):t\geq 0)
is renewed at each repair time
T_{rep}^{(r)}[m]
,
m\geq 1
, or also at each system failure time
T_{fail}^{(r)}[m]
.
This holds due to the memoryless property of the exponential triggers and the strong Markov property of the Lévy subordinator
\mathbf{L}
in the definition (
2.2
) of the LFMO distribution, and because under the
r
-out-of-
n
:R policy
all
failed components are
completely
repaired at the repair times.

We now prove parts 1. to 5. of Theorem
3.1
.

To prove 1., first recall that
T_{fail}^{(r)}
is the first system failure time when operating with the
r
-out-of-
n
:R policy, assuming that we start with all components working; and in the same context,
T_{rep}^{(r)}
is the first repair time.
To analyze the event
\{T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\}
it is sufficient to focus on the failure times without repair policy.
Denote by
T_{fail}
the system failure time when there is no repair policy, and in this case the first repair would occur at time
\min\{T_{fail},T_{r:n}\}
, where, recall,
T_{1:n}\leq\ldots\leq T_{n:n}
are the ordered failure times of the components,
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
.
Hence, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\min\{T_{r:n},T_{fail}\}=T_{fail}\right)
. Recall now that the Samaniego signature decomposition (
13
) states that
T_{fail}
is distributed as
T_{K:n}
, where
K
is an independent discrete random variable that takes the value
k
with probability
s_{k}
.
Therefore, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\min\{T_{r:n},T_{fail}\}=T_{fail}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\min\{T_{r:n},T_{k:n}\}=T_{k:n}\right).

We obtain (
16
) by noting the trivial facts that
\min\{T_{r:n},T_{k:n}\}=T_{r\wedge k:n}
and
\mathbb{P}\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}=T_{k:n}\right)=1
for
k\leq r
. Lastly, (
17
) is a direct consequence of (
16
).

To prove 2., let
j
in
1,\ \ldots,\ n
and again note that for the event
\{N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\}
it is sufficient to focus on the event
\{N(\min\{T_{fail},T_{r:n}\})=j\}
when there is no repair policy, since each time there is a repair or a system failure, the system behaves equal in probability to a new system with no failed components, due to the Markov property of the Lévy subordinator
L
and the memoryless property of the triggers
\varepsilon_{1},\ \ldots,\ \varepsilon_{n}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N(\min\{T_{fail},T_{r:n}\})=j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(\min\{T_{k:n},T_{r:n}\})=j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{r\wedge k:n})=j\right).

Lastly, note that at time
T_{r\wedge k:n}
there must be at least
r\wedge k
dead components. So, in particular if
j<r
and
k>j
(so
r\wedge k>j
), we have
\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{r\wedge k:n})=j\right)=0
. This proves (
18
).

Now we prove 3. We start by proving (
19
) and (
20
). Recall that we assume
p^{(r)}=\mathbb{P}\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)>0
. Consider any
j=1,\ldots,n
, and note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{fail}^{(r)})=j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)}(l))=j\left|T_{rep}^{(r)}(l)=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}(l)=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)}(1))=j\left|T_{rep}^{(r)}(1)=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}(l)=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\left|T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)

where
T_{rep}^{(r)}(l)
is the
l
-th time of repair, and the second equality is due to the strong Markov property of the repair process. Indeed, after any time of repair, since all components are completely repaired, the process as a completely new process starting at zero. This proves (
19
).
Next, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j,\ T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(N(\min\{T_{fail},T_{r:n}\})=j,\ T_{fail}\leq T_{r:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(\min\{T_{k:n},T_{r:n}\})=j,\ T_{k:n}\leq T_{r:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(\min\{T_{k:n},T_{r:n}\})=j,\ T_{k:n}\leq T_{r:n}\right)

where the last equation comes from the fact that
N(T_{k:n})\geq k
, so for
k>j
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(N(\min\{T_{k:n},T_{r:n}\})=j,\ T_{k:n}\leq T_{r:n}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{k:n})=j\right)=0.

It follows that for
k\leq j
we have
\{N(\min\{T_{k:n},T_{r:n}\})=j,\ T_{k:n}\leq T_{r:n}\}=\{N(T_{r\wedge k:n})=j\}
. Indeed, the case
k\leq r
is trivial as
T_{k:n}\leq T_{r:n}
holds; and if
k>r
then
\{N(T_{r\wedge k:n})=j\}=\{N(T_{r:n})=j\}
with
j>k
implies that
T_{k:n}=T_{r:n}
. This proves (
20
).

Lastly, for (
21
), we use
\mathbb{P}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j,\ T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)})=\mathbb{P}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j)-\mathbb{P}(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j,\ T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)})
and conclude using (
18
) and (
20
).

We now prove 4. We start by observing that, by Renewal Theory, see e.g.
[
38
, Proposition 3.4.1]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{C[0,t]}{t}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{fail}^{(r)}]}{\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]}{\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}}\qquad\text{a.s.,}

since the times
T_{fail}^{(r)}
and
T_{rep}^{(r)}
are renewal times, due to the aforementioned Markov property. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]=\mathbb{E}\left[c_{sys}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{T_{fail}^{(r)}=T_{rep}^{(r)}\right\}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\mathbf{1}_{\left\{N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right\}}\right]

(30)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\{T_{fail},T_{r:n},\}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\min\{T_{k:n},T_{r:n}\}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\left[T_{k\wedge r:n}\right].

(31)

Next, for
\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{fail}^{(r)}]
and
\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}
, we see that the process up to the first system failure time
T_{fail}^{(r)}
consists of a random number —geometrically distributed on
0,1,\ldots
with parameter
1-p^{(r)}=\mathbb{P}\left(T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}\right)
— of processes starting with all components working up to the first repair time
T_{rep}^{(r)}
conditional on
T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}
, concatenated with a last process starting with all components working, up to the first repair time
T_{rep}^{(r)}
conditional on
T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}
. In particular this implies that the system will fail almost surely.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{fail}^{(r)}]

\displaystyle=c_{sys}+\left(\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}-1\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\left|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\left|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right)

\displaystyle=c_{sys}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\left(\left(\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}-1\right)\frac{1}{1-p^{(r)}}\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{r\leq j\right\}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r:n}\right)=j\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c_{sys}+\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\left(\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{r\leq j\right\}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\sum_{k=1}^{j}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c_{sys}+\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\sum_{k=1}^{j\mathbf{1}_{\left\{r>j\right\}}+n\mathbf{1}_{\left\{r\leq j\right\}}}s_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(T_{r\wedge k:n}\right)=j\right)

\displaystyle=c_{sys}+\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{cmp}(j)\,\mathbb{P}\left(N(T_{rep}^{(r)})=j\right)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,T_{fail}^{(r)}=\left(\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}-1\right)\mathbb{E}\,\left[T_{rep}^{(r)}\left|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right]+\mathbb{E}\,\left[T_{rep}^{(r)}\left|\,T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right.\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\left(\mathbb{E}\,\left[T_{rep}^{(r)};\ T_{rep}^{(r)}<T_{fail}^{(r)}\right]+\mathbb{E}\,\left[T_{rep}^{(r)};\ T_{rep}^{(r)}=T_{fail}^{(r)}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{p^{(r)}}\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}

Lastly, to prove 5., again by Renewal Theory we have that
\lim_{t\to\infty}C[0,t]/t=\mathbb{E}\,C[0,T_{rep}^{(r)}]/\mathbb{E}\,T_{rep}^{(r)}
almost surely, since the repair times are renewal times. We conclude using (
30
) and (
31
), since they also hold when
p^{(r)}=0
.
∎
