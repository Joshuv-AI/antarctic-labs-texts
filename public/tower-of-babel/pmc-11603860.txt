Global, regional, and national burden of gastrointestinal cancers among adolescents and young adults from 1990 to 2019, and burden prediction to 2040
Authors: Jieyu Peng, Shu Huang, Xiaohong Wang, Xiaomin Shi, Huan Xu, Ping Wang, Qi Chen, Wei Zhang, Lei Shi, Yan Peng, Nanjun Wang, Xiaowei Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastrointestinal (GI) cancers have heavily burdened public health. Few studies reported GI cancer burden among adolescents and young adults (AYA). To address this gap, we explored the burden of GI cancer among people aged 15–39.MethodsWe retrieved data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 Data Resources. The average annual percent change (AAPC) of rates was calculated by linear regression analysis of the natural logarithm. Bayesian age-period-cohort model was applied to predict the future burden.ResultsIn 2019, there were 171,857 (95% uncertain interval [95% UI]: 157,092–187,974) new GI cancer cases with a rate of 5.79/100,000 (95% UI: 5.29–6.33) and 91,033 (95% UI: 83,156–99,399) deaths at a rate of 3.07/100,000 (95% UI: 2.80–3.35) among AYA. The number of prevalent cases and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) were 722,573 (95% UI: 660,806–789,476) and 5,151,294 (95% UI: 4,706,065–56,188,77), with rates of 24.35/100,000 (95% UI: 22.27–26.60) and 173.57/100,000 (95% UI: 158.57–189.32) respectively. The overall rates of mortality (AAPC = -1.281, p < 0.001) and DALY (AAPC = -1.283, p < 0.001) of GI cancers declined during the past 30 years, while the incidence rate (AAPC = -0.270, p = 0.074) remained stable and the prevalence rate (AAPC = 1.066, p < 0.001) increased. The burden of colorectal cancer (CRC) and pancreatic cancer increased, while those of stomach cancer (SC) and liver cancer (LC) declined. Among the 21 GBD regions, East Asia exhibited the highest burden, while within the five SDI regions, high-middle SDI locations showed the highest rates across all four indicators. CRC, SC, and LC emerged as the primary culprits, attaining a position within the top ten absolute DALYs for all AYA cancers. There were predicted to be 315,792 new cases and 174,068 deaths of GI cancers among AYA in 2040.ConclusionsDespite the decrease in mortality and DALY rates of GI cancers among AYA, they remain prevalent. The burden varied with locations, SDI levels, sexes, and cancer types. Sufficient attention and multi-party cooperation are needed to control the widespread public health issue.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20777-2.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, particularly those affecting the esophagus, stomach, gallbladder and biliary tract, liver, colon and rectum, and pancreas, rank among the most prevalent cancers globally. The GLOBOCAN 2022 data reveals that GI cancers constituted 24.6% of all new cancer cases worldwide, leading to more than one-third (34.2%) of all fatalities and placing an immense health burden on mankind [1]. The adoption of a wholesome way of life, encompassing regular physical activity and a well-balanced weight and diet, has been proven to play a pivotal role in the progression of gastrointestinal cancer, both in the past and future [2].

The transformation of socioeconomic systems, demographics, and cultural lifestyles poses mounting public health challenges for adolescents and young adults (AYA), who fall within the age range of children to older adults and exhibit distinct clinical manifestations and characteristics. A significant number of settings lack comprehensive evaluations of the cancer burden and epidemiological patterns within this age group [3]. Previous research has investigated the burden of multiple cancers among children, adolescents, and young adults [4, 5], demonstrating the considerable role the demographic plays in global health. Although the current epidemiological research has to some extent filled the gaps, there is still room for further exploration in some specialist diseases.

Numerous studies have recorded the widespread occurrence of GI cancers worldwide, with a primary focus on individuals across all age brackets [2, 6, 7]. It is noteworthy that the high incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer (CRC) [8], substantial stomach cancer (SC) burden among AYA [9], and rising incidence of young-onset esophageal adenocarcinoma [10] are also hot research spots in recent years. Still, no formal analysis has previously been done on the global burden of GI cancers in the AYA population, which this study aimed to explore.

Materials and methods

GBD review

The data we used to analyze were retrieved from the Global Burden of Diseases 2019 Data Resources (GBD 2019, https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2019), published in October 2020. GBD 2019, which assembles data from multiple sources to integrate, validate, analyze, and disseminate disease burden, providing estimates generated by the GBD diseases and injuries analytical framework of 369 diseases and injuries and 87 risk factors from 1990 to 2019 in 204 countries and territories, was the up-to-date resource of GBD [11–13]. The related statistical methods and principles have already been introduced in previous studies [11–13], and the data resources have been widely adopted in the areas of epidemiology, public health, and preventive medicine.

Estimation of GI cancer burden

The estimation of four indicators, which include incidence, mortality, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), were used to analyze the disease burden of GI cancers among AYA. The incidence is the number of new cases in the given population over a specific period of time, and the mortality is the number of death cases caused by a disease. The prevalence is counted as the existing cases of the disease in a population at a particular moment. The DALYs are the years of healthy life lost, calculated as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs). YLLs are the multiplication of deaths and standard life expectancy at the age of death. YLDs are years lived with any short-term or long-term health loss weighted for severity by the disability weight, estimated by multiplying the prevalence with a distinct disability weight [14].

GBD estimated all-cause and cause-specific mortality based on four key principles: comprehensive data source identification, rigorous quality assessment with bias correction, standardized analytical procedures, and model performance assessment through cross-validation. Similar principles also guide the estimation of incidence, prevalence, and YLDs for diseases and injuries [15]. GBD 2019 draws from diverse data sources, including censuses, household surveys, civil registration and vital statistics, disease registries, healthcare utilization data, air pollution monitoring, satellite imagery, and disease notifications [11]. Three primary standardized tools are employed in GBD 2019: Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm), DisMod-MR, and spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression (ST-GPR). CODEm utilizes a blend of modeling techniques to analyze cause of death data, estimating rates or cause fractions by selecting covariates that demonstrate the strongest out-of-sample predictive accuracy. DisMod-MR, a Bayesian meta-regression tool, models non-fatal disease burden by integrating all available data on incidence, prevalence, remission, and mortality to ensure consistency across epidemiological parameters. ST-GPR applies spatial and temporal correlations to derive key metrics, such as risk factor exposure or mortality rates [11].

Definitions

The age range of AYA in this study was defined as 15–39 years old, which was a specific age range in the GBD 2019 and has an indirect estimation of the four measures we intend to analyze. Besides, the age range is endorsed by related international institutes and working groups of oncology [16] and has been widely used to explore different disease burdens in previous studies [4, 17]. The GBD 2019 dataset lacks data for the 15–19 age group in esophageal cancer (EC) and gallbladder and biliary tract cancer (GBTC) categories. While we considered supplementing this data with estimates from GLOBOCAN 2020, which uses cancer incidence data from population-based registries and mortality data from the World Health Organization Mortality Database [18], significant data sources and methodological differences between the two databases render direct supplementation impractical. The estimation of six GI cancer types was extracted via International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) codes: esophagus (C15), stomach (C16), colorectum (C18–21), liver (C22), gallbladder and biliary tract (C23–24) and pancreas (C25). To better compare the burden of GI cancers in adolescents, we ranked them alongside the other 24 cancers (Other malignant neoplasms, Leukemia, Breast cancer, Brain and central nervous system cancer, Cervical cancer, Tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, Lip and oral cavity cancer, Ovarian cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma, Nasopharynx cancer, Testicular cancer, Malignant skin melanoma, Other pharynx cancer, Kidney cancer, Thyroid cancer, Larynx cancer, Bladder cancer, Uterine cancer, Multiple myeloma, Non-melanoma skin cancer, Other neoplasms, Mesothelioma, Prostate cancer) among the top 30 in terms of DALYs. The socio-demographic index (SDI) is a comprehensive measure of per capita income, total fertility rate under 25 years of age, and average education level [13], which can be divided into five levels (high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and Low SDI). The SDI has been proven to reflect the strong association between a country's economic and social development and the health status of its people.

Statistical analysis

The data of aforementioned four indicators extracted from the GBD 2019 is presented with the 95% uncertain interval (95%UI), calculated using the 25th and 975th values based on the ordered 1000 draws of the posterior distribution through the GBD modeling process [4, 11]. The rates presented in the article are crude rates specific to the target age group. The JoinPoint regression model is applied to reflect changing disease burden trends. The overall changing trends have been presented with the turning points of disease development trends identified. The JoinPoint regression model divides the overall trend into several segments and calculates the annual percentage change (APC) for each segment as well as the 95% certain interval (95%CI). Prediction

Besides, the future disease burden of GI cancers is also within our research scope. We used the Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model, developed by Andrea Riebler and Leonard Held [19], to conduct the burden prediction. The BAPC model employed the second-order random walk to smoothen the priors associated with age, period, and cohort effects. The posterior distribution was derived by combining the prior knowledge with the observed data [19]. The integrated nested Laplace approximations was utilized in conjunction to accurately approximate the marginal posterior distributions, thereby circumventing any complications related to mixing and convergence [20]. In contrast to other models that rely on GBD data for prediction, the BAPC model exhibits a reduced error rate and extensive coverage, where coverage refers to the proportion of projections falling within the 95% credibility interval, reflecting the model’s performance across various age groups, sexes, and entities [21, 22]. The BAPC prediction model has already been widely used to predict the future burden in numerous GBD studies [20, 23–25].

Normalized population data and the population forecast data, which are needed in the prediction process, come from the GBD world population age standard [13] and Global Fertility, Mortality, Migration, and Population Forecasts 2017–2100 (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/record/ihme-data/global-population-forecasts-2017-2100). After generating the predicted incidence and mortality numbers, we applied annual percentage adjustments to these data to represent future trends in GI cancer for the target age group. Based on trends reported in previous studies, we selected increases and decreases of 1%, 2%, and 3% [26]. The data process, analysis, and results visualization were completed by R program (version 4.1.3), Joinpoint software (version 4.9.1.0), and Excel. The R packages, “BAPC” and “INLA” were used to make predictions.

Results

Overall burden of GI cancers from 1990 to 2019

In 2019, there were 171,857 new cases of GI cancers with an incidence rate of 5.79/100,000 (5.29–6.33), and the existing cases were 722,573 with a rate of 24.35/100,000 (22.27–26.60). GI cancers caused 91,033 deaths and 5,151,294 DALYs, exhibiting rates of 3.07/100,000 (2.80–3.35) and 173.57/100,000 (158.57–189.32) respectively. The high-middle SDI regions had a heavier burden among five SDI regions. During the 21 GBD regions, East Asia had the highest rates of all four indicators, while Western Sub-Saharan Africa displayed the lowest rates (Table 1). Among 204 countries and territories, Mongolia (499.71/100,000), Kiribati (492.80/100,000), and Solomon Islands (468.55/100,000) had higher DALY rates (Fig. 1). Supplementary Figs. 1–3 provide the national distribution of incidence, mortality, and prevalence rates. The YLLs accounted for the majority DALYs of GI cancer among AYA (Supplementary Tables 1–6). Burden of six GI cancers in 2019

Esophageal cancer

In 2019, there were 8,088 new cases of EC among AYA, at an incidence rate of 0.27/100,000 (95%UI: 0.25–0.30). There were 6,215 deaths, at a mortality rate of 0.21/100,000 (0.19–0.23). There were 19,559 cases and 343,752 DALYs in total, at the rates of 0.66/100,000 (0.59–0.73) and 11.58/100,000 (10.37–12.88), respectively (Table 3).

Among six GI cancers, the proportion of DALYs due to EC in AYA ranked fifth (6.7%). The largest EC-related proportion of DALYs was observed in the low SDI locations (10.8%) and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (17.6%) (Fig. 5). The low-middle SDI locations (13.05/100,000) and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (24.43 /100,000) had the highest DALY rates (Supplementary Table 1). The DALY rates of Malawi (47.92/100,000), Kiribati (45.14/100,000), and the United Arab Emirates (43.13/100,000) ranked among the top three worldwide (Supplementary Fig. 6).Fig. 5Proportion of DALYs due to the six gastrointestinal cancers for adolescents and young adults in different SDI locations and GBD regions in 2019. Stomach cancer

In 2019, there were 49,008 new cases, 27,895 deaths, 152,587 existing cases, and 1,573,897 DALYs of SC among AYA. The rates of incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALY were 1.65 (1.52–1.79), 0.94 (0.87–1.02), 5.14 (4.68–5.63), and 53.03 (48.81–57.40) per 100,000 population, respectively (Table 3).

The SC contributed a proportion of 30.6% to the overall DALYs of GI cancers among AYA. The largest DALY proportions were observed in the low-middle SDI locations (38.9%) and Oceania (57.7%) (Fig. 5). High-middle SDI locations (65.32/100,000) and Oceania (112.51/100,000) had the highest DALY rates (Supplementary Table 2). The highest DALY rate among 204 countries and territories was observed in the Solomon Islands (275.41/100,000) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Colon and rectum cancer

Table 3 showed that there were 76,090 new cases of CRC among AYA, at an incidence rate of 2.56/100,000 (2.37–2.79) in 2019. And 28,352 people died of it with a mortality rate of 0.96/100,000 (0.88–1.03). Estimated 486,192 existing cases and 1,633,410 DALYs were observed, with rates of 16.38/100,000 (15.07–17.82) and 55.04/100,000 (50.96–59.30).

In 2019, the CRC (31.7%) contributed most to the DALYs among AYA, and the largest CRC-related proportion of DALYs was observed in the high SDI locations (43.6%) and high-income North America (51.7%) (Fig. 5). The highest DALY rates were in high-middle SDI (78.76/100,000) and East Asia (101.56/100,000) (Supplementary Table 3). For 204 countries and territories, Taiwan (Province of China) had the highest DALY rate (146.16/100,000), followed by Seychelles (124.63/100,000) and Nauru (117.07/100,000) (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Liver cancer

In 2019, a number of 25,430 new LC-related cases with an incidence rate of 0.86/100,000 (0.77–0.96)) were observed among AYA, resulting in 18,573 deaths with a mortality rate of 0.63/100,000 (0.56–0.70). The total existing cases were 46,371, with a prevalence rate of 1.56/100,000 (1.39–1.75). And the DALYs were 1,046,876 with a rate of 35.27/100,000 (31.60–39.18) (Table 3).

LC had the third highest percentage (20.3%) of DALYs. The middle SDI locations (26.1%) had the largest DALY proportion and the highest DALY rate (54.17/100,000). For 21 GBD regions, Southern Sub-Saharan America had the largest proportion (37.5%), while the highest DALY rate was found in East Asia (107.54/100,000) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 4). Besides, for the national level, the highest DALY rate was observed in Gambia (241.70/100,000) (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Gallbladder and biliary tract cancer

Compared with other GI cancers, GBTC have relatively lower cases. The incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALY rates for 2019 were 0.13/100,000 (0.11–0.14), 0.08/100,000 (0.07–0.09), 0.22/100,000 (0.19–0.25), and 4.47/100,000 (3.79–4.96), respectively. GBTC-related burden was more pronounced on females in comparison to males (Table 3).

GBTC contributed the smallest percentage (2.6%) to the overall DALYs among six GI cancers. The largest proportions were observed in the low-middle SDI locations (3.4%), and the highest DALY rate was observed in the high-middle SDI location (5.40/100,000). For the 21 GBD regions, Southern Latin America had the largest DALY proportion (6.0%) and the highest DALY rate (9.64/100,000) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 5). Chile (14.21/100,000) had the highest DALYs rate among 204 countries and territories (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Pancreatic cancer

In 2019, there were 9,401 new cases of PC among AYA, at an incidence rate of 0.32/100,000 (0.29–0.35), leading to 7,609 deaths, at a mortality rate of 0.26/100,000 (0.24–0.28). The number of total existing cases and DALYs were 11,247 and 420,797, with rates of 0.38/100,000 (0.35–0.41) and 14.18/100,000 (13.03–15.60), respectively. (Table 3).

The proportion of DALYs caused by PC in AYA ranked fourth (8.2%) among six GI cancers. High SDI locations (12.9%) and Central Europe (16.3%) had the largest DALY proportions (Fig. 5). High-middle SDI locations (24.83/100,000) and Eastern Europe (37.13/100,000) had the highest DALY rates (Supplementary Table 6). Among 204 countries and territories, the highest DALY rate was observed in the United Arab Emirates (84.57/100,000) (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Burden change of six GI cancers from 1990 to 2019

From 1990 to 2019, the incidence, mortality, and DALY rates for EC (AAPC = -0.589, -0.858, and -0.854 respectively, all P values < 0.05) and SC (AAPC = -0.952, -1.853, and -1.859, respectively, all P values < 0.05) showed a marked decline. LC exhibited a similar downward trend across all four indicators (AAPC = -2.207, -2.629, -1.374, and -2.641 respectively, all P values < 0.05). GTBC also experienced declines in mortality (AAPC = -0.285, P < 0.05) and DALY rates (AAPC = -0.296, P < 0.05). In contrast, CRC showed significant increases in incidence (AAPC = 1.398, P < 0.05), mortality (AAPC = 0.174, P < 0.05), and prevalence rates (AAPC = 1.872, P < 0.05), except for the DALY rate, which remained stable. On the other hand, PC saw rising trends in all four indicators (AAPC = 0.896, 0.850, 1.011, 0.826 respectively, all P values < 0.05). (Table 4 and Supplementary Figs. 12–15). Prediction

AYA projected a total of 315,792 new cases of GI cancers with 174,068 deaths in 2040, reflecting an 84% and 91% surge, respectively. The number of new cases and deaths for all gastrointestinal cancers is projected to increase over the next 20 years. LC is expected to show the most substantial increase, with the number of new cases rising approximately 2.55-fold and the number of deaths increasing by about 3.06-fold. On the other hand, SC is anticipated to have the smallest increase, with a 25% rise in the number of new cases and a 14% increase in deaths.

Assuming a continued 3% annual increase, the global number of new cases of gastrointestinal cancers is projected to increase 2.42-fold by 2040 compared to 2019, while the number of deaths is expected to increase 2.56-fold. To maintain current levels, the number of new cases and deaths from EC would need to decrease by 3% annually. For SC, a 1% annual decrease in new cases and a 0–1% annual decrease in deaths would be necessary. New cases of CRC would need to decline by 2–3% per year, while deaths should decrease by 1–2% annually. In the case of LC, even with a 3% annual decrease, both the number of new cases and deaths would still show significant growth. For GBTC, the number of new cases would need to decrease by 1–2% per year, and deaths by 1% annually. For PC, both the number of new cases and deaths would need to decline by approximately 2% annually. Discussion

We extracted data from the GBD 2019 to perform an analysis of the burden of GI cancers among AYA. Our study revealed that CRC, SC, and LC continued to bear the higher burden of GI cancers globally, exhibiting more substantial alterations compared to the remaining three GI cancers within the AYA population in the last 3 decades. The burden of EC, GBTC, and PC has maintained a relatively low level. An increasing trend was observed in the disease burden of CRC and PC. Males bear a higher burden of GI cancers, except for GBTC. All cancers would encounter a formidable obstacle in the form of a surge in new cases and deaths in the next 20 years, with LC showing the most significant rise.

The high-middle SDI regions had the highest rates for all four indicators among the five SDI regions. Within the 21 GBD regions, East Asia exhibited the greatest burden. Mongolia had elevated DALY rates compared to other 204 countries and territories. The regional and national disparities in disease burden are influenced not only by SDI but also by variations in life expectancy [27] and health policies. In regions with longer life expectancies, diseases may place a greater burden on middle-aged and older populations, potentially shifting the age-related disease profile and masking the impact on younger groups. This dynamic highlights the role of life expectancy in understanding the distribution of GI cancer burden. Additionally, differences in regional health policies—including the availability of preventive strategies, screening programs, and treatment options—further contribute to observed disparities [28]. Regions with comprehensive health promotion initiatives and accessible screening programs tend to exhibit reduced avoidable deaths and DALYs, while areas with limited resources may face an elevated burden among younger populations. Further analyses of the DALY burden of specific GI cancers should consider the combined effects and policies underlying these epidemiological findings.

Our findings indicate a marked rise in the incidence and prevalence rates of CRC. The CRC burden among AYA has exhibited complex epidemiological trends in previous reports. Enormous studies reported that the incidence rates of early-onset CRC (eoCRC) had been increasing worldwide [29–31], which is consistent with the results of our research. Wang et al. also reported the increasing global incidence, mortality, and DALYs of eoCRC using the GBD data resources [8]. However, a study has reported a decline in both the incidence and mortality rates eoCRC in Italy in recent years [32]. In our study, high-middle SDI regions and East Asia had a higher DALY rate associated with high mortality, which can be attributed to the elevated incidence rates stemming from a Western way of life such as limited physical activity and consumption of alcohol and processed meat [33]. By calculating the AAPC of the changing burden trends, we found a significant increase from 2013 to 2019, accompanied by a considerable surge in new cases and fatalities over the next two decades, both of which are indicative of the impending dire circumstances in the years ahead. To maintain or reduce the current burden, certain measures must be taken to achieve the required annual reduction rates in the number of new cases and deaths. Routine colonoscopy screening is imperative for the early detection, diagnosis, and management of CRC to alleviate its burden. Despite the concerning global trend of CRC incidence among AYA, the disease burden is further complicated by pronounced regional and national variations. Consequently, any decision to lower the age threshold for routine screening should be meticulously informed by the specific epidemiological characteristics unique to each region.

Compared to the other five types of cancers, the future increase in SC is relatively modest, yet it continues to demonstrate an upward trend. We also found the burden of SC, as measured by the rates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs declined during the past 30 years. Typically, a decline in incidence reduces mortality, which in turn lowers DALY rates by decreasing both YLLs and the YLDs, highlighting the interdependence of these variables. Zhang et al. explored the incidence and mortality of SC by sex groups, observing a decreasing trend for people aged 15–49 years using the GBD data resources [34]. Meanwhile, the same result was also reported by Li et al., who extracted the estimates from the GLOBOCAN 2020 data resources [35]. We are encouraged by the beneficial changes in the burden of SC over the past 30 years; however, to maintain these improvements and achieve further positive outcomes, it is essential to reduce its incidence and mortality by at least 1% annually in the future. Additionally, by taking into account the evolving local patterns, we discovered that the pivotal moment emerged in 2001, after which the SC burden showed a significant downward trend. Evidence from multiple epidemiological studies suggesting that the time trends and broad geographical differences in SC burden are most likely due to changes in behavioral and environmental risk factors. These include improved sanitation, shifts in dietary habits (such as increased consumption of fruits and vegetables and reduced salt intake), widespread antibiotic use, and, most notably, a decline in Helicobacter pylori infection rates [36]. Our study revealed that among AYA cancers, the DALYs for female SC ranked highest among all GI cancers, attributed to the association between estrogen and its receptors [37], as well as the delayed impact of risk factors on males [38]. Furthermore, although our findings indicate that AYA residing in high-middle SDI regions experience a higher DALY rate, no discernible correlation between the DALY rate and the SDI was observed.

Among all the cancers affecting AYA, the DALYs of LC secured the tenth spot, thereby playing a significant role in the three most substantial burdens observed in GI cancers. Despite the positive decline observed in the last three decades, there was still a possibility of an underlying upward trend. A huge shift from 2001 to 2005 was detected in the burden-changing trend owing to the hepatitis vaccination [39], but the recent increasing trends were also observed for the changing epidemics of risk factors. The mounting prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [40, 41], and the insufficient focus on Hepatitis B Virus [42]/Hepatitis C Virus [43] infections in children have been identified as contributing factors to the escalating LC burden among AYA in recent times. Our research showed that, compared to other SDI regions, the middle SDI region had the highest percentage of DALYs for LC among the six GI cancers, corroborating the high incidence observed in this region across all age groups [7]. This indicates a strong link between the incidence of LC and DALY rates, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to reduce the overall disease burden. According to our projections, LC will experience a substantial increase in the number of new cases and deaths over the next 20 years. Even with an annual 3% reduction in these numbers, significant growth is still expected. Further research and projections are needed to address this serious trend and develop appropriate policies. In particular, the LC epidemic in AYA necessitates focused actions and policies in countries and regions with a significant burden. These measures encompass bolstering the promotion of hepatitis vaccination, intensifying efforts to prevent and manage hepatitis virus infections in pediatric populations, and developing comprehensive management strategies for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

The burden of EC, PC, and GBTC remained relatively low in comparison to the aforementioned three cancers, yet the guard is unable to unwind. The prevalent overweight and obesity among AYA could potentially explain the significant rise in PC burden [44, 45], which aligns with the highest DALY rate found in high-middle SDI areas. Despite this, there has been limited research conducted on EC and GBTC in AYA. In 2017, EC identified tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, and high body mass index as the primary causes of DALYs [46], which were deemed epidemic in AYA [12, 17, 47, 48]. These risk factors not only increase incidence but also have a cumulative impact over time, contributing to higher mortality rates. Older individuals are more likely to suffer from EC. A prior investigation revealed that individuals over the age of 50 tended to experience higher rates of incidence and mortality [49]. The age group of 35–39 years exhibited the highest percentage of DALYs attributed to EC in our study, thereby substantiating this conclusion. The reduction in EC burden can be ascribed to the implementation of endoscopic screening and treatment for early EC and dysplasia [50, 51], which effectively lowers both incidence and subsequent mortality, leading to a decrease in DALY rates. Concerning GBTC cancers, Ouyang et al. documented a decline in the trend of age-standardized DALY rate for all age groups [52]. Our research also recorded a comparable circumstance.

Different data sources were employed in certain studies to document the burden of GI cancer worldwide. Using the GBD data resources, Xie et al. analyzed the temporal and geographic diversity of various GI cancers and emphasized the importance of prevention [53]. Arnold et al. utilized the data from the GLOBOCAN 2020 data resources to stress the significant obstacles posed by GI cancer to public health and importance of implementing primary and secondary prevention measures [2]. Our research revealed a decrease in mortality and DALY rates of GI cancers, accompanied by an increase in prevalence rate. We discovered that between 2000 and 2001, there was a major shift in local trends, and there was an upward trend of the burden from 2016 to 2019. It was anticipated that the decision-makers in high-middle SDI regions and East Asia developed immediate plans to manage the strain in heavy burden of GI cancers. It is essential to remain vigilant and take prompt action to prevent and control the rising number of new GI cancer cases and fatalities in the next two decades.

Among AYA, the burden of GI cancers, particularly CRC and PC, which are on an upward trajectory, is intensifying. These cancers increasingly compete with other rising health challenges, such as mental health disorders [54] and metabolic diseases [17], especially in the context of overlapping risk factors such as social stress, smoking, unhealthy dietary habits, obesity, etc. This interplay heightens the complexity of health management within the AYA demographic, leading to more pronounced competition for limited healthcare resources and public health interventions. Thus, understanding these competing health burdens is essential for the strategic allocation of resources and the development of targeted health policies.

Our research delved into the health challenges of GI cancers faced by AYA, a topic that has typically been overlooked and oversimplified in previous studies. Our study fills gaps in the epidemiology of the digestive system and oncology, as well as the health burden of adolescents and young adults, providing references for the formulation of regional and national health policies at different levels of development. The results of our study also demonstrate the necessity of controlling the impact of GI cancers on people of different sexes and age groups.

Our study also had drawbacks. First, the GBD 2019 dataset lacks data for the 15–19 age group in EC and GBTC, potentially affecting completeness. Second, while life expectancy likely influences age-related disease distribution, its complex role relative to other factors prevented us from conducting a detailed analysis. Finally, input data from GBD 2019 is estimated using various statistical modellings and data sources such as censuses, household surveys, civil registration, vital statistics, disease registries, and other sources, potentially introducing biases. The slow and delayed nature of cancer registry data collection, further impacted by data protection policies, means that some recent data are often estimated rather than directly observed. This reliance on estimation can lead to amplified and extrapolated trends that may not fully represent the actual global situation.

Conclusion

GI cancer burden among AYA varied with geographic locations, SDI level, sexes, and cancer types. Males tended to suffer heavier GI burden except for the GBTC. The high-middle SDI locations and East Asia need to be given sufficient attention to the GI cancer burden among AYA. The global number of new cases and deaths from GI cancers among AYA is projected to increase significantly by 2040, with LC showing the most substantial rise. Given these trends, greater attention to this public health issue is needed, alongside collaborative efforts from countries, governments, and societies. Formulating health policies at both global and national levels to address the GI cancer burden among AYA will be essential.
