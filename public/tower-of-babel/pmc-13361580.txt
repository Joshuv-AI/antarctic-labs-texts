Investigation of acute and chronic injury mechanisms of the deltoid ligament: insights from finite element analysis and animal study
Authors: Yuandong Li, Qingqing Yang, Qiang Zhang, Huizhi Wang, Cheng‑Kung Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe deltoid ligament (DL) is the primary stabilizer of the medial ankle, but its injury mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the injury risk and mechanisms of individual DL bundles under both acute and chronic conditions to inform prevention and treatment strategies.MethodsA validated finite element model of the human foot was used to examine peak stresses in DL bundles under four acute loading scenarios. Chronic loading was simulated by applying gait loads after transecting the lateral ligaments, and the resulting DL stresses were compared with those of the intact model. Additionally, thirty-nine rats were assigned to three groups: a lateral ligament rupture group (LR, n = 13), a tibialis posterior tendon rupture group (TPR, n = 13), and a sham group (n = 13). After 6 weeks of treadmill running, the mechanical properties and histological characteristics of the DL, along with ankle joint morphology and articular stresses, were evaluated to further verify the hypothesized mechanisms of chronic injury.ResultsUnder acute loadings, the tibiocalcaneal ligament (TCL), anterior tibiotalar ligament (ATTL), and deep posterior tibiotalar ligament (dPTTL) showed the highest stress under pronation-external rotation loading. Lateral ligament rupture increased DL stress during gait. After 6 weeks of treadmill running, the LR and TPR groups showed roughened articular surfaces with osteophyte formation, increased articular stress, decreased talar bone volume fraction, lower failure load and stiffness ratios of the DL (p < 0.01), reduced fluorescence intensity of COL1, and elevated levels of COL3, MMP-2 and IL-1β compared with the sham group (p < 0.01).ConclusionThe TCL, ATTL, and dPTTL bundles are particularly susceptible to acute injury, with pronation-external rotation posing the greatest risk. Chronic degeneration of the DL occurs following rupture of the lateral ligament or tibialis posterior tendon, with a more pronounced effect after lateral ligament rupture.Keywords: Deltoid ligament, Injury mechanism, Finite element analysis, Animal study, Ankle joint biomechanics

Background

Ankle sprains are common and have long been considered to predominantly involve the lateral ligaments [1], whereas deltoid ligament (DL) injuries were thought to be rare [2]. However, advancements in diagnostic techniques have revealed that DL injuries are frequently underestimated and often occur in combination with lateral ligament injuries [3]. Due to this lack of attention, the mechanisms of DL injury remain poorly understood [4]. A comprehensive investigation of the mechanisms underlying DL injuries is essential for developing effective prevention strategies, reducing associated healthcare costs, and improving patients’ quality of life.

Previous studies have indicated that DL injuries frequently occur under specific acute loading conditions, including external rotation, eversion, pronation-external rotation, and supination-external rotation [5, 6]. However, these studies did not clarify the individual injury risks associated with the six bundles of the DL. This lack of clarity may lead to missed diagnoses of specific bundles and impede the development of surgical strategies tailored to particular injury patterns. Although recent studies have examined the injury risks of different bundles through ligament strain analysis in cadaver experiments [7–9], research has indicated that even when subjected to high tensile forces, some ligaments undergo negligible elongation. This response is likely explained by inter-ligament differences in morphology and stiffness [8]. In contrast, finite element analysis can quantify the tensile stress experienced by each bundle, providing a more effective reflection of their injury risks [10–13]. However, existing finite element analysis studies focused only on the injury risks of specific bundles (the tibiocalcaneal ligament (TCL) and anterior tibiotalar ligament (ATTL)) under partial conditions (plantarflexion, dorsiflexion, and external rotation). There was still a lack of comprehensive research on the injury risk of different bundles under various acute injury conditions [14, 15].

Additionally, several studies have suggested that injuries to the lateral ligaments and the medial tibialis posterior tendon may compromise joint stability. Such instability may result in increased compensatory loading on the DL, ultimately leading to chronic damage [16, 17]. In vitro experiments on human cadaveric specimens revealed that removal of the tibialis posterior tendon increased DL tension compared with the intact state [18]. However, the long-term effects of this condition on joint biomechanics, as well as on the histological characteristics and mechanical properties of the DL, remain unclear. Furthermore, data-based evidence is still lacking regarding the impact of lateral ligament injuries on the development of chronic injury to the DL.

Therefore, a combined biomechanical and biological investigation may provide a more comprehensive understanding of DL injury mechanisms. Computational modeling can help identify abnormal mechanical environments associated with DL injury, while in vivo experimental models allow further evaluation of the resulting structural and biomechanical changes in the ligament. Accordingly, this study aimed to: (1) use a validated finite element model of the human ankle to systematically analyze the injury risks of individual DL bundles under various acute and chronic loading conditions; and (2) use a rat model to investigate the chronic effects of transecting the lateral ligaments and tibialis posterior tendon on ankle joint morphology, bone volume fraction, articular stress, and on the mechanical and histological characteristics of the DL. We hypothesized that the TCL and ATTL would be more susceptible to injury under acute loading conditions, and that the transection of the lateral ligaments or the tibialis posterior tendon would both lead to abnormal joint biomechanics and progressive damage to the DL, with lateral ligament injury causing more pronounced cumulative microstructural damage to the DL.

Materials and methods

Finite element analysis of injury risk in DL bundles under acute and chronic loading conditions

Establishment and validation of the finite element model

All procedures related to model development were approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number: 2020−1123). The geometry of a human right foot (female, age: 46, height: 162 cm, weight: 57 kg; the same specimen used for subsequent validation) was reconstructed from MRI images (TE = 4.3, TR = 380, resolution = 0.4 × 0.4 mm, slice thickness = 0.4 mm) using Mimics 21.0 (Materialise N. V., Leuven, Belgium). The model included bones, cartilages, ligaments, the plantar fascia, and encapsulated bulk soft tissue (Fig. 1a). A total of 11 ankle ligaments were incorporated into the model as three-dimensional geometries, including five lateral ligaments (anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL), calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), anterior tibiofibular ligament (ATiFL), posterior tibiofibular ligament (PTiFL)) and six medial ligaments (talonavicular ligament (TNL), tibiospring ligament (TSL), ATTL, TCL, superficial posterior tibiotalar ligament (sPTTL), dPTTL). The plantar fascia and other ligaments were modeled as truss elements to simplify the model. A sports medicine physician validated the reconstructed structures. The three-dimensional geometries were meshed with first-order, four-node tetrahedral elements using HyperMesh (Altair Engineering, Tokyo, Japan). Mesh convergence was verified by applying a 150 N anterior drawer force until further refinement altered the tibia-talus displacement by less than 2%. Final element sizes were 0.5 mm for ligaments and cartilage and 1 mm for other structures, resulting in a total of 4,178,568 elements. Material properties, boundary conditions, and post-processing steps were defined with Abaqus (Simulia, Inc., USA, version 2016). Cartilage-to-cartilage interactions were defined as frictionless sliding contacts, which allowed for mutual sliding without penetration. Ligaments were fixed to their bony insertions to ensure consistent displacement. Additionally, binding constraints were applied between the cartilage and adjacent bony surfaces, as well as between bone and surrounding bulk soft tissue. Definitions of material properties were detailed in Table 1. These modeling procedures have been documented in our previous studies [19].

For biomechanical validation, a Ligs digital joint measurement device (Innomotion Inc., China) and an X-ray imaging system were used to perform anterior drawer tests (ADT) and talar tilt tests on the same cadaveric specimen used for model development. During ADT, the calf and heel were fixed. At the same time, a 150 N anterior force was applied to the tibia at a loading rate of 3 N/s, with simultaneous radiographic imaging (Fig. 1b). The device was then adjusted to apply a medial force to the tibia using the same loading parameters again with concurrent imaging. Subsequently, the ATFL was surgically transected, and all procedures were repeated. Two parameters were quantified using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software (Fig. 1b): (1) the anterior drawer distance, defined as the vertical distance between the posterior margins of the tibia and talus, and (2) the talar tilt angle, defined as the angle between the tibial and talar articular surfaces. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was used for analysis to reduce variability. The finite element model was subsequently subjected to the same test conditions for comparison.

The skin, adipose tissue, fascia, and musculotendinous structures overlying the ankle joint were carefully dissected to fully expose the ligamentous complexes for anatomical validation. A vernier caliper with an accuracy of 0.1 mm was used to measure the length, width, and thickness of each ligament (Fig. 1d). Given that the DL contains both superficial and deep layers, the bundles in the superficial layer were measured first, followed by careful separation to examine the deeper structures. The same methodology was used to obtain dimensional data for each ligament in the finite element model and to compare it with the experimental results.

Evaluation of stress in DL bundles under acute loading conditions

The coordinate system in the finite element model was established according to the method described by Brockett and Chapman [22] (Fig. 2a). The center of rotation was defined as the midpoint between the fibular apex and the medial malleolar apex. The Y-axis passed through this center and was oriented perpendicular to the sagittal plane. The Z-axis also passed through the center and was aligned with the longitudinal axis of the tibia, perpendicular to the Y-axis. The X-axis was perpendicular to the other two axes.

Finite element analysis of acute injury mechanisms. a Establishment of the center of rotation and the coordinate system for the ankle joint, along with a schematic representation of everyday ankle joint movements, b Illustration of the four acute injury loading conditions applied in the model, c Maximum von Mises stress in each deltoid ligament bundle at varying plantarflexion and dorsiflexion angles during external rotation, d Maximum von Mises stress in each bundle at varying plantarflexion and dorsiflexion angles during eversion, e Maximum von Mises stress in each bundle during supination-external rotation, f Maximum von Mises stress in each bundle during pronation-external rotation

The following acute loading conditions were simulated in this study (Fig. 2b): (1) a 7.5 Nm external rotation moment combined with a 285 N compressive force (approximately half body weight) applied at plantarflexion-dorsiflexion angles ranging from − 15° to 15°; (2) a 7.5 Nm eversion moment combined with a 285 N compressive force applied at the same plantarflexion-dorsiflexion angles; (3) supination-external rotation, simulated by a 10 Nm external rotation moment with a 285 N compressive force applied at 10° of inversion, 10° of internal rotation, and 10° of plantarflexion; (4) pronation-external rotation, simulated by a 10 Nm external rotation moment with a 285 N compressive force applied at 10° of eversion, 10° of external rotation, and 10° of dorsiflexion.

The peak von Mises stress in the DL bundles was compared with the ligament yield stress of 45.3 MPa, which represents the maximum yield value recorded among all samples in the cadaveric experiments by Siegler et al. [23], to evaluate injury risk.

Evaluation of stress in DL bundles during gait after lateral ligament injury

Following the methodology established by Wang et al. [21], two plate layers tied together were implemented at the base of the ankle model to simulate the ground support (Fig. 3a). The upper layer, with isotropic elastic properties (Young’s modulus: 17,000 MPa, Poisson’s ratio: 0.1), simulated a concrete pavement, while the lower layer was defined as a rigid body for applying boundary and loading conditions. A surface-to-surface frictional contact (coefficient = 0.6 [24]) was used to simulate the interaction between the plantar surface and supporting substrate. Boundary conditions and ground reaction forces were derived from gait analysis data obtained using the Vicon Motion Analysis System by Wang et al. [21], appropriately scaled based on the subject’s body weight. During the stance phase, the superior surfaces of the tibia, fibula, and encapsulated soft tissues were fixed. Ground reaction forces in the vertical, anteroposterior, and mediolateral directions were applied through the rigid plate. The plate maintained rotational freedom about the Y-axis to replicate physiological foot-shank angles, allowing unconstrained translational motion in all three planes of motion. The peak von Mises stresses in the DL bundles at ten time points (10% increments) were extracted for analysis.

Animal study on the effects of lateral ligament and tibialis posterior tendon transection on chronic degeneration of the DL

Studies have confirmed that rats and mice exhibit a plantar walking gait similar to that of humans [25, 26]. Furthermore, these rodents exhibit notable similarities to human in their ankle joint anatomy [27, 28], periarticular ligament distribution [29], and neuromuscular movement [30]. Therefore, this study used rat models to investigate the mechanisms of chronic injury to the DL systematically. Thirty-nine male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (266 ± 5 g; Shanghai Covance Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were randomly assigned to three groups: lateral ligament rupture group (LR, n = 13), tibialis posterior tendon rupture group (TPR, n = 13), and sham group (n = 13). All rats underwent 6 weeks of treadmill running, followed by various experimental assessments. These included Micro-CT scanning (5 rats per group) to evaluate bone volume fraction (BVF), joint space width, and talar stress obtained by finite element analysis; mechanical testing (10 rats per group, consisting of 5 rats from the Micro-CT scan and an additional 5 rats) to determine failure load and stiffness of the DL; and histological and immunofluorescence analysis (3 rats per group) to examine fiber morphology and inflammation of the DL (Fig. 4a).

Animal preparation

All animals were housed in specific pathogen-free facilities under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 30–70% relative humidity, adequate ventilation, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle), with three rats per cage. Standard rodent chow and autoclaved water were provided ad libitum. The experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-AF-003-2022) and adhered to ARRIVE guidelines.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were conducted by a fully trained and certified surgeon under anesthesia and aseptic conditions. The right hind paw was first shaved and then sterilized with 75% pharmaceutical-grade ethanol. In the LR group (Fig. 4a–c), a 1.5-cm lateral incision was made to expose and transect the ATFL and CFL with microscissors. For surgical consistency, a matching medial incision was also created, and both wounds were subsequently closed. In the TPR group, a 1.5-cm medial incision was used to transect the tibialis posterior tendon, with an additional lateral incision made and closed. The sham group underwent identical medial and lateral exposures (1.5-cm incisions) without ligament or tendon transection. Postoperatively, the rats were placed in a temperature-controlled chamber (37 °C) with supplemental oxygen until fully ambulatory. For 72 h post-surgery, each rat received a subcutaneous injection of 200,000U penicillin diluted with saline and was maintained at 37 °C with continuous activity monitoring. Clinical assessments of wound healing, weight-bearing, and grooming behavior were conducted daily for 7 days.

Treadmill running

All rats underwent a six-week treadmill running protocol (four-channel, single channel dimensions: 565 × 116 mm) following the method of Chang et al. [27]. The rats ran for 40 min per day at a speed of 10 m/min, for a total duration of six weeks. If a rat became fatigued and stopped running, it would slide onto an electric shock grid with a current of 1.5 mA, compelling it to continue running to avoid the shock (Fig. 4d). This timeframe was selected to simulate the transition toward a state of chronic ankle instability, representing a critical window for ligamentous remodeling and mechanical adaptation preceding the onset of severe joint degeneration. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that this level of exercise intensity and duration is sufficient to exacerbate and induce chronic degenerative changes in the related tissues [27, 28, 31].

Micro-CT analysis

After completing the 6-week treadmill protocol, five rats from each group were randomly selected, euthanized, and their right ankle joints were harvested. Each joint was secured in a custom-designed L-shaped fixture to maintain a neutral anatomical position during scanning (Fig. 5a). Micro-CT imaging (PINGSENG Healthcare Inc., China) was conducted with the following parameters: 90 kV, 0.08 mA, and a resolution of 10 μm. The acquired images were reconstructed using the provided software. The talus was first divided into medial and lateral sections along the groove of the talar dome, and each section was further equally subdivided into anterior and posterior regions, resulting in four subregions. Four regions of interest (ROIs), defined as columns inscribed within these subregions, were established for bone volume fraction (BVF) analysis across 15 consecutive slices. The BVF of the medial and lateral compartments was determined by averaging the values of ROIs 1–2 and ROIs 3–4, respectively (Fig. 5b). Joint space widths on the medial and lateral sides were quantified using Mimics software on the 20th and 60th slices in the sagittal plane, counted from the appearance of the talus.

Micro-CT analysis of articular surface morphology, talar bone volume fraction (BVF), and joint space width. a Illustration of micro-CT scanning with the specimen fixed in a custom 3D-printed frame, b Illustration showing the regions of interest for talar BVF measurement and the schematic for measuring joint space width on the medial and lateral sides of the talus, c Reconstructed bone geometries from micro-CT images, with red arrows indicating regions of surface roughness and osteophyte formation, d Comparison of medial and lateral talar BVF among groups, e Comparison of medial and lateral tibiotalar joint space width among groups

Subsequently, finite element models of the ankle joints were developed from micro-CT images (Fig. 6a). The model included five bony structures (tibia, fibula, talus, navicular, and calcaneus) and was meshed with tetrahedral elements. Bone was modeled as a homogeneous isotropic material with a Young’s modulus of 18,000 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3. The interactions between adjacent articular bone surfaces were defined as frictionless sliding contacts. The calcaneus was fixed to provide a stable boundary condition. Considering that the ligaments were not included in the model, simulations were performed using displacement-controlled loading, assuming that the ligaments and other unmodeled soft tissues would maintain the stability of the joint during loading. Two loading conditions were then applied: (a) a 0.1 mm axial displacement applied to the proximal surface of the tibia, followed by a 5° inversion rotation of the tibia; and (b) a 0.1 mm axial displacement applied to the proximal surface of the tibia, followed by a 5° eversion rotation of the tibia. The resulting stress distributions on the talar surface and the maximum von Mises stress in the medial and lateral talus were compared among the groups.

Finite element analysis of talar stress after 6 weeks of treadmill running. a Illustration of the modeling process and applied loading conditions, b Von Mises stress distribution on the talus under ankle inversion, c Statistical comparison of the maximum von Mises stress on the medial and lateral talus among groups under ankle inversion, d Von Mises stress distribution on the talus under ankle eversion, e Statistical comparison of the maximum von Mises stress on the medial and lateral talus among groups under ankle eversion

Mechanical testing

Ten rats from each group were used for mechanical testing. A medial skin incision was made, and the surrounding soft tissues were removed to expose the DL. The DL, along with its bony attachments at both insertion sites, was excised as a whole unit (Fig. 7a). Each specimen was then mounted on a tensile testing machine (Orthotek Lab, Shanghai, China) (Fig. 7b). After a 0.5 N preload was applied, the ligament underwent ten preconditioning cycles (0–0.15 mm displacement) at a speed of 0.0167 mm/s, followed by continuous loading at the same rate until failure. Load-displacement curves were recorded to determine the failure load and stiffness. The failure load was defined as the peak force before rupture. Stiffness was defined as the ratio of the applied force to the corresponding elongation within the linear elastic portion of the curve (Fig. 7c). To minimize inter-individual variability, the same testing procedure was applied to the contralateral DL. The values from the right DL were normalized by those from the left side for group comparisons.

Mechanical testing of the deltoid ligament (DL) after 6 weeks of treadmill running. a Illustration of specimen preparation, b Illustration of tensile testing, c Representative force-displacement curve of the DL showing the extracted failure load and stiffness, d Comparison of the failure load ratio of the DL among groups, e Comparison of the stiffness ratio of the DL among groups

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

The right DLs were dissected from the remaining three rats in each group for subsequent histological and immunofluorescence analyses. Collected tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin after gradient dehydration with ethanol. Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned longitudinally into 5-µm-thick sections using a microtome. For each sample, two paraffin sections were randomly selected, dewaxed with xylene, and hydrated with graded ethanol. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson staining were employed to assess the distribution and organization of cells and collagen fibers. The remaining paraffin sections underwent immunofluorescence staining (Thermo Fisher, USA), targeting type I collagen (COL1), type III collagen (COL3), matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). The stained paraffin sections were examined using an LSM 700 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) to detect the positive signals for each antibody. Image J software (version 1.50b, NIH, USA) was used for semi-quantitative analysis. Fluorescence intensities were normalized to the mean value of the sham group to obtain relative fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and data distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. When the normality assumption was violated, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied for between-group comparisons. The statistical significance threshold was set at p < 0.05, with the following conventions for significance levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. A post-hoc analysis was conducted using GPower 3.1 software to assess the statistical power of the current sample size in supporting the main conclusions (α = 0.05). All graphical representations were generated using Origin software, with continuous data expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Model validation

The anterior drawer distances and talar tilt angles predicted by the finite element model before and after ATFL transection closely matched the experimental measurements, exhibiting discrepancies within 6.5% (Fig. 1c).

The length, width, and thickness of ligaments measured in the model also agreed well with anatomical measurements, with maximum differences of 2.0 mm, 0.6 mm, and 0.2 mm, respectively (Fig. 1e).

Stress in DL bundles under acute loading conditions

Under an external rotation torque, TCL, ATTL, dPTTL, and TNL experienced higher stress than the other DL bundles, with stress levels increasing as the angles of plantarflexion and dorsiflexion increased (Fig. 2c). Notably, TCL and ATTL had already reached their yield stress at the neutral position. Applying an eversion torque yielded a similar pattern, although the overall stress within DL was lower than that under external rotation. (Fig. 2d). Under both supination-external rotation and pronation-external rotation loading conditions, the peak stresses in the TCL, ATTL, and dPTTL consistently exceeded the yield stress (Fig. 2e and f). Under pronation-external rotation loading, nearly all DL bundles exceeded the yield stress.

Stress in DL bundles during gait after lateral ligament injury

Compared to the intact model, the LR model exhibited increased maximum stress in all DL bundles (Fig. 3b). The most marked increases were observed in the sPTTL and dPTTL, with stresses rising by up to 150% and 54%, respectively.

Effects of lateral ligaments and tibialis posterior tendon transection on bone morphology, bone volume fraction (BVF), and joint space width

Three-dimensional reconstruction of CT images revealed that when compared with the sham group, the ankle articular surfaces in the LR and TPR groups were rougher and more worn, with evidence of osteophyte formation (Fig. 5c). The medial talar BVF was significantly higher in the sham group than in the LR group (0.66 ± 0.06 vs. 0.52 ± 0.09; p < 0.05; power = 0.72; Fig. 5d), and the lateral talar BVF was significantly higher in the sham group than both the LR group (0.66 ± 0.03 vs. 0.51 ± 0.03; p < 0.001; power = 0.99) and TPR group (0.66 ± 0.03 vs. 0.58 ± 0.04; p < 0.05; power = 0.88). Additionally, the medial ankle joint space was significantly more expansive in the TPR group than in the sham group (0.29 ± 0.08 vs. 0.15 ± 0.03 mm; p < 0.001; power = 0.89; Fig. 5e), whereas the lateral joint space was significantly increased in the LR group compared with the sham group (0.29 ± 0.04 vs. 0.21 ± 0.04 mm; p < 0.01, power = 0.79).

Effects of lateral ligaments and tibialis posterior tendon transection on talar surface stress

After applying an ankle inversion load, both the LR and TPR groups exhibited increased stress levels on the talar surface compared with the sham group (Fig. 6b). The maximum stress in the LR group was significantly higher than in the sham group, reaching approximately 2.4-fold (p < 0.05, power = 0.56) and 45-fold (p < 0.01, power = 0.98) increases on the medial and lateral surfaces, respectively (Fig. 6c). A similar pattern of stress distribution was observed under the eversion loading condition (Fig. 6d). The maximum stress on the medial and lateral talar surfaces in the LR group was approximately 4.9-fold (p < 0.01, power = 0.90) and 15-fold (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) that of the sham group, respectively. In contrast, for the TPR group, the corresponding increases were approximately 2-fold (p < 0.05, power = 0.81) and 10-fold (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) (Fig. 6e).

Effects of lateral ligaments and tibialis posterior tendon transection on the mechanical properties of the DL

The failure load ratio in the sham group was 1.01 ± 0.22. In comparison, the LR group showed a 41.6% reduction (p < 0.001, power = 0.96). Similarly, the failure load ratio in the TPR group was 0.70 ± 0.16, corresponding to a 30.7% decrease (p < 0.01, power = 0.94) (Fig. 7d). The stiffness ratio in the sham group was 1.04 ± 0.27. In contrast, the LR group exhibited a significantly lower value of 0.69 ± 0.22, representing a 33.6% decrease (p < 0.01, power = 0.86). Although no significant difference was observed between the TPR and sham group, the TPR group exhibited a 17.3% reduction in the mean value (Fig. 7e).

Effects of lateral ligaments and tibialis posterior tendon transection on DL histology

Compared with the sham group, DL fibers in both the LR and TPR groups exhibited disorganized arrangement and reduced overall density, which was especially pronounced in the LR group (Fig. 8a and b). Mature ligaments are primarily composed of collagen types 1 (COL1) and 3 (COL3), with type 1 being the predominant type. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the LR group and TPR group exhibited decreased fluorescence intensity of COL1 and elevated levels of COL3, compared to the sham group (Fig. 8c, d). The levels of MMP-2, which participates in extracellular matrix degradation, and the strongly pro‑inflammatory cytokine IL‑1β were elevated in both the LR and TPR groups (Fig. 8e, f). Semi-quantitative analysis revealed significantly lower COL1 contents in the LR and TPR groups, reaching approximately 43% (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) and 50% (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) of the sham group levels, respectively (Fig. 8g), indicating ligament degeneration. Conversely, the COL3 contents in the LR and TPR groups increased to 342% (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) and 173% (p < 0.01, power = 0.84) of the sham group, respectively (Fig. 8h), indicating enhanced tissue remodeling. Furthermore, the MMP-2 levels in the LR and TPR groups reached 430% (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) and 219% (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) of the sham group, respectively (Fig. 8i), indicating extracellular matrix degradation. In addition, IL-1β levels in the LR and TPR groups reached 20.6-fold (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) and 8.5-fold (p < 0.01, power = 0.99) relative to the sham group (Fig. 8j), indicating the presence of inflammation.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that, under various acute injury loading conditions, the TCL, ATTL, and dPTTL experienced greater stress than the other DL bundles, indicating a higher susceptibility to injury. Among the four acute loading conditions examined, pronation-external rotation loading posed the most significant risk of DL injury. After lateral ligament injury, the DL endured elevated stress throughout the entire stance phase of the gait cycle compared with the intact model. Furthermore, results from the animal experiments revealed that prolonged physiological activity following lateral ligament or tibialis posterior tendon injury led to abnormal articular surface morphology, widening of the joint space, increased articular stress, as well as reduced mechanical strength and degenerative histological changes in the DL. Notably, chronic damage to the DL appeared more pronounced after lateral ligament injury.

Under acute loading conditions, the TCL exhibited the highest stress among all DL bundles, which may be attributed to its unique anatomical position. As the only ligament spanning both the ankle and talocalcaneal joint complexes [32], the TCL must withstand substantial forces during joint motion to maintain stability across multiple joints. Cao et al. reported that the TCL length remained nearly unchanged during ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion [7]. This is likely due to the TCL’s high modulus (the highest among all DL bundles [20]), and its considerable thickness (the greatest among the superficial bundles [9]). These properties result in minimal strain yet high tensile loading under acute conditions. Therefore, evaluating ligament injury risk based on stress or tension may be more appropriate than relying on elongation [14]. Additionally, the ATTL and dPTTL also exhibited relatively high stress, likely due to their anatomical positioning as deep structures, with insertions located near the ankle joint’s center of rotation [33]. Their shorter moment arms generate higher internal forces compared with other bundles.

In all acute loading conditions, pronation-external rotation generated the highest DL tension. This may be explained by the combined motion of ankle eversion, dorsiflexion, and external rotation, which jointly stretch multiple DL bundles along their anatomical orientations. This finding aligned with previous research [34].

Finite element analysis indicated that severing the lateral ligament increased DL stress during daily walking, likely due to joint instability resulting from the loss of lateral support. To compensate, other ligaments, including the DL, were subjected to greater tension to maintain joint stability. This finding supports existing hypotheses on the mechanism of chronic DL injury [35]. Persistent high-stress loading can trigger inflammatory responses and micro-injuries within the ligament [36, 37], ultimately leading to ligament degeneration and progressive medial ankle instability. Consistent with this, our animal study showed that lateral ligament transection followed by prolonged activity resulted in decreased mechanical properties, notable inflammatory infiltration and fibrous micro-injuries in the DL, and joint space widening accompanied by increased articular stress, decreased talar BVF, and osteophyte formation. The elevated peak stresses observed in the experimental groups likely act as mechanical stimuli for ligament fibroblasts, triggering an inflammatory cascade in which IL‑1β serves as a key mediator and promotes the expression of the matrix‑degrading enzyme MMP‑2. As an important collagen‑degrading enzyme, MMP‑2 mediates the degradation of type I collagen, leading to disruption of the extracellular matrix and ultimately contributing to ligament degeneration and reduced mechanical properties, consistent with our histological and immunofluorescence findings.

Animal experiment revealed that transection of the tibialis posterior tendon produced degenerative effects on the ankle joint and DL similar to those caused by lateral ligament rupture. This likely reflects the fact that the loss of either medial or lateral support compromises joint stability and increases the mechanical burden on the DL. However, the impact of lateral ligament rupture was more pronounced, possibly because it directly induces excessive anterior translation and internal rotation of the talus. In contrast, tibialis posterior tendon rupture leads to a gradual collapse of the medial longitudinal arch [38]. These findings suggest that treatment of lateral ligament injuries may benefit from concurrent medial reinforcement to prevent chronic ankle instability. Notably, chronic injury mechanisms related to tibialis posterior tendon rupture were not simulated in the finite element model due to the lack of standardized strategies for representing muscular forces in biomechanical modeling [39]. To ensure the reliability of our simulation results, this study focused on the mechanical consequences of lateral ligament rupture. Overall, the results indicated that both injuries contributed to chronic DL degeneration, with lateral ligament injury exerting a more substantial effect.

It should also be noted that alterations in limb loading patterns following surgery, such as transient antalgic gait, may theoretically influence ligament loading and contribute to joint degeneration. However, in the present study, all rats underwent a one‑week recovery period before treadmill training, and no obvious limping or weight‑bearing avoidance was observed during locomotion. Therefore, the degenerative changes observed in the DL, including mechanical weakening, histological degeneration, and inflammatory responses, are more likely attributable to persistent mechanical instability rather than temporary post‑operative gait alterations.

These findings may provide insights for preventing DL injuries by highlighting high-risk bundles under specific acute loading conditions, and help clinicians to reduce the likelihood of missed diagnoses. They may also enhance our understanding of the biomechanical roles of DL bundles and support the optimization of surgical reconstruction strategies. Currently, the management of medial complex injuries is gradually shifting from traditional open repair toward arthroscopic internal bracing. Our findings may provide a biomechanical perspective supporting this clinical trend. Since deep structures such as the ATTL and dPTTL are located in deeper layers and bear substantial loads, achieving optimal visualization and anatomical tensioning through open repair may be technically challenging in some cases. In contrast, internal bracing can provide immediate mechanical augmentation for these deep components, potentially protecting the healing ligaments from excessive stress during the early postoperative phase. Furthermore, this study may provide theoretical guidance for designing flexible protective equipment, with potential for targeted material reinforcement over vulnerable regions of the ankle to ensure adequate protection.

This study has several limitations: (1) The finite element model was based on data from a single subject, which may limit its generalizability to a broader population. Individual anatomical variations in the DL bundles (such as bundle size, fiber orientation, and material properties) may influence the stress distribution among the six individual ligament bundles. For example, bundles with greater thickness or a higher elastic modulus may play a more dominant role in load sharing. Similarly, variations in fiber orientation determine the moment components sustained by each bundle during ankle motion. However, this approach allows precise control of individual variables and permits unlimited modification of specific factors without compromising the integrity of the specimen. Such modeling techniques are widely used to improve understanding of underlying biomechanical mechanisms. (2) The deltoid ligament bundles were simplified as linear elastic materials in the finite element model, whereas real ligaments exhibit nonlinear and viscoelastic behavior, particularly near the failure region. Although this simplification may affect the accuracy of peak stress prediction under high‑torque injury scenarios such as pronation-external rotation, it remains reasonable for qualitatively comparing the stress distribution among different ligament bundles and evaluating their relative vulnerability to injury. (3) The status of the ligaments and the tibialis posterior tendon was simplified as either completely ruptured or intact, without considering partial tears. Future research may further explore how mild injuries to these structures influence the biomechanical properties of the DL. (4) Although the 6‑week treadmill rat model can induce structural and mechanical changes associated with joint instability within a short period, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to the multi‑year disease course in humans. Due to species differences and the distinct time scale of disease progression, this model may not fully capture the complex biological adaptations and compensatory mechanisms occurring in humans.

Conclusion

The TCL, ATTL, and dPTTL bundles were identified as being particularly susceptible to injury under various acute loading conditions, with pronation-external rotation posing the most significant risk. Injuries to the lateral ligament and the tibialis posterior tendon led to abnormal ankle alignment, increased articular stress and osteophyte formation, as well as compromised mechanical properties and histological degeneration of the DL. Notably, the impact of lateral ligament injury was more pronounced than that of tibialis posterior tendon injury.
