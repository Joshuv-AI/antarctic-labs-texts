Evaluating the Effect of Orthodontic Treatment on Periodontal Health in Patients with Preexisting Periodontal Conditions
Authors: Mohammad K. Alam, Mohammad Y. Hajeer, Taif Alsaleh, Balqees Alhadi, Afrah Alsaleh, Raghad Alnsairy, Shahad Alruwaili
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:In many cases, orthodontic treatment is necessary to improve the alignment and functionality of teeth. But there is still uncertainty about how orthodontic equipment may affect periodontal health, especially in individuals who already have periodontal disease.Materials and Methods:For this investigation, a total of 50 patients with prior periodontal diseases were chosen. The participants were split into two groups: Group B (n = 25) was the control group and did not get any orthodontic intervention, whereas Group A (n = 25) had orthodontic therapy utilizing fixed appliances. At baseline, six months, and twelve months, periodontal health was evaluated using the Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), and Clinical Attachment Level (CAL). Paired t-tests and ANOVA were used to assess the significance of time-series changes in the data.Results:There was a statistically significant increase in plaque accumulation in Group A, as evidenced by the mean PI increasing from 1.2 ± 0.4 at baseline to 1.5 ± 0.5 at 12 months (P < 0.05). Additionally, there was a noteworthy rise in the GI, from 1.1 ± 0.3 to 1.4 ± 0.4 (P < 0.05). Though there was a modest drop in the CAL from 3.5 ± 0.8 mm to 3.4 ± 0.7 mm, it was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) and remained very steady. Group B, on the other hand, showed no appreciable variations in PI, GI, or CAL during the course of the investigation.Conclusion:Patients receiving orthodontic treatment who already have periodontal disease tend to be more prone to plaque buildup and gingival irritation. Nonetheless, the clinical attachment levels can be sustained for the duration of the treatment with proper periodontal maintenance.

INTRODUCTION

Pre-existing periodontal conditions including as gingivitis and periodontitis make individuals more vulnerable to the adverse effects of orthodontic treatment, such as heightened plaque accumulation, inflammation of the gingival tissue, and dental attachment loss.[12]

Failure to treat periodontal diseases, which are chronic inflammatory disorders that harm the tissues supporting the teeth, may lead to progressive loss of attachment and resorption of bone. The use of orthodontic equipment might provide novel challenges in maintaining periodontal health due to the potential for wires, bands, and brackets to function as additional sites for plaque buildup, therefore impeding the adherence to proper oral hygiene practices.[3] Patients with pre-existing periodontal issues should have heightened anxiety since they are at a higher risk of exacerbating their disease throughout orthodontic treatment.[45]

A multitude of academic studies have underscored the need of thorough periodontal monitoring and maintenance during orthodontic treatment to mitigate such risks. However, there is currently a lack of consensus in the literature on the extent to which orthodontic treatment may compromise the periodontal health of patients with preexisting illnesses.[67] Although some studies have reported significant challenges in preserving periodontal stability after orthodontic treatment, others have shown that the harmful effects on periodontal health may be prevented by rigorous periodontal care.[8]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 50 patients with preexisting periodontal conditions, such as chronic periodontitis, were selected from individuals seeking orthodontic treatment. The inclusion criteria required patients to be aged between 18 and 40 years, with a clinical diagnosis of chronic periodontitis, and no history of systemic diseases. Additionally, they should not have undergone periodontal treatment in the last six months. Exclusion criteria included patients who smoked, were pregnant, or had other systemic conditions that could affect periodontal health.

The participants were randomly assigned into two groups. Group A, the Orthodontic Treatment Group, consisted of 25 patients who underwent orthodontic treatment using fixed appliances. Group B, the Control Group, also included 25 patients, but they did not receive any orthodontic treatment; instead, they continued with regular periodontal maintenance.

Orthodontic treatment for Group A was carried out using fixed stainless steel brackets with a 0.022 × 0.028-inch slot and archwires made of NiTi and stainless steel. Patients were given instructions on maintaining oral hygiene, which included the use of interdental brushes and mouth rinses. Monthly orthodontic adjustments were performed, and patients were recalled every four weeks for a period of 12 months.

Periodontal health was assessed at three time points: baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. The assessment utilized the following indices: Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), and Clinical Attachment Level (CAL). The data collected from these assessments were analyzed using SPSS software version 25.

RESULTS

Patient demographics

A total of 50 patients participated in the study, with 25 patients in each group. The mean age of participants in Group A (Orthodontic Treatment Group) was 28.5 ± 6.2 years, and in Group B (Control Group), it was 29.1 ± 5.8 years. The gender distribution was similar across both groups, with 14 males and 11 females in Group A, and 13 males and 12 females in Group B.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this research illustrate the intricate relationship between persons with previous periodontal illnesses and orthodontic therapy. Our data indicate that the orthodontic treatment group saw a significant increase in plaque buildup and gingival inflammation, whereas the clinical attachment levels remained generally stable during the intervention. Consistent with previous research, our findings indicate that orthodontic equipment might exacerbate gingival inflammation and plaque retention, particularly in those who are already susceptible to periodontal issues.[12]

The higher PI and GI in the orthodontic treatment group may be attributed to the fixed orthodontic equipment, since they augmented the number of plaque retention sites and exacerbated the challenge of maintaining oral hygiene.[3] In patients with compromised periodontal health, prior studies have shown that the use of fixed appliances significantly heightens the likelihood of plaque development and gingival inflammation.[45] The findings of this study suggest that although orthodontic treatment may have adverse impacts on periodontal health, proper periodontal care might mitigate the extent of attachment loss.[6]

CONCLUSION

Ultimately, meticulous periodontal care may preserve clinical attachment levels, even if orthodontic treatment may exacerbate plaque buildup and gingival inflammation in those with pre-existing periodontal issues.
