Suzetrigine Is Not Expected to Have Clinically Relevant DDIs with Apixaban and Rivaroxaban Based on PBPK Modeling
Authors: Guanyu Wang, Elizabeth Lakota, Bo Feng, Licong Jiang, Guiqing Liang, Brian Hare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuzetrigine (VX‐548) is a first‐in‐class, oral, non‐opioid, FDA‐approved analgesic for the treatment of moderate to severe acute pain. Based on Phase 1 clinical drug–drug interaction (DDI) studies, suzetrigine is a weak‐to‐moderate cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) inducer and thus has the potential to decrease exposure and impact efficacy of CYP3A substrates, such as apixaban and rivaroxaban, which are commonly used as anticoagulants peri‐ and post‐operatively. Given this, it is important to assess the DDIs of apixaban and rivaroxaban with suzetrigine. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models are commonly used to assess DDIs mediated through modulation of CYP3A and were used here to quantify the potential effect of co‐administration of suzetrigine on apixaban and rivaroxaban PK. The PBPK models were validated using observed data from suzetrigine, apixaban, and rivaroxaban clinical DDI studies. The validated models predict that suzetrigine decreases apixaban AUCinf by 6.1% (90% confidence interval [CI]: 5.7% to 6.4%) and Cmax by 1.8% (90% CI: 1.7% to 1.9%), and rivaroxaban AUCinf by 8.4% (90% CI: 7.2% to 9.6%) and Cmax by 3.5% (90% CI: 3.3% to 3.7%). The predicted small reductions in apixaban and rivaroxaban exposures (AUCinf <10%) are not expected to be clinically relevant since a 20% no‐effect boundary is typically used to determine the clinical impact of a DDI. The PBPK model predictions are consistent with the apixaban and rivaroxaban labels, which do not warn against co‐administration with weak or moderate CYP3A inducers, such as suzetrigine.

Introduction

Suzetrigine (VX‐548; Journavx) is a first‐in‐class, oral, non‐opioid analgesic approved in the United States based on its efficacy and safety in Phase 2 and Phase 3 clinical studies for the treatment of moderate to severe acute pain. 1 , 2 Suzetrigine is a potent and highly selective inhibitor of voltage‐gated sodium channel 1.8 (NaV1.8) that specifically inhibits the transmission of pain signals (action potentials) in peripheral sensory nerves. Suzetrigine was developed to address the high unmet need in the management of acute pain for an efficacious treatment with no addiction potential and a favorable safety and tolerability profile.

Given that acute pain often occurs in perioperative and postoperative settings where polypharmacy is common, it is important to understand the drug–drug‐interaction (DDI) profile of suzetrigine to guide the use of concomitant medications. The potential suzetrigine DDIs are mediated via the cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) enzymes. 3 As a sensitive CYP3A substrate, suzetrigine exposure can be altered by inducers and inhibitors of CYP3A. Further, suzetrigine is a weak‐to‐moderate CYP3A inducer 4 and thus has the potential to decrease exposure of CYP3A substrates.

CYP3A is a common metabolic pathway 5 and many drugs are CYP3A substrates. Because many drugs are metabolized and/or eliminated via multiple pathways and suzetrigine only causes weak‐to‐moderate induction of CYP3A, suzetrigine may not result in clinically relevant DDI with all CYP3A substrates. Therefore, it is important to characterize these potential interactions.

Rivaroxaban and apixaban are anticoagulants commonly used in the peri‐ and postoperative setting. Both of these drugs are cleared via multiple pathways, including direct biliary/urinary excretion with potential contribution of P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) efflux transporters and metabolic clearance by multiple enzymes, including CYP3A. 6 Simultaneous use of combined P‐gp and strong CYP3A inducers decreases exposure to apixaban and rivaroxaban, which may increase the risk for thromboembolic events. 7 , 8 Given the serious risks if anticoagulant exposures are decreased significantly, it is important to quantify the predicted apixaban and rivaroxaban exposure reductions when co‐administered with suzetrigine.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling is a well‐established strategy to utilize in vitro and clinical absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) and DDI data to simulate untested clinical scenarios and provide recommendations for use of concomitant medications. 9 It is commonly used to predict interactions among drugs metabolized by CYP3A, including the effect of CYP3A induction on the exposures of CYP3A substrates. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

Here, we used PBPK modeling to predict the impact of suzetrigine on the exposures of apixaban and rivaroxaban, and we demonstrated that suzetrigine is not expected to have a clinically significant DDI with either agent.

Methods

PBPK model development and simulations were performed using the Simcyp population‐based simulator version 22 14 (Certara UK Limited, Sheffield, UK). The equations governing PBPK and DDI processes are implemented within the Simcyp platform and are described in the Simcyp documentation. The models were developed and validated for suzetrigine and apixaban, whereas the default Simcyp model of rivaroxaban was used to predict the DDIs between suzetrigine as the perpetrator and apixaban and rivaroxaban as victims. The extent of the DDI was assessed using AUCinf and Cmax, which are PK parameters typically used to quantify the magnitude of DDI in clinical studies. 15

Suzetrigine PBPK Model

Suzetrigine was mainly cleared via metabolism (Figure S4). CYP reaction phenotyping studies in recombinant human CYP enzymes and human liver microsomes indicated that suzetrigine and its active metabolite were mainly metabolized by CYP3A 3 (see Determination of Fraction of Metabolism for Suzetrigine [fm, CYP3A] in Supplemental Information). CYP3A fm was estimated to be 0.85 (range 0.80–0.89).

The workflow of development and validation of the suzetrigine PBPK model is shown in Figure S1. This workflow follows best practice for modeling CYP3A‐mediated interactions by first using a “bottom‐up” approach incorporating in vitro data and parameters derived from nonclinical studies and then refining and validating the model with clinical data. 9

Base Model

The base suzetrigine and active metabolite PBPK model was developed using in vitro and clinical data. The suzetrigine tablet is an immediate‐release tablet. For absorption, the advanced dissolution, absorption, and metabolism (ADAM) model was used. While the 50‐mg tablet does not contain an enteric coating, it contains hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate (HPMCAS), which has pH‐dependent aqueous solubility (soluble above pH 6.5), and thus the solubilization of suzetrigine increases at pH values greater than 6.5. Consistent with these formulation properties, the enteric‐coated tablet model in Simcyp reduces dissolution at the lower pH in the stomach and increases dissolution at the higher pH in the intestine. Therefore, the default enteric‐coated tablet model in Simcyp was used to model this process.

Using this model with in vitro permeability (12.25 × 10−6 cm/s) and solubility (0.036 mg/mL) data, the fraction of suzetrigine absorbed (Fa) was predicted to be approximately 0.98. The high predicted Fa is consistent with the small fraction of the dose (<10%) recovered as unchanged parent drug in feces in the radiolabeled human ADME study (VX‐548‐006, Figure S4).

Intravenous clearance in the base model was back calculated from the predicted bioavailability (∼0.8) and apparent oral clearance from the tablet formulation in a relative BA study (VX‐548‐011; ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05455502) and in the suzetrigine‐alone dosing period in a DDI study (VX‐548‐013; rifampin; ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05635110). Steady‐state volume of distribution was estimated with clearance fixed using the Simcyp full PBPK models with Method 2. The tissue‐to‐plasma partition coefficient (Kp scalar) was optimized to capture the overall plasma concentration profiles of suzetrigine and its active metabolite after single oral doses in Studies VX‐548‐013 (rifampin), VX‐548‐008 (itraconazole), and VX‐548‐011 (relative BA).

Calibration

The base model was calibrated by simulating plasma concentration–time profiles of suzetrigine and its active metabolite and comparing them to the following clinical studies: a single 100‐mg dose in the clinical DDI study with a strong CYP3A inducer rifampin (VX‐548‐013) and in the relative BA study (VX‐548‐011), anda single 20‐mg dose in the clinical DDI study with a strong CYP3A inhibitor itraconazole (VX‐548‐008).

Validation

The final model was validated with multiple‐dose suzetrigine PK data from healthy volunteers (VX‐548‐007; ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05560464) to assess the model's ability to predict suzetrigine clearance after multiple dosing and the exposures of suzetrigine and its active metabolite at steady state. The model was further validated with data from a clinical DDI study assessing single‐dose PK of midazolam with and without multiple dose suzetrigine (VX‐548‐010; ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05541471) to confirm that the model adequately described CYP3A induction with suzetrigine (calibrated IC50 for CYP3A4 induction = 1.097µM and CYP3A4 maximal fold induction [Indmax] = 12.92). The default midazolam PBPK model from the Simcyp library 14 was used. These validation datasets were not used for model development. The baseline characteristics of healthy volunteers in these studies are provided in Table S1.

Apixaban PBPK Model

The initial PBPK model was published by Otsuka et al, 16 and the fraction of apixaban metabolized by CYP3A (CYP3A fm) was estimated to be 0.17 based on simulation results. Published human ADME data for apixaban indicated that its elimination involves both metabolism and renal excretion, 17 and apixaban was identified as a substrate of CYP3A. 7 , 18 Given that CYP3A‐mediated metabolism represents a major metabolic pathway based on human ADME data, the CYP3A fm may be higher than 0.17. Therefore, the apixaban CYP3A fm value in the PBPK model was updated and subsequently validated using additional published clinical DDI data, as shown in Figure S5.

The initial PBPK model 16 was first evaluated by using it to predict apixaban clinical DDIs with a broad range of CYP3A inhibitors and inducers, including itraconazole, 19 clarithromycin, 20 , 21 ketoconazole, 22 and rifampin. 23 The predictions from the initial PBPK model were compared to the observed data from the clinical DDI studies. The evaluation focused on DDI studies with CYP3A inhibitors and inducers because the intended use of the model was to predict the sensitivity of apixaban to a CYP3A inducer (suzetrigine). Based on this evaluation, the apixaban CYP3A fm was adjusted in the model to improve concordance between predicted and observed DDI data.

The updated apixaban model was validated using data from six additional DDI studies that were not used during model evaluation and modification to assess the impact of the following strong and moderate CYP3A inhibitors on apixaban exposure: diltiazem, 22 ritonavir, 24 posaconazole, 19 voriconazole 19 , 25 (2 different dosing regimens), and cobicistat. 25

The final validated PBPK model was used to predict the PK of apixaban when co‐administered with suzetrigine.

Rivaroxaban PBPK Model

The rivaroxaban PBPK model from the Simcyp library 14 was used without modification to predict the PK of rivaroxaban co‐administered with suzetrigine. In this model, an advanced ADAM model was used for absorption, and a full PBPK model with a permeability‐limited kidney model was used to describe the disposition. The model was validated with clinical DDI data. 14 The fm by CYP3A in the rivaroxaban model was estimated to be 0.40 based on a simulation using this model.

Prediction of Impact of Multiple Suzetrigine Dosing on Apixaban and Rivaroxaban PK

The change in apixaban and rivaroxaban exposure in the presence of suzetrigine was assessed using the developed and validated PBPK models. Simulations were conducted with single 10‐mg doses of apixaban or rivaroxaban before and after administering the clinical maintenance regimen of suzetrigine (50 mg q12h) for 14 days. Fourteen days of dosing is sufficient to evaluate the full CYP3A induction potential of a therapeutic. 15 , 26 , 27 The AUCinf and Cmax ratios for apixaban and rivaroxaban in the presence and absence of suzetrigine were calculated.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to account for uncertainty in the estimation of the fm for CYP3A. For apixaban, these sensitivity analyses were conducted with CYP3A fm values ± 0.1 around the estimated midpoint. The uncertainty in the CYP3A fm value for rivaroxaban was assessed by reviewing published clinical DDI studies with strong CYP3A inhibitors. 19 , 24 , 28 , 29 These studies showed an average twofold increase in the rivaroxaban AUC in the presence of a strong CYP3A inhibitor, indicating that the CYP3A fm could be up to 0.50. Therefore, simulations were conducted for rivaroxaban with CYP3A fm values of 0.40 (published model) and 0.50 (sensitivity analysis).

Simulation Design

All scenario simulations were performed with the default Healthy Volunteers Simcyp population (default 30% CV for PK parameters). For each simulation in model calibration and validation, 10 virtual trials of healthy volunteers were simulated, with trial size, gender ratio, and age range matched to each clinical trial design. For the simulations of suzetrigine with apixaban and rivaroxaban, 10 virtual trials were simulated, each including 10 virtual healthy volunteers, for a total number of 100 subjects. The proportion of females was set at 0.5, and the age range was 18–65 years old.

Results

Suzetrigine PBPK Model

The suzetrigine PBPK model was developed with non‐clinical and clinical data describing the ADME properties of the suzetrigine tablet formulation depicted in Figure S4. The CYP3A fm was estimated to be 0.85 based on CYP reaction phenotyping data, indicating that suzetrigine is extensively metabolized by CYP3A (see Supplemental Information for information about how fm was determined).

A clinical DDI study with itraconazole confirmed that suzetrigine is metabolized primarily by CYP3A. The strong CYP3A inducer rifampin significantly decreased suzetrigine AUCinf (93% reduction for suzetrigine and 85% for its active metabolite), and the strong CYP3A inhibitor itraconazole significantly increased suzetrigine AUCinf (4.8‐fold increase for suzetrigine and 4.4‐fold increase for its active metabolite), as shown in Table S2.

Suzetrigine metabolism was adequately characterized in the PBPK model. As shown in Table 2, the predicted suzetrigine and active metabolite AUCinf and Cmax ratios from the PBPK model were in good agreement with the observed data from the rifampin and itraconazole DDI studies (Figures S6 and S7). These results are consistent with CYP3A being the primary enzyme for suzetrigine metabolism.

Sensitivity analyses with larger CYP3A fm in the apixaban and rivaroxaban models predicted that suzetrigine decreases AUCinf of apixaban and rivaroxaban less than 12% and Cmax less than 5% (Table S6) as a worst‐case scenario. These sensitivity analyses were conducted with CYP3A fm values 0.1 unit larger than the value used in the final models (fm = 0.35 for apixaban and 0.50 for rivaroxaban in the sensitivity analyses compared to 0.25 for apixaban and 0.40 for rivaroxaban in the final models).

Discussion

A PBPK modeling approach was employed to predict the impact of suzetrigine dosing on the exposures of the commonly used anticoagulants apixaban and rivaroxaban. Since suzetrigine is a weak‐to‐moderate CYP3A inducer and apixaban and rivaroxaban are CYP3A substrates, clinical CYP3A inhibitor and inducer DDI studies were used to calibrate and validate the PBPK models.

The 90% confidence intervals for the predicted reductions in apixaban and rivaroxaban exposures with co‐administration of suzetrigine were less than 10% for AUCinf and less than 4% for Cmax (Table 6 and Figure 1). Exposure changes of less than 20% are generally considered not to have a clinically relevant impact on drug efficacy based on a no‐effect boundary referenced in regulatory guidance to interpret the clinical impact of DDIs. 15

Furthermore, the small predicted change in apixaban exposure is not expected to be clinically meaningful because similar rates of stroke and systemic embolism were found in a Phase 3 clinical trial of apixaban in patient subgroups with ∼25% lower apixaban exposure due to higher body weight or being administered a lower apixaban dose (2.5 vs 5 mg). 30 These results from a Phase 3 clinical trial indicate that a 25% reduction in exposure does not result in loss of efficacy for apixaban. While a similar analysis for rivaroxaban has not been published, the small predicted changes in its exposure with suzetrigine are also not expected to be clinically meaningful based on the 20% no effect boundary referenced in regulatory guidance. 15

Furthermore, this small predicted change in apixaban and rivaroxaban exposures is expected to be the maximum effect of suzetrigine because CYP3A induction reaches maximum in ∼2 weeks 27 and the PBPK simulation was conducted with suzetrigine dosed for 14 days. A shorter suzetrigine dosing duration is expected to result in even smaller reductions in apixaban and rivaroxaban exposures.

Overall, as described above, the PBPK model predicts only small changes in apixaban and rivaroxaban exposure when it is co‐administered with suzetrigine, and these changes are therefore not expected to have clinical importance.

The small effects of suzetrigine on apixaban and rivaroxaban exposure are consistent with apixaban and rivaroxaban being less sensitive CYP3A substrates than midazolam because they are eliminated by multiple other pathways in addition to being metabolized by CYP3A. For apixaban, urinary excretion represents a significant elimination pathway (24.5%–28.8% of the recovered dose, 17 mostly as unchanged drug). While CYP3A plays a major role in its oxidative metabolism, other CYPs, including CYP1A2, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, and 2J2, 18 also play minor roles. For rivaroxaban, approximately 36% of the dose is excreted as unchanged drug in urine, approximately 14% of the dose is eliminated via hydrolytic cleavage, and approximately 32% of the dose is eliminated via oxidative pathways. 31 In terms of rivaroxaban oxidative metabolism, besides CYP3A, CYP2J2 also plays a significant role. 32 Hence, the impact of CYP3A induction on apixaban and rivaroxaban was small due to their multiple clearance pathways besides CYP3A‐mediated metabolism.

The modeling results with suzetrigine are also consistent with the US product labels for apixaban and rivaroxaban. Apixaban 7 and rivaroxaban 8 US product labels warn against co‐administration with strong CYP3A inducers that are also P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) inducers, but do not make any statements about co‐administration with weak or moderate CYP3A inducers. As shown in the digoxin DDI study results (Table S2), suzetrigine does not induce the P‐gp transporter, 3 and suzetrigine is a weak‐to‐moderate CYP3A inducer (not a strong inducer). The small changes in exposure that are predicted when suzetrigine is co‐administered with apixaban or rivaroxaban are therefore consistent with the drug interaction information provided in apixaban and rivaroxaban product labels.

A limitation of this work is that clinical apixaban–suzetrigine and rivaroxaban–suzetrigine DDI studies were not conducted. However, use of validated PBPK modeling to predict enzyme‐mediated DDIs when clinical data with strong index perpetrators are available for validation, as is the case here, is well accepted by regulatory agencies. 33

Overall, based on the PBPK modeling reported here, together with information in the US product labels, clinically relevant DDI for apixaban and rivaroxaban with suzetrigine is not expected.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reports PBPK model predictions for the potential interaction of suzetrigine, an approved drug for acute pain, with the anticoagulants apixaban and rivaroxaban. The predicted effect of suzetrigine on the exposures of apixaban and rivaroxaban is small and not expected to be clinically meaningful. These results are consistent with apixaban and rivaroxaban US labels, which do not warn against use with weak‐to‐moderate CYP3A inducers and recommend no dosing modifications with weak or moderate CYP3A inducers.
