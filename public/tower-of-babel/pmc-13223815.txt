Traumatic cerebral venous air embolism associated with open skull fracture: Radiographic differentiation from pneumocephalus for clinical decision-making
Authors: Masamichi Endo, Satoru Sugiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral venous air embolism (CVAE) is a rare condition associated with various etiologies, including trauma. While intracranial air is a recognized finding in clinical practice, the pathway of its entry is rarely documented. An 84-year-old male presented following a head trauma after a fall. Computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed an open parietal skull fracture without associated intracranial hemorrhage. It also demonstrated that intracranial air was strictly restricted to the superior sagittal and cavernous sinuses. Consequently, a CVAE was diagnosed based on the air distribution, ruling out pneumocephalus. The patient remained clinically stable and was discharged after a 5-day hospital stay. Careful evaluation of the distribution of intracranial air on CT may aid in distinguishing CVAE from pneumocephalus and support appropriate clinical decision-making.Keywords: Cerebral venous air embolism, Open skull fracture, Radiological differentiation, Pneumocephalus, Clinical decision-making

Introduction

Cerebral venous air embolism (CVAE) is a rare condition reported in the literature [[1], [2], [3]]. It can occur in association with various etiologies, including trauma, central venous catheterization, and surgical procedures [2,4]. While venous air is occasionally observed in clinical practice, the direct radiographic identification of venous air entry routes is exceedingly rare. In contrast, pneumocephalus is defined as the presence of air within the intracranial compartment, including the extradural, subdural, subarachnoid, intraventricular, and intracerebral spaces [5]. Pneumocephalus associated with an open skull fractures is considered an indirect indicator of dural injury and often requires surgical intervention depending on the clinical context [6]. Although both conditions involve intracranial air, the treatment strategies may differ depending on the underlying pathophysiology and distribution of air [6,7]. Here, we report a case of traumatic CVAE in which careful evaluation of the distribution of intracranial air on CT allowed radiographic inference of the venous air entry route and effective differentiation from pneumocephalus, thereby facilitating appropriate clinical decision-making.

Case presentation

An 84-year-old male with dementia who was receiving anticoagulant therapy for atrial fibrillation fell at home. He was transferred to our hospital after bleeding from the head. On arrival, the patient was alert, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4V5M6, and demonstrated no neurological deficits. Physical examination revealed a laceration in the parietal region and exposed skull bone. Head CT imaging showed no intracranial hemorrhage but revealed a depressed, open fracture of the left parietal bone (Figs. 1A and B). The intracranial air was also noted. These findings initially raised suspicion for pneumocephalus in the setting of an open depressed skull fracture, in which surgical intervention, such as debridement or duraplasty, may be considered depending on the clinical context [8]. However, the air distribution was strictly confined to the superior sagittal and cavernous sinuses (Fig. 1C and D). Bone-window CT demonstrated that the fracture line extended into the diploë and that air was present within the diploic vein (Figs. 1E and F). Based on these findings, CVAE was diagnosed. The presumed pathophysiology may have involved air entry through the diploic vein at the site of the open skull fracture, inferred from the anatomical relationship and the distribution of intracranial air. No peripheral or central venous catheterization was performed prior to the detection of intracranial air. Crucially, no air was detected outside the venous structures. In this case, surgical intervention was initially considered in the context of an open depressed skull fracture with associated intracranial air. However, conservative management was ultimately selected because there were no additional clinical or radiological findings that warranted surgical intervention according to established guidelines [6]. Follow-up CT demonstrated a decrease in intracranial air, suggesting redistribution or resorption, with possible passage of air into the superior vena cava (SVC) (Figs. 2A–C). The wounds were thoroughly irrigated and sutured. The patient was admitted to hospital and carefully observed. The patient remained stable and was discharged after a 5-day hospital stay.

Discussion

CVAE has been reported in association with various etiologies, including trauma [4], although most reported cases are iatrogenic, including those related to central venous catheter manipulation [9,10], epidural catheter placement [8], and chest drain insertion [11]. Reports describing radiographic identification of the venous air entry route following head trauma are exceedingly limited.

Rao et al. [12] reported a CVAE associated with a temporal bone fracture; however, the route of air entry into the venous system was not conclusively identified. Cihangiroglu et al. [3] described a traumatic injury to the superior sagittal sinus identified intraoperatively; however, their report did not involve an open skull fracture, and air entry was considered to be facilitated by surgical exposure of the sinus to the atmosphere. In contrast, our case provides radiographic findings suggestive of the route of air entry using radiographic imaging. Bone-window CT showed that the fracture line extended into the diploë and that air was present within the diploë vein, representing a rare and highly distinctive finding. To our knowledge, radiographic inference of a venous air entry route based on the distribution of intracranial air, particularly involving the diploic space and venous sinuses, has rarely been described in traumatic cases.

Clinical outcomes of CVAEs vary markedly, ranging from favorable recovery to critical illness [4,13]. Previous studies have suggested that symptom severity and prognosis largely depend on the volume of entrained air [14]. In the present case, the initial diagnosis of pneumocephalus with suspected dural injury could have led to surgical intervention, which may have exacerbated air entrainment. Kambara et al. [13] reported a case in which air embolism was exacerbated during wound observation, highlighting the importance of careful wound management in patients with open skull fracture. In our patient, careful interpretation of the CT findings, particularly the distribution of intracranial air, allowed differentiation of CVAE from pneumocephalus and contributed to appropriate clinical decision-making. Conservative wound management was selected, and follow-up CT confirmed complete resolution of intracranial air without clinical deterioration. In general, management of cerebral air embolism is determined based on clinical severity and associated findings. Conservative treatment, including observation and supportive care, is often sufficient in asymptomatic or mild cases, whereas more aggressive interventions may be required in severe cases [15]. Although most reports describe cerebral air embolism in general, CVAE represents a subtype of cerebral air embolism, and these management principles are considered applicable.

Prompt assessment and appropriate treatment selection are essential in emergencies. In elderly patients, unnecessary surgical intervention may increase the risk of massive air embolism, prolonged hospitalization, and complications, such as delirium or cognitive decline. In the present case, conservative management based on an accurate radiological diagnosis resulted in favorable outcomes and early discharge. This case highlights the importance of distinguishing CVAE from pneumocephalus based on imaging findings, as misinterpretation may lead to unnecessary surgical consideration. In particular, radiographic inference of venous air entry based on diploic involvement and sinus-restricted distribution may provide a useful diagnostic clue in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Careful evaluation of the distribution of intracranial air on CT is essential to differentiate CVAE from pneumocephalus. In the present case, accurate radiographic interpretation allowed the identification of the venous air entry route and may help avoid unnecessary surgical consideration in selected clinical contexts.
