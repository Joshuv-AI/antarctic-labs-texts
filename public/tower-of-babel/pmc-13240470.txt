Taste and Smell Disturbances Associated With Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency: A Case Report
Authors: Takanori Izumi, Eri Doi, Yuki Tsutsui, Noboru Horinouchi, Rie Utsunomiya, Katsuhiko Yoshimura, Machiko Morita, Naoki Matsuda, Seiji Shiota

Background

Adrenal insufficiency commonly presents with nonspecific symptoms such as fatigue, anorexia, and weight loss, which often delay diagnosis. This is particularly relevant to secondary adrenal insufficiency, in which classical features such as hyperpigmentation or electrolyte abnormalities are absent [1]. While gastrointestinal and constitutional symptoms are well recognized, taste and smell disturbances are rarely regarded as diagnostic clues and may therefore be overlooked. Nevertheless, previous reports have suggested that alterations in taste and smell perception can occur in adrenal insufficiency and may precede more typical systemic manifestations [2].

Here, we report a case of secondary adrenal insufficiency with persistent taste and smell disturbances as the predominant presenting symptoms. This case suggests the clinical importance of recognizing non‐classical manifestations as potential early indicators of adrenal insufficiency.

Case Presentation

A 54‐year‐old man presented with a 3‐month history of persistent taste and smell disturbances accompanied by generalized fatigue, loss of appetite, progressive weight loss, and diffuse body pain, including thigh stiffness. He reported that food emitted a foul odor resembling sewage and that most foods tasted bitter or lacked flavor. These symptoms gradually worsened, resulting in marked anorexia and approximately 20 kg of weight loss over a 2‐month period. He initially consulted an orthopedic clinic and later a rheumatology department, where autoimmune disease was excluded. Because of persistent systemic symptoms, he was referred to our hospital for further evaluation.

His medical history included hypertension, for which antihypertensive medication had been discontinued after weight loss‐associated hypotension. He had consumed alcohol daily and had smoked approximately 40 cigarettes per day until symptom onset. He was not taking any regular medications other than zinc and iron supplements and had no history of using systemic or inhaled corticosteroids. Family history was notable only for maternal lung cancer. On admission, he was 167.5 cm tall and weighed 58.1 kg. Vital signs were stable. Physical examination revealed no pallor, jaundice, lymphadenopathy, edema, or skin hyperpigmentation. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, and no neurological deficits or sinonasal abnormalities were observed.

Laboratory studies showed mild anemia (hemoglobin, 11.6 g/dL), slightly elevated blood urea nitrogen (25.9 mg/dL), and normal serum levels of sodium, potassium, zinc, and glucose (Table 1). Both early morning serum cortisol (0.28 μg/dL) and plasma adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) (< 1.5 pg/mL) were markedly decreased. Intravenous methylprednisolone was initiated shortly after the identification of adrenal insufficiency because intravenous hydrocortisone was temporarily unavailable due to supply restrictions. Dynamic endocrine testing, including the rapid ACTH stimulation test and the corticotropin‐releasing hormone stimulation test, was subsequently performed after initiation of glucocorticoid therapy. Cortisol response was blunted in the rapid ACTH stimulation test, and both ACTH and cortisol responses were diminished in the corticotropin‐releasing hormone stimulation test, confirming secondary adrenal insufficiency (Table 2). Contrast‐enhanced pituitary magnetic resonance imaging showed no structural abnormalities or abnormal enhancement suggestive of hypophysitis. After 4 days of intravenous glucocorticoid therapy, the patient was transitioned to oral hydrocortisone for maintenance treatment. After starting steroid therapy, dysgeusia, parosmia, and diffuse body pain gradually improved, nausea resolved, and oral intake increased. Extensive screening for malignancies and otolaryngological evaluation revealed no alternative causes for the taste or smell disturbances.

Initial thyroid function tests showed suppressed TSH (0.018 μIU/mL) with elevated free thyroxine (fT4, 2.18 ng/dL), while free triiodothyronine (fT3) was not measured. On follow‐up, TSH remained suppressed (0.061 μIU/mL) with normalization of fT4 (1.70 ng/dL) and fT3 (2.55 pg/mL). Subsequently, TSH increased to 12.5 μIU/mL with decreased fT4 (0.74 ng/dL) and relatively preserved fT3 (2.64 pg/mL), indicating a transition from thyrotoxicosis to hypothyroidism. These findings, along with positive anti‐thyroid antibodies and ultrasonographic findings, were consistent with destructive thyroiditis. Given the coexistence of adrenal insufficiency, glucocorticoid replacement was initiated prior to thyroid hormone therapy, and levothyroxine was introduced thereafter. Additional pituitary evaluation revealed low insulin‐like growth factor 1. Growth hormone stimulation tests, including the arginine infusion test and the growth hormone–releasing peptide‐2 (GHRP‐2) test, demonstrated severely impaired growth hormone secretion, consistent with adult growth hormone deficiency (Table 2). The patient had no history of traumatic brain injury, and information regarding perinatal abnormalities was not available. As the patient did not exhibit any symptoms related to growth hormone deficiency and declined treatment, growth hormone replacement was not initiated. Free testosterone levels were also found to be low (Table 1), while luteinizing hormone and follicle‐stimulating hormone levels were within the normal range. Although the patient had nonspecific symptoms such as fatigue that may overlap with hypogonadism, these improved after glucocorticoid replacement therapy, suggesting that they were primarily attributable to adrenal insufficiency.

He was discharged on hydrocortisone and levothyroxine and has remained clinically stable for more than 3 months during follow‐up, with sustained improvement in appetite and sensory function.

Discussion

Adrenal insufficiency frequently presents with nonspecific symptoms that can delay diagnosis, particularly in secondary forms where mineralocorticoid deficiency and electrolyte abnormalities are absent [1, 3]. Although gastrointestinal and constitutional symptoms are well recognized, taste and smell disturbances are rarely considered part of the clinical spectrum. This case demonstrates that dysgeusia and parosmia can provide important diagnostic clues to an underlying adrenal insufficiency.

Adrenal insufficiency is classified into primary, secondary, and tertiary forms according to the level of dysfunction [1]. Secondary adrenal insufficiency results from impaired pituitary secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone and is most commonly caused by pituitary tumor or its treatment, including surgery or radiotherapy [3]. Tertiary adrenal insufficiency arises from hypothalamic dysfunction or suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, often due to exogenous glucocorticoid use [1]. In the present case, magnetic resonance imaging of the brain revealed no structural abnormalities of the hypothalamic–pituitary region, and no clear etiology for secondary adrenal insufficiency was identified. Autoimmune or idiopathic pituitary dysfunction could not be definitively excluded, representing a limitation of this case. Although impairment of both the ACTH–cortisol axis and growth hormone secretion was observed, other pituitary axes, including the thyroid and gonadal axes, were relatively preserved, suggesting partial rather than global pituitary dysfunction.

Adrenal hormone deficiency has been shown to influence taste and smell perception. Previous studies have reported altered sensitivity to taste and odor stimuli among patients with untreated adrenal insufficiency, with improvement following glucocorticoid replacement [4, 5]. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, these findings suggest functional rather than structural impairment of sensory pathways. Previous studies have reported increased sensitivity to taste and olfactory stimuli in adrenal insufficiency, as reflected by lowered detection thresholds [4, 5]. In contrast, our patient presented with dysgeusia and parosmia. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the difference between objective sensory thresholds and subjective perception. Heightened sensitivity at the receptor or neural level may clinically manifest as distorted or unpleasant sensory experiences rather than improved perception. This suggests that adrenal insufficiency affects sensory processing in a complex manner. In addition, the patient reported specific qualitative abnormalities in smell and taste perception, suggesting true chemosensory dysfunction rather than nonspecific appetite‐related symptoms. However, it is possible that olfactory disturbance contributed to the perceived taste abnormalities. The lack of objective testing limits the ability to fully distinguish primary gustatory dysfunction from secondary effects of olfactory impairment. In our patient, the gradual improvements seen in taste and smell disturbances after steroid replacement support the reversibility of this condition. These findings are consistent with previous reports [4, 5] suggesting that adrenal insufficiency can affect chemosensory perception, while also highlighting that such disturbances may present alongside systemic symptoms in clinical practice.

However, our case has several limitations. First, objective taste and olfactory testing was not performed in this case, as the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency had already been established at the time of otolaryngological evaluation and priority was given to assessing the response to glucocorticoid therapy. Second, follow‐up endocrine testing after initiation of glucocorticoid replacement was not performed.

Taste and smell disturbances are commonly attributed to sinonasal disease, upper respiratory tract infection, medication effects, smoking, nutritional deficiencies such as zinc deficiency, and malignancy [6]. Consequently, systemic endocrine disorders are seldom considered during initial evaluation. Adrenal insufficiency is particularly prone to being overlooked because early symptoms are nonspecific and routine laboratory abnormalities may be absent [1]. This case provides an important lesson for generalists: when taste and smell disturbances are unexplained or accompanied by systemic symptoms such as anorexia, weight loss, or fatigue, adrenal insufficiency should be included in the differential diagnosis. Awareness of this non‐classical presentation may help prevent diagnostic delay and allow timely initiation of glucocorticoid replacement therapy.

Conclusion

Taste and smell disturbances may provide useful clinical clues to adrenal insufficiency and may be reversible with appropriate treatment. Clinicians should consider adrenal insufficiency in patients presenting with unexplained sensory disturbances, particularly when accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fatigue, anorexia, or weight loss.
