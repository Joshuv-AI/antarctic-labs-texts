A rare case of huge intracranial yolk sac tumor presenting as an orbital mass in an infant: case report and literature review
Authors: Gashaw Arega, Melaku Moges, Abinzer Tirist, Tseganesh Mekonnen Hailemariam, Isa Salo Abdo, Fadil Nuredin Abrar, Ilili Amin Aliye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Primary intracranial yolk sac tumor (YST) with orbital involvement is an exceedingly rare extragonadal germ cell tumor, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature. Clinically, primary intracranial yolk sac tumor with orbital involvement may present with symptoms that mimic more common benign or malignant orbital disorders in children, potentially leading to diagnostic delays that can adversely impact survival. Diagnostic imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are instrumental for assessing the tumor’s size, precise localization, and extent. Definitive diagnosis relies on serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels and histopathological evaluation of biopsy samples. Multimodal therapy with surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy is the cornerstone of therapy. Our case report showed a primary intracranial yolk sac tumor with orbital extension in a 10-month-old infant, and brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) revealed a heterogenous suprasellar, parasellar mass with orbital extension. The infant underwent radical left globe exenteration, left pterional craniotomy, and subtotal resection of the tumor. The histopathology and the elevated AFP biomarkers confirmed the diagnosis of the primary intracranial yolk sac tumor.

Background

Extra-gonadal germ cell (EGGCT) tumors are rare childhood cancers commonly found in mid-line structures like the mediastinum, sacrococcygeal, and retroperitoneum, and rarely seen in the head and neck region. Extragonadal intracranial yolk sac tumor presenting as a protruding orbital mass is an extremely rare clinical phenomenon [1–4]. These primary intracranial YST with orbital extension is extremely rare extra-gonadal tumor and has a rapid progressive course with a tendency to invade surrounding structures. The appropriate optimal management is based on case reports and literature reviews due to the rarity of the tumors in this area [4–6].

Here, we report a ten-month-old male infant diagnosed with a primary intracranial yolk sac tumor after presenting with a complaint of two months of left eye proptosis and bilateral loss of vision. Orbital and Brain MRI with contrast showed a large lobulated heterogenous mass in the Left Orbit, Supra -Sellar and Para-Sellar region encasing vascular structures. The infant underwent radical globe excision, left pterional craniotomy, and subtotal resection of the mass and the biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of optic pathway yolk sack tumor. The serum-AFP and CSF-AFP were elevated and confirmed the diagnosis of primary intracranial YST with orbital involvement and the infant started on carboplatin, etoposide, and bleomycin-based chemotherapy.

Case presentation

A ten-month-old male infant presented with a complaint of left eye swelling of two months duration associated with offensive discharge from the protruded mass, excessive eye tearing, protrusion of the left eye, and bilateral loss of vision.

The general physical examination revealed normal vital signs and unaffected anthropometric measurements. The pertinent physical examination showed a 3 cm-by-3 cm protruded mass from the left eye globe with watery discharge and tearing. Chest X-ray and abdominopelvic ultrasound were normal.

Brain MRI showed a large heterogenous lobulated ill-defined left orbital mass with extension through the superior orbital fissure to the suprasellar and left para sellar region with mass effect on the left medial temporal lobe. The left intra-orbital optic nerve has been displaced superolateral with 180 degrees encasement with medial displacement of the intracranial part. The mass has completely encased the cavernous and supra clinoid internal carotid artery (ICA) and proximal middle cerebral artery (MCA). The mass had a posterior extension to the Cavernous sinus, Meckel’s cave, and Prepontine cistern. Pre-operative CT scan showed a heterogenous mass in the sellar and parasellar region with erosive changes of the adjacent skull base (Fig. 1A–E).

After discussing the treatment portion with the parents, the child was taken to the operating theater. A team from neurosurgery and oculoplastic surgeon from ophthalmology were involved in the surgical treatment. Under general anesthesia, left-side frontotemporal craniotomy was done, and orbitotomy of the lateral wall and roof of the left orbit was performed. The orbital tumor was identified, most structures of the orbit were destroyed by the tumor and the left optic nerve was visualized separately from the tumor. The orbital part of the tumor was removed together with inoculations of the infected left orbit, and then partial debunking of the tumor on the temporal fossa was performed. The tumor extending to the cavernous sinus was left unresected due to the high risk of vascular and cranial nerve injury. The child was stable postoperatively and extubated in the ICU on subsequent days. The child developed right-side body weakness temporarily, which improved after the child was transferred to the ward. The excised tissue was subjected to histopathology and sections showed tissue fragments composed of round to polygonal cells having round to oval vesicular prominent cheery red nuclei growing in a microcytic pattern and confirmed the optic nerve areas primary yolk sac tumor (Fig. 2A–D). Our case report showed a primary intracranial yolk sac tumor with orbital extension in a 10-month-old infant presented with proptosis of the left eye, and bilateral visual loss. Brain MRI revealed heterogenous suprasellar, parasellar mass with orbital extension. CT scan of the head showed skull base involvement with an erosive change. The infant underwent radical left globe exenteration, left pterional craniotomy, and subtotal resection of the tumor. The histopathology and the AFP biomarkers confirmed the diagnosis of the primary intracranial yolk sac tumor.

Discussion

Extragonadal germ cell tumors of the head and neck constitute about 5% of germ cell tumors [1–3]. Yolk sac tumors more frequently occur in gonadal sites than in extragonadal locations. Rare variants of malignant extragonadal germ cell tumors in the head and neck region include intracranial yolk sac tumors, primary orbital yolk sac tumors, and optic pathway yolk sac tumors [4]. Previous studies have reported that primary yolk sac tumors can involve different sites in the head and neck region including; the left pterygoid region, upper lip, para-pharyngeal and skull base, posterior fossa, floor of the mouth, and right masticator space and orbits [5–11].

Radiographic features of this tumor were nonspecific with MRI findings of a heterogenous soft tissue mass in the orbit and surrounding tissues being reported. CT scans usually showed bony involvement with erosive changes with orbital involvement of variable degrees. [5, 7, 16–18]. The tissue biopsy and the CSF oncoproteins helped to reach the diagnosis in our case [12].

Thus, serum and CSF AFP are important tumor biomarkers for assessment of the therapeutic effect, and predictions of progressive disease or relapse during the follow-up period in children with yolk sac tumors. His pathologically, major histologic characteristics include Schiller-Duval bodies, a festoon-like pattern, a hepatoid pattern with hyaline globules, and a poly vesicular vitelline pattern, and immunohistochemically identified AFP is also a pivotal biomarker for the diagnosis of childhood yolk sac tumor [6, 11, 12].

The mainstay of treatment recommended for most extragonadal germ cell tumors in the intracranial and head and neck region includes a multidisciplinary approach including surgical resection of the mass and systemic chemotherapy [11]. A multiagent systemic chemotherapy regimen, including bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin/carboplatin, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, vincristine, and 5-fluorouracil, has significantly improved the outcome of primary yolk sac tumors in the orbital region [12]. Radiation can be given as adjuvant and palliative management for advanced cases [1, 13–15].

In our case, the infant underwent radical left globe exenteration, left pterional craniotomy, and subtotal resection of the tumor. The histopathology and the AFP biomarkers confirmed the diagnosis of the primary intracranial yolk sac tumor. The child started on systemic multiagent chemotherapy with carboplatin, etoposide, and bleomycin, and took two cycles. But despite the multimodal therapy the child developed progressive disease and passed away after four months of diagnosis.

Conclusion

Primary intracranial yolk sac tumors with orbital involvement should be considered in young children presented with proptosis and orbital mass. Orbital imaging with MRI, AFP biomarkers, and histopathological examination of the tissue is crucial for the definitive diagnosis of extragonadal yolk sac tumors in the head and neck region. Early presentation, and early diagnosis of yolk sac tumors (Germ cell tumors) is important to achieve treatment with a favorable prognosis resulting in a longer patient overall survival. As a rare case of primary intracranial yolk sac tumor with orbital involvement, this single-patient case study provides a valuable contribution to the literature.
