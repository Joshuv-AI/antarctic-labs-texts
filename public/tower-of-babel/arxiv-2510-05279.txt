Anisotropic fractional area measures
Xiaxing Cai

Abstract
. The anisotropic s s -fractional area measures are introduced as the first variation of the anisotropic fractional s s -perimeter P s ​ ( K , L ) P_{s}(K,L) , with L L an origin symmetric convex body and s ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) s\in(0,1) . As s → 1 − s\rightarrow 1^{-} , the anisotropic s s -fractional area measure converges to the mixed area measure of K K and the moment body of L L . The Minkowski problem of these measures are solved. Finally, a necessary condition for the convexity of optimizers in the anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequality is derived.



1.
Introduction

For a bounded Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an origin symmetric convex body
L
and
s\in(0,1)
, Ludwig
[
33
]
defined the anisotropic fractional
s
-perimeter of
E
with respect to
L
by

P_{s}(E,L)=\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}}\frac{1}{\|x-y\|_{L}^{n+s}}dxdy,

where
E^{c}
is the complement of
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L}
is the norm with closed unit ball
L
. Here we say
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex body if
L
is a convex compact set with nonempty interior.
The anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequality states that there is an optimal constant
\gamma_{s}(L)>0
such that

P_{s}(E,L)\geq\gamma_{s}(L)|E|^{\frac{n-s}{n}},

where
|E|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
E
.

The classic fractional
s
-perimeter of
E
, denoted by
P_{s}(E)
, is
P_{s}(E,B^{n})
where
B^{n}
is the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The fractional isoperimetric inequality is

P_{s}(E)\geq\gamma_{n,s}|E|^{\frac{n-s}{n}},

where the equality is attained precisely by balls, up to a null set (see
[
12
]
). When
L\neq B^{n}
, little is known about
\gamma_{s}(L)
and the optimizer of the anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequality. Ludwig
[
33
]
considered the limiting behavior of
P_{s}(E,L)
as
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
, which showed that the optimizers are not homothetic to the unit ball. By a result of Kreuml
[
28
]
, if
L
is unconditional, the optimizer is an unconditional star body.
The anisotropic fractional perimeter is closely related to the theory of fractional Sobolev norms and fractional isoperimetric inequalities, which have attracted considerable attention over the past three decades (see
[
11
,
21
,
28
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
40
,
46
]
). For more recent developments, we refer to
[
7
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

Variational methods, particularly those based on the first variation of volume or perimeter, have played a fundamental role in the study of geometric inequalities and related extremal problems. In the landmark work
[
37
]
by Lutwak-Xi-Yang-Zhang (Xi-LYZ), the authors established a family of variational formulas for chord integrals
I_{q}(K)
for
q>0
. In particular, when
q\in(0,1)
, the chord integral
I_{q}(K)
is proportional to the fractional perimeter
P_{1-q}(K)
. Motivated from this, we establish the variational formulas for
P_{s}(K,L)
and the associated Minkowski problem is solved in Theorem 1.1.

The classical Minkowski problem seeks to characterize convex bodies via their surface area measures, which arise as the first variation of the volume functional under geometric perturbations of the body. Aleksandrov
[
1
]
established the following variational formula:

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}V_{n}([h_{0}+tf])=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)\,dS_{n-1}([h_{0}],v),

(1.1)

where

[h_{0}+tf]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot v\leq h_{0}(v)+f(v),~~\text{for all}~~v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\},

is the Wulff shape generated by
h_{0}+tf
. Here
h_{0},f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
are continuous functions on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, and
x\cdot v
is the canonical inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The identity (
1.1
) holds whenever
[h_{0}]
is a convex body, where
S_{n-1}([h_{0}],\cdot)
denotes its surface area measure.

The classical Minkowski problem:
Given a finite Borel measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, what are the necessary and sufficient conditions on
\mu
such that there exists a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\mu=S_{n-1}(K,\cdot)?

As a generalization of volume, the notion of mixed volume (to be discussed in detail in Section 2.3) captures more subtle geometric interactions among convex bodies. In particular, the first mixed volume of
K
and origin symmetric
L
can be viewed as the anisotropic perimeter of
K
with respect to
L
, which is given by

P(K,L)=\int_{\partial K}\|\nu_{K}(x)\|_{L^{*}}dx.

(1.2)

The set
L^{*}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot y\leq 1,~~\text{for all }~~y\in L\}
is the polar body of
L
and
\nu_{K}
is the unit outer normal of
K
at
x\in\partial K
. In the special case where
L=B^{n}
, the anisotropic perimeter
P(K,B^{n})
reduces to the classical perimeter, that is, the surface area of
K
.
The definition

P(K,L)
naturally extends to bounded Borel sets
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this setting,
\partial K
is replaced by the reduced boundary of
E
and
\nu_{K}
is replaced by the measure theoretic unit outer normal of
E
. For basic background of sets of finite perimeter, we refer to
[
38
]
. By a result of Ludwig
[
33
]
, for a bounded Borel set
E
with finite perimeter, there is

\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)P_{s}(E,L)=P(E,ZL),

where
ZL
is the moment body of
L
given by

h_{ZL}(v)=\frac{n+1}{2}\int_{L}|v\cdot x|dx.

Associated with the mixed volume is the mixed area measure, which describes the first variation of the mixed volume under Minkowski sum. Given convex bodies
K
and
Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, we denote by
K+tQ=\{x+ty:x\in K,y\in Q\}
the Minkowski sum of
K
and
tQ
. By the linearity of mixed volumes with respect to Minkowski sum and scalar multiplication, one has

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0^{+}}P(K+tQ,L)=(n-1)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{Q}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,L,v).

Note that the derivative is one-sided, which poses additional difficulties for the variational method.
When
L=B^{n}
, the mixed area measure reduces to the
(n-2)
-th area measure of
K
, denoted by
S_{n-2}(K,\cdot)
, which was introduced by Aleksandrov, Fenchel and Jessen in the 1930s.

The quermassintegrals, which include volume and surface area as typical cases, form a special family of mixed volumes whose associated Borel measures are denoted by
S_{i}(K,\cdot)
, for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.
The Minkowski problems concerning area measures
S_{i}(K,\cdot)
are commonly known as Christoffel-Minkowsi problems.
The Chirstoffel problem is for the case
i=1
, while the case
i=n-1
is the classic Minkowski problem. As a longstanding problem, the Christoffel–Minkowski problem has benefited from significant contributions via PDE techniques, resulting in several important partial results (see
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
). For the solution of the Christoffel problem, see also
[
14
,
15
,
43
]
and Berg and Firey
[
44
, Section 8.3.2]
. We also refer
[
5
,
6
,
10
,
39
]
to some recent results.

An alternative perspective on
S_{n-2}(K,\cdot)
was proposed by Lutwak-Xi-Yang-Zhang
[
37
]
(Xi-LYZ), who developed two-sided variational formulas for chord power integrals

I_{q}(K)=\int_{{\rm Aff}(n,1)}|K\cap l|^{q}dl,

where
{\rm Aff}(n,1)
denotes the affine Grassmannian of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
dl
denotes the Haar measure on it.
The variational approach leads to a family of
geometric measures
F_{q}(K,\cdot)
for
q>0
, known as chord measures. As
q\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the chord integral
I_{q}(K)
converges to the surface area of
K
. Remarkably, Xi-LYZ
[
37
]
shows that
F_{q}(K,\cdot)
also converges to
S_{n-2}(K,\cdot)
, which offers a potential route to the Christoffel–Minkowski problem via suitable limiting arguments.

The landmark work by Xi-LYZ
[
37
]
initiated the study of geometric measures in integral geometry and has inspired a variety of subsequent developments, including
L_{p}
and Orlicz chord Minkowski problems; see, for example,
[
20
,
25
,
30
,
31
,
29
]
. More recently, Xi and Zhao
[
42
]
extended techniques introduced in
[
37
]
to develop a new family of affine geometric measures, the limit of which they defined as the affine area measure. Here, “affine” indicates that the measure is either affine covariant or affine contravariant. The associated affine Minkowski problems—Minkowski-type problems for such affine geometric measures—have attracted increasing attention in recent years. For additional results on affine geometric measures, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
,
8
,
9
,
32
]
. We also refer to
[
26
]
for a broader overview of research on Minkowski-type problems.

Our first main result establishes the following variational formula: given an origin symmetric convex body
L
and
s\in(0,1)
,

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}P_{s}(K_{t},L)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v),

where
K_{t}
is the Wulff shape generated by
h_{K}+tf
with a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
. The measure
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
is called the anisotropic
s
-fractional area measure of
K
with respect to
L
.

A natural question is to study the limiting behavior of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
and
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
. In section 4, we show that

s\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)\rightarrow S_{n-1}(K,\cdot)

as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
and when
K
has
C^{2}
boundary and everywhere positive Gauss curvature,

(1-s)\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)\rightarrow S_{n-2}(K,ZL,\cdot)

as
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
.

In section 5, we study the Minkowski problem of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
, formulated as Theorem
1.1
. This problem seeks necessary and sufficient conditions on a Borel measure
\mu
under for the existence of
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
such that

\mu=\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot).

In the smooth setting, with
d\mu=gdv
absolutely continuous, the Minkowski problem can be reformulated as the Monge–Ampère equation,

\tilde{V}_{n+s}([h],L,\nabla h(v))\det(\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}^{2}h(v)+h(v))=g(v),

(1.3)

where
\tilde{V}_{n+s}([h],L,\nabla h(v))
denotes the dual mixed volume of
[h]-\nabla h(v)
and
L
, as introduced in Section 2.2. The notation
\nabla h
and
\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}^{2}h
denotes the Euclidean gradient and spherical Hessian of
h
respectively, where
h\in C^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
is continuously twice differentiable and extended to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a
1
-homogeneous function. The smooth Minkowski problem amounts to finding a convex solution of (
1.3
), whereas Theorem
1.1
below resolves the problem without any regularity assumptions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
. Suppose
\mu
is a finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
and
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an origin symmetric convex body. There is a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mu=\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
if and only if
\mu
is not concentrated in any subsphere and

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}vd\mu(v)=o.

In the final section, we establish a necessary condition for the convexity of the optimizer in the anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequality.

2.
Preliminary

In this section, we collect some basic definitions and results on convex bodies and anisotropic fractional
s
-perimeters. We refer the reader to
[
13
,
38
,
44
]
for standard notation and background on convex bodies and sets of finite perimeter.

2.1.
Convex bodies

We work in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the canonical inner product
x\cdot y
and norm
|x|
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}^{k}
the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which coincides with the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For convenience, we sometimes write
|E|
to denote the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
B^{n}
and
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
denote the unit ball and the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. The volume of
B^{n}
is denoted by
\omega_{n}:=|B^{n}|
.

A set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a convex body if it is compact, convex, and has nonempty interior.
The family of all compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{n}
,
and
\mathcal{K}^{n}\subset\mathcal{C}^{n}
denotes the class of convex bodies.
We write
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}
for the subclass of origin-symmetric convex bodies.

For a compact convex set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its support function
h_{F}:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

h_{F}(v)=\max\{v\cdot x:x\in F\}.

The class
\mathcal{C}^{n}
is equipped with the Hausdorff metric
d_{H}
, which can be expressed in terms of support functions as

d_{H}(F_{1},F_{2})=\|h_{F_{1}}-h_{F_{2}}\|_{\infty}=\max_{v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|h_{F_{1}}(v)-h_{F_{2}}(v)|,

for any
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
.

For
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and a boundary point
z\in\partial K
, we say that
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
is a unit outer normal of
K
at
z
if
v\cdot z=h_{K}(v)
. If
z
has a unique unit outer normal, then we denote it by
\nu_{K}(z)
. By
[
44
, Theorem 2.2.11]
, the Gauss map
\nu_{K}(z)
is defined for almost every
z\in\partial K
. For a Borel set
\eta\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, the inverse Gauss map
\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)
is defined by

\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)=\{z\in\partial K:\exists v\in\eta~~\text{such that}~~v\cdot z=h_{K}(v)\}.

For
z\in\partial K
and
v\in\nu_{K}^{-1}(\{z\})
, the hyperplane
H=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot v=h_{K}(v)\}
is called the support hyperplane of
K
, which is also the tangent space of
\partial K
at
z
and denoted by
T_{z}K
.

The surface area measure
S_{n-1}(K,\cdot)
is then given by

S_{n-1}(K,\eta)=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)),

for each Borel subset
\eta\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
.

2.2.
Mixed volumes

The mixed volume of
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
, denoted by
V(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})
, is nonnegative and symmetric, that is,

V(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})=V(K_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,K_{\sigma(n)}),

for every permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The functional
V:(\mathcal{C}^{n})^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is also multilinear with respect to Minkowski addition and nonnegative scalar multiplication:

V(aK+bL,K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})=aV(K,K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})+b(L,K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})

for
a,b\geq 0
.

Recall that every twice continuously differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
f\in C^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
, can be written as the difference of two support functions and that
C^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
is dense in
C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
. By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a Borel measure on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, denoted by
S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n},\cdot)
, such that

V(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K_{1}}(v)dS(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n},v).

This measure
S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n},\cdot)
is called the mixed area measure.

An important special case arises when
K=K_{1}=\ldots=K_{i}
and
B^{n}=K_{i+1}=\ldots=K_{n}
. In this case, the mixed volume is denoted by

W_{n-i}(K)=V(\underbrace{K,\ldots,K}_{i},\underbrace{B^{n},\ldots,B^{n}}_{n-i}),

which is called the quermassintegral for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Here,
i=n
corresponds to the volume of
K
, while
i=n-1
corresponds to its surface area. The mixed area measure associated with
W_{i}(K)
is denoted by
S_{i-1}(K,\cdot)
and is called the
(i-1)
-st area measure of
K
. In particular,
S_{n-1}(K,\cdot)
is the surface area measure.

2.3.
Dual mixed volumes

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
z\in K
. The radial function of
K
with respect to
z
is defined by

\rho_{K,z}(u)=\max\{t\geq 0:z+tu\in K\},~~u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}.

When
z=o
, we simply write
\rho_{K}:=\rho_{K,o}
.

For
q\in\mathbb{R}
and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, the
q
-th dual mixed volume of
K
and
L
with respect to
z
is defined by

\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,L,z)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}}\rho_{K,z}(u)^{q}\rho_{L}(u)^{n-q}du,

where

\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}=\{u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}:\rho_{K,z}(u)>0\}.

For
z\in\operatorname{int}K
, the set
\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}
coincides with the entire sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
,
whereas for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every
z\in\partial K
, it is either an open or a closed hemisphere.
We denote by
\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,z)
the dual mixed volume with respect to
L=B^{n}
.

Note that when
z\in
int
K
and
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the quantity
\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,L,z)
is always finite. For
z\in\partial K
and
q>0
, since
\rho_{L}
is bounded, the integrand is bounded and hence
\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,L,z)
remains finite. When
z\in\partial K
and
q<0
, the situation becomes more delicate due to the potential singularity of
\rho_{K,z}(u)
.

By a result of Xi–LYZ
[
37
, Lemma 4.9]
, the function
\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,\cdot)
is integrable over
\partial K
for every
q>-1
. As
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
is a bounded convex body, its radial function satisfies
\rho_{L}(u)\leq c
for some constant
c>0
and all
u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Consequently,

\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,L,z)\leq c^{n-q}\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,z)

for all
z\in\partial K
.
It then follows that
\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,L,\cdot)
is integrable over
\partial K
for every
q\in(-1,0)
.
In particular, for every such
q
,
\tilde{V}_{n-q}(K,L,z)<\infty
for almost every
z\in\partial K
.

2.4.
Anisotropic fractional
s
-perimeter

Suppose
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Borel set and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
. Then:

\bullet

P_{s}(E,L)=P_{s}(E^{c},L)
.

\bullet

P_{s}(E+x,L)=P_{s}(E,L)
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\bullet

P_{s}(\lambda E,L)=\lambda^{n-s}P_{s}(E,L)
, for any
\lambda>0
, where
\lambda E=\{\lambda x:x\in E\}
.

\bullet

P_{s}(E,L)
is lower semicontinuous with respect to
E
. That is, if
|E\triangle E_{i}|\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
, then
P_{s}(E,L)\leq\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}P_{s}(E_{i},L)
.

In the following, we focus on the case
E=K
, where
K
is a convex body. Setting
z=x-y
and applying polar coordinates gives

P_{s}(K,L)=\frac{1}{s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{K}\rho_{K,y}(u)^{-s}dydu.

We may write
y=y^{\prime}+tu
with
y^{\prime}\in u^{\perp}
, so that

\int_{K}\rho_{K,y}(u)^{s}dy=\int_{K|u^{\perp}}\int_{f_{K}(y^{\prime})}^{g_{K}(y^{\prime})}(g_{K}(y^{\prime})-t)^{-s}dtd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y^{\prime}),

where
f_{K}(y^{\prime})=\min\{t\in\mathbb{R}:y^{\prime}+tu\in K\}
and
g_{K}(u)=\max\{t\in\mathbb{R}:y^{\prime}+tu\in K\}
. Hence,

P_{s}(K,L)=\frac{1}{s(1-s)}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{K|u^{\perp}}(g_{K}(y^{\prime})-f_{K}(y^{\prime}))^{1-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y^{\prime})du.

The quantity
g_{K}(y^{\prime})-f_{K}(y^{\prime})
is denoted by
X_{K}(y^{\prime},u)
, and is referred to as the X-ray function of
K
. Equivalently, it can be defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
by

X_{K}(y,u)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(K\cap(y+\mathbb{R}u)).

This function records the length of the intersection between
K
and the line
y+\mathbb{R}u
. In terms of
X_{K}
, the anisotropic fractional perimeter can be expressed in terms of
X_{K}
as

P_{s}(K,L)=\frac{1}{s(1-s)}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{u^{\perp}}X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\,du.

(2.1)

In particular, when
L=B^{n}
, the functional
P_{s}(K):=P_{s}(K,B^{n})
is proportional to the chord power integral of
K
, namely,

P_{s}(K)=\frac{n\omega_{n}}{s(1-s)}I_{1-s}(K)=\frac{n\omega_{n}}{s(1-s)}\int_{\mathrm{Aff}(n,1)}|K\cap l|^{1-s}\,dl,

(2.2)

which follows from the Blaschke-Petkantschin Formula (
[
45
, Theorem 7.2.7]
).

3.
The variational formulas and anisotropic fractional area measures

This section focuses on the variational formula for the anisotropic fractional perimeter and the associated geometric measures. Before proving the variational formula, we introduce the anisotropic fractional area measure, which naturally arises in the process.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
. The anisotropic
s
-fractional area measure of
K
with respect to
L
is given by

\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\eta)=\frac{n}{s}\int_{\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)}\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z),

where
\eta\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
is a Borel set.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f:{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. We write

K_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot v\leq h_{K}(v)+tf(v),~~\text{for all}~~v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\},

(3.1)

which is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for sufficiently small
\delta>0
and
|t|<\delta
.

We will use the following result in
[
37
]
, which provides the derivative of the X-ray function.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
37
, Lemma 5.2]
Suppose
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
,

is a continuous function and

is the Wulff shape. If

where
y^{+}
and

.

For the variational formula, we need the following identity for the anisotropic

-fractional area measure. A similar formula in the case

appears in

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
and
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
. Suppose
f
is a continuous function on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Then

2n\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{\partial K}X_{K}(z,u)^{-s}f(\nu_{K}(z))d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)du.

Proof.

By the definition of
\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)

\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\int_{\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K,z}(u)^{-s}dud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

\partial K(u)=\{z\in\partial K:\rho_{K,z}(u)>0\}.

Then by Fubini’s theorem and the fact that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)

\displaystyle d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

(3.2)

\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\frac{1}{2n}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{\partial K}X_{K}(z,u)^{n+s}f(\nu_{K}(z))d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)du,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
s\in(0,1)
,
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
,
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
f
is a continuous function on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Let
K_{t}
be the Wulff shape as defined in (
3.1
). Then

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}P_{s}(K_{t},L)=2n\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(u)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,u).

Proof.

By the result in
[
37
, Lemma 5.4]
, there is a class of nonnegative integrable functions
\phi_{t}(y,u)
defined for
u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
and
y\in u^{\perp}
such that

\Big|\frac{1}{t}\Big(X_{K_{t}}(y,u)^{1-s}-X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}\Big)\Big|\leq\phi_{t}(y,u),

and

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{u^{\perp}}\phi_{t}(y,u)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)du=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{u^{\perp}}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\phi_{t}(y,u)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)du,

where
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\phi_{t}(y,u)
is integrable.

Since
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
is bounded, there exists
r>0
such that
\rho_{L}(u)<r
for all
u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Hence,

\Big|\frac{1}{t}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\Big(X_{K_{t}}(y,u)^{1-s}-X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}\Big)\Big|\leq r^{n+s}\phi_{t}(y,u).

By the representation (
2.1
), Lemma
3.2
and the dominated convergence theorem, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}P_{s}(K_{t},L)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{s(1-s)}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{u^{\perp}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\frac{X_{K_{t}}(y,u)^{1-s}-X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}}{t}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{u^{\perp}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}X_{K}(y,u)^{-s}\Big(\frac{f(\nu_{K}(y^{+}))}{u\cdot\nu_{K}(y^{+})}-\frac{f(\nu_{K}(y^{-}))}{u\cdot\nu_{K}(y^{-})}\Big)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)du.

Note that
d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{u^{\perp}}(y)=|z\cdot u|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{\partial K}(z)
, where
y=z|_{u^{\perp}}
. Therefore, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}P_{s}(K_{t},L)=\frac{1}{s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{\partial K}X_{K}(z,u)^{-s}f(\nu_{K}(z))d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)du.

Combining with Lemma
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{P_{s}(K_{t},L)-P_{s}(K,L)}{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s}\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\int_{\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}}\rho_{K,z}(u)^{-s}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}dud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle=2n\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v),

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now turn to some basic properties of the measure
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
. Note that
P_{s}(K,B^{n})
is proportional to the chord integral, and that
P_{s}(K,L)
is invariant under translations of
K
, i.e.,
P_{s}(K,L)=P_{s}(K+x_{0},L)
for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, it is positively homogeneous of degree
n-s
in
K
, that is,
P_{s}(\lambda K,L)=\lambda^{n-s}P_{s}(K,L)
for all
\lambda>0
. The measure
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
satisfies the following properties:

\bullet

When
L=B^{n}
, the measure
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,B^{n},\cdot)
is proportional to the
(1-s)
-th chord measure.

\bullet

\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)=\mathcal{A}_{s}(K+x_{0},L,\cdot)
for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\bullet

\mathcal{A}_{s}(\lambda K,L,\cdot)=\lambda^{n-s}\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
for any
\lambda>0
.

In particular, we show that its centroid lies at the origin. To this end, we first recall the following technical lemma from
[
42
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
42
, Lemma 3.2]

Let
q>-1
and
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
. If
F:K\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is concave, and
F>0
in the interior of
K
and vanishes on
\partial K
, then

\int_{K}x\cdot\nabla(F^{q+1}(x))dx=-n\int_{K}F(x)^{q+1}dx.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
and
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
. Then

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}vd\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v)=o

Proof.

By the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
and Lemma
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{s}\int_{\partial K}z\cdot\nu_{K}(z)\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{\partial K}z\cdot\nu_{K}(z)X_{K}(z,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)du.

For fixed
u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, one can write

K=\{y+tu:y\in K|u^{\perp},f_{K}(y)\leq t\leq g_{K}(y)\},

where
f_{K}(y)
and
g_{K}(y)
are given in Section 2.4.

Since
K
is convex,
f_{K}
and
-g_{K}
are convex on

and therefore locally Lipschitz. Then
X_{K}(y,u)=g_{K}(y)-f_{K}(y)
is concave on

. Note that for almost all

, the boundary
\partial K
does not contain any line parallel to
u
. With such a direction

fixed, it follows that for almost all

\nu_{K}(y+f_{K}(u)u)=\frac{\nabla f_{K}(y)-u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla f_{K}(y)|^{2}}},~~\nu_{K}(y+g_{K}(y)u)=\frac{-\nabla g_{K}(y)+u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla g_{K}(y)|^{2}}}.

We then have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}z\cdot\nu_{K}(z)X_{K}(z,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=

\displaystyle\int_{K|u^{\perp}}(y\cdot\nabla f_{K}(y)-f_{K}(y))X_{K}(y,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{K|u^{\perp}}(-y\cdot\nabla g_{K}(y)+g_{K}(y))X_{K}(y,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Since
g_{K}(y)-f_{K}(y)=X_{K}(y,u)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}z\cdot\nu_{K}(z)X_{K}(z,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=

\displaystyle\int_{K|u^{\perp}}X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

\displaystyle-\int_{K|u^{\perp}}y\cdot\nabla X_{K}(y,u)X_{K}(y,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

It then follows from Lemma
3.5
, with

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}z\cdot\nu_{K}(z)

\displaystyle X_{K}(z,u)^{-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{K|u^{\perp}}\Big(X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}-\frac{1}{1-s}y\cdot\nabla(X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s})\Big)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-s}{1-s}\int_{K|u^{\perp}}X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Consequently,

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v)=\frac{n-s}{2s(1-s)}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\int_{K|u^{\perp}}X_{K}(y,u)^{1-s}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}du.

By (
2.1
), we finally have

P_{s}(K,L)=\frac{2}{n-s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v).

(3.3)

Since
P_{s}(K+x_{0},L)=P_{s}(K,L)
for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by (
3.3
) and the translation invariance of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}(h_{K}(v)+x_{0}\cdot v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K+x_{0},L,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}(h_{K}(v)+x_{0}\cdot v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v),

which implies that

x_{0}\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}vd\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v)=0.

By the arbitrariness of
x_{0}
, we conclude the proof.
∎

4.
Limiting behavior of anisotropic fractional area measures

The limiting behavior of the anisotropic fractional
s
-perimeter has been studied by Ludwig
[
33
]
, revealing an interesting connection to classical geometric invariants. Specifically, it was shown that

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}sP_{s}(E,L)=n|E||L|,

and

\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)P_{s}(E,L)=P(E,ZL),

where
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Borel set with finite perimeter and
ZL
is the moment body of
L
.

Motivated by these results, we study the corresponding limiting behavior of anisotropic
s
-fractional area measures, which arise as the geometric derivatives of
P_{s}(\cdot,L)
in the setting of convex bodies.

The following theorem shows that the anisotropic
s
-fractional area measure converges to the surface area measure as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}

Theorem 4.1
.

If
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, then

s\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)\rightarrow\frac{|L|}{2}S_{n-1}(K,\cdot),

as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}

Proof.

Suppose
(s_{i})
is an arbitrary convergent sequence in
(0,1)
and
s_{i}\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
.
Let
f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
. Then
f
is bounded on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
; that is, there exists
c>0
such that
|f(v)|\leq c
for all
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
,

s_{i}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{i}}(K,L,v)=n\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

Since
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, there exists
l>0
such that

\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\leq l

for all
u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
and
s\in(0,1)
. For an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, note that
\rho_{K,z}(u)+\epsilon
is bounded on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Then there is
M>0
such that

\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)\leq l\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,z)\leq\frac{l}{n}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}(\rho_{K,z}(u)+\epsilon)^{-s_{i}}du\leq\omega_{n}lM^{-s_{i}},

and hence

f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)\leq\omega_{n}clM^{-s_{i}}.

Note that

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\omega_{n}cl\int_{\partial K}M^{-s_{i}}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\omega_{n}cl\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial K)=\omega_{n}cl\int_{\partial K}\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}M^{-s_{i}}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

Therefore by the dominated convergence theorem, we have

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

Recall that
\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,L,z)
is finite for almost every
z\in\partial K
. After removing a set of measure zero
\eta
, we may assume that
\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)
and
\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,z)
are finite for all
z\in\partial K\backslash\eta
and for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. For
z\in\partial K\backslash\eta
, we have that
\rho_{K,z}^{-s_{i}}
is integrable on
\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}
. Moreover,

\rho_{K,z}(u)^{-s_{i}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s_{i}}\leq l\rho_{K,z}(u)^{-s_{i}}.

The dominated convergence theorem now implies

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{V}_{-s_{i}}(K,L,z)=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}}\rho_{K,z}(u)^{-s_{i}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s_{i}}du=\frac{1}{2n}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n}du=\frac{|L|}{2}.

Hence, we have

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}s_{i}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{i}}(K,L,v)=\frac{|L|}{2}\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\frac{|L|}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)dS_{n-1}(K,v),

which concludes the proof.
∎

As
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
, the limiting behavior of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
becomes more subtle. We consider the smooth case here. In what follows, we focus on the case where
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
has

boundary with everywhere positive Gauss curvature. That is, at each point

Let

be the support function of

, and let

and

denote its Euclidean gradient and Hessian, respectively. For
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, the point

lies on
\partial K
. The principal radii of curvature which are the eigenvalues of

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\Big(\frac{\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)}{2}+o(1)\Big)drd\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-s)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\rho_{L}\Big(\frac{(r\theta,f(r\theta))}{|(r\theta,f(r\theta))|}\Big)^{n+s}\frac{\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)}{2}r^{-s}drd\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)+(1-s)o(1)\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{-s}dr.

By (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle(1-s)\int_{D_{1}}

\displaystyle\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}du

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-s)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int_{0}^{\delta}(\rho_{L}(\theta,0)^{n+1}+\epsilon)\frac{\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)}{2}r^{-s}drd\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)+o(1)\delta^{1-s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{1-s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}(\rho_{L}(\theta,0)^{n+1}+\epsilon)\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)+o(1)\delta^{1-s}.

Let
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
. By the arbitrariness of
\epsilon
, we have

\limsup_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{D_{1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}du\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\rho_{L}(\theta,0)^{n+1}\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)+o(1).

For
u\in D\backslash D_{1}
and
(y^{\prime},f(y^{\prime}))=\rho_{K}(u)u
, we have
\rho_{K}(u)\geq\delta
. Therefore
\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}\leq c
for some constant
c
and for all
u\in D\backslash D_{1}
and
s\in(0,1)
. Then

\limsup_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{D\backslash D_{1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}du\leq\limsup_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{D\backslash D_{1}}cdu=0,

which implies

\limsup_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{D}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}du\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\rho_{L}(\theta,0)^{n+1}\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)+o(1).

Letting
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we then have

\limsup_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{D}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}du\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{n-2}}\rho_{L}(\theta,0)^{n+1}\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta).

By the other side of the bound in (
4.2
) and Fatou’s Lemma, we repeat the same arguments and get

\liminf_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{D}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s}\rho_{K}(u)^{-s}du\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{n-2}}\rho_{L}(\theta,0)^{n+1}\kappa_{K}(o,\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

[
42
, Lemma 4.1]

Suppose
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
has
C^{2}
boundary and everywhere positive Gauss curvature. Let
z\in\partial K
and
v
be the normal vector of
K
at
z
. For
u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap v^{\perp}
, we have

\kappa_{K|u^{\perp}}(z|u^{\perp})=\frac{\kappa_{K}(z)}{\kappa_{K}(z,u)}

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
has
C^{2}
boundary and everywhere positive Gauss curvature. Let
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
and
\eta\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
be a Borel subset. Then we have

S_{n-2}(K,ZL,\eta)=\frac{2}{n^{2}-1}\int_{\eta}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap v^{\perp}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}\frac{\kappa_{K}(z,u)}{\kappa_{K}(z)}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(u)dv,

where
z=\nu_{K}^{-1}(z)\in\partial K
.

Proof.

Step 1: We first show that

S_{n-2}(K,ZL,\eta)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}S_{n-2}(K,[-u,u],\eta)\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}du

(4.5)

Since
C^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
is dense in
C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
and every
f\in C^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
is the difference of two support functions, it suffices to show

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,ZL,v)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,[-u,u],v)du,

where
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonempty convex compact set.

Note that

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,ZL,v)=nV(F,K,\ldots,K,ZL),

where the mixed volume
V(F,K,\ldots,K,ZL)
is symmetric in its arguments. Then by the definition of
ZL
and Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,ZL,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{ZL}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,F,v)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|v\cdot u|\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}dudS_{n-2}(K,F,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|v\cdot u|dS_{n-2}(K,F,v)du.

Similarly,

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|v\cdot u|dS_{n-2}(K,F,v)=V([-u,u],K,\ldots,K,F)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,[-u,u],v).

(4.7)

Then by (
4.6
), (
4.7
) and the Fubini’s theorem, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,ZL,v)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,[-u,u],v)du,

which gives (
4.5
).

Step 2: We aim to show

S_{n-2}(K,[-u,u],\eta)=\frac{2}{n-1}\int_{\eta\cap u^{\perp}}\frac{\kappa_{K}(z,\theta)}{\kappa_{K}(z)}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta).

For a convex compact set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by the Cauchy projection formula we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|v\cdot u|dS_{n-2}(K,F,v)

\displaystyle=v(K|u^{\perp},\ldots,K|u^{\perp},F|u^{\perp})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap u^{\perp}}h_{F}(v)dS^{\prime}(K|u^{\perp},v),

where
v(K|u^{\perp},\ldots,K|u^{\perp},F|u^{\perp})
denotes the mixed volume in
u^{\perp}
and
S^{\prime}(K|u^{\perp},\cdot)
denotes the surface area measure of
K|u^{\perp}
in
u^{\perp}
. Recall that the density of the surface area measure is the reciprocal of the Gauss curvature, which implies

\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|v\cdot u|dS_{n-2}(K,F,v)=\frac{1}{n-1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap u^{\perp}}\frac{h_{F}(v)}{\kappa_{K|u^{\perp}}(z|u^{\perp})}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(v),

where
z=\nu_{K}^{-1}(v)\in\partial K
.
Combining with Lemma
4.3
, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{F}(v)dS_{n-2}(K,[-u,u],v)=\frac{2}{n-1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap u^{\perp}}h_{F}(v)\frac{\kappa_{K}(z,u)}{\kappa_{K}(z)}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(v).

Step 3: By step
1
and step
2
, we have

S_{n-2}(K,ZL,\eta)=\frac{2}{n^{2}-1}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{\eta\cap u^{\perp}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+1}\frac{\kappa_{K}(z,u)}{\kappa_{K}(z)}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(v)du.

Since

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap u^{\perp}}f(u,v)dvdu=\iint_{u\cdot v=0}f(u,v)d(u,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap v^{\perp}}f(u,v)dudv,

we obtain the desired result.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
has
C^{2}
boundary and everywhere positive Gauss curvature and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
. Then

(1-s)\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)\rightarrow\frac{n^{2}-1}{2}S_{n-2}(K,ZL,\cdot),

as
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
.

Proof.

Let
(s_{i})
be a convergent sequence in
(0,1)
, where
s_{i}\rightarrow 1
as
i\rightarrow\infty
. Assume
f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
is arbitrary and hence
f
is bounded. That is,
|f(v)|\leq c
for all
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
.

Since
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, its radial function
\rho_{L}
is bounded. Then there exists
l>0
such that

\tilde{V}_{n+s_{i}}(K,L,z)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}}\rho_{L}(u)^{n+s_{i}}X_{K}(z,u)^{-s_{i}}du\leq\frac{l}{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{z}^{+}}X_{K}(z,u)^{-s_{i}}du=l\tilde{V}_{n+s_{i}}(K,z),

and therefore

(1-s_{i})f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s_{i}}(K,L,z)\leq cl(1-s_{i})\tilde{V}_{n+s_{i}}(K,z)

It was shown in
[
37
, Corollary 4.14]
that

\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}\int_{\partial K}(1-s)\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\int_{\partial K}\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

Then the dominated convergence theorem implies

\displaystyle\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}(1-s_{i})\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{i}}(K,L,v)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n}{s_{i}}\int_{\partial K}(1-s_{i})f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s_{i}}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle=n\int_{\partial K}\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}(1-s_{i})f(\nu_{K}(z))\tilde{V}_{n+s_{i}}(K,L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

Combining the last equation with Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(1-s)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)

\displaystyle d{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{i}}(K,L,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(z))\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap\nu_{K}(z)^{\perp}}\rho_{L}(\theta)^{n+1}\kappa_{K}(z,\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\cap v^{\perp}}\rho_{L}(\theta)^{n+1}\frac{\kappa(z,\theta)}{\kappa_{K}(z)}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\theta)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z),

where
z=\nu_{K}^{-1}(v)\in\partial K
. Hence we obtain

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}(1-s_{i})\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{i}}(K,L,v)=\frac{n^{2}-1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)dS_{n-2}(K,ZL,v),

which concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Minkowski problems for anisotropic fractional area measures

The existence of solutions to the Minkowski problem is closely related to an optimization problem of a suitable functional under geometric constraints. In this section, we begin by considering the following optimization problem:

\underset{K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}}{\inf}\Big\{\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mu(v):P_{s}(K,L)=1\Big\},

(5.1)

where
\mu
is a finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mu
be a finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Suppose
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
and
s\in(0,1)
. If
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
satisfies
P_{s}(K_{0},L)=1
and is a minimizer to the optimization problem (
5.1
), in the sense that

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K_{0}}d\mu=\underset{K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}}{\inf}\Big\{\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mu(v):P_{s}(K,L)=1\Big\},

then there exists
c>0
such that

\mu=\mathcal{A}_{s}(cK_{0},L,\cdot).

Proof.

Let
f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
be arbitrary and
h_{t}=h_{K_{0}}+tf
, where
|t|\leq\delta
for some sufficiently small
\delta>0
such that

K_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot v\leq h_{t}(v),~~\text{for all}~~v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\}

is a convex body.

Denote by
\gamma(t)=P_{s}(K_{t},L)^{-\frac{1}{n-s}}
and
\overline{K}_{t}=\gamma(t)K_{t}
, which implies that
\gamma(0)=1
. Since
P_{s}(cK,L)=c^{n-s}P_{s}(K,L)
for any
c>0
, we have
P_{s}(\overline{K}_{t},L)=1
. Let

\phi(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\gamma(t)h_{t}(v)d\mu(v).

Note that
h_{\overline{K}_{t}}(v)=\gamma(t)h_{K_{t}}(v)\leq\gamma(t)h_{t}(v)
for any
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Since
K_{0}
is the minimizer of the optimization problem (
5.1
), we have

\phi(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\gamma(t)h_{t}(v)d\mu(v)\geq\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{\overline{K}_{t}}(v)d\mu(v)\geq\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K_{0}}(v)d\mu(v)=\phi(0),

for all
t\in(-\delta,\delta)
.

Theorem
3.4
implies that
\gamma(t)
is differentiable at
t=0
and

\gamma^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{2n}{n-s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,v)

Therefore, we obtain

0=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\phi(t)=-\frac{2n}{n-s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,v)+\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d\mu(v).

Since
f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
is arbitrary, the desired result
\mu=\mathcal{A}_{s}(cK_{0},L,\cdot)
follows directly, where
c=(2n/n-s)^{\frac{1}{n-s}}
.
∎

The following lemma gives a sufficient condition for the existence of a solution of the optimization problem (
5.1
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
. Suppose
\mu
is a finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
. If
\mu
is not concentrated in any subsphere and

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}vd\mu(v)=o,

then there exists
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
such that
P_{s}(K_{0},L)=1
and
K_{0}
is a minimizer to (
5.1
).

Proof.

Suppose
(K_{i})
in
\mathcal{K}^{n}
is a minimizing sequence to (
5.1
), where
P_{s}(K_{i},L)=1
. Since the centroid of
\mu
is the origin, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K+x}(v)d\mu(v)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mu(v)+\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}x\cdot vd\mu(v)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mu(v),

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Together with the fact that
P_{s}(K+x,L)=P_{s}(K,L)
, we can assume
K_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
without loss of generality.

Let
M_{i}=\max_{u\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\rho_{K_{i}}(u)=\rho_{K_{i}}(u_{k_{i}})
for some
u_{k_{i}}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Then we have

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K_{i}}(v)d\mu(v)\geq M_{i}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\max\{u_{k_{i}}\cdot v,0\}d\mu(v).

Since
\mu
is not concentrated in any subsphere and the centroid of
\mu
is the origin,
\mu
is not concentrated in any closed hemispheres. Thus,

u\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}\max\{u\cdot v,0\}d\mu(v)

is a positive continuous function on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
and therefore has a uniform lower bound
c_{0}>0
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. Then we have

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K_{i}}(v)d\mu(v)\geq M_{i}c_{0}.

Note that
(K_{i})
is a minimizing sequence. Thus
M_{i}
is uniformly bounded, and there exists
M>0
such that
K_{i}\subset MB^{n}
for all
i
. By the Blaschke selection theorem, there exists a compact convex set
K_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, up to a subsequence,
K_{i}\to K_{0}
.

It remains to show that
K_{0}
has nonempty interior, i.e.,
K_{0}
is a convex body. Since
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, there is
c>0
such that

\frac{1}{c}P_{s}(K_{i})\leq P_{s}(K_{i},L)\leq cP_{s}(K_{i}),

where
c
only depends on
L
.

Recall from Section 2.4, by (
2.2
),

P_{s}(K_{i})=\frac{n\omega_{n}}{s(1-s)}\int_{{\rm Aff}(n,1)}|K_{i}\cap l|^{1-s}dl.

Then by Jensen’s inequality, we have

P_{s}(K_{i})\leq\frac{n\omega_{n}}{s(1-s)}\Big(\int_{{\rm Aff}(n,1)}|K_{i}\cap l|dl\Big)^{1-s}=\frac{n\omega_{n}}{s(1-s)}V(K_{i})^{1-s}.

Therefore,

1=P_{s}(K_{i},L)\leq\frac{cn\omega_{n}}{s(1-s)}V(K_{i})^{1-s}.

By the continuity of
V
, we obtain
V(K_{0})>0
, which implies that
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
.
∎

We are now in the position to prove Theorem 1.1.

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

The necessity is provided by Lemma
3.6
, where

P_{s}(K,L)=\frac{2n}{n-s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}h_{K}(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,v)>0

implies that
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
is not concentrated in any subsphere.

Let us now assume that
\mu
is not concentrated in any subsphere and with the centroid at the origin. By Lemma
5.2
, we have the existence of
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
such that
\mu=\mathcal{A}_{s}(K,L,\cdot)
.
∎

6.
Anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequalities

In the last section, we derive a necessary condition for the convexity of the optimizer in the anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequality

P_{s}(E,L)\geq\gamma_{s}(L)|E|^{\frac{n-s}{n}},

using the variational formula (
3.4
).

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
. If
K_{0}
is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

P_{s}(K_{0},L)=\gamma_{s}(L)|K_{0}|^{\frac{n-s}{n}},

then there exists
c>0
such that

\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,\cdot)=cS_{n-1}(K_{0},\cdot),

and therefore,
\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K_{0},L,z)
is constant for almost every
z\in\partial K_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
is an optimizer in the anisotropic fractional isoperimetric inequality. Let
f\in C({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
be arbitrary. We denote the associated Wulff shape by

K_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot v\leq h_{K_{0}}(v)+tf(v),~~\text{for all}~~v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\},

where
|t|<\delta
for some sufficiently small
\delta>0
such that
K_{t}
is a convex body.

Consequently, we have

P_{s}(K_{t},L)\geq\gamma_{s}(L)|K_{t}|^{\frac{n-s}{n}},

which implies that the function

\psi(t)=P_{s}(K_{t},L)/|K_{t}|^{\frac{n-s}{n}}

attains its minimum at
t=0
. By Theorem
3.4
and Aleksandrov’s variational formula (
1.1
) for the volume functional,

0=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\psi(t)=\frac{2n}{|K_{0}|^{\frac{n-s}{n}}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)d\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,v)-\frac{(n-s)P_{s}(K_{0},L)}{n|K_{0}|^{2-\frac{s}{n}}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}f(v)dS_{n-1}(K_{0},v).

By the arbitrariness of
f
, we obtain

S_{n-1}(K_{0},\cdot)=c\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,\cdot),

where
c=\frac{2n^{2}|K_{0}|}{(n-s)P_{s}(K_{0},L)}
.

Moreover, by comparing the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,\cdot)
with
S_{n-1}(K_{0},\cdot)
, where

\mathcal{A}_{s}(K_{0},L,\eta)=\frac{n}{s}\int_{\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)}\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K_{0},L,z)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z),~~S_{n-1}(K)=\int_{\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z),

we have

\tilde{V}_{n+s}(K_{0},L,z)=\frac{(n-s)P_{s}(K_{0},L)}{2n^{2}|K_{0}|}=\frac{n-s}{2n^{2}}\gamma_{s}(L)|K_{0}|^{-\frac{s}{n}},

for almost every
z\in\partial K
.
∎
