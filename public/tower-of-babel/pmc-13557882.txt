Clarifying the nutritional and functional context of gut microbiome signals during chemotherapy for advanced non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jiahui Xiong, Chenghua Lu
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the prospective study by Trachu et al. evaluating gut microbiome dynamics in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) receiving chemotherapy (1). The authors reported that platinum-based chemotherapy was associated with reduced gut microbial richness, whereas baseline Akkermansia appeared enriched, although without statistical significance, in patients who derived clinical benefit (1). Because microbiome-based prediction in chemotherapy-treated NSCLC remains less developed than that in the immunotherapy setting, their work adds useful data to a relatively underexplored field (2,3). We would like to highlight one issue that may warrant further clarification: how host nutritional and functional status should be incorporated when interpreting microbial shifts and putative biomarkers.

In this cohort, baseline albumin was the only clinical factor significantly associated with alpha diversity, with lower diversity observed among patients with hypoalbuminemia (1). This finding is clinically relevant because, in advanced lung cancer, albumin may reflect overlapping processes including systemic inflammation, cachexia, dietary restriction, tumor burden, and treatment tolerance. These processes may shape both the gut microbiome and chemotherapy outcomes. Rather than treating albumin solely as a covariate, it may be useful to consider it part of the host context in which a microbial signature is expressed. If available, stratified or interaction-based analyses incorporating albumin, weight loss or body mass index, inflammatory markers, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, and chemotherapy regimen could clarify whether the Akkermansia signal is independent of nutritional reserve or is more evident in patients with preserved metabolic and functional status.

A second consideration concerns the timing and interpretation of reduced microbial richness after chemotherapy. Stool samples obtained at the first response evaluation may reflect not only cumulative chemotherapy but also interval infection, appetite change, gastrointestinal toxicity, corticosteroid exposure, antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, and prebiotic/probiotic use. Non-antibiotic medications can influence bacterial communities, and chemotherapy may perturb both microbial ecology and mucosal immunity (2,4). A within-patient model aligned with chemotherapy cycles and medication-exposure windows could help distinguish short-lived dysbiosis from more durable ecological remodeling. Such an approach might also clarify whether richness loss differs by platinum partner, response category, or subsequent adverse-event trajectory.

The genus-level Akkermansia finding deserves particular attention. Prior lung cancer cohorts have linked Akkermansia or Akkermansia muciniphila with favorable treatment outcomes (5,6), and broader oncology studies have connected gut microbiome composition with antitumor immunity (7,8). Still, a relative-abundance signal alone may not translate readily into a predictive test or adjunctive strategy. Functional layers would make this observation more interpretable. For example, metagenomic or metabolomic profiling of short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, tryptophan metabolites, and mucin-degradation pathways could help determine whether Akkermansia is a mechanistic contributor, a marker of preserved mucosal ecology, or a correlate of better baseline health. Absolute bacterial quantification may also complement relative abundance, particularly when chemotherapy reduces overall richness.

In sum, Trachu et al. advance the discussion of the gut microbiome in chemotherapy-treated NSCLC by moving beyond immunotherapy-centered evidence. Clarifying the nutritional, temporal, and functional context of these microbial signals would enhance interpretability and facilitate responsible translation into future biomarker-driven studies or microbiome-modulating supportive strategies.
