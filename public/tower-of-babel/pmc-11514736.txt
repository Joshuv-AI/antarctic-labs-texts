Revised Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC 2.0): cultural adaptation, validity and reliability of the Malay version
Authors: Dina Syazana Ho Imran Ho, Mohd Hasni Jaafar, Azmawati Mohammed Nawi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurveys on Patient Safety Culture™ Hospital Survey (HSOPSC) developed by the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) has been adopted worldwide. The Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) version 2.0 was released in 2019, but there have been no publications to date of its translation and validation for use in Malaysia. This study aimed to translate and cross-culturally adapt the revised HSOPSC 2.0 into the Malay language and determine its psychometric properties including the content, face, and construct validity, and reliability analyses.MethodsThis study was conducted from April – June 2023 and divided into three stages: translation and cultural adaptation; content and face validation; and construct validation using confirmatory factor analysis and reliability testing among 319 healthcare personnel from a public university hospital in Malaysia.ResultsThe translated instrument demonstrated excellent content validity (I-CVI = 0.80 ~ 1.0, SCVI-average = 0.96) and face validity (I-FVI = 0.80 ~ 1.0, SFVI-average = 0.98). Reliability testing was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.60 ~ 0.80) but indicated that reverse-coded items were poorly perceived. Confirmatory factor analysis showed a satisfactory model fit for the translated instrument (RMSEA = 0.08, GFI = 0.80, CFI = 0.80, and χ2/df = 2.96). Six items had very low factor loadings (< 0.40), with two constructs “Staffing and Work Pace” and “Response to Error” having AVE < 0.4, but acceptable CR ≥ 0.6. No items were removed from the questionnaire despite low factor loadings following a consensus from an expert panel.ConclusionThe Malay version of the HSOPSC 2.0 containing ten domains and 32 items demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties following expert consensus, with acceptable reliability and construct validity for measuring patient safety culture. Given factor loadings smaller than 0.40 in six items, broader validation is suggested to support the use of the translated instrument in the Malaysian healthcare setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11802-6.

Introduction

Patient harm due to unsafe care is a growing public health concern and one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide [1]. As countries strive to achieve universal health coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals, the strides made in improving access to healthcare services can be undermined by unsafe care [1, 2]. On average, an estimated one in 10 patients is subject to an adverse event while receiving hospital care in high-income countries, with another 134 million events occurring in low- and middle-income countries [1–5]. This contributes to 2.6 million deaths, and a social cost of USD 1–2 trillion a year [5]. Avoidable harm in healthcare practices arises from a multitude of causes, including unsafe medication practices and medication errors, healthcare-associated infections, peri-operative and anaesthetic-related complications, inappropriate or unskilled use of medical radiation and diagnostic errors [6]. Despite a large body of work to reduce medical errors, as many as 16% of hospitalised patients still experience a medical error with adverse events representing the third leading cause of death in the United States [7, 8].

Past investigations into failures in healthcare have identified weak patient safety culture as a common contributing factor to adverse events [9]. The concept of safety culture was coined in 1986, in the aftermath of the Chernobyl accident and Challenger crash, and can be defined as the shared beliefs, attitudes, values, and norms, which influence the behaviours and attitudes of employees when delivering care to patients [10, 11]. It represents the outcome of group and individual beliefs, attitudes, values, and behaviour in ensuring safety in an organisation [12]. The Institute of Medicine recommends developing a patient safety culture to assist healthcare organisations in improving patient safety [13]. An effective assessment of safety culture can aid in identifying problem areas that could lead to errors and unfavourable outcomes, build staff awareness on patient safety, evaluate the current state of patient safety culture in the organisation, identify strong points of safety culture and areas for improvement, analyse safety culture trends over time, evaluate the impact on safety culture of initiatives and interventions to improve patient safety, draw comparisons within and between health organisations, and improve both patient and employee satisfaction [14–16]. Therefore, targeting practice change though patient safety culture is considered a key strategy for strengthening and enhancing patient safety and outcomes in hospitals [2, 17–19].

Typically, when evaluating the culture of patient safety in healthcare, both healthcare and non-healthcare personnel are asked to complete questionnaires about their own experiences. These questionnaires are then utilised to track their perspectives on patient safety issues, pinpoint areas that require urgent attention, and inspire measures aimed at minimising the occurrence of patient adverse events [15]. The most widely used instruments in understanding an organisation’s safety culture and the determinants of patient safety are the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) and the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ) that was developed by the University of Texas [16, 20]. The HSOPSC is a survey encompassing 12 dimensions for patient safety culture, measured by 42 items. Its validity and reliability have been widely examined and it has performed satisfactorily in psychometric analyses [7, 14, 16, 21]. In 2019, the AHRQ released a revised version of the HSOPSC that has fewer items and different item wordings and composite measures. Overall, pilot testing of the Surveys on Patient Safety Culture™ (SOPS®) Hospital Survey 2.0 (HSOPSC 2.0) showed higher scores and better internal consistency than the HSOPSC 1.0 [22]. The AHRQ recommends that the HSOPSC 2.0 be used instead of the original version. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of careful testing of translated versions of the HSOPSC before use to ensure the applicability of the instrument in the target context [23]. However, there is no published evidence to date of the validation of this instrument in the Malay language. Therefore, the aims of the study were to translate and cross-culturally adapt the revised HSOPSC 2.0 into the Malay language (hereafter called the HSOPSC-BM 2.0) and determine its psychometric properties, including the content, face, and construct validity, and reliability analyses for use in Malaysian hospital settings.

Methods

Study design and participants

To produce a Malay version of the HSOPSC 2.0, a three-stage study was conducted including translation and cultural adaptation, content and face validation, and construct validation using confirmatory factor analysis. In the first stage, the HSOPSC 2.0 was translated into the Malay language and culturally adapted by a panel of bilingual expert translators and subject matter experts to evaluate equivalences in semantics, concepts, and questionnaire items. In the second stage, content validity was assessed by a panel of five subject matter experts. Subsequently, face validation was conducted among ten purposively selected healthcare personnel to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the translated instrument. In stage three, a cross-sectional study was conducted in a 350-bedded public university hospital in the East Coast of Malaysia to evaluate equivalence in measurement by means of confirmatory factor analysis of the instrument’s construct validity and reliability.

Instrument

The first version of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) was released by the United States Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) in 2004 to assess patient safety culture in healthcare organisations. The original instrument measures 12 dimensions of safety culture with a total of 42 items [22]. It had satisfactory psychometric analyses, is easily accessible, and is widely used in healthcare organisations around the world. In 2019, the AHRQ released a new version of the questionnaire based on feedback and suggestions from users and stakeholders named the SOPS Hospital Survey 2.0 (HSOPSC 2.0) [24]. The HSOPSC 2.0 comprises of 32 items grouped into ten composite measures and eight single-measure items. Overall, pilot testing showed that HSOPSC 2.0 scores were higher than HSOPSC 1.0, with Cronbach’s alphas for the 10 subscales of the HSOPSC 2.0 ranging from 0.67 to 0.89 in the pilot study [22]. Since its release, the HSOPSC 2.0 has been utilised in 400 hospitals worldwide [22]. The English version of the adapted survey guide used in this study is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary file 1).

The items are scored on a 5-point Likert-type scale of agreement, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), or frequency, ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The frequency of response for each survey item are calculated by combining the two lowest response categories (e.g., strongly disagree/disagree and never/rarely) and the two highest response categories (e.g., strongly agree/agree and most of the time/always). The midpoints of the scales will be reported as a separate category (neither agree nor disagree or sometimes). The survey includes both positively worded items and negatively worded items. For positively worded items, percent positive scores are the combined percentage of respondents within a hospital who answers, “strongly agree” or “agree,” or “always” or “most of the time.” For negatively worded items, percent positive response is the combined percentage of respondents who answers, “strongly disagree” or “disagree,” or “never” or “rarely”. To calculate the score on a particular safety culture composite measure, the percent positive scores for each item in the composite measure are averaged [25].

Phase 1: translation and cultural adaptation

Translation was commenced upon receiving permission from the AHRQ. Back-to-back translation was done by a panel of translators. Forward translation was first carried out by two professionals – a public health specialist as the subject matter expert and a linguist. The two versions of each set of questionnaires from the translators were then synthesised and consolidated into a single instrument by two subject matter experts and the primary investigator. The translators were also involved in this process for grammar and language clarifications. Two independent bilingual translators, a public health specialist, and a linguist then reverse-translated the synthesised instrument from the Malay language to English. The goal of the reverse-translation process is to establish a conceptual “word-by-word” meaning [23, 26]. The reverse-translated instruments were then synthesised and consolidated into a single instrument, then examined in comparison to the original instrument by two subject matter experts and the primary investigator. Ambiguities and discrepancies were discussed before deciding on the finalised version of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0.

Phase 2: content validity and face validity

The finalised version of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 was reviewed by a 5-member expert panel consisting of professors of public health and top-level hospital managers to evaluate the instrument’s content validity. The experts were also asked to check if the items were culturally appropriate to the Malaysian population and provide suggestions for improvement and adaptation, if necessary. Using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, and 4 = very relevant), the experts were asked to evaluate the usefulness, consistency, clarity, and cultural relevance of the items. Where the panel did not choose at least 3 (quite relevant), they were asked, in an open-ended question, to comment on the item and suggest possible adjustments to its wording. The content validity index (CVI), indicating the inter-rater agreement among experts, was calculated. I-CVI was computed by the number of experts giving a rating of three or four for each item divided by the total number of experts. The scale-level content validity index-average (SCVI-average) was calculated by computing the mean of the I-CVI scores. I-CVI scores above 0.80 are considered acceptable, and an SCVI-average score above 0.90 is considered excellent [27–29].

The version obtained from the expert panel contributions underwent face validation. Semi-structured, face-to-face cognitive interviews were conducted in a purposive sample of ten healthcare personnel, that included doctors, nurses, pharmacists, and hospital administrators, to assess operational equivalence, verbal comprehension, and item clarity [30]. All participating healthcare personnel are involved in direct patient-care activities. They were asked to indicate how well they understood each item of the instruments on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (item not clear and not understandable) to 4 (item very clear and understandable). For items deemed unclear, participants were asked to provide suggestions to improve clarity. The face validity index (FVI) was computed by calculating the scale average (SFVI-average). Following analysis of the pre-test results, the final version was produced for use in Phase 3.

Phase 3: psychometric analyses of the instrument

(i) Study setting, sample size determination and data collection

For Phase 3, we used convenience sampling to recruit healthcare personnel from a 350-bedded public university hospital on the East Coast of Malaysia. This hospital was selected based on the Malay language being the primary language for communication. A total of 2240 healthcare personnel comprising of doctors, nurses, medical assistants, allied health professionals and support staff work at the recruiting hospital. Healthcare professionals and support staff with at least six months working experience and are involved in direct patient care were eligible to participate in the study. The questionnaire forms were distributed as hardcopies to 450 healthcare personnel by the Clinical Management Unit and Nursing Department on behalf of the research team. Study participants were informed about the study aims and methods, the voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality of the responses. Responses were returned in sealed envelopes to the research team, along with signed consent forms. Data was collected from 1st May 2023–31st May 2023.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to examine the construct validity of the translated instrument [30–32]. As the factors and items of the HSOPSC 2.0 have been determined and piloted by the original authors, we only conducted a confirmatory study of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 to assess if the 32 items fit the measurement model well [22]. Sample size determination for CFA remains a challenge as it is impacted by the total number of factors and indicators, as well as the size of factor loadings [33]. For example, Furr (2018) recommends a sample size of 50 respondents for simple CFA models, while other researchers suggest sample sizes ranging from 5 to 20 respondents per item [28, 33]. Our final sample size of 319 (9 cases per item) is adequate and exceeds the minimum sample requirement of 160. This study did not proceed with Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) prior to CFA because the HSOPSC 2.0 had already undergone extensive exploratory analysis and validation in various countries [22, 30–32]. Our goal was to confirm the pre-determined factor structure of the instrument in the Malay language [34].

This study was conducted at Sultan Ahmad Shah Medical Centre @IIUM, which is affiliated with the International Islamic University of Malaysia (IIUM). Consequently, the study was approved by the IIUM Research Ethics Committee, Research Management Centre, International Islamic University of Malaysia (IREC No: IREC 2023-132-1). Research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were provided with a Respondent Information sheet and written consent was obtained from all participants before they completed the questionnaires.

(ii) Data analysis

Descriptive statistics

Data was analysed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) for Windows 27.0 and AMOS (Analysis of Moment Structures) 28.0 programs. Statistical significance was set at 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate respondents’ characteristics and each subscale. As per the tool developers’ recommendation, the percentage of positive responses for each subscale were calculated by calculating the mean percentage of respondents who answered, “strongly agree” or “agree”, or “always” or “most of the time” for positively worded items and answered, “strongly disagree” or “disagree”, or “never” or “rarely” for negative worded items.

Reliability testing

The reliability of the instrument refers to the stability and consistency of the instrument developed and is represented by Cronbach’s Alpha (α). Cronbach’s α values of 0.80–1.00 are considered very good, and values 0.60–0.79 are acceptable [27].

Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmation of the factorial structure of an instrument provides one type of evidence that is important for establishing an instrument’s construct validity [28]. CFA was used to test the construct validity of the translated instrument. The factor structure of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 was examined through CFA using maximum likelihood estimation. To achieve good psychometric properties, factor loadings ≥ 0.40 are preferred as they indicate higher levels of reliability [35, 36]. Assessment of the measurement model includes estimating the composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), Cronbach’s α, and reliability of individual items [36]. An AVE of ≥ 0.50 indicates that the dimension explains ≥ 50% of the variance of the items that comprise the dimension, whereas CR represents the aggregation of the items under a latent construct [36, 37]. An acceptable CR value should be > 0.60. Required threshold values for AVE are ≥ 0.5, but AVE ≥ 0.4 can be accepted if the reliability is ≥ 0.60 for the particular construct [36]. Based on the findings of the CFA, the convergent validity and discriminant validity covering 32 items across 10 dimensions were assessed to demonstrate the construct validity further. The convergent validity was determined, with AVE and CR values of ≥ 0.4 and ≥ 0.6 respectively deemed acceptable [36].

To confirm the model fit, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), goodness fit index (GFI), and Chi Square/degrees of freedom (χ2/df) were evaluated [36, 38]. An RMSEA ≤ 0.08, CFI ≥ 0.8, GFI ≥ 0.8, and χ2/df < 5 are values considered an acceptable fit [36].

Results

Results of phase 1: translation and cultural adaptation of the instrument

Some suggestions were made to adapt the HSOPSC 2.0 to the Malaysian culture. Item F4 was reworded as “when transferring patients from one unit to another, important patient care information is often left out”, so that there is congruence between this item with items F5 and F6 from the same subscale.

Results of phase 2: content validation and face validation

The inter-expert CVI was 0.8 ~ 1.0 with an SCVI-average of 0.96. Pre-testing resulted in an I-FVI of ≥ 0.8 or higher across all items, with an SFVI-average of 0.98. The results indicate excellent content and face validity of the translated instrument. Table 1 shows the CVI and FVI scores of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0.

Results of phase 3: psychometric analysis

(i) Participants’ characteristics

The survey was sent to 450 healthcare personnel. 417 returned the questionnaires, producing a response rate of 92.6%. Out of this, 319 questionnaires were eligible for analysis, after removing questionnaires with missing values and straight lining responses. Table 2 shows the participants’ characteristics. The mean age was 31.4 years (SD 5.52), the majority were of Malay ethnicity (98.4%), and more than 90% of respondents had higher education levels (diploma or higher). Most of the respondents comprised of nursing staff (71.8%), followed by doctors (14.1%); and worked in medical services (28.2%) or surgical services (63.3%). Most participants had been working for less than five years (71.2%) and a majority worked at least 30 h per week (92.4%).

(iii) Reliability testing and confirmatory factor analysis

The reliability of the constructs of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 ranged from 0.60 to 0.80. All ten dimensions have items that would increase the Cronbach’s α for the construct if an item were removed. Most of the items had factor loadings ≥ 0.4. The three items with the lowest factor loadings were C7r (0.09) (“In this unit, staff are afraid to ask questions when something does not seem right”), A2 (0.20) (“In this unit, we have enough staff to handle the workload”), and A10 (0.22) (“When staff make errors, this unit focuses on learning rather than blaming individuals”). Convergent validity was accepted based on Cronbach’s α, AVE and CR values of ≥ 0.60, 0.40, and 0.60 respectively for all dimensions, except for Staffing and Work Pace and Response to Error, which had AVE < 0.6. For the dimensions Staffing and Work Pace, Communication About Error, and Communication Openness, expert validation was sought prior to accepting convergent validity in view of very low factor loading of the items [34]. The results of the CFA for the original model of the HSOPSC 2.0 showed an adequate model fit. The absolute fit indices, incremental fit and parsimonious fit showed an acceptable model (RMSEA = 0.078, GFI = 0.81, CFI = 0.80, χ2/df = 2.96). An alternative model where items A2 and A10 were removed sequentially due to low factor loading < 0.4 and AVE < 0.4 was tested to ascertain if a 30-item 10-domain would perform better psychometrically. Discussion

In this study, the HSOPSC 2.0 was translated and culturally adapted to the Malaysian healthcare context, and the psychometric properties of the translated instrument were examined to determine its suitability for use in Malaysia.

The translation and cultural adaptation process of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 were undertaken by a committee of four bilingual public health specialists and linguists. No overt changes were required to adapt the questionnaire to the Malaysian context. In Korea, item A5 “this unit relies too much on temporary, float, or PRN” staff had been removed from the Staffing and Work Pace dimension, as this term is uncommon and irrelevant in Korean healthcare systems [31]. However, after discussion with subject matter experts and top-level hospital administrators, this item was retained in the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 to be interpreted in the context of relying on staff borrowed from other work areas or units due to staff shortage in a particular work area or unit. The I-CVIs, I-FVI, SCVI-average and SFVI-average were all above 0.80, providing evidence of excellent content and face validity of the translated instrument. Suggestions made during face validation were to include “patient care information” in item F4r, so that the item reads “when transferring patients from one unit to another, important patient care information is often left out”. The word information alone may feel too vague to some healthcare personnel.

Except for the Organization Learning – Continuous Improvement, we found that Malaysian healthcare personnel assigned fewer positive scores to all dimensions of patient safety culture compared to the pilot study in the U.S. and the validation study of the Brazilian version of the HSOPSC 2.0 [22, 39]. The lower percentage positive scores across most composite measures were also observed in the validation studies conducted in China and Korea, as opposed to data from the U.S. and Brazil [31, 32]. This may imply that healthcare work cultures are similar in the Asian region. The highest overall positive response rate for the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 was seen for Organizational Learning – Continuous Improvement and Teamwork. This dimension intends to measure the extent to which work processes are regularly reviewed and that changes are made and continuously evaluated to ensure the efficacy [40]. This is an encouraging finding, particularly in a university hospital, as it is acknowledged by healthcare personnel that any changes to improve patient safety are continuously being carried out and evaluated.

The dimensions Response to Error and Reporting Patient Safety Events received the lowest scores of positive responses, with only 35.0% and 37.2% of respondents in this study respectively reporting that they felt they were being treated fairly and received support when they were involved in errors and that patient safety events were regularly reported. This was consistent with the Korean study where only 22% of the participants agreed or strongly agreed that their work culture was not punitive [31]. A non-punitive culture remains a big challenge for many countries [32]. Although the Malaysian Ministry of Health has worked hard to encourage the reporting of adverse events through the Malaysian Patient Safety Goals 2.0, many healthcare workers still do not have confidence in the non-punitive policy regarding adverse event reporting, particularly if the mistake is a near-miss event or does not result in direct patient harm; and fear they will be penalised [6, 32, 41]. This may further explain the low scores seen on Response to Error, Reporting Patient Safety Events, and Communication Openness dimensions. The low ratings for response to error and reporting patient safety events are problematic as a non-punitive response to errors and having a healthy level of reporting are important for improving patient safety and quality of care [31, 32]. In order to foster work environments that are not punitive, hospital administrators and nurse managers should exhibit a willingness to embrace change, encourage transparent communication, and establish secure spaces for staff to openly address issues and mistakes related to patient safety [31]. This approach will enable the organisation to learn from these errors and enhance the quality and safety of patient care.

Staffing and Work Pace is another controversial issue in the healthcare industry. The positive response rate in this study was only 41.1%, with similar low scores seen in studies across the world, regardless of geographical location. This dimension was found to be weak in around 60% of studies that have evaluated safety culture using the HSOPSC, suggesting that healthcare personnel often feel overloaded for staffing-related reasons [32]. This scenario became especially acute during the Covid-19 pandemic. Similarly, it was this dimension that scored the lowest percentage of positive responses – 56% in the U.S. study and 13% in the Korean study [30–32]. Secondary data analysis of an open-source World Bank data revealed that in the year 2019, average doctors to population ratio of high-income countries was at 3.3 doctors for every 1,000 population. Upper-middle income countries had 2.2 doctors to 1,000 population, while lower-middle- and lower-income-countries were at 0.7 and 0.5, respectively [42]. In 2019, Malaysia had 2.0 doctors for every 1,000 population [42]. For nurses, that ratio stands at 1:300, as opposed to the World Health Organisation (WHO)’s optimum number of one nurse per 200 patients. As adequate staffing is imperative in improving patient safety and quality of care, substantial efforts should be made to alleviate the shortage and unequal distribution of healthcare personnel in the country.

The lower positive response rates in this study compared to those in countries like the U.S. and Brazil may be indicative of systemic and cultural differences in how patient safety culture is perceived [31, 32]. Barriers, such as sociocultural background and previous negative experiences of being disrespected or ignored, and a system of inherent hierarchical structure lead to withholding voice and avoidance of speaking up, discouraging open communication about safety concerns [43]. Low levels of psychological safety and less encouraging work environments are persistently highlighted by demotivated junior doctors, while repeated calls on comprehensive plans to change the working culture and put a stop to workplace bullying have fallen on deaf ears. Addressing these cultural barriers requires the implementation of effective strategies to turn the workplace environment around. Healthcare institutions should actively promote a non-punitive reporting culture, bridge the divide between senior and junior healthcare personnel, handle safety incident reports with the utmost seriousness, and protect whistleblowers at all costs [43, 44].

The Cronbach’s α for most composite measures did not reach the 0.70 threshold recommended by Nunnally and Bernstein [27]. However, more recent resources have considered Cronbach’s α coefficients greater than 0.60 to be acceptable [45]. Similarly, low Cronbach’s α values were reported in the Chinese validation study of the HSOPSC 2.0 and was attributed to the diverse culture of organisational leadership, policy beliefs, and management patterns between China and the U.S. [32]. The lower Cronbach’s α coefficients seen in our study, Korea and China compared with that documented in U.S. and Brazil may imply that similar healthcare management patterns are applied in the Asia. The lower reliability and suboptimal factor loadings observed in the Staffing and Work Pace and Response to Error dimensions may reflect specific challenges in the Malaysian healthcare context. Staffing shortages and a high work pace are common issues that could influence how healthcare personnel perceive these items [43]. Additionally, the reliability analysis showed that the α value would increase if one item from each dimension were removed. Specifically, deleting reverse or negatively worded items from the instrument tended to result in a better α value. This demonstrates the tendency for Malaysian healthcare personnel to respond poorly to negatively worded questionnaire items. It may be beneficial to conduct a separate study with a reworded questionnaire where all items are positively worded to examine the resultant reliability.

According to the results of the CFA, this study confirmed the 10-factor structure of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 with satisfactory psychometric properties and an acceptable model fit. However, factor loading was extremely low for three items, namely C7r (“In this unit, staff are afraid to ask questions when something does not seem right”), A2 (“In this unit, we have enough staff to handle the workload”), and A10 (“When staff make errors, this unit focuses on learning rather than blaming individuals”). This resulted in poor convergent validity for two constructs – Staffing and Work Pace and Response to Error – both having an AVE < 0.4. Item C7r belonging to the construct Communication Openness had the lowest factor loadings among all the items in the model, but yielded a satisfactory AVE and CR. After removing items A2 and A10 that had resulted in poor convergent validity of their respective constructs, the 30-item model resulted in a similar acceptable model fit as the original construct. Further discussion with an expert panel resulted in suggestions made to maintain the original 32-item model in the HSOPSC-BM, despite below acceptable factor loadings and AVE values, as the items were deemed important to identify patient safety culture and had acceptable Cronbach’s α and CR. The Brazilian version of the HSOPSC 2.0 also found low factor loadings present in ten items, that would entail removing those items to explore a factor model that best fits the data collected [30]. However, the researchers decided to not exclude any items on the theoretical basis that the AHRQ HSOPSC 2.0 is recognised as applicable to the reality of Brazilian health services based on other studies [39]. In Malaysia, no previous published study validating the use of a Malay version of the HSOPSC 2.0 has been undertaken, hence, it is suggested that the translated instrument undergo broader validation in a multi-site setting encompassing various healthcare organisations in Malaysia and with a larger sample size to increase generalizability and confirm its reliability.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. One of the key limitations of this study is that it was conducted within a single public university hospital. As such, the findings may not be representative of all Malaysian hospitals. This provides a limited scope for generalising the safety culture findings to all Malaysian hospitals. Secondly, stability over time via test-retest reliability was not assessed as a second administration of the instrument was not feasible given the limited time to conduct the research, Given these limitations, future research should involve a larger and more diverse sample from multiple hospitals, including larger public hospitals and private institutions, to ensure the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 is applicable across various healthcare settings in Malaysia. Researchers should also incorporate test-retest reliability to evaluate the instrument’s temporal stability. Additionally, this study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to assess changes in patient safety culture over time or in response to interventions. To address this limitation, we recommend future longitudinal studies to monitor the stability and effectiveness of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0. Such studies would provide valuable insights into how patient safety culture evolves and the long-term impact of improvement efforts. Finally, there is a lack of local published data on the psychometric properties of the HSOPSC 2.0, and thus we were not able to compare our results to other published studies of the instrument’s use in Malaysia.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the psychometric properties of the HSOPSC 2.0 in the Malaysian healthcare setting. The findings provide preliminary evidence of the content validity, face validity, construct validity, and reliability of the translated and adapted instrument. The CFA results support the use of a 32-item, 10-dimension HSOPSC-BM 2.0. Broader validation context is required in future research before implementing the use of the HSOPSC-BM 2.0 in the Malaysian healthcare setting.
