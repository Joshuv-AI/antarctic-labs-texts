Early support in occupational health care reduced work disability risk
Authors: A Siukola, M Korhonen, L Vähätalo, E Kervinen, T Reho, K Winell, M Viljamaa, R Sauni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the dependency ratio declines, ensuring good work ability (WA) among the working-age population becomes increasingly important. Occupational health care (OHC) provides services to support WA, but more information is needed on their effectiveness and timeliness.AimsThis study examined the relationship between the timing of WA support services and work disability among OHC patients with mental health and/or musculoskeletal disorders.MethodsThis study used patient data on individuals first diagnosed during the follow-up period (2019–22) with mental health and/or musculoskeletal disorders from a nationwide OHC provider in Finland (n = 54 933), linked with national registers on disability benefits, sickness absences and sociodemographic information. The association between the timing of the first WA support action (including WA contacts and negotiations) and work disability was analysed using logistic regression adjusted for sex, age and the number of OHC contacts. Work disability was defined as being granted a permanent or temporary disability pension or accumulating >300 days of sickness absence.ResultsDelayed initial WA support contact after the first diagnosis of a mental health or musculoskeletal disorder increased the risk of work disability. A similar finding was observed regarding the timing of WA negotiations among patients with mental health disorders.ConclusionsOHC practice should emphasise the early initiation of WA support services for patients diagnosed with a mental health or musculoskeletal disorder, as delayed support was shown to be associated with work disability.

Introduction

Due to the declining demographic dependency ratio in Finland and in other high-income global North countries, it is necessary to prolong working careers and ensure the work ability (WA) of the working-age population [1]. Mental and musculoskeletal disorders are common reasons for a disability pension and sickness absence in working-age people [2–4]. In Finland, occupational health care (OHC), in collaboration with employers, has a legally mandated role in supporting employees’ WA, and legislation ensures access to WA support services for all employees [5].

WA support services provided by OHC may include recommendations for work trials and the evaluation of the need for medical rehabilitation, vocational rehabilitation, work accommodation, part-time sick leave and ergonomic improvements. These actions are typically planned in WA negotiations involving the employee, an employer representative and an OHC professional. [6,7] While there are studies on WA negotiations [6,8–10], work accommodations [11] and early interventions to prevent work disability [12–13], research on the timing of WA support services in preventing work disability remains limited. This study aims to address this gap.

Methods

This study used register data from a nationwide Finnish OHC provider and included individuals with at least two OHC contacts between 2018 and 2022 (n = 109 511). The data were linked with national registers on disability benefits, sickness absences and sociodemographic information.

The study population included patients diagnosed with either a mental health disorder (F), a musculoskeletal disorder (M) or both (F M). The abbreviations used are based on the ICD-10 classification [14]. Only patients whose first diagnosis or sickness absence due to an F or M diagnosis occurred between January 2019 and June 2022 were included, if they had no related OHC contact, sickness absence due to these disorders or WA support service contacts during the previous 12 months. Follow-up periods ranged from 180 to 730 days. The final study population comprised 54 933 patients, divided into F-group (n = 5103), M-group (n = 29 401) and F M-group (n = 20 429). All other diagnoses that patients may have had weren’t considered in the analyses.

The outcome variable was work disability, defined as receiving a permanent or temporary disability pension or accumulating over 300 days of sickness absence. WA support services included WA negotiations and WA contacts, such as examinations, guidance and advice provided by an OHC physician, OHC nurse, physiotherapist, psychologist or social services specialist.

Frequencies were examined for sex, age and OHC contacts. Logistic regression was used to examine the association between the timing of the first WA support service (within 0–30, 31–90, 91–180, 181–730 days) and work disability (occurring during 180–730 days), adjusted for sex, age and the number of OHC contacts. Statistical significance was determined as P < 0.05. The analyses were conducted in the Fiona remote access system provided by Statistics Finland, using R version 4.2.2. Since this study used only register data, no ethical approval was requested.

Results

The proportion of women was 64% in the F-group, 49% in the M-group and 65% in the F M-group (Table 1). Most patients in the F group were under 45 years of age and over 45 years in the M-group. In the F M-group, the age was evenly distributed. Over 90% of the study population had at least one WA support service contact within 730 days after the first diagnosis.

The timing of the first WA support contact was associated with work disability (Table 2). Patients in the F- and M- groups whose first WA support contact occurred over 30 days after their diagnosis had a higher risk of work disability compared with those who received support within 30 days. In the FM-group, an increased risk of work disability was only observed among those whose first contact occurred >180 days after the first diagnosis.

No association was found between WA negotiations and work disability in the M-group. However, in the F- and F M-groups, the risk of work disability was higher when the first WA negotiation occurred over 90 days after diagnosis, compared with negotiations held within 30 days (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study suggests that when a patient has either an F or M diagnosis but not both, and when the first WA support contact occurs over 30 days after the first diagnosis, the risk of work disability is increased compared with patients whose contact occurs within 30 days. Similar associations were found for WA negotiations in the F- and F M- groups. In these groups, the risk of persistent work disability increased when WA negotiations were held over 90 days after the first diagnosis. The higher incidence of partial sickness absence related to mental health disorders [11], often preceded by WA negotiations, may partially explain why WA negotiations were found to be significant for work disability in the F-group but not in the M-group. It is already known that WA support actions such as changes to working hours or job descriptions agreed during WA negotiations can reduce sickness absences and improve WA [3,7]. This study adds to that evidence by highlighting the importance of timely implementation, especially early-stage WA contacts following an F or M diagnosis.

A limitation of our study is that studied disorders were analysed only at the ICD-10-chapter level, encompassing conditions of varying severity. We could not adjust the groups for other concurrent diseases. There was also no information about how different workplace cultures are connected to seeking or accessing WA support services. The strength of the study is comprehensive multi-register data. Future research should explore in more detail the content of WA support services in relation to job retention and the prevention of work disability. Further clarification is also needed regarding which OHC services are the most effective for specific diagnostic groups or health conditions.
