Clinical breakpoint meropenem concentration modulate outer membrane vesicle production in enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis
Authors: Saniya Kozhakhmetova, Ayazhan Bekbayeva, Elena Zholdybayeva, Tatyana Krivoruchko, Natalya Dashevskaya, Elizaveta Vinogradova, Nazira Kamzayeva, Zhanagul Khassenbekova, Almagul Kushugulova, Samat Kozhakhmetov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic concentrations at clinical breakpoint levels can influence bacterial physiology and virulence responses, yet their effects on outer membrane vesicle (OMV) production in anaerobic pathogens remain understudied. This study examined how breakpoint meropenem concentrations affect the ultrastructure, inflammatory responses, and OMV production of Bft + enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) in a rat intra-abdominal infection model. Forty male Wistar rats were divided into negative control (n = 8), positive control (B. fragilis infection, n = 16), and experimental group (B. fragilis infection + meropenem at clinical breakpoint concentration 1.0 mg/L for 8 days, n = 16). Transmission electron microscopy, morphometric vesicle analysis, hematological studies, and qRT-PCR cytokine expression analysis were performed on days 8 and 16. Both B. fragilis infection groups showed systemic inflammation with critical hematocrit reduction to 27–28% compared to negative control (p ≤ 0.001). Breakpoint meropenem did not improve infection outcomes; bacterial load showed no significant differences, and OMV production demonstrated only a non-significant trend toward increase. However, the experimental group exhibited inflammation progression with significant elevation in IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α expression by day 16 (p ≤ 0.05). Notably, OMV size distribution changed dramatically under breakpoint treatment, shifting toward significantly smaller vesicles (< 90 nm; Cliff’s δ = −0.49, p ≤ 0.0001). The study demonstrates that breakpoint meropenem concentration triggers sustained stress response in enterotoxigenic B. fragilis, producing pronounced changes in OMV profile and suggesting a mechanism facilitating antibiotic resistance development.

Introduction

Intra-abdominal infections (IAIs) represent one of the most significant challenges in modern surgery and intensive care, serving as a leading cause of sepsis and mortality in intensive care units. According to updated IDSA 2024 guidelines, mortality rates for complicated intra-abdominal infections can reach 25–30% with inadequate antimicrobial therapy. Empirical antimicrobial therapy plays a crucial role in the effective treatment of these infections; however, the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant microorganisms has led to an impending crisis, particularly regarding gram-negative bacteria (Sartelli et al. 2024).

Among IAI pathogens, obligate anaerobes play a substantial role, particularly members of the Bacteroides fragilis group, which are detected in 60–80% of complicated intra-abdominal infections. Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) strains comprise 5–20% of the total B. fragilis population and are characterized by the production of zinc-dependent metalloprotease fragilysin, encoded by the bft gene. This toxin not only causes destruction of intercellular contacts in epithelial cells through E-cadherin cleavage but is also associated with colorectal cancer development, emphasizing the particular clinical significance of this pathogen (Kim et al. 2025)(Zakharzhevskaya et al. 2017a, b).

Bacteroidetes play an important role in host metabolism by degrading proteins and complex carbohydrates. Additionally, B. fragilis bacteria influence gut microbiota-host interactions due to their broad metabolic and immunoregulatory potential. Bacteroides spp. employ various host interaction strategies, including the production of metabolites and outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) (Ahmadi Badi et al. 2020).

OMVs are spherical bilayer liposomes ranging from 20 to 400 nm in size that are naturally secreted by gram-negative bacteria during growth. These structures can encapsulate lipopolysaccharides, phospholipids, genetic elements, as well as periplasmic and cytoplasmic protein components. Outer membrane vesicles perform diverse functions: protein and toxin transport, horizontal transfer of resistance genes, nutrient acquisition, intercellular communication, and host immune response modulation. Two types of vesicles have been described in B. fragilis: classical spherical blebbing vesicles and less common tubular structures. A distinctive feature of B. fragilis vesicles is their ability to transport fragilysin, which significantly enhances bacterial pathogenic potential and ensures direct toxin delivery to epithelial cells (Shagaleeva et al. 2024)(Zakharzhevskaya et al. 2017a, b).

OMV biogenesis is influenced by many factors, including temperature and nutritional deficiency, but antibiotic stress is particularly critical. Antimicrobial concentrations at clinical breakpoint levels represent threshold values between therapeutic efficacy and treatment failure, frequently occurring at infection sites due to pharmacokinetic fluctuations and barrier diffusion. Studies demonstrate that such threshold concentrations can induce adaptive changes in bacterial physiology, including enhanced biofilm formation, expression of various virulence factors, activation of stress response systems, and modulation of outer membrane vesicle production.

Recent studies have demonstrated that various antibiotic classes can modulate vesicle production in gram-negative pathogens. Kim and colleagues found that Enterococcus faecium cultured with subinhibitory concentrations of vancomycin or linezolid produced 3.0 and 1.5 times more membrane vesicles, respectively. Similar results were obtained in 2024 by Li and colleagues, who established that subinhibitory concentrations of various antibiotics significantly increased OMV secretion from E. coli, with OMV particle sizes being larger in antibiotic-treated groups (Kim et al. 2019; Li et al. 2024a, b).

Contemporary research has revealed an important role of B. fragilis vesicles in immunomodulation. Outer membrane vesicles can modulate immune responses, suppress inflammatory processes in early stages of host defense, and activate immune system cells. B. fragilis vesicles, by interacting with TLR2 on dendritic cells, trigger IL-10 and TNF-α secretion, activating regulatory T-lymphocytes and Th17 cells (Omelchenko et al. 2023).

Meropenem is a broad-spectrum carbapenem frequently used for treating severe nosocomial infections, including intra-abdominal infections. Its mechanism of action is based on inhibiting bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis through binding to penicillin-binding proteins. However, recent years have witnessed increasing B. fragilis resistance to carbapenems, associated with metallo-β-lactamase production encoded by the cfiA gene. According to 2024 data, up to 38% of clinical B. fragilis isolates in some regions carry the cfiA gene, with resistance activation occurring through mobile genetic elements such as IS1186 (Yekani et al. 2022; Zholdybayeva et al. 2024).

Our previous experimental data with non-pathogenic strains in a rat abdominal infection model indicate that subinhibitory meropenem concentrations can modify B. fragilis vesiculogenesis, leading to altered OMV size characteristics and shifts in host inflammatory response (Kozhakhmetova et al. 2024). A 2021 study also demonstrated that subinhibitory metronidazole concentrations in B. fragilis led to enhanced vesicle production and cytokine response modulation in an experimental infection model (Ribeiro de Freitas et al. 2022).

Clinical breakpoint concentrations represent the threshold between susceptible and resistant categories according to standardized guidelines. These concentrations, while technically within the susceptible range, may represent suboptimal therapeutic levels in complex clinical scenarios due to pharmacokinetic variability, tissue penetration limitations, and the formation of protected infection niches. Understanding bacterial responses at breakpoint concentrations is particularly relevant as these levels are frequently achieved in clinical practice and may influence treatment outcomes.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to comprehensively investigate the effects of clinical breakpoint meropenem concentrations on the ultrastructure of enterotoxigenic B. fragilis, outer membrane vesicle production, and systemic inflammatory response in an experimental rat model of intra-abdominal infection.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolation

An enteropathogenic B. fragilis strain 60–24-NCB-KZ (BioProject: PRJNA1141923; NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) data available at URL https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/?term=SRX26809821) was used as the study organism. The isolate was obtained in 2024 from a 66-year-old patient diagnosed with acute sigmorectal paraproctitis.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Cultivation of B. fragilis strain 60-24-NCB-KZ was performed on selective Bacteroides Bile Esculin Agar medium (Cat#M805, HiMedia, India) at 37 °C for 48 h under anaerobic conditions using AnaeroGen gas-generating sachets (Cat#AN0025A, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of carbapenems were determined using gradient diffusion method with MIC TEST STRIP (Liofilchem S.r.l., Italy) featuring concentration gradients of 0.002–32 μg/ml for ertapenem (#92157), imipenem (Cat. #92054), meropenem (Cat. #92084), and doripenem (Cat. #92040). Bacterial suspensions were prepared in physiological saline to 1.0 McFarland standard and evenly applied to the surface of Brucella Agar Base (HiMedia, Cat. #M074, India) with 5% defibrinated sheep blood. Test strips were placed according to manufacturer's instructions followed by incubation at 37 °C in anaerobic atmosphere for 48 h.

According to EUCAST version 15.0 interpretation criteria, the strain exhibited resistance to imipenem (MIC 1.5 mg/L) and meropenem (MIC 1.0 mg/L), while maintaining susceptibility to ertapenem (MIC 0.19 mg/L) and doripenem (MIC 0.38 mg/L). The presence of the enterotoxin bft gene was confirmed by PCR using specific primers (Bft1_F_158-178 5'-acgatgtatcggactttggca-3', Bft1_R_284-304 5'-tggtagaatccttgtccctgc-3').

Experimental model

The experimental abdominal infection model was adapted from (Ribeiro de Freitas et al. 2022). The study utilized 40 male Wistar rats aged 7 months with body weight exceeding 400 g. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and EU Directive 2010/63 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the National Center for Biotechnology of the Republic of Kazakhstan (Protocol No. 9 dated November 7, 2022).

Through laparotomy, previously autoclaved sterilized perforated table tennis balls with 250–300 small holes of no more than 1.5 mm diameter were placed into the intraperitoneal cavity of laboratory animals. After animal recovery and implant vascularization (after 6 weeks), animals were inoculated with B. fragilis 60-24-NCB-KZ at 1.0 ml (10⁸ CFU/ml) in 0.85% NaCl solution. Animals were then divided into three groups: Negative control group (NG, n = 8): animals received intraperitoneal injection of 1.0 ml 0.85% NaCl solution; Positive control group (PG, n = 16): animals were inoculated with the parental enterotoxigenic B. fragilis strain 60-24-NCB-KZ; Experimental group (EG, n = 16): animals were also inoculated with the parental enterotoxigenic B. fragilis strain 60-24-NCB-KZ and additionally received meropenem at clinical breakpoint concentration (1.0 mg/L, equivalent to the strain’s MIC) for 8 days at 48 h intervals (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Experimental design for investigating the effects of breakpoint meropenem concentrations on outer membrane vesicle production in enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis. Electron microscopy

Purulent exudate samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at room temperature for 2 h. Post-fixation was performed in 2% osmium tetroxide solution at + 4 °C for 1 h, after which samples were washed with distilled water and dehydrated in a series of ascending ethanol concentrations (50%, 70%, 96%, absolute ethanol) for 15 min in each solution. Embedding was performed in epon-araldite mixture with polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h.

Ultrathin sections of 60 nm thickness were obtained using a Leica UC7 ultramicrotome (Germany) with a diamond knife. Sections were contrasted with 2% uranyl acetate and Reynolds lead citrate. Transmission electron microscopy was performed using a JEM 1400Plus microscope (JEOL, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV in bright-field mode.

Quantitative morphometric analysis of vesicle production was performed on 25 randomly selected electron micrographs for each experimental group at standardized × 50,000 magnification. Outer membrane vesicle counting was conducted per bacterial cell with diameter measurements. Vesicle identification criteria included spherical or tubular shape, bilayer membrane structure, size of 20–400 nm, and localization on the bacterial cell surface or in its immediate vicinity.

Hematological analysis

Blood sampling was performed from the tail vein into EDTA-K2 tubes. Analysis was conducted using an automated Rayto RT-7600 analyzer (Rayto, China), determining 24 hematological parameters including hematocrit, hemoglobin, white blood cell count, and erythrocyte indices.

Tissue analysis

Histomorphological analysis of abdominal cavity connective tissue was performed on specimens extracted from encapsulated spheres. Specimens were fixed in 10% buffered formalin solution. Histological sections of 3–5 μm thickness were prepared using a CUT 4062 rotary microtome (SLEE medical, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to standard protocols. Van Gieson staining was employed for connective tissue differentiation (Gattuso 2009). Microscopic examination was conducted using a CELENA® X High Content Imaging System (Logos Biosystems, South Korea). Three histological sections from each animal were analyzed at all time points. Each specimen was examined in triplicate.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

RNA from exudate was extracted using the EZNA blood RNA kit (Cat. #R6814-01, Omega bio-tek Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was determined using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and quality was verified by electrophoresis in 1% agarose gel. Complementary DNA synthesis was performed using the BioMaster RNAscribe RT Plus kit (#R02-100 Biolabmix, Russia) on a Cfx96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA).

Expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, MCP-1) and anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using the BioMaster HS-qPCR SYBR Blue system (#MHC030-400, Biolabmix, Russia) and specific primers with normalization to the HPRT1 reference gene. Amplification conditions included: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 61 °C for 15 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity.

Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method according to Livak and Schmittgen (2001); Livak and Schmittgen (2001). HPRT1 was selected as the reference gene as the most stable constitutive gene not involved in inflammatory response. For each sample, ΔCt was calculated using the formula: ΔCt = Ct(target gene)—Ct(HPRT1). Subsequently, ΔΔCt was determined for experimental groups relative to control: ΔΔCt = ΔCt(experimental)—ΔCt(control). Relative expression levels were expressed as 2^(-ΔΔCt), reflecting the fold change in target gene expression compared to the control group.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Python 3.12 and the NumPy 2.0.1 (Harris et al. 2020), SciPy 1.15.1 (Virtanen et al. 2020), statsmodels 0.14.4, Matplotlib 3.10.0, and seaborn 0.13.2 libraries (Waskom 2021). For group comparisons, a one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis test, Student’s t-test for independent samples (with or without Welch’s correction), or Mann–Whitney U-test were used, depending on the data distribution and homoscedasticity. A Chi-squared test was used to analyze contingency tables. When comparing hematologic data, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the FDR at 0.05 (denoted as q). A comparison of immunological data was performed without multiple comparison adjustments due to the low variability of these data (denoted as p). The significance level for all tests was set at ≤ 0.05 for raw and adjusted values. Data in the barplot figures are presented as median and 95% CI obtained by bootstrapping with 9999 repetitions.

Results

Characterization of the study strain

The clinical isolate B. fragilis 60-24-NCB-KZ was identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry with a high confidence index (2.24). Cultural properties of the strain were confirmed by growth on selective Bacteroides Bile Esculin Agar with characteristic medium darkening due to esculin hydrolysis in the presence of bile acid salts (Fig. 2). The antimicrobial resistance profile, determined by gradient diffusion method (E-test), demonstrated selective resistance to carbapenems. The strain exhibited resistance to two of four tested carbapenems: imipenem (MIC 1.5 mg/L) and meropenem (MIC 1.0 mg/L), while maintaining susceptibility to ertapenem (MIC 0.19 mg/L) and doripenem (MIC 0.38 mg/L). This resistance pattern is consistent with EUCAST interpretation criteria and indicates the possible presence of class A β-lactamases or metallo-β-lactamases with selective hydrolytic activity spectrum.

The identified combination of enterotoxigenicity and antibiotic resistance represents particular clinical interest, as ETBF strains are associated with the development of diarrheal diseases and colorectal carcinogenesis, while the presence of carbapenem resistance significantly limits therapeutic options for infections caused by this pathogen.

Clinical observations and survival

The experimental model of intra-abdominal infection was established using an adapted protocol from Ribeiro de Freitas et al. on 40 rats over a 16-day observation period. We observed one fatality in the experimental group. All animals following surgical intervention were housed in individual cages, received standard diet (rat/mouse maintenance #V1534-300, 10 mm; ssniffSpezialdiäten GmbH, Soest, Germany) and water ad libitum. From implantation to euthanasia, comparative assessment of survival and general behavior was conducted in experimental and control groups.

Animals in the experimental group (EG) exhibited more pronounced reduction in activity and appetite during the first 3–5 days after initiation of breakpoint meropenem exposure compared to the positive control group (PG). Signs of abdominal abscess formation (local tenderness, abdominal wall tension) were more pronounced in the experimental group.

Animal euthanasia was performed in accordance with international principles of animal care and according to recommendations of the Eurasian Economic Commission Board dated November 14, 2023, No. 33 “Guidelines for Working with Laboratory (Experimental) Animals in Preclinical (Non-clinical) Studies” at two time points: first—8 days after completion of meropenem exposure, second—16 days after meropenem exposure.

Analysis of hematological parameters revealed pronounced systemic effects of B. fragilis intra-abdominal infection and modifying influence of breakpoint meropenem concentration on inflammatory response characteristics in the experimental group (EG) (Fig. 3). The negative control demonstrated normal hematocrit values of 46.4[44.4–48.9]%, while groups with B. fragilis infection showed critical reduction of this parameter, reflecting development of anemia of inflammation. In the positive control, hematocrit was 28.0[26.8–29.6]% on day 8 with minimal dynamics to 28.4[27.0–30.6]% on day 16, whereas in the experimental group values of 29.5[26.9–29.6] on day 8 and 29.7[28.8–31.0]% on day 16 were observed.Fig. 3Hematological parameters of systemic inflammatory response in B. fragilis intra-abdominal infection. The leukocyte response demonstrated group-specific patterns. On day 8, the positive control (PG) exhibited leukocyte counts of 16.7[13.4–27.7] × 10⁹/L compared to 13.4[12.0–14.9] × 10⁹/L in the experimental group (EG), with a trend that did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.11). The relatively attenuated leukocytosis in the experimental group may indicate immunosuppressive effects of breakpoint meropenem concentration on neutrophil and macrophage functions, or alternatively, OMV-mediated modulation of immune response characteristics.

Inflammatory response analysis

Molecular analysis of inflammatory response by qRT-PCR revealed statistically significant differences in expression of individual cytokines (Fig. 4). IL-1β demonstrated significant increased expression in the experimental group compared to positive control dynamics on day 16 (p ≤ 0.05), indicating enhanced pro-inflammatory activation under breakpoint meropenem concentrations. Similarly, IL-1α showed statistically significant elevation in the experimental group (EG) (p ≤ 0.05 on day 16), confirming activation of inflammasome pathways. TNF-α also demonstrated significantly higher expression in the experimental group (EG) by day 16 (p ≤ 0.05), which is consistent with the concept of inflammatory process chronification in persistent infection.Fig. 4Expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in abdominal cavity exudate during B. fragilis intra-abdominal infection. The remaining investigated cytokines did not reach statistical significance levels, however, they demonstrated characteristic trends. MCP-1 showed borderline significance on day 8 (p = 0.08) between groups, with subsequent tendency toward elevation by day 16 in the experimental group (p = 0.35). IL-6 also demonstrated borderline elevation (p = 0.08) on day 16, which may reflect acute-phase response activation. IL-8 and IL-10 showed no statistically significant differences between groups at all time points, which may be related to high inter-individual variability or insufficient sample power for these parameters.

The observed borderline values for MCP-1 and IL-6 additionally support the concept of pro-inflammatory microenvironment formation under breakpoint antibiotic concentration in the experimental group (EG). Vesicular delivery of pathogenic factors may contribute to maintaining local inflammatory response through pattern recognition receptor activation and subsequent induction of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways.

Outer membrane vesicles were moderately represented (3–5 per cell). In the experimental group (EG) on day 8, exposure to breakpoint meropenem concentration induced significant adaptive changes: thickening of the periplasmic space to 60–120 nm with flocculent structures of low electron density, intracellular vacuolization (vacuoles 200–400 nm), and increased production of small vesicles (30–80 nm) with formation of multiple aggregates on bacterial surfaces. By day 16, the positive control showed preservation of stable ultrastructure with minimal degenerative changes. Morphometric analysis in the experimental group revealed high variability in vesicle formation between individual bacterial cells with persistence of structural adaptations, indicating formation of long-term resistance.

Vesicle production and bacterial load

Morphometric analysis of exudate from infection foci revealed important differences between groups in outer membrane vesicle production and their size characteristics (Fig. 6). Outer membrane vesicle sizes on day 8 had a median of 170.0 nm in the positive control (PG) and 155.0 nm in the experimental group (EG), with significant differences (p ≤ 0.001, δ = 0.14). On day 16, the median sizes were 180.0 nm in the positive control and 84.0 nm in the experimental group, with highly significant differences (p ≤ 0.0001, δ = −0.49). Temporal dynamics showed a slight increase in vesicle sizes in the positive control (p ≤ 0.01, δ = 0.1), while in the experimental group the decrease was pronounced (p ≤ 0.0001, δ = −0.41). The dynamics of bacterial load changes did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) between groups. Tissue morphological changes and inflammatory process chronification

Histopathological analysis revealed differences in reparative processes between groups. On day 8, the experimental group was predominantly characterized by pronounced neovascularization with formation of a broad capillary band (87.5% of cases) and thick connective tissue band (50% of cases), while the positive control was dominated by minimal capillary proliferation (50%) and narrow connective tissue band (75%).

By day 16, the positive control showed normalization of reparative processes with homogeneous neovascularization pattern (71% of cases with broad capillary band). The experimental group maintained heterogeneity of morphological changes: persistence of thick connective tissue band (42.8%) with simultaneous absence of fibrosis in 28.5% of samples, indicating disrupted coordination of reparative processes.

Detailed analysis of fibrosis distribution revealed marked heterogeneity in tissue response patterns. On day 8, the positive control group (PG) had 6 cases with no/mild fibrosis (scores 0–1) and 2 cases with advanced/severe fibrosis (scores 3–4), whereas the experimental group (EG) demonstrated an inverse pattern with 2 cases showing no/mild fibrosis and 4 cases presenting advanced/severe fibrosis. By day 16, this distribution shifted: the PG showed 2 cases with no/mild fibrosis and 5 cases with advanced/severe fibrosis, while the EG maintained heterogeneity with 3 cases each in the no/mild and advanced/severe categories. This pattern underscores the variable tissue response in the experimental group under breakpoint meropenem exposure.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that clinical breakpoint meropenem concentrations induce a complex adaptive program in enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, characterized by altered OMV sizes, structural cell wall reorganization, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression. These findings extend our previous observations on the effects of subtherapeutic carbapenem doses on non-pathogenic B. fragilis strains (Livak and Schmittgen 2001) and confirm the universality of vesicular response as a stress adaptation mechanism in anaerobic pathogens.

Both groups showed increases in total OMV numbers over time, with the experimental group (EG) demonstrating a more substantial increase (68.0[57.5–100.0] to 231.5[63.5–394.8]) compared to the positive control (PG) (105.5[81.0–186.2] to 173.5[150.0–230.8]) by day 16. Toyofuku et al. (Toyofuku et al. 2023) in their systematic review emphasize that outer membrane vesicles serve as a universal response mechanism to various stress factors, including antibiotics. Similar results were obtained by Li et al. (Li et al. 2024a, b) for Escherichia coli, where subinhibitory concentrations of various antibiotics increased OMV secretion 2–fivefold. The seminal work by Kadurugamuwa and Beveridge (Kadurugamuwa and Beveridge 1995) laid the foundation for understanding this phenomenon by demonstrating the protective role of vesicles upon antimicrobial peptide exposure in Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Enhanced vesicle production in B. fragilis in the experimental group (EG) may be mediated by activation of two-component regulatory systems. Cao et al., identified the BfmRS system in B. fragilis, which regulates vesicle biogenesis in response to osmotic pressure changes and cell wall integrity (Cao et al. 2022). β-lactam impact on peptidoglycan creates local instability zones, which according to the Schwechheimer and Kuehn model (Schwechheimer and Kuehn 2015), leads to enhanced vesicle budding. The temporal dynamics of vesicle production in our study indicate long-term metabolic reprogramming, potentially mediated by epigenetic mechanisms, as demonstrated by Shi et al. (Shi et al. 2024).

The dramatic shift toward smaller vesicles (< 90 nm) observed in the experimental group by day 16 may have functional implications beyond mere size distribution. Smaller OMVs possess an increased surface area-to-volume ratio, potentially enhancing their capacity for cellular penetration, interaction with host immune cells, and delivery of virulence factors including fragilysin. This size-dependent functional variation could explain the enhanced local pro-inflammatory cytokine expression observed despite reduced systemic leukocyte mobilization, as smaller vesicles may more efficiently traverse tissue barriers and activate pattern recognition receptors on resident immune cells while evading systemic detection.

The observed periplasmic space expansion represents a substantial adaptive reorganization of B. fragilis in response to carbapenems, with the amplitude of these structural changes substantially exceeding previously described adaptation mechanisms in gram-negative bacteria. Structural alterations in the cell envelope under antibiotic exposure have been documented in various bacterial pathogens: cell wall thickening characterizes vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (Cui et al. 2003), while periplasmic proteome remodeling occurs in Acinetobacter baumannii upon imipenem exposure (Scribano et al. 2019).

Formation of flocculent structures within the expanded periplasmic space likely represents accumulation of protective proteins, including CfiA class β-lactamases, providing localized antibiotic neutralization prior to interaction with penicillin-binding proteins. Clinical studies have shown that ST157 B. fragilis strain with insertion sequence IS1187 located upstream of the cfiA gene is associated with high-level carbapenem resistance (Kim et al. 2025). Modeling of resistance development to β-lactams and β-lactamase inhibitors demonstrated that resistance mechanisms include increased β-lactamase activity, reduced drug uptake, and enhanced efflux, which is consistent with the observed structural adaptations (Tandar et al. 2024). Structural-dynamic modeling of the TolC efflux system revealed key positions in the periplasmic entrance affecting efflux movement and demonstrated how structural shifts in TolC facilitate β-lactam resistance through periplasmic space modulation (Kantarcioglu et al. 2024).

Shweta et al. confirm that bacterial membrane vesicles serve as a mechanism for horizontal transfer of biological material both to host cells and between different bacterial species (Shweta et al. 2014). Dell’Annunziata et al. experimentally demonstrated that outer membrane vesicles contain DNA molecules and can facilitate horizontal transfer of virulence genes between different bacterial species, including interspecies transfer from E. coli O157:H7 to E. coli JM109 (Dell’Annunziata et al. 2021). Studies show that vesicles actively participate in horizontal gene transfer, including antibiotic resistance genes. Shen et al. confirmed the role of vesicles in spreading the carbapenemase gene blaOXA-232 among Enterobacterales, demonstrating the practical significance of vesicular resistance gene transfer (Shen et al. 2024). For B. fragilis, vesicle production may ensure efficient transfer of resistance gene-carrying plasmids, which is consistent with the bacterial persistence and adaptive mechanism development observed in our study under breakpoint meropenem concentrations.

Toxin-antitoxin systems in anaerobic bacteria may be activated under antibiotic exposure, potentially leading to temporary metabolic arrest and tolerance formation, though we did not directly assess toxin-antitoxin gene expression in the current study. Additionally, vesicles can modulate host immune response through TLR-dependent mechanisms, as demonstrated in previous studies. The observed dissociation between systemic and local inflammatory responses manifested as an attenuated initial leukocyte reaction concurrent with significant elevation of key pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-1α, and TNF-α by day 16, suggesting complex immunomodulatory effects of OMVs. The temporal dynamics to day 16 demonstrated divergent patterns between groups: resolution of leukocytosis in the positive control contrasted with its persistence in the experimental group, indicating sustained inflammatory activation under breakpoint antibiotic exposure. This contradiction may reflect the immunomodulatory action of B. fragilis vesicles, which can interact with host immune cells and modulate inflammatory response through TLR-dependent mechanisms (Caruana and Walper 2020). Additionally, outer membrane vesicles can transport virulence factors, including fragilysin, ensuring direct toxin delivery to target cells and locally activating inflammatory signaling pathways, explaining elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression with concurrent initial reduction in systemic leukocytosis (Zakharzhevskaya et al. 2017a, b). Our previous observations showed similar dissociation between local and systemic inflammatory response upon subinhibitory meropenem concentration exposure in non-pathogenic B. fragilis strains (Kozhakhmetova et al. 2024). This pattern emphasizes the complexity of vesicle-mediated immunomodulation in persistent bacterial infection, representing both an immune evasion strategy that dampens systemic responses and a treatment consequence wherein breakpoint concentrations induce stress-driven OMV production without achieving bacterial clearance, creating conditions that favor chronic infection persistence over either resolution or acute progression.

The early marked neovascularization observed in the experimental group (EG) at day 8 may represent a dysfunctional angiogenic response occurring in the context of ongoing bacterial stress and altered inflammatory signaling induced by breakpoint meropenem concentrations. The enhanced OMV production and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-1β, IL-1α, TNF-α) create a microenvironment that may promote rapid but disorganized angiogenesis. This premature vascular response, occurring before adequate bacterial clearance and inflammation resolution, could potentially result in formation of immature, structurally compromised vessels unable to support normal tissue architecture. The persistence of heterogeneous histological patterns to day 16, with variable fibrosis distribution ranging from absent to severe within the experimental group, suggests disruption of the normal temporal sequence of inflammation → resolution → organized repair, in contrast to the more uniform progression toward organized fibrotic encapsulation observed in the positive control. Future studies should specifically examine vascular maturation markers (such as pericyte coverage, basement membrane integrity, and endothelial junction proteins), endothelial cell function, and direct assessment of vessel permeability to validate this proposed mechanism of pathological versus physiological healing under breakpoint antibiotic exposure.

The pronounced hematocrit reduction observed in both infected groups corresponds to anemia of chronic inflammation. Weiss et al. in their review emphasize that anemia of inflammation is the most common form of anemia in hospitalized and chronically ill patients, developing against the background of prolonged immune system activation in infections, autoimmune diseases, and malignancies (Weiss et al. 2019). The lack of recovery dynamics in erythrocyte parameters between time points indicates pathological process chronification and establishment of a sustained inflammatory state. Tissue morphological changes with enhanced fibrosis formation (3.0[1.5–3.5] points on day 16) and neovascularization in the experimental group additionally confirm inflammatory process chronification, which is consistent with the concept of innate immune system functional memory (Netea et al. 2020).

The observed heterogeneity in fibrosis distribution within the experimental group likely reflects multiple interacting factors rather than treatment dosage variation, as all animals received identical meropenem dosing regimens. These factors include: (1) individual variation in bacterial colonization patterns within the implanted spheres, creating focal areas of higher and lower bacterial density, (2) differences in local meropenem penetration due to abscess formation and variable vascularization, resulting in spatial gradients of antibiotic concentration, (3) heterogeneous OMV production and distribution among bacterial subpopulations, leading to localized differences in immune stimulation, and (4) individual host immune response variability influencing the magnitude and character of inflammatory reactions. This heterogeneity contrasts with the more uniform fibrotic progression observed in the positive control group and actually supports our hypothesis that breakpoint concentrations create microenvironmentally unstable conditions that disrupt coordinated healing responses. The formation of infection microniches with varying drug exposure levels may explain why some tissue areas exhibit advanced fibrosis while adjacent regions show minimal scarring, reflecting the complex interplay between local antibiotic penetration, bacterial stress responses, and host tissue repair mechanisms.

The obtained results have substantial clinical implications. Breakpoint carbapenem concentrations are frequently observed in intra-abdominal infections due to impaired tissue perfusion and antibiotic sequestration in abscesses. Contemporary studies show that achieving pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic targets (100% fT > MIC) with standard meropenem dosing is achieved in only 56% and 26% of critically ill patients for target concentrations of 2 mg/L and 8 mg/L respectively (Gijsen et al. 2021). Our data, together with previous observations, justify the use of prolonged meropenem infusions to maintain concentrations above MIC. Current IDSA/ESCMID recommendations (Sartelli et al. 2024) emphasize the importance of achieving PK/PD targets, especially for anaerobic pathogens. Studies show that prolonged infusions significantly improve therapeutic target achievement compared to standard infusions (Abdulla et al. 2020; Kişi et al. 2024).

Combined strategies aimed at suppressing vesicle production appear promising and warrant systematic investigation. Future studies should directly assess: (1) toxin-antitoxin gene expression and OMV proteomic cargo under breakpoint exposure, (2) functional immunomodulatory capacity of size-fractionated OMVs using primary immune cell assays, (3) tissue pharmacokinetics correlating local meropenem levels with OMV production, (4) horizontal gene transfer frequencies under breakpoint conditions, and (5) combination therapies targeting both bacterial viability and OMV biogenesis. Contemporary research shows the possibility of outer membrane vesicle modification to reduce their pathogenic potential through genetic engineering (Huang et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024a, b). Development of specific inhibitors of transport systems controlling OMV biogenesis represents a promising direction (van der Pol et al. 2015). Application of specific metalloprotease inhibitors may serve as an alternative approach to reducing ETBF strain virulence, considering the zinc-dependent nature of fragilysin (Zakharzhevskaya et al. 2017a, b).

Structural change persistence 8 days after antibiotic exposure completion indicates formation of epigenetic “memory” of stress response. Contemporary data demonstrate that epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, stably alter gene expression without genomic sequence modification and play a key role in bacterial persistence formation (Pan et al. 2023). In B. fragilis, multiple stress response systems may participate in epigenetic regulation of vesicle production and resistance.

Study limitations include relatively small sample size; 16 day observation period insufficient for assessing long-term adaptive changes; absence of tissue meropenem pharmacokinetic measurements, but these features did not affect the reliability of main results and identification of key patterns in B. fragilis vesicular response to breakpoint carbapenem concentrations.

In conclusion, this study reveals a complex network of B. fragilis adaptation mechanisms in response to breakpoint meropenem concentration, with altered outer membrane vesicle production as the central element. These findings, complementing our previous observations, emphasize the critical importance of achieving and maintaining therapeutic antibiotic concentrations in treating anaerobic infections and open new targets for adjuvant therapy.
