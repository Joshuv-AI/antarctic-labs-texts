Risk-averse Fair Multi-class Classification
Darinka Dentcheva, Xiangyu Tian

Abstract
We develop a new classification framework based on the theory of coherent risk measures and systemic risk. The proposed approach is suitable for multi-class problems when the data is noisy, scarce (relative to the dimension of the problem), and the labeling might be unreliable. In the first part of our paper, we provide the foundation of the use of systemic risk models and show how to apply it in the context of linear and kernel-based multi-class problems. More advanced formulation via a system-theoretic approach with non-linear aggregation is proposed, which leads to a two-stage stochastic programming problem. A risk-averse regularized decomposition method is designed to solve the problem. We use a popular multi-class method as a benchmark in the performance analysis of the proposed classification methods. We illustrate our ideas by proposing several generalization of that method by the use of coherent measures of risk. The viability of the proposed risk-averse methods are supported theoretically and numerically. Additionally, we demonstrate that the application of systemic risk measures facilitates enforcing fairness in classification. Analysis and experiments regarding the fairness of the proposed models are carefully conducted. For all methods, our numerical experiments demonstrate that they are robust in the presence of unreliable training data and perform better on unknown data than the methods minimizing expected classification errors. Furthermore, the performance improves when the number of classes increases.

Introduction

Classification is one of the most important machine learning methods that can be used in many real-life situations, such as fraud detection, health anomalies, natural disaster detection, etc. Our paper presents a new risk-averse design of the classification model.

Robustness in classification design has become an important area of study due to the increasingly complex data being analyzed in applications where misclassification may have very serious consequences. The objective of robust classification is to develop models that are stable and accurate even when data is incomplete, noisy, or with polluted labels. In adversarial situations, mislabeling typically occurs.

The goal of our paper is to explore the approach to multi-class classification via coherent measures of risk. Coherent measures of risk have been used in classification as well as in other contexts of statistical learning because they have a built-in robustness while being amenable to efficient numerical treatment. In the context of classification, coherent measures of risk have been used mainly in a binary classification scenario in
[
40
,
22
,
29
]
. In
[
22
]
, the authors use an overall risk function to measure the risk of the entire system while in
[
40
]
, the authors proposed a new family of loss functions, which apply different risk measures on different classes. They argue that in different tasks, the importance of each class should be different, therefore treating them differently can lead to a more flexible and more adequate model. In this paper, we propose a new method for multi-class classification which demonstrates the potential of those risk measures to properly evaluate and enforce fairness. Furthermore,
when the given information is inaccurate, the new risk-averse method provides better performance and forces fairness at the same time. There is no trade-off between performance and fairness in contrast to what is noted in the extant literature (e.g.,
[
37
]
.)

Various approaches to robustness with respect to polluted data exist in the literature and quite a few papers deal with this issue.
Providing an exhaustive survey is beyond the scope of this paper; we only mention several approaches and some associated works. Methods investigated by a number of researchers employ robust optimization with various constructions of uncertainty sets; we refer to
[
20
,
5
,
6
]
. This method does not only work with noisy features, but also works when the labels are polluted. Another approach is based on chance constraints, e.g.,
[
25
,
4
]
.
Preprocessing the data is also a common option. Such as the work in
[
28
]
, it has proposed a method to deal with the noisy labels in binaray classification problems. The method can take a mislabeled training set, and find a subset of it that is more likely to be correctly labeled. In
[
30
]
, the robustness of the classification model against the noisy labels in deep neural networks is investigated. Their method is compatible with multi-class setting. The preprocessing methods have the potential to be combined with other methods that focus on the model formulation.

Other work addresses robustness to corrupted data by tailoring the risk estimation and the loss function (
[
3
,
21
,
38
]
). In
[
26
]
, the traditional empirical risk, which is the estimator of the expectation of the loss function, is replaced by the mean of the medium value estimator. The authors argue that the minimizer of that estimator is less sensitive to the corruption of the training data than the empirical risk minimizer, and hence, it is more robust. The work in
[
39
]
proposed a tunable
\alpha-
loss as an effective generalization of the cross entropy loss, which can be either more robust or more sensitive depending on the actual application.

Other influential work regarding robust classification design changes the framework of the learning process, see
[
18
,
7
,
19
]
. These approaches are typically based on combinations of methods. For example, AdaBoost
[
18
]
iteratively reweights the training data, giving more weight to the instances that were misclassified in previous rounds, which can be interpreted as data preprocessing. Additionally, a form of adaptive loss function is employed, where more emphasis is put on the samples that are harder to classify.

In our paper, we propose a new way to estimate the risk which is particularly suitable for multi-class scenarios. Instead of the empirical risk, we use coherent risk measures for systemic risk. While coherent risk measures are widely used in finance and insurance, the theory and numerical methods for systemic measures of risk are less developed. As risk is not additive, the systemic measures of risk aim at risk aggregation and, respectively, risk allocation to the units of a system in a coherent way. We explain the notion and its relevant properties in due course.
The theory of risk measures is presented in
[
2
]
.
Our work explores the use of systemic measures of risk, which naturally can accommodate two or more classes in a classification problem. While this framework is generally aligned with the ideas of risk-sharing in classification models similar to
[
40
]
, or proposal is more general and unifies the approaches to risk-sharing as it is based on an axiomatic foundation (
[
1
]
). Additionally, we argue that the risk-averse design is more helpful in a more uncertain and diverse situation, which becomes more complex in a general multi-class setting. We have shown that using systemic risk measures provides not only robustness but is very suitable for analyzing and enforcing fairness.
Additionally, we analyze the behavior of the proposed methodology on a risk-averse counterpart of a popular method for multi-class classification. We have compared the proposed model to the risk-neutral model using an empirical risk estimator with corrupted data. The risk-averse model shows significantly more robustness and better generalization towards the unknown in risky situations. The results show that the advantage of the proposed risk-averse model over the risk-neutral model gets more pronounced when more classes are involved. Additionally, we have devised a new optimization method for solving the resulting two-stage problem and have shown its convergence.

Coherent Measures of Risk for Random Vectors

The risk of a loss function can be evaluated using a univariate coherent measure of risk according to the widely accepted axiomatic framework that is proposed in
[
2
]
and further analyzed in
[
10
,
17
,
34
,
35
,
31
,
11
]
, and many others work.
We refer to
[
11
]
for an extensive treatment of risk measures and stochastic optimization with such measures.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{k})
be the space of random vectors with realizations in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, defined on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, which have finite
p
-th moments,
p\in[1,\infty)
, and are indistinguishable on events with zero probability. We shall assume that the random variables represent losses since we plan to use them as errors in classification.

When we deal with scalar-valued random variables, then a lower semi-continuous functional
\varrho:\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called a
coherent risk measure
if it is convex, positively homogeneous, monotonic with respect to the a.s. comparison of random variables, and satisfies the following translation property:

\varrho[Z+a]=\varrho[Z]+a\text{ for all }Z\in\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P),\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

If
\varrho[\cdot]
is monotonic, convex, and satisfies the translation property, it is called a
convex
risk measure. While the theory and methods for risk measures of scalar-valued random variables are well-developed, less work is associated with high dimensional risks, that is, measures of risk for random vectors. The need for special attention to random vectors arises in part from the challenge that univariate measures of risk are not additive, unlike the expected value functional.

We denote the
k
-dimensional vector, whose components are all equal to one by
\mathbf{1}
, and the random vector with realizations equal to
\mathbf{1}
by
\mathbb{I}
. We adopt the following definition, introduced in
[
1
,
11
]
:

Definition 1
.

A lower semi-continuous functional
\varrho:\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a systemic coherent risk measure with preference to small outcomes iff it satisfies the following properties:

A1.

Convexity: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have:

~\varrho[\alpha X+(1-\alpha)Y]\leq\alpha\varrho[X]+(1-\alpha)\varrho[Y].

A2.

Monotonicity: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})
, if
X_{i}\geq Y_{i}
for all components
i=1,\dots,N

P
-a.s., then
\varrho[X]\geq\varrho[Y]
.

A3.

Positive homogeneity: For all
X\in\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
t>0
, we have
\varrho[tX]=t\varrho[X]
.

A4.

Translation equivariance: For all
X\in\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\varrho[X+a\mathbb{I}]=\varrho[X]+a\varrho[\mathbb{I}].

It is shown in
[
1
,
11
]
that if the systemic risk measure
\varrho
is proper, lower semicontinuous, and satisfies those axioms, then it can be represented as follows:

\varrho[X]=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}}\langle\zeta,X\rangle.

(1)

The set
\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}\subset\mathcal{L}_{q}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
is defined as:

\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}=\Big\{\zeta\in\mathcal{L}_{q}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})~|~\int_{\Omega}\zeta(\omega)dP(\omega)=\mu_{\zeta},~\zeta\geq 0\text{ a.s.},\;\langle\mathbb{I},\mu_{\zeta}\rangle=r\Big\},

where
r\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant. The set
\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}
is the convex subdifferential
\partial\varrho(0)
of the risk measure. A systemic measure of risk
\varrho
is normalized if
\varrho[\mathbb{I}]=1
, in which case,
r=1
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}
.

This entails that for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}
,
\zeta P
can be interpreted as a probability measure on the space
\Omega\times\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. In the special case when
N=1
, we obtain the widely used dual representation of coherent measures of risk for scalar-valued random variables

\varrho[X]=\sup_{\frac{dQ}{dP}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X],

(2)

where
\frac{dQ}{dP}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the measure
Q
with respect to the reference measure
P
.
Hence, the dual representation shows that the risk measure takes into account the “worse” expectation of random vectors taken with respect to measures that augment the original probability measure
P
and are absolutely continuous with respect to it. This entails that using such risk functionals provides robustness when uncertainty about the distributions involved is present. This is always the case when we base our decisions on observed data.

Another motivation to pursue classification with systemic measures of risk is the possibility of addressing fairness without sacrificing robustness. We shall see the potential in this regard in due course.

We consider labeled data consisting of
N
subsets
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{N}
of
n
-dimensional vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The labels of the data points in
S_{i}
are denoted by
y_{i}
, and
y_{i}=i
stands for the
i
-th class. The size of each class is the cardinality
|S_{i}|=m_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
. The data points in the subset
S_{i}
are the observations of the
n
features of the
i
-th class. We identify the
N
-dimensional random vector
Z
with the classification error (measured in some way) associated with a given classifier for this classification problem, that is,
Z_{i}
represents the classification error of class
i
,
i=1,\dots,N
.

As already mentioned in the previous section and argued in
[
40
]
, the risk is non-additive, which means that the overall risk of the system does not equal to the direct sum of the risk of each class. In many applications, the system’s risk is computed by linear aggregation of the risk of individual components assigning appropriate wights to each of them:

\varrho_{\rm s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\varrho_{i}[Z^{i}]

The argument to do so is that a Pareto-efficient risk allocation to each class
i
can be obtained by a scalarization of the vector of risks. That means that whenever
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1
the risk allocation vector
\mathcal{R}=\big(\varrho_{1}[Z^{1}],\dots,\varrho_{N}[Z^{N}]\big)

is a minimal element of the set of feasible risk allocations. The advantage of this method is that its calculation is very efficient. However, it is not always clear how to adjust the importance of each class by changing the weights
\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. When the number of classes increases, navigating through all possible choices of weights to find the most suitable solution might become a difficult task. Therefore, we propose a new way of scalarization by using a nonlinear aggregation of the risk associated with the individual classes.

Consider a finite probability space
(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},p)
, where
\Omega_{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
,
p
is a probability mass function, and
\mathcal{F}_{N}
contains all subsets of
\Omega_{N}
. Given a collection of
N
measures of risk
\varrho_{i}:\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\dots,N,
we associate with the random error vector
Z
, a random variable
R_{Z}
on the space
\Omega_{N}
as follows. The realizations of
R_{Z}
are

R_{Z}(i)=\varrho_{i}[Z_{i}],\quad i=1,\dots N.

Now, let a coherent measure of risk
\varrho_{\rm o}:\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},p)\to\mathbb{R}
be chosen. We define the measure of systemic risk

\varrho_{\rm sys}:\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P;\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\varrho_{\rm sys}[X]=\varrho_{\rm o}[R_{Z}].

(3)

This type of measure satisfies the axioms in Definition
1
as shown in
[
1
]
.
With our goals in mind, we shall pay special attention to the following systemic risk evaluation.
We may choose as
\varrho_{\rm o}
to be the mean-upper-semideviation risk measure of order
p
(
p\geq 1
) and evaluate all components of the error vector
Z
by the same law-invariant coherent measure of risk
\varrho[\cdot]
. The description of the total risk evaluation is the following:

\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\varrho[Z_{i}]+\kappa\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\Big(\varrho[Z_{i}]-\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\varrho[Z_{j}]\Big)_{+}^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(4)

with
\kappa\in[0,1]
.
When
p=1
, the expression simplifies as follows

\displaystyle\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\bigg(\varrho[Z_{i}]+\kappa\Big[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}p_{j}(\varrho[Z_{i}]-\varrho[Z_{j}])\Big]_{+}\bigg)

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\varrho[Z_{i}]+\kappa\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}p_{i}p_{j}\big|\varrho[Z_{i}]-\varrho[Z_{j}]\big|

This representation shows that this risk measure is an aggregation of the individual risk of the components, which puts an additional penalty on the deviation of the individual risks from that average. This property is crucial to our treatment of fairness in classification.

Various versions of support vector machines constitute very powerful and one of the most popular classification approaches. As a first step in our work, we extent the risk-allocation ideas presented in
[
40
]
to the multi-class situation and to the application of kernels before we proceed to the non-linear risk aggregation (
3
). We elaborate that the linear aggregation is a special case of a systemic risk measure.

Most popular approaches for multi-class classification use techniques called One-vs-All and One-vs-One.
For
N
classes, the One-vs-All method identifies
N
separate binary classifiers such that the
i
-th classifier discriminates between the
i
-th class and all the rest of the data. After the training process, the prediction will be the class with the highest score among the
N
classifiers. The One-vs-One method identifies
\binom{N}{2}
classifiers for every pair of the
N
classes. After the training process, for a new data point, all the
\binom{N}{2}
classifiers would be applied, and the prediction will be the class that has been chosen most frequently by those classifiers.
There are many designs that do not require solving multiple optimization problems to classify multiple classes.
We consider as a benchmark other methods such as Crammer-Singer method
[
9
]
, Lee, Lin, and Wahba’s method
[
27
]
, and Weston and Watkins’ method
[
41
]
, which solve only one optimization problem.

The Crammer-Singer method is a very well-known and frequently cited classification method. The method determines
N
linear classifiers
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\psi_{i}(x)=\langle v^{i},x\rangle-\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,\dots N
, by solving the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{v,\gamma,Z}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}z^{i}_{\ell}\right)+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|v^{i}\|^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle z^{i}_{\ell}\geq\psi_{j}(x^{i}_{\ell})-\psi_{i}(x^{i}_{\ell})+1\quad i,j=1,\ldots,N,\;j\neq i,\,\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle Z_{i}\geq 0\quad i=0,\ldots,N,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}.

In this formulation,
Z_{i}
stands for the random variable with realizations
z^{i}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}.

The problem determines
N
linear classifiers such that ideally

\psi_{i}(x)>\max_{j=1,\dots,N\;j\neq i}\psi_{j}(x)

(7)

for all observed data points
x
in the
i
-th class. The data points
x
in the training set
S_{i}
, which violate (
7
) are identified by the positive values of the corresponding realizations of
Z_{i}
.
Observe that, even though
N-1
comparisons are needed between the
i
-th class and the rest, the variables
Z_{i}
identify the maximum violation among all the comparisons. In the training problem, we can view the objective function as the minimization of the expected sum of maximal violations for all classes in a soft-margin formulation. The random vector
Z
with components
Z_{i}
comprises the error for all classes.

The primary advantage of the Crammer-Singer method is that it solves a single, joint optimization problem for all classes, which has the benefit of capturing the dependence between data in the various classes in a better way.
We use the Crammer-Singer method to illustrate our framework and to compare the behavior of the risk-averse formulation with the original risk-neutral formulation.

3
Risk-Averse Kernel Method

When using kernel-based methods, we assume that a pre-Hilbert space is given, i.e., a space
\mathcal{Z}
, where the inner product is defined and a reproducing kernel

K:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
exists. More precisely, a non-linear mapping
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
exists such that

K(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}}
denotes the inner product in
\mathcal{Z}
.
The function
\varphi
is defined implicitly by the choice of the kernel. We shall use the empirical estimation of the risk measures involved.

For all the classifiers
\psi_{i}(x)=\langle v^{i},x\rangle-\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,\dots N
, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\gamma_{i}=0
.
Using formulation (
8
) and the mean-upper-semideviation risk measure with order 1 for all the classes, for a kernel
K
with its associated mapping
\varphi
, we obtain the following formulation:

\displaystyle\min_{v,\gamma,Y,Z}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left(z_{j}^{i}+y_{j}^{i}\right)\right)+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v^{i}\right\|^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle z^{i}_{\ell}\geq\langle v^{j},\varphi(x^{i}_{\ell})\rangle-\langle v^{i},\varphi(x^{i}_{\ell})\rangle+1,

\displaystyle i,j=1,\ldots,N,\;j\neq i,\quad\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle y_{\ell}^{i}\geq z_{\ell}^{i}-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}z_{j}^{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,\;\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle Z_{i}\geq 0,\;Y_{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

In order to obtain the dual problem of (
9
), we assign Lagrange multipliers
\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}
to the constraints that determine the misclassification, and
\delta_{\ell}^{i}
to the constraints that compute the semi-deviation, and we get the following Lagrange function:

\displaystyle L(v,Y,Z,\mu,\delta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left(z_{j}^{i}+y_{j}^{i}\right)+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v^{i}\right\|^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}\big(\left\langle v^{j},\varphi\left(x_{\ell}^{i}\right)\right\rangle-\left\langle v^{i},\varphi\left(x_{\ell}^{i}\right)\right\rangle+1-z_{\ell}^{i}\big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\delta_{\ell}^{i}\Big(z_{\ell}^{i}-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}z_{j}^{i}-y_{\ell}^{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}+\delta_{\ell}^{i}-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\delta_{j}^{i}\geqslant 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i};

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}-\delta_{\ell}^{i}\geqslant 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}.

Introducing shorthand notation for

A_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}\varphi\left(x_{\ell}^{i}\right),\quad B_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{j}}\mu_{i,\ell}^{j}\varphi\left(x_{\ell}^{j}\right),

Additionally, we introduce the matrices

and the kernel matrices

with elements

on row
\kappa=1,\dots,m_{i}
and column

, and
i,j=1,\dots,N
.

Here
K(x,y)=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\rangle
.
We obtain from (
14
) the following

\displaystyle\|v^{i}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}(A_{i}-B_{i})^{2}=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\Big)^{\top}K_{ii}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\Big)+\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\Big)^{\top}K_{jj}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\Big)

(16)

\displaystyle-\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\Big)^{\top}K_{ij}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\Big)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\sigma\|v^{i}\|^{2}-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}\langle v^{i},\varphi(x_{\ell}^{i})\rangle+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{i,\ell}^{j}\langle v^{i},\varphi(x_{\ell}^{j})\rangle)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\sigma\|v^{i}\|^{2}-\langle v^{i},\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}\varphi(x_{\ell}^{i})-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{i,\ell}^{j}\varphi(x_{\ell}^{j})\rangle)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\sigma\|v^{i}\|^{2}-\langle v^{i},2\sigma v^{i}\rangle)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(-\sigma\|v^{i}\|^{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\big)^{\top}K_{ii}\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\big)-2\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\big)^{\top}K_{ij}\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\big)^{\top}K_{jj}\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\big)\Big]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}

Therefore, the dual problem takes on the form:

\displaystyle\min_{\mu,\delta}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}(M_{j}^{i})^{\top}\big)K_{ii}\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i}\big)-2(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{j}^{i})^{\top}K_{ij}\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\big)

(18)

\displaystyle+\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\big)^{\top}K_{jj}\big(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}M_{i}^{j}\big)\Big]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\mu_{j,\ell}^{i}+\delta_{\ell}^{i}-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\delta_{j}^{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}-\delta_{\ell}^{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle\mu\geq 0,\;\delta\geq 0.

After solving the dual problem, we obtain the optimal Lagrange multipliers
\hat{\mu}
. Then, according to (
14
), for a new data point
x
, the result with respect to the
i
-th class can be calculated as:

\displaystyle\langle v^{i},\varphi(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\hat{\mu}_{j,k}^{i}\langle\varphi\big(x_{k}^{i}\big),\varphi(x)\rangle-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\hat{\mu}_{i,k}^{j}\langle\varphi\big(x_{k}^{j}\big),\varphi(x)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\hat{\mu}_{j,k}^{i}K(x_{k}^{i},x)-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\hat{\mu}_{i,k}^{j}K(x_{k}^{j},x)\right)

In conclusion, we do not need to know the exact form of the mapping
\varphi
for the risk-averse multi-class classification problem. We arrive at the following statement.

Proposition 1
.

A new data
x
is classified in class
\kappa
,
\kappa\in\{1,\dots,N\}
if the maximum

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\hat{\mu}_{j,k}^{i}K(x_{k}^{i},x)-\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\hat{\mu}_{i,k}^{j}K(x_{k}^{j},x)\right)

(19)

is achieved for
i=\kappa,

where
\hat{\mu}
is the solution of problem (
18
).

Nonlinear Risk Aggregation in Multi-class Classification

In this section, we discuss the application of the systemic risk measures with non-linear aggregation. As already presented
one way to calculate the system’s risk is via weighted linear aggregation as in (
3
)

\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\varrho_{i}[Z_{i}],

where the random vector
Z
has components
Z_{i}
indicating the classification errors in each class
i=1,\dots,N
.
The advantages of this method lays in its efficient calculation and easy assignment of the importance of each class via the weights
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}\}
reflecting the view of the decision maker. However, when the number of classes increases, navigating through all possible choices of weights to find the most suitable solution might become a very difficult and cumbersome task. We propose a new way of scalarization, which is nonlinear and fits the axiomatic framework of systemic risk. We return to the system-theoretic definition given in (
3
).
Consider the probability space
(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},p)
, where
p
is a vector in the
N
-dimensional simplex (denoted
S^{N}_{+}
) and
\mathcal{F}_{N}
contains all subsets of
\Omega_{N}
. The vector
p
is a probability mass function of the space
\Omega_{N}
and we assume that we observe data from class
i
with probability
p_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
.
The probabilities
p_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,N
can be given by prior knowledge of the class distribution, or simply use the sample size of each class as an estimate, i.e.,
p_{i}=\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}{m_{j}}}
. Furthermore, it is shown in
[
1
]
that we can define the systemic measure of risk by using the maximum of a family of coherent univariate risk measures on
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},p)
instead of a single measure
\varrho_{\rm o}
. Additionally, recall that expectation is also a coherent measure of risk and could be used as
\varrho_{\rm o}
. This entails that the weighted linear combination could replace
\varrho_{\rm o}
in definition (
3
), interpreting it is an expected value with respect to the probability mass function, given by the vector of the weights. Hence, this approach provides
a unifying view
on risk-allocation to multiple system’s components and to classification in multi-class scenario.

From this new perspective, the classification problem can be formulated as a two-stage stochastic optimization problem. The first stage problem is:

\displaystyle\min_{v,\gamma}

\displaystyle\varrho_{\rm o}[R(v,\gamma)]

(20)

Here
R(v,\gamma)
is the random variable providing the risk of the second stage.
In this stage, we decide the best classifiers
\psi_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,N
to calculate the optimal systemic risk. As an example, we can choose the mean-semi-deviation to be the first-stage risk measure leading to the following first-stage formulation:

\displaystyle\min_{v,\gamma}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R(v,\gamma)]+c(\mathbb{E}[(R(v,\gamma)-\mathbb{E}[R(v,\gamma)])_{+}^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}

(21)

We emphasize that the proper evaluation of the risk requires to include the constraints
\|v^{i}\|=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,N.
While it is possible to solve the non-convex problem which would include those constraints, we discuss a method, which ignores them. Due to the non-linearity of the risk measure, no technical issue regarding the solubility of the problem arises.
Fixing a coherent risk measure
\varrho_{i}[\cdot]
as the inner measure for the second-stage risk of the
i
-th scenario, we calculate the risk for each class by the problem:

\displaystyle R_{i}(v,\gamma)=\min_{Z_{i}}

\displaystyle\varrho_{i}[Z_{i}]

(22)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle z^{i}_{\ell}\geq\psi_{j}(x^{i}_{\ell})-\psi_{i}(x^{i}_{\ell})+1\quad j\neq i,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i},

\displaystyle Z_{i}\geq 0\quad i=1,\dots N.

As an example, if we choose the mean-semi-deviation with order
p=1
for each class, the second-stage problem takes on the form:

\displaystyle\min_{Z_{i},Y_{i}}

\displaystyle\frac{c_{i}}{m_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}y^{i}_{\ell}+\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}z^{i}_{\ell}

(23)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle z^{i}_{\ell}\geq\langle v^{j},x^{i}_{\ell}\rangle-\gamma_{j}-\langle v^{i},x^{i}_{\ell}\rangle+\gamma_{i}+1\quad j\neq i,\,\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i},

\displaystyle y^{i}_{\ell}\geq z^{i}_{\ell}-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}z_{j}\quad\ell\in 1,\ldots,m_{i},

\displaystyle Z_{i},Y_{i}\geq 0.

In this formulation, the
i
-th class is identified with the
i
-th scenario of the classical two-stage problem.
We would like to point out that the mean-semideviation measures have particularly nice features regarding fairness. We shall describe our method using a generic coherent risk measure at the first and second stages. We also note that theoretically, the method can use individual risk measures for each of the classes but we believe that measuring classification errors with the same risk function facilitates fairness and might be more appropriate.

Another noteworthy fact is that at the second stage, calculation is extremely easy.

When using this type of systemic risk measure we no longer need to worry about how to adjust the weights according to the importance of the classes.

To solve the two-stage problem (
21
)-(
22
), we propose a modified risk-averse multi-cut decomposition method. We base our numerical approach on the risk-averse version of the multi-cut method in
[
23
]
, to which we introduce regularization.
In order to declutter notation, we introduce the notation

\vartheta=(v,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{N(n+1)}\quad\text{ with }\vartheta_{i}=(v^{i},\gamma_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\text{ for }i=1,\dots,N.

The set
\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}
in the dual representation of a coherent measure of risk
\varrho:\mathcal{L}_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
\mathcal{A}_{\varrho}=\partial\varrho(0)
: the convex subdifferential of
\varrho
at
0
.
This representation becomes simple when
\Omega
is finite.
In our problem, let
R
represent the second-stage risk whose components (realizations)
R_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
are given by
R_{i}=\varrho_{i}[Z_{i}(\vartheta)]
of each scenario. The evaluation of
R
is based on finitely many scenarios. Hence, we represent
R
by a vector of
N
components. Abusing notation, we can view the dual set
\mathcal{A}_{\rm o}
of the measure
\varrho_{\rm o}(\cdot)
as a subset of the unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
containing probability measures. The dual representation becomes

\varrho_{\rm o}[R(\vartheta)]=\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm o}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}R_{i}(\vartheta_{i}).

(24)

Similarly, the dual sets
\mathcal{A}_{i}
associated with
\varrho_{i}
become subsets of the unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
containing probability measures. The dual set for many popular risk measures is described in various works; see e.g.,
[
11
]
.

In the course of the proposed iterative method, the set
\mathcal{A}_{\rm o}
is approximated by a finite subset of measures
\{\mu^{\kappa},\kappa=1,\ldots,k\}
that are collected in each iteration. The first-stage objective value is approximated by a variable
\alpha
and the second-stage objective value of the
i
-th scenario is approximated by
r_{i}
. According to (
24
), we construct the following cuts:

\alpha\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}^{\kappa}r_{i}\quad\kappa=1,\ldots,k.

(25)

The subgradients
\mu_{i}^{k}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
at iteration
k
are calculated by identifying those elements in
\mathcal{A}_{\rm o}
that satisfy the following relation for the second stage risk
R^{k}
calculated at that iteration:

\varrho_{\rm o}[R^{k}]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}^{k}R_{i}^{k}.

(26)

The constraints (
25
), which may be called the objective cuts, approximate the first-stage objective from below.
For the second-stage problem of the
i
-th scenario, let
\pi_{ij}^{k}
stand for the optimal Lagrange multipliers associated with the inequality constraints

z^{i}_{\ell}\geq\langle\vartheta_{j}^{k},(x^{i}_{\ell},-1)\rangle-\langle\vartheta_{i}^{k},(x^{i}_{\ell},-1)\rangle+1\quad\ell=1,\dots m_{i},

where
\vartheta_{j}^{k}=(v^{jk},\gamma_{j}^{k})
for
j=1,\ldots,N
stands for the first-stage decision variables at the
k
-th iteration of the method.
Further, let
D_{i}
denote the expanded data matrix for class

D_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}x^{i}_{11}&x^{i}_{12}&\ldots&x^{i}_{1n}&-1\\
x^{i}_{21}&x^{i}_{22}&\ldots&x^{i}_{2n}&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
x^{i}_{m_{i}1}&x^{i}_{m_{i}2}&\ldots&x^{i}_{m_{i}n}&-1\end{pmatrix}.

The subdifferential of the
i
-th second-stage optimal value function
\partial R_{i}(\vartheta^{k})
with respect to
\vartheta^{k}
contains vectors

whose components are of the following form for an optimal Lagrange multiplies

g_{ij}^{k}=\begin{cases}-D_{i}^{\top}\sum_{s\neq i}\pi_{is}^{k}&\text{if }j=i.\\
D_{i}^{\top}\pi_{ij}^{k}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\quad i,j=1,\dots,N.

(27)

We construct the following regularized master problem (with a constant
\sigma>0
) to approximate the first-stage problem (
21
) at iteration
k
:

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha,r,\vartheta}

\displaystyle\alpha+\sigma\|\vartheta-w^{k}\|^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle\ \text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}^{\kappa}r_{i},\quad\kappa=1,\ldots,k,

\displaystyle r_{i}\geq R_{i}^{\kappa}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle g_{ij}^{\kappa},\vartheta_{j}-\vartheta_{j}^{\kappa}\rangle\quad\kappa=1,\ldots,k,\ i=1,\ldots,N,

\displaystyle\alpha,r_{i}\geq 0\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

We modify the risk-averse multi-cut decomposition method by using regularization for several reasons. First, the regularization term controls the length of the steps during each iteration, avoiding inefficient long shots toward the optimal solution. Second, it allows us to dispose of the non-convex constraints
\|v^{i}\|=1,i=1,\ldots,N
simplifying the constraint set to the soft-margin formulations. Additionally, the regularization method we formulate below has the property of converging over unbounded regions.

Note that the solution of problem (

\alpha^{k}
provides the approximation of the objective function of (
20
) calculated at iteration
k
. The new proximal center
w^{k}=(w_{1}^{k},\ldots,w_{N}^{k})
is updated within each iteration based on the relation among

\varrho_{\rm o}[R(\vartheta^{k})]

\varrho_{\rm o}[R(w^{k-1})]
, and
\alpha^{k}
.

The proposed regularized multi-cut method uses parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
; it works as follows:

Step 0.

Set
k=1
. Choose initial decision variable
\vartheta^{1}
with
\|v^{i}\|=1
for all
i=1,\dots,N
.

Step 1.

For each
i=1,\ldots,N
, solve the second-stage problem (
22
). Let

be its optimal value. Calculate the subgradients
g_{i}^{k}=(g_{i1}^{k},\ldots,g_{iN}^{k})
by formula (

Step 2.

Calculate the systemic risk

R^{k}

and

\mu^{k}
at point

by solving (

Step 3.

Determine the new center
w^{k}=\{w_{i}^{k},i=1,\ldots,N\}
as follows. If

or

\varrho^{k}\leq(1-\theta)\bar{\varrho}^{k-1}+\theta\alpha^{k-1},

then set

and

(descent step). Otherwise, set

and
\bar{\varrho}^{k}=\bar{\varrho}^{k-1}
(null step).

Step 4.

Solve the master problem (

\alpha^{k},\vartheta^{k},r^{k}

Step 5.

If
\bar{\varrho}^{k}=\alpha^{k}
, then stop (

The core of this method is the regularized decomposition method, where a risk measure on the recourse replaces the ordinary expected value. A multi-cut method is used to approximate the risk measure.

Theorem 1
.

The regularized multi-cut method generates a sequence

\{w^{k}\}
which converges to an optimal solution
\vartheta^{*}
of problem (
21
).
Furthermore, the optimal value of the master problem (

\varrho^{*}_{\rm sys}=\varrho_{\rm o}[R(\vartheta^{*})]

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\vartheta^{k+1}-w^{k}\|=0.

Proof.

First, we observe that the second stage problem (
22
) is always solvable and, hence, the two-stage problem has a complete recourse.
Therefore the domain of the functions
R_{i}(\vartheta)
is the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and the random variable
R(\vartheta)
is bounded, which entails that the systemic risk is always finite. Due to the proximal term, the objective function of problem (
28
) has compact level sets. Hence the master problem is always solvable. After solving the second stage problem for all classes, we obtain all subgradients
g_{i}^{k}
,
i=1,\dots N
necessary for the cutting plane approximation of the function
R_{i}(\cdot)
. The approximation of the systemic risk measure by cutting place uses the separate approximations of

. Therefore, the convergence of our method follows by the convergence properties of the regularized decomposition method for two stage problems described in
[

. We only need to argue that the assumptions made there are satisfied: existence of an optimal solution and the existence of an uniform bound
C
such that

\|g_{ij}^{k}\|\leq C
for all
k=1,2,\dots
and all

. Due to the complete recourse and the fact that the risk measure is the support function of a compact closed set (by its dual representation), we conclude that an optimal solution the two-stage problem exists. The only situation, in which the uniform boundedness of the vectors
g_{ij}^{k}
may be violated is when
\vartheta^{k}
is a boundary point of the domain of
R(\cdot)
. Since the domain is the entire space, we infer that this assumption is satisfied as well.
∎

Risk-averse classification and fairness

The issue of fairness has attracted significant attention in the machine learning community recently, highlighting concerns about biased outcomes in automated decision-making. These algorithms, used in areas such as job recruitment, credit risk assessment and others, might carry on or even aggravate social biases if not carefully designed. As a result, researchers and practitioners are increasingly focused on ensuring these technologies being both accurate and equitable, aligning them with principles of fairness and social justice.

Current work regarding fairness machine learning can be categorized into three different approaches: pre-processing the data before learning
[
15
,
24
,
42
]
, forcing fairness through the learning process or within the learning model
[
37
,
33
,
12
,
16
]
, and post-processing the results after learning
[
8
,
32
]
.

Our proposed risk-averse formulation based on mean-semideviation as the outer risk measure suggests that fairness within the learning model between the classes is forced. In this category, we have seen models that directly modify the loss function, such as
[
37
]
, which uses a penalty term on the distance between the distributions of predictions within the two groups. Other works use extra constraints to force fairness, e.g.
[
12
]
. Our two-stage model naturally involves a ’penalty’ term within the formulation as it penalizes the deviation of the risk for each class from the average risk. in this way there is some similarity to
[
37
]
. However, the application of the two-stage model requires a different training process.

We use the upper mean-semideviation risk measure in the objective function of the first stage. Recall the formulation (
21
), the objective has two terms: an expected value of the misclassification risk of each class, and an expected value of the shortfall of the risk of each class and the average risk. The second term, the shortfall, can be viewed as a penalty term with parameter
c\in[0,1]
, penalizing every class whose risk is higher than the average risk. We can call it a fairness term since it prevents any class from being overlooked and having a risk too much higher than other classes. We argue that this is a very attractive property that can force fairness in classification.

We consider the scenario in which a fair-sensitive categorical feature with values
\mathcal{S}={1,\dots,S}
is present in the data points to be classified. To make the prediction fair with respect to the groups within all classes, we introduce a contextual risk-measure
\varrho_{c}[Z]
. Assume that
Z=f(\vartheta,X,Y)
, where
\vartheta
determines the classifier,
X
stands for a random data point and
Y
stands for the value of said feature, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is measurable, convex and monotonically non-decreasing with respect to the second argument for any value of the first and the third argument. We need to consider the classification error
Z_{i}
in every context
s\in\mathcal{S}
for all classes
i=1,\dots N.

Consider the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
where the random vector
Z
lives and define the spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F}|Y=s,P(X|Y=s)).
A contextual risk measure is a composition of coherent measures of risk evaluating the risk given a context and an aggregation measure providing the total risk for all contexts.
More precisely, we fix risk measures
\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}|Y=s]
for each
i=1,\dots,N
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
and choose

\varrho_{c}[Z_{i}]
as an aggregation measure to obtain the total risk of each class over all contexts. Additional aggregation by an outer measure
\varrho_{\rm o}[Z]
will aggregate the risk over the classes.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\rho_{i,s}[\cdot]
be coherent measures of risk for all
i=1,\dots,N
and all
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
risk measures
\varrho_{i}:\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{F}_{S},P_{i|Y})\to\mathbb{R}
be coherent measures of risk for all
i=1,\dots,N
, and

\varrho_{\rm o}:\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},p)\to\mathbb{R}
be coherent as well.
Denote

V_{c}(i,s)=\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}|Y=s]\quad\text{and}\quad W(i)=\varrho_{i}[V_{c}(i,\cdot)],\quad s\in\mathcal{S},\;i=1,\dots,N.

Then the risk measure
\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z]=\varrho_{\rm o}[W]
satisfies axioms A1-A4 of systemic measure of risk.

Proof.

(i) Given any
Z^{1}
,
Z^{2}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we consider the random vector
Z^{3}=\alpha Z^{1}+(1-\alpha)Z^{2}
. It follows that

V_{c}^{3}(i,s)=\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}^{3}|Y=s]\leq\alpha\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}^{1}|Y=s]+(1-\alpha)\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}^{2}|Y=s]=\alpha V_{c}^{1}(i,s)+(1-\alpha)V_{c}^{2}(i,s).

by the convexity of
\rho_{i,s}[\cdot]
for all
i=1,\dots,N
and all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Hence

W^{3}(i)=\varrho_{i}[V_{c}^{3}(i,\cdot)]\leq\alpha\varrho_{i}[V_{c}^{1}(i,\cdot)]+(1-\alpha)\varrho_{i}[V_{c}^{2}(i,\cdot)]=\alpha W^{1}(i)+(1-\alpha)W^{2}(i).

by the same arguments. Analogously,

\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z^{3}]=\varrho_{\rm o}[W^{3}]\leq\alpha\varrho_{\rm o}[W^{1}]+(1-\alpha)\varrho_{\rm o}[W^{2}]=\alpha\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z^{1}]+(1-\alpha)\varrho_{\rm sys}[Z^{2}],

which establishes the convexity property.

(ii) Suppose the vectors
Z^{1},Z^{2}
satisfy
Z^{1}\leq Z^{2}
a.s. This implies that
Z^{1}_{i}\leq Z^{2}_{i}
a.s. and for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Using the monotonicity of the risk measures
\rho_{i,s}[\cdot]
,
\varrho_{i}[\cdot]
and
\varrho_{\rm o}[\cdot]
, we infer the monotonicity of
\varrho_{\rm sys}[\cdot].

(iii) The positive homogeneity follows in a straightforward manner from the definition.

(iv) Given a random vector
Z
and a real constant
a
, we calculate the risk
V^{+}_{c}(i,s)
of the translated error in context
s
as follows:

V^{+}_{c}(i,s)=\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}+a|Y=s]=\rho_{i,s}[Z_{i}|Y=s]+a=V_{c}(i,s)+a\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathcal{S},\;i=1,\dots,N.

The second equality holds by the translation property of the coherent measures of risk. This implies that

W^{+}(i)=\varrho_{i}[V_{c}^{+}(i,\cdot)]=\varrho_{i}[V_{c}(i,\cdot)+a]=W(i)+a
by the same argument. Hence,

\varrho_{\rm o}[W+a]=\varrho_{\rm o}[W]+a,
concluding that property (A4) holds as well.
∎

This structure of measuring risk allows us to enforce fairness within each class by choosing the aggregate measures
\varrho_{i}
to be such that deviation from the average risk or excessive risk above a certain quantile is penalized.
Consider as an example the case of two classes
A
and
B
, both with a categorical feature
Y
with values
\mathcal{S}=\{1,2\}
.
We could calcluate the risk of the groups
\varrho_{A_{i}}
and
\varrho_{B_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, which would be the measures
\rho_{i,s}
with
i=A,B
and
s=1,2.
We use the upper mean-semideviation of order 1 for the
\varrho_{i}
, which can be calculated to have the following form:

\displaystyle\varrho_{A}=\sum_{i=1,2}p_{A_{i}}\varrho_{A_{i}}+\kappa p_{A_{1}}p_{A_{2}}|\varrho_{A_{1}}-\varrho_{A_{2}}|,

\displaystyle\varrho_{B}=\sum_{i=1,2}p_{B_{i}}\varrho_{B_{i}}+\kappa p_{B_{1}}p_{B_{2}}|\varrho_{B_{1}}-\varrho_{B_{2}}|,

where
\kappa\in(0,1)
and
p_{A_{i}}
is the (estimated) conditional probability of a data point to be in class
A
with feature
Y=i,

i=1,2.

In that case, we may use a risk-neutral evaluation for the outer risk measure
\varrho_{\rm o}
, i.e.

\varrho_{\rm o}=p_{A}\varrho_{A}+p_{B}\varrho_{B}.

We still can use our numerical method for this model; we only need to change the way we calculate the subgradients and we can apply the regularized decomposition method to solve the modified fairness classification model. We test our ideas via numerical experiments and report them in the next section.

One comment is due regarding the enforcement of fairness. We use the example of two classes to make the argument. To make the prediction of the two classes fair against the grouse with feature
Y=1
and
Y=2
, we could split the original two classes into four classes
A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}
, and then learn the data by training our two-stage model. The penalty term can then force the misclassification risk of each class to be close, which can force classification fairness. Therefore, in a way, our approach also involves preprocessing the data before learning.

6
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we demonstrate the performance of the proposed models and methods with respect to robustness and fairness. We use the well-known MNIST dataset to illustrate how the risk measures can affect the behavior of a classification model on noisy and mislabeled data sets. We use a data set that is known to be non-linearly separable for detecting Electrical Faults, classification data from
[
14
]
, to show the viability of the developed risk-averse kernel formulation. Our third set of experiments uses the drug usage dataset
[
13
]
to present the potential of the proposed risk-averse classification method to enforce fairness.

For the first set of experiments, we try to gain a better insight as to when the risk-averse methods provide more robustness. To this end, we devise several perturbations and apply a few different kinds of noises to the training data. We emphasize that the test data are left untouched.

Under every kind of perturbation, we compare the risk-neutral method (
6
), which we use as a benchmark and our risk-averse method (
8
). We wish to test the performance of the risk-averse method under different situations and we believe the situation is more chaotic with a larger number of classes to classify. For the first few experiments, we will only use the samples from 3 out of 10 classes to maintain a low-risk situation. This will not weaken our result, as we run through all possible selections and compare the two methods under the same setting to ensure that the comparisons are thorough and do not depend on randomness. We will increase the number of classes to 10 in the later experiments to make the system more risky, and we observe that the performance of the risk-averse method gets even better with a larger number of classes.

In addition, we only use up to 1000 samples from each class. This will also strengthen our arguments if we observe better results with the risk-averse model. We eventually want to demonstrate that the risk-averse method should be a better option under risky situations, which include the situation where you can only get access to a limited amount of data.

For the risk-averse method, we adopt the upper mean-semi-deviation measure in the first as well as in the second stage problems.
The numerical formulation is now:

\displaystyle\min_{v,\gamma,Z}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{m_{i}}\left(c_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}y_{i,\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}z_{i,\ell}\right)+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|v^{i}\|^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle z^{i}_{\ell}\geq\left(\langle v^{j},x^{i}_{\ell}\rangle-\gamma_{j}\right)-\left(\langle v^{i},x^{i}_{\ell}\rangle-\gamma_{i}\right)+1,

\displaystyle i,j=1,\ldots,N,\;j\neq i,\;\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle y_{i,\ell}\geq z_{i,\ell}-\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{i}}z_{i,l},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\displaystyle Z^{i},Y^{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

Two sets of parameters in (
29
)) need to be defined: the probability mass function
p
and the weight
c_{i}
of the semi-deviation in the calculation of the risk measures. For simplicity, we assume that
p
is the uniform measure on
\Omega_{N}
and
c_{i}
of all the classes are set as the same number between 0 and 1. These parameters are used to adjust the level of importance of each class, which can be very useful under some circumstances. The MNIST dataset is very balanced, which would require less adjustment of these parameters. While we do agree that navigating through different selections of these parameters will potentially create better results, our main focus here is to compare the robustness that the risk measures can provide, so these parameters have not been tuned; they are identical for all classes.

It provides a framework for assessing whether one distribution generally yields higher values than another. The first and the second order of stochastic dominance can be defined as follows:

Definition 2

(First-order Stochastic Dominance (FSD))
.

A distribution
F
is said to first-order stochastically dominate another distribution
G
if
F(x)\leq G(x)
for all
x
. This condition implies that the distribution
F
generally yields higher values across the entire range of outcomes. Mathematically, it is expressed as:

F(x)\leq G(x)\quad\forall x.

Definition 3

(Second-order Stochastic Dominance (SSD))
.

A distribution
F
second-order stochastically dominates another distribution
G
if the area under the CDF of
F
up to any point
x
is less than or equal to the area under the CDF of
G
up to the same point, with at least one strict inequality. This incorporates attitudes towards risk, implying that
F
is preferred by all risk-averse decision-makers. It is formalized as:

\int_{-\infty}^{x}F(t)dt\leq\int_{-\infty}^{x}G(t)dt\quad\forall x,

with strict inequality for some
x
.

One can define the stochastic dominance recursively up to order
p
, but the first two orders are most commonly used. It is clear that the dominance gets weaker as the order increases, with the first order being the strongest given that one distribution yeilds higher value almost always.

It is clear that with any selection of the risk level, the risk-averse method is significantly better than the original Crammer-Singer method since it almost gives a first-order stochastic dominance. Moreover, we can see that the choice of risk level matters a lot, as a smaller risk level clearly provides better results. However, this risk level cannot be too close to 0, as we have also tested. A risk level of 0.01 would provide a result with no significant difference from the risk-neutral model.

For a more thorough comparison, we have also calculated the average ROC curve and AUC of each class over all the attempts. In the calculation of the curve, one needs to calculate the true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) corresponding to different decision thresholds. In the Crammer-Singer method, the threshold
t
is involved in the inequality

\psi_{i}(x_{i})-\max\big(\psi_{j}(x_{i})\,|\,j\neq i\big)>t.

In the general case, when deciding if an observation belongs to class
i
, the threshold is set to 0, which means that if an observation has the highest decision score with the classifier of the
i
th class, then it belongs to the
i
th class. In our experiment, we vary this threshold from its minimum possible value to its maximum, and for every class, we calculate its average TPR and NPR over all the attempts. Then we plot the ROC curve and report the AUC. Similar to any other metrics we use, all these are calculated using both our method and the risk-neutral method on the same training and test set during each attempt.

Fig.
5
shows the ROC and AUC of the best and the worst class based on the difference in the AUC scores. For the same risk level, we consider the class to be the best when the difference between the AUC scores of the risk-averse method and the score of the risk-neutral method is the largest among the differences of all 10 classes. Likewise, when this difference is the smallest, we consider this class to be the worst. As we can see, when the risk level is set too high (
c=0.95
), the difference between the two methods is very small, almost invisible. When we decrease the risk level to a relatively more appropriate value,
c=0.35
, the risk-averse method begins to perform better compared to the risk-neutral method, showing a larger difference in AUC values and a more significant gap between the ROC curves. Moreover, we can see that no matter the risk level, even in the worst case, our method has a higher AUC score, meaning that for all 10 classes, our method does better classification than the risk-neutral method in terms of AUC.

6.5
Fair Risk-averse Classification

We have also conducted experiments on data regarding fairness using the two-stage formulation. The data we utilized is the Drug Consumption Dataset
[
13
]
from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The Drug Consumption (quantified) dataset consists of data on the consumption of 18 different drugs among 1,885 participants. This dataset includes demographic information such as age, gender, education level, and country of residence. Additionally, it contains scores on various personality traits, including neuroticism, extraversion, and openness to experience, measured using the NEO-FFI-R questionnaire. The primary objective of the dataset is to explore the patterns and factors associated with drug consumption, both legal and illegal.

This dataset contains usage of many different drugs, and for each drug, the consumption is represented by a categorical variable, indicating the frequency of use, starting from ’Never used’, ’Used over a decade ago’, to ’Used in the last day’. In order to test our SVM model and compare it with other baseline methods, we need to make the target binary. We slightly change the objective to classifying the drug consumption into ’Used in the last year’ and ’Not used in the last year’.

We try to address the fairness issue by considering the gender’s effect on the drug usage problem. Traditionally, using SVM on this dataset is very simple, given that it is a binary classification problem. However, as we mentioned before, in our method using two-stage multi-class SVM, the two classes (’Used’ and ’NotUsed’) are separated into four classes (’Male_Used’, ’Female_Used’, ’Male_NotUsed’, and ’Female_NotUsed’). The semi-deviation part within the formulation penalizes the risk of any class that is too different from the others, thus forcing fairness among the classes.

To evaluate the fairness of the results and compare among different methods, it is crucial to quantify fairness using some fairness metrics. Previous research on fairness has considered several different metrics with the most commonly used metrics being the Statistical Parity.
It is defined as follows:

Definition 4
.

Given a labelled dataset
D=(X_{i},L_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[N]}
, where
X_{i}
represents the vector of features,
L_{i}\in\{0,1\}
represents the label, and
Y_{i}\in\{G_{0},G_{1}\}
represents a sensitive attribute, a classifier
\psi
satisfies statistical parity if

P[\psi(X_{i})=1\mid Y_{i}=G_{0}]=P[\psi(X_{i})=1\mid Y_{i}=G_{1}].

Here,
P
is the frequency within the given dataset used as an estimation of the conditional probability.

In other words, the estimated probability of a positive classification is the same across the values of the sensitive attribute
Y
that indicate groups
G_{0},\,G_{1}
. The difference between the frequencies can evaluate the fairness of a given classifier. Another commonly used fairness metric is the Statistical Rate, defined as follows.

Definition 5
.

Given a labeled dataset
D=(X_{i},L_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[N]}
with the same notation as the above definition, and
0<\tau\leq 1
, a classifier
\psi
has a statistical rate
\tau
if

\tau=\min\left(\frac{P[\psi(X_{i})=1\mid Y_{i}=G_{1}]}{P[\psi(X_{i})=1\mid Y_{i}=G_{0}]},\frac{P[\psi(X_{i})=1\mid Y_{i}=G_{0}]}{P[\psi(X_{i})=1\mid Y_{i}=G_{1}]}\right)

.
Same as before,
P
is a frequency within the given dataset.

The closer
\tau
is to 1, the more fairness the classifier
\psi
has between the groups
G_{0},G_{1}
.

Again, the probabilities here can be numerically estimated by the frequencies of the predictions in each group. In the following experiment, we will use these two metrics to evaluate and compare the fairness between different models.

Apart from fairness, we use F1-score to evaluate the performance of the models. The calculation of the F1-score is trivial for the binary classification. For the two-stage model with 4 classes, we do not calculate the F1-score of each class. Instead, we only consider the two original classes (’Used’ and ’NotUsed’) when calculating F1-score, since correctly classifying gender was never our goal. We take out
30\%
out of each class as the test set. Same as all the previous experiments, during each run, we apply all methods to the same training set and test set for a fair comparison.

In the following experiments, we consider the traditional binary SVM, and the method from
[
37
]
as baselines. For the second baseline method, we implement it with the SVM method. For the penalty term that enforces fairness, We adopt the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) as the distance function, and take the quadratic regularization. Like before, we compare these methods under different conditions.

We conduct several experiments with both linear-scalarization model and the two-stage fairness model and report their results.

With the linear scalarization, we tried to create different risky scenarios to test how our model with coherent risk measures behaves. The situations include data mislabeling, feature removal, sample-size reduction, and increasing the number of classes. We found out that when the data condition is good, there is no significant advantage in using the risk-averse model. However, as the situation gets riskier, our risk-averse model will start to outperform the baseline. Moreover, from the risk-chart, the training risk of the risk-averse model is generally higher than the risk-neutral model, whereas the risk of the test set is generally lower with the risk-averse model, showing that the risk-averse model provides better generalization towards the unknown data. A simple test on the kernel formulation is also made. Results show that the risk-averse method works perfectly fine with the kernels when dealing with non-linear-separable data.

With the two-stage model with non-linear scalarization, we wish to explore its possibility of enforcing fairness in classification tasks. The results show that, under any circumstance, the two-stage formulation can enforce fairness when compared to a traditional model. When the data is contaminated with mislabeling, it also enforces fairness better than the baseline fairness model from
[
37
]
. Moreover, if we introduce more pollution by mixing up the genders, it can enforce fairness without sacrificing performance when compared to the baseline fairness method. We also brought up a special formulation with the first-stage risk measure modified for the fairness issues. Experiments show that the model’s capability to enforce fairness is increased by this modified risk function.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have extended the risk-averse methods in classification by introducing a systemic point of view, which is particularly essential in multi-class scenarios. Using measures of this type allows us to enforce fairness without additional technical and computational burden. We have demonstrated that the risk-averse classification methods provide additional robustness and generalize better to unknown data.
Furthermore, their superior performance over a risk-neutral counterpart improves with the increase of the number of classes.
Additionally, we have extended the non-linear separation techniques based on kernels from the risk-neutral to the risk-averse case. Our numerical experiments confirm the efficiency and robustness of the kernel-based risk-averse method as well.
