Topological isomerization unlocks exceptional elasticity and strength of cellulosic triboelectric aerogels
Authors: Qiguan Luo, Feng Gao, Zheying Liu, Tao Liu, Song Zhang, Bin Luo, Chenchen Cai, Jinlong Wang, Yanhua Liu, Yayu Bai, Kang Yu, Shuangxi Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAerogel materials often aim for high elasticity, which typically compromises strength. This challenge in balancing elasticity and strength constrains their applications in wearable circuits, intelligent robots, and flexible sensors. This study presents a topological isomerization strategy based on molecular chain rearrangement to enhance both the elasticity and strength of cellulosic triboelectric aerogels. During the molecular chain rearrangement, ethylenediamine forms competitive hydrogen bonds with hydroxyl groups, disrupting the inherent hydrogen bond network of cellulose and exposing additional active hydroxyl groups. Furthermore, through in situ interfacial polymerization and covalent crosslinking, a polymer topological network is established. This results in a flexible cellulosic triboelectric aerogel with improved elasticity and strength; even after 20,000 cycles of compression, the aerogel exhibited a recoverability exceeding 95% and could support loads over 2000 times its own weight. Concurrently, the transformation of the crystal structure due to the reconstruction of the hydrogen bond network leads to the rearrangement of surface functional groups, which modifies the surface potential and ensures stable triboelectric output across a broad range of temperatures and humidity levels. This strategy provides innovative methods for the design and fabrication of lightweight, ultra-elastic, and extreme-environment-resistant 3D porous materials, which are essential for advancing next-generation sensor devices.Subject terms: Structural materials, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

The development of lightweight porous aerogels characterized by exceptional elasticity and strength is crucial for various fields, including aerospace, new energy development, and life sciences1–4. Elastic recoverability is essential for engineering applications, while strength is a key determinant of structural stability and long-term reliability5. However, achieving both elasticity and strength in lightweight porous aerogels often presents a contradiction6,7. This conflict primarily arises from the stiffness of the fibers in the pore walls and the interactions among these fibers. High-stiffness fibers can impart significant strength to the material, but this often compromises its elasticity. Conversely, when the pore walls consist of flexible fibers, elasticity can be enhanced, yet the strength typically decreases sharply. Balancing the stiffness and flexibility of the fibers remains a significant challenge.

Cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) have garnered significant attention due to their exceptional mechanical properties8. Comprising both crystalline and amorphous regions, CNFs exhibit an ordered molecular arrangement in the crystalline region that imparts high strength, while the amorphous region enhances flexibility. Consequently, CNF is extensively utilized as raw material for porous aerogels9,10. However, pure CNF aerogels lack elasticity. This limitation arises from the hydroxyl-rich surface of CNF, which facilitates strong, irreversible hydrogen bonding interactions. During compression, the close contact between fibers makes separation challenging, leading to structural collapse and performance degradation11,12. The application of amines in the treatment of cellulose has been demonstrated to enhance its reactivity by increasing the exposure of active hydroxyl groups and reconstructing hydrogen bond networks13–15. For instance, amine treatment can facilitate isomerization, converting cellulose I into cellulose III, which increases the number of hydrogen bonds in the cellulose chains that are accessible to the aqueous solvent16. This transformation significantly improves the binding affinity of cellulose to enzymes. Although cellulose III exhibits heightened sensitivity to specific chemical treatments, there are limited reports on the further functionalization of cellulose aerogels or amine-treated fibers17,18. Thus, achieving a precise control of the interactions at the CNF surface to balance elasticity and strength in aerogels has emerged as a critical issue in current research on cellulose-based aerogel materials.

To enhance the mechanical elasticity of cellulose aerogels, researchers have undertaken significant efforts. For instance, employing a multi-scale hierarchical structure can reduce the rigidity and viscosity of the pore walls, thereby improving the elasticity of cellulose aerogels. However, cellulose aerogels produced using this approach often retain hydrophilicity and exhibit low mechanical strength11. It has been reported that cellulose aerogels can be synthesized from wood through a top-down method, coupled with chemical modifications to disrupt intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which can improve elasticity and alter the hydrophilicity and mechanical strength of the aerogel to some extent19,20. Nevertheless, the strong hydroxyl network characteristic of type I cellulose nanofibers, along with the limited availability of exposed free hydroxyl groups21, often leads to the agglomeration of modifiers or the formation of heterogeneous composite structures during the modification process. This results in poor interfacial bonding between the nanofibers and functional layers, causing detachment of the functional layer during compression and significantly diminishing the service life of the aerogel22. Consequently, the development of a cellulose aerogel that can be effectively functionalized while exhibiting superelasticity, high strength, and resistance to extreme environments remains a critical challenge.

Here, we report a universal topological isomerization strategy that effectively transforms cellulose aerogels from a brittle state to one characterized by superelasticity and strength. Building upon previously reported mature amine-treated cellulose processes13,16,23–25, this study utilizes the competitive hydrogen bonds formed between small-molecule ethylenediamine (EDA) and cellulose to disrupt the hydrogen bond network in pre-formed cellulose I aerogels, promoting molecular chain rearrangement and driving its crystalline isomerization towards the more reactive cellulose III. Our findings indicate that this process enhances the reactivity of cellulose III without compromising the macroscopic pore structure of the aerogel. Additionally, we developed a uniform polymer topological network through in situ polymerization and crosslinking, achieving the objective of maintaining fiber stiffness while effectively shielding inter-fiber hydrogen bonds. Following topological isomerization, the cellulose I aerogel yielded a cellulose III-based triboelectric aerogel (CNF-III@Si) that exhibits both superelasticity and high strength. Moreover, this aerogel demonstrates an ultrafast compression recovery rate and temperature-invariant elasticity, thereby creating favorable conditions for applications in extreme environments. Topological isomerization, which induces electron cloud redistribution and alters microstructure, enhances triboelectric output performance. Consequently, triboelectric nanogenerators constructed from aerogels exhibit rapid response times and highly stable outputs. Moreover, the stable output signal, sustained across a broad range of temperatures and humidity levels, underscores its significant potential for high-precision flexible sensing in extreme environments. The molecular chain topological isomerization strategy elucidated in this study offers innovative approaches for addressing the elastic design of bio-based materials, as well as a broader spectrum of brittle porous materials, thereby significantly advancing the development of next-generation sustainable flexible electronic devices.

Results

Topological isomerization of cellulose aerogels

Maintaining the stiffness of fibers and effectively shielding the hydrogen bonds between them are crucial for achieving the elasticity and strength of cellulose-based aerogels. Natural cellulose nanofibers exhibit strong intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which can hinder the separation of fibers after adhesion26. Additionally, these bonds significantly reduce the penetration of chemical modification reagents into the fiber surface, leading to uneven surface modification and inadequate shielding of hydrogen bonds27. Traditional amine treatment of cellulose powder or film materials has been demonstrated to enhance cellulose reactivity15,16,18,28. Based on these findings, this study examines crystalline isomerization in the aerogel state to improve hydroxyl group accessibility and reorganize the hydrogen bond network, thereby increasing cellulose reactivity. By infiltrating anhydrous EDA into the interior of the CNF-I aerogel fibers, the amino and hydroxyl groups formed strong competitive hydrogen bonds, disrupting the hydrogen bond network of cellulose29. During EDA treatment, the crystal form transitions from cellulose I to cellulose III (CNF-III), which is retained after removal of the temporarily bonded EDA (Fig. 1a). The EDA employed in this process can be recovered and reused via vacuum distillation, achieving a recovery rate of approximately 90%. Variations in N2 adsorption and pore diameter distribution indicate that the pore size of the micropores or mesopores remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Additionally, SEM images reveal that the pore walls and structure were intact before and after ethylenediamine treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3). These pieces of evidence suggest that the treatment of CNF-I aerogel with EDA does not modify its three-dimensional pore architecture and maintains its original honeycomb porous structure.

The transformation of cellulose crystal form involves molecular chain rearrangement while preserving a certain degree of crystallinity. This process enables CNF-III to expose a greater number of active hydroxyl groups, thereby maintaining a degree of fiber strength24. The exposure of these hydroxyl groups facilitates in situ polymerization and cross-linking on the surfaces of monomer fibers, resulting in the formation of a polymer topological network (Fig. 1a). This network effectively shields hydrogen bonds, yielding a triboelectric aerogel (CNF-III@Si) with both superelasticity and high strength. It can support over 2000 times its own weight, and its strength significantly surpasses that of pure cellulose and pure cellulose modified aerogel (CNF-I@Si) (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, its elastic recoverability and Young’s modulus outperform those of previously reported lightweight porous materials (Fig. 1c)30–41. Given these remarkable mechanical properties, CNF-III@Si is utilized in triboelectric nanogenerators, demonstrating high and stable output performance. Even at 200 °C, the output voltage remains at 41.6 V (Fig. 1d). Compared to other reported triboelectric aerogels (Supplementary Table 1), CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogel has advantages in mechanical properties, high-temperature resistance, and triboelectric properties, showing its excellent application potential. These findings illustrate that topological isomerization serves as an effective strategy to address the limitations of lightweight porous materials in achieving a balance between superelasticity, high strength, and ultralow density.

Evolution process of crystalline isomerization

The conversion of cellulose I to cellulose III through amine treatment is a well-established process. Previous studies have demonstrated that cellulose III is typically more reactive than both cellulose I and cellulose II in the preparation of cellulose derivatives24,25. In this study, the crystal isomerization induced by ethylenediamine treatment of CNF-I aimed to expose a greater number of active hydroxyl groups on the fiber surface, thereby facilitating chemical modification. We theoretically demonstrated the feasibility of this approach. Molecular dynamics simulation (MD) was employed to investigate the impact of crystal isomerization on the accessibility of cellulose hydroxyl groups. The molecular dynamics simulation system comprises 30 octameric glucan chains chains organized into five layers, with six chains per layer, to construct the cellulose model (Fig. 2a, b). This arrangement is based on the consensus regarding the minimum number of molecular chains necessary to form a fundamental cellulose fiber. In comparison to CNF-I, CNF-III fibers exhibit a greater number of exposed hydroxyl groups on their surfaces. These hydroxyl groups, along with the oxygen atoms in the pyranose rings and glycosidic bonds, establish a comprehensive hydrogen bond network that includes both intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds. This hydrogen bond network is crucial for the accessibility of cellulose hydroxyl groups, ultimately influencing the modification effects and various properties of the aerogel. Figure 2c illustrates the distinct hydrogen bond distributions of CNF-I and CNF-III (see Supplementary Fig. 4 for more details). Notably, the number of intrachain hydrogen bonds per chain in the CNF-I crystal core exceeds that in CNF-III, as indicated by the corresponding 6×5 grid portion of the distribution diagram. Although the number of intramolecular hydrogen bonds in the solvent-exposed chains is lower than that in the crystal core chains for both crystal forms, the value in CNF-I remains elevated, significantly reducing its hydroxyl accessibility16. In terms of interchain and intra-lamellar hydrogen bonds (corresponding to the top part of the distribution diagram), CNF-III displays fewer hydrogen bonds in both the crystal core and solvent-exposed chains, which enhances the interaction between the solvent and CNF-III. Conversely, the number of inter-chain hydrogen bonds in the interlayer, corresponding to the five layered parts, shows an opposing trend. Interlayer CNF-III possesses a greater number of O3–O5 inter-chain hydrogen bonds, which are advantageous for maintaining the fiber’s strength15,42. Numerous intramolecular O2–O6, O3–O6, and O3–O5 hydrogen bonds exist within the CNF-I sheet, where they contribute to maintaining the linear integrity and conformational rigidity of individual cellulose chains within the same fibril layer43. In contrast, CNF-III lacks O2–O6 intramolecular hydrogen bonds within the sheet. As a result, the C6 primary hydroxyl groups in CNF-III experience reduced intramolecular constraint, leading to increased conformational freedom and lower chain stiffness compared to CNF-I. Furthermore, the average number of inter-sheet (interlayer) hydrogen bonds between adjacent fibril layers in CNF-III exceeds that in CNF-I, which enhances interlayer cohesion and contributes to the improved mechanical strength of the aerogel.

According to the conformational energy order (gt <gg <tg), the primary hydroxyl groups in the gt conformation exhibit greater activity and freedom43. Statistical analyses of the torsion angles from molecular dynamics simulations demonstrate that CNF-III predominantly adopts the gt conformation (Supplementary Table 2 and 3)44. During the simulated thermodynamic process (Supplementary Movie 1), the fluctuations of the fibers in the CNF-III crystal core, both inter-sheet and intra-sheet, are more pronounced than those observed in CNF-I. This observation indicates that the molecular chain of CNF-III possesses enhanced structural flexibility, which facilitates the accessibility of hydroxyl groups16. To investigate the molecular-level reactivity differences between CNF-I and CNF-III, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to assess how the distinct conformations influence the electronic structure, thereby elucidating the reactivity variations resulting from crystal isomerization. Dimer theoretical models of CNF-I and CNF-III were constructed in the tg and gt conformations, respectively, encompassing all chemical components of the cellulose molecular chain, including the β-O-4 glycosidic bond45. The DFT results indicated that CNF-III exhibited a higher HOMO energy level (EHOMO (CNF-III) = −3.83 eV, EHOMO (CNF-I) = −4.0 eV) and a narrower band gap (Egap (CNF-III) = 3.35 eV, Egap (CNF-I) = 3.64 eV, Supplementary Fig. 5) compared to CNF-I. From an electronic structure perspective, the elevated HOMO energy level suggests that electrons in CNF-III can be more readily excited or engaged in nucleophilic reactions, reflecting its enhanced overall electron supply capacity46. Additionally, a smaller band gap signifies that the energy required for intramolecular electrons to transition from the HOMO to the LUMO is reduced, typically indicating a more chemically active electronic structure and suggesting that CNF-III possesses greater reactivity.

The evolution of crystal isomerization was further examined through experimental analysis. The XRD patterns observed at 11.8°, 17.0°, and 20.1° correspond to the diffraction peaks of type III cellulose at the (010), (002), and (100)/(012)/(11¯0) mixed crystal planes, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6a)47. This finding indicates that the aerogel lattice underwent reconstruction, resulting in a successful transformation of the crystal form with a conversion rate of 92.6% (Supplementary Fig. 6b). The solid 13C NMR spectrum revealed a significant splitting of the mixed single peak for C2/C3/C5 (68.6/71.3/67.7 ppm) of cellulose III (Supplementary Fig. 6c), which aligns with previous reports on the transformation from cellulose I to cellulose III.28 Furthermore, the crystal isomerization preserved a crystallinity of 45.8% (Supplementary Note 1), ensuring that the fiber retained a certain degree of stiffness. The TGA results indicate that the thermal degradation of CNF-III can be categorized into four distinct stages (Supplementary Fig. 7). The first stage, occurring below 100 °C, involves the removal of adsorbed and bound water from the material. The second stage pertains to the thermal behavior of residual components within the aerogel, where residual amines may exert an autocatalytic degradation effect on the amorphous regions of cellulose48. The third stage is associated with the decomposition of cellulose crystalline components. The final stage encompasses the carbonization process of the material49. In comparison to CNF-I, CNF-III exhibits a lower pyrolysis char rate following thermal degradation, likely due to its more porous crystal structure, which renders it more vulnerable to damage and degradation at elevated temperatures. This finding aligns with trends documented in the existing literature18. TG/DTG thermal stability analysis further indicated that the CNF-III molecular chain exhibits greater flexibility than CNF-I while maintaining high stability. The fitting of the hydroxyl characteristic peaks in FTIR analysis elucidates the distribution and intensity variations of intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds in cellulose. The fitting results indicate that CNF-Ⅰ and CNF-Ⅲ maintain identical compositions, as both exhibit intramolecular hydrogen bonds (O(2)H…O(6) and O(3)H…O(5)) and intermolecular hydrogen bonds (O(6)H…O(3′)). This consistency is attributed to the molecular chain stacking modes of CNF-Ⅰ and CNF-Ⅲ, with CNF-Ⅲ preserving the parallel arrangement characteristic of CNF-Ⅰ50. However, differences in the distribution and strength of these bonds are evident. Specifically, the proportion of weak intramolecular hydrogen bonds in CNF-Ⅲ increases, with O(2)H…O(6) rising from 7.5% to 10.6%. Conversely, the proportions of strong hydrogen bond components decrease, with O(3)H…O(5) and O(6)H…O(3′) declining by approximately 2.1% and 1.0%, respectively. This alteration may result from the introduction of ethylenediamine between the cellulose molecular chains, which increases interchain spacing and diminishes the highly ordered and geometrically constrained strong interchain hydrogen bonds in cellulose Ⅰ. The O(6)H···O(3′) bond induces a degree of relaxation in the conformation of glucose units, thereby reducing the stability of the medium-strength intrachain hydrogen bonds O(3)H···O(5)15. The characteristic peak shifted from 3353 cm−1 to 3361 cm−1, accompanied by a decrease in hydrogen bond energy of 4.12 kJ mol−1 (Supplementary Table 4), indicating a significant increase in the accessibility of hydroxyl groups51. Conversely, the strong intramolecular hydrogen bonds (O(3)H…O(5)) and intermolecular hydrogen bonds (O(6)H…O(3′)) in CNF-III exhibited notable blue shifts, with hydrogen bond energies decreasing by 5.59 kJ mol−1 and 1.73 kJ mol−1, respectively (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 8). The transformation from cellulose I to cellulose III is accompanied by a transition from a strong, ordered hydrogen bond network to a weaker network that possesses a greater number of bonds with lower energy, which is a significant aspect of the crystalline structure of cellulose III52. The analysis of hydroxyl characteristic peaks in the FTIR data reflects the types and relative intensities of intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Additionally, molecular dynamics simulation statistics provide insights into the number and spatial distribution of hydrogen bonds. Together, these analyses confirm from different perspectives that the crystal transformation induces changes in the hydrogen bond network.

The change in the chemical shift of C6 demonstrates that the conformation of the primary hydroxyl group on the glycoside unit transitions from tg (49.6%) and gg (38.2%) to gt conformation (78.4%) (Fig. 2f, g), corroborating previously reported conformational changes18. This transformation arises from the disruption of hydrogen bonds in CNF-III and the subsequent rearrangement of the molecular chain, which enhances the hydroxyl group’s degrees of freedom and reactivity, thereby facilitating the functional modification of cellulose. Furthermore, water contact angle experiments indicated that CNF-III aerogels completely absorbed a droplet in just 2 ms (Supplementary Movie 2), whereas CNF-I required approximately 16 s for the same process (Supplementary Movie 3). Static moisture absorption tests further indicated that, in a moderate 70% humidity environment, CNF-III aerogel achieved equilibrium within 30 minutes, exhibiting an equilibrium water absorption rate of 24%, in contrast to only 5.7% for CNF-I (Supplementary Fig. 9). The substantial increase in water absorption by CNF-III suggests a significantly enhanced accessibility of its hydroxyl groups, along with a more relaxed hydrogen bond network. This observation aligns well with the FTIR analysis of hydroxyl absorption peaks, the conformational changes identified through solid-state NMR, and the accelerated dynamic water absorption behavior noted in contact angle measurements. These pieces of evidence suggest that CNF-III aerogels possess a greater number of active hydroxyl groups at the surface and exhibit higher hydroxyl accessibility compared to CNF-I aerogels. By integrating the alterations in the characteristic infrared peaks of hydroxyl groups, the chemical shift variations of C6 in solid-state NMR, the contact angle measurements, and static moisture absorption tests, we validated the molecular dynamics simulations concerning hydrogen bond distribution and conformational transitions, as well as the DFT analysis related to reactivity changes. Additionally, our findings indicate that CNF-III aerogel presents a greater number of surface-active hydroxyl groups, with enhanced accessibility compared to CNF-I aerogel. This characteristic will promote more efficient functionalization of CNF-III aerogel while preserving its high strength.

In situ polymerization and cross-linking to construct topological networks

The elasticity of cellulose aerogels is primarily attributed to the shielding effect of hydrogen bonds among the fibers. Previous studies indicate that chemical modification using silanes or petroleum-based polymers can effectively shield these hydrogen bonds, thereby improving elastic recovery12,53. In this context, uniform functionalization of the fiber surfaces is essential, as it directly influences the effectiveness of hydrogen bond shielding and ultimately impacts the material’s elastic properties. Leveraging the high hydroxyl accessibility of type III cellulose aerogels, a uniform polymer topological network can be established through vapor-phase-mediated in situ interfacial polymerization (Fig. 3a). At 80 °C, vapor-phase methyltrimethoxysilane (MTMS) and water molecules diffuse onto the pore wall fibers, where they undergo in situ polymerization and crosslinking, resulting in a dense cross-linked network. Annealing produces a lightweight porous CNF-III@Si aerogel with a density of only 12.1 mg cm−3 (Fig. 3b). We propose a molecular topological network construction mechanism based on two distinct molecular arrangements and hydroxyl accessibility, as illustrated in Fig. 3c. While the deposition of polymethylsiloxane on the pore walls of CNF-I@Si may be discontinuous and localized, CNF-III@Si aerogels demonstrate a dense polymer topological network that encompasses both the pore walls and junctions. Variations in the water contact angle provide indirect confirmation of these differences (Supplementary Fig. 10). The low hydroxyl accessibility of CNF-I hinders the formation of a continuous cross-linked network on its surface; in contrast, the high hydroxyl accessibility of cellulose III facilitates the hydrolysis and in situ polymerization of MTMS on its surface, resulting in a stable and dense hydrophobic Si(CH3)-O-Si(CH3) topological network (Fig. 3d), which effectively shields the hydrogen bonds between fibers.

As shown in Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 11, both CNF-Ⅰ@Si and CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogels possess smooth pore walls. However, a comparison of the silicon element distribution via EDS indicates that the pore walls of the CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogel are enveloped by a more uniform and denser polymer network. This is beneficial for shielding the hydrogen bonds between fibers. The two-dimensional small-angle X-ray scattering (2D-SAXS) pattern of CNF-Ⅲ@Si exhibits pronounced anisotropic scattering features, accompanied by stripe-like scattering that is elongated in a specific direction (Supplementary Fig. 12a). This observation suggests that the aerogel preserves the orientation structure induced by ice crystal growth during directional freezing on a macroscopic scale, as evidenced by a Herman’s orientation factor of −0.394 (Supplementary Fig. 12b). FTIR spectroscopy indicates that CNF-III@Si displays pronounced characteristic peaks at 1258 cm−1, 702 cm−1, and 658 cm−1, which correspond to the stretching vibrations of Si-CH, Si-C, and Si-O, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13a)54. This observation suggests that CNF-III forms a stable covalent bond with Si(CH3)-O-Si(CH3), rather than merely a physical association. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results demonstrate that, under identical reaction conditions, the cross-linked polysiloxane in the CNF-III aerogel exhibits nearly double the hydroxyl accessibility compared to that of the CNF-I aerogel (Fig. 3f). The C1s spectrum of CNF-III@Si can be deconvoluted to reveal a C-Si bond at 281.9 eV, with a peak area significantly larger than that of CNF-I@Si (Supplementary Figs. 13b, c and 14a). Furthermore, curve fitting of the Si2p peak indicates that 99.2 eV and 99.9 eV correspond to Si-O bonds and Si-O-Si bonds, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 13d and 14b). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was used to determine the silicon content with precision. The findings revealed that the absolute silicon content in CNF-I@Si and CNF-III@Si aerogels was 0.82% and 1.45%, respectively, indicating that the latter was 1.76 times greater than the former (Supplementary Table 5). This quantitative data directly suggests that CNF-III experienced a more thorough silanization reaction. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that enhanced hydroxyl accessibility facilitates the molecular network reconstruction process in CNF-III@Si, resulting in a denser and more uniform polymer topology.

Topological networks shield hydrogen bonds to improve mechanical properties

As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the CNF-I aerogel exhibited significant plastic deformation following the initial compression, achieving a maximum compressive stress of 81.6 kPa. Finite element simulations indicated that stress is primarily concentrated at the junctions of the honeycomb pore walls (Fig. 4b). The compression process involves both the close contact and separation of the fibers within the pore walls. Consequently, the structural integrity of these connections is essential for the mechanical properties of the aerogel. However, the presence of strong hydrogen bonds among the fibers in the pore walls of pure cellulose aerogels leads to tight adhesion post-compression, which hinders structural recovery and results in a lack of rebound resilience (Fig. 4c). Topologically isomerized CNF-III@Si aerogels exhibit remarkable strength and superelasticity. The compressive strength of these aerogels reaches 180.6 kPa, which is significantly greater than that of CNF-I@Si aerogels, measured at 135.7 kPa (Supplementary Fig. 15a). During the initial 500 cycles of compression, CNF-Ⅰ@Si aerogel demonstrated considerable plastic deformation, whereas CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogel exhibited less than 2% plastic deformation (Supplementary Fig. 15b–d). After 20,000 cycles of compression at 50% strain, the maximum strength of CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogel was maintained at 108.5 kPa, with its geometric height and shape remaining nearly unchanged (Fig. 4d). The observed decrease in strength is likely attributable to the accumulation of damage at the microstructural level, including skeletal bending, node rearrangement, or progressive interface fatigue, rather than an overall structural collapse. Moreover, even after numerous cycles, the aerogel maintains a high modulus of 2.0 MPa and a low energy dissipation coefficient of 0.464 (Fig. 4e), underscoring its exceptional strength, superelasticity, and fatigue resistance. This performance can be attributed to the inherent strength of the CNF-III fibers and the uniformly coated polymer topological network on their surface, which effectively shields the hydrogen bonds between the fibers, enabling them to separate even under close contact (Fig. 4f).

The microstructure and surface polymer network distribution of CNF-III@Si aerogels before and after cyclic compression exhibited negligible changes, with elements remaining uniformly distributed (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). This observation indicates that the structure remained intact and did not delaminate. The average pore diameter decreased by approximately 7.5 μm before and after cyclic compression, with no significant change in pore distribution, indicating that the aerogel’s pore structure maintained substantial integrity (Supplementary Fig. 18). Consequently, the CNF-III@Si aerogel achieved an elastic recovery rate is 95% (Fig. 4g), significantly surpassing that of pure CNF and CNF-I@Si aerogel. Furthermore, the stable polymer topology and porous structure on the surface of the CNF-III@Si aerogel contribute to its excellent thermal insulation properties, evidenced by low thermal conductivity in both axial and radial directions at 10% relative humidity (The axial and radial thermal conductivities are 0.0320 and 0.0328 W·m−¹·K−¹, respectively. Supplementary Fig. 19). Axial heat transfer within the aerogel is affected by the directional pore channels created during the process of directional freezing. However, the extremely high porosity and discontinuous solid skeleton introduce significant interfacial thermal resistance, which inhibits the establishment of efficient heat conduction pathways. In the radial direction, although heat flow frequently intersects with pore walls and fiber connections, the low solid volume fraction and numerous gas-solid interfaces limit effective phonon transport. Therefore, bidirectional heat transfer is primarily governed by gas-phase conduction and interfacial scattering, rather than a discontinuous solid-phase thermal path, resulting in comparable axial and radial thermal conductivity values. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicates that the thermal degradation temperature of CNF-Ⅲ@Si reaches 290 °C (Supplementary Fig. 20). This enhanced thermal stability arises from the cross-linking of hydroxyl groups on the cellulose surface with the topological polymer network, which forms stable Si–O–C and Si–O–Si networks on the pore walls. The high bond energy and excellent thermal stability of the Si–O bonds effectively suppress chain breakage and volatilization during heating. Furthermore, the Si-O network serves as a protective barrier, impeding heat and mass transfer while promoting the formation of a silicon-rich carbon layer at elevated temperatures. This process further delays thermal degradation and contributes to the improved char yield observed in the TGA results. The compression process exhibits a near-zero Poisson’s ratio (Supplementary Fig. 21), highlighting significant advantages in structural deformation. Given these exceptional thermal insulation and mechanical properties, CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogel retains compressibility even under extreme low-temperature (−196 °C) and high-temperature (200 °C) conditions (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Movie 4). Additionally, CNF-III@Si aerogel demonstrates an impressive recovery rate of approximately 1258 mm s−1, which exceeds that of most previously reported aerogel materials (Fig. 4h)54–60. As shown in Fig. 4i, CNF-III@Si aerogel can endure the free fall of a steel ball weighing over 185 times its own weight from a height of 20 cm, rebounding within 20 ms (Supplementary Movie 5), thereby demonstrating exceptional impact resistance. Its excellent thermal insulation properties, high strength, and superelasticity confer significant advantages for triboelectric applications, establishing a more reliable material platform.

Elastic triboelectric aerogel-based on topological isomerization

The mechanical stability and rapid recovery characteristics of triboelectric aerogels are essential for their output performance and signal response. Previously reported cellulose triboelectric aerogels frequently do not satisfy the durability requirements of triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) during continuous contact-separation operation modes, primarily due to inadequate elasticity or strength61. Consequently, high elasticity and rapid recovery are critical for ensuring the long-term stable output of TENGs. Figure 5a depicts the operating principle of a TENG in contact-separation mode. In this configuration, perfluoroethylene propylene copolymer (FEP) functions as the negative triboelectric layer, CNF-III@Si aerogel serves as the positive triboelectric layer, and copper conductive adhesive acts as the electrode. During cyclic operation, FEP first contacts the aerogel, leading to surface charge transfer. Gradual compression then induces structural deformation, resulting in the generation of additional electrons within the structure, which in turn induces opposite charges at the electrodes, thereby producing output (Fig. 5b)62. To investigate charge generation and transfer at the atomic level, DFT calculations were used to analyze the potential distribution, band gap, and Fukui function of a simplified CNF-Ⅲ@Si molecular model. Balancing computational resource constraints with the necessity for atomic-level insights into core interactions, this study examined three structural models of the aerogel interior (Fig. 5c): ungrafted CNF-Ⅲ molecular chains represented as cellulose tetramer; partially grafted CNF-Ⅲ molecules depicted as cellulose tetramer with one -Si(OH)₂CH₃ group (CNF-Ⅲ@Si-1); and a cellulose chain cross-linked polymer topology network in which all C6 segments of the dextran tetramer were grafted with siloxane (CNF-Ⅲ@Si-2). Although these simplified models may diverge from reality, they are designed to elucidate the local electronic structure of the bonds between silanes and the cellulose surface, as well as the fundamental trends in charge transfer. This approach provides valuable insights into the potential distribution, charge generation, and transfer of CNF-Ⅲ@Si. The electrostatic potential distribution indicates that CNF-Ⅲ, CNF-Ⅲ@Si-1, and CNF-Ⅲ@Si-2 all exhibit favorable electropositive properties (Fig. 5d), rendering them suitable candidates for triboelectric materials. DFT calculations indicate that the hydroxyl groups of CNF-III and the unsubstituted hydroxyl groups (C2-OH and C3-OH) in CNF-III@Si exhibit significant HOMO and Fukui electrophilicity, with HOMO energy levels of −3.55 eV, −5.16 eV, and −5.54 eV, respectively. When the CNF-III@Si aerogel contacts FEP (ELUMO = −0.33 eV), the hydroxyl groups function as electron-donating groups, working in conjunction with the strongly electron-withdrawing fluorine atoms to facilitate charge generation and transfer (Fig. 5e).

Output voltage at different humidity levels (h) and temperatures (i).

The strong electron-donating ability and electropositive nature of CNF-Ⅲ@Si contribute to the exceptional triboelectric output performance of TENGs. Supplementary Fig. 23 illustrates a comparison of the triboelectric output performance between CNF-I@Si and CNF-III@Si aerogels. At 25 °C, the surface charge density, output current, and voltage of the TENG based on the CNF-III@Si aerogel surpass those of the CNF-I@Si variant. Conversely, at elevated temperatures of 200 °C, the output voltage of the CNF-I@Si aerogel-based TENG experiences a significant decline, whereas the output voltage of the CNF-III@Si aerogel-based TENG remains stable at 41.6 V. The mechanical nonlinear behavior of porous elastomer materials results in a sensing hysteresis phenomenon in the signal curve. Further testing assessed the output performance under varying compressive strengths. The output voltage increased with compressive strength, beginning at a lower limit of 0.05 kPa, which corresponded to a voltage of 2.4 V. The TENG exhibited high stress responsiveness (Fig. 5f). When subjected to an external force of 70 kPa, the output voltage of the TENG reached 81.5 V, with response and recovery times of 71 ms and 69 ms, respectively (Fig. 5g). The output voltage remains constant within the 0.5–2.5 Hz range (Supplementary Fig. 24). The triboelectric aerogel demonstrated rapid response characteristics. Stability is a critical indicator for assessing sensor performance and application potential. The output voltage of the TENG remained stable at a frequency of 2.0 Hz and a compressive stress of 70 kPa. After 12,000 cycles, the output signal change was less than 0.5%, which is adequate for the application requirements of most daily human activities (Supplementary Fig. 25). Further investigation revealed that the CNF-III@Si aerogel-based TENG delivered a maximum output power density of 310 mW m−², nearly twice that of the CNF-I@Si aerogel (160 mW m−², Supplementary Fig. 26). It could charge a 0.1 μF capacitor to 12 V within 120 s, compared with only 2.1 V for the CNF-I@Si counterpart (Supplementary Fig. 26), demonstrating effective self-powered energy storage without external power input. The aerogel maintained stable output performance across a range of temperatures, including 25 °C, 50 °C, 75 °C, 100 °C, 150 °C, and 200 °C (Supplementary Fig. 27). Additionally, the output signal amplitude remained consistent across a broad humidity range of 58% to 98% (Fig. 5h). This stability can be attributed to the hydrophobic properties of the CNF-III@Si surface, which inhibits the formation of conductive pathways by water vapor on or within the aerogel, thereby preventing charge dissipation. Notably, even at 200 °C, the output voltage remained as high as 42 V (Fig. 5i). This resilience is primarily due to the exceptional thermal insulation properties of the aerogel, which effectively mitigates the dissipation of surface charge induced by elevated temperatures. The outstanding sensing performance of the TENG based on CNF-III@Si aerogel underscores its potential for widespread application.

Discussion

This paper presents a topological isomerization strategy for the preparation of cellulosic triboelectric aerogels characterized by both superelasticity and high strength. The mature amine treatment process for cellulose facilitated the reconstruction of the hydrogen bond network, thereby enhancing the accessibility of hydroxyl groups in the cellulose aerogel while preserving the stiffness of the fibers. Subsequent in situ polymerization and cross-linking on the fiber surface create a polymer topological network that effectively prevents adhesion between fibers. This approach successfully preserves fiber strength while mitigating hydrogen bond interactions among fibers, thereby addressing the inherent conflict between elasticity and strength. Furthermore, topological isomerization imparts a stable structure and consistent electron donation capacity to the aerogel, enhancing the stability of its triboelectric output performance. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that superelastic and high-strength triboelectric aerogels can be synthesized through topological isomerization.

Methods

Materials

A 1.2 wt.% aqueous suspension of cellulose nanofibers (CNF, length 1–3 μm, diameter 4–10 nm) was purchased from Guilin Qihong Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Sodium chloride (NaCl), anhydrous ethylenediamine (EDA, 99.9%) and methyltrimethoxysilane (MTMS, ≥ 99%) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd. (China). All reagents were commercially available and used without further purification.

Preparation of CNF-Ⅰ Aerogels

To prepare the CNF-I aerogel, a 1.2 wt.% CNF aqueous suspension was diluted to 0.5 wt.% by adding deionized water. The diluted suspension was thoroughly mixed and reserved for subsequent use. The mixture was poured into a polytetrafluoroethylene mold (2 × 2 × 2 cm3) with a copper base and subjected to directional freezing in liquid nitrogen. The pre-frozen sample was freeze-dried using a SCIENTZ-10N freeze dryer (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) at -70 °C and 1.0 Pa for 48 h, yielding the final CNF-I aerogel. The volume of the freeze-dried aerogel is approximately 99.5% of that of the original wet gel, primarily attributable to radial shrinkage, which measures approximately 1.995 × 1.995 × 2 cm³. Given the strong hydrophilicity of CNF-III, all dried samples were stored in a desiccator to prevent re-humidification.

Preparation of CNF-Ⅲ aerogels

CNF-III aerogel was synthesized by treating CNF I aerogel with EDA. The preparation process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 28. CNF I aerogel was placed in a sealed glass container with 500 mL of EDA, fully submerged using a mesh, and treated at room temperature for 12 h. Subsequently, it was washed by immersion in 200 mL of anhydrous ethanol, and replaced with fresh ethanol at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, and 8 h, respectively, until the conductivity stabilized within 2 h. The aerogel was then air-dried at room temperature to a constant weight, and the resulting CNF III aerogel was sealed and stored in a desiccator for future use. The treated EDA can be recovered through vacuum distillation.

Preparation of CNF-Ⅰ@Si and CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogels

The vapor-phase deposition setup is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 29. Before the reaction, the air inside the reactor was purged three times with dry nitrogen. The aerogel was positioned within a sealed hydrothermal reactor with a volume of 350 mL. At the bottom of the reactor, two beakers were situated, one containing 1.0 mL of deionized water and the other 1.0 mL of MTMS. The aerogel was placed on a grid, and the sealed reactor was subsequently placed in an 80 °C oven for 12 h to prepare CNF-I@Si and CNF-Ⅲ@Si aerogels.

Fabrication of the triboelectric nanogenerator

An aerogel block was selected as the positive triboelectric material. The effective contact area of the aerogel is approximately 4 cm², and its thickness is about 0.5 cm. A fluorinated ethylene propylene copolymer (FEP) film with identical cross-sectional dimensions (4 cm²) was employed as the negative triboelectric material. Electrical connections were established using double-sided copper electrode adhesive serving as the conductive interface.

Characterizations

Solid state 13C NMR was conducted using a Bruker Avance Ⅲ HD 400 spectrometer. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed on a Bruker AXS diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). The IR measurements were performed on a PerkinElmer FTIR Spectrometer Frontier (Nicolet iS 10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For calculations of hydrogen bond energies and bond lengths, see Supplementary Note 5. The silicon (Si) content was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 720ES), with operating conditions of 1.2 kW RF power and a 15.0 L/min plasma gas flow rate. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA 8000 Perkin Elmer, USA) under a 100 mL/min nitrogen flow with a temperature ramp from 25 to 600 °C at 10 °C/min. The thermal conductivity was evaluated by Hot Disk instrument (TPS2500S, Switzerland). The surface morphology and elemental distribution of the aerogels were recorded using a scanning electron microscope (Thermo Scientific Apreo 2 C) with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (OXFORD ULTIM Max65). The surface chemical composition of the aerogels was recorded using XPS (ESCA Lab 250Xi, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). N2 sorption-desorption isotherms on aerogels were measured using a surface area analyzer (ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, USA), and the specific surface area was determined using Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. Average porosity and pore size distributions were measured using mercury porosimetry technique (AutoPore V 9520, USA) over a pressure range of 0.01 to 440 MPa. The water contact angle of aerogels was measured using a contact angle meter (DSA35, Kruss, Germany). A 3 µL drop of deionized water was placed on the aerogel surface using a micro syringe.

Mechanical testing

The mechanical properties of the aerogel were determined by conducting a compression test using a precision texture analyzer (TMS-Pro, USA). The aerogel was compressed with a pressure sensor of 500 N at a displacement rate of 25 mm min−1 until the compression strain reached 50%. To investigate the impact resistance of the aerogel, a high-speed camera (Photron Mini AX100, Japan) was employed to observe and analyze the process of a steel ball dropping, striking the aerogel, and subsequent rebound.

Moisture absorption test

CNF-I and CNF-III aerogels of identical dimensions were pre-baked at 105 °C until the weight change fell below 0.02 g. Subsequently, these aerogels were placed in a desiccator containing a saturated NaCl solution, which corresponds to a relative humidity of 70% ± 5% at 25 °C. The weight was measured at regular intervals until it stabilized. The static moisture absorption (SMA) rate was calculated based on the mass change, expressed as: SMA% = (mt – m0)/m0 × 100%, where mt denotes the weight at a specific time interval, and m0 represents the initial dry weight.

Triboelectric output performance

To conduct standardized measurements of electrical output, a TENG was actuated by a linear motor (LinMot E1100, Switzerland) in contact-separation mode to assess the output signal. The linear motor control unit moved a consistent distance and applied uniform pressure at a reciprocating frequency of 2.0 Hz, except when varying output signals at different frequencies was necessary. The acceleration was maintained at 1.0 m/s², with a maximum speed of 3.0 m/s, while the contact pressure was monitored using a pressure sensor (HY Chuangan HYPS017Z, China). This setup enabled precise control over the contact and separation of the two materials. The generated electrical signals were recorded by an electrometer (Keithley 6514, USA) and a data acquisition card (NI-USB6259, USA), subsequently transmitted via cable to a computer for the measurement of open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current, and transferred charge. The final data were processed using computer software for display and recording. The test conditions for temperature and humidity were maintained at 25 °C and 65% RH, unless specific requirements dictated otherwise (different humidity or temperature).

Output signals at various frequencies were obtained by adjusting the speed of the linear motor, while maintaining constant conditions. The triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) was secured to a heating plate using aerogel, and the output signal was recorded at different temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 30). To measure the output signal under varying humidity levels, the TENG was positioned within a custom-designed humidity-adjustable sealed chamber (Supplementary Fig. 31).

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted on CNF-I and CNF-Ⅲ to characterize structural and dynamical features of both allomorphs. The models comprised 30 (6 × 5) octameric glucan chains for each cellulose polymorph, with crystallographic parameters obtained from literature and subsequently optimized14,63. Based on the consensus on the minimum limit of cellulose chains in basic cellulose fibers14,63, 30 octamer cellulose chains were selected as a reference model. The refined parameters are documented in Supplementary Table S6, S7. All simulations utilized the CVFF force field within the LAMMPS software package under NPT conditions. Temperature and pressure were regulated by a Langevin thermostat (damping coefficient = 1.0 ps−1) and a Nosé-Hoover-Langevin barostat with stochastic components (oscillation period = 200 fs, damping period = 100 fs), respectively. Nonbonded interactions employed a 10.0 Å cutoff in coordinate space, with periodic boundary conditions applied throughout.

The cellulose fibrils were immersed in a rectangular water box. To model an infinitely long fibril, each octameric cellulose chain was covalently linked to its periodic image along the fiber axis. The solvated system was first subjected to local energy minimization, followed by a short NPT-MD simulation (~0.5 ns) during which the temperature was gradually raised from 100 K to 298 K. Subsequently, a 200-ns NPT-MD production simulation was performed at 298 K and 1.01325 bar. The first 20 ns of this run were treated as equilibration, and the remaining 180 ns were used for data analysis. A timestep of 2.0 fs was used, and covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm. See the Supplementary Note 2 for more details.

Density functional theory calculations

All of the density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out with Materials Studio DMol3 program from Accelrys64,65. All initial structures were fully geometry-optimized prior to electronic structure analysis to obtain energetically stable conformations. Geometry optimizations were performed using the hybrid exchange–correlation functional B3LYP in combination with a double numerical basis set with polarization functions (DNP). The geometry optimizations were considered converged when the total energy, maximum force, and maximum displacement reached 1.0 × 10−⁵ Ha, 0.002 Ha Å−¹, and 0.005 Å, respectively. The self-consistent field convergence threshold was set to 1.0 × 10−⁵ Ha. It is important to note that these models may not accurately represent real reactivity or actual charge transfer processes. However, they offer valuable insights into the changes in reactivity at the molecular level and elucidate how electrons are generated and transferred in aerogels during contact separation. See the Supplementary Note 4 for more details.

Finite element simulation

To illustrate the stress distribution during the compression of honeycomb aerogels and to analyze their structural stability, finite element simulations of this physical process were performed using COMSOL Multiphysics software. See the Supplementary Note 3 for more details.
