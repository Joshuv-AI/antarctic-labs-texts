Awareness and willingness to participate in drug clinical trials among college students: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Li Wang, Jingxuan Liu, Ruiguang Zhang, Yangfang Tang, Fu Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drug clinical trials (DCTs), conducted in medical institutions before new drugs are registered and marketed, play a vital role. Well-informed participants are crucial to the successful execution and reliable outcomes of these trials. Consequently, raising awareness of clinical trials among college students is critical for advancing China’s clinical research landscape.College students from the northwestern, central and northern regions of China, who met the eligibility criteria, were selected through convenience sampling for a cross-sectional survey conducted from January to March 2022. The questionnaire comprised three sections: general information, awareness of DCTs, and willingness to participate. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0.A total of 814 students participated in our study, with 444 students (54.5%) assessed as having sufficient awareness of DCTs. Statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed between participants with adequate and inadequate awareness in terms of age, gender, education level, educational background, household type, city type, medical expenditure, healthcare pressure, and financial pressure. However, a negative correlation was found between awareness of DCTs and willingness to participate. Logistic regression indicated that educational attainment and background were key predictors: whereas higher education levels correlated with increased awareness, a medical background specifically predicted greater willingness to participate. The generalizability of these results is limited by the study’s sampling approach and the non-national scope of its college student cohort.The findings reveal a need to improve both awareness and willingness to participate in DCTs among college students. To address this, student education and outreach efforts should be strengthened - particularly through innovative approaches such as engaging research professionals to disseminate trial information via new media platforms. Clear, detailed communication of trial processes can help enhance student understanding while addressing common concerns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27298-0.

Introduction

Worldwide, there is increasing emphasis on developing novel drugs [1]. Drug clinical trials (DCTs), conducted in medical institutions prior to new drug registration and market approval, play a pivotal role in this process. These DCTs span Phase I through IV and involve both healthy volunteers and patient populations. By providing additional treatment options, DCTs are particularly valuable for individuals who lack effective therapeutic alternatives [2–4].

Nonetheless, significant challenges remain, such as difficulties in participant recruitment, insufficient enrollment, high dropout rates, and prolonged trial durations. The most critical challenge throughout the clinical trial process lies in effectively identifying, recruiting, enrolling, and retaining participants to ensure trial completion [5]. Globally, reports indicate that fewer than 5% of cancer patients participate in DCTs, and only about half of these trials achieve their target sample sizes [6, 7].

College students represent a key demographic for clinical trial recruitment. Compared to the general population, they generally possess higher educational levels and scientific literacy, making them both an ideal target group and an important focus in medical ethics considerations. The purpose of this study is to assess college students’ awareness of DCTs, identify influencing factors, and evaluate their willingness to participate. Based on the findings, corresponding educational and promotional measures will be proposed to address misconceptions and alleviate concerns, thereby fostering greater active participation and facilitating the wider promotion of DCTs among this demographic.

Study enrollment and data collection

As outlined in Professor Yan Yan’s Medical Statistics [8], the recommended sample size should be 5–10 times the number of independent variables. Based on this guideline, an initial sample size of 290 was calculated. To account for potential errors and improve data reliability, the sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in a final minimum requirement of 319 participants. This cross-sectional study employed convenience and snowball sampling to recruit college students across Northwestern, Central, and Northern China between January and March 2022. Potential participants were initially invited through university-affiliated online channels (e.g., student WeChat groups and campus bulletin board systems). Subsequently, a snowball sampling approach was employed, where enrolled students were encouraged to share the survey link with their peers. The non-random sampling method and regional scope limited the representativeness of the sample, as explicitly noted in its limitations.

The study enrolled eligible participants based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, all of whom provided informed consent after being fully informed of the study purpose. The questionnaire was distributed online. To prevent missing data, the system required respondents to complete all items before submission, and all responses were subsequently verified on-site. To achieve the target sample size, the survey was distributed multiple times and used a snowball approach, where participants shared it through their online networks. This strategy improved participation rates and expanded the dissemination scope. Data quality was strengthened by using appropriate measurement tools, standardized researcher guidelines, and a thorough data review process.

Questionnaire design and assessment

This research initiative assembled a team of 5 investigators dedicated to questionnaire development, comprising 2 individuals with senior professional designations and 3 with intermediate professional affiliations. Their primary duties encompassed literature review and analysis, determination of questionnaire items, data collection and statistical analysis, questionnaire refinement, assessment of questionnaire reliability and validity, and ultimately, questionnaire finalization.

The questionnaire items were carefully phrased to be neutral and non-leading, avoiding language that might suggest a “correct” or “desirable” answer. For example, instead of “Do you agree that clinical trials are beneficial?“, we asked about specific perceptions and knowledge.

Designed by researchers, the general demographic information includes 13 items: age, gender, education level, educational background(specifically whether medical-related), household type, marital status, city type, income, medical insurance, medical expenditure, pressure to seek medical care, and financial pressure.

This questionnaire is a self-assessment scale developed by Li Wang [9], consisting of 29 items across 2 dimensions. The first dimension includes 20 items assessing knowledge about DCTs, such as trial processes and grouping methods. Each item is rated on a three-level scale: “yes” (2 points), “no” (1 point), and “unclear” (0 points). All items are scored positively, with a maximum total score of 40 points. A score of 24 points or higher is deemed qualified, and the higher score indicating a higher level of awareness about DCTs. (A cut-off score of 24 points (60% of the maximum score of 40) was used to define “qualified awareness”. This threshold was selected based on a commonly applied criterion in educational and psychological testing for distinguishing adequate from inadequate knowledge, and it aligns with the methodology employed in the prior validation study using this scale [9, 10]). The second dimension comprises 9 items evaluating willingness to participate in clinical trials, including willingness and motivation for participation.

Specifically, ' pressure to seek medical care ' was defined for participants as ‘the perceived level of worry or burden associated with potential or actual healthcare needs and costs,’ while ' financial pressure ' was defined as ‘the perceived level of financial strain in meeting everyday expenses.’

The scale was rigorously re-validated for use with the college student population in this study. Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.973), and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) coefficient was 0.975, with both values approximating 1.

Results

General information

The study comprised 814 college students, with a gender distribution of 477 females (58.6%) and 337 males (41.4%). Education levels included: 390 participants (47.9%) with associate degrees or below, 388 undergraduates (47.7%), and 36 graduate students or above (4.4%). Medical-related educational backgrounds were reported by 328 participants (40.3%), while 486 (59.7%) came from non-medical fields. Regarding residential status, 611 participants (75.1%) held rural household registration compared to 203 urban registrants (24.9%). The majority of students (53.2%, n = 433) were covered by the urban-rural resident medical insurance scheme. Healthcare expenditure patterns revealed that 570 participants (70.0%) reported negligible medical costs, with an additional 221 (27.1%) reporting low expenses. Overall, participants reported relatively low levels of medical and financial pressure. Complete demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Analysis of awareness of DCTs

The study showed that among 814 college students surveyed, 444 (54.5%) demonstrated adequate knowledge of DCTs, who distributed across different educational levels as follows: 141 had associate or below education, 272 held undergraduate education, and 31 had graduate or above education (Fig. 1), while 370 (45.5%) showed insufficient understanding. A majority of participants (n = 561) reported some prior awareness of DCTs, with 531 students (65.2%) correctly identifying DCTs as an essential stage in new drug development. However, 253 respondents remained unfamiliar with this concept.

The basic knowledge assessment highlighted significant gaps in understanding, with nearly half of participants unaware of fundamental aspects such as study design and objectives. In contrast, students displayed stronger comprehension of informed consent procedures, personal rights protection, and privacy safeguards, reflecting contemporary college students’ heightened awareness of individual rights and self-protection.

Analysis of willingness to participate in DCTs

The survey of 814 college students revealed limited prior engagement with DCTs, with only 75 respondents (9.3%) reporting participation experience. However, 342 students (42.1%) expressed willingness to participate in future trials. Regarding trial advocacy, 281 participants (34.5%) indicated they would recommend DCTs participation to acquaintances, while a substantial majority (n = 623, 76.5%) demonstrated interest in receiving DCT-related information. Notably, 489 respondents (60.3%) expressed willingness to contribute to public awareness initiatives about DCTs (Fig. 2).

Among the 75 college students who had participated in DCTs, 49 (65.3%) had an associate degree or lower, while 26 (34.7%) had a bachelor’s degree or higher. The survey identified several key factors influencing willingness to participate: contributing to medical progress (57.2%), access to updated treatment methods (45.7%), economic incentives (44.1%), access to more convenient healthcare (40.2%), and trust in physicians (32.9%). Conversely, reasons for refusal included: concerns about trial risks (70.3%), insufficient understanding of DCTs (61.2%), worries about personal privacy and information leakage (36.5%), reluctance to be treated as a “guinea pig” (40.3%), and unwillingness to invest additional time and energy (26.0%). Discussion

Clinical trials represent a critical phase in evaluating the safety and efficacy of new drugs, serving as an essential bridge between new drug research & development and clinical application. Through systematic data collection and analysis, researchers establish the therapeutic profile of new drugs, which is an important criterion for evaluating the approval and marketing of new drugs [11]. Public awareness and participation willingness in clinical trials significantly influence new drug research & development [12, 13]. As a key demographic for trial recruitment, college students play a vital role in ensuring successful participant enrollment due to their unique characteristics as potential trial participants.

Our analysis identified a foundational gap in awareness: only 54.5% of college students demonstrated adequate understanding of DCTs, which, while still limited, was significantly higher than the 29.5% awareness level reported among the general public in a prior study by Luo et al. [14]. Educational level was a significant predictor of awareness, compared to respondents with an associate degree or below, undergraduate students showed 2.64-fold greater awareness (95% CI: 1.00-6.97), and graduate students demonstrated markedly 10.9-fold higher awareness (95% CI: 4.16–28.79).

Furthermore, medical education background was the strongest predictor of awareness, with medically-trained participants demonstrating a 4.65-fold higher awareness (95% CI: 3.22–6.72) compared to their non-medical counterparts. This heightened awareness likely stems from their specialized curriculum and greater academic exposure to clinical trial information, underscoring the need for tailored educational outreach to non-medical groups.

Our findings revealed a clear positive correlation between educational attainment and awareness of DCTs, yet a negative correlation between awareness and actual participation rates. The relationship between awareness and willingness to participate, however, was more complex and culturally nuanced. While higher education predicted greater awareness, it did not correspond to a higher intent to enroll—a contrast to observations in Western settings such as Canada, where education level correlates positively with both awareness and participation intent [15].

This divergence suggests that awareness operates differently in decision-making across cultural contexts. In settings with historically high trust in biomedical research, awareness may reinforce confidence to volunteer. In our study, however, heightened awareness among Chinese college students—particularly those from non-medical backgrounds newly introduced to DCTs—appeared to amplify their perception of potential risks (e.g., safety, data privacy) and ethical considerations (e.g., the role of financial compensation), leading to more cautious deliberation.

Our findings align with those of Walsh et al. [16] in Ireland, which identified altruism and healthcare access as primary motivators. Research by Luo An et al. [14] further indicates that among the general public—especially potential patients—decision-making largely depends on trust in physicians and medical institutions. In contrast, the college students in this study, as potential healthy volunteers, based their decisions more on a calculated assessment of factors such as financial compensation, personal health benefits, and time costs, reflecting greater individual autonomy. This distinction necessitates a shift in recruitment communication strategies for student populations. Messaging should move away from relying on authoritative endorsements and instead emphasize transparent risk–benefit explanations, clear information on safeguards, and rational appeals that address their specific considerations.

Notably, only 34.7% of students with participation experience held undergraduate degrees or higher, a pattern consistent with Li Meixin’s earlier observations [17]. This contrasts markedly with findings from Canadian studies, where educational background positively correlated with both awareness and willingness to participate [15]. We hypothesize that in the Chinese context, more educated students demonstrate greater health literacy and autonomy in medical decision-making. Their heightened awareness of patient rights may lead to more selective participation, as they carefully weigh the potential risks and benefits of clinical trial involvement. Therefore, we recommend institutional policies to integrate trial education into university programs and enhance ethical safeguards.

This study reveals a significant relationship between household type, city type, and awareness of DCTs (P < 0.05). Awareness of DCTs is lower in Fourth-tier city and below cities compared to first-tier city. This discrepancy may be attributed to factors such as a lack of medical resources, fewer large-scale hospitals and limited clinical trial sites [18–20].

Statistical differences were also observed in medical expenditure, pressure to seek medical care and financial pressure. In this study, the subjects were college students, most of whom did not have a fixed monthly income, resulting in a right-skewed distribution. Specifically, 93% of college students had a monthly income ranging from 0 to 2000. Therefore, drug clinical trials are particularly attractive to college students due to the provision of free medications and treatments. Previous studies in China have also found that economic factors may significantly influence students’ participation in DCTs [21]. A study conducted in the United States surveyed 610 individuals from Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) and those involved in research oversight [22]. The results indicated that 61% of respondents were concerned that the financial incentives, non-monetary incentives, and medical services provided by DCTs might inappropriately influence potential participants [22]. This discrepancy raises important ethical considerations for clinical trial teams, including ensuring informed consent, evaluating the potential for coercion, and mitigating undue influence in the recruitment process. Furthermore, our research findings show that trust in doctors is not the main factor driving students’ willingness to participate in DCTs. However, studies by Xing Shujun [23] and Li Meixin [17] found that healthcare professionals significantly influence patient participation in Chinese DCTs. This difference may be due to variations in research subjects.

Conclusions

Clinical trials are a crucial pathway for pharmaceutical products to enter the market. As a significant factor influencing research progress, it is necessary to enhance public awareness and participation. For DCTs, Only 54.5% college students had adequate awareness, and 42.1% college students expressed participation willingness, so interventions should focus on education and ethical protection. For example, universities and media platforms could invite medical experts to disseminate information about DCTs. This would enhance public awareness and understanding, and help reduce misconceptions and biases about DCTs. Meanwhile, as one of the vulnerable populations in clinical trials, it is essential for clinical research teams and ethics committees to consider measures that protect students’ autonomy while enabling them to benefit from DCTs.

Limitations

Despite this study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged: (1) Sampling methodology The over-reliance on convenience and snowball sampling, combined with the geographic concentration of participants in China’s northwestern and central regions, resulted in the underrepresentation of college students. These dual methodological constraints restrict the generalizability of the results to the national level. (2) Subjective measures: The assessment of “pressure to seek medical care” and “financial pressure” relied on self-reported data without standardized evaluation metrics, potentially affecting measurement reliability. (3) Self-report bias: The reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias, and the awareness scale, though validated in our sample, requires further validation in broader populations. Subsequent research should consider employing stratified sampling for demographic factors and increasing the sample size to address a broader range of issues related to DCTs.
