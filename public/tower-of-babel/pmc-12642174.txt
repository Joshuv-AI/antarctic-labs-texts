Overlapping autoimmune encephalitis with tripartite autoantibody positivity (NMDAR, GFAP, mGluR5) associated with papillary thyroid carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Yanxiang Chen, Guonan Zhou, Kai Dai, Zhijian Tan, Hui Wang, Fuhua Peng, Ying Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune encephalitis (AE) with concurrent triple neuronal/glial autoantibody positivity is exceptionally rare, and its correlation with thyroid carcinoma remains undocumented. We report for the first time a case of overlapping syndrome characterized by co-existence of anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and anti-metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) antibodies, association with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC).Case presentationA 50-year-old Chinese Han woman presented with headache, mental behavioral abnormalities, and memory loss, which further developed with fever, involuntary movements, and impaired consciousness. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination revealed lymphocytic-predominant pleocytosis and elevated protein levels. CSF was positive for the three antibodies mentioned above. After treatment with methylprednisolone, rituximab, and immunoadsorption with staphylococcal protein A column (SPA-IA) treatment, her consciousness became clear and mental state returned to normal, except for memory loss during the onset of illness. Whole-body nuclear PET/CT detected thyroid tumor. She underwent radical thyroidectomy and resumed normal life without recurrence during an 18-month follow-up.ConclusionThis is the first reported case of this unique triad, highlighting the importance of considering underlying malignancy in patients with multiple neuronal/glial antibody co-existences.Keywords: Anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), Anti-mGluR5 antibody, Anti-NMDAR antibody, Autoimmune encephalitis, Overlapping syndrome, Papillary thyroid carcinoma

Introduction

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) represents a rapidly evolving field of neuroimmunology, characterized by heterogeneous antibodies targeting neuronal or glial antigens, often associated with paraneoplastic syndromes. The co-occurrence of multiple neural autoantibodies, known as overlapping syndrome, poses significant challenges to diagnosis and treatment. Among these antibodies, anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and anti-metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) antibodies exhibit distinct clinical and oncological profiles. Anti-NMDAR encephalitis, the most common AE, presents with psychiatric symptoms, seizures, dyskinesias, and autonomic dysfunction. Approximately 23.8–59% of cases are associated with tumors, predominantly ovarian teratomas [1, 2], though there have been reports of rare links to thyroid carcinoma [3, 4]. Autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy (A-GFAP-A) causes meningoencephalomyelitis, optic papillitis, and myelitis, with neoplasms reported in 38% of cases (e.g., teratomas, melanomas) [5]. Anti-mGluR5 encephalitis (Ophelia syndrome), first described in Hodgkin lymphoma [6], manifests primarily as limbic encephalopathy. Fewer than 30 cases have been reported worldwide, with exceptional associations outside hematological malignancies [7, 8]. Although dual antibody positivity (e.g., NMDAR/GFAP [5, 9], mGluR5/NMDAR [10]) has been increasingly recognized, there have been no previous case reports of triple positivity for anti-NMDAR, anti-GFAP, and anti-mGluR5 antibodies. Recent literature described a case with overlapping syndromes of anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG)/NMDAR/mGluR5 autoantibodies [11]. Here, we report a case of novel overlapping syndrome with triple positivity for anti-GFAP, anti-NMDAR, and anti-mGluR5 antibodies associated with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), expanding the spectrum of paraneoplastic autoimmunity.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old Chinese Han female patient, with no previous medical or psychiatric history, presented with headache for 18 days and psychiatric symptoms (agitation, limb twitching, memory loss) for 11 days. Eight days prior to the current admission (September 11, 2023), an initial workup at an external hospital revealed lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF): the white blood cell (WBC) count was 196 × 10⁶/L (98.8% lymphocytes), elevated protein (0.80 g/L), and normal glucose (3.14 mmol/L). CSF cytology showed scattered lymphocytes. Infectious examinations were negative, including HSV-1/2 polymerase chain reaction (PCR), CSF metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), and pathogen cultures. Brain enhanced MRI demonstrated leptomeningeal enhancement, ruling out intracranial hemorrhage. She received empiric treatment for suspected infectious meningoencephalitis with aciclovir and methylprednisolone (1,000 mg/day IV ×5 days, tapered to 500 mg/day from September 8, 2023). Agitation and hallucinations were managed with risperidone and olanzapine. During the tapering of steroid therapy, she developed persistent low-grade fever with neurological deterioration manifested as psychiatric fluctuations (alternating agitation and mutism), movement disorders (chorea and dyskinesia in bilateral upper limbs; myoclonus in lower limbs), and autonomic dysfunction (severe constipation > 72 h and urinary retention).

On admission (September 11, 2023), she exhibited mutism with occasional agitation and impaired communication. Neurological examination revealed neck stiffness, intact pupillary reflexes, normal limb reflexes, and bilaterally negative Babinski’s signs. Motor and sensory assessments were limited by uncooperativeness. Initial laboratory investigations showed WBC count (10.91 × 10⁹/L) with neutrophilia (95.7%) and lymphopenia (2.9%), hypokalemia (2.96 mmol/L), hypernatremia (151 mmol/L), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 29.1 mg/L, ESR 34 mm/h, IL-6 9.23 pg/mL). Thyroid function tests indicated hypothyroidism (FT3: 2.26 pmol/L; TSH: 0.1653 µIU/mL) with elevated anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (324.91 IU/mL). Coagulation studies revealed elevated fibrinogen (5.20 g/L) and D-dimer (1.76 µg/mL). Tuberculosis screening (antibody/T-SPOT.TB) was negative. Lumbar puncture at admission showed: opening pressure 140 mmH₂O, glucose 3.14 mmol/L (normal), red blood cell (RBC) 160 × 10⁶/L (traumatic tap), WBCs 120 × 10⁶/L (98% lymphocytes), and elevated protein (0.565 g/L). Oligoclonal IgG bands were detected in the CSF but were absent in the serum.

After admission, due to the clinical suspicion of AE, comprehensive autoantibody analysis was performed on CSF and serum using a cell-based assay (CBA) at the Guangzhou Immuno Art Clinical Laboratory Center. The assay was based on HEK293 cells transfected with full-length human antigens and EGFP (Fig. 1). The results revealed the presence of anti-NMDAR antibodies in the CSF (1:100) and serum (1:10), anti-mGluR5 antibodies in the CSF (1:30), and anti-GFAP antibodies in the CSF (1:32). All other tested antibodies, including anti-LGI1, anti-GABA-B, anti-GABA-A, anti-CASPR2, anti-AMPA1, anti-AMPA2, anti-IgLON5, anti-GAD65 and anti-DPPX antibodies, central nervous system (CNS) demyelinating antibodies (AQP4, MOG, MBP), and paraneoplastic antibodies (Hu, Yo, Ri, CV2/CRMP5, Ma2, Amphiphysin, SOX1, Zic4, Tr/DNER, PCA-2), were negative in both CSF and serum. These findings confirmed overlapping AE with anti-NMDAR, anti-GFAP, and anti-mGluR5 antibodies. An electroencephalogram (EEG) demonstrated diffuse slow-wave activity under chloral hydrate sedation. On day 3 post-admission, brain MRI revealed left frontotemporal-predominant dural thickening involving the left cerebral hemisphere, with diffusion weighted imaging (DWI), T2-FLAIR, and T1-weighted gadolinium-enhanced sequences showing corresponding hyperintensity (Fig.2A, F, K).

On day 1 post-admission, she received intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1,000 mg/day ×5 days, tapered to 500 mg/day ×3 days, 250 mg/day ×2 days, and 120 mg/day ×1 day). On day 4 (September 14, 2023), she received rituximab (500 mg IV), and her mutism persisted but agitation was relieved. Staphylococcal protein A column (SPA-IA) treatment was initiated on day 7 post-admission and a total of eight sessions were completed. Subsequently, a second methylprednisolone pulse was administered on day 13 (September 23, 2023). Following the final SPA-IA treatment session on day 21 (October 1, 2023), she demonstrated marked neurological improvement, regaining consciousness and coherent speech, but still suffered from recent memory impairment, persistent bilateral lower limb weakness (Medical Research Council [MRC], grade 4/5), and unresolved urinary retention. A third methylprednisolone pulse began on day 22 (October 2, 2023) to consolidate response. On day 24 (October 4, 2023), a follow-up brain MRI demonstrated a partial decrease in left frontotemporal dural on DWI, T2-FLAIR, and enhancement (Fig. 2B, G, L). On day 28 (October 8, 2023), CSF analysis revealed a decrease in anti-NMDAR IgG antibodies (titer 1:30), while serum anti-NMDAR IgG antibodies remained unchanged (titer 1:10), and tests for anti-GFAP and anti-mGluR5 antibodies in CSF were negative. An 18 F-FDG PET/CT scan identified a hypermetabolic nodule in the left thyroid, classified as ACR TI-RADS category 5, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 6.2. An ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy conducted confirmed the diagnosis of papillary thyroid carcinoma (Bethesda category VI) on day 36 (October 16, 2023) (Fig. 3). After discharge on October 19, 2023, the patient was admitted to a local hospital and subsequently underwent a radical thyroidectomy on October 27, 2023. During her initial follow-up on November 12, 2023, she still had urinary retention. A repeat CSF analysis indicated a decrease in anti-NMDAR titers (1:10) and negative for anti-GFAP and anti-mGluR5 antibodies. A brain MRI conducted on November 14, 2023 showed no significant meningeal thickening or enhancement (Fig. 2C, H, M). In light of these findings, she underwent an additional course of methylprednisolone pulse therapy on November 14, 2023. Post-treatment, her urinary retention resolved, and her neurological function was remarkably restored. At her second follow-up on February 1, 2024, she demonstrated sustained neurological stability, had resumed work, and continued on maintenance methylprednisolone at a dosage of 8 mg/day, despite a rebound in CSF anti-NMDAR titers (1:30) and stable brain MRI findings (Fig. 2D, I, N). By her third follow-up on June 1, 2024, no symptoms were reported. Repeat CSF analysis showed anti-NMDAR antibodies were at a titer of 1:10. She subsequently received another cycle of rituximab, followed by maintenance therapy of mycophenolate mofetil to prevent recurrence. During the subsequent 12-month period, she remained in sustained remission without clinical recurrence. Discussion

We reported the first case of an overlapping autoimmune encephalitis syndrome characterized by triple positivity for anti-NMDAR, anti-GFAP, and anti-mGluR5, which was associated with PTC. This unique triad expands the spectrum of paraneoplastic neurological syndromes and underscores the critical role of malignancy screening in patients with multiple neuronal/glial autoantibodies. While dual antibody overlaps have been increasingly recognized (e.g., GFAP/NMDAR [9], NMDAR/mGluR5 [10]), triple positivity remains exceptional. A recent report described triple positivity for MOG, NMDAR, and mGluR5 antibodies [11]. The pathogenic mechanisms in such multi-antibody syndromes may involve epitope spreading triggered by sustained neuroinflammation or cross-reactive tumor antigens [12]. Notably, all three antibodies in our patient exhibited CSF-predominant positivity, supporting the notion of intrathecal synthesis and direct involvement in CNS pathogenesis [13].

Anti-NMDAR antibody encephalitis is one of the most common types of AE. It was first described in 2007 [14]. The typical clinical manifestations include psychiatric symptoms, seizures, movement disorders, autonomic nervous system dysfunction, and decreased consciousness [1, 14]. Our patient exhibited several of these features, including mental behavioral abnormalities, seizures, and impaired consciousness, indicating the overlap with anti-NMDAR encephalitis. The pathogenesis of anti-NMDAR antibody-associated encephalitis is well established to involve the binding of anti-NMDAR antibodies to the NR1 subunit of the NMDAR, leading to internalization and degradation of the receptor, thereby reducing the number of functional receptors on the cell surface and disrupting synaptic plasticity and neuronal communication [1]. The clinical phenotype of this patient was likely dominated by anti-NMDAR encephalitis, and the co-detection of anti-NMDAR antibodies in her CSF (1:100) and serum (1:10) suggests that a similar pathogenic mechanism may contribute to her neurological deficits [15]. In addition to direct antibody-mediated receptor blockade, immune-mediated inflammation also plays an important role in the disease process and contributes to the pathogenesis. Activated immune cells secrete cytokines and chemokines, which recruit more immune cells to the CNS, further aggravating the inflammatory response and neuronal damage [16].

A-GFAP-A was firstly reported by Fang et al. from the Mayo Clinic in 2016 [5], is an inflammatory CNS disorder targeting astrocytes. The clinical manifestations of A-GFAP-A are diverse, typically presenting as subacute meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis, or a combination of these syndromes [5]. Approximately 40% of patients experience prodromal symptoms such as fever and headache, and about 30% may have associated tumors [5]. The most common tumor is ovarian teratoma (33.8%), and other associated tumors include lung adenocarcinoma, B-cell lymphoma, glioma, multiple myeloma, melanoma, etc [17]. In our case, initial headache and memory loss progressed to encephalopathy with seizures, autonomic dysfunction (urinary retention), and persistent bilateral lower limb weakness. These features are associated with A-GFAP-A and overlap with anti-NMDAR/mGluR5 antibodies, representing the first report of association with PTC. CSF analysis revealed lymphocytic-predominant pleocytosis and elevated protein levels, consistent with previous reports indicating that 90% of patients exhibit a lymphocytic response in CSF and 80% have mild-to-moderate protein elevation [13]. Leptomeningeal enhancement in A-GFAP-A is the most frequent imaging pattern, followed by linear perivascular radial enhancement [18]. In our case, brain MRI revealed left frontotemporal-predominant dural thickening and leptomeningeal enhancement. The asymmetric left frontotemporal MRI changes may result from focal blood-brain barrier disruption or regional inflammation, as also reported in other autoimmune encephalitis cases [19].

Anti-mGluR5 antibody-associated encephalitis is a relatively rare type of AE, with clinical manifestations mainly including limbic encephalitis-like symptoms such as memory impairment, psychiatric symptoms, and seizures [8]. The pathogenesis may involve synergistic disruption of glutamatergic signaling. Anti-mGluR5 antibodies uncouple mGluR5-mediated potentiation of NMDA receptors through protein kinase C-dependent pathways [20], whereas concurrent anti-NMDAR antibodies directly induce receptor internalization [1]. The combined receptor blockade creates a bidirectional excitation-inhibition imbalance [21]. During agitation phases, impaired GABAergic interneuron regulation (secondary to NMDAR hypofunction) permits uncontrolled cortical excitation. The co-occurrence of these antibodies represents a distinct entity where mGluR5 dysfunction amplifies NMDAR hypofunction [22], thereby driving corticolimbic network dysrhythmia that manifests as psychiatric lability [23].

Flanagan et al. found that about 22% of GFAP-antibody-positive patients could detect anti-NMDAR antibodies [5]. The coexistence of NMDAR and mGluR5 antibodies is exceedingly rare [10], triple positivity has yet to be reported. We hypothesize that PTC-driven molecular mimicry could be a pathogenic mechanism, supported by: (i) serological parallels (anti-SOX1 seroconversion post-resection [24], α3-nAChR antibodies predicting occult PTC [25], (ii) PTC-derived inflammatory cytokines disrupting blood-brain barrier integrity; and (iii) documented symptom/antibody improvement post-tumor resection in PTC-associated anti-NMDAR/LGI1/AMPA encephalitis [3, 26, 27]. Elevated anti-thyroglobulin antibody levels (324.91 IU/mL) further indicate concomitant thyroid autoimmunity, potentially amplifying paraneoplastic B-cell responses [28]. The patient’s clinical course and treatment response provide further evidence may support these mechanisms. Notably, her rapid neurological improvement following combined methylprednisolone pulse, SPA-IA, and rituximab treatment—coupled with a decline in CSF anti-NMDAR titers from 1:100 to 1:10 and negative results for CSF anti-mGluR5/GFAP antibodies—supports the pathogenic role of these antibodies. IA selectively removes pathogenic IgG, driving significant clinical improvement in AE mediated by neuronal surface antigens [29]. Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B cells, has been increasingly used in the management of AE, especially in cases refractory to first-line treatments. Recently, a multicenter cohort study showed that rituximab significantly delayed relapses in patients with anti-NMDAR antibody encephalitis, suggesting its role as a valuable second-line therapy [30]. This dual strategy in our patient targets both the immediate removal of pathogenic antibodies and the long-term suppression of B cell-mediated antibody production. Moreover, the resolution of symptoms after thyroidectomy and no recurrence during the 18-month of postoperative follow-up. Clinical and serological improvement following tumor resection is a hallmark of paraneoplastic neurological syndromes and has been documented in other cases of PTC-associated autoimmune encephalitis, underscoring the critical importance of early tumor resection [3, 24]. A key question in our case is whether all three antibodies contributed to pathogenesis. Anti-NMDAR antibodies are well-established as directly pathogenic, and their CSF-predominance and correlation with clinical course support a central role [1]. Anti-mGluR5 antibodies may synergistically disrupt glutamatergic signaling, potentially amplifying NMDAR hypofunction [20]. While the pathogenic role of anti-GFAP antibodies in this unique triad is less clear, the persistent bilateral lower limb weakness and urinary retention, poorly explained by anti-NMDAR or anti-mGluR5 encephalitis, suggest its involvement [5]. Although we cannot definitively rule out that one or more antibodies were bystanders, the clinical and paraclinical improvements following B-cell and antibody-targeting immunotherapy suggest that all three were clinically relevant.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the proposed mechanisms of molecular mimicry or epitope spreading remain speculative without direct molecular evidence from the tumor tissue. Second, we cannot definitively establish whether all three antibodies were equally pathogenic or whether some were just bystanders. Further functional studies and larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings and elucidate the precise of immunopathogenesis.

Conclusion

We describe a novel triad of anti-GFAP, anti-NMDAR, and anti-mGluR5 antibody positivity in a patient with PTC. This case suggests that thyroid malignancy may be associated with complex overlapping syndromes. Early tumor resection combined with aggressive immunotherapy is critical for neurological recovery. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for underlying neoplasms in patients with multi-antibody encephalitis, particularly when antibody profiles span both glial and neuronal targets.
