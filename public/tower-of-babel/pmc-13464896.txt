A case of primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of the heart
Authors: Qian Li, Dezhi Li, Zhengren Du, Yanjin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary cardiac diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is an exceptionally rare entity in the published literature. We have reported here the case of a 73-year-old Chinese man who presented with intermittent chest tightness as the chief complaint. Echocardiography identified a pedunculated, moderately echogenic mass (approximately 49 × 36 mm) within the left atrium, attached to the interatrial septum and mobile with the cardiac cycle, initially suggestive of a myxoma. The patient underwent surgical resection of the cardiac mass without any preoperative diagnosis. Histopathological examination confirmed DLBCL. Notably, the patient received neither postoperative radiotherapy nor chemotherapy. At nearly 3 years of follow-up, he remains alive with a favorable prognosis and sustained disease-free survival.

Background

Primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) of the heart represents an exceptionally rare and highly aggressive subtype of primary cardiac lymphoma (PCL). It primarily involves the heart and/or pericardium, often presenting with cardiac symptoms due to direct myocardial infiltration. This condition is characterized by rapid progression and elevated mortality, and the disease poses considerable diagnostic challenges owing to its nonspecific clinical and imaging features, thereby frequently necessitating surgical biopsy for definitive histopathological confirmation. Despite the availability of multimodal treatment approaches incorporating surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the clinical outcomes generally remain unfavorable [1]. Herein, we have reported a rare case of primary cardiac DLBCL and discussed its clinical course and management.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old man was admitted to our institution on February 8, 2023, with a 6-month history of intermittent chest tightness and dyspnea, experiencing symptomatic exacerbation during the week prior to admission, which was relieved by rest. During this period, he intermittently sought care at local hospitals, where symptomatic relief was achieved with medications including isosorbide mononitrate and metoprolol. In the week preceding admission, the frequency and severity of his symptoms had exacerbated, now occasionally accompanied by cough and sputum production. He denied any abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, vomiting, palpitations, or crushing precordial pain. His mental status, sleep, appetite, and bowel and bladder functions remained largely unaffected, with no reported weight loss.

Medical history

The patient had no significant medical history, such as hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, trauma, surgery, or blood transfusion. He denied any history of infectious diseases such as hepatitis B or tuberculosis, as well as no known allergies to food or drugs.

Physical examination

On admission, his vital signs were within the normal ranges: body temperature 36.5 °C, pulse 75 beats/min, respiratory rate 15 breaths/min, and blood pressure 120/70 mmHg. The patient was conscious and cooperative. No jaundice or superficial lymphadenopathy was detected. Cardiac examination revealed no precordial bulge or palpable heave. Auscultation demonstrated regular heart sounds without murmurs and clear lung fields bilaterally. On examination, the abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no organomegaly or shifting dullness.

Admission laboratory and imaging findings

Cardiac biomarkers were elevated: troponin T 0.598 ng/mL, CK-MB 19.78 ng/mL, myoglobin 163.20 ng/mL, and ischemia-modified albumin 83.1 U/mL. Routine blood tests, coagulation profile, liver and renal functions, electrolytes, urinalysis, stool examination, and electrocardiogram displayed no significant abnormalities.

Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) identified a filling defect within the left atrium (Fig. 1a). Chest CT revealed pericardial effusion and bilateral pleural effusions (Fig. 1b). Echocardiography demonstrated a pedunculated, moderately echogenic mass (approximately 49 × 36 mm) in the left atrium, which was attached to the interatrial septum and mobile with the cardiac cycle, suggestive of a myxoma (Fig. 2). Doppler ultrasound-guided thoracentesis localization displaced fluid-filled hypoechoic areas in the right (33 × 16 mm) and left (50 × 21 mm) pleural cavities, both with internal acoustic transmission. No tumor formation was detected in other organs. Based on the clinical presentation and ancillary investigations, a provisional diagnosis of “cardiac tumor” was established. Following stabilization of the patient’s condition, surgical resection was performed. Intraoperatively, a translucent, friable mass was identified and excised en bloc along its pedicle at the interatrial septum using an electrosurgical knife.

Pathological findings

Gross examination revealed an irregular mass measuring 5.5 × 4 × 3 cm, partially encapsulated, with a grayish-white to grayish-red, soft cut surface. Histopathological examination demonstrated a diffuse infiltration of medium-to-large-sized atypical lymphocytes with irregular nuclear contours, vesicular chromatin, and prominent nucleoli. Mitotic figures were abundant, consistent with a high Ki-67 proliferation index (80%). No geographic necrosis or fibrinoid necrosis was identified within the tumor. Following extensive sampling of the entire resected specimen, no evidence of a myxoid matrix—such as abundant stroma, capillary proliferation, or the characteristic ring-like structures of myxoma cells—was observed. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed the following profile: CD20(+), Bcl-6(+), Ki67-MIB1 (80%), c-Myc (20%), P53 (sporadic +), whereas CK, Desmin, CD3, Bcl-2, CD31, CD34, CD10, MUM-1, CD5, and Cyclin D1 were negative. EBER in situ hybridization was negative. These findings definitively excluded the diagnosis of cardiac myxoma. The final histopathological diagnosis confirmed DLBCL in the germinal center subtype (Fig. 3). In accordance with the 2015 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of cardiac and pericardial tumors [2], the patient was definitively diagnosed with primary DLBCL of the heart. The patient’s family elected to withhold the diagnosis from the patient, with the concern that recommending further investigations (e.g., PET-CT for staging) or adjuvant chemotherapy/radiotherapy would provoke undue distress and potentially worsen his clinical status. Consequently, neither PET-CT staging nor postoperative adjuvant therapy was pursued.

During hospitalization, the patient received comprehensive supportive care, including nitroglycerin for coronary vasodilation, sacubitril/valsartan for heart failure management, digoxin for inotropic support, atorvastatin calcium for lipid control, cefazolin for infection prophylaxis, furosemide and spironolactone for diuresis, and dopamine for hemodynamic support. The patient’s condition improved steadily, and he was discharged on February 17, 2023.

No further antitumor therapy was administered after the patient’s discharge. Telephonic follow-ups were conducted in December 2025 and January 2026, confirming that the patient remained in good health with sustained clinical stability.

Discussion

PCL is an exceedingly rare malignancy, constituting < 2% of all primary cardiac tumor cases and < 0.5% of extranodal lymphoma cases [1, 3]. Two distinct definitions of PCL have been proposed. The first, typically a postmortem diagnosis, defines PCL as an extranodal lymphoma strictly limited to the heart and/or pericardium without extracardiac involvement. The second, a broader clinical definition, classifies PCL as a primary cardiac tumor in patients presenting either with a cardiac mass at diagnosis or with cardiac symptoms predominantly attributable to myocardial lymphoma infiltration [1]. According to the U.S. SEER database (1975–2016), the overall incidence of PCL is 0.011 per 100,000 person-years, with DLBCL accounting for 63.9% of cases. This yields an estimated incidence of primary cardiac DLBCL of approximately 0.007 per 100,000 person-years [4].

PCL exhibits significant pathological heterogeneity and can be classified by cell lineage into B-cell lymphoma, T-cell lymphoma, and other rare subtypes, with prognosis varying considerably among them. DLBCL is the most common subtype and is associated with an extremely poor prognosis. Reports indicate that the 2-year survival rate for patients with cardiac DLBCL is only 6.9% [5]. and the median survival is less than one month without treatment, extending to approximately 18 months with treatment [6]. Immunophenotypically, DLBCL expresses multiple B-cell markers, including CD20, CD79a, and PAX5, although one or more of these antigens may be absent. A small subset of DLBCL cases may express CD30, particularly in the anaplastic variant, and 5%–10% of DLBCL cases may express CD5. Cardiac T-cell lymphoma is even rarer and more aggressive, with a prognosis worse than that of B-cell lymphoma. The median survival for cardiac T-cell lymphoma is typically only two months [6]. The most common immunophenotype is CD2+, sCD3–, cCD3+, and CD56+, with CD56 being the most consistently associated natural killer (NK)-related marker. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is almost universally positive. Other T-cell markers, such as CD5, CD7, and CD8, are usually negative [7]. Another subtype, fibrin-associated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (FA-DLBCL), typically arises in the context of localized, persistent chronic inflammation and is closely associated with EBV infection. Most cases exhibit high expression levels of CD30, Ki67, and PD-L1, while MYC and p53 expression is low to moderate. FA-DLBCL does not produce mass effect or infiltrate normal tissue; complete surgical resection is therefore curative. Regardless of treatment modality (surgery alone or chemotherapy), the prognosis is generally favorable. However, compared to tumors in other sites that are more amenable to complete resection, patients with primary cardiac or vascular involvement may experience disease recurrence or persistence [8].

Anatomically, PCL demonstrates a right-sided predominance, most commonly involving the right atrium, followed by the right ventricle. Moreover, left-sided cardiac involvement has been documented. In contrast, nearly 80% of primary cardiac tumors are benign, with myxoma representing the most common entity. Considering the frequent location of myxomas in the left atrium, differential diagnosis between PCL and myxoma remains a critical step in clinical evaluation. The clinical manifestations of PCL are nonspecific and heterogeneous, largely dependent on the extent and location of cardiac involvement [9]. The common presentations include pericardial effusion, heart failure, arrhythmias, and dyspnea, along with chest pain and cardiac tamponade. The nonspecific and variable clinical presentation of PCL complicates its timely diagnosis. Echocardiography (both transesophageal and transthoracic), CT, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) constitute the main imaging modalities for detecting cardiac tumors.Transthoracic echocardiography, given its noninvasive nature and accessibility, is often the first-line modality for identifying cardiac masses or pericardial effusion. Nevertheless, definitive diagnosis of PCL ultimately depends on histopathological examination supplemented by immunohistochemical profiling.

The existing management options for PCL include surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Surgery is primarily indicated for obtaining diagnostic tissues and relieving any obstructive symptoms or complications; however, except for FA-DLBCL, it does not significantly improve long-term prognosis or overall survival when used alone [10].Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, with regimens such as R-CHOP, COP, and CHOP-E being widely employed. For DLBCL, the incorporation of the anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab into the R-CHOP protocol has notably enhanced the survival outcomes. Complete response (CR) rates of up to 71% have been reported in the literature [11]. The current evidence suggests that patients who receive combined surgical resection and chemotherapy achieve better survival outcomes compared to those treated with either modality alone [12]. Furthermore, in hemodynamically unstable cases, surgical intervention remains a critical and urgent measure to stabilize the patient’s condition [13].

The patient’s presenting symptoms, such as chest discomfort and exertional dyspnea, are consistent with commonly reported manifestations of PCL, such as chest pain and dyspnea. In contrast to the typical right-sided predominance reported for PCL, the tumor in this case was located in the left atrium. This presentation, along with other distinctive clinical features, may reflect individual variability or differences in healthcare access. Despite these variations, it is important to emphasize that PCL remains a highly aggressive malignancy associated with rapid progression and elevated mortality.

However, the fact that this patient did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy merits discussion. In this case, following complete surgical resection of the tumor, the family declined systemic treatment and withheld the diagnosis from the patient to avoid potential psychological distress. Remarkably, the patient achieved nearly three years of disease-free survival without receiving any anticancer therapy. Complete surgical resection, favorable tumor biological characteristics (GCB subtype, low c-Myc expression), and host immune factors may have contributed to this unusual clinical course. While this case does not alter standard treatment protocols, it suggests that long-term survival without adjuvant therapy is possible in highly selected circumstances. These distinctive features warrant further investigation to better elucidate their impact on disease progression and management.

We acknowledge that the absence of a bone marrow biopsy or aspiration represents a limitation of this case report. Ideally, complete lymphoma staging would require PET-CT and bone marrow examination. However, following surgery, the patient’s family declined further diagnostic procedures, and the patient remained asymptomatic without receiving any systemic therapy. From a clinical standpoint, the nearly three-year disease-free survival strongly argues against the presence of clinically significant occult systemic disease. Therefore, although our staging is incomplete according to strict hematological criteria, the diagnosis of primary cardiac lymphoma remains clinically reasonable in this specific context.

Conclusion

When a cardiac mass is completely resectable, surgical treatment should be performed promptly. This case suggests that in highly selected patients—such as those with complete tumor resection and favorable tumor biology—long-term survival may be achieved even in the absence of adjuvant therapy. More importantly, recognizing the clinical and pathological differences among the various subtypes of PCL (including DLBCL, FA-DLBCL, and T-cell lymphoma) is essential for developing individualized treatment strategies. Further research is warranted to elucidate the biological heterogeneity of PCL and to optimize personalized management.
