Machine learning-based clinical-radiomics model for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in sinonasal squamous cell carcinoma: a preliminary 2-year follow-up study
Authors: Yue Geng, Jingfeng Cheng, Dandan Shen, Yin Wang, Jiawei Kuang, Zuohua Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo construct and validate an optimal machine learning (ML)-based clinical-radiomics model integrating clinical and radiomics features for predicting recurrence risk within 2 years after radical surgery in patients with sinonasal squamous cell carcinoma (SNSCC).MethodsThis retrospective study included a total of 207 patients with pathologically confirmed SNSCC who underwent preoperative MRI. Patients were divided into a training cohort (n = 151) from the headquarter hospital and an independent testing cohort (n = 56) from the branch hospital. Radiomics features were acquired from diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences. Clinical, radiomics, and combined clinical-radiomics models were constructed and validated using various ML algorithms, with the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) computed to evaluate their classification performance. The optimal clinical-radiomics model that integrates multimodal features was subsequently identified.ResultsDuring the two-year period after radical surgery, 93 patients (44.9%) experienced recurrence at follow-up. The clinical-radiomics model, developed with seven predictive features (Range, surgical margin status, Kurtosis, T4 stage, Idn, Robust Mean Absolute Deviation, and Ki-67 levels) using ANOVA feature selection and LDA classifier, achieved optimal discriminative performance. In the training cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.85, in contrast to 0.76 for the clinical model and 0.79 for the radiomics model. This superiority was maintained in the testing cohort, where it achieved an AUC of 0.83, compared to 0.71 for the clinical model and 0.77 for the radiomics model. The combined model consistently outperformed the standalone models across both cohorts.ConclusionsThe present study developed an ML-based clinical-radiomics model that exhibited promising performance for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC. Given its preliminary nature, this model has the potential to serve as an intelligent decision support tool, advancing precision medicine in SNSCC management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02022-3.

Introduction

Sinonasal carcinomas, which encompass the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, represent approximately 3–5% of head and neck malignancies, with squamous cell carcinoma of the sinuses (SNSCC) comprising the majority (>50%) [1]. SNSCC is characterized by high aggressiveness and heterogeneity, complicating management and leading to elevated rates of recurrence and mortality [2]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and equipment, as well as the utilization of neoadjuvant therapies, surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for SNSCC [3]. Recurrence within two years following radical tumor resection significantly impacts long-term outcomes, with reported incidences ranging from 45.4% to 54.7% [4, 5]. Addressing the challenge of reducing recurrence rates is crucial. Therefore, developing a reliable predictive tool to identify recurrence risk after radical resection is essential for initiating individualized precision treatment.

Currently, the Tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system serves as the most widely utilized indicator for guiding clinical treatment plan-makning and predicting the prognosis of SNSCC patients. Factors such as age, primary site, histologic grade, and Ki-67 levels have also been associated with recurrence risk and prognosis in this patient population [2, 5]. However, reliance solely on independent clinical features often fails to adequately reflect the biological characteristics and recurrence potential of a tumor, particularly in the context of tumor heterogeneity, which can significantly limit the predictive power of these parameters. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), recognized as the most commonly employed functional MRI sequence for sinonasal lesions, allows for non-invasive analysis of the microstructural characteristics of biological tissue based on the basis of water diffusivity quantified by the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) [6]. Radiomics, a burgeoning field, transforms medical images into mineable high-dimensional quantitative features. These features may reflect intratumor heterogeneity, which is closely related to cancer diagnosis, the prediction of therapeutic response and recurrence, as well as survival prognostication [7–9]. Unlike traditional clinical analysis, which relies on individual thresholds or cut-offs for risk stratification, radiomics-driven machine learning (ML) offers a paradigm shift. ML, as an artificial intelligence-driven analytical framework, utilizes iterative computational optimization to holistically analyze multimodal datasets holistically through various algorithms, automatically learning the optimal combinations and weighting schemes of these variables to predict outcomes [10, 11]. Although a previous study highlighted the potential utility of ADC-based radiomic nomograms for predicting recurrence risk in advanced SNSCCs [4], the diagnostic efficacy of ML-based ADC models in this specific context remains to be thoroughly investigated.

To address the limitations of clinical parameters and leverage the ability of radiomics to quantify tumor heterogeneity, this study aims to develop an optimal clinical-radiomics ML model integrating multimodal features for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC. The proposed model employs reproducible computational pipelines to fuse clinical-radiomics data, thereby generating individualized recurrence probabilities rather than relying on population-level thresholds.

Materials and methods

Patients

The Ethics Committee granted approval for the retrospective study involving imaging and clinical data, thereby waiving the requirement for informed consent.

Consecutive patients who underwent surgical resection of the primary tumor and had pathologically confirmed SNSCC were enrolled between January 2016 and June 2022. The criteria for patient inclusion and exclusion were illustrated in Fig. 1. Clinical and radiological follow-up visits were conducted for all patients every 3 to 6 months. The follow-up period commenced on the date of surgical completion and continued until the patient experienced a recurrence. For patients without any signs of recurrence, follow-up was ensured for a minimum of two years. The primary clinical outcomes were classified into two groups: recurrence within a 2-year follow-up period and non-recurrence within a 2-year follow-up period. Recurrence was determined by (I) histopathological confirmation of SCC in biopsied or surgically resected tissue, and (II) the observation of a new and clearly developing or enlarging mass lesion at serial follow-up visits.

Consequently, the final cohort comprised 207 patients. The training cohort included 151 patients from the headquarter hospital, whereas the independent external testing cohort included 56 patients from the branch hospital. These cohorts differ from those in our previously published study, which used data from the headquarter hospital, including patients with incomplete postoperative follow-up [8]. In contrast, the current study expanded the dataset by incorporating newly collected data from the headquarter hospital and integrating data from the branch hospital, thereby ensuring non-overlapping cohorts and a focus on patients with more comprehensive follow-up information.

Radiomics image acquisition, preprocessing, segmentation and feature extraction

MR examinations were conducted using a 3.0-Tesla scanner. The training cohort was scanned at the headquarter hospital using a Magnetom Verio system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with the 12-channel head coil, while the testing cohort was scanned at the branch hospital using a Magnetom Prisma system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with the 64-channel head coil. The imaging series utilized included T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), DWI, and contrast-enhanced T1WI. DWI was performed with the Readout Segment of Long Variable Echo Train sequence (RESOLVE) to acquire axial images, with the following parameters: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) of 3600/65 ms or 3000/58 ms; b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm²; slice thickness 5 mm; field of view of 240 × 240 mm; readout segments of 5. The workstation automatically generated the ADC map. The detailed parameters for MR scans conducted at both headquarter and branch hospitals were provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Prior to region of interest (ROI) delineation, all original images underwent sequential preprocessing steps: (I) resampling to a uniform voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ using B-spline interpolation; (II) gray-level normalization by mean-centering with standard deviation scaled to 100; and (III) gray-level discretization with a bin count of 64.

ADC images segmentation was executed in 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org/, open source software) via the ‘Segment Editor’ module, with 3D ROIs manually delineated along the tumor contour in each slice, referencing both T2WI and contrast-enhanced T1WI images. Care was taken to exclude hemorrhagic, necrotic, and cystic regions. The identified axial images were subsequently utilized to reconstruct the entire tumor volume. In the training cohort, tumor segmentation was carried out by Radiologist A, who has 8 years of experience in oncological head and neck imaging, and was redrawn by Radiologist B, who has over 15 years of experience. Both radiologists remained blinded to the patient’s clinical information, resolving any discrepancies through consensus. The inter-observer reliability in radiomics feature measurement was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Radiomics features with ICC values of 0.75 or higher were retained for subsequent analyses. In the testing cohort, tumor segmentation was performed alone by Radiologist A.

The 3D ROI data were imported into the ‘Feature Extraction’ module within PyRadiomics-based FeAture Explorer software (FAE, Version 0.5.12, 10.1371/journal.pone.0237587) for subsequent extraction of radiomic features [12]. The radiomics feature classes encompassed 14 shape features, 18 first-order features (intensity histogram), and 24 texture features (gray-level co-occurrence matrix, GLCM). Ultimately, a total of 56 radiomics features were extracted from the ADC images for each tumor.

Unless otherwise specified, all other parameters of pre-processing and radiomic feature extraction remained as a default configuration.

Clinical characteristics acquisition and feature extraction

Baseline clinical characteristics, including age at diagnosis, gender, smoking history, maximum diameter, lesion laterality, tumor involvement depth (T stage), regional lymph node involvement (N stage), distant metastases (M stage), histologic grade, Ki-67 levels, surgical margin status, and additional treatments after the initial surgery, were extracted from the patients’ medical records for stratification. In total, 12 clinical features were documented for each patient. Further details on the above indicators were summarized in Table 1. TNM staging was calculated according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging Manual, while histologic grade was assigned according to the World Health Organization Classification System (Broder’s classification). An age threshold of sixty years was used as a criterion for age stratification, as most patients with SNSCC were diagnosed during their sixth decade or later [1, 2]. A maximum diameter cutoff of 5 cm was established as the threshold for differentiating tumor size [4, 5]. Ki-67 levels were classified as high or low expression, with a cut-off of 50% according to previous studies [5, 13, 14]. In three patients with M1 stage, solitary pulmonary metastases were observed (single nodule, longest diameter < 1.5 cm on preoperative CT). All patients underwent complete resection of metastatic lesions through video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), thereby achieving curative-intent local control.

Machine learning-based classification models construction and validation

The overall analytical framework of the ML-based models was illustrated in Fig. 2. The detailed stepwise modeling pipeline, including image preprocessing, radiomics feature extraction, feature selection, model training, cross-validation, and bootstrap resampling, was presented in Fig. 3.

The construction and validation of the clinical model, radiomics model, and clinical-radiomics model were conducted within the ‘Model Exploration’ module of FAE. The training cohort served to train the ML models while the testing cohort was employed to evaluate their performance. To balance the dataset and facilitate the development of classification models, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was initially applied to the training cohort. Subsequently, Z-score normalization of the feature matrix was subsequently conducted to mitigate the influence of varying feature scales and thus improve feature comparability. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) with a threshold of 0.9 was employed to identify redundant features for removal.

To construct the radiomics model, we utilized three feature selection methods [analysis of variance (ANOVA), Kruskal-Wallis (KW), recursive feature elimination (RFE)] and three types of ML classifiers [support vector machine (SVM), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), logistic regression (LR)] for analyzing radiomics features. A nested five-fold cross-validation framework was implemented to build the final model based on the training cohort. The outer cross-validation loop was utilized for the selection of the number of features and the type of classifier, and the inner loop being employed for the hyperparameter tuning of the selected classifier. All random seeds in our experiments were fixed to 0 to ensure reproducibility. The cross-validation splits were defined in the FAE framework, and the configurations can be found in the config.json file used in FAE. Then the determined selected features and classifer were used in the whole training cohort to build the final model. For the clinical model, clinical features were analyzed using KW and LR. The optimal clinical feature and radiomics feature were integrated to construct the combined clinical-radiomics model.

Statistical analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to evaluate each model’s performance, and the area under these ROC curves (AUC) was assessed. Additionally, accuracy (Acc), Youden Index, specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for comparative purposes. FAE software (Version 0.5.12) was utilized to conduct these statistical analyses.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Between-group comparisons were conducted with the independent-samples T-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed variables; categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ² test. These analyses were performed in SPSS (Version 24.0; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Discriminative performance was compared by testing differences in receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC) among the clinical model, the radiomics model, and the combined clinical-radiomics model using DeLong’s test, implemented in R (Version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The Brier score, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were calculated and plotted in Python (Version 3.10.0; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA).

Reporting guidelines and quality assessment

The present radiomics study was conducted in accordance with the Checklist for EvaluAtion of Radiomics Research (CLEAR) guidelines [15]. The methodological quality was evaluated using the METhodological RadiomICs Score (METRICS) [16].

Results

Patient profiles

Of the 207 patients (mean age, 54 ± 13 years; range, 22–78 years) who participated in the study, 93 (44.9%) experienced recurrence within two years, while 114 (55.1%) remained recurrence-free. Among those who experienced recurrence at follow-up, the median time to recurrence was found to be 14 months. Table 1 showed a detailed comparison of the clinical features between the recurrence and non-recurrence groups. No significant differences were found in clinical features or recurrence patterns between the training and testing cohorts (p > 0.05).

Performance of the prediction models

The training cohort comprised 151 patients (66 with recurrence and 85 without), while the testing cohort included 56 patients (27 with recurrence and 29 without). Table 2, along with Figs. 4, 5 and 6, presented the predictive performance, ROC curve analysis results, different feature selection-classifier combination AUCs, and optimal contribution analysis for the clinical, radiomics, and clinical-radiomics models for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC.

Using KW feature selection and the LR classifier, the clinical model was developed on the basis of five optimal features: surgical margin status, T4 stage, Ki-67 levels, gender, and smoking history. The clinical model demonstrated sub-optimal predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.76 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.69–0.85) in the training cohort and 0.71 (95% CI, 0.58–0.84) in the testing cohort.

Using RFE feature selection and the LDA classifier, the radiomics model yielded the highest AUCs on the basis of six optimal features: Range, Kurtosis, Idn, Robust Mean Absolute Deviation, 90th Percentile, and Joint Average. Nonetheless, the predictive capacity of the radiomics model remained limited, with an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI, 0.67–0.90) in the training cohort and 0.77 (95% CI, 0.60–0.95) in the testing cohort. Table 3 summarized the optimal radiomics features of patients in the recurrence and non-recurrence groups in the radiomics model.

Using ANOVA feature selection and the LDA classifier, the final optimal clinical-radiomics model was constructed, integrating the five optimal clinical features and six radiomics features. The resulting model, which was developed using seven features (Range, surgical margin status, Kurtosis, T4 stage, Idn, Robust Mean Absolute Deviation, and Ki-67 levels), demonstrated robust performance, with an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.77–0.95) in the training cohort and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.67–0.97) in the testing cohort. The clinical-radiomics model demonstrated robust performance with consistent discrimination and calibration across validation methods (Table 4). The five-fold cross-validation yielded a mean AUC of 0.82 ± 0.03, with AUC values of 0.78, 0.83, 0.85, 0.81, and 0.84 for each fold. The calibration curves of the clinical-radiomics model for both the training cohort and testing cohort were presented in Fig. 7.

The clinical-radiomics model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to both the standalone clinical model (AUC = 0.83 vs. 0.71, p = 0.023, 95% CI, 0.041–0.199) and the radiomics model (AUC = 0.83 vs. 0.77, p = 0.038, 95% CI, 0.028–0.132), as assessed by DeLong’s test for ROC curve comparisons, with all reported p-values indicating statistical significance (p < 0.05). In the testing cohort, the DCA demonstrated that the clinical-radiomics model provided the greatest net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, outperforming both the clinical and radiomics models (Fig. 8).

Discussion

The present study constructed and validated an ML-based clinical-radiomics model, which exhibited superior predictive performance compared to a standalone clinical model and a radiomics model for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC. The clinical-radiomics model integrated complementary clinical and radiomics features, employing ML algorithms to systematically weigh predictive variables and optimize output robustness. This finding not only validated the efficacy of multi-source information fusion in improving the predictive power of the model, but also provided an intelligent tool for precision medicine and individualized treatment decisions for patients at high risk of recurrence after radical surgery in SNSCC.

Clinical features are essential in the processes of disease diagnosis and prediction, as they directly reflect the current physiological and pathological states of patients. However, this study revealed that the predictive performance of the clinical model, which was based solely on clinical features, was relatively sub-optimal. Radiomics, which extracts sub-visual yet quantitative features from medical imaging, has emerged as a significant imaging biomarker in oncology. It utilizes key image features for the monitoring, diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction of tumors, thereby facilitating the integration of medical imaging with personalized medicine [7–9, 17]. Nevertheless, our findings indicated that the predictive capacity of the radiomics model, which relies exclusively on radiomics features, was still limited. To enhance the predictive efficacy of the model, five selected clinical features were integrated with six radiomics features, thus constructing the clinical-radiomics model. ML algorithms were employed for feature selection and model training. Following rigorous cross-validation, the combined model, which incorporated seven optimal features (three clinical and four radiomics), yielded the highest AUC values, significantly improving the accuracy of predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC. In the testing cohort, the AUC of the clinical-radiomics model was 0.83, compared to 0.71 for the clinical model and 0.77 for the radiomics model. The clinical-radiomics model demonstrated significantly superior predictive performance compared to standalone clinical or radiomic models.

The clinical-radiomics model identified in this study comprised three optimal clinical features. T4 stage, positive surgical margins, and Ki-67 levels exceeding 50% were regarded as more effective predictors of a high recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC, consistent with existing clinical guidelines and literature reports. Previous studies had demonstrated that the T4 stage was an independent predictor of early recurrence in advanced SNSCC [4, 5]. Patients with T4-stage SNSCC who suffered orbital or brain invasion frequently faced incomplete tumor resection and high recurrence rates [18]. The T4 stage was more clearly associated with poorer prognosis than the T3 or lower stages were [19]. Furthermore, positive surgical margin status were closely linked to increased rates of local recurrence and worsened overall survival [18, 19]. This finding underscored the clinical significance of positive surgical margins as a crucial prognostic factor and suggested that such patients often required more frequent monitoring and intensive adjuvant treatment decisions to improve their treatment outcomes. High Ki-67 proliferative status is a significant prognostic indicator for patients with sinonasal malignancies [20]. A preliminary study conducted by Lin et al. revealed that Ki-67 levels exceeding 50% were associated with an elevated recurrence risk after surgery in SNSCC [5]. The present study corroborated this finding of previous research. Additionally, Lin et al. observed that histologic grade was a significant predictor of postoperative recurrence; however, this association was not validated in our study. This discrepancy was likely due to variations in the composition of the study samples, including differences in patient populations, disease staging, and treatment regimens. In the future, larger and more homogeneous samples should be sought to further validate the relationship between histologic grade and recurrence.

The clinical-radiomics model included four optimal radiomics features: Range, Kurtosis, Robust Mean Absolute Deviation, and Idn. These features reflected the histogram characteristics and texture intensity, thereby providing information on the intratumoural heterogeneity that cannot be evaluated through traditional imaging methods. High levels of intratumoral heterogeneity in head and neck SCC are associated with poor prognosis; indeed, greater degrees of heterogeneity correlate with worse outcomes [21, 22]. Range, Kurtosis, and Robust Mean Absolute Deviation are classified as first-order features, derived from histogram analysis, which evaluates each voxel’s signal intensity value along with its distribution within the ROI [23]. The findings of this study suggested that the ADC signal Kurtosis values were higher in the recurrence group compared to those in the non-recurrence group. Additionally, a previous study investigating the survival of patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) indicated that patients with higher Kurtosis values exhibited significantly worse recurrence-free survival (RFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) [24]. We hypothesized that high Kurtosis values may reflect the presence of hypovascular or hypoxic tissues, as well as necrosis within tumors, which are recognized factors contributing to poor patient prognosis [24, 25]. Furthermore, the larger Range and Robust Mean Absolute Deviation indicated that the intratumor histogram intensity values and their distributions exhibited significant and highly asymmetric properties, as well as a large degree of dispersion in pixel intensities. Idn is a parameter associated with the GLCM. Among the texture features, the GLCM effectively reflects the extent of signal mixing within lesions by examining the relative relationships between the distribution and location of voxel gray levels [26]. Specifically, a high Idn value indicates that the gray level pairs within the image exhibit greater consistency, suggesting that the texture is more uniform. In this study, a lower Idn value may further elucidate a high degree of heterogeneity and structural abnormalities in recurrent SNSCC. These factors are associated with a more aggressive malignant potential and poorer prognosis, as well as treatment resistance [27]. Additionally, the radiomics analysis included tumor shape features, which did not directly contribute to the predictive performance of the model. This finding may be attributed to the unique anatomical configuration of the sinonasal region, which may act as a physical barrier, limiting space for tumor growth and consequently making it challenging for tumors to exhibit differences in their underlying biological characteristics.

In traditional clinical analysis, risk or prognostic assessment often relies on predefined variable thresholds or cut-offs in univariate or multivariate frameworks. Conversely, ML algorithms are employed to automatically learn optimal feature combinations and weighting schemes for outcome prediction [10, 11]. This study utilised a ML-based clinical-radiomics model to predict the recurrence risk after surgery in SNSCC, optimizing the degree of automation in the prediction process. In comparison with the radiomic nomograms of multi-parameter MRI combined with clinical indicators constructed by Lin et al. [4], this study directly mines the deep patterns of data through end-to-end feature learning, thus avoiding the dependence of nomograms on manual feature selection and clinical data integrity. ML models address the operational complexities associated with traditional threshold-dependent analyses by automating the integration of multiple variables. The automation process yields probabilistic outputs that are user-friendly and directly inform clinicians regarding recurrence risk stratification. Notably, even the simplest ML model - logistic regression - is a multivariate model that uses all the included variables to compute a prediction probability. There is no fixed cut-off per variable; rather, the model learns from patterns in the data to weight each feature appropriately. More complex models (e.g., random forests, and gradient boosting) further enhance this by capturing nonlinear relationships and interactions between features. Nevertheless, nomograms have certain advantages in terms of interpretability and clinical integration. In future research, a framework combining the two approaches could be developed, with the aim of achieving a balance between automated efficiency and clinical interpretability.

A recent MRI radiomics study of early recurrence in SNSCC enrolled 68 patients (training cohort n = 47; testing cohort n = 21), with local failure (within 12 months post-treatment) as the primary endpoint [28]. It developed the radiomics model and the clinical-radiomics combined model from 186 radiomic features, using binary logistic regression for clinical features. Consistent with our findings, the combined model (AUC = 0.850) outperformed the standalone clinical model (AUC = 0.438, P = 0.016) and slightly surpassed the radiomics model (AUC = 0.838, non-significant, P = 0.904), supporting that integrating clinical and radiomic features enhances predictive performance. As demonstrated in our study, the radiomics feature classes encompassed 14 shape features, 18 first-order features (intensity histogram) and 24 texture features (GLCM). In conclusion, a total of 56 radiomics features were extracted from the ADC images for each tumor. While advanced features such as wavelet-transformed textures and higher-order statistics may capture more complex patterns, we deliberately prioritized simpler, interpretable features in this study. Based on the principle of Occam’s Razor, if a model using fewer and clinically interpretable features already achieves satisfactory performance, the inclusion of more complex and less interpretable features may not be necessary. This decision also facilitates the clinical translation and regulatory approval processes. Our primary goal was to balance model performance with interpretability and clinical applicability. Consequently, we focused on linear classifiers — LDA, LR, and linear SVM — which are known for their transparency and ease of interpretation. In clinical settings, simpler models are often preferred for their explainability and regulatory compliance. Among the classifiers tested, LDA achieved the best balance between performance and interpretability in our dataset. We have strengthened this analysis by incorporating Brier scores to quantify calibration performance. The Brier score was 0.142 in the training cohort and 0.168 in the testing cohort, both of which indicate strong calibration. Furthermore, our stability table reports Brier scores with 95% confidence intervals — an uncommon detail in previous studies — offering a clearer assessment of model calibration and stability. This study used nested cross-validation to assess the internal stability of the model. The inner loop was responsible for hyperparameter optimization, while the outer loop was used to select the classifier and feature number. The performance of the model during validation was assessed by averaging AUC values across the outer folds. The five-fold cross-validation yielded a mean AUC of 0.82 ± 0.03, indicating that the model has good stability within the training cohort. However, it is important to emphasize that the final model evaluation was conducted on independent testing cohorts to assess generalization and reduce overfitting concerns. In addition to conventional performance metrics, calibration curves and DCA were conducted. Both assessments demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted and observed outcomes and indicated a favorable net clinical benefit, thereby reinforcing the robustness and potential clinical utility of the proposed clinical-radiomics model, despite the limitation of a relatively small sample size. Although nested cross-validation-based internal validation reduces overfitting compared to standard cross-validation, its performance estimates may still be optimistic compared to true external validation. This is particularly relevant given our modest cohort size (n = 151 for training), where the nested procedure could not fully compensate for data scarcity. Our final model was built to take the clinical and radiomics features as inputs and outputs a predicted probability for each case, enabling individualized recurrence probabilities rather than relying on population-level thresholds. To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have systematically investigated ML models integrating clinical and radiomics features for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC, as developed in our study. To aid reproducibility, we have provided detailed information on the feature extraction, selection, and model building pipeline in the Materials, which will allow others to replicate the workflow. The ML-based clinical-radiomics model provided a valuable foundation for clinicians to stratify patients at risk of recurrence more accurately and effectively identify high-risk groups, thereby optimizing treatment decisions. These decisions may include the addition or intensification of adjuvant therapy as well as the reduction in follow-up intervals to improve monitoring.

This study has several limitations beyond the inherent constraints of a retrospective design. First and foremost, the study was constrained by the lack of external validation utilizing independent datasets from multiple centers, as all the data were derived from a single institution, primarily due to the rarity of SNSCC, which results in limited eligible cases at other institutions. Second, and critically, the sample size (n = 207) was significantly below the minimum requirement (n = 381) determined by the Riley criteria for developing robust predictive models [29, 30]. An internal validation strategy was implemented using distinct training (headquarter hospital) and testing (branch hospital) cohorts acquired from two different MR scanners. This configuration inherently introduced protocol variations, which partially mimic the technical disparities encountered across different institutions. Additionally, the divergent clinical settings and patient demographics between the two sites were purposefully leveraged to approximate external validation challenges. Nevertheless, reliance on a single institution and the overall limited sample size remain significant constraints on the model’s potential generalizability and stability. Third, manual segmentation of tumor volume has proven to be challenging and contentious. It is essential to explore semi-automated or automated segmentation methods to accurately define the ROI. Fourth, some valuable biological characteristics, such as gene mutation data, were not incorporated into the model due to incomplete data availability, which may serve as crucial indicators for clinical treatment [31]. Although this study strictly adhered to the CLEAR checklist [15] and METRICS guidelines [16], achieving a methodological quality score of 81.3%, several items were not fully reported. This is also one of the limitations of this study, which will be further improved in subsequent research. Finally, despite the clinical-radiomics model achieving a high AUC, both the sensitivity and specificity of this model remained predominantly below 0.9. Given the strong scanner-dependency of DWI features, incorporating additional T1WI, T2WI and CE-T1WI sequences could reduce sequence-specific noise and improve model robustness in external validation. Consequently, the results of our study should be considered preliminary findings [5, 32]. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes, integrating multiple imaging sequences, will provide supportive evidence for clinical implementation.

Conclusions

This study developed an ML-based clinical-radiomics model that exhibited promising and stable performance for predicting recurrence risk after radical surgery in SNSCC. Given the limited sample size and quasi-external validation design, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Future investigations will prioritize multi-center external validation to further confirm clinical utility, thereby advancing toward precision medicine and facilitating individualized treatment decisions in SNSCC management.
