Atraumatic A3 Pulley Rupture in Ulcerative Colitis: A Discussion of Implications for Inflammatory Pathology of the Hand
Authors: Alejandro J Friedman, Yesmeen M Elgabori, Wilfrido J Castillo, Eloy D Tabeayo Alvarez, Steven M Koehler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease can profoundly affect the joints and tendons of the hand. We present here the first published case of an atraumatic rupture of the A3 pulley of the right second digit in a 27-year-old woman with ulcerative colitis. The patient presented with pain and functional compromise of the second digit as well as a 90° flexion contracture at the proximal interphalangeal joint. Imaging revealed rupture of the A3 pulley and bowstringing of the flexor digitorum profundus tendon despite no history of hand or finger trauma. Multidisciplinary conservative management and adjustment of antirheumatic medication resulted in partial resolution of the flexion contracture and return of pain-free function.Key words: Dactylitis, Inflammatory bowel disease, Pulley rupture, Ulcerative colitis

Gut dysbiosis and systemic inflammation in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) can have profound effects on joints and limbs. Musculoskeletal manifestations are common and represent an important mechanism through which IBD affects quality of life, function, and independence. Overall, musculoskeletal involvement occurs in approximately 50% of patients with IBD, although subclinical abnormalities involving joints or tendons may be even more prevalent.1 Peripheral oligoarthritis or polyarthritis is a common presentation and is generally associated with poorer IBD control and worse clinical prognosis.1

Recent work on the “gut-joint axis” has brought these manifestations into sharper focus and begun to elucidate molecular and immunologic mechanisms underlying joint and tendon involvement.

Historically, IBD-associated spondyloarthritis has been reported to manifest predominantly in the lower limbs, causing pain and mobility loss secondary to destruction of joints and entheses. The upper limbs may also be involved, typically presenting as synovitis or tenosynovitis affecting the proximal interphalangeal (PIP), metacarpophalangeal, or carpal joints. Dactylitis has also been described.1

Pathology of the finger flexor pulley mechanism, however, has not been previously reported in association with IBD. Given the extensive inflammatory changes that may occur within or adjacent to the joint capsule, extension of inflammation to surrounding fibrous structures is biologically plausible.

In this report, we describe a female patient who presented with finger pain and stiffness and was found to have a nontraumatic rupture of the A3 pulley of the right index finger. This case highlights a potential manifestation of inflammatory arthropathy affecting the flexor mechanism and illustrates how systemic inflammatory disease may contribute to structural failure within the hand.

Case Presentation

Informed, signed consent for publication of clinical details and images was obtained prior to the generation of this report. In writing this report, we adhered to the CARE (for CAse REports) guidelines.

A 27-year-old woman with a history of ulcerative pancolitis, initially diagnosed 5 years prior, was referred to hand surgery by another hand surgeon at our institution with worsening stiffness and functional limitation of both hands.

Roughly 4 years after initial diagnosis, the patient presented to the orthopedic surgery department for joint pain and stiffness. The patient experienced episodes of bilateral polyarthritis affecting the lower limbs, with a restricted range of motion and articular cartilage destruction most pronounced in the right knee. These symptoms were moderately controlled with physical therapy, intra-articular steroid injections, and adjustment of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs.

Monthly vedolizumab infusions stabilized most gastrointestinal symptoms. However, the patient had developed severe weight loss, with a weight of 80 pounds and a body mass index (BMI in kg/m2) of 13.9 at a primary care visit 1 month prior to presentation. Before being diagnosed with ulcerative colitis (UC), the patient’s stable weight was roughly 120 pounds, which decreased to a nadir of 77 pounds or a BMI of 13.22.

Although she had been awaiting rheumatology evaluation, she continued to follow up with hand surgery and podiatry for progressive musculoskeletal symptoms, which ultimately resulted in reliance on a walker and difficulty with sit-to-stand transfers.

She was referred to hand surgery after reporting intermittent painful swelling and stiffness of the right index finger. The patient described 3 months of intermittent swelling of the right index finger without preceding trauma. She reported inability to actively move the finger and pain with passive motion.

Physical examination demonstrated swelling and tenderness of the right index finger involving both the distal interphalangeal and PIP joints. Range of motion was limited by pain. A severe flexion contracture of approximately 90° at the PIP joint was present, with associated soft-tissue thickening and hyperpigmentation.

The left ring finger also demonstrated interphalangeal swelling and tenderness, although no deformity was observed. No other abnormalities of the hands were noted. C-reactive protein at a primary care visit immediately prior to the patient’s first hand surgery appointment was 4.8 mg/dL, indicating massive systemic inflammation.

Given the widespread tendon and joint pathology in the setting of elevated inflammatory markers, the patient was treated with oral prednisone at 30 mg daily, which was tapered over the course of several months and discontinued fully within 6 months.

With these modalities, the patient reported improvement in pain and swelling but persistent stiffness at follow-up. Surgical capsular release of the PIP joint was not pursued due to the underlying osteoarthritic degeneration. Although the A3 pulley was ruptured, the digit maintained functional flexion and repair was unlikely to meaningfully improve function in the setting of progressive inflammatory arthritis.

Conservative management restored grasp strength and allowed the patient to perform functional tasks without pain. She was counseled that PIP joint arthrodesis could be considered in the future if pain or dysfunction progressed.

During the same period, worsening endoscopic findings demonstrated progression of colitis to a Mayo score of 3 (previously 2), prompting a transition in therapy from vedolizumab to ustekinumab.

Over 6 months of occupational therapy, the flexion contracture improved from approximately 90° to 60°, although progressive stiffness developed in contralateral fingers. Continued follow-up with GI and rheumatology in addition to optimization of medical therapy resulted in a sharp and stable decrease in C-reactive protein to 0.6 ng/dL 2 months after presentation to hand surgery. Although formal nutritional intervention was not activated, the patient was able to gain weight over the course of hand surgery follow-up, from 80 pounds at initial presentation to 89 pounds, corresponding to a BMI of 15.3.

Discussion

This patient with progressive UC experienced simultaneous progression of musculoskeletal symptoms, ultimately presenting with severe, mobility-limiting polyarthritis. Although inflammatory changes in the lower limbs were consistent with synovitis and articular degeneration, the pathology observed in the hand was more consistent with tenosynovitis and tendon sheath degeneration. These findings produced symptoms resembling dactylitis, a known peripheral manifestation of UC, and ultimately resulted in atraumatic rupture of the A3 pulley of the right index finger.

The A3 pulley arises from the PIP joint volar plate and, although A3 is commonly thought to be less important in flexor tendon kinematics than A2 or A4 rupture, compromise of A3 can increase tendon excursion and produce bowstringing, effectively compromising the flexor mechanism.

Flexor pulley rupture typically occurs following repetitive or acute trauma, most commonly in rock climbers. In this case, however, a combination of dactylitis, enthesitis, and chronic tenosynovitis likely weakened the fibrous structures and resulted in atraumatic rupture.

Because the hands are frequently involved in IBD-associated arthropathy and the incidence of IBD continues to increase, hand surgeons should be familiar with its musculoskeletal manifestations.

Although pulley rupture represents a novel presentation, dactylitis is a recognized peripheral manifestation of IBD, occurring in approximately 2% to 15% of affected patients.2 Enthesitis and symmetric, small-joint inflammation of the hands are also associated with disease progression and poor disease control, although these findings often occur independently of gastrointestinal disease activity.3

IBD is driven by dysregulated intestinal mucosal immunity characterized by expansion of Th17 cell populations and increased production of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-17, and interleukin-23.4

Patients with IBD-associated spondyloarthropathy may also develop additional extraintestinal manifestations, including uveitis and sacroiliitis or ankylosing spondylitis.5 Genetic predisposition also contributes to disease expression. Several human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles—including HLA-B27, HLA-DRB1∗01:03, HLA-B35, HLA-B44—have been associated with increased risk of peripheral arthritis in IBD.6 Additional loci involving Th17-mediated inflammatory pathways have also been implicated.7

The proposed “gut-joint inflammatory axis” offers a mechanistic explanation for joint involvement in patients with gastrointestinal inflammation.6 Activated mucosal leukocytes can circulate systemically and interact with adhesion molecules within the synovium, including ICAM-1/CD54, VAP-1, CD18-ICAM-1, α4β7a4b1-integrin-VCAM-1, L-selectin, and CD44.6

Supporting this hypothesis, immune cells bearing gastrointestinal immunologic signatures have been identified within the synovium of patients with IBD-associated spondyloarthritis and ankylosing spondylitis.8 Combined with the systemic inflammatory state characteristic of UC, these mechanisms provide a biologically plausible explanation for localized inflammation affecting distal joints and tendons.

Surgeons should also recognize a possible association between vedolizumab therapy and the development of enthesitis or dactylitis, which may have contributed to our patient’s presentation. Vedolizumab is an α4β7 integrin antagonist that functions to reduce lymphocyte trafficking to the gastrointestinal tract. Because these lymphocytes are rerouted rather than inactivated or destroyed, they may be aberrantly redirected to joints and entheses in up to 21% of patients taking vedolizumab.9 Notably, Dupre et al.10 found that 91% of patients who developed joint complications from vedolizumab had previously failed tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitor therapy; indeed, our patient had failed infliximab 2 years prior to presentation. During follow-up, and concurrent with improvement in symptoms, the patient’s therapy was transitioned from vedolizumab to ustekinumab.

Although it is likely that the patient’s joint and tendon symptoms were due to uncontrolled UC or an adverse effect from medication, other potential explanations are possible. The patient’s severe weight loss and low BMI at presentation place her in the range of severe protein-energy malnutrition. In this state, collagen synthesis, maturation, and crosslinking are affected, reducing the tensile strength of tendons. Although the patient’s course of prednisone during hand surgery follow-up was relatively short and was discontinued by 6 months, review of clinical records shows that previous prescriptions for prednisone had been given by other physicians over the years since diagnosis. This chronic exposure, in addition to microtrauma to the fingers and joints resulting from the use of assistive walking devices, could have contributed to the patient’s presentation as well. It is also possible that these mechanisms could have contributed synergistically to the patient’s condition and hand pathology.

Ultimately, this patient improved with multidisciplinary conservative management, including systemic corticosteroids, occupational therapy, and adjustment of her antirheumatic regimen. Surgical intervention aimed at restoring function is more appropriate after inflammatory control. In the setting of persistent pain with deformity or arthrosis, palliative silicone spacer arthroplasty or arthrodesis can be appropriate. In cases of pulley rupture where joints are preserved, pulley reconstruction may restore native biomechanics. However, as in this case, intervention must always be balanced against an active inflammatory environment and compromised soft tissues.

Limitations of this report include those inherent to case reports as well as the absence of only late-treatment QuickDASH scores collected by occupational therapists, resulting in the reliance on clinical range of motion measurements and physical exam findings as the only outcomes we can report both pre- and posttreatment. Because we are presenting only one case, a causal relationship cannot be proposed between the patient’s active, severe UC and her A3 pulley rupture. Further prospective studies to examine this association may be useful in advancing our understanding of the relationship between IBD and hand pathology. For instance, ultrasound screening could be used to define the incidence of pulley pathology in patients with UC.

In conclusion, this case illustrates a manifestation of IBD-associated inflammatory arthritis—atraumatic rupture of the flexor pulley system of the finger. Recognition of such presentations may assist hand surgeons in identifying underlying systemic inflammatory disease and coordinating multidisciplinary care.
