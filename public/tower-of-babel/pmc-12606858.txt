Design and functional validation of a needs-driven multifunctional integrated nursing wheelchair for elder individuals with disability
Authors: Zhang Zhewen, Yu Hongliu, Zhuang Zhijie, Zhu Xuanrui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To address the complex care needs of elder individuals with disability, this study employs a Quality Function Deployment (QFD)-based approach to systematically prioritize functional requirements and guide the engineering design of a multifunctional intelligent wheelchair. The developed system integrates posture transition, omnidirectional mobility, toileting assistance, and bathing docking into a unified platform. Key innovations include a synchronized dual-actuator posture transition mechanism enabling progressive sit-to-stand support with phased center-of-gravity adjustment, a Mecanum wheel-based omnidirectional chassis for enhanced maneuverability in confined spaces, and a detachable waterproof backrest system facilitating transfer-free toileting and bathing operations. Prototype validation using CAD modeling and functional testing confirms that the integrated design significantly reduces the frequency of user transfers, minimizes secondary injury risks associated with positional changes, and improves overall caregiving efficiency and user comfort. Future work will focus on the development of multimodal interaction frameworks and adaptive decision-making systems to further enhance operational autonomy for users with communication impairments.

Introduction

With global population ageing, the number of older adults experiencing mobility impairments, declining balance, and reduced self-care capacity continues to grow [1, 2]. While wheelchairs were once primarily used by individuals with spinal cord injury or limb amputation, they have now become essential for enabling older adults to maintain fundamental daily activities such as toileting, bathing, and safe transfers. At the same time, the increasing shortage of professional caregivers—especially in developing regions—has intensified the gap between rising care demands and limited service capacity [3–5].

To address increasingly complex challenges in elderly care, wheelchair technologies have advanced considerably in recent years [6, 7]. Modern designs incorporate electric mobility systems [8, 9], elevation and posture-adjustment modules enabling sit-stand-lie transitions [10], and omnidirectional mobility systems that enhance maneuverability in confined environments [12, 13]. These developments demonstrate substantial progress in expanding wheelchair functions beyond basic mobility.

Several studies have attempted to address transfer-related challenges for severely disabled users by developing specialized devices such as transfer robots [14, 15], humanoid lifting systems [16], standing-assist chairs [17], and toileting aids [11, 18, 19]. Despite these advances, current wheelchair systems still face major limitations. Many existing solutions are designed for single, task-specific functions—such as mobility or posture regulation—without integrating multiple caregiving capabilities into a unified platform. Consequently, older adults with disabilities often require repeated manual transfers between beds, toilets, and chairs, typically with caregiver assistance [20]. This process not only increases the risk of musculoskeletal injuries for caregivers, particularly in the lower back and shoulders [21, 22], but also exposes older adults to secondary risks during uncertain transfer procedures.

In light of these challenges, there is an urgent need for a unified multifunctional wheelchair solution that can accommodate the diverse care needs of older adults with disabilities, while reducing the frequency of transfers between multiple devices and alleviating caregiver workload. This study introduces a novel integrated nursing wheelchair specifically designed for this population. Guided by the Quality Function Deployment (QFD) approach, user needs were translated into engineering specifications to develop a compact and modular system [23]. The proposed wheelchair integrates four core caregiving functions—posture transition, toileting assistance, standing support, and bathing adaptability—into a single platform. Its compact structure, compatibility with home environments, and modular adjustability address unmet requirements of existing devices, offering a more inclusive and practical solution for elderly care. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II presents the QFD-driven design framework and 3D modeling; Section III describes prototype construction and functional testing; Section IV discusses experimental results and outlines future research directions.

Analysis of the current status of nursing wheelchair research

QFD-based investigation and analysis of elderly care needs

Prior to developing the multifunctional care wheelchair, the research team conducted a systematic needs assessment targeting both caregivers and elderly individuals with disabilities. By integrating semi-structured interviews, standardized questionnaires, and field observations, two predominant challenges were identified: biomechanically demanding operations (e.g., patient transfers and posture adjustments) and insufficient functional support, particularly in privacy-sensitive activities such as toileting and bathing. Concurrently, the study systematically evaluated improvement potentials in safety mechanisms (equipment stability and misuse prevention) and comfort features (pressure distribution and ergonomic adaptability) of existing assistive devices, thereby establishing a robust empirical foundation for subsequent engineering design.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted focusing on three dimensions: biomechanical loads during physical caregiving tasks (e.g., lifting and assisted standing), functional limitations of current devices in daily activity support (e.g., toileting and bathing), and specific user requirements for safety features (e.g., anti-tip mechanisms) and comfort enhancements (e.g., pressure-relieving seat cushions). A total of 48 participants were involved in the needs assessment phase, including 36 professional caregivers and 12 elderly individuals with moderate-to-severe physical disabilities who were regular wheelchair users.

Participants were recruited from three long-term care facilities in Shanghai. Caregivers had an average of 6.8 years of caregiving experience (range: 2–17 years), and their age ranged from 25 to 59 years old. The elderly participants were aged 68–88, most of whom required daily assistance with toileting and posture transitions. A majority of the elderly participants reported requiring daily assistance with activities such as toileting and posture adjustment, which was later confirmed as a high-frequency, high-burden category in the quantitative task analysis.

In parallel, a structured questionnaire was designed to systematically collect caregivers’ demographic data (e.g., age, caregiving tenure) and task load parameters (e.g., task types, execution frequency, and per-session duration), as summarized in Table 1. Post-task physiological fatigue was assessed via Visual Analog Scales (VAS), supplemented by open-ended questions to prioritize device improvement needs and solicit specific optimization suggestions. The questionnaire emphasized task-load correlation analysis to establish a data-driven foundation for subsequent functional prioritization. The questionnaire was distributed to the same 36 caregivers, and 33 valid responses were collected (response rate: 95.8%). The sample covered a broad age and experience spectrum, providing a representative cross-section of the professional care population in institutional settings.

Posture transition mechanism

The posture adjustment system adopts a three-linkage architecture (Fig. 4), where Link 1 (backrest), Link 2 (seat), and Link 3 (leg rest) achieve coordinated motion via an optimized four-bar linkage mechanism. To improve drive efficiency, a synchronized dual-degree-of-freedom control is implemented using a single linear actuator that simultaneously drives the backrest and leg rest. To meet ergonomic requirements, a telescopic compensation mechanism is integrated into the leg rest, which automatically adjusts the shank support length during sit-to-stand transitions to accommodate biomechanical variations caused by the shifting of joint centers. This not only ensures smooth limb support but also contributes to maintaining the user’s balance by stabilizing the center of gravity (CoG) throughout the posture transition [24].

Importantly, the full recline posture also enables horizontal transfers, allowing users to move safely and comfortably between the wheelchair and a bed or other surfaces [25, 26]. To enhance comfort and prevent limb strain during such transfers, the leg rest compensation mechanism dynamically adapts to changes in leg length that occur due to the relative motion of the knee joint during recline. The kinematic diagram (Fig. 4) illustrates the sit-to-recline transition, where the linear actuator adjusts the backrest inclination (θ₁) from 96° to 180° and the leg rest elevation (θ₂) from 90° to 180°, achieving a seamless and fully extended 180° semi-reclined posture suitable for resting or transfer scenarios.

Standing assistance mechanism

Based on posture transition capabilities, dual linear actuators (L1/L2) are integrated to provide safe, progressive standing assistance. As shown in the motion sequence (Fig. 5), a phased center-of-gravity control strategy is adopted: Initially, actuator L1 increases the backrest inclination from 95° to 101°, transitioning users to a semi-reclined position (hip angle Φ₁=105°) with a 120 mm posterior shift of the center of gravity to prevent forward tipping. Subsequently, synchronized activation of L1 and L2 elevates the backrest-seat assembly (angular velocity ω = 2°/s), resulting in a 260 ± 10 mm vertical center-of-gravity lift to achieve a full standing posture (knee angle Φ₂ = 155°).

Toileting assistance device

Needs assessment indicated a high dependency on toileting assistance among moderately to severely elderly individuals living with disability, with significant fall risks during postural transitions. To mitigate transfer risks, a downward-flipping central seat panel is incorporated. As shown in Fig. 3, actuator L3 rotates the central seat panel downward while simultaneously elevating the waste diversion device to align with the toilet. Users can complete toileting without transfer when the wheelchair is positioned above the toilet. The system directs waste into the toilet, effectively preventing environmental contamination.

Omnidirectional chassis and detachable backrest

To enhance maneuverability in confined indoor environments, the chassis adopts four Mecanum wheels (Fig. 3), enabling forward/backward movement, lateral translation, and in-place rotation. This omnidirectional system enables flexible navigation through narrow corridors and tight turning spaces, significantly improving operational convenience.

For bathing scenarios, a detachable backrest system was developed (Fig. 3). By rotating the armrest, the locking mechanism is disengaged, allowing quick removal of the intelligent navigation-integrated backrest. The perforated support backrest improves cleaning efficiency, enabling direct bathing on the wheelchair and minimizing transfer-associated fall risks.

Prototype development and experimental validation

The physical prototype demonstration of integrated wheelchair system

A full-scale functional prototype of the integrated care wheelchair was fabricated based on the finalized CAD models and modular design specifications. The physical prototype incorporates all key assistance modules, including posture adjustment, standing support, bed-to-chair transfer, toileting assistance, and bathing docking capabilities. Figure 6 displays the assembled prototype, while detailed system specifications are listed in Table 3. All performance parameters were empirically verified through bench tests and field simulations. The overall dimensions (800 mm × 650 mm × 1500 mm) were determined based on ergonomic constraints and typical spatial allowances in home and institutional settings, ensuring the wheelchair can maneuver in doorways and bathrooms with limited clearance. The total weight of the system (42 kg) includes structural components, actuators, onboard electronics, and batteries, measured using a calibrated industrial-grade platform scale. The maximum load capacity of 180 kg was selected to accommodate overweight users and provide safety redundancy. The travel speed (5 km/h) aligns with standard assisted walking pace, allowing safe and coordinated indoor mobility. The height adjustment range (450–650 mm) is optimized to match the surface heights of most hospital beds (typically 480–600 mm), enabling level transfer and minimizing lift gaps.

Control system design

The quality of human–machine interaction is a core criterion for evaluating wheelchair control systems. Therefore, prior to building the proposed control system, we reviewed existing interaction modes, including joystick, voice, eye-tracking, tongue switches, posture-based inputs, and brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) [27–30]. While these methods expand accessibility, many require sustained attention or fine motor control, making them less suitable for elderly users with cognitive or perceptual impairments [31, 32]. To address this limitation, recent studies have highlighted shared autonomy and navigation support as promising solutions [33]. In this work, we implement a multimodal interaction framework tailored to older adults: joystick control for those able to operate independently, and voice-assisted control for those requiring caregiver support. Both inputs are processed by an onboard Jetson Nano, which fuses sensor data and user commands to generate motion instructions for the central controller, thereby enabling real-time coordinated control of the multifunctional nursing wheelchair. As shown in Fig. 7, the integrated control system comprises four subsystems: a sensing module, a user interaction module, a computational control unit, and an actuator system. The sensing module includes a 3D LiDAR unit (Unitree Robotics, China) with a maximum scanning range of 15 m and measurement error of 5–8 cm, enabling accurate environmental perception, obstacle avoidance, and path planning in three-dimensional space. In addition, two ultrasonic sensors mounted bilaterally provide high-precision proximity detection (0.5–2 mm error) during bed docking, ensuring accurate lateral alignment through guidance of the omnidirectional Mecanum wheelbase.

System-level hardware architecture of the multifunctional elderly care wheelchair

The actuator system (Fig. 8) includes five linear actuators and four independently driven Mecanum wheels. Among them, actuators L1 and L3 coordinate to achieve multi-posture transitions by adjusting the seatback tilt and compensating for footrest extension. Actuators L1, L2, and L3 together enable assisted standing functionality, gradually elevating the user from a seated to a semi-standing position. Actuator L4 controls the opening and closing of the toileting mechanism, which allows users to defecate while remaining seated after aligning the wheelchair over a toilet. Actuator L5 provides vertical lift capability for height adjustment, ensuring level alignment with beds of different heights and enabling safe lateral transfers. All actuators adopt a screw-driven linear design, which provides stable force output and precise telescopic motion control. The Mecanum wheel chassis, driven by four hub motors, supports omnidirectional movement including lateral translation and zero-radius rotation. This design allows the wheelchair to operate flexibly in confined environments such as bathrooms or beside hospital beds, significantly reducing the need for manual repositioning by caregivers.

The coordinated architecture of the hardware and control systems ensures that the wheelchair can perform complex care-related tasks with high precision, while maintaining modular adaptability and user safety. The combined use of real-time environmental sensing, closed-loop feedback control, and high-degree-of-freedom actuation establishes a robust platform for intelligent mobility and multifunctional assistance in eldercare scenarios. In addition to functional integration, durability and maintainability were also key considerations in the system design. All mechanical components were constructed using wear-resistant materials and modularized assemblies to facilitate long-term use in daily care environments. The actuators and sensing modules are designed for easy replacement and low maintenance, ensuring operational reliability and reducing downtime. These considerations collectively enhance the practical applicability of the wheelchair in institutional and home-based eldercare settings.

The physical prototype demonstration of integrated wheelchair system

The experimental testing in this study was conducted using the physical prototype operated by members of the research team acting as test users. At this stage, the primary aim was to validate the mechanical functions and assistive capabilities of the developed wheelchair under controlled conditions. As the current prototype is designed specifically for elderly individuals with significant mobility impairments, the tests were performed by healthy researchers who intentionally limited their movements to simulate typical use cases of target users. No human subjects with actual disabilities were involved in this phase of testing.

Multi-posture transition validation testing

Comprehensive functional tests were performed, covering posture adjustment, standing assistance, bed transfer, toileting aid, and bathing docking. Standing assistance tests (Fig. 9) demonstrated that during the sit-to-stand transition, the system initially shifts the equivalent center of gravity backward (backrest angle α = 9° relative to the vertical axis), then synchronizes the backrest and leg rest movements to achieve a 165° hip joint angle in the sagittal plane, and finally adjusts the seat inclination to 75° (β). This phased standing strategy improves lower limb circulation via adjustable back support, while the 75° seat inclination facilitates active posture adjustment for rehabilitation. Gradual transitions prevent forward leaning and reduce the risk of orthostatic hypotension associated with sudden 90° elevation [45].

Experimental results and discussion

No noticeable vibration or mechanical interference was observed during the experimental procedures, as assessed through direct visual inspection by the operator. To gain deeper insight into the wheelchair’s posture transition capabilities, the NOKOV optical motion capture system was employed to synchronously record and analyze the joint angle parameters of both the wheelchair and the user throughout the transition process. As illustrated in Fig. 13, the changes in the user’s knee joint angle (θ₂) and hip joint angle (Φ₂) exhibited a strong correlation with the corresponding variations in the wheelchair’s knee joint angle (θ₁) and hip joint angle (Φ₁). Minor discrepancies between the two sets of measurements were attributed to the displacement of the knee joint’s rotational center during flexion, which effectively altered the equivalent length of the lower leg. To compensate for this variation, an automatic footrest adjustment mechanism was introduced, enabling dynamic length correction of the user’s lower leg and allowing full knee extension to 180°, thereby maintaining horizontal alignment with the wheelchair.

During the standing support phase, an intentional initial offset was observed between the user’s and the wheelchair’s knee joint angles. This strategy was adopted to enable gradual adaptation of the user’s knee joint to the increasing vertical load, thereby minimizing the risk of instability due to sudden force application. In the final standing posture, the wheelchair’s hip joint was deliberately not adjusted to a perfectly vertical position. This design choice reflects the limited weight-bearing capacity of elderly users with disabilities; by introducing a slight backward inclination, a portion of the user’s body weight is redirected onto the seat, effectively reducing the risk of forward tilting and loss of balance.

Fig. 13Multi-posture Transition Validation Testing

Multi-posture Transition Validation Testing

In assessing the wheelchair’s assistive transfer capability, a critical performance metric is its ability to achieve precise horizontal alignment with a hospital bed. This requires the wheelchair to successfully transition into the appropriate posture and autonomously navigate from a predefined starting position to the designated docking location. According to our prior experimental observations, a horizontal docking gap of ≤ 5 mm between the wheelchair and the bed is sufficient to ensure a safe and effective transfer process. To validate this capability, we conducted 10 experimental trials. These trials involved 10 laboratory volunteers recruited to help simulate the transfer procedure. To better represent variability in elderly users, the volunteers’ heights ranged from 155 to 170 cm and their weights ranged from 45 to 80 kg. Each volunteer participated in a single trial, allowing us to assess the wheelchair’s docking performance across a representative range of body sizes.

To eliminate confounding variables, all trials were conducted with the wheelchair initialized at the same starting point. A fixed control program and movement trajectory were employed to autonomously direct the wheelchair to the bed docking location, during which posture adjustment and vertical elevation were executed as needed. As shown in Fig. 14, the recorded docking times remained consistently around 17 s across all trials. Additionally, the maximum lateral gap between the wheelchair and the bed did not exceed 5 mm, thereby fulfilling the predefined safety requirements for bed-to-wheelchair transfer operations.

Furthermore, the results from the joint angle monitoring during posture transitions (Fig. 13) and the repeated docking and transfer tests (Figs. 14 and 15) suggest that the system maintains stable motion trajectories and accurate alignment throughout critical care-related operations. No instability, abrupt mechanical responses, or misalignments were observed during these tests that could lead to secondary falls. Although the current study did not include direct biomechanical simulations or clinical evaluations of fall risk, the experimental outcomes indicate that the system is capable of mitigating key risk factors commonly associated with secondary falls, such as sudden postural shifts, improper alignment during transfers, or loss of balance. These findings provide preliminary support for the claim that the system has the potential to reduce secondary fall risks in practical caregiving scenarios.

In practical deployment, however, the system is intended to operate under fully autonomous control, with real-time path planning and execution based on sensor data, including 3D LiDAR, processed by a central controller. Although the autonomous navigation module is still under development, it remains a primary focus of future work. Nonetheless, the results obtained from the current functional verification experiments demonstrate that the wheelchair effectively fulfills a range of assistive tasks, including posture transitions, bed docking, and toilet alignment. These outcomes validate the feasibility and robustness of the integrated design and provide a solid foundation for further system enhancement and real-world application in intelligent caregiving environments.

Conclusions

Guided by a QFD-based analysis of caregiving demands for older adults living with disability, this study integrated posture adjustment, toileting assistance, height adaptation, standing support, and omnidirectional mobility into a unified multifunctional wheelchair platform. Prototype development and validation experiments indicate that the proposed design addresses diverse daily care needs, helps reduce secondary fall risks during transfers and posture transitions, and improves task efficiency. Importantly, the system is intended to meet the care requirements of elderly users while reducing transfer frequency across multiple devices and easing caregiver workload.

Current limitations include accommodating users with severe cognitive or verbal communication impairments. Future work will explore multimodal interaction frameworks (e.g., vision-based gesture recognition, simplified natural-language commands, and adaptive habit-learning) together with efficient, low-power sensor fusion and context-aware decision making to further lower the burden of direct control for both elderly users and caregivers.

Future studies will incorporate trials with older adults, including those with varying levels of mobility limitations, to evaluate usability, safety, and ergonomic performance in real-world scenarios following ethical approval and appropriate risk assessment protocols. In addition, long-term clinical and deployment studies will assess impacts on caregiver workload, user autonomy, and secondary-injury prevention. Through iterative design optimization and AI integration, the goal is to establish a scalable intelligent care platform adaptable to the evolving needs of elderly and disabled populations in home and institutional settings.
