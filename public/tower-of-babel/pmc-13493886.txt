Hybrid physics-informed machine learning framework for calibration-free degradation prediction of lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Ali M Eltamaly, Zeyad Almutairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium-ion battery degradation prediction traditionally requires chemistry-specific laboratory calibration, limiting scalability across diverse operating conditions and cathode materials. This work proposes a Hybrid Physics-Informed Machine Learning Degradation Model (PIML-DM) that enables calibration-free and physics-guided SoH prediction without chemistry-specific laboratory parameterization using only operational telemetry. The framework integrates a dual-branch architecture in which an LSTM network learns nonlinear aging residuals, while a physics-constrained loss enforces Arrhenius temperature kinetics, Wöhler fatigue stress, and strict monotonicity. To rigorously evaluate cross-chemistry robustness, the framework is trained on the NASA LCO dataset, validated on the Oxford NCA dataset, and benchmarked using a locally acquired LFP dataset. Despite the substantially different voltage signatures and degradation pathways across these chemistries, the PIML-DM achieves sub-0.5% RMSE and maintains physically consistent SoH trajectories without prior calibration. The results demonstrate that shared physics-guided degradation priors enable robust generalization across previously unseen battery chemistries, including LFP systems, while substantially reducing the need for chemistry-specific laboratory characterization and calibration procedures. Introduction

Background and motivation

The global transition toward sustainable electrification is fundamentally underpinned by lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), which have established themselves as the dominant technology for electric vehicles (EVs) and grid-scale energy storage systems (ESS). As deployment scales into the gigawatt-hour range, the ability to accurately quantify and predict State of Health (SoH) evolution has emerged as a central techno-economic challenge. LIB degradation is a coupled, multi-scale process driven by complex electrochemical, mechanical, and thermal stressors1. Variations in Depth of Discharge (DoD), temperature, and Crate drive mechanisms, such as Solid-Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) thickening, lithium plating, and active material fatigue, progressively reduce usable capacity and power capability2.

Misestimation of these degradation pathways carries significant consequences. Overestimation of battery health can lead to premature system failure and catastrophic safety incidents, while underestimation necessitates excessive system oversizing, significantly inflating the total cost of ownership. Consequently, robust degradation prediction is essential for system design, warranty planning, and real-time control strategies. However, a critical bottleneck remains: conventional aging models typically require extensive laboratory calibration, pre-cycling tests3,4, or periodic capacity measurements that interrupt operation and restrict applicability across diverse chemistries5.

Limitations of existing modeling paradigms

Current modeling strategies for LIB aging are generally categorized into three distinct classes, each possessing critical limitations for scalable real-world deployment:

Empirical4 and Semi-Empirical Models5: These approaches rely on fitted functional relationships, often exponential or power-law forms, to approximate the impact of stress factors on SoH. While their computational simplicity suits rapid evaluation, they suffer from a fundamental lack of flexibility. Models calibrated on one dataset often fail when applied to dynamic, real-world profiles or alternative cell chemistries, requiring costly recalibration.

Recent advances in transfer learning, transformer architectures, and adaptive machine learning have significantly improved the generalization capability of data-driven battery prognostics frameworks. Recent studies demonstrated strong cross-condition and transferable SOH estimation performance using feature-transfer strategies, interpretable feature engineering, and adaptive learning methods9–11. These developments have substantially improved the scalability and data efficiency of modern battery prognostics approaches.

Nevertheless, many purely data-driven frameworks remain predominantly statistical and may still exhibit limited physical interpretability, sensitivity to distribution shift, or physically inconsistent extrapolation under unseen operating conditions11,12. Furthermore, many existing approaches do not explicitly enforce physics-guided degradation behavior or degradation monotonicity during optimization. These limitations motivate the integration of physics-guided regularization within the proposed hybrid framework.

Research gap and objectives

Recent advances in Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) attempt to bridge these gaps by incorporating mechanistic insights into data-driven structures. While promising, existing PIML frameworks remain constrained by a reliance on partial laboratory calibration or periodic reference capacity measurements11. Existing hybrid frameworks still rely on chemistry-specific calibration, periodic reference measurements, or explicit electrochemical parameter identification. The proposed framework instead enables calibration-free deployment using operational telemetry alone.

To address this, this study proposes a Hybrid Physics-Informed Machine Learning Degradation Model (PIML-DM). This framework delivers a calibration-free, non-invasive, and self-adaptive deployment degradation prediction framework using only operational signals (voltage, current, temperature). The architecture integrates a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) backbone to capture non-linear aging residuals with a physics-constrained loss function that embeds Wöhler fatigue theory, Arrhenius thermal kinetics, and Crate stress factors. By enforcing physics-guided degradation behavior and monotonic capacity fade, the model eliminates non-physical predictions. Validation across heterogeneous datasets demonstrates a sub-0.5% prediction error and a substantial reduction in laboratory characterization requirements.

Unlike conventional, recent semi-empirical and hybrid battery prognostics frameworks have increasingly incorporated adaptive parameter-learning strategies in which degradation-related coefficients are treated as latent variables optimized directly from operational data. These developments have substantially improved the flexibility and scalability of semi-empirical modeling approaches compared with traditional fixed-parameter formulations. Nevertheless, many existing approaches still rely on chemistry-dependent initialization, domain-specific calibration procedures, parameter re-identification, or adaptive retraining when transferred across different battery chemistries or operating conditions. In contrast, the proposed framework evaluates frozen-weight cross-chemistry transfer without chemistry-specific recalibration or electrochemical parameter re-estimation during deployment.

Recent studies have investigated transferable and data-efficient battery prognostics under resource-constrained conditions. Su et al.16 demonstrated that SoH prediction robustness strongly depends on data sufficiency and cross-domain transferability under heterogeneous operating conditions. However, despite improved transfer-learning capability, such approaches remain primarily data-driven and do not explicitly enforce physics-based degradation consistency, motivating the development of the proposed physics-informed framework.

Related work

Research into LIB degradation modeling has evolved significantly, spanning empirical lifetime estimation, detailed electrochemical formulations, and modern data-driven techniques. This section synthesizes these developments, highlighting the specific limitations that motivate the proposed PIML-DM framework.

Empirical and semi-empirical models

Empirical models3–5,17,18 represent the earliest and most widely adopted class of degradation predictors. These approaches, such as those by Marongiu et al.19 and Serrao et al.20, typically utilize curve-fitting techniques on controlled laboratory cycling data. They derive simple relationships describing capacity fade as a function of stress factors like temperature and DoD. While their computational simplicity facilitates system-level simulation, their primary weakness is a lack of transferability. Empirical relations are inherently chemistry-specific; a model calibrated on Lithium Iron Phosphate, LiFePO (LFP) cells often fails when applied to Lithium Nickel Manganese Cobalt Oxide (NMC) chemistries without complete recalibration21. Even semi-empirical extensions that incorporate cycle-counting rules or fatigue formulations continue to require extensive laboratory characterization to determine fitting parameters18.

Physics-based electrochemical models

In contrast, physics-based models aim to describe degradation by resolving the governing mechanisms within the cell, such as SEI growth, particle cracking, and electrolyte decomposition. Models such as those reviewed by Zhang et al.22 offer valuable mechanistic interpretability and are indispensable for cell design optimization. However, their practical application in operational systems is severely limited by two factors: the need for detailed internal parameters that are rarely measurable in situ, and a high computational cost. As noted by Raissi et al.23, the complexity of solving coupled partial differential equations makes these models unsuitable for real-time integration into standard BMS hardware.

Data-driven and machine learning approaches

The advent of deep learning has popularized data-driven models due to their ability to map complex, non-linear correlations in voltage, current, and temperature trajectories7,8,24,25. Techniques ranging from neural networks to Gaussian Process Regression, as demonstrated by Severson et al.7 and Attia et al.8, have shown promising performance on curated datasets. Despite their predictive power, these models generally lack interpretability and struggle to extrapolate26,27.

Although modern AI-based battery prognostic models have achieved excellent predictive accuracy, purely data-driven learning frameworks may still generate non-monotonic or physically inconsistent degradation trajectories when extrapolating beyond the statistical distribution represented in the training data, particularly in long-horizon forecasting scenarios11,22. This limitation has motivated increasing interest in physics-informed neural networks, constrained optimization methods, and hybrid degradation-learning frameworks that embed electrochemical priors directly into the learning process11,23.

Recent transformer-based and transfer-learning approaches have improved cross-domain transferability and data efficiency9,10. However, many existing methods still rely primarily on statistical feature learning without explicitly enforcing physics-guided degradation behavior or degradation monotonicity during optimization16. The proposed framework, therefore, aims to complement these advances by integrating physically guided constraints with sequential representation learning.

Recent studies have also focused on improving SoH estimation through efficient feature engineering. Su et al.12 proposed an incremental-capacity feature-selection framework that balances estimation accuracy and computational efficiency for lithium-ion battery SoH prediction. Their work demonstrated the importance of selecting informative degradation features while reducing redundancy and computational complexity. However, such approaches still rely heavily on handcrafted feature extraction and do not explicitly enforce physics-based degradation constraints during learning.

Physics-informed hybrid frameworks

Hybrid approaches, often grouped under PIML, seek to combine the adaptability of data-driven methods with the rigor of mechanistic understanding14,20,23,24,28,29. Raissi et al.23 pioneered Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), where differential equations serve as constraints within the loss function15. In the battery domain, works by Hofmann et al.28 and Sharma et al.14 have integrated physical constraints to improve stability. However, despite these advances, existing PIML methods still rely on at least partial laboratory calibration, parameter identification, or periodic reference measurements.

The work proposed herein fundamentally departs from these precedents. Unlike prior hybrid methods that require laboratory characterization, the PIML-DM framework learns degradation dynamics exclusively from routine BMS telemetry while preserving physical consistency through embedded mechanistic constraints. By eliminating the need for calibration and ensuring real-time adaptability, this approach addresses the scalability and economic challenges that have historically hindered advanced battery health prediction.

Contributions and theoretical foundations

The rapid acceleration of global electrification strategies demands energy storage systems that are not only energy-dense but also predictable and economically viable over long operational horizons. While existing literature has established robust frameworks for battery modeling, a critical dichotomy persists between mechanistic fidelity and computational feasibility. This study bridges that divide by introducing the Hybrid PIML-DM, a framework designed to operate autonomously in real-world environments without the prohibitive cost of laboratory calibration.

Scientific contributions

This work advances the state of the art in LIB prognostics through five distinct scientific contributions that address the limitations of empirical, electrochemical, and purely data-driven paradigms.

First, we establish a Calibration-free Hybrid Degradation Framework. Traditional semi-empirical models19,20 and electrochemical formulations22,23 rely heavily on pre-determined parameters such as diffusion coefficients or stress factors that must be derived from extensive laboratory characterization. This requirement creates a “calibration bottleneck” that hinders scalability. The PIML-DM circumvents this by treating degradation parameters not as static constants to be measured, but as latent variables to be inferred. By utilizing a dual-branch neural architecture, the model autonomously learns the specific aging dynamics of a cell directly from operational telemetry (voltage, current, temperature), effectively enabling “zero-shot” deployment on new battery chemistries without prior cycling tests24.

Second, this study introduces Physics-Constrained Deep Learning Integration. While data-driven models7,8 offer exceptional flexibility in mapping non-linear aging trajectories, they frequently suffer from “physical hallucination” predicting thermodynamically impossible phenomena such as spontaneous capacity recovery or inverted temperature dependence when extrapolating beyond training data30. To resolve this, fundamental electrochemical laws are embedded directly into the learning objective. By incorporating Wöhler fatigue theory and Arrhenius thermal kinetics as regularization terms in the loss function, the PIML-DM forces the neural network to converge on solutions that are not only statistically accurate but also electrochemically consistent. This ensures that predicted degradation remains monotonic and thermodynamically valid, even in unexplored operating regions23.

Third, this study introduces the concept of Real-Time Adaptive Learning as a future deployment-oriented extension of the proposed framework. Unlike static models that gradually lose accuracy as degradation dynamics evolve, the proposed adaptive mechanism is designed to enable progressive refinement of internal network representations using streaming BMS telemetry during long-term operation. Such adaptive updating may improve robustness near degradation knee points and under evolving operational conditions. However, to preserve strict zero-shot evaluation integrity, the adaptive learning mechanism was not activated during the cross-chemistry benchmarking experiments reported in this study. All reported results correspond exclusively to frozen-weight inference without online retraining or parameter updating.

Fourth, the framework achieves Cross-Chemistry Generalization. The weakness in empirical modeling is the lack of transferability; a model calibrated for LFP fails when applied to NMC chemistries. By grounding the learning process in fundamental physics, specifically, the universal nature of Arrhenius kinetics and mechanical fatigue, the PIML-DM extracts generalized aging features that transcend specific cathode chemistries. Validation across heterogeneous datasets, including NASA LCO and Oxford NCA, confirms the model’s ability to transfer learned physics to unseen chemistries with minimal error14,28.

Finally, this work bridges the gap between interpretability and precision. Purely data-driven “black box” models often fail to provide actionable insights into why a battery is degrading. By structuring the PIML-DM to output the contributions of physical stress factors (thermal vs. mechanical), this framework offers explainable diagnostics. It allows operators to distinguish whether capacity fade is driven primarily by high Crate thermal stress or DoD-related fatigue, facilitating targeted life-extension strategies.

Theoretical foundations

To rigorously justify the PIML-DM architecture, the problem of battery degradation must be reformulated from a static estimation task to a differential state-space problem governed by coupled electrochemical and mechanical phenomena.

Phenomenological mechanisms of degradation

LIB aging is a multi-scale process driven by the irreversible consumption of cyclable lithium and the structural degradation of host materials.

Solid-Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) Growth: The primary driver of capacity fade in the early lifecycle is the formation and thickening of the SEI layer on the anode. This parasitic side reaction consumes lithium ions and increases internal impedance. The reaction rate is fundamentally governed by temperature and follows Arrhenius kinetics, where degradation accelerates exponentially with thermal stress6.

Mechanical Fatigue and Active Material Loss: During lithiation and delithiation, electrode particles undergo volume expansion and contraction. Over thousands of cycles, this repetitive mechanical stress leads to particle cracking, electrical isolation, and Loss of Active Material (LAM). This mechanism mirrors classical mechanical fatigue and is theoretically described by Wöhler curves, which relate the magnitude of stress DoD to the number of cycles until failure31.

Methodology

The methodological framework of this study is designed to ensure reproducibility, data integrity, and rigorous validation. The PIML-DM is implemented as a modular deep learning pipeline that ingests raw BMS telemetry and outputs probabilistic health predictions. This section details the data acquisition strategy, the harmonization of heterogeneous datasets, the hybrid neural architecture, and the specific training protocols employed.

Data acquisition and harmonization

A significant challenge in data-driven battery research is the variability in testing protocols across different datasets. To ensure the PIML-DM is robust and chemistry-agnostic, a multi-source data strategy was employed, harmonizing three distinct datasets into a unified tensor format.

Sliding window generation

To capture the path-dependent nature of degradation, data were structured into multi-channel sliding windows. Each input sample Xt consists of a matrix of dimension L times 4, where L = 60 represents the window length (in seconds or sampling steps) and the 4 channels correspond to Voltage, Current, Temperature, and SoC. This windowing approach allows the model to learn short-term dynamic responses (e.g., voltage relaxation, internal resistance heating) that serve as precursors to long-term aging.

Hybrid network architecture

The PIML-DM architecture is a dual-branch neural network designed to fuse sequential data learning with physical law enforcement. The architecture was implemented using TensorFlow and custom layers in MATLAB.

The core of the data-driven estimator is an LSTM network32. LSTMs were selected over standard Feed-Forward Networks (FNNs) due to their ability to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and capture long-term temporal dependencies essential for modeling cumulative fatigue.

The fusion layer

The outputs of the LSTM branch (the residual degradation) and the Physics Layer (the theoretical degradation) are concatenated and passed through a final fully connected layer to output the predicted SoH and the instantaneous degradation rate dSoH/dt.

The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10− 3. To ensure convergence on the complex loss landscape, a learning rate scheduler that decays the rate by a factor of 0.95 every epoch is utilized. Early stopping was implemented with a patience of 8 epochs to prevent overfitting. The training was executed with typical convergence achieved in under 30 epochs, corresponding to approximately 8 s per epoch.

It should be noted that the monotonicity constraint is imposed on the predicted latent degradation trajectory rather than on instantaneous raw capacity measurements. Therefore, the proposed regularization does not attempt to eliminate reversible electrochemical recovery effects observed experimentally, but instead constrains long-term degradation evolution during sequential forecasting to improve physical consistency and extrapolation stability.

Zero-shot evaluation protocol

To rigorously evaluate the cross-chemistry generalization capability of the proposed PIML-DM framework, a strict zero-shot evaluation protocol was implemented to ensure complete isolation between the source-domain training data and the target-domain validation datasets.

The NASA randomized battery usage dataset (LCO chemistry) served as the sole dataset for model development, including training, hyperparameter optimization, and validation. The dataset was partitioned chronologically into 70% training data, 15% validation data, and 15% testing data. The training subset was used for parameter optimization through backpropagation, while the validation subset was employed exclusively for hyperparameter tuning, learning-rate scheduling, and early-stopping control. The held-out NASA test subset was utilized only for the final in-domain evaluation after training convergence.

To preserve strict zero-shot transfer conditions, the Oxford NCA dataset and the proprietary LFP dataset were treated as completely external blind-validation datasets. No information from the Oxford or LFP datasets was used during:

All model weights, including the learnable coefficients within the physics-informed layer, were frozen after completion of training on the NASA dataset. The Oxford and LFP datasets were subsequently evaluated without retraining, fine-tuning, parameter re-estimation, or domain adaptation. Furthermore, normalization statistics were computed exclusively from the NASA training split. Specifically, the mean and standard deviation used for Z-score normalization were derived only from the NASA training subset and then reused unchanged for the NASA validation/testing partitions as well as the external Oxford and LFP datasets. This protocol prevents information leakage from target chemistries into the training pipeline and ensures methodological consistency for zero-shot evaluation.

All reported benchmark results in this study correspond exclusively to frozen-weight zero-shot evaluation without online adaptation. During cross-chemistry benchmarking on the Oxford NCA and proprietary LFP datasets, no online updating, continual learning, parameter adaptation, or streaming-data refinement mechanisms were activated. The evaluation, therefore, reflects the intrinsic transferability of the learned physics-guided degradation representations under strictly fixed model parameters. The online adaptive learning mechanism discussed in this work is proposed as a future deployment-oriented extension intended for long-term field operation under evolving degradation conditions. This adaptive mechanism was not activated during the reported zero-shot benchmarking experiments and therefore does not influence the presented cross-chemistry evaluation results.

The resulting evaluation framework, therefore, assesses the ability of the proposed PIML-DM to generalize degradation behavior across unseen battery chemistries using only physics-guided transferable representations learned from the source-domain operational telemetry.

Computational efficiency and edge deployment

A critical requirement for this study was ensuring that the resulting model is deployable on standard BMS hardware. The computational complexity of the trained inference model is dominated by the LSTM matrix multiplications, scaling as O(W. H2), where W is the window size, and H is the number of hidden units. With the selected architecture (W = 60, H = 64), the total number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) per inference step is sufficiently low for real-time execution.

In our validation setup, inference benchmarking was performed on an Intel Core i7 CPU using a batch size of 1 under MATLAB execution. The average inference time was measured at 0.15 s on a standard CPU, utilizing less than 5 MB of memory. This confirms that the PIML-DM meets the stringent low-power, low-latency requirements of embedded automotive and grid-storage applications.

Input data and experimental framework

To ensure the proposed PIML-DM possesses both high fidelity and robust generalization capabilities, a comprehensive multi-source data strategy was employed. The validity of any data-driven framework is fundamentally constrained by the quality and diversity of its training data. Consequently, this study moves beyond reliance on a single homogeneous dataset, instead aggregating data from three distinct sources that represent a spectrum of operational realities: the stochastic volatility of the NASA randomized battery usage dataset, the controlled precision of the Oxford battery degradation dataset, and a proprietary experimental dataset derived from a local laboratory test5.

This heterogeneous data strategy is designed to encourage the model to learn transferable degradation behavior rather than overfitting to a single operational protocol. By exposing the neural network to both chaotic, real-world load profiles (NASA) and pristine, constant-current laboratory cycles (Oxford), the architecture is encouraged to learn transferable degradation representations regularized by shared physics-inspired degradation trends.

Dataset characterization

NASA randomized battery usage dataset

The NASA Prognostics Center of Excellence (PCoE) dataset serves as the primary training ground for capturing non-linear behavior under stochastic loading. Unlike standard cycle-life tests that utilize repetitive Constant Current-Constant Voltage (CC-CV) protocols, this dataset subjects varying commercial 18,650 LCO cells to randomized current profiles.

Operational relevance

The randomized load profiles mimic the erratic power demands of an EV33,34 driving in an urban environment or a grid ESS performing frequency regulation. The current fluctuates rapidly between high-rate discharge pulses and regenerative charging spikes.

Thermal variance

The dataset includes runs at ambient temperatures ranging from 24 °C to 43 °C. This thermal variability is critical for training the Arrhenius component of the PIML-DM, enabling the model to learn the activation energy (Ea) associated with SEI thickening.

Degradation threshold

Data is recorded continuously until the cells reach 70% of their nominal capacity, providing a complete view of the “knee-point” transition where degradation accelerates.

Results and discussion

The performance of the proposed PIML-DM is evaluated across three datasets representing fundamentally different lithium-ion chemistries: LCO (NASA), NCA (Oxford), and LFP (local dataset). The objective is not only to assess predictive accuracy but also to understand how the hybrid physics–ML structure behaves under diverse degradation pathways, voltage signatures, and thermal sensitivities. The following subsections provide a detailed, essay-style analysis of the model’s behavior, with emphasis on generalization, mechanistic consistency, and practical interpretability.

Training dynamics and physics-guided convergence

The evolution of the loss functions during training (Fig. 3) reveals important insights into how the PIML-DM internalizes degradation behavior for data sets. During early epochs, the LSTM branch rapidly reduces the data-driven loss, indicating that it successfully captures coarse patterns in behavioral aging associated with cycle count, temperature, and throughput. However, without physical constraints, such rapid learning frequently leads to overfitting hidden nonlinearities or sensor fluctuations, which manifests as unrealistic SoH “recovery events” or temperature-inverted responses. The physics-informed loss counteracts these tendencies by penalizing violations of Arrhenius kinetics, Wöhler fatigue behavior, and monotonic SoH decay.

Training and physics loss convergence for different data sets.

This two-phase convergence, initial rapid empirical fitting followed by slow regularization through physical consistency, illustrates the synergy between physics and deep learning. By the mid-training stage, the physics-informed branch begins dominating the loss landscape, guiding the model toward physically interpretable representations that remain data-coherent but no longer mimic deviations specific to a single chemistry. This behavior appears important for achieving cross-chemistry robustness, especially when eventually transferring the model from LCO to NCA and LFP. The strong transferability observed in the present study should not be interpreted as evidence that degradation mechanisms are identical across chemistries, but rather that the proposed framework learns sufficiently transferable degradation representations under the evaluated operational domains.

Figures 4, 5, 6 and 7 demonstrate that the PIML-DM provides highly accurate predictions on the LCO dataset used for training. The scatter plot between predicted and measured SoH confirms minimal bias and negligible systematic drift even at higher cycle numbers. A particular strength of the model is its ability to reproduce the onset of knee-region behavior without explicit cycle-lifetime indicators, something that purely empirical models consistently struggle with.

Overall, the analysis supports the robustness of the proposed framework under chemistry-dependent parameter variability and further strengthens the validity of the proposed physics-informed learning strategy for heterogeneous lithium-ion battery systems.

Uncertainty quantification and confidence analysis

Reliable deployment of battery prognostic models in practical BMS requires not only accurate predictions but also confidence estimation associated with the predicted SoH. To assess predictive uncertainty, an uncertainty-quantification analysis was incorporated into the proposed PIML-DM framework using Monte Carlo (MC) dropout inference.

During inference, dropout layers remained active, and multiple stochastic forward passes were performed for each input sequence. Specifically, 100 Monte Carlo samples were generated for each prediction, enabling estimation of both the predictive mean and prediction variance. The final SoH estimate was computed as the mean of the stochastic predictions, while the predictive uncertainty was represented using ± 1 standard deviation confidence intervals.

Figure 12 illustrates the predicted SoH trajectories together with the associated uncertainty bands for representative cells from the NASA, Oxford NCA, and local LFP datasets. The confidence intervals remain narrow during stable degradation phases and gradually widen near end-of-life regions where degradation acceleration and operational variability become more pronounced. This behavior is physically reasonable because uncertainty naturally increases during highly nonlinear aging stages.

The uncertainty analysis further demonstrates that the proposed physics-informed constraints contribute to stabilizing the prediction distribution. Compared with purely data-driven models, the PIML-DM exhibits lower variance and fewer abrupt uncertainty spikes under noisy operating conditions. The embedded Arrhenius and fatigue-based constraints effectively regularize the prediction space and suppress nonphysical deviations that commonly amplify uncertainty in unconstrained neural-network models.

Quantitatively, the average prediction uncertainty remained below ± 0.42% SoH for the NASA dataset and below ± 0.65% for the cross-chemistry validation datasets, indicating stable generalization behavior despite chemistry variations and operational heterogeneity.

These results demonstrate that the proposed framework provides not only accurate SoH estimation but also meaningful confidence information that can support risk-aware battery management, predictive maintenance scheduling, and reliability-aware operational decision-making in EV and grid-scale energy storage systems.

Input window-length sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the influence of temporal memory length on degradation-learning performance, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted using different sliding-window lengths. Since lithium-ion battery degradation is inherently path-dependent and governed by cumulative thermal, electrochemical, and mechanical stress history, the selection of the temporal input horizon plays a critical role in balancing prediction accuracy, long-term dependency capture, and computational efficiency.

The proposed PIML-DM framework was retrained using three different input-window configurations consisting of 30, 60, and 120 sampling steps while preserving identical training, validation, and testing protocols. All hyperparameters, optimizer settings, physics-informed loss coefficients, and network architectures were maintained unchanged to ensure fair comparison. The experiments were performed using the NASA LCO training dataset under the same zero-shot evaluation conditions described previously.

The results demonstrate that the temporal window length significantly influences the model’s ability to capture degradation dynamics. Using a short window length of 30 sampling steps reduces computational cost but limits the network’s ability to capture cumulative electrochemical stress history, thermal accumulation effects, and fatigue-related degradation memory. Consequently, the model exhibits reduced prediction accuracy and weaker representation of long-term degradation evolution.

Increasing the window length to 60 sampling steps substantially improves prediction accuracy and stability. This configuration provides sufficient temporal context for the LSTM branch to learn the coupled effects of temperature, C-rate, depth-of-discharge, and transient voltage behavior while maintaining moderate computational complexity. The results indicate that the 60-step window achieves the optimal trade-off between degradation-memory representation and training efficiency.

Further increasing the window length to 120 sampling steps yields only marginal improvements in prediction accuracy while substantially increasing training time and memory consumption. Excessively long windows introduce redundant temporal information and increase optimization complexity without providing proportional gains in degradation representation. Additionally, longer sequences increase the risk of over-parameterization and slower convergence during backpropagation through time.

Overall, the analysis confirms that the selected 60 × 4 input representation provides an effective balance between temporal dependency learning, physical degradation-memory capture, and computational efficiency. These findings further support the suitability of the proposed framework for real-time battery management system deployment under constrained computational environments.

Conclusion and future work

This work presents a Hybrid Physics-Informed Machine Learning Degradation Model (PIML-DM) that delivers transferable, non-calibrated state of health (SoH) estimation across multiple battery chemistries. Unlike conventional methods that rely on explicit electrochemical parameter identification or laboratory-derived calibration curves, the PIML-DM learns directly from field-measured telemetry while enforcing universal degradation principles via physics-guided constraints. The results demonstrate that this hybrid architecture can achieve high accuracy on the source LCO chemistry and, more importantly, preserve accuracy and physical fidelity when transferred to NCA and LFP datasets without retraining or tuning.

The findings highlight a fundamental advantage of the hybrid approach: machine learning alone is insufficient for universal battery prognostics, whereas electrochemical models alone lack flexibility. The combination of the two yields a model that is both expressive and constrained, capable of capturing nuanced nonlinearities while avoiding nonphysical degeneration. The success on the LFP dataset, despite its drastically different voltage characteristics, underscores the robustness and generalizability of the approach and sets the stage for practical deployment in modern BMS platforms.

Looking ahead, several promising extensions can further enhance the model’s accuracy, interpretability, and applicability. Incorporating additional physics mechanisms, such as cathode-specific structural degradation for NCA or diffusion-driven dynamics for LFP, may allow the model to capture intermediate phenomena not explicitly represented in the present loss function. Integrating electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) features, now increasingly available in commercial BMS hardware, could enable fine-grained separation of SEI growth, charge-transfer resistance, and active-material loss. At the system level, scaling the model to pack-level prognostics through graph neural networks would unlock the ability to model cell interactions, thermal gradients, and balancing behavior. Finally, embedding online uncertainty quantification or adopting federated learning strategies would allow deployment across distributed storage fleets while maintaining privacy and interpretability.

Future work will investigate fully integrated online adaptive learning strategies for long-term field deployment under evolving operational conditions and degradation regimes. While the present study intentionally isolates frozen-weight zero-shot transferability to rigorously evaluate cross-chemistry generalization, future adaptive extensions may further improve robustness under aging drift, battery-to-battery variability, and degradation knee-point transitions in practical BMS environments.
