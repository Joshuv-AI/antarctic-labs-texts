Evaluation of strength characteristics of artificial permafrost based on electrical resistivity
Authors: Zhijie Li, Wei Shen, Ya Chu, Yikun Chen, Chao Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial ground freezing (AGF) is widely applied in complex geotechnical environments; however, in situ evaluation of frozen soil strength remains limited. This study employs a self-developed embedded four-electrode system to investigate the strength and electrical properties of water-rich silty clay from the Yangtze River floodplain. Results indicate that unconfined compressive strength (UCS) increases with decreasing temperature in a “slow–sharp–slow” pattern and rises with dry density. A critical water content of 22% produces peak strength, beyond which frost-heave cracking reduces strength, while near-saturation (~ 30%) promotes the formation of an ice skeleton that partially restores it. Resistivity exhibits stepwise growth with decreasing temperature, primarily due to free water freezing and increased tortuosity, with sharper conductivity losses at higher water contents. A conduction shift occurs at a critical dry density, transitioning from pore water dominated to bound water and soil matrix dominated pathways. Resistivity follows a power-law relationship with saturation, controlled by bound water freezing and frost-induced deformation. Both UCS and deformation modulus correlate strongly and linearly with resistivity. A resistivity-based model is proposed to link strength with microstructural and moisture changes. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analyses reveal that higher water content enlarges pores, while freeze–thaw cycles transform flaky aggregates into honeycomb structures and convert micropores into macropores, highlighting the coupled influence of phase transitions and pore evolution on strength. These findings enhance understanding of the frozen soil–resistivity coupling mechanism and provide a basis for accurate strength evaluation using resistivity.Keywords: Artificial permafrost resistivity, Four-phase electrode method, Unconfined compressive strength, Electrical prediction model, Microscopic damage mechanismsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Artificial ground freezing (AGF) is widely applied in geotechnical engineering projects under complex geological conditions, as well as in emergency responses to sudden engineering challenges1. Frozen soil is a complex four-phase system composed of mineral particles, ice, water, and gas. The phase transition and migration of pore water can not only cause surface deformation but also affect the mechanical behavior of artificially frozen ground. This is due to the filling and cementation effects of pore ice, as well as the alterations in pore structure and connectivity induced by water–ice phase changes2. Currently, strength assessment of frozen soil mainly relies on laboratory tests and field temperature measurements to develop empirical models. However, these traditional methods are limited by low efficiency, long duration, and high cost, making comprehensive and accurate evaluation difficult3,4. Therefore, developing rapid in situ methods for evaluating the strength characteristics of artificially frozen ground is crucial for ensuring construction safety and efficient assessment in frozen ground engineering.

Research on the mechanical properties of artificially frozen soil primarily focuses on four aspects: (1) analyzing the influence of freezing temperature, ice content, and unfrozen water content on unconfined compressive strength, and exploring the strength variation mechanisms associated with ice–water phase transitions through microstructural analysis5; (2) establishing strength criterion models under varying confining pressures and stress release conditions to clarify the effects of the ice skeleton, soil skeleton, and their interactions; (3) investigating softening mechanisms, strength evolution, and creep behavior under complex stress conditions6; and (4) examining damage evolution under freeze–thaw cycles and developing damage constitutive models to support long-term stability evaluation. The mechanical behavior of artificially frozen soil is jointly determined by water–ice phase transitions, microscopic pore structure, and freeze–thaw-induced damage.

Electrical resistivity testing, as a non-destructive, rapid, and cost-effective geophysical technique, has been widely used in hydrogeology and geotechnical engineering7. Soil resistivity serves as a key indicator for in-situ property evaluation, as it reflects the medium’s water content and pore structure. In artificially frozen soils, both strength and electrical properties are influenced by the coupled effects of temperature, dry density, water content, and pore structure. Due to its distinct phase transitions and physicochemical characteristics, frozen soil is particularly suitable for resistivity-based testing. Therefore, investigating the correlation and mechanisms between strength and resistivity under various freezing conditions, and establishing predictive resistivity models that bridge macroscopic behavior and microstructural evolution, is essential for in-situ strength assessment of frozen ground.

In recent years, several researchers have explored the correlation between the strength and electrical responses of artificially frozen soils through laboratory studies. Jia et al.8 conducted simultaneous tests of unconfined compressive strength (UCS) and resistivity using a two-phase electrode method, revealing the melting mechanism of pore ice in high-pressure zones from the perspective of resistivity variation. Ming and Bai9,10 considered the dynamic variation in pore tortuosity under subzero conditions and developed a pore-network conduction model that reflects the evolution of unfrozen water content in pore fluids and on particle surfaces. Since unfrozen water is a key factor influencing the mechanical properties of frozen soils, Zhao et al.5 conducted a dedicated investigation on the relationship between unfrozen water content and strength characteristics of artificial frozen soils, demonstrating an exponential relationship between soil strength and unfrozen water content.

Fu et al.11 established an empirical function relating resistivity and UCS through laboratory testing; however, the coupling mechanisms of initial state parameters (such as moisture content and dry density) were insufficiently addressed, leading to high data discreteness in the developed predictive model. Cai et al.12 performed compression tests on frozen soils using an improved oedometer and similarly observed that frozen soils exhibit distinct electrical responses sensitive to stress variation during loading. Hou et al.13 investigated the resistivity-based nondestructive testing model for UCS of cemented tailings under multiple freeze–thaw cycles. Nevertheless, the above studies have not yet provided an in-depth explanation of the electrical response mechanism of strength from a micro-damage and failure perspective.

Overall, existing studies on the engineering evaluation of artificial frozen soils still lack systematic research on the correlation between strength and resistivity and their governing mechanisms. Qualitatively, current strength evaluation models fail to account for the pressure-melting effect in high-stress zones and do not adequately analyze the microscale influences of key parameters such as freezing temperature and dry density. Quantitatively, the physical interpretation of parameters in existing resistivity–strength models remains ambiguous, and there is limited discussion regarding the applicable range and data dispersion of these models. Moreover, few studies have addressed the electrical response characteristics or predictive models of mechanical parameters such as deformation modulus in elastoplastic analyses.

In laboratory electrical testing of artificial frozen soils, although the two-phase electrode method is widely adopted, it is prone to interference from polarization and contact resistance14. At low temperatures, the decrease in water content causes significant measurement errors, which hinders the derivation and validation of reliable strength–resistivity models. Therefore, a nondestructive and efficient electrical testing method is needed. The van der Pauw method, commonly used for surface resistivity measurements of cemented soils and other hard geomaterials, determines resistivity through the solution of multipath electric potential Eq15. Accordingly, by replacing traditional electrode probes with copper ring electrodes in a four-electrode configuration, resistivity parameters reflecting both the microstructural and macroscopic properties of frozen soils can be obtained in a nondestructive and rapid manner. Based on this approach, further investigation into the strength–resistivity coupling mechanism and electrical response characteristics of artificial frozen soils can be conducted, considering the evolution of unfrozen water content and pore structure during the freezing process.

This study focuses on water-rich silty clay sampled from the floodplain facies of the Yangtze River. Mechanical and resistivity properties were measured under varying freezing temperatures, water contents, and dry densities. The relationships between these parameters, temperature, and microstructure were analyzed to reveal the governing mechanisms. Resistivity is proposed as a bridge linking the macroscopic engineering behavior of frozen soil to microstructural and moisture changes. A predictive model for engineering parameters based on resistivity is established. Furthermore, the model’s precision and data dispersion are explained from a microscopic perspective, considering electromechanical responses to freezing mechanisms. Microstructural tests are also conducted to further elucidate damage mechanisms following freeze–thaw cycles. The study aims to quantify engineering properties of frozen ground using resistivity parameters and to provide both data support and theoretical foundations for the application of resistivity-based methods in frozen soil evaluation.

Experimental methods

Synchronized testing of deformation, resistivity, and moisture in artificially frozen soil

This study employed a synchronized testing system to simultaneously monitor deformation, moisture, and resistivity during freeze–thaw cycles (Fig. 4). The system consists of five components: a constant temperature and humidity control unit (LHS-150HCN), a distributed surface resistivity testing device based on an LCR meter, a TDR moisture–fiber-optic temperature sensor (TDR-315 N), a displacement sensor (2308-3AC), and a data acquisition and analysis system. It was used to measure unidirectional frost heave and thaw settlement as well as the corresponding resistivity of frozen soil under varying freezing temperatures, dry densities, and water contents.

Test items and parameter settings for artificial frozen soil experiments.

In a controlled temperature and humidity environment, surface resistivity was measured using a custom-modified distributed four-electrode Wenner array system, following the ASTM G57-20 standard16. Cylindrical soil specimens (5 cm in diameter, 10 cm in height), prepared using static compaction, were placed in acrylic sleeves with embedded copper ring electrodes (serving as distributed measurement points) connected to the acquisition device via sidewall leads. Extruded polystyrene (XPS) boards were wrapped around the sleeves to enhance lateral confinement. At both the top and bottom of each specimen, permeable stones were installed, with copper ring electrodes inserted between the soil and the stones. Conductive paste was evenly applied to the copper electrodes before testing to improve conductivity and reduce noise. Deformation was recorded in real-time using two mechanical digital dial gauges (accuracy: 0.01 mm), fixed with magnetic bases to the loading platform. Their probes were positioned on either side of the top permeable stone to capture axial deformation. Moisture and surface temperature were continuously monitored using the TDR sensor, which features a volumetric water content accuracy of ± 0.02% and temperature accuracy of ± 0.02 °C. All sensor leads exited through the sidewalls or copper permeable stones and were connected to the data acquisition hub. Once all components were confirmed functional, the environmental chamber and acquisition system were activated simultaneously.

Preliminary tests indicated that the coupling of moderate-to-high water content and dry density had a significant influence on soil strength. Therefore, freeze–resistivity tests were conducted under six controlled temperatures (0 °C, − 1 °C, − 2 °C, − 5 °C, − 15 °C, − 30 °C), covering 17 representative initial conditions: For water contents of 22% and 26%, dry densities ranged from 1.4 to 1.6 g/cm³ at 0.05 g/cm³ intervals; For dry densities of 1.5 and 1.55 g/cm³, water contents ranged from 14% to 30% in 4% intervals. The test plan is illustrated in Fig. 3 and detailed in Table 2. All specimens were preconditioned at 2 °C for 2 h prior to freezing to ensure uniform initial moisture and temperature. For each condition, three parallel specimens were prepared (Fig. 3; Table 2), and the variation in frost heave and resistivity among them was required to be within 2%. Final results were reported as the average values of frost deformation and resistivity across the three replicates.

Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) testing of artificially frozen soil

UCS tests were conducted on specimens previously used for frost heave measurements, using an Instron 5985 universal testing machine (Fig. 4). First, the low-temperature chamber was set to the same freezing temperature used in the resistivity tests and maintained for 2 h. The acrylic-encased specimen was then quickly transferred to the chamber of the UCS apparatus. After an additional 2-hour temperature holding period to ensure internal equilibrium, the specimen was subjected to unconfined compression at a loading rate of 0.5 mm/min. The maximum compressive strength was recorded. The deviation of UCS among the three parallel specimens at each freezing temperature was controlled within 2%. A total of 306 matched data pairs of resistivity and UCS peak strength were obtained, processed via data acquisition systems and subsequent computer analysis.

BET and SEM microstructural analyses of artificially frozen soil

Preliminary tests and previous studies showed that water content had a more significant effect on the disruption of the soil skeleton and particle aggregates than dry density. Therefore, BET analysis was conducted on thawed specimens with a constant dry density of 1.5 g/cm³ and varying water contents (14%–30%, in 4% intervals) to characterize pore structure changes. Compared to variations in dry density under the same water content, these specimens exhibited more distinct structural changes after thawing. The BET-specific surface area and pore characteristics were obtained using an Autosorb NOVA 2200e system via nitrogen adsorption–desorption at 1 atm and 77.3 K. This provided data on specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter for assessing the impact of freeze–thaw cycles on pore structure.

At lower initial water contents (14%, 18%), the amount of ice crystals formed was limited, resulting in minimal effects on the soil skeleton and pore structure compared to specimens with higher water contents (22%, 26%, 30%). Therefore, SEM observation focused on specimens with dry density of 1.5 g/cm³ and moderate-to-high initial water contents to examine microstructural damage after frost heave and thaw settlement. SEM imaging was conducted using a Regulus8100 microscope. Prior to imaging, specimens were coated with gold using an ion sputtering device to enhance surface conductivity and imaging quality. SEM was operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV with magnifications of 3000–4500×, suitable for observing particle-level microstructures. Analysis focused on the disintegration of particle aggregates and pore morphology to reveal microstructural damage features.

Sample preparation

Initial field samples of undisturbed soil were pretreated by removing impurities and coarse particles. The remaining material was oven-dried and ground according to the Standard for Soil Test Methods17, then passed through a 0.1 mm sieve for later use. Silty clay was then mixed uniformly with deionized water according to the designed mix ratios and sealed in a container for 24 h of curing. A five-point sampling method was used to verify that the variation in water content across different locations in the container met standard specifications, ensuring uniform moisture distribution.

Sample preparation followed the “under-compaction method” proposed by Ladd et al.18. The prepared soil was divided into six layers and compacted into a cylindrical metal mold (50 mm in diameter, 100 mm in height), with each upper layer compacted to a dry density 2% higher than the one below. This method minimizes particle segregation and improves the reliability of test results. The preparation procedure is illustrated in Fig. 4. Artificially frozen soil specimens were prepared under 17 representative initial conditions: At water contents of 22% and 26%, dry densities ranged from 1.4 to 1.6 g/cm³ at 0.05 g/cm³ intervals; At dry densities of 1.5 and 1.55 g/cm³, water contents varied from 14% to 30% in 4% increments. All specimens were prepared in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment (25 °C, 28% RH). Upon completion, each sample was wrapped in plastic film and sealed in a plastic bag, then placed in a constant temperature and humidity chamber to minimize moisture loss.

After completing the first round of resistivity–frost heave and thaw settlement tests, specimens with a dry density of 1.5 g/cm³ and various water contents (fully frozen at − 30 °C and then thawed) were selected. Each specimen was divided equally into three sections along its height. Since freeze–thaw-induced tension and compression damage tends to concentrate in the middle portion, a representative subsample was taken by collecting and mixing 5 g of soil from the central area of both the upper and lower surfaces of the middle section. These subsamples were dried in a 105 °C convection oven for 24 h, ground, and passed through a 0.5 mm sieve before being sealed and stored. The prepared samples were then used for subsequent microstructural analyses of freeze–thaw damage, including both quantitative BET analysis and qualitative SEM imaging.

Analysis of test results of unconfined compressive strength of artificially frozen soil

Variation of unconfined compressive strength with temperature

The variation in unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of clay under different water contents and dry densities with decreasing temperature is shown in Fig. 5. As observed, when the freezing temperature ranges from 0 °C to − 2 °C, strength fluctuations across all water content and dry density conditions are minor and relatively insignificant. This is primarily because the actual freezing point of pore water in soil is approximately − 2 °C. Two main factors contribute to this depression in freezing point: (1) the presence of dissolved inorganic salts and organic matter in the pore water produces a solute effect that lowers the freezing point, and (2) surface tension within the fine capillaries of the soil pores inhibits ice formation19. Therefore, only a small volume of pore water freezes near 0 °C, resulting in no significant strength increase compared to unfrozen soil.

Comprehensive variation of unconfined compressive strength of artificial frozen soil with temperature at different dry densities and moisture contents: (a) Different dry densities; (b) Different moisture contents.

Between − 2 °C and − 5 °C, a substantial portion of the free water in the pores freezes, leading to a marked increase in strength. Figure 5a shows that specimens with higher dry density exhibit the greatest strength gain. This is attributed to the denser arrangement of soil particles and lower porosity, which allows ice crystals to effectively fill and support the fine pores. Figure 5b indicates that the most significant strength increase occurs at a moderate water content of 22%. At low water contents, the limited amount of ice formation contributes minimally to the development of a soil–ice skeletal structure. Conversely, at high water contents, the soil skeleton is inherently weaker, and insufficient bonding occurs at the soil–ice interfaces, limiting strength enhancement at this stage.

When the temperature drops below − 5 °C, nearly all free water within the pores freezes, further amplifying strength differences among specimens with different initial conditions. Specimens with higher water contents (> 22%) and higher dry densities (> 1.5 g/cm³) show notable increases in strength. As freezing continues to lower in temperature, bound water on particle surfaces begins to freeze according to the theory of sequential layer freezing. This process reduces the thickness of adsorbed water films and significantly strengthens inter-particle bonding20, ultimately resulting in a substantial increase in soil strength. A comparison between Figs. 5a, b reveals that, with continued temperature decrease, the variation in strength gain among specimens with different dry densities (at the same water content) is smaller than that among specimens with different water contents (at the same dry density). This suggests that, under subzero conditions, the initial water content exerts a more dominant influence on the strength of artificially frozen soils than changes in pore structure induced by variations in dry density.

Variation of unconfined compressive strength with moisture content and dry density

As shown in Fig. 6a, when the dry densities are fixed at 1.5 g/cm³ and 1.55 g/cm³, the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of artificially frozen soil exhibits a temperature-dependent relationship with water content. As the freezing temperature decreases, the UCS increases across all water content conditions. However, due to the coupled effects of soil–ice phase transitions and pore structure evolution, the relationship between strength and water content displays notable complexity. At low water contents (14% and 18%), the amount of freezable water is limited. Even at lower temperatures, the formation of ice crystals remains insufficient to significantly enhance the structural stability of the soil.

Comprehensive variation of unconfined compressive strength of artificial frozen soil at different freezing temperatures with dry density and moisture content: (a) Different moisture contents; (b) Different dry densities.

At a moderate water content of 22%, as the freezing temperature drops further (down to − 30 °C), all free water and a portion of bound water freeze. Ice crystals fill the interparticle pores completely, forming a dense ice crystal network that tightly integrates the soil skeleton with the ice skeleton. This network efficiently transmits and disperses external loads, resulting in excellent bonding and a highly stable soil–ice structure. Consequently, strength peaks near this optimal water content. When the water content increases to 26%, the system enters a supersaturated ice condition below − 5 °C, and strength begins to decrease. The substantial phase transition from water to ice causes volumetric expansion. If the expansion exceeds the compensating effect of negative pore water pressure, the soil undergoes frost heave, fracturing the original soil skeleton and creating new freeze-induced cracks. At this stage, the structural support shifts to an ice-dominated skeleton, reducing overall strength21. At a higher water content of 30%, the pores approach full saturation and contain ample freezable water. Although this leads to the formation of a dense ice skeleton with larger average pore sizes and coarser ice crystal branches, the resulting robust physical structure partially restores compressive strength. Nevertheless, it still falls short of the peak strength achieved at the critical water content of 22%.

Figure 6b demonstrates that, at fixed water contents of 22% and 26%, UCS increases with rising dry density. Higher dry densities promote a more uniform pore water distribution, allowing the freezing process to more effectively fill particle-scale pores with ice crystals. This facilitates the formation of a stable, saturated pore ice structure22, and the interaction between the ice crystal network and the soil skeleton significantly enhances the structural integrity of the frozen soil. Notably, once the freezing temperature drops below − 2 °C, the rate and magnitude of strength increase both accelerate. More free water transforms into ice, and in the micro-pores of high-density soils, the combined effects of expansion pressure and frost pressure lead to a pronounced increase in effective stress within the soil skeleton. By − 30 °C, the partial freezing of bound water further strengthens the bonds between soil particles and between particles and ice crystals, stabilizing the ice–soil interface. This dense ice crystal network, particularly in soils with moderate to high water content (22% and 26%), results in a more cohesive and mechanically robust structure. As a result, dry density increasingly enhances the soil’s resistance to external loads with decreasing temperature, and this reinforcing effect becomes more pronounced at lower freezing temperatures.

Variation of unconfined compressive strength with saturation

The variation of unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of artificially frozen soil with saturation under different freezing temperatures is shown in Fig. 7.

The unconfined compressive strength of artificial frozen soil varies with saturation at different freezing temperatures.

Consistent with the previously described temperature-dependent strength behavior: when the freezing temperature is above − 5 °C, only a small amount of free water freezes due to solute effects and surface tension, which is insufficient to alter the soil–ice skeletal structure, resulting in negligible strength changes. As the freezing temperature decreases, strength increases with rising saturation; however, due to the coupled effects of ice skeleton formation from the water–ice phase change and pore structure alterations induced by frost heave, the relationship between strength and saturation exhibits unique characteristics.

At low saturation levels, the limited amount of ice crystals cannot form a continuous network within the larger pore spaces, and the soil skeleton primarily bears the load. As saturation increases, the soil–ice skeleton interaction gradually dominates, and soil strength reaches its peak once a critical saturation level corresponding to ice crystal saturation is achieved. If saturation increases further, oversaturation of ice crystals causes expansion pressures that compress and damage the soil skeleton. When initial pore water is fully saturated, the ice skeleton predominantly supports the strength, leading to a partial rebound in strength. This transition and interaction between soil and ice skeletons cause the UCS to follow a distinctive trend with saturation: an initial increase followed by a fluctuating plateau phase after saturation exceeds approximately 80%. With further temperature reduction, all free water freezes and fills the soil skeleton pores, accelerating the rate of strength gain. When adsorbed water also begins to freeze, strength further improves, and the strength differences among soils with varying initial saturations become more pronounced. Additionally, the amplitude of these fluctuations increases as the temperature decreases.

Analysis of experimental results on electrical properties of artificial permafrost

Electrical resistivity is an inherent property of soil, influenced by multiple factors such as water content, salinity, mineral composition, and cementation degree. As a typical four-phase medium composed of water, soil, ice, and air1–3, artificially frozen soil’s strength and resistivity are primarily affected by its initial dry density, initial water content, and freezing temperature. Investigating the resistivity of frozen soil—which reflects water–ice phase changes and pore structure evolution—helps clarify the mechanisms and patterns of strength and resistivity variation during freezing. By linking microstructural and moisture changes during freezing, this approach seeks to reveal the relationship between resistivity parameters and the engineering properties of artificial frozen soil, ultimately establishing resistivity-based methods for evaluating these engineering characteristics.

The resistivity response of artificial frozen soil during freezing is governed by the coupled effects of freezing temperature, initial dry density, water content, and saturation. Studying the variation of electrical parameters under these multiple influencing factors is crucial for understanding the underlying conduction mechanisms and for developing correlations with engineering performance.

Resistivity variation with temperature

The variation of freezing temperature directly affects the amount of unfrozen water, which in turn influences the electrical resistivity of frozen soil by altering the activity of conductive ions. The comprehensive changes of resistivity in artificially frozen soil with different water contents and dry densities as temperature varies are shown in Fig. 8, with the following observed trends: The resistivity of artificial frozen soil continuously increases as temperature decreases. Between 0 °C and − 2 °C, due to solute effects and surface tension, the freezing point of the soil is depressed, and water remains predominantly in the form of unfrozen water. Within this temperature range, the decline in temperature primarily weakens the activity of conductive ions rather than causing phase transformation. Consequently, the soil resistivity exhibits only a minor increase of approximately 10–30 Ω·m, showing a slight upward trend with decreasing temperature23.Notably, soils with lower water content exhibit higher resistivity values and greater rates of increase with decreasing temperature compared to soils with higher water content.

Comprehensive variation of artificial frozen soil resistivity with temperature at different moisture contents and dry densities: (a) Different moisture contents; (b) Different dry densities.

In the freezing temperature range of − 2 °C to − 5 °C, the resistivity increases rapidly, with an overall increment of approximately 50–800 Ω·m. Soils with higher initial water content exhibit a markedly greater rise in resistivity than those with lower water content. This behavior arises because electrical conduction in cohesive soils occurs through three parallel pathways: conduction along the soil skeleton, through the pore water, and via water–soil coupling interfaces. The corresponding conductive path schematics and equivalent circuit representations for samples with different water (ice) states are illustrated in Figs. 9 and 10. At this stage, most free water in soil pores freezes, and ice crystals fill the pores, which on one hand reduces pore water—the primary conductive pathway—thus lowering water content; on the other hand, ice obstructs conductive pathways and increases the tortuosity of electrical flow. Due to the combined effects of reduced cross-sectional area and fewer conductive paths, soil resistivity increases by orders of magnitude. The higher the initial water content, the more pronounced the weakening of pore conduction channels caused by the water–ice phase transition24.

Schematic diagram of conductive pathways in samples with different water (ice) content states.

Schematic diagram of equivalent resistance for samples in different water (ice) states.

When the freezing temperature ranges from − 5 °C to − 30 °C, most free water in the soil has frozen. Bound water, which adsorbs onto soil particle surfaces, freezes sequentially from loosely bound water on the outside to more strongly bound water inward25. The conductive pathways formed by remaining bound water gradually freeze and become blocked. However, since the total amount of bound water is small and freezes slowly, resistivity at this stage increases steadily and continuously but at a slower rate.

Variation of resistivity with moisture content and dry density

Figure 11(a) shows the variation of electrical resistivity in artificially frozen soil with different water contents at fixed dry densities of 1.5 g/cm³ and 1.55 g/cm³ across various freezing temperatures. In the freezing temperature range of 0 °C to − 2 °C, since the pore water has not yet reached its freezing point, soil resistivity decreases as the initial water content increases. Although lower temperature tends to increase resistivity by reducing ionic activity, the effect of increased water content dominates by enhancing pore water connectivity, increasing conductive pathways, and reducing electrical tortuosity, thus leading to a net decrease in resistivity.

Comprehensive variation of artificial frozen soil resistivity with dry density and moisture content at different temperatures: (a) Different moisture contents; (b) Different dry densities.

When the freezing temperature drops to − 2 °C, resistivity increases with increasing water content. The rate of increase is slower in the low water content range (14%–18%) because the limited pore water allows only a small amount of ice crystals to form, which cannot substantially alter the conductive pathways. In cohesive soils, electrical conduction occurs through three parallel routes—along the soil skeleton, through the pore water, and via water–soil coupling interfaces. As a result, several conduction channels remain active even as freezing progresses, leading to a relatively minor rise in resistivity. The schematic diagrams and equivalent circuit models illustrating the conductive paths under different water (ice) states are shown in Figs. 9 and 10. In the moderate water content range (18%–26%), resistivity varies markedly with water content under different freezing temperatures, showing a pronounced increase of approximately 10²–10⁴ Ω·m. This indicates that soils within this moisture range are highly sensitive to temperature-induced phase changes, as the transformation of pore water into ice substantially alters the connectivity and continuity of conductive pathways. With similar pore conditions, more ice crystals form, and once a critical saturation threshold is reached, the connected ice crystal network effectively blocks internal conduction paths, increasing pore tortuosity and severely impeding current flow, causing resistivity to rise rapidly24. At high water contents (> 26%), the rate of resistivity increase slows with further water content increase. Here, pore water approaches saturation, and ice content reaches a supercritical state. The frost heave caused by the water–ice phase change significantly exceeds the contraction due to negative pore water pressure26, causing soil frost heave deformation and new freeze-induced cracks. Conductive pathways are mostly limited to the soil skeleton and a small amount of bound water, as shown in the schematic diagram of conductive paths for samples with different water (ice) states in Fig. 9. Compared to the moderate water content range, although frost heave deformation increases electrical tortuosity, the cross-sectional area remains unchanged, resulting in a slightly slower resistivity growth rate. However, because a large amount of water freezes to ice, resistivity remains at a high level. As freezing temperature continues to drop, more pore water converts to ice crystals, further reducing conductive pore water pathways and increasing tortuosity through the ice crystal network. Consequently, at the same water content, resistivity and its growth rate both increase as temperature decreases.

Figure 11(b) depicts the variation of resistivity with dry density at fixed water contents of 22% and 26% across different freezing temperatures. In the 0 °C to − 2 °C range, since pore water remains unfrozen, resistivity decreases with increasing dry density. Higher dry density tightens soil particle packing, raises pore water saturation, increases conductive paths through the soil skeleton, enlarges effective pore diameters, improves connectivity, and reduces electrical tortuosity24.

When the freezing temperature reaches − 2 °C, resistivity first increases then decreases with rising dry density, peaking near 1.55 g/cm³. Before this critical dry density, increasing dry density compacts particles, uniformly disperses water in smaller pores at the same water content, and increases pore water saturation. After reaching the ice saturation threshold in pores, the ice crystal network reduces conductive paths and increases tortuosity; meanwhile, frost heave damages conductive paths in the soil skeleton, causing resistivity to rise with dry density. Beyond the critical dry density, as freezing temperature decreases, conductive pathways dominated by free water gradually transition to those controlled by bound water. Although bound water conducts less effectively than capillary or gravitational water, its role becomes significant once free water fully freezes. Additionally, with increasing dry density and frost suction, soil particles are compressed and inter-particle cementation strengthens. Under conditions of high dry density and low freezing temperature, the influence of bound water and soil skeleton conduction on overall conductivity is amplified, leading to a decrease in resistivity.

Resistivity variation with saturation

The variation of electrical resistivity in artificially frozen soil with saturation at different temperatures is shown in Fig. 12. When the freezing temperature is above − 2 °C, due to solute effects and capillary tension, the soil has not reached its freezing point. In this range, resistivity decreases as saturation increases because higher saturation enhances pore water connectivity, increases conductive pathways, and reduces tortuosity. As saturation approaches full saturation, the connectivity of pore water reaches an optimum, causing the rate of resistivity decrease to slow down. At this stage, temperature has little effect on resistivity.

The resistivity of frozen soil varies with saturation at different temperatures.

When the freezing temperature falls below − 2 °C, resistivity increases with saturation, but the growth rate gradually slows down. As previously analyzed, increasing water content promotes the formation of ice crystals in soil skeleton pores. The higher the dry density, the more limited the pore space for ice crystals, making it easier to reach an ice saturation threshold. With decreasing temperature, some conductive pathways formed by bound water freeze, and increasing initial water content combined with continuing temperature reduction induces frost heave, further weakening the conductive pathways in pores and the soil skeleton9, as illustrated by the schematic diagrams and equivalent circuit models of conductive paths for samples with different water (ice) states in Figs. 9 and 10. Therefore, the number and tortuosity of conductive pathways in artificial frozen soil are significantly influenced by initial water content and pore structure, which in turn affect the macroscopic resistivity. It is evident that the initial saturation corresponding to the ice crystal saturation level better reflects the resistivity variations of soil at various freezing temperatures from both macroscopic and microscopic perspectives.

Prediction of parameters of artificial permafrost engineering properties based on resistivity indexes

Based on the above analysis, the variation patterns of unconfined compressive strength of artificial frozen soil with initial water content, temperature, dry density, and saturation have been clearly established. From a microscopic perspective, the intrinsic mechanisms driving strength changes—caused by water–ice phase transitions and alterations in pore structure under different freezing temperatures—have also been revealed. Simultaneously, the electrical resistivity of artificial frozen soil exhibits similar response patterns to freezing temperature-induced changes in ionic activity, water–ice phase transitions, and parameters such as initial water content, dry density, and saturation. From an electrical standpoint, this explains the conductive pathways and mechanisms under varying water (ice) states. Therefore, by using the water–ice phase transition and microstructural changes at different freezing temperatures as a bridge, a correlation between resistivity and engineering characteristic parameters can be established, enabling the prediction of artificial frozen soil engineering properties based on resistivity measurements.

Based on previous analyses, both resistivity and UCS of artificial frozen soil are significantly affected by temperature-induced water-ice phase transitions and changes in pore structure. Accordingly, following a temperature-dominant principle, the resistivity data in Fig. 13 (showing UCS versus resistivity under normalized parameters) is divided into three temperature intervals: near 0 °C (0 to -2 °C), medium (-2 to -15 °C), and low temperature (-15 to -30 °C). Within each interval, the dispersion between resistivity and strength data is discussed, with in-depth analysis of asynchrony, sensitivity differences, and inconsistencies in microscopic mechanisms governing resistivity and strength changes with varying water content, dry density, and temperature.

In near-zero temperature interval (0℃ to -2℃, the soil has not reached the freezing point. Resistivity slightly increases due to reduced ionic activity, but no significant water-ice phase transition or pore structure change occurs, resulting in negligible change in UCS. Consequently, strength predictions based on resistivity in this interval tend to be overestimated. In moderate temperature interval (-2℃ to -15℃), Temperature decreases cause extensive ice crystal formation and alteration of pore structures. According to previous mechanisms: UCS increases with water content until a critical peak at ice crystal saturation, then decreases due to freeze-thaw cracking, followed by slight recovery when the ice skeleton dominates load-bearing20,21. Meanwhile, resistivity sharply rises as ice crystals fill pores and increase electrical tortuosity; post-saturation freeze-thaw deformation further reduces equivalent conduction path diameter, maintaining resistivity growth24, as illustrated by the schematic diagrams and equivalent circuit models of conductive paths for samples with different water (ice) states in Figs. 9 and 10. This differing response around the ice crystal saturation threshold leads to high noise and data dispersion in strength predictions near 1000 Ω·m resistivity. In low temperature interval (-15℃ to -30℃), for medium-to-high water content samples, the soil skeleton is damaged by ice skeleton compression and crystal network cutting, with ice skeleton becoming the primary load bearer. Adsorbed water on particle surfaces freezes, densifying the ice skeleton and causing strength recovery. Resistivity, however, continues to increase significantly due to pore blockage by ice crystals, increased tortuosity, and reduction of soil skeleton conduction paths; conduction is dominated by a small amount of adsorbed water. Under the amplified effect of low temperature and high water content, near 10,000 Ω·m resistivity, the model overpredicts UCS.

Overall, the resistivity-strength prediction model performs best within the moderate freezing temperature range (-2℃ to -15℃), at low-to-medium water contents (14%-22%), and intermediate resistivity values (100–1000 Ω·m). From electrical and mechanical response perspectives, this study clarifies causes of dispersion between measured and predicted values across temperature ranges and defines the effective applicability range of the model for quantitative inversion.

Prediction of deformation modulus E50

The deformation modulus is defined as the ratio of compressive stress to compressive strain under unconfined conditions, reflecting the material’s ability to resist elastic-plastic deformation and is used in elastic-plastic analysis27. Artificial frozen soil exhibits nonlinear deformation, and its deformation modulus is influenced by the proportion of water-ice phase change, ice creep characteristics, stability of the ice-soil skeleton bonding, and microscopic pore structure. Therefore, the secant modulus at 50% of the peak unconfined compressive strength (E50) is commonly used to characterize its deformation properties. Under normalized state parameters and temperature, the relationship between unconfined compressive strength and E50 of artificial frozen soil is shown in Fig. 14(a): E50 increases with decreasing freezing temperature, following a temperature variation trend similar to that of strength. Further analysis shows that when the freezing temperature is between 0℃ and − 5℃, E50 lies above the linear regression line. At this stage, ice content is low and overall strength is relatively weak, but the fine particles dominate; under 50% of qu, elastic deformation prevails with limited plastic deformation (pores are difficult to further compress), so stiffness increases faster than strength. Similar to the resistivity response pattern of unconfined compressive strength, the behavior can be divided into three temperature segments: Near zero temperature (0℃–2℃), there is no significant water-ice phase change or pore structure alteration, and E50 shows no notable variation. Prediction results based on resistivity generally overestimate the deformation modulus in this range. In the moderate temperature range (-2℃ to -15℃), the temperature decrease causes abundant ice crystal formation and changes in pore structure; ice saturation damages the pore framework and induces frost cracks. During compression, pressure melting effects and ice creep reduce E50, while bonding strength at the ice-soil interface decreases, leading to increased deformation6. However, resistivity reflects the state before compression and is not affected by pressure melting. After ice saturation, frost heave deformation further reduces conductive pathways, causing resistivity to continue rising24. At low temperatures (-15℃ to -30℃), the soil skeleton is dominated by the ice skeleton, with freezing of bound water strengthening the ice-soil interface bonding and enhancing deformation resistance. Yet, ice creep remains influenced by high-pressure melting (increasing with strength). Resistivity, measured prior to compression, is unaffected by pressure melting; with unfrozen water freezing, conductive pathways further reduce, and pore tortuosity increases, causing resistivity to rise markedly. Under the combined amplification of low temperature and high water content, when resistivity is near 10,000 Ω·m, the model tends to overpredict E50 compared to actual values. In summary, the resistivity-E50 prediction model’s optimal performance region coincides with that of the strength prediction model: moderate freezing temperatures (-2℃ to -15℃), low to medium water content (14%-22%), and mid-range resistivity values (100–1000 Ω·m).

Analysis of destruction mechanism of microscopic damage of artificial frozen soil

At a dry density of 1.5 g/cm³, the changes in microscopic pore parameters (specific surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter) of artificial frozen soil before freezing and after thaw-settlement with varying water content are shown in Fig. 15. Comparing the initial state with post-thaw parameters reveals that at low water content (14%-18%), the specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter all increase after the first freeze-thaw cycle relative to the original soil sample. Under these conditions, ice crystal expansion within pores forms a dense network that compresses, cuts, and reshapes the soil skeleton. Simultaneously, unfrozen water migrates disorderly toward the freezing front under freeze suction28, reducing pore water and increasing the combined effects of negative pore water pressure and ice crystal frost heave deformation, which increase pore network tortuosity. During deformation, a small number of micropores are generated, and the freeze-thaw process also roughens pore channel surfaces21, ultimately causing the above parameters to rise.

Micro-pore parameters of artificially frozen soil at a dry density of 1.5 g/cm³ before freezing and after thaw settlement as a function of moisture content: (a) Surface area; (b) Pore volume; (c) Mean pore diameter.

However, due to the coupled effects of soil-ice phase change and pore structure, the relationship between strength and water content shows particular characteristics: at low water content, the amount of freezable water is limited, so even at low temperatures the number of ice crystals remains small. When the temperature drops to -30℃, freezing of the electrical double layer water film thins the unfrozen water film, and reduced water content enhances matric suction, strengthening particle cementation. The soil skeleton’s shrinkage exceeds ice crystal expansion, causing overall sample contraction; pores are not saturated with ice crystals, and strength is mainly governed by soil-ice skeleton synergy. Changes in pore structure parameters before and after freeze-thaw in this stage are minor, indicating that the ice crystal network does not significantly alter the pore structure or enhance soil stability—microscopically confirming the strength variation trend of low water content samples (Fig. 16).

Schematic diagram of the soil ice support framework and deformation in the frozen state of saturated and unsaturated soil.

At medium to high water content (> 22%), the increases in specific surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter before and after freeze-thaw are significantly greater than those in the low water content group. Adequate freezable water supports full ice crystal expansion; new pores form, causing notable changes in pore structure parameters with water content. At this point, pores reach ice crystal saturation, and the dense ice crystal network tightly binds the soil-ice skeleton, effectively transmitting load. The soil-ice structure is stable and well-cemented, and strength peaks22. At this stage, the phase transition from water to ice not only reduces the conductive pathways through pore water but also forms an interconnected ice crystal network that effectively blocks internal conduction paths. The increased tortuosity of the pores significantly impedes current flow, causing a rapid rise in resistivity. The electrical response and strength exhibit a certain degree of synchrony, reflecting the changes in the pore structure induced by the water–ice phase transition. When water content increases to 26% and freezing temperature drops below − 5℃, ice crystal oversaturation causes frost cracks, compressing and damaging the soil skeleton. The skeleton support shifts to be ice-dominated, causing strength to decline (Fig. 16). Compared with the moderate water content range, although frost heave deformation at this stage further increases the tortuosity of conductive pathways, the rate of resistivity growth is slightly slower. Meanwhile, the microstructural parameters undergo significant changes during this stage. The marked changes in microscopic structural parameters at this stage further confirm the related strength trends (Fig. 15). At high water content (30%), after freeze-thaw, specific surface area and pore volume slightly decrease, while the average pore diameter continues to increase (Fig. 15). Near saturation, ice crystal oversaturation compresses and cuts the soil skeleton, coarsens ice crystal branches, and expands soil particle pores into larger pores. The number of micropores decreases, causing slight declines in specific surface area and pore volume. The expansion of frost cracks causes a sharp increase in average pore diameter. At this stage, the dense, coarse ice skeleton leads to a strength rebound compared to the 26% water content sample but remains lower than the peak strength observed at 22% water content (Fig. 16).

At low water contents (14%-18%), the limited amount of freezable water results in minimal freeze-thaw induced changes to the microstructure. Therefore, SEM analysis was conducted on samples with medium (22%) and high water contents (26%, 30%) to investigate the micro-morphological changes of soil particle aggregates. SEM images of artificial frozen soil with a dry density of 1.5 g/cm³ before freezing and after thaw-settlement under different water contents (Fig. 17) show that unfrozen samples exhibit typical clay aggregate laminar features, with clay minerals oriented to form a dense stacked structure containing minor pores and microcracks. With freeze-thaw cycling, soil particles gradually transition from a laminar structure to a honeycomb-like porous structure29, and the volumetric expansion during freezing is positively correlated with water content—the higher the initial water content, the more pronounced the soil fragmentation. This structural evolution originates from the coupled water-ice phase transition mechanism: pore water undergoes a “penetration–crystallization–expansion–melting” cycle during freeze-thaw, causing the laminar structure to fracture and rearrange into a honeycomb pore network. Experimental observations indicate that when water content exceeds the critical ice crystal saturation threshold, wedge stresses generated by ice crystal growth in high water content (> 22%) samples significantly compress the soil skeleton (Fig. 16), leading to the propagation and merging of frost cracks accompanied by particle detachment from soil aggregates. Ultimately, the laminar aggregates dissociate and reorganize, forming continuous, rougher-surfaced pores with larger average diameters within the soil matrix21.

SEM images of artificial frozen soil with a dry density of 1.5 g/cm³ before freezing and after thawing at different moisture contents.

Combining the SEM image analysis qualitatively validates the observed trend that microscopic pore parameters increase with initial water content but at a slowing rate. This further clarifies and confirms the intrinsic mechanism whereby the coupled water-ice phase change and pore structure evolution govern the variation of artificial frozen soil strength with initial water content.

Conclusions

This study investigates the mechanical and electrical properties of artificial frozen silty clay from the Yangtze River floodplain under varying freezing temperatures and initial conditions. The electrical evaluation mechanism of unconfined compressive strength (UCS) was analyzed, and a resistivity-based prediction model was developed to bridge electrical and engineering parameters. From mesoscopic and microscopic perspectives, the discreteness and accuracy of the model were interpreted, and the micro-damage mechanism of artificial frozen soil was revealed.

The unconfined compressive strength (UCS) shows a “gradual–sharp–moderate rise” trend with decreasing temperature, increasing with dry density and peaking at a critical water content of 22%. Beyond this value, frost-heave cracking reduces strength, while near-saturation (~ 30%) causes a slight recovery due to the dominance of the ice skeleton.

Beyond this value, frost-heave cracking reduces strength, while near-saturation (~ 30%) causes a slight recovery due to the dominance of the ice skeleton.

Resistivity changes in stages with decreasing temperature—rising slowly during initial freezing and sharply at lower temperatures due to free-water freezing and increased pore tortuosity. Higher water content weakens conductive paths, and a critical dry density marks the shift from pore-water to bound-water conduction. Resistivity and saturation follow a power-law relation, with low temperatures amplifying sensitivity through bound-water freezing and frost-heave deformation.
