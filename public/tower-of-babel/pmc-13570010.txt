Spondyloarthritis in the peripartum period: a systematic review of current knowledge and clinical implications
Authors: Noémie Gross, Anna Molto, Renaud Felten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Spondyloarthritis (SpA) commonly affects women of reproductive age, yet evidence on fertility, pregnancy outcomes and postpartum disease activity remains fragmented. The growing recognition of non-radiographic axial SpA (axSpA) and the wider use of biologics have increased the need for reproductive-health data in this population.Objectives:To systematically review the evidence on fertility, pregnancy course, therapeutic management, obstetric and neonatal outcomes and postpartum disease activity in women with SpA.Design:Systematic literature review.Methods:We performed a systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE using predefined MeSH-based equations addressing fertility, pregnancy and the postpartum period in SpA. English and French studies were included without date restriction. Data extraction followed a PICO/PECO framework and findings were synthesized thematically according to PRISMA guidance.Results:Of 337 publications identified, 167 met the inclusion criteria. Evidence indicates a modest reduction in ovarian reserve and longer time-to-pregnancy in some women with SpA, partly influenced by non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) exposure. Disease activity during pregnancy is generally stable in PsA but more variable in axSpA; flares occur in up to 40% of pregnancies, especially after biologic discontinuation. Overall pregnancy outcomes are favourable, though risks of preterm birth, caesarean delivery and small-for-gestational-age infants are modestly increased. Biologic therapies, especially tumour necrosis factor inhibitors, appear safe and contribute to disease control. Postpartum flares are common, particularly in axSpA, and breastfeeding is generally compatible with most treatments.Conclusion:When disease activity is well controlled and treatment is optimized, reproductive outcomes in women with SpA are largely favourable. Avoiding unnecessary NSAID exposure, maintaining appropriate biologic therapy when indicated and close peripartum monitoring are key to improving outcomes. High-quality prospective studies remain needed to refine clinical guidance.

Introduction

Spondyloarthritis (SpA) is a chronic inflammatory rheumatic disease primarily affecting the axial skeleton, peripheral joints and entheses. 1 Among its phenotypes – axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), reactive arthritis and enteropathic arthritis – axSpA and PsA are the most frequently encountered in women of reproductive age. 2 The onset of SpA typically occurs during the second or third decade of life, aligning with peak childbearing years. 3 In recent years, the clinical landscape of SpA has undergone a significant transformation. The recognition of non-radiographic axial SpA (nr-axSpA) has expanded diagnostic criteria and revealed a striking female predominance in this subgroup. Studies estimate that women represent up to 70% of patients with nr-axSpA, 4 contrasting with the historically male-dominated profile of radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA). This shift has profound implications for reproductive health, as a growing number of women with active SpA are navigating pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum recovery while managing chronic disease. Because most women with SpA are diagnosed during their fertile years and pregnancy is common in this population, understanding the interplay between disease activity, treatment safety and obstetric outcomes is timely and clinically essential. Pregnancy in women with SpA presents unique challenges. Although immunological adaptations of pregnancy may modulate disease activity, the peripartum period is frequently associated with disease flares, especially in axSpA. 2 Moreover, SpA has been linked to increased risks of adverse obstetric outcomes, including preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age neonates, preeclampsia and caesarean delivery.2,3

Despite the growing interest in reproductive health among patients with chronic inflammatory diseases, data on SpA in the peripartum period remain limited and heterogeneous. 5 Existing studies vary in design, population size and outcome measures, making it difficult to draw definitive clinical recommendations. Furthermore, the safety and management of biologic and conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs and csDMARDs) during pregnancy and lactation remain debated. 6

Given the increasing prevalence of SpA among women of childbearing age and the potential impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes, a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence is warranted. This systematic review aims to evaluate the existing literature on SpA in the peripartum period. By consolidating available data, we seek to inform clinical practice and identify gaps for future research.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review of the literature using the PubMed/MEDLINE database. The aim was to collect and synthesize publications addressing fertility, pregnancy and the postpartum period in women with SpA, to provide an up-to-date overview of existing data.

Three search equations were constructed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, translated and validated via the CISMeF (Catalogue et Index des Sites Médicaux de la Langue Française) and the official MeSH database:

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible publications had to be written in English or French and specifically address one or more of the following periods: fertility, pregnancy or postpartum. We included all types of studies – observational studies, interventional trials, case series, case reports and systematic reviews – without restriction on study design. Furthermore, they had to focus on women diagnosed with SpA, including PsA, and explore the relationship between the disease and these reproductive phases.

Articles were excluded if they did not pertain to SpA or did not address fertility, pregnancy or the postpartum period based on their title and abstract. Additional exclusion criteria included the absence of an abstract, publication in languages other than English or French and article types such as correspondence, illustrations or research protocols. Studies focusing exclusively on male fertility were also excluded. Notably, no restriction was applied regarding the year of publication, allowing for an analysis of the evolution of scientific interest over time.

Study selection process

The selection of studies was carried out in two distinct stages. First, titles and abstracts were screened to eliminate publications that were clearly irrelevant. Second, full-text articles were reviewed to confirm eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Duplicate entries were identified and removed to ensure a unique dataset.

Data extraction

To ensure a structured and clinically meaningful synthesis, the extracted information was organized according to the PICO/PECO framework – Population, Exposure/Intervention, Comparator and Outcomes.

Synthesis of findings

Extracted data were synthesized and compared thematically. Key findings were organized and presented in summary tables to facilitate interpretation and highlight patterns across the literature.

Results

Flow diagram

The literature search yielded 337 publications across 3 research equations: fertility, pregnancy and postpartum. After removing duplicates and applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 167 articles were retained for full-text analysis. Among these, 34 focused on fertility and preconception, 42 addressed SpA disease activity during pregnancy, 103 the therapeutic management, 80 the pregnancy outcomes and 36 explored the postpartum period (Figure 1). Temporal and proportional analysis of publications

To contextualize this research, we conducted a broader PubMed search for ‘Spondylarthropathies’[MeSH], which yielded over 30,000 publications during the same period. Thus, articles specifically addressing the peripartum period represent less than 1% of the total scientific output on SpA, underscoring the relative underrepresentation of reproductive health in this field.

However, a temporal analysis revealed a clear upward trend. Between 2000 and 2010, fewer than 20 articles addressed SpA in the context of pregnancy or fertility. From 2011 to 2020, this number more than doubled, with a notable acceleration after 2015. In the last 5 years (2020–2025), over 60 articles were published on this topic, reflecting growing clinical awareness and research interest (Supplemental Figure 1). This increase coincides with the broader recognition of nr-axSpA and its predominance in women, as well as the expansion of biologic therapies requiring reproductive safety data. This evolution of publication volume suggests a paradigm shift: reproductive health in SpA is no longer a marginal concern but an emerging priority in rheumatology research. Nevertheless, the literature remains fragmented, with few large-scale prospective studies and limited data on newer therapeutic agents.

Fertility and preconception

The impact of SpA on female fertility remains a subject of debate. Earlier studies, such as those by Ostensen et al.,7 –10 suggested that fertility in women with SpA was comparable to that of the general population. However, more recent research has identified a reduction in ovarian reserve among affected women, evidenced by lower anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels and diminished antral follicle counts.11,12 TTP was found to be prolonged in this population, with 21%—32% of women experiencing a TTP exceeding 12 months.2,13 In some cohorts, subfertility rates reached as high as 45%. 2

Several factors were associated with reduced fertility, including advanced maternal age, nulliparity, longer disease duration, chronic pelvic pain, fatigue and psychological distress.14,15 Interestingly, no direct correlation was found between disease activity and AMH levels. 16

Regarding pharmacological influences, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), particularly COX-2 inhibitors, were linked to delayed conception.17,18 Corticosteroids at doses above 7.5 mg/day were also associated with reduced fertility. 14 Sulphasalazine appeared to have no negative impact and was considered safe when supplemented with folic acid. Anti-tumour necrosis factor (anti-TNF) agents did not impair fertility and may even facilitate conception by reducing systemic inflammation.16,19

Complementary examinations, such as AMH testing, were frequently used to assess ovarian reserve in this population.12,16

Disease activity during pregnancy

Disease activity during pregnancy varied by SpA subtype. In women with PsA, 75%—80% experienced remission or low disease activity throughout gestation.21,22 In axSpA, most studies report overall stable disease activity during pregnancy, although disease flares occur in a subset of patients (approximately 25%—30%), often peaking in the second trimester or following treatment discontinuation.23,24 Several studies noted that disease activity tended to peak in the second trimester before declining in the third.25,26 In approximately 21% of cases, pregnancy coincided with the initial onset of SpA symptoms. 9 The recent GR2 study 27 is a French multicentre prospective cohort that included 71 pregnancies in women with SpA. At baseline (first trimester), disease activity was generally low, with a mean Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score including C-reactive protein (ASDAS-CRP) of 1.9 ± 1.2 and a mean Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) of 3.0 ± 2.1. These indices remained stable throughout pregnancy: ASDAS-CRP: 1.9 ± 1.2 (T1), 2.0 ± 1.0 (T2), 1.7 ± 1.0 (T3), p = 0.60 and BASDAI: 3.0 ± 2.1 (T1), 2.8 ± 2.0 (T2), 2.9 ± 2.1 (T3), p = 0.92. Despite this apparent stability, disease relapse occurred in 28 out of 71 pregnancies (39.4%), defined by treatment intensification and/or a significant increase in disease activity scores. Of these, 20 patients required therapeutic escalation. In multivariable analysis, a history of biologic DMARD (bDMARD) use was significantly associated with relapse during pregnancy (odds ratio (OR) 5.4, 95% CI 1.1–27.3). These findings suggest that although most women with SpA maintain stable disease activity during pregnancy – particularly when treated with TNF inhibitor (TNFi) use (62% of patients received a TNFi during gestation) – nearly 40% remain at risk of flare. This underscores the importance of close monitoring and individualized therapeutic planning, especially for patients with prior bDMARD exposure. 27

Pregnancy outcomes

Obstetric complications in women with SpA have been frequently reported. Maternal risks included preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, hypertension, placenta previa and oligohydramnios.2,3,25 Foetal risks encompassed prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), low birth weight and increased rates of caesarean delivery.23,35 Caesarean sections were more common and were often scheduled in advance. 36 Nevertheless, several studies confirmed that uncomplicated pregnancies are achievable when disease activity is well controlled.9,30,34

A large multicentre registry study published by Meissner et al. 25 in 2022 investigated pregnancy outcomes in 304 women with axSpA, pooling data from 4 European rheumatology registries. A total of 332 pregnancies were analysed; the live birth rate was 98.8%, indicating highly favourable outcomes. Pre-eclampsia occurred in 2.2% of cases, preterm birth in 4.9%, low birth weight in 3.1% and small-for-gestational-age neonates in 9.5%. Caesarean delivery was reported in 27.7% of pregnancies, with nearly half of these being emergency procedures. Disease activity remained well controlled throughout gestation. The pooled mean CRP increased from 4 mg/L before conception to 9.4 mg/L in the second trimester, while BASDAI scores remained below 4 at all time points. Prior to pregnancy, 53% of patients were treated with TNFi, with 27.5% continuing treatment into the first trimester and 21.4% into the third. Most patients (78.4%) received pre-conception counselling and had planned pregnancies. These findings suggest that with close disease monitoring, individualized treatment strategies and multidisciplinary care in expert centres, pregnancy outcomes in women with axSpA can approach those of the general population.

Therapeutic management during pregnancy

Therapeutic management during pregnancy require careful consideration. In recent years, certolizumab pegol (CZP) has emerged as the preferred anti-TNF agent due to its minimal placental transfer, as demonstrated in the CRIB study, which measured CZP levels in maternal blood, cord blood and infant plasma and found negligible foetal exposure even during the third trimester. 47 In contrast, other biologics were typically discontinued in late pregnancy and need more data. NSAIDs were considered safe during the first and second trimesters but were contraindicated in the third due to risks of foetal complications. Methotrexate was strictly contraindicated and recommended to be stopped before conception. Sulphasalazine was deemed compatible with pregnancy, and corticosteroids were used cautiously, as high doses above 10 mg/day were associated with increased risk of prematurity.

The 2024 update of the EULAR recommendations for the use of antirheumatic drugs in reproduction, pregnancy and lactation provides a comprehensive framework for managing patients with inflammatory rheumatic diseases, including axial and peripheral SpA. 6 These guidelines were developed through a systematic literature review and expert consensus, and they reflect the most current safety data available.

For patients with SpA, the recommendations emphasize the following:

TNFi remain the cornerstone of treatment during pregnancy when disease control is needed. CZP is considered the safest option due to its minimal placental transfer. All TNFi may be used throughout pregnancy when needed to effectively control maternal disease, rather than being limited to the first trimester. For non-TNFi biologics, EULAR recommends they may be used if needed to effectively control maternal disease (lower level of evidence than TNFi) and specifically lists abatacept, ixekizumab, sécukinumab and ustekinumab; agents with very limited/no pregnancy safety data (guselkumab and risankizumab) should be reserved for situations where no other pregnancy-compatible medication can adequately control disease.

The EULAR task force also stressed the importance of preconception counselling, shared decision-making and multidisciplinary care involving rheumatologists, obstetricians and midwives. These recommendations align with the trends observed in our review and provide a validated framework for clinical decision-making in women with SpA during the reproductive period.

CZP remains the biologic with the most reassuring pharmacokinetic data during pregnancy due to its minimal placental transfer. However, recent evidence and updated guidelines support a less restrictive approach to biologic therapy overall. Several biologics may be continued during pregnancy when disease control requires it, particularly during early and mid-pregnancy. Treatment decisions should therefore be individualized based on disease activity and maternal–foetal considerations.

Postpartum

The postpartum period was associated with a high risk of disease flares, particularly in women with PsA and r-axSpA.24,33,48 However, patients who continued biologic therapy postpartum tended to maintain stable disease activity.26,32 Breastfeeding appeared to have a protective effect, with lower disease activity observed among lactating women. 49

Regarding treatment compatibility with breastfeeding, CZP and adalimumab were considered safe during breastfeeding.50,51 Methotrexate remained contraindicated, and data on other biologics were limited. NSAIDs and corticosteroids were generally compatible with lactation, although timing precautions were advised for corticosteroid administration.

In the 2024 update of the EULAR recommendations for the use of antirheumatic drugs before conception, during pregnancy and lactation, special emphasis was placed on breastfeeding. 6 The task force reaffirmed that women should not be discouraged from breastfeeding if they are receiving compatible medications, and that postpartum therapeutic decisions should be made jointly with the patient, balancing the risks of drug exposure against those of uncontrolled disease. In this latest version, all bDMARDs are now considered compatible with lactation, based on their pharmacokinetic properties and minimal transfer into breast milk. Data remain more limited for non-TNFi biologics, but their structural and molecular similarities to TNF inhibitors support a low-risk assessment. Furthermore, the updated recommendations confirm that NSAIDs are compatible with breastfeeding and that methotrexate is contraindicated.

Discussion

This systematic review consolidates current knowledge on the impact of SpA during the peripartum period, encompassing fertility, pregnancy and postpartum outcomes. Our findings highlight both reassuring evidence and important gaps that warrant further investigation.

Several robust findings emerge from the literature. Fertility is not universally impaired in women with SpA, yet TTP is frequently prolonged, and ovarian reserve may be reduced in some cohorts.11 –14 During pregnancy, disease activity varies by subtype: PsA often improves,21,22,32,33 whereas axSpA – particularly r-axSpA – tends to flare, most commonly in the second trimester.23 –25,27 When well-controlled, pregnancy outcomes are generally favourable, with live birth rates approaching those of the general population.25,34 Nevertheless, active disease is consistently associated with increased risks of obstetric complications, including preeclampsia, IUGR, prematurity and caesarean delivery.2,3,35,36,40,46 Postpartum, flares are common, especially in women who discontinue biologic therapy,26,32,33,48 whereas breastfeeding appears protective. 49 CZP stands out as the safest biologic option during pregnancy and lactation, with reassuring pharmacokinetic data from the CRIB study.47,51

Despite these advances, the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous. Most studies are observational, with small sample sizes and variable outcome definitions, which limits comparability.24,52 Data on newer biologics and small molecules are scarce, 51 and long-term child development outcomes remain largely unexplored. Postpartum disease activity is inconsistently reported, and interpretation of imaging findings – particularly sacroiliac MRI – can be confounded by physiological changes after delivery.53,54 Finally, access to preconception counselling and multidisciplinary follow-up is not uniformly reported,25,35 leaving gaps in understanding the broader determinants of outcomes.

These findings underscore several key messages for clinical practice. First, preconception counselling should be systematically offered, addressing fertility, medication safety and disease monitoring.2,17,25 Second, maintaining low disease activity is crucial, as active disease rather than treatment per se appears to be the main driver of adverse outcomes.36,40,46 Third, therapeutic planning should integrate evidence-based guidance: CZP, other TNFi and, in selected cases, other bDMARDs may be continued,6,47 while methotrexate and leflunomide must be discontinued well in advance of conception. 6 Postpartum care requires vigilance, particularly regarding disease flares and breastfeeding support.26,32,33,49 Importantly, our synthesis suggests a window of vulnerability in the postpartum period, during which disease flares are frequent and careful follow-up is essential. Standardized protocols for monitoring – combining clinical scores (BASDAI, ASDAS), inflammatory markers (CRP), and, where appropriate, imaging – could improve consistency of care and early detection of complications. Multidisciplinary coordination between rheumatologists, obstetricians, midwives and paediatricians is essential to optimize outcomes for both mother and child.6,50,52

Future studies should aim to fill critical gaps. Large prospective registries with standardized outcome measures are needed to better define the risks of newer biologics and small molecules, as well as the long-term health of children exposed in utero or via breastfeeding.25,51 More work is also required to clarify the biological mechanisms linking chronic inflammation to subfertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes.11 –14,17 Finally, interventional studies testing structured multidisciplinary care models could provide evidence on how best to deliver integrated reproductive health services for women with SpA.6,52

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the complex interplay between SpA and the peripartum period. While fertility is not universally impaired, women with SpA – particularly those with axial involvement – may experience delayed conception and reduced ovarian reserve. Pregnancy outcomes are influenced by disease activity and therapeutic management, with increased risks of preeclampsia, prematurity and caesarean delivery observed in several cohorts. The postpartum period remains a vulnerable phase, marked by frequent disease flares and diagnostic challenges.

Despite these risks, favourable maternal and neonatal outcomes are achievable when the disease is well controlled and care is coordinated across specialties. Anti-TNF agents such as CZP offer safe and effective options during pregnancy and lactation, and breastfeeding appears to confer protective benefits.

This review underscores the need for prospective, standardized studies to better define clinical pathways and optimize reproductive care in women with SpA. Rheumatologists, obstetricians and midwives must collaborate to ensure informed decision-making and tailored support throughout the reproductive journey.
