Treatment with proton pump inhibitors is associated with secondary bacterial infections and sepsis in patients with COVID-19: a retrospective analysis of their joint impact on in-hospital prognosis
Authors: Vanessa Bianconi, Massimo R Mannarino, Filippo Figorilli, Federica Ricciutelli, Stefania De Carlo, Valentina Zullo, Martina Corba, Amirhossein Sahebkar, Alessia Greco, Rita Lombardini, Rita Paltriccia, Matteo Pirro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and objectives. Secondary bacterial infections (SBIs) contribute to worse in-hospital outcomes in patients with Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Treatment with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) is associated with an increased risk of bacterial infections in different clinical settings. However, the association between PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and the occurrence of either SBIs or secondary bacterial sepsis (SBS) as well as their joint impact on clinical outcomes of patients hospitalized for COVID-19 are not clarified.Patients and methods. We retrospectively analyzed preadmission PPI use, in-hospital occurrence of SBIs and SBS, and in-hospital outcomes of a cohort of patients hospitalized for COVID-19.Results. Among 1087 patients, 447 (41%) were on PPI treatment prior to hospital admission. During the hospital stay, 197 (18%) and 223 (20%) patients were diagnosed with SBIs and SBS, respectively. The composite endpoint of intensive care unit (ICU) admission/in‐hospital death was met by 214 (20%) patients. Preadmission PPI treatment was independently associated with up to a 2.1-fold and 1.7-fold increased risk of SBIs and SBS, respectively. The occurrence of SBS was independently associated with up to a 2.2-fold increased risk of ICU admission/in-hospital death. A significant preadmission PPI treatment x SBS interaction emerged in predicting ICU admission/in-hospital death (F = 5.221, pinteraction = 0.023).Conclusions. PPI treatment prior to hospital admission for COVID-19 is associated with an increased risk of SBIs and SBS. In addition, it interacts with SBS in predicting in-hospital prognosis. An appropriate use of PPIs may attenuate the risk of adverse clinical outcomes during hospitalization for COVID-19.Keywords: COVID-19, infections, prognosis, proton pump inhibitors, sepsis

Introduction

The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) caused by SARS-CoV-2 continues to be associated with high morbidity and mortality rates, especially in elderly and immunocompromised individuals [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), SARS-CoV-2 has infected more than 750 million people, causing more than 6.5 million deaths as of November 2023 [2]. Clinical manifestations of COVID-19 mainly involve the upper and the lower respiratory tract, ranging from mild symptoms to severe interstitial pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). However, either direct or indirect involvement of other organs and systems can adversely affect the prognosis of patients with COVID-19 [3].

Like other viral respiratory infections, COVID-19 is associated with a high prevalence of secondary bacterial infections (SBIs) [4,5]. According to a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis by Musuuza et al. [4], the pooled prevalence of SBIs in the general COVID-19 population (both in-patients and out-patients) is approximately 20%, rising significantly among the intensive care unit (ICU) inpatients. The occurrence of SBIs appears to be associated with poor clinical outcomes of COVID-19, including the need of mechanical ventilation, prolonged length of in-hospital stay, and in-hospital death [4].

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are widely prescribed in clinical practice for a variety of acid-related gastrointestinal diseases, although their inappropriate use is also commonly recorded [6–9]. Many studies suggest that PPIs may impair the natural host defense against bacterial infections by reducing gastric acid secretion [10–13]. Indeed, hypochlorhydria may promote gastric bacterial colonization and alteration of gut microbiota, thus increasing susceptibility to enteric infections [10,11]; in addition, it may promote bacterial pneumonia possibly due to microaspiration of bacterial gastric content [12,13]. An increased incidence of bacterial infections involving organs and systems other than the gastrointestinal tract and lungs has also been associated with PPI treatment [14–16] and additional mechanisms beyond reduced gastric acid secretion have been proposed to explain it. Thus, for instance, PPIs have been suggested to exert a significant immunosuppressive effect, which may be implicated in the pathogenesis of infections at different anatomical sites, including the urinary tract and bloodstream [14–18].

A large use of PPIs has been consistently observed in patients hospitalized for COVID-19 [16–18]. In addition, two previous studies have explored the retrospective association between PPI treatment and the risk of SBIs during COVID-19 in small-sample datasets [19,20]. However, in this clinical setting, the associa­tion between PPI treatment and SBIs according to the type of pathogen or the site of origin has not been analyzed so far. Moreover, there are no literature data on the association between PPI treatment and secondary bacterial sepsis (SBS, that is a suspected or microbiologically diagnosed SBI associated with organ dysfunction) in patients with COVID-19. Finally, although previous studies have analyzed the association between PPI exposure and COVID-19-related in-hospital outcomes [21–23], no previous study has attempted to clarify whether SBI or SBS may mediate the detrimental impact of PPIs on in-hospital prognosis.

This single-center retrospective study of patients hospitalized for COVID-19 was aimed at evaluating: (1) the association between PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and the occurrence of SBIs (grouped according to the type of pathogen and site of origin) or SBS, and (2) the joint impact of PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and either SBIs or SBS in predicting in-hospital prognosis.

Methods

Study population

Clinical data of COVID-19 patients who were consecutively referred to the Internal Medicine ward of the ‘Santa Maria della Misericordia Hospital’ of Perugia (Italy) from March 2020 to October 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. The study protocol was developed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee. Inclusion criteria were the following ones: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) a positive result on real-time reverse-transcriptase-PCR assays testing for SARS-CoV-2 on nasal or pharyngeal swab specimens at hospital admission; (3) informed written consent for clinical data to be used for subsequent studies (Figure 1). Exclusion criteria were the following ones: (1) any documented bacterial infection or antibiotic therapy for suspected bacterial infection up to 2 weeks before diagnosis of COVID-19, and (2) incomplete medical records (Figure 1).

Selection of the study population.

Data collection

All data were collected from electronic medical records by a highly trained team of physicians. Recorded clinical variables included demographic characteristics, pre-existing medical conditions, concomitant treatments, laboratory tests performed at hospital admission, physical and instrumental examinations performed at hospital admission, any diagnosis of SBI after hospital admission, any diagnosis of SBS after hospital admission, and in-hospital outcomes (ICU admission, in-hospital death, and hospital discharge). The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated as a surrogate marker of comorbidity at hospital admission by integrating information on coexisting medical conditions [24,25]. The severity of COVID‐19 at hospital admission was established according to the National Institute of Health (NIH) classification [26,27]. Diagnosis of SBIs was recorded based on the presence of clinical manifestations of bacterial infection and at least one positive culture test from qualified biological samples (either sputum, endotracheal aspirate, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, blood, urine, or feces), which were collected and transferred to the microbiology laboratory for standard processing (pathogen isolation, species identification, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing) [28,29]. All SBIs were categorized according to the type of pathogen [Gram-positive (+) versus Gram-negative (-) and multi-drug resistant (MDR) versus non-MDR] and site of origin (respiratory tract SBIs, bloodstream SBIs, urinary tract SBIs, and gastrointestinal tract SBIs). All SBIs that were diagnosed from biological samples collected up to 48 h of hospital admission were recorded as coinfections, whereas all the other SBIs were recorded as superinfections. Diagnosis of SBS was set in the presence of any suspected or microbiologically documented SBI and a ≥ 2-point increase of Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score (SOFA) score, which was estimated as a proxy for organ dysfunction by integrating six clinical/laboratory data [1 – partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2); 2 – platelets; 3 – bilirubin; 4 – mean arterial pressure as the compositum of systolic and diastolic blood pressures assessed through sphygmomanometer; 5 – creatinine; 6 – Glasgow Coma Scale] according to Sepsis-3 criteria [30–32].

Statistical analysis

The SPSS statistical package version 24.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA), was used for all statistical analyses. The Shapiro test was used to verify the normality of the study variables. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians (25th–75th percentile). The independent samples t-test, Mann–Whitney U-test, and Chi-squared test were used for two-group comparisons. Unadjusted and multi-adjusted logistic regression analyses were used to assess (1) the association between PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and the occurrence of either SBIs or SBS, and (2) the association between the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death and either PPI treatment prior to hospital admission, the occurrence of SBIs, or the occurrence of SBS. In all multi-adjusted analyses, independent variables were selected among significant covariates of the dependent variable and were grouped in different models according to their clinical significance (demographic variables in Model 2, demographic variables and pre-existing clinical conditions in Model 3, demographic variables and concomitant medications in Model 4, demographic variables and clinical parameters at hospital admission in Model 5). The interaction terms between PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and either SBIs or SBS in predicting the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in‐hospital death were explored through general linear model univariate analyses as follows: (1) the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death was assumed as the dependent variable, (2) PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and either SBIs or SBS were selected as fixed factors, (3) the F coefficient and its p value were estimated by testing between-subjects effects (PPI treatment prior to hospital admission x SBIs or PPI treatment prior to hospital admission x SBS). For all analyses, statistical significance was assumed at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed clinical data of 1087 patients admitted for COVID-19 at the Internal Medicine ward of the ‘Santa Maria della Misericordia’ Hospital of Perugia (Italy) between March 2020 and October 2022. At hospital admission, 134 (12%), 243 (23%), and 710 (65%) patients had mild, moderate, and severe COVID-19, respectively. Four hundred and forty-seven (41%) patients were taking PPIs prior to hospital admission, whereas 640 (59%) were not. During the hospital stay, the therapeutic management of COVID-19 and concomitant clinical conditions was performed according to available scientific recommendations and common clinical practice.

SBIs and SBS

During the hospital stay, a total of 233 SBIs were recorded in 197 (18%) patients. Baseline characteristics of patients who were diagnosed with SBIs as compared to those who were not are shown in Supplementary Table 1. One hundred and forty-one (60%) SBIs were diagnosed through a positive urine culture, 49 (22%) SBIs through a positive bloodstream culture, 22 (9%) SBIs through a positive culture from respiratory tract specimens (either sputum, tracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid), and 21 (9%) SBIs through a positive stool culture (Supplementary Table 2). Superinfections accounted for 47%, 53%, 86%, and 86% of urinary tract SBIs, bloodstream SBIs, respiratory tract SBIs, and gastroenteric tract SBIs, respectively; all the other SBIs were coinfections. Fifty-four (38%) urinary tract SBIs and 6 (27%) respiratory tract SBIs were polymicrobial, whereas all the other SBIs were monomicrobial. One hundred and fifty-five (66%) SBIs were caused by Gram (-) bacteria and 116 (50%) SBIs by Gram (+) bacteria, with 38 (17%) SBIs being caused by both Gram (-) and Gram (+) bacteria. The most frequent isolates were Escherichia coli (n = 55), Clostridium difficile (n = 20), Staphylococcus aureus (n = 12), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (n = 9), in urinary tract SBIs, gastroenteric tract SBIs, bloodstream SBIs and respiratory tract SBIs, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). Sixty-five (28%) SBIs were caused by MDR bacteria, with Staphylococcus aureus being the most common MDR isolate (Supplementary Figure 1).

During the hospital stay, diagnostic criteria for SBS were met by 223 (20%) patients, of whom 25 (11%) were complicated by septic shock. Among patients who were diagnosed with SBS, 159 (71%) had a microbiologically documented SBI, whereas 64 (29%) had a suspected SBI. Baseline characteristics of patients who were diagnosed with SBS as compared to those who were not are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

In-hospital outcomes

During the hospital stay, 58 (5%) patients were admitted to ICU, 174 (16%) patients died, and 214 (20%) patients met the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in‐hospital death. The median time from hospital admission to ICU admission was 3 (2–6) days. The median time from hospital admission to in-hospital death was 8 (4–14) days. Baseline characteristics of patients who met the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death as compared to those who did not are shown in (Supplementary Table 4).

PPIs in relation to SBIs and SBS

The proportion of patients who were diagnosed with SBIs was significantly higher among those who were on PPI treatment prior to hospital admission as compared to those who were not [118 (26%) versus 79 (12%), p < 0.001]. Rates of urinary tract SBIs, bloodstream SBIs, and respiratory tract SBIs were significantly higher among PPI-treated as compared to PPI-untreated patients, whereas rates of gastroenteric tract SBIs showed a non-significant trend toward an increase in PPI-treated as compared to PPI-untreated patients (Supplementary Figure 2, Panel A). The comparison between the proportion of PPI-treated and PPI-untreated patients who were diagnosed with SBIs caused by either Gram (+) or Gram (-) bacterial species did not reach statistical significance [Gram (+): 76 (64%) versus 42 (53%), p = 0.115; Gram (-): 68 (58%) versus 54 (68%), p = 0.129]. Comparable rates of SBIs caused by either Gram (+) or Gram (-) bacterial species were observed in PPI-treated and PPI-untreated patients at all anatomical sites (Supplementary Figure 2, Panel B). The proportion of patients who were diagnosed with SBIs caused by MDR bacterial species did not differ significantly according to PPI treatment prior to hospital admission [40 (34%) versus 23 (29%), p = 0.480]. Comparable rates of SBIs caused by MDR bacterial species were observed in PPI-treated and PPI-untreated patients at all anatomical sites (Supplementary Figure 2, Panel C). At logistic regression analysis, PPI treatment prior to hospital admission was significantly associated with the occurrence of SBIs, even when adjusted for multiple confounders (Table 2).

PPIs in relation to in-hospital outcomes

A significantly higher rate of the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death was found in PPI-treated as compared to PPI-untreated patients [108 (24%) versus 106 (17%), p = 0.002]. At logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for multiple confounding factors, PPI treatment prior to hospital admission was not significantly associated with the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death (Supplementary Table 5).

SBIs and SBS in relation to in-hospital outcomes

A significantly higher rate of the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death was found in patients who were diagnosed with either SBIs [51 (26%) versus 163 (18%), p = 0.015] or SBS [73 (33%) versus 141 (16%), p < 0.001] as compared to those who were not.

At logistic regression analysis, the occurrence of SBIs was not significantly associated with the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death when adjusted for multiple confounders (Supplementary Table 6), whereas the occurrence of SBS was (Supplementary Table 7).

Interaction between PPIs and either SBIs or SBS in predicting clinical outcomes

Categorizing the study population according to PPIs and SBIs, the highest rate of the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in‐hospital death [37 (32%)] was observed among PPI-treated patients who were diagnosed with SBIs, whereas the lowest rate of the same endpoint [92 (16%)] emerged among PPI-untreated patients who were not diagnosed with SBIs (Figure 2, Panel A). While the occurrence of SBIs was associated with a significantly higher rate of ICU admission/in‐hospital death among PPI-treated patients, the same endpoint did not differ significantly according to the occurrence of SBIs among PPI-untreated patients (Figure 2, Panel A). While PPI treatment was associated with a significantly higher rate of ICU admission/in‐hospital death among patients who were diagnosed with SBIs, the same endpoint did not differ significantly according to PPI treatment among patients who were not diagnosed with SBIs (Figure 2, Panel A). A non-significant interaction was observed between preadmission PPI treatment (yes versus no) and the occurrence of SBIs (yes versus no) in predicting the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in‐hospital death (F = 3.033, pinteraction = 0.082).

Discussion

In this large single-centre retrospective study of 1087 patients hospitalized for COVID-19, we found: (1) a significant independent association between preadmission PPI treatment and the occurrence of either SBIs (caused by different bacterial species in different anatomical sites) or SBS, and (2) a significant interaction between preadmission PPI treatment and the occurrence of SBS, but not between preadmission PPI treatment and the occurrence of SBIs, in predicting the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death.

Two previous studies reported a significant association between preadmission PPI treatment and the occurrence of secondary infections in COVID-19 patients [19,20]. Luxenburger et al. carried out a retrospective study in a population of 152 patients hospitalized for COVID-19, whose small sample did not allow for a robust assessment of the role of PPI treatment as an independent risk factor for SBIs [19]. Li et al. performed a retrospective study of 238 patients hospitalized for COVID-19 in which PPIs were listed among determinants of secondary infections, either bacterial or fungal, but no further insight was provided on their possible pathophysiological link [20]. Thus, no previous studies have ever explored the impact of PPI treatment prior to hospital admission for COVID-19 on different types of SBIs nor clinical prognosis of patients with COVID-19 in relation to the occurrence of SBIs. Furthermore, no data from previous studies are available on the association between PPI treatment prior to hospital admission and SBS nor on the joint impact of PPIs and SBS on clinical outcomes of patients hospitalized for COVID-19. Accordingly, this is the first study to dissect the association between PPIs and different types of SBIs, to describe the association between PPIs and SBS, and to analyze the interaction terms between PPIs and SBIs/SBS in predicting the clinical prognosis of patients with COVID-19.

Noteworthy, the rate of PPI treatment prior to hospital admission for COVID-19 was very high (41%), reflecting similar rates described in other cohorts of patients hospitalized for COVID-19 and suggesting a possible overuse of these drugs in clinical practice [33]. In addition, the observed rate of SBIs was in line with that reported in previous studies in COVID-19 patients, with Gram (-) bacteria emerging as the most common pathogens regardless of site of infection [34] and with MDR pathogens representing nearly one-third of all isolates [35]. However, in contrast with previous studies showing SBIs from the respiratory tract as the most common ones among patients hospitalized for COVID-19 [36,37], urinary tract SBIs were those prevalent in our cohort, followed by bloodstream SBIs, respiratory tract SBIs, and gastroenteric tract SBIs. A higher ease of access to qualified microbiological samples from sites of infections other than the respiratory tract might explain, at least in part, our results. Indeed, in the presence of suspected respiratory tract SBIs, the coexistence of advanced age, a high comorbidity index, and severe COVID-19, which were prevalent conditions in our cohort, might have precluded the possibility of obtaining sputum samples and performing invasive procedures for collecting endotracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, likely due to poor patient compliance and/or to an unfavorable risk-benefit ratio.

From a pathophysiological perspective, there are several mechanistic explanations possibly underlying the association between PPIs and the occurrence of SBIs and SBS. First, by reducing gastric acid secretion PPIs can contribute to gastric bacterial colonization and bacterial overgrowth along the digestive tract [38, 39]. This mechanism may directly promote bacterial infections of the gastroenteric tract, but it may also indirectly facilitate bacterial pneumonia by promoting microaspiration of gastric content and secondary bacterial localization into the lungs [40]. Second, PPIs can impair the immune response against infections at a systemic level [17,41,42]. This, in turn, may contribute to the onset of bacterial infections in different anatomical sites beyond the gastroenteric and respiratory tract. In addition, it may promote the development of organ dysfunction and bacterial sepsis [17,41,42].

Nonetheless, it should be considered that an increased occurrence of SBIs and SBS in patients hospitalized for COVID-19 may also result from the intrinsic ability of SARS-CoV-2 to impair host defense against infections. Indeed, SARS-CoV-2 can subvert airways defense mechanisms against bacterial infections by altering the quantity and quality of mucus secretion, reducing ciliary function, and damaging epithelial barrier integrity [43,44]. In addition, SARS-CoV-2 can impair host defense against bacterial infections by reducing total lymphocyte count and depressing T lymphocyte function [45]. Accordingly, it cannot be excluded that COVID-19 might somehow amplify the deleterious impact of PPIs on individual susceptibility to bacterial infections and sepsis.

Noteworthy, our results do not suggest any differential impact of PPI treatment on SBIs from Gram (+) and Gram (-) bacteria nor on SBIs from MDR and non-MDR bacteria. However, in the light of inconsistent literature data [46–48] and the relatively small size of each subgroup analyzed in this study, as to whether PPI could preferentially increase the susceptibility to specific bacterial infections remains uncertain.

The observed significant interaction between PPIs and SBS in predicting the composite endpoint of ICU admission/in-hospital death enriches available literature data on the role of PPI in COVID-19 by providing a possible explanatory cue for the reported association between the use of these drugs and adverse clinical outcomes of COVID-19 [21–23]. Indeed, it suggests the existence of an indirect link between PPIs and an increased risk of worse in-hospital prognosis of patients with COVID-19 in which the intermediary is represented by SBS. In this regard, it should be highlighted that the presence of organ dysfunction along with any bacterial infection, discriminating between SBS and SBIs, may be crucial in mediating the unfavorable impact of PPI on clinical outcomes of COVID-19. Accordingly, no significant interaction was observed between PPIs and SBIs in predicting adverse clinical outcomes, with nearly one-third of SBIs having been recorded without any clinical sign of organ dysfunction.

Overall, our results have important clinical implications. First, they improve awareness of possible PPI overuse and encourage greater appropriateness in the prescription of PPI to limit the occurrence of PPI-related adverse events, including bacterial infections. Second, they highlight that PPI treatment represents a risk factor for the occurrence of different types of SBIs and SBS among patients hospitalized for COVID-19, thereby suggesting the need for an active surveillance of these clinical complications especially among patients with COVID-19 on PPI treatment prior to hospital admission. Third, they remark on the need to promptly suspect and identify bacterial infections and the associated organ dysfunction to treat them and possibly halt their deleterious impact on the in-hospital prognosis of patients with COVID-19.

The main strength of the study is represented by the large sample size, which increases the reliability of estimates. However, some limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, due to its retrospective design, potential selection and information bias cannot be excluded. Second, observed results do not allow to demonstrate but only allow to suggest a causality between PPIs, SBIs, SBS, and in-hospital prognosis. Third, despite all regression analyses having been adjusted for multiple confounders, the potential confounding effect of unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Fourth, it was not possible to perform additional analyses on the impact of either duration or doses of PPI treatment due to the unavailability of this information in medical records.

Conclusions

In conclusion, PPI treatment prior to hospital admission for COVID-19 is associated with an increased occurrence of in-hospital SBIs and SBS. In addition, PPI treatment prior to hospital admission interacts with SBS in predicting adverse clinical outcomes of patients hospitalized for COVID-19. Extensive strategies aimed at promoting an appropriate prescription of PPIs are warranted to attenuate the possible unfavorable impact of these drugs on the risk of infective complications and worse clinical outcomes during hospitalization. In addition, extensive and rational measures should be adopted to prevent, oversee, and treat SBIs and SBS in patients hospitalized for COVID-19.
