Adaptation and validation of the Parents’ Self-stigma Scale into Turkish and its association with parenting stress and parental self-efficacy
Authors: Yahya AKTU, Ercan ARAS
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn the present era, parents frequently stigmatize themselves for their children’s negative behaviors and inadequate social skills. Parents’ self-stigma (PSS) may lead to a decrease in parental self-efficacy and quality of marital and family life. In light of these reasons, the principal objective of this study to assess the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the PSS Scale (PSSS) as developed by Eaton et al. (2019) and to investigate the indirect effect that parenting stress has on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy.MethodsWe collected data from a total of 1,118 parents via random sampling, with the first part of the study involving 645 participants (Mage = 32.64 ± 7.28) and the second part of the study involving 473 participants (Mage = 27.43 ± 9.87). In the first part of the study, we employed structural equation modeling for the confirmatory factor analysis and Pearson’s correlation coefficient for the criterion-related validity, average variance extracted, and composite reliability analyses. Moreover, we calculated Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and Guttman split-half coefficients for the reliability analyses. In the second part of the study, we utilized Hayes’ bootstrapping method to assess the indirect effect of parenting stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy.ResultsThe first part of the study confirms the PSSS’s 11-item, 3-factor structure, showing the Turkish form to have acceptable goodness-of-fit indices, and found Cronbach’s alpha for the PSSS to be 0.89. Furthermore, the first part of the study demonstrates a significant negative correlation between marital life satisfaction and PSS. Meanwhile, the second part of the study has determined PSS to be positively related to parenting stress and negatively related to parental self-efficacy. The second part of the study also indicates parenting stress to have an indirect effect on the association between PSS and parental self-efficacy.ConclusionsThe study indicates the Turkish version of the PSSS to be a valid and reliable instrument in Turkish culture for measuring parents’ PSS levels regarding their children, with higher scores indicating greater PSS. The scale can be effectively used in both research and clinical settings. The study also suggests parental stress to have a possible impact on the association between PSS and parental self-efficacy. Furthermore, addressing the variables of PSS and parenting stress in family-focused interviews and therapeutic interventions may contribute to increasing parental self-efficacy.

Introduction

Parents’ self-stigma (PSS) is a sensitive issue that can limit parents’ social lives. PSS refers to the negative thoughts that parents may have about themselves, such as bad-parent self-beliefs, self-blame, and self-shame [1]. Due to the potential for stigmatization, parents may experience social isolation and develop negative self-perceptions [2] Some parents may feel that their children’s negative behaviors and lack of social skills reflect badly on them [3]. Thus, parents may experience exclusion and social stigmatization, as well as family problems [4, 5]. For instance, in the event of a conflict with their child, a parent may experience feelings of inadequacy, worthlessness, and helplessness, which may lead to a fear of being rejected by their child. Such conditions may have a detrimental impact on the parent’s marital and relational dynamics within the family unit [1]. PSS may be accompanied by guilt, feelings of inadequacy [6], depressive symptoms, stress, and anxiety [7]. PSS may have a range of negative outcomes, including increased workload for family members, heightened family problems, negative impacts on parents’ psychological well-being, withdrawal from work life, and economic difficulties [4, 8]. PSS can be described as a cognitive and emotional phenomenon influenceable by a variety of factors.

Parenting stress is one of the factors associated with PSS [9]. Stress can occur in various roles [10], and being a parent is one role that can be perceived as a stress factor [11, 12]. Parenting involves fulfilling certain duties and responsibilities, and meeting these obligations is important for ensuring the well-being of both the parents as well as the children [13]. Parenting stress is characterized by the difficulty or strain that parents experience in their relationship with their children while parenting [14]. For example, parenting stress can occur when a parent faces difficulties fulfilling their child-related responsibilities and is often characterized by the difficulties parents experience raising children [15]. Individuals under stress, parents included, may experience such physical symptoms as low energy, headaches, and fatigue [16]. However, acknowledging that various factors can contribute to parental stress is important. For instance, the arrival of a new baby that does not meet the family’s expectations can become a source of stress. Similarly, a baby with developmental issues can also lead to parental stress. Moreover, as a result of a child’s difficult behaviors, a parent may start doubting their parental responsibilities and experience stress [3]. Factors that increase parental stress are thought to include the nature of parents’ duties, work conditions, family relationships, chronic illnesses, and children’s characteristics [17]. Therefore, parental stress and PSS can be said to be positively related.

Another factor associated with PSS is parental self-efficacy [18], which refers to how successfully an individual fulfills their parenting role [19, 20]. In other words, parental self-efficacy can be defined as parents’ belief in their ability to raise their children healthily. Questioning one’s own parenting competence reduces confidence in one’s parental abilities [21]. Tahmassian et al. [22] found that higher perceptions of social support were linked to lower levels of parenting stress, with support networks perhaps enhancing parental self-efficacy. Crnic and Ross [19] found parental self-efficacy to be negatively associated with parental stress. Similarly, Hong and Liu [23] determined low parental self-efficacy to increase parental stress. Furthermore, important causes for this are thought to include developmental delays, behavioral problems, parental attitudes, and perceptions of parental efficacy [24]. The literature contains evidence that parents who have difficulty coping with a child with a chronic illness and who perceive themselves as inadequate experience higher levels of stress [25]. Another the study identified low parental self-efficacy as a factor that increases parental stress [26]. Parental self-efficacy increases the belief that problems related to their children will be resolved, enables parents’ self-confidence, and allows them to experience less stress. An individual who is unable to fulfil one’s parental role may experience feelings of inadequacy. Parents may feel that they cannot provide enough support for their children, which can lead to an increase in workload and stress levels in the family [27]. Low parental self-efficacy is associated with parental stress and a decrease in parent-child communications [28]; therefore, parental self-efficacy can be said to be negatively related to parental stress and PSS.

The present study

This study has two main objectives. The first is to adapt the Parents’ Self-Stigmatization Scale (PSSS) developed by Eaton et al. [1] to Turkish culture. The second is to examine the indirect effect of parental stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy. The PSSS measures parents’ negative self-perceptions and self-stigmatization because of their children. The scale consists of the subdimensions of self-blame, self-shame, and bad-parent self-beliefs. Self-blame represents the responsibility and guilt of having a problem child. Self-shame refers to the shame and embarrassment of having a problem child. Bad-parent self-beliefs express negative or poor parenting perceptions and thoughts. In Turkish culture, parents are known to use cognitive distortions associated with stigmatization [29–31]. The PSSS can measure the level of stigmatization related to parenting among Turkish parents. In this way, parents can become aware of their misperceptions about stigmatization related to their children. An additional important value of the study that the Turkish version of the PSSS is the first instrument to be able to measure Turkish parents’ level of self-stigmatization.

The PSSS differs in some aspects from the Affiliate Stigma Scale [32] and the Parents’ Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness Scale [1, 33]. These differences are related to the multidimensionality of the PSSS. Unlike other scales, the PSSS includes self-blame, self-shame, and negative self-beliefs. While other scales have focused on parents in relatively limited situations, the PSSS focuses on parents over a full range of situations. Furthermore, qualitative interviews with parents were conducted while developing the PSSS to create an item pool. This shows the PSSS to be compatible for obtaining more valid results. Compared to other scales, the PSSS also has relatively few items and dimensions that measure different aspects, making it easy to use. A relationship has been noted to exist for the brief form of a scale with participants’ response rates, willingness to respond, and data quality [34]. Moreover, a large number of scale items can lead to inattention when answering the scale [35]. Having participants spend a short time responding to measurement instruments has a positive effect on data collection [36]. For instance, having a small number of items may reduce the amount of time participant need to respond, which may also increase the likelihood that more participants will participate in a study. This method allows for healthier data collection and more accurate data analysis. In this regard, having fewer items on a measurement tool can be said to effectively decrease the required time while increasing participant attention and data quality. Thus, the fact that the PSSS consists of 11 items and three sub-dimensions shows the measurement tool to be more useful.

As a result of the literature review, no measurement tool could be found for determining the level of parents’ self-stigma in Turkish culture. Determining the level of PSS and identifying the causes of stigmatization are important for planning effective studies on parents and families. However, given the increasing number of studies on parents in the field of mental health, the need exists to adapt the PSSS to Turkish culture. Meanwhile, although an important link between parental self-efficacy and PSS has been described, limited information is found about how parenting stress functions in this relationship [1, 9, 18, 19, 27]. In line with this, we aim to examine the psychometric properties of the PSSS and introduce it to Turkish culture. We also aim to reveal the indirect effect of parental stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy.

The literature review revealed no Turkish version of the PSSS to exist and PSS to be associated with parental self-efficacy and parenting stress. Parenting stress has also been hypothesized as perhaps having a function in the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy. The hypotheses developed in this context are as follows: The Turkish adaptation of the PSSS is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring parents’ self-stigmatization (H1). A relationship exists between PSS and parental self-efficacy (H2), as well as between PSS and parenting stress (H3). Furthermore, the model suggests the presence of a relationship between parenting stress and parental self-efficacy (H4). Parenting stress is also hypothesized to mediate the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy (H5).

Part I

The first part of the study conducts confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs), criterion-related validity analyses, and reliability studies regarding the PSSS.

Method

Participants

This part of the study adapts the PSSS to Turkish society and uses the simple random sampling method to collect data from 645 parents (483 females and 162 males) in three provinces with a low socioeconomic status in Türkiye’s Eastern and the Southeastern Anatolian regions for the CFA. Simple random sampling ensures each participant has an equal chance of being selected [37]. We also preferred random sampling to ensure that the participants were representative of the population. In order to conduct a power analysis, the following values were calculated: α = 0.05, effect size = 0.10, and power level = 0.90. Thus, the requisite number of participants was determined to be 415. We then decided to conduct the study on 645 participants to make sure that the sample size is sufficient for the purposes of the study [38]. Table 1 presents the participants’ demographics.

Participant demographics

Table 1 shows 74.9% of the participants in the CFA to be female. The participants’ ages range between 21 and 65 (Mage = 32.64 ± 7.28). The educational background of the participants is as follows: 6.2% have only completed primary school, 15.2% have completed up to secondary school, 13.5% have completed up to high school, 32.2% have completed a university degree, and 2.6% have completed postgraduate studies. Of the participants, 71.5% follow a democratic parenting style and 86.8% are coparents with one or more children living at home.

Procedure

In the absence of a suitable measurement tool for PSS in Türkiye, we elected to adapt the PSSS into Turkish. To do so, we first obtained permission in 2023 from Dr. Eaton, one of the authors who developed the PSSS. Subsequently, we obtained permission from the Igdir University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee in Türkiye (Decision No. 2023/6). The scale was translated with the assistance of two English linguists and then reviewed by two linguists with a PhD in Turkish. Two experts in the field of psychology, as well as a measurement and evaluation expert in response to the feedback revised the scale. Subsequently, the Turkish and English versions of the scale were administered to 38 university students studying in the English Language Teaching Department. The correlation coefficient between the Turkish and English versions of the scale was found to be 0.79. Adjustments were made to the scale instructions, and the finalized scale form was piloted face-to-face with 33 parents. The pilot application revealed an internal consistency coefficient of 0.83, with item-total correlations ranging between 0.30 and 0.65. Between 30 and 50 individuals was determined as a sufficient number of participants for pilot testing [39, 40]. Following the pilot study, we administered the scale to a larger sample to obtain the final version. Prior to commencing the research, we received informed consent from all participants. We assured the parents that the information provided would be kept strictly confidential. Parents were then invited by social media to participate in the study using the simple random sampling method. Data from the 645 participants who completed the voluntary consent form were collected online between May-October 2023. All the participants received informed consent before the execution of the research. Data collection took approximately 20 min. Lastly, we conducted validity and reliability analyses over the results.

Measures

Parents’ self-stigma scale

The PSSS was developed by Eaton et al. [1], consists of 11 items, and has a three-dimensional structure. The self-blame component of the scale has five items, while the self-shame and bad-parent self-beliefs components each have three items. The measurement tool is a 5-point Likert-type self-report scale whose answers ranges from “Never” (1) to “Always” (5), with Items 3, 7, and 9 being reverse scored. The scores obtainable from the scale range between 11 and 55. The CFA results indicate the fit indices to be at acceptable levels (χ2(25) = 30.71, RMSEA = 0.03, NFI = 0.97, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99). Additionally, the scale was found to have internal consistency (α = 0.82).

Married life satisfaction scale

The Turkish version of this scale, which was originally developed by Johnson et al. [41], was conducted by Çelik [42] and consists of one dimension and five items. It is a 7-point Likert-type self-report scale scored between “Strongly disagree” (1) and “Strongly agree” (7). The CFA results show excellent fit indices (χ2 / df = 1.416, RMSEA = 0.036, SRMR = 0.01, RFI = 0.99, IFI = 1.00, GFI = 0.99, AGFI = 0.97, CFI = 1.00). The scale produces scores ranging between 5 and 35 and has an internal consistency of 0.85.

Demographic information form

The researchers developed this survey to gather data on such variables as gender, age, perceived income status, and educational status.

Statistical analysis

Prior to undertaking the data analysis, we needed to screen for missing values and range values and to ascertain the normality of the dataset using the program SPSS Statistics 27.0. According to the assumptions of normality, the skewness and kurtosis values are recommended to be within the range of ± 1.5 [43], which is what we found. To test the factor structure of the PSSS, we transferred the data to SPSS AMOS (version 24) and performed CFA. To test the normality of the variables, we determined the Mahalanobis distances to exceed the values in the Chi-square table. In this instance, we selected the asymptotically distribution-free (ADF) method as the preferred approach for conducting the analysis, in accordance with the recommendations set forth in the literature [44]. Following this, we examined the Chi-square model fit (χ2 / df), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), comparative fit index (CFI), normed fit index (NFI), and goodness-of-fit index (GFI). In general, a ratio of χ2 / df > 2; RMSEA and SRMR > 0.05; and CFI, GFI, NFI, and TLI > 0.95 indicate excellent fit. Conversely, a ratio of χ2 / df > 5; RMSEA and SRMR > 0.08; and CFI, GFI, NFI, and TLI > 0.90 indicate acceptable fit [43]. As part of the structural equation modeling, we examined validity indices to ensure convergent and divergent validity. Average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) are recommended to respectively be greater than 0.50 and 0.70. When AVE is less than CR and CR is greater than 0.60, an AVE of around 0.50 is considered acceptable [38, 44].

We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient to analyze the relationships the PSS and its subdimensions have with the variables measuring marital life satisfaction to ensure criterion validity. The Pearson correlation coefficient was assumed to be 0.10, 0.30, and 0.70, respectively, for small, medium, and large effects [38, 45]. We evaluated the internal consistency of the PSSS using Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and Guttman’s half-split coefficients. To ascertain whether any significant differences occurred between the upper and lower 27th percentiles, we also conducted the independent samples t-test. The internal consistency was found to be greater than 0.70, and the item-total correlation was set at greater than or equal to 0.32 [38, 45].

Part II

The second part of the study examines the indirect effects of parenting stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy.

Method

Participants

The first part of the study has been conducted with participants living in three provinces with a low socioeconomic status in Türkiye’s Eastern and the Southeastern Anatolian regions. Data were collected from 473 parents through simple random sampling. In order to conduct a power analysis, the following values were calculated: α = 0.05, effect size = 0.10, and power level = 0.90. Therefore, the requisite number of participants was determined to be 415. The study was conducted on 473 participants to provide a sufficient sample size for the purposes of the study [38]. The participants’ demographic information is as follows. Of the parents who participated in the study, 76.7% are female. The participants’ ages range between 21 and 65 years (Mage = 27.43 ± 9.87). Of the participants, 19.9% have a primary school, 13.1% a secondary school, 28.1% a high school, 36.2% a university, and 2.7% a postgraduate level of education. According to the variable of perceived income level, 60.0% have a middle socioeconomic status. The variable of perceived income level refers to whether the participants perceive themselves to be in the low-, medium-, or high-level income group. Regarding the variable of number of children, 31.9% of the participants have one child and 26.6% have two children. Of the participants, 27.9% live in a suburb and 31.5% in smaller cities. Of the participants, 90.5% are coparents with one or more children living at home.

Procedure

The second part of the study’s procedures are identical to those in the first part of the study. We obtained the approval of the ethics committee for the first part of the study from the Igdir University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee in Türkiye (Decision No. 2023/6). Parents were randomly selected to participate in the study. We received informed consent from all participants and told them their information would be kept strictly confidential. We collected data from the 473 participants who had completed the informed consent form using an online data collection method between June-November 2023. All the participants received informed consent before the execution of the research. We changed the order of the scales to collect healthy data during the implementation phase. The questionnaires took approximately 20 min to complete.

Measures

Parents’ self-stigma scale

We utilized the Turkish adaptation of the PSSS from The first part of the study to evaluate parental stigmatization in the first part of the study.

Perceived parental self-efficacy scale

This scale was developed by Caprara et al. [47] and adapted to Turkish by Demir and Gündüz [48]. Although the original scale has 12 items, the Turkish version only has 11. The measurement tool consists of one dimension. The scale is a 7-point, Likert-type, self-report scale whose responses range from “Not well at all” (1) to “Very well” (7). Scores between 5 and 35 are obtainable from the scale. The factor loadings of the scale range between 0.53 and 0.84. The internal consistency of the scale was found to be 0.92.

Parenting Stress Scale

Kaymak-Özmen and Özmen [49] developed a one-dimensional measurement tool with 16 items in the form of a 4-point, Likert-type, self-report scale. The scale is designed to assess the stress that parents with school-age children experience as a consequence of the challenges they encounter in their daily lives. Scores range from 16 to 64 and are obtained by selecting responses ranging from “Never” (1) to “Always” (4). Sample items from the scale include: I find myself inadequate at educating my child and I think I am a failure as a parent. The CFA results of the scale indicate good fit indices (χ2/ df = 2.43, RMSEA = 0.05, RMR = 0.03, GFI = 0.93, AGFI = 0.91, CFI = 0.91), with internal consistency being calculated as 0.85.

Demographics

We developed a demographic information form to collect data on variables such as gender, age, perceived income, and educational status.

Statistical analysis

We first evaluated the data in terms of data quality. As a result, we determined that no data were missing and that all the data are correct, accurate, and suitable for data analysis. We then subjected the data to an analysis to determine their compliance with the normality assumptions. The existing literature recommends that skewness and kurtosis values for the data should fall within the range of ± 1.5 [43]. After this, we performed an analysis of variance (ANOVA) to ascertain the impact the variable of parenting status has on the PSS and parenting stress. In order to satisfy the prerequisites for ANOVA, we calculated the homogeneity of variance. Accordingly, the result from Levene’s test should be greater than 0.05, and the p values from the ANOVA table should be less than 0.05. In the event of a statistically significant difference, the least significance difference (LSD) test is recommended as a post-hoc test [38]. The results of the analysis indicate Levene’s test for the variables of PSS and parenting stress to yield a non-significant result (p > 0.05), while the ANOVA table shows a significant outcome (p < 0.05). To identify which specific parenting status groups exhibit the observed difference, we conducted a post-hoc analysis using the LSD test.

Next, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient to analyze the relationships among the variables of PSS, parenting stress, and parental self-efficacy. This correlation coefficient is classified as low (0.10–0.29), medium (0.30–0.69), or high (greater than or equal to 0.70) [38, 45]. We then examined whether multicollinearity problems were present among the variables. To this end, we looked for tolerance values lower than 0.10, variance inflation factors (VIFs) lower than 10, and condition indices lower than 30 [50]. As a result, we determined these criteria to be satisfied and no multicollinearity issues to be present. Lastly, we conducted a bootstrap analysis to assess the predictor coefficients. We assigned the independent variable as PSS, the dependent variable as parental self-efficacy, and parenting stress as the mediating variable. We then examined the indirect effect of stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy by employing the bootstrap test to look for indirect effects, with 10,000 bootstrap samples being applied to Model 4 at a 95% confidence interval (CI). In order to interpret the indirect effect as significant, the CIs were ensured to have no zero values [51]. Data were analyzed using SPSS 27 and PROCESS Macro 4.2.

General discussion

This study initially examined the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the PSSS was developed by Eaton et al. [1]. Subsequently, the study investigated the mediating role of parenting stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy by first evaluating the validity and reliability of the scale and then the direct and indirect relationships between variables. In this regard, all five hypotheses have been accepted. The results demonstrate the Turkish version of the PSSS to exhibit comparable psychometric properties to the original scale. Moreover, a negative correlation has been observed between PSS and marital life satisfaction. Furthermore, the results demonstrate parenting status to exert a notable influence on both PSS and parenting stress. Finally, the relationship between PSS and parental efficacy is mediated by parenting stress.

In support of the first hypothesis, the study results demonstrated the Turkish version of the PSSS to be a valid and reliable measure. This study additionally examined the convergent and divergent validity of the Turkish version of the PSSS within the scope of validity studies. The results indicated the convergent and divergent validity analyses for the self-blame subscale and the poor parental self-beliefs subscale to be acceptable, while the convergent and divergent validity analyses for the self-shaming subscale were satisfactory [38, 44]. These results indicate that the PSSS meets the criteria for convergent and divergent validity.

The study also examined the criterion-related validity of the relationship between PSS and married life satisfaction in the context of validity studies. Specifically, the correlation results indicate a significant negative relationship between PSS and married life satisfaction, with higher PSS scores indicating lower married life satisfaction scores. This finding is consistent with relevant research in the literature, such as Molero et al. [9], who also reported a negative correlation between marital satisfaction and affiliated stigma. Similarly, Gaziel et al. [52] found a negative correlation between self-stigma and life satisfaction. Mak and Cheung [5] observed stigmatization to increase the stress related to child caregiving. Based on these findings, parents who experience stigmatization can be concluded to have lower levels of marital satisfaction.

In the first part of the study, we calculated Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s Omega, Guttman split-half reliability, and lower and upper 27% t-test values as part of reliability studies. The results indicate high values for Cronbach’s alpha, Guttman split-half, and McDonald’s omega coefficients. In addition, the t-test results for the lower and upper 27th percentiles reveal high item-total correlations for the PSSS, as expected. The satisfactory internal consistency suggests the Turkish version of the PSSS to be a reliable tool for measuring self-stigma among Turkish parents. This consistency ensures that the scale can be effectively used in both research and clinical settings.

Phase II of the study initially investigated the influence of parenting status on PSS and parenting stress. Subsequently, we analyzed the indirect effect of parenting stress on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy. The findings indicate parenting status to have a notable impact on both PSS and parenting stress. In other words, coparents with one or more children tend to have significantly lower levels of PSS and parenting stress compared to parents with other parenting statuses. In general, coparents with one or more children reported lower levels of stress and exhibited less self-stigmatization. These findings align with those of previous studies on family and marital mental health regarding parenting status [30, 53, 54]. The status of parents within a family unit represents one of several factors that may affect the mental health of the family as a whole. The absence of one parent in the family unit may result in social stigmatization or exclusion [55, 56]. For instance, the absence of a father in the family unit may lead other people to perceive the family as incomplete or inadequate. Recent studies have indicated self-stigmatization to be more prevalent in single-parent families [30, 57]. This result indicates that a parent may engage in self-stigmatization, whereby they perceive themselves as an inadequate parent and incapable of providing sufficient care for their child. Thus, parents with children may have more opportunities to manage the household, raise children, and cope with economic challenges compared to parents in other parenting situations, including coparenting [58]. The fact that coparents with one or more children experience less parenting stress and PSS may be because coparents share more with the child and have positive communications with the child.

This research showed PSS to negatively predict parental self-efficacy, as anticipated by Hypothesis 2. In accordance with the second objective of the present study, we examined the indirect impacts of parenting stress on the correlation between PSS and parental self-efficacy. Our first finding in this regard is that a significantly negative correlation exists between PSS and parental self-efficacy. This result suggests PSS to be a crucial factor affecting parental self-efficacy, with high levels of PSS able to decrease parental self-efficacy. This finding supports Hypothesis 2, as well as previous research that has indicated PSS to be linked to reduced parental self-efficacy [9, 18, 59]. Corrigan et al. [18] suggested PSS to be a significant determinant of parental self-efficacy, while Chan and Leung [59] found self-stigmatization to be associated with decreased self-efficacy. Molero et al. [9] similarly reported self-stigma to be linked to low self-efficacy. Corrigan et al.’s [60] study detected the self-efficacy levels of self-stigmatizing mothers to decrease. This suggests that PSS may have a negative impact on parental self-efficacy.

Consistent with Hypotheses 3 and 4, the second part of the study indicated PSS to positively predict parenting stress, while parenting stress negatively predicts parental self-efficacy. The study reveals a significantly positive correlation between PSS and parenting stress, with this result being supported by previous studies [61–63]. For instance, Cheung et al. [62] found parenting stress to be associated with PSS, while Chan et al. [61] concluded parenting stress to increase PSS. Other studies, such as Chu et al.’s [64] and Hasson-Ohayon et al.’s [65], have reported similar results that also indicate PSS to increase parenting stress. The present study also detected a significantly negative relationship between parenting stress and parental self-efficacy. Previous research can be said to support all these results. Hong and Liu [23] found an association between parental self-efficacy and parenting stress. Crnic and Ross [19] also reported a negative relationship between parental self-efficacy and parenting stress. Based on these results, a negative link can be concluded to exist for parental self-efficacy with both PSS and parenting stress.

In support of Hypothesis 5, the study has concluded the association between PSS and parental self-efficacy to be mediated by parenting stress. This study demonstrated parenting stress to indirectly affect the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy. In other words, this finding provides evidence that parenting stress may have an indirect effect on the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy. This result can be explained by the Why Try Model [18], which proposes stigmatization to have three factors (i.e., awareness, agreement, and implementation). When these factors are present, parents may avoid behaviors that express self-efficacy. The theory also suggests that self-efficacy arises as a result of stigmatization. Parents may perceive themselves negatively or blame themselves, which can prevent them from seeing themselves as adequate and competent. Eaton et al. [4] stated that parents experience more stress and perceive themselves as less competent when self-stigmatizing. Therefore, reducing PSS and stress is crucial for enhancing parental self-efficacy.

Limitations and future directions

This study has both limitations and strengths. The first limitation is that the data were obtained online from a sample of parents aged 21–65. To enhance the generalizability of the findings across diverse socioeconomic groups of parents, face-to-face data collection methods may need to be employed. Another limitation is the risk of response bias due to the measurement tools being based on self-report measures. The parents’ responses may also include social bias. Future studies may wish to corroborate the quantitative results using qualitative and mixed-method research to ensure social validity. The lack of test-retest reliability is third limitation of the study. To evaluate the psychometric properties of the PSSS, researchers can examine test-retest reliability to measure invariance over time. Fourthly, the study employs a cross-sectional, correlational survey model. Although the results indicate a correlation among the variables of PSS, parenting stress, and parental self-efficacy, longitudinal and experimental studies are necessary to ascertain the existence of a causal relationship. Fifthly, the data presented in this study were collected between May-October 2023. External conditions may have affected the participants’ circumstances. Thus, we recommend collecting data during the same period to ensure the validity of the results. Sixthly, the present study focused on adapting the PSSS to a sample of Turkish parents. We advise examining the PSS using qualitative research methods in order to gain a deeper understanding of the cultural context and its dynamics. Seventhly, the majority of participants in this study were female. Further research is required to ascertain the generalizability of the findings to all parents. Lastly, this study has examined parental self-efficacy and parental stress, which are associated with PSS. Future studies can examine other variables associated with PSS, such as parenthood regret and parental burnout.

Conclusions

The study has confirmed the original 11-item PSSS’s three-dimensional structure for a sample of Turkish parents. The research findings demonstrate the Turkish version of the PSSS to be a valid and reliable tool for measuring parental self-stigmatization levels regarding their children in Turkish culture, with higher scale scores suggesting greater parental stigmatization. Validity and reliability analyses of the Turkish version of the scale are comparable to those of the original version. Moreover, the study has revealed coparents with children to experience lower levels of parenting stress and PSS. Furthermore, the study also indicates parenting stress to mediate the relationship between PSS and parental self-efficacy. This is the first study in Türkiye to examine the variables of PSS, parental self-efficacy, and parenting stress together. The current study is important for revealing the underlying mechanisms in the association between PSS and parental self-efficacy. Based on the results, intervention programs for parents can be implemented in order to increase parental self-efficacy and to reduce parental stress and PSS.
