Reduce Computational Cost In Deep Reinforcement Learning Via Randomized Policy Learning
Zhuochen Liu, Rahul Jain, Quan Nguyen

Abstract
Recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) have leveraged neural networks to achieve state-of-the-art performance across various control tasks. However, these successes often come at the cost of significant computational resources, as training deep neural networks requires substantial time and data. In this paper, we introduce an actor-critic algorithm that utilizes randomized neural networks to drastically reduce computational costs while maintaining strong performance. Despite its simple architecture, our method effectively solves a range of control problems, including the locomotion control of a highly dynamic 12-motor quadruped robot, and achieves results comparable to leading algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Notably, our approach does not outperform other algorithms in terms of sample efficnency but rather in terms of wall-clock training time. That is, although our algorithm requires more timesteps to converge to an optimal policy, the actual time required for training turns out to be lower.



1
Introduction

Control problems are often framed as sequential decision-making tasks and modeled using Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). These problems are typically solved by learning an optimal control policy through Reinforcement Learning (RL). When the state and action spaces are finite and the system model is known, optimal policies can be derived using dynamic programming (DP) algorithms. However, in many real-world applications, such as playing the board game Go or controlling a quadruped robot, the state and action spaces are often large or continuous, and the system model is unknown, making traditional DP algorithms inapplicable.

To address the challenge of unknown system models, model-free RL algorithms (
Mnih et al. 2015
;
Schulman et al. 2015a
;
Mnih et al. 2016
;
Haarnoja et al. 2018
;
Lillicrap et al. 2015
) rely on empirical sample-based approximations rather than explicit models. While these methods require a data generator, this requirement is often not restrictive. For example, game-playing agents can explore strategies by playing the game, and locomotion controllers can learn policies using high-fidelity physics simulations. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of model-free RL across various domains (
Silver et al. 2016
;
Silver et al. 2018
;
Bellegarda et al. 2022
;
Bellegarda & Ijspeert 2022
;
Kumar et al. 2021
;
Siekmann et al. 2021
).

To handle continuous state and action spaces, approximation-based RL algorithms (
Kakade 2001
;
Van Hasselt & Wiering 2007
;
Wang et al. 2019
) have been proposed. These methods approximate the value function and policy to generalize across inputs and outputs. In recent years, neural networks have become the dominant choice for function approximation due to their scalability, allowing them to handle increasingly complex problems (
Kendall et al. 2019
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2016
;
Raghu et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2017
).

Modern RL algorithms are typically categorized as policy-based (
Sutton et al. 1999
;
Gu et al. 2016
), value-based (
Watkins & Dayan 1992
;
Mnih et al. 2013
), or actor-critic (
Konda & Tsitsiklis 1999
;
Wu et al. 2017
), which combines benefits of both approaches. Nevertheless, model-free algorithms are slow to converge because they require a large number of samples to achieve a good estimation of the expectation. The application of deep neural networks amplifies this problem due to their high amount of trainable parameters and the consequent need of sufficient training data to avoid overfitting.

In this paper, we focus on continuous MDP problems, where both the state and action spaces are continuous, hence requiring function approximation for both the policy and value functions. Specifically, our work adopts
randomized
neural networks as function approximators, where only the last layer is fine-tuned while all other hidden layers remain randomly initialized. Previous study (
Rahimi & Recht 2008a
;
Rahimi & Recht 2008b
;
Gallicchio & Scardapane 2020
) has shown that such networks can match or even surpass the performance of fully trainable neural networks while significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters.

To leverage the advantages of randomized neural networks, we propose the RANDomized POlicy Learning (RANDPOL) algorithm. RANDPOL is an actor-critic algorithm that employs randomized neural networks for both the policy and value functions. The actor network is updated via stochastic policy gradient and the critic network is updated via temporal difference. Fine-tuning the last layer can be interpreted as learning a linear combination of random basis functions from a function space.

Notably, RANDPOL not only matches the performance of fully trainable neural networks but also requires less time for training. We conduct comprehensive evaluations in OpenAI Gym (
Brockman et al. 2016
) environments and compare our approach with other widely used stochastic on-policy algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (
Schulman et al. 2017
), Advantage Actor Critic (A2C)
(
Mnih et al. 2016
)
, and Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2015a
)
. Additionally, we demonstrate RANDPOL’s real-world applicability by learning an end-to-end locomotion control policy for a quadruped robot.

2
Preliminaries

Random function approximation error bound analysis.
Because our algorithm is based on randomized neural networks, it is important to know how well the randomized function basis is able to approximate other classes of functions that we are interested in.
Rahimi & Recht 2008a
shows that a target function within a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) can be approximated by certain kind of nonlinear random functions with an error within
O(1/\sqrt{K})
with high probability, where
K
is the number of random functions.
Rahimi & Recht 2008b
further expands the conclusion that random shallow neural networks can be used as random function bases for approximation with a bounded error of
O(1/\sqrt{K})
. In addition,
Gallicchio & Scardapane 2020
shows that deep random neural networks can also be used as random function bases to reduce the training time in magnitudes, with a trade off of very small or even negligible loss of accuracy.

Empirical bellman operator.
In reinforcement learning, an optimal value function is learned via a Bellman Operator
G:\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
such that
[Gv](x)=\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\{r(x,u)+\gamma\mathbb{E}[v(x^{\prime})|x,u]\}\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
. However, when the system model is unknown, computing the expectation becomes challenging.
Haskell et al. 2019
shows that we can replace the Bellman Operator
G
with an empirical Bellman Operator
\widehat{G}
such that
[\widehat{G}v](x)=\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\{r(x,u)+\gamma\frac{\gamma}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v(x_{i}^{\prime})\}
, where
\{x_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are samples of the next state collected from a generator given the current state
x
and the performed action
u
. Furthermore,
Haskell et al. 2019
shows that by combining the empirical Bellman Operator
\widehat{G}
with function approximation and applying
\widehat{G}
to
v
sufficiently many iterations
K
,
v_{K}
will converge to the fixed point
v^{*}
with a high probability.

Problem formulation.
Consider an infinite horizon discounted reward continuous MDP
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},P,r,\gamma)
where
\mathcal{X}
is the state space,
\mathcal{U}
is the action spaces,
P(\cdot|x,u)
is the transition probability kernel,
r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor.

Let
\Pi
denote the class of stochastic markov policies mappings
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}({\mathcal{U}})
, the space of probability distributions over the action space. For a trajectory
\tau=(x_{0},\pi(x_{0}),x_{1},\pi(x_{1}),\dots,x_{T},\pi(x_{T}))
of length
T
, we represent its discounted return as
R(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}r_{t}
and its probability given policy
\pi
as
P(\tau|\pi)=\rho_{0}(x_{0})\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}P(x_{t+1}|x_{t},u_{t})\pi(u_{t}|x_{t})
, where
\rho_{0}
is the probability distribution of the initial state of the trajectory.

The expected return of a policy
\pi
is then denoted as
J(\pi)=\int_{\tau}P(\tau|\pi)R(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}[R(\tau)]
and our objective is to find the optimal policy
\pi^{*}=\arg\,\max_{\pi}J(\pi)
.

One common approach to represent the policy is by using parameterized functions such as neural networks. In this way, we can compute the gradient of the expected return
J(\pi_{\theta})
with respect to parameters
\theta
and maximize
J(\pi_{\theta})
through gradient ascent:
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+\beta\nabla_{\theta}J(\pi_{\theta_{k}})
. By taking the logarithm over the trajectory probability
P(\tau|\pi)
, we can represent the gradient of
J(\pi_{\theta})
as following:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\pi_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}P(\tau|\pi_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}\log P(\tau|\pi_{\theta})R(\tau)

(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\nabla_{\theta}\log P(\tau|\pi_{\theta})R(\tau)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(u_{t}|x_{t})R(\tau)]

and we can replace
R(\tau)
by an advantage function (
Schulman et al. 2015b
)
A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x_{t},u_{t})=Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(x_{t},u_{t})-V^{\pi_{\theta}}(x_{t})
, where
Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R(\tau)|x_{0}=x,u_{0}=u]
and
V^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R(\tau)|x_{0}=x]
.

Since our algorithm takes advantage of randomized function approximation, we need a function space to draw randomized functions from. Let
\Theta
be a set of parameters and let
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. According to
Rahimi & Recht 2008a
, the feature functions need to satisfy
\sup_{(x,\theta)\in\mathcal{X}\times\Theta}|\phi(x;\theta)|\leq 1
, e.g., the Fourier features
\phi(x;\theta)=\cos(w^{\prime}x+b),\theta=(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. We formally define the function space as

\mathcal{F}(\Theta):=\left\{f:f(x)=\int_{\Theta}\phi(x;\theta)\alpha(\theta)d\theta\right\}

where
|\alpha(\theta)|\leq C\nu(\theta)
for some constant
C\geq 0,\forall\theta\in\Theta
. We are interested in finding the best fit within finite sums of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{J_{Q}}\alpha_{j}\phi(x;\theta_{j})
. Doing classical function fitting leads to nonconvex optimization problems because of the joint dependency between
\alpha
and
\theta
. Instead, we fix a density
\nu
on
\Theta
and draw a random sample
\theta_{j}
from
\Theta
for
j=1,2,\dots J_{Q}
. Once these
(\theta_{j})_{j=1}^{J_{Q}}
are fixed, we consider the space of functions

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}(\theta^{1:J_{Q}}):=\left\{f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{J_{Q}}\alpha_{j}\phi(x;\theta_{j})\right\}

where
||(\alpha_{1},\dots\alpha_{J_{Q}})||_{\infty}\leq C/J_{Q}
. Now, it remains to calculate weights
\alpha
by minimizing a convex loss.

We also need a function space for approximating the policy. Since
\mathcal{U}
is multi-dimensional, policy
\pi(\cdot|x)
has the form
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots\pi_{d_{U}})
, where
d_{U}
is the dimension of the action space. For each coordinate
k
, we define
\Pi_{k}(\Theta)
, a function space similar to
F(\Theta)
. Let
\psi:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a feature function. Then, for
1\leq k\leq d_{U}
, we define the coordinate projection space as

\Pi_{k}(\Theta):=\left\{f:f(x)=\int_{\Theta}\psi(x;\theta)\alpha(\theta)d\theta\right\}

where
|\alpha(\theta)|\leq C^{\prime}\nu(\theta),\forall\theta\in\Theta
. Let
\widehat{\Pi}_{k}(\theta^{1:J_{\pi_{k}}})
denote an approximation of
\Pi_{k}(\Theta)
which is defined similar to
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}(\theta^{1:J_{Q}})
.

Rationale.
The motivation behind choosing randomized function spaces (where the parameters are chosen randomly) is that randomization is cheaper than optimization, especially when the system is getting complicated, and such methods have already been well-studied, as introduced in Sec.
2
. Existing work (
Amit & Geman 1997
;
Rahimi & Recht 2007
;
Moosmann et al. 2006
) has also provided adequate evidence that algorithms utilizing randomized functions are fast and accurate enough in different applications.

With the definition of the function space that we adopt in our algorithm, we now present the steps to update the critic network and the actor network.

Because we have a stochastic policy in a continuous MDP, the stochastic policy gradient method is a common choice to optimize the policy. In particular, we replace the expectation in Equation
1
by an empirical average of samples and replace the return by an advantage function. By subtracting a baseline
V^{\pi}(x,u)
which is independent of
u
from

To avoid confusion with parameters in the value function, we denote the learnable parameters
\alpha

\alpha^{\prime}

\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}}J(\pi_{\theta,\alpha^{\prime}})=\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}}\frac{1}{N_{\pi}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\pi}}\log\pi(u_{n}|x_{n},\theta,\alpha^{\prime})A^{\pi}(x_{n},u_{n})

(4)

||\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...,\alpha_{J}||_{\infty}\leq C/J_{\pi}

\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}}J(\pi_{\theta,\alpha^{\prime}})=\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\frac{1}{N_{\pi}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\pi}}\log\pi(u_{n}|x_{n},\theta,\alpha^{\prime})A^{\pi}(x_{n},u_{n})-h\cdot||\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\alpha_{2}^{\prime},...,\alpha_{J_{\pi}}^{\prime}||_{\infty})

(5)

To assess the efficacy of RANDPOL, we evaluated it on four OpenAI Gym environments: two Box2D tasks, LunarLander and BipedalWalker, and two MuJoCo
(

To ensure the correctness of our algorithm’s implementation and training setup, we implemented RANDPOL using the Stable-Baselines3 library

Raffin et al. 2021

and utilized fine-tuned training results from the same library, which are reported in Open RL Benchmark (ORLB)
(
Huang et al. 2024

We recognize that hardware specifications can significantly impact an algorithm’s training speed. To quantify the extent to which improved hardware affects computational efficiency, we ran each baseline algorithm on the two MuJoCo environments using the same machine as where we run RANDPOL, and compared its frames per second (FPS) with those reported in ORLB. These two environments were chosen because the fine-tuned hyperparameters for the three baseline algorithms use a single learning agent, as does RANDPOL across all four environments. In contrast, the three baseline algorithms employ varying numbers of parallel learning agents in two Box2D environments, making a direct comparison of time efficiency difficult.

To ensure consistency, we followed the same execution commands provided in ORLB for each baseline algorithm, and used the same fine-tuned hyperparameters, which are available in RL Baselines3 Zoo
(
Raffin 2020
)
. The FPS comparison, shown in Table

Learn a Quadruped Robot Controller

We aim to evaluate our algorithm’s effectiveness in solving real-world problems, such as controlling a quadruped robot’s locomotion through end-to-end reinforcement learning. The robot model used in our experiments is the Unitree Go1, which features 12 motors controlling the hip, thigh, and calf of each leg. To prevent hardware damage during training, we employed the PyBullet physics engine
(
Coumans & Bai 2016–2021
)
for simulation and conducted all learning in a simulated environment.

Our simulation setup closely follows that of
Bellegarda et al. 2022
, who proposed an effective framework for training an end-to-end high-speed running controller. This setup incorporates dynamics randomization, a random heavy payload off-center on the robot, random rigid obstacles on the ground, and variable ground friction, making the learning process significantly more challenging.

We adopted this simulation setup but fine-tuned certain terms and reward function weights to better accommodate the Go1 robot rather than the A1 model used in their study. Additionally, while
Bellegarda et al. 2022
utilized a Cartesian PD controller (which outputs foot positions as commands) for ease of learning, we trained both a Cartesian PD controller and a joint PD controller (which outputs motor torques as commands) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of our algorithm. Given the complexity of this task, we expanded our performance comparison to include off-policy algorithms, specifically Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)
(
Lillicrap et al. 2015
)
, Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
, and Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3)
(
Fujimoto et al. 2018
)
.

During the training, all algorithms use the same number of 16 parallel learning agents, same set of hyperparameters, and same neural network architecture. RANDPOL’s neural network architecture is slightly different due to its essence of a linear combination of random function. Full list of hyperparameters, neural network architecture, and the number of trainable parameters are provided in Appendix
A
.

As shown in Figure

(a), RANDPOL outperforms all other algorithms in both controllers not only in cumulative reward but also in learning stability by having the smallest performance variance across different training trials. Moreover, Figure
3

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel actor-critic algorithm for continuous MDPs. Both the actor and critic leverage randomized functions to approximate the optimal policy and value functions, effectively reducing computational burden when using deep neural networks. Through comprehensive empirical evaluation, we demonstrated the algorithm’s ability to rapidly learn an optimal policy and adapt across diverse challenging tasks.

Future work.
Our algorithm learns an effective linear combination of randomized functions, which currently limits it to MLP architectures.
Gallicchio & Scardapane 2020
has established a strong foundation for randomized convolutional and recurrent networks in supervised learning. Given the widespread use of these architectures in imaging and sequential decision-making tasks, extending our approach to incorporate more advanced randomized networks could significantly enhance its applicability to solving MDPs.
