ECPR in Maternal Cardiac Arrest With Intrauterine Fetal Demise
Authors: Saumya Prasad, Niti Dalal, Aabha Divya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: cardiac assist devices, cardiomyopathy, pregnancy

Maternal cardiac arrest is among the most demanding emergencies in clinical medicine. It is rare enough that most clinicians manage it only once or twice in a career, but unforgiving of any delay. Among more than 10 million United States delivery hospitalizations, cardiac arrest occurred at a rate of 13.4 per 100,000, with roughly two-thirds of affected patients surviving to discharge.1 Encouragingly, peripartum patients supported with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) achieve survival among the highest of any ECMO population.2 In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Pahuja et al3 describe a 32-year-old woman at 24 weeks and 5 days with newly diagnosed intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), refractory cardiogenic shock, and PEA arrest who survived with near-normal ventricular function at 3 months.

The patient was reported to be in supraventricular tachycardia at approximately 200 beats/min. After adenosine failed to restore sinus rhythm, administration of procainamide, followed by amiodarone, precipitated hemodynamic collapse. Fetal cardiac activity, although present earlier that day, was noted to be subsequently absent. In a patient with an ejection fraction of 5%-10%, antiarrhythmic-induced rate reduction further depressed cardiac output, and the resulting uteroplacental hypoperfusion was incompatible with fetal survival. Amiodarone carries an Food and Drug Administration Class D designation and is associated with fetal thyroid suppression and neonatal bradycardia,4 but its use was appropriate when lesser agents had failed, and maternal survival was at stake. The IUFD should therefore be understood as a consequence of the underlying disease rather than a failure of management.

The 2015 American Heart Association scientific statement mandates that resources for perimortem cesarean delivery be assembled at the moment of arrest in the second half of pregnancy (Class I) and the procedure performed within 4 minutes without Return of Spontaneous Circulation (Class IIa).5 Uterine evacuation relieves aortocaval compression, improves venous return, and increases the effectiveness of chest compressions. In this case, however, resuscitative hysterotomy was not performed because obstetric personnel and equipment were not available in the catheterization laboratory, chest compressions on a narrow stretcher limited safe operative exposure, and pre-existing Impella arterial access made extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) the fastest route to perfusion.

The 2025 Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) guideline endorses ECPR in pregnant and peripartum patients and recognizes that it may be performed concurrently with, or immediately after, perimortem cesarean delivery, depending on institutional capability.2 However, this logic applies only when fetal viability is not at issue. In the present case, IUFD was confirmed before ECPR was initiated. Had the fetus been viable and ≥28 weeks of gestation, ECPR-first would not be defensible. Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) with a live fetus in situ imposes nonpulsatile perfusion on a uteroplacental circulation that is poorly autoregulated and depends on maternal arterial pulsatility for adequate oxygen delivery. Available data indicate that fetal mortality is higher when ECMO is initiated during an ongoing pregnancy than following delivery.6 At viable gestation, cesarean delivery before cannulation is therefore not merely a guideline recommendation but a physiologic necessity. Even in confirmed IUFD, uterine evacuation, once maternal stabilization permits, is important because a retained fetoplacental unit can release thromboplastic tissue factor, activating the extrinsic coagulation pathway and creating a substrate for disseminated intravascular coagulation in a patient requiring systemic anticoagulation.7 This framework is summarized in Figure 1.

Decision Framework for Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation in Maternal Cardiac Arrest With Intrauterine Fetal Demise

In maternal cardiac arrest, fetal viability and maternal stability determine the sequence of resuscitative hysterotomy and extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation. When the fetus is viable, uterine evacuation should precede VA-ECMO cannulation to improve maternal venous return and avoid fetal exposure to nonpulsatile extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) flow. When intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD) or nonviability is confirmed, rapid extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) may be prioritized if cannulation capability is immediately available, with uterine evacuation pursued after maternal stabilization to reduce ongoing coagulation risk. DIC = disseminated intravascular coagulation; VA-ECMO = Veno arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

A systematic review identified 45 cases across 26 publications, with maternal survival approaching 78%.6 Two features of the present case merit particular attention. First, femoral venous cannulation was feasible despite the gravid uterus. Although caval compression is often presumed to make blind-wire femoral access difficult, the venous cannula was advanced into the right atrium at 24 weeks’ gestation, achieving effective flow. This should temper reflexive pessimism about cannulation in pregnant patients. Second, the later identification of a pathogenic Titin (TTN)-truncating variant provided a plausible explanation for fulminant biventricular failure. TTN-truncating variants are the most common genetic cause of dilated cardiomyopathy and have been described in women with peripartum cardiomyopathy.8 However, this diagnosis was made at 3-month follow-up, not during resuscitation. At presentation, the differential included tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, fulminant myocarditis, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and sepsis-related dysfunction. TTN testing is an elective investigation and yields no information actionable in the resuscitative window, and the authors have framed it as an explanation of the acute presentation.

The most contested issue is retention of the IUFD during ECMO. The authors titrated unfractionated heparin to a deliberately conservative activated partial thromboplastin time of 50-70 seconds to maintain rapid reversibility and monitored appropriately. Expectant management during active ECMO support was appropriate. At 24 weeks, delivery would require either a classical (upper section) cesarean section, with substantially higher blood loss than a lower-segment approach, or pharmacologic induction of vaginal delivery. Neither is low risk during therapeutic ECMO anticoagulation. The 11-day retention period was therefore clinically justified. After decannulation on hospital day 7, however, active induction deserved explicit consideration. Spontaneous labor after periviable IUFD is unpredictable, and passive waiting prolongs exposure to thromboplastic disseminated intravascular coagulation. Vaginal misoprostol is the pharmacological agent of choice for second-trimester IUFD induction, achieving delivery in over 85% of cases within 24-40 hours of first administration.9

The central lesson is organizational rather than technical. Successful ECPR depended on pre-existing vascular access, immediate cannulation capability, and multidisciplinary coordination. The 2025 ELSO guideline also emphasizes maintaining equipment and readiness for emergent cesarean delivery while a patient is on ECMO, underscoring that true preparedness must include both resuscitative pathways.2 Institutions caring for high-risk obstetric patients, especially those with advanced heart failure or ECMO programs, should define ECPR activation triggers, assign cross-disciplinary roles in advance, and rehearse scenarios in which standard algorithms must be adapted in real time.

Pahuja et al show what ECPR can achieve when readiness and coordination are already in place. Resuscitative hysterotomy and ECPR are not competing interventions but complementary options within the same decision continuum. Their sequencing depends on what can be performed most rapidly and safely, with 1 critical exception: when the fetus is viable, cesarean delivery before cannulation is a physiological imperative. In a field where randomized trials are unlikely, carefully reported and rigorously interpreted cases remain one of our most useful guides.
