Assessment of maternal knowledge towards Rhesus incompatibility and their attitude about its preventive measures in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulrahman Adel Hawari, Jumanah Y. Nassar, Alaa M. Kadah Salim, Eatedal M. AlShareef, Nada M. AlFarrah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Rh incompatibility arises when a Rh-negative woman carries a Rh-positive fetus, which can lead to Rh alloimmunization and complications. A tertiary care center in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, reported a tenfold higher prevalence of Rh alloimmunization compared to developed countries. This study aimed to assess maternal knowledge, attitudes, and perspectives on Rh incompatibility, including its complications and preventive measures, in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.Methods:This is a descriptive, cross-sectional study that was applied among community mothers, primiparous, and multiparous females living in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Data collection took two months, from June 1, 2024, to July 31, 2024. The data were analyzed using SPSS version 29 and the quantitative variables are presented as numbers and percentages.Results:The mean age of participants was 38 ± 10 years, with equal representation of Saudi and non-Saudi women (50% each). Marital status distribution showed 93% married, 5% divorced, and 2% widowed. Key factors linked to higher knowledge scores included younger age, higher education levels, and antenatal care attendance. Younger mothers had better scores, as did those with higher educational attainment and antenatal care experience.Conclusion:Our findings suggest a significant gap in understanding the Rh factor, despite the majority of the participants being aware of it. Moreover, higher levels of education and younger age are associated with better knowledge scores, perhaps providing an opportunity for focused educational programs in the future.

Introduction

The Rh blood group system is comprised of proteins presented on red blood cells (RBC), “Rhesus” related to Rhesus monkeys when first discovered.[1] If a person has the protein, then they are said to be Rh-positive, and those who do not are Rh-negative. Rh incompatibility is a condition that develops when a woman who is Rh-negative becomes pregnant with a fetus in her womb and has Rh-positive blood. During delivery, the fetus’ blood can pass into the maternal circulation, and her body recognizes the Rh protein as a foreign substance and starts producing antibodies against the protein.[2] The first pregnancy will pass uneventfully, but in the next pregnancy, these antibodies can pass into the second fetus and cause serious complications. The severity of the symptoms varies ranging from mild to life-threatening. Initially, the antibodies destroy the fetus’ RBCs, leading to a decrease in RBCs number. Low RBCs tax the heart to compensate by pumping stronger to deliver oxygen to the body, and as a result, cardiac failure may happen. Moreover, attempts from the liver and spleen to produce more RBCs cause hepatosplenomegaly. In severe cases, this can interfere with the liver’s ability to produce other proteins, such as albumin, leading to a condition called hydrops fetalis. Antenatally, if the mother is Rh-negative and not sensitized, she should receive anti-D immunoglobulin at 28 weeks to prevent antibody formation.[3]

The prevalence of rhesus (Rh) negativity differs across various racial and ethnic groups. Around 15% of Caucasians are Rh-negative, while the rate is lower in African-Americans at 5-8%, and even lower among Asians and Native Americans at 1-2%. Local population studies in Saudi Arabia, involving male and female volunteers, indicate that the prevalence of the Rh-negative blood group is 8% in the Eastern region and 7.2% in the Southwestern region. A tertiary care center in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, concluded that the prevalence of Rh alloimmunization was tenfold higher than that reported in the developed countries.[4] Therefore, women’s knowledge about the Rh-negative blood group and the consequences that may occur in Rh incompatibility can play a crucial role in preventing serious fetus comorbidities. A previous cross-sectional study conducted in Pakistan found that only 2% of women had knowledge about consequences related to the Rh-negative blood group during pregnancy, and 94.3% of the mothers were not aware of the crucial precautions that should be taken if a mother’s blood group is Rh-negative.[5] In Saudi Arabia, particularly, a previous study in Arar has shown only 38% of the mothers have knowledge about Rh incompatibility.[6] Nevertheless, there are no studies that have been conducted to evaluate the knowledge of women in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the maternal knowledge, attitude, and perspective regarding rhesus incompatibility, including its complications and preventive measures in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

Methodology

Study design

A descriptive, cross-sectional study type was applied among the community in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The data collection took 2 months starting from June 1, 2024, to July 31, 2024. A widespread electronic questionnaire was used to recruit participants at random from the community via several online platforms. Participants were told in the questionnaire about the purpose of the study and that participation was completely voluntary. In order to maintain anonymity and confidentiality, no personally identifiable information about the participants was present when the data were assembled. The approval from ethics committee is obtained in 18/04/2024.

Questionnaire tool

The survey was derived from an earlier published research paper that Abimbola and Olasubomi[7] Ogun State, Nigeria. It was created using Microsoft Forms, an online tool for creating questionnaires. The survey was created in Arabic, which is the community’s spoken language, after being translated from English. An expert speaker of both languages made revisions to make it more readable for the general population. The questionnaire was a total of 35 closed-ended questions divided into the following 4 categories: Consent (1 question), Demographics (9 questions), Knowledge of Rhesus Factor (9 questions), Knowledge of Rhesus Incompatibility Prevention (6 questions), and Attitude Toward Rhesus Incompatibility Prevention (10 questions). The participant knowledge was evaluated using a knowledge score from 0 to 10 based on the right answers that the participant chose while filling out the questionnaire.

Study populations (Inclusion/Exclusion criteria)

The study included community mothers, primiparous, and multiparous females and living in Jeddah city. Furthermore, the participants who refuse to join the study, males and non-pregnant females who do not have any offsprings, and mothers or non-pregnant females who are not living in Jeddah city were excluded from the analysis.

Sample size and data collection

The sample size was calculated by using raosoft website based on the size in Jeddah (5,494,613). The calculation came to be 385 with 0.5 margin of error, 50% of the response distribution, and 95% confidence level, although the study has reached more responses from our required number of 450; but after applying the exclusion criteria, it was 390. The survey was disseminated across various social media platforms. Those who answered all 35 questions in the five-category survey were sent a comprehensive case analysis of their responses. Participants who did not complete the questionnaire were not allowed to continue their participation. The Microsoft Forms supplied spreadsheets from which the data was obtained.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 29, and the quantitative variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Participants’ t test was used to assess the relation between quantitative variables, the Chi-square test (Fisher›s exact test) was used to examine the association between qualitative variables, and an ANOVA test was conducted to determine the influence of the independent variables on the dependent variables. A P value less than 0.05 was considered as significant data. Data were analyzed by mean, median, and mode with frequencies and percentages that were calculated for quantitative variables.

The participants educational levels included (89%) of the mothers, who were highly educated, postgraduates, and undergraduates, in comparison with the high school-educated participant which included only (10%), with (0%) primary education. Our results showed a significant correlation between the knowledge score and the participants’ educational levels, as an ANOVA test was conducted to examine the differences in knowledge scores based on education status. Discussion

Rhesus incompatibility refers to a condition that occurs when Rh-negative mother gets pregnant with Rh-positive baby.[8] The immune system will recognize these antigens as foreign body and may do serious outcomes. In this study, we aimed to assess mothers’ knowledge and attitudes toward Rhesus incompatibility. The participant knowledge was evaluated using a knowledge score. Our study found (51%) of the women were familiar with Rh factor; however, it was not correlated with a high knowledge score. This result is consistent with the findings of Abimbola and Olasubomi[7] that over 80% of pregnant women have a fair knowledge of rhesus factor. However, previous study conducted in Arar, Saudi Arabia, found that only (38%) of mothers had knowledge about Rh incompatibility.[6] Furthermore, a similar study has been conducted in four hospitals in Ethiopia and found that (35.3%) of women had a good knowledge of Rh incompatibility.[9] These discrepancies highlight significant variations in awareness and the understanding of Rhesus incompatibility among different populations and suggest the need for targeted educational interventions.

Our study identified several factors associated with higher knowledge score among the participants. The first factor was younger age group as shown in [Figures 2 and 3], and younger age participants tend to have higher knowledge scores. Interestingly, in a previous reported study they found women of young ages (21–25 years old) most likely to be affected by Rhesus incompatibility.[10] This indicates the importance of focusing on expanding the knowledge of younger generations, as they are more likely to be affected. In contrast, a previous study conducted in Bisha, Saudi Arabia, found increased awareness among older women.[11]

Second factor was the education levels, where participants with higher education levels tend to have higher knowledge scores, and a similar result found in a cross-sectional study conducted in Baghdad showed a higher educational level is associated with higher level of knowledge.[12] This is supported by a previous cross-sectional study that found higher education levels were associated with secondary education and above, and the lowest levels of knowledge were associated with those who never received formal education.[7] This could be explained by the importance of education that can influence the knowledge by providing a wide range of accessibility through social media or even visiting healthcare providers to gain more knowledge about their condition. Higher education institutions usually provide access to various databases and seminars where individuals can develop a critical thinking to draw informed conclusion about the serious issues.

Additionally, the third factor was attending antenatal care as shown in Figure 5. Those who attended antenatal care tend to have higher knowledge scores. This finding emphasizes the crucial role of healthcare providers in influencing mother’s understanding of Rh incompatibility. The possible reason might be related to the individualized counseling that may address all the mother concerns and answer her questions clearly until ensuring proper effective understanding. The quality of interaction in antenatal care can enhance and encourage decision-making that may lead to early intervention and improve the outcomes. Unfortunately, a multicenter interview-based study found (40%) of women refused to do a regular antenatal visit if the pregnancy is normal. They demonstrated a lack of knowledge about the risks that may be associated with pregnancy and did not feel the importance of visiting the doctors during pregnancy.[13] The differing findings on Rh incompatibility knowledge between the studies in Jeddah, Bisha, and the other regions may be attributed to demographic variations, access to healthcare, cultural factors, temporal changes in medical education, study methodologies, and the presence of targeted health education initiatives. These factors collectively illustrate how regional differences can influence health awareness and education.

Attending antenatal care

Lastly, the results of the attitude regarding Rh incompatibility prevention highlighted the need for comprehensive educational campaigns to raise awareness and improve the understanding of this critical condition, as a poor attitude was observed among participants regarding the importance of Rh incompatibility. As shown in [Figure 1], (66%) of Rh-negative women did not do the screening test during pregnancy, (58%) did not discuss with their husband about incompatibility probability, and (53%) did not checked their blood group and Rh factor. Yet, as [Figure 4] illustrates most of our participants (60%) expressed agreement and a positive attitude toward seeking treatment in case they had incompatibility, and over (63%) indicated that the reason behind the hesitancy to undergo the rhesus incompatibility test was not because they are afraid to have positive result. Therefore, the results shown in [Figures 1 and 2] conclude that there is lack of knowledge about the incompatibility, importance of the incompatibility test, and the measures to prevent it, as most of the participants stated that the Rh incompatibility is not important to be done, yet if they have incompatibility, they will definitely take the test or the prophylactic measures, and that most probably as mentioned before, related to multiple variables and factors that are contributing to this considerable lack of knowledge. In contrast, a previous cross-sectional study done by Abimbola and Olasubomi[7] concluded that their results did not show a relationship or association between the knowledge about Rh factor incompatibility, and the attitude to Rh incompatibility prevention. Furthermore, a study done in Nigeria concludes that (56%) of the participants demonstrated average positive attitude toward Rh incompatibility test, while (60%) of the participants showed that the test is very important and are not afraid to do the it.[14]

Conclusion

In conclusion, the purpose of our study was to determine mother’s knowledge regarding Rhesus incompatibility, its complications, and preventive measures in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Our findings suggest a significant gap in understanding the Rh factor, despite the majority of the participants being aware of it. Moreover, higher levels of education and younger age are associated with better knowledge scores, perhaps providing an opportunity for focused educational programs in the future.

The key advantage of the research is the high number of 390 participants, the use of an appropriately designed questionnaire, and the correlation analysis of demographic characteristics and knowledge scores. The survey focuses on a certain group of society in Jeddah, offering insight into the prevalence of Rhesus incompatibility. It examines the population’s understanding and knowledge of the condition, and by focusing on them, the study aims to increase their awareness and understanding, which may help guide future healthcare strategies and practices in the upcoming future.

Nevertheless, the study’s limitations such as self-reported data carry some risks of bias in reporting. Moreover, the nature of the study design, cross-sectional, limits the establishment of the relationship between the knowledge of the population and demographic variables. Due to cultural and healthcare differences, the study findings may not be applicable to other regions of Saudi Arabia.

We recommend that future studies consider the role of educational intervention in changing the knowledge about the Rhesus factor and covering a wider area of the population.
