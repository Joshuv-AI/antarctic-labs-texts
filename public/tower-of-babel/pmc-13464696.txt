Efficacy and safety of BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors versus placebo in adults with IgA nephropathy: a systematic review with pairwise and network meta-analysis
Authors: Nada Mansour, Ramy M. F. Elbarody, Ahmed M. Kamel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundB-cell activating factor (BAFF) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) drive the galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) production that underlies IgA nephropathy (IgAN). Several BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors have entered randomized testing, but none has been compared head-to-head.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE, Scopus, the Cochrane Central Register, Web of Science, and Embase to July 2026 for randomized, placebo-controlled trials of BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors in adults with biopsy-proven IgAN. Random-effects pairwise meta-analysis used the standardized mean change for proteinuria and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), change-score standardization for biomarkers, and risk ratios (RR) for safety, and a frequentist star network meta-analysis with placebo as reference ranked agents by P-score.ResultsSeven trials (1,590 adults) evaluating telitacicept, atacicept, and sibeprenlimab were included. Versus placebo, BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibition reduced proteinuria (SMCR −0.73, 95% CI −0.88 to −0.58, p < 0.001, I2 = 0%) and a small difference in eGFR favouring active treatment (+0.21, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.38, p = 0.016, I2 = 0%). Serum Gd-IgA1 and total IgA fell substantially but with high heterogeneity (I2 > 95%). Serious adverse events (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.90) and treatment discontinuations (RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.88) were fewer with active treatment, overall adverse events did not differ (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.16), and injection-site reactions were more frequent (RR 5.98, 95% CI 2.39 to 14.96). In the network, the exploratory P-score for proteinuria reduction was highest for sibeprenlimab (0.94), with no significant between-agent difference, and atacicept showed the only significant reduction in serious adverse events (RR 0.24, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.70).ConclusionsIn adults with IgAN, BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors consistently and substantially reduced proteinuria versus placebo, with a small short-term eGFR difference and no evident short-term safety signal, although longer follow-up is required to define uncommon, infectious, and immunological risks. Whether this antiproteinuric effect, seen over approximately 6 to 12 months, translates into durable preservation of kidney function remains uncertain. The between-agent and biomarker estimates are exploratory, and longer trials reporting kidney-failure endpoints are needed to confirm a disease-modifying effect.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05267-4.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and an important cause of progressive chronic kidney disease, with between 20 and 50% of affected adults reaching kidney failure within two to three decades of diagnosis [1, 2]. The disease arises through a widely accepted multi-step, or four-hit, autoimmune process in which circulating galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) is recognised by anti-glycan autoantibodies, forms immune complexes, and deposits in the glomerular mesangium, where it provokes inflammation, mesangial proliferation, and podocyte injury [1, 3]. Immune-mediated kidney injury in IgA nephropathy also reflects mechanisms beyond the BAFF/APRIL axis, including epigenetic regulation of renal inflammation, endothelin-1 signalling implicated across autoimmune diseases, and the innate immune functions of resident structural cells [4–6]. Circulating Gd-IgA1 and its corresponding autoantibodies therefore sit upstream of glomerular injury and have become attractive pharmacodynamic targets.

For many years management was confined to supportive measures, namely blood-pressure control, maximal blockade of the renin-angiotensin system, and, more recently, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibition, which reduced the risk of kidney-disease progression in patients with IgAN [7, 8]. Two agents have since broadened the treatment landscape, the dual endothelin and angiotensin receptor antagonist sparsentan and a gut-directed targeted-release formulation of budesonide, each of which lowered proteinuria and slowed eGFR decline in phase 3 trials [9, 10]. Even with these advances, a substantial proportion of patients retain residual proteinuria and continue to lose kidney function, and a persistent reduction in proteinuria remains both the principal modifiable prognostic marker and an accepted surrogate endpoint for therapeutic trials in IgAN [2, 11].

The central role of Gd-IgA1 has redirected drug development toward the B-cell compartment that generates it. B-cell activating factor (BAFF) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) are tumour-necrosis-factor-family cytokines that support the survival, maturation, and immunoglobulin class-switching of the mucosal B cells responsible for Gd-IgA1 production; both are raised in patients with IgAN and track with disease activity [3]. Broad B-cell depletion with rituximab did not lower Gd-IgA1 or proteinuria in IgAN, a result attributed to the persistence of antibody-secreting plasma cells that the BAFF and APRIL pathway continues to support [12], which strengthened the rationale for targeting the cytokines themselves. Pharmacological inhibition of this pathway, whether by the dual BAFF and APRIL antagonist telitacicept, the dual antagonist atacicept, or the selective APRIL-neutralising antibody sibeprenlimab, has consequently been pursued as a mechanism-based therapy, and each agent has now been tested against placebo in randomised trials [13–15].

These trials have, however, evaluated different agents, doses, and follow-up schedules, and none has compared one BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitor directly against another. The relative efficacy and safety of the available agents therefore remain uncertain, and clinicians lack a synthesised estimate of how the class as a whole performs against placebo. We undertook a systematic review with conventional pairwise meta-analysis and a prespecified network meta-analysis to quantify the effect of BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibition on proteinuria, kidney function, immunological biomarkers, and safety in adults with IgAN, and to compare the individual agents through indirect comparison.

Methods

Protocol, registration, and reporting

This systematic review, pairwise meta-analysis, and network meta-analysis was conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 statement [16] and the PRISMA extension for network meta-analyses [17]. The protocol was prepared before data extraction and was submitted to PROSPERO and registered (CRD420261456498). The review question was structured around adults with biopsy-proven primary IgA nephropathy, BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors, placebo on background supportive care, efficacy and safety outcomes, and randomized placebo-controlled trials.

Eligibility criteria and information sources

Eligible studies were randomized, placebo-controlled trials enrolling adults aged 18 years or older with biopsy-proven primary IgA nephropathy who received a BAFF and/or APRIL-pathway inhibitor, including telitacicept, atacicept, sibeprenlimab, povetacicept, zigakibart, blisibimod, or belimumab, on a background of supportive care. Studies were excluded if they enrolled pediatric populations, secondary IgA nephropathy, kidney transplant recipients, healthy volunteers, open-label or single-arm cohorts, observational designs, or studies without an extractable outcome of interest. PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science Core Collection, and Embase were searched from inception to July 2026, and trial registries, reference lists, and relevant reviews were checked for ongoing or awaiting-classification studies.

Study selection, data extraction, and outcomes

Records were de-duplicated and screened independently by two reviewers at title/abstract and full-text stages, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Data were extracted independently and in duplicate using a piloted extraction form. Extracted variables included trial design, phase, intervention dose, comparator, sample size, follow-up, baseline proteinuria, baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), efficacy outcomes, and safety outcomes. The primary efficacy outcome was change from baseline in proteinuria, expressed as 24-hour urine protein-to-creatinine ratio or 24-hour urinary protein according to trial reporting. Secondary outcomes were eGFR change, serum galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), serum total IgA, serious adverse events, any adverse event, adverse events leading to study-drug discontinuation, proteinuria remission, and injection-site reactions.

Data conversions and effect measures

Continuous outcomes were analyzed on the absolute scale without logarithmic transformation. When means and standard deviations were not reported, central tendency and dispersion were reconstructed from reported medians, interquartile ranges, ranges, geometric summaries, standard errors, or confidence intervals using deterministic conversion rules [18–22]. For proteinuria and eGFR, the standardized mean change with raw-score standardization (SMCR) was calculated using the baseline standard deviation [23]. Biomarker outcomes reported as percentage or absolute change with a measure of precision were analyzed as standardized mean change using change-score standardization (SMCC) with the Hedges correction [24]. The pre–post correlation was varied (r = 0.3–0.9) for the SMCC biomarker outcomes. Dichotomous safety and remission outcomes were summarized as risk ratios (RRs), with a continuity correction applied only to zero-event comparisons [25]. When multiple active doses were reported, the prespecified primary or clinically relevant active node was used; ORIGIN 2b 75 mg and 150 mg arms were combined for the main atacicept comparison.

Risk of bias and statistical analysis

Risk of bias was assessed independently and in duplicate with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool [26] across the five standard domains, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Pairwise meta-analyses were fitted separately for each outcome using random-effects models. Inverse-variance models with the restricted maximum-likelihood estimator were used for continuous outcomes, and Mantel-Haenszel random-effects models with DerSimonian-Laird tau estimation and continuity correction were used for binary outcomes [27]. Heterogeneity was quantified with I-squared [28] and prediction intervals were estimated where applicable. Prespecified subgroup analyses grouped trials by mechanism class, and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for biomarker outcomes, including a serum IgA refit excluding TELIGAN because its dispersion was imputed. Because fewer than ten trials contributed to each outcome, small-study effects and publication bias were not formally tested, and certainty of evidence was rated with the GRADE framework [29] for proteinuria, eGFR, serious adverse events, and any adverse event; CINeMA [30] was not applied because the star-shaped network without closed loops precluded a separate inconsistency assessment. The primary analysis included only peer-reviewed trials; BRIGHT-SC and RAINIER, available only in non-peer-reviewed form, were tested in sensitivity analyses.

Network meta-analysis, software, and ethics

Frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis [31] was performed for each outcome using placebo as the reference treatment. Because all included trials compared an active treatment with placebo and no head-to-head trials were available, the network formed a placebo-anchored star and statistical inconsistency could not be estimated. Treatments were ranked with P-scores [32]. Transitivity was examined descriptively by comparing trial phase, baseline proteinuria, baseline eGFR, and background SGLT2 inhibitor and renin-angiotensin system inhibitor use across treatment nodes. Analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the meta, metafor, netmeta, estmeansd, and tidyverse packages [33]. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed. Ethical approval was not required because the review used aggregate data from published studies.

Results

Study selection

The database searches identified 658 records; after removal of 204 duplicates, 454 records were screened by title and abstract and 37 reports were assessed in full text. The database yields and dual-reviewer screening agreement are summarised in Supplementary Table S1. Thirty reports did not proceed to synthesis (Supplementary Table S2). Seven randomized, placebo-controlled trials remained for synthesis (Fig. 1). Four further trials were eligible by design but were not included in the primary synthesis: RAINIER (povetacicept; NCT06564142), reported only in non-peer-reviewed form [34], without baseline data for the standardised analysis but examined in a relative-effect sensitivity analysis; BRIGHT-SC (blisibimod; NCT02062684), reported only in sponsor announcements [35] and examined in a sensitivity analysis; NCT04905212 (telitacicept), terminated without reported results; and the ongoing BEYOND trial (NCT05852938) (Supplementary Table S3).Fig. 1PRISMA 2020 flow diagram. Risk of bias

Across the 29 trial-by-outcome assessments, 17 were at low risk of bias and 12 raised some concerns, with none at high risk (Fig. 2; Supplementary Figs. S1–S5). Concerns were limited to the reporting of allocation concealment and missing-outcome-data handling in the two smallest trials (Telitacicept phase 2 and JANUS) and to selective reporting for exploratory biomarker outcomes. The primary outcome, proteinuria, was at low risk of bias in five of the seven trials; the two phase-2 trials (Telitacicept phase 2 and JANUS) raised some concerns arising from the randomisation process and the handling of missing outcome data (Fig. 2). Proteinuria (primary outcome)

Across all seven trials, BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibition reduced proteinuria relative to placebo (SMCR −0.73, 95% CI −0.88 to −0.58, p < 0.001, I2 = 0%, Fig. 3A; Table 2). Kidney function and immunological biomarkers

Estimated GFR was slightly higher with active treatment than with placebo (SMCR +0.21, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.38, p = 0.016, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3B). Circulating galactose-deficient IgA1 (reported by five trials) and total serum IgA (reported by four trials) were both reduced (Gd-IgA1 SMCC −4.55, 95% CI −6.26 to −2.84, p < 0.001, I2 = 96.3%; serum IgA SMCC −3.43, 95% CI −5.06 to −1.80, p < 0.001, I2 = 95.1%), with substantial between-trial heterogeneity (Figs. 3C and 3D; Table 2).

Safety

Serious adverse events were less frequent with active treatment than placebo (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.90, p = 0.020, I2 = 8.8%), as were adverse events leading to study-drug discontinuation (RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.88, p = 0.027, I2 = 0%) (Figs. 4A and 4C). In absolute terms these were 20 of 758 versus 42 of 715 and 5 of 758 versus 15 of 715, respectively (Supplementary Table S7). The incidence of any adverse event did not differ from placebo (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.16, p = 0.57, I2 = 66.5%) (Fig. 4B). Proteinuria remission occurred more frequently with active treatment (RR 3.03, 95% CI 2.28 to 4.03, p < 0.001, I2 = 0%), and injection-site reactions, a local tolerability finding distinct from systemic and infectious outcomes, were more common (RR 5.98, 95% CI 2.39 to 14.96, p < 0.001, I2 = 59.1%) (Figs. 4D and 4E; Table 2). No deaths or opportunistic infections were reported. Serious infection was tabulated as a distinct outcome in only one trial (none with atacicept versus three with placebo in ORIGIN 3). Reductions in IgG and IgM were reported as adverse events in the two telitacicept trials (IgG decreased in 13.2% versus 0% in TELIGAN and in about 30% of active-arm patients in the phase 2 trial), and the thresholds defining hypogammaglobulinaemia differed across trials (IgG < 3 g/L, <300 mg/L, and <400 mg/dL), so systematic comparison was not possible (Supplementary Table S8).Fig. 4Safety of BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors versus placebo (pairwise meta-analysis). Network meta-analysis

In the placebo-anchored star networks (Fig. 5), all three agents reduced proteinuria relative to placebo, with no statistically significant differences between agents; sibeprenlimab ranked highest (SMCR −0.87, 95% CI −1.08 to −0.65, P-score 0.94), followed by telitacicept and atacicept (each SMCR −0.65) (Fig. 6; Table 3). For serious adverse events, the outcome with the next most consistent evidence (I2 = 0%), atacicept had a lower risk than placebo and ranked highest (RR 0.24, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.70, P-score 0.93), whereas the other agents did not differ significantly from placebo (Fig. 6; Table 3).Fig. 5Network structure for the network meta-analysis. Heterogeneity and sensitivity analyses

Heterogeneity was negligible for proteinuria, eGFR, adverse events leading to discontinuation, and proteinuria remission (all I2 = 0%) and low for serious adverse events (I2 = 8.8%); it was moderate-to-substantial for any adverse event (I2 = 66.5%) and injection-site reactions (I2 = 59.1%), and high for the two biomarker outcomes (Gd-IgA1 and serum total IgA, I2 > 95%). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed that the biomarker estimates were not driven by any single trial (Gd-IgA1 range −4.16 to −5.16; serum IgA range −3.01 to −4.17), and the serum IgA estimate was essentially unchanged when the trial with imputed dispersion [13] was removed (SMCC −3.35, 95% CI −5.67 to −1.03, k = 3) (Supplementary Figs. S25 and S26). All trials measured proteinuria from 24-hour urine collections (six as the protein-to-creatinine ratio, one as total protein); none used a spot sample. The pooled estimate changed minimally when restricted to the 24-hour protein-to-creatinine ratio (SMCR −0.72), to the three phase 3 trials (−0.75), or by assessment window (week 24, −0.69; week 36 to month 12, −0.75), and when BRIGHT-SC was added (−0.68) (Supplementary Table S6). The relative reduction changed from 46.1% to 47.1% when RAINIER was added (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

In this systematic review with pairwise and network meta-analysis of seven randomised, placebo-controlled trials, we evaluated inhibition of the BAFF and APRIL pathway in adults with IgA nephropathy. Pooling the available evidence, the class produced a large and statistically homogeneous reduction in proteinuria versus placebo, a small accompanying gain in eGFR, and marked falls in the upstream immunological biomarkers Gd-IgA1 and serum total IgA. Over the trial periods there was no evident short-term safety signal. To our knowledge this is the first quantitative synthesis to place telitacicept, atacicept, and sibeprenlimab within a single network and to rank them by indirect comparison, and it adds a class-level estimate to a literature so far composed of individual placebo-controlled trials.

The magnitude of the proteinuria effect is the central finding and is biologically coherent. Because BAFF and APRIL sustain the mucosal B cells that generate Gd-IgA1, neutralising this pathway should reduce the antigenic driver of immune-complex formation, and the parallel and substantial reductions we observed in circulating Gd-IgA1 and total IgA are consistent with engagement of that mechanism [3]. A fall in proteinuria of the size seen here is clinically meaningful in IgAN, where an early and sustained reduction in proteinuria is the strongest modifiable predictor of long-term kidney survival and an accepted surrogate endpoint for therapeutic trials [11]. Set against the contemporary agents that have reduced proteinuria through haemodynamic or gut-mucosal mechanisms, namely sparsentan and targeted-release budesonide [9, 10], BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibition offers a complementary, immunologically upstream approach to the same prognostic target.

The contrast with earlier B-cell-directed treatment is instructive. Broad CD20 depletion with rituximab neither lowered Gd-IgA1 nor reduced proteinuria in IgAN, most plausibly because the long-lived antibody-secreting plasma cells that generate Gd-IgA1 do not express CD20 and continue to receive survival signals from BAFF and APRIL [12]. By acting on those survival cytokines rather than on a single B-cell surface marker, the agents synthesised here engage a point in the pathway that broad depletion leaves intact, which offers a mechanistic explanation for the consistent biomarker and proteinuria responses we observed and supports the rationale for upstream cytokine blockade in this disease.

A further distinction within the class concerns the breadth of cytokine blockade. Sibeprenlimab inhibits APRIL alone and leaves BAFF signalling intact [14, 36], whereas telitacicept and atacicept neutralise both BAFF and APRIL [13, 15, 37–39]. This difference is mechanistically relevant because broader blockade of B-cell survival signals lowers circulating immunoglobulin more extensively and may carry a greater theoretical risk of hypogammaglobulinaemia, whereas selective APRIL inhibition is intended to suppress the pathogenic galactose-deficient IgA1 response while preserving wider humoral defence [3]. Across the trials synthesised here, the selective and dual agents alike reduced serum IgA and, for the dual agents, IgG and IgM, yet none reported an excess of infection over the trial periods and serious hypogammaglobulinaemia was not observed [36, 38, 39]. The pooled safety estimates are therefore reassuring in the short term, but they cannot speak to the consequences of sustained immunoglobulin lowering, and whether the selective and dual approaches differ in infection risk over years of exposure remains an open question that the present indirect evidence cannot resolve.

The small eGFR signal should be interpreted with more caution than the proteinuria result. Its confidence interval, although excluding the null in the pairwise analysis, was narrow and its prediction interval crossed zero, and the follow-up of the contributing trials was short relative to the slow trajectory of kidney-function loss in IgAN. A genuine effect on eGFR is plausible, since proteinuria lowering has translated into eGFR preservation for other IgAN therapies [9, 10], but the present data can only be regarded as an early and indirect indication rather than as evidence of a disease-modifying effect on kidney function. This distinction matters because proteinuria and biomarker responses are intermediate outcomes, and only longer trials powered for eGFR slope or kidney failure can establish whether the class alters the natural history of the disease [2]. The contributing trials generally described stabilisation of eGFR against a declining placebo trajectory rather than an absolute gain, which is consistent with a class that slows rather than reverses functional loss [15, 36, 38].

The biomarker findings reinforce mechanism but must be read against their heterogeneity. The pooled reductions in Gd-IgA1 and total IgA were large, yet the I-squared values above 95% indicate that the trials differed substantially in the magnitude of the biomarker response, most likely reflecting differences between agents, assays, dose intensity, and the heterogeneous statistical forms in which the trials reported biomarker change. We therefore treated these estimates as exploratory and did not allow them to carry the principal conclusions. One plausible explanation for the contrast with the homogeneous proteinuria result is that biomarker assays are more sensitive to between-trial methodological variation than is a standardised clinical measurement of proteinuria. A further consideration is that biomarker suppression and proteinuria reduction were not always concordant within trials, since one atacicept study reported its largest galactose-deficient IgA1 reduction at a dose that did not produce its largest proteinuria response [39]. This dissociation cautions against treating the biomarker estimates as direct surrogates for the clinical effect and reinforces our decision to weight them as exploratory.

Within the network, the indirect comparisons are best viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as a definitive ranking. The highest proteinuria rank for sibeprenlimab and the singular serious-adverse-event reduction for atacicept are of interest, but every between-agent estimate rests on a single placebo-anchored comparison for each agent, because the network was a star with no closed loops. Such a network cannot be checked for incoherence, and its validity depends wholly on transitivity, that is, on the assumption that the trials were similar enough in their effect modifiers for indirect comparison to be fair. We assessed transitivity qualitatively and found no obvious imbalance, yet residual differences in trial phase, background therapy, and population cannot be excluded, and the apparent superiority of any one agent should not be over-interpreted from these data. The consistent direction of effect supports a class-level pathway effect, but the differing magnitude of proteinuria reduction across trials and the indirect nature of the comparisons do not establish that the agents are equivalent or interchangeable; the P-scores are exploratory. Background SGLT2 inhibitor use in particular rose over the enrolment period, from not reported in the earliest trials to approximately half in the most recent, whereas renin-angiotensin system blockade was near-universal.

Future trials could address several of these uncertainties. Head-to-head comparisons between BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibitors, or at minimum the adoption of common biomarker assays, reporting standards, and assessment time points, would convert the present star network into one that can be tested for coherence and would sharpen the between-agent estimates. Investigators may also consider longer follow-up with eGFR slope and kidney-failure endpoints, prespecified reporting of infection and hypogammaglobulinaemia, and harmonised dosing, so that the durability of the proteinuria effect and the long-term immunological safety of sustained BAFF and APRIL blockade can be established. The durability question is given added weight by the observation that biomarker and proteinuria responses began to return toward baseline after treatment was withdrawn in one trial, which suggests that continued pathway suppression may be needed to sustain benefit [36]. Until such evidence is available, clinicians may reasonably regard this class as a promising, mechanism-based option that complements established supportive care rather than as a proven means of preventing kidney failure.

This review has limitations. The evidence base is small, comprising seven trials of three agents, and several contributing trials were phase 2 studies with modest sample sizes, which limits the precision of the less-populated outcomes. The low statistical heterogeneity for proteinuria should be interpreted with caution given the small number of trials and does not by itself establish clinical homogeneity. The biomarker analyses carried high heterogeneity and required conversion of dispersion measures and, for one trial, imputation of a missing dispersion, although leave-one-out analysis showed that this imputation did not drive the result. The network was star-shaped, so inconsistency could not be assessed and the indirect comparisons depend on an untestable transitivity assumption that we examined only qualitatively. Follow-up was short relative to the disease course, the included trials used differing doses and assessment schedules that we standardised analytically, and the synthesis necessarily reflects only trials that have reported results, so the possibility of unpublished or unreported data affecting the class estimate cannot be excluded. Because follow-up was short and no trial reported kidney-failure outcomes, the present evidence supports an antiproteinuric effect on a background of supportive care rather than a disease-modifying effect on long-term kidney function. Because the safety outcomes rested on few events in trials not powered for safety, these lower event rates should not be read as evidence that active treatment is safer than placebo, and longer follow-up is required to define uncommon, infectious, and immunological risks. These constraints place the firmest weight on the homogeneous proteinuria and serious-adverse-event findings and the least on the heterogeneous biomarker and indirect estimates.

Conclusion

Pooled evidence from randomised, placebo-controlled trials indicates that BAFF/APRIL-pathway inhibition substantially and consistently reduces proteinuria in adults with IgA nephropathy, with a small short-term eGFR difference and no evident short-term safety signal, although longer follow-up is required to define uncommon, infectious, and immunological risks. Whether this antiproteinuric effect, seen over approximately 6 to 12 months, translates into durable preservation of kidney function remains uncertain. Given the small number of trials, the star-shaped network, and the short follow-up, these findings establish a class-level effect on proteinuria rather than a proven effect on kidney survival. Longer head-to-head trials reporting kidney-failure endpoints are needed to confirm a disease-modifying benefit and to define the relative position of individual agents.
