Abstract

Introduction

The logic

Instead,

Regular Expression Characterization

Arguably, the most natural and concise formulation of the
\mathsf{FC}
-definable regular languages is the regular expression formulation:
Take the star-free generalized regular expressions (which are usual regular expressions without Kleene star, but with complement) and add the Kleene star of terminal words.
For example,

denotes complement, describes the language of words that are not of the form
(\mathtt{ab})^{2n}
for any
n>0

To formalize this class, we use the

[
PZ19

However, we rely on the results and formulations given in the more recent version

PZ25

The star-free closure takes a class of regular languages

\mathcal{C}

\{\mathtt{a}\}
for terminal symbols, and is closed under concatenation, union, and complement.
A main result of this paper shows
that the class of
\mathsf{FC}
-definable regular languages is exactly

\mathcal{R}:=\{w^{*}\mid w\in\Sigma^{*}\}

Using the notion of star-free closure allows us to draw upon the results and techniques of

PZ25
]

If

is a

of

.
If

,

.
The set of all factors of

.
We use

A

is a set

\cdot
and an identity element

.
When the multiplication and identity are clear from context, we shall denote a monoid simply by its set
M
.
Notice that
\Sigma^{*}

Given two monoids

is a function

f(x\cdot y)=f(x)\cdot f(y)

.

The

is the relation

defined as

if and only if
xuy\in L\Leftrightarrow xvy\in L

.
The set of equivalence classes in

with respect to

is denoted

.

Given a language

, the set

with the multiplication

forms a monoid called the

, which we often denote with

, see
[
Pin10
]
for more details.
We say that

is

if there exists some

such that
x^{n}=x^{n+1}
; otherwise,

is
periodic
.
A monoid

is aperiodic if every element is aperiodic (see Schützenberger
[
Sch65
]
).
We note that for any finite monoid

, and any

, there exist

(e.g., see Section 6, Chapter 2 of Pin
[

]
).
We call an element

i=i\cdot i
.

For a monoid

, a subset

is a

if

forms a group (with
the same multiplication as

, but with

The Logic FC

We assume the reader is familiar with the standard concepts of first-order logic (for example, see Libkin
[
Lib04
]
).
However, we shall look at the particular logic with which this paper is concerned in more detail.
The definitions given here are based on the definitions given by Thompson and Freydenberger in
[
TF24a
]
– who define
\mathsf{FC}
in a slightly more technical way than its original definition by Freydenberger and Peterfreund
[
FP21
]
.

\mathsf{FC}
is built on one fixed signature
\tau_{\Sigma}:=\{R_{\circ},\varepsilon\}\cup\{\mathtt{a}\mid\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma\}
for every terminal alphabet
\Sigma
, where
R_{\circ}
is a ternary relation symbol and where
\varepsilon
and each
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
is a constant symbol.
Given a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, the
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
w
is defined as
\mathcal{A}_{w}:=(A,R_{\circ}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}},\varepsilon^{\mathcal{A}_{w}},(\mathtt{a}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}})_{\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma})
where

•

A:=\mathsf{facts}(w)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
is the universe,

•

R_{\circ}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}}:=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathsf{facts}(w)^{3}\mid a=b\cdot c\}

•

\mathtt{a}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}}:=\mathtt{a}
if
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}\geqslant 1
, and
\mathtt{a}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}}=\perp
otherwise, and

•

\varepsilon^{\mathcal{A}_{w}}:=\varepsilon
.

We call such a structure
\mathcal{A}_{w}
an
\mathsf{FC}
-structure
.

Note that if
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}=0
, then
\mathtt{a}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}}=\perp
.
However, we usually deal with those words

where
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}\geqslant 1
for all
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
.
Therefore, we tend to write
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
rather than
\mathtt{a}^{\mathcal{A}_{w}}\in A
.

Let
X
be a fixed, countably infinite set of variables (disjoint from
\Sigma
and
\tau_{\Sigma}
).
An
\mathsf{FC}
-formula is a first-order formula whose atomic formulas are
R_{\circ}(x,y,z)
, where

,

, and

As syntactic sugar, we write

for atomic

as concatenation.
More formally:

{defi}

Let
\mathsf{FC}
be the set of all
\mathsf{FC}

•

If

•

•

We allow atomic formulas of the form

We also use

.

In

{defi}

The language defined by a sentence

L_{1}:=\bigl((\{\varepsilon\}\mathrel{\cup}\Sigma^{*}\mathtt{b})\cdot(\mathtt{aa})^{*}\mathtt{a}\cdot(\mathtt{b}\Sigma^{*}\mathrel{\cup}\{\varepsilon\})\bigr)\mathrel{\cup}\bigl((\{\varepsilon\}\mathrel{\cup}\Sigma^{*}\mathtt{a})\cdot(\mathtt{bb})^{*}\mathtt{b}\cdot(\mathtt{a}\Sigma^{*}\mathrel{\cup}\{\varepsilon\})\bigr).

For any

The symmetric statement holds for the analogous factors

One can therefore verify that

3.2.
Group Primitive Languages

We now introduce a new class of regular languages defined by a restriction on the syntactic morphism:

{defi}

[Group Primitive Language]
Let
M
be a finite monoid.
A morphism
\mu\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow M
is
group primitive
if
|\varrho(\mu^{-1}(x))|=1
for all periodic elements
x\in M
.
A language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is
group primitive
if it is regular and its syntactic morphism is group primitive.

As an aside, let us note the connection to languages recognized by aperiodic monoids.
Recall that an aperiodic monoid is a monoid where all elements
x
satisfy
x^{n}=x^{n+1}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
This can alternatively be phrased as follows: An aperiodic monoid is a monoid where all subgroups are trivial (see
[
Sch65
]
, for example).
In comparison, while the syntactic monoid of a group primitive language may contain non-trivial subgroups, the preimage of any element of a non-trivial subgroup (under the syntactic morphism) is a language of the form
\{w^{i}\mid i\in I\}
for some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.
This is because if
G\subseteq M_{L}
is a subgroup, then the identity
e_{G}
of
G
is the only aperiodic element (if we take
x^{n}=x^{n+1}
with
x\in G
, then a simple cancellation argument implies
x=e_{G}
).
Consequently,
|\varrho(\mu^{-1}(x))|=1
for every
x\in G\setminus\{e_{G}\}
and thus
\mu^{-1}(x)=\{w^{i}\mid i\in I\}
for some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover, this implies
\mu^{-1}(e_{G})=\{w^{j}\mid j\in J\}
for some
J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
by the following reasoning:
pick any
u\in\mu^{-1}(e_{G})
and any
w^{i}\in\mu^{-1}(x)
and notice that
\mu(w^{i}\cdot u)=\mu(w^{i})\cdot\mu(u)=x\cdot e_{G}=x
and thus
w^{i}u=w^{r}
since
w^{i}u\in\mu^{-1}(x)
.
Taking
w^{i}u=w^{r}
, we get
u=w^{r-i}
.

{exa}

Let
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}
.
The language
L_{1}:=(\mathtt{aa})^{*}
has the syntactic monoid
\{e,x,y\}
with
e
being the identity,
xx=e
, and
yx=xy=yy=y
.
The syntactic morphism
\eta_{L_{1}}\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\{e,x,y\}
is:
\eta_{L_{1}}(w)=e
if
w\in(\mathtt{aa})^{*}
,
\eta_{L_{1}}(w)=x
if
w\in\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{aa})^{*}
, and
\eta_{L_{1}}(w)=y
if
|w|_{\mathtt{b}}\geqslant 1
.
Clearly,
x
is the only periodic element, however as
\eta_{L_{1}}^{-1}(x)=\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{aa})^{*}
, we have that
L_{1}
is a group primitive language since
\varrho(\mathtt{a}(\mathtt{aa})^{*})=\{\mathtt{a}\}
.

Now consider
L_{2}:=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid|w|_{\mathtt{a}}\text{ is even}\}
.
The syntactic monoid for
L_{2}
is
\{e,x\}
with
e
being the identity and
xx=e
.
The syntactic morphism
\eta_{L_{2}}\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\{e,x\}
is
\eta_{L_{2}}(w)=e
if
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}
is even, and
\eta_{L_{2}}(w)=x
otherwise.
Again, it is clear that

is periodic, however,
\mathtt{ba}
and
\mathtt{baaa}
are in
\eta_{L_{2}}^{-1}(x)
which means that
L_{2}
is not group primitive as
\varrho(\mathtt{ba})\neq\varrho(\mathtt{baaa})
.

3.3.
Automata with Loop-Step Cycles

A deterministic finite automaton (DFA)

4.
On Loop-Step Cycles and Group Primitive Regular Languages

In this section we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let
L
be a regular language and let
\mathcal{M}
be a minimal DFA with
L=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})
.
If
L
is not group primitive, then
\mathcal{M}
has a loop-step cycle.

Proof 4.1
.

Let
L
be a regular language that is not group primitive.
Let
\mathcal{M}:=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},F)
be a minimal DFA with
L=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})
.
Let
\eta_{L}\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow M_{L}
be the syntactic morphism of
L
, where
M_{L}
is the syntactic monoid for
L
.
Since
L
is not group primitive,
\eta_{L}
is not group primitive.
That is, there exists a periodic element

g\in M_{L}
where

|\varrho(\eta_{L}^{-1}(g))|\geqslant 2
.
Hence, there exist words

w,v\in\eta_{L}^{-1}(g)
such that
\varrho(w)\neq\varrho(v)
.

Since
g
is periodic, we
have that
g^{n}\neq g^{n+1}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
However, there exists
y\geqslant 0
and
m>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we get
g^{y+mx}=g^{y+m}
;
for example, see Section 6, Chapter 2 of
[
Pin10
]
.
We choose
m
as small as possible.
Note that
m\geqslant 2
because
g
is periodic.
We let

G:=\{g^{y+m},g^{y+m+1},\dots,g^{y+2m-1}\}
.
Observe that
G
has
m
elements, and
G
is closed under multiplication with
g
, i.e.,
g\cdot h=h\cdot g\in G
for every
h\in G
.

For any words
u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
we have:

\eta_{L}(u)=\eta_{L}(u^{\prime})
if and only if

\delta^{*}(q,u)=\delta^{*}(q,u^{\prime})
for all
q\in Q
; see Proposition 4.28 of
[
Pin10
]
.
By our choice of
w
and
v
we have:
w,v\in\Sigma^{+}
,
\varrho(w)\neq\varrho(v)
, and
\eta_{L}(w)=\eta_{L}(v)=g
.

In particular, due to the fact that
m
is chosen to be as small as possible, for any

with
z,z^{\prime}\geqslant y+m
we have:

v^{z}\sim_{L}v^{z^{\prime}}\iff\eta_{L}(v^{z})=\eta_{L}(v^{z^{\prime}})\iff g^{z}=g^{z^{\prime}}\iff z-y\equiv z^{\prime}-y\pmod{m}.

Thus, since
m\not\equiv m{+}1\pmod{m}
, we have
v^{m+y}\not\sim_{L}v^{m+y+1}
. Hence, there exists some state
p\in Q
such that
\delta^{*}(p,v^{m+y})\neq\delta^{*}(p,v^{m+y+1})
.
Let
p_{i}:=\delta^{*}(p,v^{m+y+i})
for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,m{-}1\}
.
Note that
p_{0}\neq p_{1}
(because
p_{0}=\delta^{*}(p,v^{m+y})\neq\delta^{*}(p,v^{m+y+1})=p_{1}
), and that
\delta^{*}(p_{i},v)=p_{i+1}
holds for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,m-2\}
.

Furthermore,
\delta^{*}(p_{m-1},v)=p_{0}
due to the following:
Clearly,
\delta^{*}(p_{m-1},v)=\delta^{*}(p,v^{y+2m})
. Furthermore,

since

\eta_{L}(v^{y+2m})=g^{y+2m}=g^{y+m}=\eta_{L}(v^{y+m})
.
Thus, for
every

we have

\delta^{*}(q,v^{y+2m})=\delta^{*}(q,v^{y+m})
. In particular for

this yields

\delta^{*}(p,v^{y+2m})=\delta^{*}(p,v^{y+m})=p_{0}
.

In summary, we now know that
\delta^{*}(p_{i},v)=p_{i+1\pmod{m}}
.
But
note that the states
p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{m-1}
are not necessarily pairwise distinct.
However, we know that, for all
x\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we have that
v^{m+y}\sim_{L}v^{mx+y}
.
Therefore, we get a set of states

of size
m^{\prime}
, where
2\leqslant m^{\prime}\leqslant m
, such that
q_{0}=p_{0}
,
q_{1}=p_{1}
and
\delta^{*}(q_{i},v)=q_{i+1\pmod{m^{\prime}}}
.
This is because we know that we eventually need to “loop back” to state
p_{0}
, which prohibits
\delta^{*}(p_{i},v)=p_{j}
where
p_{j}\neq p_{0}\neq p_{i}
, and thus we obtain our set
\{q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{m^{\prime}-1}\}
.

Since

for all
x\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, it follows that
\delta^{*}(q,v^{m+y})=\delta^{*}(q,v^{mx+y})
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and all
q\in Q
.
Furthermore, we have that
w,v\in\eta_{L}^{-1}(g)
, thus

for all
z\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, this implies for all

and the state

that
\delta^{*}(q_{i},w^{m+y})=q_{i}
for all
0\leqslant i<m^{\prime}
.
We let

and obtain that

for all

.
Furthermore,
\varrho(w^{\prime})=\varrho(w)\neq\varrho(v)
.

Consequently, we now know that

are

w^{\prime}
and
v
are words with
\varrho(w^{\prime})\neq\varrho(v)
, and
\delta^{*}(q_{i},w^{\prime})=q_{i}
and
\delta^{*}(q_{i},v)=q_{i+1\pmod{m}}
for all

where
0\leqslant i<m^{\prime}{-}1
.
This proves that
\mathcal{M}
has a loop-step cycle and therefore completes the proof of
6

The contraposition of

shows the step
\ref{item:mainthm:lsc}\Rightarrow\ref{item:mainthm:gp}
of

.

7.2.
Some Background on Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Games

The following definitions concerning Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games follow closely to the definitions given in Chapter 3 of Libkin
[
Lib04
]
.
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé-games are played by two players called
Spoiler
and
Duplicator
.
The board consists of two relational structures
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
over the same signature.
They play for a predetermined number
k\in\mathbb{N}
of rounds, and each round is as follows:

•

Spoiler picks a structure
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{B}
, and then picks some element of their chosen structure.

•

Duplicator responds by picking some element in the structure that Spoiler did not choose.

In order to define winning conditions, we first define a
partial isomorphism
.

{defi}

[Partial Isomorphism]
Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two relational structures over the same signature
\tau
, with the universes
A
and
B

Let
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in A^{n}
and
\vec{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in B^{n}
.
Then

defines a partial isomorphism between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
if:

•

a_{i}=a_{j}\iff b_{i}=b_{j}
for every
i,j\in[n]
;

•

a_{i}=c^{\mathcal{A}}\iff b_{i}=c^{\mathcal{B}}
for every constant symbol
c\in\tau
and every
i\in[n]
.

•

(a_{i_{1}},\dots,a_{i_{m}})\in R^{\mathcal{A}}\iff(b_{i_{1}},\dots,b_{i_{m}})\in R^{\mathcal{B}}
for every relation symbol
R\in\tau
with arity
m
, and every sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in[n]^{m}
.

Let
\tau
be a relational signature with constant symbols
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two
\tau
-structures, and let
\mathcal{G}
denote the
k
-round Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game over
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
Then, the

of
\mathcal{G}
are
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},\dots,c_{r}^{\mathcal{A}})
and
\vec{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{k},c_{1}^{\mathcal{B}},\dots,c_{r}^{\mathcal{B}})

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{B}
chosen in round

.
Then, Duplicator wins

if and only if

forms a partial isomorphism.

For a

\mathcal{G}
over
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, we say that Duplicator has a

for

if and only if there is a way for Duplicator to play such that no matter what Spoiler chooses, Duplicator can win
\mathcal{G}
.
Otherwise, Spoiler has a winning strategy for
\mathcal{G}
.
If Duplicator has a winning strategy for

, then we write

.
If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are
\mathsf{FC}

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are uniquely determined (up to isomorphism) by some

.
Therefore, we can simply write

.

Each first-order formula
\varphi
has a so-called

, denoted by the function
\mathsf{qr}
.
Let
\varphi,\psi
be first-order formulas over the same signature.
We define

•

If

is an atomic formula, then
\mathsf{qr}(\varphi):=0

•

\mathsf{qr}(\neg\varphi):=\mathsf{qr}(\varphi)
,

•

\mathsf{qr}(\varphi\land\psi)=\mathsf{qr}(\varphi\lor\psi):=\mathsf{max}\left(\mathsf{qr}(\varphi),\mathsf{qr}(\psi)\right)

•

.

Let
\mathsf{FC}(k)
denote the set of sentences
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
such that

.
Note if

[

]
.

It is known that winning strategies for Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games completely characterize the expressive power of first-order logic.
We state this correspondence using a slight adaptation of Theorem 6.19 from Immerman
[

]

Theorem 14

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite or infinite structures over a finite signature, and let

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{A}\in P

\mathcal{B}\notin P

.

As

-structures are simply a class of structures over the signature

(see

]

The meaning of the top line of

The second line states that

is smaller than (or equal to) the second largest value of

To deal with the case where

Concatenation

To simulate concatenation, we define the ternary relation
\oplus
over elements of
\psi_{\mathsf{univ}}(\mathcal{A})

We wish to have that

if and only if

.
To realize this behaviour, we look at various cases.
However, we shall only look at one case in detail, as the others follow analogously.

•

Case 1,
|u|_{\mathtt{1}},|v|_{\mathtt{1}}\geqslant 2
.
For this case, we have that

Thus

\underbrace{(m,x_{l},x_{r^{\prime}},n^{\prime})}_{t_{uv}}=\underbrace{(m,x_{l},x_{r},n)}_{t_{u}}\oplus\underbrace{(m^{\prime},x_{l^{\prime}},x_{r^{\prime}},n^{\prime})}_{t_{v}}\iff n+m^{\prime}=x_{r+1}=x_{l^{\prime}-1}

It is clear that one could write an

The formula also ensures that none of the components are

One can now rewrite

We define a mapping

First, let

•

7.5.
Loop-Step Cycles and Non-Definability in
\mathsf{FC}

This section gives the actual proof that if a minimal DFA
\mathcal{M}
has a loop-step cycle, then
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.
We rely on the following result:

Lemma 22

(Thompson and Freydenberger
[
TF24a
]
)
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2},v_{1},v_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
where
\mathsf{facts}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{facts}(w_{2})
is equal to
\mathsf{facts}(v_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{facts}(v_{2})
, and let
r
be the length of the longest word in
\mathsf{facts}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{facts}(w_{2})
.
If
w_{1}\equiv_{k+r+2}v_{1}
and
w_{2}\equiv_{k+r+2}v_{2}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, then
w_{1}\cdot w_{2}\equiv_{k}v_{1}\cdot v_{2}
.

We are now ready for the main result of
Section 7
.

Theorem 23
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},F)
be a minimal DFA.
If
\mathcal{M}
has a loop-step cycle, then
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

Proof 7.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},F)
be a minimal DFA
that has a loop-step cycle, i.e.,
there exist
n\geqslant 2
pairwise distinct states
p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{n-1}
and words
w,v\in\Sigma^{+}
where
\varrho(w)\neq\varrho(v)
and where:

•

\delta^{*}(p_{i},w)=p_{i}
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

•

\delta^{*}(p_{i},v)=p_{i+1\pmod{n}}
.

Note that since
\mathcal{M}
is minimal, all states must be
reachable, and thus there exists some word
p\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
\delta^{*}(q_{0},p)=p_{0}
.
Furthermore, there must exist some word
s\in\Sigma^{*}
such that

\delta^{*}(p_{j},s)\in F
for some
j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and

\delta^{*}(p_{\ell},s)\notin F
for some
\ell\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}

(the reason is that
otherwise, we could combine states to get a smaller automaton that
accepts the same language, contradicting the minimality of

\mathcal{M}
; for details see Section 4.3 in Chapter 2 of
[
RS97
]
).

We choose such words
p
and
s
and indices

0\leqslant j,\ell<n
and keep them fixed throughout the
remainder of this proof. That is,
\delta^{*}(q_{0},p)=p_{0}
and

\delta^{*}(p_{j},s)\in F
and
\delta^{*}(p_{\ell},s)\not\in F
.

Let
h\colon\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
where:

•

h(\mathtt{0}):=w^{n|v|}\cdot v^{n|w|+n+1}
, and

•

h(\mathtt{1}):=w^{2n|v|}\cdot v^{n+1}
.

Observe that
|h(\mathtt{0})|=|h(\mathtt{1})|
, because

|h(\mathtt{0})|=2n|v||w|+n|v|+|v|,\quad\text{and}\quad|h(\mathtt{1})|=2n|v||w|+n|v|+|v|.

Notice that

\delta^{*}(p_{i},h(\mathtt{0}))=\delta^{*}(p_{i},h(\mathtt{1}))=p_{i+1\pmod{n}}

for any
i\in\{0,\dots,n{-}1\}
.

Theorem 16 of
[
Lis11
]
states that for two
primitive words
x,y\in\Sigma^{+}
where
x\neq y
, the word
x^{n}y^{m}
is primitive for any

.
Note that Theorem 16 of
[
Lis11
]
is a direct consequence of Theorem 9.2.4 of
[
Lot97
]
.

From
\varrho(w)\neq\varrho(v)
we thus obtain that

h(\mathtt{0})
and
h(\mathtt{1})
are two distinct primitive words of equal length.
Thus,
h
is a uniform morphism.
Therefore, we can apply
21
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}

and any choice of finite sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{N}
whose minimal elements
are sufficiently large.

Recall that
\delta^{*}(p_{i},h(\mathtt{1}))=p_{i+1\pmod{n}}
for every
i
where
0\leqslant i<n
.
Furthermore, note that
\delta^{*}(p_{i},h(\mathtt{0})^{x})=p_{i}

for every

x\in\mathbb{N}
with
x\equiv 0\pmod{n}
.

For any finite set
S\subset\mathbb{N}
such that
x\equiv 0\pmod{n}
holds for all
x\in S
, consider the word

\alpha_{S}:=p\cdot h(\bar{w}_{S})\cdot s.

Note that the word
\bar{w}_{S}
contains
|S|{+}1

\mathtt{1}
, and between any two occurrences of the letter

\mathtt{1}
, there are
x

\mathtt{0}
,
for some
x\in S

r\in\{0,\ldots,n{-}1\}

following is true:

If
|S|{+}1\equiv r\pmod{n}
, then

\delta^{*}(p_{0},h(\bar{w}_{S}))=p_{r}
.

Due to our particular choice of the words

and the indices

we therefore obtain:

•

If

•

If

Recall that our goal is to show that

Let us fix an arbitrary

Let
P_{k^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a

From

.
As

is
r(k{+}m{+}20)

Provided that the minimal elements in

8.
Conclusions

In this paper, we have provided a decidable characterization of the
\mathsf{FC}
-definable regular languages in terms of (generalized) regular expressions, automata, and algebra (
2
).
Moreover, we have shown that this characterization is decidable, and is in fact
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for minimal DFAs (
3
).

A promising next step would be to study the expressive power of fragments of
\mathsf{FC}
.
The existential-positive fragment is particularly interesting due to its tight connection with core spanners
[
FP21
]
and word equations (see Section 6.2 of
[
Fre19
]
).
