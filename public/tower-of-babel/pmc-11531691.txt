Introduction

Smooth pins, often referred to as Kirschner wires (K-wires), are widely used fixation implants in orthopedic surgery for provisional fixation, percutaneous stabilization, and guiding cannulated instruments [1]. While complications can arise during their use, they are typically minor. Pin tract infections are the most common complications [2]. Pin migration, though exceptionally rare, can have significant consequences due to the potential for serious, even life-threatening, complications [3].

Most reported cases of pin migration involve the shoulder girdle including the sternoclavicular, clavicle, acromioclavicular, and glenohumeral structures, while migration from other regions is notably less frequent [4]. This complication typically occurs near the site of implantation, with distant migration being highly unusual [5].

This case report aims to explore the underlying mechanisms driving distant migration while also offering suggestions on preventive measures to reduce its incidence.

Case presentation

The patient was a 50-year-old Persian male who presented with a type 4 Garden fracture of the right femoral neck following a fall [6]. After initial assessment and exclusion of other injuries, the patient underwent operative fixation. Closed reduction and internal fixation were performed using three 6.5 mm cannulated screws. During this procedure, one of the guide pins broke, and attempts at removal were unsuccessful.

A postoperative radiograph showed satisfactory reduction of the fracture. The three screws were appropriately positioned, and the broken guide pin was visible (Fig. 1). The patient was discharged with instructions to not bear weight on the affected leg, instead using two crutches.

Postoperative X-ray of the right hip. The X-ray demonstrates satisfactory position of screws and fracture reduction. Note the presence of a broken guidewire

The patient visited for follow-up 2 weeks postoperatively. The incision was healing well, and sutures were removed. The hip pain had decreased since the surgery. However, a follow-up radiograph revealed an unexpected absence of the broken guide pin.

The fracture and implant positions remained unchanged from the previous radiograph. Given the patient’s absence of symptoms elsewhere, imaging studies of the abdomen, pelvis, chest, and lower extremities were obtained. The broken guide pin was identified within the chest, adjacent to the 11th rib (Fig. 2).

Abdominopelvic X-ray at 2-week follow-up. The image shows migration of the pin to a distant location on the right chest wall near the 11th rib

Following a thoracic surgery consult, a computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed the migrated pin within the right chest wall, next to the 11th rib (Fig. 3). A 3 cm anterolateral thoracotomy was performed under fluoroscopic guidance to extract the pin. A chest tube was inserted due to iatrogenic pneumothorax.

Computed tomography (CT) scans of the patient. A Abdominal CT scan: The red circle highlights the migrated pin in the chest wall, adjacent to the 11th rib. B Chest CT scan: The red arrows indicate lung inflammation caused by irritation from the pin’s tip

The chest tube was removed 2 days postoperatively, and the patient was discharged in stable condition. At 2-week follow-up, the patient reported no respiratory symptoms and improved hip pain. The thoracotomy incision had healed well, and sutures were removed.

The X-ray at 8 months post-surgery revealed signs of bone union in the hip. Additionally, the patient reported no hip or lung pain, and his gait appeared normal (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Migration of orthopedic implants, though uncommon, can lead to serious and even life-threatening complications. Researchers are constantly trying to understand how and why these implants migrate postoperatively. While this issue is more frequent with smaller implants such as wires and pins, there have also been reported cases of migration with larger implants such as screws and even spinal rods [7–9].

Distant implant migration usually originates from the proximal extremities (mostly the shoulder region) rather than other locations. However, there are some reports of the distant migration from the distal extremities (that is, hands and feet). Seipel et al. reported a case of distal radius fracture where one of the used pins migrated to the heart, and Botha et al. reported a case where a distal radius pin migrated to the cubital fossa [10, 11]. These cases show that distant implant migration can also occur from distal extremities. The site of insertion in our case was also the hip joint, which is an uncommon location for this complication.

The timeframe for detecting implant migration can vary greatly, ranging from just a few days after surgery, as our case, to several decades later. Irianto et al. reported a case where a migrated intramedullary clavicle pin was discovered only 4 days after surgery [12]. Conversely, in some cases migration of pins was not detected until over 20 years after surgery [9, 13].

This complication may affect all age groups. While most reported cases involve adults, there are a few documented cases involving pediatric patients [14, 15]. Surprisingly, some patients with distant migrated implants experience no symptoms, and migration is discovered incidentally [16, 17].

The underlying causes of implant migration are not fully understood, and various theories have been proposed. Some suggest that excessive limb movement may play role as a risk factor for pin migration, but there have been some cases of pin migration in patients immobilized using cast [18]. Others propose that implants may enter the vascular system and migrate through blood vessels. However, the diameter of some migrated implants, such as a spinal rod, is much larger than the blood vessels in that area, making this theory unlikely [7].

Some researchers hypothesize that unbending a pin after insertion may increase the risk of migration [19]. Given the pin in our case broken accidentally and so it was unbent, this observation aligns with their theory.

Conclusion

For patients who undergo Kirschner wire implantation, it is crucial to prioritize implementation of all preventive measures against pin migration. If these measures are not feasible due to surgical limitations, the surgeon should monitor the pin position periodically.
