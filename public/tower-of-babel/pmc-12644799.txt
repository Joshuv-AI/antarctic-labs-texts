Exploring attentional mechanisms underlying the gender homophily in equity crowdfunding decisions using web-based eye-tracking
Authors: Marco Barone, Candida Bussoli, Lucrezia Fattobene, Aiqing Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestor characteristics can influence preferences for entrepreneurial investment opportunities, with gender playing a crucial role. Using webcam-based eye-tracking, this study examines the attentional mechanism underlying gender homophily, a tendency to prefer projects led by entrepreneurial teams of one’s own gender, in equity crowdfunding (EC). Results confirm gender homophily among female investors, who fixate more quickly and look longer at entrepreneurial teams’ gender composition. Such a tendency is moderated by their financial knowledge with inexperience females showing a stronger effect. These findings support a top-down attentional control process, where attention is guided by goal-oriented selection of specific information rather than a bottom-up, stimulus-driven response. Mediation analyses further reveal that increased attention towards female-led teams significantly influences female investors’ investment preferences, linking attentional biases to decision outcomes. Male investors do not exhibit comparable gender-based effects. This study advances the understanding of how selective signal processing, shaped by investors’ endogenous characteristics such as gender and financial knowledge, influences investment decisions. Our results offer new insights into gender dynamics in entrepreneurial finance, elucidating the cognitive foundations of investor behavior and offering practical implications for EC platform designs and financial literacy interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25732-8.Keywords: Gender homophily, Equity crowdfunding, Attentional mechanisms, Top-down attentional control, Webcam-based eye-trackingSubject terms: Business and management, Business and management, Information systems and information technology, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Crowdfunding has emerged as a popular mechanism to raise financial resources for new ventures and start-ups in the last decade1. Among various crowdfunding models, equity crowdfunding (EC) has experienced the most significant growth2. According to Statista3, the EC market has reached USD 1.71 billion in 2024 and is expected to grow at a 5.14% compound annual growth rate, reaching USD 2.09 billion by 2028. This innovative investment model enables entrepreneurs to secure funding directly from a diverse pool of investors through online platforms4,5, offering equity stakes in the company in return for their financial contributions. In this way, investors become shareholders, aligning their interests with the success of the business.

One key factor driving the substantial growth of EC is its costs and equity providers. Compared to traditional financing methods, EC offers significantly lower costs for registering securities and provides access to a larger, more diverse pool of potential investors6. These benefits have attracted broader entrepreneurial participation and fostered a more inclusive finance ecosystem, increasing diversity of market participants in terms of age, gender, and ethnicity6,7. Indeed, EC platforms has shown a higher degree of gender diversity compared to other financial markets. Research has also demonstrated the crucial impact of gender diversity within entrepreneurial teams on influencing investors’ decisions8 and enhancing the success rate of crowdfunding campaigns9–11. Notably, teams with a higher proportion of women are more likely to attract investors, contributing to the success of EC campaigns11,12.

However, the success of EC campaigns is not solely determined by the gender composition of entrepreneurial teams but also the gender of the potential investors4,13. An intriguing phenomenon in EC research is the so-called gender homophily effect, whereby investors prefer EC projects led by entrepreneurs of the same gender10,11,14–17. This phenomenon is rooted in the homophily theory18,19, which suggests that individuals tend to form strong social connections or endorsements with others who share similar characteristics, such as demographic factors. In the context of funding decisions, the concept of homophily has been extended beyond the traditional individual level—interpersonal choice homophily, where individuals prefer to interact with similar others due to affinity, trust, and ease of communication—to the group level, called activist choice homophily, where individuals support co-group members based on shared identity and perceived disadvantage20. This perspective is particularly relevant in crowdfunding, where female investors more likely back female entrepreneurs not only due to individual-level similarities but also as a way to counter structural barriers faced by women in entrepreneurship.

In line with Greenberg & Mollick20, prior research in EC has found that the gender homophily effect is most pronounced among female investors11,14,15, highlighting the role of investor experience13. For instance, Bapna & Ganco14 found that inexperienced female investors exhibit a significantly greater interest in ventures led by female founders than those led by male founders. The gender homophily effect is absent among experienced female investors. In contrast, male individuals, regardless of their experience, show no preference based on founders’ gender. The authors indicated that inexperienced female investors may rely more heavily on heuristics due to low decision confidence. The article also suggested that this reliance may stem from gender homophily or prevailing stereotypes that associate entrepreneurship with masculinity. As investors gain more experience, this effect diminishes, which fosters a gender-blinding approach.

The limitations in women’s financial experience and financial knowledge21 are more evident in the start-up funding decisions, where high uncertainty and significant information asymmetries amplify these challenges22–25. In such an environment, the signaling theory26, widely applied to crowdfunding contexts27, posits that investment decisions could be influenced by low-cost signals, such as visual cues, optimistic rhetoric, press releases, and forecasts4,28–31. The presence of certain signals can shape the attention of potential investors and how they interpret these signals32,33. In particular, under high uncertainty, investors may heavily rely on these signals to guide their decisions34,35.

A critical assumption of signaling theory is individuals’ rationality, which suggests that investors should consistently notice and respond to signals based on their intended meanings32. However, this assumption may overlook investors’ cognitive constrains when they scan, interpret, and process multiple signals simultaneously, as well as the fact that only a subset of these signals capture attention36,37. In other words, attention functions as a selective filter for information processing38,39; individuals engage in selective attention and rely on low-effort methods to absorb evaluative information rather than thoroughly processing all available data37. Drover et al.32 further argued that signaling theory largely treats the receiver’s cognition as a “black box,” providing limited insights into the cognitive mechanisms behind how signals capture attention and how multiple, potentially competing signals are interpreted. This perspective is especially critical in contexts where both signaling behaviors (e.g., those conveyed through online platforms), and signal interpretation (e.g., investors’ perception), play a crucial role in shaping investment decisions (e.g., EC decisions). Consequently, a significant gap in the literature emerges in understanding how investors perceive, prioritize, and integrate diverse signals in their decision-making processes.

This issue is particularly relevant in the context of EC, where a digital, dynamic, and visual information-rich environment shapes investment decisions29. Such an environment is characterized by strong information asymmetries and uncertainty4, which require investors to filter and prioritize multiple signals under time and cognitive constraints. These features make EC an especially suitable setting to investigate attention allocation in investment decisions.

Cognitive research suggests that individuals attend to signals through two distinct routes: top-down attentional control, a voluntary process where attention is guided by pre-existing, internal goals, prompting active searches for relevant information, and bottom-up attentional control, an involuntary process where attention is reflexively and passively drawn to salient or unexpected stimuli32,40. Both attentional control processes are a pre-decision selection mechanism that determines which information gain access to encoding41. This feature is critically different from heuristic or stereotype-based evaluation in behavioural finance, which operates on already-encoded information for which cue to use or rely on by decision makers42.

To illustrate how these two attentional routes may explain the gender homophily effect, it is plausible that the gender composition of entrepreneurial teams functions as one of the signals on EC platforms, guiding inexperienced female investors’ evaluations through a top-down attentional process, wherein investors selectively attend to this signal that reinforces their pre-existing gender homophily. Alternatively, it is equally plausible that the preference for female-dominated projects emerges through a button-up process, in which female investors reflectively attend to and deliberate on financial information or product characteristics shown on the EC pages rather than focusing on the team gender composition. Without capturing how visual attention incorporates signals into the decision-making process, it is impossible to disentangle these two routes that may produce the gender homophily effect.

One way to measure visual attention is to use eye-tracker. This method not only preserves the natural decision-making processes but also enables researchers to quantify the weight and relevance of the information processed in an unobtrusive manner. Researchers have shown that eye gazes toward information or choice options provide critical access to individuals’ valuation processes43–47. Specifically, information that undergoes greater visual attention (i.e., reflected by longer fixation or other eye gaze metrics) is associated with information extraction, evidence accumulation and enhanced value formation48. Using eye-tracking, Butticè et al.49 investigated investors’ visual attention to different signals presented on EC pages. Contrary to signaling theory’s prediction that investors would attend to the most observable signals, they found that investors allocate attention unevenly across signals, with attention patterns varying according to investors’ investment knowledge and experiences. However, Butticè et al.49 did not explore the potential influences of homophily in the EC context, particularly how investors’ own gender may influence their attention allocation and information processing.

Understanding how investors’ own gender modulates visual attention in EC decisions is important. If gender influences which signals investors attend to and how they process information, this could explain why certain types of ventures—such as those led by female entrepreneurs—receive more or less investor interest. Insights from analyzing spatial attention allocation patterns could help clarify whether gender-based preferences arise from prioritizing team gender composition over other visual signals. Additionally, analyzing the temporal dynamics of visual attention could shed light on whether this preference results from a top-down (vs. a button-up) attentional approach, where investors’ characteristics (e.g., gender) influence how information is processed, ultimately contributing to the formation of the gender homophily effect among female investors.

Based on the empirical findings that female investors tend to invest EC projects led by female founders and signaling theory’s implication that the gender composition of entrepreneurial teams could serve as a salient signal, we hypothesize that:

H1afemale (vs. male) investors will allocate more attention to the gender composition of entrepreneurial teams when it is dominated by female individuals;

H1a

female (vs. male) investors will allocate more attention to the gender composition of entrepreneurial teams when it is dominated by female individuals;

H1bfor female investors, the increased attention towards female-dominated entrepreneurial team will positively mediate their preferences to invest in the project.

H1b

for female investors, the increased attention towards female-dominated entrepreneurial team will positively mediate their preferences to invest in the project.

Here, we report an experiment in which we employed webcam-based eye-tracking to measure participants’ ocular movements while they were evaluating EC campaign projects. While stationary eye-tracking has been increasingly used in prior neurofinance or neuromarketing research50,51, the use of webcam-based eye-tracking is underrepresented (e.g.,49). This technology allows researchers to sample participants more conveniently in an ecologically valid setting (e.g., test participants at their natural online environments, precisely where potential investors make their EC decisions)52. Moreover, webcam-based eye-tracking enables a targeted focus on the U.S. market, supported by a long-established SEC regulatory framework, a mature and diverse investor base, extensive EC experience, and high digital investment adoption. Focusing on participants from this market will generate relevant and informative insights for contemporary EC markets.

In this study, we sourced our EC campaign projects from real EC websites such as Crowdcube, Seedrs, BacktoWork, and Mamacrowd, including both successful and unsuccessful campaigns53. To test our hypotheses, we manipulated gender composition of the entrepreneurial teams with three conditions, controlling for potential confounding factors about campaign-related metrics. To evaluate decisions, participants were asked to assess the investment attractiveness of EC projects and we used normalized dwell time (NormDT) and time to first fixation (TTFF) towards the area of interests (AOIs) as the primary eye-tracking metrics to understand how investors of different genders process EC project information in the presence of varying gender cues according to our hypotheses. Based on previous literature, financial knowledge, financial self-efficacy, socio-demographic and socio-economic variables were used as controls.

Results

The preference for female-led EC projects by female investors

Firstly, to assess the presence of a homophily effect, we constructed two separate models—one for female and one for male investors—to examine the relationship between entrepreneurial team gender composition and attractiveness score. For female investors, the total effect of all-female Team Composition, compared to all-male teams, was a significant positive predictor of attractiveness score (Coef. = 0.189, SE = 0.072, p = 0.008), even accounting for the influence of information from all other AOIs in the campaigns (see Table 1). Conversely, for male investors, the model revealed a non-significant total effect (Coef. = 0.099, SE = 0.074, p = 0.183) for the difference between all-male and all-female Team Compositions on attractiveness score (see Table 2). These findings suggest that female investors exhibit a stronger preference for all-female teams, supporting the gender homophily hypothesis, whereas male investors do not display a comparable gender-based preference.

Investors’ gender specific attention to female-dominated entrepreneurial teams

Next, we analyzed how investors’ gender influenced their attention to specific information on EC pages. A mixed-effects model that includes the interaction term between investor gender and Team Composition gender (representing three team gender composition conditions: all-female, all-male, and balanced) found a significant interaction effect between female participants and all-female team compared to all-male Team Composition (Coef. = 21.309, SE = 9.116, p = 0.019). Compared to male investors, female investors spent significantly more dwell time in the AOI of Team Composition when the team was entirely women versus entirely men (see Fig. 1 and Table 3). Notably, the difference in dwell time emerges only when examining gender-based contrasts within the Team Composition conditions. Analyses of the other two AOIs show that female participants generally allocate more visual attention than their male counterparts (see Supplementary Table S1). These results indicate that female investors focus more on all-female teams compared to all-male teams, consistent with the gender homophily hypothesis, and suggesting that participant gender selectively guides attention to Team Composition cues. No comparable effect is observed for male investors, and the pattern is specific to the Team Composition AOI (Table 3).

To further investigate whether the tendency to view Team Composition is more pronounced among female investors with less experience, we employed a mixed-effects model analyzing TTFF, testing the interaction between investors’ gender and their financial knowledge (FK). TTFF is sensitive to capture the initial information that individuals seek during visual exploration. We found a significant interaction (Coef. = − 618.399, SE = 242.211, p = 0.011) together with a non-significant effect of FK (Coef. = − 75.813, SE = 218.824, p = 0.729), and a marginal effect of investors’ gender (Coef. = − 2251.727, SE = 820.574, p = 0.006) (Fig. 2). Since TTFF indexes initial attention orienting, the significant interaction indicates a gender-specific moderating effect of FK at the early stage of information encoding prior to evaluation. In particular, among female investors, lower FK was associated with shorter TTFF on the AOI representing Team Composition, indicating a faster orientation toward this information. This pattern was not observed among male investors. These results suggest that, in women, FK selectively modulates top-down early attentional processes toward Team Composition rather than a generic reliance on gender cue as heuristic.

Specifically, among women, higher FK is associated with shorter time to first fixation on the other three AOIs, which contain more financial or project-related information and require greater cognitive effort. This suggests that women with lower FK may rely more on homophily cues compared to both men and women with higher FK who tend to allocate their attention to cognitively demanding, project-relevant information.

The results from the above analyses indicated that investors’ own gender altered what information to focus on when evaluating the attractiveness of EC projects. In line with H1a, we found that female investors, particularly these with lower FK, not only allocated more attention to team gender composition when entrepreneurial teams are female-dominated, but also more quickly sought out this information in general. This temporal pattern of TTFF also supports the involvement of a top-down attentional process at play. More specifically, the reduced TTFF toward the Team Composition AOI among female investors with lower FK supports the activation of a top-down attentional process, whereby internally driven goals or cognitive heuristics, such as gender homophily, guide the early intentionally prioritization of socially salient signals, rather than reflexive, stimulus-driven attention.

Mediation of increased attention on team composition on EC project attractiveness

To understand how female investors’ attention, quantified as NormDT, influenced their evaluations of EC projects, we constructed a mediation model to assess the mediating effect of attention to the AOI of Team Composition on the relationship between entrepreneurial team gender composition and attractiveness score. We found that for female investors there was a significant indirect effect of team gender composition (all-female vs. all-male) on the rated attractiveness of EC projects. A 95% confidence interval based on 50,000 Monte Carlo iterations confirmed that the indirect effect (Coef. = 0.0012) was positive and significant (lower limit = 0.000053, upper limit = 0.002885) (see Table 1 and Fig. 4). This result confirmed H1b. In contrast, the same mediation model for male investors found the total effect was not significant (Table 2). These findings indicate that female investors’ visual attention to Team Composition explain the effect of team gender composition on attractiveness ratings, confirming that gender-based attentional processes play a significant role in shaping investment evaluations. In contrast, male investors’ ratings appear unaffected by Team Composition through this attentional pathway.

Discussion

In this research, we investigate the cognitive processes underlying the gender homophily effect in EC investment decisions. By employing the webcam-based eye-tracking technique, we captured investors’ visual attention patterns, which approximate their information processing strategies. Our analyses revealed several key insights.

First of all, we confirmed the presence of the gender homophily effect in our experiment when the gender composition of entrepreneurial teams was systematically manipulated. We found that female investors rated the EC projects more attractive when the projects are led by all-female teams, and such an effect was absent among male investors.

Second, the analyses of the eye-tracking data demonstrated that investors’ own gender plays a crucial role in shaping their visual attention during their EC evaluation decisions. More specifically, consistent with H1a, we found that female (vs. male) investors exhibited significantly greater times looking at Team Composition when teams were female-dominated. This suggests that gender cues on the EC pages are particularly salient for female investors when evaluating entrepreneurial projects. The means the gender composition information is not simply noticed, but actively processed during investment decisions. More importantly, female investors with lower level of FK oriented their attention toward the Team Composition AOI much more rapidly, whereas male investors did not show similar patterns. This temporal signature aligns well with top-down attention control that selects what information is encoded based on participants’ FK. Such early, pre-decisional orienting cannot be fully explained by the heuristic reliance on Team Composition for EC decisions as suggested by Bapna and Ganco14. While heuristics may still shape how encoded information such as gender cues is weighted during evaluation, the initial top-down control process explains why investors encode different cues from the same display. This finding thus directly addresses the call to investigate investor characteristics that can mitigate homophily13.

Third, mediation analyses confirmed that this increased visual attention toward Team Composition AOI positively influenced female investors’ evaluation for projects led by female-dominated teams. This finding demonstrates that visual attention is a key cognitive mechanism linking gender homophily to investment decisions. Notably, in our experiment, male investors did not exhibit a similar attention pattern or preference, corroborating previous research suggesting that men do not display strong gender-based investment biases11,14,15.

Our findings contribute to both homophily theory and signaling theory. Extending the homophily theory18,19, we demonstrated that gender homophily is not merely a behavioral inclination, but a result derived from specific measurable cognitive processes. Specifically, female investors exhibited longer visual dwell times on female-led teams, which mediated their assessment of EC project attractiveness; however, this attentional pattern as well as the mediating effect were not found among male investors. This finding deepens our understanding of gender homophily effect in investor decision-making by recognizing it driven by unique visual attention patterns.

Building on the signaling theory, our study provides new insights into selective signal processing during investment decisions characterized by high uncertainty such as EC decisions4,31. Rather than processing all available signals in a button-up manner, as what the classic signaling theory would assume, our findings reveal that investors actively filter signals in a top-down, goal-driven approach, strategically directing their attention to relevant information. Importantly, our findings extend signaling theory by showing that attentional selectivity is not simply determined by signal observability, as also demonstrated by Butticè et al.6, but rather by investors’ endogenous characteristics. Thus, the perceived relevance of information is individually determined by factors such as knowledge, experience, and gender, highlighting a personalized, goal-driven process of signal filtering.

Our study also makes a methodological contribution by employing webcam-based eye-tracking to study investment decisions. Given that attention allocation largely occurs unconsciously, eye-tracking provides a more reliable and accurate measure of information processing compared to self-reported methods54–56, which are often subject to biases and inaccuracies due to reliance on retrospective recall. Moreover, webcam-based eye-tracking has demonstrated reliability comparable to laboratory-based setups57,58 while offering greater flexibility for data collection in naturalistic environments. This flexibility allows researchers to conduct studies in more realistic contexts, such as our online EC investment decision experiment, thereby enhancing the ecological validity of the findings without compromising measurement accuracy49,57,59,60.

Our findings have practical implications for EC platforms, fundraisers, and policymakers. The role of visual attention in shaping gender homophily suggested that platform design choices, such as the prominence of Team Composition information, might influence investment outcomes. Platforms seeking to promote gender diversity in EC could benefit from interface adjustments that balanced gender visibility without reinforcing homophilic biases. In particular, platforms may adopt tailored signaling strategies61 that calibrate the salience of Team Composition relative to financial and project-related information (e.g., rather than prominently displaying team photos with gender icons, platforms could present smaller team indicators alongside detailed financial and project information, such as funding goals, projected return on investment, or key milestone). Similarly, they can introduce external validation mechanisms8,62 (e.g., a campaign could display badges for financial audits, third-party expert reviews, or accelerator program endorsements next to the team info), and carefully manage the frequency of information updates63 (e.g. updates about funding progress or key milestones could be released weekly in a standardized format, rather than continuously showing team changes or minor edits that highlight certain team members), thereby enhancing transparency and supporting more equitable investment outcomes. Female fundraisers could emphasize their presence in the entrepreneurial team and seeking platforms with a larger proportion of active female participants, to enhance appeal. Additionally, policymakers aiming to enhance financial inclusivity should consider interventions that improved financial knowledge among female investors21, reducing reliance on heuristic decision-making.

Despite its contributions, this study had limitations. While webcam-based eye-tracking offers scalable, naturalistic insight into attention allocation mechanisms, it also suffers from context-dependent variability in calibration and data quality (e.g., camera hardware, lighting, head movement). Although we documented calibration success and valid-gaze thresholds, residual variability may attenuate effects. Future work should incorporate stationary eye-trackers, and pair gaze with neurophysiological measures (e.g., EEG, fMRI) to capture complementary affective and cognitive processes that accompany attentional gating. Second, our sample consisted primarily of investors with a mean age in the mid-50 s, potentially constraining the external validity of our findings for younger, more digitally engaged crowdfunding participants as well as for professional investors. Future studies should examine whether investment experience influences investment behavior through distinct oculomotors patterns, and also consider the impact between general financial experience versus specific EC experience. Third, our participants were exclusively US-based, which raises the issue of cultural bias. Since EC market maturity and gender norms vary across cultures, and given that geographic context moderates both crowdfunding success and the effectiveness of signaling strategies64, cross-market replications (e.g., in Europe and other regions) are needed to establish further conditions for gender homophily and signal use.

Materials and methods

Apparatus

This study utilized webcam-based eye-tracking technology provided by RealEye. The software employs the JavaScript Media Devices API to access participants’ webcams, supporting resolutions starting at 640 × 480 and a sampling rate ranging from 30 to 60 frames per second, depending on the capabilities of each participant’s webcam. RealEye’s algorithm incorporates the I-VT (Velocity-Threshold Identification) fixation filter, which operates on data with a sampling rate of over 20 Hz. Default parameters include a maximum gaze duration of 33 ms and a minimum fixation duration of 100 ms. To improve accuracy, a median filter configured to 21 frames is applied for noise reduction.

Visual stimuli

The visual stimuli (Fig. 5) consisted of 24 actual EC campaigns, with campaign details manually collected from online platforms such as Crowdcube, Seedrs, BacktoWork, and Mamacrowd. Following Genevsky et al.53, an equal number of successful (12) and unsuccessful campaigns were selected. Each visual stimulus was processed dividing it into four AOIs: a brief description of the business Idea (Idea), key Business Highlights (Buss_High) including market projections, the campaign’s progress and stable financial data such as pre-money valuation (Num_Inf), and details about the entrepreneurial Team Composition (Team_Comp). To ensure consistency and control for potential biases related to ethnicity, physical appearance, and team size16,65,66, the entrepreneurial team information was standardized by representing each team member as an icon (pink for female and blue for male). Each team consistently comprised four members, configured in one of three compositions: two men and two women (balanced), four women (all-female), or four men (all-male). This use of standardized icons aimed to eliminate visual variability in physical characteristics and minimize potential confounding effects of team numerosity on participants’ perceptions during the experiment. The number of text lines and font size were standardized across all sections to ensure comparable oculomotor patterns, thereby minimizing exogenous visual salience on the EC pages and reducing the possibility that TTFF reflects stimulus-driven capture67.

Procedure

Participants were fully briefed on the procedure, viewed two prototype campaigns, and completed a calibration process before the experiment began. The experiment consisted of 24 slides, each displaying an EC campaign, with each slide presented for a maximum of 60 s. Participants were instructed to freely examine each EC campaign, which appeared in a randomized order. They could advance to the next trial by either clicking the mouse or pressing any key on the keyboard. After viewing each EC campaign, participants rated the investment attractiveness using a 7-point Likert scale (ATTScore). To stabilize gaze and standardize the initial eye position, a 500 ms fixation cross was shown between trials. Following the eye-tracking session, participants completed a questionnaire on the Pavlovia platform, providing demographic information, financial self-efficacy, financial knowledge, and details about their crowdfunding and financial investment experience. An overview of the experimental procedure is provided in Fig. 6.

Measures

Eye-tracking

Eye position data were analyzed using a standard AOI approach, focusing on four predefined AOIs: business idea (Idea), Business Highlights (Bus_High), Numerical Information (Num_Inf), and Team Composition (Team_Comp). The primary eye-tracking metric used was the Normalized Dwell Time (NormDT), expressed in milliseconds per percent pixel (ms/px%). NormDT represents the ratio of the total dwell time—defined as the cumulative duration of all fixations within an AOI, measured in milliseconds—to the AOI coverage. AOI coverage is calculated as the proportion of the stimulus area occupied by the AOI, expressed as a percentage of pixels (px%). The second eye-tracking measure used is Time to First Fixation (TTFF) that measures the time interval, in milliseconds, between the onset of a stimulus and the first fixation on an AOI. It reflects the amount of time it takes for a respondent to focus on a specific AOI after the stimulus is presented, with a shorter TTFF indicating the AOI is engaging for the investor.

Investors’ individual differences

Socio-economic, demographic and personality variables, along with gender, are frequently utilized in economic literature, often serving as control variables (e.g.,68–72). Therefore, we gathered data on age and education. Additionally, we included a question about self-identification as left-handed, which has been suggested to affect visual attention distribution potentially73,74.

Financial knowledge (FK) was assessed using the ‘big five’ FK questions developed by Lusardi & Mitchell75. These questions cover interest rates, inflation, risk diversification, mortgages, and bond pricing and have been utilized across more than 20 countries. Each participant’s FK score is determined by summing the number of correct answers they provided. A correct answer is scored as 1, while incorrect, blank, or “do not know” responses receive a score of 0. Hence, the FK index ranges from 0 to 5, comprising only integer values. FK was included as a moderator to examine how it influences gender differences in TTFF toward financial information on EC pages, as it may affect how individuals attend to such content76.

Financial self-efficacy (FSE) was evaluated using a six-item scale developed by Lown77. Based on Bandura’s78 framework, Lown77 adapted Schwarzer and Jerusalem’s79 general self-efficacy scale to assess confidence in financial matters. Each question was rated on a scale of 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater FSE. Participants’ overall FSE scores were calculated by summing their responses across the six items, resulting in scores ranging from 6 to 24, with only integer values possible. FSE was included as a control variable due to its documented influence on financial decision-making and its association with both compound and elemental personality traits80.

EC campaigns

In addition to the main variables of interest, in the analysis we included several campaign-related metrics as control variables to account for potential confounding factors and ensure the robustness of the analysis. These measures were derived from real campaigns and presented to participants through standardized visual stimuli during the experiment, and include: the number of words in the Idea section (IdeaNW), the number of investors who have already committed to the campaign (NInv), the campaign funding target (Target), the amount already raised (Raised), the percentage of the funding goal achieved (%Raised), the number of days remaining until the campaign deadline (Days), the pre-money valuation (PreMoney), and the number of words in the Bus_High section (BHNW). Following Gelman81, we scaled Target, Raised, and PreMoney using the natural logarithm to address differences in scale.

Pilot study

The pilot test was conducted on 24 US Dynata participants (11 men and 13 women, aged 22 to 72). Excellent calibration scores were achieved, all above the threshold value (above three out of 6). All participants exhibited increased attention to stimuli in the protocol, exceeding 10 s on most of them. All campaigns shown in the pilot study were used in the main study. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.782 for the pilot study. The effect size of 0.2436 falls between medium and large according to Cohen’s83 criteria (revised edition of 1988). With α = 0.05 and power (1—β error probability) = 0.80, a minimum sample size of 107 participants is required.

Participants

The research experiment was distributed via the data platform Dynata to 221 participants. The application of exclusion criteria resulted in a sample of 139 individuals (59 men, Mage = 55.22, SD = 16.43) (Table 4). Participants were all right-handed and without reported eye or neurological diseases. A significant portion of the sample (50.36%) holds a bachelor’s, master’s or doctoral degree. The majority (60.43%) are in domestic partnerships or married. Income-wise, 40.29% reported earning over USD 4000 monthly, and 52.51% declared it as 1000 to 3999. Only 6.47% of the subjects have prior crowdfunding experience. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the internal ethics committee of LUM Giuseppe Degennaro University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. Data for this study were collected using the RealEye online eye-tracking platform (https://www.realeye.io). All data were handled anonymously and in accordance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Data analysis

The analysis utilized linear mixed-effects models, also known as hierarchical linear regression models, to account for the hierarchical structure of the data84. This method enables the inclusion of random effects beyond those associated with the overall error term. Mediation analysis followed the framework proposed by Baron and Kenny85 and employed the Monte Carlo method86 with 50,000 iterations to evaluate the statistical significance of the indirect effect. Robust errors for heteroskedasticity were used in all models87.

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.782 with a noncentrality parameter (μ) of 2.6366 and a noncentral standard deviation of 1, resulting in an estimated one-tailed power (1 − β error probability) of 0.839, consistent with our a priori directional hypothesis; the main predictor, All-female, showed an effect size of 0.115.
