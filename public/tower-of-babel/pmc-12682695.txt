Factors Affecting the Decision for Surgery in Patients with Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Using a Nationwide Cohort in South Korea
Authors: Taeho Oh, Jong Won Gil, Sang Ki Lee, Young Sun An, Sun Youl Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarpal tunnel release (CTR) effectively relieves symptoms and improves the quality of life for patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). While studies have explored factors such as age and sex that influence surgical decisions, the roles of socioeconomic factors and insurance coverage remain underexplored. Our study addressed this gap by analyzing a nationwide cohort database in South Korea to understand surgical patterns and potential cost-saving effects based on insurance type and income level. Our hypothesis was that income level and insurance type would influence the decision to undergo surgery in patients with CTS.MethodsThis study selected patients with CTS who underwent CTR from South Korea’s National Health Insurance Service-sample cohort and analyzed their demographic and treatment data to assess surgery rates and times to surgery, considering factors such as age, sex, residential district, income, disability status, and insurance type. Regression analysis and analysis of variance were used to determine the influence of these variables on surgical decisions and times to surgery, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsMultivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that income level did not significantly influence surgical decision-making. Female patients experienced longer times to surgery than male patients (p = 0.034). Younger patients waited longer, but the trend reversed for those aged ≥ 50 years (p < 0.001). In contrast, patients with public medical aid insurance (p = 0.022) and those with disabilities (p < 0.001) had shorter times to surgery. Income and residential district had no significant impact on times to surgery.ConclusionsIndividuals covered by comprehensive public aid insurance had quicker surgical interventions. Female patients undergo surgery more frequently, but they tend to delay it. The likelihood of undergoing surgery increases with age until the 50s. Individuals with disabilities undergo surgery more frequently and promptly. These findings suggest that physicians should encourage timely surgical interventions among female patients and younger patients who tend to delay surgery. For healthcare policymakers, implementing targeted education programs and maintaining support for public medical aid insurance may facilitate quicker access to surgical care and address delays among specific demographics.Keywords: Carpal tunnel syndrome, Carpal tunnel release, Insurance type, Income level, Nationwide cohort study

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), a prevalent upper extremity mono–neuropathy, significantly affects a large portion of the population.1) This condition, often associated with excessive hand use, obesity, and systemic conditions such as diabetes, leads to debilitating symptoms, including pain, numbness, and loss of hand functionality.1,2,3) Carpal tunnel release (CTR), an effective surgical intervention, has been widely employed to relieve symptoms and enhance patients’ quality of life.1,4)

Aspects of CTS, including epidemiology, risk factors, and management strategies, have been studied.2,4) While initial surgical management is generally deemed cost-effective, delays to treatment can potentially exacerbate disability.5) Trends have revealed how patient characteristics, such as age, sex, and comorbidities, influence the decision-making process related to surgical intervention.6) However, a significant knowledge gap exists in understanding how socioeconomic factors, particularly insurance type and corresponding income level, influence the management of CTS. Furthermore, there is a lack of studies utilizing nationwide cohort databases on these topics.

In healthcare, financial considerations often play a pivotal role in patient management decisions.7) Economic burden, insurance coverage, and income levels may significantly impact patients’ choices, the accessibility and timeliness of care, and potentially, the outcomes. Given the costs associated with CTS surgery, the patient’s socioeconomic status and insurance type may influence the management trajectory.

This study aimed to fill this research gap by (1) identifying patients undergoing CTS surgery, (2) examining whether there is a cost-saving effect based on insurance type and income level, (3) using a nationwide cohort database in South Korea to uncover critical insights that could guide clinicians in delivering more equitable and economically efficient care for patients with CTS.

METHODS

The Institutional Review Board of Eulji Medical Center approved the design and protocol of this study (IRB No. EMC 2023-03-016). Informed consent of patients was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

Patients

Patients with CTS who underwent CTR were selected from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) cohort version 2.2 (NHIS-Sample) of South Korea. The NHIS established the National Health Information Database, a claims database for research purposes that stores all records of healthcare and long-term care services.8,9) The NHIS constructed and provided researchers with the NHIS-Sample, representative administrative data for health policy and biomedical research purposes, and has published a detailed cohort profile. The NHIS-Sample consists of 1 million people selected by a systematic stratified random sampling method from 48,222,537 individuals as of December 31, 2006.9) Under a compulsory social insurance system established by the National Health Insurance Act, all participants could be followed up until 2018, except for instances of death or emigration. The NHIS maintains the personal, demographic, and medical treatment data of the entire Korean population. The key variables in the NHIS-Sample cohort included all inpatient and outpatient medical claims data, such as the codes for treatment procedures, prescriptions, and diagnoses.

Demographic Variables

Age was grouped into categories of under 30 years, over 70 years, and between 30 and 70 years, further divided into 10-year ranges. Income level was determined according to the type of health insurance subscription. For employee-insured individuals, income was calculated based on the actual monthly salary (including wages and bonuses) to which the applicable premium rate was applied. In contrast, for self-employed individuals with regional insurance, a “standard income” was computed by comprehensively considering not only the reported income but also assets such as real estate and automobiles. This measure—widely used by the NHIS for policy and research evaluations—was subsequently classified into deciles. The top 8–10 deciles were categorized as “high” income, deciles 4–7 as middle income, and deciles 1–3 as low income.10) Insurance type was broadly divided into regular health insurance and public medical aid health insurance based on the health insurance classification criteria applicable to all South Korean citizens. Individuals covered by public medical aid health insurance receive full coverage for all medical treatments and therapies, with the state bearing all costs. Residential district classification included residents of 6 metropolitan cities, including Seoul, categorized as urban areas; cities with populations over 50,000 that are not metropolitan as small to medium-sized cities; and cities with smaller populations as rural areas. Disability was defined as receiving financial support after receiving a disability rating from the South Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare.

CTS Cohort

Based on a previous study,11) the inclusion criteria for the incident CTS cohort included the following diagnostic codes, as defined by the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, tenth revision (ICD-10): G56.0 (CTS). Patients were defined as individuals above 18 years of age who underwent their first CTS surgery between 2002 and 2018 following operative codes: 04.43 (release of carpal tunnel), 04.43E, 04.43D (carpal tunnel release), 04.43B (mini-open carpal tunnel release), 04.43A (conventional open carpal tunnel release), and 04.43C, 04.43E, 04.43F, 04.43G (endoscopic carpal tunnel release). Of these, patients who underwent CTR surgery before January 1, 2007, were excluded to maintain the validity of the study design. The incidence date (time-zero) of CTR was defined as the date of diagnosis from the outpatient clinic to the acute care hospital that fulfilled the inclusion criteria. The primary endpoint of this study was whether the patient underwent surgery for neuropathy. Secondary variables included the time to surgery (from the date of diagnosis) for patients who underwent surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Regression analysis was performed to determine the contribution of input variables, such as age, sex, residential district, household income, disability, and insurance type, to the surgical resolution of neuropathy and time to surgery. The chi-square test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to determine the differences in the rates of and times to surgery between patients in different age groups. The chi-square test was used to analyze surgery rates since they were reported in whole number groups, and ANOVA was used for time to surgery, as these were continuous data. To determine the effect of any single patient variable compared to others, logistic regression was performed, and significance (p < 0.05) or a statistical trend (p < 0.10) was established. Statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.1 (SAS Institute). Means are presented with standard deviations. The p-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics for Receiving Surgery

Table 1 and Fig. 1 show the demographics of patients receiving surgery. During the cohort period, 29,273 patients in South Korea were diagnosed with CTS, of whom 3,926 (13.4%) underwent CTR. In each group, the proportion of patients with CTS undergoing surgery was higher among females (15.0%) than males (8.5%) (p < 0.001). In each group, the proportion of patients with CTS undergoing surgery increased with age, peaking at 50 years (16.0%), followed by a decreasing trend (p < 0.001). According to residential district data, rural residents (14.6%) had a higher rate of surgery than urban dwellers (13.1%) (p = 0.024). Patients with disabilities (16.0%) had a higher surgery rate than those without (13.2%) (p < 0.001). Furthermore, sex, age, residential district, and disability showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). In contrast, income level and insurance type did not significantly influence the decision to undergo surgery.

Patient Demographics for Times to Surgery

The time to surgical decisions differed significantly according to sex, age, insurance type, and disability status (Table 3, Fig. 2). Female patients (561.2 days) had a longer time than male patients (478.8 days) (p = 0.034). Patients < 50 years had relatively extended periods (689 to 746 days); however, it decreased with age for those ≥ 50 years (p < 0.001). Patients with public medical aid health insurance had a shorter period than those with regular health insurance (415.4 days vs. 554.1 days, p = 0.022). Moreover, patients with disabilities had a shorter period than those without disabilities (364.3 days vs. 564.5 days, p < 0.001). However, income level and residential district did not significantly differ in the time to surgical decisions.

DISCUSSION

We aimed to explore potential disparities, such as income level, insurance type, and residential area, in treatment among patients categorized by age, sex, and socioeconomic status. Given the chronic and progressive nature of CTS,12) we were particularly interested in identifying any variations in time to surgery among these patient groups, particularly those with public medical aid insurance.

The strengths of this study that distinguish it from previous studies on factors influencing the decision to undergo surgery for CTS and the time to surgery are as follows: First, our patient data were sourced from a single country’s comprehensive nationwide cohort database, lending substantial power to our findings, even when accounting for potential statistical confounders. Second, our research examines the impact of medical decision-making and patients’ financial status on South Korea’s distinctive healthcare policy, a country with a nationwide compulsory insurance policy in place since the 1970s. Conditions such as CTS, with its relatively high diagnostic rate and typical surgical treatment, serve as a valuable metric for national healthcare expenditure planning. However, unlike this policy-oriented viewpoint, clinicians often favor a treatment outcome-centered approach. The findings of this study revealed that while a general level of compulsory health insurance coverage did not cause significant differences in the decision to undergo surgery compared to mandatory full-coverage health insurance for low-income individuals, it shortened the decision-making period for surgery, thereby reducing medical expenditure. Considering the frequent relief of symptoms following surgical release with the progressive nature of these neuropathies, clinicians need to be mindful of factors that may delay treatment. Consistent with other studies, our study showed an increased likelihood of surgery with increasing age.6) Furthermore, consistent with previous studies,6,13) our logistic regression analysis demonstrated that age is a significant factor in the decision to undergo surgery. However, unlike previous studies,6) we categorized age more appropriately, revealing that patients in their 50s had the highest proportion of surgeries and made decisions about surgery significantly faster than younger groups. Patients under 40 years tended to delay surgery, whereas those in their 40s demonstrated the second-highest surgery rates. These trends may be linked to age and occupational activity; however, we did not establish a clear causal relationship, suggesting the need for further exploration. Since surgical release is frequently recommended as the first-line treatment,14) understanding why younger patients have lower surgical rates is essential for future studies.

Furthermore, consistent with previous studies,6) we found that less than half of female patients with a single neuropathy underwent surgical treatment. However, the incidence rate in our study was markedly lower (15.0%). In previous studies,6) the frequency of electromyography testing for CTS was within 60%. However, in this study, it was difficult to reflect electromyography results due to the use of multi institution-based big data. This may have been influenced by the low incidence rate. Notably, while a previous study6) reported fewer women than men undergoing surgery (41% vs. 63%), our findings showed the opposite. This may reflect factors that encourage women to opt for surgery, such as expectations of symptom improvement through conservative treatment and fear of symptoms worsening.15) Consequently, many women continue conservative treatment until surgery becomes necessary. This underlines the need for clinicians to consider these factors when planning treatment.

This study revealed that, contrary to previous studies, rural residents have a higher rate of undergoing surgery compared to urban residents. The higher rate of surgery among rural residents compared to urban area residents in CTS patients may be attributed to various factors such as differences in access to healthcare services, socioeconomic status, occupational hazards, sex or age ratio, or even cultural factors. Despite relatively good accessibility to medical care in South Korea, rural residents may need to exert more effort to seek treatment at hospitals compared to urban dwellers. This could lead to a preference for more fundamental treatments.

Our findings also highlight that while income level does not significantly affect surgical decisions, certain groups, such as females and younger patients, tend to delay surgery. This underscores the need for physicians to proactively discuss timely surgical options with these patients to prevent prolonged symptoms and potential complications. Additionally, we recommend that healthcare policymakers consider implementing targeted education and intervention programs to address these delays among specific demographics. Maintaining and potentially expanding support for public medical aid insurance and disability services could also facilitate quicker access to surgical care, as these factors appear to enable prompt surgical interventions.

Our study had some limitations. First, this was a retrospective review; therefore, patients did not follow a standardized preoperative clinical evaluation regimen. In addition, occupational data that could contribute to a more thorough understanding of the socioeconomic context were not available in the database. Moreover, owing to our research’s large-scale, data-centric nature, we could not verify the severity of CTS, reasons for surgery refusal, or reasons for surgery delay. Another limitation is our classification of insurance types without accounting for supplementary private insurance. Given that regular health insurance covers a significant portion of medical costs and that carpal tunnel surgery is relatively inexpensive in Korea, the distinction between insurance types may not significantly affect patients’ decisions to undergo surgery. Additionally, the widespread use of private supplemental insurance could further mitigate out-of-pocket expenses. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that, when analyzing nationwide big data, there was no statistically significant difference in surgical rates among the various insurance types. This finding could be interpreted positively, suggesting that moral hazards—such as the over–recommendation of surgery based on insurance type—may not be a concern. However, the fact that individuals facing relatively higher financial burdens experience a prolonged time to surgery is indicative of systemic inefficiencies, serving as a cautionary signal for both patients and clinicians.

Income level does not significantly impact the decision to undergo surgery or its delay; however, individuals with full-coverage public aid insurance exhibited a trend toward quicker surgical intervention. Rural residents were more likely to undergo surgery than urban dwellers, but there was no significant difference in time to surgery. Females undergo surgery more frequently but tend to delay it. The likelihood of undergoing surgery increases with age until 50 years, after which there is a tendency for faster surgical decisions.5) Individuals with disabilities and full-coverage public aid insurance undergo surgery more frequently and promptly.
