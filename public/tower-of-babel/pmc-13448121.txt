Domain-Adapted Foundation Models for Single-Click Surgical Instrument Segmentation in Spinal Endoscopy
Authors: Bong-Su Mun, Zhi Zhao, Sang-Min Park, Jiwon Park, Hyun-Jin Park, Hyung Rae Lee, Ho-Joong Kim, Jin S Yeom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAccurate segmentation of surgical instruments in endoscopic images is essential for developing computer-assisted surgical systems, yet creating annotated training datasets remains labor-intensive. This study aimed to evaluate the efficiency of point-based interactive segmentation using foundation models for surgical instrument annotation in spinal endoscopy. MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study comparing point-based segmentation performance between Segment Anything Model (SAM) 2.0, SAM 2.1, and domain-adapted MedSAM 2.1 for surgical instrument segmentation in spinal endoscopy. A test set of 308 images from 10 patients undergoing full-endoscopic lumbar decompression/discectomy or biportal endoscopic decompression/discectomy was evaluated. Models were assessed using standardized point prompts placed at the ground truth mask centroid. Primary outcomes included Dice similarity coefficient at single-click and success rate (Dice≥0.85). Secondary analysis evaluated click-to-quality metrics across iterative refinement up to 10 clicks. ResultsAt single-click, MedSAM 2.1 achieved significantly higher Dice scores than SAM 2.1 (0.937±0.074 vs. 0.844±0.222, p<0.001; 95% confidence interval [CI] for difference, 0.070–0.116) and SAM 2.0 (0.937±0.074 vs. 0.824±0.236, p<0.001; 95% CI for difference, 0.088–0.138), with a 3-fold reduction in variability (standard deviation: 0.074 vs. 0.222). External validation on the publicly available Spine Endoscopic Atlas dataset (202 images from 10 patients) confirmed generalizability, with MedSAM 2.1 achieving Dice 0.918±0.083, compared to 0.724±0.281 for SAM 2.1 and 0.708±0.290 for SAM 2.0 (both p<0.001). Interrater agreement between 2 independent annotators demonstrated excellent reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient, 0.991; 95% CI, 0.986–0.994; mean pairwise Dice, 0.953±0.051). The MedSAM 2.1-assisted workflow reduced annotation time by 75.8% compared to manual polygon annotation (7.7±13.3 seconds vs. 31.8±18.2 seconds per image; p<0.001; 4.1-fold speedup). SAM 2.1 improved progressively with additional clicks (Dice 0.844 to 0.943 at 10 clicks), while MedSAM 2.1 showed nonmonotonic progression (0.937 to 0.947), indicating near-optimal first-click predictions. ConclusionDomain-adapted MedSAM 2.1 improved single-click annotation accuracy and efficiency for surgical instruments in spinal endoscopy, suggesting that low-resource domain adaptation may facilitate institutional dataset construction for surgical artificial intelligence research.Keywords: Surgical instrument segmentation, Spinal endoscopy, Segment Anything Model, Interactive segmentation, Foundation model, Domain adaptation

INTRODUCTION

The advent of minimally invasive spine surgery has fundamentally transformed how we approach spinal disorders [1]. Endoscopic techniques such as uniportal endoscopic (UE) and biportal endoscopic (BE) spine surgery now offer patients meaningful advantages: less tissue trauma, shorter hospitalizations, and quicker return to daily activities [2,3]. Systematic reviews and comparative studies have further validated these techniques across diverse patient populations [4-7]. Surgeons must operate entirely through 2-dimensional video displays showing highly magnified operative fields, a constraint that demands exceptional hand-eye coordination and spatial reasoning. Compounding this challenge, the endoscopic environment introduces a constellation of imaging obstacles, including specular reflections off wet tissue surfaces, obscured views from bleeding, smoke plumes generated during radiofrequency ablation, and motion blur. These factors impede real-time visualization and contribute to the well-documented learning curve of 30 to 50 cases before achieving procedural proficiency [8-10].

Against this backdrop, the ability to accurately segment surgical instruments within endoscopic video has emerged as a foundational capability for advancing surgical technology. Manually labeling instruments frame by frame is extraordinarily labor-intensive, creating a substantial bottleneck for teams seeking to build the large-scale datasets that modern machine learning methods require [11]. Once achieved, however, such segmentation underpins diverse applications: automated workflow analysis, real-time safety monitoring systems, and the broader development of AI-assisted surgical platforms [12-17].

Efforts to automate this process have evolved considerably. Early approaches relied on classical computer vision techniques, while subsequent deep learning architectures, most notably U-Net and its descendants, achieved meaningful improvements in segmentation accuracy [18,19]. Fully automatic methods for surgical instrument segmentation have demonstrated Dice scores approaching 0.90, though achieving this performance requires construction of large-scale domain-specific annotated training datasets from scratch [20]. The recent introduction of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) represents a departure from this paradigm [21]. By enabling promptable segmentation through simple point clicks or bounding boxes, SAM and its video-capable successor SAM 2 open the door to interactive annotation workflows that could dramatically accelerate dataset creation [22]. Subsequent work on domain-adapted variants, particularly MedSAM, has shown that fine-tuning these foundation models on medical imagery yields substantial performance gains [23]. Recent adaptations for surgical applications, including SurgicalSAM and Surgical-DeSAM, have demonstrated promising results in laparoscopic and robotic surgery environments [24,25].

Although a recent open-source dataset for endoscopic spine instrument segmentation has been introduced [26], existing work has focused exclusively on fully automatic segmentation, and interactive methods that could accelerate dataset creation remain unexplored. We focused on instrument segmentation because reliable instrument localization is a foundational prerequisite for downstream tasks such as anatomical segmentation, target structure identification, and intraoperative safety monitoring. Rather than proposing a novel segmentation architecture, the present study evaluates interactive segmentation workflows for offline annotation in spinal endoscopy by: (1) comparing point-based segmentation performance between general-purpose SAM 2.0/2.1 and domain-adapted MedSAM 2.1; and (2) introducing a click-to-quality (CTQ) framework that quantifies the trade-off between user interactions and segmentation accuracy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Design and Ethics

This retrospective technical validation study was designed to evaluate the performance of interactive point-based segmentation models for surgical instrument identification in spinal endoscopic surgery images. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB approval number: B-2601-1023-101). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified surgical video data, the requirement for individual patient consent was waived by the IRB.

2. Patient Population and Dataset

Endoscopic surgical images were retrospectively collected from 20 patients who underwent endoscopic spinal surgery at our hospital between January 2024 and December 2024 for the main study; an additional 2 patients were recruited for interrater reliability and annotation timing assessments. The cohort comprised 10 patients who underwent UE lumbar decompression or discectomy (interlaminar approach) and 10 patients who underwent BE decompression or discectomy (interlaminar approach). Intraoperative video recordings were captured using an HD endoscopic video system at 720p resolution (1,280×720 pixels) with a frame rate of 30 frames per second and LED illumination, recorded in MPEG-4 format with H.264 codec. Video frames were extracted at every 300 frames (10-second intervals) using OpenCV version 4.5.0 in Python 3.12.

Images were included if they contained at least one clearly identifiable surgical instrument with adequate quality permitting visual identification of instrument boundaries. Frames were excluded if no surgical instruments were visible, if visual obstruction occurred due to motion blur or bleeding, if images were out of focus precluding boundary delineation, or if the operative field was obscured by burring or bone debridement.

A case-level split strategy was employed to prevent data leakage, with all images from a single patient assigned exclusively to one split to avoid temporal correlation bias. The training set comprised 550 images from 10 patients used for MedSAM 2.1 fine-tuning, and the independent test set comprised 308 images from the remaining 10 patients used for final model evaluation. All analyses reported in this study were performed on the independent test set of 308 images.

3. Ground Truth Annotation

Ground truth segmentation masks were created by a spine surgeon with 2 years of clinical experience after fellowship (BSM). The annotator manually delineated instrument boundaries using polygon-based annotation tools. Each image was reviewed to ensure accurate boundary delineation, with particular attention to challenging regions such as instrument tips and areas with specular reflections.

4. Interrater Reliability and Annotation Timing

A separate set of 100 images from 2 patients not included in the training or test sets was independently annotated by the original annotator and a second annotator (SMP, spine surgeon with 12 years of endoscopic spine surgery experience), blinded to each other’s results. Interrater agreement was assessed using pairwise Dice and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; 2-way random effects, absolute agreement, single measures). Using the same subset, both annotators performed manual polygon annotation and MedSAM 2.1 single-click annotation with manual correction, with annotation time recorded per image.

5. Model Specifications

Three segmentation models were compared in this study: SAM 2.0, SAM 2.1, and MedSAM 2.1 (Table 1) [21-23]. SAM 2.0 and SAM 2.1 were evaluated in a zero-shot transfer setting to assess the generalization capability of foundation models pretrained on natural images to the surgical imaging domain. MedSAM 2.1 was derived from SAM 2.1 through decoder-only fine-tuning, freezing the image encoder while training only the mask decoder and prompt encoder. Training was performed using AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1×10-4 and weight decay of 0.01. The loss function combined Dice loss, binary crossentropy, and boundary loss with weights of 1.0, 1.0, and 0.5, respectively. Extensive data augmentation was applied during finetuning, including random 90° rotations, horizontal and vertical flips, brightness and contrast adjustments, CLAHE (contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization), gamma correction, elastic and grid and optical distortions, Gaussian blur, Gaussian noise, and coarse dropout. Training utilized mixed precision (FP16) with cosine annealing learning rate scheduling including a 5-epoch warmup period. Training was conducted for a maximum of 35 epochs, with early stopping implemented (patience of 10 epochs) to prevent overfitting; actual training duration depended on validation performance convergence. The total training time was approximately 20 minutes on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (Intel Core i9-14900K, 64 GB RAM).

6. Single-Click Evaluation Protocol

All models were evaluated using a standardized single-click point prompt protocol. For each test image, a single positive point was placed at the geometric centroid of the ground truth segmentation mask to ensure reproducibility and eliminate interoperator variability in the evaluation. When generating predictions, multimask output was enabled to produce 3 candidate masks, and the mask with the highest predicted Intersection over Union (IoU) score was selected as the final output, consistent with standard SAM inference practice. Images were processed at native resolution without resizing. All models received identical click positions computed algorithmically from the ground truth centroid, ensuring fair comparison across models.

7. CTQ Evaluation Protocol

To evaluate practical efficiency in interactive segmentation workflows, a multiclick simulation study was conducted measuring the number of clicks required to achieve target segmentation quality thresholds. The iterative refinement protocol proceeded as follows: after the initial positive click at the ground truth centroid, the prediction mask was compared to ground truth, and an error map was computed as the symmetric difference between the predicted mask and ground truth. The largest connected component in the error map was identified, and if this region represented a false-negative area (missed by the prediction), a positive click was placed at its center; if it represented a false-positive area (incorrectly included background), a negative click was placed at its center. The prediction was regenerated with accumulated clicks, and this process repeated until one of 3 stopping criteria was met: the target Dice coefficient threshold was achieved (0.80, 0.85, or 0.90), the maximum of 10 clicks was reached, or convergence was detected with Dice improvement less than 0.01 over 2 consecutive clicks.

8. Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses evaluated model performance by (1) surgical type (UE: 5 patients, 147 images; BE: 5 patients, 161 images); (2) instrument size, classified into large vs small via median split of mask area (threshold: 207,183 pixels); and (3) instrument visibility, classified as fully visible (convexity ratio ≥0.8) or partially occluded (convexity ratio<0.8), where convexity ratio=mask area/convex hull area.

9. Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measures included the Dice Similarity Coefficient and Intersection over Union (IoU). The CTQ metrics measured the efficiency of interactive segmentation: CTQ-80 represented the number of clicks required to reach Dice ≥0.80, CTQ-85 the clicks to reach Dice ≥0.85, and CTQ-90 the clicks to reach Dice ≥0.90. Segmentation quality was categorized into 5 levels: excellent (Dice ≥0.90), good (0.85–0.90), acceptable (0.80–0.85), poor (0.70–0.80), and failure (<0.70). Secondary outcome measures included the failure rate defined as the proportion of images with Dice<0.70 at single-click, success rates at clinically relevant thresholds (Dice ≥0.80, ≥0.85, ≥0.90), and inference time measured in milliseconds per image.

10. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12 with SciPy ver. 1.15.0. For primary model comparison analyses, all 3 models were evaluated on the identical 308-image test set, producing paired observations; paired statistical tests were therefore used. For comparisons of continuous metrics (Dice, IoU, inference time) across 3 paired groups, the Friedman test was used as the omnibus test, followed by post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction. For comparison of success rates across 3 groups, Cochran Q test was used as the omnibus test, followed by post hoc pairwise McNemar tests with Bonferroni correction. Odds ratios were calculated for discordant pairs. For subgroup analyses comparing independent image groups (UE vs. BE, large vs small instruments, fully visible vs. partially occluded), Mann-Whitney U-tests were used for continuous outcomes. All hypothesis tests were 2-sided with a significance level of α=0.05, and the Bonferroni-corrected threshold for pairwise comparisons was α/3=0.0167.

RESULTS

1. Single-Click Segmentation Performance

The fine-tuned MedSAM 2.1 achieved a validation Dice coefficient of 0.949 on the held-out training subset prior to final evaluation on the independent test set. A total of 308 test images from 10 patients were evaluated using a standardized single-click point prompt placed at the ground truth mask centroid. MedSAM 2.1 demonstrated significantly superior single-click segmentation performance compared to baseline models (Table 2), with an absolute improvement of 9.3 percentage points over SAM 2.1 (95% CI, 0.929–0.945 vs. 0.819–0.869) and 11.3 percentage points over SAM 2.0 (95% CI, 0.798–0.850), consistent with prior reports of MedSAM’s advantages in medical imaging domains [23]. MedSAM 2.1 exhibited reduced variability, with a standard deviation approximately 3 times lower than baseline models (SD: 0.074 vs. 0.222), indicating more consistent segmentation quality across diverse surgical scenarios. All 3 models maintained real-time inference capabilities with processing times of approximately 110 milliseconds per image.

2. Success Rate and Quality Distribution

The clinical utility of segmentation models depends critically on the proportion of cases achieving acceptable quality thresholds. At the Dice ≥0.85 threshold, MedSAM 2.1 achieved a 90.6% success rate (95% CI, 86.8%–93.4%) compared to 67.9% (95% CI, 62.4%–73.0%) for SAM 2.1 and 66.2% (95% CI, 60.7%– 71.4%) for SAM 2.0 (Cochran Q omnibus, p<0.001; post hoc McNemar test with Bonferroni correction: OR=8.0, MedSAM 2.1 vs. SAM 2.1, p<0.001) (Supplementary Table 1). Complete segmentation failures (Dice<0.70) decreased from 21.8% (95% CI, 17.4%–26.7%) with SAM 2.0 to 1.9% (95% CI, 0.9%–4.2%) with MedSAM 2.1, representing a 91.3% relative reduction. 5. External Validation on the Spine Endoscopic Atlas Dataset

To assess generalizability, all 3 models were evaluated on the publicly available Spine Endoscopic Atlas (SEA) test set (202 images from 10 patients) [26]. MedSAM 2.1 achieved significantly higher Dice (0.918±0.083) than SAM 2.1 (0.724±0.281) and SAM 2.0 (0.708±0.290) on external data (all p<0.001; Supplementary Table 2). The performance gap between datasets was substantially smaller for MedSAM 2.1 (Dice difference 0.019) than for baseline models (0.116–0.120), suggesting robust generalization rather than institutional overfitting.

6. Surgical Type Comparison: UE vs. BE

No statistically significant difference in Dice scores was ob-served between UE (5 patients, 147 images) and BE (5 patients, 161 images) procedures for any model (Mann-Whitney U-test; all p>0.05; Supplementary Table 3). MedSAM 2.1 maintained high performance in both groups (UE: 0.927±0.089; BE: 0.946± 0.054), suggesting that a single domain-adapted model generalizes across endoscopic approaches.

7. Interrater Reliability

The interrater agreement study on 100 images demonstrated excellent agreement between 2 independent annotators, with a mean pairwise Dice of 0.953±0.051 and ICC of 0.991 (95% CI, 0.986–0.994) (Supplementary Table 4).

8. Annotation Timing Study

The MedSAM 2.1-assisted workflow reduced annotation time by 75.8% compared to manual polygon annotation (7.7±13.3 seconds vs. 31.8±18.2 seconds per image; 4.1-fold speedup; Supplementary Table 5). Individual speedup ranged from 2.8-fold to 8.9-fold between annotators (both p<0.001).

9. Subgroup Analysis by Instrument Size and Visibility

MedSAM 2.1 demonstrated significantly higher Dice for large instruments than small instruments (0.953±0.058 vs. 0.920± 0.087, p<0.001), whereas baseline models showed no significant size-dependent difference (Supplementary Table 6). For partially occluded instruments (n=11), baseline models exhibited substantial performance degradation (SAM 2.0: 0.598±0.241; SAM 2.1: 0.662±0.263), whereas MedSAM 2.1 maintained robust performance (0.899±0.091), demonstrating a smaller performance drop (0.048) compared to baseline models (0.219–0.306).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the efficiency of interactive segmentation workflows, rather than developing a novel architecture, for offline surgical instrument annotation in spinal endoscopic surgery, comparing general-purpose SAM 2.0/2.1 with domainadapted MedSAM 2.1. The primary contributions are twofold: (1) systematic evaluation of point-based interactive segmentation workflow efficiency in this domain, and (2) the CTQ framework that quantifies the practical trade-off between user interactions and segmentation accuracy. MedSAM 2.1 demonstrated significantly superior performance compared to SAM models, with substantially higher success rates and markedly reduced complete failures.

Our finding that MedSAM 2.1 outperformed baseline SAM models is consistent with prior studies demonstrating the benefits of domain adaptation for medical image segmentation. Ma et al. [23] reported that MedSAM outperformed the original SAM across diverse medical imaging modalities, with improvements ranging from 5% to 30% depending on the modality. Our improvement falls within this expected range. Shvets et al. [20] reported Dice scores of 0.90 for automatic instrument segmentation in robotic surgery using deep learning approaches, though their fully automatic method required extensive domain-specific training data. Our domain-adapted model achieved comparable performance using point prompts with substantially fewer training images.

The substantial reduction in performance variability and the marked reduction in complete failures are particularly notable for annotation workflows requiring predictable, consistent performance. Recent work on SAM-based surgical instrument segmentation has shown promising results: SurgicalSAM by Yue et al. [24] achieved mean IoU of 89.1% on the EndoVis2018 dataset, and Surgical-DeSAM by Sheng et al. [25] demonstrated 84.9% mean IoU on the EndoVis2018 dataset. However, these studies focused on laparoscopic or robotic surgery environments. More recently, SurgSAM-2 demonstrated real-time surgical video segmentation at 86 FPS through efficient frame pruning [27], and Xu et al. [26] introduced the first open-source endoscopic spine instrument dataset comprising 48,510 images from 119 patients with external validation across 2 centers. While these advances address automatic segmentation, our work is complementary in that it evaluates interactive segmentation efficiency for spinal endoscopy, addressing how to create annotated datasets efficiently rather than how to segment automatically.

Several domain adaptation strategies have been proposed for SAM in medical and surgical contexts. The Medical SAM Adapter introduces lightweight adapter modules for cross-modality adaptation, though requiring large-scale training data [28]. SurgicalSAM learns task-specific class prompts for automatic surgical segmentation [24], while Surgical-DeSAM decouples prompting to generate bounding boxes without ground truth at inference [25]. Our decoder-only fine-tuning approach differs in 3 aspects: (1) it targets interactive annotation efficiency rather than automatic segmentation; (2) it requires substantially fewer training samples (550 images); and (3) the CTQ framework evaluates the interaction-quality trade-off, a dimension not addressed by existing methods.

The divergent multiclick refinement behavior between models deserves nuanced interpretation. While SAM 2.0 achieved the highest 10-click Dice (0.960±0.079), marginally exceeding MedSAM 2.1 (0.947±0.067), this reversal of ranking at 10 clicks requires important context. Achieving SAM 2.0’s 10-click performance requires 10 user interactions per image, whereas MedSAM 2.1 delivers near-comparable accuracy (0.937±0.074) with a single click, representing a 10-fold reduction in user effort. MedSAM 2.1’s domain-specific training appears to produce highly accurate initial predictions; when first-click accuracy is already high, additional clicks may introduce competing signals rather than improvement. For annotation workflows prioritizing throughput, MedSAM 2.1’s single-click approach offers a substantially better efficiency-accuracy trade-off. For workflows where marginal accuracy gains justify higher annotation effort, such as rare or diagnostically critical cases, SAM 2.0 with iterative refinement represents a rational alternative. The CTQ framework precisely quantifies this trade-off and enables evidencebased workflow design decisions.

The low-resource domain adaptation demonstrated here addresses the annotation bottleneck in surgical AI development by accelerating human-supervised annotation rather than replacing it. In this workflow, the model generates an initial mask that the annotator reviews and corrects. Model-only outputs (single-click Dice 0.937) do not match human inter-annotator agreement (pairwise Dice 0.953; ICC=0.991) and are not a substitute for human-reviewed labels.

The CTQ framework offers 3 concrete applications in annotation workflow design. First, for resource planning with annotation budget constraints, CTQ curves allow estimation of expected annotation time under different quality thresholds: at the observed MedSAM 2.1 CTQ-85 of 1.7 clicks per image and a per-image time of 7.7 seconds, a 1,000-image dataset can be annotated to Dice ≥0.85 in approximately 2 hours, enabling prospective resource allocation. Second, for model selection decisions, the CTQ profile allows direct comparison under a fixed interaction budget: at 3 clicks, MedSAM 2.1 achieves Dice 0.945 versus 0.907 for SAM 2.1, providing quantitative guidance for institutions choosing between models given their workflow constraints. Third, for quality triage, cases where no model achieves the CTQ target within 10 clicks can be automatically flagged for manual annotation, enabling an efficient hybrid workflow that prioritizes human effort on difficult cases.

External validation on the SEA dataset demonstrated robust generalizability, with MedSAM 2.1 showing a performance gap of only 0.019 compared to our institutional dataset, substantially smaller than the 0.116–0.120 gap for baseline models. This suggests that decoder-only fine-tuning captures generalizable instrument features rather than overfitting to site-specific characteristics. The consistent performance across UE and BE surgical types and the subgroup analyses further support this interpretation: MedSAM 2.1 demonstrated smaller performance decrements in challenging conditions (small or partially occluded instruments), indicating that domain adaptation confers particular benefit where general-purpose models struggle most.

The interrater reliability study demonstrated excellent agreement (ICC=0.991) between 2 independent annotators, supporting the reliability of single-annotator ground truth for surgical instrument segmentation. The variability in annotation speedup between annotators (2.8- to 8.9-fold) suggests that efficiency gains depend on familiarity with the AI-assisted workflow, though even the less experienced annotator achieved substantial time savings.

All 3 models maintained inference times of approximately 110 milliseconds per image, adequate for interactive annotation workflows. Regarding reproducibility, our fine-tuning was performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090, a widely available consumer-grade GPU rather than a research-only platform. The decoder-only approach requires only approximately 6 GB of VRAM for inference (Table 1), achievable on mid-range GPUs such as the NVIDIA RTX 3070/4070 series. We estimate that equivalent fine-tuning on an RTX 3070 would require approximately 30–60 minutes rather than 20 minutes, a modest increase that should not preclude adoption. Model weights, fine-tuning code, and evaluation scripts are publicly available at our repository to facilitate adoption across diverse research environments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the training and internal test sets comprised only 20 patients from a single institution, all undergoing lumbar procedures. Although external validation on the SEA dataset demonstrated generalizability, both datasets originate from East Asian institutions; multicenter validation across diverse geographic settings, spinal levels, instrument types, and patient populations remains necessary. Second, the centroid-based click protocol and the automated highest-IoU mask selection represent idealized simulations rather than real-world annotator behavior. User studies with actual annotators are warranted to validate the observed efficiency gains. Third, the annotation timing study involved only 2 annotators from the same institution, and the observed variability in speedup (2.8- to 8.9-fold) suggests that efficiency gains may depend on individual user experience with AI-assisted workflows; larger-scale user studies across institutions are warranted. Fourth, performance was evaluated only on spinal endoscopy; generalization to other surgical domains requires separate validation. Fifth, the subgroup analysis by instrument visibility was limited by the small number of partially occluded cases (n=11), insufficient to draw definitive conclusions; future studies with larger occluded subsets are needed. Sixth, this study evaluated offline annotation efficiency rather than intraoperative deployment; real-time intraoperative use would require additional technical integration with surgical video systems, sterile-compatible user interfaces, and regulatory pathway navigation, representing a distinct and substantially more complex validation task. Finally, while SAM 2’s video propagation capabilities could enable annotation of video sequences by requiring interaction only on keyframes [22], this functionality was not evaluated in the current study.

CONCLUSION

Domain-adapted MedSAM 2.1 achieved significantly superior single-click segmentation performance (Dice 0.937, success rate 90.6%) compared to SAM 2.0/2.1 in an offline annotation setting, with near-optimal first-click predictions that showed nonmonotonic progression with additional refinement and robust generalizability on external validation. The 75.8% reduction in annotation time under human-supervised interactive workflows provides a practical pathway for individual institutions to build locally optimized annotated datasets for downstream surgical artificial intelligence development.
