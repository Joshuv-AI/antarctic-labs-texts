Reply to: Comment on “Combination of immunotherapy-based systemic therapy and radiotherapy achieves durable response and favorable survival in recurrent head and neck cancer”
Authors: Jingbo Wang, Gulidanna Shayan, Junlin Yi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We sincerely thank Dr Topkan et al. 1 for their careful reading of our article and for raising the methodological comments regarding the definition of survival endpoints. We have reviewed the concerns thoroughly and provide the following response.

Overall survival (OS) is the widely accepted gold standard endpoint in cancer studies. According to the NCI dictionary, OS is defined as the length of time from either the date of diagnosis or the initiation of treatment for a given disease. 2 As proposed by the commentators, the starting point for survival time estimation is conventionally the randomization or initiation of treatment in clinical studies. Nevertheless, it should be noted that this definition is consensus-based and more adapted to prospective studies with predefined intervals between the diagnosis, screening, randomization, and treatment commencement. In retrospective real-world data analysis, there may be considerable variation in terms of these intervals, introducing more marked biases into the estimation of survival. In our study cohort, most patients were diagnosed with disease recurrence during the COVID-19 pandemic, and many could not receive timely treatment following the confirmation of recurrence, thereby introducing lead time in certain patients. Notably, this interval is not merely a logistical gap but also a reflection of the inherent biological behavior of the recurrent tumor. Therefore, calculating OS from the recurrence diagnosis is methodologically coherent and biologically holistic, as it integrates both the natural history of disease and the therapeutic efficacy of treatment.

Apropos of the endpoints for assessing therapeutic efficacy of a specific approach in an observational study, we agree that the use of treatment initiation as a starting point is better aligned with conventional reporting standards and more preferable for comparison and integration across studies. We appreciate this constructive suggestion and therefore conducted an additional set of survival analyses with survival indices measured from the start of treatment. This re-analysis was undertaken specifically to examine whether the interval between recurrence diagnosis and treatment initiation might have artificially exaggerated the survival outcomes in this cohort of recurrent HNSCC receiving immunotherapy-based systemic therapy and locoregional radiotherapy.

Unsurprisingly, the supplementary analyses yielded a scenario that was basically consistent with our previous recurrence diagnosis-anchored analyses. Using treatment initiation as the time origin, the median progression-free survival (PFS) and locoregional progression-free survival (LRPFS) were 16.4 and 19.4 months, while the median OS and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) were still not reached. The 12-month OS, PFS, LRPFS, and DMFS were 92.3%, 62.2%, 68.9%, and 92.3%, respectively. The 18-month OS, PFS, LRPFS, and DMFS were 83.9%, 44.4%, 51.7%, and 92.3%, respectively, remaining clinically promising and favorable. For the sake of clarity, we presented both sets of survival data under the two analytical frameworks in Table 1. The consistently advantageous trajectories of both sets of survival assessment indicate that the encouraging outcomes observed in our study are not artifacts of the time origin selection for endpoint definition.

Taken together, these additional analyses support the robustness of our core findings. In this selected cohort of patients with locoregionally recurrent, radiation-naïve HNSCC, first-line immunotherapy-based systemic therapy combined with locoregional radiotherapy achieves promising OS and tumor control with durable response.
