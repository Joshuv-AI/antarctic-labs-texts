Prevalence and risk factors of hepatitis B and C among prison inmates in Nigeria: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Victor Abiola Adepoju, Abdulrakib Abdulrahim, Donald Chinazor Udah, Safayet Jamil, Bukola Olanrewaju Olaniyi, Bashar Haruna Gulumbe, Yusuff Adebayo Adebisi, Qorinah Estiningtyas Sakilah Adnani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B (HBV) and hepatitis C (HCV) infections are significant global public health challenges. Correctional facilities face amplified and disproportionate risks due to overcrowding, limited healthcare access, and high-risk behaviours. There is a lack of consolidated prevalence and risk factor data on viral hepatitis in Nigerian correctional settings. This study synthesizes evidence on the prevalence of HBV and HCV, risk factors, and regional disparities within Nigerian correctional facilities.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 cross-sectional studies (2009–2024). Databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Dimensions and Google Scholar) were searched. A random-effects model was used to calculate pooled prevalence estimates. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to explore heterogeneity and regional variations. Studies reporting prevalence data using validated diagnostic methods (rapid tests and/or ELISA) were analysed. We extracted information on study characteristics, geographic regions, inmate demographics, testing modalities, and reported risk factors. Risk factors were evaluated using odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsPooled HBV prevalence in Nigerian correctional facilities was 14% (95% CI: 10–19%; n = 2,586) and HCV 10% (95% CI: 4–19%; n = 861). Northern Nigeria reported higher HBV prevalence (19% vs. 6% in the South), while HCV was marginally higher in the South compared to the North (14% vs. 8%). Illicit drug use (HBV: OR = 8.96; HCV: OR = 9.26), blood oath-taking (HBV: OR = 6.57; HCV: OR = 4.57), and marital status (married inmates, OR = 4.37) were significant risk factors. Sharing sharp objects increased HCV risk (OR = 2.14). Other variables, such as blood transfusion, intravenous drug use, condom use, and duration of incarceration, did not show statistically significant associations, likely due to heterogeneity across studies.ConclusionsThis study highlights a high prevalence of HBV and HCV among inmates in Nigerian correctional facilities, primarily influenced by structural and behavioural determinants. These findings point to the urgent need for tailored, comprehensive interventions, such as routine screening, targeted HBV catch-up vaccination, and harm reduction initiatives within the prison system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25614-8.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections are significant global public health challenges. They collectively affect hundreds of millions of individuals worldwide. HBV alone accounts for an estimated 296 million chronic infections, while HCV affects around 50 million people globally, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality, mainly through complications like cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. These viruses, primarily transmitted via blood and other body fluids, disproportionately affect vulnerable populations, including those with limited access to healthcare services [2]. Despite the availability of HBV vaccines and highly effective direct-acting antiviral therapies for HCV, significant gaps in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment persist, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

Prisons are environments where the risk of HBV and HCV transmission is significantly amplified. Overcrowding, poor sanitation, inadequate healthcare, intravenous drug use, and risky behaviours such as unprotected sexual activity and tattooing create a fertile ground for the spread of bloodborne infections [3, 4]. These settings often lack comprehensive screening, vaccination, or treatment services, exacerbating the public health seroprevalence burden [5]. Prison inmates, representing a highly marginalized group, frequently originate from backgrounds of socioeconomic deprivation and are at an elevated risk of acquiring and transmitting HBV and HCV during incarceration and upon reintegration into the community [6].

Several studies have documented the prevalence of non-communicable and communicable diseases, including viral hepatitis B and C, in Nigerian correctional facilities [4, 7–10]. In Nigeria, HBV and HCV represent a dual epidemic, with HBV classified as hyper-endemic and HCV becoming an increasing concern [11]. According to the 2018 Nigeria HIV/AIDS Indicator and Impact Survey, 8.1% of adults in Nigeria have HBV, and 1.1% have HCV [12]. Estimates suggest that more than 20 million Nigerians have HBV and/or HCV [13]. The prevalence of HBV and HCV is higher among specific key populations, such as those in correctional facilities, due to factors such as poverty, overcrowding, inadequate testing, and infection control and prevention protocols [14, 15]. Despite the pressing need, prison-based healthcare systems in Nigeria remain under-resourced, with limited routine HBV vaccination programs or systematic HCV screening initiatives [4]. These gaps in healthcare provision highlight the critical need for targeted interventions within correctional facilities, not only to address the high disease seroprevalence among inmates but also to prevent community-level transmission upon their release.

While there are many studies on the prevalence of HBV and HCV among Nigerian prison populations, no study has systematically synthesized these findings [10, 14, 15]. Reported prevalence rates vary widely, ranging from 4% to 29.6% for HBV and from 3.5% to 12.3% for HCV [15–18]. Reports also show significant regional and demographic variability [15–17]. Identified risk factors for the transmission of viral hepatitis in correctional settings include illicit drug use, unprotected sexual practices, sharing of sharp objects, and extended periods of incarceration [14, 19]. However, the absence of consolidated data limits our understanding of the true prevalence and the associated risk factors at a national level and this lack of evidence impedes the development of evidence-based policies and interventions.

To address critical evidence gaps in the epidemiology of viral hepatitis in Nigerian prison settings, this review systematically synthesizes data on prevalence, geographic distribution, diagnostic approaches, and key risk factors, with the aim of informing public health action and guiding policy engagement. The review follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and aligns with the Nigerian national hepatitis framework.

Methods

The study followed rigorous procedures consistent with PRISMA 2020 guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility [20]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID CRD42025634935). The review spanned from 15 January 2025 to 15 March 2025 and involved the analysis of 13 cross-sectional studies. These studies were selected, analyzed, and synthesized using meta-analytical techniques and sensitivity analyses.

Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review was designed to assess the prevalence of HBV and HCV among Nigerian prison inmates with a focus on inclusivity across all ages, genders, and regions. The inclusion criteria prioritized studies that distinctly reported HBV and/or HCV prevalence among inmates, determined using validated diagnostic methods such as rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and/or Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA).

To be eligible, studies must be primary research, such as cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, or randomized controlled trials that report extractable prevalence data for HBV and/or HCV, with HBV infection status defined as HBsAg-reactive (irrespective of platform) and HCV infection status defined as anti-HCV reactive. Studies should also, if available, report risk factors associated with HBV and HCV as secondary data. Only studies conducted in Nigeria and published in English between 2009 and 2024 were included to ensure alignment with the review’s geographical, linguistic, and temporal focus, and to reflect contemporary developments in the epidemiology of HBV and HCV among inmates in Nigeria. The 2009–2024 timeline was selected to reflect a turning point in prison health governance in Nigeria. From 2009 onwards, several reforms and programmatic shifts, such as improved health information systems and increased donor interest in prison health, led to more reliable and accessible data. Earlier studies, before 2009, were sparse, inconsistent in diagnostic methods, and largely inaccessible in standard databases. This window also allows the inclusion of studies using modern diagnostics such as ELISA and HCV RNA confirmation, which align with World Health Organization (WHO)-preferred standards.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Four electronic databases, namely PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Dimensions, as well as Google Scholar, were searched on 17 January 2025 by two authors (VAA and SJ), as detailed in Table S1. Comprehensive search terms were used to identify relevant studies, including keywords related to Hepatitis B and C, prison populations, and Nigeria. An example of the search string used in PubMed includes: “(Hepatitis B OR HBsAg) AND (Hepatitis C OR Anti-HCV) AND (Prison OR Correctional facilities OR inmates) AND (Nigeria).” The search resulted in 56 unique records after duplicate removal using Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai/) [21]. The detailed screening process is outlined in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). Reference-list (backwards) snowballing of all eligible full-text articles was undertaken, but no additional studies met the inclusion criteria. Google Scholar retrievals were screened for theses, conference proceedings, and non-governmental organization reports; however, none satisfied the methodological criteria for inclusion (e.g., validated diagnostics, extractable prevalence data).Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart of the study selection process

Study Selection

Titles and abstracts of the identified studies were independently screened by four authors (AA, BHG, BOO, SJ) to determine relevance. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and evaluated against predefined inclusion criteria by the same authors. Any disagreements were resolved by one author (VAA). Out of the 79 records initially identified, 23 were excluded as duplicates. After screening, 30 full-text articles were assessed, and 13 studies were included in the final analysis. Per PRISMA 2020 guidelines, the reasons for exclusions at each stage of the screening process were documented. The detailed summary of studies included and excluded, and reasons for exclusion, is provided in a data extraction table appended in Table S2.

Data Extraction

Data were meticulously extracted using a standardized extraction form to ensure uniformity and completeness. Exposure variables include Individual- or study-level risk factors reported by the authors (e.g., illicit drug use, blood oath ritual, marital status, tattooing, sharing sharp objects, duration of incarceration). Outcome variables include HBV or HCV infection, defined as a positive HBsAg or anti-HCV result by RDT or ELISA. We collected information on study characteristics, which include author name, year of publication, and geographic location; region/geopolitical zone, sample size and characteristics of the prison inmates; testing modalities used for HBV and HCV detection (e.g., rapid tests, ELISA); prevalence rates; and key findings on risk factors. The data were independently extracted by three authors (VAA, DCU, and BOO), while two authors (AA and SJ) independently verified all extracted data to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Risk-of-Bias Assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed by two authors (BHG and SJ) using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for cross-sectional studies [22]. Each study was evaluated for factors such as sampling strategy, diagnostic accuracy (outcome misclassification), and the completeness of reporting. Studies were categorized as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias (Table S3).

Data Synthesis and Meta-Analysis

The data were synthesized using an online meta-analysis software available here: metaanalysisonline.com. Given the observed heterogeneity among studies (I² statistic > 50%), we employed a random-effects model to calculate pooled prevalence rates for HBV and HCV. Forest plots were generated to visualize the pooled estimates. Subgroup analyses were conducted to stratify prevalence rates by geographical region (North vs. South Nigeria) and test modality (rapid test vs. rapid test combined with ELISA). Sensitivity analyses were also performed to test the robustness of the findings. These involved excluding the smallest study (sample size of 50) and removing studies with the highest and lowest prevalence rates to assess their impact on the pooled estimates. Finally, in a post-hoc analysis requested during peer review, we repeated the meta-analysis after force-including the four studies excluded at full-text screening. Pooled prevalence and heterogeneity statistics were compared with the primary estimates (Table S4). Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and quantitatively using Egger’s regression test (two-tailed α = 0.05) for both HBV and HCV prevalence meta-analyses.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the included studies. A total of 13 studies were included in the systematic review, and all reported a cross-sectional design (See Fig. 1). The studies span 15 years and were published between 2009 and 2024. A total sample of 2,586 and 861 was included for the HBV and HCV assessment, respectively. Sample sizes ranged from 50 [23] to 344 inmates [24]. Twelve studies reported HBV prevalence (4.7%–29.2%), while five assessed HCV prevalence (3.5%–29.6%). Testing methods for viral hepatitis B and C vary. Six studies used RDT only [17–19, 23, 25, 26]. While another six studies combined RDT with ELISA for testing [14, 16, 24, 27–29]. While one study did not report the testing method [15]. The studies spanned multiple Nigerian states from the Northern region (Borno, Sokoto, Taraba, Nasarawa, and the Federal Capital Territory) and the Southern states (Cross River, Ondo, and Rivers). Of the 13 studies, 8 were from Northern Nigeria [14, 16–18, 23, 24, 28, 29]. While the remaining 5 studies were from Southern Nigeria [15, 19, 25–27]. Tables 2 and 3 show a meta-analysis of risk factors for HBV and HCV and reveal distinct patterns of association. Illicit drug use was found to be the strongest predictor and significantly associated with both HBV (OR: 8.96, 95% CI: 3.08–26.07, P < 0.001) and HCV (OR: 9.26, 95% CI: 1.10–78.00, P = 0.01). Marital status (being married) was also significantly associated with increased risks for both HBV (OR: 4.2, 95% CI: 1.70–10.40, P < 0.01) and HCV (OR: 4.79, 95% CI: 1.17–19.64, P < 0.01). Blood oath-taking showed a strong association with HBV (OR: 6.57, 95% CI: 1.80–24.00, P = 0.01) and HCV (OR: 4.57, 95% CI: 1.10–19.00, P = 0.01). Sharing sharp objects significantly increased the risk of HCV (OR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.04–4.39, P = 0.01), while the combined estimate across HBV and HCV was also significant (OR: 1.91, 95% CI: 1.15–3.16, P = 0.01). Other factors, such as blood transfusion, condom use, intravenous drug use, duration of incarceration, and tattoos, did not show statistically significant associations. Discussion

This systematic review aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of HBV and HCV infections and associated risk factors among inmates in Nigerian correctional facilities. We synthesized data from 13 articles (12 studies reported data on HBV and five reported data on HCV). We found a pooled HBV prevalence of 14% (95% CI 10–19) and HCV prevalence of 10% (95% CI 4–19) among Nigerian inmates—figures that exceed both the national estimates for the general population (HBV ≈ 9.5%; HCV ≈ 1%) and the pooled prevalence reported in recent multi-country prisoner meta-analyses from Brazil, India and Iran (HBV ≈ 10–19%; HCV ≈ 8–18%) [30–32]. The excess burden confirms Nigerian prisons as important, yet currently under-served nodes in the national hepatitis elimination agenda. The findings underscore the disproportionately high prevalence of HBV and HCV in Nigerian correctional facilities and regional disparities driven by differing vaccination coverage, cultural practices, and conditions of correctional facilities. The high figures in the current estimate underline an urgent public health concern within Nigerian correctional facilities and highlight the correctional facility context as a reservoir of viral hepatitis that could amplify community transmission upon inmate release.

Subgroup meta-analysis demonstrated a marked north-south gradient. HBV prevalence was 19% in northern prisons versus 6% in the south. For HCV the pattern reversed slightly (south 14% vs. north 8%). These differences likely reflect uneven vaccine roll-out, cultural practices (e.g., blood-oath rituals) and variable harm-reduction coverage. This emphaises the need for region-specific prison-health packages. The higher HBV prevalence in the North may be attributable to fewer opportunities for vaccination, more pronounced risk-taking behaviours as well as poorer infection control and sociocultural practices. Blood oath-taking, a ritual that facilitates direct blood mixing, was identified in this review as one of the common risk behaviours [19]. These regional discrepancies suggest that interventions must be tailored to the local epidemiological context and support the need for expanding immunization efforts beyond infancy to include high-risk adults in correctional settings.

Pooled effect estimates revealed the hierarchy of risk factors. Illicit drug use (OR 9.02) and marital status (OR 4.37) remained significant for both HBV and HCV. Blood oath-taking was significant for HBV (OR 6.57) and borderline for HCV (OR 4.57; 95% CI 1.10–19.0). Sharing sharp objects was significant only for HCV (OR 2.14). Other hypothesised factors (duration of incarceration, intravenous drug use, tattoos) were not significant after pooling, mirroring findings in global meta-analyses of data from correctional services. These findings align with global literature, which depicts the correctional setting as a hotspot for HCV, primarily fuelled by the sharing of needles among people who inject drugs [2, 6]. Marital status might stand in as a surrogate for unprotected sexual activity or multiple sexual partners, as indicated by the recurrent associations between marriage, sexual concurrency, and sexually transmitted infections in specific cultural contexts [10]. Meanwhile, blood oath-taking, a ritual found in many Nigerian cultural practices, involves the direct mingling of blood from multiple individuals without sterilization and is consistently associated with an increased risk of viral hepatitis. Furthermore, illicit drug use, not strictly defined by intravenous injection but also including non-injectable forms that involve shared paraphernalia or group drug use sessions, was found to be the strongest predictor for HBV infection among inmates in Nigeria. This suggests that the broader spectrum of illicit drug practices may better capture the risky behaviours occurring within correctional settings. The finding could also mean that in many correctional facilities in Nigeria, less official forms of non-injecting drug use like sniffing or smoking and other associated risk behaviours could potentiate the spread of viral hepatitis. In contrast, the association between conventional intravenous drug use and viral hepatitis was not statistically significant. This contrasts with a report from an Egyptian correctional facility which found that history of intravenous drug use and a period of > 10 years spent in prison were the most significant predictors of HBV and HCV infections [33]. This might reflect underreporting or underestimation of injection practices among inmates or differences in policing and drug law practices and enforcement across states in Nigeria. Other variables, such as blood transfusion, condom use, tattoos, and duration of incarceration, did not show statistically significant associations with HBV and HCV. The finding regarding the duration of incarceration contrasts with global data that show repeated and prolonged incarceration as a significant risk factor for HCV [34, 35]. The nonsignificant associations observed might be due to study heterogeneity, potential underreporting, or variability in study methodologies.

Study limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the included studies were all cross-sectional, limiting causal inference. Second, variations in diagnostic approaches (RDT vs. ELISA vs. confirmatory RNA) may have introduced measurement bias. Third, underreporting and weak health records in prison settings may affect prevalence accuracy. Fourth, we excluded non-English publications, which may have omitted relevant grey literature. Finally, heterogeneity in study designs and reporting prevented meta-analysis of some risk factors.

Study implications

The evidence presented carries substantial implications for policy and programming in Nigerian correctional setting. The markedly high prevalence of HBV and HCV among inmates calls for universal screening at entry, coupled with rapid linkage to care and treatment. Currently, routine hepatitis screening is rare in these settings (only opt out), leaving many inmates unaware of their infection status, a situation that undermines efforts to meet the WHO’s 2030 elimination targets [6]. Moreover, This systematic review suggests the lack of data on HBV vaccination coverage among inmates in Nigeria unlike Douala New Bell Prison, Cameroon, which suggested that only 4.3% of inmates had received the HBV vaccine [36]. This shows a critical gap that could be addressed through mandatory HBV vaccination strategy upon admission into the Nigerian correctional facility. Such measures, when combined with targeted health education that addresses culturally specific practices like blood oath-taking with emphasis on the importance of safe practices in drug use and personal hygiene, would likely curb transmission of viral hepatitis B and C in Nigerian correctional facilities. In addition, while some risk factors did not show significance in our quantitative synthesis, their potential role should not be dismissed. The observed high heterogeneity in measures such as duration of incarceration suggests that future studies should employ more standardized data collection methods to capture these nuances. Prospective cohort designs that follow inmates before, during, and after incarceration could elucidate the timing and context of new infections and ultimately guide more effective intervention strategies. The new sensitivity analysis that reintroduced methodologically weaker studies did not change pooled estimates, reinforcing the robustness of our findings.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates a substantial seroprevalence of HBV (14%) and HCV (10%) among Nigerian prison inmates. These are largely driven by a confluence of structural and behavioural factors such as illicit drug use, blood oath-taking, and marital status. These prevalence estimates for HBV and HCV in Nigerian correctional facilities exceed estimates in the general population and reveal significant regional disparities in viral hepatitis infection within Nigerian correctional settings. The findings call for tailored, region-specific interventions. Expanding screening, HBV vaccination, and harm-reduction programs in correctional settings is imperative to stem the tide of viral hepatitis. Also, prison health must be integrated into the broader national public health strategy. These focused efforts can fast-track Nigeria on the path toward the WHO’s 2030 elimination targets and yield enduring benefits for both incarcerated individuals and the wider Nigerian community.
