Near-Optimal Regret for Efficient Stochastic Combinatorial Semi-Bandits
Zichun Ye, Runqi Wang, Xutong Liu, Shuai Li

Abstract
The combinatorial multi-armed bandit (CMAB) is a cornerstone of sequential decision-making framework, dominated by two algorithmic families: UCB-based and adversarial methods such as follow the regularized leader (FTRL) and online mirror descent (OMD). However, prominent UCB-based approaches like CUCB suffer from additional regret factor log ⁡ T \log T that is detrimental over long horizons, while adversarial methods such as EXP3.M and HYBRID impose significant computational overhead. To resolve this trade-off, we introduce the Combinatorial Minimax Optimal Strategy in the Stochastic setting (CMOSS). CMOSS is a computationally efficient algorithm that achieves an instance-independent regret of O ⁡ ( ( log ⁡ k ) 2 ​ k ​ m ​ T ) O\big((\log k)^{2}\sqrt{kmT}\big) under semi-bandit feedback, where m m is the number of arms and k k is the maximum cardinality of a feasible action. Crucially, this result eliminates the dependency on log ⁡ T \log T and matches the established Ω ⁡ ( k ​ m ​ T ) \Omega\big(\sqrt{kmT}\big) lower bound up to O ⁡ ( ( log ⁡ k ) 2 ) O\big((\log k)^{2}\big) . We then extend our analysis to show that CMOSS is also applicable to cascading feedback. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets validate that CMOSS consistently outperforms benchmark algorithms in both regret and runtime efficiency.



1
Introduction

The stochastic multi-armed bandit (MAB) is a fundamental
T
-round sequential decision-making game that has been widely studied in the past two decades
(
Audibert and Bubeck 2009
;
Auer et al. 1995
;
Auer et al. 2002
;
Robbins 1952
)
.
In this game, one player faces
m
arms. In each round
t\in[T]
, the player chooses an arm
A_{t}
among the
m
arms and gets a reward drawn from a predetermined but unknown reward distribution with mean
\mu_{A_{t}}\in[0,1]
. The arm with the largest reward mean
\mu_{*}
is called the best arm. The total expected regret is defined as
\mathbf{E}\left[R(T)\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mu_{*}-\mu_{A_{t}}\right)\right],
where the expectation is over the randomness from the player’s strategy and the random reward outcome. And the aim is to minimize the minimax regret, namely the regret of the best algorithm against the worst input.

The Upper Confidence Bounds (UCB) algorithm
(
Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer 2002
)
is a well-known approach for addressing the stochastic MAB problem, achieving an expected regret of
O\left(\sqrt{mT\log T}\right)
. Building on this foundation, its variant, the Minimax Optimal Strategy in the Stochastic case (MOSS) algorithm
(
Audibert and Bubeck 2009
;
Degenne and Perchet 2016
;
Ménard and Garivier 2017
)
, improves the regret bound to
O\left(\sqrt{mT}\right)
by refining the upper confidence bounds and employing a distinct regret analysis. These results align with the minimax lower bound of
\Omega\left(\sqrt{mT}\right)
established in
(
Auer et al. 2002
)
, demonstrating their optimality in the stochastic setting.
In parallel, the EXP3 algorithm
(
Auer et al. 1995
;
Littlestone and Warmuth 1994
)
provides a solution for adversarial environments. EXP3 is a classic implementation of the follow the regularized leader (FTRL) framework using a negative entropy regularizer
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2012
)
.
This approach, also known through the dual perspective of online mirror descent (OMD), achieves a regret of
O\left(\sqrt{mT\log m}\right)
in both adversarial and stochastic regimes, notably avoiding dependence on
\log T
.

In many real-world applications, however, decision-making problems often extend beyond the simple MAB framework to involve
combinatorial actions
among multiple arms. For instance, in online advertising
(
Kveton et al. 2015a
)
, an advertiser could select up to
k
web pages to display ads, constrained by a budget. Each user visits a subset of pages, and the advertiser must learn the unknown click-through probabilities to maximize clicks. Similarly, in viral marketing
(
Gai, Krishnamachari, and Jain 2012
;
Tang et al. 2017
)
, a marketer selects seed nodes in a social network to initiate information cascades, learning influence probabilities to maximize the reach of campaigns.
These scenarios highlight the need for algorithms that efficiently address the combiantorial MAB (CMAB) problem.

While a number of works
(
Combes et al. 2015
;
Kveton et al. 2015c
;
Chen et al. 2016
;
Wang and Chen 2017
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
have focused on the CMAB problem, existing algorithms either suffer from additional
\log T
terms in their regret bounds or incur high computational costs in practice. Among them, the Combinatorial UCB (CUCB) algorithm
(
Chen, Wang, and Yuan 2013
;
Kveton et al. 2015c
;
Wang and Chen 2017
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
is one of the most prominent and widely adopted approaches. The CUCB algorithm extends the classic UCB framework by selecting the action (super arm) from a feasible set that solves a combinatorial optimization problem defined over the upper confidence bounds of the base arms. Kveton et al.
(
Kveton et al. 2015c
)
establish an upper regret bound of
O\left(\sqrt{kmT\log T}\right)
for CUCB, which matches the lower bound of
\Omega\left(\sqrt{kmT}\right)
only up to a
\sqrt{\log T}
factor.

A separate line of work
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2012
;
Combes et al. 2015
;
Uchiya, Nakamura, and Kudo 2010
;
Zimmert, Luo, and Wei 2019
)
focuses on adapting algorithms derived from FTRL and OMD frameworks that could be applied to CMAB setting. The best-known result in this line
(
Zimmert, Luo, and Wei 2019
)
achieves a regret of
O\left(\sqrt{kmT}\right)
when
k\leq\frac{m}{2}
and
O\left((m-k)\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{m}{m-k}\right)T}\right)
when
k>\frac{m}{2}
, successfully removing the
\log T
term. However, these adversarially designed algorithms are often computationally expensive,
limiting their practical utility. This gap—between computational efficiency and regret optimality—has remained unresolved for over a decade, highlighting the critical need for “MOSS-like” algorithms in CMAB that achieve minimax-optimal regret without
\log T
dependence.

The
\log T
factor of many UCB-style algorithms is due to ensuring that the empirical mean of every arm must lie within its confidence interval. To achieve optimal instance-independent regret bound, the MOSS algorithm bypasses this requirement. MOSS operates by classifying arms and then directly bounding the expected plays of suboptimal arms with relatively large gaps, using modified confidence intervals to facilitate this analysis. However, this is not directly tractable in the CMAB setting. First, how to classify the suboptimal actions based on their gaps; second, how to bound the expected number of times each suboptimal action is pulled; and finally, how to tractably aggregate the regret contributions from a potentially exponential number of such actions.

Our technical contribution lies in bridging the gap between the MOSS-style analysis and the combinatorial structure of CMAB, which prior work such as CUCB does not address. To this end, we introduce new analytical tools that enable regret-optimal and computationally efficient learning. Our key innovations include:

1.

Targeted action filtering.
We identify suboptimal actions with gaps exceeding the worst-case underestimation error of the optimal action. Actions with gaps smaller than a chosen threshold
\Delta
contribute at most
T\Delta
to the regret, allowing us to focus on a tractable subset of actions.

2.

Constraint decoupling via classification.
In combinatorial settings, each action involves multiple arms, which complicates concentration analysis. To address this, we introduce a partitioning scheme that classifies suboptimal actions based on structural properties. This enables us to decouple constraints at the arm level and apply refined concentration bounds.

3.

Regret decomposition.
After the preparation of the first two points, we need to choose a proper way to calculate the regret to avoid redundant computations. Since each suboptimal arm may participate in an exponential number of actions, directly summing over all possibilities is infeasible. We instead decompose the total regret into per-arm contributions and use an integral-based approximation to tightly and efficiently estimate the overall impact. See Section
3.2
for more detailed analysis.

Furthermore, we show that CMOSS could also be extended to cascading feedback. More details are given in Section
3.3
. We also run comparative experiments evaluating the performance of CUCB and CMOSS in Appendix
C.4
.

We also conduct comparative experiments involving CMOSS, CUCB, EXP3.M, and HYBRID on both synthetic and real-world datasets under semi-bandit feedback, including ablation studies. Detailed descriptions of CUCB, EXP3.M, and HYBRID are provided in Appendix
B
for completeness. The results demonstrate that, under noisy conditions and after sufficient exploration rounds, CMOSS incurs slightly higher computational complexity than CUCB while achieving notably lower cumulative regret. Compared to EXP3.M and HYBRID, CMOSS attains better cumulative regret with significantly reduced computational complexity. This performance profile highlights CMOSS’s superior balance between exploration and exploitation, underscoring its effectiveness in such environments. Further details are provided in Section
4
.

Related work

Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB).
The MAB problem was first introduced in
(
Robbins 1952
)
. Audibert et al.
(
Audibert and Bubeck 2009
)
later established an optimal regret bound of
\Theta(\sqrt{mT})
for both stochastic and adversarial settings. Our work focuses on the stochastic setting, where the UCB algorithm
(
Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer 2002
)
plays a pivotal role and serves as the foundation for many CMAB studies. To eliminate the
\log T
factor, the MOSS algorithm was proposed in
(
Audibert and Bubeck 2009
)
, with an anytime modification introduced in
(
Degenne and Perchet 2016
)
, achieving a regret of
O\left(\sqrt{mT}\right)
. In contrast, works such as
(
Auer et al. 1995
;
Littlestone and Warmuth 1994
)
targeted the adversarial setting and proposed the EXP3 algorithm, which also eliminates the
\log T
factor and achieves a regret of
O\left(\sqrt{mT\log m}\right)
. However, EXP3 incurs significantly higher computational overhead compared to UCB-based methods such as MOSS. Our work builds primarily on MOSS and extends it to the combinatorial setting.

Stochastic Combinatorial Multi-Armed Bandits (CMAB).
The stochastic CMAB problem has been extensively studied in the literature
(
Chen et al. 2016
;
Chen, Wang, and Yuan 2013
;
Combes et al. 2015
;
Kveton et al. 2015a
;
Kveton et al. 2015c
;
Wang and Chen 2017
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. The seminal work by Gai et al.
(
Gai, Krishnamachari, and Jain 2012
)
pioneered the study of stochastic CMAB with semi-bandit feedback, which inspired algorithmic advancements and improved regret bound across various settings
(
Kveton et al. 2015a
;
Kveton et al. 2014
;
Tang et al. 2017
)
. For the instance-dependent regret, Wang et al.
(
Wang and Chen 2018
)
proposed the combinatorial Thompson sampling (CTS) algorithm with an offline oracle, achieving a regret of
O\left(\frac{m\log T}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)
, where
\Delta_{min}
is the minimum gap between the expected reward of the optimal action and any suboptimal action. The bound essentially matches asymptotically on
T
.

For the instance-independent regret, one line of works focused on the adversarial setting and designed algorithms based on the principle of OMD and FTRL. Several studies
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2012
;
Dani, Kakade, and Hayes 2007
;
Uchiya, Nakamura, and Kudo 2010
;
Zimmert, Luo, and Wei 2019
)
proposed enhancements such as COMBEXP, EXP3.M, with the best
(
Zimmert, Luo, and Wei 2019
)
achieving a regret of
O\left(\sqrt{kmT}\right)
when
k\leq\frac{m}{2}
and
O\left((m-k)\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{m}{m-k}\right)T}\right)
when
k>\frac{m}{2}
. However, these methods suffer from high computational overhead. Another line of works
(
Chen, Wang, and Yuan 2013
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Wang and Chen 2017
;
Wang and Chen 2018
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
used UCB-based methods. Chen et al.
(
Chen, Wang, and Yuan 2013
)
proposed the CUCB algorithm and analyzed the regret of CUCB with an approximation oracle. Then Kveton et al.
(
Kveton et al. 2015c
)
derived an upper bound of
O\left(\sqrt{kmT\log T}\right)
for CUCB and established a lower bound of
\Omega\left(\sqrt{kmT}\right)
. To address the degradation caused by the
\log T
term over long horizons, we refine the upper confidence bound and propose the CMOSS algorithm. It achieves strong performance while maintaining low computational costs, even after removing the
\log T
term.

Preliminaries

We begin by introducing the general framework of the stochastic CMAB and establishing notations used throughout the paper. The CMAB problem is modeled as a sequential decision-making game between a learning agent and an environment. The environment consists of
m
independent base arms
\mathtt{arm}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{arm}_{m}
, each associated with a random reward
X_{i}\sim D_{i}
supported on
[0,1]
and having mean
\mu_{i}
. The distributions
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are fixed by the environment before the game begins and are drawn from a known class
\mathcal{D}
. The learner knows
\mathcal{D}
but not the specific instantiation of each
D_{i}
.

The action space
\mathcal{A}
consists of all subsets of
[m]
such that
\forall A\in\mathcal{A}
,
\left|A\right|\leq k
. The
T
-round decision game starts as follows: in each round
t\in[T]
, the player selects an action
A_{t}
from
\mathcal{A}
based on the feedback history, and the environment draws from every distribution
D_{i}
a sample
X^{(t)}_{i}
. Let
\bm{\mu}
represents the vector of the mean rewards of all arms. After the action
A_{t}
is played, under semi-bandit feedback, the outcomes of
X^{(t)}_{i}
for all
i\in A_{t}
are observed as the feedback to the player. Under cascading feedback, the arms in
A_{t}
are probabilistically triggered and only the arms in the triggered set
\tau_{t}
could be observed. In both cases the player receives
r(A_{t},\bm{\mu})
as the reward in round
t
.

The player’s objective is to minimize the expected difference between the cumulative reward of the best action
A^{*}
and the player’s own cumulative reward. That is, the player aims to design an algorithm to minimize the expected regret

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[R(T)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r(A^{*},\bm{\mu})-r(A_{t},\bm{\mu})\right)\right].

Notations.

\mathtt{U}(a,b)
represents the uniform distribution on
(a,b)
. Denote
\Delta_{A_{t}}=r(A^{*},\bm{\mu})-r(A_{t},\bm{\mu})
as the gap of
A_{t}
.
\mathbb{1}\left[S\right]
is the indicator variable of whether event
S
happens. For every
i\in[m]
, we use a random variable
T_{i,t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{1}\left[i\in A_{s}\right]
to denote the number of times
\mathtt{arm}_{i}
has been pulled when the
t
-th round ends. For two sets
A
and
B
,
A\setminus B=\left\{a\;\middle|\;a\in A\land a\notin B\right\}
. And
\log
represents the logarithm with base
2
.
\lfloor x\rfloor
represents the largest integer that is no more than
x
.
\ln^{+}(x)=\ln\max\left\{1,x\right\}
.

1\leq k_{A}\leq k
. We denote
\left(\mu^{A}_{i}\right)_{i\in[k_{A}]}
as the mean rewards of the arms in
A\setminus A^{*}
where
\mu^{A}_{k_{A}}\geq\mu^{A}_{k_{A}-1}\geq\cdots\geq\mu^{A}_{1}
and
\overline{\mu^{A}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in[k_{A}]}\mu^{A}_{i}}{k_{A}}
. Define

D_{A}=\left\{i\in[k_{A}]\;\middle|\;\mu^{A}_{i}\leq\overline{\mu^{A}}+\frac{3\Delta_{A}}{4k_{A}}\right\}

as the set of arms in
A\setminus A^{*}
whose mean reward is no more than the
\overline{\mu^{A}}+\frac{3\Delta_{A}}{4k_{A}}
. Then we have the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

For every suboptimal action
A\in\mathcal{A}
, if
\left|D_{A}\right|\leq\lfloor\frac{k_{A}}{2}\rfloor
, then there exists
1\leq\ell_{A}\leq\lfloor\log k_{A}\rfloor
such that

\overline{\mu^{A}}-\mu^{A}_{\lfloor\frac{k_{A}}{2^{\ell_{A}}}\rfloor}\geq\frac{\left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{\ell_{A}}}{16}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{A}}{k_{A}}.

Lemma
2
reveals that every suboptimal action
A_{t}
could be quantitatively characterized by a fundamental trade-off: it must contain either a few arms with mean rewards far from the action’s average mean,
\overline{\mu^{A}}
, or conversely, many arms with rewards clustered near it. Therefore before the algorithm starts, we could divide the action set
\mathcal{A}
into
1+\lfloor\log k\rfloor
exhaustive and mutually exclusive sets
\left(\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\right)_{0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\log k\rfloor}
, where

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\left\{A\in\mathcal{A}\;\middle|\;\left|D_{A}\right|>\lfloor\frac{k_{A}}{2}\rfloor\right\}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}=\left\{A\in\mathcal{A}\;\middle|\;\left|D_{A}\right|\leq\lfloor\frac{k_{A}}{2}\rfloor\land\ell_{A}=\ell\right\}
. If the choice of
\ell_{A}
for
A
is not unique, we choose
\ell_{A}
to be the smallest valid value without loss of generality. Leveraging this classification of actions, we can bound the indicator for selecting a suboptimal action
A_{t}
:

Lemma 3

(Indicator decomposition lemma)
.

For every
0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\log k\rfloor
, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\mathbb{1}\left[A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\cap\mathcal{A}_{B}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{\ell+1}}{k_{A_{t}}}\sum_{i\notin A^{*}}\mathbb{1}\left[\left(\hat{\mu}_{i,t}+\rho_{i,t}\geq\mu_{i}+\frac{\left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{\ell}}{16}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{A_{t}}}{k_{A_{t}}}\right)\land i\in A_{t}\right].

Note that the coefficient
\frac{2^{\ell+1}}{k_{A_{t}}}
is critical to control the quantity of the summation. Then we need to solve challenge 3. We choose to give the actions in every
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
a regret upper bound. That is, for every
0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\log k\rfloor
,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\left[A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\cap\mathcal{A}_{B}\right]\cdot\Delta_{A_{t}}\right]=O\left(\log k\sqrt{kmT}\right).

For any fixed
0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\log k\rfloor
, we bound the regret caused by executing actions in
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
. After applying Lemma
3
, we recount the regret by switching the summation of base arms with the summation of actions that a certain arm is involved. To avoid redundant computations for regret, we could arrange the sequence of actions that maximizes the value.

Let each
\mathtt{arm}_{i}
be contained in
N_{i,\ell}
suboptimal actions

such that
A_{i,\ell,n}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell},\forall n\in[N_{i,\ell}]

\Delta_{A_{i,\ell,n}}

\Delta_{i,\ell,n}
and

. Then we have the following lemma:

Lemma 4
.

For every
0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\log k\rfloor
, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\left[A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\cap\mathcal{A}_{B}\right]\Delta_{A_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1540\sum_{i\notin A^{*}}\Bigg(\sum_{n=2}^{N_{i,\ell}}\frac{k_{i,\ell,n}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{\delta}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell,n}^{2}}{k_{i,\ell,n}^{2}}\right)}{\Delta_{i,\ell,n}^{2}}\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\left(\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell,n}}{k_{i,\ell,n}}-\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell,n-1}}{k_{i,\ell,n-1}}\right)+\frac{k_{i,\ell,1}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{\delta}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{i,\ell,1}^{2}}{k_{i,\ell,1}^{2}}\right)}{\Delta_{i,\ell,1}}\Bigg).

By perceiving

as the variable

over the relevant domain, while the second term by the maximum of

over its effective domain. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\left[A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{B}\right]\Delta_{A_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor\log k\rfloor}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\left[A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\cap\mathcal{A}_{B}\right]\Delta_{A_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left((\log k)^{2}\sqrt{kmT}\right).

Bring this back to
eq.

1

\mathbf{E}\left[R(T)\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{A_{t}}\right]=O\left((\log k)^{2}\sqrt{kmT}\right)
.

3.3
Extension to cascading feedback

In this section we introduce the cascading feedback
(
Kveton et al. 2015a
;
Kveton et al. 2015b
;
Li et al. 2016
)
and give our regret upper bound. In this model, when
A_{t}
is chosen, playing this action means that the player reveals the outcomes of the arms one by one following the sequence order until certain stopping condition is satisfied. The feedback is the outcomes of revealed arms and the reward is a function form of all arms in
A_{t}
. In particular, in the disjunctive form the player stops when the first 1 is revealed, or he reaches the end and gets all feedbacks. In the conjunctive form, the player stops when the first 0 is revealed or she reaches the end with all 1 outcomes.

Let
p_{A,t}
be the the probability of observing all base arms of
A
at round
t
. And denote
p^{*}=\min_{t\in T}\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}}p_{A,t}
, which is the minimal probability that an action could have all base arms observed over all time. Then we have the following regret bound:

Theorem 5
.

For any stochastic combinatorial cascading feedback instance with
m
arms, time horizon
T
, and the maximum number of arms that could be chosen in a round is
k
, then the expected regret of Algorithm
1
is
O\left(\frac{(\log k)^{2}}{p^{*}}\sqrt{kmT}\right)
.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.2
. In Appendix
C.4
, we also present comparative experiments evaluating the performance of CUCB and CMOSS under cascading feedback.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we study the stochastic CMAB problem and propose the CMOSS algorithm, which is not only computationally efficient but also has a regret of
O\left((\log k)^{2}\sqrt{kmT}\right)
under semi-bandit feedback. We then extend our analysis to cascading feedback. Comparative experiments with CUCB, EXP3.M and HYBRID under semi-bandit feedback show that CMOSS has at least one-way significant advantages in cumulative regret and computation cost over the other three algorithms, with the other one showing small differences.

Several promising directions for future research emerge from this work. First, tightening the
\frac{1}{p^{*}}
factor under cascading feedback and other scenarios which contain probabilistically triggered arms, could yield a more general framework and facilitate the analysis of a broader range of problems including episodic reinforcement learning
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, recent refinements to the UCB algorithm
(
Lattimore 2015
;
Mukherjee et al. 2018
)
, were shown to empirically outperform MOSS in terms of regret. Adapting these more sophisticated UCB variants to the CMAB setting presents a natural and compelling extension.

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\left|\prod_{n=1}^{l}(1-\mu_{i_{n}})\prod_{n=l+1}^{k}(1-\mu_{j_{n}})-\prod_{n=1}^{l+1}(1-\mu_{i_{n}})\prod_{n=l+2}^{k}(1-\mu_{j_{n}})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{l}(1-\mu_{i_{n}})\cdot\prod_{n=l+2}^{k}(1-\mu_{j_{n}})\cdot\left|\mu_{j_{l+1}}-\mu_{i_{l+1}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left|\mu_{j_{n}}-\mu_{i_{n}}\right|=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\mu_{j_{n}}-\sum_{n=1}^{k}\mu_{i_{n}}.

Let
p_{A,t}
be the the probability of observing all base arms of
A
at round
t
. And denote
p^{*}=\min_{t\in T}\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}}p_{A,t}
. Therefore in each round
\left[\tau_{t}=A_{t}\right]\geq p^{*}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the history before the learning agent chooses action at time
t
. Thus
\mathcal{F}_{t}
contains feedback information at all time
s<t
. We could bound the regret as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[R(T)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{A_{t}}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{A_{t}}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{p_{A_{t},t}}\left[\tau_{t}=A_{t}\right]\;\middle|\;A_{t}\right]\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{p^{*}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{A_{t}}\left[\tau_{t}=A_{t}\right]\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{(\log k)^{2}}{p^{*}}\sqrt{kmT}\right),

(7)

where
eq.

7
applies Theorem
1
to this case.

For the conjunctive case,
r(A_{t},\bm{\mu})=\prod_{i\in A_{t}}\mu_{i}
. The rest analysis follows the same pattern as the conjunctive case.

Algorithm 5

Input

1:

\hat{L}_{0}=(0,\ldots,0),\eta_{t}=1/\sqrt{t}

for
