Genome-wide analysis of phytochrome-interacting factor (PIF) families and their potential roles in light and gibberellin signaling in Chinese pine
Authors: Yingtian Guo, Chengyan Deng, Guizhi Feng, Dan Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs) are a subgroup of transcription factors within the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family, playing a crucial role in integrating various environmental signals to regulate plant growth and development. Despite the significance of PIFs in these processes, a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of PIFs in conifers has yet to be conducted. In this investigation, three PtPIF genes were identified in Chinese pine, categorized into three subgroups, with conserved motifs indicating the presence of the APA/APB motif and bHLH domain in the PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 proteins. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 proteins belong to the PIF7/8 and PIF3 groups, respectively, and were relatively conserved among gymnosperms. Additionally, a class of PIF lacking APA/APB motif was identified in conifers, suggesting its function may differ from that of traditional PIFs. The cis-elements of the PtPIF genes were systematically examined, and analysis of PtPIF gene expression across various tissues and under different light, temperature, and plant hormone conditions demonstrated similar expression profiles for PtPIF1 and PtPIF3. Investigations into protein-protein interactions and co-expression networks speculated the involvement of PtPIFs and PtPHYA/Bs in circadian rhythms and hormone signal transduction. Further analysis of transcriptome data and experimental validation indicated an interaction between PtPIF3 and PtPHYB1, potentially linked to diurnal rhythms. Notably, the study revealed that PtPIF3 may be involved in gibberellic acid (GA) signaling through its interaction with PtDELLAs, suggesting a potential role for PtPIF3 in mediating both light and GA responses. Overall, this research provides a foundation for future studies investigating the functions of PIFs in conifer growth and development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10915-w.Keywords: Chinese pine, Phytochrome-interacting factors, Bioinformatics, Light, GA signaling

Background

Plants adapt their developmental patterns seasonally, with temperature and light serving as two significant stimuli triggering these seasonal fluctuations [1, 2]. Phytochromes (PHYs) are pigments in plants that detect signals related to light and temperature [3]. Among the various phytochromes (phyA to phyE), phyA and phyB play crucial roles in regulating plant growth in response to light and temperature signals [4–6]. Upon detecting light or temperature stimuli, phytochromes are translocated by phytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs) from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby regulating plant growth [3, 7].

PIFs are members of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family of transcription factors, characterized by a conserved motif known asthe active phyB binding (APB) or active phyA binding (APA) domain located at the N-terminal region [8, 9]. To date, eight PIFs (AtPIF1–8) have been identified in Arabidopsis [9], classified into five distinct classes (Class I-V): AtPIF3, AtPIF2/6, AtPIF4/5, AtPIF1, and AtPIF7/8 [10]. This classification serves as a reference for the evolutionary analysis of PIFs in other species. Notably, AtPIF1 and AtPIF3 contain both APB and APA motifs, which facilitate their interaction with phyA and phyB, while other AtPIFs that possess only the APB domain are regulated by phyB [11]. Nonetheless, APA/APB motifs are necessary for phyA/phyB binding. Beyond Arabidopsis, PIFs have also been identified in other angiosperms with their numbers range from 4 to 14, encompassing members from PIF1 to PIF8 [10]. However, the quantity and sequence characteristics of PIFs in gymnosperms remain undetermined.

The PIF family is widely acknowledged for its essential role in regulating plant growth and development by integrating various signals, including light signaling and environmental cues [4–6, 11–17]. The primary physiological processes regulated by PIFs encompass the inhibition of seed germination [18], promotion of hypocotyl elongation [19], supression of internode length [5], induction of leaf senescence [20], and modulation of flowering [4, 21]. Additionally, PIFs integrate signals from various endogenous hormones, such as auxins, gibberellins (GAs), abscisic acid (ABA), brassinosteroids (BR), and jasmonic acid (JA), to regulate plant growth [11, 12, 22–24]. Notably, GAs specifically antagonize light signaling during plant growth [22]. In the absence of GAs, DELLA proteins inhibit the binding of PIF3 and PIF4 to their target gene promoters or promote the degradation of PIF proteins through the ubiquitin-proteasome system [15, 25]. Interestingly, PIFs upregulate the expression of the GA biosynthetic enzyme GA20ox3 in dark-grown seedlings [26], suggesting that the gibberellin pathway influences the transcription and accumulation of PIFs, and then the feedback of PIFs regulate gibberellin synthesis.

Chinese pine (Pinus tabuliformis), a member of the Pinaceae family native to China, holds significant economic and ecological value [27]. Research on Chinese pine has led to the construction of high-quality genomes [28], and the establishment of a unique regulatory network governing reproduction and development [29]. Furthermore, numerous transcription factors, inccluding MADS-box, APETALA2/ethylene responsive factor (AP2/ERF), Nuclear Factor Y (NF-Y), Teosinte branched1/Cycloidea/Proliferating cell factor (TCP), and CONSTANS-like (COL), have been isolated and characterized through genomic analysis [28, 30–32]. Consequently, Chinese pine serves as an excellent model for investigating gene functions in gymnosperms. To further explore the characteristics and functions of PIF transcription factors in conifers, a genome-wide analysis of PIFs in Chinese pine is warranted. In this study, we constructed a comprehensive analysis of PIFs, examining their physicochemical properties, motif distribution, phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal locations, and expression profiles. Furthermore, we built co-expression networks based on diurnal cycles and analyzed the interactions of PtPIFs with PtPHYA/B and PtDELLA, respectively. Overall, our findings enhance the understanding of PIF evolution in gymnosperms and provide a foundation for further exploration of PIF functions in the growth and development of conifers.

Results

Identification and characterization of the PtPIF, PtPHYA and PtPHYB gene family

As shown in Table 1, the putative PtPIF, PtPHYA, and PtPHYB proteins were isolated from the genomes of Chinese pine using the BLASTP program and Hidden Markov Model (HMM). Table 1 details the characteristics of the identified proteins, including gene ID, protein length, grand average hydrophilicity (GRAVY), protein molecular weight, predicted position, isoelectric point (pI), and the homology of the identified proteins. The results indicate that the length and size of PtPIF protein are considerably smaller than those of PtPHYA and PtPHYB. The GRAVY values ranging from − 0.113 to -0.722 across all proteins, demonstrate that the identified proteins are hydrophilic, irrespective of their gene associations. Furthermore, the predicted localizations of all identified PtPIF, PtPHYA, and PtPHYB proteins are in the nucleus, which aligns with their expected role as transcription factors.

Motifs (MEME) analysis and phylogenetic analysis of PtPIFs

To investigate the conserved PtPIF motifs, we present the results of the MEME analysis in Fig. 1. Similar to those found in Arabidopsis, all PtPIFs exhibited a conserved bHLH domain (motif 1), whereas PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 contained both the APB (motif 2) and APA domains (motif 7). Although PtPIF2 lacks both the APB domain and APA domains, the Conserved Domain Database (CCD; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) identified the presence of the bHLH_AtPIF_like domain (ID: cd11445), confirming its classification within the PIF family. Additionally, PtPIF1 displayed a histidine substitution for glutamine (ELxxxxGH) in the APB domain (ELxxxxGQ) and a lysine substitution for arginine (NFxxFxK) in the APA domain (NFxxFxR; Additional file 1: Figure S1). Therefore, further verification is necessary to assess whether these amino acid substitutions influence the interactions between PtPIF1 and PHYA/PHYB.

To comprehensively understand the phylogenetic relationships among PIFs in Chinese pine and other plants, we selected known PIF proteins from eight land plants (Additional file 2: Appendix S1): A. thaliana [33], Oryza sativa [11], Populus trichocarpa [10], Pinus taeda (https://treegenesdb.org/org/Pinus-taeda), Pinus lambertiana (https://treegenesdb.org/org/Pinus-lambertiana), Pinus pinaster (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/plaza), and Pinus sylvestris (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/plaza). An unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method. As shown in Fig. 2, these proteins were divided into seven subgroups, groups I-VII. Notably, Groups I, II, IV, and VII were exclusive to angiosperms, whereas Group VI was unique to conifers. This distribution suggests that Groups III (PIF3) and V (PIF7/8) are shared by both angiosperms and conifers, indicating their independent evolution from a common ancestor of spermophytes. Furthermore, analysis revealed that eight members of Group VI exclusively possessed bHLH_AtPIF_like domains, while the N-terminal APB domains of four short proteins (ranging from 101 to 276 AA) were absent (Additional file 3, Additional file 4: Table S1). Although PSY00020194 (331 AA), Pt6G36020 (332 AA), PITA01578 (407 AA), and PITA20563 (469 AA) are comparable in length to typical PIFs, they lack the APB domain and other unidentified functional regions at their N-terminus. The PtPIFs PtXG51980, Pt6G36020, and Pt8G26240 were classified into Groups V, VI and III, respectively. Moreover, the PIFs of Chinese pine exhibited high similarity to the PIF proteins of P. taeda and P. sylvestris, likely due to the close evolutionary relationships among these three species [28].

Chromosome localization and syntenic analysis of PtPIF genes

Chromosome mapping results indicated that PtPIF1, PtPIF2, and PtPIF3 were located on chromosomes 6, 8, and 10, respectively (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, no tandem or dispersed duplications were identified among these three genes, suggesting that the PIF gene family is relatively conserved. To further investigate the evolutionary mechanisms of the PIF gene family, we established a comparative analysis with two other gymnosperm species—Ginkgo biloba [34] and Sequoiadendron giganteum [35], in which have recently undergone genome assembly at the chromosome level. The results demonstrate that PIF1 and PIF3 exhibit collinearity with G. biloba and Giant Sequoia (Fig. 3B). This finding aligns with the evolutionary relationships of PIFs in other gymnosperms, suggesting that these evolutionarily conserved genes may play significant roles during evolution.

Expression profiling of the PtPIF genes in different tissues

Based on the transcriptome data (Additional file 5: Table S2), boxplots were used to illustrate the expression profiles of PtPIFs across various plant organs/tissues, including hypocotyls, seedling needles, sapling needles, sapling shoots, adult needles, adult stems, adult shoots, male cones, female cones, adult roots, and calluses. As shown in Fig. 4, the expression patterns of PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 were similar, exhibiting the highest levels of expression in female cones and the lowest in calli. In contrast, PtPIF2 displayed distinct expression characteristics, with low levels in the needles and elevated levels in the roots and stems. These results suggested that PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 may share similar biological functions, which warrant further investigation.

Expression analysis of PtPIFs in response to abiotic stresses and hormones

PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 are classified within the of PIF7/8 and PIF3 categories, respectively. Consequently, we utilized the promoters of PtPIFs and AtPIF3/7/8 genes to examine the distribution and quantity of their cis-acting elements, aiming to elucidate the functional characteristics of PtPIFs (Fig. 5A). The results showed that the development-related cis-elements were the least abundant, while light-associated elements were the most abundant, aligning with patterns observed in cotton and Arabidopsis PIFs [4]. In the context of stress response, the antioxidant component ARE was found to be predominant; in the light response, the G-box occurred most frequently, followed by Box 4 components. In addition, plant hormone-responding elements, such as those of gibberellin (P-box; TATC-box), MeJA (TGACG-motif; CGTCA-motif), auxin (TGA-element), abscisic acid (ABRE), and salicylic acid (TCA-element), were identified in the promoters of PtPIFs. In summary, the promoters of the PtPIF gene family primarily contain elements responsive to light, drought, anoxia/anaerobiosis, ABA, GA and MeJA. This suggests that PtPIFs may play a potential role in plant stress defense and hormone response. Notably, certain cis-elements were specific to individual genes; for instance, WUN-motifs, which are involved in wound response, were found exclusively in the promoter of PtPIF3, while MeJA response elements (CGTCA-motifs) were present solely in the PtPIF2 promoter. These findings indicated that the PIF gene family may have undergone functional differentiation during evolution.

Based on the cis-elements analysis, we assessed the expression profiles of PtPIF genes in response to various light, temperature and hormone treatments. As shown in Fig. 5B and Additional file 6: Table S3, the expression levels of PtPIFs were inhibited by UVB-1d and UVB-3d treatments. In contrast, UVB-7d treatment appeared to restore the expression levels of PtPIFs expression levels. Moreover, the expressions of PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 were upregulated by short far-red, short red, long far-red and long red light, whereas PtPIF2 exhibited no significant change. Under temperature treatment, the expressions of PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 decreased at high temperature (40 °C), while PtPIF2 remained unaffected. Notably, PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 exhibited different response to hormonal treatments; PtPIF1 was induced by JA, SA, and ACC, while PtPIF3 was inhibited by IAA, ZT, ABA, and BL. Collectively, these data suggested that PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 may be similarly regulated by stress treatments.

Analysis of the interactions of PtPIFs with the PtPHYA/B protein

PIFs and PHYA/Bs are capable of detecting light-related signals; therefore, we next analyzed the expression profiles of PtPIF genes throughout the diurnal cycle. As shown in Fig. 6A and Additional file 7: Table S4, PtPIF1 expression peaked at 6:00 p.m. and gradually increased overnight. The expression of PtPIF2 reached its highest levels at 4:00 p.m. and 8:00 p.m., followed by a rapid decline at night. In contrast, PtPIF3 levels decreased during the day but rapidly re-accumulated overnight, peaking at 4:00 a.m. Notably, the expression profiles of PtPIF1 and PtPHYA1 exhibited similarity, while the expression patterns of PtPIF2 and PtPHYA2 showed resemblance. Additionally, the expression patterns of PtPIF3 and PtPHYB1 were found to be alike. Therefore, we analyzed the correlation between the daily expression cycles of the three genes and found that a significant correlation between PtPIF3 and PtPHYB1 (R2 = 0.5395, P = 0.0065; Fig. 6B). Overall, the expression levels of PtPIFs and PtPHYA/Bs were regulated by circadian rhythms.

Based on the combined results shown in Fig. 5A, we hypothesized that PtPIF3 might interact with PtPHYB1. To confirm this hypothesis, we conducted yeast two-hybrid and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays. The results showed that PtPIF3 could interact with PtPHYB1 in yeast cells as well as in the cell nuclei of tobacco leaves (Fig. 6C), indicating that an interaction between PHYB and PIF also exists in conifers.

Analysis of protein-protein interaction network and diurnal cycle co-expression network

To understand the interactions of PtPIFs, PtPHYA/Bs and other genes in Chinese pine, the STRING online tool was utilized to predict their functional relationships using an orthology-based method (Fig. 7A). The results showed 192 gene pairs within the interaction networks, with each of PHYB1 and PHYB2 interacting with 48 genes, while PHYA1 and PHYA2 each interacted with 31 genes. Notably, genes associated with circadian system, such as ZTL, PHOT2, LHY1, GI, FKL2, PRR4, COP1, and ELF3 were present in the networks, suggesting a potential role for PtPIFs and PtPHYA/Bs in regulating circadian rhythm. Additionally, genes related to ethylene (EIL3, 4, 5), auxin (AUX1, LAX), and gibberellins (DPL1-3, RGA) were also detected, indicating a comprehensive involvement of PtPIFs and PtPHYA/Bs in plant hormone signal transduction.

Homologies of clock genes have been identified in conifers, demonstrating a high degree of conservation [30, 36]. Consequently, we utilized diurnal transcriptome data to investigate the relationships between PtPIFs and PtPHYA/B with clock genes, including PtGI, PtCOP1, PtLHY1, PtTOC, PtZTL (Additional file 8: Table S5, Fig. 7B and C). Our analysis revealed a positive correlation between PtPIF3, PtPHYB1, PtLHY1, and PtCOP1, while a negative correlation was observed between PtPIF3, PtPHYB1, and PtGI. These results suggested that PtPIF3 and PtPHYB1 may play significant roles in the growth and development of Chinese pine as clock genes.

PtPIFs interact with the PtDELLA protein in response to GA signals

Previous studies have demonstrated that GAs can influence the transcription and accumulation of PIFs [22]. Consequently, a histogram was constructed based on RNA-Seq data from Chinese pine treated with either GA or paclobutrazol (PAC) to analyze the expression patterns of PtPIFs. As shown in Fig. 8A, PtPIF1, PtPIF2, and PtPIF3 were induced by GA3; however, the expression level of PtPIF3 did not exhibit significantly. In addition, there was no difference in the expressions of other genes except for PtPIF2 being induced by PAC, which maybe time-dependent (i.e., affected by the sampling time). Moreover, the interactions between PIFs and RGA (a type of DELLA protein) were foundwithin the PIF protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 7). To further investigate the relationship between PtPIF1, PtPIF2, PtPIF3, and PtRGA, we employed HDOCK [37, 38]. As depicted in Fig. 8B, PtPIF1/2/3 interacted with PtRGA, with all confidence scores (SC) exceeding 0.95. Furthermore, the BiFC assay corroborated the interaction between PIF1/2/3 and PtRGA (Fig. 8C). Taken together, these data speculated that PtPIF1/2/3 may be involved in GA signaling through their interactions with DELLA proteins. Consequently, we selected PtPIF3 (PtPIF3-PtPHYB1 and PtPIF3-PtRGA) as a candidate gene for investigating light and GA signal transduction.

Discussion

PIFs, which can integrate light and temperature signals to regulate plant growth and development, contain signaling modules that are conserved from Physcomitrella patens to higher plants [7, 39, 40]. While systematic analyses of PIF families have been conducted in various species, including P. patens [41], A. thaliana [9], O. sativa [42], Zea mays [43], Solanum lycopersicum [44], Brassica napus [33], Malus domestica [45], and Populus tremula [5], there is a notable absence of such studies in conifers. This study presents an analysis of the PtPIF gene family, offering insights for future research on the PIF gene and for the selection of important trait genes in conifers.

In the present study, we identified three potential PtPIFs in Chinese pine that share conserved bHLH and APB/APA domains. PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 exhibited APA motifs that were similar to those of AtPIF1 and AtPIF3 [10], suggesting a closer evolutionary relationship between these proteins. Phylogenetic tree analysis showed that PtPIF1 was closely related to AtPIF7/8, indicating that PtPIF1 may be functionally redundant with AtPIF7 and AtPIF8. Notably, our analysis found that the PtPIF2 protein lacks the APA/APB domain, and analogous genes were identified in other gymnosperms, including Pinus taeda, P. lambertiana, P. pinaster and P. sylvestris. The expression of PtPIF2 was detected across various tissues, suggesting that PtPIF2 may still encode a functional protein. Numerous studies have demonstrated that bHLH transcription factors, including PIF, bind to G-box and E-box sequences as homodimers and heterodimers [46, 47]. One hypothesis posits that PtPIF2 interacts with canonical PIF proteins to form heterodimers to exert its effects. Furthermore, PIF3 is present from non-vascular to seed plants [10], suggesting that it may have evolved some time before the last common ancestor of seed and non-vascular plants [39]. Interestingly, we found PIF3 gene in all five gymnosperms, and they have high homology among them, which seems to support the above view.

PIFs are regulated by various environmental stressors, including phytohormones, light, and temperature, and exhibit different tissue-specific expression patterns while sharing distinct functions in various physiological processes [3, 7, 12]. Analysis of expression pattern in different tissues of Chinese pine revealed that all PtPIF genes were constitutively expressed, with the highest levels observed in female cones (PIF1 and PIF3) and stems (PIF2). These spatial expression patterns appear to differ from those of AtPIFs, ZmPIFs, MdPIFs and BnaPIFs [33, 45, 48]. These results indicated that there are conserved PtPIF family genes that fulfill a range of biological functions across various plant developmental processes. Upon exposure to light and low temperature, the abundance of PIF mRNAs changed significantly, while the PIF proteins were rapidly degraded [7]. In Arabidopsis, AtPIF4 can be induced by temperature treatments [4, 12],, and AtPIF4/5 proteins can be destabilized by UV-B and low temperature [6, 49], whereas AtPIF3 remains stable under cold conditions [50]. Analysis of cis-acting elements in the PtPIFs promoter sequences revealed a significant abundance of light-responsive elements. Our data also confirmed that PtPIFs expression were influenced by UV-B and different light qualities (white, red and far-red). For instance, expression of PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 were inhibited by UV-B but induced by far-red light, while PtPIF1 expression decreased at 40 °C and PtPIF3 expression increased at 30 °C. Previous studies have shown that hormones such as IAA, ABA, BR, and JA, play crucial roles in regulating plant growth and responses to stress conditions [12, 33, 43, 51]. In this study, we observed that PtPIF2 expression was reduced following treatments with JA and ACC. Additionally, motifs associated with the JA response, specifically the CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif, were identified in the promoter of PtPIF2. Moreover, PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 exhibited distinct variations in response to the hormone treatments. These findings suggested that PtPIFs were influenced by multiple abiotic stressors, leading us to hypothesize that these proteins may possess complex regulatory roles in the response to abiotic stress, warranting further study.

PIFs are regulated by phytochrome-dependent phosphorylation, ubiquitination and proteasome-mediated degradation in response to light. The dynamic regulation of PIF DNA-binding and transcriptional activities necessitates the involvement of PIF-interacting partners [7, 52]. PHYA/B plays a crucial roles as a partner in PIF-interactions and is integral to light response processes, including those triggered by red light and far-red light [3]. In the present study, four PtPHYA/Bs were identified, and the expression patterns of both PtPHYA/Bs and PtPIFs in relation to the circadian rhythm were analyzed. The results show that PtPIF1 correlates PtPHYA1, PtPIF2 with PtPHYA2, and PtPIF3 with PtPHYB1, demonstrating similar expression patterns. Notably, a strong correlation was observde between PtPIF3 and PtPHYB1. Previous in vivo and in vitro experiments have eatablished that PtPIF3 interacts with PtPHYB1, suggesting that a synergistic effect in their response to light regulation; however, the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. DELLA, another significant PIF cooperating protein, enables PIF to combine light and gibberellin signaling pathways [15, 26]. DELLAs can negatively regulate the abundance of PIF proteins by inhibiting their DNA-binding capacity, thereby hindering their ability to integrate light signals [22]. In this study, we employed a protein-protein interaction network to investigate the regulatory relationship between PtPIFs and RGA. Additionally, we applied GA and PAC treatments were applied, and the results showed that PtPIFs can respond to gibberellin induction. Moreover, both precise predictions using HDOCK and in vivo interaction verification have shown that PtPIFs can interact with PtRGA, suggesting that PIF may play a role in the gibberellin signaling pathway. In summary, this study identified two interaction modules in Chinese pine: PtPIF3-PtPHYB1 and PtPIF3-PtRGA. Previous research has indicated that DELLA-induced AtPIF3 degradation occurs independently of the light-mediated AtPIF3 degradation pathway, and that the AtPHYB-AtPIF3 interaction remains largely unaffected by variations in DELLA protein abundance [15, 25]. Notably, it remains unclear whether the PtPIF3-PtPHYB1 and PtPIF3-PtRGA modules operate independently. If they are independent, these two modules are relatively conserved. Conversely, if they are not independent, a novel PIF3-PHYB1-DELLA (RGA) regulatory module likely exists in conifers. However, the current hypothesis cannot be verified in conifers due to several challenges associated with the genetic transformation of coniferous trees. These challenges include low transformation efficiency, a high dependence on the receptor genotype, and difficulties in plant regeneration [53]. Consequently, future efforts will focus on optimizing genetic transformation procedures, enhancing tissue culture techniques, employing nanoparticles and non-tissue culture-dependent genetic transformation methods, and utilizing genome editing tools to improve the genetic transformation of conifers [54].

Conclusions

In this study, we identified three PIF genes in Chinese pine and conducted a comprehensive analysis of their gene structure, conserved motifs, and phylogenetic relationships. Our findings revealed that PtPIF1 and PtPIF3 belong to the PIF7/8 and PIF3 categories, respectively, while PtPIF2 was classified into a distinct group that lacks the APA/APB domain. Additionally, the analysis of cis-acting elements and spatiotemporal expression profiling indicates that their expression was influenced by multiple abiotic stresses and hormones. Circadian rhythm co-expression network analysis, along with gibberellin treatment and protein interaction verification, demonstrated that the PtPIF3-PtPHYB1 and PtPIF3-PtRGA modules in Chinese pine may play a role in mediating light and gibberellin signals. The results of this study provide a valuable reference for further research on the diverse functions and regulatory networks of PIF genes in conifers.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and samples collection

Chinese pine tissue samples, including seedling needles, sapling needles, sapling shoots, adult needles, adult stems, adult shoots, female cones, male cones, adult roots, and calli were obtained by Yingtian Guo from primary clonal seed orchard located in Pingquan City, Hebei Province, China (118°44.67580’ E, 40°98.87840’ N, 560 m asl). Additionally, Chinese pine needles were collected by Yingtian Guo from Beijing Forestry University in Beijing, China (116°33.91160’ E, 40°00.08610’ N, 44 m asl) to measure diurnal expression at 2-hour intervals from 8 a.m. to 62d a.m. on July 25, 2020 [30]. Light exposure began at 5 o’clock and ended at 19 o’clock, followed by 10 h of darkness. For the gibberellin (GA) treatment, needles form 3-month-old seedlings were reared by Yingtian Guo under a 14 h:10 h day/night cycle with white light. Irrigation with GA or PAC was performed daily for 50 days, according to the hormone type and concentration treatments, as shown in Table 2. Three replicates were conducted for each treatment group. Samples were subsequently collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for further RNA-seq analysis [32]. All experimental research and field studies involving cultivated plants, including the collection of plant material, were collected during Yingtian Guo’s doctoral project (https://www.researchgate.net/lab/Shi-hui-Niu-Lab) and adhere to the applicable institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation established by the State Forestry and Grassland Administration of China.

Chinese pine gibberellins treatments for gene expression analysis

The original data for abiotic and hormonal stress related to UV-B treatment were obtained from the research conducted by Jie Xu [55], while the data for light treatment was sourced from the study by Wei Li [56]. The data pertaining to temperature treatment [57] and hormone treatment can be attributed to the research conducted by Shihui Niu [28, 32]. The methodologies for assessing plant growth conditions and treatments as well as for RNA isolation and sequencing have been detailed in prior publications.

Identification of PIF and PHYA/B family members in Chinese pine

Eight AtPIF, one AtPHYA, and one AtPHYB peptide sequences from the Arabidopsis database (http://www.arabidopsis.org/) were used as query sequences to search for proteins in the Chinese pine genome database using the BLASTP program (blast-2.6.0 +) [28]. Moreover, HMMER3.0 was used to conduct the HMM (Hidden Markov Model) search with a threshold of E < 1e − 10 to explore the PIF members which were obtained from the pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). Based on the above results, the InterPro (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) was used to determine the bHLH domain, APB and/or APA motifs. in Chinese pine. ExPASy (http://www.expasy.org/tools/) was used were employed to analyze information about the PIF and PHYA/B protein-coding genes, including the gene ID, protein sequence, molecular weight, and isoelectric point. Subcellular localization was predicted using Cell-PLoc-2 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/). The motifs were predicted using MEME software (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme), and the cis-elements were analyzed using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). Gene structures maps and heat maps of cis-acting elements were generated using TBtools v1.120, respectively [58].

Phylogenetic analysis of the PtPIF proteins

Based on all PIF proteins from A. thaliana, O. sativa, P. trichocarpa, P. taeda, P. lambertiana, P. pinaster, P. sylvestris, and Chinese pine, a neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed with 1000 bootstrap replications using MEGA 7.0.21 software. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA and subsequently uploaded to iTOL (http://itol.embl.de/) for editing.

Transcriptome data analysis of the expression of PtPIF and PtPHYA/B genes

Total RNA was isolated from various samples of Chinese pine using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Library construction and sequencing were conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (2 × 150 bp) utilizing a paired-end module. Transcripts per million (TPM) were calculated to present the expression level of each gene. The RNA-seq data were visualized as boxplots, constructed using GraphPad Prism 7 (https://www.graphpad.com/scientificsoftware/prism/).

RNA extraction and gene cloning

Total RNA was extracted from Chinese pine needles using a Plant RNA Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd.) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA was synthesized employing the PrimeScript 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara). The coding regions of PtPIF1-3, PtPHYB1, and PtRGA were amplified using Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB). The PCR products were purified and then ligated into a pMD19-T vector (Takara), which was subsequently transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α (Takara). The primers used for amplification were detailed in Additional file 9: Table S6.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays

The coding sequences of PtPIF3 were ligated into the pGBKT7 vector, while PtHYB1 was cloned into the pGADT7 vector. The constructed plasmids were co-transfected into AH109 yeast cells. The co-transformation vectors pGBKT7-Lam and pGADT7 served as negative controls, whereas pGBKT7-p53 and pGADT7 functioned as positive controls. For each interaction combination, the appropriate yeast strains were spread onto SD/-Trp/-Leu plates and incubated at 30 °C for three days. Interactions between colonies were assessed on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade plates. The primers used for cloning are list in Additional file 9: Table S6.

Network construction

The similarity distance is characterized by the Pearson correlation coefficient (Pcc) [30]. Correlation was calculated using the R package Stats (version 4.0.3), with gene selection based on a correlation coefficient of ≥ 0.8 and a p-value of ≤ 0.05. Related networks are visualized using the OmicStudio tool, available at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool.

Protein–protein docking analysis

The interactions between PtPIFs (PtPIF1/2/3) and PtRGA were estimated using the HDOCK server, employing both template-based modeling and free docking for protein-protein docking simulations [37, 38]. Based on the docking scores provided by the HDOCK server, the optimal predicted binding mode was selected for a detailed analysis of the interaction network between the two proteins. The visualization and analysis of the docked complexes were conducted using PyMOL version 2.4.0 (https://www.schrodinger.com/pymol).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

Full-length CDSs of PtaRGA were cloned and inserted into the pSPYC vector, while PtPIFs were fused into the pSPYNE vector. The plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The incubated Agrobacterium were harvested, and resuspended in an agroinfiltration buffer containing 150 µM acetosyringone, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM MES. A suspension of GV310 solution was then used to infect the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. After 72 h, YFP fluorescence was assessed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. Primers used for the BiFC assay are listed in Additional file 9: Table S6.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 17.0 statistical software (SPSS, Chicago). Values were presented as mean ± SD. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Duncan’s Post-hoc tests was utilized for comparisons among multiple groups, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.
