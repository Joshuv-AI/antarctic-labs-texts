Early life adversity and the comorbidity of psychopathology and cardiometabolic disorders: insights from the UK Biobank
Authors: Patrícia P Silveira, Andre K. Portella, Zihan Wang, João Paulo M Hoppe, Irina Pokhvisneva, Michael J Meaney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEarly life adversity is associated with both psychopathology and cardiometabolic diseases, suggesting shared developmental risk factors and biological mechanisms. We aimed to investigate the associations between early life adversity and psychopathology, cardiometabolic diseases and their comorbidity.MethodsCross-sectional study with participants from the UK Biobank cohort, composed of 502 543 individuals ranging from 37 to 73 years of age. Samples were collected between 2006 and 2010. Participants were excluded if fitting any of the exclusion criteria—dropouts, inconsistencies in reported versus genotyped sex, inconsistencies in assigned family structure, missing covariates data. We employed generalised estimated equations to evaluate the associations between self-reported adversity and outcomes. Adversity was measured through three scores: prenatal (n=234 817), postnatal (n=151 297) and cumulative (n=82 758). Outcomes were psychopathologies (mental/behaviour disorder due to substance use, mood disorders, schizophrenia, neurotic/stress-related) and cardiometabolic diseases (type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, atherosclerosis, cerebral infarction, cerebral atherosclerosis).ResultsThe cumulative combined adversity score predicted comorbidity (OR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.31 to 1.45, p<0.01), as well as all outcomes except cerebral infarction. Similar results were observed analysing prenatal (OR=1.27, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.31, p<0.01) and postnatal (OR=1.43, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.50, p<0.01) adversity scores.ConclusionEarly life adversity may be associated with a common cascade of events linked to both psychopathologic and cardiometabolic diseases, which has profound implications for early prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

The association between early life adversity, including the prenatal and postnatal periods, and increased risk of cardiometabolic or mental disorders later in life is well established, but the link between early adversity and the comorbidity between these conditions in adulthood is less clear. Studies suggest that (a) there is a link between fetal adversities and increased risk of cardiometabolic and/or mental disorders later in life, (b) there is a common neuroregulatory mechanism shared between them and (c) co-occurrence of more than one disease increases with ageing. However, previous studies are all virtually focused on extreme forms of adversity, such as abuse and neglect, leaving the role of less intense but more prevalent forms of adversity unexplored.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Exposure to early life adversity was significantly associated with increased risk of mental disorders and cardiometabolic diseases, as well as their comorbidity. Results were similar considering either prenatal or postnatal adversity separately. Early life adversity may be linked to cascading events shared with mental and cardiometabolic processes. The perinatal period is critical for a healthy development, sensitive to even less intense forms of adversity.

Introduction

Early development, including fetal and early postnatal periods, is the foundation of a healthy life-course trajectory.15 Exposure to adversity during these phases can affect the development of several biological systems leading to physiological modifications and maladaptive behaviours that increase the risk for diseases later in life and to premature mortality.612 The world is experiencing a continuous and unprecedented level of ageing where the number of seniors is projected to increase at a faster rate than the general population.13 This exceptional circumstance is expected to be accompanied by a major increase in the overall burden of disease and disability due mostly to chronic non-communicable diseases.14 Of interest in this context is the co-occurrence of more than one disease, referred to as comorbidity or multimorbidity,15 a condition that increases with age.16 In primary care settings, comorbidity is actually more prevalent than conditions of a single disease and is associated with significant increase in healthcare utilisation, lost productivity and functional disability.17

A number of important factors are associated with the development of comorbid chronic diseases during the ageing process.18 While most have a clear shared physiopathological mechanism, such as the case of insulin resistance for comorbid cardiometabolic diseases,19 20 the links between mental and metabolic conditions are less obvious.21 While mental and metabolic conditions derive from numerous environmental and genetic factors, they appear to share overlapping mechanisms related to disruptions of neuroregulatory systems, inflammation and dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.22 23 Inflammatory processes and HPA function are both strongly influenced by early life adversity.2428 These findings suggest that early life adversity might increase the risk for comorbid mental and metabolic conditions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a cross-sectional analysis of the UK Biobank (UKB) cohort that consists of 502 543 individuals, men and women, recruited between 2006 and 2010,29 aged 37–73 years. Individuals had to be registered with the UK National Health Service and living within 25 miles of 1 of 22 assessment centres. Recruitment was unselected to provide a population-based cohort.

The information about family relatedness was not collected in the UKB; hence, we used the kinship coefficient (Variable ID22012) to infer family structure. We defined ‘family’ as a set of the first-degree relatives (parent-offspring, twins and siblings) with kinship coefficient greater than 2−5/2.30 All subjects with a kinship coefficient less than the set cut-off of 2−5/2 were assigned to single-member families (ie, family consisting of a single subject). For the remaining subjects with the first-degree relatedness, we applied the following procedure: for each related pair, both subjects and all other subjects with the first-degree relationship to either of the two subjects were assigned to one family. Subjects that can be assigned to multiple families were excluded from further analysis (38 subjects).

Specific exclusion criteria for this study were the following: dropouts, inconsistencies in reported versus genotyped sex, inconsistencies in assigned family structure, missing data in body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status (SES; Townsend Deprivation Index) and smoking status. Reported early life adversity

The mental health online follow-up included questions from the Childhood Trauma Screener:31 report of feeling hated during childhood by a family member sometimes, often or very often (“When I was growing up… I felt that someone in my family hated me”, ID20487), report of having nobody or rarely having someone to take them to doctor during childhood when needed (“When I was growing up… There was someone to take me to the doctor if I needed it”, ID20491), report of never or rarely feeling loved during childhood (“When I was growing up… I felt loved”, ID20489) and any report of physical or sexual abuse during childhood (any positive answer to “When I was growing up… People in my family hit me so hard that it left me with bruises or marks”, ID20488 and “When I was growing up… Someone molested me (sexually)”, ID20490). Being exposed to maternal smoking in early life, ID1787, was taken from the early life factors touchscreen questionnaire at the Assessment Centre. Maternal illnesses (high blood pressure, diabetes or severe depression), ID20110, were obtained from the family history touchscreen questionnaire. The verbal interview provided information on birth size in kilograms (ID20022) recoded for below 10th percentile or above 90th percentile at term,32 as there is no information on gestational age in this dataset. Both low and high birth weight were considered adversity exposures.3337 The reported cumulative combined adversity score was created attributing a single point for the presence of each one of the seven events, and the score represents the summation of the points.3840 We also performed a sensitivity analysis separating the cumulative combined adversity into prenatal (birth weight, smoking during pregnancy, illnesses of the mother) and postnatal components (felt hated, physical or sexual abuse, did not feel loved, did not have someone to take them to the doctor).

Outcomes

UKB receives linked health data from various sources, including the Cancer Registry, National Health Service Digital (Hospital Episode Statistics), Death Registry and primary care (general practitioner) providers, with International Classification of Disease version 10 (ICD-10) being a key coding system. Variable ID41270 is a summary of the distinct diagnosis codes a participant has received, and the diagnosis is coded according to the ICD-10.41 We used four categories of psychopathology (mental/behavioural disorders due to psychoactive substance use; schizophrenia, schizotypal/delusional disorders; mood (affective) disorders; and neurotic, stress-related/somatoform disorders) and five cardiometabolic outcomes (ischaemic heart disease, type 2 diabetes, atherosclerosis, cerebral infarction and cerebral atherosclerosis). Presence of one of the three diagnoses (ischaemic heart disease, atherosclerosis, cerebral atherosclerosis) was coded and described in the tables as cardiovascular disorder. For the comorbidity variable, we considered individuals that had any of the four psychopathology diagnoses in addition to any of the five cardiometabolic diagnoses.

Statistical analysis

We applied Generalized Estimated Equations (GEE) to account for relatedness between subjects in exploring associations between the environmental predictors (reported adversity score or its components) and the outcomes. The model was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, SES and smoking status, since all these covariates were found to be associated with the outcomes. The GEE model was specified with a logit link function with a binomial distribution, an exchangeable correlation structure, robust errors and the above-defined variable ‘family’ as a cluster. The cumulative, postnatal and prenatal adversity scores were considered in the analyses as continuous variables, whereas each component of the adversity scores was a binary variable indicating presence/absence of a specific adversity. The OR for the adversity scores should be interpreted as follows: for each unit increase in the adversity score, there is X times increase in the odds of the condition, holding all other variables constant. The ORs for the binary variables of adversity components should be read as the presence of the adversity component increases the odds of the condition by X times, holding all other variables constant. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to adjust for multiple tests (45 tests for the postnatal score, 36 tests for the prenatal score and 72 tests for the cumulative combined score). All the analyses were repeated adjusting by the ancestry group (Variable ID21000, categorised into white, mixed, Asian or Asian British, black or black British, Chinese and other), and these results are displayed in online supplemental table S3. Also, an unadjusted analysis (no covariates included) was performed and the results are reported in online supplemental table S4. Data were analysed using R.42 Statistical significance was accepted as p<0.05.

Results

After applying specific exclusion criteria (dropouts, inconsistencies in reported vs genotyped sex, inconsistencies in assigned family structure, missing data in BMI, SES and smoking status), the total sample size for this study was 234 817 participants with prenatal adversity score, 151 297 participants with postnatal adversity score and 82 758 participants with cumulative combined adversity score (see figure 1). Using data collected from the medical records on ICD-10 codes obtained from all episodes of hospital inpatient in either primary or secondary position,41 we calculated the prevalence of mental diseases that ranged from 0.29% for schizophrenia to 4.68% for mental/behavioural disorders due to psychoactive substance use. For cardiometabolic disease, the prevalence ranged from 0.99% for cerebral infarction to 8.77% for cardiovascular disorders. The comorbidity between mental and cardiometabolic disease had a prevalence of 2.77%. When comparing individuals who had the postnatal/prenatal or cumulative combined adversity score available versus those without available data on sex proportion, age at recruitment and Townsend Deprivation Index, there are statistical differences in which the sample with available data has a higher proportion of females, is younger and has a higher deprivation index (p<0.00001 for all comparisons). These results and demographic characteristics are provided in tables 1 and 2 and online supplemental table 5.

In the GEE analysis adjusted for age, sex, BMI, SES and current or past smoking (variables known to be independently associated with chronic adult disease, and correlated to the prevalence of these diseases in our data), the cumulative combined adversity score was significantly associated with psychopathology and cardiometabolic comorbidity (OR=1.38, 95% CI 1.31 to 1.45, p<0.01 FDR adjusted), meaning that for one-unit increase of the adversity score, the odds that a subject has a diagnosis increase by 1.38 times (about 38% increase). The cumulative combined adversity score is associated with all mental health outcomes (mental and behavioural disorders due to psychoactive substance use, mood disorders, neurotic disorders and schizophrenia), as well as with type 2 diabetes and ischaemic heart disease, but not with cerebral infarction (table 3). The strength of the associations with the cumulative combined adversity score was similar for both mental and cardiometabolic disorders (ORs between 1.18 and 1.35), though somewhat stronger for mental disorders.

When considering prenatal and postnatal time periods separately, both prenatal and postnatal adversity scores were found to be associated with mental and cardiometabolic outcomes as well as with comorbidity. The results were very similar to the cumulative combined adversity score, with the exception that the postnatal score also was associated with cerebral infarction (OR=1.12, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.24, p<0.05) (online supplemental table 1) whereas the prenatal score did not predict schizophrenia (online supplemental table 2). In general, the ORs for mental disorders were higher for the postnatal adversity score than for the prenatal adversity score (online supplemental tables 12 and figure 2A).

We then analysed the individual components of the cumulative combined adversity score (table 3, onlinesupplemental tables 1 2). The majority of the individual adversities were associated with cardiometabolic and mental disorders as well as with comorbidity. Three components related to early childhood events (self-perception of being hated during childhood, being not loved and not having someone to take them to the doctor) had higher ORs for association with mental disorders as compared with the components of prenatal adversity (altered birth weight, exposure to maternal smoking during gestation and maternal illnesses) (table 3, onlinesupplemental tables 1 2 and figure 2B). Two components, self-perception of being hated during childhood and not having someone to take the individual to the doctor when needed, were significantly associated with all the outcomes (p≤0.038 FDR adjusted). Exploring the sex-specific effect of adversity, no significant sex difference was observed in the effect of cumulative adversity on the comorbidity (OR=0.94, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.04, p=0.23). Moreover, the results remain unchanged when the analysis was adjusted by ancestry group (online supplemental table S3). The results of unadjusted analysis results are very similar to the adjusted analysis findings (online supplemental table S4).

Discussion

Our results confirm the well-established links in the literature between early adversity and several chronic diseases in adulthood. The present findings extend previous research by demonstrating that early adversity is associated with the comorbidity of mental health and cardiometabolic disorders. While a handful of studies have explored this topic, they have either focused on a set of ‘physical conditions’ that clustered cardiometabolic with other conditions (eg, asthma, arthritis, cancer)4345 or studied specific measurements of mental conditions (eg, internalising symptoms, depression) or physical traits (eg, fat mass percentage, overweight/obesity).4650 To our knowledge, this is the first study to show that early life adversity increases the risk of both psychopathology and cardiometabolic conditions occurring together. The combination of these diseases represents a large portion of the global burden of disease,14 and prevention during childhood is strongly recommended to contribute to a healthy growth and development, long-term well-being and improved human capital.

Most studies investigating the long-term consequences of childhood adversities focus on extreme forms of adversity such as abuse (physical, emotional, sexual), household challenges (violence, substance abuse, mental illness, divorce) and neglect (physical and emotional).51 One example is a study based on Germany that assessed childhood adversity based on what phase of World War II the child was born, considering the negative impacts during war, and the famine which occurred afterwards. While their definition of comorbidity includes any co-occurrence of more than one condition (by far the most common definition),52 53 they present a significant increased frequency of heart diseases, hypertension and anxiety, as well as a trend for diabetes mellitus.54 Another study based on the USA also focused on childhood adversity caused by trauma, using direct exposure to the September 11 attacks. Children belonging to the ‘high exposure’ group were over three times more likely to have a physical/psychiatric comorbidity in early adulthood compared with those non-exposed.43

While extreme forms of adversity are of unquestionable importance, the more prevalent situation in an average community sample is that of exposure to somewhat less intense but more common forms of adversity that nevertheless associate with an increased risk for poorer health outcomes. These forms of adversity are often highly intercorrelated such that the risk for poor health outcomes will depend on a multitude of disadvantages.55 56 Such factors include birth outcomes, socioeconomic position and parental mental health, each of which cuts across the entire population and predicts the later risk for metabolic and mental health outcomes,38 40 creating the opportunity to define the relative risk for each individual across the entire population. Previous studies corroborate this by demonstrating the association of less intense childhood trauma and mental/physical outcomes, interestingly using the UKB sample as well.49 57 Another limitation of many studies investigating the long-term effects of childhood adversity is the unique focus on the postnatal environment. There is strong evidence for the link between prenatal factors and an increased risk for later psychopathology and cardiometabolic disease (reviewed in Silveira et al2, O’Donnell and Meaney4 and Gómez‐Ilescas and Silveira28). For example, a recent study has found that being born with lower birth weight (LBW), reflecting multiple forms of fetal adversity, increases the risk of cardiometabolic and psychiatric conditions in adulthood.58 Our concept of early life adversity combines the cumulative effects of fetal life adversity and childhood adversity and incorporates not only psychological trauma but also overall adverse environmental exposures. We adopted a framework that considers early life as a broad critical period covering both fetal and childhood years.59 Indeed, both prenatal and postnatal exposures are associated with cardiovascular and psychopathology risk at different ages.2 4 5 28 60 61 We confirmed the distinct associations of prenatal and postnatal adversity with mental and cardiometabolic outcomes in an analysis that considered these measures separately. Interestingly, there was very little difference in the overall results in the association of pre/post and combined score with adult cardiometabolic and psychopathology comorbidities, consistent with the idea of a critical period that spans from fetal to early postnatal life. This framework is aligned with a life-course perspective.62

Soares et al also described an association between self-reported postnatal adversity (using the same instrument used in this study) and different cardiovascular outcomes in the UKB sample (ICD-10 codes for any cardiovascular disease, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease or ischaemic heart disease, self-reported diagnosis of vascular/heart problems, self-reported diagnosis of blood clot in the leg or in the lung, use of blood pressure medication, SBP ≥140 mm Hg and DBP ≥90 mm Hg, self-reported diagnosis of high blood pressure, self-reported diagnosis of heart attack or angina, and self-reported diagnosis of stroke).63 These associations were found in both men and women, and adversity-sex interactions were not observed for most outcomes. These findings agree with those from our analyses, where we also did not detect a significant interaction effect between early life adversity and sex on the comorbidity of cardiometabolic and psychiatric disease. In some contrast with these findings, Soares et al suggest that the effects may be slightly stronger in women.63 This could be due to methodological differences where Soares et al split the analyses by sex,63 and because the cardiometabolic component of our comorbidity outcome includes type 2 diabetes.

There is a significant bidirectional relationship between type 2 diabetes and depression.64 65 Longitudinal studies indicate that the presence of diabetes increases the risk of developing depression by approximately 25%, and that a diagnosis of depression increases the risk of type 2 diabetes by 40–60%.65 66 The odds for comorbid depression also increases with one or more non-psychiatric coexisting chronic conditions, especially coronary artery disease, chronic arthritis and stroke.67 Similarly, substance abuse increases the risk of metabolic syndrome and diabetes,68 and adults with type 2 diabetes have significantly higher rates of substance use.69 Among adult patients with schizophrenia, diabetes averages a high prevalence of 15%, and this association persists after controlling for use of antipsychotic drugs and obesity.70

Our results demonstrated the presence of a significant association between early life adversity and comorbidity of cardiometabolic diseases and psychopathology, while adding evidence for the known association with individual chronic diseases. We emphasise the correlational nature of these findings. Despite the strong associations reported here and previously,61 63 there are very few studies applying causal inference methods to dissect out the relationship between early life conditions and later mental or physical problems,71 72 and this area merits further investigation. For example, Ruiz et al tested whether the effects of early life stress on hippocampal neurogenesis are related to deficient HPA axis feedback and comorbidity of depression and metabolic risk using animal models, showing an age-related decrease in hippocampal neurogenesis correlated with onset of depression and metabolic risk.23

It is also important to note that there are complex combinations of genetic and environmental factors, including gene-environment correlations. Genetic associations with adversities linked to psychopathology have been described;73 74 therefore, the reported associations here with perinatal adversity could be confounded with genetic liability for health problems. For example, adoptees from the UKB have an increased genetic risk (ie, higher polygenic scores) for depressive symptoms, schizophrenia and neuroticism, suggesting that higher genetic risk for mental health problems is correlated with the circumstances leading to an adoption.75 The association between LBW and physical/mental comorbidities is moderated by the genetic background.58 Although genetic risk for multiple mental and physical illnesses can be estimated in the UKB through the calculation of polygenic risk scores, in this study, we were focused on the associations between the early environment and adult outcomes, and not on genetic correlations or gene-environment interactions. Through the calculation of a practical and broad measure of early life adversity in this large sample, our study contributes to the field and will allow the exploration of well-powered gene-environment correlation and gene-environment interaction investigations.

Even though we present analyses adjusted by age, sex, BMI, SES and smoking, residual confounding by socioeconomic trajectory, education or early life health status and perinatal complications may still bias the observed associations. Studies with a longitudinal design rather than a cross-sectional evaluation like the UKB are essential to disentangle these relations.

Our findings are limited by non-responder bias, in which the analysed sample is statistically different from the total sample of the study in terms of age, male:female ratio and socioeconomic status. We have included these variables as covariates in the analyses to overcome this limitation. It is important to note that, compared with the general UK population, the UKB sample is not representative, being affected by a healthy volunteer selection bias, non-random sampling and location-based recruitment near urban assessment centres. Participants are more likely to be older, female and from less deprived areas than the general population.76 This suggests that the subsample of this study may be more similar to the UK population (younger and with a higher deprivation index) than the general UKB sample.

Another limitation for studies with the UKB is the retrospective design that relies on self-reports of early life adversity. Prospective and retrospective measures of childhood maltreatment reveal discord.77 The reasons for the disagreement may be due to many factors beyond memory recall bias, such as under-reporting to avoid legal problems. Baldwin et al suggested that prospective assessments of early adversity may capture only severe cases, while retrospective assessments may identify more true cases.77 Although not interchangeable, both prospective and retrospective measures of childhood adversity are consistently associated with adult disease.61 It is also interesting to note that psychopathology seems to emerge as a function of subjective rather than objective experience of childhood maltreatment, as the retrospective measures of childhood adversity are more strongly associated with psychopathology.78 Since stress responses can be triggered by perceived threats,79 the subjective appraisal of exposure to adversity may indeed be more strongly linked to psychopathology.

Retrospective designs do not assure total accuracy of all forms of collected information. However, specifically for birth weight, recall seems relatively accurate. In a meta-analysis of 40 studies, the agreement between recalled and recorded birth weight was 90%.80 Moreover, the majority of these studies did not find influence of education levels or SES on the recall ability.80 Of direct relevance to our research, the self-report of early life context, including birth weight, has been reported as valid and reliable to operationalise epidemiologic studies.81 With regard to the Childhood Trauma Screener, it has been shown to have solid psychometric properties, with acceptable internal consistency for childhood abuse and convergent validity with the Adversity Childhood Experiences51 questionnaire.82

The UKB allows the exploration of data from multiple participant measures, including laboratorial findings but also imaging and other tests which could allow the investigation of sensitive outcomes (eg, presence of atherosclerosis plaques). However, we decided to focus on ICD-10 codes as outcomes. For example, for type 2 diabetes, the ICD-10 code represents a more accurate and holistic reflection of a clinical diagnosis of diabetes compared with relying on a single, potentially unconfirmed, HbA1c measurement. A critical limitation of using a random or single HbA1c value for diagnosis in a real-world setting is that many individuals may already be on diabetes treatment at the time of the HbA1c measurement. If an individual is already on glucose-lowering medication (eg, metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin), their HbA1c level will likely be suppressed and may fall below the diagnostic threshold, even if they have underlying diabetes requiring treatment. Relying solely on HbA1c in such cases would lead to under-ascertainment of individuals with diabetes, as treated patients might be misclassified as non-diabetic or pre-diabetic based on their medicated HbA1c levels. Conversely, an ICD-10 diagnosis inherently accounts for the need for treatment. The ICD-10 coding system provides a standardised and clinically validated framework for disease classification. Its use ensures that our definition of disease aligns with established clinical practice guidelines and facilitates comparability with other studies that use routinely collected healthcare data. We believe this approach strengthens the validity of our findings by precisely identifying individuals who have undergone a thorough diagnostic process by their healthcare providers.

Systematic analysis and meta-analysis studies show that individuals exposed to adversity early in life are at higher risk of maladaptive behaviours, diseases and conditions such as lower physical activity, overweight/obesity, diabetes, smoking, heavy alcohol use, poor self-rated health, cancer, heart disease, respiratory disease, sexual risk taking, mental illness and problematic alcohol use.5 According to preclinical and clinical models, early adverse events disrupt the homeostatic control of immune responses and lead to an enduring inflammatory dysregulation, expressed as increased blood levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor alpha, and such low-grade inflammation persists throughout adulthood.83 84 Elevated levels of these mediators are believed to contribute to most of the age-associated diseases such as diabetes, osteoporosis and atherosclerosis since all share an inflammatory pathogenesis.85 Palmer et al demonstrated an association between increased inflammation (measured by CRP) in childhood and increased risk of developing psychotic disorder, severe depression and higher levels of insulin resistance.86 The concept of atherosclerosis, the primary cause of coronary artery disease, stroke and peripheral vascular disease, as a chronic inflammatory disease is widely accepted.87 Inflammation is also involved in the pathophysiology of atrial fibrillation, the most common cardiac arrhythmia.88 Likewise, major psychiatric disorders such as depression, substance abuse, psychosis and anxiety are also associated with low-grade systemic inflammation,89 90 where they seem to precede the development of such diseases.91 92 Early life adversity may be related to a common cascade of events linked to both cardiometabolic and mental diseases, which are inherently associated with the ageing process.93 Our results reinforce the UKB with its rich phenotypic, biological and neuroimaging assessments as a key resource to help understand the long-term consequences of early adversity exposure and delineate specific vulnerabilities for targeted prevention over the life course with implications for early prevention, diagnosis and treatment.
