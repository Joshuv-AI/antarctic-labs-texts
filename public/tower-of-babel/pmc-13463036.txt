Design and development of a high-efficiency sustainable wireless charging system for autonomous electric vehicles powered by renewable energy sources for remote locations
Authors: Chaloemphol Kaewthep, R Venugopal, Subbulakshmy Ramamurthi, Velmurugan Palani, Ch N Narasimha Rao, Liew Tze Hui, S M Hasan Mahmud, Mohammed Al Awadh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the growing demand for Electric Vehicles (EVs), the expansion of sustainable and highly efficient charging system based solutions, particularly in areas where the conventional energy sources are limited. This article aims to reduce dependency on the conventional grid by developing a highly-efficient wireless autonomous EV charging system powered by solar Photovoltaic. The work focuses on developing a completely “green autonomous EV” wireless autonomous EV charging system by integrating the solar energy harvesting and a wireless inductive power transfer system which offers a clean, renewable and convenient energy source for wireless EV charging applications. This study has been carried out with the standards set for WPT systems (like SAE J 2954). For the simulation and experimental study, the proposed system is designed for 3.3 kW. From the simulation results and experimental prototype outcomes, it is evident that the system achieves a high efficiency charging, while the use of photovoltaic power makes the system environmentally sustainable. This research provides a viable solution for islanded locations, promoting the adoption of renewable energy sources for autonomous EV charging infrastructure, and contributing to the reduction of carbon emissions. Introduction

Motivation

Increased adoption of autonomous EVs exemplifies a serious step towards reducing greenhouse gas emissions and reducing dependence on fossil fuels. However, the deployment of AEVs faces several challenges across the globe, one of the most prominent being the availability of convenient and sustainable charging infrastructure, especially in remote or islanded locations that lack access to reliable power grids. In such isolated environments, traditional charging solutions relying on grid electricity may not be feasible due to the challenges associated with logistical and costs of establishing grid connections1. Instead, renewable energy sources like solar photovoltaic (PV) present a sustainable alternative. Harnessing solar energy offers not only an environmentally friendly solution but also helps mitigate the dependence on fossil fuel-based energy systems in these regions. However, for completely realizing the potential of solar-powered charging for AEVs, there is a need for efficient, user-friendly, and scalable charging systems. However, AEVs can be charged by means of conductive and inductive wireless. Wireless charging, which eliminates the need for physical cables and connectors, can offer the convenience required for easy AEV adoption while reducing wear and tear on components. Coupling wireless charging technology with PV systems holds the promise of creating a highly efficient, sustainable charging infrastructure for AEVs in off-grid or islanded locations. By integrating advanced energy management, power electronics, and renewable energy technologies, this system can provide an eco-friendly, reliable, and convenient charging solution, contributing to the global transition towards clean energy and sustainable transportation systems2.

Literature background

EVs penetration into modern transportation is inevitable. The major challenges associated with AEVs are limited drive range, battery life span, charging infrastructure, inconvenient charging experience, high cost and sustainability. Charging infrastructure challenges are limited and grid dependant charging stations, charging time, renewable energy integration and compatibility. The feasible solution for the research challenges associated with the charging of AEV may be conventional grid independent, renewable sources integrated wireless comfortable charging.

Several studies have explored wireless power transfer (WPT) for AEVs, focusing on improving energy efficiency, charging speed, and convenience. A study by Ramadoss, et al.3 proposed an improved resonant wireless charging system that increased energy transfer efficiency to over 90%, making it more viable for real-world applications. Kohar, et al.4 discussed the key developments in wireless charging, focusing on resonant inductive coupling as the most promising technique due to its efficiency and practicality. Meanwhile, Newbolt, et al.5 proposed dynamic wireless charging systems that allow AEVs to charge while in motion, thereby improving charging convenience and reducing time.

Kashani, et al.6 developed a solar-powered charging station with a focus on maximizing energy conversion efficiency. Their system used advanced MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) algorithms, which improved PV utilization under varying environmental conditions. Another study by Venugopal and Chandrasekar7 proposed an autonomous off-grid PV-powered charging system optimized for islanded regions. Subudhi, et al.8 explored hybrid systems where solar energy is stored in a battery during the day for use at night or during cloudy conditions. Their system showed improved stability and reliability in islanded locations. Similarly, Venugopal, et al.9 developed a hybrid PV and wind energy-based charging station for AEVs, focusing on energy reliability in isolated areas. Efficient energy management is essential for PV-powered charging systems, especially in islanded locations where grid power is unavailable. Takeda10 presented an intelligent energy management system for off-grid AEV charging stations that dynamically adjusts the charging rate based on solar energy availability and AEV demand. Kumar and Samanta11 demonstrated a system that integrates AI-driven demand prediction algorithms to optimize the charging schedule and reduce energy wastage. Kavin, et al.12 explored the impact of environmental factors like temperature and shading on PV efficiency and their subsequent effects on wireless charging performance. Huang, et al.13 addressed the issue of misalignment in wireless charging systems, developing a novel compensation topology to reduce energy loss.

Rituraj, et al.14 introduced a new class of power electronics designed to minimize switching losses, improving the overall efficiency of wireless power transfer systems by up to 15%. Zhang, et al.15 conducted a life-cycle assessment of a PV-powered wireless charging system and concluded that it reduces overall carbon emissions by 30% compared to conventional grid-powered charging stations. Zade, et al.16 Studied the environmental benefits of solar-based AEV charging stations in remote areas, finding that they contribute significantly to reducing fossil fuel reliance. Shahin, et al.17 explored the design of PV-based micro grids for AEV charging in remote areas, showing how such systems could be scalable and economically viable.

Circuit diagram synthesis and operating modes

Proposed work

The circuit diagram in Fig. 2 represents a system that integrates a photovoltaic (PV) energy source with an autonomous electric vehicle (AEV) battery through a complex power conversion process, involving DC-DC converters, resonant compensation, and rectification. The system begins with a PV panel, which generates a direct current (DC) output denoted as VDC. The capacitors C1 and C2 provide filtering or energy storage to smooth out the voltage also act as boost capacitors for the boosting purpose. The inductors LB1 and LB2 are placed in parallel with the PV source and act as boost inductors helps in achieving boost functionality. The most prominent part of the converter model is dual T-Type converters to attain 5 level output at the inverter. The two T-Type cell DC-DC converters are labelled as “T-Type Cell 1” and “T-Type Cell 2”. For wireless power transfer capability wireless coils along with simple Series- series (S–S) compensation were employed. The compensation capacitors are named as CT and CR for transmitter and receiver correspondingly. Similarly, coils of transmitter and receiver are named as LT and LR. The resonant compensation circuit helps in efficiently transferring power through inductive coupling (represented by mutual inductance m). The series-series compensation enhances power transfer efficiency by reducing reactive power losses, making the system more suitable for wireless or high-efficiency power transfer applications. The h bridge rectifier on the receiver side converts the high frequency AC voltage into DC voltage. The output voltage from this rectifier is directed towards charging the AEV battery. On the rectifier side capacitor CDC provides additional smoothing to stabilize the voltage delivered to the battery. The terminal voltage and current of AEV battery is terminated as Vb and Ib respectively. The circuit is designed to efficiently transfer energy from a PV source to an AEV battery. It uses two T-Type cell DC-DC converters to modulate and regulate the power, followed by a series-series resonant compensation network to ensure efficient energy transfer, likely in a wireless or partially wireless manner. The energy is then rectified and used to charge the AEV battery. The combination of high-efficiency converters, resonant circuits, and a rectifier ensures minimal energy loss throughout the process.

Operating sub modes and current paths

The system’s operation relies on specific sub modes to manage current flow and charge storage components. In the first sub mode, the inductors LB1 and LB2 are charged by the voltages across the respective DC-link capacitors C1 and C2. This charging process occurs through independent power loops, such as C1, LB1, and S1, and C2, LB2, and S2. In the complementary sub mode, capacitors C3 and C4 are charged by the inductors LB1 and LB2, as well as by the voltages across C1 and C2. These charging paths include power loops such as C1, LB1, S3, S5, and C3, as well as C2, LB2, S4, S5, and C4. During the generation of zero and the first positive or negative voltage levels, the power loops function as integrated boost circuits, known as Dual-Boost (DB) circuits. In this configuration, the four-quadrant switch S5 remains continuously ON, while the paired switches S1/S2 and S3/S4 alternate based on a complementary duty cycle D. These switching patterns facilitate consistent energy transfer between components, ensuring efficient charging and discharging of the inductors and capacitors over each switching interval.

Capacitor voltage regulation and DB circuit dynamics

The dual sub mode operation of the dual T-type inverter plays a crucial role in regulating the voltage of capacitors. In the first sub mode, the boost inductors LB1 and LB2 are charged, while in the second sub mode, capacitors C3 and C4 are charged. This coordinated operation allows the inverter to maintain an energy balance between the main DC-link capacitors C1 and C2 and the auxiliary capacitors C3 and C4. Such dynamic energy balancing enhances overall voltage stability and supports high-efficiency power conversion. For the zero and first positive or negative voltage levels, the consistent activation of the DB circuits is critical. With S5 held ON and switches S1, S2, S3, and S4 toggling as per their respective duty cycles, the inverter effectively generates intermediate voltage levels. This design ensures synchronized charging and discharging cycles for the capacitors and inductors, enabling smooth and reliable power delivery to the grid.

Generation of top positive and negative voltage levels

The generation of the highest positive and negative voltage levels involves a distinct operational mode compared to the intermediate levels. These voltage levels are produced through the series connection of capacitors C3and C4. As illustrated in Figs. 9 and 10, switches S1and S2 remain constantly ON, enabling the grid current to flow through while discharging C3and C4. In this mode, no charging occurs for C3and C4, as their energy is fully utilized for output voltage generation. To ensure symmetrical operation of the inductors LB1and LB2, switches S1and S2 are operated under a consistent DC duty cycle D.

Experimental result analysis

Overview

The comprehensive experimental setup presented in Fig. 13 represents a sophisticated and well-integrated approach to wireless power transfer, combining innovative inverter design, resonant compensation, and robust energy storage. This setup highlights the potential of integrating WPT technology with renewable energy systems, paving the way for efficient and sustainable autonomous EV charging solutions.

Hardware setup.

The geographical location of the setup, with coordinates pointing to a 100 kW photovoltaic (PV) plant at SRM Institute of Science and Technology in kattankulathur, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India. This PV plant might serve as the primary energy source for the DC input to the inverter, emphasizing the system’s renewable energy integration. The heart of the system is the proposed 5L T-type inverter, a critical component responsible for converting DC power to high-frequency AC, necessary for resonant wireless power transfer. The inverter is connected to the primary compensator, which includes resonant capacitors and coils that ensure the proper alignment of voltage and current phases, optimizing the transfer efficiency. Between these compensators are the wireless coils, which facilitate inductive power transfer across an air gap. These coils are the core of the WPT mechanism, enabling efficient energy transfer without physical connections. On the receiver side, the rectifier converts the high-frequency AC from the wireless coils back into DC, which is then used to charge the battery pack22. The battery pack is an integral part of the system, acting as energy storage and providing stable output voltage and current to the connected load. Control signal processing are managed by an FPGA Spartan 6 board, which likely handles the inverter’s switching logic and ensures precise modulation for optimal operation. A Keysight Digital Storage Oscilloscope (DSO) captures and displays waveforms for voltage and current at various stages, offering insights into the system’s performance. The setup also includes voltage and current probes, which are strategically placed to monitor key parameters like inverter output, wireless coil characteristics, and battery charging dynamics.

The operating frequency of 85 kHz was selected to align with the international wireless power transfer standard defined in SAE International SAE J2954, which specifies a nominal operating band of 81.39–90 kHz for EV wireless charging systems. At 85 kHz, the reduced size of passive components like inductors and compensation capacitors enhances the system’s ability to achieve high packing densities and allows for a more compact system design. However, the increasing frequency will increase switching losses. Therefore, in these designs, soft-switching techniques such as Zero Voltage Switching (ZVS) and Zero-current Switching (ZCS) will be used to minimize turn-on losses and maximize overall efficiency. Skin and proximity effect losses in the wireless coils become more substantial at higher frequencies, which increases the effective AC resistance and associated copper losses in coils. To alleviate this effect, Litz wire is used to decrease AC resistance, resulting in improved coil efficiency. Additionally, the coil structure has been designed to achieve the best balance between the highest Q value and an acceptable thermal performance. Additionally, magnetic core losses generally increase with an increase in frequency. Therefore, ferrite materials have been selected that are specifically designed for high-frequency applications. Furthermore, proper EMI filtering and shielding measures have been incorporated into the designs to meet regulatory requirements and provide stable performance.

Conclusions

This paper provides evidence of an experimental system development for a continually available renewable energy based Autonomous Electric Vehicle (AEV) wireless power (charging) system. The system architecture comprises of a multi-phase DC/DC power conversion device (high gain boost converter), a multiphase DC/AC inverter (3 or more levels with T type configuration) and a frequency compensated (series-series compensation) inductive power transfer (IPT) system operating at 85 kHz as defined in SAE International SAE J2954 (Level 1). The system is rated at 3.3 kW and uses a 5 kWp photovoltaic (PV) solar array located at the SRM Institute of Science and Technology (SRMIST) in Kattankulathur, Chennai, India. The experimental results showed peak DC/DC efficiency of 92.6% with 160 mm air gap separation between the transmitter and receiver coils, indicating that the system provides effective transfer of electrical power with minimized conduction and switching losses. The added use of a multi-level inverter improves the quality (or smoothing) of the voltage waveforms that result from the inverting process while the optimized series-series compensation improves the power transfer capability of and the overall stability of the IPT system. The combining PV generation with standardized inductive wireless charging, the study provides a physically and economically environmentally responsible and socially accountable means to charge AEVs in rural areas where it would otherwise be impossible. Furthermore, this research establishes an example framework for developing the necessary supporting infrastructures for a sustainable transportation infrastructure.
