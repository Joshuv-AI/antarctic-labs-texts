Antibacterial properties of bioengineered silver nanoparticles from Pastinaca sativa against Enterococcus faecalis biofilms in endodontic therapy
Authors: Amir Ali Didar, Shaghyegh Ghadimi, Ailar Yousefbeigi, Pouria Farahani, Zahra Bahman, Hossein Khajeh Salehani, Zohreh Asgari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEndodontic therapy often faces the challenge of complete infection eradication and preventing recurrence, with Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) being the most significant cause of treatment failure. Using materials with antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties can increase the success rate of endodontic treatment. Therefore, this study investigated the properties of silver nanoparticles synthesized using Pastinaca sativa (P. sativa) root extract (Ag@PSR).MethodsIn this study, P. sativa root extract was used as both the reducing and stabilizing agent. Synthesis parameters (silver nitrate concentration, time, and temperature) were optimized using UV-Vis spectroscopy. Characterization analyses, including XRD, FTIR, FESEM, and TEM, were performed on the Ag@PSR nanoparticles. The antibacterial, anti-biofilm, and antioxidant properties of Ag@PSR nanoparticles were also investigated.ResultsOptimal reaction conditions were determined to be a 15 mM silver nitrate concentration, a temperature of 85 °C, and a reaction time of 15 min for the synthesis of Ag@PSR nanoparticles. XRD analysis confirmed the synthesis of silver nanoparticles. FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of functional groups from the extract within the Ag@PSR structure. Morphological and size analysis using FESEM and TEM images revealed the formation of spherical, uniform nanoparticles with sizes ranging from 15 to 40 nm. Antibacterial assays revealed that Ag@PSR exhibited an MIC of 500 µg/mL against E. faecalis. Similar inhibitory activity was also observed against other tested strains. Moreover, Ag@PSR reduced E. faecalis biofilm formation by 86.7%. These nanoparticles also scavenged 91% of DPPH radicals at a concentration of 500 µg/mL.ConclusionThese results indicate the high potential of Ag@PSR nanoparticles as a multifaceted agent for treating oral infections and improving dental materials, especially in endodontics.Keywords: Pastinaca sativa, Green synthesis, Silver nanoparticles, Antibacterial, Anti-biofilm, EndodonticsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Materials science, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The human oral cavity, with approximately 500 different bacterial species, forms a complex and dynamic ecosystem. In this environment, polymicrobial biofilms are considered the norm. Biofilms are intricate structures of bacteria encased in an extracellular matrix1. The formation of these biofilms is the primary cause of dental caries and periodontal diseases. Due to their complex structure, biofilms exhibit high resistance to the body’s immune defenses and antibacterial agents, making their eradication challenging2.

Endodontic therapy is one of the main treatment methods for combating dental infections. The primary goal of this treatment is to eliminate the microbial biofilm from the root canal system and establish an optimal seal within the root canal3. In this method, the removal of root canal infection is attempted through mechanical instrumentation, irrigation, and medication. However, ensuring complete biofilm removal and preventing secondary infection in this treatment method is difficult4.

Among the numerous oral bacterial species, Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis), as a Gram-positive facultative anaerobic bacterium, is recognized as the primary cause of persistent root canal infections. This bacterium’s high ability to form biofilms and its intrinsic resistance to multiple antibiotics make its complete eradication difficult5. Given the existing challenges and the increasing resistance of bacteria, especially the emergence of multi-drug resistant strains like vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), the need to develop novel therapeutic solutions is felt more than ever6. Therefore, many studies have focused on developing new sealing materials with high antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties that can prevent the establishment of secondary infections7.

In recent years, nanotechnology and the use of nanoparticles have offered innovative solutions for addressing human challenges, including antibiotic resistance. Among various types of nanoparticles, metallic nanoparticles have demonstrated promising antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties, making them a highly promising avenue for developing novel antimicrobial treatments8,9. Among different metallic nanoparticles, such as gold, silver, zinc oxide, copper oxide, magnesium oxide, platinum, and others, silver is recognized as the most significant antibacterial agent10. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have also garnered considerable attention due to their small size, high surface-to-volume ratio, stability, and various biological properties. They’ve been extensively studied in biomedical applications, particularly as antimicrobial and anti-biofilm agents11. Furthermore, many studies have shown that AgNPs also possess antifungal, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties12. Therefore, the use of AgNPs can be a promising solution for developing dental materials with anti-biofilm and biocompatible properties to prevent and treat oral infections, especially root canal infections caused by E. faecalis. Recent studies have demonstrated their potential in endodontic therapy13. For instance, Saygi et al. reported that silver nanoparticles synthesized via a green method, owing to their antibacterial properties, can be used as an intra-canal irrigant for posterior teeth14. Similarly, Fan et al. observed that resins incorporating silver nanoparticles effectively eliminated bacteria15.

AgNPs can be synthesized by chemical, physical, and green methods. Physical synthesis of nanoparticles requires advanced facilities, high costs, and significant time. Chemical synthesis of nanoparticles, due to the use of toxic and hazardous reducing agents, prevents their use in biomedical studies, especially in clinical applications16. In contrast to these limitations, the green synthesis method for nanoparticles has been developed as a cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and biocompatible approach that does not require advanced facilities. This method uses plant extracts as reducing agents for nanoparticle synthesis, resulting in nanoparticles with a plant extract coating that can improve their properties17.

The plant Pastinaca sativa (P. sativa) is a biennial plant belonging to the Apiaceae family. Furanocoumarins, flavonoids, and polyacetylenes are the most prominent active compounds in P. sativa, responsible for its wide range of medicinal properties. Studies have shown that these compounds exhibit anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, vasodilatory, antifungal, and antibacterial activities18. Furthermore, P. sativa possesses high antioxidant properties, which can help neutralize free radicals and protect cells from oxidative damage19.

In the present study, AgNPs were synthesized for the first time using P. sativa root extract (Ag@PSR) via a green method. The nanoparticles were thoroughly characterized in terms of their structure and morphology. Furthermore, their antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities were evaluated specifically against E. faecalis, the primary causative agent of root canal treatment failure, and their antioxidant properties were also assessed. These findings highlight the novelty and potential of Ag@PSR nanoparticles as a multifunctional, eco-friendly, and biocompatible agent for improving endodontic therapy outcomes.

Methods

Materials

Silver nitrate (AgNO₃, 99.99% purity) was used for nanoparticle synthesis. Methanol from Sigma-Aldrich was used for extraction. For microbial testing, two Gram-negative bacteria: Escherichia coli (E. coli; ATCC 25922) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae; ATCC 9997); three Gram-positive bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus; ATCC 29213), Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans; ATCC 35668), and Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis; ATCC 29212); and the fungus Candida albicans (C. albicans; ATCC 10231) were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. Zantox kits (Kavosh Arian Azma Co) were utilized to assess antioxidant properties. In all experiments, double-distilled water was used as the solvent.

Extraction

The roots of P. sativa were collected from a wild population in the Qazvin region, Iran. The roots were identified by Dr. M.R. Joharchi from the Ferdowsi University of Mashhad Herbarium (No. 51255). The roots of P. sativa were acquired and washed three times with distilled water. The roots were then dried in the shade and ground. Next, 100 g of the plant roots were mixed with 500 mL of methanol, and this mixture was placed on a shaker for 72 h. After this period, to remove plant debris from the extract, the plant suspension was filtered using a Buchner funnel and Whatman 41 filter paper. Following filtration, a rotary evaporator was used to reduce the solvent volume and concentrate the extract. The resulting concentrated extract was then placed in a 40 °C incubator for 24 h to ensure complete solvent removal. The obtained dried extract was stored in the refrigerator until further experiments (Scheme 1).

Preparation of P. sativa root extract.

Synthesis of Ag@PSR nanoparticles

To synthesize Ag@PSR nanoparticles, similar to the study by Zare-Bidaki et al.20 with slight modifications, silver nitrate was used as the silver ion source and P. sativa extract as both the reducing and capping agent.

For the synthesis, 24 mg of silver nitrate was first dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water and vigorously stirred on a stirrer at 85 °C. Next, 100 mg of P. sativa extract was added to 10 mL of water and sonicated for 10 min to ensure complete dispersion. To enhance the reducing activity of the phytochemicals present in the extract, the pH was adjusted to 12 using a NaOH solution. This alkaline condition facilitated the deprotonation of hydroxyl groups in the plant metabolites (–OH → –O−), thereby improving their ability to reduce Ag⁺ ions and stabilize the formed nanoparticles. Subsequently, the pH-adjusted plant extract was added dropwise to the silver nitrate solution under vigorous stirring.

Upon the addition of the P. sativa extract, the silver nitrate solution rapidly changed color to dark brown. The resulting suspension was vigorously stirred for 15 min. After this period, the absorbance of the suspension was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer across the 300 to 500 nm wavelength range. The obtained suspension was then centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting precipitate was washed three times with distilled water to completely remove excess ions and unreacted plant extracts. Finally, the remaining precipitate was dried in a 40 °C incubator for 24 h, and the resulting Ag@PSR nanoparticles were stored for further experiments (Scheme 2).

Ag@PSR nanoparticles synthesis.

Optimization of Ag@PSR nanoparticle synthesis parameters

In the current study, to optimize the synthesis of Ag@PSR nanoparticles using P. sativa extract, the extract concentration was kept constant, and three key parameters were optimized: silver nitrate concentration, reaction time, and reaction temperature. The selected ranges of silver nitrate concentration, reaction time, and temperature were based on preliminary experiments and literature reports20, with the optimal conditions determined by UV-Vis monitoring of nanoparticle formation.

First, silver nitrate concentration was optimized. For this, various concentrations of silver nitrate (5, 10, 15, and 20 mM) were prepared. Then, 10 mL of plant extract with a concentration of 10 mg/mL and a pH of 12 was added dropwise to the silver nitrate solutions. The reaction was conducted for 30 min at room temperature. After this period, the absorbance of the resulting suspensions was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer across the 300–500 nm wavelength range. The silver nitrate concentration that showed the highest absorbance was considered the optimal concentration.

Subsequently, the reaction was performed using the optimal silver nitrate concentration at different time points. After the addition of the plant extract, the absorbance of the resulting suspension was measured immediately, and then at 15, 30, 45, and 60 min. The time point at which the absorbance did not show a significant increase was considered the optimal reaction time.

Finally, the reaction was conducted using the optimal silver nitrate concentration and optimal reaction time at different temperatures: room temperature, 55 °C, and 85 °C. The silver nitrate solution was prepared and placed at these temperatures, and the plant extract was slowly added. After 15 min, the absorbance of the resulting suspensions was examined, and the temperature that yielded the highest absorbance was considered the optimal temperature.

Nanoparticle characterization

The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized using multiple techniques. UV-Vis spectroscopy confirmed nanoparticle formation. X-ray Diffraction (XRD, Philips PW 1800, 10–80°) was used to determine crystallinity and size. Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR, PerkinElmer Spectrum Two, Model L160000U) spectra of both nanoparticles and plant extract were recorded to identify functional groups involved in synthesis. A dynamic light scattering (DLS) device (NanoBrook 90Plus-Brookhaven Instruments, model 18051; USA) was used to measure the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential. Surface morphology was examined using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM, TESCAN MIRA3), while Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, Zeiss-EM10C-100 KV) provided detailed information on particle morphology and size.

Assessment of antibacterial and antifungal properties

In this study, the antibacterial and antifungal properties of Ag@PSR nanoparticles and the P. sativa root extract were evaluated against five bacterial strains and one fungal strain using the broth microdilution method, based on the study by Mortazavi-Derazkola et al.21.

Initially, 100 µL of various concentrations of silver nanoparticles (2 mg/mL) and plant extract (8 mg/mL) were dispensed into sterile 96-well plates. Subsequently, a 0.5 McFarland standard of the bacterial strains (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, S. mutans, and E. faecalis) and the fungal strain (C. albicans) was prepared. This 0.5 McFarland suspension was then diluted 1:150 using Müller Hinton Broth, resulting in a final bacterial concentration of approximately 106 CFU/mL. Then, 100 µL of this diluted suspension was added to each well of the 96-well plate. The plate was gently shaken for 30 s and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the plate was visually inspected, and the lowest concentration of the substance at which no turbidity was observed was reported as the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Assessment of Anti-Biofilm effects against E. faecalis

In the present study, the anti-biofilm effects of Ag@PSR nanoparticles were investigated using the crystal violet method (Scheme 3), with slight modifications based on the study by Swidan et al.22.

Anti-biofilm effects of Ag@PSR nanoparticles by crystal violet assay.

Initially, concentrations of 250 (1/2 MIC), 125 (1/4 MIC), and 62.5 (1/8 MIC) µg/mL of Ag@PSR nanoparticles were prepared using Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB, Merck, Germany) containing 1% glucose. 100 µL of these solutions were dispensed into sterile 96-well plates. Next, a 0.5 McFarland standard of E. faecalis was prepared and diluted 1:150 using TSB containing 1% glucose. 100 µL of this diluted bacterial suspension was then added to the wells containing the nanoparticles. Additionally, 100 µL of TSB medium containing 1% glucose along with 100 µL of diluted bacterial suspension was considered as the positive control. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

After 24 h, the supernatant was gently discarded, and the wells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, 200 µL of methanol was used to fix the bacteria. After discarding the methanol, 200 µL of 0.1% crystal violet stain was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The stain was then removed from the wells, and the wells were washed three times with PBS. Finally, 200 µL of isopropanol was added to each well, and the optical density (OD) of the wells was measured at 570 nm.

The amount of biofilm formation was calculated based on the OD of each well compared to the OD of the positive control wells (ODc) using the following formula:

% of biofilm compared to the control group = (OD of sample well / OD of positive control well) × 100.

Assessment of antioxidant property

In this study, the antioxidant property was assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) method following the Zantox kit protocol (Kavosh Arian Azma Co, Iran)23.

To perform the DPPH test, various concentrations of P. sativa root extract (10, 7.5, 5, and 2.5 mg/mL) and Ag@PSR nanoparticles (62.5, 125, 250, and 500 µg/mL) were prepared. Then, 10 µL of each nanoparticle suspension were added to the wells of a 96-well plate and incubated with 250 µL of DPPH solution for 15 min in the dark. After this incubation period, the absorbance of the wells was measured at 517 nm and compared with the absorbance of the control group. In this kit, Trolox was used as the standard. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

The percentage of DPPH radical inhibition was calculated using the following formula:

DPPH radical inhibition (%) = ((Ac-As)/Ac) × 100.

In this formula, Ac represents the absorbance of the DPPH solution as the control, and As represents the absorbance of the wells containing nanoparticles after 15 min of incubation with the DPPH solution. The concentration of a substance that causes 50% inhibition of DPPH radicals was also reported as the IC50 value.

Results and discussion

Optimization of Ag@PSR nanoparticle synthesis

In the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles, plant extracts reduce Ag⁺ ions to Ag0, causing the silver nitrate solution to rapidly turn brown due to Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR). SPR, observed around 400–500 nm using UV-Vis spectroscopy23, provides information on nanoparticle formation, size, and yield. Higher SPR intensity indicates more nanoparticles, a shift to shorter wavelengths suggests smaller particles, and a broader peak reflects a wider size distribution24. In the current study, three crucial parameters for silver nanoparticle synthesis—silver nitrate concentration, temperature, and reaction time—were optimized for the synthesis of Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

Initially, the reaction was conducted in the presence of various silver nitrate concentrations (5, 10, 15, and 20 mM). The results from the UV-Vis analysis (Fig. 1a) showed that initially, as the silver nitrate concentration increased, the absorbance of the resulting suspension significantly increased. This increase was observed at 10 and 15 mM concentrations. However, with a further increase in silver nitrate concentration to 20 mM, the absorbance of the nanoparticles significantly decreased. A similar trend was observed in a study by M. Zare-Bidaki et al., where increasing silver nitrate concentration initially increased absorption intensity, but excessive silver nitrate led to a decrease in UV-Vis absorption20. This reduction in absorption intensity might be due to the aggregation of nanoparticles at high silver nitrate concentrations, which diminishes absorbance25. Based on the UV-Vis results of Ag@PSR nanoparticles, a 15 mM silver nitrate concentration was selected as the optimal synthesis concentration.

UV-Vis absorption of Ag@PSR nanoparticles at different silver nitrate concentrations (a), various reaction times (b), and different reaction temperatures (c).

Next, with a 15 mM silver nitrate concentration, the UV-Vis absorbance of the Ag@PSR suspension was measured immediately after the start of the reaction, and then at 15, 30, 45, and 60 min. The results (Fig. 1b) indicated that the absorbance increased up to 15 min after the reaction began, but no further change in the absorbance of the Ag@PSR nanoparticle suspension was observed thereafter. Accordingly, an optimal reaction time of 15 min was determined for the synthesis of Ag@PSR nanoparticles. The lack of an increase in absorption intensity after 15 min suggested that the P. sativa extract can rapidly form silver nanoparticles (within 15 min) and reach reaction equilibrium. Consistent with the current study, various research has shown that using plant extracts for silver nanoparticle synthesis can lead to rapid nanoparticle formation26.

Finally, the Ag@PSR nanoparticle synthesis reaction was also performed at room temperature, 55 °C, and 85 °C (Fig. 1c). The results showed that increasing the temperature significantly increased nanoparticle synthesis, with the highest UV-Vis absorbance for Ag@PSR nanoparticles measured at 85 °C. Therefore, 85 °C was considered the optimal temperature for the Ag@PSR nanoparticle synthesis reaction. A study by Razali et al. also indicated that increasing temperature can lead to higher activation energy for molecules, thereby increasing the reaction rate27.

In a study by Singh and Mijakovic, silver nanoparticles were green-synthesized using Viridibacillus bacteria over 48 h28, whereas in the current study, Ag@SPR nanoparticles were synthesized in 15 min. Based on available research, the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles using plant extracts generally offers a faster reaction rate compared to green synthesis using bacteria29,30.

XRD

XRD analysis is a valuable tool for examining the crystalline structure of nanoparticles. Each crystalline material possesses a unique XRD spectrum based on its specific crystal lattice. In this study, the XRD spectrum of Ag@PSR nanoparticles is presented in Fig. 2.

XRD spectrum of Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

The results revealed that Ag@PSR nanoparticles exhibited four distinct peaks at 2θ angles of 38.1º, 44.2º, 64.5º, and 77.4º. Based on previous studies, these peaks correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) Bragg planes, respectively. The observed XRD pattern for Ag@PSR nanoparticles showed excellent agreement with the face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice structure of silver (Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card number: 01-087-0717). This finding is consistent with research by Kordy et al., where synthesized silver nanoparticles displayed four peaks at similar angles (38.31º, 44.42º, 64.67º, 77.54º)31. Furthermore, silver nanoparticles synthesized using Artemisia turcomanica32 and Perilla frutescens33 plant extracts also exhibited an FCC structure. The presence of only four peaks corresponding to the silver crystal structure and the absence of any other peaks in the XRD spectrum indicate the high purity of the synthesized nanoparticles.

Beyond identifying the crystalline structure, XRD analysis can also be used to determine the crystalline size of nanoparticles. This is achieved by substituting the XRD analysis results into the Debye-Scherrer formula (D = 0.9 λ/βcosθ). In this formula, D represents the crystalline size, λ is the wavelength of the X-ray radiation used, β is the corrected full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the peak in radians, and θ is the Bragg angle (half of 2θ) in radians34. Based on this calculation, the crystalline size of the Ag@PSR nanoparticles was determined to be 42.7 nm, which is comparable to the crystalline size of silver nanoparticles synthesized using Berberis vulgaris extract, which was approximately 50 nm35.

FTIR

FTIR analysis was performed in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 to identify the functional groups involved in the biosynthesis and stabilization of Ag@PSR nanoparticles (Fig. 3).

FTIR spectrum of Ag@PSR nanoparticles and P. sativa root extract.

In the spectrum of P. sativa extract, the absorption band at 3402 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching of phenolic or alcoholic groups, while the peak at 2926 cm−1 is related to C–H stretching of aliphatic chains36. The band at 1628 cm−1 represents C=O stretching of amide or carbonyl groups, the band at 1416 cm−1 corresponds to C–H bending or COO− stretching, and the peak at 1055 cm−1 is assigned to C–O stretching of alcohols or ethers37,38.

The FTIR spectrum of Ag@PSR nanoparticles displayed similar bands with slight shifts and changes in intensities, confirming the presence of plant-derived functional groups on the nanoparticle surface (Fig. 3). For instance, the broad O–H band around 3400 cm−1 showed a shift, suggesting hydrogen bonding, while the reduced intensity of the C =O band indicates the involvement of carbonyl groups in silver ion binding.

Similar findings were reported by Elangovan et al. where silver nanoparticles synthesized using Hemigraphis colorata extract showed bands at 3402 and 1635 cm−1, attributed to OH stretching and carbonyl groups of the extract, respectively39, which were involved in silver nanoparticle formation. Similarly, Sahli et al. observed a resemblance between the FTIR spectrum of silver nanoparticles synthesized using Rhazya stricta extract and the extract’s own FTIR spectrum40.

DLS and zeta potential

The hydrodynamic diameter of the Ag@PSR nanoparticles was determined using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis, based on the Brownian motion of the particles41. As depicted in Fig. 4, the average hydrodynamic diameter of the Ag@PSR nanoparticles was found to be 86.6 nm. Furthermore, the particle size distribution, expressed by the polydispersity index (PDI)42, was 0.307. It is important to note that the hydrodynamic diameter encompasses the actual size of the nanoparticle plus the surrounding hydration layer, which is why it is typically larger than the nanoparticle’s core size.

DLS results for Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

Zeta potential quantifies the surface charge of particles and serves as a crucial indicator for evaluating the stability of a suspension. A higher surface charge (either positive or negative) generally correlates with increased colloidal stability43. The results from the zeta potential analysis of the Ag@PSR nanoparticles indicated a zeta potential of −5.58 mV.

EDS, FESEM, and TEM

The elemental composition of Ag@PSR nanoparticles was investigated using Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS). The results from the EDS analysis (Fig. 5) showed that these nanoparticles showed a major peak at 3 keV, corresponding to silver, and a carbon peak at 0.28 keV. The presence of carbon in the EDS spectrum of Ag@PSR nanoparticles confirms the inclusion of functional groups from the plant extract in the nanoparticle structure, thereby validating the reducing role of the extract’s functional groups in nanoparticle formation. Furthermore, no other elements were observed in this spectrum, indicating the high purity of the synthesized nanoparticles. Similar observations of silver and carbon elements were reported in the EDS spectra of nanoparticles by Swidan et al.22 and Jabbar et al.44, mirroring the findings for Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

EDS analysis results of Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

FESEM images (Fig. 6) were used to examine the surface morphology of Ag@PSR nanoparticles. The FESEM analysis revealed that the nanoparticles were spherical and uniform in size. Furthermore, these images indicated that the synthesized nanoparticles were homogeneous, suggesting that P. sativa extract is a suitable reducing agent for synthesizing silver nanoparticles with uniform dimensions.

FESEM images of Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

To conduct a more detailed investigation into the morphology and size of Ag@PSR nanoparticles, TEM analysis was performed (Fig. 7). The TEM image further confirmed the synthesis of uniform, spherical nanoparticles. Based on the TEM images and the corresponding nanoparticle size histogram distribution (Fig. 8), the size of the Ag@PSR nanoparticles was measured to be in the range of 15 to 40 nm. In some areas, aggregation of nanoparticles was observed, which can be attributed to the presence of P. sativa root extract on the surface of the Ag@PSR nanoparticles. Additionally, a closer examination of Fig. 7 revealed a darker halo around the nanoparticles, attributed to the P. sativa extract coating, was also observed. A study by Abdel-Rahman found that silver nanoparticles synthesized using Portulaca oleracea L. extract had a spherical morphology and a size ranging from 5 to 40 nm45. The size and shape of nanoparticles are among the most crucial factors influencing their biological properties, as they directly impact the surface area and reactivity of the nanoparticles46.

Assessment of antibacterial and antifungal properties

In the current study, the antibacterial and antifungal properties of Ag@PSR nanoparticles and the P. sativa root extract were investigated against five bacterial strains (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, S. mutans, and E. faecalis) and the fungus C. albicans using the broth microdilution method. The results of this assessment are presented in Table 1.

Antibacterial and antifungal properties of P. sativa root extract and Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

The findings revealed that the P. sativa root extract was unable to inhibit the growth of any of the tested bacterial strains at the maximum concentration used (4 mg/mL). It could only inhibit the growth of C. albicans at a concentration of 4 mg/mL. In contrast, Ag@PSR nanoparticles successfully inhibited the growth of all tested bacterial and fungal strains. Ag@PSR nanoparticles exhibited the highest inhibitory effect on the C. albicans fungal strain, inhibiting its growth at a concentration of 62.5 µg/mL. Among the bacterial strains studied, Ag@PSR nanoparticles showed the most significant inhibitory effect on E. coli and S. aureus, inhibiting their growth at a concentration of 250 µg/mL. Furthermore, they inhibited the growth of E. faecalis at a concentration of 500 µg/mL.

In the current study, the nanoparticles showed a stronger antifungal effect against C. albicans compared to the bacteria studied. This difference is likely due to the distinct types of microorganisms. Fungi are eukaryotes, whereas bacteria are prokaryotes, and the structure of their cell walls is completely different20.

Various studies have indicated that the antibacterial properties of nanoparticles are related to their shape and size, with smaller nanoparticles having a higher surface-to-volume ratio exhibiting greater antibacterial activity47. Ag@PSR nanoparticles, with their small size (15 to 40 nm) and spherical shape, possess a high surface-to-volume ratio. A study by Acharya et al. showed that silver nanoparticles can destroy the bacterial cell wall. They also found that a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio of the nanoparticles leads to stronger antibacterial effects. In this study, spherical silver nanoparticles exhibited greater antibacterial activity than silver nanorods, likely due to the difference in their surface area48.

Silver nanoparticles eliminate bacteria through multiple pathways. Initially, nanoparticles adhere to the bacterial surface. This adherence causes damage to the bacterial membrane. With membrane damage, not only do intracellular components leak out, but nanoparticles can also more easily enter the bacterium49. Once inside the bacterium, nanoparticles can damage various proteins and bacterial DNA, disrupting vital bacterial mechanisms50. Furthermore, nanoparticles can induce the formation of free radicals within the bacterium. These radicals further damage the bacterium, ultimately leading to cell death51,52.

As shown, the MIC values of our synthesized nanoparticles are approximately similar to those reported in previous studies, highlighting their potential as an effective antimicrobial agent.

E. coli, K pneumoniae, S. aureus, S. mutans, and E. faecalis are all recognized as oral bacteria53. Among these bacterial strains, S. mutans and E. faecalis are considered the primary causes of root canal treatment failure due to their anaerobic nature54. Assessment of anti-biofilm effects against E. faecalis

The anti-biofilm activity of Ag@PSR nanoparticles at three concentrations — 250 µg/mL (1/2 MIC), 125 µg/mL (¼ MIC), and 62.5 µg/mL (⅛ MIC) — was evaluated using the crystal violet assay. The results of this test (Fig. 9) demonstrated that Ag@PSR nanoparticles at the 1/2 MIC concentration caused the greatest reduction in E. faecalis biofilms, with the biofilm percentage at this concentration being 13.3% compared to the control group. As the concentration decreased, the percentage of biofilm formation increased, reaching 87.3% at the ⅛ MIC concentration compared to the control group. Based on these findings, Ag@PSR nanoparticles present a promising option for eradicating E. faecalis biofilms.

Percentage of E. faecalis biofilms in the presence of Ag@PSR nanoparticles compared to the control.

Biofilms typically consist of bacterial cells encased in an extracellular matrix, which includes extracellular polysaccharides, DNA, and extracellular proteins62. Due to their intricate structure, biofilms can prevent the penetration of antibacterial agents, making their eradication more challenging. In root canal treatment, one of the most significant challenges is the complete decontamination of the root canal using antibacterial materials and establishing a proper seal to prevent re-infection and treatment failure63. Nanoparticles, owing to their small size, can penetrate these structures more easily, and their diverse antibacterial mechanisms make them more likely to disrupt these structures (Scheme 4)64. Based on conducted studies, one of the mechanisms by which nanoparticles reduce bacterial biofilm is their entry into the bacteria, which disrupts the function of enzymes involved in bacterial adhesion and prevents the formation of exopolysaccharides. This ultimately leads to a reduction in biofilm formation65.

Anti-biofilm and antibacterial mechanism of Ag@PSR nanoparticles.

Based on the antibacterial properties of Ag@PSR nanoparticles, it can be concluded that these nanoparticles are a suitable option as an antibacterial and antifungal agent. Furthermore, given their ability to prevent biofilm formation by E. faecalis, which is a primary cause of root canal treatment failure, these nanoparticles warrant further investigation for their potential use in antibacterial compounds for root canal therapy.

Assessment of antioxidant property

Free radicals are unstable molecules that can damage cells and contribute to various diseases, including inflammatory conditions. Substances with antioxidant properties can prevent cellular damage by neutralizing these free radicals66. In dentistry, antioxidants play a crucial role in maintaining oral health, especially in cases of inflammation and infection. Materials with antioxidant properties can reduce inflammation and aid in the healing process during root canal treatment67,68.

In the present study, the antioxidant properties of a P. sativa root extract, Ag@PSR nanoparticles, and the Trolox standard were investigated using the DPPH method. The results of this assay are presented in Fig. 10. The findings revealed that the antioxidant property was dose-dependent, meaning that as the dose increased, the percentage of DPPH radical inhibition also increased. According to a study conducted by Hasoon et al., the observed antioxidant activity was also dose-dependent69. An examination of the results showed that the IC50 values for the extract (Fig. 10a), nanoparticles (Fig. 10b), and Trolox (Fig. 10c) were 5.88 mg/mL, 167.45 µg/mL, and 513.13 µg/mL, respectively. Based on these results, the Ag@PSR exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, indicating that the synthesis of silver nanoparticles using the P. sativa root extract enhanced the antioxidant property.

DPPH radical inhibition in the presence of various concentrations of P. sativa root extract (a), Ag@PSR nanoparticles (b), and Trolox (c).

Herbal extracts contain various active compounds that exhibit antioxidant properties by donating a hydrogen atom from their hydroxyl groups to the DPPH free radical. Silver nanoparticles also show significant antioxidant effects by transferring hydrogen from the nanoparticles to free radicals, and the presence of effective plant compounds on the surface of silver nanoparticles can influence their antioxidant activity70. Various studies, including one by Alzubaidi et al., have shown that silver nanoparticles possess greater antioxidant effects than the extract alone71. Similarly, in a study by Baran et al., the IC50 of the synthesized silver nanoparticles for inhibiting the DPPH free radical was found to be 151.3 µg/mL, which is comparable to the current study72.

As mentioned, materials with antioxidant properties can reduce inflammation and assist in the healing process during root canal treatment67. Based on the results of the current study, Ag@PSR nanoparticles not only possess antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-biofilm properties but also exhibit high antioxidant capabilities. The findings suggest that Ag@PSR nanoparticles could be a suitable option for use in dental materials for root canal restoration and as sealing materials.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Pastinaca sativa extract can be used as a green, eco-friendly, and non-toxic agent for the synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag@PSR). The optimized nanoparticles, with spherical morphology and sizes ranging from 20 to 40 nm, exhibited notable antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, and anti-biofilm activities. These results highlight the potential of Ag@PSR nanoparticles as a cost-effective and biocompatible candidate for dental applications, particularly in managing oral infections and enhancing root canal treatments.

Future studies should focus on in vivo evaluations of the nanoparticles’ biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy, their incorporation into dental biomaterials, and long-term assessments of their clinical performance in managing oral infections and improving root canal treatment outcomes.
