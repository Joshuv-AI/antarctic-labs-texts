Common Data Elements for Preclinical High-Frequency Oscillations in Epilepsy
Authors: Ziyi Wang, JiaoJiao Guo, Christos Panagiotis Lisgaras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNormal brain function relies on coordinated oscillations within and across neural circuits. In epilepsy, disruption of these oscillations may reflect pathological excitability associated with seizure generation and/or the emergence of comorbidities. Among these oscillatory activities, high-frequency oscillations (HFOs, typically within 80-500 Hz) are increasingly considered promising electrophysiological biomarkers of epileptogenicity. Preclinical models provide a powerful platform for elucidating the mechanisms underlying HFO generation and for evaluating candidate therapeutic strategies. However, substantial heterogeneity persists across preclinical HFO studies in recording techniques, signal definitions, and reporting practices. This variability limits reproducibility and hampers comparisons between preclinical findings and human data, highlighting the need for greater harmonization. To that end, we propose a set of common data elements (CDEs) organized across 4 domains: (1) HFO recording, (2) HFO definition and detection, (3) HFO reporting and quantification, and (4) biological and disease context CDEs to capture subject- and model-specific parameters that may affect HFO interpretation and comparisons across studies. CDEs are classified as either core, representing the essential information recommended for all HFO studies; or optional, representing additional information recommended when relevant to the experimental design or research question. For practical implementation, CDEs are operationalized through structured case report forms that can be integrated into Research Electronic Data Capture systems for scalable implementation. By promoting harmonized practices, HFO CDEs aim to facilitate a common reporting language in preclinical and translational HFO research in epilepsy, enhance methodological rigor and transparency, and facilitate comparisons across laboratories and experimental models.Keywords: biomarkers, epilepsy, seizures, surgery

Introduction

Brain oscillations are central to cognition and are ubiquitously expressed across brain circuits supporting complex computations.1,2 Normal brain function relies on the coordinated and precise modulation of these rhythms, with their experience-dependent enhancement playing a critical role in the plasticity required for learning. 3 In brain disorders characterized by cognitive deficits, however, normal brain rhythms are often compromised, as is their coordination within and across brain regions, giving rise to altered network communication and potentially contributing to clinical symptoms.4-6 In many cases, normal oscillatory activity is further distorted by the emergence of abnormal electrical patterns,7-9 contributing to an abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG) phenotype.

Among the diverse range of oscillations that the human brain can generate, oscillations exceeding 80 Hz, termed “high-frequency oscillations” or HFOs, have attracted sustained attention in the epilepsy community as putative electrophysiological indicators of epileptogenic tissue. 10 Based on frequency, HFOs are commonly divided into ripples (80-250 Hz) and fast ripples (250-500 Hz), although the exact frequency boundaries may vary across studies. 11 While physiological brain rhythms can typically reach up to 200 Hz, particularly in hippocampal circuits,12,13 pathological conditions such as epilepsy may be characterized by the emergence of higher frequency activity alongside otherwise physiological oscillations (e.g., ripples).14,15 Historically, fast ripples have been closely associated with areas where seizures emerge,14,16 and surgical resection of such areas has been associated with postoperative seizure freedom in a subset of patients.17,18 However, the occurrence of fast ripples in presumably normal brain tissue (nonlesional tissue without epileptiform or seizure activity) has been identified, although their rate of occurrence has been considered too low to carry clinical significance. 15 These observations suggest that fast ripples may occur in the normal brain, with their emergence in epilepsy recently suggested to result, at least in part, from chance aggregation of action potentials. 19 In some cases, however, presumably normal/physiological (e.g., ripples) and pathological oscillations may co-occur in both time and space, which may interfere with normal brain circuit function, 9 and/or have distinct implications for abnormal brain processes such as seizure generation. 20

Beyond epilepsy, HFOs have been investigated in animal models of Alzheimer's disease (AD) neuropathology. 21 Wide-band EEG recordings in AD dementia 22 and Down syndrome 23 have shown that HFO abnormalities may emerge before and after cognitive decline, suggesting potential utility for longitudinal monitoring. 24 Additional studies in psychiatric disease models, including schizophrenia-like phenotypes, have identified disrupted high-frequency synchronization25,26 in line with prior evidence of disrupted network activity in mouse models carrying genetic risk variants for schizophrenia. 27 Together, these findings suggest that the role of HFOs in brain disorders may be wider than initially appreciated, highlighting the need for improved methodological frameworks to enhance their mechanistic understanding and translational utility across different pathophysiological conditions.

Despite this growing interest, there is an unmet need for standardized workflows spanning the full HFO research continuum, from recording through reporting. One reason is that HFOs lack a universally accepted definition. In this context, methodological consistency is essential because it can enhance reproducibility and promote efficient cross-laboratory, cross-disease comparisons, and broaden the applicability of HFO-based biomarkers. In the context of big data and open science, it is increasingly imperative to manage data according to FAIR Principles 28 and promote transparent, 29 rigorous, 30 and adequately powered study designs. 31 To meet these needs, structuring data from the outset to be easily Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable by other researchers is recognized as essential. 28 To address these challenges in the context of HFO research, we propose common data elements (CDEs) and organize them in case report forms (CRFs) spanning HFO recording, detection, reporting, and biological/disease context domains.

Common Data Elements for HFOs

While prior efforts have developed CDEs and CRFs for general electrophysiology applications,32-34 the field currently lacks a unified framework specifically tailored to HFOs. Thus, we propose a pipeline for preclinical HFO studies, organized into the following domains: (1) HFO recording, (2) HFO definition and detection, (3) HFO reporting and quantification, and (4) biological and disease context CDEs (Figure 1). Across these 4 domains, we define structured CDEs and operationalize them into example CRFs to support rigorous, transparent, and comparable data collection and interpretation across studies. These domains are designed to be applied in tandem for each individual rodent.

Specifically, the HFO recording CRF captures acquisition parameters and recording context; the HFO definition and HFO detection CRF specifies how HFOs were operationally defined and detected; the HFO reporting and HFO quantification CRF documents how the resulting detections were summarized, contextualized, and statistically analyzed; and lastly the biological and disease context CRF describes subject-, model-, disease-, and brain-state-related parameters that may influence HFO characteristics. Within each CRF, CDEs are classified as core, representing the essential information recommended for all HFO studies to improve rigor and interpretation; or optional, representing the bonus information recommended to improve data quality when relevant to specific experimental designs or research questions. Collectively, these CRFs connect biological context, signal acquisition, event identification, and final interpretation. HFO Recording CDEs

Acquisition Parameters

Sampling rate fundamentally determines the highest frequencies that can be resolved without aliasing and influences the temporal precision of HFO detection. In order to record HFOs up to 500 Hz, a sampling rate of at least 2 kHz has generally been recommended,35,36 together with an anti-aliasing filter set around 500 Hz. 36 Inadequate sampling may suppress or distort ripples and fast ripples, 36 whereas higher sampling rates improve recording fidelity at the cost of increased high-frequency noise or artifact contamination.

It is recommended that hardware filters remain broad: the high-pass cutoff (the filter used to attenuate low frequencies) should be kept low enough to preserve slow-wave context, whereas the low-pass cutoff (the filter used to attenuate high frequencies) should be set high enough to retain the highest frequency band of interest and be paired with an appropriate anti-aliasing filter. 37 Notch filters used for mitigating line-noise and their harmonics can further modify the spectral profile and, if applied aggressively, they can introduce ringing artifacts that resemble high-frequency events. 38 Amplifier coupling and configuration are also relevant methodological parameters as they may affect baseline noise characteristics and thereby influence the threshold and detectability of HFOs. In particular, low-noise amplifiers can improve the signal-to-noise ratio in the fast ripple frequency range and thereby enhance the detectability of such signals. 39

Electrode Characteristics

Electrode characteristics determine the spatial scale and sensitivity with which neuronal activity is sampled. 40 The same biological activity may appear differently depending on electrode size and design. For example, in the case of clinical recordings, macroelectrodes preferentially capture synchronized activity over larger neuronal populations. On the other hand, microelectrodes are more sensitive to locally generated high frequency events. 41 Supporting this notion, a study conducted in humans found that both HFO rate and amplitude decreased with increasing electrode size. 42

Electrode impedance influences HFOs, noise, and signal transfer, and its effect depends on the full recording chain, including electrode type, size, amplifier input impedance, and overall system noise. 43 Electrode type, contact, and packaging material also influence signal quality and signal-to-noise ratio, affecting the visibility and amplitude of recorded HFOs. 44 The number and arrangement of recording contacts may constrain spatial sampling and the interpretation of HFO distributions across brain regions. Intracranial recordings in humans have shown that HFOs are less common across multiple contacts, however, when they occur at several contacts, they may not be synchronous. 41 Indeed, single contacts provide only limited spatial information. Linear arrays can sample activity along a depth axis, and planar grids or flexible grids can map how HFOs spread across the cortical surface.40,45 High-density array configurations offer even finer spatial resolution and may reveal small, spatially restricted HFO generators that would otherwise be missed or merged when using wider contact spacing. Similarly, the use of commercial, modified, or in-house electrodes may introduce methodological variability that should be explicitly documented (Table 1).

Montage Configuration

Because each recorded signal is measured relative to a reference and stabilized by a ground, reference location and ground placement influence the local field potential, common and volume conducted activity, as well as environmental noise represented in the trace. Various EEG display montages are used to capture epileptiform events in clinical EEG. 46 However, the most commonly used montages are: referential, standard average, and bipolar montages. 46 Bipolar and common-average derivations, for example, may enhance focal activity and limit widespread signals, whereas referential recordings may preserve broader field activity but may also be more susceptible to volume conduction. 47 For example, in intraoperative electrocorticography, a bipolar montage yields more and higher-amplitude ripples compared to an average montage, while an average montage captures more unique ripples and fast ripples compared to a bipolar montage.45,48 On the other hand, ground placement contributes to signal stability and to the mitigation of common-mode noise. As a result, a poorly positioned or unstable ground can lead to significant noise shared across channels and make recordings more vulnerable to environmental and electrical interference. 33 These effects are particularly important in HFO analysis, where small amplitude high-frequency events may be obscured or distorted by excessive noise introduced during the acquisition stage.

HFO Definition and Detection CDEs

One of the challenges in defining and detecting HFOs is related to incomplete knowledge of underlying mechanisms to support a clear and consistent definition of HFOs. Considering the lack of a formal HFO definition, HFO findings, and interpretations may differ substantially depending on how events are operationally defined, detected, validated, and separated from artifacts. This is a central challenge in HFO research because the frequency range of most HFOs under investigation overlaps substantially with the frequency range of filtered sharp transients, muscle activity, broadband noise, and other artifacts that mimic HFOs. As a result, methodological choices at the detection stage can strongly influence reported HFO characteristics. Below we detail four main areas that can contribute to standardized definition and detection of HFOs: frequency definition, event criteria and thresholding, detection method, validation and artifact rejection (Table 2).

Frequency Range Definition

HFOs comprise a broad range of relatively brief events (typically 20-50 ms), often ranging from about 80 up to 500 Hz or even higher. 49 In most HFO epilepsy studies, HFOs are subdivided into ripples and fast ripples, with one relatively common convention defining ripples as 80 to 250 Hz and fast ripples as 250 to 500 Hz. 11 However, it should be noted that alternative cutoffs have also been used, including 80-200/200-500 Hz, 80-240/240-500 Hz, or mixed schemes extending the fast ripple range up to 600 Hz, suggesting that subtype boundaries are partly methodological rather than reflecting universal biological boundaries.20,50 In practice, that means that the same event may be labeled as a ripple in one pipeline and a fast ripple in another. Moreover, acquisition bandwidth or filtering can also alter the estimated peak frequency. 36 Gliske et al 36 showed that the same HFO event could be variably classified as a ripple (<200 Hz) or a fast ripple (>250 Hz) under different sampling and anti-aliasing filter settings. Similarly, Pearce et al 51 identified not only ripples (100-250 Hz) and fast ripples (250-500 Hz), but also mixed-frequency events, showing that some HFOs span both bands rather than fitting cleanly into one frequency band. Therefore, it is recommended that studies report (1) whether and how ripple and fast ripple bands were defined, and (2) whether the categories were mutually exclusive, and whether overlapping or mixed events were allowed, and how events containing both frequency components were classified.20,52 Moreover, if the analysis includes an investigation of high-frequency activity (HFA; defined according to references15,53]) or very HFOs between 500 Hz and 2 kHz (very HFOs)54-57 beyond the conventional ripple and fast ripple bands (above 500 Hz), these signals should be explicitly defined and reported separately.

The frequency range investigated strongly depends upon the use of offline/online hardware and software filtering. The software high-pass cutoff determines which lower-frequency components are removed before detection or review, and this can change whether the same raw event appears as a ripple, a fast ripple, or a pseudo-oscillation generated from filtering of a sharp transient. Likewise, the software low-pass cutoff constrains the upper frequencies available for analysis and can reduce sensitivity to fast ripples when set too low. 36 Indeed, it was shown that lower anti-aliasing cutoffs reduce HFO detection sensitivity and alter feature estimates, including peak frequency of detected HFOs. 36 This matters because sharp transients, single unit or interictal spike components can generate false ripples after high-pass filtering, 58 especially when filtering is used without time-frequency confirmation or raw-signal inspection. 59

Event Criteria and Thresholding

Event criteria and thresholding directly determine which high-frequency bursts are retained as HFOs and which are rejected as subthreshold background fluctuation, sharp transients, or artifactual events. HFOs are commonly operationalized as oscillatory events containing at least 4 cycles, with ripples typically lasting longer than fast ripples. 60 In most cases, events longer than 6 ms are considered as putative HFOs, because most filtering artifacts/transients are relatively short in duration, 58 although longer electromyography (EMG)-related events can occur. Thus, stricter minimum duration or cycle requirements may preferentially exclude short fast ripple candidates and shift detection toward long duration events such as ripples. Conversely, looser criteria may increase sensitivity to brief events but also increase vulnerability to false detections. Thresholding exerts a similarly strong effect. Most automated detectors identify HFOs when an energy, amplitude, envelope, or line-length measure exceeds a chosen cutoff relative to baseline activity. Raising this cutoff enriches the detected dataset for high-amplitude events and reduces the prevalence of lower-amplitude HFOs, while lower thresholds increase yield at the expense of specificity. The choice of baseline also alters detection outcome, because thresholds derived from the whole recording, from channel-specific activity, or from selected quiet baseline segments can produce very different HFO counts.

Detection Method

Visual marking is the most widely used approach to identify HFOs and is currently regarded as a gold standard. However, manual analysis can be extremely time-consuming and subjective, with results influenced by reviewer expertise. Thus, automatic detection methods have been developed to improve efficiency and reproducibility. Different HFO detection algorithms emphasize different signal properties, including amplitude, energy, waveform complexity, oscillatory regularity, and spectral compactness, directly affecting which HFOs are identified.61,62 Root mean square (RMS) and Hilbert-based detectors are primarily sensitive to transient increases in band-limited signal energy, 63 whereas line-length methods respond more strongly to rapid waveform changes. 64 On the other hand, wavelet- or time-frequency-based detectors evaluate whether an HFO candidate forms a compact high frequency “island” in the time-frequency domain, which makes them better aligned with the visual idea of a localized oscillatory event. 65 Machine-learning and deep-learning methods can incorporate multiple signal features at once and, in some settings, further distinguish artifacts from physiological HFOs, and more epileptogenic HFOs.66,67 For example, a variational autoencoder identified morphologically defined pathological HFOs that were linked to spikes and seizure onset zone contacts, and predicted postoperative outcome better than unclassified HFOs. 68 Additional approaches include the extraction of HFO features using deep learning to predict the occurrence of ripples from the raw trace. 69 Novel strategies using ensemble models that combine several artificial intelligence methods are promising in identifying putative HFO events based on consensus detection criteria.12,70

Overall, these methodological differences may yield different HFO counts and morphological characteristics. Detector type should therefore be reported, together with implementation characteristics, software platform, and any custom code and test data, because these parameters shape the final HFO population.

Validation and Artifact Rejection

Validation and artifact rejection directly shape the final HFO dataset because they determine which candidate detections are retained as true oscillations and which are excluded as false-positive events after automated HFO detection. 71 This validation process presents a central challenge for HFO research, as detector performance can only be quantified against a ground-truth dataset, yet the ground truth itself depends on how HFOs are identified. In practice, studies have approximated ground truth in several ways, including agreement between two or more reviewers, overlap between reviewer markings and automated detections, or, less favorably, the judgments of a single reviewer.48,60,62 In this context, Cohen's kappa statistic has been used to assess agreement between two reviewers or between visual and automated HFO detection. 72 Cohen's kappa ranges from −1 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect agreement, 0 indicating chance-level agreement, and negative values indicating agreement worse than chance. 72 It should be noted that visual HFO review shows only moderate inter-rater reproducibility, as event acceptance remains partly reviewer-dependent, and manual inspection is time-consuming, particularly for prolonged recordings and datasets that include a high number of recording channels.

Artifact rejection has equal direct effects. Filtered sharp transients, harmonics, EMG, movement artifacts, and broadband noise can all create false oscillations after band-pass filtering and thereby inflate HFO counts or even distort subtype assignment. Requiring time-frequency confirmation or applying postdetection rejection criteria of putative HFOs can reduce false positives and can substantially change the final HFO burden. 58 Accordingly, studies should report how the validation set was defined, whether reviewer agreement was required, whether detector-reviewer overlap was used, whether time-frequency confirmation was applied systematically, and which artifact classes were explicitly rejected and why.

Visualization of HFOs

It is important to include raw unfiltered traces because they reveal whether a candidate HFO is embedded in a biological waveform or instead occurs on a sharp transient, movement artifact, or represents a broadband burst occurring across multiple channels. Raw traces should also be accompanied by bandpass-filtered traces of both ripples and fast ripples to appreciate whether the putative event is confined to a specific frequency. Bandpass-filtered traces make brief oscillations easier to inspect visually and in relation to raw signals, but they also allow assessments of sharp events that typically appear as ripple-like events after filtering.58,59 In this case, time-frequency plots are valuable as they may change HFO interpretation by revealing whether the candidate HFO forms a compact or isolated high frequency “island” or a broader, less specific spectral pattern, indicative of a filtered sharp transient. 59 In practice, this means that the same HFO events may appear convincing in a filtered trace yet be rejected after raw-signal or time-frequency review. Broader spectral representations along the entire HFO frequency continuum (i.e., 80-500 Hz) similarly help distinguish “concentrated” high-frequency power from harmonics or broadband contamination of the time-frequency domain usually linked to broadband artifactual events. Moreover, it should be noted that time-frequency normalization strategies may help optimize the representation of HFOs in intracerebral signals. 73 Overall, these effects are more than illustrative, they can change the final HFO count, the apparent spatial distribution of accepted events, and the specificity of the retained HFO dataset.

Quantification

HFO detections can be summarized in different ways, including event rate, amplitude, channel-normalized rate, behavioral state-specific rate, or network-based measures.74,75 HFO rate, typically expressed as events per minute per channel, remains the most widely used metric, but it is not interchangeable with other summaries. For example, it has been suggested that HFO amplitude can show greater temporal stability and better discrimination of seizure-onset channels than rate, indicating that channels with relatively few but consistently large HFOs are important and may be underestimated when only counts are reported. 76 Likewise, reporting the total number of events per minute versus events per channel per minute changes whether burden is driven primarily by total detections or by local channel activity. Moreover, pooling HFO counts across vigilance states can obscure higher HFO rates during the specific sleep-wake states. 77 HFO rate may also vary depending on the recording duration, thus quantification along longer recordings is preferable. 78 Moreover, it should be noted that HFOs may follow a diurnal rhythm, which may affect their rates depending on when recordings were conducted. 79

Summary statistics also affect the result, as the mean is more easily pulled upward by short periods with many HFOs, whereas the median is less influenced by these extreme values and thus may better reflect the typical HFO level. Finally, single-channel quantification may miss network structure, as it has increasingly been recognized that HFOs can occur as coordinated discharges rather than isolated channel events.80,81

Contextual Relationships

HFOs coupled to interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) often behave differently from isolated HFOs, and combining IED and HFO information can alter localization and outcome prediction relative to either feature alone.82-84 Coupling to slow waves further changes interpretation. Both ripples and fast ripples show preferred phase relationships to the sleep slow-wave cycle, and HFOs locked to specific slow-wave phases can appear more closely linked to epileptogenic tissue than uncoupled events. 85 Overlap between ripples and fast ripples adds another layer of context, because some HFOs span both bands rather than fitting into a single subtype.20,52 These contextual relationships do not merely annotate the event, but they change the detected HFO phenotype by altering subtype assignment, which may affect pathological specificity. Thus, HFO reporting should specify whether spike coupling, slow-wave coupling, and ripple-fast ripple overlap were assessed, and how these relationships were operationally defined.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis may change the HFO phenotype because HFO data are usually nested and nonindependent. Indeed, many events come from the same channel, many channels come from the same animal or patient, and repeated recordings may come from the same subject. If these dependencies are ignored and every event is treated as a statistically independent observation, effect sizes can be inflated and significance can be overstated. Repeated-measure or hierarchical models can change conclusions further because they account for clustering within subjects and channels. When HFOs are measured across multiple channels, nights, sleep stages, or experimental conditions, mixed-effects and nested models or other hierarchical approaches are better aligned with the data structure than simple pooled tests. 86 Without that structure, apparent differences may partly reflect subject-specific HFO burden rather than a true group-level condition effect. Stratification by vigilance state, anatomical region, or seizure period likewise changes whether HFO effects appear condition specific. Lastly, information on power analysis, including the dataset or published literature used to inform effect-size estimates, as well as whether sex was considered in the analysis, would enhance rigor, reproducibility, and interpretability. For studies including female animals, documentation of whether estrous-cycle stage was assessed or considered may also be relevant.

Reproducibility and Code Availability

Publicly available software, code, and test datasets make methodological choices visible and testable, allowing other investigators to reproduce detections, compare detectors on the same data, and determine whether reported HFO effects are robust across datasets. Shared tools also improve multicenter comparability by reducing undocumented differences across sites.52,87 Reproducibility and availability should therefore be reported explicitly, including whether code is public, on request, or unavailable, which repository was used, and which software version generated the final HFO set. If possible, raw data and code should be saved in a public repository along with README files that document data structure, any required software, and steps needed to reproduce analyses.

Biological and Disease Context Specific CDEs

HFO characteristics are shaped by the biological and disease context in which recordings are obtained (Table 4). Animal characteristics, such as species, strain, sex, age, and developmental stage may significantly influence the occurrence and properties of HFOs. Moreover, hormonal levels and the stage of the estrous cycle, which significantly impact hippocampal network function, 88 may in turn affect the expression of HFOs. In disease models, HFO interpretation also depends on the disease model used, stage in the disease continuum, seizure burden, and the time between disease induction or epilepsy onset and recording. For instance, HFOs may differ quantitatively between AD and epilepsy models 89 or with the presence of epilepsy and seizures in AD dementia (versus no seizures 22 ). They may also scale with dementia progression, independently of epilepsy. 23 Moreover, HFOs emerge relatively fast after brain injury,90,91 conferring some predictive value for seizure emergence,16,92,93 thus timing after disease/epilepsy induction is an important variable to report.

HFO characteristics vary across brain regions, 15 pathological proximity, 14 as well as brain state.77,94 For example, in hippocampus, physiological sharp wave-ripples (SWRs) are most prominent in area CA1, 95 but area CA2 13 and the CA3 subfield 96 can also contribute to ripple generation. Moreover, while the dentate gyrus may show limited SWR activity, it has been deemed critical in dentate spike generation,97,98 which indirectly affects SWR rate. In epilepsy models, fast ripples have been reported in CA1, 9 CA2,99,100 CA3,50,101,102 the dentate gyrus,21,103 as well as the fasciola cinereum, 104 suggesting that pathological HFO activity can emerge at multiple locations rather than as a feature of a single subfield. Location within a hippocampal cell layer may also influence HFO characteristics as shown for the case of ripples. 105 Lastly, consideration of proximal-distal locations 106 as well as dorsal and ventral differences101,107 in the expression of HFOs should also be considered. Proximity to a pathological entity may also change HFO measurements, because HFO rates are higher inside versus outside of an epileptogenic region and they may also be influenced by the temporal proximity to seizures.14,74

HFOs can also be influenced by the sleep-wake cycle. Hippocampal SWRs occur predominantly during nonrapid eye movement (NREM)/slow-wave sleep and awake immobility, and at lower rates during active waking states such as during exploration. 95 Likewise, pathological HFOs also depend on behavioral state. For instance, interictal HFO rates are generally highest during NREM sleep, and sleep is thought to improve the spatial specificity of HFOs for epileptogenic tissue compared to wakefulness or REM sleep.77,94 For example, recordings obtained during slow-wave sleep often yield more robust detection of both ripples and fast ripples, whereas pooled recordings across wakefulness, REM, and NREM could dilute this effect and may contribute to limited interpretability. 77 However, a recent study showed that wakefulness can also contain frequent HFOs, 19 which is in line with a previous study conducted in epileptic rodents, highlighting the occurrence of HFOs during wakefulness and their role in cognitive processing, 108 with the latter linked to increased HFO rates as reported in humans. 109 Furthermore, HFO rate may change over time, so short recordings can potentially misclassify channels with respect to pathology, thus longer or state-matched segments may provide more stable HFO rates and more reliable channel classification.78,110 Overall, we propose explicitly reporting animal and disease characteristics, brain regions, proximity to pathology, brain state, and recording duration, because these factors strongly influence the interpretation of HFO findings and associated outcome measures. 75

Discussion

HFO research in preclinical models is anchored to methodological decisions that shape outcome measures and interpretation. Here, we propose an HFO-specific CDE framework organized according to the temporal and logical progression of a typical HFO preclinical experiment, from signal acquisition all the way to interpretation. Each CDE/CRF domain functions as a modular unit that researchers can expand or customize to suit their specific needs or experimental designs, while preserving interoperability across laboratories. To balance completeness with practical use, the framework separates core elements from those that are only applicable in certain experimental settings. This allows studies to report a consistent minimum dataset while adding context-specific information when needed. This structure captures the sequential decisions that determine whether HFOs can be reliably detected, consistently defined, and transparently reported.

A key strength of the proposed framework is that it treats HFO analysis as a linked workflow rather than an isolated detection step. What is ultimately reported as an HFO depends on what the recording system captures, how candidate events are defined and accepted, and how detections are summarized, contextualized, and reported. Structuring these stages within a common protocol may help investigators distinguish methodological variability from biological variability and thereby improve cross-study and cross-disease comparisons. The proposed CDEs are specific to HFO research and are not intended to stand alone, but to complement prior CDEs focused on electrophysiology.32-34 This is particularly relevant in preclinical research, where differences in recording hardware, electrode scale, brain state selection, and detector implementation can strongly influence HFO rates and subtype distributions. It is also highly relevant to human studies, as multicenter HFO studies have increasingly been conducted17,83,87 to enhance rigor, reproducibility, and statistical power.

Many of the proposed CDEs were informed by human HFO research. This provides an important translational bridge between preclinical and clinical electrophysiology. However, preclinical CDEs should not simply reproduce the structure of human CDEs, but rather add value by addressing key limitations faced in human HFO research. For example, clinical studies are often limited in their ability to precisely determine the exact location of recording electrodes, perform recordings in definitively normal tissue or tightly control recording conditions such as anesthesia or other confounds. Preclinical studies help overcome these challenges by offering precise targeting and localization, high-resolution recordings from normal tissue, and circuit level interventions, all of which can be incorporated into future CDE versions. HFO interpretation, however, depends not only on technical recording and analytical parameters but also on the biological and disease context of the subjects from which the recordings are obtained. We therefore consider biological and disease context to be critical determinants of HFO interpretation. Reporting these variables may improve interpretation and help determine whether differences across studies reflect recording or analytical methods, disease progression, or underlying biological variation. Thus, future iterations of the CDE structure should refine the framework based on emerging evidence and needs of preclinical studies, allowing preclinical CDEs to remain clinically aligned while being biologically tailored to outstanding mechanistic questions. In turn, a more standardized approach to HFO reporting may help clarify which differences across studies reflect true biological phenomena and which instead reflect analytical design or species-dependent effects.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. This protocol does not resolve ongoing conceptual questions in the field, including the lack of a universally accepted HFO definition and the incomplete separation of physiological from pathological events. 11 It also needs to remain practical. If the workflow becomes too detailed or burdensome, adoption may be limited. In addition, the proposed CDEs have not yet been validated through broad use by the HFO research community. Thus, their practical value should be tested by comparing studies that use a standardized HFO CDE framework with those using existing nonstandardized methods. Until then, these CDEs should be regarded as exploratory. Community and end-user feedback is critical and will guide future revisions to help determine which elements should remain core, and which should be recommended or supplemental. As the field advances, further versions could be aligned with and potentially incorporated into the NINDS Preclinical CDE Repository, improving their accessibility and compatibility with broader preclinical neurophysiology standards.
