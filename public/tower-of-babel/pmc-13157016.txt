Introduction

The accumulation of conventional petroleum-based plastics poses serious threats to ecosystems and human health, creating an urgent need for alternative, environmentally friendly materials. Polylactic acid (PLA), known for its excellent biocompatibility, barrier properties, mechanical performance, and biodegradability, has become an ideal substitute and is widely used in agriculture, food packaging, medicine, textiles, and automotive engineering (Rai et al., 2021).

Polylactic acid (PLA): An emerging bio-based polymer for multiple applications

In agriculture, PLA is used to produce mulch films, seedling protection nets, fruit protection covers, and fishing nets. These applications help reduce agricultural waste and allow PLA to serve as a matrix for embedding herbicides, promoting crop growth and increasing yields (Shao et al., 2022). Due to its excellent barrier properties, PLA inhibits microbial growth in food, thereby extending shelf life; it is widely used in food packaging, including tea bags, disposable tableware, and straws (Swetha et al., 2023, Fard et al., 2025, Falzarano et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2023). In the medical field, PLA is extensively utilized in surgical sutures, stents, drug delivery systems, and implantable devices due to its biocompatibility (Yang et al., 2024a, Yang et al., 2024b, Patel et al., 2020).

Currently, the global production and market scale of PLA are expanding rapidly. In 2019, global PLA production reached approximately 190,000 tons, with a market value exceeding US$500 million. It is projected to reach US$3.3 billion by 2028 (Kurowiak et al., 2023, de Albuquerque et al., 2021). However, after release into the environment, PLA products gradually break down into secondary micro-plastics (MPs) and nano-plastics (NPs) through the combined action of light, microorganisms, and mechanical abrasion (Lyn et al., 2024). These PLA-MNPs are widely distributed in the environment: they can exist as dust and aerosols, spreading via air currents (Luo et al., 2024, Zhang, 2024); they have been detected in freshwater, lakes, rivers, and coastal waters (Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b, Covello et al., 2024); and in soil, sources include residual agricultural films, atmospheric deposition, and irrigation water (Sathiaseelan et al., 2024, Yu et al., 2025). Sources of polylactic acid (PLA) micro-/nano-plastics (PLA-MNPs).

As a bio-based polymer, the usage of PLA has increased rapidly in recent years. Although PLA is considered biodegradable, its environmental degradation process still generates micro- and nano-plastic particles. With the expanding applications of PLA in packaging, textiles, biomedical, and other fields, it has become an emerging source of micro- and nano-plastic pollution. Its environmental and health impacts urgently require systematic investigation.

Comparative analysis of PLA-MNPs vs. petroleum-derived microplastics

While PLA is regarded as a biodegradable polymer, the environmental behavior of its micro- and nano-plastic forms (PLA-MNPs) is complex and markedly different from petroleum-derived microplastics like polystyrene (PS) or polyethylene (PE). A clear and comparative understanding of their persistence, transformation pathways, and resultant ecotoxicological impacts is crucial for accurate risk assessment.

Environmental persistence

Conventional microplastics (e.g., PS, PE), with stable carbon–carbon backbones, resist biological breakdown. They persist for centuries, undergoing only slow photo-oxidation and mechanical fragmentation into smaller, equally persistent particles. In contrast, PLA-MNPs contain hydrolysable ester bonds, making them inherently biodegradable. However, their persistence is highly influenced by environmental conditions. In optimal environments—such as aerobic soils, composts, or oxygen-rich water with active microorganisms, PLA-MNPs can mineralize within months to a few years, indicating a certain degree of persistence in aquatic sediments (Seeley et al.,2020). Conversely, in suboptimal conditions like anoxic, cold, or nutrient-poor environments (e.g., deep-sea sediments or salt marshes), degradation slows dramatically (Zou et al.,2024). For instance, PLA films showed only superficial degradation after 32 weeks in a marine marsh, with a degradation efficiency far lower than that under ideal laboratory conditions (Weinstein et al.,2020). This variability means PLA-MNPs can exhibit short-term persistence in many natural settings, bridging the gap between “biodegradable” and “persistent”.

Transformation products

This divergence in persistence leads to fundamentally different transformation pathways and products. The primary fate of PLA-MNPs is hydrolysis, breaking ester bonds to form soluble lactic acid oligomers (dimers being most common), which are further degraded to lactic acid monomers. These products are readily assimilated by microbes as a carbon source and ultimately mineralized to CO2 and water (Costa and Lackner, 2025). Although oligomers may transiently persist (e.g., up to 168 h in complex marshes), they do not accumulate long-term. This pathway contrasts sharply with that of conventional microplastics, which fragment into smaller, more bioavailable micro-/nano-plastics or form oxidized compounds that resist further microbial degradation, leading to permanent environmental accumulation.

Environmental behavior and ecotoxicological implications

The different chemical fates drive contrasting environmental behaviors and toxicological outcomes. PLA-MNPs degrade faster than conventional plastics in most environments, but their surface properties evolve during aging. Hydrolysis increases surface area and creates hydrophilic groups, which can significantly boost the adsorption capacity of aged PLA for certain pollutants (e.g., antibiotics) by 1.22–3.18 times (Shen et al.,2023). In contrast, conventional plastics primarily adsorb pollutants via physical mechanisms, with less dramatic change upon aging.

Perhaps the most striking difference is their effect on biogeochemical cycles. PLA-MNPs can stimulate microbial activity and serve as an organic carbon source. Studies show they promote processes like nitrification and denitrification in sediments, increasing the abundance of relevant functional genes (Zou, et al.,2024). Conventional microplastics typically inhibit or have no significant effect on these cycles, often suppressing microbial diversity.

The toxicity mechanisms differ. For PLA-MNPs, acute toxic effects often stem from short-term stress induced by degradation intermediates (e.g., lactic acid altering local pH or oxidation state) or physical irritation. Their toxicity can decrease after environmental conditioning (e.g., biofouling). For conventional microplastics, toxicity arises from chronic physical damage, leached additives, and the long-term vector effect of concentrating persistent organic pollutants and heavy metals on their surfaces, facilitating trophic transfer (Ainali et al.,2022).

These differences necessitate distinct risk assessment frameworks. For conventional microplastics, the paradigm focuses on persistence, bioaccumulation potential, and chronic additive toxicity. For PLA-MNPs, the focus shifts to degradation kinetics under realistic environmental conditions, the short-term ecological effects of lactic acid oligomers, and context-dependent toxicity. A critical insight is that PLA-MNPs cannot be universally classified as “low-risk.” Their degradation may be slower than assumed in many environments, and their acute toxicity to some organisms (e.g., earthworms, mussels) can be comparable to conventional plastics. Therefore, risk assessments must be environment-specific, recognizing that PLA-MNPs may pose a low risk in composting facilities but a moderate, short-term risk in aquatic sediments.

Biological exposure and health effects of PLA-MNPs

PLA-MNPs can enter organisms through multiple pathways, including inhalation (e.g., mask fragments (Shahsavani et al., 2025) or from atmospheric deposition during dining/drinking activities (Fang et al., 2022)), ingestion (e.g., through vegetables and fruits (Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b) or contact with PLA food packaging (Cui et al., 2025, Soo et al., 2022)), and dermal contact (e.g., skincare products containing PLA abrasive particles (Nam and Park, 2020)). Beyond organ-specific damage, PLA-MNPs induce a spectrum of systemic health hazards, including genotoxicity, immune dysregulation, cytotoxicity, and developmental toxicity. For instance, PLA-MNPs readily induce genotoxic responses (Nam and Park, 2020) and have been shown to trigger oxidative stress and genetic damage in insects (Alaraby et al., 2024). In fish, MPs can provoke immune responses by regulating neutrophil, granulocyte, or cytokine production (Sharifinia et al., 2020), while in the gastrointestinal tract, MPs/NPs induce oxidative stress and inflammation, leading to cytotoxicity (Kumar et al., 2024, Sharifinia et al., 2020). PLA exposure also induces developmental toxicity in zebrafish larvae (Luan et al., 2023). Moreover, PLA particles can absorb heavy metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), acting as vectors and resulting in combined toxicity (Campanaro et al., 2025, Feng, 2022, Zhong et al., 2024).

The intestine, the primary organ for digestion and absorption, serves as a crucial barrier against harmful substances, and its functional integrity is essential for maintaining systemic homeostasis. As shown in Table 3, the intestine is a significant target organ and accumulation site for both PLA-MNPs and traditional MPs, supported by multiple studies documenting their presence and pathological effects in intestinal tissues (Table 3). Both types of particles have been shown to accumulate in the intestine, causing direct damage, including barrier disruption and microbial imbalance. Moreover, MNPs can trigger cascading systemic effects by disrupting intestinal barrier functions (mechanical, chemical, immune, and biological), altering gut flora, and interfering with metabolic pathways (see Fig. 1) (Table 4). To address the translation gap and strengthen the human relevance of this review, we have expanded the discussion to incorporate key findings from established human-relevant in vitro models. Studies utilizing human intestinal epithelial (Caco-2) cell monolayers have demonstrated that PLA-MNPs can compromise barrier integrity (reduced TEER), induce oxidative stress, and provoke pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-8) release (Ferreira et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2025). Complementary work with human intestinal organoids—which better recapitulate the cellular diversity of the gut—has shown impaired mucus production (reduced MUC2) and disruption of stem cell differentiation upon exposure (O’Mahony et al., 2025). More sophisticated microfluidic gut-on-chip systems, incorporating fluid flow and mechanical strain, have revealed that cellular uptake and oxidative stress are influenced by shear forces, while also confirming the disruption of tight junctions and induction of apoptosis (Zhao et al., 2025). Beyond the gastrointestinal tract, models like human liver organoids have shown inflammatory responses (Alnasser, 2025), human placental trophoblasts have indicated endocrine-disruption risks (Poinsignon et al., 2025), and a human spermatocyte surrogate model (GC-2 cells) has pointed to mitochondrial dysfunction as a mechanism of reproductive toxicity (Zhao et al., 2025). To the best of our knowledge, only one observational study in human volunteers has been reported in the literature with regard to the effects of PLA-MNPs on human health (Gümperlein et al., 2018). This represents a critical knowledge gap in literature, as there is no direct human evidence to clarify the long-term health risks or establish exposure-disease correlations (Pan et al., 2026, Banaei et al., 2023). However, indirect evidence from animal and in vitro studies suggests potential health risks of PLA-MNPs exposure. Adolescent mice exposed to PLA-MPs for 28 days showed obvious cardiac developmental toxicity, through ferroptosis and senescence pathways at doses relatable to estimated human intake (Pan et al., 2026). Exposure in mice reduced sperm quality, and additives (e.g., DEHP, TFA) from PLA fabrics can leach into sweat, posing potential reproductive/endocrine risks (Zhao et al., 2025, Teng et al., 2025). PLA-NPs from commercial teabags can be internalized by intestinal cells and disrupt barrier integrity, with long-term exposure possibly increasing permeability (Banaei et al., 2023).

Research using human organoids (e.g., cardiac organoids) and planned human metabolism tracking of PLA oligomers may help translate animal data to human risk assessment (Zhou et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023). Notedly, future studies such as the effects of plastic exposures on children's health will serve as a foundation for identifying health risks in generations exposed to MPs (Trasande et al., 2025).

Existing studies have revealed that PLA-MNPs can accumulate in various organs and induce toxic effects, particularly intestinal barrier disruption. However, key molecular mechanisms remain elusive, particularly how PLA-MNPs interfere with metabolic pathways to mediate organ damage, and the causal relationships along the gut–liver axis require further elucidation. For instance, MPs impair short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) production, alter metabolic functions, and modulate immune pathway activity (Guo et al., 2025). They also disrupt gut microbiome structure, while microbial metabolites (e.g., SCFAs, polyamines, polysaccharide A) regulate host gene expression via epigenetic mechanisms such as histone acetylation and DNA methylation (Iurk et al., 2024, Rehman et al., 2025). Furthermore, MP exposure combined with a high-fat diet exacerbates gut dysbiosis, mucin thinning, intestinal inflammation, and alters nutrient transporter gene expression (Deng et al., 2023, Okamura et al., 2023), subsequently promoting lipid accumulation, reduced energy expenditure, and adipose tissue dysfunction (Kou et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2024b).

Notably, PLA-MNPs can be metabolized by gut microbes and incorporated into the succinate pathway, altering the gut metabolic phenotype and reducing SCFAs, a key energy source for the intestinal epithelium (Qian et al., 2025). PLA-MNPs also cause structural damage in the liver and intestine, characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration and mitochondrial damage (Duan et al., 2024). At the molecular level, PLA may inhibit bile acid synthesis by up regulating the fibroblast growth factor receptor FGFR4 and activating the JNK/ERK pathway, leading to bile acid metabolic disruption and liver injury (Yang et al., 2024a, Yang et al., 2024b). Recent evidence suggests that foodborne and airborne PLA-MNPs affect the body through distinct pathways: foodborne exposure causes gut microbiota imbalance, metabolite changes, and liver transcriptome disturbances, whereas airborne exposure affects respiratory microbiota, alters lung and serum metabolites, and similarly disrupts the liver transcriptome (Zha et al., 2024a, Zha et al., 2024b).

Furthermore, PLA degradation products such as lactic acid may indirectly impair the intestinal barrier by modulating the gut microbiota or exacerbating inflammation (Zha et al., 2024a, Zha et al., 2024b) and promote fibrosis by inducing local acidosis, activating inflammasomes, and promoting collagen deposition (Thin et al., 2025, Wei et al., 2023). These findings indicate that PLA-MNPs mediate multi-organ toxicity through direct and indirect metabolic interference and the activation of inflammation–fibrosis cascades.

Application prospect of Drosophila in studying PLA-MNP–induced intestinal injury and metabolic disruption

The fruit fly, Drosophila melanogaster, has become a well-established complementary model in toxicological research, particularly for its strengths in genetic manipulation, high-throughput screening, and studies of gut and metabolic pathways. This model has been instrumental in elucidating the toxicity of PLA-MNPs (Legaz et al., 2016, Alaraby et al., 2024, Rocabert et al., 2025).

Notably, a hypothesized yet experimentally underexplored link exists between PLA-MNPs exposure and the disruption of host metabolic pathways, particularly those involving aromatic amino acids. Tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine, along with their microbial-derived metabolites, are crucial regulators of intestinal barrier integrity and immune homeostasis (Scott et al., 2020). For instance, tryptophan is metabolized via the kynurenine (Kyn), serotonin (5-HT), and indole pathways. Gut flora-derived indole metabolites (e.g., IPA, IAld) can enhance barrier function by activating receptors such as the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Conversely, dysregulation of these pathways is implicated in intestinal diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer (Xue et al., 2023).

However, it is critical to distinguish established general pathophysiology from PLA-MNPs-specific mechanisms. While alterations in aromatic amino acid metabolism are observed in various gut disorders and following exposure to some environmental stressors, direct experimental evidence that PLA-MNPs selectively modulate these pathways—particularly through lactic acid release or specific AhR activations are currently lacking. The proposition that PLA degradation may influence tryptophan metabolism remains speculative and is primarily inferred from: 1) the known role of gut microbiota in metabolizing both PLA-derived lactic acid and dietary tryptophan, and 2) studies on other MNPs showing potential immune-metabolic interference.

Therefore, Drosophila model presents a valuable opportunity to test this hypothesis directly. Its genetic tractability allows for targeted manipulation of metabolic enzymes (e.g., IDO, TPH) or receptors (e.g., AhR), enabling researchers to determine whether PLA-MNPs-induced intestinal injury is mediated through these specific pathways or occurs independently of them. Future studies using this model should combine PLA-MNPs exposure with metabolomic profiling and genetic screens to clarify whether and how aromatic amino acid metabolism is involved, moving beyond correlation to establish causality.

In summary, PLA exposure disrupts gut microbiota composition and stability, which in turn dysregulates intestinal immune metabolism. This disruption, coupled with the direct damage PLA inflicts upon the intestinal barrier's mechanical, chemical, immune, and biological components, culminates in inflammatory injury. Critically, the dysregulation of aromatic amino acid metabolism—where tryptophan, phenylalanine, and their metabolites serve as pivotal modulators of barrier function—emerges as a possible central mechanism linking these insults to the pathogenesis of intestinal disease (see Fig. 2).

Analytical challenges in detection of PLA-MNPs in biological samples

While this review synthesizes growing evidence on the intestinal and systemic toxicity of PLA‑MNPs, it is crucial to acknowledge the ongoing scientific debate concerning the real‑world relevance of such findings for human health. A key uncertainty lies in the still poorly defined actual human exposure levels to PLA‑MNPs. Most toxicological studies, including those summarized here, employ administered doses that may not reflect realistic environmental or dietary exposures. Humans are likely exposed to low, chronic, and mixed polymer loads, whereas experimental models often use high, acute, and purified particles. This gap complicates the extrapolation of mechanistic insights to human risk.

Moreover, the detection and quantification of MNPs in biological samples, though technically advancing—face challenges in standardization, sensitivity, and background contamination control (Zhao et al., 2024, Zielli et al., 2026). Variations in analytical methods (e.g., spectroscopy, mass spectrometry) and sample processing protocols hinder cross‑study comparisons and the establishment of baseline exposure values. While the presence of MNPs in human tissues (e.g., blood, placenta, feces) confirms their bioaccessibility, the toxicological significance of these detected levels remains uncertain. Thus, the current value of biological MNPs analysis lies primarily in hazard identification and understanding biodistribution and cellular interactions, rather than in definitive risk characterization.

Another layer of complexity arises from the dynamic nature of PLA‑MNPs in the environment and the gastrointestinal tract. Aging, degradation, and interaction with other pollutants can alter their physicochemical properties and biological effects. The emphasis of this review on intestinal injury and metabolic disruption underscores the gut as a critical interface for MNPs toxicity, but it also highlights the need for more integrated exposomics approaches that account for real‑world particle transformations and mixture effects.

Conclusions and perspectives

PLA is a widely employed biodegradable polymer in agriculture, food, and medical sectors. Following environmental release, PLA products fragment into PLA-MNPs, which enter the body via inhalation, ingestion, or dermal contact. This review summarized current evidence on the environmental distribution, exposure routes, and multi-organ toxicity of PLA-MNPs, with a particular emphasis on intestinal injury. We have described how PLA-MNPs can compromise the four intestinal barriers and have explored the theoretical implication of disrupted aromatic amino acid metabolism in barrier dysfunction, while explicitly noting the preliminary and associative nature of this proposed link.

A key conclusion is the need to clearly separate robustly observed toxicological endpoints from proposed molecular mechanisms that await validation. For instance, while PLA-MNPs-induced gut barrier damage and microbiota shifts are increasingly documented, the downstream metabolic consequences, including potential interference with tryptophan-AhR signaling—require direct experimental confirmation. Future research must prioritize studies designed to test specific mechanistic hypotheses, such as using targeted metabolomics to quantify changes in tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine pathways following controlled PLA-MNPs exposure. Also employing genetically modified models (e.g., AhR-knockout, IDO-deficient) to determine the necessity of these pathways in mediating toxicity; Conducting careful control experiments to disentangle the effects of lactic acid (a PLA degradation product) from those of the particles themselves.

Furthermore, human exposure scenarios remain poorly quantified, and the extrapolation of high-dose experimental data to real-world, low-dose chronic exposure carries uncertainty. Advancing the field will depend on integrating exposure-relevant studies with mechanistic toxicology, ensuring that future investigations not only elucidate potential toxicity pathways but also rigorously assess their relevance under realistic human exposure conditions.
