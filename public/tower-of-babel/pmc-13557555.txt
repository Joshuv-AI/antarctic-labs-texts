ADA assays for high-dose biologics: redefining drug tolerance through clinical insights
Authors: Manisha Saxena, David Janik, Carsten Krantz, Annie St-Pierre, Maria Jadhav, Lydia Michaut, Florent Bender
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe validation of anti-drug antibody (ADA) assays is vital in biologics development, with regulatory bodies like EMA and FDA emphasizing drug tolerance. In patient care, drug tolerance assessments should reflect actual clinical use. We developed an ADA assay for a fully human monoclonal antibody used in oncology, addressing the challenges posed by high circulating drug levels and target biology. Various assay formats were tested using multiple positive controls and drug concentrations that mimic real-world exposure and drug-to-ADA ratios. Assessing different positive controls at various concentrations was key to characterizing sensitivity and drug tolerance. An assay initially showing low drug tolerance with one monoclonal control performed adequately with others. Rather than limiting assessments to a single sensitivity level, we evaluated assay performance using clinically relevant drug and ADA concentrations. This approach ensures the assay’s sensitivity and drug tolerance are meaningful for patient management and therapeutic decisions. Early and ongoing collaboration with health authorities supported alignment of clinically relevant performance criteria and interpretation strategies. Ultimately, this patient-oriented strategy guarantees ADA results that inform patient safety and treatment effectiveness for high-dose biologic therapies.

Introduction

The development of biologic therapies has revolutionized the treatment of various diseases, but it also presents unique challenges in terms of immunogenicity. Biologic therapies can induce the formation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), which may impact the safety and efficacy of the treatment. Monitoring ADA responses is crucial for understanding the immunogenicity of biologics and for making informed decisions regarding dose adjustments and patient management. This paper presents a comprehensive ADA monitoring strategy that addresses the scientific, technical, and regulatory challenges associated with high-dose biologics therapies.

Regulatory submissions must include comprehensive validation reports detailing drug tolerance (DT), assay sensitivity, positive control (PC) characteristics, and sample matrix effects. In parallel, industry and regulatory bodies are actively working to harmonize ADA assay validation, cut points, and reporting standards to enhance clarity and comparability across studies [1].

Recognizing the complexity of linking ADA detection to clinical outcomes, regulators emphasize that not all detected ADAs affect safety or efficacy, urging sponsors to correlate ADA findings with pharmacokinetic (PK), safety and efficacy data to establish clinical relevance [2,3]. As expectations for clinically meaningful immunogenicity assessments increase, prospective dialog with health authorities is essential to ensure alignment of assay performance criteria and interpretation strategies.

Drug tolerance remains a central technical and regulatory challenge in ADA assay development and clinical interpretation. Regulatory agencies, including the EMA and the FDA, have issued guidelines that define the criteria for ADA assay validation encompassing drug tolerance [4,5]. Drug tolerance refers to the ability of an ADA assay to detect ADAs in the presence of circulating therapeutic drugs, up to a specified drug concentration. It describes the extent to which residual or excess drug may interfere with ADA detection (e.g., through competition or complex formation). The FDA’s 2019 guidance identifies 100 ng/mL as a recommended minimum assay sensitivity, based on the premise that such ADA levels may be associated with potential clinical impact [5]. However, this value was not intended to function as a universal or context-independent threshold for drug tolerance determination. Subsequent scientific discussion has emphasized that drug tolerance and sensitivity should be interpreted in relation to therapeutic exposure, assay format, and intended clinical use, rather than applied as fixed analytical criteria across all programs [6]. These considerations are particularly relevant for high-dose biologics, where circulating drug concentrations may greatly exceed levels at which low-titer ADA could exert a meaningful pharmacokinetic or clinical effect.

Some have interpreted that drug tolerance has to be demonstrated at 100 ng/mL of PC, irrespective of the concentration of the drug in samples. This strict interpretation of the FDA guidelines resulted from an over interpretation of the recommended sensitivity at 100 ng/mL. However, there are situations, especially in oncology treatment, where the concentration of biological drugs is extremely high and dosing is frequent. In the case presented here, our modeling experiments predicted human exposure was close to 612 µg/mL in the trough concentration (Ctrough) samples, which are the samples typically collected for ADA analysis. Under these circumstances, developing an assay with sensitivity of 100 ng/mL in the presence of such high drug concentrations was challenging, and the relevance of the clinical data interpretation was questionable.

Regulatory agencies recommend that sponsors examine assay drug tolerance early on in assay development”; the sponsor may examine drug tolerance by deliberately adding different known amounts of PC antibody into ADA-negative control samples in the absence or presence of different quantities of the therapeutic protein product to determine whether the therapeutic protein product interferes with ADA detection. Results obtained in the absence and presence of different quantities of the therapeutic protein product under consideration should be compared” [5].

Multiple strategies can help address the challenge of drug tolerance in ADA assays. Platforms like Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) and Gyrolab, when combined with acid dissociation and target depletion strategies, can meet clinical drug tolerance requirements while maintaining an assay sensitivity [7,8]. Acid dissociation protocols can boost drug tolerance by up to 20-fold without compromising sensitivity, though they may reduce recovery of certain ADA types or introduce bias [7,9]. Techniques such as solid-phase extraction and immunocapture enhance the ADA detection in drug-rich samples by separating ADA from the drug prior to assay [3]. Despite these advances, drug tolerance remains a bioanalytical challenge.

Target interference in clinical ADA assays occurs when soluble drug targets, especially in dimeric or multimeric forms, interact with assay reagents, leading to either false-positive or false-negative ADA results, a challenge most pronounced in bridging immunoassays. This interference can lead to inflated ADA incidence, misinterpretation of immunogenicity data, and flawed clinical decisions [10–13]. Notable examples include denosumab studies where soluble RANKL led to up to 50% apparent ADA positivity, resolved by osteoprotegerin inhibition [14]; fulranumab assays affected by NGF interference requiring redesign [15]; and tocilizumab assays impacted by IL-6R interference, necessitating optimization of dilution and blocking steps [10].

This paper presents a case study of an ADA assay developed for NIS793, a therapeutic monoclonal antibody used in oncology [16,17]. NIS793 is a human IgG2 monoclonal antibody targeting transforming Growth Factor beta (TGFβ) isoforms.

TGFβ plays a pivotal role in tumor microenvironment regulation [18], immune suppression [19–22], and fibroblast activation [23,24], making it a target of choice for combination therapies with immune checkpoint inhibitors aimed at enhancing anti-tumor responses. The development of a robust ADA assay for NIS793 required meticulous attention to potential interference from both the drug and its target while keeping aligned with agency expectations for sensitivity, drug tolerance, and clinical relevance.

Due to the high concentration of the drug in the study samples and the nature of the target, significant challenges had to be solved during assay development. Additionally, elevated levels of TGFβ complexed with latency-associated peptide (LAP), known to be acid-labile [25], introduced an interference risk. Therefore, standard methods, incorporating acid dissociation steps to improve drug tolerance required rigorous evaluation.

Multiple assay formats and platforms were evaluated to optimize ADA assay performance. A range of PCs were tested at concentrations of 100 ng/mL and above, together with drug levels representative of clinical study samples. The impact of the target on each method’s performance was also carefully assessed, enabling selection of the assay with the most relevant drug tolerance and minimal target interference and with the agreement of regulatory agencies. This required early, prospective and regular engagement with health authorities to align assay performance criteria and interpretation strategies with clinically relevant exposure conditions. Building on the lessons learned from this investigation, we recommend evaluating multiple PCs to meaningfully characterize drug tolerance and extending performance assessments beyond the conventional 100 ng/mL sensitivity level to adequately represent assay sensitivity under drug concentrations representative of actual clinical study samples.

ECLIA ADA assay

A homogeneous bridging electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) without acid dissociation was initially developed to detect ADA against NIS793 (Figure 1(a)). The assay followed a standard three tier approach with screening, confirmation, and titration tiers [26]. The assay used biotinylated NIS793 as a capture reagent and sulfo-tagged NIS793 for detection. A streptavidin-coated Quickplex plate with MSD was blocked with Superblock Blocking Buffer (Thermo). PC and samples were diluted 25-fold in Superblock Blocking Buffer without (screening) or with (confirmatory) NIS793 (440 µg/mL). PC and samples were diluted by 1:1 with Master Mix (Superblock Blocking Buffer with NIS793-biotinylated, NIS793-Sulfo tagged) and incubated. The final minimal required dilution (MRD) in the assay was 50. After incubation, the sample/Master Mix dilution was transferred to the blocked and washed streptavidin coated MSD plate and incubated. After incubation, the streptavidin coated MSD plate was washed, read buffer was added, and then plate was read on the MSD Quickplex reader. Bead-based ECLIA ADA assay

A bead-based enrichment method was also developed to deplete both drug and target from samples, and its performance was compared to the original ECLIA method (Figure 2). The assay also follows a standard three tiers approach with screening, confirmation, and titration tiers [26]. NIS793-coupled to Tosylactivated Dynabeads, (Thermo) in neutralizing buffer, were added to the wells of an Axygen low adhesion plate. PCs and samples were not acid treated but were directly added to each well, diluted 1:6.25 in NGB 1 Buffer containing beads, and the plate was incubated at 4°C for 12–24 hr with shaking. A magnet was used to collect the beads, and the liquid phase was removed. The beads were washed and resuspended in an acidic buffer for 10 min with shaking. These first steps eliminate TGFβ bound to LAP. Separately, anti-TGF-B1 (R&D Systems) beads were prepared in the NGB 1 Buffer and were added to a new Axygen low adhesion plate. The acidified NIS793-beads were removed with a magnet, and the supernatant was transferred to the second Axygen low adhesion plate and mixed with the anti-TGF-B1 beads at a dilution of 1:1. The plate was covered and incubated at room temperature for 2 hr while shaking. Drug tolerance, assay sensitivity, and target interference

Drug and target interference were evaluated by spiking samples with varying concentrations of NIS793 and TGFβ, including up to 1000 µg/mL of drug and 20 µg/mL of TGFβ1 (Sigma-Aldrich). Drug tolerance and sensitivity were assessed using multiple PCs: mAb1, mAb2, and mAb3 and pAb1, each tested at different concentrations (including 100 ng/mL).

The drug tolerance at each PC level was estimated using the intercept method by applying linear regression to extrapolate from assay responses above and below the screening cut-point. The intercept of the regression line with the cut-point was used to estimate the drug concentration at which the assay maintains tolerance. Sensitivity of the assay was defined as the lowest concentration of PC at which consistent assay responses were produced above the screening cut-point. Target interference was considered present when assay responses exceeded the screening cut-point.

Cross validation ECLIA ADA assay and bead-based ECLIA ADA assay

Thirty predose subject samples were selected from an NIS793 clinical trial. Each sample was determined to be negative for ADA using the ECLIA ADA method. The samples were split into three sets of 10; 10 were left neat, 10 were spiked at 34 ng/mL mAb3, and 10 were spiked at 5000 ng/mL mAb3. Samples were analyzed at screening and confirmatory for comparison using the Bead-based ECLIA method protocol. Results were then compared for screening and confirmatory tiers, with the confirmatory tier being the deciding factor of equivalence (Table S1).

Alternative approaches: PandA, ACE, SPEAD

Alternative sample pretreatment methods were tested to improve drug tolerance, including antibody competition, affinity capture elution (ACE) [27], precipitation and acid dissociation (PandA) [28], and solid-phase extraction with acid dissociation (SPEAD) [29]. Assay performance was evaluated for drug tolerance, target interference, sensitivity, background, and precision (percent CV), with failed run rates recorded for robustness assessment.

Results

Optimizing and selecting optimal ECLIA ADA assay

Clinical ADA assays are initially designed for early-phase suitability and become more complex during clinical development with greater attention to sensitivity and drug tolerance. This ensures detection of low ADA levels even in the presence of high drug concentrations. We developed ADA assays for the monoclonal anti-TGFβ antibody NIS793 using a homogeneous bridging ECLIA without acid dissociation (Figure 1(a)). The assay involved biotinylated NIS793 for capture and a sulfo-tagged NIS793 for detection, with three tiers: screening, confirmatory, and titration. Validation was performed according to regulatory guidelines, using an anti-idiotypic mouse monoclonal antibody (mAb2; see Table 1) as a PC.

Target interference against the endogenous circulating TGFβ was assessed taking into consideration the reported endogenous concentrations of 500 to 18,300 pg/mL [30]. Our data showed that total TGFβ (primarily TGFβ bound to NIS793) accumulated to 400 to 67,100 pg/mL during treatment with NIS793 (data not shown and Table 2). It was noted that TGFβ-dimers had a significant impact on assay response. This response occurred independently of the presence of PC and was observed in both screening and confirmatory assays. Results indicated that when TGFβ concentrations exceeded 100 pg/mL without a positive control, positive screening signals appeared, likely caused by bridging of TGFβ dimers in the assay (Figure 1(b)). Introducing NIS793 reduced this signal below the cut point, resulting in a negative screening signal at concentrations of 440 µg/mL of NIS793 for TGFβ up to 1000 pg/mL. For TGFβ concentrations of 2000 pg/mL and above even 100 µg/mL of NIS793 was sufficient to completely abrogate the false-positive signal. These findings suggest that higher levels of NIS793 effectively scavenge TGFβ dimers responsible for bridging the ECLIA ADA assay, thereby eliminating the signal. The assay was implemented in clinical studies Phase I through Phase III (Table 2). In the first-in-human (FIH) study, an overall ADA incidence rate of 9.1% and 5.1% was observed in the single agent and combination-dose escalation groups, respectively; however, no anti-drug antibodies were detected in subsequent studies employing dosing regimens up to 30 mg/kg administered intravenously every Q2W or Q3W. At these dose levels, the actual Ctrough ranged between 64.1 and 908 μg/mL. Across the Phase II and III studies, no effects on pharmacokinetics were observed, and no changes in target engagement, as assessed by total TGFβ measurements, were detected. In addition, no immune-mediated reactions indicative of ADA formation were reported in patients treated with NIS793. In conclusion, it is possible that very low levels of ADA were produced but remained undetected; however, if present, they had no apparent clinical impact.

Given that a clinically meaningful impact of ADA on pharmacokinetics would require ADA concentrations proportional to those of the drug, we calculated the molar ratio of ADA to drug at the actual Ctrough range. For NIS793, this ratio was determined to be 1:641 - 1:9080 (Table 2). Experimentally, the drug tolerance was established to be less than 0.0781 μg/mL NIS793 at 100 ng/mL of mAb2 (Table 4). Assay development to improve drug tolerance

Several alternative methods to improve ADA assay drug tolerance were assessed. The evaluated strategies included antibody competition in bridging formats targeting TGFβ, as well as various acid dissociation techniques, such as ACE, SPEAD, and PANDA. Antibody competition alone within the bridging format led to increased interference and background noise. All approaches utilizing acid dissociation generated positive signals even without a positive control, which was likely attributed to the release of TGFβ from LAP or TGFβ-NIS793 complexes. TGFβ remains inactive as part of a “latent” complex with LAP, an N-terminal pro-peptide of TGFβ that stays attached following cleavage and inhibits activation until it is released [25]. It is believed that acid dissociation releases either LAP-bound TGFβ or dissociates TGFβ-NIS793 complexes which upon dimerization bridges the ECLIA ADA assay (Figure 1(b)).

Overall, despite comprehensive efforts, none of these ADA sample pre-treatments surpassed the original assay regarding drug tolerance and target interference. Consequently, two parallel strategies were pursued to address these challenges. Bead-based ECLIA ADA assay

A bead-based assay incorporating depletion and acid dissociation was developed to remove both NIS793 and TGFβ from samples, thereby enhancing drug tolerance and minimizing target interference. This method also addresses the potential impact of TGFβ released from latent LAP. The procedure utilizes magnetic beads in a two-step process: initially enriching anti-NIS793 antibodies from the sample, followed by the removal of target TGFβ. The enriched anti-NIS793 antibodies are subsequently isolated using biotinylated NIS793 and detected by sulfo-tagged NIS793 within a standard bridging-assay format. Notably, this staggered method enables the selective depletion of NIS793 and TGFβ interference through a stepwise process, thereby exhibiting enhanced performance compared to previously described techniques of depletion or acid dissociation alone (Figure 2).

The drug tolerance of the bead-based assay was demonstrated by its ability to detect ADA in the presence of up to 1000 µg/mL NIS793 at a positive control concentration of 100 ng/mL. Target interference was evaluated separately in the absence of positive control to assess the ability of TGFβ to generate an assay signal, with measurable positive signal observed at concentrations up to 10,000 pg/mL (Table 4). Overall, the drug tolerance and target interference were improved with a bead-based assay.

The performance of the bead-based assay was compared to the ECLIA ADA assay through a cross-validation experiment, which analyzed sera either neat or spiked with LPC mAb3 at 34 ng/mL and HPC at 5000 ng/mL across both assays. Results showed concordance in 29 out of 30 samples, with only one un-spiked sample testing positive exclusively in the bead-based assay (Table S1).

Influence of positive control selection on drug tolerance determination

In addition to the development of a new assay, we conducted a more in-depth evaluation of the ECLIA ADA assay, focusing particularly on understanding how the selection of a surrogate PC influences drug tolerance. Recognizing that both drug tolerance and target interference are highly contingent upon the specific PC antibody used as described in published articles [1,26], we aimed to systematically assess how various PCs impact the ECLIA ADA assay’s performance. To achieve this, we tested two novel anti-NIS793, a mouse monoclonal antibody from an alternative immunization campaign (referred to as mAb1) and a rabbit pAb1 and compared these to the previously established data generated with mAb2. Each of these three PCs was spiked from 100 ng/mL up to 10,000 ng/mL in the presence of increasing concentrations of NIS793 (up to 1500 µg/mL). These experiments were analyzed using the bridging ECLIA ADA assay (Figure 3 and Table S2). Importantly, rather than setting a predefined drug tolerance limit, we intentionally chose to vary the PC concentrations to better emulate the dynamic and variable nature of the in vivo ADA response. The data obtained from using mAb1 and pAb1 showed that, when present at 500–10,000 ng/mL, the assay could tolerate more than 10 µg/mL of NIS793. At the highest mAb2 concentration tested (10,000 ng/mL), the bridging assay demonstrated tolerance of up to 162 µg/mL of NIS793. Notably, when the new mouse mAb1 was used, the assay already exhibited drug tolerance of up to 120 µg/mL at the lowest PC concentration tested (100 ng/mL), and even the highest concentrations of NIS793 did not result in false-negative outcomes. The rabbit pAb1 displayed drug tolerance that was intermediate between the two monoclonals, with the assay tolerating less than 10 µg/mL NIS793 at 100 ng/mL PC but exceeding 1500 µg/mL at 10000 ng/mL pAb1.

After thorough evaluation with multiple PCs, the ECLIA ADA assay showed strong performance even at the elevated drug concentrations observed in NIS793 clinical studies. Considering its ease of use, robustness, and lower failure rate, the ECLIA ADA assay was selected over the bead-based method. The results of the ECLIA investigation were shared with Health Authorities and were accepted as adequate to support the program. Collectively, these results underscore that drug tolerance in this assay is strongly influenced by the type of surrogate PC employed and that differences in the affinity or avidity of the PC can lead to substantially different apparent drug tolerances. Consequently, it is recommended not to rely solely on a single PC for determining drug tolerance and target interference. Instead, a comprehensive characterization of assay susceptibility should involve multiple PCs of distinct origins to ensure a robust and representative assessment of assay performance.

Discussion

Assay development and technical challenges

The development of an ADA assay for biologic therapy targeting TGFβ presented significant challenges due to the biology of TGFβ and the high-circulating drug concentrations in the patient population. These factors are known to lead to both false positives/negatives and limited assay sensitivity.

Several sample pretreatment methods, such as acid dissociation and bead-based enrichment, were explored to improve drug tolerance. While bead-based method increased drug tolerance and reduced target interference, they introduced unacceptable assay variability and operational complexity, limiting their suitability for routine clinical use. Through iterative development, a robust and reproducible assay was achieved, resulting in the selection of an optimized ECLIA method that does not require acid dissociation. In summary, the optimal ADA assay is not necessarily the most technically complex; rather, it is the one that effectively balances sensitivity, drug tolerance, and operational practicality.

Impact of positive control selection on nominal assay performance

The nature of the PC antibody was found to be a critical factor for determination of the nominal drug tolerance and sensitivity of the assay. The positive control antibodies used in this study were originally generated and selected as functional anti‑NIS793 reagents and were subsequently employed as critical reagents across multiple assay platforms, including PK, PD, and bioanalytical method development activities. In this context, all monoclonal antibodies demonstrated robust and consistent binding to NIS793, supporting their suitability as functional surrogate controls. Monoclonal antibodies could yield either very high or very low drug tolerance, while polyclonal antibodies provided intermediate performance, highlighting the importance of evaluating multiple PCs and testing them at various concentrations. Importantly, as discussed in the manuscript and supported by published literature, apparent ADA assay performance, including sensitivity and drug tolerance, does not necessarily correlate with antibody binding affinity measured in isolated systems. Rather, assay format, bridging geometry, epitope accessibility, and positive‑control format (monoclonal versus polyclonal) play a dominant role. The inclusion of a polyclonal antibody in this work was intended to better approximate the heterogeneity and avidity effects expected in endogenous human ADA responses. Our findings demonstrate that relying on a single PC at 100 ng/mL can misrepresent assay performance. In such situations, there is a risk that laboratories may arbitrarily select a PC that suggests favorably high assay performance in order to align with perceived regulatory expectations. Initial indications of poor drug tolerance with a single monoclonal PC were overturned when alternative monoclonal and polyclonal controls were assessed, showing that nominal assay performance depends strongly on the nature and concentration of the PC. This observation is consistent with the findings of Zanghi et al. [31] and Weiner et al. [6], who concluded that apparent ADA assay drug tolerance and, by implication, sensitivity can be strongly driven by the choice of positive control/surrogate ADA reagent and that a historically used PC can underestimate true/clinically relevant DT. In their reassessment of an atezolizumab ADA assay, the historical goat polyclonal PC at 100 ng/mL significantly underestimated DT, whereas an alternative rabbit anti-idiotype monoclonal surrogate demonstrated substantially improved DT and better alignment with clinical sample behavior, underscoring that nominal assay performance metrics can shift materially with PC selection. In conclusion, the choice and concentration of positive control antibodies are pivotal in accurately determining ADA assay drug tolerance and sensitivity, with single controls at standard concentrations often misrepresenting true assay performance.

ADA impact on drug efficacy

As outlined in the recent European Immunogenicity Platform (EIP) recommendations [32] and IQ consortium survey [33], a structured immunogenicity risk assessment (IRA) integrates product-, process-, patient-, and treatment-related risk factors to assign an overall IG risk level. This assessment also serves to guide fit-for-purpose mitigation and bioanalytical monitoring strategies, as described in these recent guidelines. Consistent with this approach, NIS793, a human IgG2 monoclonal antibody targeting TGFβ isoforms without an endogenous counterpart, is expected to have a low immunogenicity risk. Clinical evaluations confirmed a low ADA incidence (Table 2) and no clinically relevant immunogenicity-related events [16].

Anti-drug antibodies can alter the pharmacokinetics of biotherapeutics through direct neutralization of drug activity or by promoting increased systemic clearance [34–36], which may in turn affect pharmacodynamic readouts and clinical efficacy. Increasingly, it has been recognized that the clinical relevance of ADA cannot be inferred from analytical detectability alone but must be interpreted in the context of contemporaneous drug exposure. When circulating drug concentrations are high, low-level ADA may have limited potential to affect pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, or safety, even if analytically detectable. Prior work has emphasized that meaningful interpretation of immunogenicity data requires consideration of the relative magnitude of ADA in relation to circulating drugs, rather than reliance on absolute ADA concentration thresholds [37]. This exposure-aligned perspective supports integrating ADA magnitude, drug concentration, and intended clinical questions when defining drug tolerance criteria and interpreting ADA-negative results in late-stage development.

In clinical indications requiring high-dose regimens–as in the studies presented here–on-treatment mAb concentrations approached 908 µg/mL NIS793. Under these conditions, an ADA assay sensitivity of 100 ng/mL corresponds to an ADA-to-drug molar ratio of roughly 1:9080. At such a ratio, any ADA-mediated effect on drug exposure would not be detectable and would likely fall within routine PK assay variability.

Therefore, ADA assay validation aimed at assessing the impact of PK should clearly account for the quantitative link between ADA concentrations and levels of drugs present in circulation. A conservative threshold for clinically meaningful PK/PD impact may be defined as the point at which approximately 99% of the drugs remain unbound (i.e., an ADA-to-drug molar ratio of ~1:100) In this context, evaluating drug tolerance at 100 ng/mL of PC may be clinically irrelevant for monoclonal antibody drugs without endogenous counterparts, circulating at levels of up to 1 mg/mL, and for which target modulation efficacy is established.

In summary, for monoclonal antibody therapies like NIS793 administered at high concentrations, low-level ADA detected by highly sensitive assays is unlikely to impact drug efficacy or pharmacokinetics. Therefore, the clinically meaningful assessment of ADA effects may be better guided by ADA levels relevant to the therapeutic context, while recognizing that factors such as affinity, avidity, isotype, and others may also contribute, albeit to a lesser extent, rather than relying solely on default sensitivity thresholds.

Impact on patient safety

The likelihood that rare, low-level ADA produces clinically meaningful safety signals (e.g., acute hypersensitivity and immune complex disease) is low when the circulating drug is in vast excess [38]. For products with low immunogenicity risk, a 1:100 ADA-to-drug molar ratio represents a conservative threshold for potential safety relevance. At such sub-stoichiometric levels, approximately 99% drugs remain unbound, limiting conditions that favor immune complex formation, complement activation, or Fc-mediated effector engagement. Consequently, the safety risk associated with failing to detect very low ADA levels in the presence of a 100-fold molar excess of the drug is expected to be minimal.

These findings support integrating immunogenicity risk factors–including dosing regimen, anticipated drug exposure, and patient characteristics–directly into ADA assay validation to ensure a clinically relevant assessment of ADA response. In this approach, the IRA not only defines which assays should be employed but also specifies the criteria they must meet, extending beyond the current practice that focuses primarily on assay characterization.

We recommend characterizing drug tolerance under drug concentrations reflective of study samples and across multiple PCs and concentration ranges. This approach aligns with current regulatory expectations and the bioanalysis scientific community’s current trend toward clinically relevant assay validation, enabling meaningful interpretation of immunogenicity data. This strategy should be prospectively discussed with health authorities to ensure alignment of clinically relevant assay performance criteria and interpretation strategies.

Integrating immunogenicity risk factors and validating ADA assays under clinically relevant drug concentrations and PCs ensures that safety assessments accurately reflect the true clinical impact of low-level ADA, especially when drug is present in large excess.

Reporting of ADA negative results at high drug concentrations

Shankar et al. [26] proposed classifying ADA-negative results as inconclusive when sample drug concentrations exceed an ADA assay’s demonstrated drug tolerance, to mitigate the risk of false-negative results due to drug interference. This practice has since been echoed in multiple authoritative sources [11,39–41].

Operationally, drug tolerance is defined as the highest drug concentration at which a predefined positive control level remains detectable according to the assay’s acceptance criteria. Importantly, the drug tolerance value established during validation is not determined by drug concentration alone, it is a multifactorial measure that depends on positive control characteristics, such as concentration, affinity, and clonality. As demonstrated by Zanghi et al. [31] and confirmed in this work, positive control selection is a key driver of an assay’s nominal sensitivity and, by extension, its nominal drug tolerance. Accordingly, changing the positive control identity or concentration can materially change the drug tolerance estimate. In conventional validation strategies, the predefined positive control level used to establish drug tolerance is 100 ng/mL. This choice stems from FDA’s 2019 guidance, which designates 100 ng/mL as the minimum ADA assay sensitivity because such ADA levels may be associated with clinical impact [42,43]. However, the clinical interpretability of a drug tolerance threshold set at 100 ng/mL diminishes when clinical samples contain very high drug concentrations. Under these conditions, demonstrating detectability of low positive control primarily reflects the ability to detect very low ADA levels, which are unlikely to impact safety or efficacy when a large excess of circulating drug is present.

While flagging certain ADA results as inconclusive with respect to drug tolerance remains important for signaling potential false-negative risk, the relevance of applying the default 100 ng/mL sensitivity-based threshold warrants reconsideration in specific settings. In oncology indications with high-frequency, high-dose regimens, a nominally low drug tolerance limit set using a very low positive control level may classify a large fraction of samples as inconclusive, despite limited clinical relevance of undetectably low ADA in the observed drug exposures.

To better align drug tolerance characterization with assay intent and the exposure context, we propose that clinical relevance be explicitly incorporated into both drug tolerance assessment and result interpretation. A practical approach is to evaluate drug tolerance at positive control levels commensurate with ADA concentrations expected to be clinically meaningfulfor example, defined relative to contemporaneous drug concentration (i.e., a drug-to-ADA molar ratio) or based on a drug-binding fraction considered relevant for neutralization. Under this framework, the drug tolerance value should be calculated and reported using the clinically relevant positive control level selected, given that drug tolerance is inherently dependent on positive control properties.

This rationale underpins our concept of clinically relevant drug tolerance: drug tolerance assessments should employ positive control conditions (including identity, affinity, and concentration) that reflect the anticipated clinical drug burden and the ADA levels relevant for interpretation. This ensures that “inconclusive due to drug interference” qualification of negative results are not driven by an overly conservative validation configuration, such as a positive control concentration that is not meaningful within the relevant clinical exposure range.

Therefore, ADA-negative results at high drug concentrations should be interpreted using clinically relevant drug tolerance thresholds, rather than default sensitivity-based values, to avoid inconclusive classifications that do not reflect clinical significance.

Recommendations

Findings from this program underscore the need for fit-for-purpose ADA assay characterization, strategic investment in diverse critical reagents (including multiple positive controls spanning different affinities and epitopes), and early engagement with regulators to align on evolving expectations.

For high-dose biologic therapies, validation and interpretation should be anchored to clinically relevant exposure conditions, including anticipated on-treatment drug concentrations and ADA to drug ratios. In such settings, traditional sensitivity benchmarks (for example, 100 ng per mL) may not reflect clinically meaningful safety risks or potential PK and PD impacts when circulating drugs are very high. Incorporating exposure-based considerations enables more reliable ADA monitoring and scientifically justified interpretation of immunogenicity outcomes.

Accordingly, for ADA assays intended to evaluate PK impact, we recommend explicitly incorporating the quantitative relationship between ADA magnitude and circulating drug concentration during validation (Figure 4) and evaluating and reporting drug tolerance across clinically relevant ADA and drug combinations rather than relying on default thresholds. Consistent with the industry recommendations for ADA assay validation and reporting [1], drug tolerance should be presented as a spectrum across ADA (positive control) and drug concentrations, with maximum expected on-treatment levels clearly defined and contextualized against validated tolerance. Critically, within-study validation in patient samples is essential to confirm that drug tolerance remains appropriate under the current study matrix and exposure conditions. Drug tolerance, defined at 100 ng/mL of a surrogate positive control, has become a widely adopted convention and provides a conservative benchmark across development programs. However, this practice is typically applied independently of the drug concentrations present in clinical samples, despite growing evidence that the clinical relevance of ADA detection depends on the quantitative relationship between the ADA magnitude and circulating drug levels [26,31]. As discussed here, considering the ADA-to-drug molar ratio offers an additional, exposure-aligned framework to contextualize assay sensitivity and drug tolerance, particularly for high-dose biologics where low-level ADA is unlikely to impact pharmacokinetics or safety. While this ratio-based perspective can help bridge assay performance with clinical interpretation, how best to formally integrate such concepts into standardized validation and reporting practices remains an open question and an area of active discussion within the field [1,11,31].

In order to avoid labeling results as inconclusive when, they are not, we would also like to suggest refining the drug tolerance definition. Building on “the highest drug concentration that still yields a positive result at the assay cut point” [11,26,39–41], we recommend specifying that this determination should be made at a clinically relevant positive control level.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings support moving beyond a single, default drug tolerance cutoff toward an exposure-aligned interpretation framework. ADA-negative results should not be automatically labeled inconclusive solely because measured drug concentrations exceed a default tolerance value, particularly when that value is anchored to a positive control level that is not representative of the clinical exposure range. Instead, applying clinically relevant drug tolerance thresholds enables interpretation of ADA-negative results that reflect the true drug to ADA relationship in each sample. While the use of multiple positive control thresholds requires additional assay characterization and data handling, this approach is both feasible and especially valuable in late-stage development, when clinically relevant limits can be substantiated and data support robust, clinically meaningful conclusions.

We build on the cross-industry recommendations [1], including validation of clinically relevant drug tolerance concentrations using multiple positive control levels, and extend these principles to patient-centered practices spanning within-study method validation, study conduct, and reporting. Finally, we provide data demonstrating the relevance and regulatory acceptance of this approach in real-world settings, which we anticipate will inform and support broader industry adoption.

Future perspective

Over the next 5–10 years, ADA testing is likely to transition from a predominantly assay-centric activity to a more integrated, exposure-aware, and risk-based activity. As biologics are typically administered at high doses in oncology indications with increased frequency, assay validation paradigms based solely on fixed sensitivity benchmarks are expected to become less informative. Consequently, we anticipate that ADA assay design, validation, and interpretation will increasingly incorporate quantitative factors such as drug exposure, target engagement, and the relationship between ADA magnitude and potential clinical impact.

Within this evolving framework, exposure-based concepts–such as the ADA-to-drug molar ratio–appears to be a meaningful parameter to contextualize assay sensitivity and drug tolerance in settings where circulating drug concentrations are high. Applied as a complement rather than a replacement to conventional sensitivity thresholds, such approaches will support the interpretation of ADA-negative results when drug is present in large excess [26,31]. However, the broader adoption of exposure-aligned metrics will require greater cross-program experience and proactive sharing of evidence by industry with regulators to support a change in the current practice [1,11].

At the same time, well-characterized positive controls and systematic evaluation of drug tolerance across clinically relevant ADA and drug concentration ranges are expected to become standard practice. Advances in assay platforms, sample handling strategies, and reagent design will further enhance robustness under high drug and target burden conditions while maintaining operational feasibility. Collectively, these developments are expected to support immunogenicity assessment strategies that are more closely aligned with patient safety, pharmacokinetic impact, and therapeutic decision-making, enabling more confident and clinically meaningful use of ADA data in late-stage development and post-approval settings.
