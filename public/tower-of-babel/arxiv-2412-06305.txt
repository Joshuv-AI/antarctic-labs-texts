Quasi-likelihood-based EM algorithm for regime-switching SDE Thanks: * Corresponding author
Yuzhong Cheng 1 *, Hiroki Masuda 2

Abstract
. This paper considers estimating the parameters in a regime-switching stochastic differential equation(SDE) driven by Normal Inverse Gaussian(NIG) noise. The model under consideration incorporates a continuous-time finite state Markov chain to capture regime changes, enabling a more realistic representation of evolving market conditions or environmental factors. Although the continuous dynamics are typically observable, the hidden nature of the Markov chain introduces significant complexity, rendering standard likelihood-based methods less effective. To address these challenges, we propose an estimation algorithm designed for discrete, high-frequency observations, even when the Markov chain is not directly observed. Our approach integrates the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, which iteratively refines parameter estimates in the presence of latent variables, with a quasi-likelihood method adapted to NIG noise. Notably, this method can simultaneously estimate parameters within both the SDE coefficients and the driving noise. Simulation results are provided to evaluate the performance of the algorithm. These experiments demonstrate that the proposed method provides reasonable estimation under challenging conditions.



1.
Introduction

We consider the problem of parameter estimation for stochastic differential equations (SDEs) with Markovian switching, a class of hybrid systems that integrates continuous dynamics with discrete events.
More precisely, It is a stochastic process with two components, one component
X
describes the evolution of the process, and another component
\alpha
describes the switching between different regimes.
This framework is widely applicable in modeling complex systems where continuous processes interact with discrete state transitions, such as those encountered in finance, ecology, and engineering.

In this paper, we focus on applying an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to estimate parameters in Markov switching equations with hidden states. Besides the EM algorithm, another widely used approach to this problem is the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, with relevant literature found in
[
Hahn et al., 2010
]
and
[
Hibbah et al., 2020
]
.

For the EM approach, a previous study by
[
Chevallier and Goutte, 2017
]
introduced a two-step method to estimate parameters in Markov switching stochastic differential equations with Lévy noise. Their approach first employs an EM algorithm designed to estimate parameters in a Markov switching SDE driven by Wiener noise, followed by a second step in which the parameters in the Lévy noise are estimated by fitting the noise distribution to each regime. Their study assumes fixed observation time steps.

In contrast, this paper also considers Markov switching SDEs with Lévy noise but proposes an EM algorithm that can simultaneously estimate parameters in both the drift coefficient and the Lévy noise components. Our method is based on a quasi-likelihood approach for Lévy noise under high-frequency observations, allowing for an adaptive, unfixed time step size.

2.
Quasi likelihood approach for NIG Lévy noise

In this section, we introduce a Cauchy quasi-likelihood function for the equation (
1.1
), where the driving noise
Z
is modeled as a NIG Lévy process.
Quasi-likelihood functions are well established in the literature and are an effective approach for approximating the true likelihood function. Gaussian quasi-likelihood functions are commonly used for models driven by a Wiener process, while for more general driving noises, such as jump processes, non-Gaussian quasi-likelihood functions may also be employed. This has been demonstrated to yield satisfactory statistical properties, as discussed in
[
Masuda, 2019
]
and
[
Clément and Gloter, 2020a
]
.

When
Z
is a NIG Lévy process where
Z_{1}
follows
\operatorname{NIG}(a,0,\delta,0)
where the density is given in (
1.3
), we have that
Z_{h}
and
\Delta_{j}Z
follow the same distribution, and thus
Z_{h}
follows an
\operatorname{NIG}(a,0,\delta h,0)
distribution. Therefore, by Euler approximation, we have the approximate expression for
\Delta_{j}Z
:

X_{t_{j}}-\left(X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(\alpha_{t_{j-1}})-X_{t_{j-1}})h\right)\approx\Delta_{j}Z\sim\operatorname{NIG}(a,0,\delta h,0).

Also it is known that for any
c,d\in\mathbb{R}

Y=cX+d\sim\operatorname{NIG}\left(\frac{a}{|c|},0,c\delta,d\right),

when
X\sim\operatorname{NIG}(a,0,\delta,0)
, see
[
Chevallier and Goutte, 2017
]
.
From this, we obtain that

\frac{\Delta_{j}Z}{\delta h}\sim\operatorname{NIG}(ah|\delta|,0,1,0).

Substituting the approximate expression for
\Delta_{j}Z
into the above relation, we have

\frac{X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)}{\delta h}\approx\frac{\Delta_{j}Z}{\delta h}\sim\operatorname{NIG}(ah|\delta|,0,1,0),

where

\mu_{j-1}(\lambda):=X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(\alpha_{t_{j-1}})-X_{t_{j-1}})h.

As noted in
[
Kawai and Masuda, 2013
]
this probability density converges to a standard Cauchy distribution as
h\to 0
. Thus, we can use the Cauchy distribution to approximate the NIG distribution for small-time step sizes. We let

\bm{\eta}
be a standard Cauchy random variable independent of
X
and
\alpha
, then the conditional distribution of
X_{t_{j}}
given
(X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}})
may be approximated by

X_{t_{j}}\approx\delta h\bm{\eta}+\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)=\delta h\bm{\eta}+X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(\alpha_{t_{j-1}})-X_{t_{j-1}})h,

when time stepsize
h
be sufficiently small. We set
f(X_{t_{j}}|{X}_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
be a Cauchy density function given by

(2.1)

f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)=\frac{1}{\delta h\pi\left(1+\left(\frac{X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)}{\delta h}\right)^{2}\right)},

and now use this
f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
to approximate transition density of
X_{t}
from
t_{j-1}
to
t_{j}
.
Observe that (
1.3
) contains the Bessel function, which makes it computationally demanding. A notable advantage of using the Cauchy distribution approximation is that it avoids this complexity.

Under the guidance of above ideology, we define Cauchy quasi-likelihood function
\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\theta)
which is given by

(2.2)

\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta).

The Cauchy Quasi Maximum Likelihood Estimator (CQMLE) seeks to maximize the Cauchy quasi-likelihood function,
\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\theta)
, concerning the parameter
\theta
. However, it is not feasible to directly maximize
\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\theta)
as it contains the unobserved process
\alpha
. Thus, function
\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\theta)
here is not a proper choice in our situation. It should be noted that if
\alpha
were observable,
\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\theta)
would be a suitable choice for maximization.
We propose an EM algorithm-based approach utilizing a Cauchy quasi-likelihood approximation in the next section.

Remark 2.1
.

Since we use the Euler approximation to construct the quasi-likelihood function, our approach is not limited to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck-type SDE (
1.1
). In general, we can apply it to the broader class of regime-switching SDEs with the following form:

dX_{t}=\mu(X_{t},\alpha_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t},\alpha_{t})dZ_{t}.

See also Remark
3.1
.

3.
EM algorithm approach

In this section, we consider the EM algorithm approach for equation (
1.1
).

3.1.
Standard EM algorithm revisited

We will use the EM algorithm in the next subsection. The EM algorithm introduced by
[
Dempster et al., 1977
]
is an efficient iterative method to compute the Maximum Likelihood estimator when some variable is unobserved.
Here we introduce the basic background of the EM algorithm. Let
X
and
Y
be random variables, and define
p(x,y;\theta)
as the joint density function of
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
p(y|x;\theta)
as the conditional density function of
Y
given
X
, where
\theta\in\Theta
,
\Theta
is a bounded convex domain of parameters. Suppose that the variable
X
is observed and
Y
is a latent variable. The EM algorithm is to estimate
\theta
under the incompletely observed data. The log-likelihood function
l_{n}(\theta)
of
n
i.i.d. observations
\{(X_{1},...,X_{n}\}
is defined by

l_{n}(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left(\int p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)dy_{j}\right).

The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is to find some
\hat{\theta}_{n}\in\Theta
which maximizes the function
l_{n}(\theta)
. The EM algorithm uses an auxiliary function to calculate the MLE through iteration. We introduce a function
Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, which is called finite-sample Q-function, defined by

(3.1)

Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})\log p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)dy_{j}\right).

The EM algorithm consists of the following two steps. Given an initial value
\theta^{(0)}

•

The first E-step is to compute the finite-sample Q-function
Q_{n}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})
,

Q_{n}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int p(y_{j}|X_{j};\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})\log p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)dy_{j}\right),

for
m=0,1,...
;

•

The second M-step is to find

\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m+1)}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}Q_{n}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)}).

After doing the E-step and M-step, we have the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m+1)}
through
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)}
. By repeating the E-step and M-step, the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m+1)}
is expected to converge to the MLE by the following reason.

Observe that, for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle l_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left(\int p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)dy_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left(\int\frac{p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)}{p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})}p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})dy_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int\log\left(\frac{p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)}{p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})}\right)p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})dy_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int\log\left(p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta)\right)p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})dy_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int\log\left(p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})\right)p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})dy_{j}

\displaystyle=Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int\log\left(p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})\right)p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})dy_{j},

where the inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality. It is clear to see that when
\theta=\theta^{\prime}
the inequality becomes an equality because
\frac{p(X_{j},y_{j};\theta^{\prime})}{p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta^{\prime})}=p(X_{j};\theta^{\prime})
. Let

\Delta_{n}(\theta):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int\log\left(p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta)\right)p(y_{j}|X_{j};\theta)dy_{j},

so that we have

(3.2)

l_{n}(\theta)\geq Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\Delta_{n}(\theta^{\prime}),

for all
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
. Our goal here is to find a value
\theta
that maximizes the log-likelihood function
\theta\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rightarrow$\cr\kern-0.6458pt\raise 1.18399pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[0.522]{$\mid$}}\cr}}}l_{n}(\theta)
. Now we have the function
Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\Delta_{n}(\theta^{\prime})
is bounded from above by the log-likelihood function
l_{n}(\theta)
and the relation
l_{n}(\theta^{\prime})=Q_{n}(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})-\Delta_{n}(\theta^{\prime})
.
Hence we know that each
\theta
maximizing the function
Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\Delta_{n}(\theta^{\prime})
should increase the log-likelihood function
l_{n}(\theta)
. Thus, instead of directly maximizing function
l_{n}(\theta)
, we can try to maximize the function
\theta\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rightarrow$\cr\kern-0.6458pt\raise 1.18399pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[0.522]{$\mid$}}\cr}}}Q_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\Delta_{n}(\theta^{\prime})
. This is the basis of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. We have

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m+1)}

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}\left(Q_{n}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})-\Delta_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}Q_{n}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)}),

where we drop the term which contains no
\theta
. Since

Q_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m+1)},\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})-\Delta_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})\geq Q_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)},\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)})-\Delta_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)}),

by (
3.2
), we have

l_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m+1)})\geq l_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(m)}).

This shows that the EM algorithm iteratively increases the log-likelihood function
l_{n}(\theta)
.

The main advantage of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm is that it provides a convenient framework for solving Maximum Likelihood Estimation problems when unobserved or missing data is present. By maximizing the finite-sample Q-function instead of the log-likelihood function directly, the EM algorithm makes it easier to handle situations where some data is not directly available.

3.2.
EM algorithm for our Switching SDE

Now, we shift our focus back to the switching SDE model. Our approach for estimating the parameter
\theta
is built upon an EM algorithm tailored for diffusions with Markovian switching, as described in
[
Chevallier and Goutte, 2017
]
. However, our method diverges from
[
Chevallier and Goutte, 2017
]
in several key aspects. Specifically, we operate in a high-frequency observation setting, where the time step size
h
is sufficiently small. Instead of relying on the commonly used Gaussian likelihood approximation, we leverage the asymptotic distribution of the Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) Lévy process over small time intervals to construct a Cauchy quasi-likelihood function.
As was mentioned, the EM algorithm is particularly well-suited for models involving latent variables, as it offers a computationally feasible approximation to the maximum likelihood estimator. A critical component of this method is the definition of an appropriate target function—in our case, a quasi-likelihood function—to be maximized through iterative steps of the EM algorithm.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{0,n}=(X_{t_{0}},X_{t_{1}},...,X_{t_{n}})
denote all observations of
X
,
\mathbb{A}_{0,n}=(\alpha_{t_{0}},\alpha_{t_{1}},...,\alpha_{t_{n}})
. Define
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
as the joint density function of
\mathbb{X}_{0,n}
and
\mathbb{A}_{0,n}
under
\theta
with respect to some
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\times\mathcal{S}^{n+1}
. Since the continuous-time Markov chain
\alpha
is not directly observed, we investigate an EM algorithm to estimate the parameters. Following the idea in the standard EM algorithm, we integrate the joint density function
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
over the unobserved variables
\mathbb{A}_{0,n}
to define the log-likelihood function
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\theta)
as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{n}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\log\left(\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)\right).

By following the standard EM algorithm framework, we derive the following: for any
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{n}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\log\left(\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\frac{p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)}{p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)}p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\log\left(\frac{p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)}{p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)}\right)p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\log\left(p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)\right)p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\log\left(p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)\right)p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right).

Within the standard EM algorithm framework, the function

H_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}):=\sum_{\mathbb{A}_{0,n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\log\left(p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)\right)p\left(\mathbb{A}_{0,n}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)

is commonly used for iterative optimization. However, in our context, the explicit form of the function
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
is unknown, making a direct use in the EM algorithm unsuitable. To address this issue, we introduce a quasi-likelihood approximation for
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
as a feasible alternative. By employing the quasi-likelihood approximation, the function
H_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
can be reformulated to accommodate its use within the EM algorithm framework.

We now detail the procedure to modify the function
H_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
.
First, observe that

\displaystyle p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)=p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)p(\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta).

Recall that the Markov chain
\alpha
and the Lévy process
Z
are assumed to be independent. Consequently, the process
X
can be viewed as a solution to an inhomogeneous Markovian Lévy-driven stochastic differential equation, conditional on
\alpha
. The Euler approximation for
X_{t_{j}}
is expressed as

\displaystyle X_{t_{j}}\approx X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(\alpha_{t_{j-1}})-X_{t_{j-1}})h+\Delta_{j}Z.

This approximation provides an approximated representation for the density of
X_{t_{j}}
conditional on
X_{t_{j-1}}
and
\alpha_{t_{j-1}}
, which can be modeled by the Cauchy density function
f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
as in (
2.1
). Hence, we approximate
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
by the following:

\displaystyle p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)

\displaystyle=p(X_{t_{n}}|\mathbb{X}_{0,n-1},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n-1}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)

\displaystyle\approx p(X_{t_{n}}|X_{t_{n-1}},\alpha_{t_{n-1}};\theta)p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n-1}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n-1};\theta)

\displaystyle\approx f(X_{t_{n}}|X_{t_{n-1}},\alpha_{t_{n-1}};\theta)p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n-1}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n-1};\theta).

We employ the following two approximation steps:

•

In the first approximation, we approximate
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
as the product of
p(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
, utilizing the Markov property of
X_{t}
conditioned on
\alpha_{t}
.

•

In the second approximation, we approximate
p(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
using the Cauchy density
f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
in (
2.1
). This approximation is particularly useful in high-frequency scenarios and serves as the foundation for our quasi-likelihood approach to approximating
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
.

This two-step approximation allows us to construct the following quasi-likelihood approach suitable for high-frequency observations.
By induction, we derive:

(3.4)

p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)\approx\prod_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta).

Additionally, note that
p(\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
can be expressed as the product of transition densities
p(\alpha_{t_{j}}|\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)
.
Consequently, we approximate
p(\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
as:

\displaystyle p(\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}p(\alpha_{t_{j}}|\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(I_{\{\alpha_{t_{j}}=\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\}}\left(1+q_{\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\alpha_{t_{j-1}}}h+o(h)\right)+I_{\{\alpha_{t_{j}}\neq\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\}}\left(q_{\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\alpha_{t_{j}}}h+o(h)\right)\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\approx\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(I_{\{\alpha_{t_{j}}=\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\}}\left(1+q_{\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\alpha_{t_{j-1}}}h\right)+I_{\{\alpha_{t_{j}}\neq\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\}}q_{\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\alpha_{t_{j}}}h\right),

where
I
is the indicator function.
The Cauchy quasi-likelihood approximation of
p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)
combines (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), leading to:

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle(\mathbb{X}_{0,n},\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)=p(\mathbb{X}_{0,n}|\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)p(\mathbb{A}_{0,n};\theta)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\approx\prod_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}};\theta)\left(I_{\{\alpha_{t_{j}}=\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\}}\left(1+q_{\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\alpha_{t_{j-1}}}h\right)+I_{\{\alpha_{t_{j}}\neq\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\}}q_{\alpha_{t_{j-1}}\alpha_{t_{j}}}h\right).

Using this approximation, it is natural to define the modified function
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\log\biggl\{

\displaystyle f\left(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i;\theta\right)\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\biggr\}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right),

with which we will replace
H_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
in the EM algorithm.

In
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\theta^{\prime})
, the conditional probabilities of all latent variables
\mathbb{A}_{0,n}
in
H_{n}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
are reduced to the conditional probabilities of two consecutive variables
\alpha_{t_{j-1}}
and
\alpha_{t_{j}}
. This modification aligns the logarithmic terms in the summation and simplifies the computational process.
The components of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\theta^{\prime})
, except for
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)
, are given explicitly.
This facilitates differentiation with respect to the parameter
\theta
, which is essential for optimization in the EM algorithm.
The term
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\theta^{\prime}\right)
is computable using numerical methods, which will be discussed in Section
3.3
.

Remark 3.1
.

We should mention it here that in the second step of approximation (
3.4
), we utilize the Cauchy approximation
f\left(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i;\theta\right)
due to the Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) distributional property of the driving Lévy noise. In high-frequency settings, the NIG density can be effectively approximated by a Cauchy density.
In general, an appropriate quasi-likelihood should be selected according to the specific distributional characteristics of the driving Lévy process. As long as the high-frequency sampling
h\to 0
for infinite-activity non-Gaussian
Z
is concerned, one natural (and often optimal) choice is the
\alpha
-stable approximation for
\alpha\in(0,2)
. We refer interested readers to
[
Masuda, 2019
]
,
[
Masuda, 2023
]
,
[
Clément and Gloter, 2020b
]
, and the references therein.

To formulate the EM algorithm based on the modified function
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\theta^{\prime})
, we proceed as follows.

Initialization:

Begin by specifying an initial value for the parameter vector:

\hat{\theta}^{(0)}=(b(1)^{(0)},b(2)^{(0)},...,b(N)^{(0)},\lambda^{(0)},\delta^{(0)},Q^{(0)}),

where
\hat{\theta}^{(0)}
serves as the starting point for the iterative procedure.

First E-step:

In the first E-step, calculate the function
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\theta^{\prime})
by substituting the initial parameter values
\hat{\theta}^{(0)}
into
\theta^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(0)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\log\biggl\{

\displaystyle f\left(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i;\theta\right)\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\biggr\}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(0)}\right),

First M-step:

In the first M-step, maximize
\theta\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rightarrow$\cr\kern-0.6458pt\raise 1.18399pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[0.522]{$\mid$}}\cr}}}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(0)})
to obtain an updated parameter estimate:

\hat{\theta}^{(1)}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(0)}).

Iterative Steps:

After obtaining
\hat{\theta}^{(1)}
, set
\theta^{\prime}=\hat{\theta}^{(1)}
in equation (
3.7
), and repeat the above mentioned E- and M-steps iteratively.
Suppose we are at the
m+1
-th step:

E-step:

the finite-sample Cauchy quasi-likelihood function for
m+1
step is

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\log\biggl\{

\displaystyle f\left(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i;\theta\right)\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\biggr\}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

M-step:

M-step is to compute the maximizer of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})
. We let

\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)}).

Note that we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\geq\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}).

To compute
\hat{\theta}
in each step, for example at
m+1
step, we compute the derivatives of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})
with respect to
\theta
. First, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\log

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\delta h\pi\left(1+\left(\frac{X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)}{\delta h}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\log

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1+q_{ii}h}{\delta h\pi\left(1+\left(\frac{X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)}{\delta h}\right)^{2}\right)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq i\\
k,i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{q_{ik}h}{\delta h\pi\left(1+\left(\frac{X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)}{\delta h}\right)^{2}\right)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Here we give expressions of these derivatives. First, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\delta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\left(K_{1,j}(\theta)K_{2,j}(\theta)\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

where

\displaystyle K_{1,j}(\theta):=\frac{\pi(X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda))^{2}}{\delta^{2}h}-h\pi,

\displaystyle K_{2,j}(\theta):=\frac{1}{\delta h\pi\left(1+\left(\frac{X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda)}{\delta h}\right)^{2}\right)}.

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\left(K_{2,j}(\theta)K_{3,j}(\theta)\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

where

\displaystyle K_{3,j}(\theta):=\frac{2\pi(X_{t_{j}}-\mu_{j-1}(\lambda))(b(i)-X_{t_{j-1}})}{\delta}.

For the derivatives with respect to
b(l)
,
l\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{b(l)}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}(K_{2,l,j}^{\star}(\theta)K_{4,l,j}(\theta))\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=l,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

where

\displaystyle K_{2,l,j}^{\star}(\theta):=\frac{1}{\delta h\pi\left(1+\left(\frac{X_{t_{j}}-(X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(l)-X_{t_{j-1}})h)}{\delta h}\right)^{2}\right)},

\displaystyle K_{4,l,j}(\theta):=\frac{2\pi(X_{t_{j}}-(X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(l)-X_{t_{j-1}})h))\lambda}{\delta}.

In many applications of the EM algorithm, it is impossible to explicitly find out the M-step. As can be seen from the above expressions of the first derivative,
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}
can not be computed in a closed form. Thus we have to apply some feasible numerical approaches.
One such approach is solving the M-step by using a one-step estimation method of the Newton-Raphson type, as described in
[
Lange, 1995
]
. In this case the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}
is given by

(3.9)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star}:=\hat{\theta}^{(m)}-\left(\partial^{2}_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\right)^{-1}\partial_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}).

This method suggests that a one-step Newton method at each M-step would be sufficient to ensure the convergence of the EM algorithm.
To avoid potential computational issues, we will not make direct use of the Hessian matrix
\partial^{2}_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)})
; just for reference, we give its expression in Section
5.1
.

Another approach is the first-order EM algorithm introduced in
[
Balakrishnan et al., 2017
]
.
Let
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}
be the estimate in
m+1
step computed by the first-order method:

(3.10)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}=\hat{\theta}^{(m)}+\rho\partial_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta=\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}),

where
\rho>0
is an appropriately chosen value. In the paper
[
Balakrishnan et al., 2017
]
, the value of
\rho
is chosen such that it satisfies certain conditions that guarantee the theoretical convergence of the EM algorithm.
Through these fomula we can nurmerically compute
\theta=\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}
without solving the equation
\partial_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=0
. If we use the first-order method in (
3.10
), when we enter the
(m+2)
th iteration, we let
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}=\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}
, so the new M-step in
(m+1)
th step becomes

M
′
-step:

Compute
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}
using (
3.10
)

\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}=\hat{\theta}^{(m)}+\rho\partial_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta=\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}),

and then let
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}=\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}
for the
m+2
step.

We propose the EM algorithm as in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

EM Algorithm for switching SDE

1:

Choose initial value
\hat{\theta}^{(0)}=(b(1)^{(0)},b(2)^{(0)},...,b(N)^{(0)},\lambda^{(0)},\delta^{(0)})
, a small positive
\epsilon
, and an appropriate positive
\rho
. Let
m=0
.

2:

E-step:
Compute the function
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})
where

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\log\biggl\{

\displaystyle f\left(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i;\theta\right)\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\biggr\}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

3:

M
′
-step
: Compute the estimate
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}=\hat{\theta}^{(m)}
where

\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}=\hat{\theta}^{(m)}+\rho\partial_{\theta}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta=\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}).

4:

Let
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}=\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}_{\star,\rho}
.

5:

Check the termination condition. If the difference in absolute value is less than
\epsilon
, then stop the algorithm and output
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}
as the result. If not, continue to the next step.

6:

Let
m=m+1
and return to the E-step.

The computation of
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
is presented in Algorithm
2
.

Remark 3.2
.

In this study, to focus on our primary objective, namely the estimation of
\theta
, we are assuming that the generator
Q
is known.
If computational complexity is not a concern, the EM algorithm could be extended to treat
Q
as an additional estimation target.
To incorporate
Q
into the estimation, the Cauchy quasi-likelihood function (
3.7
) in the EM algorithm can be formally modified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta,Q;\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\log\biggl\{

\displaystyle f\left(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i;\theta\right)\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\biggr\}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)}\right).

In addition, the M-step must include an update for
Q
, which can be performed as follows in an analogous way to (
3.10
):

\hat{Q}^{(m+1)}=\hat{Q}^{(m)}+\rho\partial_{Q}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)}).

The first-order derivatives of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta,Q;\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)})
with respect to
q_{ik}
are given by:

\displaystyle\partial_{q_{ll}}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta,Q;\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{h}{1+q_{ll}h}\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=l,\alpha_{t_{j}}=l|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{q_{lm}}\mathbb{H}_{n}(\theta,Q;\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{q_{lm}}\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=l,\alpha_{t_{j}}=m|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)}\right),\,\,\,\operatorname{for}l\neq m.

The computation of the probabilities
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)},\hat{Q}^{(m)}\right)
is analogous to the method described in Section
3.3
, with replace
q_{ik}
by
\hat{q}_{ik}^{(m)}
.

3.3.
Caculation of some probabilities

The iteration procedure in (
3.8
) involves probability values
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for
i,k\in\mathcal{S}
and
j=1,2,...,n
. However, computing these probabilities can be challenging as these values depend on all information
\mathbb{X}_{0,n}
. To address this, we can adopt a method proposed by
[
Kim, 1994
]
, which was originally used for dynamic linear models, and adapt it for our purposes.
In this subsection, we illustrate the method for computing these values.
The calculation of
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
can be divided into three steps:

Step 1:

Compute the filtered probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i\mid\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Step 2:

Using the results obtained in Step 1, calculate the probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k\mid\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

and the probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=k\mid\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Step 3:

Utilize the results from Step 2 to compute the joint probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k\mid\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

We will describe each part in detail and also note that, in some cases, the explicit computation of these probabilities may be difficult due to numerical issues, so we may need to use approximations instead of explicit values. By following this procedure, we can obtain reliable probability values for the iterative process. Let
p(\cdot)
be the density function of
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)
with respect to suitable measure.

3.3.1.
Step 1 : Calculation of
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{p\left(X_{t_{j}}|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}.

Note that we have

\displaystyle p\left(X_{t_{j}}|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=l,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right),

so it is enough to compute
p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
. We propose to use the following expression:

\displaystyle p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=p\left(X_{t_{j}}|\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{kk}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ki}h\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Notice that we used the approximation in the third line of (
3.11
) as follows:

(3.12)

p\left(X_{t_{j}}|\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\approx f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k;\hat{\theta}^{(m)}),

and also used the following approximation in the line of (
3.11
):

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\approx I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h.

We explain the reason why (
3.12
) holds as follows:

\displaystyle p\left(X_{t_{j}}|\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,X_{t_{j-1}};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,X_{t_{j-1}};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}

(3.14)

\displaystyle=f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k;\hat{\theta}^{(m)}).

Note that we used the Markov property of the two-component process
(X_{t},\alpha_{t})
in the second line of (
3.14
) and that we applied our Cauchy approximation toward the unknown transition probability of
(X_{t},\alpha_{t})
in the third line of (
3.14
).

So we finally obtain a formula to compute
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j}\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
, that is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)=\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=l,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k,|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)},

with the approximation

\displaystyle p\left(X_{t_{j}},\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx f(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}},\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k;\hat{\theta}^{(m)})\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{kk}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ki}h\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Note that the value
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for each
j
is obtained from the value
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
. Therefore we obtain a forward algorithm to calculate
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for all
j=1,...,n
with given initial values
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{0}}=i|X_{0};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for
i\in\mathcal{S}
.

3.3.2.
Step 2 : Calculation of
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
and
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

By straightforward computations and approximation (
3.13
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i,\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=k,\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left(I_{\{k=i\}}\left(1+q_{ii}h\right)+I_{\{k\neq i\}}q_{ik}h\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Note that
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j-1}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,j-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
is obtained in Step 1.

3.3.3.
Step 3 : Calculation of
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

We first propose the following approximation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}.

Here, we have used the following approximation in the second line:

(3.16)

\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\approx\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Below, we illustrate the reason for the approximation (
3.16
).
Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j},\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{p\left(\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j},;\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)p\left(\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}|\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{p\left(\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j},;\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}.

Now if the equation

(3.18)

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}|\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)=p\left(\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j},;\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)

holds, then the rightmost side of (
3.17
) simplifies to
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i|\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k,\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
, leading to (
3.16
) and hence
the approximation (
3.15
). In this case,
this equation implies that knowing both
\mathbb{X}_{0,j}
and
\alpha_{t_{j+1}}
,
\alpha_{t_{j}}
will not provide any additional information on
\mathbb{X}_{j+1,n}
beyond what is already contained in
\mathbb{X}_{0,j}
and
\alpha_{t_{j+1}}
. However, it should be noted that the argument is an approximate one since (
3.18
) may not always be true.

The expression (
3.15
) give us a backward method to compute
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
, knowing
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n}}=i|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
and
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,j};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for
i,k\in\mathcal{S}
,
j=0,1,2,...,n-1
.
We illustrate here the computation steps:

(1)

for
j=n-1
,
i,k\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{n}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\approx\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=i,\alpha_{t_{n}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n-1};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}.

(2)

for
j=n-2
,
i,k\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-2}}=i,\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\approx\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-2}}=i,\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n-2};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n-2};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)},

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k,\alpha_{t_{n}}=l|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right).

Note that the values
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{n-1}}=k,\alpha_{t_{n}}=l|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for all
k,l\in\mathcal{S}
are provided by the previous step (1).

(3)

Repeat this process several times until we obtain the values
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha_{t_{j}}=i,\alpha_{t_{j+1}}=k|\mathbb{X}_{0,n};\hat{\theta}^{(m)}\right)
for all
j=0,1,...,n-1
and
i,k\in\mathcal{S}
.

4.1.
Sample-path generation

The sample path of the process
X_{t}
in equation (
1.1

5.2
) in Appendix:

(4.1)

X_{t_{j}}\approx X_{t_{j-1}}+\lambda(b(\alpha_{t_{j-1}})-X_{t_{j-1}})h+\Delta_{j}Z,

where
\Delta_{j}Z\sim\operatorname{NIG}(a,0,\delta h,0)
.
There are two points to be mentioned for sample-path generation:

•

To estimate the parameters in all states, the sample path must range over the entire state space, which requires a sufficiently large terminal time
T
;

•

To ensure accuracy, the step size
h
must be small enough to satisfy the validity condition of the Cauchy approximation in the Euler approximation.

Below, we describe the method used to simulate a high-frequency sample
\{X_{t_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{n}
with
t_{j}=jh
; recall that
h=T/n
where
T
denotes the terminal sampling time.

•

First, we use the R package
spuRs
to generate Markov chain
\alpha
(see
[
Jones et al., 2014
]
for details) with a smaller step size
\delta=h/10
.

•

Using the data
(\alpha_{\delta l})_{l=0}^{10n}
, we then apply the Euler scheme to generate the data for
X
:

X_{s_{l}}=X_{s_{l-1}}+\lambda(b(\alpha_{s_{l-1}})-X_{s_{l-1}})\delta+Z_{s_{l}}-Z_{s_{l-1}},

where
s_{l}=l\delta
for
l\in\{0,1,...,10n\}
; this internally generates
\{X_{s_{l}}\}_{l=0}^{10n}
.

•

Next, we select a subsequence of the data
\{X_{t_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{n}
from
\{X_{t_{l}}\}_{l=0}^{10n}
with step size
h
; by thinning the sequence like this, we obtain high-frequency data
\{X_{t_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{n}
.

4.2.
Termination conditions

To determine when to terminate the EM algorithm, specific termination conditions need to be defined. As the convergence speed of the EM algorithm is generally slower than other numerical methods, these conditions should be carefully chosen to ensure the accuracy of the final estimation. The commonly used termination conditions are the difference of estimations, the relative difference of likelihood function values, and the relative difference of estimations. We also need a small positive value
\epsilon
as an admissible tolerance for convergence. Now suppose we are at
m+1
step, then the relative difference of likelihood function values is given by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{1}_{m,m+1}:=\frac{|\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)})-\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)})|}{|\mathbb{H}_{n}(\hat{\theta}^{(m)};\hat{\theta}^{(m)})|},

the relative difference of estimations is given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{2}_{m,m+1}:=\frac{|\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}-\hat{\theta}^{(m)}|}{|\hat{\theta}^{(m)}|},

and the difference of estimations is given by

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{3}_{m,m+1}:=|\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}-\hat{\theta}^{(m)}|.

We choose one of these three differences
\mathbb{D}_{m,m+1}
.
If the difference
\mathbb{D}_{m,m+1}
is greater than
\epsilon
, we continue to do the iteration step
m+2
. When the difference
\mathbb{D}_{m,m+1}
is less than
\epsilon
, we stop the iteration and let
\hat{\theta}^{(m+1)}
be the final estimates of parameter
\theta
.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we develop a parameter estimation method for regime-switching stochastic differential equations driven by NIG noise. The proposed algorithm is designed for discrete, high-frequency observations and can handle situations where the underlying Markov chain is not directly observed. By combining the EM algorithm with a Cauchy quasi-likelihood approximation, and exploiting the small-time asymptotics of NIG noise under high-frequency sampling, our framework enables the joint estimation of parameters associated with both the SDE coefficients and the noise distribution.
This approach represents an advance over previous methods, such as
[
Chevallier and Goutte, 2017
]
, by providing a unified treatment of hidden regimes and non-Gaussian noise in SDEs. We also presented a computational procedure for calculating the necessary conditional probabilities, including those involving the unobserved Markov chain states.

Our simulation results indicate that parameters directly linked to the Markov chain, such as those appearing in the drift and diffusion coefficients for each regime, are estimated with high accuracy. Although the estimation of the NIG noise scale parameter exhibits somewhat lower precision, the overall method still delivers reasonable estimates that improve as the sampling horizon or iteration count increases.
These findings suggest that the EM algorithm, enhanced by a Cauchy quasi-likelihood approximation, is a practical tool for analyzing complex stochastic systems involving regime-switching and non-Gaussian noise. Future research could extend this methodology to more general Lévy-driven SDEs and jointly estimate the transition intensities of the underlying Markov chain.

Proof.

\displaystyle C\Big(|X_{0}|^{2}+\left|\int_{0}^{t}f(X^{h}(s),\alpha^{h}(s))ds\right|^{2}+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X^{h}(s),\alpha^{h}(s))dw_{s}\right|^{2}

and if
s\in[t_{j},t_{j+1})
, by definition of the Euler approximation, Jensen’s inequality, and Doob’ martingale inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}_{s}-X^{h}(s)|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\left((s-t_{j})\int_{t_{j}}^{s}|f(X_{jh},\alpha_{jh})|^{2}du\right)+C\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t_{j}}^{s}\left\lVert\sigma(X_{jh},\alpha_{jh})\right\rVert du\right)

\displaystyle+C\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t_{j}}^{s}\int_{|y|<c}|F(X_{jh},y,\alpha_{jh})|^{2}\nu(dy)du\right)

\displaystyle+C\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t_{j}}^{s}\int_{|y|\geq c}|G(X_{jh},y,\alpha_{jh})|^{2}\nu(dy)du\right).

Then the growth condition and (
5.3
) finally yield

\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}_{s}-X^{h}(s)|^{2}\leq C\mathbb{E}\left((1+|X_{jh}|^{2})(h^{2}+h)\right)\leq Ch^{2}+Ch\leq Ch.

Therefore we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}|\bar{X}_{s}-X^{h}(s)|^{2}ds\right)\leq Ch.

Substituting this and (
5.17
) into (
5.16
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\bar{X}_{t}-X_{t}|^{2}\right)\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}|X_{s}-\bar{X}_{s}|^{2}ds\right)+Ch

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left(\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq r}|X_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}|^{2}\right)dr+Ch.

Now Grownwall’s inequality yields the desired result

\mathbb{E}\left(\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\bar{X}_{t}-X_{t}|^{2}\right)\leq Ch.

∎

5.3.
Markov property

In this section, we establish that the two-component process
(X_{t},\alpha_{t})
, which is comprised of the strong solution of equation
(
1.1
)
and the continuous-time Markov chain, possesses the Markov property. We prove a general result on the Markov property, which includes equation
(
1.1
)
as a special case. The proof is based on the idea behind the proof of the Markov property for Lévy-driven SDEs without Markov switching, as detailed in
[
Applebaum, 2009
]
. By applying the Markov property, we can then derive various properties and behaviors of the two-component process, which can be useful in further applications and analyses.

The Markov property of the two-component process depends on the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution to equation (
5.1
). This is ensured by Theorem 2.1 in
[
Xi and Zhu, 2017
]
, assuming that conditions (A1), (A2), and (A3) in Section
5.2
hold. The strong solution
X_{t}
is unique under these assumptions, as demonstrated in
[
Mao and Yuan, 2006
]
and
[
Xi and Zhu, 2017
]
.
To prove the Markov property, we will first state a theorem, followed by the necessary preliminary background required for the proof. Finally, we will present a detailed proof of the theorem.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assuming that the equation (
5.1
) with initial value
X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha_{0}=i\in\mathcal{S}
a.s. admits a unique solution. Then, the two-component process
(X_{t},\alpha_{t})
, which is formed by the strong solution
X_{t}
of equation (
5.1
) together with its associated Markov chain
\alpha_{t}
, is a Markov process.

To proceed, we need a property of continuous-time Markov chain and a classical Lemma.
We first note that the homogeneous continuous-time Markov chain
\alpha_{t}
with generator
Q=(q_{ij})_{N\times N}
can be represented by a stochastic integral with respect to a Poisson random measure (see
[
Mao and Yuan, 2006
]
,
[
Skorokhod, 2009
]
and
[
Yin and Xi, 2010
]
). For
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
with
j\neq i
, let
\Delta_{ij}
be the consecutive, left closed and right open intervals of the real line, each having length
q_{ij}
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{12}=[0,q_{12}),\>\Delta_{13}=[q_{12},q_{12}+q_{13}),\>

\displaystyle...\>

\displaystyle\Delta_{1N}=\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}q_{1j},\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j}\right),

\displaystyle\Delta_{21}=\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j},\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j}+q_{21}\right),\>\Delta_{23}=\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j}+q_{21},\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j}+q_{21}+q_{23}\right),

\displaystyle...

\displaystyle\Delta_{2N}=\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq 2}^{N-1}q_{2j},\sum_{j=2}^{N}q_{1j}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq 2}^{N}q_{2j}\right)

and so forth.
Define a function
h:\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

h(i,z)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}(j-i)I_{\left\{z\in\Delta_{ij}\right\}}.

This says that for each
i\in\mathcal{S}
, if
z\in\Delta_{ij}
, then
h(i,z)=j-i
; otherwise
h(i,z)=0
. Then as in Chapter 1 in
[
Mao and Yuan, 2006
]
,

(5.18)

d\alpha_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\left(\alpha_{t-},z\right)\Pi(dt,dz),

where
\Pi(dt,dz)
is a Poisson random measure with intensity
dt\times m(dz)
, and
m(\cdot)
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. The Poisson random measure
\Pi(\cdot,\cdot)
here is independent of the Wiener process
w
and Poisson random measure
N(\cdot,\cdot)
in equation (
5.1
).
Before proceeding to the proof of Theorem
5.3
, it is necessary to introduce a classical lemma; see Lemma 1.1.9 in
[
Applebaum, 2009
]
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
\mathcal{G}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{F}
. If
X
and
Y
are random variables such that
X
is
\mathcal{G}
-measurable and
Y
is independent with
\mathcal{G}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left(f(X,Y)|\mathcal{G}\right)=K_{f}(X),

for any bounded Borel measurable function
f(x,y)
, where
K_{f}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left(f(x,Y)\right)
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Now we give a proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

First define
\mathcal{G}_{s}=\sigma\{w_{s+u}-w_{s},N(s+u,A)-N(s,A),\Pi(s+u,B)-\Pi(s,B):u\geq 0,A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}-0),B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}
. Then
\mathcal{G}_{s}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, since
w_{t}
,
N(t,A)
and
\Pi(t,B)
are Lévy processes.
Let
\Phi_{0,t}(x,i)
be a solution of equation (
5.1
) with initial value
X_{0}=x
and
\alpha_{0}=i
a.s. and
\Lambda_{0,t}(i)
be a solution of equation (
5.18
) with
\alpha_{0}=i
a.s.:

\displaystyle\Phi_{0,t}(x,i)=

\displaystyle\>x+\int_{0}^{t}f(\Phi_{0,u}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,u}(i))du+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\Phi_{0,u}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,u}(i))dw_{u}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|<c}F(\Phi_{0,u-}(x,i),y,\Lambda_{0,u-}(i))\tilde{N}(du,dy)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|\geq c}G(\Phi_{0,u-}(x,i),y,\Lambda_{0,u-}(i))N(du,dy),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0,t}(i)=

\displaystyle\>i+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\left(\Lambda_{0,u-}(i),z\right)\Pi(du,dz).

Also we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{s,t}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i))=

\displaystyle\>\Phi_{0,s}(x,i)+\int_{s}^{t}f(\Phi_{s,u}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),\Lambda_{s,u}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i)))du

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(\Phi_{s,u}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),\Lambda_{s,u}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i)))dw_{u}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{|y|<c}F(\Phi_{s,u-}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),y,\Lambda_{s,u-}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i)))\tilde{N}(du,dy)

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{|y|\geq c}G(\Phi_{s,u-}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),y,\Lambda_{s,u-}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i)))N(du,dy),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s,t}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i))=

\displaystyle\>i+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\left(\Lambda_{s,u-}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),z\right)\Pi(du,dz).

Since the two component process
\left(\Phi_{s,t}(y,j),\Lambda_{s,t}(j)\right)
is determined by the increments of type
w_{s+u}-w_{s},N(s+u,A)-N(s,A),\Pi(s+u,B)-\Pi(s,B)
for
u\geq 0
, see, for example, Lemma 4.3.12 in
[
Applebaum, 2009
]
, so it is
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-measurable. Therefore the two component process
\left(\Phi_{s,t}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),\Lambda_{s,t}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i))\right)
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Meanwhile we have
\left(\Phi_{s,t}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),\Lambda_{s,t}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i))\right)=\left(\Phi_{0,t}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,t}(i)\right)
by the uniqueness of the solution. Hence, by Lemma
5.4
, we have for
f
is a bounded borel measurable function,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(f(\left(\Phi_{0,t}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,t}(i)\right)|\mathcal{F}_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(\left(\Phi_{s,t}(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)),\Lambda_{s,t}(\Lambda_{0,s}(i))\right))|\mathcal{F}_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(\left(\Phi_{s,t}(y,j),\Lambda_{s,t}(j)\right))|\mathcal{F}_{s})\right)|_{y=\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),j=\Lambda_{0,s}(i)}.

The same argument shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(f(\left(\Phi_{0,t}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,t}(i)\right)|\left(\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,s}(i)\right)\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(f(\left(\Phi_{s,t}(y,j),\Lambda_{s,t}(j)\right))|\mathcal{F}_{s})\right)|_{y=\Phi_{0,s}(x,i),j=\Lambda_{0,s}(i)}.

Therefore we obtain the Markov property of two-component process
\left(\Phi_{0,t}(x,i),\Lambda_{0,t}(i)\right)
.
∎
