Pituitary involvement in ANCA-associated vasculitis: a retrospective single-center experience and literature review
Authors: Tian Xu, Xiao Zhai, Lian Duan, Yong Yao, Kan Deng, Feng Feng, Hui You, Wen Zhang, Huijuan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe the clinical manifestations and outcomes of pituitary involvement in patients with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV).MethodsThis study evaluated pituitary hormone and imaging in patients with AAV involving pituitary treated at a tertiary hospital from October 2004 to June 2024.ResultsEighteen patients were enrolled, with a mean age of 46.7 ± 15.6 years and a female predominance (13/18). Headache was the most frequent initial symptom (10/18). The predominant pituitary dysfunction included secondary hypogonadism (10/17) and central diabetes insipidus (CDI, 10/18), with the most common MRI finding being loss of posterior pituitary hyperintensity on T1-weighted imaging (16/18). According to the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, diagnoses included granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA, 11/18) and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA, 1/18), with 6 unclassifiable cases. All but one patient tested positive for ANCA. Sellar biopsies performed in 7 cases, with 2 initial misdiagnoses of lymphocytic hypophysitis (LYH). All patients received corticosteroids in combination with immunosuppressive agents or rituximab, which were effective in most patients. Among 11 patients with median 20-month follow-up (range: 8.5–52.0), no cases demonstrated pituitary functional recovery, while disease recurrence occurred in 5 patients. Eighty-seven cases from the literature were included, mainly GPA and one case of MPA. In contrast to the more common recovery of anterior pituitary function in patients with LYH, 15 out of 60 patients with AAV involving the pituitary demonstrated resolution or improvement of CDI during follow-up.ConclusionsDiagnosing pituitary-involved AAV is challenging. Sellar biopsies with atypical features require careful interpretation. Regular systemic evaluation and repeated ANCA testing are crucial for accurate diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40618-026-02881-9.Keywords: Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis, Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Pituitary involvement, Lymphocytic hypophysitis

Introduction

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease predominantly affecting the small vessels. It encompasses three primary subtypes: granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) [1]. AAV typically involves multiple organ systems, including the kidneys, lungs, upper respiratory tract, skin, eyes and peripheral nerves [2]. Involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) has been documented in approximately 7 ~ 11% of patients with GPA and 9.8% of those with MPA [3, 4]. The involvement of CNS structures includes the dura mater, brain parenchyma, pituitary gland, spinal cord. Pituitary gland involvement is rare, observed in only 1 ~ 2% of AAV cases [3, 5–7].

The diagnosis of AAV with pituitary involvement faces significant clinical challenges. Notably, pituitary manifestations may precede systemic organ dysfunction in AAV. A literature review summarized six cases of GPA where pituitary dysfunction was the primary manifestation without involvement of other organs [6, 8]. Furthermore, ANCA negativity occurred in approximately 10% of AAV cases, leading to diagnostic pitfalls [9]. Some cases of pituitary-involved AAV with negative ANCA were also observed in previous studies [6, 8, 10, 11]. Heterogeneity of MRI imaging further complicates diagnosis. For instance, GPA-related pituitary lesions may radiologically mimic pituitary adenomas with apoplexy due to their similar MRI appearances [12]. While histopathological evaluation through pituitary biopsy could aid in diagnosis, a few cases demonstrate nonspecific inflammation histologically suggestive of lymphocytic hypophysitis (LYH) or IgG4-related hypophysitis [3, 6, 8, 13]. Importantly, it was reported that GPA diagnosis was often not considered when the initial presentation was hypophysitis, leading to delayed diagnosis of up to 12 months (range: 3–36) [5].

In this study, we discussed the features of patients with pituitary-involved AAV, including the biochemical function, imaging, histopathology of pituitary, treatment regimens and outcomes based on a single-center retrospective study. We also compared these patients to LYH from the same center and focused on the population with pituitary-involved AAV from the literature to increase our understanding of the disease.

Patients and methods

Patients with AAV and pituitary involvement, admitted to Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between October 2004 and June 2024, were evaluated in this retrospective study. A total of 18 patients diagnosed with ANCA-associated vasculitis were in accordance with 2012 Chapel Hill Consensus Conference definitions. The patients were further classified with microscopic polyangiitis and granulomatosis with polyangiitis and Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) according to the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria [14–17]. Histological confirmation of the diagnosis was obtained in six patients, five with GPA demonstrating biopsy-proven involvement of nasal mucosa (n = 2), skin (n = 1), spinal dura mater mass (n = 1), and aortic root mass(n = 1), along with one MPA case confirmed via renal biopsy. Cases that could not be diagnosed as a specific AAV were categorized as unclassifiable, following the approach used in previous studies [18–21].

Cytoplasmic ANCA (c-ANCA) and perinuclear ANCA (p-ANCA) were was performed by indirect immunofluorescent technique (Euroimmun, Germany), a negative test was considered by titer < 1:10. Specific antibodies to proteinase 3(anti-PR3) and myeloperoxidase (anti-MPO) were detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA, normal < 20 RU/mL) or chemiluminescence immunoassay ([CLIA] normal < 16 RU/mL), which performed using an fully automated chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (Shenzhen YHLO Biotech Co., Ltd., China) [22]. The Birmingham vasculitis activity score (BVAS, version 3) was assessed at the time of diagnosis and at the latest of follow-up [23].

Central diabetes insipidus (CDI) was diagnosed based on a serum osmolality > 295 mosm/kg during fluid deprivation tests, a urine osmolality < 300 mosm/kg, and an increase in urine osmolality following AVP injection. Secondary hypogonadism was defined by low serum testosterone or estradiol levels, alongside inappropriately low or normal luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels. Secondary hypothyroidism was diagnosed when serum-free thyroxine levels were below 0.81 ng/dl, in combination with an inappropriately low or normal serum TSH level. Adrenal insufficiency was defined as a morning cortisol level below 5 µg/dl (8am), accompanied by an inappropriately low or normal ACTH level. Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) was identified by an insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1) level below age-matched reference values. In individuals exhibiting hypopituitarism involving three or more hormonal axes, the detection of low IGF-1 levels may be sufficient to diagnose GHD without the need for stimulation testing [24]. Furthermore, pituitary involvement in patients with AAV was confirmed through pituitary function tests and magnetic resonance images (MRI) evaluations. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (K3852).

Literature review

We searched PubMed using the terms “Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody associated vasculitis” or “ANCA associated vasculitis” and “pituitary” to identify all articles in English, excluding cases report from the same center. In total, 53 articles with 87 cases were included for final analysis (Supplemental material).

Statistical analyses

Data were presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed continuous variables, or median and IQR for skewed distributions, and as number (%) for categorical variables. Quantitative data were analyzed with Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on distribution, while categorical data were compared using the χ2 test. The normality test was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and P-P chart. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0 software (IBM-SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics and clinical manifestations

A total of 18 patients, comprising 11 cases of GPA, one of MPA, and 6 unclassified cases, were clinically diagnosed with pituitary involvement, based on imaging findings of sellar lesions and the presence of pituitary dysfunction. The mean age of these patients was 37.2 ± 11.2 years, with a female predominance (72.2%, 13/18). The median time from onset of symptoms to diagnosis was 19 months (IQR:4–24). Headache was not only the most common initial symptom (10/18, 55.6%) but also the most prevalent systemic manifestation (14/18, 77.8%) in this cohort. Other systemic manifestations in descending order of frequency included fever (13/18, 72.2%), weight loss (8/18, 44.4%), fatigue (6/18, 33.3%), and arthralgia/myalgia (1/18, 5.6%). Systemic organ involvement was observed in 16/18 (88.9%) patients, with ear-nose-throat (ENT) manifestations predominating (13/18, 72.2%), followed by pulmonary involvement (6/18, 33.3%) and renal disease (3/18, 16.7%). Notably, two unclassifiable cases exhibited isolated pituitary involvement.

ANCA testing was performed in all 18 patients, with only one GPA case remaining persistently ANCA-negative. MPO-ANCA positivity (11/16) was predominant, while 4 patients were positivity with PR3-ANCA, and ANCA was negative in one patient. Two additional cases with ANCA positivity were detected by indirect immunofluorescence. Among patients with MPO-ANCA positivity, three patients who were initially ANCA-negative subsequently tested positive for ANCA at 14 days, 3 years, and 3 years after their initial assessments, respectively. Sixteen patients underwent the examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). As indicated by cytological tests, 33% (4 out of 12) of them suggested leukocytosis predominantly lymphocytes. Additionally, protein levels in the CSF exceeded the upper limit of normal in 56% (9 out of 16) of cases.

Pituitary dysfunction, radiological findings and histological results

In our cohort, the timing of pituitary involvement varied: it occurred before (including isolated cases), concurrently with, or following other organ involvement in 6, 4, and 8 patients, respectively. Among the latter 8 patients, the median time from symptom onset to pituitary involvement was 22 months (IQR: 10–31).

Secondary hypogonadism (10/17) and central diabetes insipidus (10/18) were the most prevalent pituitary dysfunction, followed by secondary adrenal insufficiency (8/13), growth hormone deficiency (6/17) and secondary hypothyroidism (5/18). Disappearance of posterior 'bright spot' on T1WI (16/18) was the most frequent neuroimaging findings. Other radiological findings of sellar region were summarized in the Table 1 and Fig. 1. Also, dural thickening was identified in five cases, comprising three instances of hypertrophic cranial pachymeningitis and two cases of hypertrophic spinal pachymeningitis.

Seven patients underwent pituitary biopsy during diagnosis. Unexpectedly, the pathological findings of these patients did not exhibit the characteristic histological features (namely vessel inflammation and granulomas) [2, 25]. Two GPA cases demonstrated lymphoplasmacytic infiltration patterns indistinguishable from lymphocytic hypophysitis (Fig. 2), including one with delayed diagnosis due to coexisting ANCA negativity at initial screening. Treatment and clinical outcome

The treatment regimens for all patients were detailed in the Supplemental material. all patients were administered corticosteroids, and fifteen patients underwent pulse therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone (500 to 1000 mg daily) or oral methylprednisolone (ranging from 40 to 80 mg/day), followed by oral prednisone, combining with immunosuppressive agents or rituximab. Ten patients (55.6%) received combination immunosuppressive therapy, with the most common regimens being cyclophosphamide (n = 8, 44.4%) and mycophenolate mofetil (n = 3, 16.7%). Additionally, five patients (27.8%) were treated with rituximab.

Eleven patients were followed for a median duration of 20 months (range: 8.5–52.0). At the latest follow-up, the BVAS scores for all 11 patients had decreased, with eight patients achieving remission (BVAS = 0 ~ 3). No patients exhibited pituitary functional recovery. MRI scans were performed on eight patients, with six demonstrating stable or reduced pituitary gland size. During the follow-up period, one case of pituitary enlargement and one case of pituitary stalk thickening were observed.

In addition to these two patients with recurrence, there were three additional patients who also relapsed during follow-up, characterized by the recurrence of symptoms, elevated serum ANCA titers following dose reduction or discontinuation of treatment. The treatment regimen was transitioned to rituximab in three patients: one female patient experienced disease relapse despite initial therapeutic efficacy, while the remaining two exhibited poor prognoses due to multiple organ involvement and concurrent infections.

Pituitary involvement in AAV: literature review

A total of 87 cases were included in the analysis, with demographic characteristics detailed in Table 1. All patients were GPA but one was MPA. CDI (67/86, 77.9%) and secondary hypogonadism (36/68, 52.9%) were the predominant manifestations of pituitary dysfunction. And the most frequently observed MRI findings were loss of the posterior pituitary bright spot on T1WI (27/87, 31.0%) and sellar mass lesions (35/87, 40.2%). ANCA testing was performed in 79 cases and 15% of the cases (12/79) tested negative for ANCA. Of these ANCA-positive patients, PR3-ANCA and MPO-ANCA positivity were detected in 31 and 10 cases, respectively. The transition from negative-ANCA in the initial test to positive were found in three cases. Notably, one patient who initially tested positive for ANCA became negative following treatment and remained negative upon detection of pituitary involvement [26].

Histopathological analysis of 23 cases in the literature of GPA revealed characteristic granulomatous features in the majority (see Supplemental material), while three cases exhibited only nonspecific inflammation [7, 27, 28]. Notably, one patient met pathological criteria for IgG4-related hypophysitis (IgG4+/IgG+ plasma cell ratio: 43%) but was ultimately diagnosed with GPA based on concomitant vasculitis-related histopathological findings and normal serum IgG4 levels [13]. Additionally, one GPA patient demonstrated concurrent macroprolactinoma with elevated prolactin levels [29] (Fig. 2).

Among 87 patients, 71 had a documented medication history of corticosteroids combined with either immunosuppressive agents or rituximab, 7 received corticosteroids alone, and another 7 received only immunosuppressive agents or rituximab. The medication regimens for the remaining two individuals have not been specified. Regarding overall outcomes, the majority achieved remission. And one case remained stable, one showed progression, two experienced relapse, and two died. Among 60 patients who underwent repeated pituitary hormone assessments, 39 showed no recovery of pituitary function. Among the patients with pituitary function recovery, only two exhibited complete recovery of both posterior and anterior pituitary function. Fifteen patients who recovered from central diabetes insipidus (CDI) (15/60, 25%), one achieved partial remission, while two experienced intermittent relapse. Recovery of anterior pituitary function occurred in six patients (6/60,10%), including the two cases with complete recovery of pituitary function. Specifically, five patients showed recovery of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis (5/60, 8.3%), three had recovery of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis (3/60, 5%), and one patient each demonstrated recovery of the remaining axes.

Differential diagnosis from lymphocytic hypophysitis

Fifty cases of lymphocytic hypophysitis who were previously reported by our center were enrolled [30]. Compared to LYH patients, those with AAV involving pituitary were found to be older, both in our cohort and in the literature review (37.2 ± 11.2 vs. 46.7 ± 15.6 years or 44.4 ± 14.3 years, p = 0.001). All groups exhibited a female predominance, with no statistically significant difference in gender distribution (p = 0.740 or p = 0.217). Also, central diabetes insipidus and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism were the most frequently observed manifestations of pituitary dysfunction both in LYH and in AAV groups. MRI revealed a higher frequency of imaging abnormalities in LYH cases (Table 1). As to pituitary axis recovery of patients with LYH at follow up, anterior pituitary function improvement was observed in 27.8% (10/36), primarily involving recovery of HPG axis and GH/IGF-1 axis. And CDI improvement was observed in 13.2% (5/38) [30]. Compared to LYH, the 87 reported cases of AAV with pituitary involvement primarily exhibited recovery of CDI (25%, 15/60) and gonadal axis function (5%, 3/60).

Discussion

This study presents a series of 18 cases where AAV involves pituitary gland, and retrospectively summarized clinical characteristics, pituitary dysfunction, imaging findings and histopathological results from the literature. In this cohort, a predilection of middle-aged female patients was observed, consistent with GPA findings in systematic literature review [6, 31]. Contrary to previous studies that reported a predominance of PR3-ANCA positivity, this study identified a predominance of MPO-positive antibodies in patients, which might be related to the higher proportion of MPO-ANCA-positive vasculitis in East Asia compared to PR3-ANCA-positive vasculitis [1]. Regarding pituitary function and imaging, our findings aligned with the reported observation that central diabetes insipidus and the absence of the posterior pituitary bright signal on T1WI were the most common pituitary dysfunction and imaging findings [5, 6, 32].

Previous studies demonstrated that the proposed pathophysiology of pituitary involvement in GPA includes direct invasion from nearby areas (paranasal sinuses or orbits), in-situ granuloma formation in the pituitary, and/or vasculitis of central nervous system vessels supplying the pituitary [10, 33]. In our cohort, pathological assessments of two GPA patients did not reveal typical features but instead showed infiltration by a few non-specific chronic inflammatory cells, as reported in several previous studies [7, 28]. Similarly, most of these patients were misdiagnosed as lymphocytic hypophysitis. This study found the overlapping characteristics between lymphocytic hypophysitis and AAV with pituitary involvement, including a female predominance in gender distribution, clinical symptom, pituitary dysfunction and imaging findings. Therefore, more attention should be paid to age, serum ANCA titers and involvement of other organs for further diagnosis. Furthermore, we observed that among patients undergoing pituitary function follow-up. In 87 published AAV cases, recovery of CDI was more frequent than that of anterior pituitary function (25% vs. 10%, p = 0.031). In contrast, there was no statistically difference in our LYH cohort (13.2% vs. 27.8%, p > 0.05). Furthermore, the rate of anterior pituitary recovery was higher in LYH than in the AAV cohort (27.8% vs. 10%, p = 0.024, Table 1) [30, 34–36].

In addition to LYH, it is also important to differentiate AAV with pituitary involvement from IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) affecting the pituitary. Previous studies have demonstrated that GPA can mimic IgG4-RD in certain histologic biopsies, and increased IgG4 + cells have been observed in sinonasal or orbital/periorbital biopsies of GPA [37]. In this study, we identified one GPA patient in our cohort who exhibited elevated serum IgG4 levels, and a parotid gland biopsy revealed IgG4-positive cells, although the IgG4+/IgG+ ratio was below 40%. A review of the literature revealed one case in which a final diagnosis of GPA was made based on the presence of typical histological features of GPA alongside coexisting a 43% of IgG4+/IgG+ plasma cell ratio in pituitary biopsy [13]. Recently, the co-occurrence of IgG4-RD and AAV has also been published [38].

In terms of treatment, cyclophosphamide remains one of the most frequently utilized immunosuppressive agents, as detailed in existing literature [2, 6]. Furthermore, rituximab is frequently administered to patients with refractory AAV, particularly those who exhibit inadequate responses to combined hormonal and immunosuppressive therapies [11, 39, 40]. Within our cohort, rituximab was administered to four patients: three due to disease recurrence and one in consideration of cyclophosphamide-related adverse effects. And no adverse effects were reported during the medication period. Notably, no recovery of pituitary function was observed in our follow-up of patients. Although a few cases of pituitary function recovery have been reported [41], pituitary function recovery and its contributing factors remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

The main limitation of this study is its retrospective design, which leads to incomplete data in several respects. First, not all patients had follow-up evaluations, and only a subset underwent MRI. Second, histopathological confirmation from the sellar region was available for only a small proportion of cases. Third, the patients were treated with different theraputical approach. Although rituximab represents a promising therapeutic option, it was administered to only a limited number of patients in our cohort. Future studies should aim to enlarge the subgroup receiving rituximab—currently regarded as the preferred treatment—ideally through multicenter collaborations, to evaluate corresponding outcomes and to develop a diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm.

In conclusion, the diagnosis of AAV with pituitary involvement presents significant clinical challenges. Differentiation from other pituitary lesions is important, particularly lymphocytic hypophysitis and IgG4-related hypophysitis. Notably, pituitary biopsy alone may not provide definitive diagnostic confirmation of AAV, as atypical histological findings could occur. Regular systemic evaluations include regular reassessment of pituitary function and MRI, routine blood/urine tests, autoantibodies (including ANCAs), serum IgG4, CRP, ESR, and further investigations of any suspected organ involvement. If systemic evaluation is negative despite high clinical suspicion for AAV, or if the patient fails to respond to glucocorticoid therapy, pituitary biopsy should be considered for definitive diagnosis. Repeated ANCA testing is also recommended.
