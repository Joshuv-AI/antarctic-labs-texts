Single nucleus RNA sequencing identifies PLXNA4 upregulation in end-stage arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy
Authors: Bridget Simonson, Mark Chaffin, Yasmine Guedira, Talita Z. Choudhury, Ling Xiao, Richard Ruan, Kirsten Schneider, Jessica Morris, Vera Koledova, Kenneth C. Bedi, Kenneth B. Margulies, Carla Klattenhoff, Patrick T. Ellinor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryArrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is a rare genetic form of heart disease often caused by mutations in desmosomal proteins. We carried out single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on 11 non-failing and 8 ARVC patient right ventricular samples and transcriptionally profiled 139,347 nuclei. We identified over 4,400 differentially expressed protein-coding genes between non-failing and ARVC samples across cell types. When compared to dilated, hypertrophic, and ischemic cardiomyopathy, we found that end-stage ARVC is transcriptionally similar to other forms of end-stage heart failure. Despite broad transcriptional similarities, we identified seven cardiomyocyte genes and six fibroblast genes that were uniquely dysregulated in ARVC and validated PLXNA4 as a gene with uniquely increased expression in cardiomyocytes in ARVC compared to other cardiomyopathies. Overexpression of PLXNA4 in hiPSC-derived ventricular cardiomyocytes induced immune response gene expression, suppressed calcium signaling, and altered cardiomyocyte electrophysiology, suggesting PLXNA4 may contribute to these pathways in ARVC.

Introduction

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is a progressive familial arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), affecting an estimated 1:2000–1:5000 individuals worldwide.1 ARVC is characterized by early epicardial right ventricular (RV) arrhythmias followed by cardiomyocyte death, inflammation, and replacement of RV cardiomyocytes with fibro-fatty infiltrations.2 Approximately 50% of ARVC cases are caused by autosomal dominant mutations in desmosomal proteins. In some subtypes of ARVC, left ventricular (LV) dysfunction is also observed, complicating the diagnosis of this complex and variable disease.2,3,4

Despite its clinical significance, the molecular initiation and progression of ARVC are not well understood. Arrhythmias and cardiomyocyte death are thought to occur due to disruption of the structure of the intercalated disk, causing loss of adhesion and detachment of cardiomyocytes. Inflammation and fibro-fatty infiltration are then thought to occur, possibly through inhibition of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway and activation of GSK3b.5,6,7 Recent work in left and right ventricular tissue from human patients with end-stage ARVC has shown that TNMD+ fibroblasts and CCL3+ proinflammatory macrophages may also play a role in the right ventricle in end-stage disease.8

Previous studies from our lab and others have characterized the single-nucleus transcriptional profile of healthy and diseased human cardiac tissue.9,10,11,12,13,14,15 Here we carry out snRNA-seq on samples collected from the right ventricular free wall of transplant patients with ARVC and non-failing (NF) donor controls. We show that there are many differentially expressed genes between NF and ARVC samples; however, end-stage ARVC shares a similar transcriptional profile to end-stage hypertrophic, dilated, and ischemic cardiomyopathy. We identify a small number of cardiomyocyte (CM) genes and fibroblast (FB) genes that are uniquely dysregulated in ARVC compared to other cardiomyopathies. We validate the unique increase of PLXNA4 in cardiomyocytes in ARVC and demonstrate that overexpression of PLXNA4 in iPSC-derived ventricular cardiomyocytes leads to increased inflammatory pathways and alterations in cardiac conduction and calcium signaling.

Results

In this study we performed snRNA-seq on the right ventricle (RV) of patients with ARVC (n = 9) and non-failing (NF) controls (n = 12). After strict quality control, one NF and one ARVC sample were removed due to sample failure, and 139,347 nuclei remained (Figures S1 and S2; Table S2; Figure 1A). Of the remaining patients with ARVC, two had reduced left ventricular function (LVEF: 10%, 20%), two had moderately reduced left ventricular function (43%, 47.5%), and four had preserved left ventricular function (LVEF: 55%, 55%, 57.5%, 58%) (Table S1). Several patients with ARVC also had common mutations associated with ARVC (Table S1). Using canonical marker genes and gene ontology enrichment, we identified 13 different cell types in the right ventricle (Figure S3; Tables S3 and S4). The most abundant cell types were cardiomyocytes (28.8%), fibroblasts (28.1%), and endothelial cells (12.7%) (Figures 1B and 1C). In patients with ARVC, there was a statistically credible decrease in cardiomyocytes, and non-significant increases in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, macrophages, adipocytes, vascular smooth muscle cells, lymphocytes, lymphatic endothelial cells, and neuronal cells (Figures 1B and 1C; Table S5). Masson’s trichrome staining was carried out on all samples and showed epicardial fat in NF samples and infiltrative fat and fibrosis in most ARVC samples (Figure S4A). To better understand the diversity within these global cell types, we performed sub-clustering analysis of any cell type with at least 2,000 nuclei (see STAR Methods; Figure S5). We identified between 2–11 subpopulations across cell types and detected statistically credible (FDR <0.05) changes in composition between ARVC and NF in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and macrophages. Specifically, in fibroblasts, we observed significant increases in four sub-clusters (FB-0, FB-3, FB-8, FB-9, Figure S5B), some of which had a clear activated phenotype (FB-3, FB-8), and decreases in several additional sub-clusters (FB-2, FB-5, FB-7, Figure S5B). In endothelial cells, we observed a significant decrease in EC-1 (Figure S5C), one of multiple sub-clusters that likely represent capillary endothelial cells. In macrophages, we observed a decrease in Mφ-1 and an increase in Mφ-2 (Figure S5D), likely representing the shift in the transcriptome of two populations of resident macrophages rather than distinct cell states. Additionally, we observed a strong decrease in proliferating macrophages (Mφ-5, Figure S5D). While we identified multiple sub-clusters in other cell types, they were not compositionally different between ARVC and NF.

Stark transcriptional shifts across all cell types in end-stage ARVC

In order to determine the cell-specific transcriptional changes that occur in ARVC, we examined differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between patients with ARVC and NF controls by cell type (Figure 2A; Tables S6). Overall, we observed substantial transcriptional dysregulation in ARVC, as more than 4,400 protein-coding genes were significantly differentially expressed in at least one cell type. The top five up and down regulated protein coding genes from the main cell types are shown in Figure 2A. Pathway analysis was then performed using GO:Biological Process, Reactome, and KEGG databases to assess whether DEGs between ARVC and NF were enriched in specific cellular processes (Figure S6; Table S7). In fibroblasts, there was notable enrichment of DEGs in pathways for cell junction organization, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, and extracellular matrix organization. In cardiomyocytes, DEGs were enriched in pathways, including extracellular matrix organization, regulation of neuron differentiation, neurogenesis, and neuronal systems. In macrophages, enriched pathways were involved in the immune response, such as adaptive immune response, inflammatory response, and innate immune response. Interestingly, despite the known role of adipogenesis in ARVC, significant DEGs in adipocytes were not significantly enriched in any pathways.

Since ARVC is known to have a large monogenic contribution, we were interested in examining the expression of the genes that contain mutations associated with ACM and ARVC.31 As expected, expression of the most common desmosomal and intercalated disk genes was found predominantly in cardiomyocytes (Figure 2B). The less commonly associated genes LMNA, TP63, and TGFB3 were more generally expressed in other cell types. In disease, RYR2, DSP, and JUP were significantly decreased in cardiomyocytes, TGFB3 was significantly increased in cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts, and TP63 was significantly increased in fibroblasts.

Adipogenic markers are found only in adipocytes in end-stage ARVC

As the origin of infiltrative fat in ARVC is not well understood, we next explored whether we could identify adipogenesis occurring in any cell type in our end-stage disease samples. First, we looked at the expression of two key adipogenic transcription factors, PPARy and CEBPA, across all cell types in our snRNA-seq dataset (Figure S7A). PPARy was expressed in multiple cell types such as FB, pericytes, EC, and LEC, with high expression in adipocytes. CEPBA generally had very low baseline expression over all cell types. Due to the fat droplets we observed in ARVC CMs (Figure S4B) and the theory that CMs can transdifferentiate into adipocytes,32,33 we carried out immunofluorescence staining on tissue sections with a-sarcomeric actinin (CM marker), CEPBA and PPARy. We also included vimentin, a marker frequently used to identify fibroblasts, but which also stains other cell types, including adipocytes. We saw no CEBPA+ CMs in the 4 ARVC tissues we imaged but did observe CEBPA+ vimentin+ cells (Figure S7B). To confirm whether the CEPBA+ vimentin+ cells were adipocytes or FBs, we next carried out immunofluorescent staining for CEBPA and PPARy with in situ hybridization for DCN (a marker for fibroblasts) and ADIPOQ (a marker for mature adipocytes). We saw that the CEBPA+ nuclei co-localized with ADIPOQ, confirming that the cells expressing these markers are adipocytes rather than DCN-positive FBs (Figure S7C). In sum, in end-stage ARVC, we do not observe adipogenic markers in cell types apart from ADIPOQ+ adipocytes.

End-stage ARVC has a common transcriptional profile with other end-stage cardiomyopathies

We previously observed a common transcriptional profile shared between patients with end-stage hypertrophic (HCM), dilated (DCM) and ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM)13 and wanted to explore whether this observation held true for end-stage ARVC. We therefore combined snRNA-seq data of left ventricles from our previously published HCM, DCM, and ICM studies with this ARVC dataset of right ventricle to create a map of 831,720 nuclei (Figure 3A). When considering the broad transcriptional profile across all cell types for each sample, we found that the cardiomyopathy samples tended to cluster together regardless of specific etiology or chamber, and separately from the NF controls (Figure 3B). This pattern largely held true when looking at transcriptional signatures by cell type (Figure S8A), suggesting that ARVC shares this common transcriptional profile. We did observe a separation of the NF samples in this study compared to the NF samples from the DCM, HCM, and ICM studies, which may be attributable to transcriptional differences between the left and right ventricles. When we compared the cellular composition between all cardiomyopathies, we observed that ARVC samples had a much larger decrease in CMs compared to the other cardiomyopathies, and a trend toward an increase in adipocytes and neuronal cell types that was not observed in the other cardiomyopathies (Figures 3C and S8B; Table S8).Figure 3Combined dataset of ARVC, HCM, DCM, and ICM(A) Uniform manifold approximation and projection for dimension reduction (UMAP) representation of single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (nnuclei = 831,720) from right ventricles of 11 patients with non-failing (NF) hearts and 8 patients with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), and left ventricles of 24 patients with NF, 7 patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), 11 patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), and 15 patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).(B) Principal component analysis of patients based on a pseudo-bulk representation of single-nucleus RNA sequencing across all nuclei. To do this, we tested for a statistical difference in effect estimates between each cardiomyopathy subtype and their respective controls (see STAR Methods; Figure 4A). While all comparisons to non-failing controls were performed within the chamber, ARVC and their controls were taken from the RV, whereas DCM, HCM, ICM, and their controls were taken from the LV. PLXNA4 is increased in ARVC cardiomyocytes and localizes to the intercalated disk and sarcomeres

Of the cardiomyocyte genes that were uniquely dysregulated in ARVC compared to other cardiomyopathies, six of the seven genes have previous associations with heart failure (TSC22D1,35,36 FBXO1637, SLC6A69), hypertension (ARHGAP4238), atrial fibrillation (RASSF839), or medication-induced long QT (SLCO3A140). Due to PLXNA4’s previously unreported link to heart disease and its role as a semaphorin receptor, we chose to further examine the role of this gene in disease.

Firstly, to confirm that the increase in PLXNA4 was not due to chamber differences in our ARVC dataset versus our HCM/DCM/ICM datasets, we compared PLXNA4 expression across chambers in published healthy snRNA-seq10,12 (Figures S9A and S9B) and bulk RNA-seq generated across chambers from patients with heart failure41 (Figure S9C). PLXNA4 expression was not significantly different in cardiomyocytes across LV, RV, LA, and RA tissues collected from the same healthy patients, with the exception of a significant increase (logFC = 0.61; adj p = 0.037) in LV vs. RA tissue in Tucker et al., 2020. Further, PLXNA4 expression was unchanged between patients with idiopathic or ischemic heart failure compared to controls across LV, RV, LA, and RA tissues with bulk RNA-seq. These analyses suggest that PLXNA4 is expressed at similar levels across chambers of the heart in healthy patients, and that PLXNA4 does not change in any chambers in idiopathic or ischemic heart failure.

To understand whether PLXNA4 was expressed in a particular cardiomyocyte subcluster, we next looked at PLXN44 expression over the six cardiomyocyte subclusters (Figures S5 and S10). We found that PLXNA4 is expressed similarly across cardiomyocyte populations. This suggests that PLXNA4 is expressed more broadly across cardiomyocytes, not simply in a sub-cluster of stressed cardiomyocytes.

We next wanted to confirm the increase in Plexin A4 protein in ARVC samples, so we carried out immunofluorescence staining of Plexin A4 and a-sarcomeric actinin in a subset of 4–6 NF and ARVC samples, and Plexin A4 and desmoplakin 1/2 in a subset of 2–4 NF and ARVC samples (Figures 5A and 5B, and S11–S15). For immunofluorescence imaging, we selected a range of samples that showed high, mid, and low expression of PLXNA4 in our snRNA-seq data. Interestingly, based on the snRNA-seq data, the level of expression of PLXNA4 was higher in samples with lower numbers of CMs (Figure S11A; Table S10; 1B). In RV NF and ARVC samples, plexin A4 staining was mostly localized to the intercalated disk and sarcomeres (Pearson’s coefficient r = 0.72 (desmoplakin 1/2), r = 0.59 (a-sarcomeric actinin)) and showed increased expression in disease correlating with snRNA-seq data (mean intensity of immunofluorescence staining thresholded to cardiomyocytes) (Figures 5A, 5B, S11B, S11C, and S12–S14; Table S11). In the LV of other cardiomyopathies (n = 2–3 of non-failing and cardiomyopathy, 4 images per sample), we confirmed that there was no significant increase of plexin A4 in disease (Figures S11B and S11D, and S15; Table S10). PLXNA4 expression is correlated with enrichment of actin filament, cell migration, and cell adhesion pathways

Given that PLXNA4 (plexin A4) is a receptor in the semaphorin signaling pathway, we next sought to determine if the expression of all vertebrate plexins, plexin A4’s known co-receptors NRP1 or NRP2,42 and semaphorins were altered in ARVC (Figure S16). We found that several semaphorin ligands and plexin receptors were differentially expressed in ARVC compared to NF across multiple cell types. Notably, PLXNA4 was also significantly increased in endocardial and lymphatic endothelial cells; and in cardiomyocytes, there was also increased expression of additional semaphorin receptors PLXNA2, PLXNB2, and ITGAV. Cardiomyocytes showed increased expression of SEMA3B, SEMA5A, and SEMA5B, with decreased SEMA4B and SEMA6A; fibroblasts had increased SEMA3B, SEMA3C, SEMA3D, and SEMA6D; and SEMA6D increased in pericytes.

To understand the downstream effects of PLXNA4 in cardiomyocytes, we next identified genes that had correlated expression with PLXNA4 in cardiomyocytes using the approach from CS-CORE.43 To do this, we identified the top 5,000 most highly expressed genes in all cardiomyocytes (n = 40,169) and then ran CS-CORE to estimate gene-by-gene correlations in each sample separately. We then carried out Stouffer’s Method to meta-analyze correlation results for PLXNA4 in ARVC and NF separately (Figures S17A and S17B). Using gene set enrichment analysis on the ranked Z-scores from these meta-analyses, we identified a set of pathways with positive correlations to PLXNA4 expression (Figures S17C and S17D; Table S12). Interestingly, we found that the pathways positively enriched with PLXNA4 expression in ARVC widely differ from those in NF samples. Pathways enriched with PLXNA4 expression in disease include pathways involved in cell migration, cell adhesion, and actin and cytoskeletal components, whereas those enriched in NF samples were mostly involved in cardiac contraction. Plexins play a key role in cell migration, adhesion, and tissue morphogenesis through regulation of actin filaments, primarily through the Rho family of GTPases.44 These analyses suggest that in ARVC, PLXNA4 is involved in downstream signaling leading to alterations in actin filaments and increased cardiomyocyte adhesion and mobility.

PLXNA4 expression positively correlates with area of fat from Masson’s trichrome staining

To better understand how cardiomyocyte expression of PLXNA4 may relate to ARVC biology, we tested for associations between PLXNA4 and clinical or histopathological data. Specifically, we carried out differential expression analysis of PLXNA4 cardiomyocyte expression in patients with ARVC and a range of features including BMI, LVEF, LV mass index (LVMI), the fraction of cardiomyocytes determined by the snRNA-seq analysis, and the proportion of fat and fibrotic tissue as measured from Trichrome staining. While underpowered with only 7 or 8 patients with ARVC, we saw a significant negative association (p = 0.0103) between PLXNA4 expression and age, and a significant positive association (p = 0.0067) between PLXNA4 expression and the percent of the tissue that was fat (Figure S18). Due to the small numbers of patients with ARVC and the genetic heterogeneity (maximum of 2 carriers of a genetic mutation for a known gene), we were unable to carry out any analyses to compare genetic mutations due to a lack of power. To ensure that the main differential expression result was not influenced unduly by the clinical and histological features described above, we repeated our primary differential expression test adjusted for various covariates (Figure S19) and observed a consistent, albeit slightly attenuated, logFC estimate for PLXNA4 between patients with ARVC and NF. These data suggest that PLXNA4 is increased in samples with greater histological fat burden.

PLXNA4 overexpression and knockdown lead to transcriptional dysregulation of ARVC-causing genes and semaphorin signaling pathway genes

To further understand the role of PLXNA4 in cardiomyocytes, we carried out mRNA sequencing on human iPSC-derived ventricular cardiomyocytes (hiPSC CMs) with either lentiviral overexpression or siRNA knockdown of PLXNA4 (Figure 6). We confirmed increased levels of PLXNA4 expression with lentiviral overexpression (log2 fold-change = 2.2, FDR-adjusted p = 4 × 10−11) and decreased levels with knockdown (log2 fold-change = −3.6, FDR-adjusted p = 3 × 10−13). We identified 8,375 genes that were significantly changed with overexpression, and 6,329 genes that were significantly changed with knockdown of PLXNA4 (Figures 6A and 6B; Table S13), including a number of genes that are associated with ARVC (Figure S20A). For example, in PLXNA4 overexpression, we saw increased TGFB3 and decreased RYR2 and DSP, which correlates with the expression profile of these genes in our ARVC snRNA-seq data in CMs (Figure 2). We also observed increased PKP2, LMNA, TTN, DSG2, TMEM43, and DSC2 expression and decreased CTNNA3 expression. With PLXNA4 KD, we saw decreased expression of DES, JUP, LMNA, DSP, TTN, CTNNA3, DSG2, SCN5A, DSC2, and increased expression of CDH2. PLXNA4 overexpression causes up-regulation of immune response pathways, down-regulation of cardiac conduction pathways, and affects cardiomyocyte electrophysiology

To identify the pathways that are dysregulated in cells with overexpression or knockdown of PLXNA4, we carried out pathway analyses using GO:Biological Processes, Reactome, and KEGG databases (Table S14). Focusing on the GO:Biological Process ontologies dysregulated in PLXNA4 overexpression, we carried out clustering of pathways based on the genes that were significantly down- or up-regulated after PLXNA4 overexpression (Figures 6C and 6D, see STAR Methods). Clusters of ontologies were manually labeled based on the central theme of each group. Down-regulated pathways included those involved in cell cycle, cardiac muscle development, ECM organization, and cardiac contraction and conduction (Figure 6C). Up-regulated pathways included those involved in immune responses, cytoskeletal organization, and wound healing (Figure 6D).

Due to the disease-relevant roles of decreased cardiac conduction and contraction and increased immune response in ARVC, we were interested in further exploring these clusters. The genes that drove clustering of the pathways within the cardiac contraction cluster included genes such as the pacemaking gene HCN4, subunits of voltage dependent calcium channels CACNA1C, CACNA1G, and CACNA1D, and subunits of sodium voltage gated channels SCN2B and SCN5A. Genes that drove clustering for cardiac conduction pathways included subunits of voltage-dependent calcium channels CACNA1C and CACNA1G, genes involved in intracellular potassium concentrations such as KCNQ3, KCNJ4, and KCNIP4, and GJA1, the gene encoding connexin 43 (Table S14). The pathways that contribute to the downregulation of cardiac contraction and cardiac conduction pathways include regulation of muscle contraction, membrane depolarization during cardiac muscle cell action potential, and potassium ion transport and sodium ion transmembrane transport (Figure 6E).

Genes that drove clustering of pathways in the immune response cluster included members of the interleukin family IL4R, IL13RA2, and IL1B, members of the major histocompatibility complex HLA-F, HLA-E, and HLA-DQB1, and NPPA (Table S14). Pathways that clustered in the immune response cluster included mast cell activation, regulation of leukocyte degranulation, T cell-mediated immunity, and regulation of cytokine production involved in immune response (Figure 6F).

To validate the transcriptional changes observed in cardiac conduction pathways in PLXNA4 overexpression, we carried out action potential duration (APD) electrophysiology measurements on hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes with KD or overexpression of PLXNA4. While KD of PLXNA4 did not significantly change APD at 75% repolarization (APD75, percent increase = 7.4%, p = 0.4509, Figures 6H and S21E–S21H) overexpression of PLXNA4 led to a significant decrease in APD75 (percent decrease = −13.0%, p = 0.002, Figures 6G and S21A–S21D), a finding that would increase cardiomyocyte susceptibility to arrhythmias.

PLXNA4 is increased in a mouse model of ARVC but not in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte models of ARVC

Finally, we identified two publicly available bulk RNA-seq on hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte models of ARVC; one that was generated from patients with a c.2358delA variant in DSG245 and a second from hiPSC-CMs with siRNA knockdown of PKP2,46 as well as a Pkp2 cardiac specific knockout mouse model of ARVC.46 Interestingly, after re-analysis of both hiPSC cardiomyocyte models, we observed a significant decrease in expression of PLXNA4 in the DSG2 cells compared to control and a downward trend in PLXNA4 expression in cells with siRNA knockdown of PKP2 relative to control (Figures S22A and S22C). We also saw no correlation of our DEGs from our PLXNA4 overexpression hiPSC cardiomyocytes with the DEGs identified in either of these studies (Figures S22B and S22D). Re-analysis of the Pkp2 knockout mouse showed a significant upregulation of Plxna4 with Pkp2 knockout relative to control in the LV, and an upward, but non-significant, increase in the RV (Figure S22E). These analyses suggest that hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte models of ARVC alone are not enough to recapitulate increased PLXNA4 expression in cardiomyocytes and that further studies to understand the role of PLXNA4 in ARVC would need to be carried out in more complex ARVC models of disease.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized snRNA-seq to carry out in-depth transcriptional analyses on the right ventricular free wall of patients with end-stage ARVC and non-failing controls. We demonstrate that end-stage ARVC is transcriptionally similar to other end-stage cardiomyopathies, and we identified a small number of genes that were uniquely dysregulated in ARVC. Further validation of PLXNA4, a gene that was uniquely upregulated in cardiomyocytes, confirmed its unique upregulation in ARVC and showed that it is localized to the intercalated disc and sarcomeres in cardiomyocytes. PLXNA4 expression was also correlated with the fat burden of tissues measured from Masson’s Trichrome Staining. Overexpression of PLXNA4 in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes led to increased transcription of genes found in immune response pathways and decreased expression of genes in cardiac conduction and contraction, suggesting that PLXNA4 may contribute to these pathways in ARVC.

Due to the role of adipocytes specifically in ARVC compared to the other cardiomyopathies, we were interested to observe that we did not see transcriptional shifts in adipocytes in end-stage ARVC compared to NF. Further, the transcriptional signature of adipocytes appears largely similar across patients with NF and end-stage cardiomyopathy, regardless of disease etiology. There has been much interest in the origin of adipocytes in ARVC. Studies in human biopsies, hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, or mouse models with mutations in ARVC genes have demonstrated that adipogenesis could occur from either the epicardium via the transcription factor TFAP2A,47 from cardiomyocytes undergoing adipogenesis via inhibition of β-catenin, or in human tissue from cardiac mesenchymal stromal cells.48 In our dataset, we did not identify any subclusters of cardiomyocyte nuclei transitioning to adipocytes and found no transcriptional fingerprint of β-catenin inhibition and activation of adipogenesis. We did, however, observe large lipid droplets within cardiomyocytes in disease, similar to what has been observed in hiPSC-derived CMs incubated in adipogenic differentiation media49; however, there was no evidence that ARVC CMs expressed CEBPA, a marker of adipogenesis. We did not capture enough epicardial cells or mesenchymal stromal cells in our study to determine whether these cell types could contribute to adipogenesis. The lack of changes we saw specifically in adipocytes could be due to the end-stage nature of these samples, and the ability to study these cell populations in biopsies collected from patients at earlier stages of disease may lead to more information about how the fibro-fatty regions of tissue develop in humans.

Despite having a unique pathology, end-stage ARVC shared a similar transcriptional signature as end-stage HCM, DCM, and ICM, suggesting similar convergence across end-stage heart failure. However, in this study we identified a novel gene, PLXNA4, that is uniquely increased in CMs in ARVC. When PLXNA4 was overexpressed in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, it led to decreased expression of genes involved in calcium signaling, increased expression of genes found in immune response signaling, and changes in cardiomyocyte APD. PLXNA4 is an A-type plexin that has been shown to bind to members of the Semaphorin 3 and 6 subclasses.50,51 PLXNA4 has previously been shown to have roles in the brain, endothelium, cancer, immune cell responses, and cell migration,44,50,52,53,54,55 and it regulates downstream signaling through Rac1 and RhoA.44,50 More recently, PLXN-SEMA signaling was shown to play an important role in the organization and compaction of cells in the left ventricular wall during human cardiac development, with the location of PLXNA2+PLXNA4+ CMs controlled by signaling from attracting SEMA3+ compact fibroblasts and repellant SEMA6+/6B + endothelial cells.56 Interestingly, these interactions were not observed in the developing right ventricle; however, knockout of SEMA3 in the developing mouse demonstrated decreased compaction in both the right and left ventricles, suggesting that plexin-semaphorin signaling is important in the organization of cardiomyocytes in both ventricles during development. In our dataset, we observed expression of semaphorin ligands in a number of cell types, with increased expression in ARVC occurring in CMs, fibroblasts, and pericytes. Further understanding of which ligands bind to Plexin A4 in CMs in ARVC may also help to understand the underlying signaling mechanism that is occurring in disease.

In cardiomyocytes in human adult cardiac tissue, Plexin A4 protein was localized at the intercalated disk and sarcomeres, two crucial structures in cardiomyocytes responsible for adhesion and propagation of conduction and contractility, respectively. Plexin A4 staining was higher in cardiomyocytes that were found within tissues that had a higher fat burden, and PLXNA4 expression correlated with enrichment of cell adhesion and actin binding pathways in ARVC. Combined, these data suggest that the increase in Plexin A4 may be a compensatory mechanism to increase cardiomyocyte adhesion within these regions of cardiomyocyte loss. The localization of Plexin A4 to the sarcomeres has not been previously observed, and while semaphorin/plexin signaling plays a role in cytoskeletal organization and F-actin remodeling in neurons, this is due to downstream signaling via Ras GTPases and MICAL proteins rather than localization to these structures.52

Previous studies carried out in hiPSC CM models and in mouse models of ARVC have shown that mutations in ARVC-causing genes lead to increased CM pro-inflammatory cytokine release and an increase in inflammatory and immune response pathways.45,57 Inhibition of GSK3b and NF-kB signaling in mice and zebrafish models of ARVC has led to the inhibition of ARVC-like phenotypes, suggesting that inflammation plays a key role in disease.58,59 More recently, the occurrence of inflammatory “hot phases” during progression of disease, especially in patients with DSP mutations,60 is thought to contribute to the development of arrhythmias. In humans, macrophages enriched in interferon stimulating genes have been shown to be increased in the left ventricle of patients with ARVC with PKP2 mutations.9 A more recent single cell RNA-seq study of matched peripheral blood and non-cardiomyocyte cells isolated from the myocardium of patients with end-stage heart failure also identified NLRP3 in myeloid cells in patients with ARVC, and treatment of Dsp2 mutant mice with MCC950, a compound which inhibits NLRP3, showed protective effects in this ARVC model.8 These studies suggest that drugs targeting the inflammatory component of ARVC may be promising as therapeutics for patients.

We also observed decreased cardiac contraction and conduction pathways in hiPSC-derived CMs overexpressing PLXNA4 and a decrease in APD75. Many of the key genes that drove the clustering of these pathways are associated with other arrhythmic diseases such as atrial fibrillation61 and Brugada syndrome.62 The development of arrhythmia’s in ARVC is thought to occur due to the diminished cell-cell connection caused by desmosomal breakdown.63 Previous studies in rat neonatal rat ventricular myocytes have shown that PKP2 knockdown leads to decreased Nav1.5 (SCN5A) leading to decreased sodium current densities,64 and in conditional PKP2 knockout mice there is decreased expression of genes controlling intracellular calcium, such as CACNA1C, also contributing to arrhythmias.65 Our bulk RNA-seq and functional data in hiPSC-derived CMs suggests that PLXNA4 overexpression causes an arrhythmogenic phenotype; however, this could be due to a compensatory mechanism in response to cardiomyocyte stress. Interestingly, we did not observe any changes in APD75 with KD of PLXNA4, which may be due to the small but significant upregulation of PLXNA2 and PLNXD1 in these cells, which could act as a compensatory mechanism.

Notably, while we saw a decrease in PLXNA4 in published bulk RNA-seq datasets of ARVC hiPSC cardiomyocyte models, we did see increased expression in a mouse model of ARVC. This study rescued the ARVC phenotype in these mice by introducing Pkp2 by adenovirus, which also led to a trend toward normalization of Plxna4 expression. This, along with the correlation of PLXNA4 expression with fat burden from histology as well as the correlation of PLXNA4 expression with pathways including cell adhesion and actin pathways, suggests that PLXNA4 may be increased in ARVC due to more complex interactions between other cell types such as adipocytes that are not fully captured in hiPSC cardiomyocyte cultures.

In summary, we have shown that end-stage ARVC is transcriptionally similar to other end-stage cardiomyopathies. We identified a novel gene, PLXNA4, that is uniquely increased in cardiomyocytes in the right ventricle of patients with ARVC and correlates with fat burden. High PLXNA4-expressing cardiomyocytes in ARVC are also enriched for signaling pathways such as migration, adhesion, and actin pathways. This suggests that increased PLXNA4 expression is consistent with the hypothesis that PLXNA4 upregulation represents a compensatory response to help cardiomyocytes adhere in fibro-fatty regions. Overexpression of PLXNA4 in hiPSC-derived ventricular cardiomyocytes causes transcriptional changes that mimic some of the changes observed in cardiomyocytes in disease, suggesting that PLXNA4 contributes to a disease phenotype. Further work in complex models of ARVC would allow increased understanding of the biological mechanisms of PLXNA4 in cardiomyocytes in ARVC.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of this study include those already described in previous studies carried out by our group.11,12,13 Limitations that are specific to this study include: only performing snRNA-seq on the right ventricle of patients with ARVC, rather than both the right and left ventricles. Combining ARVC snRNA-seq from the RV with snRNA-seq with LV data from NF, HCM, DCM, and ICM may have also introduced variation in our joint analyses. We also used hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes to carry out validation studies, which are immature models of cardiomyocytes and do not completely recapitulate the effects observed in mature adult cells in complex tissue or end-stage disease. Finally, we compared bulk mRNA sequencing data with snRNA-seq data, which requires a number of assumptions about the similarity of gene transcription in the nucleus compared to whole cells in bulk.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human samples

Adult right ventricular free wall tissue samples were collected from organ donors by Myocardial Applied Genetics Network (MAG-Net; www.med.upenn.edu/magnet) as previously described.11,12,13 Non-failing samples were collected from organ donors with no previous history of heart disease, and ARVC samples were collected from explanted hearts of donors receiving a heart transplant. Briefly, hearts were arrested in situ with at least 1 L ice-cold crystalloid cardioplegia solution and were transported on ice until cryopreservation (always less than 4 h). Tissue from the right ventricle was collected, flash frozen and stored at −80 until used. Written informed consent for research use of donated tissue was obtained from next of kin in all cases. Research use of tissues was approved by the relevant institutional review boards at the Gift-of-Life Donor Program, the University of Pennsylvania, Massachusetts General Hospital and the Broad Institute (IRB-5999).

Method details

Library preparation

Nuclei extraction was carried out as previously described.11,12,13 In brief, around 30 mg of tissue was sectioned in 100 μm sections on a cryostat (Leica CM 1950). Sections were resuspended in 2 mL of NIM2 buffer (250 mM sucrose, 25 mM KCl, 0.05% IGEPAL-630, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 μM DTT, 10 mM Tris pH 8.0), mixed using a cut tip and moved into a 7 mL dounce. Samples were dounced 10 x using dounce A and 2 mL of NIM2 buffer was added. Samples were incubated for 2 min and then dounced 20 x using dounce B. Samples were carefully moved to a 15 mL tube and centrifuged at 40 x g for 2 min at 4°. Avoiding the debris pellet, supernatants were filtered through stacked 40 μm and 10 μm filters and both washed with 4 mL of PBS wash buffer (0.01% BSA, 5 mM MgCl2, PBS), followed by 2 mL of wash buffer through the 10 μm filter. Supernatants were centrifuged at 550 x g for 5 min to pellet nuclei, buffer was removed and 6 mL of PBS wash buffer gently added without disturbing the pellet. Samples were centrifuged at 550 x g for 5 min at 4°, supernatants removed and nuclei resuspended in 100 μL of NSB (PBS wash buffer +0.4 U μl−1 murine RNAse inhibitor) buffer. Nuclei were counted and 6,000 were loaded into the 10x genomics 3′ v 3.1 kit. Protocol was carried out as written except for the following: GEMS were incubated at 4°C for 15 min after emulsion generation to promote nuclei lysis, and the reverse transcription protocol was lengthened to be 42°C for 20 min then 53°C for 120 min. Libraries were quantified using qPCR and a BioAnalyser and sequenced on Novaseq S1 aiming for 15,000 reads per nuclei.

snRNA-seq data processing

snRNA-seq samples were processed with the 10x Genomics toolkit CellRanger, v4.0.0. First, base call files were de-multiplexed and converted to FASTQ using cellranger mkfastq. Using cutadapt v1.183167 we then trimmed homopolymer repeats (A30, C30, G30, and T30), the template-switch oligo (AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTACATGGG), and the template-switch oligo reverse complement (CCCATGTACTCTGCGTTGATACCACTGCTT) with parameters max_error_rate = 0.1, min_overlap = 20 for homopolymers and max_error_rate = 0.07, min_overlap = 10 for the template-switch oligo and reverse complement. We aligned the trimmed reads to a GRCh38 pre-mRNA human reference (v2020-A) using cellranger count with --expect-cells 6000 and default settings.

Count output was further processed with CellBender v0.268 to identify non-empty droplets and correct ambient background RNA from count matrices. Parameters for each sample can be found in Table S2. Additionally, for each droplet in a sample, we calculated the proportion of reads mapping to exons, introns, or spanning both with the tool scR-Invex (https://github.com/broadinstitute/scrinvex).

snRNA-seq sample quality control

To identify low quality samples, we inspected cellranger count metrics for each sample (Table S2; Figure S1A). For two experiments (P1643, P1581), the number of cells identified by CellRanger was abnormally high, and the mean reads per cell was very low. Similarly, the unique molecular identifier (UMI) curves showed poor distinction between empty and non-empty cells (Figure S1B). These two experiments were discarded from downstream analysis. Finally, we assessed the average expression of Y chromosome genes for all patients to ensure that phenotypically classified males and females had the appropriate expression of these genes (Figure S1C). No outliers were detected ensuring that there were no sample swaps.

snRNA-seq map construction and nuclei quality control

All non-empty droplets as determined by CellBender (n = 249,862) were aggregated using a procedure similar to Chaffin et al., 2022 and Simonson et al., 2023. All analysis was performed with scanpy v1.8.169 unless otherwise noted. First, the top 2000 highly variable genes were calculated using the method described in Seurat v380 and implemented in scanpy v1.8.1. Data was then log-normalized by dividing counts by the total number of UMI per nucleus, multiplying by a factor of 10,000, and taking the log of this normalized value +1. Expression was subset to the 2000 most variable genes and then the data was scaled to unit variance and zero mean. We then calculated the top 50 principal components (PCs) and aligned these PCs across samples with Harmony 0.1.7,72 using the patient of origin as a batch indicator. We calculated the neighborhood graph across nuclei using these 50 adjusted PCs, setting the number of neighbors to 15 and using cosine distance. We then ran the UMAP algorithm with min_dist = 0.2 to project the snRNA-seq data into 2 dimensional and ran high resolution clustering with the Leiden algorithm (resolution = 2.0) identifying 39 clusters (Figure S2A).

We then sought to remove low quality nuclei using the following quality control (QC) metrics: 1. Percent of reads mapping to mitochondrial genes (%MT), 2. Proportion of reads mapping exclusively to exons (exon_prop), 3. Transcriptional entropy as calculated in the ndd v1.0.6 library (entropy), 4. Estimate of the likelihood that a droplet is a doublet as estimated using Scrublet71 v0.2.3 (doublet_score), 5. Total number of UMI (nUMI), 6. Total number of unique genes detected (nGenes), and 7. log(nGenes)∗entropy. First, at a cluster level, we identified any cluster heavily enriched for either %MT/exon_prop and/or doublet_score. This removed 5 clusters representing cytoplasmic contamination and 10 clusters representing doublets, leaving 205,504 nuclei (Figure S2B).

For remaining nuclei, additional nuclei QC was performed by estimating distributional cutoffs for QC metrics based on the 3rd quartile (Q3) + 1.5 x interquartile range (IQR) and/or the 1st quartile (Q1) - 1.5 x IQR within cluster/sample combination. These distributional cutoffs are important to estimate within cluster/sample because QC metrics tend to vary inherently across cell types due to biological differences and across samples due to technical differences (e.g., sequencing depth). Specifically, we set upper cutoffs for nUMI, nGenes, %MT, exon_prop, entropy, doublet_score, and log(nGenes)∗entropy and an additional lower cutoff for nUMI, nGenes, entropy, and log(nGenes)∗entropy. If a given cluster/combination contained ≤30 nuclei, hard cutoffs were set at nUMI >15000, nGenes >6000, entropy <8, exon_prop >0.18, doublet_score >0.3, and log(nGenes)∗entropy >75. Finally, a hard cutoff was set universally for %MT > 5%, nUMI >150, and nGenes >150. After removal, this retained 166,549 nuclei (Figures S2C and S2D).

All remaining nuclei were remapped using an analogous process as with the initial pre-QC map construction with an additional step to regress out technical effects of %MT and nUMI prior to PCA calculation. In the initial map construction, it is helpful to not remove these technical effects to help identify low quality clusters representing cytoplasmic contamination/dying cells, but at this stage these features can be viewed as technical noise to remove. Leiden clustering with a resolution of 0.6 was used to identify global cell type. As an additional QC step, all global cell types were sub-clustered to identify further misclassified/doublet nuclei. In brief, for each cell type, a new map was constructed by selecting nuclei of that cell type, calculating the top 2000 highly variable genes as above, log-normalizing expression, regressing out the effect of %MT and nUMI, calculating the top PCs (15 PCs for mast cell, 20 PCs for proliferating macrophages, 25 PCs for lymphatic endothelial cells, and 30 PCs for all other more prevalent cell types), aligning PCs using Harmony with patient as a batch indicator, calculating a neighborhood graph using 15 nearest neighbors with cosine distance, and applying Leiden clustering at various resolutions. Resolution was iterated starting at 0.05 and going in increments of 0.05 to a maximum resolution of 1. If at any point a sub-cluster emerged with no genes that had an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) > 0.6 comparing normalized expression in that sub-cluster to all other sub-clusters, clustering was halted. We then calculated the top 50 marker genes for each global cell type based on AUC to identify sets of canonical markers of the major cell types. Using these cell type canonical markers, we calculated the fraction of those 50 markers that had a log fold-change >2 comparing each sub-cluster to all other sub-clusters in a cell type. When a sub-cluster had a log fold-change >2 for more than 20% of markers of another global cell type, this sub-cluster was deemed either a doublet or misclassified sub-cluster and removed (Figures S2E and S2F).

Finally, the remaining 139,435 nuclei were remapped one additional time, but only regressing out the technical effect of %MT. Final Leiden clustering was applied at a resolution of 0.5, and two very small clusters (<50 nuclei) of endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes, respectively, were excluded from downstream analysis for simplicity leaving a total of 139,347 nuclei.

snRNA-seq marker gene selection

To determine cluster cell types, we identified genes that were significantly upregulated in each Leiden cluster using two approaches. First, using log-normalized expression, we calculated an AUC for each gene comparing each cluster identity to all other clusters. Second, to account for correlation of gene expression across nuclei from a given patient, we performed a differential expression test using a pseudo-bulk approach. For each patient/cell type combination, we summed expression across all nuclei in that group for all genes if there were more than 20 nuclei. We then removed any lowly expressed genes detected with the function filterByExpr() as implemented in edgeR v3.30.3.70 After DESeq274 normalization, differential expression testing for the remaining genes was performed with the limma-voom pipeline77,81 using a model ∼0 + cluster + patient, to account for the paired nature of the data (e.g., each patient is represented across multiple cell types). Contrasts were then extracted comparing each cluster to all other clusters. Multiple testing was accounted for using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Marker genes were selected as protein coding genes expressed in at least 25% of nuclei from the given cell type, with AUC >0.60, log fold-change from the limma-voom model >2, and an FDR-adjusted two-sided p-value <0.01.

Pathway enrichment for marker genes was performed GOStats v2.46.0.78 Marker genes as defined above were tested for enrichment in Gene Ontology Biological Processes82 using the full list of genes tested as background. Only ontologies with >10 genes but <500 genes were considered. One-sided p-values were estimated, and multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. We then selected ontologies with FDR <0.05 and odds ratio for enrichment >6 to characterize the expression profiles of each cell type.

snRNA-seq cell type composition testing

scCODA v0.1.883 was used to detect cell type composition shifts between ARVC and NF patients, including sex as a covariate. For the global cell type analysis, we selected pericytes as the reference cell type as they displayed a similar prevalence between ARVC and NF patients, with low variance. We used default priors in all analysis (Normal prior for intercepts, spike-and-slab prior for each covariate on a cell type, a non-centered parameterization of a Normal distribution prior for significant effects). For Hamiltonian Monte Carlo settings, we used the default 20,000 iterations with 5,000 iterations as burn-in. Because scCODA is a Bayesian method, we do not obtain p-values, but testing was performed at the default FDR = 0.05 and credible intervals are reported.

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated composition testing using Dirichlet Regression, as implemented in the R package DirichletReg v0.7-1. Two-sided p-values are reported, and multiple testing was accounted for using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

Sub-clustering analysis

For cell types with more than 2,000 nuclei we performed a sub-clustering analysis. First, we selected the top 2,000 highly variable genes within a cell type and generated a new latent space using scVI v1.3.375 with 10 latent variables and “gene-batch” dispersion correcting for the individual patient as a batch covariate. Next, we generated a neighborhood graph using cosine distance and 5 nearest neighbors. Finally, a UMAP embedding was generated with min_dist = 0.1. Leiden clustering was performed at a range of resolutions to derive final cluster labels. To identify marker genes for each sub-cluster, we performed marker gene detection as in the global map, but only requiring more than 10 nuclei for the limma-voom test. Sub-cluster marker genes were selected as protein coding genes with a logFC >0.50 and a two-sided FDR-adjusted p-value <0.05 from limma-voom, AUC >0.50, and expressed in at least 10% of nuclei for the target sub-cluster. In cases where a sub-cluster was too rare to test with the limma-voom approach, protein coding genes with AUC >0.60 that were expressed in at least 10% of the target sub-cluster were selected.

snRNA-seq differential expression

To identify genes up and down-regulated in ARVC compared to NF patients, differential expression testing was performed by cell type using a similar approach to marker gene identification. For each cell type with sufficient nuclei from both ARVC and NF, pseudo-bulk expression was created per patient by summing expression for each gene across all nuclei from the given patient. Summation was only performed if a patient had at least 25 nuclei of the cell type. Mitochondrial genes, ribosomal genes, and genes expressed in less than 1% of nuclei from either ARVC or NF were excluded. The filterByExpr() function from edgeR v3.30.3 was used to remove additional lowly expressed genes. DESeq2 normalization was followed by differential expression testing with the limma-voom pipeline, specifying a linear model adjusting for age and sex (model: ∼1 + disease + age + sex). Differential expression was performed using the CellBender background-adjusted counts as well as the raw CellRanger counts. Genes showing a significant association (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR corrected two-sided p-value <0.05) with a consistent direction of effect in both tests were deemed significantly differentially expressed.

For each cell type, an additional heuristic annotation was generated for each gene to indicate the likelihood that the gene was derived from ambient background noise of another cell type. This heuristic consists of two components: 1. A measure of overall expression for each gene in the dataset, and 2. A measure of specificity of each gene to the given cell type being tested. Details of the metric are provided in Simonson et al., 2023. Differential expression results are provided regardless of this metric, but we have generally filtered out genes with a background probability heuristic >0.40 from additional downstream analysis to minimize the role of background contamination influencing results.

To identify systematic enrichment of differentially expressed genes in particular pathways and ontologies, over-representation tests were performed using 3 databases: 1. Gene Ontology: Biological Processes, 2. Reactome pathways,76 and 3. KEGG pathways.66 For each cell type, all genes tested were mapped to Entrez identifiers using the mapIDs() function in AnnotationDbi. Unmapped genes or genes with a background contamination heuristic >0.40 for the given cell type were discarded. For the remaining genes, significantly differentially expressed genes (Benjamini-Hochberg corrected two-sided P-value <0.05 with both CellRanger and CellBender) with appreciable effect estimates (|logFC| > 1) were flagged and tested for overrepresentation using a hypergeometric test. Testing was done on the combination of all these genes (|logFC| >1), and separately for significantly downregulated (logFC < −1) and significantly upregulated (logFC >1) genes, as some pathways/ontologies do not possess a clear directionality. For gene ontology analysis, we used the R package GOStats v2.46.0; for Reactome pathways, we used the R package ReactomePA v1.24.0; for KEGG pathways, we used the R package clusterProfiler v3.14.3.84 Any pathway/ontology with <10 genes or >500 genes was excluded. One-sided p-values were estimated, and multiple testing correction was then performed across all cell types/directionalities for each database, separately, using the Benjamin-Yekutieli correction. Only pathways/ontologies with FDR<0.05 were considered significant.

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the differential expression testing adjusting for the following covariates: Body mass index, LV ejection fraction, LV mass index, proportion of cardiomyocytes from the snRNA-seq data, and percent fat or fibrosis as measured with Trichrome staining.

Combined ARVC, ICM, DCM, and HCM analysis

A comparison of ARVC to ICM, DCM, and HCM was performed by aggregating the snRNA-seq data from ARVC samples with previously published snRNA-seq data from left ventricles of ICM (Simonson et al., 2023) and DCM/HCM (Chaffin et al., 2022) patients. All 831,720 nuclei were combined together, and a map was constructed similarly to the ARVC global map. First, the 2000 most highly variable genes were identified across all nuclei. Second, data were log-normalized by dividing counts by the total number of UMI per nucleus, multiplying by 10,000, and taking the natural log of these normalized values plus 1. Third, data was reduced to the 2000 highly variable genes and scaled to mean 0 and variance 1. Fourth, the top 100 principal components were calculated and aligned by patient using Harmony. A small subset of non-failing patients were present in both the ARVC data and either the ICM or DCM/HCM data. As our primary goal with sample alignment is to account for transcriptional differences across patients, these samples were given the same batch covariate in Harmony, despite being from separate studies (and chambers). Fifth, a neighborhood graph was constructed using 15 nearest neighbors, the 100 Harmony-adjusted PCs, and cosine distance. Finally, a UMAP embedding was calculated using this neighborhood graph and setting min_dist = 0.2. Clusters were generated using the Leiden algorithm with a resolution of 0.75.

Marker genes were calculated similarly to the procedure in the ARVC dataset alone, as described in snRNA-seq marker gene selection. The only difference was that instead of summing UMI counts within each patient, they were summed within each patient/study combination to account for the fact that a few non-failing patients were included in multiple studies. These marker genes were used to assign cell type labels.

To capture broad transcriptional patterns across patients from different disease groups, we performed PCA at the sample level by summing expression across all nuclei within each patient. Mitochondrial genes, ribosomal genes, and genes expressed in less than 1% of nuclei were excluded. Furthermore, when performing PCA by cell type, genes that had a background probability heuristic ≥0.10 were also removed to ensure that principal components reflect transcription driven by the cell type of interest. Ellipses by patient group represent normal-probability contours at 95% probability as estimated with dataEllipse for the car R package.

Compositional testing was performed using scCODA with Endothelial1 as the reference cell type, and Dirichlet Regression as a sensitivity check.

Pairwise differential expression was calculated using the limma-voom approach, similar to the description in snRNA-seq differential expression. The model was specified as ∼0+disease+age+sex, where the ‘disease’ variable had 7 levels: NFARVC, NFDCM/HCM, NFICM, ARVC, DCM, HCM, and ICM. For non-failing patients that appeared in multiple studies, only the sample from the ARVC study was retained. Once the model was fit, a contrast was extracted for ARVC vs. NFARVC, DCM vs. NFDCM/HCM, HCM vs. NFDCM/HCM, and ICM vs. NFICM. To compare these effect estimates to one another (e.g., to compare whether the change in expression between ARVC vs. NFARVC is different from the change in expression between DCM vs. NFDCM), explicit interaction contrasts were extracted for each pair of comparisons (e.g., [DCM vs. NFDCM/HCM] vs. [ARVC vs. NFARVC]). Two-sided p-values were calculated and multiple testing correction performed with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Background contamination probability heuristics were calculated separately for each patient set included in a particular contrast. If any were >0.4, the difference was deemed likely to be driven by background RNA.

PLXNA4 chamber specificity

snRNA-seq data from Tucker et al., 202012 and Litviňuková et al., 202010 was used to quantify the cardiomyocyte specific expression of PLXNA4 across chambers. Briefly, in each study, we restricted to cardiomyocytes and then ran a limma-voom differential expression test on pseudo-bulk data, as described in previous sections, using the model ∼0+chamber+donor_id to control for the paired structure of the data. We then extracted contrasts to compare expression across all pairwise comparisons.

Bulk RNA-sequencing data from Sandstedt et al., 202341 was used to look for differences in PLXNA4 expression between a combination of idiopathic/ischemic heart failure and donor controls in each chamber. Published count data was obtained from https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.23686845. A differential expression test between heart failure and donor controls was performed in each chamber separately using limma-voom, analogously to the pseudo-bulk test for snRNA-seq data described above, using a model of ∼0+disease+sex.

PLXNA4 correlation with other histological or clinical features

We performed a series of differential expression tests, restricted to ARVC patients, to identify clinical or histological features correlated with PLXNA4 cardiomyocyte expression. We considered the following features: age, body mass index, LV ejection fraction, LV mass index, fraction of cardiomyocytes in the snRNA-seq dataset, percent fat (as measured histologically), and percent fibrosis (as measured histologically). For each feature, we performed differential expression testing analogously to our primary ARVC vs. NF comparison using limma-voom, but without any additional covariate adjustment given the small sample size. We then extracted the p-value and logFC estimate for PLXNA4 from the full set of differential expression results.

PLXNA4 gene correlation with CS-CORE

We identified genes that had correlated expression with PLXNA4 across cardiomyocyte nuclei using the Python implementation of CS-CORE v1.0.0.43 First, across all cardiomyocyte nuclei, we identified the top 5,000 most expressed genes. To account for variability across patients, we then performed this analysis within each patient separately, using default settings, to estimate correlation coefficients, z-scores, and p-values. Using Stouffer’s method, we meta-analyzed Z-scores for the correlation with PLXNA4 across samples in each disease group, weighted by the square root of the number of nuclei from each patient. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed by ranking genes by the resulting Z score (excluding PLXNA4 itself) using fgsea v1.38.0.79 Only pathways with more than 10 genes and less than 500 genes were considered. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction procedure at FDR <0.05.

Trichrome staining

Fresh frozen tissue was sectioned in 10 μm sections and were stained for fibrosis using Masson Trichrome (Abcam ab150686) following manufacturers protocol. Sections were imaged using the Aperio VERSA Brightfield Fluorescence and FISH Digital Pathology Scanner at 40x.

BODIPY staining

For samples stained with BODIPY, staining was carried out as described below with the inclusion of BODIPY (0.25 μg/ml, Invitrogen D3922) with the secondary antibodies.

Photobleaching and RNAscope and immunofluorescence staining of human tissue

10 μm fresh frozen tissue sections were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at RT and then washed 3x in PBS.

In order to decrease background fluorescence and lipofuscin, tissue sections were photobleached before in situ hybridization and immunofluorescence staining. Slides were placed in trays in ice-cold PBS and photobleached for 48 h (protocol modified from Sun et al., 201773). In brief, in a cold room, a black 395 mm high bin was placed in a large ice bucket containing ice. Slides in PBS were placed on top of ice packs inside the bin. A 300 W full spectrum grow light (Platinum LED Lights, catalog number P300) was placed on top of the bin and turned on full. Apparatus was covered to protect from UV light. Ice, ice packs, and cold PBS were replaced at the beginning and end of each day to keep the slides cold. After 48 h, samples were removed from the set up and immunofluorescent staining or in situ hybridization were performed.

For immunofluorescent staining, samples were permeabilized with 0.1% TX-100 in PBS for 5 min, washed 3 x in PBS for 5 min each and blocked in 7% donkey serum (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h. Slides were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°. Primary antibodies used were alpha-sarcomeric actinin (1:50, Abcam ab9465), Plexin A4 (1:100, Thermo Fisher PA5-115772, Des1/2 (1.100, Progen 61003), Vimentin (1:300, Abcam ab24525), C/EBPα (1;100, Cell Signaling 2295S). Slides were washed 3x in PBS for 5 min each and then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:200 dilution) and Hoescht (1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at RT. Slides were washed 3 x in PBS for 5 min each and then mounted with Prolong Gold. Slides were imaged on a Leica SP8 at 20× magnification with 4 images per sample taken.

For combined in situ hybridization and immunofluorescent staining validation, RNAscope was first carried out on photobleached samples using the Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent kit V2 (ACD Bio) following manufacturers protocols using the following probes: DCN (catalog number 589521, ADIPOQ (catalog number 495581-C2). Samples were then washed 3x in PBS and blocked in 7% donkey serum for 1 h. Staining for C/EBPαa was then carried out following the above protocol.

Image analysis was carried out on (Fuji is just) ImageJ v 2.1.0 on 16 bit images. For Pearson Correlation analysis, images were analyzed using the JaCOP plugin and the R value averaged across all images (4 images per sample). To analyze Plexin A4 protein levels in cardiomyocytes, images were thresholded for cardiomyocytes then the mean pixel intensities were measured. Four images per sample were analyzed and then the average value per sample was graphed. All raw images are uploaded in Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21890062).

Generation of hIPSC cardiomyocytes

Maintenance of hIPSCs in 2D culture

Chipsc22-generated pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) (Takara, Y00285) were maintained at 5% CO2 at 37° in Matrigel-coated flasks (Thermo Fisher, A14700) as colonies in an undifferentiated state using feeder-free defined cultures with StemFlex medium. Media was changed daily. Cells were passaged in a 1:6 ratio after reaching 90% confluence using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies, 07920). Subsequently, the cells were transitioned to a 3D suspension culture. Mycoplasma test was performed by kit (Thermo Fisher, 123).

Maintenance of human hIPSCs in 3D culture

Chipsc22-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) from Takara (Y00285, Male) were expanded using a 3D culture approach employing StemScale from Thermo Fisher. Adherent human PSCs were dissociated from the culture vessel and subsequently introduced into StemScale PSC Suspension Medium along with a ROCK inhibitor. The suspended hiPSC cultures underwent a 50% medium exchange on both the first and third days following seeding. To generate spheroids, on the fourth day cells were passaged using StemPro Accutase Cell Dissociation Reagent and placed on an Innova 2000 orbital shaker (Sigma, EPM1190-0000) and agitated at 90 RPM within a normoxic incubator.

hIPSC-differentiation to cardiomyocytes

Cells were growing in a controlled normoxic gaseous environment with 10% CO2 at 37°C. Differentiation occurred as follows. hiPSC-derived spheroids were pelleted and washed with DPBS containing 0.1% BSA, and then suspended in Basal Differentiation Media (DMEM/F12 with HEPES, 540 mg/L sodium bicarbonate, 1% non-essential amino acids (Thermo Fisher, 11140), 10 μg/mL holo-transferrin, 200 μM ascorbic acid, 63 ng/mL progesterone (Sigma, P8783), and 14 ng/mL sodium selenite). The media was supplemented with 50 ng/mL activin A, 10 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D Systems, 314-BP-050), 20 ng/mL bFGF (Shenandoah, 100-28-500UG), and 2 μM CHIR-99021 (Selleckchem, S2924). The following day (day 1), media was changed to Basal Differentiation Media without CHIR-99021. The next day, to initiate the transition into cardiac mesoderm, spheroids were treated with 10 μM XAV-939 (APExBio, A1877), 1 μM IWR1-Endo (APExBio, B2306), and 1 μM IWP-2 (APExBio, A3512). On day 3, cells were treated with 10 μM XAV-939. From days 6–17 spheroids were maintained in Basal Differentiation Media, supplemented with 4 μg/mL insulin. To enhance spontaneous beating and expedite maturation, 100 nM endothelin-1 (Alfa Aesar, J66977) was introduced from days 9–11. On day 17, spheroids were collected, washed, and dissociated for 1 h at 37°C using 1 mg/mL collagenase II (Thermo Fisher, 117010015) in Collagenase Digestion Buffer (5 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher, 11360070), 20 mM glucose (Sigma, G8769), and 20 mM taurine (Sigma, T0625) in DPBS) and plated onto fibronectin-coated plates (Corning, 356008) at a density of 0.5 million cells per cm2. Non-cardiomyocytes were eliminated by culturing in Lactate Media for 4 days (DMEM without glucose (Thermo Fisher, 11966025), 540 mg/L sodium bicarbonate, 4 mM lactate (Sigma L6402), 200 μM ascorbic acid, and 14 ng/mL sodium selenite.). Cells were then maintained in Cardiomyocyte Maintenance Media (DMEM/F12, 10% FBS, and 1% Pen/Strep) until day 30, when cultures were enriched for cardiomyocytes using EasySep Human PSC-Derived Cardiomyocyte Enrichment Kit (StemCell Thechnology, 17965) and cells were phenotyped and plated for experiments. Cells were karotyped to confirm they were karotypically normal (Cell Line Genetics) and mycoplasma testing was conducted to confirm clean cultures (MP0035, Sigma).

Lentivirus production

Control and PLXNA4 lentivirus plasmids were ordered from GeneCopoeia (PLXNA4 cat #: EX-H4634-Lv105, control vector cat #: EX-NEG-Lv105). Lentivirus was made using the Dharmacon Trans-Lentiviral packaging kit following manufacturers protocol (Kit number: TLP5920). In brief, Lenti-X cells were grown to 70% confluency in DMEM +10% FBS +1% penstrep, and passaged 1:2 two days in a row. On the third day, cells were plated at 7.5 million cells per T75 flask in 19 mL of media. The following afternoon, when cells were 85–95% confluent, cells were transfected with lentivirus package plus plasmids. 16 h after treatment media was changed to high glucose DMEM containing 5% FBS, and 2 mM L-glutamine and cells were incubated for 48 h. After 48 h, media was removed, filtered with a 0.45 filter and PEG-IT was added to make a 1 x PEG solution. Solution was inverted to mix and stored at 4° overnight. The following day, media containing virus particles was centrifuged at 1500 × g for 30 min at 4° and pellets were resuspended in 250 μl of PBS containing 0.1% BSA. Virus was aliquoted into single use aliquots and stored at −80° until needed.

hiPSC cell treatment

After bead selection, cells were plated in maintenance media containing 10% FBS for RNA and protein experiments (1 million cells per well, 12 well plate) or imaging experiments (45,000 cells per well, 96 well plate). The day after plating, media was changed to maintenance media without FBS and cells were treated with either PLXNA4 siRNA (Thermofisher, silencer select Assay ID: 110470), scramble siRNA (Thermofisher, silencer select Assay ID: 4390843), PLXNA4 lentivirus or control lentivirus. Media was changed on all treatments. 72 h after treatment cells were harvested for total RNA extraction (RNeasy kit, Qiagen, 74106) following manufacturers protocols.

mRNA libraries

Cells were lysed and RNA extracted using Zymo quickRNA microprep kit following manufacturers instructions (R1050, Zymo Research). mRNA libraries were carried out on 1 μg of RNA following manufacturers protocols (Kapa RNA hyperprep kit with riboerase). Sample quality control was carried out using qPCR and a BioAnalyser and sequenced on Novaseq SP aiming for 25 million reads per sample.

mRNA analysis

mRNA libraries were aligned to a GENCODE v36 decoy-aware transcriptome using salmon v1.4.085 correcting for GC-bias (--gcBias). Differential expression was performed using the limma-voom framework after removing lowly detected genes using the edgeR function filterByExpr(). Normalization factors were calculated using DESeq2 normalization and contrasts were explicitly extracted for PLXNA4 knockdown versus control and PLXNA4 overexpression versus control. Two-sided p-values were estimated, and multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

To identify substantially dysregulated pathways, a similar approach was taken to the analysis of the snRNA-seq analysis described above. For each test, genes were split into significantly upregulated (logFC >1, FDR-adjusted two-sided P-value <0.05) and significantly downregulated (logFC <-1, FDR-adjusted two-sided P-value <0.05) gene sets and tested for enrichment in Gene Ontology Biological Processes, Reactome, and KEGG. For gene ontology analysis, we used the R package GOStats v2.46.0; for Reactome pathways, we used the R package ReactomePA v1.24.0; for KEGG pathways, we used the R package clusterProfiler v3.14.3. Any pathway/ontology with <15 genes or >500 genes was excluded. An FDR correction was applied separately for each pathway database using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

In the overexpression analysis, there were many GO:BP pathways with significant enrichment. To consolidate this information, we clustered pathways based on the degree to which the enrichment was driven by the same set of genes. First, we identified the complete set of genes driving the enrichment in any pathway. Second, we created a binary vector for each pathway indicating which genes drove the enrichment of the pathway. Third, we calculated a neighborhood graph and corresponding UMAP embedding based on the Jaccard distance between pathways. Finally, we assigned pathways to clusters using the Leiden algorithm. For up-regulated pathways, we used 5 nearest neighbors, min_dist 0.5, and Leiden resolution 1.0; for down-regulated pathways, we used 6 nearest neighbors, min_dist 0.9, and Leiden resolution 1.2.

Published hiPSC-CM and mouse bulk RNA-sequencing analysis

Published raw bulk RNA-seq from hiPSC-CMs derived from a patient with a c.2358delA variant in DSG245 was reprocessed as follows. First, we trimmed the Illumina universal adapter sequence from reads with cutadapt. Next, reads were aligned to a GENCODE v36 decoy-aware transcriptome using salmon v1.4.0. Differential expression testing between DSG2 mutants and wild-type was performed using limma-voom as in previous analysis.

RNA-seq from Wu et al., 202446 was provided as count matrices and transcript per million (TPM) quantifications. We performed differential expression using limma-voom with the model ∼1 + group + timepoint, where group indicates whether the sample was wild-type or siPKP2 and the timepoint indicates the day the sample was taken (day 2, 4, 6, or 8). For cardiac knockout mice, we performed differential expression between Pkp2 knockout mice and control separately in each chamber. Using the limma-voom model of ∼0 + group, we extracted all pairwise contrasts between groups included in their dataset.

Cellular electrophysiology phenotyping

Beating Chipsc22 ventricular cardiomyocytes, or commercially purchased iCell Cardiomyocytes (Female, Catalog number 01434, FujiFilm) were dissociated using STEMdiff Cardiomyocyte Dissociation Kit (Stem Cell Technologies) and seeded onto a fibronectin-coated 96-well plate (Greiner Bio-One) at 55,000 cells per well. Cardiomyocytes were infected and transfected as described above. After 7 days of treatment, cells were stain with FluoVolt (Life Technologies, F10488) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. FluoVolt’s fluorescence of ACM was captured at ∼100 frames/sec on a Vala Sciences IC200-KIC Imager with electrical pacing at 1 Hz. Cardiomyocytes were maintained in a heating chamber at 37°C with 10% CO2 throughout the recordings. The raw data were processed and analyzed with CyteSeer 2.9.2.10 (Vala Science), and durations of action potentials at 75% recovery (APD75) were used to determine effects on cellular electrophysiology. To control for differences in baseline APD75 across experiments and plates, APD75 values were normalized to respective controls on each plate. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to test for statistical differences in normalized APD75 using two-sided p-values.

mRNA extraction and qRTPCR

After electrophysiology measurements were taken, cells were lysed and RNA extracted using Zymo quickRNA microprep kit following manufacturers instructions (R1050, Zymo Research). cDNA was made using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (1708890, Bio-Rad) and qRTPCR was carried out using probes for PLXNA4 and HPRT (IDT assay IDs Hs.PT.58.3100864 and Hs.PT.58v.45621572, respectively) on a Bio-Rad CFX-Opus 384 PCR machine. Expression of PLXNA4 was normalized to HPRT.

Protein quantification

After electrophysiology measurements were taken, cells were lysed on ice in Cell Lysis Buffer (9803, Cell Signaling Technology) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (HALT 78445, ThermoScientific) and 1 mM PMSF (P7626,Sigma). Cells were incubated on ice with rocking for 30 min and then scraped, sonicated and centrifuged at max speed for 10 min at 4° to pellet debris. Protein concentration was measured using a micro BCA protein kit (23235, ThermoFisher) and 0.5 μg/μl of protein per well was run on a Simple Western Jess Western Blotting system following manufacturers protocols for replex with chemiluminescence. Levels of Plexin A4 (MAB5856, R and D) and GAPDH (2118S, Cell Signaling Technology) were measured. Expression of Plexin A4 and GAPDH was quantified using ImageJ. Full Western Blot images can be found at Zenodo https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21890062.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical significance thresholds were set at an adjusted p < 0.05 across all analyses, accounting for multiple testing corrections accordingly for each distinct analysis.
