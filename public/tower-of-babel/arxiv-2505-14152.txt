Linearized renormalization
L. L. Salcedo

Abstract
Using an infinitesimal approach, this work addresses the renormalization problem to deal with the ultraviolet divergences arising in quantum field theory. Under the assumption that the action has already been renormalized to yield an ultraviolet-finite effective action that satisfies a certain set of renormalization conditions, we analyze how the action must be adjusted to reproduce a first-order change in these renormalization conditions. The analysis then provides the change that is induced on the correlation functions of the theory. This program is successfully carried out in the case of super-renormalizable theories, namely, a scalar field with cubic interaction in four space-time dimensions and with quartic interaction in three space-time dimensions. Relying on existing results in the theory of perturbative renormalization, we derive explicit renormalized expressions for these theories, each of which involves only a finite number of graphs constructed with full propagators and full n n -point vertices. The renormalizable case is analyzed as well; the derived expressions are ultraviolet finite as the regulator is removed but cannot be written without a regulator. In this sense, the renormalization is not fully explicit in the renormalizable case. Nevertheless, a perturbative solution of the equations starting from the free theory provides the renormalized Feynman graphs, similar to the BPHZ program. For compatibility with the preservation of the renormalization conditions, a projective renormalization scheme, as opposed to a minimal one, is also introduced. The ideas developed are extended to the study of the renormalization of composite operators and the Schwinger-Dyson equations.



I
Introduction and conclusions

Quantum field theory is an extremely beautiful and successful tool in
theoretical physics for describing the interactions between particles and in
other areas such as condensed matter
[
2
,
1
]
. It
accommodates the Standard Model of Particles which currently is being actively
tested
[
4
,
3
]
. Perturbative calculations using
Feynman diagrams typically display divergences associated with virtual
particles in loops in the asymptotic region of large loop momenta. The same
ultraviolet (UV) divergences appear in the correlation functions for fields at
short distances. Such divergences are not pure mathematical artifacts, rather,
they signal the failure of the model to include the proper degrees of freedom
relevant at very short distances
[
5
]
. In general, a field
theory is designed to work at scales below a certain momentum cutoff
\varLambda

and becomes inconsistent above it, developing unphysically large
contributions. Through the process of renormalization, the divergences can be
removed by enlarging the action through the addition of suitable counterterms,
in general involving operators of increasingly larger
dimension. Renormalizable and super-renormalizable theories are those that
only require a finite number of operators in the action to produce finite
correlation functions. While a renormalizable theory such as Quantum
Chromodynamics needs not, and in fact does not, describe the physics at
arbitrarily short scales, it remains an internally consistent theory at all
scales. Quantum electrodynamics is also renormalizable at the perturbative
level, although it develops a non-perturbative Landau pole at extremely high
energies
[
6
]
. Due to the difficult renormalization problem,
only a few quantum field theories currently exist from a rigorous mathematical
point of view, at the non-perturbative level
[
7
]
. These
theories exist in lower-dimensional spaces where ultraviolet divergences are
less severe. Certainly, no four-dimensional renormalizable gauge theory has
yet been constructed in a mathematical sense.

The physics of the non-perturbative renormalization and its naturalness were
greatly clarified after the analysis by Wilson and others. Nevertheless, even
if the perturbative analysis does not cover the whole subject of
renormalization, it is still an indispensable and insightful tool in any
theory. The systematics of the renormalization at the perturbative level has
been the subject of intense mathematical work in the past. In particular, the
work of Bogoliubov and Parasiuk
[
8
]
and Hepp

[
9
]
allowed to organize in a systematic way the renormalization
of the momentum space Feynman diagrams of any theory and the identification of
the counterterms. Epstein and Glaser
[
10
]
developed an
equivalent procedure in space-time space (see
[
11
]
for a modern
presentation). The forest formula by Zimmermann provided an explicit solution
to Bogoliubov’s recursion that, as a by-product, allowed to yield manifestly
renormalized ultraviolet-finite Feynman graphs for massive theories

[
13
,
12
]
. The same subtraction rules work
regardless of the regularization and apply to the massless case and gauge
theories, where dimensional regularization becomes the method of choice

[
14
]
.

A field theory is solved when its correlation functions are known. In
perturbation theory, this corresponds to summing the relevant Feynman
graphs. The effective action functional is the classical action, or tree-level
action, that reproduces the correlation functions of a theory, while the full
propagator and full vertices contain all loop effects

[
15
]
. In
[
16
]
, the following
problem was posed and answered: If a perturbation is added to the action, must
the Feynman graphs be recomputed, including both the old and new vertices, to
obtain the new effective action? Such procedure corresponds to a scheme
‘
S_{\text{free}}+S_{I}+\delta S
’, where
S=S_{\text{free}}+S_{I}
is the
original action, and
\delta S
is the perturbation. In fact, it is not
mandatory to follow such route. The new effective action can also be obtained
using the scheme ‘
\varGamma+\delta S
’, where
\varGamma
is the effective action
of the original theory. In this scheme, standard Feynman graphs are used with
vertices of
\delta S
, as well as full vertices and full propagators of the
effective action, but include only graphs without unperturbed loops. This
means that every loop must include a vertex of
\delta S
. If the vertices of

\delta S
are deleted in one such graph, what remains is a tree graph of

\varGamma
.

In this work, we explore how the above result can be exploited in the context
of renormalization theory. In a renormalizable theory, every correlation
function remains finite (barring coincident points) when the parameters in the
action are given a suitable dependence on the UV regulator, say
\varLambda
, as
the regulator is removed, i.e., for large
\varLambda
. In this way, the theory
becomes a renormalized one. In a theory that is already renormalized, one can
introduce a first-order perturbation in the action. In the
‘
\varGamma+\delta S
’ scheme, all the UV divergences from the loops inside

\varGamma
have been canceled, but the perturbation
\delta S
introduces new
loops and thus new divergences that need a regulator. These new divergences
must be canceled through a suitable dependence on the regulator of the
parameters in
\delta S
. As usual, the cancellation of divergences leaves
finite ambiguities in the parameters. Rather than applying a minimal
subtraction scheme, the approach we adopt here is to enforce some
renormalization conditions on the correlation functions by defining some
renormalized parameters. Correspondingly, the subtractions appearing in, e.g.,
the forest formula, follow a projective renormalization scheme. The procedure
then yields the change in the correlation functions induced by the first-order
variation in the renormalized parameters.

This infinitesimal approach, which is implemented through a form of
Schwinger’s principle, has an obvious limitation, namely, it does not directly
provide the renormalized effective action, only its variation as the
renormalized parameters change. On the other hand, the renormalization takes
place at the linear level, hence it is structurally simpler and the
divergences are softer. Another virtue is that the expressions involve the
full propagator and vertices, then the equations found are
non-perturbative. This means that each equation involves only a finite number
of graphs constructed with the propagator and vertices of the effective
action, rather than an infinite number of graphs of the action, much as the
Schwinger-Dyson equations do. Of course, a different question is that some of
the theories only exist, in principle, in a perturbative sense, i.e., as a
formal power series in the coupling or in
\hbar
. The fact that the equations
do not directly depend on perturbation theory is an interesting feature, which
could potentially be adapted for a non-perturbative construction of the
theory. This is not attempted in this work.

The linearized approach explored here is applied to super-renormalizable
theories, namely, scalar
\phi^{3}_{4}
and
\phi^{4}_{3}
. The fact that in these
theories the UV divergences are rather mild allows us to obtain explicit and
fully renormalized non-perturbative equations for the variation of the
effective with respect to the renormalized parameters. No regulator is
needed. Not unexpectedly, the renormalizable case, e.g.
\phi^{3}_{6}
or

\phi^{4}_{4}
, is not so well behaved since the UV divergences are stronger
there. In the renormalizable case, expressions are found that still involve
only a finite number of graphs constructed with the propagator and vertices of
the effective action, however, the momentum integrals need to be
regulated. The renormalizability of the theory guarantees that there is a
finite limit after removing the regulator, but the limit itself cannot be
expressed in closed form.
In the renormalizable case, the equations display a finite number of explicit
loops, but the divergences involved are not accounted by a naive expectation
from these loops only.
Ultimately, the reason why the limit cannot be taken explicitly is that the
composite operators in the perturbation
\delta S
activate subdivergences
involving vertices from
\delta S
and propagator lines (of the action) hidden
within the effective action even if the latter has been renormalized.
Nevertheless, the enforcement of the renormalization conditions, which is
built into the formulation, ensures that when the equations are solved
perturbatively as a formal series in the renormalized coupling, each
contribution is a subtracted and UV-finite Feynman graph so that the regulator
can be removed there.

The subject of perturbing the action to first order is, of course, closely
related to the study of composite operators and their renormalization. In
fact, most of the tools developed in one case immediately apply to the other,
so this topic is also discussed.

The linearized renormalization is also closely related to the subject of
Schwinger-Dyson equations and their renormalization. In both cases, the
equations follow from graphs involving vertices and lines from the effective
action and from the action itself (in the case of the Schwinger-Dyson
equations) or from
\delta S
in the linearized renormalization approach. The
two sets of equations are related and fulfill consistency conditions between
them.

The paper is organized as follows: Sec.
II
revisits the effective
action machinery. The concept of
\langle A\rangle^{\phi}
and some related diagrammatic
properties are discussed. Our form of the Schwinger principle is also

H

\phi^{3}_{4}

\phi^{4}_{3}

from UV divergences and express the variation of the effective action with
respect to the renormalized parameters.
The renormalizable case is addressed in Sec.
IV
. First, the
projective renormalization scheme is introduced. This is presented within the
framework of a Bogoliubov-Parasiuk-Hepp-Zimmermann (BPHZ) approach, by
specifying the operation
T
to be employed in the forest formula. Then, the
linearized equations are constructed. As previously mentioned, these equations
are less explicit than those of the super-renormalizable case since the
regulator cannot be lifted in their non-perturbative form. However, the
regulator can be removed in a perturbative solution of the equations and
this yields subtracted UV-finite Feynman graphs.
Sec.
V
deals with the renormalization of composite operators,
building on the formalism previously developed for the linearized
renormalization. In the super-renormalizable case this approach produces
UV-finite non-perturbative expressions for the matrix elements of the
operators. The renormalization approach is adapted to the Schwinger-Dyson
equations in Sec.
VI
. The renormalized Schwinger-Dyson equations
follow the same pattern as the linearized renormalization and the composite
operators; they are manifestly UV-finite in the super-renormalizable case but
not in the renormalizable one.
Some properties of the effective action exposed in Sec.
I
, including
the Schwinger principle, are proven in App.
A
. App.
B

illustrates properties of the projective renormalization scheme developed in
Sec.

C
elucidates further the concept of
anti-canonical pattern discussed in Sec.
IV.2
by analyzing a simple
subcase of the scalar theory
\phi^{3}_{6}
. It also illustrates the consistency
of other results obtained in the main text. Finally App.
D
discusses
a technical aspect of the linearized approach, namely, that the renormalized
results do not depend on the assumption that the effective action has already
been renormalized prior to its perturbation.

II
The Schwinger action principle

For simplicity, let us consider a theory
\phi^{\kappa}_{d}
, a single scalar
field
\varphi(x)
with action

S[\varphi]=\int d^{d}x\left(\frac{1}{2}Z(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\varphi^{2}+\frac{1}{\kappa!}g\varphi^{\kappa}\right).

(1)

Here, a Euclidean signature-like notation is used. For convenience, an explicit
wave function renormalization factor
Z
has also been introduced.

The generating functional
Z[J]
of the Green or correlation functions is then

Z[J]=\int D\varphi\,e^{\int d^{d}x({\mathcal{L}}(x)+J(x)\varphi(x))}:=e^{W[J]}

(2)

so that

\begin{split}\langle T\varphi(x_{1})\cdots\varphi(x_{n})\rangle&=\frac{1}{Z[J]}\frac{\delta}{\delta J(x_{1})}\cdots\frac{\delta}{\delta J(x_{n})}Z[J]\Big|_{J=0},\\
\langle T\varphi(x_{1})\cdots\varphi(x_{n})\rangle_{c}&=\frac{\delta}{\delta J(x_{1})}\cdots\frac{\delta}{\delta J(x_{n})}W[J]\Big|_{J=0}.\end{split}

(3)

Here and in what follows, we will use a convention with Boltzmann weight

e^{+S}
instead of
e^{-S}
. This implies that
Z
,
m^{2}
,
g
,
S[\varphi]
,

W[J]
,
\varGamma[\phi]
, etc, have non-standard signs. In particular, the free
propagator in momentum space becomes

D_{0}(k)=-(Zk^{2}+m^{2})^{-1}.

(4)

While this convention may be inconvenient for performing detailed
calculations, it enjoys the crucial advantage that there are no minus signs
between Green functions contributions and their Feynman graphs, nor in the
Feynman rule of the vertices.

The effective action functional

(10)

\phi^{i}
is known as the classical field. The expansion

II.2
Schwinger’s action principle

If the current
J
is not set to zero in the functional integral, the
expectation value of an observable
A[\varphi]
becomes

\langle A\rangle^{J}=\frac{1}{Z[J]}\int D\varphi\,e^{S[\varphi]+J_{i}\varphi^{i}}A[\varphi].

(14)

Let us introduce the notation

\langle A\rangle^{\phi}:=\langle A\rangle^{J=J[\phi]}.

(15)

That is,
\langle A\rangle^{\phi}
denotes the expectation value in the presence of the
current
J
such that
\langle\varphi\rangle^{J}=\phi
. In particular

\langle\varphi^{i}\rangle^{\phi}=\phi^{i}.

(16)

Under a first-order variation of the action
S\to S+\delta S
, the generator
of the connected Green functions is modified as

\delta W[J]=\langle\delta S\rangle^{J}.

(17)

As a consequence, for the effective action one obtains immediately (see
Appendix
A
) that

\delta\varGamma[\phi]=\langle\delta S\rangle^{\phi},

(18)

which is likely a version of Schwinger’s quantum action principle.
This identity will play a central role in this work.

The first-order variation in Eq. (
18
) is consistent (in the technical sense

[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}]=0
); therefore, if one wants to compute variations of
higher order it suffices to use the identity recursively. Alternatively, the
theorem proven in
[
16
]
applies: If
S
is perturbed to

S+S_{I}
the new correlation functions follow from using the Feynman rules of
‘
\varGamma+S_{I}
’ (namely, the propagator and vertices of
\varGamma
plus vertices
of
S_{I}
) but retaining only graphs such that any loop must have at least one
vertex of
S_{I}
.

\begin{split}G[J,\phi]&=\langle e^{J_{i}\varphi^{i}}\rangle^{\phi}=\frac{Z[J+J[\phi]]}{Z[J[\phi]]}\\
&=:e^{J_{i}\phi^{i}}+H[J,\phi],\end{split}

(30)

so that

\begin{split}G[J,\phi]&=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}J_{i_{1}}\cdots J_{i_{\ell}}{\hat{G}}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}[\phi]\\
&=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}\frac{1}{n!}G^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}J_{i_{1}}\cdots J_{i_{\ell}}\phi^{j_{1}}\cdots\phi^{j_{n}}.\end{split}

(31)

In addition, for an observable

,

A[\varphi]=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}A_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}\varphi^{i_{1}}\cdots\varphi^{i_{\ell}},

(32)

one can define

G^{A}[\phi]:=A[\phi]+H^{A}[\phi]:=\langle A[\varphi]\rangle^{\phi}.

(33)

This functional can be expanded as

G^{A}[\phi]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}G^{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\phi^{j_{1}}\cdots\phi^{j_{n}},

(34)

where

G^{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}A_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}G^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}.

(35)

From Schwinger’s principle, it follows that if
S[\phi]
is perturbed by adding
a term
\delta S[\phi]=\delta\lambda A[\phi]
,

\delta\varGamma[\phi]=\delta\lambda\langle A[\varphi]\rangle^{\phi}=\delta\lambda G^{A}[\phi].

(36)

Hence, diagrammatically,
G^{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
is given by connected and
irreducible graphs with amputated legs
j_{1}\ldots j_{n}
, constructed with the
propagator and the vertices of the action plus exactly one vertex of the
composite operator
A[\phi]
.
4
4

4

The Feynman rules of these vertices are

A_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}
and diagrammatically are represented by a single
point.
We will refer to the
G^{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
as the
amputated
matrix elements
of
A
, since the standard matrix elements of
A
,

\langle A\varphi^{i_{1}}\cdots\varphi^{i_{m}}\rangle
, can be recovered from them, to
wit, by adding tree graphs of
\varGamma
(in general several trees) in all
possible ways saturating all amputated legs

and fields
i_{s}
, provided
that each tree contains at most one end of type
j_{r}
(so that no new loops
are generated).

II.4
Momentum representation conventions

In momentum space we use the following conventions:

\varGamma[\phi]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d^{d}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\tilde{\varGamma}_{n}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\tilde{\phi}_{1}(p_{1})\cdots\tilde{\phi}_{n}(p_{n})

with
\tilde{\phi}(p)=\int d^{d}x\,e^{ipx}\phi(x)
and

\tilde{\varGamma}_{n}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=(2\pi)^{d}\delta(p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n})\varGamma_{n}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1})

(38)

due to invariance under translations.
5
5

5

The functions

\varGamma_{n}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
or simply
\varGamma_{n}(p)
, are only defined in the
subspace
\sum p=0

\varGamma_{n}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1})
for convenience. The same notational liberty
will be taken for similar functions, e.g.
H_{n}(q;p)
below.

Likewise, we introduce the momentum version of the coefficients

H^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
, denoted

\begin{split}\tilde{H}^{\ell}_{n}&(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell};p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\\
&=(2\pi)^{d}\delta\big(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\big)H^{\ell}_{n}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell};p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\end{split}

(39)

or simply
(2\pi)^{d}\delta(\sum q+\sum p)H^{\ell}_{n}(q;p)
. Explicitly, for

n=\ell=2
,

\begin{split}H^{2}_{2}(q_{1},&q_{2};p_{1},p_{2})=\\
&D(q_{1})D(q_{2})D(q_{1}+p_{1})\varGamma_{3}(p_{1},q_{1},q_{1}+p_{1})\\
&\quad+D(q_{1})D(q_{2})D(q_{1}+p_{2})\varGamma_{3}(p_{2},q_{1},q_{1}+p_{2})\\
&\quad+D(q_{1})D(q_{2})\varGamma_{4}(p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}),\end{split}

(40)

where

D(p):=(-\varGamma_{2}(p))^{-1}

(41)

denotes the full propagator (connected 2-point function).

Similarly
H^{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
corresponds to
\tilde{H}^{A}_{n}(p)
, or

H^{A}_{n}(p)
after extracting
(2\pi)^{d}\delta(\sum p)
if
A[\phi]
is
translationally invariant, and the same goes for
G^{A}_{n}(p)
.

In what follows, different types of Feynman graphs will appear:

i)

S
-graphs. They are constructed using the free propagator
D_{0}(k)

and the vertex of the action
g
, equivalently,
s^{ij}
and

g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
.

ii)

\varGamma
-graphs. They are constructed using the full propagator
D(k)
and the full vertices

\varGamma_{n}(p)
, equivalently,
D^{ij}
and
\varGamma_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
.

iii)

\hat{\varGamma}
-graphs. These are those of the Theorem, using

{\hat{D}}^{ij}[\phi]
as line and
\hat{\varGamma}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}[\phi]
as
vertices and
\phi
is not set to zero.

Here, and in what follows, we use a Euclidean signature.

III
Linearized renormalization of super-renormalizable theories

We want to investigate the idea of using the Schwinger principle (
18
) to
perform the renormalization of a renormalizable theory. In this Section we
start with the more amenable case of a super-renormalizable theory.

A (first-order) infinitesimal variation of the bare action
S
, in DeWitt
notation

\delta S[\phi]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\delta g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\phi^{i_{1}}\cdots\phi^{i_{n}},

(42)

will induce an infinitesimal variation in the effective action

\begin{split}\delta\varGamma[\phi]&=\langle\delta S[\varphi]\rangle^{\phi}\\
&=\delta S[\phi]+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\frac{1}{\ell!}\delta g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}{\hat{H}}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}[\phi],\end{split}

(43)

and

\delta\varGamma_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}=\delta g_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\frac{1}{\ell!}\delta g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}H^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}.

(44)

In the spirit of
[
16
]
, our point of view is that

\varGamma[\phi]
is already renormalized and the correlation functions are free
from ultraviolet (UV) divergences, and the same goes for the functionals
{\hat{H}}

and the coefficients
H
, which are finite combinations of the propagators and
vertices of
\varGamma[\phi]
. However, new UV divergences arise in

\delta\varGamma[\phi]
since
\langle\delta S[\phi]\rangle
requires the expectation
value of the composite local operators present in the action. In Eq. (
44
)
the divergences are introduced by the sum over the indices

i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}
, or equivalently due to loops in a momentum space
representation. To render
\delta\varGamma[\phi]
finite, such divergences have
to be canceled by divergences in the bare parameters of the action,

\delta g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}
.

S_{0}[\phi]=g_{0,\alpha}O^{\alpha}[\phi].

(161)

A sum over
\alpha
is implicit,
\alpha\in\{m,Z,g\}
(here
m,Z,g
are
merely labels, not variables). The bare couplings
g_{0,\alpha}
are
m^{2}_{0}
,

Z_{0}
, and
g_{0}
and have a dependence on
\varLambda
. This action yields the
regulated effective action
\varGamma[\phi;\varLambda]
.

As is well-known, the theory will be perturbatively renormalizable
(super-renormalizable) when the coupling constant has zero (positive) mass
dimension. The (regulated) effective action itself depends on
\varLambda
and is
UV-finite. This means that it is possible to choose the
\varLambda
dependence
in the bare parameters of the action,
g_{0,\alpha}(\varLambda)
, in such a way
that the limit
\varLambda\to\infty
of
\varGamma[\phi;\varLambda]
, denoted

\varGamma[\phi]
, exists and is finite for sufficiently regular configurations

\phi
.

In the renormalization program developed by Bogoliubov and collaborators

[
23
,
12
]
the dependence of the bare parameters on
the regulator is obtained through counterterms. Let the functional
S_{b}
,

S_{b}[\phi]=m^{2}\,O^{m}+Z\,O^{Z}+g\,O^{g}=g_{b,\alpha}O^{\alpha}[\phi]

(162)

be (what we will call) the
basic action
. Here
g_{b,\alpha}
(that is,

m^{2}
,
Z
,
g
) are
finite
(
\varLambda
-independent) parameters that
will serve as coordinates in the manifold of effective actions of the theory
and need not have a particular physical meaning. The basic action yields,
using the Feynman rules, an effective action
\varGamma_{b}[\phi;\varLambda]
which
is UV divergent. To remove the divergences and obtain
\varGamma[\phi]
,
counterterms
S_{\rm ct}
are added to the basic action,

S_{0}=S_{b}+S_{\rm ct}\,.

(163)

The graphs in
\varGamma
and
\varGamma_{b}
are identical and only differ by the
couplings,
g_{0,\alpha}
and
g_{b,\alpha}
, respectively. The basic
parameters are finite and
\varGamma_{b}
diverges from the loops.
\varGamma
is
finite after the divergences of the loops are compensated by the divergences
in the bare parameters.

The counterterms can be determined recursively within a perturbative or loop
expansion. At each new order, the new Feynman graphs of that order have the

subdivergences
automatically canceled by lower order counterterms, while
the remaining superficial divergences are primitive and give a new
contribution to the counterterm action. For a graph with all subdivergences
subtracted, some prescription, implemented by a linear operation
T
, extracts
the divergent component while
(1-T)
gives a purely finite component. The
operation
T
can be chosen in many ways, but it must fulfill three essential
requirements, i)
T^{2}=T
(idempotent), ii) the component
(1-T)
must be
finite, hence any possible divergences must be isolated by
T
, and iii)
T

must always act in the same form when acting on the same graph or subgraph.

Denoting, as usual,
\bar{R}
the operation of recursively extracting all
subdivergences (but not the overall divergence), the counterterms can be
summarized in the following formula:

S_{\rm ct}=-T\bar{R}(\varGamma_{b}-S_{b})

(164)

so

S_{0}=S_{b}-T\bar{R}(\varGamma_{b}-S_{b}).

(165)

\varGamma_{b}-S_{b}
is the sum of the graphs of
\varGamma_{b}
with loops.
Explicitly
[
13
,
24
]
on each graph of

\varGamma_{b}
,

\bar{R}=\prod_{\gamma}{}^{\prime}(1-T_{\gamma}),

(166)

where
\gamma
denotes every possible subgraph with loops of effective-action
type, that is, amputated, connected, and irreducible. The full graph itself is
excluded from the product. The factor
1-T_{\gamma}
removes any possible
divergent component of the subgraph
\gamma
. The subtractions are to be
applied orderly, with smaller subgraphs first. The prime indicates that, upon
expansion, monomials with factors of the type
T_{\gamma_{1}}T_{\gamma_{2}}
are
to be removed when the subgraphs
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are overlapping,
that is, they share at least one line but neither is a subgraph of the other.

Another standard definition is

R:=(1-T)\bar{R}.

(167)

The operation
R
represents the removal of all subdivergences, including the
overall divergence. The effective action is obtained after applying the
operation
R
to the graphs of
\varGamma_{b}
with loops; therefore,

\varGamma=S_{b}+R(\varGamma_{b}-S_{b}).

(168)

The meaning of this relation is that if one adds all (regulated)
effective-action-like graphs produced by
S_{0}
, upon removal of the regulator
the functional
\varGamma[\phi]
is obtained on the LHS, and the same result is
obtained on the RHS by using instead
S_{b}
plus systematic subtraction of the
divergences in the graphs with loops.

As said, there is much freedom in the choice of
T
. The most usual choices are
of the minimal type, that is,
T_{\rm min}
vanishes if the graph or subgraph has no
divergences. This is the case of minimal subtraction (MS) in DR, where
T_{\rm min}

selects the principal part in the power series in
\epsilon
, or in BPHZ,
where
T_{\rm min}
extracts the component of the Taylor series in powers of the
external momenta up to a degree equal to the degree of divergence (or nothing if
there is no divergence). In minimal schemes

T_{\rm min}\varGamma=T_{\rm min}S_{b}=0,\qquad(1-T_{\rm min})S_{0}=S_{b}\,.

(169)

The first equations follow from the fact that
\varGamma
and
S_{b}
are finite,
the latter equation indicates that
S_{b}
is the finite component of
S_{0}
,
because the counterterms are strictly divergent and so
T_{\rm min}S_{\rm ct}=S_{\rm ct}
.

Here we will discuss a different, non-minimal, scheme, which will be called

projective renormalization scheme
. As already noted in
Sec.
III.3
, a perturbative solution of the equation for

\partial\varGamma_{n}(p)/\partial g
provides something similar to the BPHZ
expansion, where each graph is renormalized before integration. However, in
BPHZ the subtraction is minimal, while here even finite graphs are
subtracted since what is enforced are the
renormalization conditions

(RC).

To see this in more detail, let us consider the renormalizable case, where in
principle all the three bare parameters need renormalization. The effective
action manifold is coordinated by three renormalized finite parameters

g_{R,\alpha}
, namely,
m_{R}^{2}
,
Z_{R}
and
g_{R}
, defined through some RC. A
typical choice (for the massive case) is that of renormalization conditions at
zero momentum, or more generally at a scale

\begin{split}\varGamma_{2}(p)\Big|_{\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}}-\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}^{2}\frac{\partial\varGamma_{2}(p)}{\partial(p_{1}^{2})}\Big|_{\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}}&=m_{R}^{2},\\
\frac{\partial\varGamma_{2}(p)}{\partial(p_{1}^{2})}\Big|_{\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}}&=Z_{R},\\
\varGamma_{\kappa}(p)\Big|_{\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}}&=g_{R},\end{split}

(170)

where
m_{R}^{2}
,

and

are three finite
\varLambda
-independent
parameters. Here

refers to the

\big|_{\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}}

(171)

If the theory is massless, there are only two parameters:
Z_{R}
and
g_{R}
. In
the
renormalizable

\varGamma_{\kappa}(p)\Big|_{\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}}&=g_{R},\end{split}

(172)

for

O^{m}
is not present in the

16

16

IV.2
Linearized renormalization

The UV divergences in the renormalizable theories are more severe than those
of super-renormalizable ones, correspondingly, the results that we have
obtained are also less conclusive.

According to Schwinger’s principle, for a theory with bare action (
161
)

\delta\varGamma[\phi]=\delta g_{0,\alpha}\langle O^{\alpha}\rangle^{\phi}.

(195)

Our point of is be that the unperturbed effective action is already
renormalized and only the new divergences need regularization. To do this a
cutoff will be assumed. The possibility of using DR is briefly discussed
below.

We will use the notation
G^{\alpha}[\phi]:=\langle O^{\alpha}\rangle^{\phi}

already introduced in (
33
)
, that is,

G^{\alpha}[\phi]:=\frac{\partial\varGamma[\phi]}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}},

(196)

or equivalently

G^{\alpha}_{n}(p):=\frac{\partial\varGamma_{n}(p)}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}},

(197)

and correspondingly,

\begin{split}\delta\varGamma_{n}(p)&=\delta g_{0,\alpha}G^{\alpha}_{n}(p)\\
&=\delta m_{0}^{2}\,G^{m}_{n}(p)+\delta Z_{0}\,G^{Z}_{n}(p)+\delta g_{0}\,G^{g}_{n}(p).\end{split}

(198)

We remark that here we are using the notation based on the functions

G^{\alpha}_{n}(p)
while

were used in Sec.
III
(e.g., in
(
60

G^{\alpha}[\phi]=O^{\alpha}[\phi]+H^{\alpha}[\phi]
, that is,

\begin{split}G^{m}_{n}(p)&=\delta_{n,2}+H^{m}_{n}(p),\\
G^{Z}_{n}(p)&=\delta_{n,2}p^{2}+H^{Z}_{n}(p),\\
G^{g}_{n}(p)&=\delta_{n,\kappa}+H^{g}_{n}(p).\end{split}

(199)

The functions

only include the loop terms while

contain also tree graphs. Apart from this difference, the
formulas given for
H^{\alpha}_{n}(p)
in the super-renormalizable case (e.g.
(

the renormalizable case up to the different dimension in the momentum

G^{\alpha}[\phi]
, or equivalently
G^{\alpha}_{n}(p)
, are the quantities that we
can obtain explicitly from
\varGamma[\phi]
, and they are divergent by loops.
The quantities that are completely finite are, instead,

G_{R}^{\beta}[\phi]:=\frac{\partial\varGamma[\phi]}{\partial g_{R,\beta}},

(200)

\begin{split}\delta\varGamma_{n}(p)&=\delta g_{R,\alpha}G^{\alpha}_{R,n}(p)\\

(201)

In order to relate both sets of quantities, let us introduce the matrix

W^{\alpha}{}_{\beta}:=\frac{\partial g_{R,\beta}}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}},

(202)

which depends on the regulator and is divergent. In terms of it

G_{R}^{\beta}[\phi]=\frac{\partial\varGamma[\phi]}{\partial g_{R,\beta}}=\frac{\partial g_{0,\alpha}}{\partial g_{R,\beta}}\frac{\partial\varGamma[\phi]}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}},

(203)

therefore

G_{R}^{\beta}[\phi]=(W^{-1})^{\beta}{}_{\alpha}\,G^{\alpha}[\phi],

(204)

or equivalently

G_{R,n}^{\beta}(p)=(W^{-1})^{\beta}{}_{\alpha}\,G^{\alpha}_{n}(p).

(205)

The matrix
W
can be computed from
G^{\alpha}[\phi]
by using the RC in
(
176
),

W^{\alpha}{}_{\beta}=\frac{\partial g_{R,\beta}}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}}=\frac{\partial{\hat{T}}_{\beta}\varGamma[\phi]}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}}={\hat{T}}_{\beta}\frac{\partial\varGamma[\phi]}{\partial g_{0,\alpha}}

(206)

hence

W^{\alpha}{}_{\beta}={\hat{T}}_{\beta}G^{\alpha}[\phi].

(207)

The consistency condition

{\hat{T}}_{\alpha}G_{R}^{\beta}[\phi]=\delta^{\beta}_{\alpha},

(208)

which follows from the definition of
G_{R}^{\beta}
and the RC, is also derived from
the relations (
204
) and (
207
).

To be more explicit, for the RC in (
170
) with
\mu_{\mathrm{RC}}=0
the matrix

takes
the form

W=\begin{pmatrix}G^{m}_{2}(0)&\partial_{p^{2}}G^{m}_{2}(0)&G^{m}_{\kappa}(0)\\
G^{Z}_{2}(0)&\partial_{p^{2}}G^{Z}_{2}(0)&G^{Z}_{\kappa}(0)\\
G^{g}_{2}(0)&\partial_{p^{2}}G^{g}_{2}(0)&G^{g}_{\kappa}(0)\end{pmatrix},

(209)

while for the massless case RC in (

The relation

More specifically, the choice

(276)

\begin{split}\varGamma_{i}&=h_{i}+\frac{1}{2}g_{ijk}D^{jk}\\

\varGamma_{ij_{2}j_{3}}&=g_{ij_{2}j_{3}}+g_{ijk}D^{ja}D^{kb}D^{cd}\varGamma_{j_{2}ac}\varGamma_{j_{3}bd}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2}g_{ijk}D^{ja}D^{kb}\varGamma_{j_{2}j_{3}ab}.\end{split}

(279)

The diagrammatic representation of these equations is displayed in Fig.
10
.
This is an infinite hierarchy of exact relations fulfilled by the theory

\phi^{3}
. Furthermore, through systematic iteration (inserting the LHS in the
RHS) the hierarchy produces the perturbative series in powers of the coupling

g
in terms of Feynman graphs (
S
-graphs). In such a construction the
perturbative counting is assigned as follows:

i)

m_{ij}
,

\varGamma_{ij}
and
D^{ij}
, as well as
h_{i}
and
\varGamma_{i}
, are of
O(g^{0})
,

ii)

g_{ijk}
and
\varGamma_{ijk}
are of
O(g)
, and

iii)

the vertices

\varGamma_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
(
n\geq 4
) are of
O(g^{n})
.

In each iteration
D^{ij}
and
\varGamma_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
are expressed using

s^{ij}
and
g_{ijk}
plus terms of higher order. Then, at each step, there
are contributions involving only
s^{ij}
and
g_{ijk}
plus a remainder that
contains the full propagator and vertices. The terms constructed solely with

S
no longer evolve and these are the standard Feynman graphs of the theory

S
, while the remainder becomes of higher order at each iteration. Hence, the
procedure not only produces the perturbative series but also provides a closed
form for the exact remainder corresponding to the truncated series.

Since, as noted above, the RHS of (
277
) is not manifestly symmetric in
the indices
i,j_{2},\ldots,j_{n}
, there is an ambiguity in how to expand

D^{ij}
and
\varGamma_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
present on the RHS using the SDE. A
simple prescription is to use a symmetrized version of the SDE, which will
also be useful later; namely, the RHS of Eq. (
277
) is symmetrized by
hand. This gives rise to the
symmetrized
SDE:

\varGamma_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}=g_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\sum_{\ell\geq 3}\frac{1}{\ell!}g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}{H^{\prime}}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\qquad n\geq 1

(280)

where

{H^{\prime}}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{q=1}^{\ell}\sum_{p=1}^{n}\delta^{i_{q}}_{j_{p}}H^{i_{1}\ldots\widehat{i_{q}}\ldots i_{\ell}}_{j_{1}\ldots\widehat{j_{p}}\ldots j_{n}}\qquad n\geq 1,\quad\ell\geq 3.

(281)

By construction the coefficients

are completely symmetric and vanish for
\ell=1,2
.
Correspondingly, in the momentum space, there are functions

H^{\prime}{}^{\ell}_{n}(q;p)
in the subspace
\sum q+\sum p=0
.

In complete analogy with the relations

\begin{split}\delta\varGamma[\phi]&=\delta S_{0}[\phi]+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\frac{1}{\ell!}\delta g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}{\hat{H}}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}[\phi]=\delta g_{0,\alpha}G^{\alpha}[\phi],\\
G^{\alpha}[\phi]&=O^{\alpha}[\phi]+H^{\alpha}[\phi],\end{split}

(282)

the SDE in (
280

\begin{split}\varGamma[\phi]&=:S_{0}[\phi]+\sum_{\ell\geq 3}\frac{1}{\ell!}g_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}{\hat{H}}^{\prime}{}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell}}[\phi]=:g_{0,\alpha}G^{\prime}{}^{\alpha}[\phi],\\
G^{\prime}{}^{\alpha}[\phi]&=:O^{\alpha}[\phi]+H^{\prime}{}^{\alpha}[\phi].\end{split}

(283)

Similar to the functionals
G^{\alpha}[\phi]
, the
G^{\prime}{}^{\alpha}[\phi]

or the functions

are expressed in terms

\varGamma
-graphs, a finite number of graphs for each given number

of
(amputated)
p
-legs. As in (

\ell

q
-legs are
attached to a vertex
O^{\alpha}
.

The integral over

is, in general, UV divergent and
explicit

sense that a regulator
\varLambda
bounds the integration region, but otherwise
it is not present in the integrand. As was the case for
\delta\varGamma[\phi]

through Schwinger’s principle in previous Sections, here we assume that the

\varGamma
-graphs, is already renormalized. The regulator is needed only for the

\varGamma
-graphs and the divergences are compensated

explicit in the formula. The consistency of this

Applying the forms
{\hat{T}}_{\beta}
on both sides of (

Clearly, the matrix

Appendix A
Derivation of some formulas

A.1
Proof of Eq. (
18
)

Under the first-order variation
S\to S+\delta S
, it follows from (
8
)
that
\delta W[J]=\langle\delta S\rangle^{J}
. Hence, applying the variation
\delta

to
W[J]=\varGamma[\phi]+J_{i}\phi^{i}
, with
J=J[\phi]
and
\delta\phi=0
, we
obtain

\delta W[J]+\delta J_{i}\partial^{i}W[J]=\delta\varGamma[\phi]+\delta J_{i}\phi^{i}\,.

(300)

Since
\phi^{i}=\partial^{i}W[J]
, it follows that

\langle\delta S\rangle^{J}=\delta\varGamma
which proves Eq. (
18
).

A.2
Proof of the Theorem around Eq. (
19
)

The action producing the expectation values

\langle\varphi^{i_{1}}\cdots\varphi^{i_{n}}\rangle^{\phi}
is just

S^{\prime}[\varphi]=S[\varphi]+J[\phi]\cdot\varphi
, and therefore the
corresponding generator of the connected Green functions is
W[J^{\prime}+J]
. Here
the current is
J^{\prime}
while
\phi
, and so
J=J[\phi]
, are parameters. For

n\geq 2
such generator may be changed to

W^{\prime}[J^{\prime}]=W[J+J^{\prime}]-(J+J^{\prime})\cdot\phi\,.

(301)

Although this functional would give a vanishing value for
\langle\varphi\rangle^{J}
,
it correctly reproduces the expectation values for
n\geq 2
. The effective
action
\varGamma^{\prime}[\phi^{\prime}]
that at the tree level produces the same Green
functions (for
n\geq 2
) is then obtained as the Legendre transform of

W^{\prime}[J^{\prime}]
, as follows.

Using the Legendre-transform relations of the type

\varGamma[\phi]=\inf_{J}(W[J]-J\cdot\phi),\qquad W[J]=\sup_{\phi}(\varGamma[\phi]+J\cdot\phi),

(302)

we can write

\begin{split}\varGamma^{\prime}[\phi^{\prime}]&=\inf_{J^{\prime}}(W^{\prime}[J^{\prime}]-J^{\prime}\cdot\phi^{\prime})\\
&=\inf_{J^{\prime}}(W[J+J^{\prime}]-(J+J^{\prime})\cdot\phi-J^{\prime}\cdot\phi^{\prime})\\
&=\inf_{J^{\prime}}(\sup_{\phi_{1}}(\varGamma[\phi_{1}]+(J+J^{\prime})\cdot\phi_{1})-\phi\cdot(J+J^{\prime})-J^{\prime}\cdot\phi^{\prime}).\\
\end{split}

(303)

The extremum with respect to
J^{\prime}
requires
\phi_{1}=\phi+\phi^{\prime}
, hence

\varGamma^{\prime}[\phi^{\prime}]=\varGamma[\phi+\phi^{\prime}]+J[\phi]\cdot\phi^{\prime}\,.

(304)

Here
\phi^{\prime}
is the classical field and
\phi
is a
parameter. This effective action has a vanishing one-point vertex.

The statement of the Theorem in Eq. (
19
) is that the derivatives of

\varGamma[\phi]
of order two or higher produce the correct propagator and
vertices to be used in the Feynman rules at tree level to reproduce

\langle\varphi^{i_{1}}\cdots\varphi^{i_{n}}\rangle^{\phi}
, for
n\geq 2
. Those Feynman
rules obviously derive from the effective action in Eq. (
304
), hence the
statement is proven.

It can be noted that the Legendre transformation of the functional
W[J+J^{\prime}]
,
namely,
\varGamma[\phi^{\prime}]+J\cdot\phi^{\prime}
, also produces the correct Green
functions, albeit with different propagator and vertices; however, it contains
a non-vanishing one-point vertex. A resummation of those graphs to eliminate
the one-point vertex is directly provided by the effective action in Eq. (
304
).

Alternatively, the Theorem can be obtained by recursively applying
derivatives, starting from the two-point function:

\langle\varphi^{i}\varphi^{j}\rangle_{c}^{J}=\partial^{i}\partial^{j}W[J]=\frac{\partial\phi^{j}}{\partial J_{i}}.

(305)

By the symmetry
W\leftrightarrow-\varGamma
of the Legendre transformation, we
also have

-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varGamma[\phi]=\frac{\partial J_{i}}{\partial\phi^{j}}.

(306)

This proves

\langle\varphi^{i}\varphi^{j}\rangle^{\phi}_{c}=((-\partial^{2}\varGamma)^{-1})^{ij}
. Then for

n=3

\langle\varphi^{i}\varphi^{j}\varphi^{k}\rangle_{c}^{J}=\partial^{i}\langle\varphi^{j}\varphi^{k}\rangle_{c}^{J}=\partial^{i}\phi^{a}\partial_{a}((-\partial^{2}\varGamma)^{-1})^{ij}

(307)

produces the third equation in (
20
). The rule is that each

\partial^{a}=\partial/\partial J_{a}
generates a new leg from either a vertex
or a (external or internal) line, while each

\partial_{a}=\partial/\partial\phi^{a}
generates an amputated leg.
