Each generic polytope in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} has a point with ten normals to the boundary
Ivan Nasonov, Gaiane Panina

Abstract
. It is conjectured since long that each smooth convex body 𝐏 ⊂ ℝ n \mathbf{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} has a point in its interior which belongs to at least 2 ​ n 2n normals from different points on the boundary of 𝐏 \mathbf{P} . The conjecture is proven for n = 2 , 3 , 4 n=2,3,4 . We treat an analogous problem for convex polytopes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} and prove that each generic polytope has a point in its interior with at least 10 10 normals to the boundary. This bound is exact: there exists a tetrahedron with no more than 10 10 normals emanating from a point in its interior. The proof is based on piecewise linear analog of Morse theory, analysis of bifurcations, and some combinatorial tricks.



1.
Introduction

It is conjectured since long that for any smooth convex body
\mathbf{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists a point in its interior which belongs to at least
2n
normals from different points on the boundary of
\mathbf{P}

[

[

So, each

6

For

[
6
]
, and (to the best of our knowledge) nothing is known for higher dimensions.

In this paper we solve a similar problem for compact convex polytopes with non-empty interior.

Each generic

Three necessary remarks are:

•

In the smooth case the bases of the normals to
\partial\mathbf{P}
emanating from

are the critical points of the
squared distance function

SQD_{y}(x)=|x-y|^{2}:\partial\mathbf{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Generically,

is a Morse function, thus Morse theory gives a tool.
With some efforts, Morse-theoretic machinery extends also to polytopes
[
5
]
. So counting normals to the boundary is equivalent to counting critical points of

.

•

Each polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has a point with at least
8
normals to the boundary. This follows straightforwardly from Morse counts, see

]
: let

be the center of the sphere of maximal radius contained in
\mathbf{P}
. Then the function

has at least four local minima (the tangent points) and at least one maximum, therefore at least three saddles.

•

The bound in Theorem

is exact: there exists a tetrahedron with no more than
10
normals from a point in its interior
[
5
]
.

The
main idea in a nutshell
looks like this: (1) take a very special point
y
in the polytope
\mathbf{P}
with some guaranteed number of emanating normals. (2) Force
y
to move along a carefully chosen straightline trajectory. At bifurcation moments od the movement, the emanating normals born or die. Analyze this birth-death process.

We use this idea twice: in Proposition
1
, where
y
travels along a special ray emanating from a vertex of
\mathbf{P}
, and in the proof of the main theorem, where
y
travels along a special (saddle-type) binormal of
\mathbf{P}
.

Finally, a combinatorial trick puts the pieces together.

2.1.
Active regions, bifurcation set and its sheets

Let
\mathbf{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

Let

contains the base of some normal emanating from

The

\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})
is the union of the boundaries of active regions. It is the piecewise linear counterpart of the focal set that appears in the smooth case.

The bifurcation set is piecewise linear. Its parts not lying on
\partial\mathbf{P}
(

) fall into two types, red and blue, see Fig.
1
.

That is,

(1)

The active region of a face

is the intersection of the infinite right angled prism based on
F
and the polytope
\mathbf{P}
.

The side faces of the prism are called
blue sheets
of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})
.

(2)

The active region of an edge
E
sharing two faces
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
is the intersection of the polytope
\mathbf{P}
with the wedge-like polyhedron bounded by
two planes containing
E
and orthogonal to
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
(these are blue sheets), and another two planes containing the endpoints of

and orthogonal to

(

).

(3)

The active region of a vertex

is the inner normal cone of
\mathbf{P}
at the vertex
V
(bounded by red sheets) intersected with the polytope
\mathbf{P}
.

Although the function
SQD_{y}
is non-smooth,
it can be treated as a Morse function. So counting critical points of
SQD_{y}
amounts to counting normals, exactly as it is in the smooth case.

Lemma 1
.

For
y\notin\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})
, we have:

(1)

Local maxima of
SQD_{y}
are attained at some vertices of
\mathbf{P}
.

(2)

Local minima of
SQD_{y}
are attained at some faces of
\mathbf{P}
.

(3)

Saddles of
SQD_{y}
are attained at some edges of
\mathbf{P}
.

Convention:
from now on, we call local maxima (local minima) just maxima and minima for short.

Lemma 2
.

If
y
crosses transversally one sheet of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})
, a pair of critical points of
SQD_{y}
either dies or is born.

(1)

Crossing a blue sheet amounts to birth-death of a minimum and a saddle.

(2)

Crossing a red sheet amounts to birth-death of a maximum and a saddle.

We say that a point

projects
to a face

(of

(or of the edge

An edge of a polytope with an acute dihedral angle is called an

.

Lemma 3
.

(1)

A point

projects to a face iff the face contributes a minimum of
SQD_{y}
.

(2)

Let
e
be an acute edge of
\mathbf{P}
. Then a point

projects to

SQD_{y}
attains a saddle at the edge

.

Lemma 4
.

For a convex polytope
\mathbf{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a point
y\notin\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})
, the number of critical points of

(equivalently, the number of normals emanating from

2+2s
,
where

is the number of saddles.

In particular, the number of normals emanating from

In the paper we restrict ourselves to
generic polytopes
:

Definition 2
.

A polytope
\mathbf{P}
with the vertex set
\mathbf{V}
is generic if affine hulls of any two (two-element or three-element) subsets of

\mathbf{V}
are neither parallel nor orthogonal, unless this is dictated by unavoidable reasons. For example,

i:

No two edges are orthogonal.

ii:

No edge is parallel to a face, unless they are incident.

Important observations are: (1) For a generic polytope, the sheets of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})
intersect transversally.
(2) Any polytope can be turned to a generic one by a close to
id
projective transform. (3) If
\mathbf{P}
is generic, then any sheet of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{P})

related to a face contains no vertices of
\mathbf{P}
except for vertices of the face.

Definition 6
.

A bigon (or a lune) is a spherical polygon with two edges. It is bounded by two halves of big circles.
The minimal bigon
Big(P)
of a spherical polygon
P
is the bigon containing
P
which is minimal by inclusion, or, equivalently, with the minimal angle between its edges.

Lemma 10
.

P
intersects each edge of its minimal bigon
Big(P)
by a segment whose interior contains the midpoint of the edge, see Fig
4
.

Proof.

Denote the edges of
Big(P)
by
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
, and their midpoints by
R
and
T
.
Firstly, prove that
R
and
T
lie on
\partial P
. Indeed, if
R\notin\partial P
a rotation of
l_{1}
decreases the bigon.

Figure 4.
P
intersects
Big(P)
by two segments, containing the midpoints
R
and
T
.

Next, prove that
P
intersects each edge of
Big(P)
by a segment. If not, two cases are possible:

(1)
P
intersects each of the edges by a point. Then a rotation decreases
Big(P)
.

(2)
P
intersects one edge by a point, and the other edge by a segment. Then necessarily the intersection point is the midpoint of the edge, say,
R
, and also the intersection segment contains
T
. Let us show that
P
is not skew. Consider the segment
RT
; it lies in
P
and cuts the bigon into two equal halves. Let
X
be the midpoint of
RT
, then
X\in Core(P)
and
SQD^{sph}_{X}
has a short maximum at
R
and a short minimum at
T
. Since
|XR|=|XT|
,
SQD^{sph}_{X}
has additionally at least two short maxima lying in different halves of bigon. Altogether we have 3 short maxima. A contradiction.

∎

Corollary 1
.

The midpoint
X
of
Big(P)
lies in
P
. Besides,
X\in Core(P)

Proposition 3
.

If
P
is skew, then
Big(P)
is acute-angled.

Proof.

Assume the contrary:
Big(P)
is obtuse-angled. Then all the angles of
P
are also obtuse (this follows from minimality).

The proof is based on two lemmata.

A spherical segment
AB
is
short
if
|AB|<\pi/2
. Otherwise, the segment is called
long
.

Definition 7
.

Let
A
be a vertex of
P
. Set
\mathcal{AR}^{spher}(A)
be the set of points

such that
A
is a short maximum of
SQD_{Y}^{spher}
.

If

Proof.

Let short edges

Lemma 12
.

If
P
is skew and
Big(P)
is obtuse-angled, then
P
has at most
4
vertices.

Proof.

The midline
RT
cuts the bigon into two halves. If
P
has at least 5 vertices, then by Lemma
11
there exists a long edge
AB
, contained in one half of the bigon.
For the midpoint
X
of the bigon, we have:
|XA|<\pi/2,|XB|<\pi/2,|AB|>\pi/2
. Then
\angle XAB
and
\angle XBA
are acute, so
X
projects on
AB
. Then
SQD^{sph}_{X}
has at least 3 minima. Since
X\in Core(P)
, the function
SQD^{sph}_{X}
has at least 3 short maxima, so
P
is not skew.

∎

It remains to analyze triangles and quadrilaterals.

(1)

P

(2)

Lemma 14

Let

Assume that

Lemma 15

|M_{1}M_{2}|>\pi/2

Proof.

Assume the contrary, that is,
|M_{1}M_{2}|<\pi/2
