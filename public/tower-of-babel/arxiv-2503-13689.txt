Exact statistical tests using integer programming: Leveraging an overlooked approach for maximizing power for differences between binomial proportions
Stef Baas

Abstract
Traditional hypothesis testing methods for differences in binomial proportions can either be too liberal (Wald test) or overly conservative (Fisher’s exact test), especially in small samples. Regulators favour conservative approaches for robust type I error control, though excessive conservatism may significantly reduce statistical power. We offer fundamental theoretical contributions that extend an approach proposed in 1969, resulting in the derivation of a family of exact tests designed to maximize a specific type of power. We establish theoretical guarantees for controlling type I error despite the discretization of the null parameter space. This theoretical advancement is supported by a comprehensive series of experiments to empirically quantify the power advantages compared to traditional hypothesis tests. The approach determines the rejection region through a binary decision for each outcome dataset and uses integer programming to find an optimal decision boundary that maximizes power subject to type I error constraints. Our analysis provides new theoretical properties and insights into this approach’s comparative advantages. When optimized for average power over all possible parameter configurations under the alternative, the method exhibits remarkable robustness, performing optimally or near-optimally across specific alternatives while maintaining exact type I error control. The method can be further customized for particular prior beliefs by using a weighted average. The findings highlight both the method’s practical utility and how techniques from combinatorial optimization can enhance statistical methodology.



Keywords
Average power, Berger and Boos’ procedure, Boschloo’s test, Fisher’s exact test, Knapsack problem, Maximin Power.

1
Introduction

Statistical tests for comparing response proportions in a randomized trial
are among the first concepts presented in statistics courses. They are also frequently encountered in practice, as binary outcome measures are very common in confirmatory two-arm superiority clinical trials, with a frequency of use of at least 28% reported in
Thompson et al. 2025
.

One of the most commonly used exact tests to compare response proportions is Fisher’s exact test
(
Fisher 1934
)
. Fisher’s exact test is a conditional test ensuring type I error control for each potential total sum of treatment successes. This property makes Fisher’s exact test a preferred option from a regulatory perspective, particularly when testing in small samples or close to the parameter boundaries (i.e., for proportions close to 0 or 1). Using a query, we found that around
25\%-30\%
(about 2000 analyses) of two-sample binary or categorical data analyses in clinical trials publicly reported on
https://clinicaltrials.gov
used Fisher’s exact test (see
Appendix A
). This value can be seen as a lower bound for the amount of studies that can benefit from the alternative tests presented in this paper.
However, Fisher’s exact test is also often viewed as (too) conservative(see, e.g., Section 3.5.6 of
Agresti 2002
) and consequently can be expected to be substantially less powerful than other tests. Another commonly used test for this problem is the asymptotic Z-test (or Wald test) which can yield higher power but also be (too) liberal (i.e., leading to type I error rate inflation), especially if using the Z-unpooled test in small samples
(
Eberhardt and Fligner 1977
)
.

Many tests exist for the two-sample binomial proportions hypothesis testing problem, however, which test has highest power depends on the configuration of success probabilities and treatment group sizes; no
test is guaranteed to have highest power out-of-the-box.
Mehrotra et al. 2003
show that a poor choice of test statistic in this setting can actually lead to a test with lower unconditional power than Fisher’s exact test.

In this paper, we propose an integer programming-based approach to construct unconditional exact one-sided two-sample tests for a difference in binomial proportions that ensure a maximum power measure of interest.
The underlying optimization problem can be classified as a knapsack optimization problem, where the value of items included in the knapsack is maximized, given that the combined weight of all included items does not exceed a threshold.
In our case, items packed correspond to datasets for which the test yields a rejection, the value of the knapsack equals power, and the weight of the knapsack equals type I error.
This integer programming approach to construct binomial tests is not new, and was, to the best of our knowledge, first proposed in
Paroush 1969
, who considered six formulations of the knapsack problem but did not consider evaluation of the corresponding type I error and power under these formulations.
The integer programming approach was also more recently considered for the two-sided test on proportions in
Keer 2023
.
We propose one-sided knapsack-based tests that maximize: (1) the average power, where the power is averaged over the parameter space under the alternative hypothesis and has an analytic expression, (2) a weighted average power, which could reflect prior beliefs of the experimenter or which tailors the test to an area covering a specific alternative hypothesis and (3) the minimum power over a set of parameters,
noting that this maximin approach is different from the maximin test considered in
Keer 2023
, who maximized the minimum deviation of the type I error rate from the significance level.

Our theoretical contributions to the development of this optimization-based approach to construct exact tests are of crucial importance to facilitating wider uptake in practice. First, we propose a method to construct a (non-trivial) p-value based on this test and substantiate the approach by theoretical results from the literature. Similarly to the approach sketched in Section 4.3.2.2 of
Keer 2023
, our strategy is to explicitly enforce the nesting of rejection regions when iterating over different possible p-values. Second, for the one-sided case we make use of an analytic expression to compute average power exactly, and furthermore enforce Barnard’s convexity condition. Lastly, we provide a detailed analysis of the type I error rate and power profiles for the approach, considering both balanced and unbalanced treatment group size configurations, and comparing the approach with state-of-the-art tests in the literature such as Berger and Boos’ modification of the pooled-z test and Boschloo’s test (which were not considered in
Keer 2023
).
Our paper presents comparisons for these tests for multiple treatment group configurations and up to a trial size of 100 participants and furthermore shows the performance of the knapsack-based approach for a clinical trial application involving 280 participants.

The current paper is organized as follows.
Section 2
describes the data model, hypotheses, and theoretical properties for the one-sided two-sample binomial testing problem.
Section 3
describes the proposed average power knapsack-based test, other (comparator) knapsack-based tests, and comparator unconditional exact tests.

Section 4
provides a detailed type I error and power comparison of the considered tests.
Section 5
considers a case study based on the Merck Research Laboratories Trial, also considered in
Mehrotra et al. 2003
, where, next to the one-sided p-values under the considered tests, we also provide a detailed type I error and power comparison.
Section 6
provides a discussion and concludes the paper.

One-sided two-sample binomial exact testing problem

In this section, we formalize the one-sided two-sample binomial testing problem which formalizes the problem of comparing response proportions mentioned in
Section 1
. Let the tuple of unknown success probabilities be denoted by
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}=(\theta_{\text{C}},\theta_{\text{D}})
, where
C
denotes the control treatment and
D
the developmental (experimental) treatment. The same convention (i.e., first C then D ) will be used throughout the current paper
to construct tuples from variables for the control and developmental treatment.
Let
n_{\text{C}},n_{\text{D}}\in\mathbb{N}
be the fixed and known treatment group sizes, and
n=n_{\text{C}}+n_{\text{D}}
be the trial size. The total sums of treatment successes
\bm{S}=(S_{\text{C}},S_{\text{D}})
have a binomial distribution:

\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(\bm{S}=\bm{s})=\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\binom{n_{a}}{s_{a}}\theta_{a}^{s_{a}}(1-\theta_{a})^{n_{a}-s_{a}}.

(1)

The one-sided two-sample binomial testing problem involves testing

H_{0}:\theta_{\text{D}}\leq g_{0}(\theta_{\text{C}})\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\theta_{\text{D}}>g_{1}(\theta_{\text{C}}),

(2)

where
g_{h}:[0,1]\mapsto[0,1]
is an increasing function for each
h
. For instance, superiority testing in terms of the absolute difference in success rates corresponds to
g_{0}(\theta)=g_{1}(\theta)=\theta+\delta_{1}
for
\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
, and testing superiority in terms of relative risk, i.e.,
(1-\theta_{\text{C}})/(1-\theta_{\text{D}})\geq\delta_{2}
corresponds to
g_{0}(\theta)=g_{1}(\theta)=1-(1-\theta)/\delta_{2}

for
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
. We have
g_{0}=g_{1}
in the above two examples, but these functions can also be chosen differently such that the union of the parameter space under the null and alternative hypotheses does not equal
[0,1]^{2}.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}:\bm{s}\leq\bm{n}\}
be the set of possible realisations of successes per arm in the experiment
and
d:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\{0,1\}
be a non-randomized test decision function, indicating the outcome of the test. We then restrict to (unconditional) exact tests for
H_{0}
in the following, i.e., consider
d
such that for a significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
and a set
\Theta_{0}
of parameters satisfying the null hypothesis
H_{0}
we have
\max_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d(\bm{S})=1)\leq\alpha.

Letting
\Theta_{1}
be the parameter range under
H_{1}
, the one-sided two-sample binomial exact testing problem now amounts to finding
d
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d(\bm{S})=1)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d^{\prime}(\bm{S})=1),\quad

\displaystyle\forall\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\Theta_{1},d^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{S}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d(\bm{S})=1)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\Theta_{0}.

The above problem has no solution in case the problem of maximizing
\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d(\bm{S})=1)
for fixed
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\Theta_{1}
under the constraint
\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d(\bm{S})=1)\leq\alpha,\quad\forall\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\Theta_{0}
yields different solutions for different
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in\Theta_{1}
, hence the need to evaluate different decision functions
d.

Remark 1

(Numerical calculation of rejection rate)
.

As also indicated in, e.g.,
Mehrotra et al. 2003

for a particular decision function. Namely, given a decision function
d
the rejection rate can be calculated as:

\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(d(\bm{S})=1)=\sum_{\bm{s}:d(\bm{s})=1}\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\binom{n_{a}}{s_{a}}\theta_{a}^{s_{a}}(1-\theta_{a})^{n_{a}-s_{a}},

or, letting
\bm{d}
be the vectorization of

,
\bm{g}
be the vectorization of

, and
\bm{p}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}
be the vectorization of

with

d
(parameters
\mathbf{\theta}
) can be evaluated for fixed
\bm{g}\circ\bm{p}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}
(

).

Barnard’s convexity constraint

We show that under the assumed model we can restrict to the boundary of the parameter range under both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}

Let
\partial\bm{s}_{\text{C}}=(1,0)

and

(3)

be the boundary of the parameter range under hypothesis

Remark 3

(Exact type I error control)
.

When formulating the knapsack tests above, it was assumed that type I error control in the finite set

is sufficient, note that under this requirement, following

\tilde{\Theta}_{0}=\cup_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}}\{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in[0,1]^{2}:\theta_{\text{C}}\geq\theta_{\text{C},i,0}\text{ and }\theta_{\text{D}}\leq\theta_{\text{D},i,0}\}\subset\Theta_{0}.

If, however, type I error control for the total set
\Theta_{0}
is desired, this can be attained using a Lipschitz bound.
In the setting of (

\{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}_{i,0}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}\}

can be mapped to a sequence of increasing null control success rates

and
\theta_{|\mathcal{I}_{0}|,\text{C}}=1

From

3.3
Knapsack-based p-values

How to obtain a p-value from a knapsack-based test is not immediately clear, and multiple methods can be proposed.
One way to construct a p-value would be to use the approach in
Röhmel and Mansmann 1999
for the decision vector
\bm{d}
, however, this would lead to only two possible p-values per outcome vector: one, and the maximum type I error rate that follows from
\bm{d}
.
Another way to obtain a p-value for all vectors of total successes
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}
while ensuring optimal power for one significance level
\alpha
is to (1) solve the APK problem for significance level
\alpha
, (2) choose a set of potential p-values
\mathcal{P}
with
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
and (3) in case
d^{\alpha}_{\bm{s}}=0
solve the APK problem for sequentially increasing
\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, until
d^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{\bm{s}}=1
where we furthermore impose
\bm{d}^{\alpha}\leq\bm{d}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
or in case
d^{\alpha}_{\bm{s}}=1
solve the APK problem for sequentially decreasing
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, until
d^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{\bm{s}}=0
where we furthermore impose
\bm{d}^{\alpha}\geq\bm{d}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. In the former case, the p-value is
\alpha^{\prime}
while in the latter case the p-value is the last significance level
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}
where
d^{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}_{\bm{s}}=1
(i.e. the first value in
\mathcal{P}
higher than
\alpha^{\prime}
).
Letting
p_{\bm{s}}
be the p-value for each outcome vector
\bm{s}
obtained in this manner, it can be verified that
\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}_{0,i}}(p_{\bm{S}}\leq\tilde{\alpha})\leq\tilde{\alpha}
for all
\tilde{\alpha}\in(0,1)
,
i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
. The test that rejects when
p_{\bm{s}}\leq\alpha
equals the APK test determined in step (1) above and hence has the same optimality guarantees, while tests rejecting when
p_{\bm{s}}\leq\alpha^{\prime}
for
\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha
can have lower average power than the APK test based on
\alpha^{\prime}
.

Comparators: Other (non-knapsack-based) unconditional exact tests

where for
\theta\in(0,1)

\text{T}_{\text{FE}}(\bm{s})=\sum_{\bm{s}^{\prime}:s^{\prime}_{\text{D}}\geq s_{\text{D}}}\binom{n_{\text{C}}}{s^{\prime}_{\text{C}}}\binom{n_{\text{D}}}{s^{\prime}_{\text{D}}}/\binom{n}{s_{\text{C}}+s_{\text{D}}}.

The summed terms in the above equation correspond to the (hypergeometric) distribution of
S_{\text{D}}\mid(S_{\text{C}}+S_{\text{D}})
, hence
\text{T}_{\text{FE}}(\bm{s})
is the probability that
S_{\text{D}}\geq s_{\text{D}}
given
S_{\text{C}}+S_{\text{D}}=s_{\text{C}}+s_{\text{D}}
.

In addition to FET, we include one-sided variants of Berger and Boos’
(
Berger and Boos 1994
)
version of Boschloo’s test (denoted B
∗
test) and Berger and Boos’ version of the Z-pooled exact unconditional test (denoted
\text{Z}_{\text{P}}^{*}
test). These tests are as they appear in
Mehrotra et al. 2003
, where the B
∗
test is based on the one-sided FE test as defined above, and the
\text{Z}_{\text{P}}^{*}
test is defined based on the one-sided Z-pooled statistic in favour of the developmental treatment.
The unconditional p-value for the B
∗
and
\text{Z}_{\text{P}}^{*}
tests is found by the simplicial homology global optimisation algorithm as implemented in the
SciPy

Python
package (see the
SciPy shgo documentation
), this algorithm is also used to compute Boschloo’s test in
Python
.

We note here that we have also implemented and evaluated the results for the unmodified version of Boschloo’s (B) test and the unconditional Z-pooled (
\text{Z}_{\text{P}}
) test from
Mehrotra et al. 2003
. As we saw that Berger and Boos’ modification greatly balances the performance of these tests for unbalanced treatment group sizes, sometimes leading to power gains of 30% while not leading to substantial power losses in comparison to the B and
\text{Z}_{\text{P}}
tests, we have chosen to only report results for the B
∗
and
\text{Z}^{*}_{\text{P}}
tests.

4
Comparison of tests

In this section, we compare the FE, B
∗
, Z
{}_{\text{P}}^{*}
, MPK and APK tests, looking at a significance level
\alpha
of 2.5%. For the B
∗
and Z
{}_{\text{P}}^{*}
test we set
\gamma=0.0005
(half the value used in
Mehrotra et al. 2003
, as we are doing one-sided testing).
We consider trial sizes
n\in\{20,50,100\}
, for the MPK test let
\tilde{\Theta}_{1,n}=\{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}:\theta_{\text{D}}=\theta_{\text{C}}+\delta_{n},\;\theta_{\text{C}}\in\{0,0.01,\dots,1-\delta_{n}\}\}

for
\delta_{20}=0.5,

\delta_{50}=0.25
and
\delta_{100}=0.15
, and set
\partial\tilde{\Theta}_{0}=\{0.00,0.01,\dots,1.00\}
for the knapsack tests.
The WAPK test was excluded from the comparison as the included tests will be compared based on their general performance across the parameter space for
H_{1}
, whereas the WAPK test can be used to tune the test to a specific belief on this parameter space.

A first thing of note about

\alpha

\gamma

\alpha

The type I error rate profile for the APK test has a more stable and smooth behaviour than the one for the MPK test. When the imbalance in treatment group sizes is larger the type I error rate profiles become more asymmetrical around
0.5

5
Case study: Merck Research Laboratories trial

In this section, we analyze the trial by Merck Research Laboratories described in Section 3.1 of
Mehrotra et al. 2003
. In order to make the trial setup more consistent with the rest of the current paper, we assume for this example that treatment group 1 (consisting of 132 participants) was the developmental group, while treatment group 2 (consisting of 148 participants) was the control treatment group.
Furthermore, the goal of the trial is reformulated to testing whether the proportion of trial participants without a specific type of rash is higher in the developmental treatment group than in the control treatment group.
The observed proportions of participants that experienced no rash were
\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}=140/148
and
\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}=131/132
.

In addition to the tests included in the previous section (where we set
\delta_{280}=0.1
to determine
\tilde{\Theta}_{1}
for the MPK test), to investigate power gains when more information about the alternative hypothesis is used, we now add the WAPK test (
Subsection 3.2
) with
\mathbb{Q}
an independent beta distribution for both arms with
\alpha_{\text{C}}=140
,
\beta_{\text{C}}=8,\alpha_{\text{D}}=131
,
\beta_{\text{D}}=1
.
We also add a second version of the MPK test, denoted MPK2, which maximizes the minimum power over 100 equidistant values of
\theta_{\text{C}}
in the 95% Clopper-Pearson interval based on 140 successes out of 148 trials (see, e.g.,

Discussion

In this paper, we refined an integer programming approach to construct power-maximizing unconditional one-sided exact tests for differences in binary proportions.
Our numerical evaluation indicates that the use of the average power knapsack test, seen to provide a stable performance across the parameter space, often outperforms Berger and Boos’ version of both Boschloo’s test and the Z-pooled test in terms of unconditional power. Based on our case study (where we considered using prior knowledge about the parameters), the average power knapsack test again might be preferred as the other tests did not provide substantial and consistent power gains for parameter values close to those assumed. When considering the p-values for the actual realisation of successes, the weighted average and simple hypothesis knapsack tests showed a higher degree of significance and based on the degree of certainty about the true parameter configuration, one of the latter tests might be preferred.

In terms of unconditional power Fisher’s exact test was often outperformed by the other tests in our evaluation, and hence is the worst-performing test in terms of this measure. Fisher’s exact test is the most powerful non-randomized conditional exact test
(
Ripamonti et al. 2017
)
and should hence be preferred in cases where conditional inference is desired.
In the current paper, we did not aim to contribute to or take sides in the discussion of conditional versus unconditional inference (see, e.g.,
Ripamonti et al. 2017
for a recent discussion), but
aimed to investigate which test has the higher unconditional power, thus complementing the work by
Mehrotra et al. 2003
.
We furthermore note here that randomized tests, where the test decision can be based on a biased coin toss, have been shown to yield higher power in theory but were not considered in this paper as they are currently not being used in practice, particularly for confirmatory clinical trials.

The proposed knapsack test maximizes average power for the one-sided setting. The average power measure has been argued for as an evaluation measure in the literature:
Andrés and Mato 1994
, using the name “long term power”, argue that in the long term, each parameter configuration will be encountered an equal amount of times on average, while also stating (in a similar vein to
Rice 1988
who also argued for the weighted average approach) that the measure makes sense when any value for them is deemed equally likely. For the two-sided two-sample binomial testing problem,
Andrés and Mato 1994
show that the average power is proportional to the number of points in the critical region, hence the packing problem (Eqn. (4.20)) in
Keer 2023
optimizes average power for the two-sided case.
Andrés and Mato 1994
show that Barnard’s CSM test also maximizes average two-sided power under Barnard’s convexity and symmetry constraints while the one-sided average power takes on a more difficult form, hence maximization of the latter power measure using an integer programming approach is more straightforward (and flexible) than trough a construction similar to Barnard’s CSM test.

One downside of the integer programming approach is the computation time: it was necessary to use the Gurobi optimization tool (which requires a license) to compute knapsack-based tests for trial sizes of 100 participants within a reasonable time.
This is partially why we do not consider trial sizes of 300 participants as in
Mehrotra et al. 2003
. Although solutions with reasonable optimality gaps can possibly be computed for such trial sizes, we also expect that the major gains of the knapsack-based tests are for smaller trial sizes up to around 100 participants.

Another downside of the integer programming approach is that the way to compute
p-values
is not immediately evident. We have proposed a method to construct p-values in the current paper, but these p-values only guarantee an optimal performance for a specific significance level.

Ripamonti et al. 2017
mention that modern (adaptive) clinical trial designs often rely on the combination of evidence from different arms, stages, and hypotheses, where p-values from multiple 2
\times
2 tables are combined through combination methods, multiple comparison adjustments, or the closed testing principle. As a more homogeneous quality of the p-value over different significance levels might be required, further research is needed to make the integer programming approach better suitable to such approaches. In such settings, when (part of) the amount of tests to be performed is known, one could also consider (the combination with) an approach similar to
Ristl et al. 2018
.

Several other areas are left for future research.
Keer 2023
considers the application to
r\times 2
tables, and it would be interesting to consider if a similar average power knapsack approach could be used in this setting and to apply the approach to other discrete distributions.
Gutman and Hochberg 2007
and
Ristl et al. 2018
use an integer programming approach to construct (optimal) rejection regions for intersection hypothesis tests based on multiple binary endpoints. It could be interesting to consider an unconditional exact approach that is more in line with ours, as
Gutman and Hochberg 2007
consider the combination of decisions of multiple two-sample integer programming tests, whereas
Ristl et al. 2018
consider a transformation of the multi-outcome setting to the extension of Fisher’s exact test to categorical data.
Furthermore, future research could focus on other estimands than the absolute difference in success rates.

Peer and Azriel 2024
constructed optimal confidence intervals based on an integer linear programming approach.
As the program used to construct confidence intervals in
Peer and Azriel 2024
took a large amount of computation time (the maximum trial size considered was 21 participants), it would be interesting to compare this approach to exact confidence intervals found by inverting the (average power) knapsack test of this paper, as our approach allows for larger trial sizes.
