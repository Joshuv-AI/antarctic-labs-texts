Antimicrobial treatment of egg-infested olives is associated with perturbation and carry-over changes on bacterial communities in the olive fly Bactrocera oleae (Diptera, Tephritidae)
Authors: Laura Maretto, Ivana Carofano, Isabel Martinez-Sañudo, Marco Falasco, Hannes Schuler, Markus Riegler, Jennifer Morrow, Luca Mazzon, Nicoletta Faraone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe olive fly Bactrocera oleae (Rossi) is one of the most destructive olive pests, threatening olive and olive oil production worldwide. Traditionally managed with synthetic insecticides, its control now requires alternative strategies due to regulatory restrictions. The disruption of the obligate association between the pest and its primary symbiont Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, Capuzzo et al. (2005) has been reported following the application of a Zn–Cu–citric acid bio-complex compound (Dentamet) to olives infested with newly laid eggs. In this study, the effects of Dentamet exposure on the composition and structure of the B. oleae bacterial community were evaluated by comparing flies from treated (Dentamet-treated [DT]) olives, with untreated (non-treated [NT]) olives under laboratory conditions, and olives collected from untreated orchards (wild-in-controlled-environment). Bacterial communities were characterized by 16S rRNA gene sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform, and quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR) was used to estimate the abundance of Ca. E. dacicola and 4 additional bacterial genera involved in detoxification and nutrient metabolism. Metabarcoding analyses revealed differences between DT and NT samples, including reduced bacterial diversity in DT larvae and a shift in the adult bacterial community structure supported by beta diversity analyses. qPCR analysis showed a lower abundance of Ca. E. dacicola in DT larvae (significant), pupae, and adults, alongside decreases in the 4 investigated bacterial genera. Overall, these findings suggest that Dentamet exposure is associated with changes not only in the primary symbiont but also in the broader bacterial community, with potential implications for key microbial interactions.Keywords: microbiota, dysbiosis, Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, Bactrocera oleae, IPM

Introduction

The olive fruit fly, Bactrocera oleae (Rossi) (Diptera, Tephritidae), is widely recognized as the most damaging pest affecting olive (Olea europaea L.) cultivation throughout the Mediterranean region, as well as in other olive-growing areas worldwide (Raspi and Viggiani 2008, Daane and Johnson 2010, Živković et al. 2025). This monophagous insect lays its eggs within the mesocarp of olive drupes, where larval development occurs through 3 instars. As the larvae feed internally, they compromise the integrity of the fruit, leading to premature drop, pulp degradation, and a subsequent oxidative deterioration of the resulting olive oil (Neuenschwander and Michelakis 1978, Malheiro et al. 2015, Taiti et al. 2025). The consequent economic losses are substantial and are often compounded by the degradation of key quality parameters in virgin olive oil, such as increased free acidity, elevated peroxide values, and reduced phenolic content (Gomez-Caravaca et al. 2008, Gucci et al. 2012, Stavrianakis et al. 2025).

Historically, the management of B. oleae infestations has relied heavily on broad-spectrum insecticides, among which the systemic organophosphate dimethoate was one of the most widely applied due to its efficacy and its relatively low persistence in olive oil (Cabras et al. 1997, Skouras et al. 2007, Kumral et al. 2020). However, growing awareness of its environmental and toxicological impact, coupled with regulatory changes, resulted in the ban of dimethoate in the European Union (EU Regulation 2020/1643) (Ortis et al. 2025). The lack of efficient insecticides resulted in an urgent need to explore alternative control options grounded in ecological sustainability and host-specific vulnerabilities.

The olive fruit fly harbors Candidatus Erwinia dacicola (hereafter Ca. E. dacicola), an obligate primary endosymbiotic bacterium that is vertically transmitted and located both in the midgut and within a specialized cephalic organ known as the esophageal bulb (EB). During oviposition, females release symbiont cells via contractile perianal glands and smear them onto the egg surface, thereby ensuring transgenerational transmission (Girolami 1973, Capuzzo et al. 2005, Bigiotti et al. 2021). This coordinated mechanism enables the establishment of the symbiosis early in larval development and highlights the essential role of Ca. E. dacicola in supporting B. oleae development, as it contributes to nitrogen assimilation and to the detoxification of phenolic compounds within the olive drupes, an environment inherently limited in available nitrogen and rich in phenolics (Ben-Yosef et al. 2015, Siden-Kiamos et al. 2022).

In addition to this primary symbiont, the olive fruit fly harbors gut microbial communities comprising taxa with metabolic, immunological, and ecological functions that contribute to host fitness and adaptation (Ben-Yosef et al. 2015, Blow et al. 2020). The integration of these symbiotic relationships into key physiological functions has positioned the olive fly’s microbiota as a central target for innovative pest control approaches. It is anticipated that insects that rely on environmentally acquired or egg-smeared symbionts are particularly vulnerable to interventions targeting early symbiont acquisition, thus providing a potential target for symbiotic control approaches (Gonella et al. 2020).

A recent study showed that impairing symbiont transmission in B. oleae by dipping egg-infested olives into antimicrobial agents led to significant reductions in symbiont load and corresponding decreases in larval development and pupation rates (Perin et al. 2023). Among the tested agents, a (4%)Zn–(2%)Cu–(23.8%) citric acid biocomplex, commercially available as Dentamet (Diachem, Italy), originally developed as a micronutrient EC foliar fertilizer in olive groves, has emerged as a particularly promising candidate. This formulation combines documented antimicrobial properties (Perin et al. 2023), which are likely attributable to the presence of copper ions, known for their broad-spectrum activity against bacterial membranes and metabolic processes, with demonstrated oviposition-deterrent effects against B. oleae (Checchia et al. 2022).

The present study was designed to investigate the impact of the Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex on the microbial community associated with B. oleae across all developmental stages. By integrating high-throughput 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding with quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR), we aimed to evaluate changes in bacterial community structure and symbiont abundance following exposure to this biocomplex. Particular attention was given to quantifying changes in the relative abundance of taxa within the core microbiota, as well as shifts in Ca. E. dacicola. To simulate realistic field conditions, the Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex was applied at concentrations and with an application method similar to standard open field treatments. Overall, our findings aim to deepen the current understanding of olive fruit fly-microbiota interactions and assess the prospects of microbiota-targeted interventions as part of integrated pest management frameworks for olive cultivation.

Material and Methods

Origin and Rearing of Olive Flies

Infested olives were manually collected from a pesticide-free olive grove located in Fossacesia (42°14′34.6″ N, 14°29′09.1″ E, Chieti, Abruzzo Region, Italy) in September 2023. The olives were transported to the laboratory of Entomology of the DAFNAE Department at the University od Padua to rear out adult flies from infested fruit. Newly emerged flies were transferred into cubical mesh cages (BugDorm, MegaView Science Co., Ltd., Taichung, Taiwan; side: 30 cm) to establish age-synchronized cohorts. The flies were fed a dry diet composed of sucrose and dried yeast extract (Sigma-Aldrich) in a 4:1 ratio, with water supplied via a sponge refreshed every 7 to 10 d.

Experimental Design

Three experimental groups were established, each representing a different condition affecting the gut microbiota (Fig. 1). The first group, referred to as the wild-in-controlled-environment (WCE), was derived from mature larvae obtained from olives collected in the Fossacesia field and subsequently maintained under microbiologically controlled laboratory conditions, following the procedures described by Capuzzo et al. (2005) and Mazzon et al. (2010). Briefly, mature larvae emerging from olives were surface-sterilized with sodium hypochlorite (NaClO 1%), rinsed with sterile water, and allowed to pupate in sterilized sand. Pupae were re-sterilized, rinsed, and individually placed in sterile vials until adult emergence. All emerging adults were then collected and preserved in 96% ethanol at −20 °C about 24 h after emergence, until further analysis. Obtaining adults from surface-sterilized pupae and keeping them in axenic conditions allowed us to minimize the risk of microbiota contamination by free-living environmental bacteria.

Experimental design illustrating the establishment of the 3 evaluated experimental groups. The wild-in-controlled-environment (WCE) group (F0 generation) was obtained from mature larvae emerging from field-collected infested olives, which were surface-sterilized and reared under microbiologically controlled laboratory conditions. Adults obtained from the same wild olives were used for oviposition to generate the F0 generation. After a 24-h oviposition period, infested olives were divided into 2 groups: non-treated (NT) and treated (DT). The DT fruits were treated with a Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex solution (5.47 ml/l, label-recommended dose) and dried under sterile conditions. Individuals from each experimental group were sampled at 3 developmental stages (larva, pupa, and adult) for downstream microbial community analyses.

The second group, designated as Dentamet-treated (DT), consisted of individuals reared from olives treated with Dentamet ([4%]Zn–[2%]Cu–[23.8%] citric acid biocomplex, hereafter Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex) 24 h after oviposition by lab-reared field individuals, while the third group, designated as non-treated (NT), comprised individuals that developed from untreated olives after oviposition by lab-reared field individuals (Fig. 1). For this, adult flies emerging from infested Fossacesia olives were allowed to oviposit in uninfested olives manually collected from an insecticide-free orchard in Cazzano di Tramigna (Verona, Veneto Region, Italy; 45°28′09.5″N, 11°12′37.5″E). Upon arrival at the laboratory, the fruits were carefully inspected to exclude any drupes showing signs of damage, decay, or pre-existing B. oleae infestation; moreover, a subset of olives was incubated as negative control to verify the absence of infestation. Then the selected olives were used as oviposition substrates for mature 10-d-old females. Exposure to oviposition lasted 24 h, after which 380 infested olives, showing a maximum of 3 oviposition punctures, confirmed under a stereomicroscope (Leica, Mannheim, Germany), were selected for the experiment. For the DT group, the 190 selected fruits were sprayed uniformly over the entire surface with the Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex at 5.47 ml/l concentration, which represents the label-recommended dose, until close to the point of runoff, then dried on mesh trays under a laminar flow hood at room temperature. Treated and untreated fruits were kept in separate containers according to the treatment and incubated at 20 to 25 °C to allow egg hatching and larval development.

Individuals from each group were sampled at 3 key life stages: third-instar larvae (10 d post-oviposition), pupae (13 d), and newly emerged adults (within 24 h after emergence). Sampled larvae and pupae were first rinsed with sterile distilled water, then stored in absolute ethanol, and preserved at −20 °C until further processing. From each adult, the esophageal bulb (EB) and midgut (G), representing distinct compartments of the digestive tract, were carefully dissected. All procedures were performed under a stereomicroscope in a laminar flow hood using sterile instruments. Prior to dissection, flies were immobilized by keeping flies in vials at 4 °C for 20 min, then surface-sterilized by rinsing in a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min, followed by at least 2 rinses in sterile water. For EB extraction, the head was separated from the body, and the EB was isolated by splitting the head capsule into 2 parts starting from the occipital foramen. For G extraction, the abdomen was opened, the whole intestine was extracted, and the G tract was obtained by sectioning between the cardiac valve and the Malpighian tubules. Each EB and G sample was preserved in absolute ethanol in a sterile microcentrifuge tube and stored at −80 °C.

Genomic DNA Extraction and 16S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from 36 samples, each consisting of an independent pool of 3 specimens. For each life stage (larva, pupa, adult-EB, adult-G) and experimental condition (WCE, DT, NT), 3 pooled samples were processed using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, DE), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA yield was evaluated using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, California, United States), paired with a Qubit 1× dsDNA High Sensitivity Quantification Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, California, United States).

Library preparation and sequencing of the v3 to v4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene were carried out by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). Briefly, the 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, California, United States), following the Illumina 16S rRNA gene Metagenomic Sequencing Library protocol. The v3 to v4 hypervariable region was targeted using the 341F and 805R universal primers (Herlemann et al. 2011). Sequencing was conducted on a MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, California, United States), generating paired-end reads suitable for downstream microbial community analyses.

Raw sequencing data were processed in R (v4.4.1, “Race for Your Life”) (R Core Team 2024) using the “dada2” package (v1.34.0) (Callahan et al. 2016). Initial quality inspection of both forward and reverse reads was carried out using the plotQualityProfile() function to visually assess base quality across read positions. Based on these profiles, forward and reverse reads were quality-filtered and truncated at 260 and 220 bp, respectively, using the filterAndTrim() function to remove low-quality tails and discard reads with ambiguous bases. The resulting high-quality reads were then dereplicated with derepFastq(), collapsing identical sequences into unique representatives while retaining abundance information. Denoising was performed with the dada() function, which applies a probabilistic error model to distinguish biological variations from sequencing, resulting in Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Denoised forward and reverse reads were subsequently merged using mergePairs(). To eliminate potential artifacts introduced during PCR amplifications, chimeric sequences were identified and removed using the removeBimeraDenovo() function. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the assignTaxonomy() function, which implements a naïve Bayesian classifier to compare ASVs against the SILVA SSU reference database (v138.2) (Quast et al. 2013, Callahan 2024). Following taxonomic annotation, ASVs assigned to chloroplast or mitochondrial origin were removed from the dataset to exclude non-bacterial sequences from downstream analyses.

To minimize bias introduced by uneven library sizes, Cumulative Sum Scaling normalization was performed prior to the calculation of alpha- and beta-diversity metrics. Subsequent processing of the ASV count matrix and corresponding taxonomic assignments was conducted in RStudio (v2025.05.0 + 496 “Mariposa Orchid”), using a combination of the tibble (Müller and Wickham 2023) and TaxaPhyloseq (McMurdie and Holmes 2013) packages for data structuring and manipulation. Microbial diversity analyses were performed with the MicrobiotaProcess package (Xu et al. 2023). Alpha diversity was assessed by calculating genus-level richness, Shannon diversity index, and Simpson’s evenness index. Beta diversity was assessed by calculating the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix visualized using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA), and, for compositional comparison, by calculating the Euclidean distance matrix derived from Centered Log-Ratio (CLR)-transformed data, visualized using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Prior to CLR transformation, zero values in the ASVs table were replaced using the Count Zero Multiplicative method using the zCompositions package (Palarea-Albaladejo and Martín-Fernández 2015). PCoA and PCA served as complementary ordination techniques to evaluate the consistency of group separation patterns across distance metrics. Differences in community composition among groups were statistically tested using PERMANOVA and ANOSIM, while group dispersion was evaluated with PERMDISP. When the global test indicated a significant overall effect, pairwise comparisons within groups were conducted, with P-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction, applied separately within each test and distance metric. All plots were generated using the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

To further investigate potential differences among microbiota across the 3 experimental conditions (WCE, DT, and NT), alpha and beta diversity analyses were also performed after excluding the sequences of the primary symbiont Ca. E. dacicola. This approach allowed a more detailed assessment of shifts within the lower-abundance bacterial community.

The number of independent biological replicates was limited by sample availability; therefore, the analyses were conducted with a relatively small sample size, potentially affecting statistical power. This aspect was taken into account when interpreting the results.

Quantification of Core Bacterial Genera

To quantify bacterial abundance, quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR) assays were performed on the same genomic DNA using a QuantStudio 6 platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, California, United States). These assays targeted the primary symbiont Ca. E. dacicola and 4 bacterial genera, Acinetobacter, Bacillus, Corynebacterium, and Flavobacterium that were found within the microbiota of B. oleae analyzed in this study (see Results below). Primer pairs for these 4 genera were designed in NCBI Primer-BLAST (Ye et al. 2012) using sequences derived from the metabarcoding dataset generated in this study (Supplementary Table S1) and were subsequently tested in silico to ensure specificity and to exclude any potential off-target amplification.

Every reaction mix was set up in a 10 µl final reaction volume, using 5 µl of PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, California, United States), 300 nM of each forward and reverse primer, and 2 µl of gDNA. Primer specificity for the newly designed assays was tested experimentally through melt curve analysis (60 to 95 °C, 0.5 °C increments) to confirm the presence of a single amplification product. Amplification efficiency for each of the 4 primer assays was determined using a 6-point (Acinetobacter and Flavobacterium) or a 7-point (Bacillus and Corynebacterium) 10-fold serial dilution series of gDNA. Amplification efficiency was calculated from the slope of the resulting standard curve as E = 10^(−1/slope) − 1. All 4 assays showed amplification efficiencies within the acceptable range of 90% to 110% (Supplementary Table S1). Relative quantification of the primary symbiont and the 4 targeted bacterial genera was performed using the 2−ΔΔCq method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001) and the β-actin gene of B. oleae as the reference gene (Livadaras et al. 2021). Non-template controls were included in each qPCR run to exclude contamination.

All statistical analyses were performed in RStudio using the dplyr package (Wickham et al. 2023). Significant differences among group means were evaluated using either one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD test or the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction, depending on the data distribution.

Results

Composition and Diversity of the B. oleae Microbiota

The metabarcoding analysis of the 16S rRNA gene yielded a total of 5,675,032 paired-end reads. After quality filtering and denoising with the dada2 pipeline, 5,224,252 high-quality paired-end reads, with an average merged sequence length of 409 bp, were retained for downstream analyses. The 154 identified ASVs were classified into 87 reference-based bacterial taxa. Overall, 98.1% of the annotated sequences were classified at phylum level, 96.8% at class level, 96.1% at order level, 90.3% at family level, and 89.6% at genus level, at which all subsequent analyses were conducted to maximize the taxonomic resolution for this dataset. To explore whether these taxonomic differences translated into broader patterns of microbial community structure and diversity, we assessed alpha and beta diversity metrics across developmental stages and treatments. Patterns in alpha (Supplementary Table S2) and beta diversity analyses showed that the gut microbiota of B. oleae responded to the Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex exposure in a stage-dependent manner.

Alpha diversity metrics in third-instar larvae revealed significant differences in genera richness (P = 0.017; Fig. 2A) and Simpson’s evenness (1/D × S) (P = 0.021; Fig. 2B) across DT, NT, and WCE groups. Genera richness was lowest in the DT group (2.33 ± 0.333), followed by the NT group (4.67 ± 1.20), and highest in WCE specimens (7.00 ± 0.577). In contrast, the evenness index was highest in the DT group (0.463 ± 0.066), decreased in the NT group (0.258 ± 0.066), and was lowest in the WCE group (0.159 ± 0.024). The Shannon diversity index (H′) did not differ among the groups, but showed a similar trend to genera richness, with the DT group showing the lowest value and the WCE group showing the highest value. In contrast, at the third-instar larval stage, no significant differences were observed in the beta diversity (PERMDISP P = 0.459, PERMANOVA P = 0.196). Both PCA and PCoA showed a visual trend toward tighter clustering of WCE specimens relative to a broader spread of DT and NT specimens (Fig. 3A and B); however, this pattern was not statistically supported. At the pupal stage, no significant differences in alpha diversity were observed across treatments. Similar to alpha diversity, beta diversity analyses revealed no significant differences across the 3 experimental conditions (PERMDISP P = 0.148 and PERMANOVA P = 0.245). At the adult stage, no significant differences were observed across richness or diversity indices, although a significant difference in sample dispersion was detected (PERMDISP P = 0.038) (Fig. 4A and B), with ordination analyses showing a broader spread of the DT specimens relative to a tighter cluster formed by the NT group.

To further explore the differences among the microbiota associated with the experimental conditions, sequences assigned to Ca. E. dacicola, accounting for 97.8% of the total read counts in our dataset, were excluded from the analysis. At the larval stage, the significant differences in genera richness (P = 0.017) remained evident across the groups (Fig. 2C), with WCE specimens exhibiting the highest level of richness (6.00 ± 0.577), followed by NT (3.67 ± 1.20) and DT specimens (1.33 ± 0.333). Shannon diversity, Simpson’s evenness (Fig. 2D), and beta diversity metrics all indicated no significant differences in community structure (Fig. 3C and D, PERMDISP P = 0.770, PERMANOVA P = 0.473). At the pupal stage, both alpha (Supplementary Table S2) and beta diversity metrics (PERMDISP P = 0.675, PERMANOVA P = 0.183) remained non-significant across the experimental conditions. In adult specimens, alpha diversity indices did not reveal significant differences across treatments. Notably, beta diversity metrics uncovered significant differences in community composition (Fig. 4C and D, PERMANOVA P = 0.013), with ordination plots showing a more scattered DT group and a more tightly clustered NT group, reiterating patterns observed in the presence of the primary symbiont.

Although alpha and beta diversity metrics did not consistently yield significant differences within individual developmental stages and experimental conditions, the analysis of relative abundances of bacterial taxa revealed compositional shifts across treatments at all life stages (Supplementary Fig. S1). A closer examination of relative abundance patterns showed that, at the larval stage (Supplementary Fig. S1A), the microbiota of laboratory-reared specimens was largely represented by Rosenbergiella, which accounted for 97% of the relative abundance in DT larvae and 99.6% in NT larvae. In contrast, WCE larvae were primarily associated with Enterobacter (73.9%) and Sphingobium (17.3%). At the pupal stage (Supplementary Fig. S1B), DT specimens were characterized by Pseudomonas (29.8%), Bradyrhizobium (21.5%), and Ligilactobacillus (17.2%). NT pupae showed a marked predominance of Rosenbergiella (96.4%), whereas WCE pupae, by comparison, displayed a more balanced composition, with Pantoea (28.5%), Rosenbergiella (28.4%), and Methylophilus (17%) as the most abundant genera. In adults (Supplementary Fig. S1C), Rosenbergiella accounted for 88% of the relative abundance in the DT specimens, while NT adults' relative abundance was mainly composed of Staphylococcus (34%) and Paucibacter (10.4%). In WCE adults, the most abundant genera were Pseudomonas (12.7%), Micrococcus (11.9%), and Streptococcus (10.4%). Tissue-specific analyses further revealed distinct compositional profiles (Supplementary Fig. S1D). In the EB, DT specimens were characterized by Rosenbergiella (84%), NT specimens were primarily associated with Prevotella (23.3%) and Staphylococcus (18.4%), and WCE specimens showed higher relative abundances of Micrococcus (28.9%) and Pseudomonas (20.2%). In the midgut, DT specimens were predominantly composed of Rosenbergiella (89.4%), NT specimens were characterized by Staphylococcus (42.9%) and Paucibacter (16.4%), while WCE specimens were mainly associated with Escherichia–Shigella (16.4%), Sutterella (14.9%), and Staphylococcus (13.9%).

At the pupal stage, the treatment resulted in a significant decrease in the log fold change of Bacillus and Flavobacterium, while no significant changes were observed for Acinetobacter and Corynebacterium. Discussion

The use of antimicrobial compounds to disrupt host–microbe associations has been previously investigated as a strategy to control several insect pests, such as the stink bugs Halyomorpha halys and Nezara viridula (Gonella et al. 2022, Gonella and Alma 2023, Prieto et al. 2023), as well as B. oleae (Perin et al. 2023). In this context, our study provides new insights into the microbiota-level effects of the Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex on B. oleae within the developmental stages of flies emerged from treated olives infested with eggs, as well as new evidence on bacterial taxa potentially capable of persisting through metamorphosis.

Effects of Antimicrobial on Olive Fly Microbiota

The significant reduction in bacterial diversity observed in treated (DT) larvae, with genus richness almost 50% lower than in NT laboratory-reared group, is consistent with a treatment-associated shift in the bacterial community. This reduction in microbial richness aligns with previous reports showing that metal-based treatments can disrupt olive fly gut microbial communities, with consequent effects on host health and development (Bigiotti et al. 2019, Sinno et al. 2020, Malandrakis et al. 2024). Although the differences in Shannon diversity were not statistically significant, DT larvae showed a trend toward reduced overall bacterial complexity. Moreover, the higher evenness observed in the DT larvae indicates a shift towards a more homogenized community dominated by fewer taxa, possibly due to selective survival under Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex exposure. The absence of significant differences in alpha and beta diversity analyses at the pupal stage may indicate a stage-dependent attenuation of treatment effects, though it could also reflect the limited statistical power derived from the small sample size used in this study, which may constrain the ability to detect subtle community shifts. While stage-dependent microbiome resilience has been documented in other insects (Chouaia et al. 2019, Hammer and Moran 2019, Mason et al. 2023), disentangling genuine biological recovery from statistical detection limits remains beyond the resolution of the present dataset. Even though beta diversity did not consistently show significant treatment effects within individual developmental stages, the significant difference in dispersion observed in adults (PERMDISP P = 0.038) suggests that the early life-stage Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex exposure may influence the composition and stability of the gut microbial community. These changes seem to involve primarily the lower-abundant taxa, pointing to a stage-dependent pattern of compositional change.

Effects of Antimicrobial on Olive Fly Microbiota Beyond the Primary Symbiont

Analyses of insect microbiota face a challenge arising from dominant bacterial symbionts that can overshadow the detection and analysis of less abundant microbial community members. This masking effect occurs when primary endosymbionts or highly prevalent facultative symbionts dominate sequencing datasets, thereby obscuring ecologically relevant but rare bacterial taxa (Schrieke et al. 2022). Such dominance has been widely documented across insect taxa. For example, in scuttle flies (Phoridae), bacterial loads varied by up to 4 orders of magnitude between individuals, with facultative symbionts such as Wolbachia and Rickettsia maintaining consistently high prevalence and abundance (Nowak et al. 2025). In Diptera, mosquito microbiomes are often dominated by Wolbachia or Asaia, resulting in rare bacterial taxa being frequently overlooked unless more refined analytical methods are applied (Schrieke et al. 2022). Our study provides a parallel case in B. oleae, where the primary symbiont Ca. E. dacicola accounted for 97.8% of the total sequences. Therefore, removing the overwhelmingly abundant primary symbiont enabled the detection of lower-abundance taxa that would otherwise remain obscured. This approach revealed that richness and compositional changes within the lower-abundance community were still detectable, suggesting that the antimicrobial treatment of infested olives is associated with shifts in the microbiota beyond those observed for the primary symbiont, potentially involving other significant core taxa as well.

Building on these refined analyses, the relative abundance data provided a more detailed view of the taxa contributing to the differences between laboratory-reared and WCE specimens, which align with established patterns in the literature (Blow et al. 2020). It should be highlighted that the bacterial taxa characterizing WCE specimens were most likely acquired prior to surface sterilization and axenic rearing, and therefore reflect a field-derived microbial legacy rather than an effect of the experimental condition itself. As such, WCE specimens cannot be considered as a wild control equivalent to NT, and the differences observed between WCE and laboratory-reared groups are attributable to the combined influence of field origin and axenic rearing history. The predominance of Rosenbergiella in laboratory-reared larvae (97% to 99.6%) contrasts with the diverse Enterobacteriaceae assemblage observed in WCE larvae, dominated by Enterobacter (73.9%). This is consistent with previous reports showing that wild B. oleae populations harbor diverse facultative Enterobacteriaceae, whereas laboratory conditions tend to favor simplified communities (Koskinioti et al. 2019, Blow et al. 2020, De Cock et al. 2020). The pupal stage further underscores developmental continuity within the microbiota, with taxa such as Pseudomonas and Pantoea, previously documented in adult B. oleae (Koskinioti et al. 2019), persisting across metamorphosis and reappearing in adult specimens, suggesting that their consistent presence may reflect functional roles within the bacterial community. Meanwhile, the detection of Micrococcus as a prominent genus in wild specimens (11.9% in adults) corroborates earlier reports including this genus in the lower-abundance component of the B. oleae microbiota (Campos et al. 2022). Moreover, the detection of Sphingobium (17.3% in WCE larvae) and Methylophilus (17% in WCE pupae) suggests that comprehensive sequencing can uncover previously overlooked taxa. Likewise, the presence of Prevotella (23.3% in NT EB) and Sutterella (14.9% in WCE midguts) expands knowledge of tissue-specific colonization patterns beyond the previously described Acetobacter tropicalis (Kounatidis et al. 2009) and Tatumella sp. TA1 (Blow et al. 2020), which have been reported as consistent symbionts of B. oleae but were not detected in this study. Moreover, the analysis of WCE adult specimens, derived from surface-sterilized larvae and emerged under sterile conditions, conducted without the dominance of Ca. E. dacicola, revealed additional bacterial taxa that would otherwise be obscured by the primary symbiont.

It is noteworthy that bacterial taxa such as Micrococcus, Pseudomonas, alongside Acinetobacter, Corynebacterium, Bacillus, and Flavobacterium, appear to be acquired during the larval stage and to persist into adulthood in the individuals reared under microbiologically controlled conditions (WCE specimens). Their persistence, as occurs for the primary symbiont, indicates their ability to endure the extensive structural reorganization taking place during pupal metamorphosis. This suggests a potential functional relevance of these taxa in metamorphic processes or in adult fitness (Hammer and Moran 2019, Campos et al. 2022). However, little is known about bacterial transmission throughout the different life stages of the olive fly, highlighting the need for further studies addressing symbiont continuity and turnover across development.

Quantitative Evidence for Antimicrobial Treatment Effects

While community-level analyses provided valuable insights into structural shifts within the microbiota, a deeper understanding of how these changes may impact host-associated functions required targeted quantification of selected bacterial taxa. Accordingly, we complemented the metabarcoding results with qPCR assays targeting both the primary symbiont and other potentially relevant bacterial taxa. The decline in Ca. E. dacicola abundance observed at the larval stage highlights the sensitivity of this essential olive fly symbiont to environmental antimicrobial exposure. Conversely, the absence of significant differences in pupal and adult developmental stages suggests a partial recovery of symbiont load and may reflect a stage-dependent resilience of Ca. E. dacicola. As Ca. E. dacicola plays a central role in nitrogen assimilation and the detoxification of aromatic compounds such as oleuropein (Ben-Yosef et al. 2014, 2015), its depletion could potentially have adverse effects on fitness. Moreover, the analyses of the selected bacterial genera (i.e. Acinetobacter, Bacillus, Corynebacterium, and Flavobacterium) further revealed a significant treatment-associated reduction in relative abundance, particularly for Bacillus and Flavobacterium. These genera have documented associations with tephritid flies and, in other systems, with functions such as nitrogen metabolism and xenobiotic degradation; but the relationship between their reduction in B. oleae and tangible functional consequences remains hypothetical. Acinetobacter has been detected in wild B. oleae populations from Crete (Koskinioti et al. 2019) and has also been reported across other tephritid species in both larval and adult stages from wild and laboratory-reared populations (Malacrinò et al. 2018, Hadapad et al. 2019, De Cock et al. 2020, Bel Mokhtar et al. 2022). Although its specific functions within B. oleae have not been directly characterized, Acinetobacter is known for direct glucose oxidation (Duine 1991), aromatic compound degradation (Zhao et al. 2023), and nitrogen metabolism (Peleg et al. 2012). We therefore hypothesize that it could play analogous roles in nutrient transformation and detoxification in B. oleae. Bacillus has been isolated from a Spanish wild B. oleae population (Koskinioti et al. 2020) and detected in olive fruit flies from Greece and Italy (De Cock et al. 2020). In tephritids, Bacillus has been associated with diverse metabolic functions, including immunity (Raza et al. 2020), development (Akami et al. 2021), xenobiotic degradation (Xu et al. 2021), and nitrogen metabolism (Blencke et al. 2003), although whether it performs comparable functions in B. oleae has not been established. Corynebacterium has been consistently recorded as a persistent genus in wild B. oleae populations throughout the Mediterranean basin (Ben-Yosef et al. 2015, Campos et al. 2022). While its functions in B. oleae remain uncharacterized, a strain isolated from Helicoverpa armigera (Lepidoptera) was shown to degrade a plant defensive compound and improve host survival under chemical stress (Gu et al. 2022); this raises the hypothesis of a similar role in host adaptation in B. oleae, which remains to be tested directly. Finally, Flavobacterium, although previously reported in other Bactrocera species such as Bactrocera jarvisi and Bactrocera tryoni (Fitt and O’Brien 1985), is documented here for the first time in B. oleae. The functional role of Flavobacterium within the Bactrocera genus has not yet been investigated; however, members of this bacterial genus are known for their involvement in multiple nitrogen transformation processes (Behrendt et al. 2022, Choi et al. 2023), leading us to hypothesize a possible contribution to the host’s nitrogen balance in a nutrient-poor environment such as the olive drupe.

The simultaneous depletion of Ca. E. dacicola and auxiliary bacteria may thus compromise key physiological processes and reduce the microbiota’s functional resilience, with potential consequences for larval development and overall host fitness (Gould et al. 2018, Gupta and Nair 2020). However, further investigations regarding the functional relevance of these auxiliary taxa are warranted to better understand their contribution to the microbiota.

Conclusions

While metabarcoding remains a valuable tool for profiling microbial communities, it is not always sufficient on its own to detect fine-scale or treatment-induced shifts in community composition (Lamb et al. 2019, Maretto et al. 2023). This limitation is particularly evident in host systems dominated by a primary symbiont, where the remaining microbiota tends to be low in richness and highly individualized. In such cases, increasing the number of biological replicates, could enhance statistical power and improve the ability to detect subtle yet biologically meaningful differences in diversity metrics or community structure. Given these constraints, the limited number of replicates used in this study, 3 pools of 3 individuals per investigated life stage, may have constrained the detection of subtle yet biologically relevant microbial shifts, as metabarcoding outcomes are highly sensitive to sample size (Nascimento et al. 2018). Nonetheless, our experimental design, which integrated complementary qualitative and quantitative approaches, provides an exploratory assessment of the microbial patterns and offers a basis for further investigations of the primary symbiont and, more broadly, of the associated microbial community.

It is important to reiterate that WCE specimens do not constitute a true wild control comparable to NT and DT: they differ both in origin (field-collected larvae versus laboratory-reared adults) and in rearing history (axenic rearing following surface sterilization versus conventional rearing), independently of antimicrobial exposure. Comparisons between WCE and NT/DT groups therefore reflect this combined effect of field origin and axenic rearing rather than treatment per se and should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment-driven divergence. With this caveat in mind, the overall comparison of WCE with laboratory-reared specimens nonetheless emphasizes the broader divergence between environmentally derived and laboratory-reared microbial communities, as previously observed by other authors (Voulgari-Kokota et al. 2022, Polidori et al. 2023). In this study, WCE larval specimens, although developed under sterile conditions, harbored a more taxonomically diverse microbiota, likely reflecting a bacterial legacy effect derived from their field origin. In contrast, NT specimens exhibited a distinct microbial profile shaped by laboratory conditions. These compositional differences highlight the role of environmental exposure in shaping the host microbiome and suggest that laboratory-based observations should be carefully contextualized, as they may slightly differ from microbiota dynamics in field populations.

Overall, our results highlighted the quantifiable effects of the Zn–Cu–citric acid biocomplex on the microbiota of B. oleae. The observed changes in both alpha and beta diversity metrics, along with the marked reduction in the primary symbiont, indicate a microbial impairment effect during the larval stage. The parallel reduction observed in core bacteria is consistent with this pattern, although the extent to which it contributes to a functional impairment remains hypothetical, as the roles of these taxa in B. oleae have not been functionally characterized. This early-life perturbation, as shown by qPCR results, appears to exert lasting effects that are not fully compensated by subsequent microbial restructuring in adult stages.

Further studies investigating microbial associates capable of persisting through metamorphosis, in addition to the primary symbiont Ca. E. dacicola, are warranted. Their persistence through metamorphosis suggests that they can constitute a selected fraction of the B. oleae microbiota that, to the best of our knowledge, remain largely unexplored despite their potential relevance for host development and fitness.

Taken together, these results provide an important foundation for understanding how antimicrobial inputs can reshape insect symbioses. Overall, our findings indicate that antimicrobial treatments are associated with compositional changes in the microbiota of B. oleae and with a marked depletion of the primary symbiont Ca. E. dacicola. The potential implications of these changes for host physiology remain to be determined. However, because 16S rRNA gene-based analyses do not allow direct inference of microbial functions, further studies integrating genomic and functional approaches are needed to clarify the biological consequences of the observed perturbations and the potential roles of the bacterial taxa involved. In particular, future work should assess whether these microbial changes translate into measurable effects on host fitness, thereby providing the basis for evaluating their potential relevance to integrated pest management strategies in olive orchards.
