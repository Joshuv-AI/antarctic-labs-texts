Preparation and Characterization of New Biodegradable Packaging Materials Based on Gelatin Extracted from Tenualosa ilisha Fish Scales with Cellulose Nanocrystals
Authors: Md. Abdul Mottalib, Md. Hasan Islam, Mohon Chandra Dhar, Kawsar Akhtar, Md. Abdul Goni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Food packaging industries generally use petroleum-based packaging materials that are non-biodegradable and harmful to the environment. Eco-friendly polymers such as chitosan (CH), gelatin (GE), and cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) are leading viable alternatives to plastics traditionally used in packaging because of their higher functionality and biodegradability. In this study, an innovative approach has been disclosed to prepare new packaging materials by utilizing chitosan, gelatin, and cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) through a simple solution casting method. GE and CNCs have been isolated from prawn shells and jute fiber, respectively. Utilization of Hilsa Tenualosa ilisha fish scale biowaste was a new and first approach for gelatin extraction. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), UV-vis spectroscopy, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to examine the functional and morphological features of fish scale gelatin, chitosan, CNCs, and the resulting composite films. The synthesized film materials were analyzed for their mechanical strength, solubility, apparent density, swelling behavior, biodegradability, light transmittance, and transparency. The impregnation of CNCs into the polymer amalgam milieu effectively enhanced their physicochemical and biological properties. The degree of swelling in composite matrices was found to be increased gradually, whereas the solubility was decreased due to the cross-linking effect of CNCs. Elongation at break in the gelatin–chitosan (GC) film was observed as 48%. However, the incorporation of CNCs into the GC matrix potentially enhanced the elongation at break property to 64.05%, 62.86%, and 59.21% in GC1, GC2, and GC3 bioplastic films, respectively. The purified chitosan–gelatin films showed a tensile strength of 12.24 N/mm2, which was increased to 13.93 N/mm2 with the addition of 1.00% CNCs. The composite films were found to be highly transparent and stable in an ambient atmosphere. However, 49–60% deformation occurred in the composite materials after 7 days, whereas 71–84% biodegradation was realized after 21 days, when the respective composite films were subjected to a natural soil environment. These novel composite films possess all essential interesting features, such as biocompatibility, transparency, smoothness of surfaces, and biodegradability, making them suitable for use as packaging materials in different industries.

Introduction

Nowadays, plastics have become an inevitable component of packaging materials for their excellent properties and relatively lower cost of preparation. However, petroleum-based plastic materials are not biodegradable and are not easy to recycle. They are usually disposed of in landfills after use, causing pollution in a terrestrial environment.1−3 Biodegradable films can serve as an effective alternative to synthetic polymers used for packaging purposes. Biopolymers are edible, nontoxic, natural, and highly renewable.4−6 Biopolymers can be produced from polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids of biowaste from animal and agricultural sources.7−9 Functional ingredients can be incorporated into biofilms to enhance their properties, which can be utilized in making bioactive packaging materials.

Gelatin is a major protein found in skin, bones, and connective tissues, which can be made by breaking down collagen.10 Gelatin materials are widely employed in numerous sectors including cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, tissue engineering, and packaging materials.11−14 Recent literature data show that the yearly global production of gelatin is expanding, particularly in Asia, and it is mostly extracted from the skins and bones of mammals. However, there are some diseases and religious concerns regarding the use of gelatins obtained from mammals, which encourage the utilization of fish gelatin as an ideal substitute for mammalian gelatin.15 Fish scales, bones, and skin are great sources of gelatin. Compared to animal gelatins, fish gelatin has fewer proline and hydroxyproline residues, which can affect its characteristic properties. Nonetheless, fish gelatin shows the ability to form a film, and thus, it has been proposed as a potential substitute for polymers derived from petroleum. In recent years, gelatin has been extracted and characterized from a variety of sources including the skin and bone of sin croaker, shortfin scad,16 black tilapia Oreochromis mossambicus, red tilapia, Nile tilapia Oreochromis niloticus, and Nile perch Lates niloticus.17 Fish scales have been collected and processed from several fish species such as grass carp, zebrafish,18Mugil cephalus,19 and silver carp.20

Bangladesh is a riverine country in which 4.62 million metric tons of several fish species grow every year. Among them, approximately 0.6 million metric tons are identified as Hilsa Tenualosa ilisha fish.21 Approximately 70.5 million metric tons of fish are produced annually, and approximately 3% of it generates inedible fish scale wastes that can be utilized to make fish gelatin. Thus, waste fish scales can be a sustainable and economical source of fish gelatin.22,23

The capacity of gelatin to form emulsions increases the scope of its use in the food industries. Gelatin is utilized as an important ingredient in a wide range of food products.24 Fish gelatin can be used to prepare transparent films for flexible electronic devices.3 However, gelatin materials show resistance to cold temperatures, flexibility, and some mechanical properties, which provide limitations to its applications in several fields.25 A mixture of synthetic and biopolymer additives such as chitosan (CH),26 starch, and pectin can be employed to enhance the properties of gelatin, which can overcome any drawbacks of its applications.27 In recent years, gelatin has been extensively utilized as a powder, film, membrane, and sheet in different fields. Due to its inherent antibacterial properties, chitosan can be utilized in preparing food packaging materials.28 Chitosan is a linear hydrophilic polysaccharide. In recent years, chitosan and its derivatives have been extensively employed in agricultural, medical, textile, and pharmaceutical industries, as well as wastewater treatment processes due to their antibacterial and antifungal characteristics. Chitosan-based packaging films are biodegradable, show good thermal stability, and are highly acceptable as eco-friendly benign materials.29 The prime goal of this study was to develop new packaging films with active polymers that contain compounds with antibacterial and antioxidant properties, which can enhance and preserve the biological qualities of food items.30

Due to its abundance, recyclability, and biodegradability, cellulose is considered one of the most intriguing bioresource materials, which makes it a prime candidate for the title ″most interesting.″ Nanocellulose (NC) extracted from natural cellulose sources has been used in a wide range of industries, such as pharmaceutical, cosmetic, electronic, and food packaging spheres. Ratna et al. (2022) reported the synthesis of an eco-friendly gelatin-based edible film (NEF) from a combination of nanogelatin and cellulose nanocrystals.31 Applications of nanofillers or nanoreinforcement materials can significantly improve the mechanical, thermal, and water barrier qualities of biopolymer films to utilize them as biodegradable active packaging materials.32,33 Jute cellulose nanofibers are frequently used in nanocomposite preparation because of their cost-effectiveness, availability, crystallinity, and high cellulose contents.

This study primarily focused on the sources, extraction process, and applications of NC for the production of environmentally friendly packaging materials. Several studies have been carried out previously on the extraction of gelatins from different sources and their applications in various fields such as the extraction of gelatins from Tilapia scales,3 the incorporation of fatty acid sucrose esters into protein films,22 and the use ofTrichoderma harzianum spores in gelatin films reinforced with cellulose nanocrystals.23 Recently, gelatin biopolymers were also isolated from the Labeo rohita scale using chemical techniques.34 However, to the best of our knowledge, there has not been any study carried out or reported in the literature yet on the extraction and characterization of gelatin from the Hilsa fish scale.

The objective of the current study was to extract and characterize gelatin from the Hilsa fish scale, as well as apply it in preparing a biogenic, nontoxic, biodegradable, transparent cellulose nanocrystal matrix with the integration of polymer blends, which can be used as an active food packaging material and meet SDG 2030 sustainable waste management goals through eco-friendly utilization of solid biowastes.

Materials and Methods

Raw Materials

Hilsa fish scales and prawn shells were collected from the Hazaribagh market in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Jute fibers were collected from the Khulna Jute Mill area, Bangladesh. The chemicals used in this work were hydrochloric acid (HCl), ethanol, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), acetone, acetic acid, nitric acid, sodium chloride, sulfuric acid, and sodium nitrite. All chemicals were of analytical grade, purchased from Merck, Germany, and used as they were received.

Methods of Testing and Morphological Analysis

A Perkin–Elmer Spectrum Two instrument (ALPHA II, Platinum-ATR, BRUKER, Germany), scanning in the range of 500–4000 cm–1, was used to collect the FTIR spectra. A Perkin–Elmer UV–visible Spectrometer Lambda-35 (Perkin-Elmer SCIEX, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was used to conduct preliminary assessments of the films. The thermal stability of gelatin, chitosan, CNC, and the synthesized biocomposite materials was studied by using thermo gravimetric analysis (TGA) (Perkin–Elmer TGA 8000, Switzerland). X-ray diffraction study was carried out on gelatin, chitosan, CNC, and biocomposite films by using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan) utilizing Cu-K radiation and a 3.0 min–1 scanning speed. The surface morphology of gelatin, chitosan, CNC, and biocomposite films was analyzed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEOL JSM 6390, Japan). The samples were compiled into aluminum stubs with double-sided carbon tape. After each specimen was sputtered with a thin layer of gold using a JEOL JFC 1600 auto fine-coater, the photographs were assessed under a 10 kV accelerating voltage. Dynamic light scattering (DLS), also known as photon correlation spectroscopy (Litesizer 500 Anton Paar machine), was used to analyze the size distribution and particle sizes of nanoparticles. The amount of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur (CHNS) in gelatin samples was estimated by elemental analysis (CHNS elemental analyzer, Vario Micro V1.6.1GmbH, Germany).

The Biochrom 20 (Pharmacia-LKB) apparatus was used to assess the amino acid contents in gelatin isolated from the fish scales. 2 g of lyophilized gelatin was combined with a mixture of 1% phenol (w/v) and 2 mL of 6 N HCl. Gelatin was hydrolyzed in a glass ampule, hermetically sealed under an atmosphere of nitrogen, and heated at 110 °C for 24 hours. After the hydrolysis process, the ampules were opened to separate phenol and HCl using 1 M NaOH and the pressure was reduced. Following four rounds of rinsing with ultrapure water (Milli-Q), the hydrolyzed residues were dried at a lower pressure while phosphorus pentoxide was present. The samples were dried, and the amino acid contents within the samples were determined after dissolving them in a sterile 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer solution (pH 2.2). Amino acid contents present in the samples were ascertained by the modification and customization of the Biochrom 20 amino acid analyzer method (Pharmacia, Barcelona, Spain).

The U-3900UV spectrophotometer (model 212–0013, USA) test cell was utilized to analyze the finished films by measuring the volume of liquid as 10 × 20 mL. An empty test cell was used as a point of reference. The absorbance was measured at 600 nm. Extraction of Gelatin from Hilsa Fish Scales

The solution casting technique was used to extract and separate fish gelatin (FG).36 Initially, the impurities were removed from the fish scales by washing them with tap water, and then, they were dried in sunlight. The dried fish scales (279 g) were treated with 0.1 M NaOH at ambient temperature for 1 h to remove impurities from protein. Excess NaOH was removed by washing the fish scales with distilled water. After that, the alkali-treated fish scales were subjected to an additional treatment with 10% HCl solution for 1 h to eliminate minerals. Distilled water was used to rinse the mixture, until it became neutral. Next, the fish scales were mixed with 350 mL of distilled water in an airtight stainless steel container. This mixture was then heated at approximately 80 °C for 8 h and centrifuged at a speed of 2,000 rpm. The supernatant was separated from the mixture obtained after centrifugation, which was used to form the films. The film-forming solution was poured onto Petri dishes and was allowed to dry for 24 h in a freeze dryer at −65 °C under vacuum conditions. 47.51 g of fish gelatin was obtained from 270 g of fish scales. Preparation of Chitosan

50 g of raw prawn shells were cleaned with deionized water with intermittent stirring. The sample was then dried at 60 °C. The residue was treated with 4% NaOH (1:5; v/v) solution at ambient temperature overnight to deprotonate protein species present in chitosan. Excess alkali was decanted, and the residual protein was washed multiple times with deionized water until a neutral pH (7) was obtained. The deprotonated protein was treated with 4% HCl (1:5; v/v) at ambient temperature for 10 h. The demineralization process was favored as CO2 levels increased. Excess acid was removed, and the resulting residue was washed with deionized water. The chitin obtained in this process was dried at room temperature (30 ± 2 °C). This chitin species was treated with a 50% NaOH solution (1:20; w/v) at 50 °C. After the deacetylation process, the excess alkali was removed, and the resulting residue was rinsed with deionized water until the pH dropped and reached 7.0. The extracted chitosan was dried at room temperature for 2 days.37 The overall extraction and separation process of chitin is shown in Figure 2.

Extraction and isolation of chitosan from raw prawn shells.

Extraction of Nanocellulose Crystal (CNC)

The dried jute fibers were ground to approximately 1–2 mm in length using a grinding machine. 1 g of fiber was added to 12% sodium hydroxide (1 M) solution, and the resulting mixture was stirred continuously for 2–3 h at ambient temperature. The mixture was filtered, and distilled water was used to wash the fibers multiple times until the pH reached 7. Subsequently, the fibers were dried at 60 °C in a hot air oven for 12 h. Then, the dried fibers were transferred to a two-neck quick-fit round-bottom flask, and a condenser was attached. 14 mL of conc. nitric acid solution was added into the round-bottom flask. The fibers were allowed to soak in nitric acid for 15 min. 1.96 g of sodium nitrite was added, and the resulting mixture was stirred continuously for 1 h at room temperature. Later, the reaction mixture was agitated for 10 h at 60 °C. The reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, and it was diluted by adding 100 mL of cold distilled water. Later, the diluted suspension was centrifuged for 15 min at a speed of 3000 rpm in order to obtain the precipitate. The resulting precipitate was collected and washed with distilled water until the pH was adjusted to 7. The precipitate obtained in this process was saved in a refrigerator at a temperature of 4 °C for further use.

Films Composed of Polymer Nanocomposites and Their Compositions

The CH–GE–CNC composite films were prepared using the solution casting method.38 The experimental procedures were slightly modified by the incorporation of CNCs and a change in the preparation technique.38 A homogeneous solution was produced by dissolving 3 g of chitosan in 100 mL of 1% acetic acid and stirring the mixture for 12 h. The resulting mixture was filtered with cheesecloth to eliminate the residual particles that did not dissolve in the solution. 7 g of gelatin was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water. The gelatin and chitosan solutions were combined at a composition of GE:CH (70:30; w/w) at 50 °C to achieve a fine morphology. The mixture was shaken for 1h to obtain a homogeneous species. 3 mL of glycerol was added, and the mixture was subjected to stirring for an additional 3 h at ambient temperature. The resultant homogeneous mixture was poured onto a Petri dish (diameter = 15 cm) at room temperature. The solution was dried at ambient temperature (25 °C) for 30 h, which eventually turned into nice film materials. The composite films were removed from Petri dishes and stored in a desiccator at 25 °C for 72 h at 50% relative humidity (RH) for further analysis. In order to produce CH–GE–CNC films, the CH–GE mixture was spiked with CNC solutions of various concentrations. The incorporation of cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) resulted in a greater surface area, higher crystallinity, and excellent biodegradability, biocompatibility, and mechanical properties in the new composite materials. In addition, CNCs possess a greater number of −OH functionalities, which can make a strong network structure in the desired composite materials.39 Different concentrations of CNC were applied to increase and optimize the desired photochemical as well as mechanical properties in the resulting new composite materials with the optimization of the reaction conditions. FTIR Analysis of Fish Gelatin

FTIR analysis on gelatin extracted from Hilsa fish scales provided significant information on the functional groups, structural moieties, and the polypeptide chains’ secondary structure of compounds present in the gelatin material. The functional group characteristics found in gelatin from the FTIR study are shown in Figure 4. The absorption bands are found within the typical region of amides, which include amides A, B, I, II, and III. These amide functionalities greatly influence the degree of molecular organization and play a significant role in the formation of collagen’s triple helix structure via intermolecular interactions. A distinctive resonance observed at 3294 cm–1 might have originated from the secondary amide’s N–H bond stretch (Figure 4). This absorption peak was observed to be lower than that appeared from free amides (3440–3400 cm–1), which indicated that the N–H groups were connected to one another via hydrogen bonds, leading to the formation of the peptide chain conformational structure.41 The characteristic resonance at 3080 cm–1might be attributed to amide B species. This absorption peak might also be associated with asymmetric stretching vibrations from =C—H and −NH3+ groups. The stretching vibrations of CH2 groups were found to be symmetric at 2925 cm–1. The characteristic resonances observed in Figure 4 revealed that dimeric intermolecular associations of carboxylic groups are responsible for combining the IR absorption bands raised from the stretching vibrations of amide A, amide B, and CH2 species.42

Analysis of fish gelatin by FTIR.

Gelatin has also undergone a degree of molecular disintegration, as evidenced by the resonances that appeared from the amide I, II, and III functionalities. The resonance observed at 1632 cm–1 confirmed the presence of the C=O group, which was actively involved in hydrogen bonding formation between amide I molecules. However, a similar resonance appeared at approximately 1660 cm–1. Amide might have attributed to the collagen structure, which was a characteristic of the triple helix structure. Amide II exhibited a stronger susceptibility to hydration than protein structural alterations despite having its close linkage to the secondary structure of protein. Amide II showed IR resonances from the stretching along with deformation vibrations of C=N—H and C–N functionalities. The different IR absorption bands found in gelatin extracted from Hilsa fish were comparable to those observed in a similar gelatin material obtained from various other sources. Amide III species was found to be unaffected by the hydration effects of water, and thus, amide III appeared as an excellent tool for determining the secondary structures of proteins, and it worked well in conjunction with amide I species. The results of the FTIR study on the experimental gelatin material were highly consistent with the data reported previously for gelatin extracted from tilapia, where amide I and amide II showed peaks at 1660 and 1656 cm–1, respectively.17 The degree of disorder that was produced due to the partial denaturation of the collagen structure was evidenced by the appearance of the absorption band at a wavelength of 1241 cm–1 due to the occurrence of amide III species in Hilsa scale gelatin. The appearance of characteristic IR bands at wavelengths greater than 1240 cm–1 highly revealed the existence of higher molecular weight peptides as amide III showed the disordered structural features of gelatin extracted from Hilsa fish scales.43

FT-IR Analysis of Chitosan and Crystalline Nanocellulose (CNC)

FTIR spectra of chitosan and CNC are presented in Figure 5. A strong absorption band appeared between 3266 and 3358 cm–1 in the FT-IR spectra of chitosan, whereas CNC showed a broad IR resonance at 3274 cm–1. These broad absorption peaks might be attributed to the O–H and N–H bond stretching. The asymmetric C–H stretching displayed the absorption bands at 2868 cm–1. The IR resonance observed at 1633 cm–1 (representing the C=O stretching of amide I) and 1321 cm–1 might have originated from the C=O stretching of amide I and C–N stretching of amide III species, which indicated the existence of residual N-acetyl groups. A strong band was observed at 1514 cm–1, which might have occurred from the N–H bending of amide II species. The resonance bands appearing at around 1421 cm–1 and 1371 cm–1 evidenced the CH2 bending and CH3 symmetrical deformations, respectively. The asymmetric stretching of the C–O–C bridge caused the appearance of an absorption band at 1145 cm–1. The bands observed at 1066 and 1025 cm–1 might have originated from the stretching vibration of the C–O bond moiety present in the chitosan structure.

FTIR spectra of chitosan and CNC.

The FTIR spectra of nanocellulose displayed resonances within the range of 500–4000 cm–1. The characteristic bands observed at 3274 cm–1 might be rationalized from the OH stretching present in the CNC structure obtained from jute fibers. However, the peak at 2886 cm–1 might have originated from the C–H symmetrical stretching of the cellulose unit. The C=C functionality of the aromatic ring present in the lignin unit produced the peak at 1438 cm–1. The C–O–C (aryl alkyl ether) stretching mode of vibration produced the peak at 1157 cm–1, and the C–O bond stretching might have caused the appearance of resonance at 1014 cm–1.44

Elemental Analysis (CHNS) of Fish Gelatin

The results of elemental analysis provided the relative amounts of different elements present in a given sample. CHNS analysis data revealed the percentages of various elements in gelatin, which were 57.56% carbon, 5.5% hydrogen, 10.3% nitrogen, 0.86% sulfur, and 25.6% oxygen. The abundances of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen determined in gelatin extracted and isolated from scales of Hilsa fish were observed to be identical to the GMIA standard values reported in the literature.48

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) and DTGA of Hilsa Fish Gelatin

Thermal stability of gelatin isolated from Hilsa fish scales was analyzed by the TGA technique (8000TM instrument, Germany). In this study, 6 mg of each gelatin sample was placed in a platinum pan and heated to 700 °C in a furnace at a consistent rate of 10 °C per minute under a nitrogen atmosphere. The results of the thermogravimetric analysis of gelatin are displayed in Figure 6. Weight losses of gelatin were observed at two phases of TGA. The initial weight loss of the sample occurred at 70–130 °C due to the removal of moisture and other volatile species. When the gelatin samples were heated to 200 °C, the second stage of degradation began. In this case, the gelatin species showed the maximum weight loss (40.54%). With the increase in temperatures from 290 to 400 °C at the third stage of TGA, a similar pattern of weight loss was found. There were sharp breakpoints or shoulder peaks in TGA curves at this stage of the thermal study, which indicated that the protein species had disintegrated (Figure 6). It is essential to have precise temperature readings during different stages of the TGA study in order to accurately forecast the thermal behavior of biopolymers at various temperatures and methods. However, DTGA of the same sample showed two sharp weight losses, which are shown in Figure 6. It has been apparent that approximately 15% of weight loss occurred at 100 °C due to the removal of moisture and volatile organic compounds, whereas major weight loss was recorded at 340 °C for the structural decomposition of gelatin.

SEM study was carried out to examine the microstructure of fish gelatin. The results of the SEM analysis of gelatin are displayed in Figure 7. The SEM images at various magnifications showed an uneven network fibrous structure with smaller voids in gelatin materials. Analysis of Composite Films by FTIR

The FTIR spectra of the nanocomposite films are depicted in Figure 9. Different characteristic resonance bands have been found in the spectra of nanocomposite films. The IR peak at 3296 cm–1 might be attributed to the NH2 functionality present in the structures of G-C films.

Results of FT-IR analysis of composite films.

The IR band observed at 2941 cm–1 might have originated from the asymmetric stretching vibrations of the CH2 group present in the alkyl chain moiety within G-C films. The IR resonance at 1661 cm–1 might be ascribed to the stretching vibrational mode of C=O bonds, and the band at 1424 cm–1 corresponds to the stretching of the C–N bond present in amide I species within the G-C films. The amide II species might have produced a characteristic band at 1560 cm–1 due to the bending stretching of the N–H bond. C–O–C bond stretching from the polysaccharide chain might have caused the formation of IR resonance at 1051 cm–1.50 The chemical composition of chitosan materials was unaffected by the addition of GE and CNC materials, as evidenced by characteristic resonances observed in the IR spectra of the respective materials. The mixing of additives did not seem to develop a new bond; rather, the intensities of the IR peaks have been observed to be increased in the spectra of the corresponding substances (Figure 8). However, the results of the FT-IR analysis strongly suggested the development of coordination linkages among chitosan, gelatin, and CNC entities. The information extracted from the FTIR analyses of synthetic films confirmed the presence of various characteristic functionalities within the structures of composite films, which were in agreement with the data reported previously in the literature from similar studies.51

The sizes of the corresponding film materials have shrunk over time in close contact with soil microorganisms, which suggested that there was spontaneous biodegradation, which occurred within the film materials in a natural soil surface environment. The biodegradability of GC films was found to be significantly higher than that of nonbiodegradable polymers. GC was a collagen derivative that could be degraded in the presence of different microorganisms in soil, and the GC films showed their potential ability to disintegrate in a natural soil environment. Previous literature studies indicated that GC films buried in the ground for an extended period might be susceptible to microbial corrosion.62 However, the results showed that the GC films are highly stable to both water and sunlight. The biodegradability of each new composite film was tested in three different ways, as mentioned above. The newly synthesized composite films were observed to be highly transparent and environmentally friendly, as well as stable under an ambient atmosphere for a long time, which made them ideally suitable for prospective consumers. Chemically modified chitosans, including partially deacetylated and carboxymethylated chitosans, were found to be very effective in showing potential immunological and antibacterial activities.63 When the same composite samples were tested in a soil environment, gradual decomposition was observed in the respective film composites, which were 60% and 49%, respectively, after 7 days, whereas 84% and 71% of degradation were found in the GC film (control) and GC–CNC film, respectively, after 21 days. In contrast to soil, which possesses a moisture content of approximately 25% by volume, air possesses only approximately 4% of moisture by volume. Thus, bacteria cannot attack composite films in air under ambient conditions, which are highly unfavorable for bacterial growth and activity. However, in soil, there is a higher concentration of moisture, which provides ideal conditions for bacterial growth and activity. Literature study showed the occurrence of serious microbial corrosion in the GC composite films when they were buried in soil for a specific amount of time.64 However, during the present investigation, a smaller surface area of each composite sample came in contact with soil environment, which might cause the slow break down or degradation in the respective composite film materials. As a result, the synthesized film composites showed excellent biodegradability, which could significantly contribute to reduce environmental pollution as well as waste disposal expenses.

Nanocellulose crystals might have formed strong interactions or chemical bonds with different functionalities available within the structures of gelatin, chitosan, and glycerol.

Tensile Strength and % of Elongation

The presence of myofibrillar protein in gelatin structures might have caused them to demonstrate excellent film-forming capability. The tensile strength of the GC film was measured as 11.24 MPa. The films containing CNC fillers showed an increase in the tensile strength from 11.64 to 13.93 MPa. Tensile strengths of composite films increased significantly when cellulose nanocrystals were incorporated into the respective materials, as evidenced by the data reported in Table 7. This increase in tensile strengths might be due to the insertion of CNC particles into the gelatin–chitosan (GC) matrix, which might have facilitated the interactions with GC in GC1, GC2, and GC3 bioplastic films. The results from a similar study reported previously in the literature showed the tensile strength of gelatin film as 9.77 ± 0.01 MPa, which was considerably lower than those found in the composite films synthesized from the present study (11.24 MPa), indicating that the incorporation of chitosan into the gelatin matrix significantly improved the strengths of the corresponding film materials.65 Moreover, crystallization of fillers might have enhanced the mechanical characteristics of bioplastic films, which was evident from the steady increase in the tensile strengths with the integration of CNC fillers.66 These findings were consistent with the results reported previously in the literature, which demonstrated that the addition of crystalline fillers could potentially increase the tensile strengths of bioplastic films.4 This suggested that the increase in bioplastic mechanical properties might be attributed to good interfacial adhesion, which could build a robust network of hydrogen bonds between the starch matrix and CNC fillers.

The results displayed in Table 7 showed how the amount of CNC greatly affected the structures of bioplastic films before they underwent breakage. Elongation at break in GC film was found to be 48%, which was lower than that observed in GC2 (62.86%). This might be rationalized by the fact that CNC in the bioplastic film worked as a filler, which might increase the magnitude of elongation at break in the respective film materials. GC1 showed the highest elongation at break, which was found to be 64.05%, and this magnitude was observed decrease to 59.21% in the GC3 film composite, where the maximum amount of CNC (1%) was used in the respective process. These results indicated that the application of a trace amount of CNC (0.05%) might had posed a strong positive impact on the elongation at break of film materials. The incorporation of CNC into the structure of the bioplastic film might have increased its strength and thus elevated its elongation at break property. This might also be the result of hydrogen bond formation between the hydroxyl moiety of cellulose structures and carboxyl groups (COOH) available in gelatin materials. However, the presence of higher concentrations of CNC might have caused the aggregation of the fillers within the structures of biodegradable films, which might result in inconsistency in the respective properties.

Swelling Degree of Composite Films

The expansion behaviors of composite films were analyzed by determining the swelling degree. The inclusion of the plasticizer glycerol into the GC matrix increased the degree of swelling of the blended films, which clearly demonstrated the degree of flexibility of the corresponding bioplastic films. It was highly noticeable when the percentage of nanoparticles in the matrix was increased to 0.1% (w/v). A greater extent of cross-linker might potentially have increased the swelling ratio by enhancing the localization of water molecules inside the polymer network.61 The presence of different water-soluble phytochemicals such as alkaloids, carbohydrates, tannins, and polyphenols on the surfaces of nanoparticles might have exerted negative effects, which was realized when CNCs were incorporated as it caused a moderate increase in the swelling of the composite films from 55.1 to 65.6% (Table 5).

Water Solubility of Composite Films

Nanocellulose is observed to be highly dispersible but not completely soluble in water. Table 5 represents the results of the solubility of GC and GC–CNC films in water. The data displayed in Table 5 showed that the water solubility of control films was found to be 41.72%, whereas the water solubility of other films was observed to decrease to 35.12% with an increase in CNC concentrations in the respective films. In comparison to other films, the chitosan with 1% CNC and 70% gelatin content had the lowest solubility in water. This might be related to the higher level of compatibility that might exist between chitosan and gelatin in the presence of 1% CNC within the matrix. It might be possible that the addition of 1% CNC changed the crystallinity of the polymer matrix, which might have resulted in the decrease in water solubility of the corresponding films. These findings of water solubility behaviors of the synthesized bioplastic films were very comparable to the data reported previously in the literature from a similar study.67

Conclusions

Herein, new packaging film materials were prepared by using biodegradable polymers (gelatin and chitosan) derived from natural sources such as Hilsa fish scales and prawn shells, with the aim of reducing environmental pollution caused by synthetic nonbiodegradable plastic packaging materials and their waste. The strength of the novel composite films was enhanced by incorporating CNC in the form of reinforcing fillers, which effectively increased the opacity of the respective materials. SEM study showed the uniform distribution of necessary components within the structures of the film materials. The new composite biofilm materials were found to be flexible and thermally stable and tolerant to water and ambient atmosphere. The surface characteristics of gelatin–chitosan films were carefully examined, which showed the presence of all essential properties to apply them as packaging materials in industries. However, smoothness on the surfaces of composite films was reduced when CNC was incorporated into their structures. On the other hand, higher extents of CNC in bioplastic films dramatically improved the tensile strength of the respective composite materials. CNC showed different functional groups and cross-linking effects, which made the polymer matrices less compact, decreasing their solubility while increasing their corresponding tensile strength as well as apparent densities. These new composite films were found to be biodegradable, and there was spontaneous biodegradation of the respective materials, which occurred in a natural soil environment, as evident from the results of corresponding experiments. The higher biodegradability and other characteristic properties of GC films made them ideally suitable to be used as potential packaging materials in different industries, and thus, they can greatly reduce the expenses associated with plastic waste disposal as well as combat environmental pollution, which may comply with the sustainable development goal of 2030 in Bangladesh and other developing countries. Because of possessing exceptional transparency, smooth surfaces, and the ability to degrade naturally, these new economically viable and environmentally friendly FG–CH film composites can potentially be transformed into ideally suitable substrates for the manufacturing of future green electronic devices and food packaging biodegradable polymeric materials.
