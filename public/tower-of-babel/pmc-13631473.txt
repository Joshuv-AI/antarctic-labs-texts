Universal charge-tag labelling of undetectable polymers for mass spectrometric characterization
Authors: Charles Killeen, Liliia Pestereva, Maxine A. Turner, Luca Swainson, Jeremy E. Wulff, J. Scott McIndoe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite massive advances in mass spectrometric technologies, there remains a class of materials largely impervious to analysis: high molecular weight aliphatic hydrocarbons. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) is used extensively as a tool in petroleomics to identify and fingerprint the polar constituents of oil, but the non-polar constituents remain challenging to analyze with ESI-MS due to poor ionization efficiency. Derivatization methods which bond electrospray-active tags to analytes impart charge and increase analyte response and have been developed for many different classes of petrochemicals, but not hydrocarbons. In this work, a method is described which renders aliphatic hydrocarbons amenable to ESI-MS analysis through C–H insertion into a substrate molecule of a diazirine-derived carbene, which is bound to a pendant alkyne. Subsequent electrospray-tagging occurs through copper-catalyzed azide alkyne cycloaddition, appending a triphenylphosphonium cation to the derivatized compound. The process was developed and benchmarked with cyclododecane as a model substrate to evaluate tagging efficiency and product distribution and then applied to characterize several commercial products composed of aliphatic compounds. While derivatization efficiency and ionization response may vary across substrates, the method provides a complementary route to established ionization techniques by enabling hydrocarbon analysis within ESI-based high-resolution mass spectrometric workflows. Its primary utility lies in enabling ESI-compatible fingerprinting of unfunctionalized compounds.

Introduction

Mass spectrometry and charge tagging

Mass spectrometry (MS) is a powerful tool for the characterization of diverse classes of molecules. It relies on the creation of gas-phase ions and their subsequent resolution with electric (and sometimes magnetic) fields.1 The capability to provide compositional information from sub-milligram amounts of complex mixtures of analytes has thrust MS to the forefront of chemical analysis. Its flexibility is evident in applications across such fields as structural biology,2 materials science,3 metabolomics4 and proteomics,5 and environmental forensics.6 Especially as sample complexity increases, effective characterization using MS is made simpler if analyte fragmentation is minimal, favouring a process referred to as soft ionization. Several soft ionization methods exist today, with matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization7 (MALDI) and electrospray ionization8 (ESI) being the most common. In ESI-MS, analyte molecules typically acquire charge through association or dissociation of ions (often protons), but may also do so through reduction or oxidation events involving electron transfer from or to the charged capillary. However, analyte molecules must possess some capability of obtaining a charge, which necessitates a molecular structure capable of binding or expelling a charged species. As a result, different classes of molecules ionize preferentially under different conditions (Fig. 1a).9

(b) Mechanistic scheme depicting the thermal generation of singlet or triplet carbenes from a diazirine-containing precursor, and the subsequent insertion of the singlet carbene into the C–H bond of a substrate molecule. (c) Reaction scheme depicting insertion of the diazirine-derived carbene into a C–H bond of the analyte molecule, and subsequent charge-tagging with the triphenylphosphonium group through CuAAC derivatization.

A conceptually different method of analyte ionization is the appending of a charged group to analytes of interest through chemical derivatization before analysis, sometimes termed “charge-tagging”.10,11 Tethering ionic groups to analytes can be made selective for specific functional groups in a mixture, and this strategy has been used in the ESI-MS analysis of compounds present in petrochemicals, including phenols,12 thiols,13 and naphthenic acids.14 Petroleomics, or the study of the components of naturally occurring petrochemicals by MS, naturally deals with incredibly complex mixtures of compounds.15 A major constituent of crude oil and petrochemical products is aliphatic hydrocarbons, which are composed only of carbon and hydrogen and lack functional groups that facilitate chemical transformations. These compounds are very difficult to characterize by ESI-MS as they lack any structural elements which promote easy ionization like Lewis/Brønsted acidic or basic sites, or redox active groups. Aliphatic compounds can be ionized for mass spectrometric analysis under hard ionization conditions (such as electron ionization),16,17 but the degree of induced fragmentation complicates analysis substantially.

Field ionization (FI) and liquid-injection field desorption ionization (LIFDI) enable analysis of low-polarity or nonpolar hydrocarbons without derivatization18,19 and have been used in petroleum and hydrocarbon characterization.20 Atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) has been shown to enable direct analysis of hydrocarbons at elevated molecular weight (up to m/z 900, C65, using isooctane as a reagent gas).21–23 Ruthenium-ion catalyzed oxidation (RICO) can be used to convert hydrocarbons to ketones, and by reduction with LiAlH4, can convert these into alcohols suitable for negative ion ESI-MS analysis. RICO has successfully characterized hydrocarbons with masses up to about m/z 1100, C70.24 As such, molecules with masses much in excess of 1100 Da are not amenable to analysis. A charge-tagging strategy for the ESI-MS analysis of aliphatic compounds would provide an orthogonal route to analysis that enables hydrocarbon mixtures to be examined within ESI-MS workflows and complements existing hydrocarbon ionization techniques, making ESI-MS an even more versatile and broadly applicable method of analysis.

Aryl diazirines as derivatization reagents

A suitable derivatization method for aliphatic compounds must be capable of reacting with inert C–C or C–H bonds while minimizing rearrangement or fragmentation. One such method exploits the thermal or photochemical generation of carbenes from precursor molecules containing trifluoromethyl aryl diazirine (TAD) functional groups (Fig. 1b).25 The singlet or triplet carbenes generated following diazirine activation can engage in low-barrier insertion to any nearby C–H or O–H bond, allowing for the efficient functionalization and crosslinking of polyolefins.26,27 Singlet carbenes—which are favoured through the use of electron-rich aromatic groups within the TAD—give much higher C–H insertion yields than triplet carbenes, since triplets are rapidly quenched by atmospheric triplet oxygen (3O2) to give undesirable ketone byproducts.28,29

To bias reactivity toward insertion into the C–H bonds of the analyte, a diazirine-based derivatization reagent must be employed under solvent-free conditions. However, the ionic salts employed in the charge-tagging methodology are insoluble in aliphatic compounds. As a result, combining an ionically tagged diazirine precursor with an aliphatic sample will lead to phase separation of the tag, promoting self-reactivity and minimizing substrate tagging. Thus, a neutral diazirine precursor was designed which could be derivatized after C–H insertion into the analyte sample. Diazirine-derived carbenes may insert more readily into O–H and N–H bonds where present.28,30 Accordingly, this methodology is expected to perform most predictably in hydrocarbon-rich materials or after polarity-based fractionation.

Method development strategy

To selectively tag the analytes after insertion, a copper-catalyzed azide alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) was chosen.31 As a type of “click” reaction, this process forms 1,2,3-triazoles efficiently under mild conditions and is widely employed for selective analyte derivatization.32,33 To this end, a diazirine reagent with a propargyl ether group was synthesized and used for C–H insertion, with the products of this reaction subsequently tagged through CuAAC with the charge-tagged organic azide (4-azidobutyl)triphenylphosphonium hexafluorophosphate (Fig. 1c). While the derivatization sequence introduces additional steps and adds mass to the analyte, it is performed on analytical-scale samples. The objective is not synthetic efficiency, but enabling detection in a unified ionization regime.

Charged group

The triphenylphosphonium cation was selected as the ionic tag because this moiety confers exceptionally high electrospray response and is chemically inert to the reaction conditions used.34 The high electrospray response of compounds containing the triphenylphosphonium group arises from its structural rigidity and the delocalization and steric shielding of its charge, which minimizes ion pairing and promotes migration to the surface of charged droplets formed during ESI-MS.35 The chemical inertness and weak coordinative ability of the hexafluorophosphate anion complement alkyltriphenylphosphonium cations, forming salts that are readily soluble in the polar organic solvents used for ESI-MS. Methanol is used as the CuAAC reaction solvent, as the un-tagged portion of the aliphatic sample remains immiscible. Thus, the CuAAC reaction between the charge-tagged azide and propargyl ether effectively promotes the solution of the derivatized analytes. As such, the solution that remains after the CuAAC reaction may be diluted with dichloromethane to low µM concentrations and analyzed via ESI-MS without any further sample clean-up required.

Results and discussion

Cyclododecane benchmarking

To benchmark the reaction stoichiometry of the C–H insertion and subsequent click reactions, cyclododecane was used as a representative substrate. The products of this reaction contain one or more propargyl ether tethers (termed m), with one or more (n) of those groups having participated in the CuAAC tagging reaction (Fig. 2a). The major analyte signal (∼94%) consists of one propargyl ether tethered through C–H insertion to cyclododecane which has subsequently undergone the expected click reaction to incorporate the triphenylphosphonium charge-tag (Fig. 2b). Other minor products were observable in this reaction, corresponding to multiple reactions on the same substrate molecule. The next most abundant species was the (2,1) product which contains one unreacted propargyl group (Fig. 2c), followed by the (2,2) doubly cationic product (Fig. 2d). The full suite of reaction products detectable by ESI-MS is shown in Fig. 2e.

While three or more C–H insertion reactions into one substrate molecule are seen to occur in low abundance, no triply cationic species derived from three CuAAC reactions on one cyclododecane molecule were observed. This effect is likely due to increasing coulombic repulsion as more cationic groups are tethered to the analyte. The dominant (90+% of the ion current) product remains the singly tagged (1,1) species under the conditions employed, suggesting that multiple charging is not inherently favoured but can occur. Nonetheless, the ability to multiply tag and charge analytes could increase the effective mass range of compounds detectable by ESI-MS, which is advantageous for analysis on instruments with limited mass ranges (e.g. quadrupoles and ion traps).

Paraffin wax

After developing a reliable protocol for analyte tagging, we applied this method of analysis to real-world substrates. As an additional measure for structural characterization, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectral data were obtained for the sample analytes. The paraffin wax analyzed consisted of aliphatic alkanes with almost no tertiary carbon atoms indicative of ring-containing compounds, providing a mass spectrum consistent with that of a sample almost exclusively composed of alkanes fitting the CnH2n+2 formula. The most abundant peak in the spectrum at m/z 996 corresponds to the formula C27H56, after subtracting the mass of the [C35H34N3O2F3P]+ (m/z 616) moiety introduced by C–H insertion and CuAAC charge-tagging. As shown in Fig. 3a, the distribution of charge-tagged alkanes visible above baseline ranges from C21H44 to C40H82 (m/z 912–1180), with experimental and calculated isotope pattern overlays showing good accordance.36 While the lower carbon number analyte signals are composed almost entirely of n-alkanes, a minor abundance of CnH2n compounds becomes visible in the higher mass range of the signal envelope. As paraffin wax is typically produced through de-oiling and solvent recrystallization refining processes,37 it follows that compounds with one degree of unsaturation (DU) would become more difficult to remove from their saturated counterparts as molecular mass increases.

Comparing the most abundant signals in the minor products of the paraffin wax derivatization process (Fig. 3b), the most abundant signal in the (2,1) product is also the C27H56 alkane peak (m/z 1252) most abundant in the (1,1) product. In the doubly charged (2,2) product, the C29H60 alkane peak (m/z 820) is the most abundant, which shows that the effect of coulombic repulsion in the CuAAC step is non-negligible but low.

Mineral oils

After developing this methodology with the cyclododecane benchmark and demonstrating its utility on the comparatively simple paraffin wax, we turned our attention to more complex samples. The mineral oils analyzed with this methodology reveal complex mixtures consisting of hundreds of compounds over a wide range of degrees of unsaturation and number of carbon atoms (Fig. 4a and b). The light mineral oil and heavy paraffin oil mass spectra both contain a repeating pattern, where sets of signals arise from hydrocarbon analytes with increasing numbers of carbon atoms. Then, within each set of signals arising from each carbon number, the relative abundance of compounds with increasing DU can be seen (see insets, Fig. 4a and b). As ESI-MS analysis is not directly usable to distinguish between cyclic alkanes and unsaturated bonds, NMR spectroscopy was used to assist in characterization. While 1H NMR provides little information regarding specific composition, it does show the absence of sp2 carbon atoms and C–H bonds present in the samples tested. Additionally, 13C{1H} and 13C{1H} DEPT-135 spectra were both used to identify tertiary carbon atoms and the lack of sp2 hybridized carbon atoms in these samples. These results in conjunction confirm cycloalkanes as the species responsible for unsaturation in these samples, rather than any alkenes or aromatic rings (for comparison of paraffin and light mineral oil NMR spectra, see SI, Fig. S19–S28). Due to the complexity of these spectra, the (2,1) and (2,2) product signal patterns are not well resolved from the baseline, unlike the paraffin wax sample.

The most abundant analytes in heavy paraffin oil are in the C30–C34 range (m/z 1030–1100), whereas the most abundant analytes in the light mineral oil sample are the C22 and C23 compounds (m/z 920–940). In both cases, signals from the repeat pattern are discernible from the baseline up to m/z ∼1400 (C56). While both oils tested contain compounds with up to five DU, they differ significantly in their relative abundance. In the light mineral oil, hydrocarbons containing 0–1 DU are present in approximately equal abundance, with decreasing abundance of signals from compounds with 2–5 DU. In contrast, compounds with 2 DU are the most abundant in the heavy paraffin oil, and those with 1, 3, and 4 DU are more abundant than those with 0.

Semi-solid hydrocarbon grease

This method of analysis is also applicable to substrates which remain semi-solid at the C–H insertion reaction temperature. Through first dissolving diazirine and the sample in a small amount of a volatile solvent and mixing through ultrasonication before solvent removal, the diazirine is dispersed well enough to limit self-reaction. To demonstrate this, Vaseline® petroleum jelly was analyzed, with the resulting spectra shown in Fig. 4c. The component distribution in this sample is narrower than in the mineral oil samples, both in molecular mass and abundance of unsaturation. Compared to the mineral oils tested, this sample is more highly saturated, with the 2–5 DU components being much less abundant. The most abundant signals arise from the C28–C31 compounds (m/z 1006–1052), and the abundance of the lowest mass C22–C23 compounds (m/z 920–940) deviates from a normal distribution, showing higher signal intensity than that of the C24 compounds (m/z 948–954).

To demonstrate the utility of ion mobility spectroscopy as an important future avenue for separating overlapping derivatization products, we conducted IMS-MS experiments on several oligomers (see the SI for details; several different drift times were observed in each case).

Synthetic motor oil

To demonstrate the utility of this methodology as a fingerprinting tool, a sample of synthetic motor oil (Castrol EDGE, SAE 5W-30) was analyzed. This analysis shows that the chemical composition of this synthetic oil is similar in mass distribution to the paraffin wax tested, and it is composed primarily of saturated alkanes, with alkanes of one DU present in about half the abundance of their saturated counterparts with the same number of carbon atoms (Fig. 4d). There is a minor component of compounds with 2–3 DU, but the synthetic motor oil is composed of fewer types of hydrocarbons than the other oils analyzed. Though synthetic motor oils contain additives such as corrosion inhibitors, detergents, and antioxidants, a fingerprint of the hydrocarbon components was readily obtained without any sample pre-treatment. Interestingly, by analyzing this sample without pre-treatment, the antioxidant bis(nonylphenyl)amine is also charge-tagged and found in the resulting mass spectrum at m/z 1037.6 (Fig. 4e). The detection of this additive demonstrates that the derivatization chemistry is not exclusive to hydrocarbons and can capture polar additives.

Selective doubly charging analytes

The diazirine-derived carbenes formed in this process react more readily with O–H and N–H bonds than with C–H bonds. As a result, a 20 wt% loading of diazirine may represent a molar excess relative to total O–H bond content in an analyte sample. When analytes contain two terminal O–H bonds, this leads selectively to doubly cationic species forming after the CuAAC reaction. While not directly applicable to alkane analysis, this can be exploited in the derivatization of oligomeric species of high molecular mass, as is the case in hydroxy-terminated poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS) (2550–3570 cSt). As shown in Fig. 5a, the repeating signals in the mass spectrum arise from [(tag)2(H(C2H6SiO)nOH)]2+ (Fig. 5b) and are separated by 37 Da, half the mass of the (Me2SiO) repeat unit of PDMS (74 Da). The doubly cationic nature of the analytes is confirmed by the isotope pattern overlay shown in Fig. 5a, with individual peaks having a 0.5 Da spacing. As the signal approaches the baseline at m/z ∼3100, the charge-tagged PDMS oligomers in this region contain over 65 repeat units, with the peak centred at m/z 3072 arising from [(tag)2(H(C2H6SiO)66OH)]2+. The molecular mass of the PDMS oligomer in this analyte is 4913.0 g mol−1, illustrating the ability for effective mass range extension. 19F NMR spectroscopy is also useful in confirming the position of derivatization for the PDMS oligomers. As shown in Fig. S18, the major signal in the 19F NMR spectrum occurred at −78.6 ppm, consistent with the product of O–H insertion.38

Conclusion

The sequential C–H insertion and electrospray-tagging process provides a complementary approach for obtaining ESI-MS fingerprints of hydrocarbon-rich materials, enabling integration of non-polar hydrocarbons into ESI-based analytical workflows. Through initial development with a cyclododecane model, the method was then applied to more complex hydrocarbon mixtures, providing characteristic fingerprinting in a semi-quantitative manner. The ability to easily resolve and group constituents of an aliphatic sample first by the carbon number and within each carbon number into a profile of unsaturation levels provides a simple fingerprinting method for alkanes in oils and greases. Through the examples shown, the relative chemical simplicity of paraffin wax is contrasted with the immense chemical complexity of other hydrocarbon products. By chemically separating the components of more complex samples by polarity, this method should be broadly applicable to complement existing derivatization methods for the polar components of crude oil in petroleomics studies. Future work will focus on quantitative validation, comparison with established techniques, and incorporation of ion mobility to improve spectral resolution.

Methods

Procedure for thermal insertion reactions

All reactions were conducted under an air atmosphere. A 20 wt% solution of 3-(4-(2-(prop-2-yn-1-yloxy)ethoxy)phenyl)-3-(trifluoromethyl)-3H-diazirine in the substrate was prepared in a vial. A minimum volume of DCM (∼0.2 mL) was added, if necessary, to create a homogeneous mixture. All vials were sealed and heated at 40 °C for 30 minutes and then sonicated (280 W for 30 seconds at r.t.). Dichloromethane was removed in vacuo, and the reaction mixture was then placed in a heating block set to 100 °C for 3 hours. Afterward, the reaction was cooled to room temperature to afford the crude product, which was used in the next step without further purification.

Procedure for the click reaction

In a 2 dram vial the following reagents were added: (4-azidobutyl)triphenylphosphonium hexafluorophosphate (8.0 mg, 0.016 mmol, 1 eq.), C–H insertion crude sample (30 mg, containing 5 mg of diazirine, 0.017 mmol, 1.06 eq.), 1.2 M CuSO4 (7.0 µL, 0.0081 mmol, 0.5 eq.), l-ascorbic acid (3.0 mg, 0.016 mmol, 1 eq.), triethylamine (25 µL, 10 eq.), and methanol (2.0 mL, 8 mM). The solution was heated to 60 °C and stirred for two hours and subsequently cooled to room temperature to allow the unreacted excess substrate to coagulate. A sample for mass spectrometric analysis was prepared by dilution with dichloromethane to a final concentration of 8 µM on starting azide.

Mass spectrometry procedure

All mass spectrometry samples were prepared in dichloromethane and infused via a syringe pump at a flow rate of 20 µL min−1, with spectra collected using a Waters Synapt G2-Si in positive mode with a capillary voltage of 3.00 kV and a cone voltage of 15 V. Optimal signal intensity was obtained using a desolvation gas flow of 100 L h−1, a cone gas flow of 100 L h−1, a source temperature of 50 °C, and a desolvation gas temperature of 150 °C. Scan time was set to 1–3 seconds, with an inter-scan time of 0.1 seconds.
