Electron Heating in Hypersonic Flows: A New Thermodynamically Consistent Model
Felipe Martin Rodriguez Fuentes and Bernard Parent University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

Abstract
Accurate prediction of electron temperature ( T e T_{\rm e} ) in hypersonic non-equilibrium flows is critical, yet hampered by inadequate models for electron heating from vibrationally excited nitrogen. Prior models often relied on ad-hoc scaling or flawed applications of detailed balance that failed to ensure the convergence of electron and vibrational temperatures ( T v T_{\rm v} ) at thermal equilibrium. This paper introduces a novel, thermodynamically consistent electron heating model derived rigorously from the principle of detailed balance. By assuming a Boltzmann vibrational distribution and employing an effective activation energy, our approach yields a simple heating-to-cooling ratio of exp ⁡ ( θ v / T e − θ v / T v ) \exp(\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm e}-\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm v}) , where θ v \theta_{\rm v} is the characteristic vibrational temperature of nitrogen. This formulation guarantees that T e T_{\rm e} correctly converges to T v T_{\rm v} at equilibrium. A key advantage is that our model can utilize total cooling rates determined from swarm experiments, leading to higher accuracy at the low electron temperatures typical of hypersonic flight. This results in simulations that better agree with flight-test data, predicting an electron temperature up to six times lower than previous models. These more reliable predictions can significantly enhance the fidelity of plasma modeling for hypersonic technologies, including radio-frequency blackout mitigation, MHD aerocapture, electron transpiration cooling, and electromagnetic shielding.

1
Introduction

\dropword

Hypersonic flight above Mach 10 generates a surrounding plasma layer with significant implications. While known to potentially interfere with radio communications [
Davis et al. 2011
;
Hartunian et al. 2007
], this plasma also enables technologies such as electromagnetic shielding [
Gregoire et al. 1992
;
Musal 1963
], plasma antennas [
Magarotto et al. 2024
], electron transpiration cooling (ETC) [
Parent et al. 2021
;
Hanquist et al. 2017
] and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) systems [
Macheret et al. 2004
;
Moses et al. 2022
;
Parent et al. 2023
;
Parent et al. 2024
].

Harnessing these applications requires accurate prediction of plasma properties, fundamentally linked to the electron temperature (
T_{\rm e}
). Electron temperature governs not only the plasma frequency, crucial for electromagnetic interactions, but also influences plasma density through processes like electron-ion recombination and ambipolar diffusion to surfaces [
Parent et al. 2022
].

Accurate modeling of
T_{\rm e}
requires a precise description of the energy exchange between electrons and heavy particles via elastic and inelastic collisions. In the non-equilibrium conditions typical of hypersonic flows where nitrogen is not fully dissociated, the dominant energy exchange mechanism involves inelastic collisions with vibrationally excited nitrogen molecules (e-V coupling). Characteristically,
T_{\rm e}
is lower than both the bulk gas and nitrogen vibrational temperatures (
T_{\rm v}
), making electron
heating
through superelastic collisions the controlling process. However, direct experimental or molecular dynamics data for these heating rates are lacking, forcing models to infer them from the corresponding electron
cooling
rates.

Several approaches have been employed to model this e-V heating. Early models, used in works like
Shneider et al. 2010
, assumed the heating rate scales with the cooling rate by a factor of
T_{\rm v}/T_{\rm e}
. Similarly,
Kim et al. 2012
and
Farbar et al. 2013
proposed scaling based on the ratio of vibrational energies,
e_{\rm v}(T_{\rm v})/e_{\rm v}(T_{\rm e})
. While these formulations correctly drive the system towards
T_{\rm e}=T_{\rm v}
at equilibrium, they lack a rigorous derivation from fundamental principles, limiting their reliability in strong thermal non-equilibrium.

More recently, attempts by
Peters et al. 2019
,
Pokharel and Tropina 2023
and
Petrova et al. 2024
utilized the principle of detailed balance to connect heating and cooling rates. Although seemingly rigorous, this specific application leads to a significant flaw: it fails to guarantee convergence to
T_{\rm e}=T_{\rm v}
at equilibrium, thus violating thermodynamic consistency.

This work introduces a novel, thermodynamically consistent model for electron heating. Our approach derives the heating rates from the corresponding cooling rates by invoking the equilibrium constant relationship between forward and reverse reactions. This relationship is expressed in terms of the vibrational temperature using an effective activation energy and assuming a Boltzmann distribution for the vibrationally excited states. This method essentially satisfies detailed balance while ensuring the net energy transfer rate depends correctly on both
T_{\rm e}
and
T_{\rm v}
. Critically, unlike the models of
Pokharel and Tropina 2023
and of
Peters et al. 2019
, our formulation guarantees the correct physical behavior: it drives the electron temperature towards the vibrational temperature as equilibrium is approached and yields zero net energy exchange precisely when
T_{\rm e}=T_{\rm v}
. This provides a robust and physically sound closure for the electron energy equation.

The proposed model’s validity and predictive capability are assessed through simulations compared against experimental data from diverse test cases: (i) plasma flows in uniform electric fields, (ii) the RAM-C-II re-entry flight experiment, and (iii) a test case to evaluate the performance of previous and proposed electron heating models towards thermal equilibrium.

2
Transport Equations

For non-neutral or quasi-neutral plasmas, the motion of the charged species with
respect to the bulk can be found through the ‘drift-diffusion’ plasma model. A very good approximation is to neglect the inertia terms of electrons and ions as well as shear stresses or forces due to collisions between charged species, since these are negligible compared to forces in electron-neutral or ion-neutral collisions when the plasma is
weakly ionized
. Thus, the species velocities can be shown to be, as outlined in
Parent 2022
:

V^{k}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle V_{i}+s_{k}\mu_{k}{E}_{i}-\frac{\mu_{k}}{|C_{k}|N_{k}}\frac{\partial P_{k}}{\partial x_{i}}&\textrm{for charged species}\\
\displaystyle V_{i}-\frac{\nu_{k}}{\rho_{k}}\frac{\partial w_{k}}{\partial x_{i}}&\textrm{for neutral species}\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
V^{k}_{i}
is the species velocity,
V_{i}
is the bulk velocity of the plasma,
E
is the electric field,
\mu_{k}
is the mobility,
N_{k}
is the species number density and
P_{k}
is the partial pressure. As well,
C_{k}
is the elementary charge of single-charge species and
s_{k}
is the sign of the charge of the species under consideration (-1 for electrons or negative ions, +1 for positive ions). For neutral species, the velocity
V^{k}_{i}
is written in terms of the diffusion term with respect to the bulk, where
\nu_{k}
is the mass diffusion coefficient of neutral species
k
,
\rho_{k}
is the mass density and
w_{k}
is the mass fraction of species
k
. For each
k
th species, either charged or neutral, we can express the mass conservation equation as follows:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{k}+\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\rho_{k}V_{i}^{k}=W_{k}

(2)

The momentum equation for the bulk flow corresponds to the Navier-Stokes equations including the electric field body force:

\rho\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\rho V_{j}\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial\tau_{ji}}{\partial x_{j}}+\rho_{\rm c}{E}_{i}

(3)

where
P=\sum_{k}P_{k}
is the sum of all species partial pressures,
\tau_{ji}
is the Navier-Stokes viscous stress tensor and
\rho_{\rm c}=\sum_{k}C_{k}N_{k}
corresponds to the net charge density.

Within the framework of a non-equilibrium, three-temperature model, additional energy transport equations are required to obtain the translational, vibrational and electron temperatures. Because the vibrational energy coupling of diatomic air molecules such as
\rm O_{2}
and
\rm NO
is weak compared to that of molecular nitrogen, only the latter is considered in the vibrational energy transport. The nitrogen vibrational energy transport equation corresponds to:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\rho_{\rm N_{2}}V_{j}e_{\rm v}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\kappa_{\rm v}\frac{\partial T_{\rm v}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\\
\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(e_{\rm v}\nu_{\rm N_{2}}\frac{\partial w_{\rm N_{2}}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)=Q_{\rm vib}+\frac{\rho_{\rm N_{2}}}{\tau_{\rm vt}}\left(e_{\rm v}^{0}-e_{\rm v}\right)+W_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}\end{array}

(4)

where
e_{\rm v}
,
e_{\rm v}^{0}
and
\kappa_{\rm v}
are the nitrogen vibrational energy, vibrational energy in equilibrium and the nitrogen vibrational thermal conductivity, respectively. As well,
Q_{\rm vib}
is the net gain of energy from the electrons to the nitrogen vibrational energy modes (which will be outlined later), while
\tau_{\rm vt}
is the vibration-relaxation time taken from
Macheret et al. 2001
.

The electron energy transport equation is derived from the first law of thermodynamics applied to electrons as done in
Raizer 1991
neglecting electron inertia terms, and takes on the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{\rm e}e_{\rm e}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\rho_{\rm e}V^{\rm e}_{j}h_{\rm e}-\kappa_{\rm e}\frac{\partial T_{\rm e}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=W_{\rm e}e_{\rm e}+C_{\rm e}N_{\rm e}\bm{E}\cdot\bm{V}_{\rm e}\vskip 2.84544pt

\displaystyle+Q_{\rm elas}-Q_{\rm cool}+Q_{\rm heat}

(5)

where
e_{\rm e}
,
h_{\rm e}
and
\kappa_{\rm e}
are the electron specific energy, electron specific enthalpy and electron thermal conductivity respectively. In the latter
Q_{\rm elas}
is the electron heating-cooling due to elastic collisions, which we take from
Parent and Rodríguez Fuentes 2024
:

\displaystyle Q_{\rm elas}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{k}\frac{3\beta_{k}^{\rm n}k_{\rm B}|C_{\rm e}|N_{\rm e}N_{k}(T-T_{\rm e})}{m_{k}(\mu_{\rm e}N)_{k}}}_{\rm elastic\penalty\ heating-cooling\penalty\ to\penalty\ neutrals}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{k}\beta_{k}^{\rm i}N_{\rm e}N_{k}(T-T_{\rm e})\frac{6k_{\rm B}C_{\rm i}^{2}C_{\rm e}^{2}\ln\Lambda}{\pi^{3}\epsilon_{0}^{2}m_{\rm e}m_{k}\overline{q_{\rm e}}^{3}}}_{\rm elastic\penalty\ heating-cooling\penalty\ to\penalty\ ions}

(6)

with
\ln\Lambda
the recommended Coulomb logarithm which can be found in
(
Huba 2002
, page 34)
and
(\mu_{\rm e}N)_{k}\equiv(\mu_{\rm e}^{\star})_{k}
corresponds to the reduced electron mobility in the
k
th neutral species.

To arrive at an expression for the inelastic electron heating rate, let us first start with the rate of electron energy loss in inelastic collisions written in standard form [
Peters et al. 2019
;
Pokharel and Tropina 2023
]:

Q_{\rm cool}=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}^{\rm n}N_{\rm e}N_{k}\sum_{l}k_{kl}\mathcal{E}_{kl}

(7)

where
l
denotes an electron impact process,
k_{kl}
is the rate coefficient of the
l
th electron impact process acting on the
k
th neutral species,
\mathcal{E}_{kl}
is the activation energy of the
l
th electron impact process of the
k
th species and
\beta_{k}^{\rm n}
is 1 when the
k
th species is a neutral and to 0 otherwise. As was shown by
Parent and Rodríguez Fuentes 2024
, the electron cooling can be expressed as a function of the
k
th species reduced electric fields
E^{\star}_{k}
and reduced mobilities
\left(\mu_{\rm e}^{\star}\right)_{k}
without any loss of generality as follows:

\sum_{l}k_{kl}\mathcal{E}_{kl}=|C_{\rm e}|\left(\mu_{\rm e}^{\star}\right)_{k}\left((E^{\star}_{k})^{2}-\frac{3k_{\rm B}(T_{\rm e}-T_{\rm ref})}{m_{k}(\mu_{\rm e}^{\star})^{2}_{k}}\right)

(8)

We can then substitute the latter in the former to obtain the electron cooling:

Q_{\rm cool}=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}^{\rm n}|C_{\rm e}|N_{\rm e}N_{k}(\mu_{\rm e}^{\star})_{k}\left((E^{\star}_{k})^{2}-\frac{3k_{\rm B}(T_{\rm e}-T_{\rm ref})}{m_{k}(\mu_{\rm e}^{\star})^{2}_{k}}\right)

(9)

Expressing electron cooling function of reduced electric fields and reduced mobilities instead of rates for all cooling processes is more accurate because the reaction rates obtained using cross-sectional data and BOLSIG+ have considerable error especially when the electron temperature is less than 1 eV as is often the case in hypersonic flows. Indeed, the reduced electric field and reduced mobility can be obtained with high accuracy even at low electron temperature using swarm experiments.

The translational temperature
T
is determined from the total energy equation, obtained by summing all energy transport equations:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho e_{\rm t}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}V_{j}\left(\rho e_{\rm t}+P\right)\\
\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\nu_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}\frac{\partial w_{\rm N_{2}}}{\partial x_{j}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\rm n_{s}}\rho_{k}(V^{k}_{j}-V_{j}){(h_{k}+h_{k}^{\circ})}\right)\\
\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left((\kappa_{\rm n}+\kappa_{\rm i})\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_{i}}+\kappa_{\rm v}\frac{\partial T_{\rm v}}{\partial x_{i}}+\kappa_{\rm e}\frac{\partial T_{\rm e}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\\
\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\tau_{ji}V_{i}+\bm{E}\cdot\bm{J}\end{array}

(10)

where
h_{k}^{\circ}
stands for the heat of formation while the sum
(h_{k}+h_{k}^{\circ})
represents the enthalpy of the
k
th species including calorically-imperfect effects as well as the heat of formation, obtained from the NASA Glenn high temperature enthalpy polynomials by
McBride et al. 2002
. As well,
\bm{J}\equiv\sum_{k}C_{k}N_{k}\bm{V}_{k}
is the current density vector and
e_{\rm t}
is the total specific energy. Transport properties such as the neutrals thermal conductivity
\kappa_{\rm n}
, ion thermal conductivity
\kappa_{\rm i}
, mass diffusion coefficients
\nu_{k}
and mobilities
\mu_{k}
are found with the
Gupta et al. 1990
model. To close the system of equations, the electric field
\bm{E}
is here found as the negative of the gradient of the electric potential obtained from Gauss’s law:

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\epsilon_{r}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{j}}\right)=-\frac{\rho_{\rm c}}{\epsilon_{0}}

(11)

with
\phi
,
\rho_{\rm c}
,
\epsilon_{0}
, and
\epsilon_{\rm r}
the electric field potential, the net charge density, the permittivity of free space, and the relative permittivity, respectively.

At solid surfaces, we assume no surface catalysis for neutral species. For charged species it is assumed that electrons and ions recombine with a probability of 1 and secondary electron emission is modeled by the effective secondary electron emission coefficient
\gamma_{\rm e}
which is set to 0.1 for all test cases.

Chemical kinetics are obtained for 11 air species
\rm N_{2}
,
\rm O_{2}
,
\rm NO
,
\rm N

\rm O
,
\rm N_{2}^{+}
,
\rm O_{2}^{+}
,
\rm NO^{+}
,
\rm N^{+}

\rm e^{-}

.

Unless otherwise indicated, the mobilities, viscosity, thermal conductivity, and other transport coefficients are taken from
Gupta et al. 1990

3
Proposed Electron Heating Model

We here outline a derivation from basic principles of a novel electron heating model that both satisfies the detailed balance principle and that is thermodynamically consistent. This is here accomplished by first defining an
effective
activation energy as the average activation energy of all vibrationally excited states, as follows:

\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}\equiv\frac{\rho_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}}{N_{{\rm N_{2}}(v)}}

(12)

where
N_{{\rm N_{2}}(v)}
is the sum of the number densities of all
\rm N_{2}
vibrationally excited states (excluding the ground state). We can substitute the partial density of nitrogen
\rho_{\rm N_{2}}
by
N_{\rm N_{2}}m_{\rm N_{2}}
with
N_{\rm N_{2}}
being the number density of all nitrogen molecules and
m_{\rm N_{2}}
the mass of one nitrogen molecule. It is emphasized that
N_{\rm N_{2}}
is not the number density of the ground state of
\rm N_{2}
. Rather, it is the sum of the number densities of all
\rm N_{2}
states, include the ground state and all excited states. Then, using the symbol
f_{v}
to denote the fraction of N
2
within the
v
vibrational state, Eq. (
12
) can be rewritten to:

\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}f_{v}=m_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}

(13)

Noting that the sum of all
f_{v}
s including the ground state
v=0
must equal 1, it follows that:

\left(1-f_{v=0}\right)\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}=m_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}

(14)

We can obtain
f_{v}
from the Boltzmann distribution described in
(
Anderson 1989
, pp. 518, 578)
and
(
Capitelli 2012
, pp. 111, 418)
given
T_{\rm v}
:

f_{v}=\exp\left(\frac{-\mathcal{E}_{v}}{k_{\rm B}T_{\rm v}}\right)\left/\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{-\mathcal{E}_{n}}{k_{\rm B}T_{\rm v}}\right)\right.

(15)

where
\mathcal{E}_{v}
is the energy of vibrational level
v
. The assumption of an equilibrium distribution of nitrogen vibrational levels is valid as long as vibrational-vibrational and vibrational-translational relaxation processes are sufficiently fast. Such was already assumed in the previous section where a single vibrational temperature describes all vibrational energies of the nitrogen molecule. We can further assume an harmonic oscillator model without introducing significant error. Then, as outlined in
(
Anderson 1989
, page 436)
and
(
Vincenti and Kruger Jr. 1965
, pp. 134, 135)
, the
n
th vibrational state activation energy can be approximated as
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\theta_{\rm v}k_{\rm B}n
. In the latter,
\theta_{\rm v}
is the nitrogen characteristic vibration temperature which is set to 3353 K, as suggested in
Barrow 1963
. Therefore, the fraction of the ground state corresponds to:

f_{v=0}=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{n\theta_{\rm v}}{T_{\rm v}}\right)}

(16)

This infinite geometric series can be shown to become simply
(1-\exp(-\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm v}))^{-1}
following
(
Anderson 1989
, page 438)
. Then, it follows that the fraction of the ground state of nitrogen becomes simply:

f_{v=0}=1-\exp({-\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm v}})

(17)

In Fig.
3
, a comparison is outlined between the latter approximate expression assuming a harmonic oscillator model including all energy levels, and the anharmonic one outlined in Eq. (
15
) summing up to 25 levels. The anharmonic energy levels are taken from
Laporta et al. 2014
. It can be seen that the error is less than 2% when the vibrational temperature is 30,000 K or less.
We can substitute Eq. (
17
) in Eq. (
14
) to obtain a much simpler expression for the effective activation energy:

\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}=\frac{m_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}}{\exp(-\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm v})}

(18)

We can further rewrite the vibrational energy
e_{\rm v}
as a function of the vibrational temperature as follows:

e_{\rm v}=\dfrac{R_{\rm N_{2}}\theta_{\rm v}}{\exp(\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm v})-1}

(19)

where
R_{\rm N_{2}}
is the specific gas constant for molecular nitrogen. Substituting the latter equation in the former and noting that
R_{\rm N_{2}}=k_{\rm B}/m_{\rm N_{2}}
, the effective activation energy simplifies to:

\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}=\dfrac{k_{\rm B}\theta_{\rm v}}{1-\exp(-\theta_{\rm v}/T_{\rm v})}

(20)

Let us keep the latter on hold. The second step in our derivation process consists of defining an
effective
electron cooling reaction rate
k_{\rm cool}
such that, when multiplied by the effective activation energy, it yields the total electron energy loss due to inelastic collisions with the nitrogen molecules. Thus the effective electron cooling rate is equal to:

k_{\rm cool}\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}}\sum_{l}k_{{\rm N_{2}}l}\mathcal{E}_{{\rm N_{2}}l}

(21)

where
\mathcal{E}_{{\rm N_{2}}l}
and and
k_{{\rm N_{2}}l}
are the activation energy and the reaction rate associated with the
l
th electron cooling inelastic process of the N
2
molecule, respectively. It is important to note that the latter assumes that the inelastic cooling processes are limited to electron-vibrational energy exchange. Indeed, this assumption stems from the definition of the effective cooling rate depending on the effective activation energy which itself is limited to the vibrational states (see Eq. (
12
)). We will subsequently expand on the limitations of this assumption after the model is fully derived.

Now, we can express the effective reaction rate for electron heating processes from the effective cooling rate and the equilibrium constant as follows:

k_{\rm heat}=k_{\rm cool}\exp\left(\frac{\theta_{\rm e}}{T_{\rm e}}\right)

(22)

where
\theta_{\rm e}
is a closure coefficient proportional to the activation energy which will be determined later.

In terms of the effective electron heating reaction rate, the electron heating due to inelastic collisions corresponds to:

Q_{\rm heat}=N_{\rm e}N_{{\rm N_{2}}(v)}k_{\rm heat}\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}

(23)

where
N_{{\rm N_{2}}(v)}
is the sum of the number densities of all
\rm N_{2}
vibrationally excited states. But recall that the definition of the effective activation energy outlined in Eq. (
12
) entails that
N_{{\rm N_{2}}(v)}\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}=\rho_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}
. Further, we can isolate
k_{\rm heat}
in Eq. (
22
) and substitute:

Q_{\rm heat}=N_{\rm e}\rho_{\rm N_{2}}e_{\rm v}k_{\rm cool}\exp\left(\frac{\theta_{\rm e}}{T_{\rm e}}\right)

(24)

Now isolate
k_{\rm cool}
in Eq. (
21
) and substitute:

Q_{\rm heat}=N_{\rm e}\rho_{\rm N_{2}}\frac{e_{\rm v}}{\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}}\exp\left(\frac{\theta_{\rm e}}{T_{\rm e}}\right)\sum_{l}k_{{\rm N_{2}}l}\mathcal{E}_{{\rm N_{2}}l}

(25)

Finally, we can rewrite
\mathcal{E}_{\rm eff}
and
e_{\rm v}
in terms of
T_{\rm v}
using Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) to arrive at:

\displaystyle Q_{\rm heat}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\dfrac{\theta_{\rm e}}{T_{\rm e}}-\dfrac{\theta_{\rm v}}{T_{\rm v}}\right)\underbrace{N_{\rm e}N_{\rm N_{2}}\sum_{l}k_{{\rm N_{2}}l}\mathcal{E}_{{\rm N_{2}}l}}_{\rm rate\penalty\ of\penalty\ N_{2}\penalty\ inelastic\penalty\ cooling}

(26)

Noting that the last terms on the RHS correspond to the rate of inelastic cooling by the N
2
molecule, we can say:

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\rm heat}}{Q_{\rm cool}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\dfrac{\theta_{\rm e}}{T_{\rm e}}-\dfrac{\theta_{\rm v}}{T_{\rm v}}\right)

(27)

Recall that
\theta_{\rm e}
is a closure coefficient which needs to be determined. To ensure that the model is thermodynamically consistent, we will here impose that the ratio between the electron heating and cooling due to inelastic collision is 1 when the electron temperature equals the vibrational temperature. This will lead to a thermodynamically consistent model by ensuring that there is no net energy exchange in the limit of thermal equilibrium. It is easy to see that such will occur when

\theta_{\rm e}=\theta_{\rm v}

(28)

Recalling Eq. (
22
), and noting that
\theta_{\rm v}=\mathcal{E}_{v=1}/k_{\rm B}
with
\mathcal{E}_{v=1}
the activation energy of the first vibrational level of the nitrogen molecule, this choice of
\theta_{\rm e}=\theta_{\rm v}
to ensure thermodynamic consistency effectively entails that the equilibrium constant is here determined from the electron cooling process which leads to the ground state gaining energy and becoming the first vibrationally excited state. To assess whether the latter assumption is sound, we can obtain the fraction of electron cooling due to vibrational excitation of the first level and above. To do this, we compute the rates of inelastic cooling in Eq. (
26
) for vibrational excitation from ground state to the first four vibrational levels. We then divide each of the latter by the rate of
\rm N_{2}
inelastic cooling due to all excitation and ionization processes (vibrational, electronic, dissociative excitation and ionization). As can be seen in Fig.
3
, assigning
\theta_{\rm e}=\theta_{\rm v}
is an excellent approximation for hypersonic flight because more than 90% of the inelastic cooling of the nitrogen molecule is due to the excitation of its first vibrationally excited state within the electron temperature range 450–10,000 K.

Such a range of electron temperature is expected in most hypersonic or reentry flows. Indeed, should the air plasma reach thermal equilibrium, the temperature of the mixture would not exceed 10,000 K unless the flight Mach number is in excess of 33. For flows in non-thermal equilibrium, electrons are weakly coupled to heavy particles, and energy transfer from heavy species to electrons occurs primarily through inelastic collisions, especially with vibrationally excited molecules. Since the vibrational temperature of nitrogen is often significantly lower than the translational temperature and evolves more slowly, the electron temperature tends to equilibrate with the vibrational temperature, keeping it below the latter and typically well under 10,000 K.

Therefore, although setting
\theta_{\rm e}=\theta_{\rm v}
does not result in an exact detailed balance process, it does result in a process that is very close to the detailed balance for hypersonic flows while being thermodynamically consistent. Thus, the electron heating model we recommend is the following:

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\rm heat}}{Q_{\rm cool}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\dfrac{\theta_{\rm v}}{T_{\rm e}}-\dfrac{\theta_{\rm v}}{T_{\rm v}}\right)

(29)

Although the above derivation was done for the nitrogen molecule only, it is easy to see that it would remain valid for other molecules and can thus be applied to find the heating associated with all cooling processes due to vibrational excitation of all molecules. Nonetheless, we here only use the proposed model outlined in Eq. (
29
) for N
2
and ignore the inelastic heating associated with NO or O
2
. Such will be justified below through the analysis of the RAM-C-II results.

We outline briefly here the differences between our proposed electron heating model outlined in Eq. (
29
) and previous inelastic electron heating models. One such prior model outlined in
Shneider et al. 2010
assumed that the net electron heating-cooling rate scales with the difference between the vibrational and electron temperatures. This can be easily shown to lead to the following expression for the ratio of electron heating over electron cooling:

\frac{Q_{\rm heat}}{Q_{\rm cool}}=\dfrac{T_{\rm v}}{T_{\rm e}}

(30)

A second electron heating model that is thermodynamically consistent and that depends on the electron cooling rates is the one outlined in
Farbar et al. 2013
and
Kim et al. 2012
. Such assumes a Landau-Teller form of the energy transfer rate between electrons and vibrational modes. In this case the ratio between inelastic electron heating rate and inelastic electron cooling rate becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\rm heat}}{Q_{\rm cool}}=\dfrac{e_{\rm v}(T_{\rm v})}{e_{\rm v}(T_{\rm e})}

(31)

A third electron heating model by
Peters et al. 2019
used detailed balancing to obtain the inelastic heating rates in standard form, considering the first 8 vibrational levels of molecular nitrogen. Similarly as done in this work, the fraction of ground state nitrogen in each vibrational level was computed as an harmonic oscillator obeying an equilibrium distribution. The latter leads to the following relation between heating and cooling rates:

\frac{Q_{\mathrm{heat}}}{Q_{\mathrm{cool}}}=\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{v}}}{T_{\mathrm{v}}}\right)\right]\left(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{8}\exp\left(-\frac{n\theta_{\mathrm{v}}}{T_{\mathrm{v}}}\right)k_{n}^{\mathrm{inv}}\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)\left(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{8}k_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)^{-1}

(32)

where the summation is made over the first eight vibrational level excitations from ground state and from detailed balance
k_{n}^{\rm inv}=k_{n}\exp\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}/k_{\rm B}T_{\rm e}\right)
. In Eq. (
32
) the forward rates for vibrational excitation
k_{n}
up to
n=8
are obtained with BOLSIG+ using the Morgan cross-section data, extracted from the LXCat database by
Pitchford et al. 2017
.

Despite being thermodynamically consistent, the first two previous “phenomenological” models by
Shneider et al. 2010
and by
Kim et al. 2012
suffer from not satisfying the detailed balance principle. While the third model by
Peters et al. 2019
applies the correct detailed balance it is not guaranteed to ensure zero energy exchange when
T_{\rm e}=T_{\rm v}
. In contrast, the model proposed herein shown in Eq. (
29
) is not only thermodynamically consistent but also satisfies the detailed balance principle because it is derived using the equilibrium constant.

As well, as shown in Fig.

4
Numerical Methods

All results presented herein were obtained using the in-house CFDWARP (Computational Fluid Dynamics, WAves, Reactions, Plasmas) code. CFDWARP has the capability to integrate efficiently with aerodynamic-size time steps the physical system outlined in the previous sections.

The coupled system of drift-diffusion, fluid transport, and Gauss’s law equations is notoriously stiff, posing a significant numerical challenge due to the wide range of physical timescales involved. To effectively manage this stiffness and ensure efficient convergence to a steady state, a multi-faceted numerical strategy is employed. The governing equations are first recast to a different form following the approach outlined in
Parent 2022
and
Parent et al. 2015
. Such permits much higher integration time steps to be used by improving the mathematical conditioning of the system without altering the underlying physics.
The time-integration to a steady-state solution is achieved with tailored algorithms for different parts of the system. The discretized fluid flow transport equations are converged using a computationally efficient Diagonally Dominant Alternate Direction Implicit (DDADI) block-implicit method by
Bardina and Lombard 1987
chosen because of its good high-frequency damping characteristics. For the elliptic Poisson equation for the electric potential, which must be solved at each step, a combination of a Successive Over-Relaxation (SOR) algorithm
Douglas 1955
and an Iterative Modified Approximate Factorization (IMAF) algorithm
MacCormack 2001
is used to accelerate convergence.

For the spatial discretization of the governing equations, the convective fluxes are approximated using the
Roe 1981
scheme, an upwind method chosen for its shock-capturing capabilities in hypersonic flows. To extend this scheme to second-order accuracy in smooth flow regions while simultaneously preventing spurious, non-physical oscillations near discontinuities, the
Van Leer 1979
Total Variation Diminishing (TVD) flux limiter is utilized. A known potential issue with Roe’s scheme at high Mach numbers is the formation of numerical artifacts known as carbuncles. Such is suppressed by applying an eigenvalue conditioning technique based on the local Peclet number, as outlined in
Parent 2017
. This ensures robustness without compromising the sharp resolution of the viscous layer. The diffusion terms are discretized using a standard and stable second-order accurate central-differencing stencil.

5.2
Re-entry Flight Test Case

The RAM-C-II flight test was conducted in the late 60s to investigate ionization around the plasma flow surrounding a reentry vehicle at a freestream velocity of around 7.65 km/s [
Akey and Cross 1970
]. The vehicle is a 1.2-meter long body with a blunt leading-edge radius of 15.24 cm and a 9 deg cone half-angle, shown in Fig.
5.1
. In the second flight test electron density was measured at altitudes of 61 and 71 kilometers where the freestream static pressure corresponds to 19.62 and 4.844 Pa, respectively.

An assessment of grid-induced numerical error is shown in Fig.
5.1
for the 61 km altitude reentry case. The mesh is refined by doubling the number of grid lines in each dimension starting from a coarser
125\times 90

500\times 360

5

In Fig.
5.1
, a comparison of vibrational temperature around the RAM-C-II body is shown for altitudes of 61 and 71 kilometers. Corresponding electron temperature contours are shown in Fig.
5.1
obtained with different inelastic electron heating models. In the case of the
Kim et al. 2012
model, the electron temperature quickly relaxes towards the vibrational temperature (see Fig.
5.1
and Fig.
5.1
a,b); in contrast, the
Peters et al. 2019
model and the proposed inelastic electron heating model exhibit a large difference between vibrational and electron temperature by a factor of 5 to 6. The reason why the
Kim et al. 2012
model leads to very fast relaxation of electron temperature towards the vibrational temperature at both altitudes is that the latter model has (i) a higher heating over cooling ratio than the other models as discussed previously in Section III.A and (ii) stronger relaxation terms compared to other models as discussed in
Parent and Rodríguez Fuentes 2024
.

A key finding is that the proposed model predicts a significantly lower electron temperature than the
Peters et al. 2019
model, as shown in Fig.
5.1
. Although both models are derived from the detailed balance principle, this discrepancy in temperature stems from a crucial difference in how the total electron energy loss is calculated. The primary cause is our model’s inclusion of inelastic cooling from all neutral species, whereas the
Peters et al. 2019
model accounts only for cooling from N
2
. In the high-temperature region behind the bow shock, a significant amount of nitric oxide (NO) is formed. This NO provides an additional pathway for electron cooling—an effect not present in the
Peters et al. 2019
model and which leads to a lower overall electron temperature.

This approach leads to a critical modeling choice: while our model incorporates electron cooling by NO, it deliberately excludes electron heating from vibrationally excited NO. As can be seen from Fig.
5.1
, substantial amounts of NO exist only within a very narrow region of approximately 2 mm immediately behind the bow shock. The flow velocity through this region is quite high, at around 2700 m/s, meaning the fluid has a short residence time. Furthermore, the time required to vibrationally excite the NO molecules through collisions is much longer than the time they spend in this narrow region; the estimated relaxation distance for NO along a representative streamline (located 6 cm above the stagnation line) is at least 7 mm following
Streicher et al. 2022
and as much as 50 mm following
Millikan and White 1963
(using a representative gas temperature of 13,000 K, and NO partial pressure of 800 Pa). Such is several times larger than the zone where NO is abundant. As a result, the NO molecules pass through this high-concentration area too quickly to become vibrationally hot. Their vibrational temperature remains low and close to the freestream value, and because of this, NO acts as an effective sink for electron energy (cooling) but contributes negligibly to electron heating. It is noted that the above predictions for NO vibrational relaxation distances are determined from
\rm NO-NO
V-T relaxation and not
\rm N_{2}-NO
V-T relaxation because the latter is about a hundred times slower than the former while the partial pressures of NO and N
2
are in the same order of magnitude in the region of interest.

The effect of these electron temperature differences on the plasma density can be assessed by measuring the maximum electron density along the vehicle axis as shown Fig.
5.1
. Here the axial distance
x
is normalized with the leading edge radius
R
such that
x/R=0
corresponds to the stagnation point. We obtain better agreement to flight test data at 61 km using the proposed electron heating model with an error being within a few percents far from the leading edge. Much larger discrepancy to experimental data is found using the
Kim et al. 2012
and
Peters et al. 2019
models, with the latter models overpredicting peak plasma density by 80% and 98% respectively further downstream of the RAM-C-II leading edge. Such differences are mostly due to the decrease in electron-ion recombination rates as a result of the higher electron temperature.

At 71 km altitude, we again observe significant changes in the electron temperature between models similar to the 61 km case. However, the electron density here is not as sensitive to electron temperature, and much better agreement to flight test data can be observed across all models. In this case all models perform similarly and lead to a maximum 20% discrepancy to flight test measurements.

The small discrepancy between the proposed model and the flight test data could be due errors in reflectometer measurements. Indeed, experimental measurements of electron density inferred from the microwave reflectometers report a factor-of-2 error bar as discussed in
Grantham 1970
, partly attributed to phase errors due to probe motion.

As well, the use of the equilibrium constant to determine electron heating rates is an approximation that may be inaccurate for systems far from equilibrium, potentially contributing to the discrepancies we observed. The equilibrium constant is formally derived for conditions where the net change in Gibbs free energy is zero, which implies a single, uniform temperature throughout the system. While detailed balance holds at the microscopic level, the macroscopic forward and reverse rates for a reaction will not balance in non-equilibrium conditions, such as the plasmas in this study where different species have different temperatures. Unfortunately, a lack of experimental data prevents us from directly assessing the error associated with using this method for electron heating rates. To gauge the potential magnitude of this error, we can instead investigate another reaction where both the forward and backward rates are well-known: the associative ionization of nitrogen,
\rm N+N\leftrightarrow N_{2}^{+}+e^{-}
. For this reaction, the forward rate (
\rm N+N\rightarrow N_{2}^{+}+e^{-}
) is well-documented and can be found in
Park 1990
. We can then calculate the theoretical backward rate (
\rm e^{-}+N_{2}^{+}\rightarrow N+N
) by applying the equilibrium constant, which we derived from specific enthalpy and entropy data in the thermodynamic tables by
McBride et al. 2002
. When we compare this calculated backward rate to experimental data for the same reaction from
Sheehan and St.-Maurice 2004
, we observe that the results differ by a factor of 2 to 5. It must be emphasized that this error assessment applies only to the associative ionization of nitrogen, not directly to electron heating by vibrationally excited nitrogen. Nevertheless, this exercise effectively demonstrates that while using the detailed balance principle offers a convenient method that ensures consistency at equilibrium, it can introduce significant errors when applied to systems far from thermal equilibrium.

6
Conclusions

A new, thermodynamically consistent model for the inelastic heating term in the electron energy transport equation has been developed and validated for hypersonic air plasmas. This novel approach to modeling electron-vibrational (e-V) relaxation satisfies the principle of detailed balance and, crucially, ensures the correct equilibration of electron and vibrational temperatures in plasmas approaching thermal equilibrium.

A key advantage of this model is its formulation, which obtains electron heating by multiplying the total electron cooling rate by a simple algebraic factor dependent only on the electron and vibrational temperatures. This allows the model to be coupled with any cooling description, including those derived from swarm experiments. Using swarm data is particularly advantageous as it correctly accounts for all inelastic energy losses as a function of electron temperature, avoiding the separate and often incomplete cross-sectional data required by other methods.

The proposed model was validated against several test cases, including the non-equilibrium plasma flow around the RAM-C-II flight vehicle. In that case, the model predicted an electron temperature several times lower than previous models, which increased electron-ion recombination rates and resulted in improved agreement with flight test data. The model was also shown to be thermodynamically consistent in a dedicated thermal equilibrium test case.

Using the detailed balance principle via the equilibrium constant to estimate backward reaction rates is not without limitations. Since the equilibrium constant is derived under conditions of thermodynamic equilibrium, our model is accurate at equilibrium but only an approximation in situations of strong thermal non-equilibrium. Direct experimental data for electron heating rates from vibrationally excited species under these non-equilibrium conditions is currently unavailable. Such data is necessary to validate or refine the detailed balance approach.

The improved accuracy and consistency of this electron energy model carry significant implications for hypersonic technologies. Applications such as MHD aerocapture, electron transpiration cooling, and electromagnetic shielding rely on accurate predictions of plasma conductivity, which is a strong function of electron temperature and density. By providing more reliable predictions, this closure model can accelerate the development of these advanced plasma-based technologies.
