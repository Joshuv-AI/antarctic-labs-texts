Autoinflammatory syndromes in chronic urticaria are rare but clinically distinct: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ethan Bendayan, Jessica SS Ho, Megha Udupa, Fatemah AlYaqout, Moshe Ben-Shoshan, Catherine Zhu, Tania Cruz Marino, Michael Fein, Elena Netchiporouk
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Chronic urticaria (CU), including spontaneous and inducible forms, is a mast cell–driven disease with heterogeneous mechanisms such as type I and type IIb autoimmunity and aberrant mast cell activation.1 Although most cases follow these pathways, autoinflammatory syndromes (AIS) may occasionally present with urticaria-like eruptions, often accompanied by systemic symptoms and poor response to standard therapies.2 Understanding the frequency and spectrum of AIS in CU cohorts is important to guide recognition and appropriate work-up.

We performed a retrospective analysis of the prospective CUTAN-R registry (2013-2025) at the McGill University Health Centre, a certified Urticaria Centers of Reference and Excellence center.1,3 Standard investigations followed international guidelines and included complete blood count, thyroid peroxidase IgG, total immunoglobulin E, and C-reactive protein (CRP).2 Additional testing (eg, autoantibodies, skin biopsy, serum protein electrophoresis, and genetic testing) was pursued when symptoms, findings, or initial laboratories were abnormal. AIS work-up was specifically triggered by features such as persistently or episodically elevated CRP, systemic symptoms (eg, fever and weight loss), extracutaneous inflammation (eg, arthralgia and colitis), episodic flares, atypical hives (eg, nonpruritic, lasting >24 hours, or resolving with bruising/purpura). AIS diagnoses were based on established clinical diagnostic criteria; genetic findings served as supportive evidence.

Among 573 patients with CU, 36.0% were <18 years and 59.0% were female. After the phenotype-guided approach outlined in Fig 1, 16 (2.8%) patients exhibited red-flag features together with abnormal initial autoinflammatory investigations, prompting further evaluation; variants in autoinflammatory genes were identified in 14. AIS diagnosis was established in 8 (1.4%): Yao syndrome (n = 4), Schnitzler-like syndrome (n = 1), familial Mediterranean fever (FMF, n = 2), and adult-onset Still disease (n = 1) (Table I).4 All cases were refractory to guideline-based CU therapy, except 1 FMF case. For confirmed AIS cases, the median age at AIS onset was 34.5 years (IQR, 23.0-50.0), 75.0% were female, and only 1 FMF case was diagnosed during childhood, before urticaria onset. The most frequent systemic features were arthralgias (87.5%), fatigue (62.5%), recurrent fevers (50.0%), and elevated CRP (42.9%). One patient with FMF had no systemic symptoms at CU presentation, likely because of prior FMF recognition and management. Six additional patients carried variant of unknown significance in AIS-associated genes or were heterozygous for autosomal-recessive AIS genes (Table I).4 Although their contribution to disease pathogenesis is biologically plausible, current evidence is insufficient for AIS diagnosis.

Stepwise, phenotype-guided approach to evaluating autoinflammatory syndromes (AIS) in patients presenting with chronic urticaria. Patients with chronic urticaria (CU) are first classified as chronic inducible urticaria (CIndU) or chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU). Red-flag features (eg, recurrent fevers, inflammatory arthralgias, gastrointestinal or ocular inflammation, wheals lasting >24 hours, residual bruising or purpura, persistently elevated inflammatory markers, or marked refractoriness to guideline-based therapy) prompt stepwise evaluation. Initial investigations target common secondary causes (autoimmune, inflammatory, and paraproteinemic conditions), with skin biopsy when urticarial vasculitis or bullous pemphigoid is suspected. Genetic testing is reserved for patients with high clinical suspicion after exclusion of secondary causes, broad autoinflammatory gene panels including full NOD2 sequencing with intronic coverage are preferred. Targeted empirical therapy may be considered in patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, and selectively for variant of unknown significance (VUS) or heterozygous variants in autosomal recessive genes when clinical concordance is high. In a Urticaria Centers of Reference and Excellence center cohort, ∼1% of patients with CU had AIS, most often Yao syndrome. This frequency reflects a phenotype-enriched tertiary referral population in which genetic testing was selectively performed, and should not be interpreted as generalizable prevalence estimate. Aside from the patient with FMF with a longstanding diagnosis, all confirmed cases initially presented as CU; subsequent identification of AIS features prompted reclassification, suggesting these represent AIS mimicking CU rather that true overlap. Clinically, red-flag features including systemic symptoms, elevated CRP, atypical eruptions, and treatment refractoriness, were present in all confirmed cases, supporting their use as pragmatic triggers for AIS evaluation in daily practice (Fig 1).4 The proposed algorithm should be viewed as an expert-informed pragmatic framework rather than a validated clinical pathway. Limitations include the single-center design, small number of AIS cases, absence of functional assays for variant of unknown significance interpretation, and potential for circular reasoning in phenotype-guided genetic testing, particularly for Yao syndrome, where variants are frequently classified as variant of unknown significance and the syndrome itself remains incompletely defined5; results may not generalize to settings with different ancestry or referral patterns.
