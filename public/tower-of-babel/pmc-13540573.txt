Socioeconomic and Clinical Factors Associated a Tertiary Referral Cohort with Keratoconus Severity and Follow-Up in a Tertiary Referral Cohort [Letter]
Authors: Tingting Wang
Published: 2026

Dear editor

I read with great interest the article by Alrefai and colleagues entitled “Socioeconomic and Clinical Factors Associated with Keratoconus Severity and Follow-Up in a Tertiary Referral Cohort”, recently published in Clinical Ophthalmology.1 The authors are to be commended for conducting a comprehensive analysis of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables in a large, well-characterized cohort of 797 patients with keratoconus (KCN) followed over an 8-year period. While this study makes valuable contributions to the evolving literature on disparities in KCN diagnosis, management, and outcomes, I wish to raise several methodological and interpretive considerations—pertaining to study design, variable definition, and causal inference—that may further enrich the discussion and inform future research directions.

Inclusion Thresholds Systemically Exclude Mild/Early Disease

The tomographic screening thresholds used in this study—BAD-D≥1.6 and Kmax ≥47 D—were intentionally selected to maximize diagnostic specificity for clinically evident KCN.2 However, these criteria inherently and systematically excluded milder forms of the disease, including subclinical and forme fruste presentations. As a result, the final analytic cohort of 797 patients represents a population deliberately enriched for established, phenotypically manifest KCN.

This raises an important methodological consideration: the reported 81.8% prevalence of severe disease at presentation may reflect, at least in part, selection bias introduced by the study’s inclusion criteria rather than accurately representing real-world patterns of diagnostic delay. Consequently, interpretation of this strikingly high proportion of severe cases warrants caution.

“Other Ocular Disease” as a Predictor May Reflect Confounding, Not Causation

In the multivariable analysis, the presence of other ocular diseases was associated with both a reduced risk of loss to follow-up (odds ratio [OR] = 0.642) and a lower likelihood of disease progression (OR = 0.576). The authors attribute this association to surveillance bias, positing that patients with coexisting ocular conditions undergo more frequent ophthalmic evaluations.

An alternative explanation, however, merits careful consideration. Patients with other ocular diseases are typically older, exhibit poorer baseline visual acuity, and bear a higher burden of systemic comorbidities—factors intrinsically associated with increased healthcare utilization.3 Rather than reflecting a causal protective effect, the presence of “other ocular disease” may serve as a proxy for overall healthcare engagement and general health status, thereby introducing confounding that undermines causal inference. Consequently, interpreting “other ocular disease” as a clinically protective factor may be misleading.

The COVID-19 Pandemic Was Mentioned but Not Rigorously Controlled

The study period (2014–2021) included the COVID-19 pandemic era (2020–2021), which the authors explicitly acknowledged in the Methods section as a potential temporal confounder. However, beyond adjusting for “year of presentation” as a covariate in the primary analysis, the study did not conduct a segmented analysis or a sensitivity analysis comparing outcomes before and after the onset of the pandemic.4

The pandemic likely induced substantial, nonlinear disruptions to healthcare access, patients’willingness to seek care, and rates of loss to follow-up—disruptions that may have interacted synergistically with socioeconomic determinants (eg, disproportionately affecting low-income populations). A simple linear year-based covariate is unlikely to adequately capture this multifaceted complexity.

Recommendations for Future Research

To advance understanding of the socioeconomic and clinical determinants of KCN severity and outcomes, we propose three methodological refinements:

Application of the Heckman selection model to the full cohort of 21,157 screened records to correct for selection bias and yield unbiased estimates of disease severity—thereby addressing the potential overestimation of the observed 81.8% prevalence of severe KCN.

KCN disproportionately affects adolescents and young adults during critical developmental windows; progressive visual impairment associated with the disease adversely impacts educational attainment, employment prospects, and mental health.5 Effective, three-tiered prevention is therefore essential: (i) Primary prevention—population-level public education and behavioral interventions aimed at reducing modifiable risk factors and incidence; (ii) Secondary prevention—community-based screening programs coupled with targeted removal of socioeconomic barriers to ensure timely detection and referral; and (iii) Tertiary prevention—comprehensive, multidisciplinary care—including corneal surgery, visual rehabilitation, and psychosocial support—to restore functional capacity and quality of life in individuals with advanced disease.

In summary, although Alrefai and colleagues have offered valuable insights into disparities in KCN care, addressing the methodological limitations outlined above—particularly selection bias, confounding by comorbidities, and the impact of the pandemic—will be critical for future studies to translate these observational findings into actionable, evidence-informed strategies that mitigate inequities and improve clinical outcomes for this vulnerable population.
