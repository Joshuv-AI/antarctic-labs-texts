Phlebotomine sand fly vector distribution, seasonality, blood meal sources, and infection rates in cutaneous leishmaniasis endemic areas of Northeast Ethiopia
Authors: Melkam Tesfaye, Ahmed Zeynudin, Zeleke Mekonnen, Araya Gebresilassie, Kamil Abamecha, Delenasaw Yewhalaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is a significant health problem in Northeast Ethiopia. Leishmania aethiopica was identified in Phlebotomus longipes sand flies 50 years ago. However, the current sand fly fauna, distribution, seasonality, host preference, and Leishmania infection rates remain poorly understood. This study investigated the sand fly vectors distribution, seasonality, blood meal sources, and Leishmania infection rates in the area. Sand fly vectors were collected via CDC light traps in four villages from February to July 2024, and Leishmania DNA and blood meal sources were identified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). A total of 1,509 sand fly vectors were collected and identified morphologically using taxonomic keys. Phlebotomine sand flies were confirmed by molecular assays. The proportion of males and females varied significantly (χ² = 10.01, p = 0.018), and Phlebotomus longipes accounted for 97.3% (1,468/1,509) of the captures. The peak abundance occurred in June and July, with the potential for high CL transmission risk. Leishmania DNA was detected in P. longipes, with infection rates of 0.93%, 0.68%, and 0.40% in Kelina, Mergeja, and Laftota, respectively. Blood meal analysis revealed a preference for cattle (70.7%), followed by goats (16.3%) and humans (3.3%). The presence of Leishmania parasites in P. longipes suggests the role of this species in CL transmission. The detection of bovine, goat, and human blood meals indicates that P. longipes is opportunistic in its feeding behavior, depending on the availability of the host, with predominant for zoophilic tendencies. In general, this study highlights the need for continued entomological surveillance and further studies on P. longipes ecology and behavior to develop effective control strategies to reduce the burden of CL in the area.Keywords: Phlebotomine sand fly vector, Seasonality, Blood meal sources, Infection rates, Cutaneous leishmaniasis, EthiopiaSubject terms: Parasitology, Medical research

Introduction

Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is a neglected tropical disease caused by infection with protozoan parasites of the Leishmania genus and is transmitted by the bite of infected female phlebotomine sand flies1. Around 98 species of sand flies can transmit Leishmania, and these species belong to the genus Phlebotomus in the Old World and Lutzomyia in the New World2. The burden of leishmaniasis falls disproportionately on the poorest segments of the global population3. The clinical manifestations range from papules to nodules, resulting in atrophic scars and leading to disfigurement and stigma that persist even after treatment4. Nearly 350 million individuals are at risk worldwide, with the disease being endemic in more than 98 countries. There are approximately 12 million cases worldwide, with 2 to 2.5 million new cases occurring annually5. An estimated 20,000–50,000 cases of CL occur in Ethiopia6, and a risk map developed by Seid et al. estimates that up to 29 million people may be at risk of the disease7. Leishmania aethiopica is the main causative species, with both cutaneous and mucocutaneous forms of leishmaniasis being endemic in the country. Localized cutaneous leishmaniasis (LCL) is the most commonly observed clinical form8, and its endemicity is largely confined to highland regions of the country.

In the Old World, 42 sand fly species are recognized as proven or probable vectors of Leishmania. Of these, 20 are implicated in the transmission of L. infantum, six in L. donovani, seven in L. major, seven in L. tropica, and three in L. aethiopica9,10. In Ethiopia, Phlebotomus sand flies are the primary vectors transmitting both visceral leishmaniasis (VL) and cutaneous leishmaniasis. Phlebotomus longipes and P. pedifer are primary vectors of L. aethiopica11. For several cutaneous leishmaniasis species that are typically zoonotic, humans may constitute an occasional source of infection12. A laboratory study on hamsters and sand flies from Kenya near Mount Elgon confirmed that P. longipes is the vector of cutaneous leishmaniasis in this area, where cutaneous leishmaniasis cases have emerged13. Among the Phlebotomus subgenus Larroussius, P. longipes is the most extensively studied species. It is typically found in highland areas, which are typically above 2,000 m above sea level (m.a.s.l.) and rarely below 1,500 m.a.s.l. In Ethiopia, it has been implicated as a vector of CL caused by L. aethiopica. Phlebotomus longipes is predominantly found in the highland regions of Ethiopia, where it thrives under unique ecological conditions14,15. It is an opportunistic feeder known to feed on cows, domestic dogs11, hyraxes, sheep/goats, birds, and humans10. The presence of multiple blood meal sources, including domestic and wild animals, suggests that these sand flies are highly adaptable to varying ecological settings, which may increase the risk of CL transmission. In addition, P. pedifer has been identified as a vector of CL in Southern Ethiopia16–19. In Northeast Ethiopia, particularly along the eastern edge of the Ethiopian Plateau in Wollo Province, several areas have a documented history of CL15. Despite these preliminary studies, CL is still a public health problem and has spread to new foci in Northeast Ethiopia20, with limited reports of sand fly vectors seasonality, distribution, host preference, and infection rates in Northeast Ethiopia.

The seasonal dynamics of sand flies are influenced by environmental factors and ecology. Phlebotomus orientalis, a VL vector, shows seasonal abundance, with a peak in the dry season (March- June) and a decrease in the rainy season21. In Southwestern Ethiopia, annual peaks of Phlebotomus celiae are correlated with the wet months of the year (February–May and October–September)22. Seasonality and host availability affect the distribution and abundance of sand flies. They require a humid environment for egg hatching, larval development, and adult survival.

The distribution of Phlebotomus species in Ethiopia is influenced by altitude, vegetation, and soil type. For example, Phlebotomus orientalis inhabits lowland Acacia-Balanite forests in Metema, Humera, and Kafta-Humera, whereas P. longipes and P. pedifers are reported in highland regions of Ethiopia10,14,23,24. Different findings have revealed that Phlebotomus species in Ethiopia are opportunistic, with broad preferences depending on habitat and host availability11,25. In Ochollo, Southern Ethiopia, P. pedifer has been shown to feed mainly on rock hyraxes (Procavia capensis) in caves and humans in indoor environments17, whereas P. orientalis prefers to feed on bovines and humans26. A study from Northeast Ethiopia reported promastigote infections in Phlebotomus longipes sand flies, with infection rates of 6.5% among blood-fed females and 3.7% among all females11. Tesfaye et al. reported that Laftota, Kelina, Mergeja, and Dessie Ber, in Kalu, Dessie Zuria, Kutaber, and Delanta Woredas, respectively, are villages in Northeast Ethiopia that are endemic to CL caused by L. aethiopica20.

In the roadmap for Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) 2021–2030, the World Health Organization (WHO) lists CL as targeted for control through one of the core strategic interventions of vector control1. The mutual initiatives of the Ministry of Health-Ethiopia are aligned with global health targets to control CL by 2030 through the implementation of sand fly control27 and vector‒human interactions with possible breeding sites28. However, there is no leishmaniasis vector control program in Ethiopia6,27, and the existing information regarding sand fly vector seasonality, distribution, host preference, and infection rates from Northeast Ethiopia remains limited. Understanding these factors provides valuable information about vector‒host interactions and transmission dynamics for CL control and public health interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to determine phlebotomine sand fly vector distribution, seasonality, blood-meal analysis, and infection rates in areas endemic for cutaneous leishmaniasis in Northeast Ethiopia.

Results

Sand fly fauna, distribution, and seasonality

A total of 1,509 sand flies (570 males and 939 females) were collected from four villages at a male-to-female ratio of ≈ 0.61:1. Kelina recorded the highest number of both traps deployed and sand flies collected, whereas Dessie Ber recorded the lowest number. Compared with male sand flies, female sand flies were more abundant in all villages, with Kelina exhibiting the highest female-to-trap ratio (Table 1).

Molecular detection and identification of Leishmania species

Polymerase chain reaction was used to target a Leishmania-specific internal transcribed spacer region-1 (ITS1) gene, which was amplified via the primers LITS and L5.8 S to generate PCR products ranging from 300 to 350 base pairs (bp). Our PCR results revealed approximately 300–350 bp of product for both the positive control and pooled P. longipes samples. Digestion of the ITS1-PCR products with Hae III produced three distinct bands at sizes of ~ 200–220 bp and nonwell-resolving 65 bp and 23 bp in 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, matching the restriction profile of the positive control, L. aethiopica (Fig. 2).

A total of 156 nonblood-fed P. longipes pools (847 samples) and 92 blood-fed individuals of the P. longipes species were analyzed via a genus-specific ITS1‒PCR assay. The infection rates of P. longipes from Kelina, Mergeja, and Laftota were 0.93%, 0.68%, and 0.40%, respectively. Leishmania DNA was detected in six pools of P. longipes species (Table 2).

Blood meal source identification of Phlebotomus longipes

All 92 blood-fed P. longipes were analyzed via multiplex PCR, and 77 were successfully identified (Fig. 3). Blood meal analysis revealed three vertebrate hosts, which included goats, cows, and humans, indicating that P. longipes fed on multiple host species (Table 3).

Analysis of blood meal sources revealed that cows were the most frequent hosts across all study sites, with the highest proportion observed in Kelina (17.4%), followed by Mergeja (15.2%), Laftota (14.1%), and Dessie Ber (4.3%). Goat blood meals were detected at all sites except Dessie Ber, with the highest proportion in Mergeja (5.4%). Human blood meals were identified in Kelina and Laftota, each accounting for 1.1% of the samples. Mixed blood meals were primarily detected in Mergeja (4.3%). No identifiable blood meal patterns were detected in 15 samples, with the highest proportion in Kelina (6.5%).

The blood meal index of cows, goats, and humans were 70.7% (65/92), 16.3% (15/92), and 3.3% (3/92), respectively, in the sampling villages.

Discussion

Phlebotomine sand flies have considerable public health importance in the tropics and subtropics, which is attributed mainly to their role as the sole vectors of leishmaniasis10. Cutaneous leishmaniasis is strongly associated with highland regions, in which its transmission can be zoonotic or anthroponotic29.

Relative differences in the abundances of the collected sand fly species were recorded among the study villages. The highest abundance of sand flies per trap per night was recorded from Kelina, followed by Mergeja. Observations from the field study revealed that the inhabitants in these villages share houses with their animals. This might have provided favorable conditions for sand fly breeding because of the favorable decay of organic waste and the availability of blood meal sources30,31, but actual resting and breeding sites have not been fully reported in the area.

In this study, Phlebotomus longipes accounted for 97.3% (1,468) of the sand flies collected, with Sergentomyia species making up only 2.4% of the sampling areas. This finding aligns with previous reports that P. longipes is the predominant and widely distributed vector of L. aethiopica in northern and central Ethiopia10,11,15,32, whereas in southern Ethiopia, in a study in Ochollo, P. pedifer was identified as a vector of cutaneous leishmaniasis11,14–17. These regional differences highlight the importance of local entomological surveys in understanding disease transmission, in which control strategies need to be tailored to specific areas on the basis of the ecology of prevalent vector species.

Interestingly, the sex ratio was strongly biased toward females, with a male-to-female ratio of ≈ 0.61:1. This is because our collection sites were indoor and domestic sites, where male sand flies tend to rest less frequently in nonexposed areas than females do11,33.

Phlebotomus longipes was more abundant in June and July across the study villages, whereas it was less abundant in March and April. The Kelina study village had the highest number of P. longipes records, peaking in July. Mergeja and Laftota show similar trends, with higher sand fly abundance in Mergeja than in Laftota. Our finding was supported by previous reports in which Phlebotomus longipes was widespread in forested areas with relatively high rainfall and in warm weather following two rainy seasons: from March to June and from October to early December15. In southern Ethiopia, Phlebotomus pedifer abundance is lowest in the wet season23. Similarly, in northwest Ethiopia, P. orientalis shows marked seasonal variation in abundance that peaks mainly during the dry season (March to April)21.

Eucalyptus globulus and other varieties of native trees were common in all the study villages. The increased relative humidity from rainfall and temperature changes during March and April significantly influenced the abundance of sand flies in the area. Understanding seasonal sand fly population dynamics enables the prediction of transmission risks for targeted vector control strategies to be implemented to reduce the risk of CL transmission21–24.

Leishmania parasites were detected in Phlebotomus longipes, with infection rates of 0.93%, 0.68%, and 0.40% in Kelina, Mergeja, and Laftota, respectively. The highest proportion of infections was observed in samples from Kelina. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that P. longipes serves as a vector contributing to the transmission of cutaneous leishmaniasis caused by Leishmania aethiopica in the Ethiopian highlands11,14,34.

This suggests that there was variation in the prevalence of Leishmania infection among the study villages, highlighting the need for targeted vector control and continued monitoring in areas with confirmed Leishmania infections.

Although P. sergenti is another suspected vector, our results confirm that P. longipes was most likely the primary and the sole vector of L. aethiopica in the area, not only because it was the only species found infected with L. aethiopica but also because of its high density and infection rate. L. aethiopica was first detected in P. longipes in Northeast Ethiopia nearly 50 years ago11,15. The infection rates from our study were lower than the reported infection rate (4%) from Northwest Ethiopia24, possibly because the sand flies were collected from indoor and peridomestic habitats, even though hyrax populations were observed in the vicinity of the study villages, and the sand fly flight range might have affected capture in indoor and peridomestic areas. Given that P. longipes was the only sand fly species found to be infected and present in relatively high abundance, our findings confirmed its role as the sole vector of L. aethiopica in the area. This finding highlights the importance of sustained entomological surveillance, ecology, and behavior of P. longipes and other potential vectors for developing localized interventions to effectively control CL transmission in this area.

A study of blood meal sources revealed that cows were the most common hosts across all sites, followed by goats. The goat blood meals were identified at all the sites except Dessie Ber, with the highest percentage in Mergeja (5.4%). Human blood meals are rare and are detected only in Kelina and Laftota. Mixed blood meals were also detected, primarily in Mergeja (5.4%). A small proportion of samples tested negative for blood meal sources, ranging from 1.1% in Dessie Ber to 6.5% in Kelina. These undetected samples may be other domestic animals not included during our molecular analysis.

The blood meal analysis of P. longipes revealed that the vector preferentially feeds on cows, followed by goats and humans, with blood meal indexes of 70.7% (65/92), 16.3% (15/92), and 3.3% (3/92), respectively, indicating the anthropozoophilic habit of the vector. This finding is in line with previous reports in the area by Ashford et al.11, which highlight the importance of domestic animals as primary blood meal sources for P. longipes in the area. Reports on other Phlebotomus species, such as P. orientalis, a vector for visceral leishmaniasis in Sudan and Ethiopia, show strong preferences for cattle and other domestic animals22,35. These findings indicate that Phlebotomus longipes exhibit both zoophilic and anthropophagic tendencies and are opportunistic feeders; however, zooprophylaxis (the use of animals to divert vectors from humans) or zoopotentiation should be further investigated.

The strengths of the present study were the utilization of advanced molecular techniques for the detection of parasites in sand fly vectors and blood meal sources of sand flies and the ability to conduct longitudinal entomological studies in multiple study villages to better understand sand fly fauna, seasonality, blood meal sources, and infection patterns. However, our study was not without limitations, as we determined blood meal sources for only cows, goats, and humans, and the identification of additional potential vertebrate hosts, such as dogs and hyraxes, was not assessed due to limited resources. In addition, owing to the occurrence of internal conflicts in the area when this study was conducted, we were not able to collect sand flies in the field and rocky caves outside villages in which hyrax might be available.

Conclusions

Overall, P. longipes was the predominant species across all the study villages, showing higher density in June and July, with the potential for high CL transmission risk. Spatial and seasonal variations in sand fly abundance highlight the need for further studies on the influence of environmental conditions, human–animal proximity, and ecological factors on transmission risk. The presence of Leishmania infections in Phlebotomus longipes suggests the role of this species in CL transmission in the study area. Higher infection rates were recorded in samples from Kelina. Bovine, goat and and human blood meal sources indicated that P. longipes is anthropozoophilic, which could be opportunistic depending on the availability of the host, but the role of domestic animals in CL zoopotentiation and zooprophylaxis should be further investigated. In general, this study highlights the need for continued entomological surveillance and further studies on the ecology and behavior of Phlebotomus longipes to develop effective control strategies to reduce the burden of CL in the area.

Materials and methods

Study area

An entomological study was conducted between February and July 2024 in four CL endemic villages (Fig. 4) in Northeast Ethiopia: Mergeja (11.31 N; 39.49 E; 2650 m.a.s.l.) in Kutaber; Kelina (11.18 N; 39.23 E; 2308 m.a.s.l.) in Dessie Zuria; Dessie Ber (11.59 N; 39.21 E; 2943 m.a.s.l.) in Delanta; and Laftota (11.18 N; 39.23 E; 2132 m.a.s.l.) in Kalu20. The study areas were rural, and inhabitants commonly raised livestock such as cattle, chickens, horses, donkeys, sheep, and goats as primary sources of livelihood. Hyrax colonies are known to occur in the vicinity of the study villages.

Map showing the location of four study villages in Northeast Ethiopia where sand flies were collected.

Sand fly collection and processing

The study villages in each district were selected on the basis of the reported burden of CL and accessibility. Houses for sand fly trapping were selected randomly at approximate distances of more than 100 m each. Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) light traps were suspended with the fan 40–50 cm above ground level. The traps were set 1 h before sunset and collected between 07:00 and 08:00 a.m. the following day. Sand flies were collected from two main locations: peridomestic (outdoor locations near the entrance and living areas of humans and domestic animals) and indoor (inside human dwellings and domestic animal shelters) locations. Sample collection was conducted for two consecutive nights per month following the methodology described by Gebresillassie et al.21.

Sand flies captured by CDC light traps were immobilized with chloroform and sorted by sand fly genus, sex, and abdominal status (Fig. 5). The female Phlebotomus sand flies were further sorted as unfed (or gravid) and freshly fed. Each sample was placed into a labeled Eppendorf tube over silica gel, indicating the date of collection, village, collection site, sex, number, and genus. The sorted and counted samples were then transported to center for malaria and neglected tropical diseases research- Wollo University and stored at − 20 °C for subsequent molecular analysis.

Similarly, freshly fed sand fly vectors were processed for blood meal source determination as follows: the head of each fed female was carefully separated from the thorax and slide-mounted for later species identification. The remaining body parts (thorax and abdomen) were individually placed in labeled Eppendorf tubes containing silica gel and cotton pads and then transported to Wollo University for storage at −20 °C for subsequent blood meal and parasite detection via PCR.

After processing, the sand flies were sorted by sex and genus (Phlebotomus or Sergentomyia spp.) and preserved in absolute ethanol for further species identification.

Morphological identification

Sand flies collected during the study were mounted on microscope slides, with their heads and genitalia separated from the thorax and abdomen. Species-level identification was performed on the basis of morphological characteristics, including male external genitalia and, for females, the pharynx, antennal features, and spermathecae, following established taxonomic keys36 (Fig. 6).

Key morphological characterstics of Phlebotomus longipes from the Kelina study village: (a) ♂ pharynx (400x); (b) terminalia (100x); (c) ♀ spermatheca (400x).

Molecular detection and identification of Leishmania parasites

Blood-fed Phlebotomus samples were sorted and labeled according to the sampling date and collection site. DNA was extracted from these samples via an animal tissue DNA isolation kit (SIMEGEN, China, Cat. No. 3101060, lot no. 20230221) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the animal tissue protocol. Conventional PCR was performed under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 48 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 2 min and a final hold at 40 °C. The extracted DNA (50 µl final volume) was stored at −20 °C until further use.

A polymerase chain reaction-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) method was employed to detect and identify Leishmania parasites. The internal transcribed spacer 1 region of ribosomal DNA was amplified via the primer pair L5.8 S (5’-TGA TAC CAC TTA TCG CACTT-3’) and LITSR (5’-CTG GAT CAT TTT CCG ATG-3’) as previously described37, which was combined with the amplification of the sand fly house-keeping 12 S ribosomal RNA gene, using the modified primers FSF165: 5’TGGCGGTATTTTAGTCTATTCAG and RSF165: 5’AACTGCACCTTGATTTGACAT. The amplification of the sand fly gene was used to rule out false-negative results. The 12 S rRNA PCR yielded a product of approximately 165 bp for all the phlebotomine sand flies examined38. Amplicons (12 µL) were electrophoresed on a 1.5% agarose gel (GSTM-Agarose, Geneshun Biotech, Lot No: 1905824) containing ethidium bromide for 1 h at 80 V and visualized under ultraviolet (UV) light. A reaction was considered positive for Leishmania when a band of 300–350 bp was observed (Fig. 2). Positive samples were then digested with the restriction enzyme HaeIII, and the resulting restriction fragment profiles were compared to those of positive controls to identify the Leishmania species (Fig. 2). To ensure the reliability and validity of the PCR results and monitor for possible contamination, both negative and positive controls were included in each run. The negative control (NC) consisted of distilled water with no L. aethiopica DNA, whereas the positive control (PC) included DNA from the reference species L. aethiopica promastigotes obtained from the Armauer Hansen Research Institute of Ethiopia. All samples, PCs, and NCs were processed under the same PCR conditions.

Blood meal source identification via multiplex PCR

To identify the sources of blood meals in blood-fed sand flies, PCR assays were conducted via species-specific forward primers and a universal primer. The universal reverse primer used was UnRev1025 (5’-GGTTG [T/G] CCTCCAATTCATGTTA-3). The forward primers used were as follows: Human741F (5’-GGCTTACTTCTCTTCATTCTCTCCT-3’), Goat894F (5’-CCTAATCTTAGTACTTGTACCCTTCCTC-3’), Cow121F (5’-CATCGGCACAAATTTAGTCG-3’), Dog368F (5’-GGAATTGTACTATTATTCGCAACCAT-3’), and Pig573F (5’-CCTCGCAGCCGTACATCTC-3’), which target human, goat, cow, dog, and pig DNA, respectively39. Conventional PCR was performed under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min; annealing at 55 °C for 1 min and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min; and a final hold at 40 °C. The final reaction volume was 50 µl. The amplified DNA products were stored at − 20 °C until further use. For negative controls, all PCR reagents were included without the addition of DNA. Additionally, male P. longipes sand flies were used as internal negative controls during all DNA extractions.

Amplicons (12 µL) were electrophoresed on a 1.5% agarose gel (GSTM-Agarose, Geneshun Biotech, Lot No: 1905824), stained with ethidium bromide for 1 h at 80 V, and visualized under ultraviolet (UV) light. The PCR products yielded fragment sizes of 334 bp, 123 bp, 680 bp, 561 bp, and 453 bp, corresponding to human, goat, dog, cow, and pig blood meal sources, respectively (Fig. 3).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 14 (Stata Corp LLC, USA) and Microsoft® Excel 2010 following thorough data entry and cleaning. Descriptive statistics including, frequencies and percentages were employed to assess the distribution, seasonality, blood meal sources, and infection rates of sand fly vectors. Results were presented using tables and a bar chart, with monthly abundance visualized to highlight seasonality across sampling villages. Pearson’s chi-square test was performed to examine sex-based variation in sand fly distribution among villages. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of P < 0.05.

Quality assurance

To ensure quality and data reliability, all quality control checks were performed before, during, and after data collection. DNA yield and quality were checked via a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer at 260/280 nm after setting the background reading to zero with an elution solution. A 260/280 ratio of ~ 1.8 corresponds to pure DNA. Our NanoDrop spectrophotometer results were confirmed by observing intact DNA fragments near the sample well after electrophoresis.

For PCR-RFLP, negative and positive controls with distilled water and L. aethiopica DNA, respectively, were included during the DNA extraction and PCR runs to ensure reliability and validity and to check for contamination or inhibition. L. aethiopica was obtained from the Amaure Hansen Research Institute (AHRI), Ethiopia, as a positive control (PC). In each extraction batch, one negative control for extraction (NC: distilled water) and a PC were included. All PCs, extraction NCs, no template controls (nuclease-free water), and samples were subjected to PCR under the same conditions.
