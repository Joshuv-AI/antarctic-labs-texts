Ursodeoxycholic acid mitigates cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury by inhibiting thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation through activation of ALDH3A1
Authors: Anqi Liu, Tongling Yang, Zhixian Li, Ruyue Luo, Chunyu Zhu, Siyu Du, Wenli Chen, Jialin Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThrombin accumulation following ischemic stroke (IS) promotes lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis to exacerbate tissue injury; however, effective interventions targeting this pathological process remain limited. Although bile acids (BAs) have demonstrated potential benefits against IS, their alterations and specific roles in IS pathogenesis are still poorly understood.This study was designed to further validate the detrimental effects of thrombin in neuronal injury, investigate BA profile changes in an IS model, and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.Serum and cerebral bile acid profiles in a mouse middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model were analyzed. Infarct volume, neurological deficits, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis were assessed. RNA sequencing was employed to explore potential mechanisms, followed by verification using pharmacological inhibitors.Results showed that MCAO induced upregulation of thrombin and its receptor PAR1 in neurons, leading to lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, and subsequent neuronal injury. Bile acid profiles in brain tissues were significantly altered, and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) levels were negatively correlated with infarct size. Furthermore, UDCA supplementation alleviated thrombin-induced neuronal lipid peroxidation, restored mitochondrial function, suppressed ferroptosis, and improved neurological outcomes. Mechanistically, transcriptomic analysis revealed significant changes in arachidonic acid metabolism and aldehyde dehydrogenase 3A1 (ALDH3A1) expression. UDCA was found to upregulate ALDH3A1, thereby mitigating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation—an effect that was reversed by ALDH3A1 inhibition. We further demonstrated that UDCA upregulated ALDH3A1 through the TGR5-PKA signaling pathway, which mediated Nrf2 nuclear translocation and its subsequent binding to the Aldh3a1 promoter. In summary, UDCA confered neuroprotection against thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation in IS through the TGR5/PKA/ALDH3A1 axis. These findings identified UDCA as a promising therapeutic candidate for IS and reveal a novel signaling mechanism underlying its neuroprotective effects.Keywords: Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), Ischemic stroke, Thrombin, Lipid peroxidation, Ferroptosis, TGR5-PKA-ALDH3A1 signaling

Introduction

Stroke remains a significant global health challenge, imposing a considerable burden on society due to its high incidence and mortality rates [1]. Annually, approximately 13.5 million new stroke cases and 5.5 million stroke-related deaths are reported worldwide [2]. Ischemic stroke (IS), which constitutes 70%–80% of all cases, is primarily driven by the occlusion of cerebral blood vessels due to thrombus formation [3] [4,5]. Thrombin, a critical mediator of thrombogenesis and the coagulation cascade, is pivotal in the pathogenesis and risk of IS [6]. Studies indicated that thrombin levels in the infarction core of the brain are nearly double those in the contralateral region, with thrombin activity positively correlating with infarct volume [7,8]. This abnormal accumulation of thrombin was considered a significant contributor to post-stroke brain injury.

Thrombin exerts its effects in the brain primarily through protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1) [9]. Under normal physiological conditions, low levels of thrombin-PAR1 signaling help maintain brain homeostasis [10]. However, after a stroke, excessive thrombin accumulation leads to sustained PAR1 activation, which triggers severe neurotoxic effects, including excessive production of reactive oxygen species, lipid peroxidation, and neuronal cell death. These pathological processes collectively exacerbate brain damage [[11], [12], [13]]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting thrombin-mediated injury represent a promising approach to attenuating the progression of ischemic stroke.

Bile acids (BAs), the primary end products of cholesterol catabolism, are increasingly acknowledged as signaling molecules that regulate lipid, glucose, and energy metabolism, as well as neuronal function within the central nervous system (CNS) [14]. Clinical and experimental studies have consistently reported dysregulated BA metabolism in stroke patients. Furthermore, specific BAs have been demonstrated to exert neuroprotective effects in models of neurodegenerative diseases [15]. For instance, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), originally developed for treating cholestatic liver disease, has shown promise in slowing disease progression in conditions such as Parkinson's disease (PD) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) [16,17]. UDCA can reduce the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and behavioral impairments by suppressing the expression of p38 MAPK and JNK [18]. Tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) alleviates Parkinson's disease by increasing p-AMPK levels in the mouse cortex [19]. Furthermore, TUDCA regulates neuroinflammation after traumatic brain injury (TBI) by inhibiting the STING/NF-κB pathway [20]. Despite these advances, there remains a paucity of knowledge regarding the specific alterations in BA profiles following IS, as well as the potential effects of BAs against thrombin mediated nerve injuries.

Aldehyde dehydrogenase 3A1 (ALDH3A1), a NAD(P) + -dependent enzyme, plays a crucial role in detoxifying both endogenous and exogenous aldehydes. This detoxification process is essential for maintaining redox homeostasis and regulating key cellular functions, including proliferation, differentiation, and responses to oxidative stress [21]. Activation of ALDH3A1 has been shown to scavenge hydroxyl radicals and preserve mitochondrial integrity [[22], [23], [24]]. In contrast, inhibition of ALDH3A1 has been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and an increase in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [25]. While the enzyme's functions have been explored in various pathological contexts, its specific role in IS remains undefined.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the expression and role of thrombin in ischemic stroke (IS), describe the changes in BAs profiles after stroke, and investigate the effects of differentially expressed BAs, particularly UDCA, on thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation and the associated transcriptome-based regulatory mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Animal study design and treatment regimen

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwestern Polytechnical University (NO. 202501161) and performed in compliance with relevant national guideline and the ARRIVE guidelines [26,27]. Male C57BL/6J mice (20–25 g) were obtained from Huaren Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shaanxi, China). The mice were housed under standard conditions at a controlled temperature (22 ± 2°C) and humidity (50 ± 10%), with a 12-h light/dark cycle and has access to food and water. All mice underwent a one-week acclimatization period prior to experimental procedures. For the PAR1 antagonist SCH79797 (MCE, New Jersey, USA), SCH79797 was dissolved in DMSO (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to prepare a stock solution, which was then diluted with saline to the desired concentration. Drug administration was initiated 24 h prior to surgery and continued daily for 3 consecutive days postoperatively. For UDCA (TargetMol, Massachusetts, USA) supplementation, UDCA was suspended in saline and administered by oral gavage beginning 4 days prior to MCAO modeling, with continued administration for 3 days postoperatively.

Experiment 1-mice were randomly divided into three groups: Sham, MCAO, and SCH (50 μg/kg, i.p.). Investigating changes in thrombin and PAR1 expression after stroke, verifying whether the thrombin-PAR1 pathway promotes the pathological progression of stroke.

Experiment 2-mice were randomly divided into two groups: Sham and MCAO. The changes in bile acids after stroke were studied (n = 15). Subsequent analyses included correlation analysis between bile acid changes and infarct volume (n = 10), and correlation analysis between histopathological staining and UDCA (n = 5).

Experiment 3-mice were randomly divided into four groups: Sham, MCAO, low-dose UDCA group (60 mg/kg), and high-dose UDCA group (120 mg/kg). The therapeutic effect of UDCA was evaluated, and the optimal dose of UDCA (120 mg/kg) was selected based on efficacy for the next experiment.

Experiment 4-mice were randomly divided into four groups: Sham + AAV-con, MCAO + AAV-con, UDCA + AAV-con, and UDCA + AAV-ALDH groups, to verify whether lipid peroxidation damage is dependent on ALDH3A1. After completing the experiment, collect mouse brains and blood samples. Fix brains in 4% paraformaldehyde or freeze them directly at −80°C. Separate serum from blood by centrifugation and freeze the serum at −80°C.

Experiment 5-mice were randomly divided into four groups: Sham, MCAO, UDCA, and UDCA + TGR5 inhibitor SBI-115 (2 μg/mouse) (MCE, New Jersey, USA) groups, to verify whether ALDH3A1-mediated lipid peroxidation damage is dependent on TGR5 signaling. After completing the experiment, collect mouse brains and blood samples. Fix brains in 4% paraformaldehyde or freeze them directly at −80°C. Separate serum from blood by centrifugation and freeze the serum at −80°C.

The MCAO/R model was developed based on previous reports [28,29]. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal administration of sodium pentobarbital. Under microscopic guidance, the right internal carotid artery, external carotid artery, and common carotid artery were carefully dissected. The right internal carotid arterywas ligated, and a small incision was made in the common carotid artery. A nylon plug (Beijing Xinong Technology Co., Ltd.) was inserted through this incision and advanced to the arterial bifurcation. After 1 h of ischemia, gently remove the nylon plug to restore blood flow and induce reperfusion. After surgery, place the mouse on a regulated heating pad (set to approximately 37°C) to maintain body temperature until it is fully awake and maintaining normal body temperature. The animals in the sham surgery group underwent identical surgical preparation and exposure of the middle cerebral artery, but without occlusion.

Stereotaxic intracranial injection

The TGR5 inhibitor SBI-115 was administered via stereotactic injection 30 min before MCAO. Perform stereotactic intracranial injection as described above [30,31]. Following anesthesia induction, mice were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. SBI-115 (2 μg/mouse) or AAV-ALDH (5.2 × 1012vg/mL) was infused into the ipsilateral lateral ventricle via a Hamilton microsyringe at a rate of 0.2 μl/min. The stereotactic intracerebroventricular injection site was referenced to bregma at coordinates: anteroposterior −1.0 mm, mediolateral −1.5 mm, and dorsoventral −3.5 mm. After infusion, the needle was retained for 5 min to prevent fluid leakage. Slowly withdraw the needle, suture the incision, and allow the mouse to recover.

Sample preparation for BA profiling and UPLC-MS/MS analysis

Combine 50 μL serum (sham or MCAO mice) with 40 μL of deuterium-labeled bile acid internal standard solution (1 μg/mL) and 200 μL methanol. Vortex for 30 s, sonicate in an ice-water bath for 3 min, and centrifuge at 12,000 × g (4°C, 10 min). Collect the supernatant and evaporate organic solvents using a centrifugal concentrator (65°C under vacuum). Reconstitute the residue in 100 μL initial mobile phase [60% 0.1% formic acid: 40% methanol-acetonitrile (1:1, v/v)], vortex to resuspend, then sonicate in an ice-water bath for 3 min. Centrifuge at 12,000 × g (4°C, 10 min) and collect the supernatant for LC-MS analysis.

Sample analysis was performed using a UHPLC system (Waters, Massachusetts, USA) to analyze bile acids in the samples. The mobile phase consisted of Phase A: 0.1% formic acid and Phase B: methanol:acetonitrile (1:1). The injection volume was 10 μL, the column temperature was 50°C, and the flow rate was 0.65 mL/min. The mobile phase gradient was as follows:0–2 min (40% B), 2–7.5 min (40–70% B), 7.5–7.6 min (70–98% B), 7.6–9 min (98% B), and 9–9.1 min (98–40% B).

2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium hydrochloride staining

The infarct size was measured using the method described previously [32]. Mouse brains were frozen at −20°C, sectioned into 2-mm-thick slices using a brain matrix, and stained with 2% TTC solution at 37°C in the dark for 30 min while being turned over every 15 min. After staining, sections were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. Infarct volume (%) was calculated as: [Contralateral hemisphere volume−Ipsilateral non-infarcted volume]/Contralateral hemisphere volume×100%.

Neurobehavioral assessment

Neurological scoring and behavioral assessments were conducted on day 3 after MCAO. Neurological function was evaluated using a modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) system, which assesses motor, sensory, and reflex functions. The total score ranged from 0 to 18, where 1-6 indicated mild impairment, 7-12 represented moderate impairment, and 13-18 denoted severe impairment. Higher scores indicated more severe neurological deficits [33]. The balance beam test requires mice to traverse a wooden beam (70 cm long × 1.5 cm wide) without falling. During a 60-s observation period, performance was scored on a 0-6 scale: 0 indicates stable balance, with increasing scores (1-6) indicating progressively poorer motor performance [34]. The rotarod test involved placing mice on a rotating rod with acceleration from 5 to 40 rpm. Fall latency (time to fall) and terminal velocity (speed at fall) were recorded.

Histopathological observation

Histopathology of brain tissue was assessed using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Nissl staining. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned coronally. Sections were stained with either HE or toluidine blue, then microscopically examined for histopathological alterations. The scores are based on the morphological structure of brain cells and the surrounding conditions of the cells. 0 points, normal; 1 point, a small amount of cell damage, no vacuoles around the cells; 2 points, obvious cell damage, with a small number of vacuoles around the cells; 3 points, obvious cell damage, with obvious vacuoles around the cells; 4 points, a large number of cells damaged, and a large number of vacuoles observed around the cells.

Cell culture and treatment

HT22 neuronal cells were cultured in DMEM (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (ExCell, Jiangs, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2. At 90% confluence, cells were passaged (1:3) or used experimentally. For thrombin injury modeling, cells were exposed to thrombin (0–10 U/ml, Merck, New Jersey, USA) for 48 h. UDCA treatment involved co-incubation with thrombin (8 U/ml) and UDCA (25–100 μM). Additional groups were co-treated with thrombin (8 U/ml), UDCA (100 μM), and either TGR5 inhibitor SBI-115 (50 μM), PKA inhibitor H-89 (10 μM), or ALDH3A1 inhibitor ALDH3A1-IN-1 (100 μM).

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was assessed via CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 8 × 103 cells/well. After treatment with the pre-designed drugs and duration, 10 μL CCK-8(Elabscience, Wuhan, China) reagent was added per well and incubated (37°C, 1 h). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

High-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

Total RNA was isolated from neuronal cells in the untreated control group, the thrombin-stimulated group, and the group co-incubated with UDCA and thrombin using Trizol reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a micro-volume spectrophotometer (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA). The RNA was fragmented, reverse transcribed, and used to construct a cDNA library. High-throughput sequencing of the cDNA library was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Data analysis included quality control and gene expression quantification. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with |LogFC| ≥ 2, p-values <0.05 and P adjust <0.05. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted to identify the functional roles of these DEGs for further investigation. The RNA-seq data were uploaded to the Sequence Read Archive under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1474982.

Lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial membrane potential detection

Cellular lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial membrane potential were detected using the BODIPY581/691 C11 lipid peroxidation assay kit and JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), respectively. HT22 cells were processed as previously described and stained per the manufacturer's protocols. Fluorescent images were acquired with an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope (Tokyo, Japan),and the relative fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and intracellular ROS detection

Mitochondrial and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production were detected using the MitoSOX Red probe (Thermo, MA, USA) and Dihydroethidium probe (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) respectively. Following the aforementioned treatments, HT22 cells were incubated with MitoSOX Red or Dihydroethidium working solution for 30 min at 37°C/5% CO2. Cells were then washed three times with warm PBS. Fluorescent images were acquired using an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope, and the relative fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I and NAD+/NADH ratio determine

MRCC-I/cAMP levels and NAD+/NADH ratios were quantified using the MRCC-I/cAMP ELISA kit(SAB, Maryland, USA) and NAD+/NADH detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) respectively. After treating the cells as described above, cells were harvested, lysed, and processed per the manufacturer's protocols. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm (NAD+/NADH) and 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Transmission electron microscopy

Mitochondrial morphology in HT22 cells was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Treated cells were detached with trypsin, pelleted by centrifugation, and fixed in glutaraldehyde fixative. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%), followed by a 1:1 mixture of 90% ethanol and 90% acetone, and finally pure 90% acetone. Subsequently, cells were embedded in resin, sectioned into 60-nm ultrathin sections, and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Ultrastructural images were acquired using an HT7800 TEM (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP detection was performed using the ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, HT22 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at 37°C for 10 min at room temperature. Glycine Solution (10X) was added and gently mixed, incubated at room temperature for 5 min, washed with PBS containing 1 mM PMSF, centrifuged and resuspended, then lysed with SDS Lysis Buffer containing 1 mM PMSF for 10 min, followed by sonication to generate DNA fragments of 200-1000 base pairs. The protein-DNA complexes were immunoprecipitated using the indicated antibody or IgG antibody. The immunoprecipitated DNA was then purified and used for qPCR analysis. The ChIP-qPCR primer sequences were as follows:

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on brain tissue sections and cells. Fix mouse brains in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrate with 30% sucrose, then embed in paraffin and section into 5 μm slices. Cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Tissue sections and cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 h, and incubated with primary antibodies: MAP2 (1:200, SAB), 4-HNE (1:100, Thermo), ALDH3A1 (1:200, SAB), thrombin (1:100, Thermo), TGR5 (1:200, SAB), PAR1 (1:100, Thermo) and Nrf2 (1:100, proteintech) at 4°C overnight. Following overnight incubation, sections were washed in PBS, incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy (3Dhistech, Budapest, Hungary).

Cellular immunofluorescence images were all subjected to quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and intergroup comparisons. In contrast, due to substantial individual variability, animal brain tissue immunofluorescence images were used solely for representative demonstration to reflect the distribution characteristics and relative intensity of fluorescence signals, and no statistical comparison of fluorescence intensity was performed.

For Western blot analysis, proteins were extracted from HT22 cells and the ischemic side of the cerebral cortex. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes.Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h, then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: ACSL4 (1:1000, SAB), 4-HNE (1:1000, Thermo), Nrf2 (1:7000, Proteintech), GPX4 (1:1000, SAB), TFRC (1:1000, SAB), ALDH3A1 (1:1000, SAB), TGR5 (1:1000, SAB), PKA (1:1250, SAB), and P-PKA (1:800, SAB). Results

Thrombin and PAR1 were upregulated in both in vitro and in vivo models of ischemic stroke

To investigate the expression dynamics of thrombin and its receptor PAR1 following ischemic injury, we first utilized an in vitro HT22 neuronal cell model of OGD/R. Immunoblotting analysis showed marked elevations in the protein levels of prothrombin, thrombin, and PAR1 (Fig. 1A), indicating that OGD/R promoted thrombin production in neuronal cells. In the in vivo component of the study, Western blot analysis confirmed a significant upregulation of thrombin and PAR1 protein levels in mice subjected to MCAO (Fig. 1B). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated increased thrombin signal intensity in the MCAO group compared to sham controls (Fig. 1C). Moreover, double immunostaining of PAR1 with the neuronal marker MAP2 indicated a significant elevation of PAR1 expression in neurons of MCAO mice, further corroborating the in vitro results (Fig. 1D).

To explore the functional contribution of thrombin-PAR1 signaling to stroke pathology, MCAO mice were administered the PAR1 antagonist SCH79797 via intraperitoneal injection (Fig. 1E). TTC staining indicated that SCH79797 treatment significantly reduced infarct volume at 3 days post-MCAO (Fig. 1F). Additionally, behavioral assessments revealed that PAR1 blockade led to significant improvements in neurological function and motor coordination, as evidenced by decreased modified Neurological Severity Scores (mNSS) and enhanced performance on balance beam tasks (Fig. 1G and H). Histopathological examinations supported these findings, with HE staining displaying normal cortical morphology in the sham group. In contrast, MCAO mice exhibited chromatin condensation, disorganized cellular arrangement, and extensive neuronal necrosis. These pathological alterations were notably less severe in SCH79797-treated mice (Fig. 1I). Similarly, Nissl staining illustrated that neuronal damage, characterized by karyopyknosis, chromatolysis, and shrunken cortical cell bodies in the MCAO group, was alleviated following SCH79797 treatment (Fig. 1J).

In summary, these results provided evidence that ischemic stroke induced the upregulation of prothrombin in neurons, leading to increased thrombin activity. The subsequent activation of neuronal PAR1 exacerbated ischemic brain injury. Importantly, pharmacological blockade of PAR1 significantly attenuated ischemia-induced neuropathology and enhances neurological outcomes.

UDCA levels were decreased in the serum and brain tissue of MCAO mice

To assess stroke-induced changes in bile acid metabolism, serum and brain samples were collected from mice subjected to MCAO or sham surgery. Analysis of serum bile acids revealed significant reductions in cholic acid (CA), chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), α-muricholic acid (α-MCA), β-muricholic acid (β-MCA), and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) in the MCAO group compared to sham controls. Consequently, total bile acid levels were markedly decreased in the MCAO group (Fig. 2A and B). In brain tissue, levels of UDCA were significantly lower in the MCAO group, although total bile acid levels did not show a statistically significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 2C and D). Correlation analysis indicated that both serum and brain UDCA levels were negatively associated with cerebral infarct volume, with UDCA exhibiting the strongest statistical significance among all bile acids evaluated (Fig. 2E and F).

Each group of 15 animals had 5 that did not undergo infarct volume measurement, but were instead subjected to section staining. To rule out selection bias, we directly compared the baseline characteristics of the 10 animals in each group that underwent infarct volume measurement (TTC) with the 5 animals used for other experiments (non-TTC). The analysis showed that within each experimental group, there were no significant differences in baseline indicators such as body weight, neurological function scores, and behavioral scores (Fig. S1A–C), suggesting the selection bias could be ruled out. Staining of sections showed that the HE staining scores and neuron loss in the MCAO group were higher than those in the Sham group. Notably, UDCA content was positively associated with the number of intact neurons, as indicated by Nissl staining (R2 = 0.8770, P = 0.0190; Fig. S1D–F). These data complemented the infarct volume measurements, collectively supporting the conclusions of the study, although the small sample size warranted cautious interpretation of the correlation.

UDCA showed protective effects against MCAO induced brain injury

To evaluate the neuroprotective effects of UDCA supplementation in response to decreased endogenous UDCA levels after stroke, we administered UDCA to MCAO mice via oral gavage for 4 days prior to and 3 days following surgery. Brain injury was subsequently assessed through behavioral and histological analyses (Fig. 3A). TTC staining results showed that UDCA treatment significantly reduced ischemic infarct volume compared to the MCAO group (Fig. 3B). Additionally, neurological function, as measured by the modified neurological severity score (mNSS), was markedly improved in the UDCA-treated group (Fig. 3C). To further assess motor coordination and balance, rotarod and balance beam tests were conducted. MCAO mice displayed impaired performance, evident by decreased running speed, shortened latency on the rotarod, and elevated balance beam scores, indicating motor dysfunction. Notably, UDCA administration led to significant improvements in these parameters, especially in the high-dose group, suggesting effective restoration of motor coordination (Fig. 3D and E). Histological evaluations supported these findings. HE staining revealed cortical chromatin condensation and disordered neuronal arrangements in MCAO mice, while these pathological alterations were substantially alleviated following UDCA treatment (Fig. 3F). Nissl staining further confirmed the neuroprotective effects of UDCA, as treatment resulted in reduced neuronal loss, decreased nuclear shrinkage, and improved cellular morphology compared to untreated MCAO mice (Fig. 3G). Overall, these results indicated that UDCA supplementation mitigated brain injury and significantly enhanced neurological function in MCAO mice.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed the possible mechanism of UDCA

To investigate the neuroprotective mechanisms of UDCA in light of the increased thrombin expression in neurons following stroke, we established an in vitro model of thrombin-induced neuronal injury using HT22 cells. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that UDCA significantly reversed the thrombin-induced (8 U/mL) decrease in neuronal viability in a concentration-dependent manner, with 100 μM UDCA nearly restoring viability to control levels (Fig. 4A). This concentration of 100 μM UDCA was subsequently selected for RNA-seq analysis. Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct gene expression changes between the thrombin (TM) and UDCA-treated groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) illustrated clear separation between the two groups, with minimal variability within each group (Fig. 4B). Within-group variability was further quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV) for selected genes, which were all below 30%, indicating biologically reasonable consistency (Table S1). Euclidean distance heatmaps confirmed that samples clustered according to treatment group, with larger between-group distances compared to within-group distances (Fig. S2A–B). PERMANOVA analysis further supported the robustness of group separation (R2 = 0.93, F = 52.5; Table S2).

Hierarchical clustering further highlighted that UDCA treatment induced substantial alterations in gene expression compared to TM alone (Fig. 4C). Differential expression analysis identified 551 genes, including 290 upregulated and 221 downregulated genes. The top 10 most significant DEGs were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 4D) and their fold changes and potential biological relevance are summarized in Table S3. The top five genes were validated by RT-qPCR, confirming the reliability of the RNA-seq data (Fig. S2C). Functional enrichment analysis highlighted pathways related to the NADPH oxidase complex, NAD(P) + nucleosidase activity, and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 4E and F). Notably, ALDH3A1, the most significantly upregulated gene, is directly involved in lipid peroxidation regulation. Given thrombin's known role in inducing lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial damage, we focused on this pathway. These findings suggested that UDCA exerted neuroprotective effects against thrombin-induced neuronal injury, potentially through the ALDH3A1-mediated pathway.

UDCA upregulated ALDH3A1 expression to alleviate lipid peroxidation

ALDH3A1 is known to play a pivotal role in detoxifying peroxidic aldehydes, scavenging free radicals, and preventing apoptosis [35]. Building on the RNA-seq findings and our preceding observations, we evaluated the expression levels of ALDH3A1. Both protein and mRNA analyses indicated that ALDH3A1 expression was significantly upregulated in a concentration-dependent manner following UDCA treatment (Fig. 5A and B). Since ALDH3A1 is an NAD + -dependent dehydrogenase that catalyzes the detoxification of aldehydes, we assessed intracellular levels of NAD+, NADH, and the NAD+/NADH ratio, a critical indicator of cellular redox balance and mitochondrial function. Treatment with thrombin (TM) led to a significant reduction in the NAD+/NADH ratio compared to controls; however, UDCA administration restored this ratio (Fig. 5C). Additionally, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (MRCC-I) leakage, a major site of NADH oxidation, was substantially decreased after UDCA treatment, further indicating improved mitochondrial function (Fig. 5D).

Considering the role of ALDH3A1 in lipid peroxidation and aldehydes detoxification, BODIPY™ C11 and dihydroethidium (DHE) staining were performed. These assays revealed that UDCA markedly attenuated TM-induced lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation in neuronal cells (Fig. 5E and F). Similarly, JC-1 and MitoSOX™ Red assays demonstrated that UDCA restored mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced the production of mitochondrial superoxide (Fig. 5G and H). Given that persistent lipid metabolism dysfunction can trigger ferroptosis, we further examined proteins associated with this pathway. TM treatment increased the expression of transferrin receptor (TFRC), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), while suppressing nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). In contrast, UDCA treatment effectively reversed these aberrant changes (Fig. 5I).

To validate our in vitro findings in vivo, we analyzed brain tissues from MCAO mice. ALDH3A1 mRNA and protein levels exhibited a dose-dependent increase following UDCA administration (Fig. 5J and K), which was corroborated by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5L). Western blot analyses further demonstrated that UDCA normalized the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins in MCAO mice (Fig. 5M). Double immunostaining of 4-HNE with the neuronal marker MAP2 revealed that UDCA significantly reduced neuronal 4-HNE intensity, indicating decreased lipid peroxidation (Fig. 5N). Moreover, UDCA treatment was shown to suppress thrombin and PAR1 expression in MCAO mice, effectively attenuating the progression of ischemic injury (Fig. 5O and P). In summary, these results demonstrated that UDCA upregulated ALDH3A1 expression, restored redox homeostasis, and alleviated lipid peroxidation, thereby providing protection against thrombin-induced neuronal ferroptosis in the context of ischemic stroke.

UDCA attenuated thrombin-induced neuronal injury through an ALDH3A1-dependent manner

To investigate whether ALDH3A1 mediates the neuroprotective effects of UDCA, we utilized the selective ALDH3A1 inhibitor ALDH3A1-IN-1 (AL-IN-1) in neuronal cells. Both Western blotting and RT-qPCR analyses confirmed that treatment with AL-IN-1 resulted in a partial suppression of ALDH3A1 expression (Fig. 6A and B). Functionally, inhibiting ALDH3A1 abolished the beneficial effects of UDCA. CCK-8 assays and NAD+/NADH measurements indicated that ALDH3A1 inhibition negated UDCA-induced neuronal survival under TM challenge, accompanied by a significant reduction in the NAD+/NADH ratio (Fig. 6C and D). Moreover, suppression of ALDH3A1 prevented UDCA from attenuating TM-induced lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation (Fig. 6E and F), while exacerbating the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 6G). Analysis of ferroptosis-associated proteins further confirmed these observations; inhibition of ALDH3A1 blocked UDCA's ability to reverse TM-induced upregulation of TFRC, 4-HNE, and ACSL4, as well as downregulation of Nrf2 and GPX4 (Fig. 6H). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that UDCA protected neurons from TM-induced lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction primarily through the activation of ALDH3A1.

To further investigate whether ALDH3A1 mediated the neuroprotective effects of UDCA in vivo, we stereotactically injected AAV-ALDH to specifically inhibit neuronal ALDH3A1 expression (Fig. 6I). Western blot analysis confirmed that ALDH3A1 protein levels in brain tissues were significantly reduced in the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group compared to the UDCA + AAV-con group (Fig. 6J). TTC staining revealed a marked increase in cerebral infarct volume in the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group relative to the UDCA + AAV-con group (Fig. 6K). Neurological function assessments showed that mice in the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group exhibited significantly higher mNSS scores and poorer performance in the balance beam test, indicating more severe neurological deficits and impaired motor coordination (Fig. 6L and M). HE staining revealed disorganized cortical cell arrangement and nuclear chromatin condensation in the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group (Fig. 6N). Nissl staining further demonstrated a reduction in Nissl bodies in the cerebral cortex following ALDH3A1 inhibition, suggesting aggravated neuronal damage (Fig. 6O). Overall, these findings indicated that inhibition of ALDH3A1 significantly attenuated the neuroprotective effect of UDCA.

Further in vivo analyses were conducted to assess ROS levels and the expression of proteins associated with lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. The results demonstrated that, compared with the UDCA + AAV-con group, the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group exhibited elevated ROS levels in brain tissue (Fig. 6P). Regarding ferroptosis-related markers, inhibition of ALDH3A1 led to marked alterations in the expression of several key regulators. Specifically, the protein expression levels of TFRC, 4-HNE, and ACSL4 were significantly upregulated in the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group relative to the UDCA + AAV-con group, whereas the expression of GPX4 and Nrf2 was notably reduced (Fig. 6Q). These findings indicated that ALDH3A1 inhibition exacerbated lipid peroxidation and promoted ferroptosis. Additionally, the protein levels of thrombin, prothrombin, and PAR1 were increased in the UDCA + AAV-ALDH group (Fig. 6R). Collectively, these results suggested that inhibition of ALDH3A1 impaired the resolution of lipid peroxidation induced by thrombin following ischemic stroke, thereby facilitating ferroptotic cell death.These underscored ALDH3A1 as a critical mediator of UDCA-driven neuroprotection.

Activation of TGR5 stimulates adenylate cyclase, resulting in the activation of protein kinase A (PKA). However, the role of the PKA pathway in TGR5-mediated regulation of ALDH3A1 and the inhibition of lipid peroxidation remained unclear. We observed that UDCA treatment enhanced the phosphorylation level of PKA in neurons challenged with TM. To investigate whether UDCA regulated ALDH3A1 through the PKA pathway, we employed the selective PKA inhibitor H-89. Treatment with H-89 significantly suppressed UDCA-induced upregulation of ALDH3A1 at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 8A and B). Functional assays further demonstrated that H-89 partially abrogated UDCA's protective effects against TM-induced neuronal injury. Specifically, H-89 inhibited the UDCA-mediated increases in the NAD+/NADH ratio and exacerbated MRCC-I leakage (Fig. 8C–E). Consistent with these findings, staining assays revealed that H-89 diminished the UDCA-induced reductions in lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation (Fig. 8F and G) and accelerated the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 8H). Furthermore, H-89 prevented UDCA from reversing TM-induced dysregulation of ferroptosis-related proteins (Fig. 8I). Together, these results indicated that the inhibitory effect of TGR5 signaling on ALDH3A1-mediated lipid peroxidation is dependent on the activation of the PKA pathway.

Discussion

Thrombin, a serine protease abundantly expressed in neurons and glial cells within the central nervous system (CNS), exerts its diverse physiological and pathological effects predominantly through the protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1) [36,37]. Beyond its canonical role in coagulation, thrombin-PAR1 signaling has been implicated in the pathogenesis of a spectrum of neurological disorders, including Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer's disease, and traumatic brain injury, where its upregulation is often associated with neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [[38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43]]. Despite this broad implication across neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory conditions, its specific role and therapeutic relevance in the context of ischemic brain injury remained inadequately defined. Our study directly addressed this critical gap, establishing the thrombin-PAR1 axis as a pivotal driver of ischemic stroke progression and thereby identifying it as a promising target for neuroprotective intervention. In this study, increased expression levels of thrombin and PAR1 in both in vivo and in vitro models of ischemic stroke were confirmed and showed a positive correlation with the extent of cerebral infarction. Notably, the blockade of PAR1 improved neurological and motor functions while simultaneously reducing infarct volume in stroke mice. Collectively, these results underscored the crucial role of thrombin-PAR1 signaling as a central driver of neuronal injury following ischemic stroke. These results suggested that targeting this pathway emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating brain injury in ischemic conditions.

In recent years, a growing body of research has revealed that bile acids (BAs) are not only implicated in metabolic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, but also play critical roles in the regulation of the CNS [[44], [45], [46]]. For example, alterations in BA metabolism have been observed in patients with multiple sclerosis, with taurocholic acid supplementation showing promise in alleviating disease symptoms [47]. Additionally, TUDCA has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in models of depression, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and cerebral hemorrhage by inhibiting oxidative-nitrosative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and neuroinflammatory responses [[48], [49], [50]]. Chenodeoxycholic acid has also been reported to reduce β-secretase 1 (BACE1) expression and amyloid-β42 (Aβ42) levels, contributing to improvements in cognitive function in experimental models [51]. Despite these broad implications, the profile and role of BAs in ischemic stroke remained poorly characterized.

In the present study, we analyzed BA profiles in both serum and brain tissue from a murine ischemic stroke model. We observed that UDCA concentrations were significantly reduced, and this decrease was inversely correlated with cerebral infarct volume (n = 10). Additionally, UDCA content was positively associated with the number of intact neurons (n = 5). We acknowledged that the small sample size for the correlation analysis warranted cautious interpretation, and validation in a larger cohort will be important in future studies. UDCA is a potent antioxidant initially approved for the treatment of cholestatic liver disease [52], and clinical studies have shown that it effectively lowers liver enzyme levels, improves liver histology, and enhances survival in patients with chronic liver diseases [53]. Notably, UDCA has also been shown to facilitate neurological recovery following spinal cord injury [54], and to ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction in the brains of patients with Parkinson's disease [55]. Our findings suggested that UDCA exerted protective effects against thrombin-induced neuronal injury following ischemic stroke. Given its FDA-approved status and well-established safety profile, UDCA represents a promising candidate for drug repurposing strategies aimed at treating neurological disorders. Aligned with these observations, our study demonstrated that UDCA supplementation effectively attenuated thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation and neuronal death, reduced cerebral infarct volume, and improved neurobehavioral outcomes in MCAO mice. Given its favorable safety record and the growing clinical interest in its neurotherapeutic potential, as underscored by its prioritization in international clinical trial initiatives, our findings positioned UDCA as a promising repurposing candidate for the treatment of ischemic stroke.

Our investigation into the mechanism by which UDCA counteracted thrombin-mediated neuronal injury was initiated by RNA-seq analysis, and the profiling revealed distinct gene expression changes between the TM and TM + UDCA groups. PCA, CV (<30%) and PERMANOVA (R2 = 0.93, F = 52.5) confirmed robust group separation with minimal within-group variability. Functional enrichment analysis further revealed that UDCA modulated pathways critically involved in oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, including the “NADPH oxidase complex,” “NAD(P) + nucleosidase activity,” and “arachidonic acid metabolism”, consistent with thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation. Therefore, we focused on lipid peroxidation and found that the most significantly upregulated gene, ALDH3A1, was directly related to this process. It should be noted that although the other genes were comprehensively described in the supplementary materials, they were not explored in depth as core content of this study due to their weaker direct association with the lipid peroxidation pathway.

Building on this genomic insight, we demonstrated that UDCA confers neuroprotection by potently upregulating ALDH3A1. As a member of the aldehyde dehydrogenase family, ALDH3A1 contributes to oxidative stress defense and lipid homeostasis by metabolizing toxic lipid peroxidation products. This upregulation was functionally consequential: UDCA restored the thrombin-impaired NAD+/NADH ratio, mitigated mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I leakage, and ameliorated mitochondrial dysfunction by recovering membrane potential and reducing superoxide production. Consequently, UDCA significantly attenuated thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation and cytosolic ROS accumulation. These improvements in redox homeostasis were accompanied by a normalized expression profile of ferroptosis-related proteins, as UDCA reversed the upregulation of TFRC, 4-HNE, and ACSL4 while restoring levels of Nrf2 and GPX4. The pivotal role of ALDH3A1 was unequivocally established through its pharmacological inhibition and AAV-mediated neuron-specific knockdown of ALDH3A1, which abolished all the beneficial effects of UDCA on neuronal survival, NAD + metabolism, mitochondrial integrity, and anti-ferroptotic signaling. These results suggested that UDCA alleviated thrombin-induced neuronal damage primarily by transcriptionally activating ALDH3A1, thereby resetting redox homeostasis, curbing lipid peroxidation, and suppressing ferroptosis. Additionally, we found that following UDCA treatment, the expression of thrombin and PAR1 in the brain was downregulated. This was attributed to the effective clearance of lipid peroxidation products and stabilization of mitochondrial function by UDCA, which alleviated neuronal injury and improved the overall brain microenvironment, thereby suppressing the ischemia-induced upregulation and secretion of thrombin and subsequently reducing the activation of thrombin and PAR1 signaling.

BAs are known to regulate lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis largely through receptors such as TGR5 and FXR [56]. The unique ability of UDCA to activate TGR5 is attributable to its specific chemical structure. UDCA possesses a 7β-hydroxyl configuration (7β-OH), distinguishing it from other bile acids like CDCA (which has a 7α-OH). This specific stereochemistry enables effective TGR5 binding, and experimental evidence showed that a methyl group at the 7α-position of UDCA significantly increased both binding affinity and activation potency compared to the unmodified form. UDCA has recently been shown to fully activate TGR5 signaling, which is reported to exert neuroprotective effects in various neurological disorders [57]. In peripheral tissues, TGR5 activation can attenuate lipid peroxidation [58], yet its role in thrombin-induced neuronal injury after ischemic stroke remained unclear. Given that the TGR5-PKA axis was a well-established mediator of bile acid-induced antioxidant responses, and considering prior evidence linking PKA to the regulation of aldehyde dehydrogenases such as ALDH2, we investigated whether this pathway governs ALDH3A1 expression.

To directly test this hypothesis, we first examined whether UDCA could activate the TGR5-cAMP-PKA signaling cascade in neurons. Consistent with previous reports that UDCA directly binds to and activates TGR5, we observed that UDCA treatment significantly increased TGR5 protein expression and phosphorylated PKA (p-PKA) levels in neurons. Importantly, direct measurement of cAMP levels, the canonical second messenger downstream of TGR5 activation, in both brain tissues and HT22 cells revealed that UDCA promoted cAMP production in a dose-dependent manner. This biochemical evidence directly supported that UDCA activated the TGR5-cAMP-PKA signaling pathway in neurons, bridging the earlier observation of increased TGR5 expression with enhanced downstream signaling activity. Next, we sought to determine whether this pathway mediated UDCA-induced upregulation of ALDH3A1. Using the selective TGR5 antagonist SBI-115, we found that pharmacological blockade of TGR5 significantly reduced the expression levels of TGR5, p-PKA, and ALDH3A1, indicating that ALDH3A1 upregulation by UDCA is indeed TGR5-dependent.

Functional validation further confirmed that the neuroprotective effects of UDCA were critically dependent on TGR5-PKA activation. UDCA treatment enhanced PKA phosphorylation in thrombin-challenged neurons, and selective inhibition of PKA with H-89 significantly suppressed UDCA-induced upregulation of ALDH3A1 at both protein and mRNA levels. Consequently, H-89 abrogated UDCA mediated improvements in the NAD+/NADH ratio and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I leakage, blunted the reduction of lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation, accelerated the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and reversed the normalizing effect of UDCA on ferroptosis-related proteins. Together, these results indicated that the TGR5-PKA axis was essential for UDCA to upregulate ALDH3A1 and counteract thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in neurons.

To identify the downstream effector of PKA responsible for ALDH3A1 upregulation, we performed bioinformatics analysis using the NCBI and JASPAR databases, which predicted that Nrf2 may bind to the promoter region of the Aldh3a1 gene. This prediction was supported by interrogation of public ChIP-seq datasets, revealing enrichment of Nrf2 at the Aldh3a1 promoter, a finding further validated by our ChIP-qPCR assays. Collectively, these results positioned Nrf2 as a plausible downstream effector of the TGR5-PKA axis. To functionally test this, we employed the PKA inhibitor H-89 to block the pathway and examined both the subcellular localization of Nrf2 and its interaction with the Aldh3a1 promoter. Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses showed that H-89 treatment impaired Nrf2 nuclear translocation. Consistently, ChIP-qPCR demonstrated that H-89 markedly reduced the enrichment of Nrf2 at the Aldh3a1 promoter, indicating that PKA activity was required for this interaction. Taken together, these results demonstrated that UDCA promoted ALDH3A1 expression by activating PKA, which in turn facilitated Nrf2 nuclear translocation and its subsequent binding to the Aldh3a1 promoter. This TGR5/PKA/Nr2/ALDH3A1 axis proved functionally essential, as the protective effects of UDCA against thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction were abolished by inhibitors targeting TGR5 (SBI-115) and PKA (H-89). Taken together, these findings delineated a novel signaling pathway through which UDCA confered neuroprotection in ischemic stroke, positioning TGR5/PKA-driven ALDH3A1 activation as a central mechanism counteracting thrombin-mediated lipid peroxidation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, sex differences represent a critical consideration in both stroke research and bile acid metabolism; however, all experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice. This limits the generalizability of our findings and has important implications for clinical translation. Future studies are therefore warranted to evaluate the neuroprotective effects and underlying mechanisms of UDCA in female animal models, thereby assessing its therapeutic applicability and potential sex-related differences. Second, although our current dosing regimen was designed to establish proof-of-concept for the identified signaling axis, a treatment schedule initiated after reperfusion (e.g., 1–3 h post-MCAO) would better align with the clinical therapeutic window for acute ischemic stroke. Such a clinically aligned regimen is essential for strengthening the translational relevance of our findings. Third, pharmacokinetic data specifically in stroke patients are currently lacking, representing an important knowledge gap. We intend to prioritize this in future investigations to more precisely define the exposure–response relationship of UDCA in the context of acute ischemic stroke. Fourth, the sample size for certain correlation analyses was limited, which warrants cautious interpretation. Validation of these exploratory findings in larger independent cohorts will be necessary in future studies.

In conclusion, our study established the thrombin-PAR1 signaling axis as a pivotal contributor to ischemic brain injury and identified a marked reduction of UDCA in the post-stroke state. We demonstrated that supplementation with UDCA conferred potent neuroprotection, attenuating thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation, neuronal death, cerebral infarct volume, and neurological deficits. Mechanistically, this protective effect was mediated through a novel TGR5/PKA/ALDH3A1 pathway, in which UDCA activated TGR5 and downstream PKA signaling. PKA activity subsequently facilitated Nrf2 nuclear translocation and its binding to the Aldh3a1 promoter, leading to ALDH3A1 upregulation and the attenuation of deleterious lipid peroxidation. Beyond revealing a previously unrecognized anti-lipid peroxidation function of ALDH3A1 in neurons, our work expanded the understanding of TGR5's role in the brain from modulating thrombin-induced lipid peroxidation to directly safeguarding neuronal redox homeostasis. These findings not only elucidated a coherent signaling cascade from receptor activation to aldehyde detoxification but also positioned UDCA and the TGR5/PKA/ALDH3A1 axis as promising therapeutic targets for mitigating ischemic stroke damage.
