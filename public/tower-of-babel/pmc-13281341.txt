Neuromuscular activation patterns of the hamstrings and trunk during eccentric exercise in athletes and physically active adults
Authors: Yanan You, Norikazu Hirose, Dai Sugimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEccentric hamstring exercises are widely used in training and rehabilitation settings, yet how neuromuscular activation patterns vary with training status remains unclear. We aimed to compare hamstring and trunk neuromuscular activation patterns elicited by two commonly used eccentric hamstring exercises—the Inclined Nordic Hamstring Exercise (INHE) and Bilateral Hip Extension (BHE)—between trained athletes and physically active nonathletes.MethodsTwenty-five healthy male participants (10 athletes and 15 physically active nonathletes) performed INHE and BHE in randomized order. Surface electromyography (sEMG) was recorded from hamstring and trunk musculature during the eccentric phase. Root mean square (RMS, normalized to %MVIC), median frequency (MF), and the biceps femoris long head to semitendinosus (BFlh/ST) activation ratio were analysed. Between-group differences were assessed using non-parametric statistics with effect size estimation.ResultsAthletes exhibited higher RMS values in the BFlh during BHE and a tendency toward BFlh-dominant activation (BFlh/ST ratios > 1), although between-group differences in the ratio were not statistically significant. Physically active nonathletes exhibited significantly higher MF values in several muscles and showed comparatively greater activation of trunk musculature under specific task conditions. They also demonstrated a comparatively greater reliance on the semitendinosus, with lower BFlh activation amplitudes and lower BFlh/ST ratios, as well as comparatively higher activation of superficial trunk muscles under specific task conditions.ConclusionsDistinct neuromuscular activation patterns during eccentric hamstring exercises were observed between athletes and physically active nonathletes, despite comparable overall activity levels. These findings should be interpreted as associative and descriptive and suggest that training status may be associated with differences in task-specific hamstring and trunk coordination strategies during eccentric loading.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01740-7.Keywords: Hamstring muscles, Surface electromyography, Neuromuscular control, Motor unit recruitment, Population-specific differences

Background

Hamstring strain injuries (HSIs) are highly prevalent in running-based sports and are associated with elevated recurrence rates and a substantial performance and healthcare burden [1, 2]. Although HSIs are most frequently reported in competitive athletes, they also occur in physically active nonathletic populations, in whom neuromuscular coordination and strength characteristics may differ despite comparable levels of habitual physical activity.

Eccentric loading of the hamstring muscle–tendon unit is the central mechanical feature of movements associated with HSI risk, particularly during tasks involving simultaneous hip flexion and knee extension. Consequently, eccentric hamstring strengthening is a core component of both injury prevention and rehabilitation protocols. Among these, the Nordic Hamstring Exercise (NHE) has strong evidence supporting its effectiveness in reducing HSI incidence [2, 3]. Nevertheless, the substantial mechanical demands of the standard NHE may limit adherence or elicit compensatory strategies, particularly in less trained individuals. To address this constraint, modified variations such as the Inclined Nordic Hamstring Exercise (INHE), have therefore been introduced to reduce external knee flexor moments and potentially alter muscular demand across the hamstring muscle group [4]. Importantly, individuals with lower levels of structured training may exhibit reduced eccentric strength, altered neuromuscular coordination, and a greater reliance on compensatory strategies during such tasks, which may influence both exercise execution and muscle activation patterns [5]. These differences highlight the importance of examining how eccentric hamstring exercises are performed and controlled not only in trained athletes but also in physically active nonathletic populations.

In contrast to knee-dominant Nordic-based exercises, hip-dominant eccentric tasks prioritize control of trunk motion around the hip joint and require tight coordinated activation of the hamstrings, gluteal muscles and trunk stabilisers. Bilateral Hip Extension (BHE), as performed on a Roman chair under bodyweight conditions, is a widely used eccentric hip-extension task that differs biomechanically from externally loaded exercises such as deadlifts or hip thrusts.

During eccentric hamstring loading, particularly in movements involving simultaneous hip flexion and knee extension, substantial anterior pelvic tilt moments are generated [6]. To maintain lumbopelvic alignment and ensure efficient force transmission, these moments must be counteracted by trunk extensor muscles, primarily the erector spinae and multifidus [7]. This biomechanical coupling highlights the importance of coordinated trunk–hip control during eccentric tasks and provides a mechanistic basis for assessing trunk stabilising muscle activity alongside hamstring activation.

Training history systematically shapes neuromuscular function through long-term adaptations in muscle architecture, strength and intermuscular coordination. Athletes engaged in structured training may therefore distribute eccentric demand across the hamstring muscles in a distinct, task-specific manner from physically active nonathletes, may differ despite broadly similar levels of habitual physical activity. Previous surface electromyography (sEMG) studies have demonstrated exercise-specific activation patterns within the hamstring muscle group and substantial inter-individual variability in muscle recruitment [8, 9]. However, direct comparisons between athletes and physically active nonathletes under standardized eccentric exercise conditions remain scarce.

Moreover, neuromuscular adaptations associated with resistance training have been shown to enhance intermuscular coordination and improve the efficiency of force transmission across joints [5, 10]. Such adaptations may result in more task-specific activation strategies, including differential recruitment of individual hamstring muscles and greater integration of trunk stabilising musculature during complex eccentric movements. In particular, given the higher mechanical demands placed on the biceps femoris long head (BFlh) during high-load eccentric actions and its greater susceptibility to strain, it has been suggested that trained individuals may develop preferential activation or protective recruitment strategies involving the BFlh [11, 12]. However, this remains an area of ongoing investigation and should not be considered definitive.

Furthermore, previous studies examining eccentric hamstring exercises have focused primarily on the hamstring muscles themselves, with relatively limited attention given to the role of trunk and lumbar stabilising muscles (e.g., Bourne et al., 2017 [13]). Given the functional coupling between the trunk and lower limb during eccentric movements, a more comprehensive assessment of trunk–hip coordination may better resolve how eccentric loads are managed across different populations.

sEMG offers a non-invasive approach for profiling muscle activation amplitude and spectral properties during dynamic tasks. While sEMG-derived measures such as root mean square (RMS) and median frequency (MF) do not directly quantify force production or injury risk, they enable objective, task-standardized descriptions of neuromuscular activation strategies under controlled task conditions. These measures should therefore be interpreted as indicators of neuromuscular control strategies rather than direct proxies for injury risk but may provide insight into mechanisms relevant to HSI susceptibility [14]. Relative activation indices, such as the ratio between the BFlh and semitendinosus (ST), have been used to characterise hamstring recruitment balance during eccentric exercise [15].

The separation of the hamstring muscle group into medial (ST) and lateral (BFlh) components is supported by their distinct anatomical and functional characteristics. The BFlh exhibits longer fascicle length changes and greater strain during high-speed running and eccentric contractions and is more frequently involved in hamstring strain injuries [11, 12]. Furthermore, previous sEMG studies have demonstrated muscle-specific activation patterns within the hamstring group, supporting the need for differentiated analysis of medial and lateral components [13].

Despite growing evidence on exercise-specific hamstring activation and emerging insights into trunk–hip coordination, it remains unclear how these neuromuscular strategies vary across populations with different training backgrounds under comparable eccentric task conditions. Therefore, this study aimed to compare hamstring and trunk neuromuscular activation patterns during two widely implemented eccentric hamstring exercises—the INHE and BHE—between athletes and physically active nonathletes. Based on previous findings that trained individuals exhibit more efficient and task-specific neuromuscular coordination, as well as evidence suggesting preferential recruitment of the BFlh during high-load eccentric tasks, we hypothesised that athletes would demonstrate a more BFlh-dominant activation pattern and greater engagement of trunk stabilising muscles [5, 13]. We hypothesised that athletes would demonstrate a more BFlh-dominant activation pattern and greater engagement of trunk stabilising muscles, reflecting potential differences associated with structured training history.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional laboratory-controlled mixed (within–between) study examined between-group differences in neuromuscular activation patterns during two eccentric hamstring exercises in athletes versus physically active nonathletes. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines where applicable. An a priori sample size estimation was conducted based on the primary within-subject comparisons using a paired design (two-tailed, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, effect size dz = 0.6), indicating a minimum required sample size of 20 participants. Further details are provided in Supplementary Methods (S3).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Waseda University (Protocol No. 2023 − 314). All experimental procedures conformed to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The present dataset was derived from a larger research project, parts of which have been previously published [16]. In the previously published study, the analysis focused primarily on task- and region-specific differences in hamstring activation within individuals. In contrast, the current study addresses a distinct research question by examining population-specific differences between athletes and physically active nonathletes. While the participant cohort and experimental protocol were shared, the outcome variables and study objectives differ substantial. The current analysis addresses distinct research questions and applies a different analytical framework with a specific focus on population-specific neuromuscular coordination strategies during eccentric exercise. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Participants

Twenty-five healthy men aged 18–35 years were recruited. The inclusion of male participants only was intended to reduce potential variability related to sex-specific differences in muscle architecture, strength, and neuromuscular activation patterns, thereby allowing a more controlled comparison between groups. All participants were required to demonstrate a minimum level of habitual physical activity, defined as scoring in the “high” category on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Participants were classified as either an athlete group or a physically active nonathlete group (hereafter referred to as nonathletes) based on self-reported training history and competitive involvement. Athletes were defined as individuals competing at the collegiate level or higher and engaged in structured, sports-specific training at least three times per week for a minimum of two years prior to participation. The athlete group encompassed individuals from a diverse range of disciplines, including soccer, swimming, weightlifting and so on. However, given the heterogeneity of sport backgrounds and the limited sample size, no further stratification by sport type was performed, and the analysis focused on general training status rather than discipline-specific adaptations. Regarding familiarity with the testing tasks, neither the athletes nor the nonathletes had previously engaged in structured training programs specifically incorporating the INHE or the specific BHE protocol. Nonathletes were defined as individuals meeting the “high” IPAQ criteria but without a history of formal competitive sports participation or structured training programs.

All participants were injury-free at the time of testing and had no history of hamstring strain injury within the preceding six months. Exclusion criteria included current musculoskeletal pain, neurological disorders, or any condition likely to compromise task execution or neuromuscular function. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Testing protocol

Before the test, all participants completed a standardized warm-up consisting of light aerobic activity and stretches for the lower limbs and hamstrings. Following the warm-up, the investigators instructed and demonstrated the two eccentric hamstring exercises, as illustrated in Fig. 1. BHE emphasizes eccentric control of the hip extensors, in contrast to free-weight or upright hip extension exercises and was selected as a controlled, hip-dominant task to assess trunk–hip coordination during eccentric loading rather than maximal force production. Participants maintained straight body alignment and slowly lowered their upper body from an extended position in a controlled manner until they reached their maximum controllable range, defined as the point at which participants could no longer maintain the prescribed movement tempo, straight body alignment (head–trunk–lower limb), or controlled descent without compensatory movements. INHE was performed on a board inclined at 45°, an angle determined during pilot testing to emphasize knee-dominant eccentric control during resisted knee extension. Participants resisted knee extension from a flexed position toward near-full extension while maintaining a straight alignment from the head to the knees and leaning forward in a slow, controlled manner until loss of control. During both exercises, participants were instructed to maintain their arms crossed over the chest to minimize upper limb contribution and ensure consistent trunk positioning. By providing standardized instructions and practice trials prior to testing, we minimised the influence of task unfamiliarity. To further standardize movement execution, a digital metronome (60 beats·min⁻¹) provided continuous auditory cues to maintain a consistent movement tempo across participants.

A The bilateral hip extension exercise (BHE) was performed on a Roman chair with the thighs supported and trunk eccentrically lowered through hip flexion to the lowest achievable position. B Inclined Nordic hamstring exercise (INHE), performed on a customized board inclined at 40°, with participants resisting eccentric knee extension until loss of control

Subsequently, the skin was prepared and sEMG sensors were attached. After electrode placement, participants performed two 5-second maximal voluntary isometric contractions (MVICs) for each recorded muscle to allow EMG signal normalization. Standardized testing positions and joint configurations for each muscle are summarized in Table S1. During MVIC testing, participants were verbally encouraged to exert maximal effort, while manual resistance was applied by the same investigator to ensure consistent loading. Body segments were stabilized as necessary to isolate the target muscle and minimize compensatory movements. For normalization, the peak RMS value obtained over a 500 ms window during each MVIC trial was calculated, and the highest value across the two trials was used as the reference for %MVIC normalization.

After completing the MVICs, reflective markers were placed on the bony landmarks of the dominant side of the lower limb (greater trochanter, lateral femoral condyle, and lateral malleolus) and the iliac crest to define segment landmarks for kinematic referencing. Participants then completed the INHE and BHE tests in a random order, with each test comprising three consecutive repetitions. Three repetitions were selected to balance signal reliability with minimization of fatigue [17]. There was a 1-minute break between each repetition and a 3-minute break between the tests [18, 19]. A repetition was considered “unacceptable” if one or more of the following criteria were met: (1) failure to maintain the required body alignment (e.g., excessive hip flexion or trunk rotation), (2) inability to control the eccentric phase (e.g., sudden collapse or loss of tempo), or (3) technical issues in data acquisition (e.g., signal dropout or marker occlusion). Unacceptable repetitions were discarded and repeated immediately. If a participant failed to perform the task properly during a trial, the test was discarded and repeated. If a participant performed five or more unacceptable trials throughout the protocol, they were excluded from statistical analysis. No participants met the exclusion threshold, and all included participants completed the protocol with acceptable repetitions for analysis.

Electromyography signal collection

In this study, sEMG data were collected using the Delsys Trigno wireless system (Delsys Inc., Natick, MA, USA) at 2000 Hz. Electrodes were placed on the dominant side (the limb used to kick a ball for the maximum distance) over the dominant rectus abdominis (DRA), erector spinae at the L3 level (L3ES), superficial multifidus at L5 (L5MF), BFlh, ST, and gluteus maximus (Gmax). Electrode positioning was confirmed by ultrasound imaging and manual resistance isometric contractions to ensure proper alignment, as illustrated in Fig. 2 and Table S2. Electrode placement followed SENIAM guidelines [20]. Before electrode placement, the corresponding areas were shaved and cleaned with ethanol to maximize adhesion and reduce skin impedance.

Surface electromyography sensor location

Only the dominant side was analyzed to reduce variability associated with bilateral asymmetry and to ensure consistency in electrode placement and signal quality. This approach is consistent with previous sEMG studies investigating hamstring activation during eccentric exercises, where the primary aim is to characterize muscle-specific recruitment patterns rather than inter-limb asymmetry [21].

Kinematics

A high-speed video camera (EX-F100, Casio Computer Co., Ltd.; 120 fps) positioned 1.5 m from the participant’s dominant side at a height of 0.6 m was used for two-dimensional motion capture. Passive reflective markers (15.9 mm diameter) were placed on the iliac crest, greater trochanter, lateral femoral condyle, and lateral malleolus of the dominant lower limb. Hip and knee joint angles in the sagittal plane were calculated offline using Frame-DIAS V software (DKH Inc.) according to International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) recommendations. Specifically, the hip angle was defined by the iliac-crest–greater-trochanter and greater-trochanter–lateral-femoral-condyle segments; the knee angle by the greater-trochanter–lateral-femoral-condyle and lateral-femoral-condyle–lateral-malleolus segments (Fig. 3). Synchronization with sEMG data was achieved by recording an optical trigger signal (DKH PH-145 optical presenter) with the video camera at the onset of sEMG acquisition. In addition to identifying the onset of the eccentric phase, kinematic data were used to quantify movement execution characteristics. Specifically, joint range of motion (ROM), peak joint angles (peak hip flexion in BHE and peak knee extension in INHE), and mean angular velocity during the eccentric phase were calculated for each repetition and averaged across trials. These variables were included to assess the consistency of movement execution between groups and to evaluate potential kinematic influences on sEMG outcomes. While the use of two-dimensional motion capture does not account for out-of-plane movements or detailed trunk kinematics, the present setup was considered sufficient for identifying the onset of the eccentric phase and quantifying primary sagittal-plane joint mechanics, which were the main focus of the analysis.

Data and statistical analysis

Preprocessing and feature extraction

Eccentric phase onset was identified through kinematic parameters, from the kinematically determined onset to the task-specific endpoint, defined as the maximum controllable range in BHE and the point of loss of control in INHE. Knee and hip joint angular velocities and accelerations in the sagittal plane were derived from filtered angle data (4th-order Butterworth low-pass, 6 Hz cutoff) using central difference differentiation [22]. For the INHE, eccentric onset was defined as the zero-crossing of knee angular velocity from positive (flexion acceleration) to negative (extension acceleration). For the BHE, onset was defined analogously using hip angular velocity [23]. To ensure the plausibility of the kinematically defined onset, a validation procedure was conducted during a pre-experimental phase. Specifically, the timing of the kinematic onset was compared with (1) hamstring sEMG activation delay (defined as the time from sEMG onset to 50% of peak amplitude) and (2) the onset of gluteus maximus activation (defined as > 3 SD above baseline,, defined as the mean sEMG amplitude over a 500 ms static period immediately prior to movement initiation while maintaining the starting posture). These comparisons were used to confirm temporal consistency between kinematic and neuromuscular events. This validation approach was adopted based on previous studies that have used temporal alignment between kinematic and EMG signals as an indirect verification of movement phase identification [18, 24, 25]. Pre-experimental demonstrated a strong temporal agreement (r ≥ 0.68, P < 0.05) and showed 93% agreement with expert video calibration (kappa value = 0.87).

Raw sEMG data were preprocessed and feature-extracted using Delsys sEMGworks Analysis software (v4.7.4, Delsys Inc., USA). First, the sEMG signal data from the MVIC trials and INHE & BHE tests underwent the Remove Mean operation to eliminate DC offset. Next, a bandpass filter (20–450 Hz, 4th-order Butterworth) was applied to suppress motion artifacts and high-frequency noise.

Two sEMG parameters were extracted from the eccentric phase. The root mean square (RMS) was used as an index of activation amplitude, and MF was derived from the power spectrum to characterise spectral properties to characterise frequency-domain properties of the EMG signal. RMS was calculated using a 100 ms sliding window with 50% overlap [18, 26] and normalized to the maximum voluntary isometric contraction (%MVIC) for each muscle. The MVIC values used for normalization were obtained from standardized isometric contractions for each muscle, as described in Sect. 2.3. No dynamic peak normalization was applied. This approach was selected to ensure consistency across participants and to facilitate comparison between conditions, despite the eccentric nature of the experimental tasks.

For each participant, the BFlh/ST (RMSBFlh divide by RMSST) ratio was calculated using the mean RMS value (%MVIC) to assess within- hamstrings recruitment pattern. The use of participant-level BFlh/ST ratios rather than group averages ensured appropriate statistical inference.

sEMG parameters analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (v3.13.0, Python Software Foundation) with the Pandas library (v2.3.3). For each participant, every iteration evaluates all variables and then averaged across the three trials to obtain a representative value for analysis. Based on the functional anatomical characteristics of the muscles, the target muscles were grouped into two functional categories: (1) core-stabilizing muscles, including the DRA, L3ES, and L5MF, and (2) lower limb dynamic muscles, including the BFlh, ST, and Gmax. A two-tier comparison strategy was adopted: within-group, muscle-specific comparisons, followed by intergroup comparison stratified by participant attributes (athletes vs. nonathletes, as defined in Sect. 2.2 Participants).

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical testing, data from multiple repetitions for each task were averaged to obtain a single representative subject-level mean. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.05), and homogeneity of variances for between-group comparisons was examined using Levene’s test (α = 0.05). Statistical analysis was conducted separately for within-group and inter-group (between-subject) comparisons according to the study design.

For comparisons involving multiple muscles (e.g., BFlh vs. ST vs. Gmax; DRA vs. L3ES vs. L5MF) within the same group, the Friedman test was employed as a non-parametric alternative to repeated-measures analysis of variance, given the non-normal distribution of certain sEMG parameters. When significant main effects were detected, Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons. For intergroup comparisons between athletes and non-athletes, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Between-group comparisons were considered secondary and exploratory due to the limited and unequal sample sizes and were therefore interpreted with caution. Given the repeated-measures structure of the experimental design (multiple muscles and exercise conditions within participants), a more comprehensive mixed-effects modelling approach could be considered. However, due to the relatively small and unequal sample sizes, as well as the non-normal distribution of several variables, a simplified non-parametric analysis framework was adopted. Accordingly, the present statistical approach was not intended to formally test group-by-exercise interaction effects, but rather to provide condition-specific and muscle-specific comparisons of neuromuscular activation patterns.

Parametric data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-parametric data are reported as median [interquartile range (IQR), 25–75%]. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Effect sizes were calculated to aid interpretation: rank-biserial correlation (r) was used for Friedman and Dunn’s post-hoc tests, and Cliff’s delta (|Δ|) was used for Mann–Whitney U tests. These metrics were derived based on standard non-parametric effect size formulations. Effect sizes are reported to facilitate the interpretation of the magnitude of observed differences.

In addition to sEMG-derived variables, extracted kinematic parameters (ROM, peak joint angles, and angular velocity) were compared between groups to assess potential differences in movement execution.

Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Methods (S1-6).

Results

All 25 participants completed the study, and their baseline characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table S3. Of the 25 participants, 10 were classified as athletes and 15 as physically active, nonathletes. Athletes and nonathletes were comparable in key anthropometric characteristics, including age (23.90 ± 4.77 vs. 24.93 ± 3.15 years), height (176.33 ± 6.87 vs. 174.99 ± 4.42 cm), weight (74.35 ± 8.11 vs. 70.54 ± 6.81 kg), BMI (23.67 ± 3.13 vs. 22.77 ± 2.27 kg/m²), and ECW/TBW ratio (0.370 ± 0.005 vs. 0.370 ± 0.006), with no significant between-group differences (all P > 0.05). Although athletes demonstrated higher IPAQ scores than nonathletes, all participants were classified as highly physically active according to the IPAQ criteria. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within a sample of generally active individuals, rather than assuming equivalent habitual activity levels between groups, and potential differences in overall activity levels should be considered when interpreting between-group effects.

To assess potential differences in movement execution, selected kinematic variables were compared between groups for both exercises. No significant between-group differences were observed in joint range of motion, peak joint angles, or angular velocity during the eccentric phase (all p > 0.05). Representative values included hip ROM (41.78° vs. 39.46°) and knee ROM (36.92° vs. 39.27°) for athletes and nonathletes, respectively. These findings suggest no clear evidence of systematic differences in gross movement execution between groups, although subtle differences cannot be excluded.

Intergroup differences in sEMG parameters

Between-group comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory given the sample size and study design.

RMS differences

As illustrated in Fig. 4, significant between-group differences in RMS values were detected in the BFlh and DRA during the BHE task, as well as in core-stabilizing musculature during the INHE task (P < 0.05; Cliff’s |Δ| range: 0.29–0.55; see Supplementary Table S4 for detailed statistics). During the BHE task, athletes exhibited significantly higher RMS values in the BFlh, whereas nonathletes showed higher RMS values in the DRA. During the INHE task, nonathletes demonstrated significantly higher RMS values in the core-stabilizing muscles, specifically L3ES and L5MF. Notably, athletes exhibited higher RMS values in BFlh, indicating greater activation of the primary dynamic lower limb muscle, whereas nonathletes demonstrated higher RMS values in the core muscle, DRA.

MF differences

As shown in Fig. 5, nonathletes exhibited significantly higher MF values during BHE for the ST, L3ES, and L5MF, and during INHE for the ST, Gmax, L3ES, and L5MF (all P < 0.05; Cliff’s |Δ| range: 0.36–0.56; details are provided in Supplementary Table S5). During the BHE task, nonathletes exhibited significantly higher MF values in the ST, L3ES, and L5MF. During the INHE task, nonathletes demonstrated higher MF values in the ST, Gmax, L3ES, and L5MF.

BFlh/ST ratio

As summarized in Table 1, athletes exhibited numerically higher BFlh/ST ratios during the BHE task, whereas nonathletes showed lower ratios. However, statistical comparisons of participant-level ratios revealed no significant between-group differences for either BHE (P = 0.19) or INHE (P = 0.76). These findings indicate that although descriptive trends suggest potential differences in relative hamstring activation, these differences were not statistically supported at the group level.

Discussion

This study observed that training status is associated with distinct neuromuscular strategies during eccentric hamstring loading. Athletes and nonathletes displayed qualitatively different coordination profiles—manifested in hamstring activation balance, spectral characteristics, and trunk–hip coordination—despite similar general activity levels. These findings suggest that structured training history, rather than habitual physical activity alone, may be associated with differences in the organisation of neuromuscular control during eccentric task execution.

Training status and hamstring activation strategies

A key finding was a tendency toward higher BFlh/ST ratios in athletes during the BHE task, contrasted with relatively lower ratios in nonathletes. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Rather than indicating a definitive dominance pattern, this observation suggests that long-term, structured training may be associated with a more targeted partitioning of eccentric demand across hamstring synergists, as supported by studies linking inter-individual activation patterns to mechanical outcomes [8, 9]. Conversely, in the absence of such training, activation appears to be driven predominantly by task constraints rather than learned coordination strategies [8, 27]. However, given the lack of statistically significant between-group differences in the BFlh/ST ratio, these interpretations should be considered exploratory. Importantly, these descriptive tendencies emerged despite comparable overall activity levels, suggesting that structured training history—beyond general physical activity—may influence neuromuscular coordination during eccentric tasks. Relative indices such as the BFlh/ST ratio may therefore provide complementary, hypothesis-generating information regarding within-hamstring recruitment patterns but should not be interpreted as definitive indicators of muscle dominance in the absence of statistical support.

Task-specific frequency characteristics and motor unit recruitment

Frequency-domain analyses provided complementary insights. A consistent median frequency hierarchy (BFlh > ST > Gmax) across both groups suggested a conserved, task-constrained recruitment architecture, aligning with known sensitivity of sEMG spectral parameters to contraction mechanics [28]. Notably, non-athletes exhibited higher median frequencies in several muscles (ST, Gmax, L3ES, and L5MF). These differences should be interpreted cautiously, as higher MF values do not necessarily indicate superior neuromuscular performance, but may reflect variations in motor unit recruitment patterns, contraction dynamics, or muscle fibre conduction velocity. One possible explanation is that nonathletes may rely on alternative or less efficient recruitment strategies under eccentric loading, potentially resulting in different spectral characteristics. In contrast, athletes may adopt more stable or task-specific activation patterns that do not necessarily manifest as higher MF values. These differences are best interpreted as descriptive markers of divergent neuromuscular strategies, influenced by neural drive and motor unit behaviour [22, 29], rather than as direct indicators of fibre-type composition or causal adaptations. Thus, spectral measures meaningfully complement amplitude-based indices in characterizing subtle, population-specific coordination patterns.

Trunk–hip coordination under eccentric loading

Both groups demonstrated greater activation of posterior trunk muscles than of the rectus abdominis, indicating that these tasks involve coordinated activation across the trunk–hip complex; however, this pattern may also be partly attributable to the extended body position required during both exercises, which inherently increases the demand on posterior trunk musculature. Athletes showed relatively greater engagement of lumbar extensors, which may reflect differences in neuromuscular activation patterns relevant to lumbopelvic control under high eccentric moments. Conversely, non-athletes demonstrated higher superficial abdominal activation under specific task conditions, particularly reflected by greater RMS values in the DRA during the BHE task (Sect. 3.2.1). This pattern may reflect a reliance on alternative stabilisation strategies, whereby less-trained individuals recruit more superficial trunk musculature to maintain trunk position under load [7]. These findings underscore the role of posterior trunk musculature in meeting the stability demands of lower-limb tasks [30], although the present study did not directly measure force production, spinal loading, or lumbopelvic stability, and therefore these interpretations should be made with caution. Accordingly, the observed differences should be interpreted as task- and posture-dependent activation patterns rather than definitive evidence of distinct trunk–hip coordination strategies, with differences observed between groups in how these demands are neuromuscular addressed.

Clinical and practical implications

From clinical and training perspectives, the present findings suggest that eccentric hamstring exercises involve coordinated activation across the hamstrings and trunk–hip complex under the present experimental conditions. Accordingly, exercise selection may consider both hamstrings loading and the associated demands on trunk stabilization. This is particularly relevant given the biomechanical coupling between eccentric hamstring loading and lumbopelvic control, where trunk extensor muscles contribute to maintaining pelvic alignment under anterior tilt moments. Therefore, eccentric exercise selection may be viewed not only as a means of targeting the hamstrings, but also as a strategy to train integrated trunk–hip neuromuscular control. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that different eccentric exercises may be selected according to specific training goals. For example, knee-dominant tasks such as the INHE may be preferentially used when the aim is to increase hamstring loading under extended muscle lengths, whereas hip-dominant tasks such as the BHE may place greater emphasis on trunk–hip coordination and posterior-chain integration. The observed group-specific strategies further indicate that an individual’s training history may inform exercise prescription. However, given that between-group differences were exploratory and not consistently statistically significant, these observations should be interpreted cautiously. While athletes exhibited specialized neuromuscular patterns, nonathletes’ greater reliance on the ST and rectus abdominis may indicate a tendency toward less differentiated recruitment patterns, supports prioritising posterior-chain engagement and balanced hamstring recruitment in less-trained populations. In practical terms, these findings may provide preliminary rationale for considering progressive eccentric training approaches in less-trained individuals, with initial emphasis on general neuromuscular control and trunk stability before introducing more demanding task-specific eccentric variations. These findings suggest that exercise prescription may benefit from a task- and population-specific approach, where different eccentric exercises are selected to emphasise either lateral hamstring loading (e.g., BFlh-dominant patterns) or more generalised neuromuscular coordination strategies. It is important to note that these cross-sectional sEMG differences do not directly translate to injury risk. Rather, they should be interpreted as reflecting underlying neuromuscular control strategies that may be relevant to mechanisms associated with HSI susceptibility. Accordingly, these results should not be interpreted as evidence that one exercise is superior for injury prevention, but rather as providing mechanistic insight that may inform more individualized programme design. However, they offer complementary, hypothesis-generating information that could support more individualized exercise progression in training and rehabilitation, especially as wearable monitoring technologies advance.

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations. First, the sample of healthy young men limits generalizability to women, older adults, and clinical populations. In addition, the relatively small sample size, particularly for between-group comparisons, may limit statistical power and increase the risk of Type II error, and these analyses should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Second, the controlled laboratory tasks (BHE, INHE) may not replicate the demands of high-speed, multi-planar sports movements. Third, although selected kinematic variables were quantified and no significant between-group differences were observed, the use of two-dimensional motion capture and the absence of full three-dimensional biomechanical analysis limit the ability to completely exclude subtle differences in movement execution. Fourth, while our athlete group encompassed a diverse range of sports based on predefined inclusion criteria, detailed information on lifetime sport-specific loading and training history was not systematically quantified, which may have contributed to the observed inter-individual variability in neuromuscular responses. Sport-specific loading may influence not only neuromuscular coordination but also underlying muscle structure, which may partly contribute to the observed activation patterns. As these factors were not directly assessed in the present study, their relative contribution cannot be determined. In addition, sEMG recordings were obtained from the dominant limb only, despite the bilateral nature of the tasks, which may not fully capture potential side-to-side differences in muscle activation. The use of %MVIC normalization, while widely adopted, may introduce variability related to the normalization procedure, particularly when comparing across individuals and muscles under dynamic conditions. Furthermore, although standardized protocols were applied, formal assessment of within-session or between-session reliability of the sEMG measures was not conducted, which should be addressed in future studies. In addition, the self-paced and partially unconstrained nature of the tasks may introduce inter-individual variability in movement strategies, particularly in trunk-dominant eccentric exercises. Finally, sEMG assesses only superficial muscle activity and provides relative activation measures, not direct force or injury risk metrics. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting neuromuscular control strategies rather than direct indicators of injury risk. Importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes determining causality. It remains unclear whether the observed neuromuscular differences are a direct adaptation to structured athletic training, or if individuals with advantageous neuromuscular profiles (e.g., BFlh-dominance) naturally self-select into higher-level athletics (i.e., survivorship bias). However, because our athlete group included disciplines with very low high-velocity eccentric hamstring eccentric demands (e.g., swimming), the observation of similar neuromuscular strategies across such diverse sports may be consistent with a generalized adaptation to structured athletic conditioning rather than exclusively resulting from genetic selection against hamstring strain injuries.

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to examine whether these neuromuscular patterns are modifiable and linked to functional outcomes. Combining sEMG with biomechanical and imaging approaches may further clarify neuromuscular strategies during eccentric loading across athletic populations and rehabilitation contexts.

Conclusion

This study identified differences in neuromuscular activation strategies during eccentric hamstring exercises between athletes and physically active nonathletes, within a generally physically active sample. Athletes tended to exhibit biceps femoris long head–dominant recruitment pattern with greater lumbar stabilizer engagement, while non-athletes showed a relative tendency toward greater semitendinosus and rectus abdominis activation. These findings suggest that structured training history may be associated with differences in muscle coordination profiles during controlled eccentric tasks. Although these cross-sectional sEMG patterns do not directly translate to injury risk, they provide descriptive and hypothesis-generating insights that may provide preliminary information to inform future research and potentially contribute to individualized exercise prescription in training and rehabilitation.
