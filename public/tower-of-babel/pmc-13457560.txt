Modulation of habenula axon terminals supports action-outcome associations in larval zebrafish
Authors: Emanuele Paoli, Virginia Palieri, Amey Shenoy, Ruben Portugues
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving behavioral performance relies on the ability to associate decisions with their positive and negative outcomes. Although neurons that associate actions with their consequences have been identified across multiple brain regions, the circuit-level mechanisms underlying this integration remain poorly understood. Here, using an operant thermoregulatory assay in larval zebrafish, we show that the dorsal habenula-interpeduncular nucleus (dHb-IPN) pathway is necessary for associating actions with subsequent thermal reward. A population of intermediate IPN (iIPN) neurons encodes actions only when they lead to reward, suggesting a role in establishing action-outcome associations. This integration is temporally precise, requiring actions and their outcomes to occur within approximately one second in both the behavioral assay and iIPN responses. We then combine circuit mapping and axon imaging to show that actions and reward signals are conveyed by GABAergic prepontine and glutamatergic dHb neurons, respectively. Finally, the integration between action and reward signals relies on presynaptic GABAB receptor-mediated modulation of dHb axon terminals by prepontine neurons. These results link a crucial computation for adaptive behavior to a specific circuit mechanism.Subject terms: Reward, Navigation

Introduction

Animals adapt their behavior by learning which actions lead to positive or negative outcomes. Across brain circuits involved in forming these associations, outcome representations are shaped by the actions that precede them1–4. The presence of such an action-outcome code across diverse circuits suggests a general strategy by which the brain links actions to their consequences. However, a general principle explaining how this integration emerges from interacting circuit components remains unclear.

A crucial mediator of adaptive behavior, particularly in aversive contexts, is the dorsal (in fish, medial in mammals) habenula-interpeduncular nucleus (dHb-IPN) pathway5,6. Its role is conserved across species and developmental stages. In mice, it contributes to the acquisition, maintenance and expression of learned, but not innate, fear and avoidance responses7–10. In adult zebrafish, it is involved in fear-induced turning behavior11, directional decision-making12 and reversal learning13. Similarly, in larval zebrafish, it controls freezing behavior, learned avoidance and homeostatic navigation14–16.

Its fundamental circuit architecture is also conserved. dHb axons project through the fasciculus retroflexus and enter the IPN at the level of the ventral midbrain. In fish, the dHb displays an asymmetric projection pattern; the left dHb projects to the dorsal interpeduncular nucleus (dIPN), while the right dHb projects to the intermediate (iIPN) and ventral IPN (vIPN). Although IPN neurons receive most of their input from the dHb, additional projections arise from other structures, including numerous hindbrain nuclei17.

Plasticity between habenula and IPN neurons has been proposed to underlie experience-dependent modifications of aversive behavior6. However, how habenular and extra-habenular inputs jointly shape IPN activity and the computations performed by this pathway remains largely unknown.

Here, using a thermoregulatory operant assay18–20, we show that larval zebrafish learn to associate reorientation swims and thermal reward to minimize exposure to aversive cold temperatures. Delaying the reward relative to the motor event impairs performance, indicating that associations rely on the precise timing between a successful choice and its positive outcome. We next found that the dHb–IPN pathway is necessary for establishing these associations. A population of neurons in the iIPN non-linearly integrates action and outcome signals, encoding action identity only when followed by a thermal reward within a short time window. This integration is temporally precise, with a time window similar to the one found in the operant assay.

Using a combination of functional imaging and circuit mapping techniques we reconstructed the circuit upstream of iIPN neurons alongside their neurotransmitter identity and characterized their functional responses. We found that the right dHb conveys thermal reward signals to iIPN neurons. This input is locally modulated at dHb axon terminals by a GABAergic population of prepontine neurons that encodes the preceding action, providing a circuit mechanism for the neural code observed in iIPN neurons.

Results

Larval zebrafish associate actions and outcomes for adaptive thermoregulatory behavior

Zebrafish larvae seek to maintain a stable body temperature of 25–28 °C14,21,22. This equilibrium is achieved by monitoring temperature changes detected during swims and by reacting to deviations from the setpoint14. Here, we leverage this behavioral strategy to study how associations between actions and outcomes are formed. More specifically, whether fish are able to associate and remember a specific swim direction with the outcome of warming its body in a cold environment (thermal reward). To this end, we developed an assay in which rapid body temperature changes can be experimentally controlled and delivered on a swim-by-swim basis.

Fish could freely swim in a chamber with water at 20 °C. Their position was continuously monitored with a high-speed camera, and a set of galvanometric mirrors was used to direct an infrared laser onto the fish’s body (Fig. S1a, see Methods)23. The laser was calibrated to increase body temperature by ∼1 °C in 1 second, corresponding to the fish inter-swim interval (Fig. S1b-d, see Methods). Given the zebrafish setpoint, IR heating is expected to have a positive valence for the larvae and to be preferred over the baseline condition of 20 °C (Fig. 1a).

We first tested this preference using an assay in which heating was contingent on the fish entering a rectangular region of interest (ROI) within the arena (Fig. S1e). Importantly, no positional cues indicated to the fish where this ROI was.

As expected, larvae (n = 21 animals) consistently preferred the warmer region compared to a control group (n = 5 animals) without IR heating (Fig. S1f-h).

We next designed an operant thermoregulatory assay in which thermal reward is paired with either right or left turns.

In this paradigm fish could increase their body temperature by turning in a specific direction (rule direction) and consequently minimize the time spent at the colder temperature. The experiment (n = 54 animals) consisted of 60 trials: 30 conditioned in one swim turn direction and 30 in the opposite. Each trial (Fig. 1b) was preceded by an inter-trial interval (ITI) of at least 5 seconds (see later) during which an IR laser followed the fish. After this the laser was turned off, initiating an aversive (cold) period of up to 10 seconds. During this cold period, turning in the rule direction allowed the fish to rapidly increase their body temperature again by receiving the thermal reward. This would lead to the next ITI before starting the next trial. If the fish did not turn in the rule direction within 10 seconds, the trial was aborted. To ensure that the fish had space to turn freely in both directions, trials were initiated only when the animal was distant from the arena borders (see Methods), resulting in a distribution of ITIs (Fig. S2a).

We quantified the direction of the first swim during the cold period and found that, over trials, fish developed a reorientation bias (Fig. S2b-c) that matched the current rule block (Figs. 1c and S2d). This directional bias was not accompanied by changes in turn magnitude (Fig. S2e) or swim rate during cold periods (Fig. S2f), and was absent during the inter-trial intervals (ITIs) (Fig. S2g–h). These results indicate that fish increase the likelihood in turning toward the previously rewarded direction rather than modifying swim kinematics or entering in a generalized aroused state to counteract aversive temperature drops. Furthermore, directional bias was absent in trials preceded by long ITIs (Fig. S2i), leading us to quantify performance from this point onward using only trials preceded by short ITIs (≤ 7.5 seconds, Fig. S2j), corresponding to more than 70% of the trials. The choice of inclusion threshold did not affect the results (Fig. S2k).

We next assessed whether the precise timing between the correct swim and thermal reward is critical for establishing action-outcome associations.

To test this, we introduced a delay of 0, 0.4, or 0.8 seconds between the two events and repeated the assay (Fig. 1d; n = 20 animals per condition, with each animal tested in only one condition). Indeed, we observed a significant impairment in task performance, as reflected by the average cold exposure time (corrected for delay; Fig. 1e) and reorientation bias (Fig. 1f).

The dHb is required for adaptive thermoregulatory behavior

Previous studies have shown that the zebrafish dorsal habenula (dHb) is crucial for experience-dependent behaviors11,13–16. To test whether this structure is required in our assay, we performed chemogenetic ablation and repeated our assay (ablation group: n = 71 animals, treatment control group: n = 72 animals) using a transgenic line labeling dHb and a sparse subset of torus longitudinalis (TL) neurons (Fig. 1g, Fig. S3a-b; see Methods). We found that ablation significantly impaired cold exposure time (Fig. 1h) and reduced reorientation bias (Fig. 1i), suggesting a deficit in establishing action-outcome associations.

Previous work has shown that, when exposed to homeostatic threats, zebrafish produce large amplitude turns as an innate defensive behavioral strategy14,24,25. Therefore, we reasoned that if dHb ablation led to a general deficit in sensory processing, then this innate behavior should be affected as well. However, the reorientation magnitude (regardless of left or right direction) of the same swims quantified earlier (first swim after temperature decrease) was similar in the two experimental groups and higher than shuffled controls (Fig. S3c). We also found no evidence of motor deficits, based on the analysis of multiple swimming kinematic parameters (Fig. S3d-g).

Together, these results suggest that the dHb is not directly involved in motor control or innate aversive responses. Instead, its role appears to be specific to guiding behavior based on past experience6,14.

Thermal responses in r-dHb neurons

Having established a crucial role for the dHb in associating actions with thermal reward, we aimed to study how these signals are encoded and potentially integrated in this region and its unique downstream target, the interpeduncular nucleus (IPN, Figs. 2aI and S4a-d). To this end, we performed calcium imaging with a laser-scanning two-photon microscope to record activity from GCaMP6s-expressing dHb neurons in a head-fixed preparation. In this protocol, temperature changes were temporally locked to the end of the animal’s swim (Fig. 2aII, see Methods). To facilitate statistical comparisons across action-outcome conditions, each swim, regardless of amplitude or direction, had a fixed probability of switching the current laser state from on to off or vice versa. As a result, swim decisions can be categorized by combinations of swim type (left, right or forward) and outcomes (thermal reward, temperature decrease and no temperature change) (Figs. 2aIII and S4e). Furthermore, the fish was continuously perfused with 20 °C water, and the IR laser power was reduced (see Methods). This configuration enabled rapid temperature increases at stimulation onset while preventing temperature accumulation (Fig. S4f-h).

We started by imaging neurons in the right subnucleus of the dHb (r-dHb, n = 14 animals), known to contain temperature-sensitive neurons14,24 (Fig. S5a-b). Consistent with this, computing evoked activity across different action-outcome combinations revealed robust responses to temperature changes and negligible responses to actions (Fig. S5c). We next quantified activity evoked by temperature stimulation irrespective of the preceding action and identified neurons that responded reliably either to thermal reward or to the opposite temperature change (34.5%, 16.9% of the total recorded neurons, Fig. S5d-e, see Methods). Thermal reward neurons had heterogeneous response profiles, which we categorized as fast-adapting (transient increase with rapid adaptation), fast non-adapting (sustained increase), and slow (Fig. S5f-h, see Methods). During our chemogenetic experiments, TL neurons were also ablated. To test whether they could account for the observed behavioral phenotype, we imaged this population in a separate cohort of animals (n = 3 animals, Fig. S5i). Given their reported role in visual processing, we delivered temperature and full-field light stimulation in open loop. TL neurons responded strongly to light but not to temperature (Fig. S5j), consistent with previous findings26, making it unlikely that their ablation explains our results.

iIPN neurons encode action identity contingent on thermal reward

We then imaged the intermediate portion of the IPN (iIPN), which receives direct projections from the r-dHb. Here, local neurons cluster their dendrites into well-defined regions called glomeruli27. To study their activity, we used a transgenic line that labels a population of excitatory (vglut2a +) neurons and recorded from three bilateral glomeruli (Figs. 2b and S6a-b; n = 19 animals).

We found that only one glomerulus showed elevated activity during our protocol (from now on referred to simply as the glomerulus). In stark contrast to what we observed in the r-dHb, glomerular activity was variable, with clear transients during thermal reward occurring only a fraction of the time. Closer inspection revealed a strong relationship between this variability and the animal’s behavior. Figure 2c provides an example, showing the activity of the left (blue trace) and right glomerulus (red trace). When the animal turns left or right but this swim does not lead to a temperature change (no outcome), the glomeruli are almost silent. However, when the same type of movement leads to the thermal reward, the glomerulus located in the hemisphere contralateral to the turned direction is strongly activated (Figs. 2d and S6c). Note that the change in temperature is delivered at movement termination and, considering the stochastic nature of our protocol, the animal cannot predict the outcome of each swim. Interestingly, once active, glomerular activity does not return to baseline like motor-modulated cells, but instead remains active throughout the stimulation period (Fig. 2c right, black arrows).

We then quantified the interaction between different action-outcome events on the basis of their hemispheric position. First, we computed the evoked ΔF/F based on all combinations of actions and outcomes. Then, we expressed each glomerulus’ tuning relative to its position and swim direction. In this analysis, the "preferred reorientation” (pref. reor.) condition includes responses from the left glomerulus during right swims and the right glomerulus during left swims, categorized by different outcomes. Conversely, the "opposite reorientation” (opp. reor.) condition groups responses from left glomerulus during left swims and right glomerulus during right swims. This analysis revealed clear anatomical structuring (Fig. 2e) suggesting an underlying lateralized circuit architecture.

The stark difference between the evoked activity when the preferred reorientation is coupled to a thermal reward and the other conditions suggests that these neurons non-linearly integrate specific motor actions with subsequent thermal feedback. To test whether this observation is a general feature in our dataset, we designed an index (motor modulation index or MMI, see Methods) specifically tailored to test this form of integration. An MMI close to zero indicates a neuron tuned to thermal reward regardless of the preceding action. A positive MMI indicates non-linear interaction, whereas a negative MMI suggests directional swim modulation only in the absence of thermal reward. This analysis revealed an MMI of 1.413, corresponding to a ∼4-fold increase in activity when the preferred action was followed by reward (Fig. 2f).

We next imaged population activity in the somata of iIPN neurons (Fig. S7a, n = 17 animals) and compared it to the activity measured in the upstream r-dHb. The MMI distribution of the latter was centered at zero, indicating that r-dHb thermal reward responses are unaffected by the preceding movement. In contrast, the distribution of iIPN neurons was shifted toward positive values (Fig. S7b-c). Finally, we tested whether iIPN thermal reward modulation depends on an intact dHb. We repeated the dHb chemogenetic ablation and, after 1 day of recovery, we imaged iIPN neurons (Fig. 2g, ablation group: n = 11 animals, treatment control group: n = 13 animals). Compared to controls, the ablation group significantly reduced the magnitude of evoked ∆F/F at reward delivery (Fig. 2h), suggesting that the r-dHb is necessary for the thermal reward component of iIPN responses.

Integration of actions and thermal reward in iIPN neurons is temporally restricted and asymmetric

To determine how action and reward signals are integrated in these neurons, we examined neuronal activity during an open-loop protocol, in which actions and temperature stimulation are decoupled (Fig. 2i, n = 21 animals, see Methods).

Even under these conditions, some stimulation trials evoked clear calcium transients, especially when the fish swam close to thermal reward (Fig. S7d). We calculated the evoked ΔF/F as a function of the lag between actions and reward. Negative lag values indicate that reward delivery followed the swim, while positive values indicate the opposite sequence. The absolute value of the lag corresponded to the interval between the two events (see Methods, Fig. S7e, left). A neuron acting as a coincidence detector would show a symmetric response profile around zero lag, whereas a sequence detector (i.e., a neuron that responds to action-outcome sequences) would display an asymmetric response shifted toward negative lags (Fig. S7e right). The two glomeruli strongly responded when the preferred motor event preceded the outcome event by less than one second (Fig. 2j, top: preferred reorientation, bottom: non-preferred reorientation). Finally, we evaluated glomerular responses during the preferred swim direction closely followed by thermal reward (< 1 second) and compared it with the arithmetic sum of the two events in isolation. We found that addition alone cannot account for glomerular responses (Fig. 2k), further supporting the non-linear integration hypothesis.

Overall, our imaging experiments revealed a significant transformation between r-dHb and iIPN neurons. While r-dHb activity signals thermal reward, the iIPN encodes which action successfully led to that event. Furthermore, this action–outcome coding is localized to a specific iIPN glomerulus per side. This, together with the lateralized action tuning, suggests a well-defined lateralized circuit underlying this computation.

iIPN neurons receive habenular and prepontine inputs

To elucidate the circuit organization, we aimed at reconstructing these neurons along with neurons targeting the glomerulus using a SBEM dataset28. We reconstructed 41 right-iIPN neurons whose dendrite was located within the IPN neuropil (Fig. 3a). They had a bipolar morphology, a single dendritic tree and their axons reached a neuropil region near the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) (Fig. 3a first column, Fig. S8a). Although these neurons projected outside the IPN, they remained close, still within rhombomere 1/2.

Using a DBSCAN clustering algorithm based on dendrite centroid positions (ε = 4.5 µm, min. samples = 5 neurons), we identified three glomeruli comprising 7, 7, and 16 iIPN neurons (Fig. 3a second, third and fourth columns), along with 12 outliers. Co-registration of the EM and imaging data highlighted the third cluster (blue neurons in Fig. 3a) as the anatomical substrate of our previous imaging results (Fig. 3b). Then, we detected axo-dendritic contacts from 4 glomerular neurons and reconstructed their presynaptic partners and other neurons terminating in the glomerulus. A significant portion of these inputs originated from the dHb (n = 39 neurons; 35 from r-dHb and 4 from l-dHb, 28/39 fully and 11/39 partially reconstructed axons, total number of synaptic contacts=103, Fig. 3c). These axons wrap around the iIPN neuropil and innervate multiple subregions, including the area corresponding to the contralateral (not reconstructed) glomerulus. To quantify the bilateral projections of habenula axons, we identified synapses formed by the fully reconstructed r- and l-dHb axons (n = 28, see Methods) throughout their full axon length and selected only those within the anterior left and right iIPN region (Fig. S8b). We then plotted their distribution along the mediolateral axis (Fig. 3d), confirming that dHb axons innervate both regions.

A second group of presynaptic partners consisted of unipolar neurons from the ipsilateral (n = 1 neuron) as well as contralateral (n = 5 neurons) dorsal prepontine region (dPPRipsi and dPPRcontra), located in the dorsal anterior part of the hindbrain. Unlike dHb neurons, their axon terminals had tufted endings29 resembling iIPN dendrites and exclusively terminated on the seed glomerulus (Figs. 3e and S8c). While these two populations were similar in morphology, we detected axo-dendritic contacts (total number of synaptic contacts=126) with the reconstructed iIPN neurons only for the dPPRcontra population, spanning the entire glomerulus (Fig. S8d). However, a further detection of synaptic contact from the dPPRipsi neuron highlighted contacts with the previously reconstructed r-dHb axons (Fig. S8e, total number of synaptic contacts=15).

To validate our EM findings, we performed targeted photoactivation experiments (Fig. 3f) using animals co-expressing a photoactivable GFP variant (paGFP) and red-labeled dHb axons (n = 4 animals for the photoactivation group and n = 2 animals for the control group)30. We used the red channel to locate the glomerulus and activated the paGFP at 800 nm to retrogradely or anterogradely label neurons with axons or dendrites in the activation region, respectively. Consistent with EM reconstructions, we labeled the somata and axons of iIPN neurons, dHb axons, dPPRipsi and dPPRcontra neurons (Fig. S9a-b). While the somata of dPPRcontra neurons were confined to a smaller region, we detected a similar number of neurons in the two prepontine populations (Fig. S9c). The neurotransmitter identity of the dPPR populations is unknown. To address this, we repeated the photoactivation experiments in fish co-expressing paGFP and red-labeled GABAergic (gad1b promoter, n = 4 animals, Fig. S9d) or glutamatergic neurons (vglut2a promoter, n = 3 animals, Fig. S9e). While dPPRcontra show a poor overlap for both markers, dPPRipsi are predominantly GABAergic (75% overlap with the gad1b marker, Figs. 3g, h and S9f).

To summarize, we found that glomerular iIPN neurons receive inputs from three distinct regions: dHb (predominantly from the temperature-responding right subnucleus), dPPRipsi and dPPRcontra.

Compartmentalized activity in dHb axon terminals mirrors iIPN glomerular tuning

We next imaged dHb axon terminals at the same location where we recorded iIPN dendritic activity (Fig. 4a, n = 9 animals). Based on the known dHb axon morphology31 and activity patterns recorded in dHb somata, we expected temperature stimulation to evoke symmetric increases in axonal activity across regions corresponding to both glomeruli. Instead, we observed that axon terminal activity was spatially heterogeneous and strongly lateralized27.

Pixel-wise principal component analysis (PCA, see Methods) highlighted this heterogeneity, showing compartmentalized activity (Fig. 4b). Some components (principal component, PC) were symmetric around the midline (PC1, PC2, PC4), whereas others were strongly asymmetric (PC3, PC5) and overlapping the underlying iIPN glomeruli. Analysis of ∆F/F responses across different action–outcome combinations revealed that the pair of functional patches overlapping the previously imaged iIPN glomeruli showed response profiles that closely matched those previously observed in the glomeruli (Fig. 4c, gray and black arrows in the second column). Indeed, when we extracted the activity from these glomerulus-like regions we found the same anatomical action tuning (Fig. 4d) and a similar MMI (Fig. 4e). In addition, we found a pair of more medial patches that responded selectively to aversive temperature decreases (Fig. 4c, light and dark blue arrows in the third column), indicating tuning to negative outcomes.

The observed compartmentalized activity may originate for two reasons. The first is due to a small population of dHb neurons that encode action-outcome associations and relay this information to the glomerulus. However, this scenario is unlikely as none of our reconstructed dHb axons project to a specific glomerulus, a result also consistent with previous studies27,31. Furthermore, similar compartmentalized activity has been observed in the dorsal and ventral IPN27,32. The second and more plausible explanation, reconciling functional and anatomical data, is that dHb axon terminals are locally modulated.

GABAB receptor modulates dHb axon terminals

A simple model that could account for the activity observed in dHb axons is that reward information transmitted by r-dHb neurons is locally modulated at the axon terminals by a population of neurons driven by the preceding movement. This enhanced axonal activity, in turn, drives iIPN neurons (Fig. S10a).

A potent modulator of habenular glutamate release, identified in both zebrafish33 and mammals8,34,35, is the presynaptic GABAB receptor (GABABR). In most brain regions, activation of presynaptic GABABR suppresses glutamate release36. In contrast, in a subpopulation of mammalian habenula axons, GABABR activation strongly enhances glutamate release, producing nonlinear responses for the same level of somatic depolarization8.

We tested the role of GABABR first using a receptor antagonist (CGP 46381). We repeated our thermoregulatory assay in freely swimming fish (treatment group: n = 20, control group: n = 20) and found that treatment significantly impaired fish performance (Fig. S11a). We then tested its effect in dHb axons. Fish underwent an initial imaging session, after which we applied the antagonist (treatment group, n = 7 animals) or water (control group, n = 9 animals) and re-imaged dHb axons (Fig. 4f). The treatment had no effect on swim frequency (Fig. S11b) nor global dHb axon activity, as assessed by pixel-wise ΔF/F standard deviation over the entire experiment (Fig. S11c-d). We then quantified its effect on the reward-tuned patch and found that GABABR blockade abolished action–outcome responses (Fig. 4g). In contrast, the proportion and response magnitude of thermal reward–tuned r-dHb somata remained unchanged after treatment (n = 6 animals per group, Fig. S11e–g), indicating that the impairment occurs at the axon terminals rather than reflecting a general reduction in temperature responses.

We then tested the opposite manipulation and activated GABABR using the agonist baclofen (Fig. S11h, treatment group: n = 12 animals, control group: n = 13 animals). To isolate the effect of GABABR from other movement-related neuromodulation, we presented temperature stimuli in open-loop. As expected from iIPN data, open-loop responses were overall weaker and more variable than in the previous closed-loop condition. Nevertheless, the agonist enhanced the peak responses in the two reward-tuned patches (Fig. S11i).

These results show that GABABRs regulate r-dHb axon terminals and, consequently, enable action–outcome responses in iIPN neurons.

GABAergic prepontine neurons convey action signals

Our previous findings suggest that GABAergic transmission in the iIPN glomeruli modulates dHb axon activity according to the animal’s movements. Given that both dHb axons and iIPN neurons are glutamatergic, we imaged GABAergic neuropil within the glomeruli, likely corresponding to dPPRipsi axon terminals (Fig. 5a, b, n = 13 animals). Consistent with our prediction, we found that these axons responded to directional swims (Fig. 5c). While we still observed weak temperature responses, the interaction between sensory and motor variables was weak and the motor component dominated (Fig. 5d, e). Action tuning mirrored that of iIPN neurons, with excitation for the contralateral direction relative to the glomerular hemisphere (left for the right dPPRipsi axons, right for the contralateral counterpart) and inhibition for the opposite reorientation. The observed inhibition from baseline during non-preferred reorientations suggests that these neurons have a non-zero baseline firing rate. This, in turn, implies that dPPRipsi may modulate dHb axon bidirectionally, enhancing r-dHb glutamatergic transmission following reorientations toward the preferred direction and weakening it after non-preferred ones.

To verify that the activity recorded from GABAergic axon terminals in the glomerulus originates from dPPRipsi neurons rather than dPPRcontra neurons, we imaged the brain region where these axon terminals originate. We then identified neurons whose activity matched that of the GABAergic axons and examined which hemisphere they were located in. If our hypothesis is correct, left-hemisphere dPPR neurons should be active during right turns and right-hemisphere neurons during left turns, matching the GABAergic axons’ tuning. If the axons instead originate from dPPRcontra neurons, the opposite hemispheric pattern would be expected.

We recorded the somatic activity of dPPR neurons at multiple dorsoventral planes using the nuclear-localized GCaMP6s expressed pan-neuronally (Fig. 5f, n = 7 animals). Next, for each neuron (number of ROIs = 4352 ± 503 per plane, Fig. S12a), we quantified evoked activity during left and right swims and computed two metrics: a polar angle describing the relative responses to the two swim directions, and the maximum response magnitude between the two conditions (Fig. 5g). These metrics were used to identify neurons that were excited by one direction and inhibited by the opposite. Finally, we retained only neurons whose spatial locations were consistent across animals and between hemispheres (see Methods).

This analysis identified two bilateral clusters of neurons with tuning properties similar to those of the GABAergic axon terminals recorded in the iIPN (Figs. 5h and S12b). The anterior cluster strongly overlapped with the somata positions of both dPPRipsi and dPPRcontra neurons. In the left hemisphere, neurons were excited by right turns and inhibited by left turns, whereas neurons in the contralateral hemisphere exhibited the opposite tuning. Given that the activity observed in GABAergic axons and dPPR somata shares the same hemispheric position-tuning relationship, dPPRipsi neurons possess the appropriate ipsilateral projections to account for the GABAergic axon imaging results.

Discussion

In this study, we describe a circuit mechanism critical for associating actions with a positive outcome in larval zebrafish.

Using an operant thermoregulatory assay18–20, we found that fish learn to associate directional swims with a thermal reward to minimize the exposure to cold temperatures. These findings highlight the importance of integrating movement and thermal feedback in ectotherms such as zebrafish to preserve thermal homeostasis14,37. Directional bias emerged within just a few trials, indicating a rapid form of learning. However, it is likely that performance may improve further with additional training sessions or by using older zebrafish38. For instance, responses after long ITIs did not follow the associated rule block. Although effects of temperature accumulation or changes in behavioral state39 during long ITIs cannot be fully excluded, this pattern may reflect early-stage learning. Ablation of the dHb impaired learned thermoregulatory responses but did not affect swimming kinematics or innate temperature avoidance. The line used for ablations also labeled a small subset of TL neurons, which support visual processing and do not respond to temperature stimulation, making them unlikely to account for our findings26. Our results are in line with previous studies linking this structure and its downstream partner, the IPN, to fear and avoidance learning8–11,15,16, navigation in a thermal gradient14, learning of directional rules12 and generally support the idea of the dHb as an experience-dependent switchboard of behavior6.

By combining population-specific functional imaging and anatomical reconstructions, we found that thermal reward and the identity of the preceding action are nonlinearly integrated in the iIPN through converging inputs from the dHb and dPPR, respectively (Fig. 6a). Moreover, the integration time window observed in iIPN neurons matched the one identified behaviorally in the operant conditioning assay. However, the ~1-second neural integration window likely reflects the temporal precision required to link a specific action to its outcome, whereas behavioral memory of the association may persist and strengthen over longer timescales with additional experience38. These results suggest that iIPN neurons evaluate the outcome of directional movements (left and right swims) and update an internal representation of action–outcome associations (Fig. 6b). This representation would then allow the animal to, through a yet undetermined downstream region, bias turning behavior in response to future aversive temperature changes. This idea aligns with previous work linking the IPN to evidence accumulation favoring swims in a particular direction40. More broadly, IPN neurons may enable zebrafish to use past experience to guide turning behavior adaptively. While this work focused on thermal reward (a positive outcome), we also identified a functional patch of dHB axons combining actions with negative outcomes (i.e. temperature decrease), suggesting that another IPN glomerulus processes negative valence signals. Further work is needed to determine whether this glomerulus follows the same circuit principles described here. It also remains unclear whether iIPN neurons are involved only in outcome evaluation or also in the acquisition and retention of learned responses.

One important question for future research is to assess whether the iIPN neurons studied at present are specialized for processing valence signals related to temperature changes or are instead multimodal. The latter hypothesis is more likely, given that the r-dHb contains chemosensory41–44, mechanosensory45 and multimodal neurons14,24. Similarly, iIPN neurons may also contribute to contextual associations beyond directional swims. However, considering the absence of reciprocal connectivity with other glomeruli, their role is likely to be restricted to lateralized contexts (e.g. stimuli presented on the left vs. right side of the body).

Other IPN glomeruli, in the dorsal (dIPN)27,32,46,47, intermediate or ventral27 subregions, may associate thermal reward with different contexts or reference frames and operate in parallel with the iIPN neurons studied here. Notably, habenular axons in the dIPN also exhibit compartmentalized activity aligned with the underlying glomerular structure27,32, and heading direction neurons (r1π neurons) synapse directly onto these axons46. Because a subset of temperature-responsive r-dHb neurons also reaches the dIPN27, similar mechanisms to those described here could in principle integrate valence signals with heading direction to support thermal gradient navigation22.

Another open question is how neurons downstream of the iIPN bias behavior. Considering the reported strong connectivity between this region and the monoaminergic systems48, neurotransmitters such as dopamine or serotonin may modulate downstream thermoregulatory circuits22,24,37,49,50.

Finally, the role of dPPRcontra neurons remains largely unknown. A small fraction of these neurons is GABAergic, and neurons in this region are directionally modulated by turning behavior, with tuning opposite to that observed in downstream iIPN neurons. One possibility is that GABAergic dPPRcontra neurons may provide axo-dendritic inhibition for the opposite directional swim, thereby refining action-outcome coding in iIPN neurons. However, further investigation is required to clarify the function of the remaining non-GABAergic dPPRcontra population. It is also possible that these neurons may not be engaged by the relatively simple task used here and could instead be recruited in other motor or sensory contexts, perhaps by modulating r-dHb axon terminals as well.

We additionally found that the integration of actions and thermal reward arises in the dHb presynaptic axon terminals (Fig. 6c). This mechanism offers an elegant and efficient solution for linking actions and outcomes as different axon segments of the same neuron could be independently modulated by distinct decisions. For instance, a single dHb axon could target both the left and right iIPN glomeruli and its segments be selectively modulated by different actions. A similar mechanism has been described in the insect mushroom body, where dopaminergic neurons modulate segments of olfactory axons in a behavioral context-dependent manner51. Interestingly, our EM reconstructions revealed that the dPPRipsi neuron also receives input from iIPN neurons (see Methods), raising the possibility that recurrent connections within this circuit could refine or sustain the action–outcome signal.

Finally, we found that action-outcome integration is mediated by GABABR, acting as a presynaptic positive modulator of r-dHb signals in response to the animal’s movements. Our results are in line with mouse studies showing that this receptor enhances glutamate release from the dHb mammalian homolog8,34,35. In mouse, this enhancement is both IPN subregion and cell-type-specific35. A similar process appears to occur in larval zebrafish, since GABABR signaling in the dIPN reduces dHb-to-IPN transmission, whereas in the iIPN it enhances it33. Importantly, this does not exclude additional mechanisms, such as r-dHb GABAR activation via volume-transmitted GABA from dPPRipsi neurons, post-synaptic inhibition, intrinsic properties of iIPN neurons, retrograde inhibition52 or other forms of neuromodulation53.

Although adult zebrafish and mammals possess a more elaborate habenula–IPN pathway than larval zebrafish, the circuit mechanism identified here may serve a similar function, a hypothesis that can be tested experimentally. To conclude, while some implementation details may be unique to the dHb-IPN pathway, the modulation of reward-related axons by choice-selective neurons may represent a general principle employed by other neural systems to associate actions with their consequences.

Methods

Zebrafish husbandry

All procedures related to animal handling were conducted following protocols approved by the Technische Universität München and the Regierung von Oberbayern (TVA 55-2-1-54-2532-101-12 and TVA 55.2-2532.Vet_02-24-5). Adult zebrafish (Danio rerio) from the Tüpfel long fin (TL) strain were kept at 27.5-28 °C on a 14/10 light cycle, and hosted in a fish facility that provided full recirculation of water with carbon-, bio- and UV filtering and a daily exchange of 12% of water. Water pH was kept at 7.0-7.5 with a 20 g/liter buffer and conductivity maintained at 750-800 μS using 100 g/liter. Fish were hosted in 3.5 liter tanks in groups of 7-17 animals and fed with Gemma micron 300 (Skretting USA) and live food (Artemia salina) twice per day. Larvae were fed with Sera micron Nature (Sera) and ST-1 (Aquaschwarz) three times a day. All experiments were conducted on 5-8 dpf larvae of yet undetermined sex. The week before the experiment, one male and one female or three male and three female animals were left breeding overnight in a breeding tank (Tecniplast). The day after, eggs were collected in the morning, rinsed with water from the facility water system, and then kept in groups of 20-40 in 90 mm Petri dishes filled with Danieau solution 0.3x (17.4 mM NaCl, 0.21 mM KCl, 0.12 mM MgSO4, 0.18mM Ca(NO3)2, 1.5mM HEPES, reagents from Sigma-Aldrich) until hatching and in water from the fish facility afterwards. Larvae were kept in an incubator at 28.5 °C and a 14/10 hour light/dark cycle, and their solution was changed daily. At 4 or 5 dpf, animals were lightly anesthetized with Tricaine 440 mesylate (Sigma-Aldrich) and screened for fluorescence under an epifluorescent microscope.

Animal strains

The Tüpfel long-fin (TL) wild-type strain was used for most of the freely swimming experiments. For imaging experiments, fish were mitfa -/- (i.e., nacre) mutants lacking melanophores to allow optical access to the brain. Transgenic lines used are:

Tg(16715:Gal4);Tg(UAS:Ntr-mcherry)14 used for dHb chemogenetic ablation and targeted photoactivation experiments.

Tg(16715:Gal4);Tg(UAS:GCaMP6s)mpn10114,54 used for dHb and TL imaging.

Tg(elavl3:GCaMP6s)55 used for imaging dHb somata.

Tg(elavl3:Hsa.H2B-GCaMP6s)56 used for imaging of iIPN and dPPR neurons.

Tg(s1168t:Gal4);Tg(UAS:GCaMP6s)mpn10154,57 used for imaging iIPN dendrites (iIPN glomeruli).

Tg(gad1b:Gal4);Tg(UAS:GCaMP6s)mpn10154,58 used for imaging GABAergic axons.

Tg(Cau.Tuba1:c3paGFP)a743730 used for photoactivations.

Tg(gad1b:RFP)59 for anatomy and targeted photoactivations.

Tg(vglut2a:DsRed)60 for anatomy and targeted photoactivations.

Experimental setups

IR laser freely swimming setup

This setup was inspired by a previous publication23 and largely follows the same design. Briefly, fish were placed in an arena 200 mm x 110 mm (preference assay) or 200 mm x 65 mm (operant thermoregulatory assay), illuminated with 750 nm IR illumination, and allowed to move freely. For all experiments, we projected a visual pattern (Optoma) on the arena floor to provide the fish with additional self-motion cues. Fish were tracked at 150 Hz (camera: Ximea USB3.0 MQ022RG-CM, objective: 25 mm Edmund Optics objective) using Stytra61, from which body position and heading were extracted. Positional data controlled the angles of two scan mirrors (Thorlabs, GVS102) using a DAQ board (National Instruments, 781438-01), maintaining a 980 nm laser beam centered on the fish’s center of mass. Based on the experimental conditions, the laser beam was either centered on the fish (thermal reward condition) or redirected to a distant heat sink outside the behavioral arena. This approach minimized delays and facilitated sharp temperature changes. In front of the camera, we additionally placed a 800 nm shortpass filter (Thorlabs, ESH0800) and a 650 nm long-pass filter. For calibrations, we fixed a thermocouple to the behavioral arena and filled it with water. Then, the laser beam was rapidly moved on the thermocouple or on a 5 mm distant region. Temperature was recorded with a temperature controller (Warner Instruments, Cl 100) connected to the behavioral PC through a LabJack series U3-LV (LabJack). The IR laser was calibrated to produce a 1 °C temperature increase within 1 second, as this interval is close to larval zebrafish’s inter-swim interval. This temperature step is followed by a slow linear increase in temperature. However, this increase is counteracted by the fish moving to a new unheated 20 °C arena spot.

IR laser setup for two-photon imaging

The same IR laser was positioned in front of a 1-axis galvanometric mirror, which switched between two states. In one state, the mirror directed the beam toward the fish, positioned frontally at an angle that avoided the microscope objective and allowed the beam to reach the fish body. In the second state, the mirror redirected the beam to a heat sink distant from the animal. To filter out potential contaminants detected by the microscope’s photomultipliers, we placed a 750 nm long-pass filter (Thorlabs, FELH0750) in front of the IR laser. To avoid temperature accumulation, laser power was reduced and the fish was perfused with 20 °C water. Laser power was calibrated to induce a temperature increase of 0.5 °C during a 10 s stimulation, representing the configuration that most closely matched the temperature changes in freely swimming experiments while avoiding overall temperature accumulation over 40 minutes given our behavioral protocol settings. Temperature calibrations were performed with the full experimental setup, identical to that used during experiments.

We presented optic flow visual stimulation at 40 Hz (Asus P2E microprojector). A red long-pass filter (Kodak Wratten No. 25) allowed the simultaneous 905 nm imaging and visual stimulation. Fish were illuminated using infrared light-emitting diodes (850 nm wavelength) and imaged from below at 200 Hz (Pike F032B, Allied Vision Technologies).

Two-photon microscopy

For all the calcium imaging experiments, we used a laser-scanning galvo-galvo two-photon microscope, as in previous studies27,32,40,46, equipped with a 20x water-immersion objective (Nikon) and a Mai-Tai DeepSea laser (Spectra Physics). The microscope was controlled using custom-made software (brunoise, https://github.com/portugueslab/brunoise). Functional imaging data were acquired at 2-5 Hz, depending on the calcium sensor kinetics being used in the experiment. Laser power and field of view were adjusted based on the particular structure being imaged and transgenic line used.

For anatomical data, we acquired between 10 and 45 frames (1 Hz rate for 10-45 seconds) per plane with a 1-2 microns inter-plane distance. Once a plane was imaged, the objective was moved by 1 micron and the acquisition restarted. Dual channel (red and green) anatomical stacks were acquired at 950-1000 nm while single channel ones (only green) at 905 nm.

Animal preparation and behavioral protocols

All experiments where behavior was recorded were run from 10.00 h to 20.00 h, room temperature was set to 20/21 °C. All visual and thermal stimulus presentations as well as behavioral tracking were performed using the Stytra package61.

Cold avoidance experiment

Here, the IR laser targeting the fish was contingent on the fish being inside an experimentally defined rectangular region (ROI). Such a region was invisible to the fish and once inside, a temperature increase was produced through the IR laser.

Operant thermoregulatory assay

This protocol (inspired by previous research19) was designed to test whether zebrafish can associate a directional choice with a specific temperature consequence. The first experiment presented comprised 60 trials: 30 trials where an increase in temperature was paired with a turn in a designated rule direction (either left or right), and 30 trials in the opposite direction. For delay and dHb ablation experiments we did 10 trials per block, as previous results highlighted a rapid learning process. When inside an experimentally defined region (not close to arena borders) a trial starts by decreasing the temperature (temperature decrease) at the end of a swim of any kind (left, forward or right). Then, the animal can increase the temperature again by turning toward the rule direction. The trial ends when the fish turns in the rule direction or after 10 seconds from temperature decrease (timeout). Then, after a minimum ITI of 5 seconds (where the laser is directed toward the animal) and the fish being away from the border, the next trial starts. As this was a self-paced experiment, its duration varied between fish. All temperature transitions were triggered by the animal’s movement, as we observed a net decrease in swim rate otherwise37. Fish were tested once, between 5 and 8 days post-fertilization (dpf), and otherwise kept in an incubator at 28 °C near the behavioral apparatus. Performance did not significantly differ with age.

Two-photon imaging behavioral protocol

This protocol was designed to assess the combined effects of actions and thermal consequences on different neuronal populations. For imaging of iIPN somata, temperature changes were stochastically triggered at the end of swim events, with a 25% probability that each swim would switch the current laser state. In the rest of the closed-loop imaging experiments, a similar protocol was applied, but once the laser turned on, it remained on for a minimum of 10 seconds. The first swim after this interval turned the laser off, initiating another 10 second long refractory period. After this, each swim had a 30% probability of turning the laser on again. This modification ensured relatively frequent temperature increases while allowing sufficient time between changes to facilitate clear baseline extraction and quantification.

Throughout the entire experiment we also presented whole field forward motion at 3 mm/s to promote swimming events with rotational as well as translational congruent visual feedback. Translational and rotational feedback were computed online as previously done46. The experiment lasted 30-60 minutes and only one plane was acquired per fish. If during the experiment the acquisition drifted, the experiment was restarted for a maximum of three times.

For open-loop temperature and light stimulation, stimuli were delivered for 10 seconds, followed by a pseudo-random inter-trial interval of 10–30 seconds.

Head-fixed preparation

For functional imaging experiments, fish were embedded in 2.5% low-melting point agarose in 30 mm Petri dishes. The agarose around the tail and nose was removed with a scalpel to allow tail movements and direct stimulation of the olfactory bulb14. Once the animal was placed under the objective, we waited 5-10 minutes with the fish engaged in the same visual closed-loop that it would experience later. For anatomical experiments, fish were embedded in 1.5%/2% low-melting point agarose in 30 mm Petri dishes.

Manipulations

Chemogenetic ablation

Targeted dHb ablation was performed using the Nitroreductase (Ntr)-Nifurpirinol (NfP) chemogenetic approach62. Ntr catalyzes the reduction of the pro-drug NfP into a cytotoxic compound, leading to cell death. By expressing Ntr in specific cells, targeted ablations can be achieved. We used the Tg(16715:Gal4):Tg(UAS:Ntr-mcherry) line16, which selectively expresses Ntr-mCherry in the dHb of larval zebrafish. Larvae were first screened for mCherry expression using an upright epifluorescence microscope. mCherry-positive larvae (ablation group) and mCherry-negative larvae (treatment control group) were then placed in separate 90 mm Petri dishes containing minimal water. Subsequently, 30 mL of a 5 µM NfP solution with 0.2% DMSO (diluted 500 times from a 2.5 mM NfP stock) was added to each dish. The Petri dishes were incubated in a dark box at 28 °C for 16 hours to prevent degradation of the photosensitive NfP. Both the preparation of the NfP solution and the transfer of fish were also conducted in the dark to minimize NfP degradation. After the incubation period, larvae were removed from the solution and thoroughly washed three times to eliminate any residual NfP and DMSO. The fish were then left to recover in fish water for one day. To evaluate the efficiency of dHb ablation, random larvae were selected before and after treatment and imaged using a confocal microscope to assess mCherry expression in dHb neurons.

Photoactivation of photoactivatable GFP

At 6–8 days post-fertilization (dpf), larvae were mounted in 1.5% agarose and placed under the two-photon microscope. The photoactivation region was selected by imaging a red structural marker expressed in either habenula axons, GABAergic or glutamatergic neuropil using a 950 nm wavelength. Photoactivation was performed at 800 nm with three repetitions of 30 second continuous scanning, separated by 20 second intervals. Laser power at the objective back aperture was approximately 14 mW. 3-5 hours after photoactivation, a region encompassing the IPN and the dorsally located dPPR was imaged using the same microscope. Labeled neurons were counted manually.

Pharmacology experiments

We used either the GABABR antagonist CGP 46381 (50 µM, CAS 123691-14-5, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or the GABABR agonist (R)-Baclofen (150 µM, CAS 69308-378, Tocris). After a first imaging session (as described elsewhere), head-fixed fish were brought under a stereomicroscope close to the experimental rig. Then, we carefully washed three times with either water (ctrl group) or agonist/antagonist (treatment group) using a Pasteur pipette. Fish were subsequently moved again to the two-photon microscope rig, left to habituate to the visual closed-loop and allow drug diffusion for 10 minutes, before the second imaging session was started. In this experiment, bath application was necessary, as intraventricular injections completely abolish fish swimming behavior, particularly immediately after injection.

Anatomical registration

Registrations were done using a combination of manual and automatic procedures using the free Computational Morphometry Toolkit (CMTK http://www.nitrc.org/projects/cmtk) (volumetric data) or the Python package dipy. Each single-plane dataset with the exception of r-dHb somata imaging has been morphed with an internal reference, to allow for average ΔF/F projections and quantifications. All volumetric data (EM, photoactivations, anatomies and multiplane dPPR population imaging) have been morphed to the Max Planck Zebrafish Brain Atlas mapzebrain (https://mapzebrain.org)63 for visualizations and quantifications.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using Python 3.8 and relevant Python libraries for scientific computing, like numpy, scipy and scikit-learn.

Preprocessing of behavioral data

All the behavioral preprocessing was performed using the Python package Bouter (https://github.com/portugueslab/bouter) and as previously described14,40,46. For head-fixed experiments, reorientation angle was computed as the cumulative tail bending over the first 70 milliseconds after swim onset, corresponding approximately to the first tail oscillation.

For freely-swimming experiments, we computed the cumulative reorientation from swim start to end. To categorize left, forward and right swims in head-fixed experiments we set a threshold of 10 degrees while during the freely-swimming thermoregulatory assay a swim had to exceed ±30 degrees to be considered a turn. By convention, negative values indicate left turns and positive values indicate right turns.

ROI occupancy

ROI occupancy was computed as the fraction of time spent inside the ROI over the entire experiment duration, multiplied by 100.

Outside ROI exposure time

For each instance, we computed the time spent from ROI exit to re-entry and then averaged this value per fish.

Reorientation bias

We computed the average reorientation angle of the first swim detected after temperature decrease. We performed shuffle analysis on the same reorientation bias values. In the original data, trials were ordered such that the first half corresponded to one rule block and the second half to the other, and mean biases for each half were computed accordingly. For shuffles, the order of bias values was randomly permuted. Then, we computed the average for the first and second halves of the shuffled sequence. We repeated this procedure 100 times and averaged the results.

Reorientation magnitude

We computed the average absolute reorientation angle of the first swim detected after temperature decrease for trials where the preceding ITI was lower than 7.5 seconds. For shuffle controls, we randomly selected swims throughout the experiment.

Cold exposure time

Cold exposure time was the average time spent from temperature decrease to thermal reward for trials where the preceding ITI was lower than 7.5 seconds. For delay experiments, this value was corrected by the applied delay to represent cold exposure time under a zero-delay condition.

Preprocessing of imaging data

All functional data were drift-corrected using the Python package fimpy (https://github.com/portugueslab/fimpy) or suite2p64. ROIs were manually extracted using napari (https://napari.org) based on clear anatomical landmarks, except for habenula somata (extracted using suite2p). Each ROI was resampled at 5 Hz with linearly spaced timepoints. Baseline fluorescence (F0) was defined as the median over the entire experiment. ΔF/F was computed as (F-F0)/F0, where F is the instantaneous fluorescence from the raw ROI time series. These time series were then smoothed with a third-order Savitzky–Golay filter over 11 frames.

Fraction of temperature-responding r-dHb neurons

Only positive modulations (increases from baseline fluorescence) were considered. To determine this fraction, we first calculated the evoked activity using a 10-second window centered on stimulus onset or offset (each condition analyzed separately). Baseline activity was subtracted, and the average response was calculated. This procedure was repeated 1000 times, each time circularly shuffling the original trace to create a null distribution of evoked activity. A p-value was computed per neuron, and the fraction of responsive neurons was defined as the proportion with p-values below 0.05.

r-dHb thermal reward response dynamics

r-dHb thermal reward response dynamics were classified by first computing the response triggered average. We then extracted the ΔF/F from 0-2 seconds and 8–10 seconds. These values were used to calculate a polar angle describing the relative ΔF/F over the two time windows. For example, a fast-adapting neuron with a greater increase in the first window compared to the second would have a polar angle close to zero, whereas a slow-adapting neuron would have an angle close to 90 degrees. Arbitrary thresholds were then set to categorize response profiles.

TL thermal reward and luminance responses

We computed the response triggered averages on z-scored traces for temperature and light trials. The 2 seconds pre-stimulus period was used as the baseline. We then extracted the peak responses within the 10 seconds stimulation window. These values were then averaged across trials.

Motor modulation index

For iIPN and r-dHb somata imaging, we computed this index only on the subset of neurons significantly modulated by thermal reward while for the rest of the experiments we directly computed it on the two traces extracted from the glomerular region (or corresponding dHb axon patches). We then computed the triggered average for every action-outcome combination and calculated the maximum ΔF/F increase over a 10 seconds window. The motor modulation index (MMI) was then computed as the negative log-ratio between: (1) the difference between left and right swims in the absence of thermal feedback and (2) the difference between left and right swims when they led to thermal reward.

Anatomy-based action-outcome responses

For every neuron we computed the triggered average for every action-outcome combination and calculated the average ΔF/F increase over the 10 seconds time window. Then, we pooled left (divided by outcome responses) responses for neurons located on the right brain hemisphere with right responses for the ones located on the left hemisphere and did the same for the opposite combination. Then we computed the median across neurons within the same animal.

Lag analysis

During open-loop stimulation, each thermal reward instance was assigned a sensorimotor lag value. We searched for the closest swim event within a 2-second window before (-2 seconds) and after (+ 2 seconds) thermal reward. The lag was defined as the difference between the timing of the first swim onset and thermal reward. Trials where the lag was positive but the fish swam at least 5 seconds before thermal reward were excluded. Finally, we did the same pooling criterion used for the "Anatomy-based action-outcome responses”. Lag values were finally binned with 0.5-seconds spacing.

Analysis ∆F/F standard deviation

Starting from the ΔF/F aligned stacks (time,y,x), we computed, for every pixel, the standard deviation over time. In this way, we obtained a 2D map (y,x). We then calculated the rigid transformation matrix for all the acquisitions using aligned stack sum projections as a common reference (a selected recording). Finally, we transformed all standard deviation maps and averaged across fish. Finally, we obtained from each map the average standard deviation value by averaging in space and calculated the ratio for the first and second imaging session across conditions.

Pixel-wise PCA

The imaging experiment data can be seen as a three-dimensional matrix (t,y,x). We flattened this matrix to obtain a two-dimensional matrix (t,y*x) and performed PCA along the y*x dimension. Then, from the PCA loadings we reconstructed the original anatomical image to generate a loading map for the first 6 PCs.

Functional screening of dPPR neurons

We first z-scored neuronal activity and computed the evoked responses for different action-outcome pairs. Responses were then averaged across outcome types to obtain a single left and right response value per neuron.

For each neuron, we derived two indices. First, we calculated a polar angle in the range [0, π], defined by the relative responses to left turns along the x-axis and right turns along the y-axis. This angle captured the directional tuning of each neuron. Neurons excited by left turns and inhibited by right turns yielded polar angles close to 3π/8, whereas neurons with the opposite tuning yielded angles close to 7π/8. Second, we computed the maximum response magnitude across the two conditions, which quantified the strength of modulation for the preferred turn direction.

Neurons were classified as left-excited right-inhibited if their polar angle fell within 3π/8±π/8 and their maximum response was above 0.4. Conversely, neurons were classified as right-excited left-inhibited if their polar angle fell within 7π/8±π/8 and their maximum response was above 0.4.

To assess spatial consistency across animals, brains were registered to the mapzebrain atlas. For each fish and within each response class, we computed the Euclidean distance between each neuron and neurons in other fish brains. A neuron was retained if a matching neuron within a 20 µm radius was identified in at least 50% of animals. Finally, neurons were included only if a corresponding neuron was also present in the contralateral hemisphere within a 10 µm radius.

Electron microscopy reconstructions

EM reconstructions were performed on an open access Serial Blockface EM dataset from a 5 dpf larval Tg(elavl3:GCaMP5G)a4598 sampled at a resolution of 14 x 14 x 25 nm28. The IPN region was identified based on prior work27,46. Reconstructions were generated by merging neurite segments identified by an automated flood-filling algorithm28. The first reconstructed neurons were from the iIPN neurons, traced by annotators at ariadne.ai AG, starting from seed points selected by the authors. Each completed neuron underwent quality review by an expert annotator. Neuron skeletons were extracted from the aggregated meshes using custom python scripts and all datapoints were transformed into the mapZbrain atlas space using a custom transformation consisting of an affine followed by a rigid transformation step. Presynaptic partners were identified by first detecting synapses with a detection algorithm, followed by manual curation of contact points and tracing of partner neurons. Partner reconstruction and quality control were performed by both ariadne.ai AG and the authors. Unlike iIPN, reconstructing partner neurons (dPPRcontra and dHb) was more challenging due to neurites extending into blurred areas of the dataset, making this reconstruction effort incomplete. As a result, many dHb axons are incomplete, due to a discontinuity in the dataset in the posterior-ventral-left IPN. Left- and right-dHb afferents were distinguished by the side of the fasciculus retroflexus along which each reconstructed axon traveled. Classification of reconstructions was done manually based on dendritic and axonal positioning. Cellular compartments (glomeruli and axons) were identified by morphological characteristics such as process thickness. iIPN glomeruli were classified using the DBSCAN clustering algorithm from the sklearn library in Python. We classified a dHb axon as “fully reconstructed” if it exhibited the characteristic morphology described elsewhere41. Finally, we identified dPPRcontra neurons by tracing back from synapses on iIPN dendritic trees, whereas we identified the dPPRipsi neuron independently during a reconstruction of iIPN postsynaptic partners (data not shown) and only later determined that it feeds back into the same glomerulus. These observations raise the possibility of recurrent connections within the dHb–IPN pathway.
