Instability of the Sherman-Morrison formula and stabilization by iterative refinement
Behnam Hashemi, Yuji Nakatsukasa

Abstract
Owing to its simplicity and efficiency, the Sherman-Morrison (SM) formula has seen widespread use across various scientific and engineering applications for solving rank-one perturbed linear systems of the form ( A + u ​ v T ) ​ x = b (A+uv^{T})x=b . Although the formula dates back at least to 1944, its numerical stability properties have remained an open question and continue to be a topic of current research. We analyze the backward stability of the SM, demonstrate its instability in a scenario increasingly common in scientific computing and address an open question posed by Nick Higham on the proportionality of the backward error bound to the condition number of A A . We then incorporate fixed-precision iterative refinement into the SM framework reusing the previously computed decompositions and prove that, under reasonable assumptions, it achieves backward stability without sacrificing the efficiency of the SM formula. While our theory does not prove the SM formula with iterative refinement always outputs a backward stable solution, empirically it is observed to eventually produce a backward stable solution in all our numerical experiments. We conjecture that with iterative refinement, the SM formula yields a backward stable solution provided that κ 2 ​ ( A ) , κ 2 ​ ( A + u ​ v T ) \kappa_{2}(A),\kappa_{2}(A+uv^{T}) are both bounded safely away from ϵ M − 1 \epsilon_{M}^{-1} , where ϵ M \epsilon_{M} is the unit roundoff.

All these point towards a cautionary note: the SM(W) formula, while very attractive for computational efficiency, can be dangerously unstable.
Fortunately, we suggest a simple remedy to the instability: iterative refinement (IR).
IR is a classical idea in NLA but has witnessed a resurged interest, partly due to the growing interest and availability of mixed precision arithmetic

More generally, IR is often able to improve the accuracy of an unstable solution, even in fixed precision

We analyze the stability of SM-IR with one IR step, and show that under some mild conditions that can be verified on the fly, the algorithm outputs a backward stable solution

That is, while the SM formula is fundamentally unstable, the instability can be fixed with IR under mild assumptions.

However, the assumptions are not always satisfied, and we are currently unable to definitively prove that SM-IR will eventually (after a modest number of IR steps) produce a backward stable solution for any linear system. Despite this, our numerical experiments suggest strongly that this is the case.
We hence conjecture that SM-IR is backward stable under very mild conditions, namely that both

have condition number bounded by
\epsilon_{M}^{-1}

Another open problem is to deal with a higher-rank case

(iii) Indirectly related: the prominence of stochastic gradient descent comes (at least partially) from its ability to find a small-norm approximate solution to an (often highly underdetermined) optimization problem

Recall that

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\alpha}_{r}|}{|\hat{\beta}|}\max_{i}\frac{|A^{-1}u|_{i}}{|\hat{w}|_{i}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\|A^{-1}\hat{r}\|}{\|A^{-1}u\|}\|A^{-1}u\|=\|A^{-1}\hat{r}\|\leq\|A^{-1}\|\|\hat{r}\|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\kappa(A))\ \mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{M}\kappa(A))=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{M}\kappa^{2}(A)).

Roughly speaking, this implies that in the small-norm solution scenario, for SM to achieve backward stability with one step of fixed-precision IR, we would require
\kappa(A)<\epsilon_{M}^{-1/2}
, which corresponds to
6.7\times 10^{7}
in double precision. In such cases the original SM residual
r
for the SM solution
\hat{x}
to
Bx=b
(i.e., before any IR steps) has a norm bounded by
\epsilon_{M}\kappa(A)\|b\|\approx 10^{-8}\|b\|
. With the first IR step, as discussed above, the deciding quantity is essentially
\|A^{-1}\hat{r}\|
which corresponds to the solution norm of a linear system
Ax=\hat{r}
. This can be bounded by
\epsilon_{M}\kappa(A)\|\hat{r}\|\lessapprox\epsilon_{M}^{2}\kappa^{2}(A)\|b\|\lessapprox\epsilon_{M}\|b\|
thereby achieving backward stability.

1.

2.

3.

The complexity of the first algorithm is cubic

in the matrix dimension,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

While GEPP/LU is known to have adversarial examples where

[
23

, and empirically LU performs exceptionally well (QR, by contrast, is always backward stable). For this reason we do not present SM-QR-IR, as (expectedly) its empirical performance is almost identical to SM-LU-IR.

In our experiments, the normwise relative backward error of an approximate solution

5\epsilon_{M}\approx 5\times 10^{-16}

Summary of experiments

In all our experiments, SM(-LU)-IR was able to compute a backward stable solution after
\mathcal{O}(1)
IR steps, without sacrificing speed. Based on these promising empirical results, we conjecture that SM-IR is backward stable when applied to numerically nonsingular linear systems. As noted in the introduction, our theory requires the assumptions to hold, so is not sufficient to prove this in full. A full proof is left as an open problem.

Appendix A
Govaerts’ work

The starting point is to introduce the scalar variable
\zeta=v^{T}x
, which serves as a convenient intermediate step in the computation. We can now regard (
1
) as equivalent to the bordered linear system

\begin{bmatrix}A&u\\
v^{T}&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
\zeta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}b\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(29)

Govaerts
[
8
]
proposed algorithms for solving general
(n+1)\times(n+1)
bordered linear systems, where the
(1,1)
block is a matrix
A
for which a black-box solver is assumed to be available. He discussed variants of block Gaussian elimination, both without and with IR. For instance, the BEC (block elimination Crout) algorithm of
[
8
]
, when applied to (
29
), employs the following LU decomposition

\begin{bmatrix}A&u\\
v^{T}&-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
v^{T}&-1-v^{T}A^{-1}u\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&A^{-1}u\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Backward and forward substitutions then give the SM formula. The following mixed forward-backward error bound is how Govaerts’s analysis of BEC
[
8
, Prop. 3.3]
adapts to (
29
).

Proposition 10
.

Let
S
be a normwise backward stable solver for
A
with stability constant
C_{S}
6
6

6

i.e., when
S
is applied for solving
Ay=b
in floating-point arithmetic, then there exist a modest constant
C_{S}
, a matrix
\Delta A
and a vector
\delta b
such that
(A+\Delta A)\hat{y}=b+\delta b
with
\|\Delta A\|_{2}\leq C_{S}\epsilon_{M}\|A\|_{2}
and
\|\delta b\|_{2}\leq C_{S}\epsilon_{M}\|b\|_{2}
.
Then,
\hat{x},\hat{\zeta}
satisfy

\begin{bmatrix}A+\Delta A&u+\delta u\\
(v+\delta v)^{T}&-1+\delta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}\\
\hat{\zeta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}b+\delta b\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}T\\
q^{T}\end{bmatrix}\hat{y}

(30)

where

\displaystyle\|\Delta A\|\leq(2+C_{S})\epsilon_{M}\|A\|+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{M}^{2}),

\displaystyle\|\delta u\|\leq C_{S}\epsilon_{M}\|u\|,

\displaystyle\|\delta v\|\leq(5+C_{IP})\epsilon_{M}\|v\|+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{M}^{2}),

\displaystyle|\delta|\leq 3\epsilon_{M}\exp(3\epsilon_{M}),

\displaystyle\|\delta b\|\leq C_{S}\epsilon_{M}\|b\|,

\displaystyle\|T\|\leq(2C_{S}+1)\ \epsilon_{M}\|A\|+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{M}^{2}),

\displaystyle\|q\|\leq(4+2C_{IP})\epsilon_{M}\|v\|+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{M}^{2}),

in which
C_{IP}\leq\frac{n}{1-n\epsilon_{M}}
is the constant appearing in the backward error for the inner product of two vectors.

Govaerts
[
8
]
also discusses two other algorithms (BED and BEM) which, when applied to (
29
) are not equivalent to the SM formula as it is commonly implemented, for example in Alg
1
or in
[
14
, p. 487]
. Directions for future work include investigating the potential implications of these alternative algorithms for the SM(W) framework.

Appendix B
Related backward error bounds

Although this paper is
not
concerned with the stability of the SMW formula for matrix inversion, in this subsection, we discuss how the backward error bounds derived by Ma, Boutsikas, Ghadiri and Drineas
[
16
]
for a computed inverse of
B
using SMW relate to Higham’s question. Although it is common knowledge that multiplying by an explicit inverse is not an advisable way of solving a linear system in practice, one might argue that, at-least in theory, SMW could first be applied to invert

, followed by a multiplication with
b
. Could the existing error bounds for matrix inversion via SMW then lead to alternative solutions to Higham’s question? Here, we present an attempt in this direction that yields a negative answer. We first outline backward error bounds derived in
[
16
]
for the low-rank perturbed matrix inversion and then examine their implications when combined with ideal forward error bounds.

Proposition 11
.

[
16
, Thm. 6]

Let
\lambda:=\|U\|_{2}\|V\|_{2}

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}

\displaystyle:=\|\tilde{A}^{-1}-A^{-1}\|_{2},

(31)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}

\displaystyle:=\|Z^{-1}-(I+V^{T}\tilde{A}^{-1}U)^{-1}\|_{2}

(32)

where
Z
is a computed approximate inverse of

. If

\displaystyle\|I+V^{T}A^{-1}U\|_{2}\leq\beta,

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}\ \text{and}\ (Z^{-1})^{-1}-V^{T}\widetilde{A}^{-1}U\ \text{are invertible},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}<\frac{1}{2\|A\|_{2}},

(33)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}<\frac{1}{2\left(\beta+\lambda\,\epsilon_{1}\right)},

\displaystyle 2\left(\beta+\lambda\,\epsilon_{1}\right)^{2}\epsilon_{2}<\frac{1}{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\|B-\left(\widetilde{A}^{-1}-\widetilde{A}^{-1}UZ^{-1}V^{T}\widetilde{A}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\|_{2}

(34)
