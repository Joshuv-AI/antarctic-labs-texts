Abstract
. Many social media platforms allow content creators to pin user comments in response to their content. Once pinned, a comment remains fixed at the top of the comments section, regardless of subsequent activity or the selected sorting order. The “Pin of Shame” refers to an innovative re-purposing of this feature, where creators intentionally pin norm-violating comments to spotlight them and prompt shaming responses from their audiences. This study explores how creators adopt this emerging moderation tactic, examining their motivations, its outcomes, and how it compares—procedurally and in effect—to other content moderation strategies. Through interviews with 20 content creators who had pinned negative comments on their posts, we find that the Pin of Shame is used to punish and educate inappropriate commenters, elicit emotional accountability, provoke audience negotiation of community norms, and support creators’ impression management goals. Our findings shed light on the benefits, precarities, and risks of using public shaming as a tool for norm enforcement. We contribute to HCI research by informing the design of user-centered tools for addressing content-based harm.



1.
Introduction

Jayan is a content creator who regularly shares fitness content on Instagram, where he has over 29,000 followers.
On March 24, 2025, he experienced an unexpected viral moment when one of his videos reached 140,000 views and attracted more than 12,000 comments.
While skimming through the comments section, he came across a particularly offensive comment: “I wake up every day happy knowing I’m not Indian

,’’.

1

We received permission from Jayan to describe this incident in the current paper.

Although Jayan had witnessed similar personal attack comments on his previous posts, this instance felt especially insulting—it attacked not just him, but also his heritage.
In response, he pinned the comment to the top of the comments section—an action now common enough that many Instagrammers recognize it and colloquially refer to it as the
‘Pin of shame’
.
This act prompted his followers to support him via posting replies that shamed the pinned commenter, resulting in over 100 follow-up responses directed at the negative commenter.
This support validated Jayan’s outrage at the offensive comment and helped ease his distress.
While the shamed commenter did not respond to this backlash, they silently deleted their comment after some time, which removed the entire comment thread (i.e., the offensive comment and all replies to it) from the comments section.

Offensive comments such as the one Jayan received are a common occurrence for creators (i.e., actively posting social media users with a large number of followers) on social media platforms
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
;
Salehi et al. 2023
;
Lyu et al. 2024
)
.
For example,
Thomas et al. 2022
surveyed 135 creators and found that 95% of them had experienced an incident of hate and harassment.
Prior HCI research has characterized the viewing of such comments as
content-based harm
and noted how it can lead to anxiety and mental health risks
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
)
.
Researchers have also examined how creators respond to such incidents and evaluated the utility of
creator-led content moderation tools
that platforms offer to counteract offenders, such as blocking
(
Geiger 2016
;
John 2024
)
, reporting
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, and word filters
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
)
.
Findings from this research show that these tools present several challenges for the creators, including the cognitive labor required to use them, lack of control and transparency about their operations, and novel privacy concerns that emerge through their deployment
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Jhaver et al. 2023b
)
.

Against this backdrop, we examine in this paper how, why, and to what effect content creators like Jayan deploy the ‘Pin of Shame’—a strategic pinning of inappropriate comments to invite shaming comments against them—to address content-based harm. This emergent regulation approach is notable in that platforms do not explicitly present comment pinning as a content moderation mechanism—creators are free to pin (i.e., force fix) any comment they choose to the top of their post’s comments section so as to afford it greater public visibility. Yet creators on multiple platforms are increasingly deploying it to induce social sanctions against a range of norm violations, including identity-based attacks (as in the case of Jayan) and personal insults (see Figure
1
).
In some cases, creators pin the inappropriate comment and leave no further reply (e.g., in Figure
1
, left), while in other cases, they explicitly signal its inappropriateness (and hence, invite public shaming) by remarking “Pin of Shame” (Figure
1
, middle), or clapping back with witty or sarcastic replies (Figure
1
, right).

This prevalent use of the Pin of Shame suggests that platform-offered moderation tools may not be addressing some of the key creator goals associated with handling norm violations. Therefore, we take a creator-centered approach to understanding the deployments of the Pin of Shame—doing so allows us to scrutinize creators’ situated experiences of online harms
(
Scheuerman et al. 2021
;
Scheuerman et al. 2018
)
, how they perceive the tradeoffs between different coping mechanisms, and how they rely on their community for both emotional and social needs.
We also evaluate how the effects of this communicative form of social sanctioning speak to recent calls for more restorative approaches to moderation—emphasizing acknowledgment, responsibility, emotional engagement, and, when possible, apology from rule violators
(
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.

Although platform-defined community guidelines often shape users’ understanding of moral transgressions
(
Kou and Gui 2021
)
, the wide variety of norm violations represented in the Pin of Shame examples reflect how content creators may be perceiving and interpreting content inappropriateness within their own ethical frameworks.
Further, by re-purposing the pinning feature—typically used to spotlight informative comments
(
Hao and Li 2024
)
—to instead highlight negative ones, creators are
inverting the platform’s sanctioning logic of reducing visibility as punishment

(
Gillespie 2018
;
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
)
.
This practice signals creators’ own vision of what comment moderation and sanctioning practices should look like. To more deeply understand what drives content creators to adopt the Pin of Shame as a coping mechanism, especially given the availabililty of alternative platform-offered moderation solutions (e.g., comment removal, reporting, blocking), we ask:

RQ 1: What motivates content creators to re-purpose the comment pinning as a comment moderation strategy?

By securing the visibility of inappropriate comments, the Pin of Shame draws users into a collective act of shaming the corresponding commenters.
This form of collective social sanctioning is novel in that content creators intentionally leverage their audience’s emotional involvement as a key procedural driver of their moderation practice.
Collective action based on moral outrages is often portrayed negatively, due to its potential to inflict mental and social harm to the shamed individuals
(
Muir et al. 2021
;
Basak et al. 2016
;
Basak et al. 2019
;
Ronson 2015
)
.
Our study seeks to complicate this framing by examining the perspectives and experiences of content creators who instigate online public shaming, especially attending to the benefits, uncertainties, and risks they perceive of this practice.
We also explore whether and how deploying the Pin of Shame offsets the drawbacks of visibility-limiting moderation practices, such as undermining free speech
(
Myers West 2018
)
and opaque decision-making processes
(
Jhaver et al. 2019a
;
Suzor et al. 2019
)
.
We ask:

RQ 2: What are the consequences of deploying comment pinning as a moderation measure?

To address these research questions and gain a deeper understanding of the Pin of Shame phenomenon from the perspective of content creators, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 20 social media creators who had used comment pinning as a strategy to address inappropriate comments.
Conducting a thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
of our interview data, we found that content creators adopt the Pin of Shame to draw public attention as a means to shame the offenders as well as shape community norms and educate viewers about those norms.
Some creators also seek to leverage the Pin of Shame to increase engagement with their posted content, operating on their own (potentially incorrect) folk theories about how algorithmic recommendations operate.
Our findings show that using the Pin of Shame allows creators to make sense of the harm, validate their emotional responses, receive social support, hold norm violators accountable, and feel empowered.
Moreover, its use enables creators to avoid direct censorship and uphold free speech while reinforcing their positive public impression and strengthening their control over the unfolding discourse.
These results support prior scholarship that has explored the constructive potential of online shaming as a tool for norm enforcement
(
Braithwaite 1989
;
Corry 2021
;
Direk 2020
;
Hou et al. 2017
;
Kasabova 2021
;
Scheff 2014
;
Trottier 2018
)
.
However, our findings also reveal how the uncalibrated nature of public shaming can cause emotional strain for the instigators (in this case, content creators) themselves.

Prior HCI and CSCW research has examined the content suppression and moderation that content creators themselves experience on social media platforms
(
Ma and Kou 2023
;
Ma and Kou 2021
;

;

;

)
. We build on this work by contributing to the strand of research that evaluates how creators manage the user-generated content within their regulation purview, specifically the comments their posts receive online
(
Jhaver et al. 2023a

Jhaver et al. 2022
)
.
Our work adds to a growing body of content moderation research that emphasizes the design of tools, techniques, and strategies aimed at empowering end users to combat content-based harms

2.
Related Work

This study draws from two related areas of research: content moderation practices and online public shaming action in response to perceived moral transgressions.
Together, these research areas inform our analysis and understanding of how content creators creatively leverage the comment pinning feature to manage and respond to harmful comments.

2.1.
Platform Affordances for Creator-led Content Moderation

Content creators on digital platforms routinely encounter harmful comments from other users, either as responses to their posted content or elsewhere on their news feeds
(
Harris et al. 2023
;
Jhaver et al. 2022
)
. Such comments include instances of online harassment
(
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
)
, hate speech, graphic content, and misinformation
(
Shea et al. 2024
)
.
In response, creators employ a range of coping mechanisms
(
Thomas et al. 2022
)
, including punitive measures (e.g., flagging, blocking, deleting
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
John 2024
;
Jhaver et al. 2018
)
), visibility-reduction tools (e.g., toxicity sliders and keyword filters
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
;
Jhaver et al. 2023b
)
), and discursive interventions like counter-speech
(
Blaya 2019
;
Blackwell et al. 2018
;
Shea et al. 2024
)
.

These mechanisms vary in their accessibility, granularity of control, scope of impact, effectuation delays, and certainty of outcomes
(
Jhaver et al. 2023b
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.
For instance, flagging a comment aims to remove it platform-wide for all users, but its impact is delayed and contingent on platforms’ flag review decisions
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
)
.
In contrast, muting or keyword-based filtering hides content only for the configuring creator, and its effects are certain and immediate
(
Jhaver et al. 2023b
;
Jhaver et al. 2022
)
.
On the other hand,
disliking
a comment does not remove or hide it, but publicly indicates disapproval—the impact is immediately visible and the dislikers usually remain unrevealed
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
.
Given these differences, content creators often tailor their choice of response to inappropriate behaviors to achieve their specific goals
(
Chen and Berger 2013
;
Gallagher et al. 2018
;
Masullo Chen et al. 2018
)
.

Within this ecosystem, the ‘Pin of Shame’ has emerged as a novel form of creator-led moderation to address online harm.
This approach is accessible only to the creators to apply to comments to their own posted content.
As noted in the previous section, it involves content creators pinning problematic comments to the top of their post’s comments section, thereby increasing the comment’s visibility and inviting public scrutiny and criticism.

Although the comment pinning feature was originally designed by platforms to highlight valued comments—typically allowing up to three pins—creators have creatively repurposed it for various goals, such as monetizing content or disclosing sponsorships
(
Hao and Li 2024
)
.
Most notably for the purposes of this study, some now use it as a mechanism of social sanction, leveraging the visibility control it provides to publicly call out norm violations.
Our study focuses on this specific use of comment pinning. We position the Pin of Shame as a form of user-driven moderation approach
(
Jhaver et al. 2023b
)
that retains the inappropriate comment while directing its strategic visibility into a mechanism of punishment and norm education.

Examining creators’ use of the Pin of Shame offers a lens into how they perceive, compare, select, and deploy different approaches to content moderation in response to specific infractions and to achieve distinct punitive and educational goals
(
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Myers West 2018
)
.
We particularly attend to how rather than simply relying on platform-imposed sanctions, creators actively use public shaming against responses to their posted content to express their own values around accountability and justice.
Prior research on the use of personal moderation tools like blocking, word filters, and toxicity sliders shows that they enable a similar assertion of user-defined norms of appropriateness
(
Jhaver et al. 2023b
;
Jhaver et al. 2018
;
Jhaver et al. 2022
)
—this contrasts with tools like flagging that are constrained by platforms’ narrow vocabulary of inappropriate conduct
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.
We examine how using the Pin of Shame allows creators to precipitate a process of collective norm negotiation, co-construction, definition, and enforcement among their audiences.
We also analyze how the Pin of Shame operates as a discursive tool that invites public reflection and user interaction, aligning with emerging CSCW research that explores restorative justice frameworks and community-based responses to online harm
(
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
;
Blackwell et al. 2018
;
Asad 2019
)
.

Content creators not only write, produce, and edit what they post, but also engage in building and maintaining relationships with their viewers
(
Hamilton et al. 2014
;
Hilvert-Bruce et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2019
;
Wohn and Freeman 2020
)
.
These engagements are driven not just by creators’ communal aspirations but also their self-promotion goals and platforms’ commercial logic
(
Boxman-Shabtai 2019
)
.
Many creators adopt “micro-celebrity” practices, treating their audience as fans, framing content creation as a performance
(
Li et al. 2019
;
Pellicone and Ahn 2017
)
, and leveraging strategic intimacy to connect with viewers through direct engagement
(
Berryman and Kavka 2017
;
Marwick 2015
;
Raun 2018
)
. The technical features of social media platforms—such as options to view, comment on, and share—further facilitate the formation of dynamic, networked communities
(
Cunningham and Craig 2017
;
Lange 2007
)
, where creators and audiences actively engage with one another
(
Burgess and Green 2018
)
.
We examine in this research how creators’ audience engagement and impression management goals align with and influence their comment moderation efforts through their use of comment pinning alongside other conversation
affordances, such as the ability to reply and remove comments.
Many HCI scholars have especially attended to how creators seek to understand the way that recommendation algorithms work on these platforms so as to enhance their visibility and subsequently profit
(
Cotter 2019
;
Ma and Kou 2021
;
Karizat et al. 2021
;
DeVito 2022
)
—we inquire how creators’ mental models of recommendation algorithms shape their use of comment pinning for norm enforcement.

Previous studies have raised significant concerns about the physical and emotional toll that enacting content moderation takes on various groups, including regular social media users
(
Jhaver et al. 2023b
)
, commercial moderators
(
Roberts 2019
;
Steiger et al. 2021
)
, volunteer moderators
(
Li et al. 2022a
;
Jhaver et al. 2019b
;
Wohn 2019
;
Dosono and Semaan 2019
;
Ruckenstein and Turunen 2020
)
, and—most relevant to our study—content creators
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
)
. Researchers have also highlighted the tensions surrounding how platforms profit from users’ unpaid moderation efforts
(
Wohn 2019
;
Li et al. 2022b
)
. We add to this literature by examining how the use of Pin of Shame is a reaction to these cognitive and emotional labor demands as well as how creators leverage their community’s support to ameliorate these demands.

In designing moderation systems, it is crucial to account for end-users’ preferences for control and transparency
(
Ozanne et al. 2022
;
Juneja et al. 2020
;
Suzor et al. 2019
)
. Currently, most social media platforms provide limited insight into the rules and decision-making processes that underlie platform-enacted moderation practices
(
Suzor et al. 2019
;
Jhaver et al. 2023a
)
. Even user-controlled moderation tools that rely on platforms’ adjudication (e.g., flagging
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
)
) or curation algorithms (e.g., toxicity sliders
(
Jhaver et al. 2023b
)
) often present a lack of transparency in their procedures, interface elements, and outcomes. Researchers have also explored how different moderation tool configurations influence users’ perceived control over their social media feeds
(
Jhaver et al. 2022
;
Jhaver et al. 2023b
)
. We attend to how content creators’ need for control influence the specific ways in which they engage in comment pinning for moderation, e.g., pinning and unpinning different comments and following up pinning with comment removal.

Prior studies also indicate that when moderation systems enact transparency in their operation, e.g., when they explain the rationale behind content regulation decisions, sanctioned users respond more positively, leading to improved post quality
(
Jhaver et al. 2019c
;
Kim 2009
)
and greater trust in the platform
(
Jhaver et al. 2019a
;
Brunk et al. 2019
)
. More importantly, such explanations also positively shape other observers’ behaviors
(
Jhaver et al. 2024
)
. We examine how the transparency afforded by pinning the shamed comment (as opposed to silently removing it) influences observers’ reactions to this social sanction.
In doing so, we add to ongoing HCI conversations around incorporating procedural fairness and transparency when building solutions to address online harm
(
Vaccaro et al. 2020
;
Shim and Jhaver 2024
;
Jhaver et al. 2019a
)
.

2.2.
Online Shaming as a Norm Enforcement Mechanism

Shame
is a self-conscious social emotion
(
Jochan and Banerjee 2021
;
Scheff 2014
;
Tangney 1995
)
often associated with embarrassment
(
Shea et al. 2024
)
and the experience of feeling small, worthless, or powerless
(
Tangney 1995
)
.
On networked social media platforms, many norm-violating acts are publicly visible, increasing the likelihood that they spontaneously elicit a
shaming
response
(
De Vries 2015
;
Jochan and Banerjee 2021
;
Tangney 1995
)
.
In other cases, online shaming is deliberately initiated by uploading videos, photos, or screenshots of norm-violating behaviors to attract public criticism
(
Corry 2021
;
Hou et al. 2017
)
, publicly listing users who have breached community norms
(
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
)
, or calling for specific violators to be held accountable.
Many affordances of popular social media platforms, such as content persistence, searchability, and shareability facilitate online public shaming
(
Kim et al. 2022
;
Jhaver et al. 2018
)
.

Such online shaming manifests as witnesses collectively condemning the norm violator for their actions and calling for accountability and behavioral change
(
De Vries 2015
;
Scheff 2014
;
Muir et al. 2021
)
in response to their perceived moral outrage
(
Basak et al. 2019
;
Nussbaum 2019
)
.
These responses are often driven by in-group dynamics, as individuals may interpret norm violations—particularly by out-group members—as symbolic threats to their collective identity
(
Stets and Burke 2000
)
.
Such shaming actions help define the boundaries of acceptable conduct
(
Hou et al. 2017
)
, educate the shamed users about the consequences of norm violations, and reinforce the internalization of community norms among observing individuals
(
Trottier 2018
;
Corry 2021
)
.
Thus, shaming holds significant potential as a form of grassroots content moderation
(
Kasabova 2021
)
, where the community plays an active role in both monitoring and upholding social norms.
Further, in repressive or authoritarian contexts, online shaming may serve as a tool for challenging existing power structures
(
Direk 2020
)
.

In some instances, online public shaming crosses moral boundaries
(
Billingham and Parr 2020
)
, inflicts emotional harm on the targeted individual, and devolves into harassment
(
Jhaver et al. 2017
)
, which perpetuates cycles of punitive outrage
(
Basak et al. 2016
;
Marwick 2021
;
Lewis et al. 2021
)
.
Given that such acts are often carried out outside the original context in which the infraction occurred
(
Ronson 2015
)
, targets of shaming are frequently absent from the conversation and unable to respond or defend themselves
(
Lewis et al. 2021
;
Marwick 2021
;
Ronson 2015
)
.
Our focus on examining the Pin of Shame captures events where both the inappropriate statement and the ensuing public reaction coexist in the same discursive space—this allows us to attend to context-rich interactions where both the broader audience and the norm violator can participate in the unfolding discourse.
Another unique affordance of the Pin of Shame (as compared to general acts of online public shaming) is that both the content creator and the violator may choose to delete the pinned comment (and its replies) and disrupt the trajectory of collective criticism at any time (as in the case of Jayan in our opening illustration)—we examine how content creators perceive such deletion.

In sum, prior HCI research has characterized online public shaming as an activity pattern that may both serve as a manifestation of online harassment
(
Jhaver et al. 2018
)
or a corrective justice
(
Vaccaro et al. 2020
)
approach to enforce community norms
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
.
Building on this prior literature, we examine how content creators adopt the comment pinning of morally outrageous comments to induce shaming as a form of content moderation and analyze its benefits and risks.
In doing so, we contribute to broader theoretical understandings of crowd-sourced social sanctioning and the role of public shaming as a tool that offers emotional accountability.

3.1.
Participant Recruitment

Our University’s

2

We will reveal the university’s name after the peer review process completes.
Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviewed the study and categorized its status as exempt on February 3, 2025.
We employed purposive sampling
(
Campbell et al. 2020
)
to select our interviewees, targeting only those content creators who had responded to negative comments by pinning them.
The comment pinning feature is not available on many social media platforms like Facebook and TikTok.
Among the platforms that offer it, we recruited participants primarily from Instagram and YouTube, as they represent mainstream social media services.
Our conversations with participants suggest that the “Pin of Shame” phenomenon occurs with notable frequency on these platforms, which further confirms their relevance as sites of participant selection for this study.

We could not find any curated list of posts displaying the Pin of Shame.
Therefore, to find potential interview subjects, we first created study accounts on Instagram and YouTube.
Next, we used these accounts to conduct keyword searches on both sites to retrieve posts with a higher likelihood of featuring pinned negative comments.
Prior research suggests that posts associated with topics like gender, disability, religion, and elections often invite disproportionate levels of harassment, hate speech, and misinformation
(
Anti-Defamation League 2024
;
Vogels 2021
;
Marwick and Caplan 2018
)
.
Therefore, we began our search for posts demonstrating the Pin of Shame using targeted keywords such as “LGBTQ,” “Non-binary,” “Disorder,” “Immigration,” “MAGA”, “WomeninMaleFields” and “Christian”, to find potentially relevant posts.
Further, we used the keyword “PinofShame” to collect content relating creators’ experiences with comment pinning as a means of shaming commenters on YouTube and Instagram.
Additionally, we liked and bookmarked posts displaying the Pin of Shame so that the platforms’ feed recommendations could further assist our data collection. Following this strategy, we observed additional instances of Pin of Shame in our study accounts’ news feeds.

Based on our review of the posts we collected, we identified 239 individuals as potential interview candidates.
For each of these individuals, we documented their account details. Additionally, the post where they deployed the Pin of Shame, its description, pinned comment, and replies to that comment were collected.
Next, we contacted these candidates. Specifically, we contacted candidates identified on Instagram using the site’s direct message feature.
However, we could contact only those candidates on YouTube who had publicly listed their email-address on their channel profile via emailing them.
During the interview recruitment phase, we also employed snowball sampling, with prior participants recommending other potential candidates.

3.2.
Data collection

In total, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 20 individuals.
All interviews were conducted between February 4th to April 10th, 2025 via Zoom, and interviewees were allowed to join either via their computers or their phones.

The interviews consisted of four parts: background information on their
channel or profile activities; general strategies for addressing negative comments; motivations and outcomes associated with their use of the Pin of Shame—this is where we focused the bulk of our discussion; and perspectives on comment pinning as a content moderation strategy.
During each interview, our lines of inquiry and follow-up questions somewhat varied, depending on each interviewee’s experience, allowing us to probe further into participants’ specific expectations, values, outcomes, and comparisons with other moderation affordances.
For example, if the interviewee had flagged inappropriate comments, the interviewer also asked additional questions, such as how that experience influenced their use of the Pin of Shame.

Before each interview began, we reviewed the data related to the Pin of Shame posts made by each participant, including the pinned negative comment and replies to that comment, to orient ourselves.
During the interview, we referenced this data using screen sharing to guide our conversation with the participant.
Some participants wanted to share their experiences regarding additional posts where they had deployed the Pin of Shame; we encouraged participants to discuss such cases with us.
The interviews were transcribed using Zoom’s transcription feature, subsequently cleaned, and then transferred to and analyzed using NVivo (v.15), a qualitative analysis software.

We concurrently conducted our preliminary data analysis with participant recruitment.
After the 15th interview, we began to observe theoretical saturation, as no new themes were emerging.
To ensure the robustness of our findings, we recruited five additional participants and concluded data collection with a total of 20 interview participants.
After each interview was completed, we compensated the participant with a $20 Amazon e-gift card.

3.3.
Data Analysis

We began by reading our interview transcripts multiple times to familiarize ourselves with the interview data.
Next, we conducted an inductive qualitative analysis of our data following the thematic analysis approach
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
.
The first author created open codes for each interview and wrote memos to document emerging insights.
Next, through an iterative coding process, we refined and consolidated the names and descriptions of codes, identifying 98 initial codes.
Throughout the coding process, the two authors discussed the selection of codes, the relations between codes and data, and how these codes relate to our research questions.

Following this, we visualized and developed the thematic connections between our codes.
To facilitate this, we physically wrote our codes on sticky notes and placed them on a wall — this process offered us greater freedom to engage in constant comparisons, rearrangements, and reflections
(
Maher et al. 2018
)
.
Using the 98 initial codes, we merged similar concepts and organized them into a hierarchical structure, represented by a set of new parent codes and corresponding child codes.
Through this process, we developed 12 parent codes and 76 child codes.
For instance, the parent code ‘Fostering norm education’ included the child codes ‘educating bystanders’ and ‘preventing similar actions in the future.’
Finally, after further refining these codes and examining the relations between them, we developed thick descriptions of parent codes as our key themes that we present as our findings.

4.
FINDINGS

In this section, we first describe content creators’ motivations, considerations, and concerns when deploying the Pin of Shame. Next, we discuss the social effects of deploying the Pin of Shame and how creators perceive these effects.

4.1.
Motivations for Deploying the Pin of shame

4.1.1.
Inviting Public Attention to Foster Norm Education

Pinning
places the selected comment at the top of the comments section. Understanding this function of the pinning affordance, most of our participants pinned negative comments to make them highly visible and more likely to attract other users’ attention. This was often done to
raise their audience’s awareness
of the harmful comments they receive. Participant P5 said:

“I pinned the comment because I just wanted you to see what people like me are going through in this country right now, like what people feel comfortable saying to us.” - P5

Beyond raising awareness, many participants also expected other users who see the Pin of Shame to engage in criticism or rebuttal against the pinned commenter.
Some saw this public criticism as a means to
humiliate and embarrass the negative commenter
.
Others view it as a tool to
educate the pinned commenters
, encouraging them to reflect on their actions, and prompting changes in their attitudes or future behaviors.
Participant P15 illustrated her expectation of using comment pinning against troll accounts:

“So if somebody has a troll account, and they make a nasty comment, and it gets pinned. And they have all these people talking back to them the next time they decide to leave a comment on another creator’s video. They’re gonna think twice. ‘Is that person gonna Pin my comment? And am I gonna get shamed again?’ ” - P15

In addition to reforming the offending commenter, participants expected that the Pin of Shame would also
educate bystanders
, i.e., people who witness the ensuing discussion, about the importance of adhering to social norms by publicly demonstrating the consequences of norm-violating behavior. For instance, P3 mentioned:

“When I pin a comment, I feel like it can influence many people. So it’s not just about me managing the situation and moving on, others can see it and think, ‘Oh, this is real.’ Some people who came to leave a negative comment might change their minds. I believe it can create secondary and even tertiary effects.” - P3

Several participants also noted that the comment pinning feature is technically
easy to adopt, intuitive to use, and brings immediate results
, making it an appealing option for them to address negative comments with minimal effort.
For example, P10 said:

“I think that pinning would probably do more than reporting something, because it has more of an immediate effect on the commenter and stuff, where it’s like, people are seeing you [the pinned commenter], and they’re seeing that you’re saying these things.” - P10

4.1.2.
Shaping What Constitutes Norm Violations

Using the Pin of Shame gives creators the
freedom to decide what should be deemed a norm violation
and which comments deserve to be shamed and criticized on their channel.
Participants recognized that this flexibility allowed them to sanction comments ranging from those that seem “ridiculous,” “judgmental,” or “condescending,” to more serious infractions, such as discriminatory statements, misinformation, and hate speech.
In contrast, flagging constrains regulation by requiring creators to select an inappropriate content category (e.g., ‘Violent Content’, ‘Intellectual Property’) under which the flagged post violates the platform’s code of conduct
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
)
.
Participants also appreciated the control over outcomes that the Pin of Shame offers, especially when compared to flagging, where outcomes (e.g., content removal) depend on whether the platform regulators consider the flagged content a violation.

Many participants observed that they frequently
pin and unpin
different negative comments, and thereby control which comments to emphasize at any moment, as a way to shape the overall direction and tone of discourse in the comments section. For example, P7 shared her experience of pinning and unpinning negative comments:

“I sometimes pin for a day and let people attack this one person. And then, when I’m tired of that person, I’ll unpin it, and then pin another.” - P7

While participants appreciated not being limited by platform guidelines when deploying the Pin of Shame, they also recognized that its
success still depends on the moral outrage
of the audience and their subsequent pushback against the pinned comment. Thus, some participants found it challenging to determine which negative comments to pin such that they would attract a broad public scrutiny and succeed in serving as the Pin of Shame.
Participant P1 said:

“Of course, like we should do it [comment pinning] smartly. If I think some comment is negative, it’s not negative for everyone. So I should be more objective, like, what if I pin these comments, and people will more like opposing with me. It’s not what I want.” - P1

4.1.3.
Leveraging Negative Comments for User Engagement and Content Promotion

Given the commercial logics of social media platforms, most creators are motivated to build greater viewership of their channel and posted content. Interestingly, we found that our participants considered their audience’s expected engagement with their content, even when addressing negative comments using the pinning feature.
Some participants
believed that news feed algorithms “love” negative comments
, and expected that posts with a contentious comments section would be recommended to more viewers. Given this understanding, they sought to leverage negative comments on their posts to boost user engagement and algorithmically promote post visibility.
P6 explained:

“…These comments are part of why this video actually went viral, because people just spent so much time in the comments section that the algorithm was like, oh, this is content that people are watching and engaging with.” - P6

Indeed, some participants deliberately
pin negative comments that have already sparked engagement
or are likely to generate further discussion, anticipating continued user interaction.
For example, Participant P13 described that when a negative comment gains popularity through replies, she strategically pins it to maintain its existing prominent position in the comments section:

“So what I see is the one [comment] that is getting the most likes. I’ll pin it and make sure it stays at the top. … I pinned it, because that’s the one that I knew most people would see. I don’t care whatever that guy’s saying.” - P13

4.2.
Consequences of Deploying the Pin of shame

4.2.1.
Holding Norm Violators Accountable

Consistent with participants’ expectations, the Pin of Shame often results in collective shaming, i.e.,
other users
post counterarguments against the negative commenter
and
defend the original poster (OP)
, transforming the comments section into a discursive space to confront, challenge, and correct inappropriate behavior.
Participants viewed this collective shaming as an
apt punishment for the pinned commenters
, a
fair
, retributive response to their infractions, and an effective way to hold them accountable. For instance, Participant P15 made an analogy between such shaming and legal punishment by emphasizing how both measures make violators face the consequences of their actions:

“Maybe it’s [the Pin of Shame is] like community service when you get caught doing something stupid. It’s like you go to the judge, and the judge says ‘You were wrong. Here’s your 25 hours of community service.’ Maybe the 25 hours of community service is 25 of my followers calling that person ridiculous for making that statement.” - P15

In response to such shaming, the
pinned commenters sometimes delete the pinned comment or apologize
to participants.
However, in other cases, the Pin of Shame incites either no reaction from the pinned commenters (e.g., in the case of Jayan) or sparks an intense argument between them and the shaming public.
Participants appreciated that using the Pin of Shame still affords them an opportunity to deploy additional moderation measures when the resulting discourse from the Pin of Shame does not yield the expected outcomes.
In the latter cases, participants usually follow up by implementing another sanction, such as reporting or deleting the concerning comment:

“I think that [the Pin of Shame] doesn’t always work, and there are instances where I do delete them, typically if they start insulting other people who are not me. You never know how someone else is handling that, or how that might affect them.” - P4

4.2.2.
Attaining Emotional Empowerment and Social Support

Using the Pin of Shame provides emotional relief to participants, including a
satisfaction and pleasure rooted in retributive justice
.
Typically, participants described the Pin of Shame as a satisfying and enjoyable experience, where the shaming of those who leave negative comments provides catharsis through a general recognition that justice has been served.

“Just watch the shit, pin the comment, and watch people respond to that person, it’s just kind of a source of amusement sometimes, and makes me feel better when other people who are trying to troll me also get trolled. I don’t know. It’s probably not like the best strategy. But it makes me feel better sometimes. I won’t lie. … It’s very satisfying to see.” - P14

Many participants felt that using the Pin of Shame reduces the emotional labor required to address negative comments just by oneself. It enables them to make sense of their hurt emotions, receive social support,
feel less isolated
in the face of negativity, and leverage their followers’ assistance to counteract negative comments.
Participant P14 shared:

“ It’s [Pin of Shame] making it more of a group conversation and kind of also showing me that people have my back, because sometimes I feel like I’m just arguing with people constantly.” - P14

Participants also noted that these
emotional benefits extend beyond themselves.

Some of them observed that when negative comments containing misogyny or hate speech are pinned, it explicitly encourages targeted in-groups to unite among themselves, develop a sense of community, and resist online hate.
Participants expected that in such cases, collective actions by their followers may allow them to experience empowerment and an emotional catharsis at their success in shaming the norm violators.

“I feel like it’s a form of entertainment for the people who see it. It’s a form of like inspiration, because I do get messages from my followers saying ‘Yo, bro, keep it up. Keep Moggin,’ like.” - P16

4.2.3.
Saving the Cognitive Effort to Address Negative Comments

Using the Pin of Shame enables participants to punish or educate negative commenters without direct engagement, allowing them to
save time and mental exertion
by relying on the involvement of other users.
This contrasts with other coping mechanisms, such as replying to or reporting the negative commenters, which require personal involvement.
Participant P11 explained that the Pin of Shame allows creators to take an observational position instead of a more demanding participatory role in addressing negative comments:

“So, I pin the type of comment like ‘You are old. You look 45’ … People who try to support me and protect me, they will start fighting with that comment as well. So it’s kind of like a battle of two people. And I’m just here with my Popcorn.” - P11

4.2.4.
Respecting Commenters’ Free Speech

The Pin of Shame socially sanctions the pinned comments while preserving their visibility, meaning that negative comments are not removed from public view.
Some participants favored this outcome over the consequences of more traditional moderation actions such as reporting or deleting comments, which can sometimes lead to a backfire effect, i.e., they receive criticism from commenters and the public for restricting free speech.
Thus, participants appreciated the Pin of Shame for its ability to
impose punishment without infringing on commenters’ freedom to express
themselves.

“I never delete comments that people can speak their mind because I like the idea of freedom of speech. You’re okay to say anything, because once you take that away from someone, then there are so many other things you can take away.” - P16

Some participants highlighted the importance of
preserving negative comments for their documentation value
.
By keeping them visible, participants aim to ensure that these comments remain part of the digital record, serving as enduring evidence that is accessible to everyone over time.
Participant P19 mentioned:

“When I see those negative comments, I think it’s such a ridiculous comment. And so I want them to know it’s a ridiculous comment in a way that you put that out there as your digital footprint.” - P19

4.2.5.
Engaging in Impression Management

Many participants acknowledged that after they pin a negative comment, they reply to it in a playful or lighthearted manner, often leaving witty remarks.
This practice aims to lower the temperature,
foster a more relaxed and entertaining atmosphere
, and shift the focus away from the rigid tone of commenter being punished through shaming.
Through such discursive efforts, participants sought to create a space where both the pinned commenter and the public could express their viewpoints more comfortably.

“When I’m replying back [to pinned comment] and I’m saying like funny things, like, ‘I’ll see you on church on Sunday’ … The way that I respond to them, it shows a lot about my character, and who I am, and people who do support me think it’s funny, and they appreciate that.” - P17

This behavior also reflects participants’ efforts to
craft an aloof persona
in response to negative comments despite pinning them.
Rather than engaging in direct confrontation or displaying emotional distress, they often employed humor or sarcasm to maintain emotional composure and preserve a positive self-image in public settings.
By reframing negative comments as sources of amusement, creators sought to invert the power dynamic, strategically signaling emotional resilience and control.
For instance, P6 said:

“I’m never serious with them. I always just like joke around. When someone says something like, ‘Oh, you’re not pretty enough to have this opinion’, [I respond] ‘Stop! You’re gonna make me sad’ with a sad face [emoji] like I’ll just joke back with them because I feel like they’re trying to hurt me, or they’re trying to put their stuff out in the world, and I’m like, I don’t care.” - P6

4.2.6.
Sensing Emotional Stress

While the Pin of Shame often provides creators and their supporters with positive emotional experiences and strategic benefits, it can also result in unintended negative outcomes.
Some participants shared that when they noticed that the pinned comment continued to receive greater support than their own or their supporters’ counter-arguments, it caused them
emotional stress and discomfort
.
For example, P19 said:

“I pinned it, and I was really scared because at first you can see like this comment has about, almost 4,000 likes, and so it’s quite a lot actually for a comment. I don’t think it’s very nice. … I was kind of worried that people were like really agreeing with this guy because I was like, ‘Wow, there’s too many people that are not nice in this world.’ ” - P19

Some participants lamented experiences when the pinned commenter became a target of excessive insults and mockery.
Participants also expressed concerns about instances when the argument initiated by the Pin of Shame attracts more haters supporting the pinned comment, or when the pinned commenter actively engages by doubling down on their original response.
In such cases, the comments section often devolves into
mutual insults and hostility
, ultimately amplifying the negativity and disrupting the otherwise constructive dialogue in that section.
Participant P4 illustrated how replies to one of her pinned comments escalated into a hostile discourse:

“Now they’re arguing with each other. [Narrating the replies to their pinned comment—] ‘It’s always the men who look like you, insane ratio.’ ‘You look like someone who listens to Juice WRLD and cries.’ I think they go to the person’s profile and [find] something to insult.” - P4

When the negative comment remains fixed at the top and continues to fuel flame wars, some participants expressed concerns about the
emotional burden of being exposed to negativity
whenever they open the comments section.
Given this risk, some participants highlighted the importance of emotional resilience when using the Pin of Shame, noting that they need to be ready to confront negativity every time they return to the comments section.

“You have to have a strong backbone to let people, hundreds and thousands of people say negative things to you sometimes at once. You have to like, get past it and then laugh at it, and it takes a long time to get to that point.” - P7

Moreover, a few participants worried that pinning negative comments may overshadow their original content by placing it in a negative light, and cause the entire post to be perceived as unpleasant.

“At first I was, I kind of do feel bad. That I make someone in the spotlight and it’s like kind of negative, or I was scared like it makes this into [post] silly video, a negative video.” - P19

5.
Discussion

Our analysis of the motivations and consequences of using the Pin of Shame offers theoretical and practical insights into content creators’ comment moderation needs, strategies, and concerns. These insights offer valuable considerations and pathways for HCI scholars and practitioners to design novel content moderation tools. We describe these insights below.

5.1.
Benefits and Risks of Deploying Public Shaming to Punish and Educate Norm Violators

Our analysis shows that the Pin of Shame leverages hyper-visibility to render inappropriate comments accountable to other social actors
(
Trottier 2018
)
by attracting their collective criticism and counterarguments
(
Basak et al. 2019
;
Nussbaum 2019
)
(Section
4.1.1
).
By doing so, it allows the community to engage in norm education
(
Trottier 2018
;
Jhaver et al. 2024
)
and coerce pinned commenters into changing their behavior
(
Shea et al. 2024
;
Muir et al. 2021
)
.
Through discussing real-world examples with the involved creators of how shaming can function positively, this study provides evidence supporting its potential benefits as documented in prior literature
(
Scheff 2014
;
Trottier 2018
;
Hou et al. 2017
;
Kasabova 2021
;
Direk 2020
;
Corry 2021
;
Braithwaite 1989
)
, particularly when it operates as a crowd-sourced form of governance.

We found that deploying such strategic online shaming can be especially empowering for creator groups who are disproportionately targeted by abuse and harassment (e.g., based on their gender or ethnicity), offering them a means to resist injustice rather than to silently endure it
(
Murumaa-Mengel and Muuli 2021
)
.
Many content creators we interviewed for this study valued this empowering property of the Pin of Shame: instead of silent comment removals or account bans, it allowed them and their followers to publicly voice their stance and speak out against inappropriate behaviors (Section
4.2.1
).
Accordingly, the Pin of Shame offers a unique set of affordances and benefits within the toolkit of moderation strategies available to creators.

However, collective shaming by the public is inherently uncalibrated, carrying the potential for conversations derailing or escalating into networked harassment
(
Marwick 2021
;
Lewis et al. 2021
;
Klonick 2015
)
.
Some of our participants reported that the arguments initiated by the Pin of Shame can occasionally devolve into doxxing or personal insults (Section
4.2.6
).
These unintended negative consequences of the Pin of Shame can cause emotional stress for content creators (Section
4.2.6
).

To mitigate such effects, comment moderation systems should be designed to detect early signals of conversation derailment sparked by the Pin of Shame and prompt nudges, e.g., by asking the pinned commenter, “Would you like to edit or remove this comment?”
This can help prevent arguments from escalating into networked harassment and uphold involved users’ safety.

5.2.
Moderation Tools Should Seek to Provide Emotional Accountability

Given the significant emotional impact that hate and harassment inflict on content creators
(
Thomas et al. 2022
)
, existing moderation practices have primarily focused on approaches that sanction perpetrators, e.g., by removing their posted content or restricting their access
(
Cai et al. 2024
)
. However, prior HCI research has shown that such punitive actions—which are easily bypassed by creating new posts or accounts—often fail to adequately acknowledge or address the emotional harm experienced by the targeted users
(
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Jhaver et al. 2018
)
.

Against this context, the Pin of Shame emerges as a unique tool that satisfies content creators’ desire to indulge in sensemaking around their harm and get retribution for their pain through shaming (Section
4.1.1
), while also allowing them to observe the entire process of social sanctioning (Section
4.2.1
).
Specifically, creators can follow the unfolding consequences—such as apologies or comment deletions by inappropriate commenters—providing them with a sense of fun and satisfaction (Section
4.2.2
) while also validating their emotional responses (Section
4.2.3
).

This process underscores the crucial role of emotional considerations in moderation design.
Echoing prior research on restorative justice approaches, particularly those centered around communication practices that seek to address harm and hold violators accountable
(
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
;
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Blackwell et al. 2018
;
Kou 2021
)
, our findings emphasize the attainment of emotional accountability as a safety goal.
Thus, novel moderation mechanisms may innovate with allowing users to share their emotions in response to inappropriate comments in unique ways and providing means to ensure emotional repair.

5.3.
Moderation Design Should Attend to Creators’ Impression Management Goals During Norm Enforcement

In addition to providing emotional support, we found that the Pin of Shame operates as a tool for impression management
(
Goffman 2023
)
.
It allows content creators to incorporate entertainment into the moderation process, such as by adding playful remarks (Section
4.2.5
).
This lighthearted approach aims to craft a more relaxed atmosphere and a favorable self-presentation to the public (Section
4.2.5
).
Thus, the Pin of Shame serves the performative needs
(
Li et al. 2019
;
Pellicone and Ahn 2017
)
of creators confronting negativity by allowing them to redirect it into positivity, and thereby enabling them to effectively showcase resilience and emotional maturity in their public persona.

While prior research has examined impression management through various dimensions of online self-presentation—such as political expression, cultural taste, and targeting the audience awareness on social media
(
Liu 2007
;
Marder et al. 2016
;
Marwick and Boyd 2011
)
—our study extends this work by revealing that even norm enforcement actions, such as comment moderation, can be strategically adopted to curate one’s public persona.
This finding also raises important questions to further explore about how different forms of creator-led moderation actions intersect with their impression management goals.
In light of these insights, we encourage platforms to design moderation mechanisms that embrace creators’ creativity and playfulness, and empower them to transform negative encounters into displays of expression, performance, and reputational agency.

5.4.
Recommender Systems Should Enact and Communicate the Use of Emotion-Sensitive Engagement Metrics to Shape Visibility

Our participants often shared a folk theory that negative comments are likely to trigger algorithmic amplification of their posted content.
Regardless of the extent to which this folk theory
(
Gelman and Legare 2011
;
French and Hancock 2017
)

aligns with actual news feeds outputs, its frequent occurrence in our interview data suggests that it motivates creators to deploy public shaming in an effort to exploit the recommendation algorithm, with the hope that it would promote their content to a broader audience (Section
4.1.3
).
Indeed, we found that some creators pin negative comments, even when doing so takes an emotional toll, such as repeated exposure to negativity or argumentative threads sparked by the pinned comment (Section
4.2.6
).

Such actions underscore a limitation in current algorithmic design: recommendation systems often give content creators the perception that their underlying algorithms prioritize engagement metrics without considering whether the interactions are constructive or hostile.
When content is promoted primarily based on high engagement driven by emotional negativity, it risks placing an emotional burden on creators, who may feel pressured to tolerate or even provoke negativity to increase visibility of their posted content.
Therefore, we emphasize the need for emotion-sensitive algorithmic design, one that not only captures engagement volume but also accounts for the emotional tone of user interactions.
Moreover, fostering creator understanding and trust in such emotionally aware systems may further shift platform dynamics toward more positive and sustainable participation.
This approach would help create healthier online environments and better support the emotional well-being of content creators.

5.5.
Moderation Tools Should Seek to Incorporate Transparency Regarding the Sanctioning Processes

The Pin of Shame represents a unique form of moderation that maintains the visibility of inappropriate comments, rather than removing them outright (Section
4.1.1
).
By preserving the original comment, creators can avoid the potential backlash often associated with removal actions—such as reporting, deleting, or muting
(
Myers West 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
—while simultaneously upholding free expression and maintaining the comment’s documentation value (Section
4.2.4
).
This works by the ensuing discussion publicly signaling the inappropriateness of the pinned comment—similar to how soft moderation approaches clarify norm violations in the labeled content
(
Singhal et al. 2023
)
—while also enabling content creators to impose further sanctions on the comment if they deem it necessary (Section
4.2.4
).

By making the entire sanctioning process visible to the public, the Pin of Shame increases the transparency of the norm enforcement process. Our participants appreciated that bystanders can observe the context of the original inappropriate statement, witness how social sanctioning is carried out, and examine the outcomes, such as whether it leads to an apology (Section
4.2.1
).
As prior research has also shown, transparency in the moderation process can have several important effects: it can enhance users’ perceptions of moderation fairness
(
Myers West 2018
;
Gonçalves et al. 2023
;
Suzor et al. 2019
)
, help prevent further violations
(
Jhaver et al. 2019c
;
Jhaver et al. 2019a
)
, facilitate norm education among witnesses
(
Jhaver et al. 2024
)
,
foster a stronger sense of belonging among community members
(
Matias 2019
)
, and encourage meaningful user contributions
(
Jhaver et al. 2019c
)
.
Therefore, ensuring the visibility of rule violations and the sanctioning process is a crucial design consideration for moderation mechanisms.

5.6.
Norm Negotiation by Content Creators and the Publics

Since individual users’ moral outrage toward a pinned comment may vary, the consequences of deploying the Pin of Shame do not always meet the expectations of content creators (Section
4.1.2
).
These moments of divergence suggest that the comment section can serve not only as a site for moral judgment, but also as a deliberative space where norms are collectively negotiated.
Given that perceptions of harm and what to do about them are shaped by cultural contexts
(
Jiang et al. 2021
)
and individual experiences
(
Jhaver et al. 2017
)
, it is important to foreground the conversational aspect of the Pin of Shame.
For example, when creators notice that a pinned comment has generated controversy over its appropriateness, they can provide context beneath the pinned comment, explaining why they deem it problematic.
Such descriptive clarifications may help align diverse perspectives and contribute to a more transparent norm-setting process.

More broadly, we found that the Pin of Shame functions as a catalyst for making visible these differing norm constructions.
Unlike conventional moderation systems, where users have limited options—such as appeals—to express disagreement with enforcement decisions
(
Vaccaro et al. 2020
;
Haimson et al. 2021
;
Clune and McDaid 2024
)
, the Pin of Shame offers a more open and visible arena for users to engage in collective debate, which creators appreciate.

Therefore, we advocate for a shift toward a more communicative model of moderation—one that embraces norm negotiation as an ongoing, participatory process.
Platforms and users alike should work to foster a culture in which norms are co-constructed through open dialogue informed by diverse criteria, lived experiences, and cultural backgrounds
(
Direk 2020
;
Billingham and Parr 2020
)
.
Such an approach could support a healthier online environment, one that recognizes the complexity of moral disagreement and encourages constructive engagement over conflict.

5.7.
Limitations and Future Work

We interviewed only the content creators who deployed the Pin of Shame in this study. It would be valuable to examine the perspectives of pinned commenters to understand how they perceive the experience of being publicly shamed, and how it shapes their attitudes about future posting on social media.
Large-scale, longitudinal analyses of pinned commenters’ behaviors can also reveal the effects of Pin of Shame on norm violators’ posting patterns.

Additionally, despite including content creators with varying follower counts, topical interests, and motivations for posting, the study is limited, as most participants were active on a single social media platform.
Future research could explore how the practice of pinning negative comments varies across platforms.
