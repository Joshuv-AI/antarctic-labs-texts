Toxic Dust: Measuring Awareness about Third-Hand Smoke Contamination in Homes Among Smokers and Non-smokers of Family Members—A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: N Nagappan, T. Manigandan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Third-hand smoke (THS) poses significant health risks through persistent toxic residues, yet awareness remains inadequate, particularly in high-smoking populations like India. This study assessed THS knowledge gaps between smokers and non-smokers in Chennai households.Methods:A community-based cross-sectional study surveyed 450 adults (225 smokers, 225 non-smokers) using multistage cluster sampling. A validated 20-item questionnaire measured THS knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Statistical analyses included t-tests, Chi-square tests, and logistic regression (SPSS v28, P < 0.05).Results:Non-smokers demonstrated significantly higher THS knowledge (mean score 6.7 vs. 4.1, P < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.18). Logistic regression identified non-smoker status odds ratio [(OR] = 3.42, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.15–5.44) and higher education (OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.82–4.59) as strong knowledge predictors. Alarmingly, 65.8% of smokers (vs. 29.3% non-smokers, P < 0.001) erroneously believed cleaning eliminates THS, while only 12.4% maintained smoke-free homes (vs. 68.9% non-smokers, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Substantial THS awareness gaps exist among smokers, compounded by misconceptions about exposure mitigation. Findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions addressing these knowledge deficits, particularly focusing on proper cleaning practices and smoke-free home implementation. The study provides crucial baseline data for developing context-specific THS risk communication strategies in urban Indian settings.

INTRODUCTION

Third-hand smoke (THS) represents a significant yet under recognized public health hazard, consisting of residual tobacco toxins that persist on surfaces, in dust, and in household materials long after active smoking has ceased.[1] These residues contain numerous carcinogenic compounds, including tobacco-specific nitrosamines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which can remain in environments for weeks to months.[2] Recent studies demonstrate that THS exposure occurs through multiple pathways, including dermal absorption, dust inhalation, and ingestion, posing particular risks to vulnerable populations such as infants and children.[3]

Despite growing scientific evidence of THS-related health risks, public awareness remains strikingly low. In India, where smoking prevalence remains high (28.6% among adults), only 22% of the population demonstrates awareness of THS hazards.[4] This knowledge gap is particularly concerning given that THS exposure in domestic environments may contribute to various health outcomes, including respiratory illnesses, impaired wound healing, and metabolic disorders.[5]

Furthermore, conventional cleaning methods are often ineffective at completely removing these residues, leading to prolonged exposure scenarios.[6] This situation is exacerbated in developing countries like India, where ventilation may be inadequate and smoking indoors remains culturally accepted in many communities.[7]

The present study was undertaken to assess and compare awareness levels about THS contamination in home environments among smoking and non-smoking populations, while identifying key demographic predictors of knowledge gaps.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This community-based, cross-sectional study was conducted among households in Chengalpet (Dist), from June to November 2024. The sample size was calculated to compare proportions between smokers and non-smokers, based on previous data showing 22% awareness among smokers and 40% among non-smokers.[8] With 80% power and 95% confidence level, we required 196 participants per group, expanding to 450 total (225 smokers, 225 non-smokers) to account for a 15% non-response rate. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sree Balaji Dental College and Hospital, BIHER, Chennai (IHEC No: SBDCH – IEC/24-05/14) with written informed consent from all participants and strict maintenance of privacy through anonymized data collection.

The study will include adults aged 18–65 years residing in selected households who are either current smokers (≥1 cigarette/bidi per day for ≥6 months) or never-smokers, serving as primary decision-makers regarding household smoking rules, and having lived in their current home for ≥1 year. Exclusion criteria include individuals with cognitive impairments affecting survey responses, those who quit smoking within the past 2 years, households with professional tobacco industry workers, and pregnant women (due to potential recall bias about exposure avoidance).

A multistage cluster sampling approach will be employed, beginning with random selection of 4 zones of Chengalpet district, followed by systematic random sampling of households within each ward, and finally random selection of one eligible adult per household. Data collection will utilize a structured questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews by trained researchers, comprising three sections: (A) Sociodemographics (age, gender, education, occupation, income, housing characteristics); (B) THS Awareness Assessment (20 items across knowledge [true/false], attitude [5-point Likert scale], and practice domains); and (C) Smoking Behavior (current status, household rules, previous residents’ history).

The questionnaire was adapted from the validated Third-hand Smoke Exposure Scale[9] and underwent rigorous validation including content validation by 5 public health experts (content validity index = 0.89), pilot testing (n = 30, Cronbach’s α = 0.82), and cognitive interviewing for cultural appropriateness.

Statistical analysis will include primary analyses using descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, and Chi-square tests, along with secondary analyses employing multivariable logistic regression, Pearson’s correlation, and factor analysis. All analyses will be conducted using SPSS v28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) with significance set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

DISCUSSION

Our study reveals critical gaps in THS awareness between smokers and non-smokers in Chengalpet households, with non-smokers demonstrating significantly higher knowledge scores (6.7 vs. 4.1, P < 0.001). These findings align with previous research showing smokers consistently underestimate environmental tobacco smoke risks,[9] but extend this evidence to the understudied domain of THS in developing nations. The nearly threefold higher odds (OR = 3.42) of adequate knowledge among non-smokers suggests that personal smoking behavior fundamentally alters risk perception, potentially through cognitive dissonance mechanisms.[10]

The strong association between education level and THS knowledge (OR = 2.89) supports health literacy models positing that education facilitates comprehension of complex environmental health concepts.[11] Our urban residence finding (OR = 1.67) may reflect greater exposure to anti-tobacco messaging in cities, consistent with Sarkar et al. work on geographic disparities in tobacco knowledge.[12] Notably, the presence of young children showed only borderline significance (P = 0.110), contrasting with Western studies[13]—a difference possibly attributable to cultural variations in child protection practices.

The behavioral data presents particular concern: while 62.7% of non-smokers reported regular surface cleaning, only 12.4% of smokers maintained smoke-free homes. This aligns with Ferrante et al. identification of an “implementation gap” where knowledge doesn’t translate to action.[14]

The study has several limitations: its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about knowledge-behavior relationships, while urban sampling and reliance on self-reported data may limit generalizability to rural populations and introduce social desirability bias. Additionally, the lack of comprehensive environmental biomarker data and cultural specificity of the Indian context may constrain cross-national comparisons of findings.

CONCLUSION

The findings reveal significantly lower knowledge among smokers (mean score 4.1 vs. 6.7, P < 0.001), particularly those with less education, and highlight prevalent misconceptions about cleaning efficacy (65.8% smokers believing cleaning removes THS). These results underscore the urgent need for targeted THS education programs focusing on smokers and emphasizing proper mitigation strategies.
