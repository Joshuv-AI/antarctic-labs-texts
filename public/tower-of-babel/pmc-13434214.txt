Interventional Mechanical Thrombectomy for Limb Salvage in Rapidly Progressive Phlegmasia Cerulea Dolens
Authors: Johan Filo, Manuel Stern, Malte Kelm, Lucas Busch, Patricia Wischmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMechanical thrombectomy can provide rapid, fibrinolysis‐free restoration of venous flow in phlegmasia cerulea dolens. This case highlights an unusually severe course with extensive iliofemorocaval thrombosis, rhabdomyolysis, MRI‐confirmed muscle necrosis, and arterial hypoperfusion despite anticoagulation, in which urgent intervention achieved limb salvage.Keywords: deep vein thrombosis, limb salvage, mechanical thrombectomy, pelvic vein thrombosis, phlegmasia cerulea dolens, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Pelvic vein thrombosis represents a rare but potentially life‐threatening form of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [1, 2]. The most severe manifestation of acute deep vein thrombosis is phlegmasia cerulea dolens, a rare but potentially limb‐threatening condition. Pathophysiologically, it is characterized by near‐total or total occlusion of the venous outflow, including collateral networks. The resulting massive venous congestion leads to profound tissue edema, a marked increase in interstitial pressure, and ultimately secondary arterial collapse. If left untreated, the severe ischemia rapidly progresses to venous gangrene and circulatory shock, often necessitating amputation [3, 4, 5].

Standard therapy consists of anticoagulation, which is often insufficient in extensive proximal thrombosis. Thrombolysis can achieve recanalization but carries a considerable bleeding risk. Mechanical, fibrinolysis‐free procedures provide new therapeutic options in this setting.

We report the successful use of the ClotTriever system in a young female patient with extensive pelvic vein thrombosis and concomitant muscle necrosis. While mechanical thrombectomy is an established treatment option for extensive deep vein thrombosis, the present case illustrates an unusually severe and rapidly progressive clinical course, in which objective signs of tissue injury and deteriorating arterial perfusion prompted urgent intervention.

Case Presentation/Examination

On March 17, 2025, a 30‐year‐old woman presented to the emergency department of a local hospital in Düsseldorf with rapidly progressive massive swelling of the left leg. Within a few hours, she developed severe pain, livid discoloration of the entire extremity, tense soft tissue swelling, and hypoesthesia. Duplex ultrasound revealed extensive thrombosis extending from the popliteal vein to the inferior vena cava.

The patient was initially admitted to the intermediate care unit and managed conservatively. Arterial perfusion of the lower leg was preserved with a normal ankle‐brachial index, and treatment with Innohep (LMWH) and analgesics was started. Risk factors included estrogen‐containing oral contraceptives and morbid obesity (BMI 39 kg/m2).

At the subsequent vascular surgical examination, the left leg was moderately swollen compared with the right, with pressure tenderness over the lateral thigh. The thigh and lower‐leg compartments were soft, with no relevant tenderness of the lower leg. The foot was warm and pink with prompt capillary refill; motor function and sensation were intact at that time. Acute compartment syndrome was excluded clinically; no compartment‐pressure measurement was documented.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Potential causes of deep vein thrombosis include inherited thrombophilias, autoimmune hypercoagulable states, systemic inflammation, malignancy, and other acquired conditions that increase thrombotic risk.

Due to progressive leg pain and rhabdomyolysis (creatine kinase up to 15,666 U/L), MRI of the leg was performed the following day, revealing diffuse swelling and early necrosis of the left vastus medialis muscle, along with impaired arterial perfusion (reduced ankle‐brachial index).

Given the rapidly progressive course, she was referred to the vascular center of the University Hospital. Following interdisciplinary discussion, urgent interventional mechanical thrombectomy was performed the same day by the Department of Interventional Angiology. Angiography confirmed complete visualization of the inferior vena cava with restored venous outflow into the deep leg veins.

Standard therapy for DVT is anticoagulation with heparins or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). In acute extensive proximal thrombosis with imminent arterial hypoperfusion due to impaired venous outflow and developing compartment syndrome, anticoagulation alone is frequently insufficient. Systemic or local thrombolysis using fibrinolytic agents such as recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (e.g., alteplase) or urokinase can provide rapid recanalization but carries significant bleeding risks—especially in young patients without cardiopulmonary comorbidities [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

While traditional surgical venous thrombectomy, potentially requiring concomitant fasciotomy, remains a therapeutic option, such procedures are now rarely performed due to limited evidence and the advancement of less invasive endovascular strategies. Catheter‐directed thrombolysis, frequently combined with pharmacomechanical devices, is another established endovascular alternative but still relies on lytic agents.

At the University Hospital, interventional options include ultrasound‐assisted catheter‐directed thrombolysis (EKOS) and mechanical thrombectomy [11]. The latter avoids the need for prolonged fibrinolysis (6–24 h) and enables immediate clinical improvement. In this case, due to arterial hypoperfusion and rapidly progressing rhabdomyolysis, mechanical thrombectomy was chosen.

The procedure was performed with the patient in the prone position using ultrasound‐guided access to the popliteal vein. The patient had complete thrombosis of the iliac and common femoral vein and partial thrombosis of the superficial femoral and popliteal veins. After initial venous access, a 13F sheath was introduced (Figure 1B). Following phlebography, a stiff guidewire was passed through the thrombotic occlusion up to the inferior vena cava (Figure 1A). The ClotTriever catheter was advanced over the wire beyond the thrombus burden. The self‐expanding nitinol coring element and collection bag were then deployed cranial to the thrombus. Mechanical thrombectomy was performed by gradually retracting the device into the access sheath, thereby coring the thrombus from the vein wall and capturing it within the integrated collection bag. Simultaneous aspiration through the sheath was applied to minimize the risk of distal embolization.

Almost all thrombotic material was removed and venous outflow restored (Figures 1, 2, 3). Final venography (Figure 1C) showed adequate inflow and outflow; only small nonflow‐limiting residual thrombi remained in the left iliac vein. Given the absence of venous compression syndrome, no stent was implanted.

Postintervention, the patient received therapeutic heparin and volume therapy for rhabdomyolysis. CK decreased from 15,374 U/L on the day after intervention to 10,400 U/L, and to 1070 U/L 5 days later.

Conclusion and Results

The patient was discharged 1 week after intervention. Secondary prophylaxis with rivaroxaban was initiated, and oral contraceptives were discontinued. The patient has a known sarcoidosis (diagnosed in May 2019), which initially manifested with recurrent fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and erythema nodosum. However, current laboratory and imaging findings showed no evidence of active disease. Further thrombophilia testing revealed no indication of antiphospholipid syndrome. Additionally, the patient will be referred for gynecological evaluation to rule out a malignant condition.

According to ESC (2019), CHEST (2021), and German S2k guideline (2023), anticoagulation for proximal DVT should be continued for at least 3–6 months. Extended or indefinite anticoagulation with DOACs may be considered if persistent risk factors (e.g., obesity, hormonal contraception, prothrombotic conditions) are present and bleeding risk is low [8, 9, 12].

In this case, thrombosis was associated with reversible risk factors (obesity and oral contraceptives); therefore, indefinite anticoagulation was not initially indicated. At 3‐month follow‐up, the patient had lost 11 kg and remained off hormonal contraception. MR angiography performed 4 months after the intervention demonstrated minimal residual thrombosis. At 1‐year follow‐up, she was clinically asymptomatic, regularly wore compression stockings, and had no lower‐leg swelling or clinical signs of chronic venous insufficiency in the affected limb. Duplex ultrasonography showed no recurrent deep vein thrombosis or pelvic venous outflow obstruction.

In young women with multiple thrombotic risk factors, decisions regarding extended anticoagulation should balance the risk of recurrent venous thromboembolism against the risk of bleeding. In view of the minimal residual thrombosis on follow‐up imaging and following angiological and haemostasiological reassessment, long‐term reduced‐dose DOAC therapy was recommended without a predefined treatment duration, with continuation to be reassessed periodically according to the patient's thrombotic and bleeding risks [13, 14].

Discussion

Phlegmasia cerulea dolens requires urgent intervention, as it is associated with high morbidity and mortality. While systemic or local thrombolysis can accelerate recanalization, it carries considerable bleeding risks.

This case demonstrates that mechanical thrombectomy is an effective and safe strategy for immediate restoration of venous outflow. The major advantage lies in rapid symptom relief while avoiding fibrinolysis and its bleeding complications. Thus, mechanical thrombectomy represents a valuable option in the management of severe proximal DVT. Although mechanical thrombectomy has previously been described in phlegmasia cerulea dolens, this case is distinguished by the rapid progression to severe rhabdomyolysis, MRI‐confirmed muscle necrosis, neurological impairment, and evolving arterial hypoperfusion, highlighting the importance of timely intervention in a threatened limb.

Previous reports have described single‐session mechanical thrombectomy in patients with phlegmasia cerulea dolens, including presentations with absent pedal pulses and sensory deficits, critical limb ischemia despite delayed treatment, and progression despite therapeutic anticoagulation [15, 16, 17, 18]. The severity and outcomes of these cases varied, including one patient who ultimately required below‐knee amputation despite restoration of venous flow [18]. In the present case, the distinctive clinical feature was the combination of extensive iliofemorocaval thrombosis, severe rhabdomyolysis with creatine kinase exceeding 15,000 U/L, MRI‐confirmed muscle necrosis, and clinical deterioration despite anticoagulation. Urgent single‐session thrombectomy restored venous outflow and was followed by durable limb preservation and favorable functional recovery.
