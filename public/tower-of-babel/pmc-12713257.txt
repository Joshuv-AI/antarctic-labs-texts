Preparation and characterization of Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus casei, glycyrrhizin, vitamin-D3, and citric acid-loaded mucoadhesive pectin microparticles for the management of dental caries
Authors: Sumita Mishra, Somnath Ghosh, Koushik Jana, Biplab Debnath, Sudhanshu Ranjan Rout, Vineet Kumar Rai, Rakesh Kumar Sahoo, Jitu Halder, Ritu Mahanty, Priyanka Dash, Chandan Das, Salim Manoharadas, Jameel Al-Tamimi, Muralidhar Tata, Neeta Mohanty, Biswakanth Kar, Goutam Ghosh, Goutam Rath

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental caries, a prevalent oral disease, results from bacterial biofilm formation and enamel demineralization, leading to tooth decay and associated complications. Conventional treatments face challenges such as poor drug bioavailability and antibiotic resistance.ObjectivesThis study aims to develop a mucoadhesive localized drug delivery system (LDDS) incorporating Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus casei, glycyrrhizin (GL), citric acid (CA), and pectin (PC) to enhance antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties for effective dental caries management.MethodsMicroparticles were synthesized via ionic gelation and characterized using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC), and Fourier-transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), etc.ResultsIn vitro assays, including antimicrobial (agar well diffusion, MIC, MBC), anti-biofilm, and anti-inflammatory (cytokine analysis), demonstrated significant antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus and S. mutans with notable reductions in inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and increased IL-10 expression. The formulation also exhibited strong antioxidant potential in DPPH scavenging assays. In vivo studies on Wistar rats confirmed prolonged retention in the oral cavity and effective antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity, further supported by histopathological analysis. The findings indicate that the developed microparticles offer a promising alternative for dental caries treatment by ensuring sustained drug release, improved bioavailability, and enhanced therapeutic outcomes.ConclusionThis study underscores the potential of probiotic-based mucoadhesive drug delivery systems in oral health management. Introduction

Dental caries is one of the most common non-communicable diseases globally, affecting people of all ages. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 2 billion people worldwide suffer from dental caries (tooth decay) in permanent teeth, while 514 million children have caries in their primary (baby) teeth [1]. Dental caries risk factors include poor oral hygiene, high sugar intake, and inadequate fluoride exposure, which weaken enamel and promote bacteria. Primarily, the demineralization of enamel, dentin, or cementum, instigated by the interplay of acidogenic oral microorganisms, such as Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), with dietary carbohydrates, constitutes the primary etiological factor [2]. These pathogenic bacteria, with their remarkable ability to adhere to dental surfaces and form biofilms, play a pivotal role in dental caries. These biofilms, by producing acids that suppress competing species, thrive in acidic environments and evade host-defined mechanisms, significantly contribute to the progression of the disease [3]. A dry mouth, caused by medical conditions or medications, reduces the protective role of saliva. Frequent snacking, genetic predisposition, socioeconomic factors, and habits like smoking and alcohol use further increase the risk [4]. Infants are especially vulnerable due to prolonged bottle-feeding with sugary liquids. The clinical management of dental caries involves bacteria removal and restorations, but selective tissue elimination remains challenging with invasive methods. Pharmacological treatments face limitations like low drug bioavailability due to salivary dilution and enzymatic degradation [5]. Antibiotics, while addressing infections, can disrupt oral flora, leading to antibiotic resistance, inflammation, and oral thrush [6]. Additionally, medications like corticosteroids reduce saliva production, increasing the risk of caries and other oral complications. This highlights the need for innovative treatments like localized drug delivery systems (LDDS), which ensure targeted medication delivery and maintain an effective drug concentration at the site.

In this context, mucoadhesive microparticles are advanced drug-delivery systems that enhance localized and controlled drug release at mucosal surfaces [7]. These microparticles are particularly valuable in treating oral, nasal, gastrointestinal, and vaginal infections, as they enhance drug bioavailability while reducing systemic side effects [8]. By reducing the dosing frequency and mitigating the risk of biofilm formation and resistance, these strategies can enhance the efficacy of dental caries treatment, improve the patient experience, and alleviate the concerns associated with conventional therapeutic approaches [9]. Chen et al. developed biodegradable poly(propylene carbonate)-based fluoride strips for sustained fluoride release and anti-cariogenic activity. Fabricated via melt blending with varying fluoride concentrations (0–5 wt%), the strips released fluoride steadily over 24 days without an initial burst effect. The 5% fluoride strips showed excellent remineralization and antibacterial properties, demonstrating their potential for managing dental caries [10]. The natural plant polysaccharide pectin (PC) has garnered significant attention due to its inherent mucoadhesive, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties, which can contribute to mitigating the secondary complications associated with dental caries [11]. It enables prolonged retention at the target site, improving drug absorption and therapeutic efficacy. A key challenge in buccal drug delivery is the unpleasant taste, which affects patient adherence. While synthetic sweeteners like sodium saccharin and aspartame are commonly used, glycyrrhizin (GL), a natural alternative, offers a promising solution for taste masking and improved patient compliance [12]. It not only effectively masks the bitter taste of drugs but also possesses a multifaceted profile, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and salivary flow stimulatory properties. These additional attributes further underscore GL’s potential to contribute significantly to managing dental caries [13]. Vitamin-D3 (VD), a pivotal nutrient for dental health, is critical in preventing dental caries. Regulating the absorption of calcium and phosphorus ensures the maintenance of robust tooth enamel. Moreover, VD has been demonstrated to influence the immune response, potentially mitigating the risk of infections associated with dental caries [14]. Although dietary sources can provide a degree of VD, formulations meant for managing dental caries enriched with VD offer a convenient and targeted means of delivering this essential nutrient directly to the oral cavity. These formulations can ensure adequate VD levels and foster oral health [15]. CA plays a significant role in dental caries management due to its antibacterial, enamel-conditioning, and pH-regulating properties. It helps disrupt bacterial biofilms, reducing the adhesion and growth of cariogenic bacteria like S. mutans. Additionally, CA enhances the efficacy of fluoride treatments by promoting enamel remineralization and improving fluoride uptake [16]. However, its concentration must be carefully controlled, as excessive use can contribute to enamel erosion. When incorporated into dental care formulations, CA supports oral hygiene and helps prevent the progression of dental caries [17]. Additionally, bio-intervention strategies using encapsulated Lactobacillus have gained attention for restoring oral microbiome balance. Studies show that Lactobacillus strains can inhibit S. mutans growth, while probiotic treatments help reduce S. mutans levels in dental plaque and saliva, supporting oral health [18]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus (L. rhamnosus) is commonly isolated from fermented dairy (e.g., yogurt, cheese), the human gut, and vaginal microbiota, and is a well-researched probiotic, particularly strains GG, SD11, GR-1, and L8020. It inhibits pathogens like S. mutans (reducing caries in rats and children), P. gingivalis, T. forsythia, S. aureus, Salmonella spp., and E. coli via acid, bacteriocins, competitive exclusion, and immune modulation [19]. Meurman et al., pioneers in applying probiotics in dentistry, discovered that L. rhamnosus GG ATCC 53,103 could colonize the human oral cavity, notably preventing dental caries [20]. Furthermore, Lactobacillus casei (L. casei) is a gram-positive, facultative anaerobic bacterium commonly isolated from fermented dairy products, the human gut, and plant materials. It exhibits strong probiotic properties by enhancing gut health, modulating the immune system, and producing antimicrobial compounds like organic acids and bacteriocins that inhibit pathogens like S. aureus (including biofilms), Salmonella spp., E. coli (including enterohemorrhagic strains), and P. aeruginosa [21]. Its postbiotic metabolites reduce intestinal inflammation (e.g., in PI‑IBS) by modulating cytokines and enhancing barrier integrity. Clinical trials show L. casei Shirota lowered gingival inflammation and bleeding scores in adults, and short‑term intake in children had cariostatic benefits by raising biofilm pH [22]. Its anti-inflammatory action via cytokine modulation and barrier improvement is supported by both oral and systemic studies [23]. These traits make L. casei a robust probiotic for dental caries and inflammation management.

Given the established advantages of L. rhamnosus, L. casei, VD, GL, CA, and PC, the present study seeks to amplify the potential benefits of these adjunct materials, including VD, CA, and PC, by utilizing mucoadhesive LDDS. Furthermore, the objectives extend to investigate the antimicrobial efficacy of L. rhamnosus and L. casei against potential oral pathogens, such as S. mutans and S. aureus, employing the zone of inhibition (ZOI) and microtiter plate assays. The safety and anti-inflammatory potential of the proposed carrier system were also evaluated using an in vitro cell line model. Hence, this platform will address bioactive compounds’ localized drug delivery and improve dental carry-managing drugs’ therapeutic efficacy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pectin and CA were obtained from Sisco Research Laboratory Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India. GL was procured from Tokyo Chemical Industries CO. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan, with a purity of > 75%. L. rhamnosus, L. casei, and S. mutans were collected from the Microbial Type Culture Collection and Gene Bank (MTCC), Chandigarh, India. S. aureus was collected from the Institute of Dental Sciences, Odisha, India. RAW 264.7 cell lines were procured from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCSS), Pune, India. All additional chemicals supplied were of analytical grade and used as received. All the investigations were conducted using the TRITON 6 ultra-pure water purification device during this research.

Experimental animals

All the animal experiments were conducted at the “Laboratory animal facility of the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Siksha ‘O’ Anusandhan, deemed to be University, Odisha, India. The research adhered to the latest CPCSEA India guidelines and the EU Directive 2010/63/EU on animal research. The study protocol identified as IAEC/ SPS/SOA/164/2024, received prior approval from the CPCSEA through the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee before the commencement of the research activities. 30 Wistar rats weighing 230-260 g were housed individually in a temperature-controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C) with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided unrestricted water and food access. For in vivo assays, the animals were then divided into five groups, i.e., group 1 (control), group 2 (retention time study), group 3 (antimicrobial activity against S. aureus), group 4 (antimicrobial activity against S. mutans), and group 5 (carrageenan-induced anti-inflammatory activity). Each group contained six animals to obtain reliable, ethically sound, and statistically valid results for our study. Before the experiments, the animals were acclimatized under these conditions to minimize stress and variability in results.

Synthesis of microparticles

Before proceeding with the formulation of microparticles, a preliminary investigation was conducted to evaluate the compatibility and influence of selected excipients, PC, VD, CA, and GL, on the growth behaviour of L. rhamnosus and L. casei. Each excipient was tested individually and in various combinations to assess its impact on probiotic viability. The growth behavior of the probiotic strains was assessed using the McFarland turbidity standard method. Ionic gelation was employed to encapsulate L. rhamnosus, L. casei, GL, CA, and VD within PC microparticles. The PC solution was prepared by dissolving 200 mg of PC in 10 ml of distilled water and stirring magnetically at 200 rpm. It was then subsequently fortified with the maintained bioactive compounds. The mixture was then vigorously stirred at 500 rpm and ambient temperature for 2 h. A 10% w/v CaCl2 crosslinking solution was prepared by dissolving 5 g of CaCl2 in 50 ml of distilled water. The PC solution was then added dropwise to the crosslinking solution, followed by mechanical stirring. The resulting microparticles were recovered through centrifugation, dispersed in a 0.5 M Tween® 80 solution, and filtered.

To ensure the complete removal of any unencapsulated drug or excess CaCl2, the microparticles were subjected to repeated washing with deionized water, followed by centrifugation to remove residual water after each wash. Subsequently, the collected microparticles underwent a two-stage freeze-drying process: initial freezing at -80 °C for 3 h, followed by lyophilization at -40 °C and a pressure of 0.3–0.5 mbar for 18 h using a freeze dryer (BIOBASE, India). Finally, the lyophilized microparticles were stored in a closed container within a desiccator to prevent moisture absorption before further analysis [24].

Characterisation of microparticles

SEM analysis

The surface morphological properties (shape and surface) of the microparticles were studied by SEM (Model EVO-15, ZEISS SEM, Germany) at a working distance of 7.7 mm, accelerating voltage (ETH) of 5 kV, and mag = 4.04 K X [25]. The optimized L. rhamnosus, L. casei, GL, VD, and CA-loaded PC microparticles were mounted on the sample holder and coated with a thin layer of gold. SEM images were captured at different magnification levels to understand the microparticle structure comprehensively.

XRD analysis

XRD is an essential analytical technique for determining the physical state of drugs in prepared microparticle formulations [26]. The physical state of a drug, whether crystalline or amorphous, can significantly influence its stability and other associated factors. To investigate the structural properties of L. rhamnosus, L. casei, GL, VD, and CA-loaded PC microparticles, XRD analysis was performed using an XRD instrument within a 2θ range of 5 to 80 degrees.

DSC analysis

DSC study was used to verify the thermal stability of the free drugs L. rhamnosus, L. casei, GL, VD, CA, and their respective PC-loaded microparticle formulations. DSC analysis was performed using a DSC-60 instrument (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Approximately 5 to 10 mg of each sample was encapsulated in a separate aluminium pan and heated at a rate of 10 °C/min. The temperature range for the heating process was 25–300 °C with a nitrogen flow rate of 30 mL/min.

FT-IR analysis

Potential drug-polymer interactions in the developed micro-formulations were investigated with FT-IR. FT-IR spectra were acquired in the 500–4000 cm− 1 range to characterize the chemical composition and interactions within the PC microparticles. Approximately 10 mg powdered samples (PC, L. rhamnosus, L. casei, GL, VD, CA, and microparticles) were loaded on the metal structure of the FT-IR spectrophotometer (Jasco FT/IR 6X, Jasco, Inc.) and then allowed for FTIR readings.

Encapsulation efficiency

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) of GL in dried pectin microparticles was determined using UV–vis spectrophotometry at 254 nm. A standard calibration curve was prepared, and 10 mg of microparticles were dissolved in 20 mL of water. EE was calculated using the ratio of extracted to initially loaded GL.

Furthermore, the encapsulation efficiency of probiotics, L. rhamnosus and L. casei, was evaluated by quantifying the number of viable cells retained within the microparticles in comparison to the initial cell count in the feed suspension. To determine EE%, 10 mg of the microparticle formulation was suspended in 20 mL of sterile peptone water under aseptic conditions to ensure uniform dispersion. The resulting suspension was subjected to serial dilutions, and viable cell counts were determined using the spread plate method on MRS agar. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to allow for optimal growth of the probiotic strains. Viability was expressed as colony-forming units per gram (CFU/g) of microparticles. The encapsulation efficiency was then calculated using the ratio of viable encapsulated cells to the total viable cells in the initial feed dispersion, following the standard formula provided in the methods section.

In vitro drug release assay

An in vitro GL release study was conducted using the everted sac technique. Approximately 5 mg of the prepared microparticles were loaded onto a dialysis membrane, submerged in 500 mL phosphate buffer solution (PBS) (pH 7.4), and stirred at 250 rpm at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals (15, 30, 60, 120, 180, 240, 300, and 360 min), 3 mL of samples were withdrawn while maintaining sink conditions. The amount of released GL was quantified using UV-spectroscopy at a maximum wavelength of 254 nm. The experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and accuracy of the results.

Mucoadhesive strength analysis

The mucoadhesive strength for the developed microparticles was assessed using a Brookfield texture analyzer. Goat mucosa was affixed to the device’s attachments, and the microparticles were adhered to the opposite attachment. The detachment force required for the microparticles to separate from the mucosa was then measured [27].

Flow property assay

The optimized pectin microparticles loaded with L. rhamnosus, L. casei, glycyrrhizin (GL), vitamin D (VD), and citric acid (CA) were evaluated for flow properties using angle of repose (AOR), bulk density (BD), tapped density (TD), Hausner’s ratio (HR), and Carr’s index (CI). AOR was determined by measuring the height and radius of a powder cone and applying the formula θ = tan⁻¹(h/r). BD and TD were obtained by measuring the sample’s bulk and tapped volumes, respectively, with HR calculated as TD/BD and CI

According to standard criteria, the microparticles exhibited AOR values of 26–30° (good flow), HR values of 1.16–1.22 (good to fair flow), and CI values of 16–20% (fair flow). The close BD (0.57 g/cm³) and TD (0.69 g/cm³) values indicated dense particles with minimal air gaps, contributing to their good overall flowability and suitability for further pharmaceutical processing [28].

In vitro assays

Preparation of the supernatants from the culture of probiotic bacteria

L. rhamnosus and L. casei cultures (106, 108, and 1010 CFU/ml) were initially thawed from frozen stock and inoculated into 5 ml of MRS broth. The cultures were then incubated at 37 °C overnight under shaking conditions. Following incubation, the cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh MRS broth and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Further, the separation of the probiotic bacteria cells from the resulting postbiotics was performed as previously reported by Jalali et al., with minor modifications [29]. In brief, the cultures were subjected to sonication and centrifugation at 6000×g at 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatants (postbiotics) were sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm pore filter. Then, the postbiotics were subjected to lyophilization using a freeze-drier (BIOBASE, India), divided into aliquots, and stored at -20 °C for future use in various assays.

To assess the formulation’s efficacy, in vitro experiments were conducted using four distinct groups: a standard drug group, a group containing the postbiotics alone, a vehicle control (VC) group with the other excipients excluding the postbiotics, and a group treated with the mixture of both VC and the postbiotics at a 20:80 ratio, which is referred to as the formulation. The standard drug group served as a benchmark for comparison, while the other groups allowed for evaluation of the formulation’s specific effects.

Anti-microbial activity

S. aureus and S. mutans are among the primary causative organisms of dental caries. Hence, they were used as model microorganisms to test the antimicrobial efficiency of the developed formulation.

Agar well diffusion assay

The agar-well diffusion method has been performed using a nutrient medium [30]. The agar well diffusion assay was performed as reported by et al., with slight modifications [31]. In brief, MHA plates cultured with S. aureus and S. mutans bacterial cells equivalent to 1 × 106 CFU/ml were spread on the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Further, 30 mg of the L. rhamnosus and L. casei postbiotics powders, collected from L. rhamnosus and L. casei at different concentrations, were weighed and mixed with 5 ml of distilled water to obtain an initial 6 mg/ml concentration. Then 15 µl of determined concentrations of standard drug (doxycycline; 100 µg/ml), L. rhamnosus and L. casei postbiotics (106, 108, and 1010 CFU/ml), VC (6 mg/ml) and formulation (6 mg/ml) for each postbiotic were placed on the diffusion well. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, and the ZOI was measured.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MICs of all four test groups (standard drug; doxycycline, L. rhamnosus, and L. casei postbiotics; 108 CFU/ml, VC, and formulation) were determined against both S. aureus and S. mutans using the broth dilution method in a 96-well microplate as outlined by Teh et al., with minor modifications [32]. Briefly, 20µL of each test group and 100µL of bacterial suspension (106 CFU/mL) were added to wells containing 160µL of MHB. The microplate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 20µL of 0.015% resazurin dye was added to each well, and the microplate was incubated for 2–4 h to observe any colour change. Wells with no colour change (remaining blue) were considered to have reached the MIC, indicating the minimum concentration of the test substance to inhibit bacterial growth.

Bacterial morphology by SEM

The impact of our formulation on the morphology of S. aureus and S. mutans was investigated using SEM. Initially, bacterial cultures were grown to log phase in the LB medium and then divided into control and test groups. The control and test groups received doxycycline and formulation at the MBC dose and were then incubated. After incubation, both groups were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and fixed with glutaraldehyde. After gradual dehydration with ethanol, the control and test groups were subjected to SEM (Model EVO-15, ZEISS SEM, Germany) at a working distance of 7.6 mm, accelerating voltage (ETH) of 5 kV, and different magnifications between 15 and 44 K X [34].

Anti-inflammatory activity

RAW 264.7 macrophage cells were used to assess the anti-inflammatory properties of the formulated microparticles. A comparative analysis was conducted between the L. rhamnosus and L. casei postbiotics (108 CFU/ml) and the microparticles. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% Pen-strep and maintained in a 5% CO2 environment at 37 °C. All the treatments were thoroughly mixed in RPMI 1640 medium. Four experimental groups were established: a negative control group, a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated group, a L. rhamnosus and L. casei postbiotics-treated group, and a microparticle-treated group. LPS was added to both the LPS-treated and the test groups, while the negative control group received only RPMI 1640 medium. ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions to quantify TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in cell culture supernatants. Antigen-antibody reactions were carried out per the ELISA kit protocol, followed by adding 50 µL of stop buffer to each well. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to determine cytokine levels [36].

Antioxidant activity using 1,2-Diphenyl-1-Picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay

A DPPH radical scavenging assay was performed according to Valko et al., with slight modifications for evaluating the formulations’ antioxidant activity [37]. 1 mM of DPPH was prepared by dissolving it in methanol. The study was performed in strict accordance with the latest guidelines of the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA), Government of India, as well as the EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. In vivo retention activity

To investigate the in vivo retention time of our prepared microparticles, rats in group 2 were fasted overnight with free access to water before the experiment. The rats were then anaesthetized by directly injecting a combination of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg) intraperitoneally. A test formulation incorporating 0.50% BaSO4 as a contrast agent was applied to the dental cavity. The retention time of the formulation within the cavity was subsequently evaluated using X-ray imaging at 2 h intervals (2 h, 4 h, and 6 h). This assessment aimed to determine the duration of the formulation’s efficacy within the dental environment [38].

In vivo antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial efficacy of the formulated microparticles was investigated, as outlined by Tong et al., by considering slight modifications [39]. Briefly, the rats in groups 3 and 4 were initially anaesthetised using a combination of ketamine and xylazine in the dose. Bacterial suspensions of S. aureus and S. mutans were prepared in PBS at a 106 CFU/mL concentration. Rats in group 3 were directly injected with a 50 µL bacterial suspension of S. aureus. Subsequently, swab samples were collected from the infection site after 24 h and inoculated onto Mannitol Salt Broth to confirm the presence and quantify the bacterial load of S. aureus. The formulated microparticles containing the postbiotics of 108 CFU/ml of L. rhamnosus and L. casei were then topically applied to the infected site, followed by taking swabs after 6 h, inoculating them at 37 °C for 24 h, and then taking OD at 600 nm to assess their antimicrobial activity. A similar procedure was followed for rats in group 4, except for inoculating swab samples onto Mitis Salivarius Broth (MSB) agar to detect S. mutans. In vivo anti-inflammatory activity

To investigate the anti-inflammatory activity, rats in group 5 were anesthetized using a combination of ketamine and xylazine in a dose. Inflammation was then induced by injecting 50 µL of a 1% carrageenan solution into the dental cavity. The prepared microparticles were then applied topically to the site of inflammation, and the rats were monitored for signs of inflammation, such as swelling and redness, at various time points (2, 4, and 6 h). Further, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) and IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, were investigated after 6 h, as per the reported protocol [40].

Histopathology

The dental pulp specimens from each rat were selected for histopathological examination. All the samples were collected under sterile conditions and fixed in 10% formalin to preserve their structure. The samples were then dehydrated using ethanol and embedded in paraffin. A microtome was used to cut thin sections from the paraffin-embedded tissue block, which were subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological examination [41].

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were statistically analysed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 for Windows. Group variances were compared using a one-way variance analysis (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at p = 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), n = 3 replicates.

Results and discussion

Characterization of microparticles

The preliminary trials revealed differential effects of the tested excipients and their combinations on the growth of L. rhamnosus and L. casei. Spectrophotometric analysis at 625 nm indicated that PC and CA supported the growth of both strains effectively, whereas VD showed a neutral to slightly positive influence on growth, particularly in combination with pectin. Overall, formulations containing PC, GL, VD and CA synergistic combinations supported optimal growth behaviour, thereby confirming the selection of excipient combinations for the preparation of microparticles aimed at preserving probiotic viability. Detailed results of the probiotic growth patterns in response to individual excipients and their combinations are provided in the Supplementary file.

SEM analysis

SEM analysis (Fig. 1A) showed spherical microparticles with a rough, porous surface, likely resulting from ionic interactions between the polymer and cross-linker. The observed porosity suggests a high surface-to-volume ratio, potentially enhancing retention time at the target site. Despite surface irregularities, the particles retained structural integrity, indicating good mechanical stability.

(B) XRD diffractograms of the microparticles and pure ingredients, (C) DSC thermograms of the microparticles and pure ingredients, and (D) FTIR spectra of the microparticles and pure ingredients

XRD analysis

XRD analysis (Fig. 1B) showed that pectin exhibited a broad peak characteristic of an amorphous structure, while glycyrrhizin, vitamin D, and citric acid displayed sharp peaks indicative of crystallinity. L. rhamnosus and L. casei showed predominantly broad peaks, suggesting amorphous nature. The final formulation incorporated features from all components, with both sharp peaks and broad humps, indicating the presence of crystalline and amorphous regions. The absence of new peaks or significant shifts confirms that no new crystalline phases formed during formulation.

DSC analysis

DSC thermograms (Fig. 1C) showed distinct thermal profiles for each component. Pectin displayed a broad endothermic event near 100 °C, indicating moisture loss. Vitamin D exhibited a sharp melting peak at ∼ 115 °C, while glycyrrhizin melted at ∼ 200 °C, confirming its crystalline nature. Citric acid showed a melting/decomposition peak between 150 and 200 °C. L. rhamnosus and L. casei both exhibited broad dehydration peaks (50–160 °C) followed by gradual degradation, reflecting amorphous, heat-sensitive structures. The formulation displayed broad endothermic peaks (∼ 70 °C and ∼ 100 °C) without distinct crystalline peaks, indicating a predominantly amorphous matrix from component interactions. The DSC results indicate that the crystalline ingredients (Vitamin D, glycyrrhizin, citric acid) lost their distinct melting transitions in the formulation, suggesting transformation into a more amorphous state. This amorphization is desirable as it can enhance solubility and bioavailability. The broad dehydration peaks from probiotic strains highlight their moisture sensitivity, aligning with the need for careful thermal handling during processing.

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR spectra (Fig. 1D) confirmed the presence of characteristic functional groups. Pectin showed broad O–H (3200–3500 cm⁻¹), ester C = O (∼ 1730 cm⁻¹), and polysaccharide C–O–C (∼ 1000–1200 cm⁻¹) bands. Vitamin D displayed C–H (∼ 2900 cm⁻¹), C = O (∼ 1650 cm⁻¹), and C = C (∼ 1600 cm⁻¹) peaks. Glycyrrhizin showed O–H (∼ 2976 cm⁻¹), –COOH (∼ 1654 cm⁻¹), and C–O (∼ 1042 cm⁻¹) vibrations. Citric acid exhibited O–H, strong C = O (∼ 1720 cm⁻¹), and COO⁻ (∼ 1600 and ∼ 1400 cm⁻¹) bands. Both L. rhamnosus and L. casei showed broad O–H/N–H stretches, amide I (∼ 1650 cm⁻¹), amide II (∼ 1550 cm⁻¹), and carbohydrate peaks. The formulation combined all key peaks, confirming successful component incorporation and preserving functional groups. FT-IR analysis supports the DSC findings, showing preserved functional groups from each component and potential hydrogen bonding or other molecular interactions within the formulation. Together, the thermal and spectroscopic data confirm successful integration of all components into a stable, predominantly amorphous matrix with maintained chemical integrity, which is advantageous for product stability and performance.

Encapsulation efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of glycyrrhizin, L. casei, and L. rhamnosus in pectin microparticles was 85.74 ± 1.7%, 82.63 ± 2.5%, and 84.44 ± 1.7%, respectively, indicating high entrapment for all components. This efficiency is attributed to the optimized ionotropic gelation process, where pectin’s anionic chains cross-linked with Ca²⁺ ions to form a stable 3D network capable of retaining both bioactive compounds and viable microorganisms. Controlled formulation parameters, including pectin and CaCl₂ concentrations and gentle stirring, minimized structural disruption, preserved cell viability, and prevented diffusion losses during particle hardening. The mild processing conditions protected probiotic cells from shear, osmotic, and thermal stress, while strong electrostatic interactions stabilized them within the gel matrix. These findings highlight the pectin microparticle system as a robust, biocompatible platform for co-delivering probiotics and phytochemicals, supporting stability, controlled release, and potential synergistic therapeutic effects in targeted mucosal delivery.

Drug release

As shown in Fig. 2, the formulation exhibited a markedly faster and higher cumulative drug release compared to plain glycyrrhizin. The formulation reached ∼ 40% release within 60 min and ∼ 69% by 180 min, eventually achieving ∼ 85% release at 360 min. In contrast, plain glycyrrhizin released only ∼ 40% over the same period. The enhanced release from the formulation suggests improved solubility and diffusion through the pectin matrix, enabling sustained drug availability without an excessive initial burst. This profile supports its potential for prolonged therapeutic action.

Mucoadhesive strength

Mucoadhesion plays a crucial role in prolonging drug residence time at the target site, thereby enhancing drug delivery. The optimized microparticles demonstrated robust mucoadhesive properties, exhibiting a detachment force of 96 g/cm2, indicating effective retention at the buccal mucosa.

Flow property

The prepared microparticles showed favorable flow characteristics, with an angle of repose (26–30°) indicating good flowability, Hausner’s ratio (1.16–1.22) reflecting good to fair flow, and Carr’s index (16–20%) suggesting fair flow. The bulk density (0.57 g/cm³) and tapped density (0.69 g/cm³) values were close, indicating dense particles with minimal air entrapment, which supported their good flow despite the fair CI. Overall, the microparticles demonstrated good to excellent flow properties, ensuring suitability for further processing and formulation into solid dosage forms.

In vitro antimicrobial potential

Measurement of ZOI

The antimicrobial assay revealed that doxycycline produced the highest zones of inhibition against both S. aureus (27.5 ± 1.22 mm) and S. mutans (25.8 ± 0.23 mm). Among the probiotic treatments, L. rhamnosus and L. casei metabolites at 10⁸ CFU/ml showed the most effective inhibition (15.8 ± 1.43 mm for S. aureus and 15.5 ± 0.32 mm for S. mutans), outperforming both 10⁶ CFU/ml and 10¹⁰ CFU/ml groups. Increasing bacterial density beyond 10⁸ CFU/ml led to reduced ZOI, likely due to excessive postbiotic accumulation, which may compromise probiotic viability and antimicrobial activity. The combined formulation of L. rhamnosus and L. casei metabolites (10⁸ CFU/ml) with vitamin C produced superior inhibition (23.7 ± 0.5 mm for S. aureus and 23.6 ± 1.79 mm for S. mutans), approaching doxycycline’s efficacy. The antimicrobial effect is likely due to postbiotics such as bacteriocins, which disrupt cell membranes, and organic acids (butyric, acetic, lactic, propionic) that alter membrane potential and pH, along with the intrinsic activity of glycyrrhizin, contributing to synergistic effects. Based on these results, subsequent experiments used the 10⁸ CFU/ml metabolite concentrations for optimal efficacy (Table 1; Fig. 3). Spacova et al. have outlined the ability of L. rhamnosus to inhibit the infection of human keratinocytes by S. aureus. The findings suggest that all the L. rhamnosus strains significantly inhibited the growth of S. aureus, which is likely attributed to the acidification of the culture medium due to lactic acid production [42]. Further, a significant reduction in the S. aureus adhesion and internalization into bovine mammary epithelial cells (bMEC), with L. casei CIRM-BIA 667 reducing internalization by 60–80%. This protective effect occurred without harming bMEC morphology or viability. Heat-killed L. casei maintained anti-adhesion activity, while live strains were necessary to block internalization. These findings highlight L. casei’s potential as an antimicrobial agent against S. aureus in bovine mastitis control [43]. L. rhamnosus and L. casei have also been found to produce inhibitory effects toward S. Typhi, S. flexneri, and P. aeruginosa [44].

The effectiveness of the resazurin assay was validated using the synthesized formulation, L. rhamnosus and L. casei, VC, CA, and the antibiotic control doxycycline. For S. mutans, the MBC values were 3000 µg/ml for formulation, 6000 µg/ml for VC, and 25 µg/ml for doxycycline (Fig. 4B and C).

Bacterial morphology using SEM

The bacterial morphology revealed distinct morphological differences among the three groups of S. aureus and S. mutans (Fig. 5). In the untreated control group, the bacteria maintained their typical morphology, characterized by intact and smooth cell surfaces (Fig. 5A and D). Significant cell wall rupture was observed in the test group treated with doxycycline, resulting in cell lysis and a loss of structural integrity, indicating the bactericidal effect of doxycycline (Fig. 5B and E). The test group treated with the formulation at its MBC resulted in a notable disruption in cell wall integrity. The bacterial cells exhibited rough and irregular surfaces with visible cell wall damage and deformation. This indicates that the formulation significantly disrupted the integrity of the bacterial cell wall (Fig. 5C and F). The observed morphological changes could be attributed to the postbiotics of L. rhamnosus + L. casei, including bacteriocin, which can form pores in the cell membrane, and organic acids like butyric acid, acetic acid, lactic acid, and propionic acid, which disrupt cell membrane potential and pH balance. These findings confirm that the formulation has a potent antimicrobial effect, significantly altering the morphology of S. aureus and S. mutans, indicating its effectiveness in disrupting bacterial cell walls.

Anti-inflammatory potential

The anti-inflammatory potential of the microparticle containing L. rhamnosus, L. casei (10⁸ CFU/mL), along with excipients such as CA, VD, and GL, was evaluated using the LPS-induced inflammation model in RAW 264.7 cell lines. Microparticle and LR + LC (10⁸ CFU/mL) at their MBC resulted in a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels compared to the LPS-treated group. Concurrently, a notable increase in IL-10 expression was observed, suggesting an immunomodulatory shift towards an anti-inflammatory profile (Fig. 6C–F). The enhanced anti-inflammatory effect of the formulation may be attributed to the synergistic action of the probiotic-derived postbiotics and the incorporated bioactives (VC, VD, and GL). These components exert anti-inflammatory effects by scavenging ROS, modulating cytokines, and suppressing pathways such as NF-κB, COX-2, iNOS, and MAPK. Although not directly assessed in this study, previous evidence suggests these mechanisms likely contributed to the observed cytokine modulation. L. rhamnosus and L. casei have also been found to exhibit anti-inflammatory effects by enhancing IL-10 and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines through modulation of NF-κB and MAPK signalling pathways [45]. An investigation carried out by Haro et al. 2009 reported alleviation in the level of serum TNF-α and IL-1β by day 5 post-infection in a mice model with pneumococcal pneumonia treated with the postbiotic extracted from L. casei [46]. A study by Jeong et al. showed that CA-treated wheat germ extract (CWG) exhibited stronger anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the secretion of major pro-inflammatory cytokines and cyclooxygenase-2 synthesis, suggesting the utility of CA treatment in enhancing the anti-inflammatory properties of wheat germ extract [47]. Taken together, our findings suggest that the microparticulate formulation exerts a potent anti-inflammatory effect by attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhancing anti-inflammatory responses.

Lactobacilli spp. are well-recognized sources of antioxidants. Recent studies confirm the strong antioxidant capacity of Lactobacillus rhamnosus cell-free supernatants (CFS), highlighting their potential in food and cosmetic applications. A study demonstrated that purple sweet potato juice fermented using L. rhamnosus significantly increased total phenolic and flavonoid contents, enhancing DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radical scavenging activities [48]. Tsai et al. reported that L. rhamnosus spent culture supernatant exhibited strong, dose-dependent DPPH (71%) and ABTS (82%) free radical scavenging abilities, with high thermal stability, indicating its suitability for cosmetic use. These findings collectively support the role of L. rhamnosus CFS as a potent antioxidants with broad applications [49]. Additionally, Zhang et al. reported that L. rhamnosus B2‑1, when fermenting whey protein, releases three novel antioxidant peptides (PKYPVEPF, LEASPEVI, YPFPGPIHNS) that not only scavenge radicals in vitro but also protect HepG2 cells from H₂O₂‑induced oxidative damage by boosting catalase and superoxide dismutase activity and reducing ROS via Nrf2–Keap1 interaction [50]. Xu et al. report that L. casei ATCC 393-based synthesis of biogenic selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) significantly reduces oxidative stress in intestinal epithelial cells. These SeNPs lower malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and enhance glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity, effectively protecting cells from oxidative damage caused by diquat or hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), suggesting their potential use as protective agents against oxidative damage [51]. As per the Li et al. report, L. casei L4 fermented coconut water shows 58–69% free radical‑scavenging due to raised total phenolic content from ∼ 18 to ∼ 72 µg GAE/mL, indicating phenolic enrichment during fermentation [52]. In vitro, biosurfactants from L. casei showed 75% DPPH scavenging at 5 mg/mL concentrations, suggesting direct radical‑neutralizing effects [53]. Li et al. demonstrated that co-fermentation of apple juice with L. plantarum and L. casei significantly enhanced in vitro antioxidant capacity, with marked increases in total phenolic content and robust DPPH/ABTS radical-scavenging activity, suggesting a synergistic mechanism involving phenolic compound biotransformation and release [54]. Collectively, these findings highlight that L. casei exerts antioxidant effects through multiple mechanisms, supporting its potential in drug delivery and therapeutic applications.

In vivo assays

In vivo retention effect

The developed microparticles exhibited strong mucoadhesive properties, enhancing drug retention at the mucosal site. In vivo X-ray imaging (Fig. 7) confirmed prolonged retention in the dental cavity. After 2 h of administration (Fig. 7B), retention was notably higher than before treatment (Fig. 7A), indicating effective adhesion. This effect is attributed to pectin (PC), a natural polymer capable of forming gels and adhering to mucosal surfaces. By 4 h (Fig. 7C), retention slightly decreased, and by 6 h (Fig. 7D), it was markedly reduced, reflecting the gradual loss of adhesion typical in oral environments. These results highlight the role of PC in sustaining drug contact time and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. The extended retention time is particularly advantageous for localized oral delivery, as it may reduce dosing frequency and improve patient compliance. Furthermore, prolonged contact can enhance the bioavailability of probiotics and bioactive compounds, maximizing therapeutic outcomes in dental caries management.

In vivo anti-inflammatory activity

The in vivo anti-inflammatory activity of the prepared microparticles was evaluated by inducing inflammation and measuring IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10 levels using ELISA (Fig. 8B–E). These cytokines, elevated in carious lesions, drive pulpitis, dentin degradation, and irreversible pulp damage. After 2 h of treatment, rats showed a slight reduction in swelling and redness, which became moderate at 4 h and markedly reduced by 6 h compared to controls. ELISA results at 6 h confirmed significant decreases in IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, along with increased IL-10. This anti-inflammatory effect is attributed to GL and VD in the formulation. The mucoadhesive microparticles modulate local immune responses, limit cytokine-mediated tissue destruction, and support pulp vitality and dentin regeneration. By combining antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory actions, the microsystem offers a comprehensive therapeutic approach for caries management.

Histopathology

The dental pulp specimens were collected and subjected to histopathological examination. Results entail that the control group did not contain any inflammatory infiltrates, indicating standard tissue structure. In contrast, the diseased group showed maximum inflammatory infiltrates, signifying severe inflammation and tissue damage. The treated group exhibited a significant reduction in inflammatory infiltrates compared to the diseased group, suggesting the treatment effectively reduced inflammation and promoted tissue recovery (Fig. 9A–F). Inflammatory infiltrates refer to the accumulation of immune cells, such as neutrophils, lymphocytes, and macrophages, within the tissue, a hallmark of the body’s response to infection or injury.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the potential of mucoadhesive microparticles incorporating probiotic postbiotics (L. rhamnosus and L. casei), GL, VD, and CA for localized drug delivery in dental caries management. The synthesized microparticles exhibited excellent mucoadhesive properties, sustained drug release, and significant antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities, making them a promising alternative to conventional treatments. The formulation effectively inhibited cariogenic bacteria, S. aureus, and S. mutans, as evidenced by the zone of inhibition, MIC/MBC, and SEM analyses, which confirmed bacterial structural disruption. Additionally, the formulation displayed strong anti-biofilm activity, preventing bacterial adherence and persistence in the oral cavity. The anti-inflammatory effects were validated by reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) and an increase in IL-10, demonstrating its potential to alleviate inflammation associated with dental infections. The antioxidant potential further supports its role in maintaining oral health by mitigating oxidative stress. The in vivo studies confirmed the formulation’s prolonged retention in the oral cavity, reinforcing its mucoadhesive capability. Compared to existing literature, this study uniquely integrates probiotics and bioactive compounds into a localized drug delivery system, offering dual benefits of antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory actions with prolonged retention and minimal systemic side effects. While previous studies focused primarily on individual bioactive components, our approach enhances therapeutic efficacy through synergistic interactions. However, the key limitations include the absence of long-term clinical trials needed to confirm safety and therapeutic efficacy in humans, as current evidence is largely short-term. Without extended follow-up, risks such as adverse effects, tolerance, or reduced effectiveness remain unclear. Another major concern is the lack of standardized dosage and administration regimens, which are essential to balance efficacy with minimal side effects. Patient compliance may also be affected by variability in individual responses influenced by age, genetics, and comorbidities. Hence, large-scale, long-duration studies are required before clinical translation. Future research should explore formulation stability, scalability, and patient-specific customization to bridge the gap between preclinical success and practical clinical applications. Ultimately, this study provides a foundation for innovative, targeted therapies in dental care, emphasizing the role of bioengineered drug delivery systems in improving oral health outcomes.
