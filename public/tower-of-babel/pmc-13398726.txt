Functional Imaging for Identification of Functionally Critical Brain Regions to Spare in Glioma Radiation Therapy Planning: A Systematic Review
Authors: Li Wen Yeo, John T Ryan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionBeyond improving target delineation, functional imaging holds significant promise in identifying critical functional brain regions. It enables a more personalised approach to radiation therapy (RT) planning, where organs‐at‐risk can be spared more effectively, potentially preserving neurocognitive function and quality of life in patients with glioma. This systematic review aims to identify the role of functional imaging in preserving neurocognitive function in patients with glioma undergoing RT.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in December 2024 according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Searches were performed in Scopus, MEDLINE and EMBASE using medical subject headings and keywords related to glioma, functional imaging, cognition and RT. Only English‐language publications were included. The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist was used to assess the validity and applicability of the studies included in this systematic review.ResultsFive studies published between 2019 and 2023 were included in the review. The studies included retrospective cohort studies (n = 2), a pilot study (n = 1), a cross‐sectional study (n = 1) and a theoretical study (n = 1). Quality assessment using the CASP checklist indicated moderate methodological quality, with limitations including small sample sizes, predominantly retrospective designs and limited prospective validation of neurocognitive outcomes. Functional imaging was shown to reduce the dose to cognitive areas and potentially protect important cognitive functions.ConclusionFunctional imaging identifies critical cognitive regions in the brain and guides radiation dose to avoid these vulnerable regions while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. Integrating functional imaging into treatment planning allows for neuroprotective strategies in RT planning and potentially minimises the risk of cognitive impairment. This review sets the stage for future research to expand the benefit of functional imaging in glioma RT planning.Keywords: cognitive function, functional imaging, glioma, neurocognition, radiation therapy

Introduction

Globally, over 250,000 new cases of primary malignant brain tumours are diagnosed each year, of which approximately 77% are gliomas [1]. Despite improvements in overall survival (OS) among patients with glioma over the past decade, neurocognitive decline remains a major challenge to the overall effectiveness of treatment. Radiation therapy (RT), a cornerstone in glioma management, is frequently associated with declines in cognitive function, significantly impacting patients' well‐being and quality of life (QOL). Neurocognitive impairment represents a significant long‐term complication of treatment, particularly for patients with glioma who often maintain relatively good functional status, have localised disease, and may experience prolonged survival [2]. As a result, this patient population is especially vulnerable to the cumulative impact of treatment‐related cognitive decline. Consequently, patients with glioma represent an important group in whom the implementation of neuroprotective strategies during RT planning may provide meaningful benefits in preserving cognitive function and overall QOL.

Cognitive dysfunction is typically characterised by deficits in one or more domains, including executive function, memory, attention, language, and perceptual‐motor skills [3]. In addition to cognitive impairments, mood disturbances and anxiety are commonly reported neurological symptoms that further diminish QOL [4]. A meta‐analysis by Morshed et al. found that the majority of patients with glioma experience impairments in at least one cognitive domain [3]. Moreover, De Roeck et al. [5] observed a significant decline in cognitive performance 1 year after treatment, compared to the general population.

As therapeutic strategies continue to extend survival, cognitive impairment is emerging as a significant medical and social concern, particularly concerning patients' autonomy and long‐term QOL [6]. This has led to a growing emphasis on maximising patient survival and local tumour control, all while prioritising the preservation of neurocognitive function and aligning treatment goals with patients' long‐term functional well‐being. Functional imaging has evolved significantly from its traditional roles in diagnostic imaging and clinical staging to becoming increasingly relevant in RT planning. These techniques provide valuable physiological, metabolic and functional information regarding tumour biology, treatment response, and the functional status of surrounding organs [6]. Such information can be leveraged to optimise RT strategies, with the potential to improve tumour control while reducing radiation‐induced toxicity to normal tissues.

The integration of quantitative, function‐based imaging has expanded the application of modalities such as fluoroethyl‐L‐tyrosine positron emission tomography (FET‐PET) and magnetic resonance spectroscopy imaging (MRSI). These functional modalities can define the extent of infiltrating tumour that may not be visible on conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), thereby improving target delineation during RT planning [6, 7]. Beyond tumour characterisation, functional imaging also offers the opportunity to map functionally important brain regions and networks that are critical for maintaining cognitive function.

As survival outcomes improve for patients with glioma, preserving neurocognitive function has become an increasingly important consideration in RT planning [6]. This has led to growing interest in identifying which healthy brain regions are most critical to spare during RT in order to minimise treatment‐related cognitive impairment [8]. Functional imaging plays an important role in this process by enabling the identification of cognitive networks and eloquent brain regions that may be vulnerable to radiation‐induced injury. By incorporating this information into treatment planning, functional imaging may support the development of more personalised neuroprotective strategies that prioritise the sparing of critical cognitive structures.

Therefore, this systematic review aims to evaluate the role of functional imaging in RT planning for patients with glioma, with a particular focus on its potential to identify critical brain regions for dose sparing and support the preservation of cognitive function.

Search Strategy

This systematic review was conducted in December 2024 based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10]. A systematic literature search was conducted in three databases: Scopus, MEDLINE and EMBASE as they were able to adequately capture the majority of peer‐reviewed studies within this emerging field. A search strategy was developed based on search terms describing RT, patients with glioma, functional imaging and neurocognition. A combination of medical subject headings and keywords was used including glioma, functional imaging, neurocogniti*, cogniti*, radiation therapy and radiotherapy. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were employed to combine these terms. The search terms were matched against the titles, abstracts, and indexed keywords of published articles to identify relevant studies. A protocol for the systematic literature searches is registered with the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO CRD42024603053).

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were chosen in accordance with PRISMA guidelines for this systematic review. Studies investigating intervention using functional imaging and studies reporting on neurocognitive outcomes were included, without restrictions on time or location. This approach ensures that all potentially relevant studies, regardless of publication date or place, are considered to maximise the breadth and depth of the evidence base, providing a more thorough and nuanced analysis. Only English‐language publications were included for clarity. Studies dealing solely with prevalence, incidence, or recurrence rates of glioma were excluded since no data on preservation of neurocognition could be extracted. Studies on brain metastasis were also excluded. Covidence [11] was used to manage publications and remove duplicates.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

After an initial screening of titles and abstracts, the shortlisted full‐text articles were reviewed in depth to confirm their eligibility for inclusion in the data extraction table. The relevant data were extracted from all papers that met the eligibility and inclusion criteria for the review. The following data were extracted and analysed: author and year of publication, title, aim, design of study, target population, sample size, methods, key findings and conclusions from the study.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) systematic review checklist. The CASP checklist was used to assess the validity and applicability of the studies included in this review. The checklist consisted of 10 questions, each with response options: yes, can't tell and no. These studies were classified as good (more than 6 ‘yes’ scores), moderate (three to six ‘yes’ scores) or poor (less than three ‘yes’ scores). Studies graded as poor were excluded. Narrative synthesis was undertaken to summarise and report the findings.

The literature search yielded a total of 48 articles on 24 December 2024, with articles from Scopus (n = 33), MEDLINE (n = 13) and EMBASE (n = 2). After the duplicates had been removed, 42 articles were screened based on title and abstract. Following the screening, 11 were retrieved for full‐text review. From which, 6 were removed due to wrong intervention and outcomes, and 5 were eventually included in this systematic review. Characteristics of Included Studies

The included studies were published in international journals between 2019 and 2023. The studies were conducted across Germany (n = 2), the USA (n = 1), Iran (n = 1) and Korea (n = 1), with study designs including prospective cohort study (n = 1), retrospective observational study (n = 1), pilot study (n = 1), theoretical study (n = 1) and cross‐sectional study (n = 1). The combined participant count across studies was 316, with varying demographics and clinical stages. Most studies focused on patients with WHO grade III or IV glioma. Sours Rhodes et al. [12] utilised resting‐state functional MRI (rs‐fMRI) to identify cognition‐related functional networks, while Kocher et al. [13] employed hybrid MR/PET imaging and Kocher et al. [14] incorporated FET‐PET alongside advanced MRI techniques for functional network analysis. Lee et al. [15] used longitudinal MRI to evaluate radiation‐related structural changes in cognitively relevant brain regions, whereas Boroun et al. [16] combined functional MRI (fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to map functional cortical regions and white matter fibre tracts for neurocognitive‐sparing treatment planning.

Sours Rhodes et al. [12] demonstrated the feasibility of sparing the DMN using rs‐fMRI‐guided plans, significantly reducing dose without compromising tumour coverage. Kocher et al. [13, 14] used multimodal imaging and normative atlases to show that DMN disruption and T2/FLAIR abnormalities in the left temporal lobe are strongly associated with verbal memory loss. Boroun et al. [16] confirmed that functional and tract‐based sparing via fMRI and DTI is dosimetrically feasible, while Lee et al. [15] revealed that cognitive‐related GM regions, particularly deep GM, undergo significant atrophy post‐RT, supporting the need for GM‐sparing approaches. Collectively, these studies highlight the value of functional imaging in identifying functionally critical brain regions and structures for functional avoidance strategies to preserve cognitive functions and treatment‐related cognitive decline.

Potential of Functional Imaging

The results from the included studies highlight the promising role of functional imaging in preserving cognitive function during RT for patients with glioma (Table 2). Sours Rhodes et al. [12] demonstrated that incorporating rs‐fMRI into RT planning enabled a 20% reduction in the mean dose and a 12% reduction in the maximum dose to the DMN (p = 0.002 and p = 0.007, respectively), with an estimated 20% reduction in the probability of memory loss, all while maintaining target volume coverage. Boroun et al. [16] found that neurocognitive‐sparing plans integrating fMRI and DTI data achieved a 25.36% reduction in the mean dose and an 18.57% reduction in the maximum dose to functional regions (p = 0.005), and a 5.59% and 20.84% reduction in the mean and maximum doses to fibre tracts, respectively (p = 0.028 and p = 0.018), also without compromising tumour coverage or exceeding dose limits to anatomical organs‐at‐risk (OARs). Kocher et al. [14] showed that T2/FLAIR abnormalities attributed mainly to RT were a major driver of progressive cognitive decline. Lesions in the left hemisphere, particularly involving nodes and tracts in the left temporal lobe, most severely impaired verbal episodic memory and other language functions. Kocher et al. [13] linked reduced DMN connectivity, especially in the left inferior parietal lobule, to impaired performance across up to 7 of 10 cognitive tests. Reduced connectivity in the left lateral temporal cortex was also found to be associated with deficits in 3 of 10 tests. Lee et al. [15] demonstrated that GM volume undergoes progressive atrophy following RT, with the steepest losses observed in regions critical for cognition and motor control (median reductions of 4.8% and 5.3%, respectively). The pronounced vulnerability of deep GM structures likely plays a key role in driving post‐RT cognitive deterioration, highlighting these areas as potential targets for neuroprotective strategies. These findings emphasise the practical use of functional imaging modalities such as rs‐fMRI and PET/MRI in identifying critical cognitive regions and integrating them into RT planning to spare these regions and minimise neurocognitive decline.

Discussion

This review highlights the growing evidence that functional imaging can play a substantial role in RT planning for patients with glioma by identifying functionally critical brain regions to spare in glioma radiation planning. The benefits and implications of functional imaging to preserve the cognitive function of patients with glioma were explored in this review.

Functional Imaging as a Tool for Neuroprotective RT Strategies

Functional imaging provides valuable insights into the spatial distribution of cognitive networks and eloquent brain regions that may not be visible on conventional anatomical imaging. By identifying functionally significant areas such as language networks, memory‐related regions and large‐scale cognitive networks, these techniques emphasise the vulnerability of these structures to radiation‐induced injury. Across the studies analysed, functional imaging contributed to the identification of cognitive‐related critical structures and highlights the importance of reducing dose and hence tissue damage to these structures. Modalities such as rs‐fMRI, structural MRI, DTI and multimodal PET/MRI helped identify brain regions and networks which are critical for cognition and higher‐order functions. Most notably, structures such as the DMN, key GM regions and language‐associated tracts, which are particularly sensitive cognitive domains, can be identified and spared [12, 16]. These cognitive domains are vulnerable to radiation injury, impacting memory, language, attention and executive functions.

Functional imaging presents a promising tool for visualising and mapping eloquent brain areas, paving the way for functional avoidance strategies which can optimise RT planning to spare highly functional tissues [13, 14, 15]. With functional imaging in place, Sours Rhodes et al. [12] reported a 20% reduction in mean dose and a 12% reduction in maximum dose to the rs‐fMRI‐defined DMN (p = 0.002 and p = 0.007), with an average relative mean‐dose reduction of 29% (range 9%–89%) and a drop in DMN V10 from 50% to 40%. Similarly, Boroun et al. [16] utilised fMRI and DTI to achieve a 25.36% mean‐dose and 18% max‐dose reduction to functional cortical regions (both p = 0.005), and 5.59% mean‐dose and 20.84% max‐dose reductions to white‐matter fibre tracts (p = 0.028 and p = 0.018). It is noteworthy that these reductions of dose to functional OARs were achieved while keeping conventional anatomical OARs within tolerance.

Emerging evidence suggests that damage to critical cognitive regions may be associated with measurable declines in neurocognitive performance. Kocher et al. [13] observed that reduced connectivity of the DMN, specifically in left temporal and parietal nodes, was associated with impaired language processing and verbal working memory. In a complementary study, Kocher et al. [14] reported that T2/FLAIR hyperintensities affecting functional nodes and fibre tracts in the left temporal lobe strongly correlated with verbal episodic memory dysfunction, highlighting the region's vulnerability. Together, these findings underscore the left temporal lobe and DMN hubs as priority targets for sparing. The GM is also identified as a critical structure in cognitive preservation. Lee et al. [15] documented progressive deep GM volume loss, particularly the thalamus, accumbens, caudate, putamen and globus pallidus post‐RT. This selective vulnerability suggests that deep GM damage might lead to cognitive decline in patients with glioma.

Therefore, incorporating functional imaging into RT planning highlights the need for neuroprotective treatment strategies that aim to minimise radiation exposure to critical cognitive structures. These results support the prioritisation of the left parietal, left temporal nodes, DMN hubs and deep GM structures in functional‐avoidance strategies [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Individualised RT planning for patients with glioma can avoid irradiation of critical regions responsible for neurocognition and can potentially reduce cognitive deficits in patients. This approach is particularly relevant in patients with glioma who may experience prolonged survival, thereby making long‐term preservation of cognitive function crucial and central [17, 18].

Dosimetric Benefits of Functional Imaging–Guided OARs Sparing

In this systematic review, the translation of dosimetric sparing to meaningful clinical endpoints demonstrated that functional‐guided RT planning is dosimetrically advantageous. Sours Rhodes et al. [12] used NTCP modelling to translate dosimetric gains into potential clinical benefits, reporting an approximated 20%–23% relative reduction in predicted memory‐loss risk when DMN regions were preferentially spared. The normal tissue complication probability modelling approach was employed to estimate the potential advantage of fMRI–guided DMN avoidance treatment plans compared with standard planning approaches. This model was used to predict the likelihood of reducing radiation‐associated memory loss, which serves as a surrogate indicator of cognitive function. This provides a quantitative bridge between treatment planning and functional outcomes, reinforcing the clinical relevance of functional‐guided RT planning.

Boroun et al. [16] reported dosimetric reductions to functional regions and fibre tracts, providing preliminary evidence that supports the feasibility of functional avoidance. While Boroun et al. [16] primarily reported dosimetric feasibility and dose reductions to functional brain regions rather than longitudinal cognitive assessments, the study set the stage for future clinical studies to determine the long‐term cognitive relevance of these dosimetric findings.

While reduction of dose to critical cognitive regions sounds promising, the true value of functional‐guided RT planning lies in the clinical outcome associated with these reductions. Few of the included studies directly assessed neurocognitive outcomes and clinical relevance has yet to be proven [12, 16]. There is insufficient clinical evidence to establish the correlation between dose reduction to critical cognitive structures and reduction in decline in cognitive function. Future prospective clinical trials which assess cognitive endpoints would allow evaluation of the actual clinical impact of dose reduction in critical cognitive regions [12].

Maintenance of Adequate Tumour Control

The feasibility of neuroprotective strategies in RT planning is demonstrated both technically and dosimetrically without compromising tumour coverage. Boroun et al. [16] maintained PTV coverage at ≥ 95% of prescription for functional‐avoidance plans and Sours Rhodes et al. [12] similarly reported no change in PTV dose coverage while achieving DMN dose reductions and no increase in brainstem dose. Functional plans also reduced the low‐dose splash to healthy brain tissues, achieving V10 reduction. Moreover, anatomical OARs constraints were respected in all optimised plans. These findings indicate that functional‐guided RT planning is feasible and meaningful functional OARs sparing can be achieved without compromising tumour coverage.

Limitations

While this review benefits from including studies that use multimodal functional imaging and neurocognitive assessments, demonstrating a generally consistent association between dose sparing and preserved function, it is not without limitations. Firstly, most studies were constrained by small sample sizes, ranging from n = 8 to 121, hence limiting statistical power and rendering findings insufficient to form definitive correlations. Large‐scale randomised controlled trials linking functional imaging‐guided planning to long‐term neurocognitive outcomes are needed to confirm whether functional‐guided radiation planning translates into measurable long‐term cognitive benefits. Secondly, there is substantial heterogeneity in tumour grade, imaging modalities and cognitive assessment methods across the studies, making comparisons difficult and limiting the ability to synthesise results. Thirdly, there is a lack of a standardised approach to the selection and prioritisation of functional brain regions in RT planning. Dosimetric approaches varied considerably, with studies varied in their use of patient‐specific functional imaging, atlas‐based methods and the types of cognitive regions included as avoidance structures. Prioritisation strategies and specific dose constraints for functional structures were also not standardised and were often exploratory or based on clinical judgement, tumour proximity and perceived functional importance. This heterogeneity limits comparability across studies and highlights the need for consensus guidelines to support the consistent integration of functional imaging into clinical practice. This reflects the early stage of this field and highlights the need for standardised cognitive endpoints and consensus dose constraints to support future clinical trial design. Lastly, the included studies are predominantly dosimetric or observational with limited prospective outcome data. Robust longitudinal studies with standardised cognitive follow‐up are essential to clarify whether dosimetric advantages reliably translate into sustained and significant neuroprotection over time.

Conclusion

In summary, this systematic review highlights the promising role of functional imaging in advancing neuroprotective strategies for glioma RT. Current evidence suggests that integrating functional modalities into treatment planning enables targeted dose reductions to cognition‐critical networks and tracts, including the left temporal lobe, deep GM and hubs of the default mode network. Importantly, these dosimetric improvements are achievable without compromising PTV coverage or exceeding OARs' tolerances, thereby reducing the risk of neurotoxicity while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. This supports a shift toward a functional‐avoidance paradigm where tumour control and neuroprotection are not mutually exclusive. This review lays the groundwork for future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal cognitive assessments and defined dose–response thresholds for functional brain regions to further validate the clinical benefits of functional imaging‐guided RT planning. Such evidence would be pivotal in supporting broader clinical integration of functional imaging for cognitive OARs sparing in patients with glioma.
