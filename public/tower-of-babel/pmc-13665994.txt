Rewilding the atoll engine
Authors: Hillary Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seabirds and native forests reconnect ocean, reef, and island processes, strengthening the physical foundations of atoll resilience.

Low-lying atoll islands are often portrayed as passive, helpless victims of sea-level rise. Yet, they are living landforms, built and maintained through interactions among coral reefs, sediment-producing organisms, waves, plants, and animals. Ecological processes influence whether reefs grow, new sediment is produced, and material deposited on islands is retained or lost. Most powerfully, it also means that local conservation can influence physical resilience to rising seas—even as rapid reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions remain essential to long-term viability of these systems. In an ambitious study spanning 28 islands in the Seychelles, the Chagos Archipelago, and Teti’aroa, Graham et al. connect these processes across the air-land-sea interface and show how seabirds and native vegetation can strengthen the ecological machinery and facilitate atoll resilience (1, 2).

Nearly two centuries ago, Charles Darwin recognized that the foundations of atolls are living structures, built upward by reef-forming organisms (3). Yet, it is largely only over the past two decades that research has revealed the individual links in the broader ecological chain connecting land and sea. Seabirds transport nutrients from the open ocean onto islands, often profoundly reorganizing terrestrial ecosystems (4). Those nutrients then move back offshore, where they influence reef fishes, corals, and other marine organisms (5–7). Corals and sediment-producing reef animals, in turn, generate the carbonate framework and loose material from which atoll islands are built and maintained (2, 8). Until now, these strands of research have largely developed in parallel. Graham et al. bring them together, tracing a near-complete loop from ocean-foraging seabirds, through native forests and coral reefs, back to the construction and stabilization of the islands on which the birds breed. By quantifying this loop, Graham et al. provide much-needed evidence that the fate of an atoll cannot be understood—or managed—by treating its land and sea separately.

The authors close this loop by using the common currency of ecosystem energetics to place processes ordinarily measured in different ways into a common analytical framework. By expressing biological activity in terms of energy flow, they test how variation in seabird abundance and native vegetation is associated with fine-root and coral productivity, parrotfish energetics, reef accretion, sediment generation, and sediment stabilization. This approach brings terrestrial, reef, and geomorphic processes into a single analysis rather than requiring their connections to be inferred across separate studies (1).

The terrestrial results show that seabird recovery and native-forest restoration are likely to be synergistic. Seabirds predominantly nest and roost—and thus deposit nutrients—both on rat-free islands and on islands with extensive native vegetation (1, 4, 5). In locations where seabird nutrients are abundant, increasing native vegetation cover is associated with substantially higher fine-root productivity (1). Those roots form a living mesh that can bind shallow island soils and help retain sediment during wave overtopping and storms (1).

Graham et al. demonstrate how this seabird nutrient subsidy extends offshore (1, 5–7). Greater seabird nitrogen input was associated with higher coral-community primary productivity and greater energy flow through parrotfishes (1). The increased coral productivity produces the carbonate framework that allows reefs to grow vertically, while the parrotfishes erode this carbonate material and generate sand-sized sediment that can be transported toward island shorelines (7–8). Linking coral productivity and parrotfish energy to greater sediment production moves beyond previous work documenting ecological enrichment and shows how that enrichment can be translated into processes directly relevant to island maintenance (1).

The resulting estimated differences among island accretion potential are notable. Sediment production is 2.3 to 2.8 times higher around rat-free than rat-infested islands, while terrestrial sites with high native vegetation cover experience an estimated 4.6-fold greater fine-root length than sites with lower native cover. Lagoonal reefs beside rat-free, seabird-rich islands have an estimated median accretion potential of approximately 5.9 mm per year—nearly twice that of reefs beside rat-infested islands. Under the study’s assumptions, this rate approaches projected local sea-level rise through 2100 under a 2°C warming scenario, suggesting that these local scale conservation actions might be sufficient to protect these systems to global threats beyond their range (1).

Rewilding atolls with seabirds (Fig. 1) and native forests is so powerful both because it acts on several sides of the resilience problem at once and because it is a cyclical feedback loop. Seabirds fertilize reefs that generate carbonate and sediment; those reefs help maintain the platform and material supply on which islands depend; native forests stabilize sediments deposited by waves while also providing habitat for the seabirds that power the cycle. To maximize conservation benefits of this engine, rat eradication, native revegetation, active seabird restoration, and protection of sediment-producing reef fishes should thus be planned as a coordinated strategy rather than as isolated conservation projects. Framing these interventions as components of climate adaptation may also create new opportunities for funding, particularly on remote islands where large engineering projects are impractical.

Of course, ecological restoration is not a substitute for emissions reductions, nor will every atoll respond identically. Island geometry, reef configuration, wave climate, fishing pressure, oceanography, and the intensity of future warming will all shape outcomes. The next steps are to determine how the gains documented here actually unfold through time and ensure that, as we pivot from observing natural experiments to actively managing restorations, we understand which elements of a restored island system are responsible. Rat-free, native-forested islands may differ not only in seabird abundance but also in populations of other native taxa that redistribute nutrients, process organic matter, and shape food webs. Long-term before-and-after studies following rat eradication, forest restoration, and seabird recovery—combined with sediment tracers, repeated topographic surveys, and ecological-hydrodynamic models—could distinguish the contribution of seabirds from that of broader ecosystem recovery and reveal whether additional taxa must be restored to maximize ecological gains (9).

Testing the framework on inhabited atolls will be equally important. Fishing and domestic animals may alter food webs and seabird recovery; buildings and shoreline infrastructure may constrain where sediment can accumulate; and wastewater and other human-derived nutrients may make additional seabird inputs less beneficial or even harmful (10). Comparisons across gradients of human habitation, nutrient loading, and coastal modification could establish where rewilding can most effectively support island resilience while remaining compatible with infrastructure and livelihood needs.

Yet, the central advance is now clear. Graham et al. provide a compelling proof of concept that rewilding can strengthen several processes underlying physical resilience—reef growth, sediment production, and sediment retention—within at least some atoll settings. While global mitigation will determine the magnitude of sea-level rise and ocean warming, local stewardship will influence whether reefs, seabirds, forests, and sediment pathways remain functional enough to respond. By revealing the atoll as an integrated ecological engine, Graham et al. show that restoring biodiversity can also restore processes that build, replenish, and stabilize land. Rewilding cannot remove the climate threat, but it can strengthen the living systems through which atolls meet it.
