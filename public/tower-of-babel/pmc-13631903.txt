Generation and epitope mapping of monoclonal antibodies targeting the avian reovirus S1133 σC protein
Authors: Haixin Deng, Chengyu He, Kakule Mayimoto, Jiezhi Ma, Xiangdong Wu, Huansheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian reovirus (ARV) causes of multisystemic, immunosuppressive infectious diseases in poultry. Among them, the σC protein, a structural component of the outer capsid encoded by the S1 genome segment, plays a crucial role in viral attachment and cellular entry. In this study, we expressed the σC protein derived from the S1133 vaccine strain was expressed in a prokaryotic system and purified it. Then, using hybridoma technology, we generated two murine monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), designated 3H and 4B and shown to specifically recognize the ARV σC protein. In order to determine the linear B-cell epitopes recognized by monoclonal antibodies 3H and 4B, we localized the epitope in the 72LDD74 region using fragment truncation and site-specific mutation techniques. Furthermore, sequence analysis across ARV genotypes showed that the 72LDD74 motif is highly conserved among genotype I strains but differs substantially in genotypes II-VI, suggesting it could serve as a genotype I-specific antigenic marker. Collectively, these results support development of genotype-targeted diagnostic assays for genotype I ARV and provide a basic tool for the interaction between ARV and the host and the pathogenesis mechanism.

Introduction

Avian reovirus (ARV) is an important pathogen that threatens the poultry industry. ARV is a non-enveloped virus belonging to the family Reoviridae and the genus Orthoreovirus. Its genome consists of 10 double-stranded RNA segments within a double-layered capsid. These segments are classified into three groups, L (L1-L3), M (M1-M3), and S (S1-S4), which encode λ, μ, and σ proteins, respectively. The outer capsid protein σC is encoded by the S1 gene segment (Martínez-Costas et al., 1997).

ARV infects poultry and wild birds, including chickens, turkeys, geese, and Muscovy ducks. Moreover, both horizontal and vertical transmission contribute to ARV spread (Chen et al., 2025). Clinically, viral arthritis/tenosynovitis is the most characteristic clinical manifestation. In addition, some strains also cause malabsorption syndrome, stunting syndrome, immunosuppression, myocarditis, and hepatitis. (Benavente and Martinez-Costas, 2007). Although ARV mortality is lower than for acute, highly virulent diseases, ARV causes growth retardation and immunosuppression, increasing susceptibility to other pathogens and causing substantial economic losses (Mosad et al., 2023).

Recent studies have documented substantial genetic and antigenic divergence between contemporary ARV field isolates and classical vaccine strains, which may contribute to incomplete cross-protection against heterologous variants (Ayalew et al., 2017; Song et al., 2024). These findings primarily reflect vaccine-induced polyclonal immunity and provide a broader context for studying σC antigenic variation, rather than implying that any single σC epitope determines vaccine protection. At the molecular level, however, the contribution of individual σC antibody-binding determinants to genotype-associated antigenic variation remains incompletely defined. As a major neutralizing antigen involved in viral entry and immune recognition, σC induces type-specific antibodies and is widely used for ARV antigenic classification, serological detection, and candidate vaccine design (Lublin et al., 2011). Consistent with this, related studies have shown that ARV with different gene clusters differ in antibody binding specificity to σC (Dawe et al., 2022). Such antigenic heterogeneity has practical implications for serological diagnosis because σC is widely used as a diagnostic antigen. In 2024, Yang et al. developed an indirect ELISA based on σC and demonstrated that σC could serve as a sensitive and specific diagnostic antigen (Yang et al., 2024). More recently, Zhao et al. combined a genotype Ⅴ-specific σC antigen with σB antigen in a dual-antigen ELISA, highlighting the potential importance of genotype-matched σC antigens for improving serological detection of emerging variants. Although existing σC-based ELISAs sensitively and specifically detect ARV antibodies, most use full-length or relatively large recombinant antigens and are not designed to resolve genotype-associated antigenic differences. As divergent ARV strains circulate, antigenic mismatch between diagnostic antigens and field strains may affect serological recognition. Defining genotype-associated σC antibody-binding determinants may therefore provide a molecular basis for developing genotype-targeted diagnostic assays.

Despite the recognized importance of σC in ARV antigenicity, only a few experimentally validated linear B-cell epitopes have been identified in this protein. The relationship between σC epitopes and genotype-associated antigenic differences remains unclear. Yin et al., identified 45ELLHRSISDI54 as a minimal linear B-cell epitope recognized by the σC-specific mAb 2B5, whereas subsequent studies using polyclonal sera from vaccinated chickens identified additional antibody-reactive regions across σC without defining monoclonal-antibody-specific minimal determinants. Thus, the fine antigenic architecture of σC, particularly genotype-associated linear epitopes recognized by individual mAbs, remains incompletely characterized. In the present study, we generated two σC-specific mAbs, 3H and 4B, against genotype I ARV and identified 72LDD74 as a common core linear recognition epitope for both antibodies. Notably, this epitope is distinct from the previously reported 45ELLHRSISDI54 epitope (Yin et al., 2013). Sequence conservation analysis showed that this motif is highly conserved among genotype I ARVs but differs in other genotypes, suggesting that it may represent a genotype-biased antigenic marker.Materials and methods

Cells, viruses and reagents

The classical genotype I ARV strain S1133, chicken hepatocellular carcinoma LMH cells (Leghorn Male Hepatoma cells) and SP2/0 myeloma cells were used in this study. Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells for cloning and Rosetta (DE3) competent cells for protein expression were obtained from ToloBio. Restriction endonucleases were purchased from TAKARA (Japan), and T4 DNA ligase was purchased from Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). DNA purification kit was purchased from Solarbio (D1300) in Beijing, China. Six-week-old specific-pathogen-free female BALB/c mice, Freund’s complete and incomplete adjuvants, ascites-inducing adjuvant, HAT and HT media, Hybridoma Feeder add factors, mouse monoclonal antibody isotyping reagents and an mAb isotyping ELISA kit were purchased from Biodragon (Wuhan, China). PEG-4000 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Anti-His tag mAb, anti-Myc tag mAb and HRP- or FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) secondary antibodies were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China).

Expression and purification of ARV σC protein

Total RNA was extracted from LMH cells infected with ARV strain S1133 using TRIzol reagent. The ARV σC gene was amplified by RT-PCR using the extracted RNA as the template, and the recombinant plasmid pCold-σC was constructed (Upstream primers: 5′-GCGAATTCATGGCGGGTCTCAATCCAT-3′; Downstream primer: 5′-GCGTCGACTTAGGTGTCGATGCCGGTACG-3′). Sigma C protein is expressed under IPTG induction and purified protein is obtained by nickel column affinity chromatography and denaturation reprocessing. The protein concentration is 2.6 μg/μl. After being verified by SDS-PAGE and Western blot highly conserved, it was further purified and used for mouse immunization.

Preparation of σC monoclonal antibodies

For the primary immunization, each mouse was injected with 400 μg of σC protein emulsified 1:1 with Freund’s complete adjuvant. A second immunization was administered 14 days later using 200 μg of σC protein emulsified 1:1 with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant, and a third immunization was performed 10 days thereafter using the same protocol. Three days before cell fusion, immunized mice received a booster injection of 100 μg of σC protein. In parallel, spleen cells from age-matched SPF BALB/c mice were collected to prepare feeder cells. Three days after the booster immunization, positive serum was collected, the immunized mouse was euthanized, and the spleen was aseptically isolated to prepare a splenocyte suspension. Splenocytes and SP2/0 cells were mixed at a ratio of 5:1, after which 1ml PEG-4000 was added to induce cell fusion. Fused cells were seeded onto feeder cells and cultured in DMEM selection medium containing 15% ZETA serum, 5% growth factor, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 2% HAT. Hybridoma cells with high antibody titers were selected and subjected to two rounds of limiting-dilution subcloning to obtain monoclonal cell lines stably secreting σC-specific antibodies. Positive hybridoma cells were subsequently injected intraperitoneally into mice to generate ascites, and antibody titers and isotypes were determined by indirect ELISA and an antibody isotyping kit, respectively.

Detection of monoclonal antibody reactivity against exogenous and endogenous σC

The immunoreactivity of the generated ARV σC mAbs was evaluated by Western blotting and confocal microscopy. To detect exogenous σC, 293T cells were transfected with the recombinant Myc-σC plasmid for 24 h and then analyzed by Western blotting using σC mAb (1:5,000), anti-Myc tag antibody and anti-GAPDH antibody as primary antibodies, with goat anti-mouse IgG as the secondary antibody. To detect endogenous σC, LMH cells were infected with ARV strain S1133 for 12 h and subjected to Western blotting using σC mAbs (1:5,000) and anti-GAPDH as primary antibodies. LMH cells were inoculated with ARV S1133 at a 1-fold extracellular infection unit (MOI). Twelve hours after infection, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed, permeated, and then subjected to immunofluorescence staining treatment. Cells were then incubated with σC mAb (1:1,000), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:500).

Epitope identification of monoclonal antibodies

To identify the epitopes recognized by mAbs 3H and 4B within the ARV σC protein, we used a mapping strategy that combined serial fragment truncation with site-directed mutagenesis. First, the σC protein was divided into two overlapping fragments, Myc-σC-N (1–200 aa) and Myc-σC-C (101–327 aa). Subsequently, the N-terminal region was further truncated into three fragments with 50-aa overlaps: Myc-σC-NA (1–100 aa), Myc-σC-NB (51–150 aa), and Myc-σC-NC (101–200 aa). Based on the preliminary reactivity results, the candidate epitope region was further narrowed to residues 51-100 aa, and a series of truncated mutants differing by five adjacent amino acids were constructed. Truncated σC fragments were cloned into the pCMV-Myc-N vector by homologous recombination to generate Myc-tagged recombinant expression plasmids. Finally, the core epitope was localized to a region composed of five amino acids, and a single point mutation was applied to each amino acid within this region, constructing five single-residue alanine replacement mutants (T71A,L72A,D73A,D74A,V75A) . After 293T cells were transfected with each plasmid for 24 h, cell lysates were collected and analyzed by Western blotting using anti-Myc antibody to confirm expression of the truncated proteins, anti-GAPDH antibody as a loading control, and mAbs 3H and 4B to assess antibody reactivity. Multiple-sequence alignment and structural modelling of antigenic epitopes

To assess epitope conservation and the potential structural basis of antibody recognition, 18 ARV amino acid sequences were retrieved from NCBI. In addition, two genotype IV strains (SRX1/PZ371002 and WNHJ/PZ371003) and one genotype VI strain (JJ615/PZ343853) isolated and identified in our laboratory were included. Multiple-sequence alignment was performed using ESPript 3.0. Preparation and characterization of ARV σC monoclonal antibodies

RT-PCR amplified an approximately 981 bp ARV σC fragment, and the prokaryotic expression vector pCold-σC was successfully constructed. After induction and purification in the prokaryotic expression system, recombinant σC protein was obtained. SDS-PAGE showed a ∼37 kDa band for the purified recombinant σC protein. Using hybridoma technology and limiting-dilution cloning, we established two hybridoma lines (3H and 4B) stably secreting σC-specific antibodies. Ascites-derived antibodies reached ELISA titers of 1:256,000. (Fig. 1A), and both mAbs were IgG2a heavy chain and a κ light chain (Fig. 1B). We next assessed the specificity of 3H and 4B against exogenous and endogenous σC by Western blotting and confocal microscopy. In 293T cells transfected with Myc-σC, both mAbs detected a specific band of approximately 37 kDa, which coincided with the signal obtained using an anti-Myc antibody and was absent from the empty-vector control (Fig. 1C). Similarly, in ARV S1133-infected LMH cells, endogenous σC was specifically detected by both 3H and 4B, whereas no corresponding band was observed in uninfected cells (Fig. 1D). Confocal microscopy revealed strong σC-associated fluorescence in ARV S1133-infected LMH cells, predominantly in the cytoplasm, negative controls showed no staining (Fig. 1E). Thus, 3H and 4B are σC-specific mAbs suitable for epitope mapping.

Epitope mapping

To define epitopes recognised by 3H and 4B, we transfected 293T cells with truncated σC constructs and analysed them by Western blotting. Both mAbs recognised the N-terminal region (1-200 aa) in initial mapping (Fig. 2A). Further truncation showed both mAbs recognised σC-NA and σC-NB but not σC-NC (Fig. 2B). Analysis of σC-NAB subfragments showed F5-F9 were recognised, but F1-F4 were not (Fig. 2C). These results localised the recognition epitope to the ⁷¹TLDDV⁷⁵ region, which distinguishes F4 (76-100 aa) from F5 (71-100 aa). Single-site mutagenesis identified L72, D73, and D74 as critical residues for recognition by both mAbs, defining 72LDD74 as the core recognition motif (Fig. 2D). Sequence alignment showed ⁷²LDD⁷⁴ is conserved in genotype I strains but differs in genotypes II-VI (Fig. 2E), suggesting the mAbs may show genotype-biased recognition. Meanwhile, this study also has certain limitations. The neutralizing activity of mAb 3H and 4B was not evaluated. 72LDD74 was only defined as a linear epitope, not a neutralizing epitope. Exploratory AlphaFold3 modeling indicates that after alanine substitution, local spatial structures around 72LDD74 may differ in electrostatic surfaces. Since these predictions have not been experimentally validated, they serve only as possible structural explanations for changes in monoclonal antibody recognition. Although the antibodies described in this study show potential for detecting ARV genotypes, epidemic monitoring, and diagnostic method development, their sensitivity, specificity, cross-reactivity, and performance in clinical samples still need thorough evaluation. Conclusion

In this study, we generated and characterized two σC-specific mAbs, 3H and 4B, against genotype I ARV. Both mAbs had an IgG2a heavy chain and a κ light chain, showed an ascites ELISA titer of 1:256,000, and specifically recognized recombinant and virus-expressed σC, supporting their utility for antigenic analysis and their potential for further evaluation in diagnostic assay development. Using sequential truncation and site-directed mutagenesis, we found that both monoclonal antibodies were located in the 72LDD74 region, indicating that their linear epitopes were highly overlapping. Sequence alignment showed ⁷² LDD⁷⁴ is conserved in genotype I but differs in genotypes II-VI, suggesting it may be a genotype-biased marker. Thus, this work expands the experimentally defined epitope landscape of ARV σC and provides σC-specific monoclonal antibodies and epitope information that may support future studies of ARV antigenic variation and the development of genotype-targeted diagnostic approaches.
