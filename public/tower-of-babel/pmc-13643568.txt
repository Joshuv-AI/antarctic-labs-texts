ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 36-year-old man with a history of insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes mellitus and May-Thurner syndrome had an iliac vein stent placed 2 years before presentation. Differential Diagnosis

The differential for a right ventricular mass included intracardiac thrombus, infective endocarditis vegetation, primary cardiac neoplasm, metastatic lesion, and migrated intravascular foreign body. Given the patient’s history of iliac vein stenting for May-Thurner syndrome, stent migration was strongly suspected.

Investigations

A transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a mass in the RV (Figures 1 and 2). A transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed the mass as circular and metallic (Figure 3). Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated that the prior-placed iliac stent had migrated and fractured, with small fragments in the left and right lungs, and a large fragment in the RV, with the remainder anchored in the intrahepatic IVC. These imaging findings established iliac venous stent migration with associated tricuspid valve involvement (Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8).Figure 4Color Doppler Transesophageal Echocardiography Demonstrating Moderate Tricuspid Regurgitation Secondary to Distortion of the Tricuspid Valve Apparatus by the Migrated StentFigure 5Transesophageal Echocardiography Demonstrating Incorporation of the Migrated Venous Stent into the Tricuspid Subvalvular Apparatus and Chordal StructuresFigure 6Alternate Transesophageal Echocardiographic View Demonstrating Incorporation of the Migrated Venous Stent into the Tricuspid Subvalvular Apparatus

Management

Given the extensive intracardiac stent burden and involvement of the tricuspid valve apparatus, the patient was referred for surgical management. Following median sternotomy and institution of cardiopulmonary bypass, the RA was opened for direct inspection. Intraoperative evaluation demonstrated a partially endothelialized stent segment extending from the IVC into the cavoatrial junction. Because of dense incorporation into the vessel wall, only the protruding intracardiac portion was removed, whereas the remaining endothelialized segment was left in situ to avoid caval injury.

A separate migrated stent fragment was identified within the RV and found to be extensively incorporated into the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus, particularly involving the anterior leaflet and chordae tendineae. The stent was carefully explanted (Figure 9); however, subsequent assessment demonstrated severe tricuspid insufficiency with destruction of the native valvular apparatus, precluding durable repair (Figure 10).

The patient therefore underwent bioprosthetic tricuspid valve replacement with a 29-mm porcine valve. The operation was completed without complication, and the patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass.

Follow-Up

The patient did well postoperatively and was discharged home.

Discussion

May-Thurner syndrome is a vascular compression disorder in which the left common iliac vein is compressed by the overlying right common iliac artery against the lumbar vertebral body, predisposing patients to venous stasis, chronic venous insufficiency, and deep venous thrombosis. Iliac vein stenting has become a widely accepted treatment modality for both nonthrombotic iliac vein lesions and post-thrombotic venous disease because of favorable long-term patency and symptomatic improvement.1, 2, 3

Although iliac venous stenting is effective, complications including in-stent thrombosis, restenosis, fracture, and migration are reported. Prior studies evaluating venous stenting outcomes have focused on rates of restenosis, recurrent thrombosis, and long-term stent patency. Histopathologic analyses of failed iliofemoral venous stents have demonstrated endothelial hyperplasia, thrombotic remodeling, and inflammatory changes associated with in-stent restenosis and eventual stent failure.2 Additionally, recurrent deep venous thrombosis following pharmacomechanical thrombolysis and iliac vein stenting has been associated with persistent venous compression, inadequate inflow, and patient-specific thrombotic risk factors.3

However, stent migration remains an uncommon but potentially catastrophic complication. A systematic review by Badesha et al4 identified only 6 episodes of stent migration among 3,429 patients treated with dedicated venous stents, corresponding to an incidence of approximately 0.17%. Similarly, major complication analyses of deep venous stenting have demonstrated that stent compression and fracture occur far more commonly than migration, emphasizing the rarity of clinically significant embolization.5

Migration into the right heart with involvement of the tricuspid valve apparatus represents an unusual complication pattern. Prior reports have described migrated venous stents causing myocardial injury, arrhythmias, valvular dysfunction, cardiac perforation, and right-sided heart failure.6 Chen et al7 previously reported a comparable case involving migration of a venous stent into the RV with entrapment within the tricuspid chordal structures, ultimately requiring prosthetic tricuspid valve replacement after unsuccessful repair. More recently, Sanchez et al8 described iliac venous stent migration into the RV with tricuspid valve entanglement requiring intervention.

However, our case demonstrates several severe and unusual features. The patient’s iliac venous stent fragmented with simultaneous embolization to the bilateral pulmonary vasculature, RV, and intrahepatic IVC. Furthermore, the intracardiac fragment became progressively endothelialized into the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus, particularly involving the anterior leaflet and chordae tendineae, resulting in severe valvular destruction. Although percutaneous retrieval techniques remain the preferred first-line approach for migrated venous stents when technically feasible, the degree of endothelialization and structural incorporation in this patient precluded safe endovascular extraction.9,10 Consequently, the patient required median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, open foreign body extraction, and bioprosthetic tricuspid valve replacement.

This case additionally highlights the importance of multimodal imaging in the diagnosing and planning treatment for migrated venous stents. Transthoracic echocardiography initially identified the intracardiac mass, whereas transesophageal echocardiography further characterized its metallic appearance and valvular involvement. CTA ultimately delineated the extent of stent fragmentation and embolization, allowing surgical planning.

Importantly, this case highlights the importance of considering stent migration in patients with prior venous stenting who present with unexplained intracardiac findings or cardiopulmonary symptoms. Earlier recognition of stent migration before progressive endothelialization may improve the feasibility of minimally invasive retrieval and reduce the need for complex open cardiac surgery.6,10

Although prior reports have documented migrated venous stents causing tricuspid valve injury requiring replacement, the combination of simultaneous stent fragmentation, bilateral pulmonary embolization, incorporation into the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus, and residual endothelialized IVC stent burden remains highly unusual in the currently available literature.

Conclusions

Iliac venous stent migration is a rare, life-threatening complication of stenting for May-Thurner syndrome. This case demonstrates an unusual presentation involving stent fragmentation with embolization to the RV, pulmonary vasculature, and intrahepatic IVC, resulting in severe tricuspid valve destruction because of migration into the subvalvular apparatus. Multimodal imaging with echocardiography and CTA was essential for diagnosis and surgical management. Considering the extensive endothelialization and valvular involvement, definitive management required open surgical extraction and valve replacement.
