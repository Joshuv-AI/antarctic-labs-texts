Mechanistic insights into yield enhancement of Chimonobambusa opienensis bamboo shoots driven by organic fertilizer combined with Azotobacter Chroococcum via soil nutrient-microbe-metabolite interactions
Authors: Wei Fan, Shangmeng Li, Zhewen Zhang, Zhijian Long, Xu Yang, Wenbo Chen, Xun Song, Xin Zhao, Fengying Guan, Ying Cao, Shaohui Fan, Shanglian Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimonobambusa opienensis is a bamboo shoot species with considerable ecological and economic value; however, its low yield constrains large-scale cultivation. Bio-organic fertilizers, which are sustainable alternatives to chemical fertilizers, can improve soil conditions and enhance productivity. However, the mechanisms by which they increase yield through soil nutrient-mediated regulation of microbe–metabolite interactions in C. opienensis remain unclear. We conducted a two-year field trial with five fertilization treatments: organic fertilizer (OF), Azotobacter chroococcum (Ac), Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (Ba), organic fertilizer combined with (A) chroococcum (OF + Ac), and organic fertilizer combined with (B) amyloliquefaciens (OF + Ba). Amplicon sequencing and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry were employed to assess the soil microbial composition and metabolic activity across treatments. The results demonstrated that soil chemical properties were the key drivers of microbial community structure. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that microbial networks in the OF + Ac group were simpler and less interconnected than those in other treatments. Non-targeted metabolomics identified 133 soil metabolites. The control group was significantly enriched in fatty acyl compounds, whereas carbon- and nitrogen-containing metabolites were markedly increased in Ac and OF + Ac treatments. Moreover, the addition of A. chroococcum (in Ac and OF + Ac treatments) induced the most pronounced shifts in microbial community composition. Network analysis of microbial ASV–metabolite associations showed that nearly all differential ASVs responded specifically to either Ac or OF + Ac treatment. Furthermore, the network connector Bradyrhizobium (ASV2748) responded exclusively to OF + Ac and exhibited significant positive correlations with numerous nitrogen-containing metabolites. Structural equation modeling proposes a model that soil nutrients were the primary drivers of microbial community changes and directly enhanced bamboo shoot yield. This study proposes a model in which the OF + Ac enhances (C) opienensis bamboo shoot yield without compromising palatability, through a soil nutrient–microbe–metabolite pathway supported by SEM results, whereas Ba also increases yield but at the expense of palatability. Introduction

Bamboo, a perennial herbaceous plant in the subfamily Bambusoideae of Poaceae, is one of the fastest-growing plants in the world [1]. Globally, approximately 1,642 bamboo species have been identified, covering about 35.07 million hectares, making bamboo a promising non-woody alternative to woody biomass [2–4]. Mature bamboo serves as a versatile raw material for construction and industry [5], while young bamboo shoots are consumed as a nutritious food rich in protein, fiber, minerals, and vitamins but low in fat [6]. Their nutritional and health-promoting properties have led to their designation as “a superfood for nutrition, health, and medicine” [7–9]. In China, bamboo shoots have a long history of use in both traditional cuisine and Chinese medicine [10]. Worldwide consumption is steadily increasing, including in Japan and Western countries [10–12]. With growing demand, scientific management practices are needed to enhance bamboo shoot yield and quality [13–15]. However, excessive reliance on chemical fertilizers poses health and ecological risks [16], disrupts soil microbial balance, and accelerates bamboo forest degradation [14, 17, 18]. These challenges highlight the urgent need for sustainable fertilization strategies. Therefore, exploring green and sustainable fertilization strategies is essential to enhance bamboo forest ecosystem stability, improve bamboo shoot yield and quality, and maintain bamboo shoots as a green and healthy food source.

Bio-organic fertilizers, defined as organic amendments inoculated with Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), represent eco-friendly alternatives to chemical inputs [19, 20]. PGPR are beneficial microorganisms isolated from the rhizosphere that promote plant growth through multiple mechanisms and thus form the biological basis of microbial fertilizers [21, 22]. Fertilization inevitably alters soil physicochemical properties, thereby reshaping microbial community structure. While conventional chemical fertilizers such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium can change soil nutrient availability and microbial composition [23–26], these effects are often accompanied by environmental drawbacks. In contrast, bio-organic fertilizers can regulate rhizosphere microbial composition, promote beneficial taxa, support nutrient cycling, and suppress pathogens [27]. For example, in Dendrocalamus farinosus, bio-organic fertilizers have been shown to enhance soil nutrient levels and modulate the associations between rhizosphere microorganisms and metabolites [28].

The rhizosphere is a hotspot of plant–soil–microbe interactions [28–30]. In natural ecosystems, plants regulate the chemical composition of the rhizosphere through root exudates, selectively recruiting microbial taxa that form functionally advantageous and stable communities [29–33]. Thus, the rhizosphere metabolome comprises chemically diverse primary and secondary metabolites secreted by both the plant host and associated microbial communities into the surrounding soil [34]. This exometabolome interacts dynamically with soil properties—such as mineral composition, pH, and nutrient content—forming a critical interface for plant-microbe interactions [35, 36]. Fertilizer-induced changes in rhizosphere metabolites can enhance key microbial traits such as chemotaxis and motility [37], intercellular signaling [38], and nutrient uptake efficiency [31, 39]. This observation indicates that plants use highly selective mechanisms to recruit specific functional microorganisms in the rhizosphere. However, the precise mechanisms underlying rhizosphere microbial community assembly in fertilized bamboo, and how root metabolome profiles correlate with microbial shifts, remain poorly understood.

China hosts over 800 bamboo species, of which 153 are edible, making it a bamboo-rich country [40]. Chimonobambusa opienensis is an endemic shoot-type bamboo species in China, mainly distributed in Ebian, Sichuan Province, where it covers about 40,000 ha and exhibits strong carbon-sequestration capacity [41]. As a major regional cash crop, C. opienensis bamboo shoots are highly nutritious and prized for their flavor. Unlike most raw bamboo shoots, they contain minimal cyanogenic glycosides and can be eaten without boiling, fermenting, or drying [6, 42, 43]. However, long-term extensive management and nutrient depletion have led to bamboo forest degradation, including large-scale flowering, die-back of C. opienensis, and reduced bamboo shoot yield. During restoration, C. opienensis faces multiple biotic and abiotic stresses. Mutualistic plant–microbe interactions that improve nutrient availability, stress tolerance, and pathogen defense are critical for resilience and productivity [44, 45]. According to the “cry-for-help” hypothesis, stressed plants recruit beneficial bacteria to the rhizosphere to enhance adaptive capacity [46]. However, how bio-organic fertilization (organic amendments combined with PGPR) reshapes the C. opienensis rhizosphere microbiota, how metabolites mediate this process, and how these amendments influence rhizosphere microecology remain unclear.

In this study, we examined the response of root metabolites and rhizosphere microbial communities in C. opienensis to bio-organic fertilizer application. Field-grown C. opienensis was treated for two consecutive years under six conditions: control, organic fertilizer (OF), microbial fertilizer Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (Ba), microbial fertilizer Azotobacter chroococcum (Ac), OF + Ba (OFBa), and OF + Ac (OFAc). These PGPR were selected because B. amyloliquefaciens enhances nutrient solubilization and pathogen suppression [19, 20], whereas A. chroococcum contributes nitrogen fixation [47]. We identified key metabolites and microorganisms as the functional drivers associated with microbial profiles and soil nutrient factors. These components significantly influenced the composition of the root microbiome. Soil nutrients were the dominant factors shaping metabolite and microbial community composition, particularly under Ac and OFAc treatments. These conditions promoted the co-enrichment of nutrient-preferring and nitrogen-fixing microbes alongside amino acid-derived nitrogenous metabolites, ultimately enhancing the phenotype, quality, and yield of bamboo shoots. Furthermore, a multi-omics network analysis combined with structural equation modeling (SEM) revealed the cascade relationship among soil nutrients, metabolites, microbial communities, and shoot yield in bamboo ecosystems. The findings thus provide a theoretical framework for understanding rhizosphere microecology under complex soil conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental design, sample collection, and processing

The field fertilization experiment was conducted in a natural C. opienensis (Chinese Virtual Herbarium, 019023120, J. R. Xue & T. P. Yi) forest in Ebian, Sichuan Province, China (29.1807°N, 103.4462°E; 1600 m above sea level). The experiment comprised 18 plots (5 m × 5 m), arranged 5 m apart, with three replicates for each of six treatments: Control (no nutrient amendment), OF (5625 g organic fertilizer/plot), Ba (56.25 g microbial fertilizer containing B. amyloliquefaciens/plot), Ac (56.25 g microbial fertilizer containing (A) chroococcum/plot), OFBa (5625 g of organic fertilizer with an additional 56.25 g of (B) amyloliquefaciens/plot), and OFAc (5625 g of organic fertilizer with an additional 56.25 g of (A) chroococcum/plot). The three replicate plots for each treatment were positioned together in sequence; the treatments were laid out as Control adjacent to OF, followed by Ba, Ac, OFBa, and OFAc. The entire experiment covered approximately 1400 m². The organic fertilizer (organic matter ≥ 45%; N + P₂O₅+K₂O ≥ 5%; N: 1.4%, P: 4.5%, K: 1.7%) was obtained from Henan Lotus Environmental Technology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). The (B) amyloliquefaciens microbial fertilizer (100 billion CFU/g) was purchased from Nongbao Biology Co., Ltd. (Guangxi, China), and the A. chroococcum microbial fertilizer (100 billion CFU/g) from Hairuike Biotech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

In May 2023, surface litter was removed, fertilizers were evenly applied, and the litter was replaced. During the first year of peak shoot production (April 2024), bamboo shoots were collected from all plots. For phenotypic traits (fresh weight, basal diameter, height), five shoots were systematically sampled from each plot as biological replicates. For quality traits, the five shoots from the same treatment were homogenized to form a composite subsample, and each parameter was measured with three technical replicates (Table S1 in Supporting Information, Evaluation indicators and methods used for quality trait assessment of C. opienensis bamboo shoots). Soil samples were collected at the same time as shoots using a five-point method in each plot (10–20 cm depth). The tightly adhering soil surrounding the roots was retained as rhizosphere soil. Three biological replicates were collected for each treatment group. Samples from each sample plot were thoroughly mixed. This pooled soil was split into two subsamples: one for chemical analyses (each index measured in triplicate. Table S2, Evaluation indicators and analytical methods for soil chemical properties), and the other flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 ℃ for DNA extraction and metabolite profiling, which were performed with five biological replicates per treatment. During the second year, all shoots from the three plots of each treatment were recorded, and each shoots was treated as a biological replicate for phenotypic data.

Ebian has a subtropical humid monsoon climate, with an average annual temperature of approximately 16.5 °C and annual precipitation of approximately 1500 mm. The soil, classified as yellow soil (Acrisol, WRB classification) per the Chinese Soil Taxonomy, has a clay loam texture. Detailed sample processing procedures and evaluations are provided in the Supporting information.

DNA Extraction, sequencing, and analysis

Total DNA from soil samples was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit. DNA quality and purity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry. Extracted DNA was used to amplify the V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and the ITS1 region of fungal ITS using primer pairs 338 F/806R and ITS1F/ITS2R, respectively. PCR products were purified using a PCR Clean-Up Kit after agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentrations were determined with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer. Sequencing libraries were constructed, and paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Sequencing data are available in the NCBI SRA database (BioProject ID: PRJNA1279781).

Bioinformatics processing and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) generation were performed using QIIME2 [48] with DADA2 [49]. Bacterial taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA138 database [50], and fungal taxonomy using the UNITE database [51]. Subsequent data processing, visualization, and statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024) and the BioCloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Microbial diversity metrics, including rarefaction curves and α-diversity indices, were calculated using Mothur. Sequences corresponding to chloroplasts, mitochondria, or archaeal taxa lacking phylum-level classification were removed. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM, using the anosim package) were used to assess microbial community structure. Permutation multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to evaluate the influence of soil factors. Singletons and doubletons were excluded, as were differentially abundant ASVs influenced by only one sample.

Differentially abundant ASVs between treatments and Control were identified using DESeq2 [52]. Correlation clustering analysis of ASVs and metabolites was performed using the vegan package [53] to identify key ASVs or metabolites significantly correlated with multiple metabolites. A Spearman co-occurrence network was constructed using ASVs with relative abundance >0.1%. Network topology metrics, including degree and closeness centrality, were compared across treatments using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Extraction and analysis of metabolites

Freeze-dried soil samples were weighed and extracted with methanol: water (4:1, v/v) containing the internal standard L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.02 mg mL⁻¹). Metabolites were extracted from the supernatant by milling, sonication, and centrifugation under low-temperature conditions. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was performed in both positive and negative ion modes using an HSS T3 column coupled to a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive system. Mass spectrometry data were processed using Progenesis QI and annotated by matching against the HMDB and Metlin databases [54]. Only metabolites detected in at least five replicates within one treatment and present in at least four replicates overall were retained. Preprocessed data were analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) with the ropls package. Model stability was evaluated via seven-fold cross-validation. Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) >1.5 and P < 0.05 (Dataset S4, Metabolite identification and their response to treatments).

Root soil metabolite–microbiota correlation

A Spearman correlation network was constructed based on the relative abundance of soil metabolites and microbial ASVs detected in at least 15 out of 25 samples. A correlation coefficient threshold of 0.7 was applied to distinguish non-random associations from random noise [55]. Only ASV–metabolite correlation pairs with coefficients above the threshold were retained for further analysis. To identify potential key metabolites and ASVs, a fast greedy algorithm was used to calculate each node’s intra-module connectivity (Zi) and inter-module connectivity (Pi) based on the network topology [56]. Nodes with Zi >2.5 were defined as module hubs, and those with Pi >0.62 were identified as connectors. Nodes meeting both criteria were designated as network core nodes [57]. Networks were constructed and analyzed using the igraph package [58] and visualized in Cytoscape [59]. Additionally, hierarchical clustering of differentially abundant ASVs and significantly associated metabolites were conducted using the vegan package [60]. Significant correlation pairs with Spearman |ρ| >0.7 and P < 0.05 were retained.

SEM analysis

SEM based on partial least squares-path modeling (PLS-PM) was used to assess the indirect effects of fertilization on C. opienensis bamboo shoot yield. This approach is suitable for predictive modeling, especially with small sample sizes and non-normally distributed data. The hypothesized pathway posits that fertilization alters soil chemical properties (e.g., pH, organic matter, AN), which in turn influence microbial community structure. These microbial changes affect root enrichment of key microorganisms and alter the relative abundance of key metabolites, ultimately enhancing bamboo shoot yield. The SEM model was implemented using the PLS-SEM method via the plspm package in R [61].

Statistical analysis

The phenotypic and quality traits of bamboo shoots, the number of emerging shoots and soil physicochemical properties were analyzed using ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD. All data met the assumptions of normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). Therefore, no data transformation was applied prior to statistical analyses. Rarefaction curves and alpha diversity indices for bacterial and fungal communities were calculated using mothur (v.1.30.2, https://mothur.org/wiki/calculators/). Differences in alpha diversity among groups were assessed by ANOVA with Tukey HSD in R. Microbial community structure similarities among samples were evaluated using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance matrices. Treatment effects were tested for significance using Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) with 999 permutations via the anosim in the vegan package in R. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA, Monte Carlo permutation test) was conducted using the nested adonis in the vegan package to assess the influence of soil physicochemical properties on community differences. When appropriate, differences between fertilization and Control groups were analyzed using ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD. Sequences appearing only once or twice across the dataset were removed. Differentially abundant taxa between treatment and Control groups were identified using the DESeq2 package in R. Count data were modeled with a negative binomial distribution. Significance was assessed with the Wald test, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Local dispersion estimation was applied, and significance thresholds were set at P < 0.01 after visually inspecting the p-value distributions. Clustering analyses of ASV and metabolite abundances were performed using the vegan package in R. Bacterial and fungal ASVs with relative abundance greater than 0.1% were included in the co-occurrence network analysis. Co-occurrence networks were reconstructed based on Spearman correlation analysis [39]. The edges were defined as those interactions with Spearman’s |ρ| >0.70 and P < 0.05, indicating strong associations. Network topological features, including average degree and closeness centrality [62], were compared between Control and fertilized bamboo soil microbial networks using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Results

OFAc improved bamboo shoot yield while maintaining overall quality

In the first year, C. opienensis bamboo shoot phenotype and daily bamboo shoot volume varied significantly across treatments. OFAc showed significantly higher bamboo shoot volume than the Control on day 5 (P < 0.05, ANOVA, Tukey HSD). However, total bamboo shoot volume did not differ significantly among treatments (Fig. 1A, B). Basal diameter differed significantly among treatments (P < 0.01, Tukey HSD, Fig. 1C), with the largest observed in Ba (3.61 ± 0.37 cm) and the smallest in Control (2.70 ± 0.16 cm). Fresh weight also varied significantly (P < 0.0001, Tukey HSD, Fig. 1D), with Ba producing the heaviest bamboo shoots (196.90 ± 27.13 g), a 95.03% increase over the Control (100.96 ± 15.74 g). Bamboo shoot height differed significantly (P < 0.01, Tukey HSD, Fig. 1E), with Ac producing the tallest shoots (33.80 ± 2.17 cm) and Control the shortest (29.20 ± 3.70 cm). Bamboo shoot yield was significantly higher in Ba (29.54 ± 6.96 kg) and OFAc (25.78 ± 2.81 kg) groups than in the Control group (P = 0.0035, Tukey HSD, Fig. 1F). To evaluate bamboo shoot quality, 38 key traits were analyzed (Fig. 1G–J, Table S3, Analysis of bamboo shoot quality after different fertilization treatments). Oxalic acid and tannin levels, which affect palatability, differed significantly among treatments (P = 0.0051 and P = 0.0004, respectively; Tukey HSD, Fig. 1G, H). Ba had the highest oxalic acid (2.47 ± 0.03 mg/g) and Control the lowest (2.11 ± 0.19 mg/g). Tannin content was highest in Ac (1.16 ± 0.09 mg/kg) and lowest in Ba (0.83 ± 0.02 mg/kg).

Calcium and iron contents also varied significantly among treatments (P < 0.0001 for both, Tukey HSD, Fig. 1I, J). OFAc showed the highest Ca (289.00 ± 31.00 mg/g) and Fe (6.61 ± 0.44 mg/g) contents, whereas Control had the lowest (Ca: 178.50 ± 8.50 mg/g; Fe: 1.83 ± 0.02 mg/g). OF showed no significant difference from Control in 37 of 38 traits (Fig. 1G–J, Table S3). In Ba, Asp, Thr, Ser, and lignin levels decreased significantly, whereas Mg and Zn levels increased (29/38 traits unchanged). Ac showed significant reductions in multiple amino acids (Asp, Gly, Ala, Val, Ile, Leu, Tyr, Lys, His, Arg, and Pro), Cu, and fiber, whereas Mg and Na increased (19/38 traits unchanged). In OFBa, fiber and five amino acids (Asp, Ala, Lys, Arg, and Pro) decreased significantly, whereas K, Mg, and Zn increased (27/38 traits unchanged). OFAc showed reductions in fiber, Mg, and Cu, with no significant changes in 33 of 38 traits (Fig. 1G–J, Table S3).

In the second year, basal diameter (P < 0.0001, Tukey HSD, Fig. S1A), fresh weight (P < 0.0001, Tukey HSD, Fig. S1B), and bamboo shoot length (P < 0.0001, Tukey HSD, Fig. S1C) again differed significantly among treatments. All fertilization treatments resulted in significantly higher values than Control. On day 5, OFAc and OFBa yielded significantly more than Control (P < 0.01, Tukey HSD, Fig. S1D). The yield trend mirrored that of the first year, with significantly higher bamboo shoot yields in Ba (15.99 ± 4.16 kg) and OFAc (13.05 ± 1.07 kg) than that in Control (P = 0.012, Student’s t test, Fig. S1E). In summary, Ba significantly increased bamboo shoot yield while also raising oxalic acid levels and significantly reduced some quality traits. In contrast, OFAc enhanced yield without compromising palatability and largely preserved nutritional value, providing empirical evidence for further exploration of the “fertilizer type–nutritional traits–yield” mechanism.

Ac and Ba had the strongest impact on soil nutrients, followed by OFAc and OFBa

Soil physicochemical properties varied significantly among fertilization treatments (P < 0.05, Tukey HSD, Table 1). Ac and Ba exhibited the lowest soil pH values, whereas OFBa and OFAc showed intermediate values, and OF and CK displayed the highest pH levels. Total nitrogen (TN) levels were highest in Ac and Ba, whereas OFAc and OFBa showed intermediate values. OFAc had the highest total phosphorus (TP), exceeding Control, OF, and Ac. Control and OF exhibited significantly higher total potassium (TK) than other treatments. Total organic carbon (TOC) and total organic matter (TOM) peaked in Ba-treated soils, followed by Ac and OFAc. Ammonium nitrogen (AN) did not differ significantly, except for elevated levels in Ac. Available phosphorus (AP) was highest in OFAc, and available potassium (AK) peaked in Ba and OFAc. Total carbon (TC) followed the gradient: Ba > Ac > OFAc > OFBa > Control > OF. These results indicate that single applications of Ac or Ba significantly improved several soil properties (TN, TP, TOC, TOM), while combined treatments (OFAc, OFBa) produced intermediate effects between single amendments and the Control, providing a basis for subsequent analysis of nutrient regulation pathways.

Soil chemistry as a key factor in shaping the soil microbiota of C. opienensis under control and fertilization treatments

To assess the impact of fertilization on microbial diversity, we analyzed 41,046 bacterial and 8,242 fungal ASVs in C. opienensis soils. Rarefaction curves based on observed ASVs approached saturation, indicating sufficient sequencing depth (Fig. S2A, B). Bacterial and fungal α-diversity differed significantly among treatments based on multiple metrics, including the Chao1 index (P < 0.05, Tukey HSD, Fig. 2A, B). Phylogenetic diversity also varied significantly (P < 0.05, Tukey HSD). OFBa showed significantly higher bacterial and fungal α-diversity than Control, whereas OF, Ac, Ba, and OFAc showed no significant differences (Fig. S2C–H). At the phylum level, bacterial communities were dominated by Proteobacteria (31.35 ± 5.02%) and Acidobacteriota (27.90 ± 6.39%), whereas fungal communities were dominated by Ascomycota (40.13 ± 5.51%) and Basidiomycota (26.72 ± 5.14%) (Dataset S1, Mean relative abundance of soil ASVs by phylum across all samples, n = 30). Only Actinobacteriota and Chloroflexi averaged more than 9% abundance among other bacterial phyla, whereas Mortierellomycota and Rozellomycota were the only other fungal phyla exceeding 12% (Fig. S2I, J). PCoA revealed distinct clustering of bacterial (R = 0.7889) and fungal (R = 0.8802) communities by fertilization treatment (P = 0.001 for both; ANOSIM, Fig. 2C, D), indicating significant compositional shifts.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) and PERMANOVA revealed soil chemical properties as key factors shaping bacterial and fungal communities. For bacteria, the following significantly influenced community composition: pH (variance explained [VE] = 7.96%, P = 0.001, Monte Carlo permutation test), TC (9.20%), TN (9.06%), TP (7.71%), TK (9.66%), TOC and TOM (both 8.36%), AN (8.33%), AP (7.64%), and AK (5.62%, P = 0.003) (Fig. 2E; Dataset S2, Results of the PERMANOVA for exploring the variance in the bacterial and fungal communities explained by the soil properties). For fungi, pH (9.60%, P = 0.001, Monte Carlo permutation test), TC (12.35%), TN (11.93%), TP (13.60%), TK (13.32%), TOC and TOM (12.01%), AN (11.96%), AP (11.64%), and AK (8.88%) were significant drivers (Fig. 2F; Dataset S2). Soil properties explained 32.44% of bacterial and 50.40% of fungal community variance (Dataset S2). These findings indicate that soil chemical characteristics play a major role in shaping the rhizosphere microbiota of C. opienensis under different fertilizations, laying a foundation for elucidating how fertilization influences microbial community structure.

Co-occurrence network of Ba was denser, whereas that of OFAc was sparser

Besides differences in microbial diversity and community composition, the co-occurrence networks of the Ba and OFAc microbiomes differed significantly from the Control (Fig. 3A–D; Fig. S3C–D). Other treatments showed no significant differences (Fig. S3A, B, D–F, H). In Ba-treated samples, bacterial network degree (P < 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test, Fig. 3I) and closeness centrality (P < 0.01, Mann–Whitney U test, Fig. 3J) were significantly higher than in Control. Fungal network degree showed no significant change (Fig. 3K), but closeness centrality was significantly lower (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test, Fig. 3L) than that in Control. Similarly, in OFAc-treated soils, bacterial network degree (P < 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test) and closeness centrality (P < 0.01, Mann–Whitney U test) were also significantly higher than Control (Fig. 3I, J), whereas fungal degree remained unchanged and closeness centrality decreased significantly (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test, Fig. 3L). Separate analysis of bacterial and fungal co-occurrence networks in Ba and OFAc communities showed that the bacterial network in Ba was more aggregated and denser than in Control (Fig. S3K), whereas its fungal network was more isolated and sparser (Fig. 3O). In contrast, both bacterial and fungal networks in OFAc were more isolated and less dense than those in Control (Fig. 3E–H). Both Ba and OFAc significantly increased C. opienensis bamboo shoot yield. However, the contrasting network structures and topological features of OFAc compared to Ba and Control warranted further investigation, providing key insights for further exploring how different fertilizer combinations influence microbial interaction patterns and their effects on yield.

A. chroococcum addition (Ac and OFAc treatments) caused the most significant shifts in community composition

To evaluate the effect of A. chroococcum addition on specific ASVs and identify those driving treatment-level differences, we used DESeq2 to compare microbial communities across treatments. Differentially enriched ASVs were identified in OF, Ac, Ba, OFAc, and OFBa treatments relative to Control (Fig. 4A, B). The addition of A. chroococcum (in Ac and OFAc) caused the most significant shifts in community composition, influencing 44 and 66 bacterial ASVs (Fig. 4E and F), and 75 and 156 fungal ASVs, respectively (Fig. 4E and F). Additionally, Ba notably altered the fungal community, affecting 126 ASVs (Fig. 4F), whereas OF and OFBa had minimal effects. Although some ASVs were commonly affected across treatments (Fig. 4E and F), many were uniquely influenced by Ac, OFAc, or Ba. Specifically, 22 bacterial and 40 fungal ASVs were uniquely affected by both Ac and OFAc, highlighting the strong influence of A. chroococcum. Meanwhile, 64 fungal ASVs were uniquely affected by Ba treatment (Fig. 4F). In contrast, OF and OFBa treatments affected relatively few ASVs—2 and 4 bacterial ASVs and 35 and 13 fungal ASVs, respectively (Fig. 4E, F; Dataset S3, DESeq2 results for responsive ASVs, their taxonomy, and the treatments they responded to). Bacterial ASVs that increased in abundance after A. chroococcum addition (Ac and OFAc) were primarily from the Acidobacteria, including Acidobacteriales, Vicinamibacterales, and Solibacteraceae (Fig. 4C). Fungal ASVs that increased were mainly from Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, such as Sordariomycetes and Auricularia (Fig. 4C). In contrast, ASVs that decreased after A. chroococcum addition were taxonomically diverse, spanning over 21 bacterial and fungal phyla, primarily Ascomycota, Rozellomycota, Basidiomycota, Acidobacteriota, Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, and Actinobacteriota (Fig. 4C, D; Dataset S3). Ba uniquely influenced fungal ASVs, mainly increasing members of Ascomycota, including Sordariomycetes, Helotiales, Sordariales, and Talaromyces (Fig. S3B). In OF, only 10 bacterial ASVs responded, with 4 increasing—three of which were Actinomycetes—and 6 decreasing, spanning five phyla. Among 38 fungal ASVs that increased, most belonged to Ascomycota (Fig. S3C, D). In OFBa, 10 bacterial ASVs responded positively compared to those in Control, including Subgroup_2 (log₂ fold change = 22.65). Most ASVs that decreased in abundance belonged to Acidobacteria (Fig. S3E). The addition of A. chroococcum (Ac and OFAc) significantly reshapes bacterial and fungal community composition, highlighting the pivotal role of specific functional microbes in fertilizer-driven community shifts and laying a foundation for subsequent metabolite–microbe interaction analyses.

A. chroococcum addition (Ac and OFAc) significantly increased the richness of soil metabolites in C. opienensis

To investigate how A. chroococcum application alters the soil microbial community, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics was used to profile metabolites in the root soil of C. opienensis under five fertilization treatments and Control. Differential metabolites were identified based on VIP scores from OPLS-DA, fold change, and P-values from univariate analysis. A total of 133 metabolites were detected (Dataset S4). Root soil metabolite composition varied across treatments, with Ac, OFAc, and OFBa showing the most pronounced differences compared to Control (Fig. S5A, B). Twenty-eight metabolites were significantly more abundant in Control than in all fertilization treatments (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A–D, Fig. S5C). Most were fatty acyls (n = 15), including fatty acids and conjugates (n = 11). The rest included organooxygen compounds (n = 7), lactones (n = 3), pteridines and derivatives (n = 2), and prenol lipids (n = 2), and others (Fig. 5A-D, Fig. S5C).

In contrast, A. chroococcum addition (Ac and OFAc) significantly increased the abundance of 70 soil metabolites, including 28 carboxylic acids and derivatives, notably amino acids, peptides, and analogues such as Gamma-Glu-Leu (Fig. 5A, B). Metabolite–microbe networks in C. opienensis soil communities

To identify co-occurring changes between metabolites and microbial ASVs, we constructed a correlation network using their relative abundances across all treatments. The root soil network (Fig. 7A) included 148 ASVs and 91 metabolites connected via 692 links—352 positive and 340 negative—with an average of six connections per node (Dataset S5, List of network connectors, module and network hubs, network topological features, and correlation strength between network nodes). We identified 11 connectivity hubs, 5 modular centers, and 1 central network cluster as potential keystone metabolites or microbes (Fig. 7B; Dataset S5). One fungal hub, Rozellomycota (ASV369), was predominantly negatively correlated with metabolites in Modules 3 and 4, showing negative links with 34 of 36 metabolites, but positive correlations with 4-O-methylgalactinol and Dibenzo-18-Crown-6. Module 2, the largest, was dominated by positive correlations between Rozellomycota ASVs and four key metabolites: 4-deoxyphysalolactone, Fa(18:3 + 1O), 3-hydroxybutyric acid, and diethylene glycol diacetate (27 of 52 links). Negative associations primarily involved bacterial and fungal ASVs from diverse phyla. Module 1 featured negative correlations between the module center Mortierellomycota (ASV151) and 19 metabolites, alongside positive correlations with 18 metabolites linked to ASVs from Proteobacteria, Acidobacteriota, Ascomycota, and Rozellomycota (99 of 118 links). Module 5 lacked a central hub and was dominated by metabolite nodes, including 1-methoxyindole-3-carbaldehyde and fungal ASVs from various phyla. The 11 network connectors included 7 metabolites and 4 microbial ASVs (Fig. 7C). These ASVs were linked to 41 metabolites via 45 positive and 16 negative associations. Bradyrhizobium (ASV2748) and Galerina (ASV1232) were positively correlated with 36 metabolites, primarily amino acids, peptides, and analogues. These connected with the three module centers and seven connectors in Module 2. In contrast, 14 of 16 negative links were formed by fungi (ASV385) and Rozellomycota (ASV234) with 13 metabolites. Approximately half (69 of 143) of ASVs in the network were identified as DESeq2-responsive; of these, 54 responded to Ac or OFAc, and 38 to Ba or OFBa (Dataset S3).

Soil nutrient changes induced by Ac and OFAc treatments drive increased C. opienensis shoot yields

To evaluate the cascading effects of A. chroococcum addition on soil chemistry, microbial community structure, root metabolites, and bamboo shoot yield, a PLS-PM was constructed (Fig. 8). The model posits that fertilization directly alters soil chemical properties (e.g., pH, organic matter, AN), which subsequently influence microbial community structure and diversity. These microbial changes promote the enrichment of key taxa and modulate metabolite abundance, ultimately enhancing C. opienensis bamboo shoot yield. The final model demonstrated good overall fit (GoF = 0.77) and convergent validity (AVE > 0.50 for all latent variables) (Fig. 8; Dataset S6, List of parameters for the PLS-PM). A. chroococcum addition was identified as the strongest driver of soil nutrient changes (path coefficient = 0.9916, P < 0.001), and soil nutrients emerged as the primary drivers of yield (path coefficient = 0.8430, P < 0.001). Soil nutrients also positively influenced microbial community structure (0.6668, P < 0.001), indirectly contributing to yield improvement through effects on key microbial taxa and metabolite profiles. Overall, the model suggests that A. chroococcum addition enhances soil nutrient status, which in turn reshapes microbial community composition, regulates functional microbes and metabolite abundance, and ultimately promotes C. opienensis bamboo shoot yield, thereby laying the foundation for proposing a “soil nutrients–microbes–metabolites–yield” regulatory model.

Discussion

Although numerous studies have analyzed root–soil metabolites and microorganisms under various fertilization regimes, few have examined co-variation patterns between metabolites and microbes in C. opienensis root soils following bio-organic fertilizer application. This study demonstrates that organic fertilizer, microbial fertilizer, and their combinations induce distinct changes in the root-soil metabolome of C. opienensis. Fatty acyl compounds increased under nutrient-limited conditions (Control), whereas carbon- and nitrogen-containing compounds increased following fertilization (Fig. 5A-D, Fig. S5C). A prominent pattern was the elevated abundance of fatty acyls in C. opienensis root soil under Control conditions. Fatty acyls are components of signaling molecules, such as N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) and diffusible signal factor (DSF) family members, which regulate microbial biofilm formation, antibiotic synthesis, and colonization [63–65]. We propose that the accumulation of fatty acyls under nutrient limitation may reflect a plant-driven strategy to reshape microbial communities, thereby potentially enhancing root adaptation and improving nutrient acquisition [38, 66]. This pattern is consistent with the idea that plants could recruit microbes capable of activating nutrients such as phosphorus, nitrogen, and iron to help alleviate nutrient limitations [67–69]. However, these interpretations are based on correlations, and further research is required to directly demonstrate the recruitment of beneficial microorganisms by these compounds in C. opienensis under nutrient limited conditions.

Compared to Control, Ac significantly increased soil TN, TOC, TOM, and AN. OFAc significantly increased TP and AP while maintaining elevated TN, TOC, TOM, and AN levels (Table 1), thus may leading to enrichment of nitrogenous metabolites such as amino acids (Fig. 3C). Alleviating nutrient limitations boosted bamboo shoot production and fresh weight (Fig. 1B) and promoted growth of parent bamboo [27]. Improved soil nutrient levels also altered the root-associated microbial community, potentially selecting for taxa adapted to carbon- and nitrogen-rich environments [65]. The most significant finding of this study was that applying Azotobacter chroococcum, alone (Ac) or combined with organic fertilizer (OFAc), significantly altered the microbial community composition of C. opienensis soil (Fig. 4) and increased metabolite abundance (Fig. 5). Additionally, OFAc significantly increased C. opienensis bamboo shoot yields for the second consecutive year (Fig. 1F, Fig. S1E). The yield improvement in the OFAc treatment likely resulted from the combined effects of organic fertilizer and A. chroococcum, whereas Ac had a weaker effect, possibly due to limited nutrient availability. Ba increased yield may owing to its multifunctional plant growth-promoting traits [70, 71]. For the Ba treatment, RDA showed that Ba-associated microbial communities were consistently positively correlated with key soil nutrient indicators such as AN, TP, and AP, similar to the OFAc group (Fig. 2E and F). PERMANOVA further demonstrated that soil chemical properties explained 22.12% and 41.24% of bacterial and fungal community variation under Ba treatment, respectively (Dataset S2). These results suggest that Ba may also promote yield through soil nutrient–microbe interactions. However, unlike OFAc, Ba treatment induced relatively weaker shifts in key microbes, metabolites, and their associations, while its significant yield increase was accompanied by elevated oxalic acid levels and reduced quality traits; In contrast, OFAc improved yield while largely maintaining nutritional quality, with lower fiber content and elevated mineral elements (Ca, Fe) that support both palatability and nutritional value (Fig. 1G–H, Table S3). The sustained yield increases observed under both Ba and OFAc treatments therefore indicate that nutrient–microbe interactions are a common foundation for yield promotion, albeit through distinct mechanisms: OFAc enhances nutrient availability in synergy with A. chroococcum, whereas Ba inoculation, even without fertilization, likely reshapes rhizosphere microbiota and nutrient cycling processes. These findings highlight that different PGPR may enhance bamboo shoot yield via different but potentially overlapping soil nutrient–microbe–plant pathways.

Changes in soil nutrient availability are well-established as key drivers shaping rhizosphere and bulk soil microbial communities [23, 31, 72]. Our findings, supported by PERMANOVA (Fig. 2E, F) and structural equation modeling (SEM, Fig. 8), confirm that Ac and OFAc treatments induced significant, treatment-specific microbial responses. DESeq2 analysis (Fig. 4) revealed that Ac and OFAc uniquely altered 44 of 66 bacterial and 75 of 156 fungal ASVs, respectively. Notably, several Rozellomycota ASVs (e.g., ASV234, ASV369) were significantly up or downregulated under these treatments (Fig. 4D). Although the functions of Rozellomycota remain underexplored [73], growing evidence indicates their widespread presence in soil and inter-root environments, where they may participate in organic matter decomposition and nutrient cycling [74–76]. These findings suggest that Rozellomycota play a specific role in amended, particularly carbon- and nitrogen-enriched, soil microenvironments and may contribute to nutrient-driven microbial network remodeling (Fig. 8). Notably, an ASV identified as Bradyrhizobium (ASV2748) emerged as a key “connector” in the metabolite–microbe co-occurrence network (Fig. 7C). Under Ac/OFAc treatment, ASV2748 exhibited extensive connectivity with microorganisms and metabolites, forming strong positive correlations with enriched amino acid metabolites (e.g., Gamma-Glu-Leu) (Figs. 5A and B and 7C). Bradyrhizobium, a well-known genus of nodule-inhabiting bacteria, promote the growth of legumes plants through symbiotic interactions [77]. It also plays a crucial role in associative nitrogen fixation (ANF) in graminaceous plants (e.g., sugarcane [78]). The roots of species such as maize [79] and switchgrass [80, 81] exude readily available carbon sources that support microbial nitrogen fixation, supplying a substantial portion of plant nitrogen demand. In our study, Ac/OFAc treatments significantly improved soil nitrogen, carbon, and phosphorus availability (Table 1), which may have provided more favorable conditions for the proliferation and activity of PGPR such as Bradyrhizobium (ASV2748) and potentially enhanced their associative nitrogen fixation. As indicated by the PLS-PM analysis, the pronounced enrichment of this strain in nutrient-rich environments, particularly with abundant organic nitrogen sources like amino acids (Figs. 5 and 6), and its strong connectivity with key metabolites suggest that it may act as a core member of the functional microbiota reshaped by Ac/OFAc treatment. The growth-promoting effects of OFAc possibly contribute, directly or indirectly, to increased bamboo shoot yield.

The OFAc microbial co-occurrence network was “sparser” than that of the Control. The OFAc network exhibited lower extent and near-centrality (Fig. 3I, J). In contrast, the Ba treatment formed a “dense” network (Fig. 3). This difference in network topology likely reflects distinct ecological strategies within the microbial communities. In the eutrophic environment created by OFAc, competitive exclusion promotes stable coexistence among dominant species and maintains ecological niche differentiation equilibrium [82]. Key microbes, including enriched Bradyrhizobium (ASV2748) and nutrient-sensitive Rozellomycota ASVs, likely prioritize enriched substrates for growth and metabolism. This supports plant nutrient uptake or probiotic biomass production instead of forming complex competitive networks, aligning with the SEM results that showed that improved soil nutrient (AN, TOC) availability drives changes in microbial community structure, subsequently enriching keystone taxa (Fig. 8). The effects of nutrient availability on community stability remain debated. Although reduced biodiversity may lower stability [83], increased negative interactions under nutrient-enriched conditions can enhance stability, as observed in both natural and synthetic communities [82, 84]. Network analysis provides valuable insights into microbial interaction patterns. However, in this study, the arbitrarily selected correlation threshold may influence network sparsity (Fig. 3A–H; Fig. S3). At present, available approaches cannot fully overcome the challenges inherent to microbiome data. Most methods rely on linear models based on correlation, regression, or probabilistic graphical frameworks. Although confounding factors are important in microbiome studies, only a few tools account for them [85]. Nevertheless, simple linear models appear to be more frequently applied in the literature, likely because more sophisticated models that incorporate biases often suffer from sharply increased computational complexity and unacceptably long running times [55]. Therefore, while our network analysis highlights potential associations within the microbial community, we acknowledge that these associations should be interpreted cautiously and validated with additional experiments. Moreover, the field plots in this study were arranged sequentially rather than randomly, which may have introduced potential spatial bias. Although all plots were established under similar environmental conditions, this limitation should be acknowledged, and future studies would ideally adopt a fully randomized design to minimize spatial effects.

In summary, Ac primarily enhanced soil TN, TOC, TOM, and AN, while OFAc further increased TP and AP in addition to maintaining these elevated nutrient levels (Table 1). This was achieved through nitrogen fixation and synergistic interactions with organic matter. However, the nutrient-enriched environment did not uniformly promote all microorganisms. Based on the metabolite–microbe co-occurrence network analysis, Ac/OFAc treatment appeared to selectively recruit microbial taxa with key ecological functions. For example, Rozellomycota fungi were associated with shifts in carbon- and nitrogen-related metabolites, while Bradyrhizobium showed strong positive correlations with amino acid metabolism, indicating its potential growth-promoting role. Bradyrhizobium (ASV2748) was identified as a significantly enriched taxon in nutrient-rich soil environments and served as a central node linking microbial and metabolite interactions. These findings suggest that nutrient-driven reconfiguration of the microbial community, characterized by the functional enrichment of beneficial taxa such as Bradyrhizobium, may contribute to the enhanced bamboo shoot yield of C. opienensis observed under OFAc treatment. Our study thus offers a theoretical and practical foundation in using specific PGPR to enhance sustainable bamboo shoot production by regulating soil nutrient–metabolite–microbe interactions.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that different fertilization strategies distinctly affect bamboo shoot yield, quality traits, soil nutrients, microbial communities, and metabolite profiles in C. opienensis. Among them, Ba treatment markedly increased yield but compromised palatability and nutritional quality, whereas OFAc enhanced yield while largely maintaining shoot quality and nutrient balance. Ac and Ba strongly altered soil nutrient status, whereas OFAc and OFBa had intermediate effects. These soil chemical changes emerged as key drivers of microbial community structure, shaping both bacterial and fungal diversity and composition. Network analyses revealed contrasting patterns: Ba promoted denser and more aggregated microbial associations, while OFAc generated more isolated and less connected networks. Addition of A. chroococcum (Ac and OFAc) induced the most pronounced shifts in microbial communities and soil metabolites, enriching amino acids, peptides, and related compounds. Integrative PLS-PM analysis further indicated that OFAc-driven improvements in soil nutrients indirectly promoted yield through microbial restructuring and metabolite modulation. Together, these findings highlight that OFAc is a promising fertilization strategy to improve bamboo shoot yield without sacrificing quality, in contrast to Ba which enhances yield at the expense of palatability. However, the causal mechanisms underlying these soil–microbe–metabolite–plant interactions remain to be fully elucidated and require further experimental validation.
