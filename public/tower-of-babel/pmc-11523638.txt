In vitro study of the treatment of dentin hypersensitivity with gallic acid combined with sodium fluoride
Authors: Xiao Han, Wenjiao Zheng, Jinping Zhao, Shan Xu, Yao Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDentin hypersensitivity (DH) is a common oral condition that is associated with severe dental pain. Pain relief is a key focus of the treatment of DH. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the blocking and antacid effects of gallic acid (GA) combined with sodium fluoride (NaF) on dentinal tubules in vitro.MethodsNinety dentin discs from human third molars were treated with 6% citric acid for 2 min. Then, the surface morphologies of ten dentin discs were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The remaining samples were randomly divided into four groups: the NaF group, which was treated with 1000 ppm NaF; the GA group, which was treated with 4000 ppm GA; the GA + NaF group, which was treated with 1000 ppm NaF + 4000 ppm GA; and the blank group, which was treated with deionized water. The dentin permeability of each sample was measured with a water-filled system before processing and after 7 days of treatment. Dentin morphology and surface deposits were observed by SEM. Then, samples from the NaF, GA + NaF and blank groups were subjected to an acid challenge by incubation with 0.02% citric acid for 2 min. SEM and a water-filled system were used to evaluate the blocking and antacid effects of NaF and GA + NaF.Results1. NaF and GA + NaF significantly decreased dentin permeability. The effect of the GA + NaF treatment was more significant. After acid challenge, both groups still exhibited decreased dentin permeability compared with the initial assessment. 2. Compared with the NaF group, the GA + NaF group had more mineral deposits on the dentin surface and dentin tubules. After acid challenge, the deposits in the GA + NaF group were still clearly visible.ConclusionThe combined effect of GA and NaF on reducing dentin permeability by blocking open dentin tubules is better than that of NaF alone. After acid challenge, the GA + NaF treatment still had a better effect.Keywords: Dentin hypersensitivity, Gallic acid, Sodium fluoride, Combined effect

Introduction

Dentin hypersensitivity (DH) is a chronic oral disease that is associated with severe pain and a low cure rate, and it affects the quality of life of patients [1]. DH occurs when teeth are subjected to thermal, mechanical, chemical or osmotic stimulation. The pathogenesis of DH is still unclear. Currently, scholars who accept the “hydrodynamic theory” believe that DH is related to unsealed dentinal tubules and high levels of dentin permeability [2–4]. Accordingly, DH could be alleviated/treated by blocking dentinal tubules to reduce fluid flow. In recent years, the application of Chinese herbal medicine for the treatment of DH has achieved good effects [5–9]. Galla chinensis has been indicated to regulate the balance of mineralization on the tooth surface and promote remineralization deposition [10–12]. Gallic acid (GA) is the main active ingredient of Galla chinensis. GA has many biological effects, such as anti-inflammatory, antimutation, antioxidation and anti-free radical effects, and its antibacterial, antiviral and antitumor effects have also been proven in the medical field [13–17]. GA significantly improves early carious enamel hardness [18], and GA/metal ion complexes block dentinal tubules by adhering to and forming stable crosslinks on the dentinal surface [19]. Moreover, sodium fluoride (NaF) has a certain effect on the treatment of dentin hypersensitivity [20, 21]. The effect of the combined application of GA and NaF in the treatment of DH is a novel direction in research. There have been no reports on the use of GA and NaF for ameliorating DH. The aim of this study was to explore the anti-DH effects of the combination of GA and NaF. After the effect was confirmed, acid resistance was further studied. We used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to observe dentinal tubules [22, 23], and dentinal fluid flow (DFF) in these dentinal tubules was evaluated with a nanolitre-scale fluid flow-measuring device [24, 25].

Materials and methods

Dentine sample preparation

Human third molars that were devoid of caries or restorations were collected after patients signed informed consent forms. Consent was obtained under a protocol that was approved by the Ethics Committee of the West China College of Stomatology, Sichuan University (WCHSIRB-ST-2013-019). After being thoroughly cleaned, the teeth were stored in 0.5% thymol at 4 °C for no longer than one month prior to use. Ninety dentine discs were cut perpendicular to the long axis of the teeth above the cementoenamel junction with a low-speed, water-cooled diamond saw (Isomet, Buehler Ltd., Lake Bluff, IL, USA). To ensure the accuracy of the sample, one slice was taken for each tooth. Each disc, with a thickness of approximately 1.0 mm, was carefully prepared and inspected to ensure that it was free of coronal enamel or pulpal exposures. To create a standard smear layer, the occlusal surface of each dentine disc was sanded with 600-grit ~ 5000-grit silicon carbide paper after the sample was prepared [26].

Experimental design

The smear layer was subsequently removed by immersing each dentin disc in 6% citric acid (pH 1.5) for 2 min and washing it in deionized water. Then, ten samples were randomly selected for SEM to obtain images of the dentinal tubules after acid etching. The remaining samples were randomly divided into four groups, each consisting of twenty samples. The etched dentin discs were washed and kept wet to evaluate the maximum permeability, which was assigned a value of 100% permeability. Then, different groups were subjected to different treatments.

NaF Group (Positive Control Group): 1000 ppm NaF;

GA Group (Experimental Group 1): 4000 ppm gallic acid;

GA + NaF Group (Experimental Group 2): 4000 ppm gallic acid and 1000 ppm NaF;

Blank Group (Negative Control Group): deionized water.

Each treatment was performed on the same wet surface of the dentin disc. The same person independently performed all the experimental operations. Different groups of dentin discs were placed in the corresponding aqueous solutions. The total contact time of the surface of the dentin disk was 3 min, and then, an electric toothbrush (TriumpH, Ole-B, Germany) was applied to each disk for 20 s. The treatment surface was kept perpendicular to the brush head, and the brushing pressure was approximately 150 g. A new toothbrush head was used for each group. After the treatment had ended, all the dentin discs were washed with deionized water and then stored in artificial saliva (20 mM HEPES buffer, 1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.9 mM KH2PO4, 130 mM KCl and 5 mM NaN3, pH 7.00) in a 37 °C incubator. The samples were processed once every 12 h, and these steps were repeated over 7 days. After 7 days, four samples were randomly chosen from each group and examined by SEM to obtain images of the processed dentinal tubule orifice [26]. The permeability of each sample after treatment was measured with the Pashley model (Lp1).

Then, samples from the NaF, GA + NaF, and blank groups after treatment were subjected to acid challenge with 0.02% citric acid for 2 min. Then, we used a water-filled system and SEM to evaluate the ability of GA combined with NaF to block the dentinal tubules after acid challenge.

Each dentin disc was connected to a water-filled system that simulated pulpal pressure at a pressure of 20 cm H2O. The fluid filtration system and a modified split-chamber unit were used to measure fluid transudation through the dentin discs [24]. A pair of rubber “O” rings with a diameter of 6 mm were used to hold the dentin discs tightly. Fluid flow was measured following the movement of an air bubble trapped within a 25-mL capacity microcapillary tube (Microcaps, Fisher Scientific, Atlanta, GA, USA) that was horizontally positioned between the pressure reservoir and the dentin disc (Fig. 1). The volume flow (µL min− 1) was determined according to the linear displacement of the air bubble [25]. The fluid flow across the dentin disc for each sample was transformed into a hydraulic conductance (Lp) (µL min− 1 cm-2 H2O− 1) by dividing the fluid flow (µL min− 1) by the exposed dentin surface area (cm2) and the water pressure (cm H2O, i.e., 20 cm H2O). The data that were used in the final experiment are the relative permeability (Lp%), that is, the percentage of the maximum Lp value (Lpn/Lpmax *100%).

Pashley model arrangement diagram

Statistical analysis

SPSS 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for Windows was used to perform the statistical analyses. The means and standard deviations of the Lp% values are reported as percentages of the original maximum permeability values [26]. Differences in the DFF rate of each group were analysed by paired t tests. DFF rate reductions among groups were compared by one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test, and the level of significance was set to P < 0.05.

SEM analysis

The samples were dried in a desiccator and sputter coated with gold in a vacuum evaporator (EIKO IB-3 ion coater, Hitachi, Japan). The dentin surface was observed with a scanning electron microscope (Fei QUANTA-200, Eindhoven, the Netherlands), and micrographs were obtained [26]. Each sample was observed and photographed at 5,000× and 10,000× magnification by SEM.

Results

The results of dentin permeability revealed that NaF and GA + NaF significantly decreased dentin permeability by 43.71% and 8.69%, respectively (P < 0.05). GA and deionized water clearly increased dentin permeability by 223.21% and 197.39%, respectively, and there was no significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1). After acid challenge, NaF and GA + NaF both increased dentin permeability to different degrees, with increases to 66.11% and 20.78%, respectively (P < 0.05); compared with LP max, both of these treatments still had a decreasing effect. Compared with the LP1 both treatments still resulted in significant differences (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

SEM revealed that GA + NaF significantly changed the surface topography of the dentin after treatment. Notably, GA + NaF produced more mineral deposits on the dentin surface and dentinal tubules than NaF did. Moreover, the GA group exhibited little demineralization on the surface, and the deionized water group showed no significant difference (Fig. 2). After acid challenge, most of mineral deposits in the NaF group disappeared, whereas the deposits in the GA + NaF group were still clearly visible. Discussion

Studies have shown that [27, 28] the dentinal tubules in the sensitive area of dentin are open. At present, the pathogenesis of dentin sensitivity has not been determined, but among the possible underlying mechanisms, the hydrodynamic theory is widely accepted [2]. According to this theory, external temperature and mechanical or chemical stimulation on the surface of exposed dentinal tubules cause a change in fluid flow (increase in amount or change in direction) in the dentinal tubules, stimulating A-δ fibres and some A-β fibres around dentin cells to produce symptoms of pain or sensitivity. Some scholars [29] believe that the severity of dentin sensitivity is related to the number of exposed dentinal tubules and the diameter of the tubules of the affected tooth. According to Poiseuille‒Hagen’s law, the flow of liquid in the tubule is proportional to the radius of the tubule and the 4th power of the pressure difference between the two ends of the tubule. Therefore, blocking exposed dentinal tubules and reducing the flow of liquid in tubules are effective strategies for treating dentin sensitivity [30]. In the laboratory, SEM is usually used to observe changes in dentinal tubule closure, and a fluid filtration system is used to measure changes in dentin permeability [26, 28, 31–34]. In this experiment, we still chose a 20-cm water column pressure [31, 35–37] to better simulate the pulpal pressure that occurs under physiological conditions. We chose to incubate the dentin discs with 6% citric acid for 2 min, followed by ultrasonic washing and washing with deionized water to remove the stain layer from the surfaces of the dentin discs and open the dentinal tubules before the discs were treated according to their assigned group. Citric acid is a polycarboxylic acid with weakly acidic properties that causes little irritation and damage to dentin, and it can maintain the integrity of dentin collagen while removing the taint layer [38, 39]. To simulate the oral environment and ensure the consistency of the experimental results, the dentin discs were preserved at 37 °C with artificial saliva.

NaF desensitization was first proposed in 1941. Some experiments have shown that the permeability of dentin can be reduced to 82.3% after treatment with 2% NaF [20]. Fluoride ions can promote dentin remineralization, and their chemical affinity for calcium ions leads to the formation of calcium fluoride (CaF2), which promotes the sealing of dentin tubules. On the other hand, NaF can bind to hydroxyapatite to form a more stable fluorapatite [21]. In this experiment, 1000 ppm sodium fluoride aqueous solution was selected as the positive control, and the results were consistent with those of previous experimental reports.

GA is further isolated from gallnut polyphenolic compounds, and it is the main active component of gallnut polyphenolic compounds. At present, the anti-inflammatory effect of GA has been extensively studied, and its anti-inflammatory mechanism is multi-pathway. GA decreased local infammation, and inhibited the in vitro release of neutrophil TNF-a and inhibited IL-1βexpression [40, 41] in a murine model of arthritis. In inflammatory bowel disease GA was found to present its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the LPS-induced phosphorylation of key signaling molecules in the NF-κB/MAPK pathway [16, 42]. Some research showed that GA could reduce the inflammatory cytokines level such as IL-6, IL-1β and IL-17 A in cell culture [43, 44]. GA not only regulates the balance of tooth mineralization and inhibits enamel demineralization [11, 45] but also effectively inhibits the breakdown of dentin collagen, effectively preventing the progression of dentin caries [25], closing exposed dentinal tubules, and repairing damaged cementum. In vitro studies have shown that GA has a remineralization effect on enamel damage [46]. GA has good effects on gingival wounds, and it seems to have promising therapeutic efficacy in enhancing oral wound healing [47]. The experimental results of the GA group revealed that GA did not reduce the permeability of dentinal tubules. SEM revealed that no granular sediment was present at the dentinal tubule orifice, and slight collapse of the dentinal tubule orifice, which was suspected to be caused by demineralization, was observed around the tubule orifice (Fig. 2). This may be related to the acidity of the GA solution (pH = 2.95). Owing to the corrosion of the dentin surface under acidic conditions and the mechanical stimulation by the electric toothbrush, the tubule orifice was further demineralized, and the dentinal tubule permeability was increased.

Experiments have shown that gallnut extract can promote enamel mineralization, exerts synergistic effects with fluorine [10, 24], and can inhibit the decomposition of collagen by type I collagenase in dentin. Collagen is the main component of the restorative dentin matrix. The dense collagen network that is formed by collagen fibres promotes the deposition of mineral salts and the formation of highly mineralized dentin. The results of our study showed that the combined application of GA and NaF effectively sealed the open dentin tubules. The effect of GA combined with NaF in reducing tubule permeability was significantly stronger than that of NaF alone. Therefore, we believe that the combined application of GA and fluorine has better effects in modulating dentin sensitivity, and this is expected to be a new choice for the treatment of dentin sensitivity.

Owing to the complexity and variability of the physiological oral environment, the saliva flow velocity, viscosity and pH all affect the experimental results. Most of the drinks on the market have acidic pH values, ranging from 2.51 to 3.37, and most of the fruit juices and soft drinks on the market contain citric acid. Therefore, to further explore the effect of changes in the oral pH on the combined application of GA and NaF, 0.02% citric acid (pH = 3.2) was selected as the acid etching system for this experiment, and the etching time was set to two minutes according to the relevant experimental protocols [48]. After the acid challenge, the dentinal tubule permeability of the NaF group still tended to decrease (P < 0.05). The resulting precipitate formed by this treatment exerted a certain antacid effect. SEM images revealed that only a very small amount of granular sediment could be observed inside and around the dentin tubule orifices after the acid challenge. This result may have occurred because the granular sediments that accumulated in the previous step were small in both form and quantity, not tightly attached to the dentin disk, partially dissolved after citric acid etching, or washed away by deionized water, resulting in increased permeability and reduced granular sediments. Compared with the initial permeability, the GA + NaF group still exhibited a significant decrease in permeability after the acid challenge (P < 0.05). SEM revealed that the amount of sediment in the dentinal tubules and around the tubule orifices in the GA + NaF group was somewhat lower than that before acid challenge. However, compared with that before GA + NaF treatment, a large amount of sediment still blocked the dentinal tubules, the tubule diameters were significantly reduced, and the tubule sealing effect was obvious (Fig. 3). Compared with that of the positive control group, after the acid challenge, the dentinal tubule permeability of the two groups increased to a certain extent, but the acid resistance effect of the GA + NaF group was significantly greater than that of the positive control group (P < 0.05). The results of SEM also supported this conclusion. Experiments have shown that the combination of GA and NaF has a significantly stronger effect on the sealing of dentin tubules than NaF alone, and the acid resistance of sediment formation in the GA + NaF group is also better than that of the NaF group. The specific reasons and mechanisms still need to be further investigated.

In this study, only SEM was used to observe dentin surfaces and detect the changes in sample permeability, and no relevant research was conducted on the specific composition of the sediment or the mechanism underlying its formation. Relevant experiments will be conducted in the future to provide a basis and strategy for the clinical treatment of dentin sensitivity.

Conclusion

The combined effects of NaF and GA on reducing dentin permeability via the occlusion of opened dentinal tubules are better than those of NaF alone. After acid challenge, both the NaF group and the GA + NaF group still exhibited decreases in dentin permeability, and the GA + NaF treatment had a greater effect on acid resistance. These findings suggest that GA + NaF may be a new option for treating DH.
