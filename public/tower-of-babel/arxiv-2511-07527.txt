Parametric Instabilities of Correlated Quantum Matter
Gal Shavit, Gil Refael

Abstract
Strongly correlated quantum materials exhibit a rich landscape of ordered phases with highly tunable properties, making them an intriguing platform for exploring non-equilibrium phenomena. A key to many of these phases is collective bosonic excitations, encoding fluctuations of the underlying order. In this work, we develop a general theoretical framework for parametric driving of such modes, whereby periodic modulation of microscopic parameters generates resonant two-boson processes. We show that the feasibility and strength of this drive depend sensitively on whether the targeted parameter alters the properties of the bosonic excitations vacuum, linking potential parametric instabilities directly to the fidelity susceptibility of the ground state. The driving facilitates nonthermal melting of the parent orders, as well as stabilization of novel steady states with experimentally distinct signatures. Through microscopic case-studies of correlated electronic systems, we identify promising driving knobs, highlight the role of quantum geometry in the collective modes susceptibility, and propose realistic experimental probes. Collective excitations are a powerful resource for steering correlated phases out of equilibrium, and will likely have several applications in quantum science. Our work provides the toolbox for controlling these excitations.

Our work shows that parametric driving of collective modes can only be made by microscopic parameters that

Consequently, a system’s response to parametric driving at a certain point on the phase diagram probes the nature of quantum fluctuations in the relevant ground-state.

The same term is also related to the fidelity susceptibility of the vacuum of fluctuations, revealing the intimate connection between these two quantities.

The consequences of triggering a parametric instability may be far-reaching.
Once the driving strength exceeds the inverse-lifetime of the driven mode, one expects the parent order to melt, albeit in a nonthermal way (cf. Ref.

II
Parametric susceptibility

In order to examine how can driven collective excitations result in parametric instabilities, we begin by reviewing the problem of parametric resonance in the simplest setting possible: a quantum harmonic oscillator.
Parametric resonances are well-established as a classical phenomenon. Nonetheless, to make contact with our findings in this work (and the formalism employed in Sec.
III
) it will be instructive to consider the quantum system.

II.1
Harmonic oscillator

Consider the Hamiltonian (setting
\hbar=1
henceforth),

H_{\rm harm.}=\frac{1}{2}\left(m^{-1}\mathbb{p}^{2}+k\mathbb{x}^{2}\right)=\omega\left(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(1)

where
\mathbb{x}
and
\mathbb{p}
are canonically-conjugate operators,
\left[\mathbb{x},\mathbb{p}\right]=i
,

\omega=\sqrt{m^{-1}k}
,
and

a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{m\omega}\mathbb{x}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{m\omega}}\mathbb{p}\right)
is a bosonic annihilation operator.

How does a small change of the bare parameters in Eq. (
1
) affect the system?
Concretely, modifying
k\to k+\delta k
,
m^{-1}\to m^{-1}+\delta m^{-1}
, brings about a two-boson term
\delta z\left(a^{2}+a^{\dagger 2}\right)

1
1

1

Introducing a
\propto\left\{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{p}\right\}
perturbation will change the relative phase between
a^{2}
and
a^{\dagger 2}
.
, with

\delta z=\frac{1}{2}\omega\left(\frac{\delta k}{k}-\frac{\delta m^{-1}}{m^{-1}}\right).

(2)

Consider periodically modulating this small-amplitude term with frequency
\omega_{d}
and phase
\varphi_{d}
, i.e.,

H_{\rm harm.}\to H_{\rm harm.}+\delta z\cos\left(\omega_{d}t+\varphi_{d}\right).

(3)

On
parametric resonance
, when
\omega_{d}=2\omega
, solving the equations of motion for
a/a^{\dagger}
in the interaction picture, one finds exponential growth in the number of bosonic excitations
\hat{n}=a^{\dagger}a
,

\hat{n}\left(t\right)\propto\exp\left(\left|\delta z\right|t\right).

(4)

This is the essence of parametric resonance in a harmonic bosonic system.

An immediate consequence of Eq. (
2
) is that parametric driving necessitates changing the ratio
k/\left(m^{-1}\right)
.
This ratio is nothing but the oscillator ground-state fluctuation squeezing, i.e.,

\frac{\langle\mathbb{p}^{2}\rangle_{0}}{\langle\mathbb{x}^{2}\rangle_{0}}=\frac{k}{m^{-1}},

(5)

with
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
evaluated in the ground state.
This simple example of a quantum harmonic oscillator provides us a key insight going forward:
In order to achieve parametric resonance in a system by changing its parameters, one must
alter its ground state
, or the so-called fluctuation vacuum.
Tuning parameters which effectively control the squeezing of the different fluctuation quadratures will indeed have the desired effect.
Identification of the fluctuation quadratures, the effective
\mathbb{x}
and
\mathbb{p}
of the collective modes, will be a key step in our analysis.

II.2
Application of parametric driving to collective modes: Preliminaries

Let us extend this structure for harmonic approximations of collective many-body modes.
Our Hamiltonian
H
will invariably be given in terms of electronic annihilation operators,

c_{{\bf k}\mu}
, for electrons with momentum
\bf k
, and internal index
\mu
, denoting, e.g., spin, valley, band and so on.
In addition, we denote
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
as a reasonable approximation to the ground state of
H
.
For example, considering plasmons, magneto-rotons, or Goldstone modes as the collective excitations of interest, the appropriate reference states are the uniform electron gas, the partially filled Landau level, and the spontaneous-symmetry-broken state, respectively.
We further assume stability of this reference state is, i.e.,
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
remains intact as the relevant reference state of
H\pm\delta H
with a small perturbation
\delta H
.

Next, we would like to assess the existence and potency of parametric driving of collective modes about the reference state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.
For that, we must construct a quantum theory of these bosonic fluctuations (see Sec.
III
).
One then encounters two important objects of this theory relevant to the question of parametric driving (and more generally, to that of quantum quenches):
The vacuum of fluctuations,
|{\rm Vac}\rangle
, and its energy
E_{\rm Vac}
.
Notice
|{\rm Vac}\rangle
is generally different than the original reference state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.
The former is a renormalized version of the latter, accounting for the zero-point motion of low-lying fluctuations (cf. the difference between the BCS variational state and the generalized RPA ground state in Ref.
Anderson 1958
).

We expect a periodic modulation of a perturbation
\delta H
to provide the parametric driving.
The potency of
\delta H
is encoded into the sensitivity of the vacuum to it.
Consider the fidelity susceptibility with respect to
\delta H

Zanardi and Paunković 2006
,

\chi_{F}^{\delta H}=-\frac{\partial^{2}\left|\left\langle{\rm Vac}\left(H\right)|{\rm Vac}\left(H+\lambda\delta H\right)\right\rangle\right|}{\partial\lambda^{2}}|_{\lambda\to 0},

(6)

and the energy susceptibility:

\kappa^{\delta H}=-\frac{\partial^{2}E_{\rm Vac}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}|_{\lambda\to 0}.

(7)

These two quantities control the two-boson terms in the Hamiltonian, necessary for parametric driving.
Consequently,
a perturbation which does not alter the vacuum of quantum fluctuations cannot induce parametric instabilities
.

As an example, consider a ferromagnet with either SU(2) or easy-axis symmetry, see Fig.
2
a.
Small departures from the ordered state must manifest as finite magnetization fluctuation in the directions perpendicular to the order.
On the other hand, these fluctuations are symmetric considering the remnant U(1) symmetry of the ordered state, as long as the change in the Hamiltonian does not alter the ordered state or spoil the unbroken symmetry.
Therefore, the ferromagnet will be impervious to parametric driving of this sort.
This is the case in recently investigated moiré transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), which exhibit a valley-polarization order parameter of the easy-axis category
Li et al. 2021
;
Anderson et al. 2023
;
Cai et al. 2023
;
Zeng et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2023b
.
Though some properties of these ordered states are tunable by a gate voltage, they remain fundamentally outside the purview of this work, due to the restrictive nature of their symmetry breaking.

Conversely, consider an easy-plane ferromagnet, Fig.
2
b.
After spontaneously ordering in some direction on the equator of the associated Bloch sphere, small out-of-plane fluctuations are greatly suppressed, while the softer in-plane collective excitation are not.
The vacuum of fluctuations is squeezed, and the degree of squeezing will depend strongly on generic Hamiltonian parameters, such as the exchange anisotropy.
Inevitably, the vacuum wavefunction is sensitive to such alterations, and parametric instabilities could then arise.
Quantum Hall double layers are a good example of this category
Suen et al. 1992
;
Eisenstein et al. 1992
;
Murphy et al. 1994
, and are our first case study (see Sec.
IV.1
below).

An SU(2) (or easy-axis)
antiferromagnet
, is a less trivial example of a squeezed vacuum (Fig.
2
c).
Consider the Néel vector in the
\hat{z}
direction.
Its rotation towards the
\hat{x}
or
\hat{y}
directions can be considered as different modes of fluctuations.
However, unlike the ferromagnet, they are not conjugate coordinate-canonical-momentum pair
Auerbach 2012
, see Appendix
A
. Squeezing is therefore natural for an antiferromagnet as well.

Furthermore, the linear dispersion expected for the Goldstone modes in easy-plane Ferromagnets, and in antiferromagnets (as opposed to a quadratic mode in SU(2) ferromagnets) is a hallmark of squeezing (see Appendix
A
).

III
Collective modes: derivation and characterization

III.1
Derivation of the collective mode Hamiltonian

As we focus our attention on the properties of the lowest-lying collective modes, we can describe our system’s ordered states by an electronic Slater determinant denoted
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

The ordered state is characterized by the density matrix,

\displaystyle P_{\mu\nu}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Psi_{0}{\Large|}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\mu}c_{{\bf k}\nu}{\Large|}\Psi_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(1+{\cal O}_{\mu\nu}\left({\bf k}\right)\right).

(8)

With no loss of generality, we assume that the density matrix is diagonal in momentum
\bf k

2
2

2

The limitations set by this assumption are rather moderate, since most density wave orders may be captured by working in a basis where one assigns an additional “flavor” index to sections of the Brillouin zone before potentially coupling them.
.

The operator
\cal O
can be thought of as the order parameter of
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.
The Slater determinant density matrix is a projection at any
\bf k
point,

P^{2}\left(\bf k\right)=P\left(\bf k\right)
(its eigenvalues are all
0
or
1
).
Thus,
{\cal O}^{2}\left({\bf k}\right)=1
, and one finds
\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\bf k}\left\langle{\cal O}_{\bf k}\right\rangle=n
,
where
n\equiv\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\bf k}{\rm Tr}P_{\bf k}
is the density.
Henceforth, we will assume for simplicity the absence of a Fermi surface in the ordered state.
As a consequence, both
P
and
\cal O
are now
\bf k
-independent matrices.
This restriction to insulating or semi-metallic ordered states is not necessary in principle.
It may be relaxed, within the same framework presented below, by a more complicated treatment of the fluctuations as compared to the one that follows (cf. Ref.
Khalaf et al. 2021
).

Next, let us parametrize small fluctuations about the ordered
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
state using:

|\Psi_{\phi}\rangle\equiv e^{-i{\hat{F}[\phi]}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle.

(9)

\hat{F}
is an hermitian
fluctuation generator
. Note that
|\Psi_{\phi}\rangle
remains a normalized wavefunction. Also, we restrict the fluctuation subspace by restricting
\hat{F}
to second-order in electronic creation and annihilation operators.
Following the time-dependent Hartree-Fock approach (using elements from Refs.
Khalaf et al. 2020
;
Wu and Das Sarma 2020b
;
Khalaf et al. 2021
), we define the operator
{\hat{F}}
as:

{\hat{F}}[\phi]=\sum_{\bf Q,p}c_{{\bf p+Q}\mu}^{\dagger}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\mu\nu}\left({\bf p}\right)c_{{\bf p}\nu}.

(10)

(Summation over repeated greek indices is implied.)
Notice that one may relate the fluctuation matrix to its hermitian conjugate,

\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)=\phi^{\dagger}_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)
.

The matrix
\phi_{\bf Q}
consists of the various complex amplitudes that parametrize the perturbation from the approximate ground state.
Crucially, in order for
{\hat{F}}
to be able to generate fluctuations about the ordered state, the matrix
\phi_{\bf Q}
must anticommute with the order parameter,
\left\{\phi_{\bf Q},{\cal O}\right\}=0
.
Pieces of
\phi_{\bf Q}
which commute with
\cal O
would result in the same density matrix for
|\Psi_{\phi}\rangle
as in Eq. (
8
), and thus do not generate fluctuations.

This suggests decomposing the fluctuation matrix in terms of the set
\left\{{{\cal M}^{i}}\right\}
, denoting the independent set of matrices in the electronic internal Hilbert space which anti-commute with
\cal O_{\mu\nu}
,

\phi^{\mu\nu}_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)=\sum_{i}\left[{{\cal M}^{i}}\right]^{\mu\nu}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf p}\right)

(11)

\Phi^{i}_{\bf Q}(\bf p)
is now just the momentum-dependent complex amplitudes of the various anticommuting directions relative to the broken symmetry.

To obtain the dynamics of small fluctuations
|\Psi_{\phi}\rangle
we derive the Lagrangian to lowest (quadratic) order in
\Phi
(which will be promoted from c-numbers to dynamic variables),

{\cal L=}{\cal B}-{\cal H},

(12)

where we calculate the following,

{\cal B}=\langle\Psi_{\phi}|i\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_{\phi}\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,{\cal H}=\langle\Psi_{\phi}|H|\Psi_{\phi}\rangle.

(13)

This may be written in a compact form by using the vector
\Phi_{\bf Q}
spanning both fluctuation-generator space and momentum space.
Specifically, the kinetic term has the form (see Appendix
B
for details),

{\cal{B}}=\sum_{\bf Q}\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}{\cal\tilde{B}}_{{\bf Q}}\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{{\bf Q}},

(14)

{\cal\tilde{B}}_{{\bf Q}}^{ij}\left({\bf p,p^{\prime}}\right)={\rm Tr}\left\{-\frac{i}{2}\left[{\cal M}^{i},{\cal M}^{j}\right]{\cal O}\right\}\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}.

(15)

This expression has notable consequences.
The
\cal B
term in the effective action determines the commutation relations between the operators in the quantized fluctuation Hamiltonian.
Eq. (
15
) indicates that
\cal O
defines pairs of conjugate canonical variables analogous to
p{\dot{q}}
terms in classical Lagrangian formulations.
The operators
\cal O
,
p
, and
q
are determined by forming a triplet product with non-vanishing (and imaginary) trace,
{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[p,q\right]{\cal O}\right\}\neq 0
.

The simplest example is an order parameter encoded by a Pauli
s_{z}
operator, with canonically conjugate variables
s_{x},s_{y}
.
Indeed, in the ordered phase,
s_{z}
may be approximated by its expectation value, and

\left[s_{x},s_{y}\right]\sim i\left\langle s_{z}\right\rangle\propto i
, a canonical commutation relation.
We take great pains to emphasize this identification, as it becomes crucial in determining the parametric susceptibility of any system of interest (including the harmonic oscillator example illustrated in the previous section).

Expanding once more to lowest order in fluctuations, we calculate the second part of the action,

{\cal H}\approx E_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\Psi_{0}|\left[{\hat{F}},\left[{\hat{F}},H\right]\right]|\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(16)

which leads to an expression similar to (
14
) for the Hamiltonian part,

{\cal{H}}=\sum_{\bf Q}\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}.

(17)

The exact details
{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}^{ij}\left({\bf p,p^{\prime}}\right)
follow the microscopic calculation of the expectation value of the double commutator in Eq. (
16
), see Appendix
B
.

The equation of motion extracted from the effective Lagrangian is,

i\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\bf Q}=i{\cal\tilde{B}}^{-1}_{{\bf Q}}{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}\Phi_{\bf Q}\equiv{\cal D}_{{\bf Q}}\Phi_{\bf Q},

(18)

where
{\cal D}_{{\bf Q}}
is the dynamic matrix, which has eigenvalue pairs
\pm{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}
.
The spectrum of collective excitation is comprised of the positive eigenvalues of this matrix
3
3

3

The collective mode spectrum is more commonly recovered by solving the characteristic equation

\det\left\{i\omega\tilde{{\cal B}}_{{\bf Q}}-\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right\}_{\omega={\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}}=0

Auerbach 2012
, which is of course an equivalent method.
. The spectrum of collective modes is not our main object of interest here. Instead, we are interested in the situations where a small perturbation
\delta H
to the equilibrium Hamiltonian
H
is capable of driving a parametric instability.

Conceptually, our program would proceed as follows:
First, construct bosonic variables out of the canonically conjugate pairs implied by Eq. (
15
).
A generalized Bogoliubov transformation is then required to diagonalize
\cal H
, such that

{\cal H}=\sum_{n,\bf Q}{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}a_{n,{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}a_{n,{\bf Q}}.

(19)

Formally, we may now promote the
a_{n,{\bf Q}}
variables to quantum
bosonic annihilation operators, obtaining the quantum Hamiltonian of the low-energy degrees of freedom.

III.2
Parametric term

We proceed by extracting the
strength of the parametric driving
introduced by a small perturbation
\lambda\delta H
to the microscopic Hamiltonian.
This may be accomplished by projection of the perturbation into the low-energy subspace of bosonic fluctuations.
[The perturbation-free Hamiltonian of these latter degrees of freedom is given by Eq. (
19
).]
Concretely, what is the amplitude of the anomalous two-boson terms in this projected Hamiltonian?
Generically, the contribution of the microscopic perturbation
has the general form,

\delta{\cal H}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\begin{pmatrix}a_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}&a_{-{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}\delta{\cal\tilde{H}}_{\bf Q}\begin{pmatrix}a_{{\bf Q}}\\
a_{-{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix},

(20)

\delta{\cal\tilde{H}}_{\bf Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&\delta{\cal Z}_{{\bf Q}}\\
\delta{\cal Z}_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}&\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix},

(21)

where
a_{\bf Q}
is a column vector of all the different
a_{n,{\bf Q}}
which diagonalize the unperturbed excitation Hamiltonian (
19
).
Clearly, the
off-diagonal

\delta{\cal Z}_{\bf Q}
part of this matrix [analogous to Eq. (
2
) in the single oscillator case] encodes the parametric susceptibility – our main concern in this work.

Practically speaking, the most challenging part of the scheme described above is the Bogoliubov transformation.
This step becomes exceedingly difficult for large vectors
\Phi_{\bf Q}
– one has to solve a set of
O\left(N^{2}\right)
coupled second-order equations, where
N
scales with the volume of the system.
Instead, one may carry out the program above in one fell swoop, by considering the dynamic matrix
{\cal D}_{\bf Q}

Xiao 2009
.
It is readily diagonalized by a similarity transformation,

T_{\bf Q}^{-1}{\cal D}_{\bf Q}T_{\bf Q}=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&0\\
0&-{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}
is a diagonal matrix whose entries are the positive
{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}
.
Now, we wish to make use of this transformation to diagonalize the bosonic Hamiltonian, and arrive at Eq. (
19
).
As detailed in Ref.
Xiao 2009
(also see Appendix
C
), the same matrices
T_{\bf Q}
also encode the Bogoliubov transformation itself,
diagonalizing the Hamiltonian
by hermitian congruence,

T_{\bf Q}^{\dagger}{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}T_{\bf Q}=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&0\\
0&{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

So we may finally extract
\delta{\cal H}
by the simple expression,

\delta{\cal\tilde{H}}_{\bf Q}=T_{\bf Q}^{\dagger}\left({\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}^{\left[H+\lambda\delta H\right]}-{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}^{\left[H\right]}\right)T_{\bf Q},

(24)

We note that
T_{\bf Q}
above is the one corresponding to the unperturbed
H
, i.e., it diagonalizes
{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\bf Q}}^{\left[H\right]}
.

In the context of parametric driving, we consider modulation of the perturbation
\delta H
with frequency
\omega_{d}
.
The main objects of interest are thus specific elements of the matrices
\left[\delta{\cal Z}_{\bf Q}\right]_{nm}
, such that

{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}+{\cal E}_{m,{\bf Q}}=\omega_{d},

(25)

which is the parametric resonance condition.

III.3
Fidelity susceptibility

The off diagonal parts of the matrix
\delta{\cal\tilde{H}}_{\bf Q}
directly encode the parametric terms induced by the perturbation.
Given this anomalous perturbation, let us examine what is the residual Bogoliubov transformation that diagonalizes the full
perturbed
Hamiltonian.
It is given by

\left(T^{\left[H\right]}_{\bf Q}\right)^{-1}T^{\left[H+\lambda\delta H\right]}_{\bf Q}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}U_{\bf Q}&V_{\bf Q}\\
V_{\bf Q}^{*}&U_{\bf Q}^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(26)

Notice that in the
\lambda=0
the residual transformation is the trivial identity.

Clearly, the off-diagonal component
\delta{\cal Z}_{\bf Q}
in the perturbed Hamiltonian is finite iff
V_{\bf Q}
is non-zero.
In the limit of small perturbations
\lambda\to 0
, the parametric susceptibility and the residual transformation are proportional to one another,

\frac{\partial\|\delta{\cal Z}_{{\bf Q}}\|}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda\to 0}\propto\frac{\partial\|V_{{\bf Q}}\|}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda\to 0},

(27)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Frobenius norm.

We may now consider the fidelity of the unperturbed fluctuation vacuum
|{\bf{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle
with the perturbed vacuum
|{\bf\tilde{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle
(Appendix
C
),

\left|\langle{\bf\tilde{0}}_{{\bf Q}}|{\bf 0}_{{\bf Q}}\rangle\right|=\left[{\det\left(\mathbb{1}_{N}+V_{\bf Q}V_{\bf Q}^{\dagger}\right)}\right]^{-1/4}.

(28)

The momentum resolved fidelity susceptibility is then,

\chi_{F,{\bf Q}}^{\delta H}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\|V_{{\bf Q}}\|}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda\to 0}\right)^{2}.

(29)

Taken together with Eq. (
27
), we find that the magnitude of the parametric driving is proportional to the square root of the Fidelity susceptibility of the fluctuation vacuum.
In the next section, we make this connection even more concrete with a generic example.

III.4
Single mode example

Let us demonstrate these principles on a parametric resonance of a single mode labeled
\bar{n}
for which,

2{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}=\omega_{d}.

(30)

For simplicity, we assume the mode is decoupled from all other modes,
\left[\delta{\cal Z}\right]_{n\bar{n}}\approx 0,\,\,\,\forall n\neq\bar{n}
, and we denote

\delta z=\left[\delta{\cal Z}\right]_{\bar{n}\bar{n}}.

The formalism discussed above is general for arbitrary connectivity of the modes, yet for the salient features we explore here (and in the illustrative examples below) it suffices to consider this simpler case.

Since the parametric driving is expected to be extremely selective due to the resonance frequency, one expects contribution mainly from the driven mode to the susceptibilities introduced in Eq. (
6
).
Decomposing the vacuum zero-point energy to a sum of its components, and the vacuum ground-state to a tensor products of all the different modes,

E_{\rm Vac}=\sum_{{\bf Q},n}E^{0}_{{\bf Q},n},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,|{\rm Vac}\left(H\right)\rangle=\bigotimes_{n,{\bf Q}}|{\bf 0}_{n,{\bf Q}}\rangle,

(31)

consider the mode resolves susceptibilities,

\tilde{\chi}_{F}^{\delta H}=-\frac{\partial^{2}\left|\left\langle{\bf 0}_{n,{\bf Q}}\left(H\right)|{\bf 0}_{n,{\bf Q}}\left(H+\lambda\delta H\right)\right\rangle\right|}{\partial\lambda^{2}}|_{\lambda\to 0},

(32)

\tilde{\kappa}^{\delta H}=-\frac{\partial^{2}E^{0}_{{\bf Q},n}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}|_{\lambda\to 0}.

(33)

Straightforward calculation (see Appendix
D
) reveals the direct relation between these susceptibilities and the so-called
parametric susceptibility
,
\frac{\partial\left|\delta z\right|}{\partial\lambda}
,

\tilde{\chi}_{F}^{\delta H}=\frac{1}{4{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial\left|\delta z\right|}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2},\,\,\,\,\,\,\tilde{\kappa}^{\delta H}=\frac{1}{{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}}\left(\frac{\partial\left|\delta z\right|}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}.

(34)

It is no surprise that these results align perfectly with Eqs. (
27
) and (
29
).
We have thus demonstrated that parametric instabilities of the correlated system directly probe the quantum fluctuations in the reference ordered state.
Introduction of a parametrically resonant perturbation is a sensitive measure of the response of quantum fluctuations to this perturbation.

What determines the susceptibility to a given perturbation?
Making an additional simplification, we assume the single mode above is also decoupled in the original
\Phi
basis, which is often the case (see below).
In that case we may fully characterize a general (mode resolved) fluctuation Lagrangian with the 2-by-2 matrices,

\tilde{\cal B}_{\bar{n},\bf Q}=\begin{pmatrix}&1\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\,\,\tilde{{\cal H}}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega+\left|\upsilon\right|\cos\phi&\left|\upsilon\right|\sin\phi\\
\left|\upsilon\right|\sin\phi&\omega-\left|\upsilon\right|\cos\phi\end{pmatrix},

(35)

with
\upsilon=\left|\upsilon\right|e^{i\phi}
.
Upon diagonalization, the energy associated with this mode is
{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\left|\upsilon\right|^{2}}
(physically stable Lagrangians have
\omega\geq\left|\upsilon\right|\geq 0
).
It is useful to define a
squeezing measure
,

r=\sqrt{\frac{\omega-\left|\upsilon\right|}{\omega+\left|\upsilon\right|}}.

(36)

Notice
r\in\left[0,1\right]
, and that
r\to 0
in the limit of maximum squeezing.

Consider now the infinitesimal perturbation,

\omega\to\omega+d\omega,\,\,\,\,\,\left|\upsilon\right|\to\left|\upsilon\right|+d\upsilon,\,\,\,\,\,\phi\to\phi+d\phi.

(37)

Straightforward calculation (Appendix
D
) then reveals

\delta z=-\frac{\omega+\left|\upsilon\right|}{2}\left[\frac{\left(1+r^{2}\right)^{2}}{2r}d\frac{\left|\upsilon\right|}{\omega}+i\left(1-r^{2}\right)d\phi\right].

(38)

Eqs. (
35
) and (
38
) together constitute a convenient
recipe
towards extracting the parametric susceptibility of the system with respect to a given perturbation.
After detailed calculations [using Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
)], we employ this recipe for several case studies in Sec. (
IV
) below.

Notably, the first term inside the parenthesis in Eq. (
38
)
diverges
in the strong squeezing limit,
r\to 0
.
Thus, we uncover a dramatic effect – the more squeezed the equilibrium fluctuation vacuum is, the more susceptible it is to parametric instabilities.

Why does a modest change of parameters (in the original unperturbed basis) lead to such a drastic behavior?
In that basis, the vacuum state contains a superposition of many excited mode pairs with differing amplitudes, such that the expectation value of the photon number is

\left\langle n_{a}\right\rangle=\frac{\left(1-r\right)^{2}}{4r}
, diverging with
r\to 0
.
The existence of abundant fluctuations in this quantum fluctuation ground state then facilitates a large parametric (or two-boson) response, even if the changes made to the Hamiltonian are exceptionally small.
One may re-phrase this point in terms of squeezing of these original coordinate variables, employing the metaphor of a lemon: the more squeezed the unperturbed vacuum is, the easier it is to further squeeze it.

Furthermore, Eq. (
38
) informs us that parametric driving in such system only occurs if one of the following conditions applies to the perturbation:
Either (i) it modifies the ratio between normal and anomalous term in the fluctuation Hamiltonian, or (ii) it modifies the relative phase of the anomalous term.
We stress once again that the former condition further measures and is highly sensitive to the degree of squeezing in the equilibrium vacuum.

Solution of the equations of motion in the periodically driven case (Appendix
D
) reveals exponential growth in time for both quadrature components of the associated
\Phi_{\bf Q}
vector proportional to
\sim e^{\left|\delta z\right|t}
.
At long times,
t\gg\left|\delta z\right|^{-1}
, the two quadratures have a relative
\pi/2
phase.
The ratio between their (exponentially growing) amplitudes is
\sim r
,
remnant of the squeezing in the equilibrium fluctuation vacuum.

The exponential growth signals the
instability of the ordered phase
with respect to the parametric resonance of this mode.
Dissipation however, may alter the eventual fate of the system.
Consider linear dissipation, independent of “photon” number, inducing a finite collective-mode lifetime
\tau
.
A necessary condition for the parametric instability is
\left|\delta z\right|>\tau^{-1}
.
We note that one source of dissipation expected for the systems we discuss, namely interactions with the particle-hole excitation continuum (e.g., the so-called Landau damping), is absent for the specific scenarios considered above.

Non-linear dissipation, with a rate proportional to the photon number, may lead to even richer phenomenology.
Such dissipation allows the exponential growth at early times, before reaching a non-equilibrium steady state with counter-balancing dissipation and parametric drive
Malz, Knolle, and Nunnenkamp 2019
.
In this state the
\Phi_{\bf Q}
quadratures display persistent oscillations at a frequency
{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}=\omega_{d}/2
, establishing a novel Floquet phase of matter
Hübener, De Giovannini, and Rubio 2018
;
Chan et al. 2023
;
Pareek et al. 2024
.
In the next section, where we discuss concrete microscopic examples, we will encounter the ramifications and signatures of such exotic phases.

IV
Case studies

In the following we will explore several possible realizations of parametrically driven correlated materials, each highlighting different facets of our general discussion above.
Practically speaking, one is interested in the precise strength of the perturbation-induced parametric term.
Given the expected exponential growth induced by it, it is reasonable to compare the energy of the two-boson term
\delta z
to the energy of the driven collective mode
{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}
.
This is an effective measure of the number of evolution cycles of that mode before the parametric amplification is evident.

We define the
parametric efficiency

\eta_{{\rm par}.}^{\left(p\right)}
with respect to a small change
\delta p
in the parameter
p
[in direct analogy to Eq. (
2
), where the efficiency is unity],

\frac{\left|\delta z\right|}{{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{\delta p}{p}\right|\eta_{{\rm par}.}^{\left(p\right)}

(39)

With regards to our general single-mode result Eq. (
38
), The parametric efficiency corresponding to the
\upsilon
and
\omega
variables is expressed entirely in terms of the squeezing parameter,

\eta_{{\rm par}.}^{\left(\upsilon,\omega\right)}=\frac{1-r^{4}}{2r^{2}},

(40)

which diverges as the squeezing becomes more extreme,
r\to 0
.
In the examples below, we will generally find that the parametric efficiency is of order unity, unless the modulated parameter drives a phase transition of the microscopic Hamiltonian.
In that case the efficiency may diverge, reflecting an expected divergence of the corresponding fidelity susceptibility.

We note that linear dissipation would compete with the driving, leading to reduced effective efficiency
\tilde{\eta}=\eta\left(1-\frac{{\cal E}_{\bar{n},{\bf Q}}}{Q\delta_{z}}\right)
, where
Q
is the quality factor of the driven mode.

IV.1
Quantum Hall double layer

We first consider the well-studied example of a double layer quantum Hall system at total odd integer filling
\nu=2n+1

MacDonald, Platzman, and Boebinger 1990
;
Yang et al. 1994
.
Both layers experience equal external chemical potential, such that in the absence of interactions and interlayer tunneling both are at filling factor
n+\frac{1}{2}
.
Before introducing the specifics of this model, we note that the analysis below easily captures the salient features of many strongly-interacting (or flat-band) double layers, expected to show a similar (or generalized) excitonic ordered state, including electron-hole double layers
Du et al. 2017
, graphene or bilayer graphene double layers
Min et al. 2008
;
Li et al. 2017b
, TMD double layers
Fogler, Butov, and Novoselov 2014
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Nguyen et al. 2025
, and moiré systems
Rickhaus et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2022
.

Projecting the problem to the
n
th Landau level, the degrees of freedom are described by fermionic annihilation operators
c_{\bf k}=\left(c_{{\bf k}1},c_{{\bf k}2}\right)^{T}
belonging to the two layers, and Pauli operators
\ell_{i}
, such that
\ell_{z}=\pm 1
for the different layers.
The projected microscopic Hamiltonian we consider has three parts,

H_{\rm QH-2\ell}=H_{\rm C}+H_{z}+H_{\rm tun.}

(41)

The interaction terms are given by

H_{\rm C}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\bf q}V_{\bf q}^{0}\tilde{\rho}_{\bf q}\tilde{\rho}_{\bf-q},\,\,\,\,\,H_{z}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\bf q}V_{\bf q}^{z}\tilde{\rho}^{z}_{\bf q}\tilde{\rho}^{z}_{\bf-q},

(42)

where the projected density operators are

\tilde{\rho}_{\bf q}=\sum_{\bf k}c^{\dagger}_{\bf k+q}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}c_{\bf k}\,\,\,\,\,\tilde{\rho}^{z}_{\bf q}=\sum_{\bf k}c^{\dagger}_{\bf k+q}\ell_{z}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}c_{\bf k},

\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}^{\alpha\beta}=\delta^{\alpha\beta}f\left({\bf q}\right)e^{-i\frac{{\Omega}}{2}{\bf k}\times\left({\bf k+q}\right)},

with

f\left({\bf q}\right)=e^{-\frac{1}{4}{\Omega}\left|{\bf q}\right|^{2}}L_{n}\left({\Omega}\left|{\bf q}\right|^{2}\right)
(
{\Omega}=l_{B}^{2}
, i.e., the magnetic length squared,
L_{n}
is the
n
Laguerre polynomial).

With nominal layer separation
d
, the two interaction strengths are given by

V_{{\bf q}}^{0/z}=\frac{V_{{\bf q}}^{C}\left(1\pm e^{-qd}\right)}{2},

(43)

with
V_{{\bf q}}^{C}
the Fourier transformed Coulomb repulsion in two dimensions.
The
H_{z}
interaction therefore accounts for the difference between intra- and interlayer interactions.
The zero momentum component of
V_{{\bf q}}^{z}
is the capacitive energy of the effective capacitor formed by the “double-plate” structure
MacDonald, Platzman, and Boebinger 1990
.
As such, the system is expected to disfavor layer-polarization (
\ell_{z}
order), instead tending towards inter-layer coherence, forming an effective easy-plane
U\left(1\right)
ferromagnet.

The
U\left(1\right)
symmetry may be broken
explicitly
by single-particle tunneling between the layers
4
4

4

The “
\rm sas
” label indicates that in the absence of interactions, the interlayer tunneling splits the energy of the layer-symmetric and layer-antisymmetric electron wavefunctions by an energy of
\pm\Delta_{\rm sas}
.
,

H_{{\rm tun.}}=-\Delta_{\rm sas}\sum_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}}^{\dagger}\ell_{x}c_{{\bf k}}.

(44)

One expects the appearance of this term to open a gap in the otherwise gapless Goldstone mode, demonstrated below.

Our reference ordered state may thus be characterized by the matrix

{\cal O}=\ell_{x}
, such that the relevant fluctuation generators are readily found to be
\left\{{{\cal M}^{i}}\right\}=\ell_{y},\,\ell_{z}
.
These identifications allow us to employ the scheme outlined in Sec.
III
in a straight forward manner, which we carry out in detail in Appendix
E
.
Focusing our attention on the lowest lying Goldstone mode mentioned above, we recover the following effective single-mode parameters,

\omega^{\rm QH-2\ell}_{\bf Q}=\frac{\rho^{0}_{s}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}E_{\rm easy}+\Delta_{\rm sas},

(45)

\upsilon^{\rm QH-2\ell}_{\bf Q}=\frac{\rho^{z}_{s}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}E_{\rm easy},

(46)

where
\rho_{s}^{0}
and
\rho_{s}^{z}
are stiffness parameters proportional to integrals over
V^{0}_{\bf q}
and
V^{z}_{\bf q}
, respectively, and the easy-plane anisotropy is

E_{\rm easy}=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{{\bf q}}\left[V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}-V_{{\bf q}}^{z}f^{2}\left({\bf q}\right)\right].

(47)

At small momenta, the spectrum of this mode is

{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}=\sqrt{\left({\cal E}_{{\bf 0}}^{{\rm QH-2\ell}}\right)^{2}+\left(\Delta_{{\rm sas}}\rho_{s}^{0}+E_{{\rm easy}}\frac{\rho_{s}^{0}-\rho_{s}^{z}}{2}\right)\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}}
,
with the gap

{\cal E}_{\bf 0}^{\rm QH-2\ell}=\sqrt{\Delta_{\rm sas}\left(E_{\rm easy}+\Delta_{\rm sas}\right)}.

(48)

As expected, for
\Delta_{\rm sas}=0
, the collective mode becomes gapless and has linear dispersion.

As an example, let us consider driving the lowest-energy
{\bf Q}=0
mode, i.e., one modifies the parameters of the system at a frequency
\omega_{d}=2{\cal E}_{\bf 0}^{\rm QH-2\ell}
.
The strength of the parametric term is obtained by employing Eq. (
38
),

\delta z^{\rm QH-2\ell}=\frac{{\cal E}_{\bf 0}^{\rm QH-2\ell}}{2}\frac{\delta\left[\log\frac{\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}{E_{{\rm easy}}}\right]}{1+\frac{\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}{E_{{\rm easy}}}},

(49)

and the corresponding parametric efficiencies,

\eta_{{\rm par}.}^{\left(\Delta_{{\rm sas}}\right)}=\eta_{{\rm par}.}^{\left(E_{{\rm easy}}\right)}=\frac{E_{{\rm easy}}}{E_{{\rm easy}}+\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}\approx 1,

(50)

where the last approximation is reasonable in most experimental systems, where the tunneling energy is smaller by orders of magnitude compared to the interaction anisotropy energy scale.
The upshot of Eq. (
49
) is that parametric driving of the correlated double-layer system may be induced by any perturbation that modifies the energetic ratio between the interlayer tunneling and the capacitive anisotropy.
One direct way to achieve this is to modulate the tunneling barrier between the layers (e.g, electrostatically or via dynamic piezoelectrical modulation),
Which directly impacts the interlayer tunneling strength
\Delta_{\rm sas}
.

An alternative, more experimentally accessible approach is to modulate a gate-voltage controlled displacement electric field.
Applied perpendicularly to the double-layer structure, it will induce layer polarization, i.e., a finite
\langle\ell_{z}\rangle
component in the order parameter, rendering the fluctuations “less squeezed” in the in-plane direction, see Fig.
3
a.
(We note that an in-plane magnetic field may lead to a related effect, where the which-layer symmetry is spontaneously broken
Radzihovsky 2001
.)
With a high enough displacement field, the in-plane coherence is destroyed and the system becomes layer polarized.
Crudely speaking, we can emulate this change by an effective change of the anisotropy energy.

Suppose that in the vicinity of the transition this anisotropy depends on the appropriate gate-voltage
V
as

E_{\rm easy}\propto V^{\alpha}
.
It is straightforward to extract

\eta_{{\rm par.}}^{\left(V\right)}=\alpha\eta_{{\rm par.}}^{\left(E_{{\rm easy}}\right)}.

(51)

Drawing from experimental results in relevant systems
Liu et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017c
, the transition has a width of order
\sim 10
mV.
This suggests that in the vicinity of the transition a
modulation of several mV
of the voltages applied are sufficient to induce a parametric instability, which becomes apparent after a few (
\lesssim 10
) driving cycles.

We note that the collective mode Hamiltonian parameters depend here on the quantum geometry of the projected wavefunctions through the
f^{2}
term in (
47
) (see also Appendix
E
and Ref.
Wu and Das Sarma 2020b
, relating the stiffness to quantum geometry]).
Such dependence arises quite ubiquitously, which we demonstrate in the next case study.
The ability to temporally modulate the quantum geometric properties of correlated electrons is thus a valuable resource in coherently driving such collective excitations.

What are the consequences of the parametrically driven collective mode in the double layer excitonic system?
While the system is pumped, or once a (dissipation-balanced) non-equilibrium steady-state is established, two remarkable things occur in the system.
First, the effective collective isospin of the electrons acquires a finite oscillating layer polarization (
\langle\ell_{z}\rangle
) component.
The electric field produced by this charge oscillating between the layers is expected to manifest as a finite bias coherent interlayer tunneling peak, that should appear at a bias-voltage corresponding to half of the driving frequency
Spielman et al. 2000
;
Spielman et al. 2001
;
Joglekar and MacDonald 2001
;
Stern et al. 2001

V_{\rm bias}=\hbar\omega_{d}/\left(2e\right)
(see Fig.

Second, the oscillating
\langle\ell_{y}\rangle
(layer coherence) component modulates the coherent phase of the condensate.
A possible route to probe this sort of modulation is by employing an excitonic analog of a Josephson junction (Fig.

One thus expects an ac-current between the two parts of the junction, counterflowing on the top and bottom layers
Kellogg et al. 2004
;
Tutuc, Shayegan, and Huse 2004
.
Through the Josephson relations, this current will be comprised of harmonics of the

IV.2
Complex orders in a flat band model

Flat band systems, particularly graphene moiré materials, have a richer landscape of collective excitations and a non-trivial complex hierarchy of symmetry breaking states
Bultinck et al. 2020
;
Wagner et al. 2022
;
Bultinck, Chatterjee, and Zaletel 2020
;
Shavit et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Kwan et al. 2024
.
We apply our approach to a model which reproduces the salient features of the hierarchy of ordered states at half-filling relative to the charge neutral point in these systems.
We will restrict our attention to a toy model which neglects the finite bandwidth of the moiré flat bands
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
, the bands’ fragile topological properties
Po et al. 2019
, as well as the physics pertaining to the spin degrees of freedom.
These features are important for determining the phase diagram of a material, but not so much for elucidating the issue of parametric instabilities.

The single-particle Hamiltonian of the two-dimensional system we consider here has valley
\tau_{i}
, pseudo-spin (or sub-lattice)
\sigma_{i}
, and orbital
\rho_{i}
degrees of freedom (all Pauli matrices),

H_{0}=-t\sum_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}}^{\dagger}\left[h_{x}\left({\bf k}\right)\rho_{x}+h_{y}\left({\bf k}\right)\rho_{y}-\Delta_{\sigma}\sigma_{z}\right]c_{{\bf k}},

(52a)

h_{x}\left({\bf k}\right)=\frac{1+\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\cos\alpha_{{\bf k}}+\frac{1-\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\cos\beta_{{\bf k}},

(52b)

h_{y}\left({\bf k}\right)=\frac{1+\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\sin\alpha_{{\bf k}}+\frac{1-\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\sin\beta_{{\bf k}}.

(52c)

The model is related to a tunable quantum metric model introduced in Refs.
Hofmann et al. 2022a
;
Hofmann, Berg, and Chowdhury 2023
.
However, presently the momentum-dependent functions
\alpha_{\bf k}
and
\beta_{\bf k}
, which determine the quantum geometric properties of the resultant flat bands, are related to each other by a 45-degree rotation, i.e.,

\alpha_{{\bf k}}=\zeta\left(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}\right),

(53)

\beta_{{\bf k}}=\zeta\left(\cos\frac{k_{x}+k_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}+\cos\frac{k_{x}-k_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(54)

The parameter
\zeta
characterizes a quantum geometry related length scale (in units of the unit cell), referred to henceforth as the quantum metric length
Ma, Hu, and Law 2025
.

The Hamiltonian Eq. (
52a
) has a total of eight
identically flat
bands: for
\Delta_{\sigma}=0
(which we assume henceforth unless mentioned otherwise) one finds four valence bands sitting at energy
-t
, and four conduction bands at
+t
.
Our interest will lie in one set of these isolated bands, e.g., the valence bands.
Within such set, reminiscent of the decomposition of flat bands in twisted graphene, one may define a good quantum number which further classifies the bands,
{\cal C}=\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}
.
(In TBG, this would correspond to the actual Chern number associated with each of these bands, yet in our model the Berry curvature vanishes everywhere – the model is topologically trivial, yet its quantum geometry is highly non-trivial for finite values of
\zeta
.)

We examine interactions between the flat band electrons, projecting to the lowest lying four bands.
The simplest density-density interaction term (that will dominate the interacting physics)
H_{\rm C}
, has the same form as in Eq. (
42
), except its density form factors are now given by

\Lambda_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}={\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\sigma_{z}\tau_{z},

(55)

with

{\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}=\cos\left(\alpha_{\bf k}-\alpha_{\bf k^{\prime}}\right)
,
and

{\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}-{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}=\cos\left(\beta_{\bf k}-\beta_{\bf k^{\prime}}\right)
.
If we consider the half-filled scenario, completely filling two bands out of the lowest four, it is straightforward to demonstrate that on the level of Hartree-Fock, the ground state manifold is comprised of all order parameters which commute with
{\cal C}
(see Appendix
F
),

\left[{\cal O}_{\rm g.s.},{\cal C}\right]=0.

(56)

Next, let us consider two sub-leading interaction terms, whose presence favors a subset of ordered state of the manifold in Eq. (
56
).
They will be given by

H_{\rm K}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\hat{M}=\sigma_{y}\tau_{x},\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}}\sum_{\bf q}u^{\rm K}_{\bf q}\rho^{\hat{M}}_{\bf q}\rho^{\hat{M}}_{\bf-q},

(57)

H_{\rm T}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\hat{M}=\sigma_{x}\tau_{x},\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}}\sum_{\bf q}u^{\rm T}_{\bf q}\rho^{\hat{M}}_{\bf q}\rho^{\hat{M}}_{\bf-q},

(58)

with
\rho^{\hat{M}}_{\bf q}=\sum_{\bf k}c_{{\bf k+q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k+q,k}}\hat{M}c_{{\bf k}}
, and
u^{\rm K/T}_{\bf q}<0
such that each interaction term favors the order parameters in its corresponding
\hat{M}
matrices.
For the sake of simplicity, we will consider these secondary interactions to be short-ranged, i.e., momentum independent.
We then employ the following definitions,

u=-\left(u^{\rm K}+u^{\rm T}\right)>0
,

u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm T}}-u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm K}}=-\Delta_{\rm TK}<0
(such that positive
\Delta_{\rm TK}
means that the so-called T-order is energetically preferable),
and

u_{\zeta}=u\left[1-J_{0}^{4}\left(2\zeta\right)\right],

(59)

(the quantum metric length dependence originates in integration over the form factors).
We will further assume that
u_{\zeta}>\Delta_{\rm TK}
.
This last assumptions simply ensures that the intervalley coherent orders precipitated by these secondary interactions are energetically favorable to all others in the
\cal C
manifold.

Before delving into the relevant fluctuations in this toy model, a brief interlude is in order, regarding the motivation for the interaction terms introduced above.
In pristine twisted bilayer graphene it has been broadly established, both analytically and numerically
Bultinck et al. 2020
;
Wagner et al. 2022
;
Hofmann et al. 2022b
, that the preferred order at half-filling is the so-called Kramers intervalley coherent order (KIVC), analogous to the K-order encouraged by Eq. (
57
).
This order, however, is sensitive to the appearance of strain in realistic devices
Parker et al. 2021
;
Wagner et al. 2022
;
Shavit et al. 2023
, often leading to the emergence of a rather different intervalley coherent phase, the so-called Kekulé spiral
Kwan et al. 2021
.

Still, even in nearly-pristine devices, with little to no strain, KIVC order is missing.
Instead, a third intervalley coherent phase appears in experiments
Nuckolls et al. 2023
– the time-reversal invariant intervalley coherent order (TIVC), analogous to the T-order preferred by Eq. (
58
).
It has been suggested that electron-phonon interactions involving exchange of valley momentum favor the formation of the TIVC state over KIVC
Kwan et al. 2024
.
This scenario is emulated in our model by the choice
\Delta_{\rm TK}>0
.

Finally, alignment of the twisted graphene devices to the underlying hexagonal Boron Nitride (hBN) substrate breaks the inversion symmetry, approximately captured by a sublattice polarization term
\sim\sigma_{z}

Jung et al. 2015
;
Zhang, Mao, and Senthil 2019
, also included as a single-particle effect in our model, Eq. (
52a
).

The following discussion is divided between two scenarios, each motivated by a distinct realization of the proposed model.
The corresponding parametric driving, and ensuing instabilities of the order parameter, will therefore have notable differences.

We find that at large wavelengths the gapless Goldstone mode has the effective parameters (Appendix

\Delta_{\rm TK}\to\Delta_{\rm TK}+\delta\Delta_{\rm TK}\cos\left(\omega_{d}t\right).

Zooming out from the specific model under consideration, this is a generic scenario of interest:
Slight modification of the system parameters, while remaining throughout the cycle within the same phase of the equilibrium phase diagram.
The parametric susceptibility, however, unlocks the possibility for intriguing out-of-equilibrium effects.

Second, probing the strength of the parametric response, one gains access not only to the gap of this mode (which is may be recovered independently by locating the resonance frequency), but also to secondary features of the ground state energy hierarchy via the quantity

\sigma

\Delta_{\sigma}

We may again organize then in canonically conjugate pairs,

\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}
with
\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}

\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}
with
\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}

\Delta_{\rm TK}\neq 0

The lowest-lying modes corresponding to the two generator pairs are further expected to be degenerate (
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{y}

We note here that the ordering scenario described here is analogous to the antiferromagnet discussed in Fig.

\Delta_{\rm\sigma}
playing the role of a staggered external magnetic field.
To see that this is the case, simply perform a Pauli matrix replacement which conserves all commutation and anti commutation relations (
s^{i}
and
p^{i}
are Pauli matrices describing fictitious “spin” and “sublattice” degrees of freedom):
The antiferromagnetic order is now
\sigma_{z}\to s^{z}p^{z}
,
Néel vector rotations (or T-order generators) are
\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}\to s^{x}
,
\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}\to s^{y}

\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}\to s^{x}p^{z}

\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\to s^{y}p^{z}

\omega^{\left(1,2\right)}_{\bf Q}=\frac{\rho_{s}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+\Delta_{\sigma}-\frac{u_{\zeta}}{2},

\upsilon^{\left(1,2\right)}_{\bf Q}=\frac{\Delta_{\rm TK}}{2}.

Indeed, as expected, the squeezing of lowest-lying collective modes is given by the breaking of T/K symmetry in the model.

{\bf Q}=0

{\cal E}^{\left(1,2\right)}_{\bf 0}=\sqrt{\left(\Delta_{\sigma}-\frac{u_{\zeta}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\right)^{2}}
.
Unsurprisingly, the gap closes when the
\sigma_{z}

\Delta_{\sigma}=\frac{u_{\zeta}+\Delta_{\rm TK}}{2}

Let us now see how the vicinity to a phase transition affects the parametric susceptibility once again.

As an example, consider small changes in the Hamiltonian parameters that affect the quantum geometry of electrons in the projected bands, modifying slightly the quantum metric length
\zeta
.
In realistic devices, this can be the result of modulating gate voltages, electric displacement fields, or strain.
More exotic possibilities include modulation of external driving in Floquet systems, where the Floquet driving subtly controls single-particle properties
Kiselev, Rudner, and Lindner 2024

The parametric efficiency to such a modification may be extracted with the help of Eq. (
38

\eta_{{\rm par}.}^{\left(\zeta\right)}=\frac{4u\Delta_{{\rm TK}}\zeta\left|J_{0}^{3}\left(2\zeta\right)J_{1}\left(2\zeta\right)\right|}{\left(\Delta_{\sigma}-\frac{u_{\zeta}+\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\right)\left(\Delta_{\sigma}-\frac{u_{\zeta}-\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\right)}.

Notice that the left term in the denominator vanishes at the phase transition to the T-order, causing a divergence in the parametric efficiency (also see Fig.

Once more, this can be understood from the fidelity susceptibility diverging at the transition.

The dependence on
\zeta
comes from the derivative of
u_{\zeta}
, and is maximized for
\zeta\approx 0.5

This particular value is not universal – the maximizing value will typically depend on the
range of interactions
.
Under our present assumptions, the
u
interactions are maximally short-ranged, thus a change of length scale associated with the projected band electrons, i.e.,
\sim\zeta

Generalizing this observation, small changes in quantum geometrical parameters are expected to have the most pronounce impact when the length-scale associated with the interaction is comparable to the one derived from the quantum metric.
The interplay between these energy scales has been demonstrated to be crucial in the context of fractional Chern insulators

Emanuel, Keselman, and Oreg 2025

A parametric response probe may thus shed much needed light on the prospect of realizing these phases in possible candidate materials.

Let us once more estimate the physical modulation amplitudes required to observe a significant parametric amplification.
We examine mirror-symmetric twisted-trilayer graphene, which is the analog of TBG, yet has an additional tuning knob: an electric displacement field which hybridizes the TBG bands with an additional Dirac-like band.
Empirically, this hybridization roughly leaves the bandwidth intact, while non-negligibly modifying the Bloch wavefunctions.
Ref.
Shen et al. 2023
reported a transition between two types of symmetry-breaking correlated phase as a function of the displacement field.
Quantitatively, a change of order 50 mV/nm in displacement field translated to a
\sim 30\%
increase of the order parameter.
This indicates that a
modulation of order 10 mV/nm
of the displacement field (which is less than
0.5\%
of the experimentally accessible field of view) can trigger appreciable parametric amplification of the collective modes.

The response to driving in this scenario would be an oscillatory appearance of T-order and K-order components in the transient order parameter, with the amplitude of the former being stronger by the squeezing factor of
r^{-1}=\sqrt{1+\frac{\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{\Delta_{\sigma}-\frac{u_{\zeta}+\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}}}
compared to the latter.
We note that in the analogous twisted graphene system, the T-order, i.e., TIVC, couples to a real-space charge density with a Kekulé pattern
Călugăru et al. 2022
;
Hong, Soejima, and Zaletel 2022
;
Nuckolls et al. 2023
;
Kwan et al. 2024
.
Thus, the parametrically driven state will give rise to a charge density wave oscillating at half the drive frequency, potentially detectable using available time-resolved STM techniques
Liang et al. 2023
;
Terada et al. 2010
.

V
Discussion and outlook

Parametric driving of non-trivial low-energy collective excitations is a new and exciting avenue for controlling, engineering, and probing many-body correlated states.
The driven dynamics proposed here are enabled by recent experimental advances, the large degree of tunability, and the rich phase diagram that new correlated electronic platforms allow.
We find that modulating microscopic parameters at the right frequency, allows one to approach phenomena that are otherwise practically inaccessible in the solid-state context.

The periodic driving we envision is simple to implement in the lab.
Typically, modulating the voltages that control density, electrical displacement fields, or strain fields would do.
In more intricate implementations (which may be more relevant for the
\sim
THz modulation range), a modulation of an optical signal may induce driving in some effective parameters.
(and may be directly driving a non-equilibrium state in the material
McIver et al. 2020
;
Merboldt et al. 2025
, or controlling a thoughtfully placed photoconductive switch
Gallagher et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2025
.)

The measurable signature of the parametric drive, i.e., the amplitude of its response, may be system-specific, and depends on the detailed character of the low-energy ordered state.
In the excitonic double layer example (Sec.
IV.1
), interlayer tunneling spectroscopy reveals clear signatures of the parametrically driven state.
Systems with richer structure, e.g., the flat-band “pseudo-TBG” model we explore in Sec.
IV.2
, has more detection possibilities, such as time-varying charge-density ordering and modulated optical responses.
In all cases, however, the parametric response is always
sub-harmonic
with respect to the driving frequency. All of our examples have
\omega_{d}
corresponding to twice the frequency of the targeted collective mode, and thus the parametric response would prominently appear at a frequency (or energy corresponding to)
\omega_{d}/2
.

A key result addresses the
strength
of the induced parametric response.
We find that the parametric susceptibility is intimately connected to the nature of the vacuum state and its dependence on microscopic parameters.
As such, parametric driving emerges as a novel probe of the
fidelity susceptibility
in the context of solid-state materials and devices.
This quantity generically gets enhanced and diverges in the vicinity of quantum phase transitions, where the nature of the vacuum ground-state changes dramatically.
Therefore, mapping the readily-implemented parametric driving response across swaths of phase space unlocks a new and complimentary way to examine quantum properties in correlated materials, detect quantum fluctuations, and discover hidden phase transitions, which may be otherwise hard to reach.

The non-trivial connection between the vacuum fidelity susceptibility and the induced parametric drive was revealed through a framework that included a quantum theory of the collective many-body fluctuations.
Importantly, our analysis emphasized the dynamical properties of these collective modes, and not only their energetics (i.e., their spectrum).
The emergent conjugate-canonical-pair structure of the collective coordinates played a key role, and our approach enables straightforward identification of such dynamical-variable pairs.
This is achieved by a special kind of
partial bosonic Bogoliubov transformation
, which isolates the parametric two-boson response of a system to certain changes of its parameters.

In our analysis,
squeezing
emerged as a main organizing principle in parametric driving of electronically ordered materials.
The two quadratures of the fluctuation, analogous to “position” and “momentum” (e.g., spin fluctuations in perpendicular directions), are to be treated on equal footing from the outset.
The low-energy Hamiltonian, however, may then differentiate between the quadratures, and produce squeezing of the vacuum fluctuations.
To induce parametric driving, a modulation must alter the squeezing, which corresponds to inducing an anomalous (i.e., two-boson) driving terms.
Indeed, the more squeezing the reference ordered state has, the stronger is its parametric response (see, e.g., Eq. (
38
)).
Intuitively, a squeezed vacuum state simply hosts a wider range of bosonic fluctuations.

The case studies presented in Sec.
IV
also showed the role quantum geometry has as a driven parameter with associated parametric instabilities.
In strongly interacting, narrow bandwidth, electronic systems, quantum geometry is crucial.
Indeed, the competition between the geometry-associated length scale and the range of intrinsic electronic interactions is key in determining the parametric susceptibility with respect to small changes in the quantum geometry (as evident in Fig.
5
b).

Our framework concentrated on periodic modulations, but could also be applied to quantum parameter
quenches
.
In a small quench, the reference electronic order remains intact, but it could excite the low-energy collective degrees of freedom.
Quantifying this response could use the method summarized in Eq. (
24
).
Inevitably, if the parametric susceptibility associated with the quench is substantial, a non-negligible amount of bosonic collective excitations will emerge.
Such excitations, recently probed in correlated optically-pumped moiré systems, may be long-lived and have intriguing consequences in the context of characterizing the underlying electronic order
Xie et al. 2024
;
Xiong et al. 2025
.
Also, one may ask how much work does the quench produce through exciting low-lying collective modes.
Once again, the fidelity of the fluctuation vacuum is expected to play the key quantitative role.
Interestingly, probing the statistics of the performed work probes the corresponding Loschmidt echo
Silva 2008
;
Quan et al. 2006
, which has thus far remained out of experimental reach in the context of electronic condensed matter systems.

An exciting future direction would be harnessing the parametric driving described here in quantum sensors and quantum information processing devices, specifically in the context of quantum-limited parametric amplification
Louisell, Yariv, and Siegman 1961
;
Mollow and Glauber 1967
;
Caves 1982
;
Yurke et al. 1989
;
Clerk et al. 2010
.
For instance, An excitonic strongly correlated double layer (see Sec.
IV.1
) could perhaps amplify a quantum signal fed into one of its gate voltages.
A parametric instability may be triggered, exciting two Goldstone bosons.
This will result in an oscillating interlayer electric field, which may be extracted as the amplified signal.
This scheme provides phase-sensitive degenerate parametric amplification, where one of the quadratures of the signal is amplified and the other attenuated.
A non-degenerate amplification setup can be explored when (i) several low-lying excitation modes are available, and (ii) joint parametric terms may be driven, e.g.,
\sim a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}^{\dagger}
.
(This is the case for our studied flat-band model, see lower right panel of Fig.
1
c.)

Finally, we speculate that effortless manipulation of the emergent bosonic collective excitations in the correlated electronic systems may have broad implications.
These excitations may be
coupled to other bosonic modes
in various settings, such as mechanical oscillators
Poot and van der Zant 2012
;
Aspelmeyer, Kippenberg, and Marquardt 2014
, microwave resonators
Blais et al. 2004
;
Schoelkopf and Girvin 2008
, superconducting qubits
Schoelkopf and Girvin 2008
;
Houck, Türeci, and Koch 2012
, and optical cavities
Verhagen et al. 2012
;
Thompson et al. 2013
.
Integrating solid-state tunable correlated electronic materials with these more established traditional quantum platforms holds great promise for applications of hybrid quantum systems
Kurizki et al. 2015
;
Clerk et al. 2020
,
e.g., quantum transduction
Arnold et al. 2020
;
Hönl et al. 2022
;
Rochman et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2025
and information storage
Hann et al. 2019
;
Bozkurt et al. 2025
.
Controllably coupling the collective excitations explored here to other bosonic modes remains a fascinating topic for future work.

) leads to the anticipated quadratically dispersing (possibly gapped) collective mode of a spontaneous ferromagnet.

Easy-plane ferromagnet.–

Conversely, in this instance let us identify
{\cal O}=s_{x}
, such that when we invoke Eq. (
69
) again, we assign
p\sim s_{y}
,
q\sim s_{z}

Given the easy-plane nature of this problem, we expect that
s_{y}
rotations are “stiffer”, since they generate a finite
s_{z}
order.
Thus,
p
and
q
are no longer on equal footing.
The low-energy collective Hamiltonian is generically (with
\Delta
the easy-plane anisotropy)

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{\rm easy-plane}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\bf Q}\frac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}\left(p_{\bf Q}^{2}+q_{\bf Q}^{2}\right)+\Delta p_{\bf Q}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\right]\left(p_{{\bf Q}}^{2}+q_{{\bf Q}}^{2}\right)+\frac{\Delta}{2}\left(p_{{\bf Q}}^{2}-q_{{\bf Q}}^{2}\right),

(71)

such that in the
{\bf Q}\to 0
limit the spectrum is approximately
{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{\Delta\rho}{2}}\left|{\bf Q}\right|
.
One recovers the expected linear Goldstone mode associated with this system.

SU(2) antiferromagnet.–

Here, the picture is slightly richer.
For simplicity, let us incorporate the antiferromagnetic ordering via introduction of a sublattice degree of freedom, described by Pauli matrices
\sigma_{i}
.
In that manner, we may treat this case using the same framework by identifying the antiferromagnetic order parameter

{\cal O}=s_{z}\sigma_{z}
.
In contrast with the easy-axis ferromagnet, here
s_{x}
and
s_{y}

are not conjugate variables
.
Instead, by enforcing sublattice as a good quantum number in the low-energy ordered manifold, we may identify to pairs of canonical variables [by referencing Eq. (
69
) again],

p^{\left(1\right)}=s_{y}\sigma_{z},\,q^{\left(1\right)}=s_{x},\,\,\,\,\,p^{\left(2\right)}=s_{x}\sigma_{z},\,q^{\left(2\right)}=s_{y}.

(72)

Notice that the
q^{\left(j\right)}
variables induce a rotation of the Néel vector, whereas the
p^{\left(j\right)}
operators introduce finite magnetization.
By definition, the latter rotations should be much stiffer.
This leads to a generic low-energy Hamiltonian,

{\cal H}^{{\rm AFM}}\approx\sum_{j,{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}\left[\left(p_{{\bf Q}}^{\left(j\right)}\right)^{2}+\left(q_{{\bf Q}}^{\left(j\right)}\right)^{2}\right]+J\left(p_{{\bf Q}}^{\left(j\right)}\right)^{2},

(73)

where
J>0
encodes the antiferromagnetic tendencies.
Comparing with the easy-plane case, one immediately obtains two branches of linearly-dispersing Goldstone modes, as anticipated for such a system.

We note that in the three examples above we did not invoke any microscopic details regarding the systems which give rise to these ordered states.
The analysis introduced in Sec.
III
is thus quite powerful in determining universal characteristics of the low-lying collective modes, before needing to perform any calculation.
Moreover, it is now clear why linear dispersion of Goldstone modes constitutes a universal signature of collective mode squeezing.
A generic squeezing term of one of the quadratures immediately converts a (gapless) mode with a quadratic dispersion into a linear one at long wavelengths.

Appendix B
Fluctuation action calculation details

B.1
Berry phase

Expanding to second order in
\hat{F}
(using
\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=0
),

\displaystyle{\cal B}

\displaystyle\approx i\langle\Psi_{0}|{\hat{F}}\frac{d}{dt}{\hat{F}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf QQ^{\prime},pp^{\prime}}}i\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\mu\nu}\left({\bf p}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{\alpha\beta}\left({\bf p^{\prime}}\right)\langle\Psi_{0}|c_{{\bf p+Q}\mu}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf p}\nu}c_{{\bf p^{\prime}+Q^{\prime}}\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf p^{\prime}}\beta}|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=i\sum_{{\bf p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{0}\left({\bf p}\right)P^{T}\left({\bf p}\right)\right\}\sum_{{\bf p^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{0}\left({\bf p^{\prime}}\right)P^{T}\left({\bf p^{\prime}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)\bar{P}^{T}\left({\bf p}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)P^{T}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)\right\}.

(74)

Using the anti-commutation between
\cal O
and
\phi
and the cyclic properties of the trace, we can write schematically
{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi P^{T}\right\}={\rm Tr}\left\{\phi\bar{P}^{T}\right\}={\rm Tr}\left\{\phi\right\}=0
, eliminating the first line.
Next, since
P
is independent of
\bf k
,

\displaystyle{\cal B}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)P^{T}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)P^{T}\right\}+\frac{i}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)\bar{P}^{T}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right){\cal O}\right\},

(75)

where we have used the summations under
\bf p,Q
to perform various manipulations.
Since the first term is a complete derivative, and employing the identity
\phi_{{\bf-Q}}\left({\bf p+Q}\right)=\phi^{\dagger}_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)
we can compactly write

{\cal B}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q,p}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi^{\dagger}_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf p}\right){\cal O}\right\}.

(76)

Decomposed,

{\cal B}=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf p^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}{\rm Tr}\left\{-i{\cal M}^{i}{\cal M}^{j}{\cal O}\right\}\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf p}\right).

(77)

B.2
Fluctuation Hamiltonian

Expanding once more to lowest order in fluctuations,

{\cal H}\approx E_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\Psi_{0}|\left[{\hat{F}},\left[{\hat{F}},H\right]\right]|\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(78)

where the first order term vanishes identically as a consequence of
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
minimizing the Hartree-Fock energy.
Our task is thus to compute the following expectation values for various Hamiltonian terms,
\left\langle\left[{\hat{F}},\left[{\hat{F}},H\right]\right]\right\rangle
, where the expectation value is with respect to
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.
Throughout the calculations detailed below, one makes extensive use of the following fermionic commutation identity,

\left[c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2},c_{3}^{\dagger}c_{4}\right]=\delta_{23}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{4}-\delta_{14}c_{3}^{\dagger}c_{2}.

(79)

B.2.1
Quadratic-in-fermions perturbations

Consider a quadratic perturbation independent of momentum,

H_{{\rm per.}}=\sum_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}\alpha}^{\dagger}h^{\alpha\beta}c_{{\bf k}\beta}.

(80)

We first iterate,

\left[F,H_{{\rm per.}}\right]=\sum_{{\bf Qk}}\left[\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k}\right)h^{s\beta}-h^{\alpha s}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]c_{{\bf k+Q}\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf k}\beta}.

(81)

After computing the second commutator, and taking the Hartree-Fock expectation value with respect to
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
,

\left\langle\left[{\hat{F}},\left[{\hat{F}},H_{{\rm per.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle=\sum_{{\bf Qk}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\left[\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right),h\right]{\cal O}\right\}=2\sum_{{\bf Qk}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)h{\cal O}\right\}.

(82)

In terms of the decomposition of the fluctuation matrix, we may re-write this as

\left\langle\left[{\hat{F}},\left[{\hat{F}},H_{{\rm per.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf p^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left[\left\{{\cal M}^{j}{\cal M}^{i},h\right\}{\cal O}\right]\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf p}\right).

(83)

B.2.2
Interacting terms

We write a generalized interaction term that will capture all the relevant kinds of interactions in the models we analyze,

H_{\rm int.}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf qkk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf q}}c_{{\bf k+q}\alpha}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}^{\alpha\beta}c_{{\bf k}\beta}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q\gamma}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\gamma\delta}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}\delta}.

(84)

Here,
\Lambda
are matrices that embody both the band-projected form factor and the structure of the interaction terms in the internal index space spanned by the Greek letters.

Upon first iteration of the commutator, one finds

\left[\hat{F},H_{\rm int.}\right]=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}V_{{\bf q}}\left\{\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi},\rho_{{\bf-q}}\right\},

(85)

where the following quantities and matrices were defined,

\rho_{{\bf-q}}=\sum_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\gamma\delta}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q\gamma}}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}\delta},

(86)

\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi}=\sum_{{\bf k}}M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\alpha\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)c_{{\bf k+q+Q}\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf k}\beta},

(87)

and

M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\alpha\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)\equiv\left[\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+q}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}^{s\beta}-\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k+q+Q}}^{\alpha s}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)\right].

(88)

Now, let us split the next iteration into two parts and write,

\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{\rm int.}\right]\right]=I_{1}+I_{2},

(89)

with

I_{1}=\left[\hat{F},\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}V_{{\bf q}}\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi}\rho_{{\bf-q}}\right],

(90)

I_{2}=\left[\hat{F},\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}V_{{\bf q}}\rho_{{\bf-q}}\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi}\right].

(91)

Calculating the different commutators we find

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}\sum_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf k}}V_{{\bf q}}\left[\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+q+Q}\right)M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)-M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+Q^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]c_{{\bf k+q+Q+Q^{\prime}}\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf k}\beta}\rho_{{\bf-q}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}\sum_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf q}}\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi}\rho_{{\bf-q,Q^{\prime}}}^{\phi},

(92)

and

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}V_{{\bf q}}\rho_{{\bf-q,Q^{\prime}}}^{\phi}\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}\sum_{{\bf k}}V_{{\bf q}}\rho_{{\bf-q}}\left[\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+q+Q}\right)M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)-M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+Q^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]c_{{\bf k+q+Q+Q^{\prime}}\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf k}\beta}.

(93)

We may collect the different terms together by manipulating the momentum summations, and we find

\displaystyle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{\rm int.}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}\sum_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf k}}V_{{\bf q}}\left[\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+q+Q}\right)M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)-M_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\alpha s}\left({\bf k+Q^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}^{s\beta}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]\left\{c_{{\bf k+q+Q+Q^{\prime}}\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf k}\beta},\rho_{{\bf-q}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{A}\sum_{{\bf Qq}}\sum_{{\bf Q^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf q}}\rho_{{\bf q,Q}}^{\phi}\rho_{{\bf-q,Q^{\prime}}}^{\phi}.

(94)

Finally, we evaluate the expectation value of this double commutator in the ground state, defining
\Lambda_{\bf k,k}\equiv\Lambda_{0}
and exploiting the momentum independence of the density matrix, yielding the following expression,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm int.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}}{A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\left(P^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}}},{\bf{\bf k}}}P^{T}+\bar{P}^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}}},{\bf{\bf k}}}\bar{P}^{T}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle-\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}}{A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\left(P^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}}},{\bf{\bf k}}}P^{T}+\bar{P}^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}\bar{P}^{T}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle...

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}}{2A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}+\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k}},{\bf{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)-2\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]\bar{P}^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}P^{T}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}}{2A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}+\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k}},{\bf{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)-2\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]P^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}\bar{P}^{T}\right\}

\displaystyle...

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf k}}\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}}{A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right),\Lambda_{0}\right\}-2\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\Lambda_{0}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]P^{T}\right\}\sum_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\Lambda_{0}P^{T}\right\}

\displaystyle...

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{V_{{\bf Q}}}{A}\sum_{{\bf k}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left[\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+Q}},{\cal O}\right]\right\}\sum_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left[\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}}},{\cal O}\right]\right\}.

(95)

The different terms in this expression, separated by
...
lines, contribute differently to the fluctuations, depending on the commutation relation of
P
(or, more to the point, of
\cal O
) with the corresponding form factors of the interaction.

B.2.3
Classifying interacting terms

Given the matrix
\cal O
, which squares to the identity matrix, we may decompose any matrix
M
,

M=\frac{1}{2}\left(M+{\cal O}M{\cal O}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(M-{\cal O}M{\cal O}\right).

(96)

Notice that the first part commutes with
\cal O
, and the second part anti commutes with it.
This motivates us to examine two different contributions from the interacting parts, decomposing the form-factors with regards to commutation with
\cal O
:

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm int.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle^{\left[{\cal O},\Lambda\right]=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}}{A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}-\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\right]\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}}},{\bf{\bf k}}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf k}}\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}}{A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right),\Lambda_{0}\right\}-2\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)\Lambda_{0}\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)\right]P^{T}\right\}\sum_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\Lambda_{0}P^{T}\right\}.

(97)

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm int.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle^{\left\{{\cal O},\Lambda\right\}=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}}{A}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k}},{\bf{\bf k}^{\prime}}}\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k}\right)-\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\right]\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{V_{{\bf Q}}}{A}\sum_{{\bf k}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\left({\bf{\bf k}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+Q}}{\cal O}\right\}\sum_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\phi_{{\bf Q}}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\cal O}\right\}.

(98)

For completeness, Let us also include the expressions with the decomposed fluctuation matrices,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm int.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle^{\left[{\cal O},\Lambda\right]=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{A}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}k}}}{\cal M}^{j}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}-\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf p}}V_{{\bf k-p}}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf pk}}}\Lambda_{{\bf kp}}{\cal M}^{j}\right]{\cal M}^{i}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf 0}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\Lambda_{0}P^{T}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{2{\cal M}^{j}\left[{\cal M}^{i},\Lambda_{0}\right]P^{T}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

(99)

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm int.}}\right]\right]\right\rangle^{\left\{{\cal O},\Lambda\right\}=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{A}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf p}}V_{{\bf k-p}}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf pk}}}\Lambda_{{\bf kp}}{\cal M}^{j}-V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}k}}{\cal M}^{j}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\right]{\cal M}^{i}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{V_{{\bf Q}}}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{j}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}{\cal O}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k}}{\cal O}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

(100)

Appendix C
Remarks regarding the Bogoliubov transformation

Our use of the
T_{\bf Q}
similarity transformation, which can be slightly modified to diagonalize the Hamiltonian itself via a Bogoliubov transformation is based on the thorough discussion in Ref.
Xiao 2009
.
This similarity transformation
T_{\bf Q}
was shown to be comprised of pairs of time-reversal pairs of eigenvectors of
{\cal D}_{\bf Q}
, which can be inferred from the structure of (
22
).
Besides being related by complex-conjugation, the positive and negative frequency eigenvectors
|v\left(\pm{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle
have opposite norm with respect to the sesquilinear form

\langle v\left(\pm{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}\right)|\left[-i\tilde{\cal B}_{{\bf Q}}\right]|v\left(\pm{\cal E}_{n,{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle=\pm 1.

(101)

A corollary to that property of the eigenvectors is that

T_{\bf Q}^{\dagger}\left(i\tilde{\cal B}_{{\bf Q}}\right)T_{\bf Q}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}
.
Combining with Eqs. (
18
), (
22
), one finds that the same matrices
T_{\bf Q}
must also encode the requisite Bogoliubov transformation, diagonalizing the Hamiltonian by hermitian congruence, hence the from of Eq. (
23
).

Recall the residual Bogoliubov transformation,

\delta T_{\bf Q}=\left(T^{\left[H\right]}_{\bf Q}\right)^{-1}T^{\left[H+\lambda\delta H\right]}_{\bf Q}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}U_{\bf Q}&V_{\bf Q}\\
V_{\bf Q}^{*}&U_{\bf Q}^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(102)

The bosonic nature of this transformation implies
U_{\bf Q}U_{\bf Q}^{\dagger}-V_{\bf Q}V_{\bf Q}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1}_{N}
.
This relation in turn guarantees two things.
First, if the off-diagonal
\delta{\cal Z}_{\bf Q}
vanishes,
V_{\bf Q}
vanishes, and
U_{\bf Q}
simply encodes a unitary transformation.
Second, the deviation of this residual transformation form unitarity comes in
V_{\bf Q}
to first order in the perturbation strength
\lambda
, and affects
U_{\bf Q}
only with a second order term.
Thus,
\frac{\partial\|\delta{\cal Z}_{{\bf Q}}\|}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda\to 0}\propto\frac{\partial\|V_{{\bf Q}}\|}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda\to 0}
.

The perturbed bosonic Hamiltonian is diagonalized in the new basis

\begin{pmatrix}b_{{\bf Q}}\\
b_{-{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\equiv\delta T_{\bf Q}\begin{pmatrix}a_{{\bf Q}}\\
a_{-{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}.

(103)

At any given momentum
\bf Q
, we may write the vacuum of
b
-bosonic fluctuations
|{\bf\tilde{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle
in terms of
a
operators acting on the unperturbed
a
-vacuum
|{\bf{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle
,

|{\bf\tilde{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\det U_{\bf Q}\right|}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}a^{\dagger}_{\bf Q}\left[U_{\bf Q}^{-1}V_{\bf Q}\right]a^{\dagger}_{\bf-Q}\right)|{\bf{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle.

(104)

Now consider the fidelity of the unperturbed fluctuation vacuum
|{\bf{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle
with the perturbed vacuum
|{\bf\tilde{0}}_{\bf Q}\rangle
,

\displaystyle\left|\langle{\bf\tilde{0}}_{{\bf Q}}|{\bf 0}_{{\bf Q}}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left[\det\left(U_{{\bf Q}}U_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\right)\right]^{-1/4}

\displaystyle=\left[\det\left(\mathbb{1}_{N}+V_{{\bf Q}}V_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\right)\right]^{-1/4}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{4}\log\det\left(\mathbb{1}_{N}+V_{{\bf Q}}V_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{4}{\rm Tr}\log\left(\mathbb{1}_{N}+V_{{\bf Q}}V_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{1}{4}\|V_{{\bf Q}}\|^{2}+{\cal O}\left(\|V_{{\bf Q}}\|^{4}\right),

(105)

from which we extract Eq. (
29
).

Appendix D
Single mode analysis

Let us consider the general form of fluctuation terms we may get for a single
\bf Q
mode,

{\cal B}_{\bf Q}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{-\bf Q}\partial_{t}{\mathbb{X}}_{\bf Q}-{\mathbb{X}}_{-\bf Q}\partial_{t}{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf Q}\right),

(106)

{\cal H}_{\bf Q}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\bf Q}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{\bf-Q}{\mathbb{X}}_{\bf Q}+{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf-Q}{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf Q}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\bf Q}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{\bf-Q}{\mathbb{X}}_{\bf Q}-{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf-Q}{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf Q}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\bf Q}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{\bf-Q}{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf Q}+{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf-Q}{\mathbb{X}}_{\bf Q}\right).

(107)

It is useful to define
z_{\bf Q}=\Delta_{\bf Q}+i\Gamma_{\bf Q}=\left|z_{\bf Q}\right|e^{i\phi}
.
In the Pauli basis
\upsilon_{i}
operating on the vector
\Phi_{\bf Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf Q}}\\
\mathbb{P}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}
, we can write the appropriate matrices

\tilde{{\cal B}}_{\bf Q}=i\upsilon_{y},\,\,\,\,\,\,\tilde{{\cal H}}_{\bf Q}=\omega_{\bf Q}+\Delta_{\bf Q}\upsilon_{z}+\Gamma_{\bf Q}\upsilon_{x}=\omega_{\bf Q}+\left|z_{\bf Q}\right|\upsilon_{z}e^{i\phi\upsilon_{y}},

(108)

and the dynamic matrix

{\cal D}_{\bf Q}=i\tilde{{\cal B}}^{-1}_{\bf Q}\tilde{{\cal H}}_{\bf Q}=e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\left(\omega_{{\bf Q}}\upsilon_{y}+i\left|z_{{\bf Q}}\right|\upsilon_{x}\right)e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}.

(109)

Next, we diagonalize the dynamic matrix.
The appropriate matrices encoding the similarity transformation are

T=\sqrt{\frac{r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}{2}}e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\begin{pmatrix}r_{{\bf Q}}&r_{{\bf Q}}\\
i&-i\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\,\,\,T^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{r_{{\bf Q}}}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}&-i\\
r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}&i\end{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}},\,\,\,\,\,\,T^{\dagger}=\sqrt{\frac{r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}r_{{\bf Q}}&-i\\
r_{{\bf Q}}&i\end{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}},

(110)

where we have defined the useful parameter

r_{{\bf Q}}=\left(\frac{\omega_{{\bf Q}}-\left|z_{{\bf Q}}\right|}{\omega_{{\bf Q}}+\left|z_{{\bf Q}}\right|}\right)^{1/2}.

(111)

Notice
r_{\bf Q}\in\left[0,1\right]
, and that
r_{\bf Q}\to 0
in the limit of maximum squeezing.
The transformation operates on the relevant matrices as,

T^{-1}{\cal D}_{\bf Q}T=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&0\\
0&-{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,T^{\dagger}\tilde{{\cal H}}_{\bf Q}T=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&0\\
0&{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix},

(112)

with
{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}=\sqrt{\omega_{{\bf Q}}^{2}-\left|z_{{\bf Q}}\right|^{2}}
.

We now proceed to examine how the Hamiltonian changes in the diagonalized basis above when a small perturbation is introduced,

\omega_{\bf Q}\to\omega_{\bf Q}+\delta\omega,\,\,\,\,\,\left|z_{\bf Q}\right|\to\left|z_{\bf Q}\right|+\delta z,\,\,\,\,\,\phi\to\phi+\delta\phi.

(113)

The off diagonal elements in this so-called equilibrium basis will induce parametric driving.
To lowest order in these perturbations, we recover

T^{\dagger}\left[\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}+\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right]T=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&0\\
0&{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&\delta{\cal Z}\\
\delta{\cal Z}^{*}&\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix},

(114)

\delta{\cal Z}=\omega_{{\bf Q}}\frac{r_{{\bf Q}}+r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}{2}\delta\frac{\left|z_{{\bf Q}}\right|}{\omega_{{\bf Q}}}-i\left|z_{{\bf Q}}\right|\delta\phi.

(115)

We can make a number of immediate observations.
First, a change of the spectrum of bosonic excitations, introduced by the perturbation,
\delta{\cal E}_{\bf Q}
, does not necessarily translate to parametric driving.
One must additionally either (i) change the ratio of anomalous to normal terms, or (ii) perturb the phase of the anomalous term.
Here, we point out an interesting effect: in the limit of strong squeezing,
r_{\bf Q}\to 0
, the parametric susceptibility to a change of the anomalous-to-normal ratio diverges as
\sim r_{\bf Q}^{-1}
.

Let us furthermore examine the energy of the bosonic vacuum.
The shift of vacuum energy compared to the unperturbed state is,

\delta E_{{\rm Vac},\bf Q}=\sqrt{\left({\cal E}_{\bf Q}+\delta{\cal E}_{\bf Q}\right)^{2}-\left|\delta{\cal Z}\right|^{2}}-\left({\cal E}_{\bf Q}+\delta{\cal E}_{\bf Q}\right)\approx-\frac{\left|\delta{\cal Z}\right|^{2}}{2{\cal E}_{\bf Q}},

(116)

where in the last step we expanded the expression to leading order in the perturbed terms.
Clearly, the susceptibility of the vacuum energy to modification introduced by
\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{\bf Q}
is proportional to the magnitude of the parametric terms introduced.

Lastly, we examine the fidelity between the unperturbed vacuum wavefunction and the perturbed on,

{\cal F}\left(\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)=\left|\left\langle{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)|{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}+\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)\right\rangle\right|.

(117)

We begin by writing the perturbed Hamiltonian in terms of the diagonalized (unperturbed) bosons [see Eq. (
114
)],

H_{\bf Q}\approx\left(\cal{E}_{\bf Q}+\delta\cal{E}_{\bf Q}\right)\left(a^{\dagger}_{\bf Q}a_{\bf Q}+a^{\dagger}_{\bf-Q}a_{\bf-Q}\right)+\left(\delta{\cal{Z}}_{\bf Q}a_{\bf Q}a_{\bf-Q}+{\rm h.c.}\right).

(118)

The ground state of this Hamiltonian may be expressed in terms of the vacuum of this
a
-bosons,

|{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}+\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle=\frac{1}{\cal N}\exp\left(-sa_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}a_{{\bf-Q}}^{\dagger}\right)|{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle,

(119)

where
s
is a complex number and
{\cal N}
is a normalization factor.
We begin by recovering the value of the latter,

\displaystyle{\cal N}

\displaystyle=\langle{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)|e^{-s^{*}a_{{\bf Q}}a_{{\bf-Q}}}e^{-sa_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}a_{{\bf-Q}}^{\dagger}}|{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\frac{\left(-s\right)^{n}\left(-s^{*}\right)^{m}}{m!n!}\langle{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)|\left(a_{{\bf Q}}a_{{\bf-Q}}\right)^{m}\left(a_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}a_{{\bf-Q}}^{\dagger}\right)^{n}|{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{\left|s\right|^{2n}}{n!n!}\langle{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)|\left(a_{{\bf Q}}a_{{\bf-Q}}\right)^{n}\left(a_{{\bf Q}}^{\dagger}a_{{\bf-Q}}^{\dagger}\right)^{n}|{\rm Vac}\left(\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\left|s\right|^{2}}.

(120)

The properties of the vacuum wavefunction are thus all captured by the parameter
s
.
We note that the vacuum “photon number”
n_{a}
, i.e., the expectation value
\left\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bf Q}a_{\bf Q}\right\rangle
in the perturbed vacuum is related to
s
,

n_{a}=\frac{\left|s\right|^{2}}{1-\left|s\right|^{2}},\,\,\,\,\,\left|s\right|^{2}=\frac{n_{a}}{n_{a}+1}.

(121)

Generally, one can readily show using (
119
) that the fidelity between vacua with different
s
-parameters,

{\cal F}\left(s_{1},s_{2}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\left(1-\left|s_{1}\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|s_{2}\right|^{2}\right)}{1+\left|s_{1}\right|^{2}\left|s_{2}\right|^{2}-2{\rm Re}\left\{s_{1}^{*}s_{2}\right\}}}.

(122)

We must now explicitly find
s
of the perturbed vacuum.
To do so, we demand that the annihilation operators which diagonalize the
perturbed
Hamiltonian Eq. (
118
) annihilate the perturbed vacuum.
First, notice we may write

T=e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
i&-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}}+\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}}{2}&\frac{\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}}-\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}}{2}\\
\frac{\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}}-\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}}{2}&\frac{\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}}+\sqrt{r_{{\bf Q}}^{-1}}}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(123)

Here,
T
is decomposed to three parts. The first part on the left rotates the phase of the anomalous terms, the second constructs bosonic variables out of the “coordinate” basis, and the last one accounts for the Bogoliubov transformation.
Taking advantage of this deconstructed form, straightforward algebra of operating on the vacuum state allows us to extract
s
in terms of the perturbations,

s=\frac{\tilde{r}-\tilde{r}^{-1}}{\tilde{r}+\tilde{r}^{-1}}\sqrt{\frac{\delta{\cal Z}^{*}}{\delta{\cal Z}}},\,\,\,\,\,\tilde{r}=\left(\frac{{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}+\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}-\left|\delta{\cal Z}\right|}{{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}+\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}+\left|\delta{\cal Z}\right|}\right)^{1/4}.

(124)

Finally, we find using the fact that the unperturbed
s
vanishes in the reference state,

\displaystyle{\cal F}\left(\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\left|s\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\tilde{r}+\tilde{r}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{1}{8}\frac{\left|\delta{\cal Z}\right|^{2}}{{\cal E}^{2}_{\bf Q}}.

(125)

Much like in Eq. (
116
), the fidelity is sensitive to the magnitude of parametric term.

D.1
Parametric driving

Let us examine the equations of motion in the presence of the periodic drive with frequency and phase
\omega_{d}
, and
\varphi_{d}
, respectively.
The perturbed dynamic matrix in the almost-diagonal basis,

\tilde{\cal D}=T^{-1}\left[{{\cal D}}_{{\bf Q}}+i{\cal\tilde{B}}^{-1}_{{\bf Q}}\delta\tilde{{\cal H}}_{{\bf Q}}\right]T=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&0\\
0&-{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}&\delta{\cal Z}\\
-\delta{\cal Z}^{*}&-\delta{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}\end{pmatrix}.

(126)

Let us absorb the unimportant
\delta{\cal E}
contribution into
{\cal E}_{\bf Q}
.
This will only act to modify the parametric resonance frequency.
The periodic driving is introduced by replacing

\delta{\cal Z}\to i\varrho e^{-i\omega_{d}t+i\varphi_{d}}
, with
\varrho=\left|\delta{\cal Z}\right|
, absorbing the phase of
\delta{\cal Z}
into that of the drive, and shifting it by
\frac{\pi}{2}
for convenience of notation.
The equation of motion is

i\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\Phi}=\tilde{\cal D}\tilde{\Phi},

(127)

where the vector
\tilde{\Phi}
is related to original coordinates by
\tilde{\Phi}=T^{-1}\Phi_{\bf Q}
.
It is useful to move to a rotating frame by defining the vector

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t}&0\\
0&e^{-i{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t}\end{pmatrix}\tilde{\Phi},

(128)

where we recover the equation of motion (assuming
\omega_{d}
satisfies the parametric resonance condition)

i\frac{d}{dt}\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}&i\varrho e^{i\varphi_{d}}\\
i\varrho e^{-i\varphi_{d}}\end{pmatrix}\Psi,

(129)

and its straightforward solution

\Psi\left(t\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\varrho t&\sinh\varrho te^{i\varphi_{d}}\\
\sinh\varrho te^{-i\varphi_{d}}&\cosh\varrho t\end{pmatrix}\Psi_{0}.

(130)

Notice that the phase
\varphi_{d}
determines which quadrature gets enhanced in the rotating frame and which is attenuated.
In the “lab” frame both oscillate with exponentially diverging amplitudes, see below.

We now manipulate to get the solution in the original coordinate basis,

\displaystyle\Phi\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=T\tilde{\Phi}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=Te^{-i{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t\upsilon_{z}}\Psi\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=Te^{-i\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\upsilon_{z}}e^{\varrho t\upsilon_{x}}e^{-i\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\upsilon_{z}}T^{-1}\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\left[Te^{-i\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\upsilon_{z}}T^{-1}\right]\left[Te^{\varrho t\upsilon_{x}}T^{-1}\right]\left[Te^{-i\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\upsilon_{z}}T^{-1}\right]\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)&-r\sin\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\\
r^{-1}\sin\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)&\cos\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}e^{\varrho t\upsilon_{z}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}&-r\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\\
r^{-1}\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}&\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\end{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)&-r\sin\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\\
r^{-1}\sin\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)&\cos\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{\varrho t}\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}&-e^{\varrho t}r\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\\
e^{-\varrho t}r^{-1}\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}&e^{-\varrho t}\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\end{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{\varrho t}\cos\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}-e^{-\varrho t}\sin\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}&-r\left[e^{\varrho t}\cos\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}+e^{-\varrho t}\sin\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right]\\
r^{-1}\left[e^{\varrho t}\sin\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}+e^{-\varrho t}\cos\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right]&e^{-\varrho t}\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\cos\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)-e^{\varrho t}\sin\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\end{pmatrix}e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\Phi_{0},

(131)

and in the last line we shifted the time
t
for brevity,

{\cal E}^{*}t\equiv{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}
.
It is useful to consider slightly different coordinates
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{X}\\
\mathbb{P}\end{pmatrix}\equiv e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}\upsilon_{y}}\Phi
, and define the initial condition

\mathbb{C}_{0}\equiv\sqrt{r^{-1}}\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\mathbb{X}_{0}-\sqrt{r}\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\mathbb{P}_{0}
,
and

\mathbb{D}_{0}\equiv\sqrt{r^{-1}}\sin\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\mathbb{X}_{0}+\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\mathbb{P}_{0}
,
such that

\mathbb{X}\left(t\right)=\sqrt{r}\left[e^{\varrho t}\cos\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\mathbb{C}_{0}-e^{-\varrho t}\sin\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\mathbb{D}_{0}\right],

(132)

\mathbb{P}\left(t\right)=\sqrt{r^{-1}}\left[e^{\varrho t}\sin\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\mathbb{C}_{0}+e^{-\varrho t}\cos\left({\cal E}^{*}t\right)\mathbb{D}_{0}\right].

(133)

Both quadratures grow exponentially in time, a consequence of the parametric resonance.
In the absence of damping, the order in the reference state is thus unstable to parametric driving.
Notice that the growth in the two quadratures is uneven, a result of the original squeezing
r
in the unperturbed Hamiltonian.

Succinctly written, we can separate the three parts of the time evolution: the squeezing, the rotation due to the driving, and the exponential growth instability,

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{X}\\
\mathbb{P}\end{pmatrix}=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\upsilon_{z}\log r\right]\times\exp\left[-i\upsilon_{y}\left({\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}t-\frac{\varphi_{d}}{2}\right)\right]\times\exp\left[\upsilon_{z}\varrho t\right]\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{C}_{0}\\
\mathbb{D}_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(134)

Appendix E
Quantum Hall double layer calculations

Recall the Hamiltonian of the quantum Hall double layer problem,

H_{\rm QH-2\ell}=H_{\rm C}+H_{z}+H_{\rm tun.}

(135)

The different terms are

H_{\rm C}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\bf q}V_{\bf q}^{0}\tilde{\rho}_{\bf q}\tilde{\rho}_{\bf-q},\,\,\,\,\,H_{z}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\bf q}V_{\bf q}^{z}\tilde{\rho}^{z}_{\bf q}\tilde{\rho}^{z}_{\bf-q},

(136)

H_{{\rm tun.}}=-\Delta_{\rm sas}\sum_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}}^{\dagger}\ell_{x}c_{{\bf k}}.

(137)

The projected density operators are

\tilde{\rho}_{\bf q}=\sum_{\bf k}c^{\dagger}_{\bf k+q}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}c_{\bf k}
,
\tilde{\rho}^{z}_{\bf q}=\sum_{\bf k}c^{\dagger}_{\bf k+q}\ell_{z}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}c_{\bf k}
,
and the form factors

\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k+q}}^{\alpha\beta}=\delta^{\alpha\beta}f\left({\bf q}\right)e^{-i\frac{{\Omega}}{2}{\bf k}\times\left({\bf k+q}\right)}=\delta^{\alpha\beta}f\left({\bf q}\right)e^{-i\frac{{\Omega}}{2}{\bf k}\times{\bf q}}
,
with

f\left({\bf q}\right)=e^{-\frac{1}{4}{\Omega}\left|{\bf q}\right|^{2}}L_{n}\left({\Omega}\left|{\bf q}\right|^{2}\right)
(
{\Omega}=l_{B}^{2}
, i.e., the magnetic length squared,
L_{n}
is the
n
Laguerre polynomial).

With nominal layer separation
d
, the two interaction strengths are given by

V_{{\bf q}}^{0/z}=\frac{V_{{\bf q}}^{C}\left(1\pm e^{-qd}\right)}{2},

(138)

with
V_{{\bf q}}^{C}
the Fourier transformed Coulomb repulsion in two dimensions.

E.1
Ground state manifold

One may simply evaluate the Hartree-Fock energies of the various terms within the ordered state,

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle H_{0}\right\rangle}{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}^{0}}{2A^{2}}\left(\sum_{{\bf k}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k}}P_{\bf k}\right\}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k^{\prime}-k}}^{0}{\rm Tr}\left\{P^{T}_{\bf k}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\bar{P}_{\bf k^{\prime}}^{T}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}^{0}}{2}\left({\rm Tr}\left\{P_{\bf k}\right\}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k^{\prime}-k}}^{0}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}{\rm Tr}\left\{P_{\bf k}^{T}\bar{P}^{T}_{\bf k^{\prime}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}^{0}}{2},

(139)

where in the last line we have assumed
P
is independent of momentum – a property of the
ordered
state.
The ordered state indeed is lower in energy, because the Fock energy term is always positive unless the density matrix is constant.
We will assume this ordered state henceforth.

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle H_{z}\right\rangle}{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}}{2A^{2}}\left(\sum_{{\bf k}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\ell_{z}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k}}P\right\}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k^{\prime}-k}}^{z}{\rm Tr}\left\{P^{T}\ell_{z}\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\bar{P}^{T}\ell_{z}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{\ell_{z}{\cal O}\right\}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k^{\prime}-k}}^{z}\left|\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}\frac{1}{4}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal O}\ell_{z}\left[{\cal O},\ell_{z}\right]\right\},

(140)

\frac{\left\langle H_{{\rm tun.}}\right\rangle}{A}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{{\rm sas}}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal O}\ell_{x}\right\}.

(141)

Parameterizing
{\cal O}=X\ell_{x}+Y\ell_{y}+Z\ell_{z}
, with
X^{2}+Y^{2}+Z^{2}=1
(so the defined
P
is indeed a projector), the ground state energy density is

\frac{E_{HF}}{A}=\frac{V_{{\bf 0}}^{0}+V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(X^{2}+Y^{2}\right)\left[V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}-\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k^{\prime}-k}}^{z}\left|\Lambda_{{\bf k},{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}\right]-\Delta_{{\rm sas}}X.

(142)

The first term is the background contribution.
The layer-asymmetric interaction chooses an interlayer coherent state as the preferred interacting ground state, with arbitrary
U\left(1\right)
phase.
The interlayer tunneling breaks this
U\left(1\right)
symmetry explicitly, making
{\cal O}=\ell_{x}
the ground state.

E.2
Fluctuation Lagrangian

The fluctuation operator can be decomposed into
{\cal M}_{1}=\ell_{y}
and
{\cal M}_{2}=\ell_{z}
components.
The Berry phase term is straightforward,

{\cal\tilde{B}}_{{\bf Q}}^{ij}\left({\bf p,p^{\prime}}\right)=2\epsilon^{ij}\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}.

(143)

The tunneling term produces a fluctuation Hamiltonian contribution

{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\rm tun.}{\bf Q}}^{ij}\left({\bf p,p^{\prime}}\right)=2\Delta_{{\rm sas}}\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\delta^{ij}.

(144)

The symmetric part of the Hamiltonian gives rise to

{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\rm 0}{\bf Q}}^{ij}\left({\bf p,p^{\prime}}\right)=\left[\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf k}}\frac{V_{{\bf p-k}}^{0}}{A}\left|\Lambda_{{\bf kp}}\right|^{2}-\frac{V_{{\bf p-p^{\prime}}}^{0}}{A}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf p^{\prime}p}}}\Lambda_{{\bf p+Q},{\bf p^{\prime}+Q}}\right]\delta^{ij}.

(145)

The layer-capacitive interaction contributes

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{H}}_{{\rm z}{\bf Q}}^{ij}\left({\bf p,p^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left[\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf k}}\frac{V_{{\bf p-k}}^{z}}{A}\left|\Lambda_{{\bf kp}}\right|^{2}-\frac{V_{{\bf p-p^{\prime}}}^{z}}{A}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf p^{\prime}p}}}\Lambda_{{\bf p+Q},{\bf p^{\prime}+Q}}\right]\delta^{ij}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{V_{{\bf Q}}^{z}}{A}\Lambda_{{\bf p^{\prime}},{\bf p^{\prime}+Q}}\Lambda_{{\bf p+Q},{\bf p}}-\frac{V_{{\bf p-p^{\prime}}}^{z}}{A}\Lambda_{{\bf p^{\prime}p}}\Lambda_{{\bf p+Q},{\bf p^{\prime}+Q}}\right]\left(1-\left(-1\right)^{i}\right)\delta^{ij}.

(146)

Notice that only the second line in this last contribution distinguishes between the
\ell_{y}
and
\ell_{z}
fluctuations – this is the squeezing term.

E.3
Single mode approximation

We may get an analytical handle on the lowest lying collective mode by employing the approximation,

\Phi_{\bf Q}^{1}\left({\bf p}\right)\approx\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{X}}_{\bf Q}f\left({\bf Q}\right)e^{-i\frac{{\Omega}}{2}{\bf p}\times{\bf Q}},\,\,\,\,\,\Phi_{\bf Q}^{2}\left({\bf p}\right)\approx\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{\bf Q}f\left({\bf Q}\right)e^{-i\frac{{\Omega}}{2}{\bf p}\times{\bf Q}}.

(147)

In the Hamiltonian, we find the following contributions to leading order in
\bf Q
,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf-Q}}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf Q}}f^{2}\left({\bf Q}\right)\left[\frac{\rho_{s}^{0}+\rho_{s}^{z}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+2E_{\rm easy}+f^{2}\left({\bf Q}\right)V_{{\bf Q}}^{z}-V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}+\Delta_{{\rm sas}}\right],

(148)

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{{\bf-Q}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bf Q}}f^{2}\left({\bf Q}\right)\left[\frac{\rho_{s}^{0}-\rho_{s}^{z}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+\Delta_{{\rm sas}}\right].

(149)

We used the definitions,

\rho_{s}^{0/z}=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{{\bf p}}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}V_{{\bf p}}^{0/z}f^{2}\left({\bf p}\right)\left|{\bf p}\right|^{2},

(150)

E_{\rm easy}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf p}}\left[V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}-V_{{\bf p}}^{z}f^{2}\left({\bf p}\right)\right].

(151)

The
\rho_{s}
quantity defines a stiffness for the fluctuations in different directions, whereas
E_{\rm easy}
is the capacitive charging energy, or alternatively, the Hartree-Fock energy difference defining the easy-plane manifold [see Eq. (
142
)].

Since we are interested in the behavior to leading order in
\bf Q
, we may absorb into the coordinate variables the global
f^{2}\left({\bf Q}\right)
factor, and renormalize the stiffness contributions to include the smooth (vanishing at
{\bf Q}\to 0
)
f^{2}\left({\bf Q}\right)V_{{\bf Q}}^{z}-V_{{\bf 0}}^{z}
interaction.
We can thus finally identify the appropriate single-mode parameters,

\omega_{{\bf Q}}=\frac{\rho_{s}^{0}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+E_{\rm easy}+\Delta_{{\rm sas}},

(152)

z_{{\bf Q}}=\frac{\rho_{s}^{z}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+E_{\rm easy}.

(153)

The associated spectrum is

{\cal E}_{{\bf Q}}=\sqrt{\frac{\left(\rho_{s}^{0}+\rho_{s}^{z}\right)\left(\rho_{s}^{0}-\rho_{s}^{z}\right)}{4}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{4}+\left(\Delta_{{\rm sas}}+E_{\rm easy}\left(\frac{\rho_{s}^{0}-\rho_{s}^{z}}{\rho_{s}^{0}}\right)\right)\rho_{s}^{0}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}+2\Delta_{{\rm sas}}E_{\rm easy}+\Delta_{{\rm sas}}^{2}},

(154)

Let us make several observations, starting with the limit
\Delta_{\rm sas}\to 0
.
In that case, for small momentum
\left|{\bf Q}\right|\ll\sqrt{\frac{8E_{\rm easy}}{\rho_{s}^{0}+\rho_{s}^{z}}}
,
one finds the expected linearly dispersing Goldstone mode,

{\cal E}_{\bf Q}\approx\sqrt{E_{\rm easy}\left(\rho_{s}^{0}-\rho_{s}^{z}\right)}\left|{\bf Q}\right|
.

In another concrete case, let us allow for finite interlayer tunneling, which is typically much smaller than
E^{\rm cap}
.
The energy of the
{\bf Q}=0
mode, i.e., the gap of the Goldstone spectrum, is roughly the geometric mean of the
E^{\rm cap}
and tunneling energy scales,

{\cal E}_{{\bf 0}}=\sqrt{2E_{\rm easy}\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}{2E_{\rm easy}}}.

(155)

The susceptibility toward parametrically driving this mode an be easily extracted,

\delta z=\frac{{\cal E}_{{\bf 0}}}{2\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}{2E_{\rm easy}}\right)}d\left[\log\frac{E_{\rm easy}}{\Delta_{{\rm sas}}}\right].

(156)

Appendix F
Flat band model

As discussed in the main text, motivated by the rich and complex hierarchy of correlated ground state of twisted multilayer graphene, we consider the following simplified model.
The model is related to a tunable-metric model introduced in Refs.
Hofmann et al. 2022a
;
Hofmann, Berg, and Chowdhury 2023
.
The single-particle Hamiltonian we explore has valley
\tau_{i}
, pseudo-spin
\sigma_{i}
, and orbital
\rho_{i}
degrees of freedom (all Pauli matrices),

H=-t\sum_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}}^{\dagger}\left[\left(\frac{1+\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\cos\alpha_{{\bf k}}+\frac{1-\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\cos\beta_{{\bf k}}\right)\rho_{x}+\left(\frac{1+\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\sin\alpha_{{\bf k}}+\frac{1-\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}}{2}\sin\beta_{{\bf k}}\right)\rho_{y}+\Delta_{\sigma}\sigma_{z}\right]c_{{\bf k}}.

(157)

The momentum-dependent functions
\alpha_{\bf k}
and
\beta_{\bf k}
determine the quantum geometric properties of the resultant flat bands, and are related to each other by a 45-degree rotation,

\alpha_{{\bf k}}=\zeta\left(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}\right),

(158)

\beta_{{\bf k}}=\zeta\left(\cos\frac{k_{x}+k_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}+\cos\frac{k_{x}-k_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(159)

The parameter
\zeta
controls the quantum metric of the projected flat bands.
The Hamiltonian Eq. (
52a
) has a total of eight bands: four valence bands sitting at
-t
, and four conduction bands at
+t
(when
\Delta_{\sigma}=0
).
Our interest will lie in one set of these isolated bands, e.g., the valence bands.
Within such set, one may define a good quantum number which further classifies the bands,
{\cal C}=\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}
.

Let us consider the simplest form density-density interactions within the projected valence bands,

H_{{\rm C}}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf qkk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf q}}c_{{\bf k+q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k+q,k}}c_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q,k^{\prime}}}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}},

(160)

where the form factors are

\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}={\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}\sigma_{z}\tau_{z},

(161)

with

{\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}=\frac{\cos\left(\alpha_{{\bf k}}-\alpha_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)+\cos\left(\beta_{{\bf k}}-\beta_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)}{2}
, and

{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}=\frac{\cos\left(\alpha_{{\bf k}}-\alpha_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)-\cos\left(\beta_{{\bf k}}-\beta_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)}{2}
.

What is the ground state manifold of this Hamiltonian at half-filling of the valence bands?
We employ Hartree-Fock calculations and find,

\left\langle H_{{\rm C}}\right\rangle=2AV_{0}+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\frac{{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}}{4}{\rm Tr}\left\{\mathbb{I}-\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}{\cal O}\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}{\cal O}\right\},

(162)

which has a very clear consequence:
The ground state manifold under the
H_{\rm C}
interaction is the one with an order parameter which commutes with
{\cal C}
,

\left[{\cal O}_{\rm g.s.},{\cal C}\right]=0.

(163)

The ground-state energy density difference between this manifold and other generalized ferromagnets is then

\frac{\Delta E_{{\rm C}}}{A}=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\left|{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}\right|^{2}
.

F.1
Symmetry breaking perturbations

Now, let us introduce perturbations which pick out specific members of this large
{\cal O}_{\rm g.s.}
manifold.
First, we consider an interaction term which picks out an order
\sim\sigma_{y}\left(\cos\theta\tau_{x}+i\sin\theta\tau_{y}\right)
(with some arbitrary angle
\theta
),

\displaystyle H_{{\rm K}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf qkk^{\prime}}}u^{\rm K}_{{\bf q}}c_{{\bf k+q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k+q,k}}\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}c_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q,k^{\prime}}}\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf qkk^{\prime}}}u^{\rm K}_{{\bf q}}c_{{\bf k+q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k+q,k}}\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}c_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q,k^{\prime}}}\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}},

(164)

with
u^{\rm K}_{\bf q}<0
.
The “
\rm K
” label hints at the analogous order in TBG, which is the Kramers intervalley coherent order (KIVC).
Next, let us introduce a similar interaction which favors
\sim\sigma_{x}\left(\cos\theta\tau_{x}+i\sin\theta\tau_{y}\right)
order,

\displaystyle H_{{\rm T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf qkk^{\prime}}}u^{\rm T}_{{\bf q}}c_{{\bf k+q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k+q,k}}\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}c_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q,k^{\prime}}}\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf qkk^{\prime}}}u^{\rm T}_{{\bf q}}c_{{\bf k+q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k+q,k}}\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}c_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}-q,k^{\prime}}}\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}c_{{\bf k^{\prime}}},

(165)

with
u^{\rm T}_{\bf q}<0
.
This interaction in the case of TBG has been related to interaction between electrons and intervalley phonons, and associated with stabilization of a time-reversal invariant intervalley coherent state (TIVC) in pristine (i.e., vanishing strain) devices
Kwan et al. 2024
.
Let us also single-out the single-particle perturbation which explicitly breaks the pseudospin symmetry at the single-particle level,

H_{SB}=-\Delta_{\sigma}\sum_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}c_{{\bf k}},

(166)

akin to sublattice symmetry breaking in TBG devices.

Hartree-Fock calculations yield

\displaystyle\left\langle H_{{\rm K}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{0}^{{\rm K}}}{8}\left|{\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma_{y}\left(\tau_{x}+i\tau_{y}\right){\cal O}\right\}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}\left(\left|{\cal F}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\cal D}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}\right){\rm Tr}\left\{P^{T}\left(\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}+\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}\right){\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}P^{T}\left(\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}+\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}\right)\right\},

(167)

\displaystyle\left\langle H_{{\rm T}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{0}^{{\rm T}}}{8}\left|{\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma_{x}\left(\tau_{x}+i\tau_{y}\right){\cal O}\right\}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}\left(\left|{\cal F}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\cal D}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}\right){\rm Tr}\left\{P^{T}\left(\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}+\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime},k}}\right){\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}P^{T}\left(\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}+\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\bar{P}^{T}\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\right)\right\},

(168)

\left\langle H_{SB}\right\rangle=-A\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}}{2}{\rm Tr}\left\langle\sigma_{z}{\cal O}\right\rangle.

(169)

We now calculate different ground-state energy densities relative to
\Delta E_{\rm C}
,

\frac{E_{{\rm T}}}{A}=2u_{0}^{{\rm T}}-\epsilon_{{\rm geo.}},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\frac{E_{{\rm K}}}{A}=2u_{0}^{{\rm K}}-\epsilon_{{\rm geo.}},

(170)

\frac{E_{\sigma}}{A}=-2\epsilon_{{\rm geo.}}-2\Delta_{\sigma},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\frac{E_{\tau}}{A}=-2\epsilon_{{\rm geo.}},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\frac{E_{{\cal C}}}{A}=0.

(171)

Here,
E_{\rm T/K}
correspond to the different IVC orders, and
E_{\sigma}
,
E_{\tau}
, and
E_{\cal C}
correspond to
\sigma_{z}
,
\tau_{z}
, and
\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}
orders, respectively.
Finally,

\epsilon_{{\rm geo.}}=-\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left(u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}\right)\left(\left|{\cal F}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\cal D}_{{\bf k,k^{\prime}}}\right|^{2}\right)>0,

(172)

and has a quantum geometric interpretation.
Using the fact that
u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T/K}}
is physically only a function of
\left|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}\right|
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{{\rm geo.}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left(u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}\right)\cos^{2}\left(\alpha_{{\bf k}}-\alpha_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}}{2}+\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}}{4}\left[e^{i\left(2\alpha_{{\bf k}}-2\alpha_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right)}+{\rm h.c.}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d{\bf k}d{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{4}}\frac{u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}}{2}+\int\frac{d{\bf k}d{\bf k^{\prime}}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{4}}\frac{u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}}{4}\left[e^{2i\zeta\cos k_{x}}e^{2i\zeta\cos k_{y}}e^{-2i\zeta\cos k_{x}^{\prime}}e^{-2i\zeta\cos k_{y}^{\prime}}+{\rm h.c.}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d{\bf q}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\frac{u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm K}}}{2}\left[1+\sum_{ab}\left[J_{a}\left(2\zeta\right)J_{b}\left(2\zeta\right)\right]^{2}e^{iaq_{x}+ibq_{y}}\right].

(173)

A comment is in order regarding the dependence of
\epsilon_{\rm geo.}
on the parameter
\zeta
.
It is in fact related to the
spatial range
of the
u^{\rm T/K}
interactions,
R
.
The Fourier transform appearing in Eq. (
173
) clearly demonstrates that the
\zeta
-dependence is quenched if
R\gg\zeta
.
The physical interpretation is simple:
\zeta
is related to the spatial width of a maximally local wave packet of electrons in the flat band of interest.
The sensitivity of the Hartree-Fock energy hierarchy to
\zeta
will then be most pronounced when the range of these interactions is comparable to this width, i.e., for
\zeta\sim R
.

Next, for the sake of simplicity, we will assume that these interactions are short range, i.e., momentum independent, and define

u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm T}}+u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm K}}\equiv-u<0
,

u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm T}}-u_{{\bf q}}^{{\rm K}}=-\Delta_{\rm TK}<0
.
Shifting all energies by a constant
-\epsilon_{\rm geo.}
, the three lowest lying manifolds have energies

\frac{E_{{\rm T}}}{A}=-\Delta_{\rm TK},\,\,\,\,\,\frac{E_{{\rm K}}}{A}=\frac{E_{{\rm T}}}{A}+2\Delta_{\rm TK},\,\,\,\,\,\frac{E_{\sigma}}{A}=\frac{E_{{\rm T}}}{A}+\Delta_{\rm TK}+u_{\zeta}-2\Delta_{\sigma}.

(174)

Here, we defined
u_{\zeta}\equiv u-\epsilon_{\rm geo.}=u\left[1-J_{0}^{4}\left(2\zeta\right)\right]
.
Hereafter, we assume
u_{\zeta}>\Delta_{\rm TK}
, such that in the absence of the explicit pseudospin symmetry breaking, the ground state is T(IVC), and its closest competitor is the K(IVC) phase.
This is presumed to be the case for pristine TBG, taking the aforementioned electron-phonon interactions into account
Kwan et al. 2024
.
Conversely, if
\Delta_{\sigma}>\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{\rm TK}+u_{\zeta}\right)
, the order parameter for the lowest-energy state is
\sigma_{z}
.

F.2
Fluctuation Lagrangian

The contribution of the primary density-density interaction is

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm C}}\right]\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf Q}ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{4}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left[V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}\right)-\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf p}}V_{{\bf k-p}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kp}}^{2}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kp}}^{2}\right)\right]\delta^{ij}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{4}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left[V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}\right)-\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf p}}V_{{\bf k-p}}\left(2{\cal F}_{{\bf kp}}{\cal D}_{{\bf kp}}\right)\right]\times

\displaystyle\times\frac{{\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}{\cal M}^{j}{\cal M}^{i}\right\}}{4}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right).

(175)

By noticing the momentum sums in the second line vanish, due to the invariance of the interaction strength to 45-degree rotation in momentum space, we are left with the sole contribution

\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm C}}\right]\right]\right\rangle=\sum_{{\bf Q}ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{4}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left[V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}\right)-\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\sum_{{\bf p}}V_{{\bf k-p}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kp}}^{2}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kp}}^{2}\right)\right]\delta^{ij}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right).

(176)

Anticipating a lowest-mode approximation in the small
\left|{\bf Q}\right|
limit, it turns out that the leading
\bf Q
contribution may be well captured by simply replacing,

\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)\approx\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{C}}^{i}_{\bf Q},

(177)

with
{\mathbb{C}}^{i}
the relevant coordinate for the
{\cal M}^{i}
generator.
This simplifies the above expression,

-\frac{1}{2A}\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm C}}\right]\right]\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q},i}\mathbb{C}_{{\bf-Q}}^{i}\mathbb{C}_{{\bf Q}}^{i}I\left({\bf Q}\right),

(178)

with

I\left({\bf Q}\right)=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kp}}V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\left[{\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}-{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}\right)+{\cal F}\to{\cal D}\right].

(179)

Expanding in small momentum, one finds

I\left({\bf Q}\right)\approx\frac{\rho_{s}}{2}\left|{\bf Q}\right|^{2}
,
with the stiffness given by the sum

\rho_{s}=\zeta^{2}\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}V_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}\left(\sin k_{x}-\sin k^{\prime}_{x}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\alpha_{{\bf k}}-\alpha_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}\right).

(180)

Scenario T:
{\cal O}=\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}

The contribution of the secondary interaction terms to the fluctuation Hamiltonian may be written as,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm T}}\right]\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{A}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm T}}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}k}}}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[{\cal M}^{j}-\tau_{z}{\cal M}^{j}\tau_{z}\right]{\cal M}^{i}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{u_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm T}}}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{j}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\tau_{z}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k}}\tau_{z}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+16\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}u_{{\bf 0}}^{{\rm T}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\delta^{ij}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right),

(181)

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm K}}\right]\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{A}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}u_{{\bf k-k^{\prime}}}^{{\rm K}}\Lambda_{{\bf{\bf k^{\prime}k}}}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[{\cal M}^{j}-\sigma_{z}{\cal M}^{j}\sigma_{z}\right]{\cal M}^{i}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{u_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm K}}}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{j}\Lambda_{{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k^{\prime}+Q}}\sigma_{z}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\Lambda_{{\bf k+Q},{\bf k}}\sigma_{z}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right).

(182)

We can consolidate these two terms, employing the assumptions regarding the short range nature of the interaction,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm T}}\right]\right]\right\rangle+\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm K}}\right]\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=u\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{A}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}\right)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[{\cal M}^{j}-\tau_{z}{\cal M}^{j}\tau_{z}\right]{\cal M}^{i}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left(\frac{u+\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}{\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q,k^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k}}+\frac{u-\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q,k^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k}}\right)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\tau_{z}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\tau_{z}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left(\frac{u-\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}{\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q,k^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k}}+\frac{u+\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q,k^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k}}\right)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\sigma_{z}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\sigma_{z}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle-16\frac{u+\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\delta_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\delta^{ij}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+u\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{A}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q,k^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k}}+{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k^{\prime}+Q,k^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\tau_{z}\right\}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal M}^{i}\sigma_{z}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle+u\sum_{{\bf Q}}\frac{1}{A}\sum_{ij{\bf kk^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf k^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\left({\cal F}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal D}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}+{\cal D}_{{\bf kk^{\prime}}}{\cal F}_{{\bf k+Q,k^{\prime}+Q}}\right)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Tr}\left\{\left[{\cal M}^{j}-\tau_{z}{\cal M}^{j}\tau_{z}\right]{\cal M}^{i}\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}\right\}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf k}\right).

(183)

Performing the crude lowest-mode approximation, we assign coordinates to the different generators in the following manner,

\tau_{z}\to{\mathbb{X}_{\bf Q}^{\rm G}}
,

\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}\to{\mathbb{P}_{\bf Q}^{\rm G}}
,

\sigma_{z}\to{\mathbb{X}_{\bf Q}^{\rm K}}
,

\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}\to{\mathbb{P}_{\bf Q}^{\rm K}}
.
We are going to focus below on the
{\bf Q}\to 0
contribution of these terms.
One interesting “casualty” of this approximation is embodied by the last two lines in the expression above.
Specifically, this term captures a coupling at larger moment between the gapless Goldstone mode and the so-called “K” mode.
Within this approximation, we can finally find the contribution

\displaystyle-\frac{\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm T}}\right]\right]\right\rangle+\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm K}}\right]\right]\right\rangle}{2A}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{u_{\zeta}+\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm G}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm G}}+\mathbb{X}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm G}}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm G}}+\mathbb{P}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm G}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm G}}-\mathbb{X}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm G}}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm G}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{\zeta}+3\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm K}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm K}}+\mathbb{X}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm K}}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm K}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{\zeta}-\Delta_{{\rm TK}}}{2}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm K}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm K}}-\mathbb{X}_{{\bf-Q}}^{{\rm K}}\mathbb{X}_{{\bf Q}}^{{\rm K}}\right).

(184)

Scenario
\sigma
:
{\cal O}=\sigma_{z}

Let us begin by considering the contribution of the additional single-particle term,

\left\langle\left[{\hat{F}},\left[{\hat{F}},H_{\sigma}\right]\right]\right\rangle=-8\Delta_{\sigma}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\sum_{ij{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\left[\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{j}\left({\bf p^{\prime}}\right)\right]^{*}\delta_{{\bf pp^{\prime}}}\delta^{ij}\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{i}\left({\bf p}\right).

(185)

It is already clear that this creates a uniform gap in the spectrum of collective excitations.

The secondary interactions supply the contribution,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},H_{{\rm K}}+H_{{\rm T}}\right]\right]\right\rangle
