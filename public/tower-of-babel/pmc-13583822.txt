Role of gender, age and rurality in benzodiazepine consumption in Catalonia, 2018–2022: a retrospective longitudinal drug utilisation study
Authors: Belén Barrera Aranda, Ignacio Aznar-Lou, María Teresa Peñarrubia-María, Elisa Chilet Rosell, Marta Puig-García, Blanca Lumbreras, Elsa López-Pintor, Abraham Beltrán Pérez, Lucy Anne Parker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBenzodiazepines are among the most widely prescribed medications for numerous indications, but their adverse effects, misuse and consequences pose an increasing public health concern. This study analyses trends in benzodiazepine use in Catalonia from 2018 to 2022, focusing on differences by age, sex and rural–urban residence.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal drug utilisation study was conducted using the Information System for Research in Primary Care primary care database, including 6 992 261 individuals aged ≥15 years registered in Catalonia between 2018 and 2022. Drugs were classified according to the WHO’s Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical system. Quantities dispensed were measured in defined daily doses (DDD) and expressed as DDD per 1000 inhabitants per day (DID) for each quarter. Data were stratified by age, sex and area of residence.ResultsBenzodiazepine consumption increased across all demographic groups between 2018 and 2022. Key findings showed significant disparities by sex, age and urban–rural area, exacerbated by the intersection of these factors. Rural women over 65 showed the highest usage levels (428 DID in 2022), while young adults experienced the largest proportional increases with an estimated average quarterly percentage change of 4.06 (95% CI 4.02 to 4.09) in women and 3.20 (95% CI 3.15 to 3.23) among men. No statistically significant structural break was detected at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting that observed fluctuations reflected the underlying temporal trend.ConclusionsThe consistent rise in benzodiazepine consumption across all age groups in Catalonia highlights gaps in prevention and management strategies. Disparities linked to socioeconomic factors, gender roles, older age and area of residence underscore structural inequities affecting older adults, women and rural populations. These findings reflect a deep-rooted public health challenge in Spain, driven by pharmacological over-reliance and insufficient attention to the impact caused by social determinants of health. Addressing this requires coordinated and multidisciplinary reforms to tackle inequity and enhance societal well-being.Keywords: Pharmacoepidemiology, Sociodemographic Factors, trends, Public Health, Drug Monitoring

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Benzodiazepines (BZDs) are widely prescribed despite their well-known risks, particularly among older adults. Prior research has shown gender and age disparities in BZD use, as well as higher prescription rates in rural settings. Most drug utilisation studies focus on national-level trends without disaggregating the interaction between sex, age and area of residency.

Introduction

Benzodiazepines (BZDs) are widely used tranquillisers that bind to the gamma-aminobutyric acid A receptors, inducing anxiolytic, sedative, hypnotic, anticonvulsant and muscle-relaxing effects.1 They are prescribed for conditions such as anxiety, depression, insomnia, epilepsy and pain. However, BZDs carry significant risks including drowsiness, impaired coordination, fatigue and severe complications like respiratory depression, which can lead to potentially fatal overdose, especially at higher doses, with prolonged use or when combined with other depressants or opioids.1,2 The strong addictive potential of BZDs, exacerbated by widespread prescription and therapy perpetuation, poses a significant challenge for deprescribing efforts,3,4 underscoring the urgent need for more responsible and evidence-based prescribing practices.3,5 Although guidelines recommend limiting BZD use to 4–12 weeks, depending on the indication, extended use is common,6–8 impacting cognitive and physical performance, increasing risks in activities like work and driving9,10 and causing significant socioeconomic repercussions.11 This issue reflects a broader public health concern, with markedly elevated rates of BZD prescription and misuse worldwide, particularly in high-income countries, including Spain, where BZD consumption ranks among the highest globally.4,12,13

This widespread consumption is driven by a complex mix of factors. In clinical practice, overprescription is often linked to healthcare professionals’ heavy workloads and insufficient training in deprescribing, alternative therapies and psychosocial skills, leading to an over-reliance on BZDs as a quick fix for complex mental health issues.14,15 But this issue transcends clinical reasons. Epidemiologically, BZD consumption is globally associated with women, older age, polypharmacy and low income.13,16

Age is a key factor, with older adults not only consuming more BZDs but also using them for longer periods,4,8,17 increasing risks of falls, cognitive dysfunction and dementia.18 Meanwhile, there has also been a notable increase in mental distress19 and BZD consumption among children and adolescents, especially among women.20,21 This trend is particularly concerning given the vulnerability of developing brains to dependence and other adverse effects,22 carrying lifelong implications.

Female sex is a consistent risk factor for BZD consumption globally.4,12,16 Research suggests that women’s heightened susceptibility to anxiety and depression may stem from greater personal distress in negative situations, temperamental factors and a stronger tendency to recognise and seek help for emotional symptoms.23,24 However, it is crucial to acknowledge the profound influence of social and cultural factors on these trends. Persistent patriarchal norms and systemic inequalities significantly shape women’s lives, making female sex a key determinant of health, economic and social disparities.25 Furthermore, evidence shows that healthcare professionals tend to prescribe certain medications, including BZDs, to women more often than to men for specific health conditions.26,27 Taken together, these findings highlight that attributing these patterns solely to biological factors would overlook the complex social dynamics at play.

Rural populations face heightened vulnerability due to precarious labour market conditions, limited education and insufficient healthcare resources,28 which have been shown to contribute to increased reliance on BZDs as a coping mechanism.29 Studies show that patients in rural areas are more likely to receive psychotropic prescriptions regardless of age, highlighting healthcare access disparities and systemic inequalities.16,30

Understanding the interaction of these different factors can be useful for developing more effective strategies that address their root causes. This study aims to describe the changes in BZD consumption from January 2018 to December 2022 in Catalonia, with a specific focus on sex, age and residential area.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective longitudinal drug utilisation study using real-world data to analyse BZD prescription trends in primary care in Catalonia from January 2018 to December 2022. The study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) statement.31 A completed RECORD checklist indicating where each item is reported is provided as a online supplemental file 1.

Study population

The study population comprises 6 992 261 individuals aged 15 and older, registered in Institut Català de la Salut (ICS) primary care centres. ICS provides public primary care to 75% of the population in Catalonia (Spanish region of around 8 million inhabitants). Data were retrieved from the Information System for Research in Primary Care (SIDIAP) database,32 which incorporates data from the ICS electronic clinical history system, Estació Clínica d’Atenció Primària (eCAP) and provides patient information linked to a unique, anonymous identifier. Researchers accessed anonymised prescription records through the SIDIAP secure research environment under a formal data use agreement. No access to identifiable records or direct database querying at the source system was permitted. Data from eCAP are internally linked within the SIDIAP database using a unique anonymised patient identifier; no linkage across external databases was performed.

No a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the study included the full population meeting the inclusion criteria in the SIDIAP database during the study period. BZDs were identified using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system codes N05BA, N05CD and N05CF which have been previously used and validated for identifying BZD prescriptions in studies using SIDIAP data.33 A complete list of ATC codes used to define BZD prescriptions, together with detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, is provided in online supplemental table S2.

Variables

BZD use was measured using the WHO’s defined daily dose (DDD) metric33 and expressed as DDD per 1000 inhabitants per day (DID), a standardised measure estimating the average number of people per 1000 inhabitants receiving one DDD each day.

Mean DID was calculated for each 90-day period using calculations from the official population estimates (SIDIAP) for 2022 according to age group, sex and rurality (rural areas are defined as municipalities with a population density of <100 people per km2 and/or a population of <30 000 inhabitants32). For years 2018–2021 we estimated the population by applying an average annual growth factor of 0.9922 derived from official population estimates from the Spanish National Statistics Institute. The population numbers are available in online supplemental table S1.

Participants were categorised into four distinct age groups to facilitate analysis of BZD usage trends across different life stages. Group 1 included individuals aged 15–24 years; group 2 comprised individuals aged 25–44 years; group 3 encompassed individuals aged 45–64 years; group 4 included individuals aged 65 years and older.

As this study involved secondary analysis of anonymised real-world data, no patient consent was required and patients were not involved in the design or conduct of the study.

Data analysis

BZD consumption trends from 2018 to 2022 were analysed by sex, age group and area of residence (urban vs rural). SIDIAP data are subject to routine quality control and validation procedures; therefore, no additional completeness checks were required beyond identifying BZD prescriptions using the predefined ATC codes. To assess differences in consumption patterns and temporal trends, we fitted Poisson regression models with a log link and an offset for the logarithm of the population at risk. Models included sex, age group, area of residence, time and their interactions. Because preliminary analyses indicated non-linear temporal trends, models including linear and quadratic terms for time were compared using the Akaike information criterion, and the quadratic specification was retained. Model-based predictions were used to estimate consumption trajectories for each demographic subgroup. Relative rates comparing population groups were expressed as rate ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. Temporal trends were summarised as estimated quarterly percentage changes derived from the fitted models. Quarter-specific percentage changes (QPC) were summarised using their geometric mean across the study period, yielding a single estimate of the average quarterly change for each demographic subgroup. Given the visual suggestion of an increase in consumption around the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, an interrupted time series analysis was performed to formally assess whether the second quarter of 2020 was associated with a statistically significant level change indicative of a structural break in the series.

Data management and visualisation were performed using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata V.17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) and R V.4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Consumption increased across demographic groups throughout the study period; however, the quadratic trend indicated a progressive flattening of the curve over time, suggesting that the rate of increase slowed in the later years of follow-up. Although variability was observed during 2020–2022, no statistically significant structural break was detected at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating that these fluctuations reflected the underlying temporal pattern rather than a pandemic-related change in consumption.

According to our data, age emerged as a significant determinant in BZD consumption patterns. Compared with adults aged 25–44 years, consumption was over four times higher among those aged 45–64 years (RR=4.10, 95% CI 4.03 to 4.17) and more than 15 times higher among those aged ≥65 years (RR=15.52, 95% CI 15.18 to 15.87). Age-related differences in BZD consumption varied significantly by both sex and area of residence, with a stronger age gradient observed among women than men and among rural compared with urban populations (table 1).

Discussion

BZD use in Catalonia increased across all demographic groups between 2018 and 2022. Older adults, particularly women aged ≥65 years in rural areas, showed the highest consumption levels, whereas the steepest increases over time were observed among younger individuals aged 15–24 years. Similar patterns are reported in other regions of Spain17,34,35 and internationally.4,13,36 Some countries have documented modest decreases in BZD use through a variety of strategies such as reimbursement restrictions,37 pay-for-performance schemes,38 specific physician training,39 national public health campaigns,40 prescription limits and reduced telephone prescribing41 or structured deprescribing programmes combining patient education, taper plans and clinician support.42 A recent review found low-certainty evidence for multiple discontinuation and deprescribing strategies, though patient education, medication reviews and pharmacist-led interventions may improve discontinuation rates of BZDs and related sedative-hypnotics.43

However, while some interventions have yielded modest improvements in BZD consumption rates, their impact remains insufficient, particularly among women, older adults, younger people and people in vulnerable situations.4,13,16 These trends suggest that the drivers of BZD use are embedded in broader social and structural factors operating in Catalonia during the study period, including gender inequalities, caregiving burden, economic insecurity, disparities in rural healthcare access and increasing social distress among younger populations. In this context, BZD consumption may reflect not only a clinical issue but also a broader societal indicator of distress.44

Approaching this problem requires evidence-based prescribing and deprescribing strategies,3,34,45 stronger healthcare systems with adequate resources, expanding access to mental healthcare and alternative therapies, enhancing health education and providing better support during critical life events.46 But the experience with healthcare interventions shows that these measures, while essential, are insufficient on their own to ensure mental well-being without addressing broader social determinants such as food and financial security, family support and access to education and physical activity.47 To address the impact of our current societal model, we must focus on those most affected by structural inequities.

The disproportionate consumption of BZDs by women is a well-documented global trend.4,23 Understanding this disparity requires examination of the complex interplay of clinical, social and structural factors, including prescription patterns, caregiving burdens, economic insecurity, the impact of patriarchy and the enduring influence of traditional gender roles. Structural inequalities, such as the over-representation of women in caregiving and low-income occupations, along with unpaid domestic labour and economic dependence,48–50 increase stress and vulnerability to prolonged trauma, including intimate partner violence, which according to the WHO affects one in three women globally.51,52 Therefore, gendered patterns in BZD consumption could reflect structural inequalities. However, gender bias in BZD prescription should also be considered, as evidence suggests that women generally receive higher cumulative BZD dosages than men, a pattern consistent across different psychiatric conditions such as anxiety disorders and major depression.26,27 These structural and gender-related inequalities may partly explain the persistently higher BZD consumption observed among women in our study.

The observed increase in BZD consumption among men has also been documented in other regions.25,53,54 Although in some contexts the rise was more pronounced in men, overall consumption levels consistently remain higher among women. In the literature, such trends are often attributed to factors such as limited access to mental healthcare and overprescription. However, an important question for future research is whether, beyond clinical need, persistent BZD demand may partly reflect broader social distress and whether addressing such distress should form part of a more comprehensive response to the growing burden of BZD consumption.

Geographical isolation may further contribute to these disparities, as our data reveal a consistently higher use of BZDs in rural compared with urban populations across all demographics, particularly in women. Similar research on the rural–urban differences revealed that rural residents are 2.5 times more likely to use BZDs compared with their urban counterparts.55 Rural areas worldwide face systemic disparities in healthcare access, which significantly impact health outcomes,56 including increased suicide risks.57 Limited availability of specialised mental health resources in rural areas leads to a greater over-reliance on BZDs as a first-line treatment for anxiety, insomnia and other mental health conditions.30 In Spain the smaller the municipality, the higher the likelihood of being prescribed psychotropic drugs, regardless of age.16 However, studies suggest that when access to healthcare is equivalent, rural residents often report better mental health and functional capacity,58 suggesting that improving access to adequate healthcare could help close the gap.

Qualitative studies highlight the challenges faced by physicians in socioeconomically deprived neighbourhoods, where BZD prescriptions often function as a form of social containment. While a close doctor–patient relationship can facilitate acceptance of non-pharmacological strategies in some cases, it may also increase physicians’ susceptibility to patient pressure and requests for medication.15,59

Beyond healthcare, rural–urban inequalities extend to fewer employment, education and leisure opportunities60 and stricter patriarchal norms.61,62 These factors contribute to increased risks of financial dependence, inadequate legal protection and mental health challenges,52,63 including depression, anxiety and substance abuse,64,65 which may partly underlie the consistently higher BZD consumption observed in rural populations in our study. The intersection of female sex, older age and rural residency results in a threefold higher relative risk of BZD consumption compared with men of the same age living in urban areas. Older adults not only consume more BZDs, but also use them for longer periods.4,8,17,34 This is particularly concerning as even modest doses of BZDs significantly increase the risks of falls and hip fractures by 50%, and by 80% if the treatment exceeds 1 month.66 Moreover, prolonged BZD use contributes to cognitive dysfunction and prolonged dependence.18 These factors may contribute to the particularly high and persistent BZD consumption observed among older adults, especially older women living in rural areas.

Deprescribing BZDs is particularly challenging among older patients, who are often reluctant to accept alternatives or dose reductions after long-term use. General practitioners report greater difficulties with this group, especially in patients with a history of long-term BZD use, due to the perception that some are ‘beyond cure’ or ‘more deserving’, and the anticipation of patient resistance to alternative approaches, including the possibility that patients may switch physicians.14,67

Age emerged as a key factor not only among older adults but also in younger populations. Despite starting from much lower baseline consumption levels, individuals aged 15–24 years showed the steepest increase in BZD use over time, particularly young women. Although absolute consumption remained substantially lower than in older age groups, this relative increase is noteworthy given that Spain is among the countries with the highest overall BZD consumption.12 The challenges faced by young people in today’s society are multifaceted and pose a significant threat to their mental well-being.19–21 Financial instability, housing insecurity and family poverty, all contribute to heightened stress and anxiety, which may lead to an increased reliance on BZDs as a coping mechanism.68 In Spain, financial barriers to independence and starting a family,69 along with social factors such as social media influence70 and the normalisation of BZD use within families,71 have been reported as possible drivers of consumption trends among young people.

But the younger generation is also confronted with broader challenges, including geopolitical instability, climate-related concerns and the societal demands of sustaining a desired lifestyle.68 Coupled with a growing sense of powerlessness over the future, these factors may foster hopelessness and an inability to envision an achievable future, contributing to widespread mental distress, characterised by anxiety, depression and a general sense of discomfort.72,73 Managing these pressures requires a set of emotional skills that are often still developing at this age, potentially contributing to greater reliance on pharmacological coping strategies. The marked increase observed among younger individuals in our study may therefore reflect the cumulative impact of these social and economic stressors on mental well-being.

Therefore, the increase in BZD consumption may be best understood as a multifactorial phenomenon, requiring an equally multifactorial approach. Strengthening healthcare systems with adequate resources and implementing evidence-based prescription and deprescription guidelines are essential. However, it also demands broader social interventions, including improving financial and food security, enhancing community support and expanding access to education and physical activities.47 These measures create the necessary conditions for individuals to address challenges beyond mere survival, fostering long-term well-being and contributing to build resilience.74 During the study period, social vulnerability remained substantial in Spain. In 2021, the at risk of poverty or social exclusion (AROPE) rate reached 27.8% in the general population, while children and adolescents were disproportionately affected, with 33.4% of individuals aged under 18 years at risk of poverty or social exclusion (AROPE rate75). The biological embedding derived from poverty and its consequences has been shown to profoundly influence lifelong mental and physical health, increasing the likelihood of psychotropic medication use in adulthood.76

Maintaining health not only delivers individual well-being but maintains individuals’ contribution to society and the economy, reducing the fiscal burden of illness and dependence on welfare,77 while the significant economic impact associated with the use of psychotropic medications further incentivises this.78 We live in a society marked by persistent social and economic inequalities that contribute to unequal health outcomes across populations, and addressing these structural injustices is essential to creating the conditions for better health, well-being and social resilience.79

This study had a series of limitations. First, our study assumes drug intake based on dispensing data, which may overestimate actual consumption. Although actual intake may be overestimated, pharmacy dispensing data are widely accepted as a pragmatic proxy for adherence, with studies showing that a substantial proportion of BZD prescriptions are indeed dispensed.80 Additionally, the SIDIAP database did not contain sufficiently detailed information on the specific indications associated with individual BZD prescriptions, limiting our ability to assess the underlying reasons for prescribing patterns across demographic groups. Although active health conditions were available, these could not be directly interpreted as prescribing indications, particularly in patients with multiple concurrent conditions. This is a common limitation of routinely collected healthcare databases, where reasons for prescription are rarely systematically recorded. Second, the exclusion of private system prescriptions could underestimate overall use, as private practices may have less oversight.81 Third, inaccuracies and limitations in eCAP data recording may introduce bias in socio-demographic analyses, particularly due to the binary sex classification in the SIDIAP database. This classification does not account for the experiences of transgender and non-binary individuals, who face distinct mental health challenges and often experience poorer outcomes.82 Future research should adopt inclusive gender identity classifications to better analyse BZD consumption and address the mental health needs of marginalised populations. Another limitation relates to external validity. As the study was conducted using data from Catalonia, caution is warranted when generalising absolute consumption estimates to other regions or countries with different healthcare systems, prescribing practices or socio-demographic characteristics. However, the study draws on a large population-based dataset from a universal healthcare system, and the observed patterns by sex, age and rurality are consistent with previous national and international evidence, supporting the broader relevance of these findings. Finally, estimations based on census-derived growth factors may introduce minor demographic discrepancies.

Conclusions

Our study revealed concerning trends in BZD consumption with an increase across all population groups. Elderly rural women exhibited the highest levels of BZD use, while consumption nearly doubled during the study period among young women aged 15–24 in both rural and urban settings. The consistently higher consumption observed in rural areas may reflect the interplay of socioeconomic precarity, gendered social roles, disparities in access to mental health services and structural disadvantages experienced at the intersection of age and sex. Despite the COVID-19 pandemic, the observed rise in BZD consumption followed pre-pandemic trends, indicating a sustained increase rather than a temporary anomaly in response to the pandemic crisis. This consistent growth, despite increasing awareness of BZD risks, underscores the inadequacy of current prevention and management strategies, suggesting a systemic public health issue driven by clinical over-reliance on pharmacological interventions and the insufficient attention to broader social determinants of health.

Addressing this crisis requires a comprehensive approach that combines targeted interventions with a broader transformation of societal values and institutional structures. We recommend enhancing healthcare access, especially in rural areas and implementing tailored support for groups at increased risk, particularly elderly women and young adults. Community and policy engagement are crucial, fostering community-driven health initiatives and policy reforms focused on mental health and social determinants. Public education and awareness campaigns should highlight BZD risks and safer treatment alternatives. Strategies to tackle systemic inequities faced by populations in vulnerable situations, alongside initiatives that alleviate caregiving burdens, are essential. Tackling the complex origins of BZD misuse requires concerted efforts from policymakers, healthcare providers, communities and individuals. This integrated approach aims not only to reduce BZD dependency but also to enhance overall quality of life, fostering a more equitable, healthy and supportive society.
