Determinants of blood transfusion-transmitted infections in donated blood
Authors: Collince Odiwuor Ogolla, Bernard Guyah, Apollo O Maima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood transfusions which save lives through their essential function bring infection risks to users especially in areas with limited medical resources.ObjectiveThe research study aimed to determine the main factors that cause blood transfusion-transmitted infections in donated blood.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study among voluntary blood donors. Introduction

Transfusion of blood helps save lives but works best during emergency situations and surgical procedures and for patients with ongoing medical needs [8, 26]. Blood transfusion enables the transfer of infectious microorganisms which creates sudden health risks for patients who receive blood [6, 27] The primary infectious agents responsible for BTRIs include Hepatitis B (HBV) and Hepatitis C (HCV) and HIV and Syphilis and Malaria [2, 3]. The risk of these infections, however, depends upon the screening procedure performed on the blood donation, the health status of the donor, and the applied methods in testing the blood prior to transfusion [5, 25].

Transfusion-transmitted infections create a major public health problem which continues to affect people worldwide. Reports from various studies state that even with screening rigorously done, the possibility of an infection exists, dependent upon donor behavior, frequency of blood donation, and quality of healthcare giving in a given area [1, 11]. The incidence of transfusion-transmitted infections in sub-Saharan Africa has created significant problems because studies show that blood donors in this region have higher levels of HBV and HCV and HIV compared to other areas [16, 17].

The blood transfusion system faces safety problems which also affect healthcare facilities across developing nations. The National Blood Transfusion Service policy in Kenya mandates that blood donations undergo testing for HIV and HBV and HCV and syphilis. Authorities do not require malaria screening in every location but they permit testing in Western Kenya which experiences malaria outbreaks [4, 15]. Some of the challenges relate to the reliability of screening tests and the distribution of risk factor prevalence that might account for transfusion-related infections. Recent studies indicated a number of potential risk factors for transfusion-related infections, namely recent surgery, multiple donations, and history of previous infections [19, 30]. A key to safe blood transfusion is identifying determinants that contribute to blood transfusion-associated infections in a given context. Knowledge of these will not only improve transfusion safety but will also be important for the overall goal of minimizing the rate of infection through targeted interventions and improvement of screening procedures. The evidence from earlier research shows that donor behavior and their adherence to deferral rules determine the likelihood of receiving transfusion-transmitted infections. The evidence from blood donor populations which were declined shows that tattoo and piercing exposure, previous infections, and insufficient pre-donation education present major dangers to blood safety [14, 18]. The research conducted in both developed nations and developing countries shows that donors do not follow deferral requirements which create a need for better donor education and post-donation counseling programs that will decrease residual transfusion hazards [24, 30]. The research results demonstrate that researchers need to identify specific factors which determine infection transmission through blood transfusions in the Kenyan healthcare system. The study aims to explore the determinants of blood transfusion-related infections and the risk factors associated among blood donors. The outcome of the study will assist in proposing recommendations that will delineate areas that need improvement in the donation and screening process.

Methods

Study design

The research study used a hospital-based cross-sectional study design to investigate the factors which cause transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) to develop in blood donors. The research was conducted during the regular blood donation drives that were organized by the blood transfusion unit.

Study population

The population of the study consisted of the voluntary blood donors who came for blood donation during the time of the study. The inclusion criteria required that donors must be aged 18 to 60 years, their weight must be 50 kg or more, and they must not have any infectious diseases which they reported themselves or doctors confirmed except those who had received complete treatment. The study excluded donors who displayed active infection symptoms and those who had received transfusion-transmitted infection and those who had donated blood within three months before the study start.

Operational definition of terms

The term history of previous infection describes a self-reported past diagnosis of transfusion-relevant infections which includes hepatitis B hepatitis C syphilis and malaria that doctors treated but which did not result in permanent donor deferral at the time of donation.

Unscreened donor history referred to previous blood donations conducted outside regulated blood transfusion services, such as informal, emergency, or unregulated settings, where standardized serological screening for transfusion-transmitted infections could not be confirmed. While Kenya operates a regulated national blood transfusion system through KNBTS, ‘unscreened donor history’ in this study refers to self-reported prior donations occurring in informal, emergency, or undocumented contexts where standardized screening could not be verified.

Multiple blood donation was defined as having donated blood more than once prior to the current donation, irrespective of donation interval.

Recruitment

The hospital donation drives were followed by consecutive eligible voluntary donors who met the inclusion criteria and were invited to participate. In order to eliminate selection bias, we applied the same eligibility criteria to all donors and recorded the number of donors refused or were excluded.

Blood donation process

Blood donation is conducted according to the Kenya National Blood Transfusion Service (KNBTS) policy which requires all donors to give blood without any payment. This study does not allow participants to use family replacement or paid blood donations. The donation process consists of eight steps which include donor mobilization pre-donation counseling medical history assessment physical examination hemoglobin screening aseptic blood collection mandatory laboratory testing for transfusion-transmitted infections and post-donation counseling.

Data collection

The study used a fully structured questionnaire for trained personnel to collect information about donor demographic characteristics which included age sex and occupation as well as donation history which documented all past donations and health history which contained information about past infections and recent surgeries and chronic medical conditions. The questionnaire was distributed to each donor. The study assigned a unique identification code to each donor which connected their questionnaire responses with their laboratory results while keeping their identity secret. The study team used venous blood samples which they obtained from each participant after the interview and eligibility screening process was finished. The blood samples were collected in vacutainer tubes which contained ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and plain tubes for transfusion-transmitted infection (TTI) screening as part of standard donor evaluation.

Laboratory analysis

Laboratory testing for transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) was conducted according to World Health Organization (WHO) and Kenya National Blood Transfusion Service (KNBTS) protocols (16). The TTIs tests for HBsAg, anti-HCV, and HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody were done on the Abbott ARCHITECT i1000SR analyzer, which uses Chemiluminescent Microparticle Immunoassay (CMIA) technology, the gold standard method. For the syphilis testing, the Treponema pallidum antibody ELISA on the Bio-Rad Evolis system was used, while the malaria testing was done using the SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag P.f/Pan RDT which was confirmed by microscopy with an Olympus CX23 light microscope. In Kenya, malaria screening is routinely conducted for all blood donations in accordance with KNBTS guidelines (17). All tests were carried out by qualified laboratory personnel in accordance with standard quality assurance measures, and the verified results were then used for the statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

The distribution and frequency of blood transfusion-transmitted infections were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as frequency distributions and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means and their corresponding standard deviation. The chi-square test was used to assess the association with demographic factors (such as age, gender, and occupation) with respect to the infection status of the donated blood. Logistic regression analysis was performed to determine risk factors associated with blood transfusion-related infections, such as history of recent surgery, multiple blood donations, unscreened donor history, and history of previous infections. odds ratios were calculated and 95% confidence intervals while considering p-values below 0.05 as statistically significant. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve served to assess multivariate logistic regression performance. For further evaluation of the robustness of the model, sensitivity and specificity were calculated.

Ethical consideration

The study observed ethical procedures as outlined in Helsinki Declaration. The study obtained informed consent from all participants who received complete information about the research goals and methods and risk elements of the research. Study personnel maintained confidentiality of all participant personal information and assessment results throughout the research period.

Risk factors for blood transfusion-transmitted infections

The risk factors associated with blood transfusion-transmitted infections were examined using multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results are summarized in Table 3. A history of recent surgery (p = 0.03), multiple blood donations (p = 0.05), and unscreened donor history (p = 0.01) were found to be statistically significant risk factors for infections. Among these, unscreened donor history had the highest odds ratio (OR = 3.4), suggesting a strong association with blood transfusion-related infections. The presence of a history of previous infections (OR = 2.6, p = 0.02) was also a significant risk factor. Non-healthcare occupation was not significantly associated with blood transfusion-related infections (p = 0.15) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study provides insights into the risk factors of blood transfusion transmitted infections in donated blood. While the prevalence of transfusion transmitted infections was fairly low in this sample, many important determinants were established that will inform the future research and public health interventions. It is possible that the very low overall prevalence of TTIs recorded in this study is indicative of highly efficient donor selection and strict adherence to national blood screening guidelines. Ntawuyamara et al., (2021) have similar findings in Burundi where a marked reduction of TTI prevalence was achieved through rigorous donor screening practices [23]. In this study, the infection most frequently captured was Hepatitis B (1.85%); the second infection was Malaria (2.78%). Such findings correspond with the earlier undertaken studies in other parts of the world, and concerning the prevalence of both in donated blood [15, 33]. The more than incidence of HIV/AIDS (0%) to such population group is in conformity with the other region studies as an improvement-the blood screening protocols brought around a decrease in risk of transmitting such infection through blood transfusions [8, 29].

The low percentages for infection could be generally attributed to rigorous screening and donor selection. Such factors came up with the study of how they significantly affected the chances of occurrence of blood transfusion transmitted infections. A recent surgical history (OR = 2.1) was strongly related to TTIs. This can be attributed to greater exposure to hospital settings, invasive operations, or perioperative blood transfusions that increase the likelihood of infection. This does not challenge the efficiency of the blood screening but rather indicates that the medical histories of the donors determine their susceptibility to infection even when screening systems are in place [12, 32]. Donors of previous blood donated themselves of history (OR = 1.8) proved to be possessing higher risk associated, in relation to studies illustrating chances for exposure to infectious agents with repeat blood donations [22, 31]. The study results show that transfusion-transmitted infections occur in this research at the same rate as previous research found in Kenya and other sub-Saharan African nations which show hepatitis B and malaria as the most common bloodborne infections in donated blood. The HBV prevalence among voluntary blood donors in Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania ranges between 1% and 6% which demonstrates ongoing blood safety challenges for the region despite better screening methods [7, 24].

Findings relating to history relating to unscreened donation were significant risk factors for infections (OR = 3.4). This corroborates results from a sub-Saharan African study, where major contributory factors to transfusion-transmitted infections were poor screening processes and insufficient health assessments [9, 10]. The thrust of this whole exercise is that proper screening all blood donations is very significant; for a single unscreened donation could cause serious consequences. Interestingly, the study also found that non-healthcare occupations had no significant association with a higher risk for infection (p = 0.15). This might mean that there are other factors outside occupation like personal health status or perhaps the medical history of the donor that would have a more immediate impact on the risks of infection. Such results have also been recorded in studies conducted in other regions where the occupation was not a significant predictor of transfusion-transmitted infections [13, 20].

The association between donor-related risk factors and transfusion-transmitted infections observed in this study is consistent with earlier reports demonstrating that incomplete risk disclosure and prior exposures substantially increase transfusion-related infection risk. Zou et al., (2006) reported higher viral infection prevalence among donors deferred for behavioral risk while Arjunan and Basavarajegowda (2024) documented increased seroprevalence of TTIs among tattooed donors [21, 28]. Furthermore, poor compliance with deferral policies and inadequate counseling have been shown to compromise blood safety, particularly in resource-limited settings [34]. The strong association between unscreened donor history and transfusion-transmitted infections observed in this study is biologically and programmatically plausible. Donors with a history of blood donation in unregulated settings, where standardized screening cannot be assured, may unknowingly contribute to residual transfusion risk. Similarly, donors with a history of previous medically treated infections or repeated blood donations may have increased cumulative exposure to healthcare environments, thereby elevating infection risk.

This study’s result carries a huge implication for improving blood donation and screening protocols. Given the alarming risk associated with unscreened donors, measures should emphasize maximization of donor health assessment coupled with all possible screening of donations using most reliable methods. Moreover, improving donor education on the dangers imposed by multiple donations and their full exposure of medical status will bring the rate of transfusion-acquired infections down. Besides this, the detection of surgery associated hazards advocates that extra care should be exercised for donors with recent surgical histories such as postponement of coming forward when completely healed. This becomes all the more important in regions where healthcare infrastructure cannot be completely counted on to ensure sterile surgical practices, as this could increase one’s chances of infection. Despite the existence of similar studies in the region, this research has provided localized data contributing evidence to the guiding of Kenya’s transfusion safety programs through risk-based donor screening.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. The relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect weaker associations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between donor characteristics and transfusion-transmitted infections. Additionally, reliance on self-reported donor histories may have introduced recall bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable context-specific evidence on determinants of transfusion-transmitted infections in a referral hospital setting. The study relied partly on self-reported donor history, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Some donors may underreport their previous infections together with their past deferrals. which includes cases where people who had previously tested positive for transfusion-transmitted infections attempt to get retested through blood donation at multiple facilities. The method used to assess unscreened donor history together with its risk estimation process will suffer from this deficiency.

Conclusion

From this study, a great number of risk factors concerning blood transfusion-transmitted infections have been described: unscreened donor history, recent surgery, or multiple blood donations. By dealing with these issues related to screening and donation management, the hospital would improve the risk hazards associated with transfusions and therefore improve patient safety. It would be useful to conduct the further analysis to refine these results towards the development of specific strategies to limit transfusion-transmitted infection globally.
