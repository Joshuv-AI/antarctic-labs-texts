Abstract
The central engines of Little Red Dots (LRDs) may be “black hole stars” (BH*s), early stages of black hole growth characterized by dense gas envelopes. So far, the most direct evidence for BH*s comes from a handful of sources where the host galaxy is completely outshone as suggested by their remarkably steep Balmer breaks. Here we present a novel scheme to disentangle BH*s from their host galaxies assuming that the [O 3 ]5008Å line arises exclusively from the host. Using a sample of 98 LRDs ( z ≈ 2 − 9 z\approx 2-9 ) with high quality NIRSpec/PRISM spectra, we demonstrate that the host-subtracted median stack displays a Balmer break > 2 × >2\times stronger than massive quiescent galaxies, with the rest-optical continuum resembling a blackbody-like SED ( T eff ≈ 4050 T_{\rm{eff}}\approx 4050 K, log ⁡ ( L bol ) ≈ 43.9 \log(L_{\rm{bol}})\approx 43.9 erg s -1 , R eff ≈ 1300 R_{\rm{eff}}\approx 1300 au). We measure a steep Balmer decrement (H α \alpha /H β > 10 \beta>10 ) and numerous density-sensitive features (e.g., Fe 2 , He 1 , O 1 ). These are hallmark signatures of dense gas envelopes, providing population-level evidence that BH*s indeed power LRDs. In the median LRD, BH*s account for ∼ 20 % \sim 20\% of the UV emission, ∼ 50 % \sim 50\% at the Balmer break, and ∼ 90 % \sim 90\% at wavelengths longer than H α \alpha with the remainder arising from the host. BH*s preferentially reside in low-mass galaxies ( M ⋆ ≈ 10 8 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm{\star}}\approx 10^{8}\,{\rm M}_{\rm{\odot}} ) undergoing recent starbursts, as evidenced by extreme emission line EWs (e.g., [O 3 ]5008Å ≈ 1100 \approx 1100 Å, C 3 ] ≈ 12 \approx 12 Å), thereby favoring BH* origins linked to star-formation. We show V-shaped LRD selections are biased to high BH*/host fractions ( ≳ 60 % \gtrsim 60\% at 5500Å) – less dominant BH*s may be powering JWST’s blue broad-line AGN. We find BH*s are so commonplace and transient (duty cycle ∼ 1 % \sim 1\% , lifetime ∼ 10 \sim 10 Myrs) that every massive black hole may have once shone as a BH*.

Excitingly, BH*s share many of their key properties with early proposed theoretical models of BH formation

It is not just the objects with large Balmer breaks, but
all
LRDs that may be powered by BH*s.
Naidu et al. 2025a

The compact optical morphology vs. extended UV is then a difference between BH* light vs. galaxy light

While all these lines of evidence are extremely promising for the “LRD = BH* + Host” picture, we are still lacking the most direct test of this hypothesis. If all LRDs are truly BH*s embedded within host galaxies, carefully subtracting the galaxy light must leave behind a spectrum resembling the “pure” BH*s such as MoM-BH*-1 and the Cliff with all the hallmark signatures of radiative transfer in a dense gas “photosphere” such as sharp Balmer breaks
(
Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Naidu et al. 2025a
, e.g.,)
and steep Balmer decrements
(
Chang et al. 2025
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)

(
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Baggen et al. 2025
;
Rinaldi et al. 2025

(
Kokorev et al. 2025
, e.g.,)

In this paper we develop a novel empirical approach for LRD/host decomposition directly on the spectra to test the nature of their central engines. Some of the cleanest clues to the formation and assembly of BHs are available via the relationship of the accreting BH with that of the stellar populations in their hosts. We exploit the vast JWST spectroscopic archive of galaxies at similar redshifts as LRDs to approximate their hosts. We then test whether the host-subtracted spectra truly resemble BH*s, thereby providing a stringent test of this paradigm. The resulting BH* and host spectra then allow us to derive population-level constraints on their properties. A preliminary version of this method was piloted in
Naidu et al. 2025a
to explain the spectrum of A2744-QSO1
(
Furtak et al. 2024
)
by subtracting a stack of observed galaxies to show the residual spectrum closely resembled MoM-BH*-1.

3
Methods: LRD Decomposition

Here we describe our approach to separate LRD hosts from their central engines. The high-level idea is that each LRD is matched with an ensemble of real, observed galaxies that are plausible approximations of the LRD’s host galaxy. By subtracting the SEDs of each of these candidate hosts from the LRD SED, we build an empirically motivated posterior for the SED of the central engine, thereby testing whether LRDs truly host BH*s.

The key advantage of this data driven approach vs. traditional SED fitting (as with galaxy+AGN models; e.g.,
Lyu et al. 2024
;
Chavez Ortiz et al. 2025
;
Taylor et al. 2025
) is that we make minimal assumptions about the nature of the central engines. SED fitting depends on the template sets used. At the moment, the development of ab initio templates (of e.g., BH* models) that are able to match the key features of LRDs is still in the early stages
(
Liu et al. 2025a
;
Santarelli et al. 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
Taylor et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

3.1
Assumptions

Our method is built on three key assumptions.

1.

Every LRD is a combination of a host galaxy and a central engine.
This assumption is motivated by LRDs whose rest-optical SEDs cannot be explained by any known stellar population and are dominated essentially entirely by a single point-source
(
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
de Graaff et al. 2025a
)
. Combining a component resembling BH* models with blue star-forming galaxies results in a V-shape SED as seen in typical LRDs
(
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Taylor et al. 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
. In what follows, we make no assumptions about the properties of the central engines (except #3 below) and instead seek to infer the host-subtracted LRD spectrum.

2.

The hosts can be approximated by peer galaxies at similar redshifts.
In other words, if the central engine did not exist, we assume the host would resemble some subset of the galaxy population cataloged in the DJA. This is motivated by the finding that typical LRDs resemble generic galaxies in the rest-UV, the wavelengths where the host is expected to dominate in the composite picture involving a red BH*
(
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
Taylor et al. 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
. Evidence for this picture comes from the extended UV morphology
(
Rinaldi et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Torralba et al. 2025a
, e.g.,)
, blue UV slopes
(
Matthee et al. 2024
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Barro et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, and lack of UV variability with all hints of variability till date reported in the rest-optical
(
Furtak et al. 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Zhang et al. 2025d
;
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025c
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, clustering studies show LRDs reside in unremarkable environments indistinguishable from generic low-mass galaxies at similar redshift
(
Lin et al. 2025c
;
Matthee et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Intriguingly,
Matthee et al. 2025
observed that the LRD host mass derived from clustering is similar to the mass inferred by assuming all the UV light arises from the host – as we will show later (§
4.3
), this works well for the least luminous LRDs, the kind studied in their work.

3.

[O
3
] emission arises purely from the host galaxy.
[O
3
]
5008
Å is the one feature accessible in virtually every LRD PRISM spectrum that appears to be powered essentially entirely by the host galaxy, and so it forms the foundation of our method. [O
3
] is always observed to be very narrow in LRDs (
\approx 50-200
km s
-1
) even with JWST’s highest resolution grating (
R\gtrsim 3000
)
(
Torralba et al. 2025b
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025c
;
Ji et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, the line-width is consistent with the stellar/dynamical masses inferred for the hosts
(
Maiolino et al. 2024b
;
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
tying its origin to ionized gas in the ISM. Indeed, on a population level, the [O
3
] luminosity in LRDs is tightly correlated with
M_{\rm{UV}}
, and the EW is closely correlated with
\beta_{\rm{UV}}

(
de Graaff et al. 2025b
)
. As discussed above (#2), the UV SEDs of LRDs are likely dominated by the host galaxy and so these correlations tie [O
3
] to the host. Finally, the two LRDs with the strongest Balmer breaks
(
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
de Graaff et al. 2025a
)
– interpreted to be almost pure BH*s outshining extremely faint hosts – are accompanied by almost non-existent [O
3
] emission. Indeed,
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Barro et al. 2025
report population trends between [O
3
] EWs and Balmer break strengths – the stronger the break, the weaker the [O
3
] EW. Indeed, theoretical models that successfully reproduce such deep Balmer breaks and the accompanying Balmer absorption invoke gas densities that are a few orders of magnitude higher than the critical density of the [O
3
] doublet leading to collisional de-excitation (
n_{\rm{H}}/\rm{cm}^{-3}\approx 10^{9-12}
vs.
\approx 10^{6}
;
Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025a
;
Chang et al. 2025
). For all these reasons we assume the [O
3
] observed in LRDs arises due to the host galaxy. We acknowledge that this is a simplifying assumption, which based on current evidence, likely holds for a large fraction of the LRD population.

Overall, our approach may be summarized as empirical SED fitting by using galaxies at similar redshifts. Matching in [O
3
] luminosity selects for a combination of the instantaneous star-formation, ionizing photon efficiency, and metallicity
(
Matthee et al. 2023
;
Meyer et al. 2024
;
Endsley et al. 2024b
, e.g.,)
. We also place constraints on the shape of the allowed SED (§
3.2
), which in turn acts as a constraint on the allowed star-formation history and stellar mass
(
Leja et al. 2019
;
Iyer et al. 2019
;
Tacchella et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. A challenge for this empirical-spirited method is that some LRD hosts appear to occupy poorly populated regions of parameter space in the DJA such that we may find few peer galaxies (e.g., ultra metal-poor
z>6
galaxies;
Maiolino et al. 2025
). However, as we will discuss in §
3.2
, this situation occurs only in a handful of LRDs.

3.2
Implementation

To construct a set of candidate host galaxies for a given LRD we select sources from the pool of all possible 10,088 hosts (§
2.2
) that satisfy:

1.

Redshift within
\Delta(z)=\pm 1.5
.

2.

L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
within
\pm 0.5
dex.

3.

Host SED
\times
(
L_{\rm{[OIII],LRD}}/L_{\rm{[OIII],host}}
)
\lesssim
the LRD SED across all
\lambda_{\rm{obs}}>0.8\mu
m.

The first two criteria select for galaxies with properties matched to the LRD host. These galaxy’s SEDs are then rescaled by a factor of
L_{\rm{[OIII],LRD}}/L_{\rm{[OIII],host}}
such that the candidate host accounts for all the [O
3
] in the LRD. We then require the rescaled host galaxy SED must not exceed the LRD’s SED at all observed wavelengths. This is a simple sanity check to ensure that a contributing component (the host galaxy) does not exceed the sum (the LRD). In particular, we compare spline fits to the LRD and host continua at
\lambda_{\rm{obs}}>0.8\mu
m to ensure the host does not exceed the LRD by more than
25\%
over any contiguous region of
\lambda_{\rm{obs}}=0.3\mu
m, failing which it is removed from the set of candidates. The
25\%
tolerance ensures that any minor differences due to e.g., stray noise pixels do not rule out otherwise reasonable candidates. We set the thresholds for all three criteria via experimentation, balancing finding close matches with finding enough matches for robust statistics (see §
3.4
for mock tests that validate these choices).

Across the 98 LRDs we study here, after filtering by the first condition requiring proximity in redshift a median of
23.4^{+29.8}_{-18.6}\,\%
candidates survive.
9.9^{+12.6}_{-8.1}\,\%
candidates remain after the second step matching in [O
3
] luminosity. And finally,
0.8^{+2.0}_{-0.7}\,\%
candidates remain after the third step requiring SEDs consistent with the LRD. Overall, the median LRD is matched to 78 candidate host galaxies. Further details of the host subtraction procedure are fleshed out below using worked examples. In the handful of cases (
N=6
) where the LRD has less than
5
host matches (for heterogeneous reasons – e.g., occurring at high redshifts with few peers, extreme [O
3
] values), we iteratively relax the
\Delta(z)
constraint to
2.0
and to
2.5
, and the
L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
constraint to 0.6 and 0.7 dex until five matches are found.

3.3
Examples

In Figure
2
we illustrate our procedure using a single LRD, MoM-233464 at
z_{\rm{spec}}=5.1
. Based on the criteria listed in §
3.2
we find 95 galaxies with similar redshift and [O

L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
and redshift is treated as an equally likely candidate host and subtracted from the LRD spectrum. To subtract the spectra, all candidate hosts are resampled onto the rest-frame wavelength grid of the LRD with flux conservation using the
spectres

(
Carnall 2017

The resulting difference spectra (
\text{LRD}-\text{Host}

3

3

We stack these difference spectra (median stacks with bootstrap resampling for errors) to report properties for every individual LRD (e.g., the fraction of light contributed by the host as a function of wavelength). Later, we stack these difference spectra to represent the central engine of the median LRD. We validate every step of this pipeline through realistic mock tests in §
3.4
that account for e.g., the impact of redshift-dependent differences in resolution.

In Figure

A key finding of our work is already apparent in this figure – the SED of the central engine (i.e.,

At the same time, it is notable that these SEDs do not have a single universal shape (e.g., exactly mirroring the Cliff or MoM-BH*-1), but vary from source to source with the continuum peaking at a range of wavelengths. For example, MoM-149501 and JADES-53757 in the bottom two panels have “cold” central engines, with much redder SEDs than the sources powering The Cliff and MoM-324506 shown in the top two rows. Before we begin interpreting results from the LRD decomposition, we perform a realistic end-to-end mock test of our pipeline to test the robustness of our results. We produce a synthetic dataset of LRDs with properties matched to our sample. Each synthetic LRD is composed of a model BH* spectrum and the observed spectrum of a host galaxy.

For realistic model BH* spectra we draw from the
CLOUDY
grid presented in
Naidu et al. 2025a

(
Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025b
;
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
Taylor et al. 2025
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
;
Golubchik et al. 2025
;
Yan et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
is currently the only one able to produce a match to both the continuum as well as Balmer lines of LRDs. For the exercise at hand, we use a subset of 154 BH* models that match the observed properties of LRDs. These models display a Balmer break strength of
>3

\alpha

5100

<0.25

). Nonetheless, what matters for the test at hand is whether the recovered BH* stack matches the injected BH* stack.

To compose a realistic sample closely paralleling the data, we produce one synthetic LRD per observed LRD. The BH* is chosen at random from the
CLOUDY

3

Once we have built the mock LRD dataset, we run exactly the same decomposition procedure that we use on observed LRDs, and obtain the results shown in Figure

<2000

We end this section by noting that the mock test results demonstrate the overall robustness of our approach. Various areas of concern (e.g., the fidelity of stacked EWs, the varying PRISM spectral resolution with redshift) are addressed in a realistic manner. All the key quantities discussed in this work – e.g., the continuum luminosities, Balmer breaks, line EWs – are recovered successfully (Table
3.4

\lesssim 2000

;
Xiao et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. In BH* models, both the extreme line strengths and Balmer decrement are a natural product of collisional excitation
(
Torralba et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
and resonant scattering
(
Chang et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
expected from the same conditions that produce the deep Balmer break
(
Yan et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Also note that the absorption strength
increasing
along the Balmer series (thereby boosting the observed Balmer decrement) is typical for BH*s
(
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025b
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
, analogous to regular stars
(
Gray 2005
, e.g.,)
and gas-cocooned Type IIn supernovae
(
Dessart et al. 2009
, e.g.,)
. Note that this is distinct from the case of absorbing gas lying completely outside the line formation region, where oscillator strengths would set the absorption depth (i.e., H
\alpha
would be absorbed more than H
\beta
; e.g.,
Draine 2011
).

3.

Fluorescent, Pumped Fe
2
and O
1
emission:
We observe several strong Fe
2

\approx 2200-3000
Å,
\approx 9000-9200
Å,
\approx 8200-8500
Å) and O
1
(e.g.,
8446
Å) lines. The [Fe
2
] forest at some wavelengths is expected to arise in the outer layers of BH* models
(
Torralba et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
and some of the complexes may be powered by Ly
\alpha
and Ly
\beta
fluorescence in the strong radiation field and dense medium
(
Sigut & Pradhan 1998
;
Kokorev et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Strong O
1
is also expected in such an environment as a result of Ly
\beta
fluorescence
(
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Tripodi et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

Blackbody-like optical continuum SED:
In Fig.
7

T_{\rm{eff}}=4047^{+156}_{-155}

\lambda_{\rm{rest}}>4000

(
Lin et al. 2025b
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Umeda et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Interestingly, various BH* models predict SEDs peaking in the rest-optical with fall-offs to shorter as well as longer wavelengths consistent with our median stack
(
Naidu et al. 2025a
;
Liu et al. 2025a
;
Kido et al. 2025

. Also note that we are able to fit the spectrum with a simple blackbody versus a modified blackbody
(

4.2
Host Galaxy Properties – LRD Hosts are Dwarf Galaxies Undergoing Starbursts

In Fig.
9
and Table
3
we compare the stack of LRD host galaxies against a redshift- and [O
3
] luminosity-matched control sample. We generate the control sample by following only the first two steps of the host matching procedure described in §
3.2
– i.e., we do not impose any constraints on the SED shape of the host galaxy to match the SED shape of the LRD. The control sources are scaled such that their [O
3
] luminosity matches the corresponding LRD before stacking. Again, in a Monte Carlo fashion over 10,000 trials we draw one control sample galaxy per LRD, and bootstrap resample these sources to produce the control stack.

Remarkably, despite being fully matched in
L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
, there are clear differences between the LRD host and control stacks. LRD hosts are fainter (
M_{\rm{UV}}=-18.5
vs.
-19.8
) and lower-mass (
\log(M_{\rm{\star}}/\rm{M}_{\rm{\odot}})=8.3
vs. 9.0) galaxies relative to the control sample. Further, in the hosts the rest-optical emission lines (e.g., H
\alpha
, [O
3
]) are far stronger (
\approx 2-3\times
in EW) than the control (see Fig.
10
). Indeed, the rest-optical lines (e.g., [O
3
]+H
\beta
) are even stronger than the average galaxy at comparable
M_{\rm{UV}}\approx-18.5
at
z\approx 6

(
Endsley et al. 2024b
;
Endsley et al. 2024a
, e.g.,)
. Moreover, C
3
] emission is apparent in the rest-UV – these are telling signs of recent starbursts and young ages
(
Roberts-Borsani et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Further, the He
1
lines (
5877
Å,
7067
Å) sensitive to the density and temperature of the ISM
(
Berg et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
are more prominent among LRD hosts, pointing to a hotter, denser ISM. Taken together these findings tie the emergence of BH*s – perhaps their formation
(
Begelman & Dexter 2025
, e.g.,)
, or their entrapment in gas
(
Alexander & Natarajan 2014
, e.g.,)
– to recent star-formation and a hot, dense ISM.

Indeed, this is borne out also by the star-formation histories of the stacks that we present in Figure
11
using the
Prospector
SED-fitting framework
(
Leja et al. 2017
;
Leja et al. 2019
;
Johnson et al. 2021
)
. The LRD host stacks shows a rising star-formation history, with a
\approx 10\times
increase between the epoch of observation and
\approx 10-25
Myrs prior. On the other hand, the control stack has a relatively steady SFH over the same time period.

In detail, to fit these stacks with
prospector
we follow the modeling choices from
Naidu et al. 2024
(in turn adapted from
Tacchella et al. 2022
;
Tacchella et al. 2023
). We use
FSPS

(
Conroy et al. 2009
;
Conroy et al. 2010
;
Conroy & Gunn 2010
)
with the
MIST
stellar models
(
Choi et al. 2017
)
and
MILES
spectral library
(
Falcón-Barroso et al. 2011
)
and assume a
Chabrier 2003
IMF. Nebular emission is modeled with the
CLOUDY

(
Ferland et al. 2017
)
grid introduced in
Byler et al. 2017
. The free parameters include seven bins describing the non-parametric star-formation history, the total stellar mass, stellar and gas-phase metallicities, nebular emission parameters, and a flexible dust model
(
Kriek & Conroy 2013
)
. We adopt a “bursty continuity” prior for the star-formation history
(
Tacchella et al. 2022
)
with the time bins logarithmically spaced following
(
Leja et al. 2019
)
up to a formation redshift of
z=20
. We hold the first two bins fixed at lookback times of 0-5 and 5-10 Myrs to capture bursts that power strong emission lines
(
Whitler et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
and that are expected to grow ubiquitous with increasing redshift. We synthesize NIRCam photometry from the stacks in all JWST bands and pass the measured emission line fluxes to the model.

We emphasize that our derived host properties hinge on the assumption that all [O
3
] flux arises from the host (see S
3
for motivation). One could posit scenarios wherein the host contribution to [O
3
] is not exactly
\approx 100\%
. For example, just as UV photons are leaking from the BH* (e.g.,
Labbe et al. 2024
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
; Figs.
5
,
12
), ionizing photons that are able to power [O
3
] in surrounding gas may also be escaping, thereby boosting the EWs and leading us to over-estimate the host galaxy EWs. For all the reasons discussed in S
3
, we deem this unlikely to be the case in the typical LRD. However, we cannot completely rule out that there is absolutely no intrinsic [O
3
] component powered by accretion that varies with other properties of the LRD, which may contribute in part to this apparent large difference in EWs between the control sample and LRD hosts. We explore what BH*-driven contamination in [O
3
] might mean for our results. We re-run our stacking procedure assuming a
\approx 20\%
contribution arising from the BH* (i.e., the hosts account for
\approx 80\%
of the observed [O
3
]). Note that the host-matching step (
L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
within
3\times
) is relatively flexible and resistant to any degree of contamination, and so the difference in results is from the SED scaling step (§
3.2
). We find the overall SED shape recovered for the BH* is preserved, albeit with a slightly weaker Balmer break (
4^{+1}_{-1}
). Critically, for the results in this section, we find the LRD hosts still lie above the star-forming main sequence and display stronger (albeit reduced) EWs relative to the control sample (e.g., EW [O
3
] of
946^{+51}_{-50}
Å for hosts vs.
296^{+44}_{-38}
Å for the control).

4.3
Wavelength-Dependent BH*/LRD Fraction

A signature feature of LRDs is the remarkable diversity across the population, as well as their strikingly different appearance as a function of wavelength. On average, LRDs show extended morphologies in the rest-UV and are completely dominated by point-sources at the H
\alpha
wavelength
(
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Akins et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Kokorev et al. 2025
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. This transition in morphology coincides with a transition from the blue UV continuum to the characteristic red optical continuum . There is also perplexing diversity, with some sources remaining unresolved point-sources with BH* signatures (e.g., the Fe
2
UV complex) far into the UV
(
Furtak et al. 2024
;
Labbe et al. 2024
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
;
Torralba et al. 2025a
;
Tang et al. 2025
;
Akins et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
.

In Fig.
12
we are able to explain these observations in terms of variations in the BH*/LRD fraction with wavelength. In the median LRD, the BH* ratio remains modest (but non-zero) in the rest-UV (
\approx 20\%
). There is an abrupt transition at
\approx 3600-4000
Å, redwards of which the BH* begins to dominate the total light. By
\approx 1\mu
m almost the entire SED (
>90\%
) is explained by the BH*.

Clear minima appear at the positions of forbidden lines that cannot be produced in the dense BH*: [O
3
] by construction, and at [S
3
] as an outcome of our procedure. Intriguingly, the BH*/LRD fraction is relatively flat at the H
\alpha
wavelength – the broad H
\alpha
emission of the BH* is balanced by similarly strong narrow H
\alpha
emission from the hosts (
\approx 830
Åvs.
\approx 940
Å; Tables
1
,
3
). Note that we lack the resolution to discern narrow and broad components in the Balmer lines, or else we might expect a sharp minima at the location of the narrow line of the host. Strikingly, the FeII-UV feature is seen in the
median
stack of all sources – this feature is extremely prominent in the most luminous sources (right panel, Fig.
12
) and has previously been reported in such objects
(
Labbe et al. 2024
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
, but here we see this is an feature occurring in the typical LRD.

To study the luminosity dependence of the BH*/LRD fraction, we construct substacks following the exact same procedure as the main stack (§
3
), but by splitting the sample in three bins by the optical luminosity,
L_{\rm{5500}}
(Fig.
12
). Such substacks will be analyzed in detail in Naidu & Sun et al. in prep., while here we focus on the strong luminosity dependence in the BH*/LRD fraction across these substacks (Fig.
13
). In particular, the most luminous sources tend to remain bright into the UV, contributing almost
\approx 50\%
of the light at
\approx 2000-3000
Å as seen via their prominent UV Fe
2
emission. On the other hand, fainter BH*s tend to ramp up gradually with wavelength and only begin to dominate the SED past
\approx 5000
Å. Nonetheless, by
\approx 1\mu
m, irrespective of how luminous an LRD is, almost all light (
\approx 90\%
) arises from the BH*.

The transition wavelength, where we witness an abrupt change in BH* fraction is interestingly not exactly at the Balmer limit
(
Setton et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. The sharp, almost vertical edge around the Balmer limit is seen most prominently among the most luminous sources – the sharpness is akin to the almost vertical Lyman-
\alpha
break seen in high-redshift galaxies. Fainter sources instead have a more gradual transition and reach a
\approx 50\%
BH* fraction at redder wavelengths. In a companion paper (Naidu & Sun+25), we show that this may be understood in terms of a correlation between the luminosity and effective temperature of BH*s (akin to the main-sequence of stars). Luminous, hot BH*s emit strongly at bluer wavelengths whereas faint, cold BH*s have SEDs that peak at redder wavelengths, with little flux around H
∞
(i.e., little continuum to “break” at the Balmer limit) and therefore show more gradual transitions.

5
Discussion

5.1
LRD = Host + BH*: A Simple Composite Model for LRDs

In this work we have shown the picture of
\text{LRD}-\text{Host}
= BH* holds not only for the most extreme sources with large Balmer breaks, but is a defining characteristic for the entire LRD population. The full diversity of LRD SEDs may be described by the diversity of BH*s themselves in various stages of mass assembly (e.g., with varying temperatures, luminosities, surface gravities) combined with the diversity of their host galaxies (e.g., with different masses, star-formation histories, dust content).

The key insight is that BH*s may be thought of as star-like objects lying at cosmological distances, shining with luminosities of
\approx 10^{10}L_{\rm{\odot}}
and
\approx 4000
K temperatures
(
Begelman et al. 2008
;
Begelman & Dexter 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Umeda et al. 2025
;
Kido et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Making this connection, and appealing to this new astrophysical building block clarifies many of the mysteries of the LRD SED. As seen in radiative transfer models of BH*s, accretion energy is reprocessed into an SED peaking in the rest-optical and falling off towards both longer and shorter wavelengths (Fig.
7
; e.g.,
Liu et al. 2025b
;
Naidu et al. 2025a
), with strong absorption below the Balmer break acting akin to a Lyman break (e.g.,
Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025
). The transition across wavelengths between optical-dominated BH* and UV-dominated galaxy (Fig.
12
) produces the V-shape continuum
(
Labbe et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
as well as the sharp contrast between the optical and UV morphology
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
.

A key open question is the origin of the UV
(
Inayoshi et al. 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Asada et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
. Our results demonstrate that even in the typical LRD (Fig.
5
), and particularly in the most luminous sources (Fig.
12
; see GN-9771 in Appendix Fig.
16
), there is significant UV emission that cannot be attributed to the host galaxy (e.g., the prominent Fe
2
UV in these stacks;
Labbe et al. 2024
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025b
). Where does this UV light come from? One possibility is that this is light leaking from the BH* itself where
n=2
Hydrogen is unable to block the light – indeed,
Torralba et al. 2025b
show such a model drawn from the family of
cloudy
models produced in
Naidu et al. 2025a
to explain the SED shape of GN-9771 (see also
D’Eugenio et al. 2025b
). A caveat is that this region of the predicted
cloudy
spectrum is sensitive to details of the modeling of the Fe atom. Another possibility
(
Inayoshi et al. 2025
;
Asada et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
is that a compact cluster immediately surrounding the BH* may be responsible for this UV – however, note that our subtraction method is agnostic to the source (e.g., clusters) of the [O
3
] (i.e., we find peer galaxies as long as there are such sources in the DJA), and so for excess UV to remain in the host-subtracted stack we would need these stars to produce a peculiar spectrum (absent in [O
3
], and with a unique shape that peaks around the Fe
2
wavelength). As challenging as it is, deep H-grating spectroscopy below the Balmer break is the most direct path to confirm if this is indeed BH* light (with e.g., line widths commensurate to the optical) in these sources (e.g., JWST-GO-8047; PI: B. Wang).

5.2
V-Shaped LRDs may be the tip of the iceberg

(

– may also be hosting BH*s.

What is the relationship between LRDs, BH*s and the overall population of bluer, broad-line sources that JWST is revealing at high-

\approx 10-50\%
of these samples depending on the luminosity studied
(
Maiolino et al. 2023
;
Matthee et al. 2024
;

;
Kokorev et al. 2024a

, e.g.,)
. However, it is noteworthy that several perplexing facets of LRDs – e.g., X-ray faintness, MIR faintness, lack of outflows, lack of optical variability, strong Nitrogen emission – are seen in broad-line samples
where no LRD selection is applied

(
Kokubo & Harikane 2025
;
Maiolino et al. 2024c
;
Yue et al. 2024
;
Isobe et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, these non-LRD broad-line sources fall on the same tight correlation (
<0.2
dex scatter) between
L_{\rm{5100}}
and
L_{\rm{H\alpha}}
followed by LRDs
(
de Graaff et al. 2025b
)
. Might some of these broad-line sources also be hosting BH*s?

From our modeling, we infer a sharp drop-off in sources with BH*/LRD fractions (measured at
\approx 5500
Å) below
\approx 60\%
(evident in Figs.
13
,
14
). In particular,
\approx 90\%
of the sources in our sample are dominated by BH*s in the rest-optical with
>60\%
BH*/LRD ratios. The lowest ratios we recover are
\approx 40\%
, occurring in a handful of sources (examples shown in Fig.
14
).

It is possible that this is a selection effect – our sources are selected to be V-shaped (via optical and UV slopes) and point-sources (
>50\%
from a point-source at 4
\mu
m)
(
Hviding et al. 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
)
, as is standard in LRD definitions
(
Labbe et al. 2023
;
Kokorev et al. 2024a
;
Akins et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. However, in our composite picture, there is no physical significance for the V-shape, which is merely a result of two components with different SED slopes being combined.

To quantify the effect of V-shape selections, we perform a simple experiment in Fig.
15
where we take our median BH* stack and host stack and vary their BH*/LRD fraction in the rest-optical. We find that the standard V-shaped selections are preferentially selecting sources where the BH* is outshining the host in the rest-optical. BH*s that are embedded in relatively brighter galaxies are currently not being classified as LRDs, and are being selected as “normal” broad-line AGN with blue UV and optical slopes or being completely missed. Note that since BH*s are not outshining the galaxy in the rest-optical, these sources may not have point-source morphologies – indeed, this is often the case among broad-line samples
(
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Maiolino et al. 2023
;
Fei et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Recently,
Yanagisawa et al. 2026
presented a striking validation of this picture with the discovery of two LRDs embedded in a strongly lensed galaxy – if not for the spatial resolution afforded by lensing, these systems would be completely outshone by their host galaxy.

A key implication is that the“overmassive” black holes being reported among the non-LRD population may also have overestimated masses and bolometric luminosities (though note some sources may resemble more “standard” accretion disks; e.g.,
Fabian et al. 2025
;
Maiolino et al. 2024a
). Another implication of this exercise is that the already abundant BH* population may be even more widespread than LRD numbers imply. What the exact fraction of BH*s among broad-line samples is remains to be seen, and will require developing sensitive proxies to distinguish between BH*-powered AGN from AGN truly resembling
z=0
samples (Fig.
6
).

Another important implication is that the dramatic redshift evolution in the number density of V-shaped LRDs –
\approx 10\times
between
z\approx 6
to
z\approx 2

(
Ma et al. 2025a
;
Kocevski et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
– may not be as straightforward to interpret as BH*s being a predominantly early Universe phenomenon
(
Inayoshi et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. It is possible that BH*s may be forming at a relatively steady rate, but the fraction of host galaxies they can outshine to produce a V-shaped spectrum falls with cosmic time, reflecting the stellar mass build up of the Universe (e.g., a
\approx 10\times
increase in the cosmic star-formation mass density at
\gtrsim 10^{8}M_{\rm{}\odot}
between
z=6
to
z=2
;
Weibel et al. 2024a
).

For now, we note that the fraction of BH*s among non-LRDs, particularly broad-line sources at high-redshift, is highly likely to be non-zero. As per our composite picture, there is no physical reason per se for a
\approx 60\%
floor in the BH* fraction, and for BH*s to occur only in V-shaped sources
(
Yanagisawa et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
. Some models envision a tight coupling of the fates of BH*s and the stars surrounding them where strong correlations may be expected between the host and BH* components – but even in such models the optical to UV ratio (proxy for BH* to host ratio) is expected to vary widely
(
Inayoshi et al. 2025
;
Zwick et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

5.3
The Lifetime and Duty Cycle of BH*s

A key open question about BH*s is how long-lived they are, which in turn has implications for how important of a phase they represent in the origin stories of massive black holes and in shaping the course of galaxy formation. The duty cycle and lifetime of BH*s can be derived similar to quasar duty cycles
(
Small & Blandford 1992
;
Martini & Weinberg 2001
;
Eilers et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
by assuming every galaxy of the characteristic host mass goes through an LRD phase:

\rm{Duty\ Cycle}=\frac{\rm{Lifetime\ of\ BH^{*}s}}{\rm{Hubble\ Time}}\simeq\frac{\phi(\rm{LRDs)}}{\rm{\phi(Hosts)}}.

(3)

Our unique constraints on the typical host stellar mass (
\approx 10^{8}\,{\rm M}_{\rm{\odot}}
) allow us to solve for the lifetime. For the median redshift of our sample (
z\approx 5
),
\approx 10^{8}\,{\rm M}_{\rm{\odot}}
galaxies occur at a number density of
\phi/\rm{Mpc^{-3}}\approx 10^{-2}

(
Weibel et al. 2024a
, e.g.,)
. On the other hand,
z\approx 5
LRDs with an
M_{\rm{UV}}\approx-18.5
(the median for our stack) are found at a rate of
\phi/\rm{Mpc^{-3}}\approx 10^{-4}

(
Kokorev et al. 2024a
, e.g.,)
. This yields a duty cycle of
\approx 1\%
and a BH* lifetime of
\approx 10
Myrs, consistent with constraints from clustering
(
Matthee et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025c
;
Pizzati et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. A caveat is that as shown in §
5.2
, V-shaped LRDs likely provide an incomplete census of BH*s – in particular, other broad-line sources may also be powered by BH*s which would mean LRD LFs are under-counting BH*s possibly by
\approx 2\times
(comparing broad-line non-LRD vs. LRD numbers at similar luminosities as our sample;
Hviding et al. 2025
), which would imply a duty cycle of
\approx 2\%
and lifetimes of
\approx 20
Myrs.

Excitingly, BH* models predict similarly short lifetimes (e.g.,
\approx 20
Myrs in
Santarelli et al. 2025
) consistent with our results. It is also interesting to note that models of star-formation burstiness that explain the scatter in the main sequence report excess variability on
\approx 10
Myr timescales relative to 50 Myr timescales
(
Simmonds et al. 2025
;
Muñoz et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
that may point to efficient, recurrent fueling of their centers via e.g., compaction events and compact starbursts that may produce conditions ripe for BH* formation or fueling
(
Tacchella et al. 2016
;
McClymont et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
.

If only
\approx 2\%
of the possible hosts at any moment are shining with a short-lived BH*, they may be thought of as flickering on and off akin to disco lights when viewed across the entire galaxy population (as opposed to long-lived, steady flood-lights). BH*s may therefore represent a crucial phase in the growth of almost every massive black hole. Indeed, assuming similar parameters (
\approx 4\%
duty cycle, lifetime of few
\approx 10
s of Myrs),
Begelman & Dexter 2025
observe that the descendants of LRDs may account for almost the entire present-day BH mass density.

5.4
How do BH*s form? Further evidence for Nuclear Star Clusters and Supermassive Stars?

BH*s bear a striking resemblance to aspects of various seeding and growth models. The cocoons of gas nourishing BH*s are highly conducive to super-Eddington accretion (e.g., spherical accretion may dissipate radiation pressure via convection;
Begelman 1979
;
Begelman et al. 2008
;
Begelman & Dexter 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025a
). Their short lifetimes and low duty cycle (§
5.3
) imply this is a commonplace phenomenon that almost every galaxy experiences across diverse environments – and so their seeding channels may be similarly abundant for instance via globular clusters/nuclear star clusters
(
Alexander & Natarajan 2014
, e.g.,)
). The presence of metals in BH* atmospheres (e.g., Fe, O in the median stack) may imply BH* formation does not require pristine conditions (see e.g., LRD analogs at
z\approx 0
;
Izotov et al. 2007
;
Izotov & Thuan 2008
;
Lin et al. 2025b
;
Ji et al. 2025c
) as these sources are detected over several billion years of cosmic time, or because metals are perhaps formed in the process.

Furthermore, we argue in Figs.
9
,
10
and
11
that BH*s preferentially live in hosts with very recent bursts of star-formation and highly dense ISM conditions. This is intriguing for several proposed channels of BH seeding and growth. For example, the dense, gas-rich nuclear star clusters (NSCs) possibly arising from these bursts may be prime sites for the formation of supermassive stars (SMSs;
\approx 10^{5}\,{\rm M}_{\rm{\odot}}
), e.g, via runaway collisions that can build up
\approx 10^{5}\,{\rm M}_{\rm{\odot}}
in a mere
\approx 2
Myrs
(
Denissenkov & Hartwick 2014
;
Gieles et al. 2018
;
Woods et al. 2019
;
Charbonnel et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. The remnants of SMSs may form BH*s, perhaps via a failed supernova scenario where the SMS fails to cast off its outer layers even after collapsing into a black hole at its center
(
Begelman et al. 2008
;
Begelman & Dexter 2025
, e.g.,)
or because the BH may be entrained in gas in its dense formation environment
(
Alexander & Natarajan 2014
;
Chon et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
. It is also intriguing to note an emerging connection between BH*s, SMSs, and globular clusters from chemical abundances, particularly Nitrogen enhancement that is prominent among broad-line AGN as well as LRDs
(
Belokurov & Kravtsov 2023
;
Charbonnel et al. 2023
;
Morel et al. 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025a
;
Isobe et al. 2025
;
Schaerer et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

It has also been proposed that SMSs may in fact be the central engines of LRDs as opposed to BH*s. There is no direct signature yet that rules SMSs out per se (e.g., broad lines could arise from electron scattering in their stellar atmospheres similar to BH*s). However, unlike BH*s we are lacking SMS models at the moment that are able to reproduce the observed emission lines, continuua, temperatures, and luminosities simultaneously
(
Nandal & Loeb 2025
;
Zwick et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Most significantly, we note that the extremely short lifetimes of SMSs –
\approx 2
Myr even under optimistic assumptions
(
Nandal & Chon 2025
)
– are at odds with our derived BH* lifetimes of 10–20 Myrs (§
5.3
).

6
Summary

\bigstar
We present a novel, empirical approach to isolate the central engines of LRDs. The key assumption we make is that the [O
3
] luminosity arises exclusively from the host galaxy. We use observed galaxies at similar redshift and with similar
L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
to approximate and subtract the hosts (§
3
, Figs.
2
,
3
). We validate this approach with detailed mock tests (Fig.
4
, Table
3.4
), and then apply it to a sample of 98 LRDs selected from the DAWN JWST Archive with high quality NIRSpec/PRISM spectra
(
de Graaff et al. 2025b
;
Torralba et al. 2025b
)
.

\bigstar
The host-subtracted median stack of our full sample represents the central engine of LRDs (Figure
5
, §
4.1
, Table
1
). This stack exhibits a constellation of features that are the hallmark signatures of BH*s, leading us to conclude BH*s are indeed the central engines of LRDs. These features include:

•

Balmer break:
The stack has a strong Balmer break (
f^{\nu}(4050\rm{\AA})
/
f^{\nu}(3670\rm{\AA})=6.50^{+3.70}_{-1.72}
that far exceeds the strongest breaks seen in quiescent galaxies. The only sources with comparable breaks are the archetypal BH*s, MoM-BH* and the Cliff. [Fig.
6
]

•

H
\alpha
strength and Balmer decrement:
The EW(H
\alpha
)
\approx 850
Å and remarkable Balmer decrement (H
\alpha
/H
\beta\approx 16
) are far more extreme than local AGN. The Balmer decrement is unlikely to be due to dust given stringent non-detections of LRDs in the FIR. Instead, these lines are expected as products of collisional excitation, resonant scattering, and star-like absorption (
\tau_{\rm{H\beta}}>\tau_{\rm{H\alpha}}
) in BH* models. [Fig.
6
]

•

Fluorescent, Pumped Fe
2
and O
1
emission:
The stack contains several strong Fe
2
and O
1
lines, including in the UV, which are expected to arise in BH*-like conditions as a result of Ly
\alpha
and Ly
\beta
fluorescence. This is also evidence that the BH* envelopes are not metal-free. [Fig.
5
]

•

Blackbody-like optical SED:
A single blackbody with
T_{\rm{eff}}=4047^{+156}_{-155}
K provides an empirical description of the rest-optical continuum. An SED peaking in the optical and falling off at longer and shorter wavelengths is a common feature among BH* models. The fit implies an
L_{\rm{bol}}=10^{43.9^{+0.1}_{-0.1}}
erg s
-1
, while the Stefan-Boltzmann law implies an enormous radius of
1321^{+168}_{-153}
au, two orders of magnitude larger than the largest known stars but comparable to local broad-line regions. [Fig.
7
]

\bigstar
Decomposing the emission of the BH* from the host galaxy allows us to cleanly infer the properties of the two separate components. We find the following host properties:

•

LRD hosts are faint (
M_{\rm{UV}}\approx-18.5
) dwarf galaxies (
\approx 10^{8}\,{\rm M}_{\rm{\odot}}
), in excellent agreement with constraints from clustering. [Fig.
11
, Table
]

•

Compared to a control sample carefully matched in
L_{\rm{[OIII]}}
, we find LRD hosts show far stronger (
\approx 3\times
) emission line EWs – in the rest-optical (e.g., EW([O
3
])
\approx 1100
Å) as well as in the rest-UV (e.g., EW([C
3
)
\approx 12
Å) – implying highly ionizing, young stellar populations. [Fig.
10
, Table
3
]

•

Indeed, LRD hosts lie above the star-forming main sequence, and display rising star-formation histories. These features hint that recent starbursts may play a role in the formation of BH*s and/or their entrapment in gas. [Fig.
11
].

\bigstar
We study how combinations of BH*s and hosts produce the remarkable diversity of LRD SEDs. We track the BH*/LRD fraction with wavelength and across various properties.

•

In the typical LRD, the BH*/LRD ratio remains modest (but non-zero) in the rest-UV (
\approx 20\%
); then, there is an abrupt transition around, but not exactly at the Balmer break (
\approx 3600-4000
Å), redwards of which the BH* begins to dominate the light; by
\approx 1\mu
m almost the entire SED is explained by the BH*.

•

The BH*/LRD fraction with wavelength shows strong luminosity dependence – the brightest (hottest) BH*s account for almost half the light in the UV and show the sharpest, almost vertical Balmer breaks. Fainter (colder) BH*s ramp up gradually with wavelength and do not display such sharp breaks. [Figure
12
, §
4.3
]

•

BH*/LRD fractions correlate with easily accessible observables, namely the LRD [O
3
] EW and Balmer break strength. This may help accelerate the search for BH*-dominated objects, enabling the study of “pure” BH*s without interference from hosts. On the other extreme, host-dominated LRDs appear to be some of the most spectacular starbursts at high-redshift with e.g., [OIII]
{\rm{5008}\AA}
EWs reaching
\approx 3000
Å. [Figs.

13
,
14
, §
4.4
,
4.5
]

\bigstar
V-shaped LRD selections are preferentially sensitive to high BH*/LRD fractions of
\gtrsim 60\%
. The fraction of BH*s among non-LRDs (e.g., “normal” broad-line AGN) is highly likely to be non-zero, and the population of BH*s may be even more widespread (up to
>2\times
) than current LRD numbers suggest. [Fig.
15
, §
5.2
]

\bigstar
Exploiting our constraints on the characteristic host mass and their corresponding number densities, we infer a BH* duty cycle of
\approx 1\%
and a BH* lifetime of
\approx 10
Myrs. BH*s are a short-lived, and yet ubiquitous phenomenon, implying they are a key phase in the origin story of possibly every massive BH. [§
5.3
]

It has long been anticipated that the early Universe held spectacular, unknown chapters of the origin story of black holes. BH*s may represent one such chapter. With a clear, resolved view of BH*s as well as the galaxies they grow in, we may now begin unraveling the detailed physics of their birth and growth.
