Genomic prediction of agronomic traits in a switchgrass (Panicum virgatum L.) half‐sib progeny panel evaluated across multiple environments
Authors: Jazib Ali Irfan, Chanaka Roshan Abeyratne, Hari Bahadur Chhetri, Doug Hyatt, Shiva Om Makaju, Chris Daum, Diane Bauer, Anna Lipzen, Kerrie Barry, Daniel Jacobson, Mitra Mazarei, Charles Neal Stewart, Ali Mekki Missaoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSwitchgrass (Panicum virgatum L.) improvement requires selection methods that remain effective across environments. Biomass yield is strongly influenced by genotype‐by‐environment (G × E) interaction. We evaluated genomic prediction models for biomass yield, spring emergence (SE), and flowering time (FT) in half‐sibs at three southeastern US locations (Watkinsville, GA; Tifton, GA; and Knoxville, TN). Predictive performance was assessed within each site‐year using five‐fold cross‐validation, comparing a parental general combining ability (GCA) baseline with Bayesian models, genomic BLUP (GBLUP), and a dominance model (GBLUPD). We further quantified the sensitivity of yield prediction to single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) density. Predictive abilities as Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) increased as the SNP number rose from the minimum of 100 to 2500–5000 SNPs, with only minimal gains observed up to 10,000 SNPs and beyond. Genomic models consistently outperformed parental GCA baseline for yield, with the highest PCC of 0.45–0.55 in 2022 for Georgia, followed by declines (PCC = 0.25–0.35) in 2024. This indicated stronger G × E (where E represents combined year‐location) impacts on mature switchgrass stands. FT showed higher predictive ability than yield, with PCC > 0.50 in Georgia and PCC > 0.28–0.33 in Tennessee. SE exhibited intermediate‐to‐high PCC of 0.65–0.70 in Georgia during 2022–2023 and a PCC of 0.45–0.47 in Tennessee. The GBLUPD provided small repeatable gains for yield and SE in comparison to GBLUP. FT exhibited genetic correlations exceeding 0.90 at Knoxville, which showed it as a transferable predictor for multi‐environment selection. Collectively, these results indicate that modest SNP sets can be sufficient for yield prediction and highlight environment‐dependent model performance.

INTRODUCTION

Switchgrass (Panicum virgatum L.) is a warm‐season, perennial C4 forage crop with high biomass potential that has been explored as a bioenergy feedstock since the mid‐1980s (McLaughlin & Kszos, 2005). It is native to the northern prairies of the Americas and has the potential to provide cost‐effective biomass production on marginal lands with low input requirements (L. Wright, 2007). Switchgrass has been promoted as a lignocellulosic feedstock for bioenergy due to its productivity, broad geographic adaptability, deep rooting, and potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions associated with transportation fuels (Happs et al., 2024; Sanderson et al., 2006; L. L. Wright et al., 2011). In the United States, switchgrass remains a model herbaceous energy crop in federal bioenergy programs because it can reportedly produce annual dry matter yields of 10 to 12.9 Mg ha−1, particularly in lowland germplasm adapted to warm‐humid regions (Heaton et al., 2004; Owens et al., 2016; L. L. Wright et al., 2011; Wullschleger et al., 2010). As of today, efforts to increase biomass yield, improve stand persistence, and stabilize performance across sites remain central to breeding targets, because the economic viability of bioenergy production from switchgrass depends on reliable yield at scale (Happs et al., 2024; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024).

Genetic improvement in switchgrass is complicated by its biology. The crop is highly heterozygous, self‐incompatible, and typically improved through recurrent selection in polycross nurseries that generate half‐sib progenies/families (HSFs) (Taliaferro, 2003; Vogel, 2013). The traditional public breeding pipeline selects superior parents based on HSF performance for biomass yield and agronomic traits, recombines them in a polycross, and advances new selection cycles (Nayak et al., 2020; Taliaferro, 2003). This approach effectively captures additive genetic variance, and narrow‐sense heritability for biomass yield in HSF can be moderate to high, indicating meaningful potential for gain per cycle (Nayak et al., 2020; Taliaferro, 2003). However, this approach is slow and expensive. Biomass yield must be phenotyped in large spaced‐plant nurseries or sward plots, where stands typically require multiple years to reach full production, and strong genotype‐by‐environment (G × E) interaction means the same family may rank very differently across locations and years (Lipka et al., 2014; Ramstein et al., 2016; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024). These realities make it difficult to (i) estimate breeding values early, (ii) identify widely adapted high‐yield genotypes, and (iii) select efficiently for multiple traits simultaneously.

Genomic selection (GS) accelerates switchgrass breeding by using dense genome‐wide markers to predict genetic merit before full multi‐year phenotyping. The basic framework originally proposed for livestock and crops to predict breeding values (BVs) from marker effects estimated in a training population has been adapted to outcrossing perennial grasses, including switchgrass (Fiedler et al., 2018; Lipka et al., 2014; Ramstein et al., 2016). In switchgrass, genomic or ridge‐regression best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP/rrBLUP) and related models have achieved moderate to high predictive abilities (often higher than 0.4–0.6) for biomass yield, plant height, cell wall composition traits, winter survivorship, and flowering time (FT), depending on the trait architecture, population, and cross‐validation strategy (Ramstein et al., 2016; Tilhou & Casler, 2022; Tilhou et al., 2023; Tilhou, Lee, et al., 2024). These outcomes demonstrate that genomic prediction can shorten breeding cycles by enabling (i) early selection among progenies in the juvenile phase, (ii) within‐family ranking, and (iii) strategic parent selection for recombination, rather than waiting multiple seasons for stand establishment and biomass harvest (Lipka et al., 2014; Tilhou & Casler, 2022). Despite this progress, several gaps remain for applying genomic prediction in operational switchgrass improvement programs targeting biomass yield across the southeastern US and similar production regions.

Switchgrass exhibits strong G × E interaction and genotype‐by‐weather interactions; adaptation‐related traits such as FT, winter survivorship, and stand persistence can drive biomass yield differences across latitudinal gradients (MacQueen et al., 2021; Tilhou et al., 2023; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024). Breeding programs need prediction models that remain useful when genotypes are deployed outside the training environment, not only within a single test site. Recent work on switchgrass biomass yield and survival across regions demonstrated that combining data from multiple locations can improve prediction, but the gain is trait‐dependent and may not be uniform across all ecotypes or families (Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024; Tilhou, Lee, et al., 2024). We still lack a clear understanding of how stable genomic predictions are across contrasting test environments and whether model performance degrades in leave‐one‐family‐out or leave‐one‐location‐out scenarios, which mimic real forward selection.

Traits that are expensive to measure, such as dry biomass yield, are often correlated with easier, or non‐destructive traits such as plant height, tiller thickness, crown perimeter, leaf length and width, and phenological traits including FT, spring emergence (SE), and lodging resistance (Lipka et al., 2014). Multi‐trait genomic prediction models that borrow information across correlated traits can improve prediction for yield, especially when yield measurements are labor‐intensive, costly, and destructive across sites and years (Tang et al., 2024). However, the practical value of those auxiliary traits can depend on the environment. For example, plant height and delayed flowering are often associated with higher biomass in northern environments, while in warmer southern environments, crown size, tiller thickness, and canopy architecture may be stronger indicators of biomass yield (Hughes et al., 2018). A breeding program operating across multiple states needs to determine whether indirect‐selection traits defined in one region (e.g., SE and FT) transfer to other regions or whether region‐specific surrogate traits (e.g., tiller thickness, crown perimeter, and leaf size) are required to inform site‐adapted selection decisions.

Most genomic prediction studies in switchgrass use GBLUP/rrBLUP as a baseline model, in which all markers are assumed to contribute small, normally distributed additive effects (Ramstein & Casler, 2019). Outcrossing polyploid grasses may also exhibit dominance and epistatic effects for fitness and biomass traits, raising the question of whether explicitly modeling non‐additive effects (e.g., additive + dominance kernels, Bayesian models [Bayes A, Bayes B, Bayes C], or nonlinear approaches) can meaningfully improve predictive ability for yield and standability traits (Wang et al., 2025). Similarly, there is no consensus on the optimal single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) density for routine genomic prediction in operational breeding populations. High‐density genotyping may capture more linkage disequilibrium and non‐additive signal but increases cost and often provides diminishing returns beyond a certain SNP threshold; however, this relationship is trait‐ and population‐specific (Ramstein et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2025). Traditionally, general combining ability (GCA) between half‐sib and parental models was used to estimate additive variance and heritability using known mating designs from the polycross (Griffing, 1956; Henderson, 1952; Shattuck et al., 1993). In contrast, genomic relationship matrices (GRMs) use realized marker sharing to capture Mendelian sampling within full‐sibs or half‐sibs (Zapata‐Valenzuela et al., 2013; Gamal El‐Dien et al., 2016). Comparing pedigree‐ and marker‐based estimates of heritability within the same polycross population can clarify the relative contributions of additive versus non‐additive genetic control for biomass yield and related traits (Bouvet et al., 2016; Muñoz et al., 2014).

The present study addresses these gaps by evaluating a large, multi‐environment switchgrass polycross population across contrasting southeastern US locations. We combined multi‐site and multi‐year phenotypes for biomass yield and related phenological traits (SE and FT) with dense genome‐wide SNP data. We aimed to (i) quantify genetic signal and G × E interaction for yield, SE, and FT across environments; (ii) evaluate how well genomic prediction models can predict yield, SE, and FT within‐ and across environments and how prediction accuracy changes under forward selection scenarios; and (iii) integrate phenotypic and genomic rankings to prioritize a shortlist of families for recombination in the next breeding cycle.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Trial sites and experimental design

Two southeastern states of the U.S. were selected to establish experimental trials at three experimental stations. One experimental station was located at the East Tennessee Research and Education Center (ETREC) Plant Sciences Unit, managed by collaborators at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville (UTK), Tennessee. The other two experimental stations were located in Georgia at the Iron Horse Plant Sciences Farm (Watkinsville) and Gibbs Farm (Tifton). The locations were selected to evaluate the performance of progeny across multiple environments. The parental accessions used in the study were primarily lowland types that exhibit good yield performance; some were coastal, and a few were of unknown ploidy. Trials were established in July 2021 with two replicates per location, using a randomized complete block design, to evaluate the switchgrass half‐sib progeny. The soil types at Gibbs Farm, Tifton, were Tifton loamy sand with 2%–5% slopes; Cecil gravelly sandy loam with 2%–10% slopes and moderate erosion at Iron Horse Farm; and 44.4% of Heiskell silt loam with 2%–5% slopes and 55.6% of Waynesboro loam with 6%–15% slopes at ETREC, Tennessee. The trials were sprayed with glyphosate at 177 mL in 15 L of water in April and May using a backpack sprayer every year, and handheld tools were used to eradicate stubborn weeds like Johnson grass. All the trials were managed as rainfed plots without any source of irrigation other than rain.

Half‐sib population development

The half‐sib progeny population (n = 800) used in this study was developed from seeds obtained through polycrossing of 50 parental accessions selected from a previously established switchgrass diversity panel consisting of 404 switchgrass germplasm accessions (Makaju et al., 2025). The diversity panel was originally established for the development of a drought adaptation index in Tifton, GA (Makaju et al., 2025), and for a nitrogen‐use efficiency study at ETREC, Knoxville, TN (Mazarei et al., 2025). Overall, parental selection was based on dry aboveground biomass yield and adaptation to drought stress. The selected 50 parents were grouped by FT features into early‐ and late‐flowering groups. Selected switchgrass plants from the diversity panel were clone replicated by splitting tillers (four splits of one parent in the greenhouse) forming 200 parent plants that were transplanted into the crossing block at Iron Horse Farm (Watkinsville) for polycrossing. Four seeds were harvested from each parent plant in the polycross block, generating 800 half‐sib progenies representing 200 HSFs. Detailed population development and the plot size information are given in Irfan et al. (2026).

Phenotypic data collection

Phenotypic data were collected from all experimental sites in Georgia for key agronomic traits such as SE, FT, and dry biomass yield. Data collection for SE began in January 2022 and for FT in May 2022. Data were also recorded for SE, FT, and yield at the experimental site in Tennessee. The biomass harvest and sampling procedure were the same as those in Irfan et al. (2026).

DNA extraction

Leaf tissue samples from all 800 progeny plants were collected by sampling juvenile leaves from the plants in the greenhouse. Genomic DNA was extracted using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method with 2% CTAB and β‐mercaptoethanol. The procedure involved grinding prechilled leaf samples with metal beads using a SPEX SamplePrep Geno/Grinder. After grinding the samples to a fine powder, 50–100 mg powder was mixed with 1 mL of 2% CTAB in a 1.5‐mL centrifuge tube and incubated at 65°C for 30 min. The samples were then cooled to room temperature and treated with DNase‐free RNase enzyme for 30 min at 37°C. After RNase treatment, the samples were centrifuged at 20°C–25°C for 6 min, and 700 µL of supernatant was transferred to new centrifuge tubes. Chloroform‐isoamyl alcohol (24:1, v/v) was added to the supernatant, and the samples were vortexed and then centrifuged for 15 min to remove all proteins and polysaccharides. 500 µL of clear supernatant was transferred to new centrifuge tubes, to which 300 µL of chilled isopropanol was added, and the mixture was incubated at −20°C for 1 h to precipitate the DNA. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed with 70% ethanol and finally resuspended in 70 µL of double‐distilled water (ddH2O).

Illumina whole DNA sequencing

The DNA sequencing was carried out by the Joint Genome Institute (JGI). The size sequencing depth for the progeny population was set as 10X. Plate‐based DNA library preparation for Illumina sequencing was performed on the Hamilton Vantage robotic liquid‐handling system using the Kapa Biosystems HyperPrep library preparation kit (Roche). Note that 200 ng of genomic DNA from samples was sheared to an average fragment of size 400 bp (approximately 300–500 bp) using a Covaris LE220 focused‐ultrasonicator. The sheared DNA fragments were size selected by using a double solid‐phase reversible immobilization cleanup (double‐SPRI) using TotalPure next‐generation sequencing (NGS) beads (Omega Bio‐tek), and then the selected fragments were end‐repaired, A‐tailed, and ligated with Illumina‐compatible unique dual‐index sequencing adaptors (IDT, Inc.). The prepared libraries were then quantified using the KAPA Illumina library quantification kit (Roche) and run on a LightCycler 480 real‐time PCR instrument (Roche). The quantified libraries were then multiplexed, and the pool of libraries was then prepared for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform using NovaSeq XP v1.5 reagent kits (Illumina), S4 flow cell, following a 2 × 150 indexed run recipe.

Genome‐wide variant calling and hard‐filtering

Across samples, the average mapping rate to the reference genome was 98.42% of filtered reads with range 96.48%–99.29%. The average genome‐wide coverage was 16.64× with range 3.02×–33.28×. Sequencing report metadata from JGI contained 800 total sequenced progeny entries (Table S1). After merging the sequencing report with progeny metadata, 776 samples matched the progeny list, while the remaining were missing from the JGI report. The 776 trimmed and filtered FASTQ samples were mapped to the P. virgatum AP13 reference genome v5.1 (Nayak et al., 2022) using bwa‐mem2 with the ‐M flag (“bwa‐mem2 mem ‐M ‐p < reference > < fastq_file > > < sam_output > ”) to mark secondary alignments (Vasimuddin et al., 2019), and the sequence alignment outputs were viewed and sorted with “samtools view” and “samtools sort” to produce sorted binary alignment (BAM) files (Danecek et al., 2021). Read groups were marked with “samtools addreplacerg” to properly label platform, library, and sample name with “PL,” “LB,” and “SM” tags, respectively. BAM files were then merged into a single file per sample with “samtools merge,” resulting in 800 sample BAM files (including extra samples that were sequenced but not shown in Table S1). Duplicate reads were then marked with GATK 4.6.1.0 with the MarkDuplicates subcommand. Finally, base quality score recalibration was performed using the BaseRecalibrator subcommand of GATK, with the “–known‐sites” parameter referencing a previous set of biallelic SNPs called on AP13 (Lovell et al., 2021). Base quality recalibration was applied to the BAM files using the ApplyBQSR subcommand, resulting in 800 analysis‐ready BAM files for downstream SNP calling.

The HaplotypeCaller subcommand of GATK was run on each of the 800 BAM files, splitting the reference genome into four pieces (3200 total commands) to generate per‐sample, per‐interval .gvcf files (“‐ERC GVCF ‐L < intervals list > ”) (McKenna et al., 2010). The 3200 .gvcf files were concatenated per sample using “bcftools concat,” producing a single merged genomic variant call format (GVCF) per sample (Danecek et al., 2021). The GATK CombineGCVFs subcommand was then run to perform a two‐stage combination into a file for joint calling. Joint genotyping was performed using a two‐stage strategy to enable parallelization and reduce memory load. First, the 800 sample gvcfs were split into 29 subgroups/sample batches (about 27–28 samples per batch), and each subgroup was combined to produce 29 intermediate multi‐sample GVCFs (sample blocks) using CombineGCVFs. Second, the AP13 reference genome was partitioned into 29 genomic intervals, and for each interval, the corresponding segments from the 29 sample blocks were combined using CombineGVCFs into a multisample gvcf suitable for joint calling. The number of batches/intervals (29) was chosen for computational convenience based on cluster resource constraints (memory and wall‐time) and does not affect the biological interpretation; it only determined how the work was parallelized. Each of the 29 regions was jointly called using the GenotypeGVCFs subcommand with the default parameters (“‐L < interval_list > ”), and the resulting variant calling formats (VCFs) were merged into a single joint called VCF using “bcftools concat.” Due to edge effects around region boundaries, multiple SNPs may be called, so we removed duplicates using “bcftools norm ‐d none.” Finally, biallelic SNPs were extracted using “bcftools view ‐m2 ‐M2 ‐v snps.”

Once variant calling was completed in the P. virgatum breeding population, only SNP variants were retained using GATK SelectVariants commands (GATK version 4.1.8.1) (McKenna et al., 2010). Hard filtering of raw SNPs was then performed using GATK VariantFiltration commands, with thresholds recommended by the GATK developers: quality by depth (QD < 2.0), Fisher strand bias (FS > 60.0), mapping quality (MQ < 40.0), strand odds ratio (SOR > 3.0), and an additional depth filter (DP < 10) to remove low‐coverage sites. All scaffolds were removed, and only chromosome variants (Chr1K–Chr9K and Chr1N–Chr9N) were retained for downstream analyses. Following hard filtering, additional quality control steps were applied in PLINK. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01, more than 15% missing genotype calls (geno > 0.15), or extreme deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p‐value < 1 × 10−150) were excluded, and individuals with more than 20% missing genotypes (mind > 0.20), if any, were removed. After applying all filtering criteria, 44,926,491 SNPs from 776 individuals were retained for downstream genomic analyses. Given that the polycross breeding population had 50 founder individuals, we further removed SNPs that presented as outliers based on a genome‐wide per‐site missingness (≥5%), sequencing depth, and sequencing quality using VCFtools (0.1.16) (Danecek et al., 2011) to obtain a random subset of 500,000 polymorphic (MAF ≥ 0.2) biallelic SNPs with reduced computational burden and reduced redundancy simply by using fewer correlated SNPs, improving numerical stability of genomic prediction models.

Genomic prediction

The 50 parents of the P. virgatum breeding population represented high‐biomass‐yielding individuals selected from a diversity panel of 404 natural accessions (Makaju et al., 2025). Phenotypic data consisted of quantitative traits related to biomass yield (kg ha−1), SE (Julian date), and FT (Julian date), measured across the same set of genotypes across three different sites (Watkinsville, GA; Tifton, GA; and Knoxville, TN) from 2022 to 2024. All phenotypic measurements were inspected for completeness and consistency, and cells with missing data points were removed from overall phenotypic dataset. Genotypic data were represented as a genome‐wide SNP marker matrix, with genotype in rows and markers in columns, and genotype values recorded as minor allele dosage. Any remaining missing genotypes were imputed to the mean of each marker column. Following imputation, the input genotype matrix was centered and scaled such that each marker had a mean of zero and unit variance.

The predictive performance of multiple genomic prediction models and a parental model that includes only the GCA of the maternal parent was assessed using a repeated (30×), fivefold cross‐validation framework. Different Bayesian genomic prediction models (Bayesian Ridge Regression, Bayesian LASSO, BayesA, BayesB, and BayesCπ) that specify alternative prior distributions on marker effects, allowing varying degrees of shrinkage and sparsity, were evaluated (de Los Campos et al., 2013). In addition, GBLUP and GBLUPD models that use an additive GRM and a GRM plus a dominance relationship matrix (DRM), which model pairwise additive and dominance‐relatedness among genets, were also evaluated to compare with the Bayesian models. All training‐test splits for repeats and folds were consistent across all models, including parent, Bayesian, and GBLUP models. Posterior distribution of marker effects and variance components was inferred via Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling, with samples thinned every 10th iteration after 2500 iterations of burn‐in for a total of 10,000 iterations as implemented in BGLR (Bayesian generalized linear regression) (Pérez & de Los Campos, 2014). These parameter settings were sufficient to achieve convergence of the model to a stationary distribution, as diagnosed using trace plots of estimates across iterations. All Bayesian, GBLUP, GBLUPD, and parental GCA models were evaluated specifically within each site‐year, independently fitting each model separately with distinct location × year combinations. The scripts/codes for genomic prediction analyses are provided on GitHub at https://github.com/roshanabeyratne/GP_switchgrass_polycross_yield/tree/main.

The statistical model underlying genomic prediction models for each of the sites for a given year can be described as follows:

In all models, y, μ, and e are n × 1 vectors containing trait phenotypes, the overall mean, and residual effects, respectively, for n genets. Here, e ∼ Ν(0, σ 2 e I), where σ 2 e is the residual variance and I an identity matrix of corresponding dimensions. In the parental model, we included the seed‐parent random effect modeled as GCA dam ∼ Ν(0,σ 2 GCA I), where σ 2 GCA = ¼(σ 2 A) provides an estimate of additive genetic variance (σ 2 A) in the parental population using the variance of dam's GCA (σ 2 GCA). In all Bayesian models, W is the scaled genotype matrix with genets in rows and markers in columns, and u is a 500,000 × 1 vector of marker effects. In Bayesian models, all variables are treated as random effects, including μ , and models differ based on the prior used for marker effects, which has implications for marker effect regularization and marker selection (de Los Campos et al., 2013). In the GBLUP model, the vector a represents genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) for genets modeled by the Z incidence matrix. Here the additive genetic effect is modeled as a ∼ Ν(0, σ 2 a G) and σ 2 a represents the additive genetic variance. The pairwise additive genetic relatedness among genets was modeled with a GRM G. Here, G was estimated using the same set of 500,000 SNPs used for Bayesian models using the VanRaden method with Z as the marker genotype matrix (VanRaden, 2008). The GBLUPD uses the same variables as the GBLUP, with the only difference being a vector of dominance effect (d) is introduced as d ∼ Ν(0, σ 2 d D ) in addition to the additive genetic effect. The pairwise dominance relatedness ( D ) is also estimated using 500,000 SNPs as before using the methods described by Vitezica et al. (2013). Predictive ability was assessed as the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) between the predicted GEBVs and the actual recorded phenotypic values. Here GEBVs for the test set in Bayesian models were obtained by multiplying each test genet's composite genotype vector by their respective marker effects estimated from the training set. Family phenotypic superiority was computed as the deviation of each family mean from the population mean [(Y/n)family − μ)], which corresponds to the standard quantitative genetic form, g ^i − g ® = bxy (xi − x ®), where (xi − x ®) is superiority relative to the population's mean and scaled by the regression coefficient to predict BV, and μ represents the grand mean of parent and progeny yields across environments (Rutkoski, 2019). The BV–GEBV overlapping analysis was done in R.

Narrow‐sense (h 2) heritabilities for all traits were calculated based on the estimates of relevant variance components obtained using the univariate GBLUPD model.

RESULTS

SNP density and predictive ability for biomass yield across environments

We evaluated the number of SNP markers affecting genomic prediction of dry biomass yield in each environment, Georgia (Watkinsville and Tifton) for the years 2022, 2023, and 2024, and Tennessee (UTK) for 2 years, 2023 and 2024 (Figure 1). Predictive abilities are shown as PCC between predicted genomic values and observed dry matter yield using the GBLUP model because it showed consistent performance across environments. Across all environments, the predictive ability increased rapidly from 100 SNPs to 2500–5000 SNPs, after which gains plateaued, indicating diminishing returns from higher marker densities. The predictive strength was not the same across locations, as evident in Figure 1. The highest overall predictive ability was observed for Tifton, where in 2022, PCC was 0.40 with 100 markers and increased to 0.55 with 2500–5000 markers. The curve then plateaued beyond that level. Tifton 2023 and 2024 followed the same trend at lower levels, with final PCC values 0.35–0.40 in 2023 and 0.30–0.35 in 2024. Intermediate predictive ability was observed in Watkinsville, where in 2022, PCC increased from 0.40 at 100 markers to 0.50 with 2500–5000 SNPs, then leveled off. In 2023, the plateau ranged from 0.35 to 0.38, and in 2024, PCC reduced from 0.30 to 0.35. This showed that the Watkinsville and Tifton sites behave almost the same in terms of SNP response curve; however, the correlation was slightly lower than Tifton. The Tennessee site (UTK) had the lowest average predictive ability and the largest year‐to‐year drop. The predictive ability increased from 0.25 at 100 SNPs to 0.40–0.42 with 2500–5000 SNPs, whereas PCC ultimately dropped in 2024 to about 0.30. These results indicated that prediction of yield in this population is sensitive to marker density at the lower marker densities but saturates quickly at 2500–5000 SNPs, and overall model performance is environment‐dependent.

Genomic prediction model comparison

We compared parental GCA‐based prediction to genomic prediction models for three traits, dry biomass yield, SE, and FT across field sites and years (Figure S1; Table S2). Model performance was evaluated within the environment (location × year) using PCC, calculated as the correlation between cross‐validated genomic predictions and observed phenotypes. Here we present the mean PCC results showing the comparison of performance of all models (Bayesian, GBLUP, GBLUP‐D, Bayesian LASSO, and Bayesian ridge regression) given in Table S3 and Figure 2. The combined analysis showed obvious differences among all prediction models across the three traits. Biomass yield exhibited overall the lowest PCC for baseline Parental_GCA and for other genomic models. The genomic models (BL, BRR, BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, GBLUP, and GBLUP‐D) ranged from 0.38 to 0.41 for biomass yield. FT showed intermediate PCC in the range of 0.43–0.44, and highest PCC was obtained for SE in the range of 0.53–0.55 (Figure 2). Overall, the difference was small among the genomic models, while Parental_GCA baseline was much lower for biomass yield, whereas it was similar genomic models for SE and FT.

Biomass yield

The genomic prediction models consistently outperformed the parental GCA baseline at every site‐year combination for biomass yield (Figure S1A). The lowest mean PCC value was 0.38 for the BL model, while for GBLUP and GBLUP‐D, the lowest mean PCC value was 0.40 and 0.41, respectively, indicating the similar performance of both GBLUP and GBLUP‐D (Figure 2). The PCC was modest with all genomic models; however, the GBLUP‐D and GBLUP models outperformed all genomic models. The site‐years combinations also showed that PCC values reduced in later years from 2023 to 2024. The detailed information on predictive abilities is provided in Table S2.

Flowering time

Mean predictive ability for FT (Figure S1B) was consistently higher than biomass yield. The mean PCC for FT ranged from 0.43 to 0.44 across genomic models, while for Parental_GCA was also 0.43. The highest PCC was observed for the GBLUP‐D and BayesC models, while there were small differences among all genomic models. These results indicated that FT was under stronger genetic control than biomass yield (Figure S1B; Table S2). This suggested that for FT, standard genomic models can recover the relevant signal without reducing efficiency.

Spring emergence

For SE (Figure S1C), an intermediate pattern was evident. The PCC for SE was generally higher than biomass yield. The mean PCC for SE as observed ranged from 0.53 to 0.55 across all genomic models where the dominance model (GBLUP‐D) showed the highest mean value. The baseline Parental_GCA also showed similar mean PCC as observed for genomic models (Figure 2; Table S2). No single genomic prediction model consistently outperformed other models for SE. However, the GBLUP‐D model provided a small repeatable improvement in several cases, mainly for biomass yield and SE, suggesting that dominance variation contributes to these traits in this half‐sib breeding population.

Flowering time

The FT showed strong phenotypic and additive genetic consistency within the regions, particularly Knoxville across years, where correlations were very high, indicating stable phenological expression under similar environmental conditions. Within Georgia location, FT rP correlations between Watkinsville and Tifton were moderate to strong (0.56–0.69), reflecting shared climatic conditions. In contrast, cross‐region rP between Georgia and UTK ranged from slightly negative to weakly positive (−0.01–0.11), suggesting low agreement in rankings and some genotype re‐ranking between regions. The rG followed the same pattern for Watkinsville and Tifton (0.59–0.81), remaining higher than rP while declining across Georgia and UTK. These results indicate that FT is under strong additive genetic control, yet its expression is modulated by regional environmental differences.

Biomass yield

Biomass yield exhibited moderate to strong rP within regions, particularly between Watkinsville and Tifton (0.53–0.80), indicating some consistency of family performance under similar regional conditions. However, cross‐region phenotypic correlations between Georgia and UTK were weaker (0.10–0.32) and more variable, reflecting substantial G × E interaction. The rG for yield was generally higher than phenotypic correlations within each location, such as in Georgia, where it was in the range of 0.63–0.92 in Watkinsville and Tifton, while showing a decline across regions between Georgia and UTK (−0.02–0.25), indicating the contribution to yield by shared genetic component for biomass yield; however, the expression is strongly environment‐dependent. In comparison to FT, yield showed greater re‐ranking across regions, consistent with its complex and cumulative biological nature.

Spring emergence

The SE showed weak rP across regions between Georgia and UTK with particularly low correlation (0.20–0.30). Even within regions, correlations were more variable than for FT. The rG for SE was similarly unstable across environments with many within Georgia observations in the range of 0.62–0.70 and some observations higher than 0.75. These patterns indicate that SE is sensitive to local environmental conditions and other environmental dynamics, making it the least transferable trait across regions in comparison to FT and yield.

Cross‐trait relationships

SE versus FT

Across environments, SE and FT generally showed negative rP. Earlier emerging families tended to flower late. This negative correlation was also evident in rG, which indicated that this association is partly heritable, meaning that families that emerge earlier tend to flower late.

FT versus yield

FT and biomass yield were positively correlated within sites and years in both matrices. Environments with families that flowered late showed higher biomass yield. The weak, positive FT–yield correlations were phenotypically evident and remained somewhat stronger at the additive genetic level. This implies that delaying reproductive transition and therefore extending vegetative growth contributes to more biomass accumulation, and this relationship is under additive genetic control and heritable.

SE versus yield

The relationship between SE and yield was weaker and more environment‐dependent. In Watkinsville and Tifton, the rP was negatively correlated, suggesting that families that emerge earlier in spring also end with higher biomass. The rG between SE and yield was also negatively correlated to yield. This indicated that early‐season emergence guarantees high biomass in all environments. The overall conclusion from both matrices is that the rG matrix is cleaner and more block‐structured, where FT and yield measurements in Georgia and UTK make visible clusters; on the other hand, SE remained more scattered. This informed that environmental noise weakens or distorts raw rP, especially for SE and yield. After removing environmental noise and estimating only the additive genetic component, FT and yield each show a strong, stable, and shared genetic basis across locations and years.

Narrow‐sense heritability (h 2) estimation from genomic markers

Flowering time

Narrow‐sense heritability (h 2) for FT was consistently moderate to high across both locations (Georgia and UTK) and years, indicating an additive genetic component (Table 1). Table 1 shows two types of heritabilities. Parental heritabilities indicate family‐level heritability (200 observations for 200 families), while individual heritabilities indicate the values for all the individuals rather than families (800 observations for 800 individuals at each location). In Knoxville (UTK), FT showed relatively high h 2 under the parental model that was above 0.70 in both 2023 and 2024, whereas the individual tree model gave lower estimates of 0.39. This suggested that, in Knoxville, family‐level information accounted for a large proportion of the observed variation in FT at the UTK site. In Tifton, FT was also heritable, but the patterns across years differed. In 2022 and 2023, the individual tree model produced a relatively high h 2 of 0.53, which was slightly higher than or comparable to the parental model that was 0.47. In 2024, both models dropped sharply (0.23–0.28). This indicated a much stronger environmental influence on FT expression in that year. In Watkinsville, h 2 for FT remained high and stable across all 3 years. The individual tree estimates were 0.50–0.60, and the parental estimates were almost similar (0.46–0.61). This consistency across 2022, 2023, and 2024 supports the conclusion that FT is under reliable genetic control in this environment.

Spring emergence

The SE showed some of the highest heritability values in the years 2022 and 2023 for Watkinsville and Tifton, but it was also the trait that changed the most across environments and years. For Knoxville (UTK), parental model estimates for SE were very high (0.70–0.80) in both 2023 and 2024, while the individual‐tree estimates were almost 0.50 after rounding off. This gap suggested that at the Knoxville site, early emergence is strongly structured at the family level and that averaging within families inflates the apparent additive signal. The genomic individual tree model produced more conservative estimates, implying more within‐family variation than the parental model accounts for. In Tifton, emergence in 2022 and 2023 was highly heritable under both models. The individual tree h 2 was 0.60–0.70, and for the parental model, it was similar (0.60‐0.70). Both models dropped in h 2 by 2024 to about 0.25, indicating that early‐season establishment in that year was driven by environmental conditions rather than inherited differences. The Watkinsville site showed the same overall pattern. In 2022 and 2023, SE had high h 2 in both models, which remained higher than 0.60. In 2024, estimates for the SE dropped to 0.37 in the individual tree model and 0.35 in the parental model. Across the sites, SE can be strongly heritable when growing conditions are favorable and uniform (e.g., Tifton and Watkinsville in 2022 and 2023). It was also evident that h 2 collapsed for the year 2024 across all sites, which might be due to changes in stress or weather impacts, indicating that selection on SE can be effective but is environment‐dependent and less reliable in years with establishment stress.

Biomass yield

The h 2 for biomass yield from GBLUP was generally lower than that observed for FT and SE, and it was more sensitive to sites and years. At Knoxville, the yield h 2 in 2023 was moderate, with an estimate of 0.41 for the individual tree model, while for the parental model, it was 0.52. The estimates in both cases dropped for the year 2024, with almost 0.37 for the individual tree and for the parental estimate as well. These values were lower and less stable than those observed for FT. The biomass yield h 2 in Tifton was moderate in 2022, at 0.44 in the individual tree estimate, while for the parental model, it was above 0.50, followed by a decline in 2023 and 2024. In 2024, both models were 0.25–0.30, which indicated that additive genetic differences among families contributed meaningfully to biomass production in 2022; however, year‐to‐year environmental noise and G × E interaction reduced repeatability in later years. In Watkinsville, yield in 2022 was moderately heritable (0.40 for the individual tree model and near 0.50 for the parental estimates). In 2023, both estimates dropped to 0.30. In 2024, the two models diverged, where the individual tree remained at 0.32 while the parental model reduced to 0.24. This suggested that in 2024 at Watkinsville, predicting biomass from parental family structure alone was less informative, likely because within‐family differences and micro‐environmental effects were large. Overall, biomass yield consistently exhibited the lowest and most variable h 2 among the three traits. This supports the interpretation that biomass yield is strongly influenced by environmental factors and G × E interactions.

Based on that, families were ranked using BV and GEBV. Overlap of selection decision was quantified at 5%, 10%, and 20% selection intensities (Table 2). DISCUSSION

Genetic architecture of biomass yield across locations and years

Biomass yield showed the weakest and most variable additive genetic signal across environments, whereas FT maintained a stronger and more stable genetic basis. This contrast is expected in perennial grasses because yield integrates many developmental and physiological processes and is strongly shaped by seasonal weather, stand dynamics, and microsite effects, all of which inflate nongenetic variance and reduce repeatability. Evidence from switchgrass multi‐environment studies consistently shows substantial G × E interaction for yield and emphasizes that multi‐site and multi‐year testing is required to separate genetic effects from environmental noise (Bhandari et al., 2010; MacQueen et al., 2021; Nayak et al., 2020).

By comparison, FT is more directly regulated by photoperiod and temperature accumulation, so genotypic rankings tend to be more consistent than for yield across sites and years. Switchgrass work focused on phenology and yield relationships repeatedly identifies FT as a robust and biologically interpretable axis of adaptation and biomass accumulation (Tilhou & Casler, 2022; Tornqvist et al., 2018). However, the strength and sometimes the direction of FT–yield relationship can vary with ecotype background and environment, so FT is best interpreted as an adaptation axis that may correlate with biomass depending on the target region.

Phenotypic and genetic trait relationships

The positive association between FT and biomass yield is biologically coherent for a vegetative biomass crop. Late flowering prolongs vegetative growth and can translate into greater accumulated biomass, which has been repeatedly observed in switchgrass field trials and is increasingly used as a yield surrogate in prediction frameworks (Lipka et al., 2014; Tilhou & Casler, 2022; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024). This association is often observed within a given genetic background and target region, while not universal across all switchgrass ecotypes and environments. Importantly, when the correlation is stronger at the additive genetic level than at the phenotypic level, this supports the interpretation that shared genetic control contributes to the relationship, rather than that the association is driven mainly by shared environmental effects. In contrast, SE often showed more context dependence than FT because it integrates overwintering physiology, local temperature and moisture thresholds, stand status, and management. Switchgrass regional analyses and winter survivorship prediction studies reinforce that emergence‐ and persistence‐related traits can display stronger environmental sensitivity than phenology traits, and they benefit from environment‐aware modeling and carefully constructed trait sets (MacQueen et al., 2021; Tilhou et al., 2023; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024).

Genetic correlations and implications for ranking influenced by G × E interaction

The observations that FT is more stable across environments than biomass yield align closely with the interpretation provided by type B genetic correlations, which considers the effect of G × E interaction on changes in mean phenotypic performance of genotypes (Poupon, 2023; Yuan et al., 2021). The type B correlations formalize the extent to which the same genetic effects are expressed consistently across environments and remain as a standard quantitative measure for the understanding of re‐rankings in perennial breeding programs (Burdon, 1977; Johansen et al., 2025). A high type B correlation for FT implied that selection on this trait can transfer across regions with relatively low risk of re‐ranking. In contrast, the lower stability typically seen for yield implied that region‐specific selection indices or explicit multi‐environment genomic prediction is necessary to achieve robust gain, which is widely used in tree breeding (Jung et al., 2025) and forage breeding programs (Johansen et al., 2025) when G × E is strong.

Pedigree versus genomic relationship models for heritability and variance partitioning

Pedigree‐based parental models, such as GCA, captured among‐family structure. The pedigree‐based models are useful for estimating expected additive variance under a mating design; however, they cannot recover Mendelian sampling within families. Higher predictive ability of parental GCA for FT and SE in Knoxville may reflect stronger family‐level additive effects for these traits in Knoxville. GRMs captured realized relatedness among individuals and thereby improved variance partitioning and individual ranking within half‐sib progenies (VanRaden, 2008). The advantage of genomic relationship models for individual selection has been repeatedly demonstrated across plant and animal breeding contexts, and in switchgrass specifically, genomic prediction studies show that marker‐based models can provide useful predictive ability for yield and related traits even in diverse, structured populations (Lipka et al., 2014; Ramstein et al., 2016; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024).

Genomic prediction model performance

The pattern where rrBLUP/GBLUP performs competitively across traits and environments, alternative models lack consistency in dominating GBLUP, which is typical for biomass yield. Under a broadly polygenic architecture, best linear unbiased prediction models are difficult to outperform in a stable way because they efficiently shrink many small effects and are robust to noise and sampling variation. This has been well‐reported previously in genomic prediction work (Endelman, 2011; Fiedler et al., 2018; Montesinos‐López et al., 2019). A driver of yield prediction performance is the representativeness of the training set for the target environments. Recent regional‐scale switchgrass genomic prediction demonstrated that predictive ability rises markedly as data from additional sites are included in the training set and highlighted the value of combining multi‐site performance information with yield surrogate traits such as FT and plant height (Ramstein et al., 2016; Tilhou, Bonnette, et al., 2024). This strongly supports the inference that, for improvements in biomass yield, training design and environmental coverage matter more than changes among reasonable model classes.

Additive versus additive‐plus dominance effects

Small and repeatable gains from additive plus dominance models are plausible in switchgrass because it is an obligate outcrossing species with high heterozygosity, and traits related to plant vigor and seasonal performance carry nonadditive variance (Sang et al., 2022; Varona et al., 2018). Methodological frameworks for modeling dominance using DRMs and dominance kernels are well established, and multiple studies in plant breeding show that incorporating dominance can improve prediction for some traits and populations, even if gains are not usual (Liu et al., 2018; Ramstein et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2016). This aligns with broader evidence from hybrid crops such as maize, where dominance‐aware models can improve prediction of heterozygous performance and where the magnitude of dominance contributions varies by trait and genomic context (Ramstein et al., 2020; Technow et al., 2012).

Practical implications of yield surrogate prediction

A key practical implication of our results is that FT is the most transferable yield surrogate across environments, whereas SE is more environment‐contingent. A surrogate is a trait that is easy, fast, cheap, and nondestructive to measure yield, while it is predictively correlated with yield that provides a basis for indirect selection. In switchgrass, GS concepts built around early or easy‐to‐measure surrogates were articulated early, and subsequent regional‐scale studies have implied that FT and height are among the most reliable surrogates for biomass yield across diverse regions, as shown by Lipka et al. (2014) and Tilhou, Bonnette et al. (2024). More broadly, multi‐trait and multi‐environment genomic prediction has repeatedly been shown to improve prediction for difficult traits when auxiliary traits are heritable, genetically correlated with the target trait, and measured widely. Durum wheat multi‐trait multi‐environment studies and multi‐trait prediction work across other crops show that gains are largest when the model exploits stable genetic covariance rather than transient phenotypic associations (Bhatta et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2020; Montesinos‐López et al., 2019). This provides a strong scientific basis for a cross‐region strategy, showing that, over the years in Tennessee, SE and FT guided adaptation‐aware decisions and that the two traits are informative and consistently measured. On the other hand, in Georgia, the surrogates for yield may carry a stronger signal for canopy and biomass accumulation. The core principle is not that surrogates are interchangeable across regions. Each region/site uses the most heritable and most genetically informative predictors available, while genomic prediction provides a common framework to compare selections across environments.

Interpretation of partial overlap between BV and GEBV selections

The observed overlap indicates that phenotypic BV and genomic estimated breeding values (EBV)/GEBV‐based selection identified overlapping but not identical sets of superior families. Such partial concordance is expected because BV reflects realized phenotypic performance across specific environments and years (Gonzalez‐Recio et al., 2014), whereas GEBVs are predictions derived from marker information and a fitted training model (Dekkers et al., 2021). Prediction error, finite training population size, relatedness structure, and G × E effects can all reduce rank concordance between phenotypic‐based and genomic‐based selection. The conceptual basis for genome‐wide prediction and its dependence on dense markers capturing relationships and linkage disequilibrium traces back to the foundational GS framework introduced by Meuwissen et al. (2001). A key practical implication is that GS can accelerate genetic gain by enabling earlier selection (Voss‐Fels et al., 2019). However, rank agreement with phenotypic selection depends on prediction accuracy and model calibration in the target population. Studies have emphasized that accuracy is influenced by training set size and composition, genetic architecture, and the match between training and prediction environments, which aligns with the incomplete overlap observed in our study (Meuwissen et al., 2001; Roorkiwal et al., 2018). Even when BV and GEBV/EBV are positively associated (as evident in the scatter plot, Figure 4A), selection is applied to the extreme tails representing the top 5% and 20%, where small changes in predicted value can considerably reshuffle ranks. This is particularly relevant when differences among candidates are modest or when the phenotype integrates environmental variance that is not captured by marker‐based models (Bhat et al., 2016). Additionally, if some HSFs have multiple entries, such as multiple unique IDs per family, the top‐k rows may collapse to fewer unique families (Table 2). Therefore, set‐based visual summaries in a Venn diagram (as evident in Figure 4B) show stronger overlap among unique families than row‐level overlap metrics. In plant breeding applications, such behavior is commonly discussed as a difference between prediction correlation and decision concordance under truncation selection, which can diverge when the selection intensity is high (Combs & Bernardo, 2013). From a selection standpoint, the intersecting set of families identified by both BV and GEBV provides a conservative high‐confidence group for advancement as it is supported by both realized multi‐environment performance (BV) and genomic prediction (GEBV). The HSFs, such as Fam_4 (J496.C), Fam_121 (J463.A), Fam_25 (J215.A), Fam_11 (J230.A), and Fam_38 (J498.B), are represented as strong candidates by repeatedly being prioritized by both metrics and occupy the upper‐right quadrant (Figure 4A). This two‐evidence strategy is consistent with GS practice, in which genomic predictions are used to screen and prioritize candidates, followed by phenotypic confirmation in multi‐environment trials to manage G × E and ensure stability (Meuwissen et al., 2001).

CONCLUSION

This study represents the GS strategy in forage breeding that shows the importance of using prediction models based on multi‐environment dataset. This study aided in clarifying trait architecture and environmental influence in shaping prediction. The moderate predictive abilities for yield exhibited greater environmental sensitivity than FT. The yield observations were more variable across locations and years, which is consistent with strong environmental influence (G × E interaction). FT showed stronger genetic control, which may also indicate its role as a yield surrogate. However, weak cross‐trait FT and yield relationship was suggestive of FT as a yield surrogate with respect to the environment and association among yield and FT. SE was heritable with indication for an environment‐specific adaptive response. It showed weaker environmental consistency relative to FT, which showed the dependence of SE on local conditions of establishment. Across traits and environments, genomic models (GBLUP and GBLUPD) provided advantages over family mean that captured realized relatedness and variations within families. For all the genomic models tested, GBLUP was robust model for polygenic traits. The modest gains from GBLUPD for yield were indicative of the contribution from nonadditive variance in outcrossing populations. Based on phenotypic BV and GEBV rankings, genomic prediction should be evaluated in integration with phenotypic BVs to make reliable selections.
