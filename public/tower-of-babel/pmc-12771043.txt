Review of Applicable Polymeric Viscosifiers and Nanomaterials in the Oil Industry
Authors: Ali Salmani Sayah, Mohammad Hossein Bagheri, Kamand Ghasemi, Sasan Dan, Mohammad Sharifi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The optimization of rheological properties in drilling and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) fluids plays a pivotal role in improving the efficiency of petroleum operations. Viscosifiers, as essential additives, enhance fluid viscosity, facilitating better cutting transport, wellbore stability, and hydrocarbon displacement. Over the years, researchers have investigated a wide range of natural, semisynthetic, and synthetic polymers to address reservoir challenges such as high temperatures, salinity, and the presence of divalent ions. While natural polymers like xanthan and guar gum offer environmental benefits, their stability under extreme conditions is limited. In contrast, synthetic polymers, particularly acrylamide derivatives, exhibit superior performance but remain susceptible to thermal degradation and salinity effects. Recent advancements, including the incorporation of nanoparticles and the development of hydrophobically modified associative polymers (HMAPs) and thermo-viscosifying polymers (TVPs), have introduced innovative solutions to these challenges. This review explores the evolution, applications, and prospects of polymeric viscosifiers in the oil industry, emphasizing their role in improving fluid performance under harsh reservoir conditions in drilling and EOR operations.

Introduction

Background and Motivation

Viscosifiers are crucial additives in various industries, particularly in oil and gas operations, where they play a key role in enhancing the fluid performance. Viscosity, defined as a fluid’s resistance to flow, is a critical parameter in drilling and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) applications. Materials that influence this property are termed viscosifiers, and they contribute significantly to improving operational efficiency and maintaining wellbore stability.

Polymers are widely utilized as viscosifiers due to their versatile chemical structures. Each polymer molecule consists of a large number of repeating units, granting it distinct physical and chemical properties. Some polymers occur naturally, while others are synthetically engineered through controlled chemical processes. Natural polymers can also be chemically modified to enhance their properties for various industrial applications.

In the oil and gas industry, most viscosifiers are polymer-based. Their effectiveness originates from their long-chain molecular structure, which interacts with solvent molecules, influences fluid movement, and improves viscosity. This interaction helps control the rheological behavior of drilling and EOR fluids, making polymer-based viscosifiers essential for optimizing performance under challenging reservoir conditions.

Polymeric viscosifiers are especially important in the EOR processes and drilling operations. In EOR, these polymers enhance oil displacement by improving the mobility ratio, thereby increasing the recovery efficiency. However, their effectiveness is often limited by harsh reservoir conditions, including high temperatures, low salinity, and pressure fluctuations. This necessitates continuous advancement in polymer technology.

In drilling operations, viscosifiers enhance fluid rheology, facilitating the efficient transport of cuttings and ensuring wellbore stability. Their ability to modify fluid properties promotes better hole cleaning, reduces differential sticking, and enhances the overall drilling performance. Drilling is a complex multidimensional activity. Although the fundamental principles of drilling physics are consistent worldwide, various factors significantly influence drilling costs and performance.

The total drilling cost is a conceptual framework that emphasizes the importance of drilling productivity. According to Cunha, drilling expenses can account for up to 40% of the overall exploration and development costs. Moreover, these expenses represent approximately 25% of the total costs associated with oilfield exploitation, primarily in the exploration and development of well drilling.

As industrialization progresses and global populations expand, the oil demand is projected to rise by approximately 1.5% annually. Forecasts further indicate that petroleum will remain the primary source of global energy for at least the next two decades. −

Significance of Polymer Flooding in EOR

EOR methods have gained significant importance within the oil and gas industry as conventional extraction techniques reach maturity, and reservoirs present increasingly complex challenges. EOR strategies are employed to extract hydrocarbons beyond the capabilities of primary and secondary recovery methods, particularly in mature reservoirs where the natural pressure has significantly declined.

By integration of EOR technologies, operators can enhance the recovery factor of a reservoir, extend its productive lifespan, and maximize economic returns. Among the various EOR techniques, polymer flooding has received considerable attention due to its demonstrated effectiveness in increasing oil recovery rates. Polymers, with their long-chain molecular structures, modify the flow properties of injected fluids, improve the sweep efficiency, and enhance hydrocarbon displacement.

The addition of water-soluble polymers increases the viscosity of injected water, resulting in a more favorable mobility ratio and reducing the level of viscous fingering. By increasing water viscosity, polymer solutions achieve a more uniform distribution within the reservoir, slowing water movement through high-permeability zones and facilitating efficient oil displacement across the formation. This improved sweep efficiency mobilizes oil from previously bypassed regions, leading to a higher overall recovery.

In gas injection EOR methods, polymers are often combined with foaming agents to create stable foam barriers that regulate the gas flow within the reservoir. The increased apparent viscosity of foamed gas reduces gas fingering and directs gas into less accessible zones, improving contact with oil and enhancing recovery. Polymer flooding also offers advantages in high-salinity reservoirs, where dissolved salts often diminish the effectiveness of conventional surfactants. Polymers such as HPAM are engineered to withstand challenging conditions, preserving their viscosity-enhancing properties in saline environments and ensuring an effective sweep efficiency.

Recent literature has expanded our understanding of polymer flooding mechanisms and performance. For instance, Wu et al. (2024) reported incremental oil recoveries ranging from 6 to 27% of original oil in place (OOIP) using synthetic polymers (SPs) under varying salinity and temperature conditions. Gaillard et al. (2023) evaluated NVP- and ATBS-based terpolymers and demonstrated long-term viscosity stability exceeding one year at 140 °C and salinity levels over 100,000 ppm in carbonate cores. Abd-Elaal et al. synthesized fluorescent polyacrylate microspheres for conformance control, which successfully redirected injection profiles and reduced residual oil saturation to as low as 13.5%, as confirmed by CMG simulation and visualization methods.

These advances reflect a trend toward tailored polymer designs with a high thermal tolerance, improved adsorption control, and compatibility with harsh reservoir conditions. This review builds on these findings by offering a critical synthesis of both synthetic and natural polymer systems and examining how nanomaterials can enhance the efficacy of polymer-based fluids under field-representative conditions.

Significance of Polymeric Viscosifier in Drilling Fluid

EOR, polymers play a crucial role in drilling operations, where they are incorporated into drilling fluids to enhance borehole stability, cool drill bits, and facilitate the transport of cuttings to the surface. SPs such as partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (PHPA) are particularly valuable in maintaining borehole integrity under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions by increasing the drilling fluid viscosity and improving rheological properties.

Since the 1970s, the use of polymer solutions as additives in drilling fluids has increased significantly, partly due to advances in chemical engineering and macromolecular synthesis. Polymer-based drilling fluids consist of polymers suspended in an aqueous medium and may exhibit linear, branched, cross-linked, or networked architectures. These fluids are primarily used for drilling through hard, abrasive rock formations. The defining characteristics of polymer drilling fluids include hydrophilicity and pseudoplasticity, enabling them to adapt their viscosity in response to changes in shear rate. ,

As illustrated in Figure , the drilling fluid market in the oil and gas industry has grown alongside the increased drilling activity. Because most oil and gas reserves are located in onshore regions, drilling operations in these areas account for a larger share of the drilling fluids market. Today, drilling fluid formulation focuses not only on enhancing the rate of penetration but also on ensuring wellbore stability, compliance with environmental regulations, and preserving reservoir characteristics. From a technological standpoint, SPs are engineered with these parameters in mind. Primarily derived from petroleum-based products, SPs are composed of smaller molecular units, allowing for exceptional versatility in their design. The chemical composition and molecular dimensions of SPs can be tailored to meet specific subsurface functional requirements.

For efficient and cost-effective drilling, drilling cuttings must be transported to the surface rapidly without causing formation damage while also cooling and lubricating the drill bit. Notably, drilling fluid expenses account for approximately 10% of total drilling costs, making them a significant operational expense (Figure ). Therefore, appropriate viscosifiers not only improve cuttings transport but also reduce fluid loss and provide effective lubrication.

Drilling costs primarily depend on the well location and depth. As the second stage of the wellbore lifecycle, drilling requires fluids that fulfill several essential roles, including suspension and transport of drill cuttings, cooling the drill bit and string, and maintaining the wellbore stability. These fluids also provide lubrication, support borehole cleaning, and enable formation evaluation. Additionally, drilling fluids help enhance drilling rates, facilitate rock fragmentation at the bit face, and ensure adequate penetration of productive layers. They also reduce corrosion, prevent blockages, and minimize adverse effects on reservoir porosity and permeability. − Figure outlines several key functions of drilling fluids.

Historically, Fauvel (1846) introduced a method for the continuous removal of cuttings using circulating water. This technique involved injecting water into the wellbore through hollow pipes with the returning water carrying drill cuttings to the surface. Since then, the core principles of drilling fluid deployment have remained fundamentally unchanged. ,

Drilling fluids are generally classified into three main types based on composition or the nature of the continuous phase: water-based muds (WBMs), oil-based muds (OBMs), and emerging foam-based systems. Oil-based fluids, composed of diesel or mineral oil with a polymer suspension, are used in approximately 15% of drilled wells worldwide. In contrast, about 80% of wells are currently drilled using WBMs. OBMs are favored in various drilling operations due to properties such as excellent thermal stability, effective cuttings transport, high lubricity, salt resistance, and shale stability, making them the preferred choice in challenging conditions. −

In the past two decades, synthetic-based drilling fluids (SBDFs) have emerged as a new generation of drilling fluid formulations, significantly improving the efficacy of traditional oil-based drilling fluids (OBDFs). SBDFs offer several advantages in terms of environmental impact: they are less toxic, enable faster penetration rates, reduce fluid-related nonproductive time, and degrade more rapidly than diesel and mineral oils.

The use of OBMs is increasingly constrained due to regulatory challenges imposed by governmental and nongovernmental agencies. Conversely, WBMs are widely considered cost-effective and environmentally friendly alternatives for drilling operations. − Despite their benefits, WBMs face challenges such as instability in shale formations under harsh wellbore conditions and an insufficient suspension capability for effective cutting transport. Consequently, enhancing WBM formulations is crucial to provide a nontoxic alternative to OBMs while ensuring environmental sustainability.

The inappropriate application of drilling fluids can compromise the entire drilling operation, leading to reduced penetration rates, increased fluid loss, greater risk of pipe sticking, and even catastrophic downhole blowouts. , Therefore, the advancement and optimization of eco-friendly drilling fluids remain essential for the sustainable exploration and production of oil and gas.

Previous Research and Knowledge Gaps

Although numerous studies have investigated the use of polymers and nanomaterials in oilfield applications, most reviews address drilling fluids or EOR systems separately. There is a limited critical comparison of their performance under extreme reservoir conditions. Moreover, the integration of biodegradable and nanocomposite viscosifiers into unified frameworks has received relatively little attention. These gaps highlight the need for a consolidated and updated review that connects laboratory performance with real field applicability.

Source of Polymers

Polymers are macromolecules composed of repeating structural units known as monomers. They can form naturally through biological and environmental processes or synthetically via controlled chemical reactions in laboratories (Figure ). In some cases, natural polymers are chemically modified to enhance their properties, making them more suitable for industrial applications.

Classification of polymers by their source.

Polymers can be classified based on origin, chemical composition, and functional role in drilling operations. This section provides an overview of polymer types, emphasizing their advantages and limitations in viscosity control for drilling fluids.

Natural polymers have been widely utilized due to their unique properties across diverse industries. Starch, for instance, is a naturally occurring polymer of glucose monomers and is extensively employed as a viscosifier in sectors ranging from food processing to oil and gas operations. While natural polymers contribute to fluid viscosity, their native forms may not always provide the specific rheological characteristics required for optimal industrial performance. Chemical modification is often necessary to tailor their properties. A prominent example is carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), a chemically modified cellulose derivative that becomes water-soluble and exhibits enhanced rheological properties, making it suitable for applications requiring controlled viscosity and flow behavior.

Advances in polymer science have enabled the precise synthesis of polymers with tailored properties. SP production allows for extensive structural modifications to optimize the performance for specific applications. Certain biopolymers, such as xanthan gum and scleroglucan, adopt specialized helical conformationsdouble-stranded and triple-stranded helices, respectively. These molecular architectures impart semirigid rod behavior in solution, providing exceptional viscosifying capabilities, structural stability, and resistance to monovalent/divalent ions, thermal stress, and mechanical degradation. ,

Natural Polymers

Natural polymers are derived from biological sources, such as plants, bacteria, and marine organisms. Their biodegradability and nontoxicity make them suitable for industrial applications, including the optimization of drilling and fracturing fluids to enhance viscosity, suspension stability, and fluid loss control. However, extreme conditions, such as high salinity and temperature, can limit their performance, sometimes necessitating chemical modification or blending with SPs.

The following are examples of commonly used natural polymers in viscosity control applications:

Xanthan Gum

Xanthan gum is a naturally occurring polymer that is generated via fermentation by the bacterium Xanthomonas campestris (Figure ). It is commonly used in drilling fluids as a viscosifier and stabilizer to enhance fluid rheology and suspension properties. Xanthan gum helps maintain wellbore stability and prevent fluid loss during drilling operations.

Guar Gum

Guar gum is extracted from the seeds of the guar plant (Cyamopsis tetragonoloba) and is another natural polymer used in the oil industry (Figure ). It is often employed in hydraulic fracturing fluids to increase viscosity and carry proppants into fractures, aiding in the stimulation of oil and gas production from reservoirs.

Semi-Natural Polymers

Seminatural polymers originate from natural polymers but undergo chemical modifications to enhance their solubility, stability, and performance under extreme conditions. They serve as rheology modifiers, fluid loss control agents, and viscosifiers in drilling and EOR operations. The following are some key examples of seminatural polymers used in viscosity control applications:

Hydroxyethyl Cellulose (HEC)

HEC is a seminatural polymer derived from cellulose, which undergoes chemical modification to introduce hydroxyethyl groups (Figure ). In the oil industry, the HEC is used as a rheology modifier and fluid loss control agent in drilling fluids. It helps improve fluid viscosity and provides filtration control to prevent fluid loss in the formation.

Carboxymethyl Cellulose

CMC is another seminatural polymer derived from cellulose and chemically modified to introduce carboxymethyl groups (Figure ). It finds applications in drilling fluids as a viscosifier and fluid loss control agent, contributing to wellbore stability and filtration control during drilling operations.

Each polymer has specific benefits and limitations. For instance, starch is commonly used to control filtrate but suffers from limited thermal stability, whereas carboxymethyl starch (CMS) improves performance but offers lower filtration efficiency than polyanionic cellulose (PAC). , Xanthan gum provides high viscosity and suspension maintenance but degrades above 120 °C. HEC exhibits better thermal stability and is employed in high-temperature drilling, cementing, and hydraulic fracturing applications (Table ). Viscosity loss at elevated temperatures is associated with molecular weight, denaturation of helical structures, hydrolysis of glucosidic linkages, and degradation of side groups (Figure ).

Artificial (Synthetic) Polymers

SPs are engineered macromolecules designed for specific physical and chemical properties. They offer precise control over molecular weight, structural configuration, and functionalization, ensuring a high stability under harsh conditions. In the oil and gas sector, SPs enhance rheological properties, improve EOR efficiency, and reduce operational challenges such as friction and fluid instability. Several SPs have been extensively utilized in viscosity control applications, as presented below.

Polyacrylamide (PAM)

PAM is an SP made by polymerization of acrylamide (AM), and due to the chemical structure of AM (Figure ), it is widely used in EOR techniques such as polymer flooding. It is injected into reservoirs to increase the viscosity of injected water, improving sweep efficiency and displacing trapped oil toward production wells. PAM enhances oil recovery rates by maximizing fluid displacement within the reservoir.

Poly­(ethylene oxide)

Poly­(ethylene oxide) (PEO) is an SP made by ethylene oxide (EO) and employed in various oilfield applications, including hydraulic fracturing and drilling fluids. It serves as an enhancement of fluid flow properties and facilitates the transport of proppants during fracturing operations. PEO also aids in controlling fluid viscosity and reducing frictional losses in drilling fluids, improving overall drilling performance according to its structure (Figure ). PAM, is nonionic polymer, and it is not suitable for EOR due to high adsorption on mineral surfaces. Partially hydrolyzed PAM (HPAM), a copolymer of acrylamide and sodium acrylate, exhibits viscosity due to chain repulsion but remains sensitive to salinity and divalent cations (Figure ). −

Stability of Polymers

Developing high-performance polymers for EOR and drilling operations has been driven by the need to maintain polymer stability and functionality under increasingly demanding reservoir conditions. As EOR operations expand into high-temperature and high-salinity environments, conventional polymers, such as hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM), face limitations in thermal and chemical stability, which reduce both the efficiency and economic feasibility in oil extraction. To address these challenges, researchers have focused on incorporating specialized monomers into the HPAM structure. These modifications enhance the resistance to thermal degradation and improve tolerance to harsh chemical conditions. Among these, copolymers of AM and 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonate (AMPS) have shown promising results for high-temperature and high-salinity applications.

Early studies in 1987 by Moradi-Araghi and Doe demonstrated that introducing monomers such as N-vinylpyrrolidone (NVP) and AMPS (also known as acrylamido tertiary butyl sulfonate, ATBS) into HPAM significantly improved the thermal stability. Specifically, AM/AMPS copolymers with 30–70% AMPS by weight showed enhanced resistance to degradation, although AM content above 9% limited effective performance at temperatures above ∼93 °C. Subsequent research by Parker and Lezzi highlighted that while AMPS is generally more chemically stable than AM, hydrolysis occurs at temperatures exceeding 100 °C, indicating constraints under extreme thermal conditions.

In 2008, Levitt and Pope investigated the long-term stability of an AM/AMPS copolymer with a 75/25 weight ratio at 85 °C. They reported no hydrolysis of AMPS over extended aging periods. Additionally, these copolymers exhibited improved tolerance to calcium ions compared to other AM-based polymers, making them particularly suitable for brines with high calcium content.

More recent studies have assessed the AM/AMPS stability under increasingly severe conditions. For example, Dupuis et al. evaluated high-AMPS copolymers in brines with salinities from 41,000 to 230,000 ppm at 120 and 140 °C. Surprisingly, higher salinity enhanced thermal stability, with the highest AMPS-content polymer retaining full viscosity at 120 °C and 90% at 140 °C after one year. Alfazazi et al. examined copolymers SAV10, SAV28, and SAV37 in formation water with 167,000 ppm salinity at 120 °C. SAV10 retained over 93% of its initial viscosity over 100 days, whereas SAV28 and SAV37 lost about 40% within 40 days.

Recent work by Seright et al. evaluated SAV10 aged at 120, 140, and 160 °C in brines with 69,000–244,000 ppm salinity, confirming its stability under extreme EOR conditions. Similarly, Masalmeh et al. studied AM/AMPS performance in Middle Eastern brine (180,000 ppm salinity, 18,000 ppm divalent cations) and found only a 15% viscosity loss at 130 °C over six months, with no significant loss at 120 °C over a year.

Extensive investigations by Swiecinski et al. (2016) and Sandengen et al. (2018) focused on polymer aging and hydrolysis kinetics under varied temperatures (60–140 °C), polymer concentrations (0.25–11 wt %), salinity (0–130 g/L), and AMPS content (up to 80 mol %). These studies used Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) to track polymer composition changes and correlate them with viscosity loss. They also examined pH buffering and CO2 effects, providing critical insights into optimizing the polymer composition for maximum stability and viscosity retention. This knowledge is crucial for EOR applications in high-temperature, high-salinity reservoirs.

Experimental Studies

This section reviews studies on polymers capable of modifying and controlling viscosity in different sectors of the oil industry, including well drilling and EOR. The focus is on the utilization of these polymers to improve the process efficiency, optimize fluid behavior, and enhance oil production under varying reservoir conditions.

Drilling Fluid Viscosifiers

Polymer materials play a pivotal role in drilling fluid engineering, primarily used to modify and optimize the rheological properties of drilling fluids. Among these, both natural and synthetic viscosifiers are critical for enhancing fluid performance. Viscosifiers increase the viscosity of drilling fluids, which is essential for effective cutting transport, wellbore stabilization, and minimizing fluid loss during drilling operations. Natural polymers such as guar gum, xanthan gum, and cellulose derivatives are widely utilized due to their ability to enhance viscosity while remaining environmentally friendly. However, these natural polymers often lose effectiveness under high-temperature or high-salinity conditions due to thermal and ionic degradation. To overcome these limitations, synthetic viscosifiers, such as PHPA and PAC, have been developed. These SPs demonstrate superior stability under extreme temperatures and salinities, making them particularly suitable for deep or offshore drilling operations.

In 1986, researchers investigated polystyrene-based viscosity controllers, introducing sulfate groups to improve their effectiveness in oil-based fluids at high temperatures. Subsequent concerns regarding the environmental impact of oil-based fluids led researchers to focus on water-based systems. In 1994, Hourdet et al. developed water-soluble acrylic viscosity controllers based on poly­(acrylic acid), producing additives with improved high-temperature stability compared to natural polymers.

Further advancements include the work of Zhong et al., who combined acrylamide with vinyl naphthalene to produce a viscosity controller stable at 80 °C for 90 days. Meanwhile, emphasized the ongoing relevance of oil-based fluids and, in 2015, investigated an environmentally friendly polymer (KLD-1) for these fluids, evaluating its effect on temperature stability. Xie et al. (2016) explored the use of nanoparticles alongside polymers with high-temperature resistance, introducing a copolymer based on acrylamide sulfonate and vinyl caprolactam, cross-linked with divinylbenzene. This copolymer demonstrated excellent rheological properties and shear resistance at 230 °C for 16 h, comparable to xanthan gum.

It is important to note that drilling fluid performance is influenced not only by high temperatures but also by low temperatures and sudden increases in viscosity. In 2017, Zhou et al. controlled the kinetic properties of aqueous-based fluids at low temperatures by adjusting polymer molecular weight, finding that cellulose-based additives performed optimally between 4 and 75 °C.

High-temperature stability remains a critical parameter for drilling fluid additives. Xie et al. (2017) studied temperature-sensitive polymers, focusing on copolymers of sodium 2-acrylamide-AMPS and normal vinyl caprolactam (NVC) with varying NVC content. Copolymers with 40–70% NVC exhibited inverse temperature sensitivity, where viscosity increased with temperature, while compositions outside this range behaved conventionally, with viscosity decreasing as temperature rose.

The past two decades have seen intense research into polymers suitable for deep drilling, where extreme temperatures prevail. Liu and co-workers synthesized a high molecular weight AMPSA-based polymer (∼8 million Da), stable for up to 72 h at 200 °C, significantly enhancing thermal endurance. Xie et al. (2018) further explored polysodium 2-acrylamide-2-methylpropanesulfonate (PAMPS) as a bentonite substitute in water-based fluids, achieving thermal stability up to 140 °C and improved viscosity control in saline environments. These modifications enhanced the wellbore cleaning efficiency and minimized fluid loss.

In 2019, Chu and Lin synthesized a copolymer containing acrylamide and diallyldimethylammonium chloride, demonstrating stability from 60 to 180 °C over 16 h. Following this, developed an amphoteric polymer combined with sodium ethylene diamine tetraacetate, achieving thermal resistance up to 180 °C, outperforming conventional additives.

Acrylamide-based polymers, particularly PHPA, remain the most widely used due to their availability and cost-effectiveness. , The high molecular weight and electrostatic repulsion from carboxylate groups enhance viscosity through increased hydrodynamic volume. ,, However, PHPA faces challenges under high salinity, temperature, and in the presence of divalent cations, which can precipitate the polymer. ,,

Hydrophobically modified associative polymers (HMAPs), thermo-viscosifying polymers (TVPs), and nanoparticles have been investigated to overcome these limitations. − HMAPs incorporate small hydrophobic segments (<5% w/v) into water-soluble polymers, forming microdomains above the critical association concentration (CAC), which substantially increase viscosity. ,,− TVPs exploit low critical solution temperature (LCST) blocks that self-assemble above an association temperature (Tass), enhancing viscosity. ,,,

PHPA viscosity depends on molecular weight and degree of hydrolysis (typically 10–35%), as higher hydrolysis increases carboxylate groups and electrostatic repulsion, expanding hydrodynamic volume. − High hydrolysis, however, increases the salinity sensitivity, especially with Mg2 + and Ca2 +, leading to precipitation. Thermal hydrolysis at elevated temperatures further exacerbates this effect. ,,

Xanthan gum, a high molecular weight polysaccharide from Xanthomonas campestris, exhibits excellent thermal stability, low salinity sensitivity, and superior shear strength compared to PAMs. , Yet, it is prone to microbial degradation, costly to produce, and can block rock pores due to cellular debris, limiting its field applicability. ,,

In 2009, a hydrophobically modified acrylamide-based terpolymer was synthesized, incorporating sodium 2-acrylamide-2-methylpropanesulfonate (NaAMPS) and 2-vinyl naphthalene (VN). The stability of the polymer solution was evaluated over a 90-day period at 60 °C in a brine solution containing sodium chloride (NaCl). The results demonstrated that the hydrophobically modified acrylamide polymer (HMAP) retained 88.3% of its initial viscosity, indicating substantial stability under these conditions. Similarly, terpolymers comprising varying ratios of acrylamide (AM), 2-trimethylammonium ethyl methacrylate chloride (TMAEMC), and 5,5,5-triphenyl-1-pentene (TP) were developed and investigated. The findings highlighted that the solution viscosity was strongly influenced by the content of hydrophobic aromatic groups (TP) within the polymer chains as well as the overall polymer concentration.

In a comparative study, the performance of PHPA was evaluated against a terpolymer composed of AM, acrylic acid (AA), and N,N-divinylnonadeca-0-dien-2-amine (DNDA). This terpolymer exhibited superior resistance to both salinity and temperature fluctuations, along with remarkable shear strength, suggesting its suitability for demanding operational conditions. Further investigations examined a hydrophobically modified PAM integrated with cyclodextrin (HCMPAM), assessing its stability under extreme salinity and temperature in comparison with a PHPA solution of similar molecular weight. HCMPAM demonstrated substantially enhanced stability, retaining 45.7% of its apparent viscosity at a shear rate of 10 s– 1 when the temperature increased from ambient to 120 °C, whereas PHPA retained only 8.9% under the same conditions.

El-Hoshoudy et al. additionally explored the effects of concentration, salinity tolerance, and temperature resistance of a hydrophobically modified acrylamide polymer (HAPAM) relative to PHPA. The optimal polymer concentration was determined to be 2000 ppm, and HAPAM exhibited excellent temperature resistance and salinity tolerance, outperforming PHPA across all tested parameters. Collectively, these studies highlight significant advancements in polymer chemistry aimed at enhancing the performance of drilling fluids in challenging environments.

TVPs have emerged as effective alternatives for improving polymer flooding efficiency in reservoirs subjected to high salinity and elevated temperatures. These polymers are typically prepared via chemical modifications that introduce hydrophobic thermosensitive segments or grafts with a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) into water-soluble polymer backbones. The primary thermosensitive components include copolymers of poly­(ethylene oxide) and poly­(propylene oxide) (PEO–PPO), poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM), and macromonomers derived from acrylamide diacetone (MPAD), among others. ,,,− At ambient temperature, these polymers behave as conventional water-soluble macromolecules; however, upon exceeding the critical association temperature (T_ass) or LCST, the thermosensitive sequences undergo reversible self-aggregation through physical interactions. The field of polymer chemistry has made substantial progress in developing advanced materials to enhance the performance of drilling fluids in the oil and gas industry. , One of the pioneering achievements in this area was the development of the first TVP specifically designed for oilfield applications. This polymer was synthesized by incorporating thermosensitive sequences derived from PEO modified with poly­(acrylic acid) amine groups, providing an early strategy to improve drilling fluid performance under varying temperature conditions. Building upon this work, a thermo-viscosifying system was later created via free radical copolymerization of AM with the macromonomer MPAD, yielding a water-soluble copolymer, P­(AM-co-MPAD), which demonstrated promising viscosity responses to thermal changes.

In a subsequent study, a terpolymer, P­(AM-AA-MPAD), exhibiting thermo-viscosifying behavior, was compared with PHPA and a sulfonated tert-butyl acrylamide–acrylamide copolymer, P­(AM-co-ATBS). The investigation focused on the effect of salinity on the rheological properties of these polymers in deionized water, synthetic seawater (TDS = 57,643 ppm), and formation brine (TDS = 213,734 ppm). While the TVP initially exhibited lower viscosity due to its reduced molecular weight, its viscosity increased significantly with rising temperature as microdomain formation occurred. In contrast, the viscosities of the PHPA and P­(AM-co-ATBS) solutions declined under elevated temperatures.

Further comparative studies examined an AM–ATBS copolymer, commercially known as Flopaam AN132SH, against another thermosensitive polymer, DP/TLB132. The results confirmed that TVP offered superior viscosifying performance relative to the commercial copolymer. Similarly, Akbari et al. (2017a,b) evaluated four acrylamide-based copolymers containing AMPS groups, including one with a thermosensitive side chain. Their findings indicated that the thermosensitive polymer displayed enhanced rheological properties, highlighting its potential as a viscosifying agent for EOR applications. Collectively, these studies underscore the continuous advancements in polymer chemistry aimed at producing specialized materials to improve operational efficiency in oil and gas extraction. ,

Wang et al. conducted a comparative study between a copolymer of AM, sodium acrylate (NaAA), and acrylic acid (PAMA) and three TVPs formed by combining PAMA with Pluronic triblock copolymers (PEOn–PPOm–PEOn): TVP-P1 (PAMA + PEO1 0 0–PPO6 5–PEO1 0 0), TVP-P2 (PAMA + PEO1 3 2–PPO5 0–PEO1 3 2), and TVP-P3 (PAMA + PEO7 6–PPO2 9–PEO7 6). Rheological analysis showed that TVP-P1 exhibited the highest viscosity within the temperature range of 25 to 95 °C, attributed to the greater proportion of PPO groups, which enhance physical interactions above the apparent softening temperature (T_ass). Moreover, the study highlighted that the chemical structure and the content of thermosensitive groups in the polymer chain critically influenced the rheological behavior of TVPs.

EOR Viscosifiers

Global oil demand is rising, and projections suggest that it will remain the primary source of energy for at least the next two decades. Meeting this ever-increasing energy demand requires maximizing oil recovery from existing reservoirs while simultaneously exploring new fields. Research shows that approximately 70% of the oil remains in a reservoir when only conventional recovery methods are applied. , Consequently, as hydrocarbon extraction intensifies, EOR techniques are gaining critical importance.

EOR methods are typically classified into four main categories: chemical flooding, thermal recovery, miscible flooding, and microbial techniques. Among these, chemically EOR (CEOR) is a widely applied strategy across various oil fields. Globally, CEOR accounts for roughly 11% of EOR projects, of which over 77% involve polymer flooding and 23% employ polymer–surfactant combinations.

Polymers improve the viscosity of the displacing fluid and reduce the water-to-oil mobility ratio, enhancing macroscopic displacement efficiency. Polymer flooding has been deployed in the field for approximately four decades, typically achieving ultimate recovery rates of 50%, with an incremental recovery of 15–20% over conventional water flooding. Additionally, polymer flooding can reduce water cut by up to 50% and improve the resistance factor by 5–15%. Standnes and Skjevrak (2014) reported over 40 successful polymer flooding projects worldwide, primarily in the United States, Canada, and China, some dating back to the 1960s. Most applications occur in sandstone reservoirs with temperatures below 60 °C, low formation salinity (<100,000 ppm), and high permeability (>200 mD).

Large-scale polymer floods are ongoing in the Daqing oil field in China, Pelican Lake and Cactus Lake in Canada, Patos Marinza in Albania, Diadema in Argentina, Marmul in Oman, and Mangala in India. Among these methods, viscosifiers play a central role in optimizing fluid flow and enhancing the sweep efficiency within reservoirs. Viscosifiers modify the viscosity of injected fluids, improving their ability to displace trapped oil from porous formations. These agents can be classified according to their chemical composition, properties, and mechanisms of action. Polymer flooding utilizes natural, seminatural, and SPs to enhance the rheological properties of injected solutions, but each type exhibits limitations under the harsh conditions encountered in certain reservoirs.

In recent years, research has focused on extending the polymer flooding success to challenging reservoir conditions. Sps such as HPAM and its copolymers are widely used due to their tunable rheological properties, availability, and cost-effectiveness. They perform well in high-temperature, low-permeability reservoirs but are sensitive to high salinity and divalent cations. In contrast, natural and seminatural polymers, including xanthan gum, scleroglucan, and guar gum, are more robust in saline and hard brines due to their conformational stability and reduced susceptibility to ionic degradation. Abdelrahman et al. demonstrated that biopolymer–nanocomposite systems maintained viscoelasticity under 120 °C and 180,000 ppm salinity, outperforming conventional HPAM blends. Similarly, Gaillard et al. reported that ATBS-NVP-based terpolymers retained over 85% of initial viscosity after one year in reservoir-like brines, confirming long-term field stability. These studies highlight the importance of selecting polymers tailored to specific reservoir conditions and advancing next-generation EOR systems.

High temperature and low permeability remain the main challenges for biopolymers, whereas high salinity and hardness are critical for SPs. HPAM dominates as the most widely used SP in EOR, accounting for more than 90% of polymer-flooding field projects. Its accessibility, cost-efficiency, and ease of on-site production have made it a primary focus of EOR research. One of the earliest polymer pilots was conducted in 1969 in Northeast Hallsville, Crane, Texas, in an oolitic limestone reservoir at 109 °C using standard HPAM, achieving remarkable success and leading to field-wide implementation.

Other notable polymer flood cases include West Yellow Creek, Mississippi, , with a relatively low-permeability reservoir at 66 °C and 133 g/L water salinity; Hitts Lake Unit, Texas, in a sandstone reservoir at 99 °C with formation water salinity of 105 g/L; Oerrel West Block; Hankensbuettel, Westblock 2, Germany; , Oerrel Middle Block, Germany; , Adorf, Germany; Sanand, India; − and Mangala, India. , These cases demonstrate the adaptability of HPAM and strategies such as preflush in fresh water to mitigate salinity challenges, successfully enhancing oil recovery across diverse reservoir conditions.

Further innovations include sulfonated PAMs with increased thermal stability. For instance, Wang and co-workers synthesized a terpolymer via micellar polymerization of n-dodecyl acrylamide, acrylamide, and 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid, reporting a stable viscosity of 18.7 cP for 30 days at 85 °C and 32 g/L water salinity. Laboratory studies have further confirmed the polymer efficacy under controlled reservoir conditions. Jung et al. (2013) demonstrated that semihydrolyzed PAM increased oil recovery by 22–31% over water flooding in a glass bead core system at 450 cP heavy oil. Xu et al. (2014) found that valan gum exhibited superior displacement efficiency compared to xanthan gum due to its aggregate formation and higher storage and loss moduli, resulting in 7.3% higher oil recovery than xanthan and 25.4% more than water injection. Zhou et al. (2014) showed that incorporating silica nanoparticles enhanced the viscosity and thermal stability of PAM solutions under high temperature and salinity. Jang et al. (2015) confirmed that xanthan gum outperformed PAM in brines at 30 g/L concentration, increasing oil recovery from heavy oil by 30% versus 22.9% for PAM. Quadri et al. (2015) reported that the natural polymer schizophyllan maintained stable viscosity at 135 °C and 220 g/L salinity in carbonate cores, demonstrating robust performance under extreme conditions.

Functional Evaluation (Rheological and EOR Tests)

A comprehensive evaluation of polymeric viscosifiers is essential to ensure their effectiveness in oilfield operations, particularly in drilling and EOR applications. Due to the complex and variable conditions encountered in reservoirs (including fluctuations in temperature, pressure, salinity, and permeability), polymers must undergo rigorous characterization to determine their rheological behavior and functional performance under simulated field conditions. Standardized testing ensures reproducibility and reliability, providing a basis for meaningful comparison across different polymer types. Although specific protocols may vary among companies, several fundamental assessments are widely recognized as critical for evaluating polymer efficiency in oilfield applications.1. Polymer solution preparation: Proper hydration, dissolution, and dispersion of polymer molecules in base fluids are crucial for achieving uniform viscosity and optimal molecular interactions. Inadequate solubility or incomplete dispersion can lead to nonhomogeneous fluids, reduced performance, and inaccurate evaluation of rheological properties.2. Rheological properties at ambient conditions: Initial characterization of viscosity, shear-thinning behavior, and viscoelasticity under laboratory conditions provides a baseline understanding of polymer-fluid interactions. These measurements reveal how polymers influence flow behavior, suspension stability, and resistance to shear forces, which are critical for handling and injection processes.3. Rheological behavior under high-temperature and high-pressure (HTHP) conditions: Polymers intended for deep reservoirs or high-temperature applications must maintain structural integrity under extreme conditions. Beyond measuring steady shear viscosity, oscillatory shear tests are employed to probe viscoelastic properties. The storage modulus (G′) indicates the elastic energy stored in the fluid, while the loss modulus (G″) represents viscous energy dissipation. The ratio of G″/G′ and the complex viscosity (η*) are key indicators of mechanical stability under shear. Polymers with a dominant elastic response (G′ > G″) exhibit enhanced conformance control and reduced degradation during injection, improving displacement efficiency in porous formations.4. Filtration control: Polymers are evaluated for their ability to reduce fluid loss by forming thin, impermeable filter cakes on the rock surface. Effective filtration control is critical in drilling and completion fluids to prevent formation damage and maintain wellbore stability.5. Mobility and flow behavior in reservoirs: The capacity of polymer solutions to modify mobility ratios and improve sweep efficiency is central to EOR applications. By increasing the viscosity of injected water or other displacement fluids, polymers enhance oil recovery and suppress viscous fingering, thereby optimizing overall displacement efficiency.

The storage modulus (G′) indicates the elastic energy stored in the fluid, while the loss modulus (G″) represents viscous energy dissipation. The ratio of G″/G′ and the complex viscosity (η*) are key indicators of mechanical stability under shear. Polymers with a dominant elastic response (G′ > G″) exhibit enhanced conformance control and reduced degradation during injection, improving displacement efficiency in porous formations.

In parallel, numerical simulations play a pivotal role in predicting polymer behavior in reservoirs and optimizing injection strategies. Advanced simulators such as CMG-STARS, CMG-GEM, Schlumberger Eclipse, and Navigator incorporate detailed transport models that account for polymer adsorption, shear degradation, and viscosity variations under different temperature, pressure, and salinity conditions. Calibration of simulation results against core flooding experiments ensures accuracy, enabling reliable forecasts of field-scale performance, optimal slug sizes, and injection rates.

Laboratory core flooding experiments involve the controlled injection of polymer solutions into cylindrical core samples under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions. The core is prepared through cleaning, drying, brine saturation, and crude oil injection, followed by aging to stabilize the fluid-rock interactions. Water flooding establishes a baseline recovery, after which polymer injection displaces residual oil. Effluent analysis provides data on oil, water, and polymer concentrations, while key parameters, such as pressure drop, resistance factor, and residual oil saturation, are measured to evaluate polymer performance.

Through these systematic evaluations, the performance of polymeric viscosifiers can be accurately quantified, guiding their selection, formulation, and deployment in drilling and EOR applications. Such a comprehensive assessment ensures enhanced operational efficiency, reduced formation damage, and improved sustainability of oilfield processes.

Conclusion

The significance of viscosifying polymers in drilling fluids and polymer-based EOR processes is well established, as these materials directly impact the operational efficiency and reservoir performance. In recent years, alongside advances in polymer synthesis and modification, there has been a growing interest in integrating nanoparticles into polymer systems. Due to their nanoscale size and unique surface properties, these additives can enhance the viscosity, thermal stability, and salt resistance of polymer-based fluids.

This review systematically compared studies on polymer additives in drilling fluids, polymer-assisted EOR, and nanoparticle systems to highlight similarities, differences, and performance trends. PAM-based polymers and their copolymers remain widely used; however, many exhibit limitations, such as viscosity loss, chemical instability, or undesirable gelation under certain conditions. Evidence from laboratory and field studies suggests that carefully designed SPs (especially copolymers and polymers reinforced with nanoparticles) can provide improved performance under challenging reservoir conditions.

Importantly, the choice of polymer or nanoparticle additive should consider the specific temperature and salinity of the reservoir. Based on the comparative tables, natural and semisynthetic polymers such as xanthan gum and guar derivatives show favorable performance at moderate temperatures (80–90 °C), maintaining sufficient viscosity and rheological stability. In contrast, SPs, particularly PAM-based copolymers and modified PAMs, are more suitable for higher-temperature applications (>100 °C), where enhanced thermal stability and salt tolerance are critical. Similarly, certain nanoparticles­(such as silica and metal oxide nanoparticles) can further improve thermal resilience and maintain viscosity under both moderate and high-temperature conditions.

Overall, the effectiveness of polymeric and nanoparticle-based additives is highly dependent on reservoir-specific conditions. Rigorous laboratory testing, including rheological measurements, filtration control, and core flooding experiments, is essential for validating the performance and guiding the optimal selection and formulation of these materials. Developing cost-effective, thermally stable, and chemically robust polymer–nanoparticle systems remains a key research focus, with the potential to significantly improve drilling and EOR efficiency.

Looking ahead, continued research should focus on designing polymers and nanoparticle-reinforced systems with stronger chemical structures, higher resistance to thermal and salinity stresses, and lower production costs. Field-scale validation of laboratory findings is crucial to ensuring practical applicability. Furthermore, exploring novel copolymers, hybrid polymer–nanoparticle formulations, and environmentally friendly viscosifying agents can provide sustainable solutions for challenging reservoir conditions and expand the toolkit available for efficient drilling and EOR operations.
