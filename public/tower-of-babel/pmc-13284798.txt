Transitions to and reversion from a persistent frequent-exacerbator state in COPD
Authors: Delphine Vauterin, Frauke Van Vaerenbergh, Lowie E.G.W. Vanfleteren, Lies Lahousse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreventing acute exacerbations in COPD (AECOPDs) is key in striving for disease stability. To investigate the differential impact of risk factors across AECOPD phenotypes, we identified baseline health determinants and comorbidities associated with transitions from non-exacerbator (NE) to persistent frequent-exacerbator (FE) and reversion.MethodsPatients ≥45 years with ≥3 months treatment for obstructive airway diseases or hospital-labelled COPD without (concomitant) asthma were identified in Belgian nationwide data between January 2017–February 2018. Factors associated with disease worsening (transitioning from NE to persistent FE), reversion (from FE to stable NE) or transition to death were investigated using multinomial logistic regression.ResultsAmong 183 762 patients (mean age 68.6 years, 48.0% female), 11.5% (21 072) never experienced AECOPDs and 11.6% (21 375) exacerbated frequently in each of three consecutive years. Among 56 933 NE at baseline (31.0%), 4.1% transitioned to persistent FE, whereas among 80 502 FE (43.8%), 7.3% reversed to stable NE. Transitions from NE towards persistent FE were associated with having lung cancer (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 3.67, 95% CI 2.33–5.78), being an ever-smoker (aOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.94–2.43) or having neuropsychiatric or musculoskeletal comorbidity. Overuse of short-acting bronchodilators (aOR 0.57, 95% CI 0.46–0.49), ever-smoking or having overuse of maintenance therapy were factors most strongly associated with lower odds of reversion. Cardiovascular comorbidities were significantly associated with increased mortality odds, but not with disease worsening.ConclusionsThe results of this cohort study support addressing smoking and inhaler overuse to promote reversion to stable NE, while managing lung cancer and neuropsychiatric or musculoskeletal comorbidities to reduce worsening.

Background

COPD is characterised by episodic symptom flare-ups, known as acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPDs) [1]. AECOPDs add to the unstable nature of COPD and severely impact patients’ quality of life [2]. Key strategies in striving for disease stability include patient education, medication adherence, smoking cessation, pulmonary rehabilitation and vaccination, all aimed at reducing symptom burden, stabilising lung function and preventing AECOPDs [2, 3]. Prevention and management of AECOPD are crucial to improving patient outcomes, reducing healthcare costs and limiting disease progression [1, 4].

Phenotyping may support a personalised approach in COPD management [5, 6]. A previously used phenotyping method is based on AECOPDs history in the previous year, typically classifying patients as non-exacerbators (NEs), infrequent-exacerbators (IEs) or frequent-exacerbators (FEs) (two or more AECOPDs in the past 12 months) [6, 7]. The FE phenotype is characterised by a rapid decline in lung function, increased respiratory symptoms, more limitations in daily activities and poorer health outcomes [7–9].

Numerous risk factors for AECOPDs have been identified, including comorbidities, higher symptom burden, and particularly, a history of AECOPDs, which is consistently acknowledged as the best predictor for future events [10]. However, while these risk factors are well established, it remains unclear what drives the transition between frequent and stable NEs. Only a few studies have examined characteristics associated with phenotype transitions, but these were limited by small sample sizes or focused exclusively on severe AECOPDs [11–13].

Therefore, we used a Belgian nationwide cohort of respiratory symptomatic patients, to identify baseline factors and assess their impact on associations with transitions between AECOPD phenotypes. Our aim was to improve understanding of the temporal variability of COPD and provide insights into phenotype-specific transitions, which could potentially inform clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective register-based study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [14] and is detailed in the supplementary materials. In short, prevalent patients aged ≥45 years with ≥1 year coverage by a Belgian health insurance fund, were identified from our Belgian nationwide source population [15] as all patients with at least 3 months of either treatment with obstructive airway diseases medication (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification R03) or a hospital diagnosis of COPD (ICD-10 J44) before the index date. The index date was defined as the second ambulatory ATC R03 dispensing in one year during the inclusion period (earliest index date 1 January 2017, latest date 28 February 2018). The end of the inclusion period was set to allow ≥2 years of follow-up prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. Follow-up ended in case of death or end of the study period (2 years after index date), whichever came first.

Patients with missing data in key variables (age and sex), emigration out of Belgium during the study period, using only magistral preparations, individuals with ≥1 year of follow-up but no further use of ATC R03 medication after the index date, as well as those with asthma (ICD-10 J45 or inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) monotherapy, biologics, cromoglicic acid or leukotriene receptor antagonists use in the year prior to follow-up) were excluded (supplementary figure S1). This study was approved by the InterMutualistic Agency (IMA) [16] and Minimal Hospital Dataset (MHD) [17] administrators and by the “Social Security and Health Chamber of the Belgian Information Security Committee” (approval code IVC/KSZG/23/008), waiving the need for individual informed consents [18].

Outcomes

All-cause mortality and AECOPDs were assessed during the year before the index date (baseline year) and in two subsequent follow-up years. Severe AECOPDs were defined as emergency department visits and/or hospital admissions with a primary diagnosis of AECOPD (ICD-10: J44.0, J44.1) or with a primary diagnosis code for chronic lower respiratory disease (ICD-10: J40–J47) or respiratory failure (ICD-10: J96) and a secondary diagnosis code for AECOPD. Hospitalisations with a concomitant pneumonia diagnosis were excluded. Moderate AECOPDs were defined as an outpatient prescription fill for an oral corticosteroid (ATC code H02AB04, except parenteral use) and/or guideline-recommended antibiotics (AB) (ATC code J01CA (penicillins with extended spectrum), J01CR (combinations of penicillins, including β-lactamase inhibitors), J01DC (second-generation cephalosporins), J01FA (macrolides, except continuous azithromycin therapy) and J01MA (fluoroquinolones) [19]. Prescription fills needed to be separated by at least 14 days to be considered as separate events. Moderate AECOPDs followed by a hospital admission for a severe AECOPD within 14 days were counted only as severe AECOPD. Patients were categorised based on the frequency of AECOPDs in their baseline year in three distinct phenotypes (e.g. NEs were individuals without AECOPD; IEs were individuals with only one moderate AECOPD and FEs were individuals with ≥2 moderate AECOPDs and/or ≥1 severe AECOPD), in line with previous research [6, 7].

Covariables

Factors potentially influencing transitions between AECOPD phenotypes were selected based on previous literature [10, 13, 20–22] and clinical interest, and are detailed in the supplementary materials. Demographic factors included age, sex and socioeconomic status (SES) at index date. Health determinants were determined in the baseline year and included obesity/overweight, history of smoking (being an ever-smoker if an ICD code related to (a history of) tobacco use or a nomenclature code for smoking cessation counselling or medication for smoking cessation, was registered between 1 January 2010 and the index date), vaccination (limited to influenza vaccination as pneumococcal vaccination was not reimbursed in Belgium during the study period) and disease control (overuse of short-acting bronchodilators (SABDs), defined as ≥3 canisters per year in the outpatient setting in line with previous research [20], and overuse of maintenance therapy (long-acting β2-agonists (LABAs) or long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs), LABA/LAMA, ICS/LABA, ICS/LABA/LAMA or xanthines (theophylline)) defined as a medication possession ratio (MPR) >1.20. The method to calculate MPR is detailed in the supplementary materials. Comorbidities were ascertained in the baseline year using ICD-coded diagnoses, medical procedure codes and/or ATC-coded medication dispensing claims and patients were (non-exclusively) categorised into six distinct groups [13, 21, 22]: upper airway (obstructive sleep apnoea), cardiovascular (including cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease and pulmonary heart disease), neuropsychiatric (including anxiety and depression), endocrine, nutritional and metabolic (including diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and stomach/duodenal ulcer), musculoskeletal (including cachexia, chronic kidney disease and osteoporosis) and lung cancer (supplementary table S2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised using mean±sd or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate; categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. We compared baseline characteristics across the three mutually exclusive baseline AECOPD phenotypes (NE, IE and FE). Standardised mean differences (SMDs) <0.2 were considered minimal. Consistent phenotype stability was defined over the 3-year observation window, comprising the baseline year and two subsequent follow-up years (figure 1). Consistently stable NE patients did not experience any AECOPD during any of the 3 years, whereas consistently persistent FE patients experienced ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe AECOPDs in each of the 3 years.

Visualisation of study design. ATC: Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification; NE: non-exacerbator; FE: frequent-exacerbator: AECOPD: acute exacerbation of COPD; IE: infrequent-exacerbator. #: factors are assessed at index date and/or baseline year.

Next to stability, transitions between AECOPD phenotypes or towards death were evaluated (figure 1). Patients were classified as having transitioned to stable NE if they had no AECOPD during both follow-up years (reversion to stable NE), and to persistent FE if they experienced ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe AECOPDs in each of the two follow-up years (disease worsening). All other patients, who did not transition to stable NE, persistent FE or death, were categorised as transitioning to IE.

Multinomial logistic regression models were used to assess associations between baseline characteristics and subsequent transitions. These models estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs), with 95% CIs, comparing the likelihood of transitioning to a different phenotype or death versus maintaining the same AECOPD phenotype as observed at baseline. Previously described baseline health determinants and comorbidity groups were explored in a minimally adjusted model (age, sex, medical coverage and a history of smoking) and subsequently incorporated in a fully adjusted model. To reduce misclassification bias, a sensitivity analysis was performed investigating only subjects with hospital-diagnosed COPD (ICD-10: J44) prior to the index date. To assess sensitivity to unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for aORs. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in R Studio (R v.4.3.0, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Overall, 183 762 patients were included in this study (supplementary figure S1), of which 56 933 patients (31.0%) were NE in the baseline year, 46 327 (25.2%) were IE and 80 502 patients (43.8%) were FE. The mean age, as well as the proportion of female patients, patients with low SES, overweight/obesity, SABD overuse, ever-smoking and the prevalence of the comorbidities, increased from NE to IE to FE (table 1).

Stability of, and transitions between, AECOPD phenotypes

During a follow-up of 334 571 persons-years, 137 754 patients (75.0%) had at least one moderate AECOPD and 11 263 patients (6.1%) had at least one severe AECOPD, of which the majority (90.2%) also experienced ≥1 moderate AECOPD. In contrast, 44 909 patients (24.4%) did not exacerbate during follow-up (figure 2 and supplementary table S3). In general, 11.5% (21 072) patients never exacerbated in either their baseline year or during follow-up and were consequently labelled as consistently stable NE. Moreover, 11.6% (21 375) patients exacerbated frequently in each of the three study years and were consequently identified as consistently persistent FE. Approximately 28 847 patients (15.7%) died within two years, of which most were characterised by a FE phenotype at baseline (60.8%). Figure 3 visualises the proportion of patients who transitioned to persistent FE, who regained a stable NE and who died. More than one in three NE patients at baseline (37%; 21 072 of 56 933) reached the consistently stable NE state, whereas 4% (2332 of 56 933) transitioned to persistent FE. While 7% of the FE phenotype at baseline (5848 of 80 502) changed to a 2-year stable NE phenotype, more than one in five FE patients (17 548 of 80 502; 22%) died within 2 years.

Association of demographics and health determinants with transitions

In general, older age, being an ever-smoker and having lower SES were associated with transition to persistent FE, death and lower odds of reversion (tables 2 and 3). Female sex was associated with lower odds of death; however, a decreased likelihood to reverse to a stable NE was observed. Influenza vaccination was significantly associated with disease worsening and lower odds of reversion to stable NE, but also with lower odds of transitioning towards death when having exacerbations at baseline. SABD overuse increased odds of transitioning towards the persistent FE phenotype (NE>persistent FE: aOR 1.41, 95% CI 1.28–1.56; IE>persistent FE: aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.33–1.54) while halving odds for reversion to stable NE (FE>stable NE: aOR 0.46, 95% CI 0.43–0.49). Furthermore, SABD overuse was one of the three factors most strongly associated with transitions to death across all phenotypes (NE>death: aOR 2.20, 95% CI 2.04–2.37; IE>death: aOR 2.13, 95% CI 1.99–2.28; FE>death: aOR 1.48, 95% CI 1.42–1.55). Overuse of maintenance therapy compared with having no overuse, was significantly associated with death from NE or IE (NE>death: aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.17–1.66; IE>death: aOR 1.56, 95% CI 1.33–1.84), and with lower reversion from FE (FE>stable NE: aOR 0.65, 95% CI 0.54–0.77) (tables 2 and 3).

Association of comorbidities with transitions

Having lung cancer was the factor most strongly associated with rapid disease worsening (NE>persistent FE: aOR 3.67, 95% CI 2.33–5.78) and with transitioning to death. Musculoskeletal comorbidity significantly increased likelihood of transitioning to persistent FE and to death from any state and was strongly associated with a decreased odds of reversion among FE patients (FE>stable NE: aOR 0.73, 95% CI 0.66–0.81).

Having a neuropsychiatric comorbidity was associated with increased odds of transitioning to persistent FE and to death, and consistently associated with decreased odds of reversion. Having obstructive sleep apnoea was associated with increased odds of gradual disease worsening to persistent FE (IE>persistent FE: aOR 1.46, 95% CI 1.18–1.80), and it increased the likelihood of death among those with exacerbations. Among NE patients, having an endocrine disorder significantly increased the odds of transitioning towards persistent FE (NE>persistent FE: aOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.23), whereas in general it reduced mortality odds. Cardiovascular comorbidities were significantly associated with an increased risk of death, but not with disease worsening (tables 2 and 3). Observed associations between health determinants and comorbidities with transition to IE were generally similar, although less pronounced compared with those for transitions to persistent FE or reversion to stable NE (supplementary table S4).

Additional analyses

In analyses restricting the study population to patients with hospital-diagnosed COPD (n=28 466), transition from NE to persistent FE was slightly more common (6.9% versus 4.1%), whereas transition from FE to stable NE was even more rare (4.6% versus 7.3%) compared with the broader COPD population. Transition from NE to persistent FE was associated with lung cancer, SABD overuse, ever-smoking or having neuropsychiatric comorbidity, but musculoskeletal comorbidity and influenza vaccination were no longer significant. Reversing from FE to stable NE was also less likely when overusing inhalers (both SABD or maintenance therapy) or being vaccinated against flu, but ever-smoking, lung cancer and musculoskeletal comorbidity were no longer significant though having lower SES or neuropsychiatric comorbidities still was (supplementary table S5). E-values, which quantify the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding needed to change the observed associations, were <2 for influenza vaccination and lower SES, indicating greater potential for unmeasured confounding, whereas E-values for lung cancer, history of smoking and inhaler overuse indicated that stronger confounding would be required to nullify these associations (supplementary table S6).

Discussion

In this large nationwide study of COPD patients, 11.5% remained never-exacerbators, 3.6% of patients transitioned towards persistent FE and 8.5% reversed from IE or FE to stable NE over a 3-year period. Among FEs at baseline, one in three persisted with frequent exacerbations, whereas more than one in five died. Besides being an ever-smoker and SABD overuse, lung cancer, neuropsychiatric and musculoskeletal comorbidities were consistently associated with disease worsening (transition to persistent FE), increased likelihood of death and lowered odds of reversion (transition to stable NE). Having overuse of maintenance therapy was additionally associated with a decreased likelihood of reversion and increased odds of death, while having a cardiovascular comorbidity was associated with transition to death but not with disease worsening.

A history of AECOPDs in the previous year is traditionally recognised as the most important predictor of subsequent AECOPDs [10], yet patients with comparable numbers of AECOPDs/year may follow different future trajectories [7, 9]. Among patients with similar AECOPD history, we investigated the impact of health determinants and comorbidities on future AECOPD, in line with the concept of striving for disease stability [2, 3]. Consistent with Hurst et al. [7], 11.6% continued to exacerbate frequently each year and the majority of patients experienced an unstable AECOPDs pattern [7, 9]. Transitions from NE to persistent FE were rare, which was also in line with previous research [7, 9, 23], and mainly associated with being an ever-smoker and lung cancer. A history of smoking was significantly associated with a higher odds of both transition towards persistent FE and death, and decreased the likelihood of reversion to stable NE. This finding complements with literature, showing that ever-smoking negatively impacts COPD progression [1] and increases AECOPDs risk [24]. Furthermore, smoking prevention and cessation remain the most effective strategies for lung cancer risk reduction, with the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) recommending additional lung cancer screening in patients at high risk [1, 25].

Neuropsychiatric (depression) or musculoskeletal comorbidities (osteoporosis) were associated with disease progression. Lung cancer, osteoporosis and depression are recognised risk factors of AECOPDs [10, 26–29] and may reflect an active systemic disease state [29, 30]. Our findings are in line with Donaldson et al. [12], showing that patients with depression have a higher likelihood to progress towards a worse phenotype, and that endocrine disorders (diabetes) seemingly did not have a strong association with reversion. Interestingly, metabolic disorders were associated with a reduced odds of death, whereas cachectic disorders showed the strongest associations with transition to death. Cardiovascular comorbidities were significantly associated with an increased risk of death, but not with transitions towards persistent FE. This is in line with Figueira-Gonçalves et al. [31] and Vikjord et al. [13], which similarly did not find a significant association between frequent exacerbations and cardiovascular disease. Since others observed that cardiovascular comorbidities are associated with an increased AECOPDs risk [10], further research is warranted to explore whether optimal management of cardiovascular diseases [31] rather alters patient's overall prognosis than disease stability.

Besides comorbidity management and smoking prevention, our study emphasises once again benefits of influenza vaccination. Influenza vaccination at baseline was associated with disease worsening, potentially reflecting reverse causation or confounding by indication, as patients with frequent exacerbations were more frequently vaccinated. However, and importantly, vaccination against influenza was still associated with a significantly lower odds of mortality, reinforcing its role as a key preventive intervention in COPD management. SABD use, without use of any maintenance therapy, has no place in COPD patients who frequently exacerbate [1] and its overuse is considered a marker of symptomatic burden and uncontrolled disease [32], nevertheless, we observed that more than 10% of FE patients did not use maintenance therapy at the index date. In line with this, patients with inhaler overuse (both SABD or maintenance therapy) may represent a subgroup with persistent symptoms and uncontrolled disease as we demonstrated that SABD overuse was associated with transitions towards persistent FE and death and a lower likelihood of reversion to stable NE, but also even overuse of maintenance therapy was associated with lower odds of reversion to stable NE and a significantly increased chance of mortality. Therefore, it is important that healthcare providers identify patients, especially those with prior exacerbations, who use SABD without maintenance therapy or inhaler overuse, to assess their disease control and optimise treatment if necessary.

This nationwide cohort study is strengthened by the large sample size of 183 762 patients and objective investigation of real-word clinical practice at a full-population scale, including data of both ambulatory and hospital care. This provided a unique opportunity to investigate AECOPD transitions over a longer period. The generalisability of our results to a broader defined COPD population, including also patients treated exclusively in ambulatory care, were confirmed by sensitivity analyses restricting the study population to patients with hospital-diagnosed COPD. Nevertheless, observational use of healthcare databases is inherently characterised by some limitations such as the probability of coding errors, misclassification bias, unmeasured confounding and the inability to confirm patients’ actual medication intake. Despite rigorous attempts to ascertain a history of smoking, some underestimation of the proportion of ever-smokers cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, despite our best efforts to exclude patients with hospital-diagnosed asthma or asthma-specific treatments, we acknowledge that individuals with undiagnosed asthma may still have been included in our analyses. Although antibiotics were selected based on Belgian guidelines for treating COPD exacerbations [19], use for other types of infections cannot be entirely ruled out, which may have led to an overestimation of moderate exacerbations. Due to lack of information, we were not able to adjust for additional protective measures (e.g. pneumococcal vaccination and rehabilitation programs) or severity markers (e.g. lung function variables, symptom control, smoking pack-years and appropriate medication escalation), which may have left the potential for residual confounding, although we defined inhaler overuse as marker of disease control and/or disease severity. Furthermore, we did not account for treatment modifications or evolving comorbidities during follow-up, which may have influenced transition probabilities. Last, we may have underestimated the amount of influenza vaccination by vaccinations provided at work or in nursing homes.

Future research should investigate whether and how (tailored) interventions during follow-up may modify AECOPD transitions, and to address our limitations, prospective research incorporating additional clinical variables currently not studied using advanced modelling approaches are recommended to validate our findings.

Conclusions

Beyond being an ever-smoker and SABD overuse, our nationwide study adds that neuropsychiatric and musculoskeletal comorbidities contribute to disease worsening, increase odds of mortality and lower the likelihood of reversion to stable NE. Lung cancer was the strongest factor associated with death, so earlier identification requires urgent attention. Cardiovascular comorbidities were less influential on exacerbation phenotype transitions but significantly associated with transition to death, whereas metabolic disorders were associated with transitions to persistent FE but also with a lower mortality risk. Addressing maintenance therapy overuse is important to promote reversion and prevent mortality. Further research is recommended considering potential unmeasured confounding in our study.
