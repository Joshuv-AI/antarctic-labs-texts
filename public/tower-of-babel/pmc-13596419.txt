Physical Activity, Emotional Intelligence, and Resilience in College Students: A Multi-Group Structural Equation Modeling Analysis Across Physical Fitness Levels
Authors: Yunchen Meng, Wang Yang, Hesong You, Shaoyu Lu, Yilin An, Shanming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study examined the relationships among physical activity, emotional intelligence, and resilience in college students with different physical fitness levels, examined the potential indirect association through emotional intelligence, and compared group-specific structural pathways using multi-group structural equation modelling.Participants and MethodsA stratified cluster sampling method was used to recruit 1,988 freshmen and sophomores from four universities in Beijing. Based on physical fitness evaluation, participants were divided into the general physical education group (n = 1,478), the physical fitness enhancement group (n = 320), and the sports rehabilitation group (n = 190). Physical activity, emotional intelligence, and resilience were assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF), Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS), and Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), respectively. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was conducted using Mplus 8.3, followed by multi-group comparative analyses to examine differences in structural pathways across groups.ResultsIn the total sample, physical activity was positively associated with emotional intelligence (β = 0.216, P < 0.001) and resilience (β = 0.070, P = 0.001). A significant indirect association through emotional intelligence was observed between physical activity and resilience (indirect association = 0.091, 95% CI [0.070, 0.114]), accounting for 56.5% of the total association. Multi-group analyses revealed significant structural heterogeneity across groups. The rehabilitation group showed a significant indirect but non-significant direct association, the enhancement group showed a significant direct association and a weak, non-significant indirect association through EI, and the general group showed both significant direct and indirect associations.ConclusionThe findings reveal heterogeneous pathways linking physical activity, emotional intelligence, and resilience across physical fitness levels. The indirect association through emotional intelligence differed across groups, highlighting the need for targeted physical activity and psychological support strategies for college students.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of the World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) global initiative, “Making Every School a Health-Promoting School”, promoting the physical and mental health of college students has become an important priority in higher education.1 However, college students currently present increasingly diverse physical characteristics, and those with poor physical fitness have become a more visible subgroup within university populations.2 Compared with their peers, students with poor physical fitness may experience disadvantages in learning and social interactions due to physiological or morphological limitations, making them more susceptible to negative emotions such as inferiority, anxiety, and social avoidance.3 Although physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as an effective strategy for promoting physical and mental health, students in this subgroup often exhibit relatively low levels of sports participation, which may be related to physical limitations, low exercise confidence, or fear of difficulty.4 Insufficient participation may further impair physical fitness and increase vulnerability to mental health problems.5 Therefore, examining the relationship between PA and psychological traits in this subgroup may help clarify the pathways linking physical and mental health.

Resilience is a key psychological trait that enables individuals to restore psychological balance when facing adversity and serves as an important protective factor for mental health.6 For college students with poor physical fitness, the development of resilience may have particular compensatory significance. Previous studies have shown that PA is an important factor associated with higher resilience, helping students shift from passive endurance to more positive cognitive coping when facing stress.7 Therefore, examining the pathways linking PA and resilience has important academic and practical value for developing psychological intervention strategies for college students with poor physical fitness and enhancing their adaptive capacity.

EI may represent an important pathway in the association between PA and resilience.8 EI refers to an individual’s ability to perceive, understand, regulate, and manage emotions.9 Physical exercise may be associated with better emotional regulation through enhanced mood, providing a sense of achievement, and strengthening perceived competence.10 Individuals with higher EI may be better able to transform positive exercise-related experiences into psychological resources for coping with difficulties, which may in turn be associated with higher resilience.11 Existing empirical studies have reported a potential indirect pathway through EI in the association between PA and resilience.12

Although the relationships among PA, EI, and resilience have been preliminarily explored, existing studies have mainly focused on general student populations, while research targeting students with poor physical fitness or physical limitations remains limited. Our previous study examined the structural relationships among PA, sleep, and resilience in physically weak college students;13 however, the role of EI and the potential differences in PA–EI–resilience pathways across physical fitness subgroups remain unclear. In the present study, the sports rehabilitation group included students with congenital or chronic diseases, physical disabilities, postoperative recovery, or sudden acute injuries that limited their participation in vigorous exercise and routine PE assessments. On the one hand, considerable heterogeneity may exist within this subgroup, and students with different physical fitness levels may display distinct psychological characteristics. On the other hand, the psychological benefits associated with PA may be constrained by individual physical fitness levels, and it remains unclear whether the pathways linking PA, EI, and resilience are consistent across different physical fitness subgroups. Therefore, using multi-group structural equation modeling to examine the stability and variation of these pathways may deepen understanding of the synergistic relationship between physical and psychological health. It may also provide empirical evidence for more targeted reforms in university physical education for students with poor physical fitness.

Based on the theoretical mechanisms linking physical and psychological health and the accumulated empirical evidence, the association between PA and resilience through the mediating pathway of EI has been preliminarily verified in general populations. However, students with poor physical fitness constitute a distinct group in university physical education. Their physiological limitations and unique psychological experiences may shape how PA is associated with psychological outcomes. Nevertheless, these structural relationships have not been systematically examined in previous empirical research. Accordingly, the present study constructed a structural equation model incorporating PA, EI, and resilience, and applied multi-group analysis to examine whether these structural pathways differed across groups with different physical fitness levels. The following hypotheses were proposed: H1: PA is significantly and positively associated with resilience among college students. H2: PA is indirectly associated with resilience through EI. H3: The structural pathways among PA, EI, and resilience differ significantly across groups with different physical fitness levels.

H1: PA is significantly and positively associated with resilience among college students.

H2: PA is indirectly associated with resilience through EI.

H3: The structural pathways among PA, EI, and resilience differ significantly across groups with different physical fitness levels.

The findings of this study are expected to enrich academic understanding of the physical and psychological development patterns of college students with different physical fitness levels, provide empirical evidence for clarifying the heterogeneous characteristics of their physical–psychological interaction mechanisms, and offer practical references for universities to develop differentiated PA intervention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Participants

A stratified cluster sampling method was used to recruit freshmen and sophomores from four universities in Beijing. According to the practical implementation standards of graded physical education (PE) teaching in these universities, participants were divided into three sampling strata: (1) The sports rehabilitation group included students who had been diagnosed by the university hospital as unsuitable for vigorous exercise and routine PE assessments because of congenital or chronic diseases, physical disabilities, postoperative recovery, or sudden acute injuries, and who had therefore been assigned to rehabilitation courses. All sports rehabilitation classes from the four universities were included, comprising approximately 240 students. (2) The physical fitness enhancement group included students who scored below 60 points on the National Student Physical Health Standard test.14 Two to three physical fitness enhancement or failing-grade PE classes were selected by manual random drawing from each university, comprising approximately 400 students. (3) The general physical education group included students who passed the physical health test, had normal physical function, were able to tolerate the exercise load prescribed in the PE syllabus, and participated in standard PE courses. Ten to fifteen general PE classes were selected by manual random drawing from each university, comprising approximately 1,800 students. BMI was not used as a criterion for group assignment.

A total of 2,260 questionnaires were distributed and collected. After excluding invalid responses according to predefined criteria, including missing values exceeding 10%, failure to pass the reverse-scored item check, and obvious patterned responses, 1,988 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. After invalid questionnaires were excluded, no missing values remained in the variables included in the analyses; therefore, no data imputation was performed. The final sample included 1,478 students in the general physical education group, 320 in the physical fitness enhancement group, and 190 in the sports rehabilitation group. Overall, the sample comprised 1,106 males and 882 females, including 987 freshmen and 1,001 sophomores.

Measurements

International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF)

The IPAQ-SF was used to assess PA levels. This seven-item questionnaire covers vigorous-intensity activity, moderate-intensity activity, walking, and sedentary time. Participants reported the frequency and duration of each activity performed during the previous seven days. Data were cleaned and processed according to the official IPAQ scoring guidelines.15 Cases were excluded if total daily PA time exceeded 960 minutes, and reported durations exceeding 180 minutes per day for any single activity category were truncated to 180 minutes. Weekly energy expenditure was then calculated as MET-min/week, and participants were classified into low, moderate, or high PA levels to evaluate overall PA participation.

Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS)

The EIS, originally developed by Schutte,16 was used to assess EI. This scale is a widely used instrument for measuring an individual’s ability to perceive, understand, express, and regulate emotions in oneself and others. It consists of 33 items across four subscales: emotion perception (12 items), self-emotion management (8 items), others’ emotion management (6 items), and emotion utilization (7 items). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with items 5, 28, and 33 reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 33 to 165, with higher scores indicating higher levels of EI. The revised Chinese version has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.93.17 In the subsequent structural equation modelling, EI was specified as a latent variable using its four dimensional indicators.

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

The Chinese version of the CD-RISC, originally developed by Connor and Davidson18 and revised by Yu,19 was used to assess resilience. The scale is widely used to evaluate psychological characteristics related to successful coping with stress, adversity, and frustration. It consists of 25 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater resilience. Based on the Chinese cultural background, the revised scale comprises three dimensions: Tenacity (13 items), reflecting perseverance in adversity; Strength (8 items), reflecting self-efficacy; and Optimism (4 items), reflecting positive attitudes. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.91, and the internal consistency coefficients for Tenacity, Strength, and Optimism were 0.88, 0.80, and 0.60, respectively, indicating acceptable reliability and supporting the structural validity of the scale.19

Procedure

Prior to the formal survey, PE teachers and research assistants responsible for data collection at each university received standardized training on administration procedures, instruction delivery, and key precautions. The survey was conducted using a paper-and-pencil format under collective administration. Data collection was uniformly arranged in classrooms or indoor PE facilities to ensure procedural consistency. Before completing the questionnaires, participants were provided with standardized instructions either orally or in writing. They were informed of the academic purpose of the study, as well as the confidentiality, anonymity, and voluntary nature of participation. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study. Participants were also explicitly informed that their personal information would be strictly protected and that they had the right to discontinue or withdraw from the survey at any time without consequence. After fully understanding the questionnaire instructions, participants completed the scales independently. The completion time for each questionnaire was approximately 10–12 minutes. On-site supervision was provided to ensure a quiet and undisturbed environment. After completion, all questionnaires were checked and collected uniformly on site.

Data Processing and Analysis

Preliminary data processing, descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and one-way analyses of variance were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30. Confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation modeling, measurement invariance testing, and multi-group analyses were performed using Mplus 8.3.

Mplus 8.3 was used to construct the structural equation model (SEM) and conduct multi-group analyses. First, the valid sample (N = 1,988) was screened to identify potential missing-value anomalies. Descriptive statistics, including the means and standard deviations of the observed variables, were then calculated for each group. Model parameters were estimated using maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ2/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR values ≤ 0.08 were considered indicative of acceptable model fit, with more stringent values of CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, and SRMR ≤ 0.08 indicating good fit.20

Before comparing structural pathways, multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to test measurement invariance across groups. Specifically, the configural invariance model was first estimated as the baseline model, followed by the metric invariance model, in which factor loadings were constrained to be equal across groups, and the scalar invariance model, in which item intercepts were further constrained. Measurement invariance was considered acceptable when ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015.21

After cross-group measurement invariance was established, a multi-group SEM was constructed.

In the total-sample SEM, course type, gender, ethnicity, grade, and BMI were entered as observed covariates rather than latent control variables, PA was specified as the independent variable, EI as the mediator, and resilience as the dependent variable. In the multi-group SEM, the path coefficients for each group, including the path from PA to EI, the path from EI to resilience, and the direct path from PA to resilience, were freely estimated. This approach allowed the direct association between PA and resilience, as well as the indirect association through EI, to be examined across groups.

The MODEL CONSTRAINT command was used to define parameter labels and calculate differences in path coefficients among the physical fitness groups, thereby testing whether the structural pathways differed across groups. Bias-corrected bootstrap procedures with 5,000 resamples were used to estimate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the path coefficients and indirect associations. Statistical significance was determined when the 95% CI did not include zero.

Results

Common Method Bias Testing

Given that PA, EI, and resilience were all assessed using self-reported questionnaires, common method bias was examined. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted using confirmatory factor analysis, in which all eight observed indicators were specified to load onto a single latent factor. The Mplus results showed that the single-factor model had poor fit (χ2/df = 227.11, P < 0.001; CFI = 0.653; TLI = 0.514; RMSEA = 0.337), with all fit indices failing to meet the recommended criteria. These findings suggest that no single latent factor accounted for the majority of the variance. Therefore, serious common method bias was unlikely to be present in this study.

Structural Associations Among PA, EI, and Resilience

Mplus 8.3 was used to construct a structural equation model to examine the indirect pathway through EI in the association between PA and resilience, while controlling for course type, gender, ethnicity, grade, and BMI. The standardized path coefficients, bootstrap confidence intervals, and decomposition of the total, direct, and indirect associations are presented in Table 3 and Figure 1. The model demonstrated good fit to the data: χ2/df = 3.30, P < 0.001; CFI = 0.992; TLI = 0.989; RMSEA = 0.034; and SRMR = 0.017. Path analysis showed that PA was significantly and positively associated with EI (β = 0.216, P < 0.001), and EI was significantly and positively associated with resilience (β = 0.421, P < 0.001). The direct association between PA and resilience also remained significant (β = 0.070, P = 0.001). Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap analysis with 5,000 resamples confirmed a significant indirect association through EI, with a standardized indirect association of 0.091 and a 95% CI of [0.070, 0.114]. Multi-Group Measurement Invariance Testing

As shown in Table 4, the configural invariance model demonstrated acceptable fit (χ2 = 187.214, df = 39; CFI = 0.988; TLI = 0.981; RMSEA = 0.076; SRMR = 0.028), indicating that the basic factor structure was consistent across the three groups. Comparisons of nested models revealed minimal changes in fit indices as equality constraints were sequentially imposed. Specifically, comparing the metric invariance model with the baseline model yielded ΔCFI = 0 and ΔRMSEA = −0.009, and comparing the scalar invariance model with the metric model yielded ΔCFI = 0 and ΔRMSEA = −0.004. Both sets of differences met the established criteria for measurement invariance (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015). Further analysis showed that the direct path from PA to resilience was significantly positive in the enhancement and general groups, but non-significant in the rehabilitation group. This indicates that the direct association between PA and resilience did not reach statistical significance among students in the rehabilitation group. Regarding the indirect pathway, the indirect association between PA and resilience through EI was significant in the rehabilitation and general groups, but did not reach conventional statistical significance in the enhancement group (β = 0.052, SE = 0.027, Z = 1.954). These findings suggest that the indirect pathway through EI was more evident in the rehabilitation and general groups, whereas this pathway was relatively weak in the enhancement group.

In summary, physical activity was positively associated with emotional intelligence and resilience in the total sample, and a significant indirect association through emotional intelligence was observed. However, the structural pattern varied across physical fitness groups. The indirect pathway was significant in the sports rehabilitation and general physical education groups, whereas the direct pathway was significant in the physical fitness enhancement and general physical education groups.

Discussion

Differences in Physical and Mental Characteristics Among College Students with Different Physical Fitness Levels

The results indicate an uneven distribution of PA behaviours and psychological resources across students with different physical fitness levels. Students in the general physical education group showed higher levels of PA and EI, which is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that better physical fitness is often associated with more favourable psychological functioning.22 However, the two physically weak groups exhibited distinct patterns of PA and psychological resources. Students in the sports rehabilitation group reported the lowest level of PA and experienced the greatest physical limitations. Nevertheless, their resilience scores were significantly higher than those of students in the physical fitness enhancement group and were even numerically higher than those of students in the general physical education group in some dimensions. These findings suggest that students with poor physical fitness may not constitute a psychologically homogeneous population, but may differ in their adaptive processes and psychological resource profiles.

The lower levels of PA observed in the two physically weak groups may have different underlying explanations. Both groups showed lower PA levels than the general physical education group, which is consistent with previous findings regarding insufficient exercise participation among physically vulnerable youth.23 For students in the sports rehabilitation group, opportunities for vigorous exercise may be restricted by health conditions, physical limitations, or medical recommendations. Their lower PA levels may therefore be partly associated with objective constraints. In contrast, students in the physical fitness enhancement group may avoid PA because of previous failure in fitness tests, concerns about body image, fear of fatigue, or limited exercise skills. These negative exercise-related experiences may be associated with lower physical self-esteem and weaker exercise motivation.24 Such differences in exercise participation may help explain the observed variation in psychological outcomes between the two physically weak groups. The relatively higher resilience observed in the sports rehabilitation group may reflect a compensatory psychological adaptation. The coexistence of lower PA levels and relatively higher resilience suggests that PA level alone may not fully account for psychological adaptation in this group. This finding does not necessarily support the assumption that poorer physical health is invariably associated with poorer psychological functioning. Students with long-term illness, disability, or injury may gradually develop coping resources through repeated experiences of chronic stress and adaptation to adversity. These experiences may be associated with greater psychological stability and stronger resilience-related resources.25 In addition, students in the sports rehabilitation group may receive more supportive and individualized physical education. Such an environment may reduce peer comparison, lower performance pressure, and enhance feelings of safety and belonging.26 This supportive context may also be associated with their relatively higher resilience.

By contrast, students in the physical fitness enhancement group showed the lowest scores across all dimensions of EI and resilience, suggesting that they may face difficulties in both physical fitness and psychological adaptation. One possible explanation is the pressure associated with failure in physical fitness tests. These students may experience negative social evaluation, body-related shame, and academic anxiety.27 However, these constructs were not directly measured in the present study and should therefore be regarded as theoretical assumptions requiring future validation. Previous evidence also suggests that students with obesity or underweight status are more likely to report inferiority, depression, and social avoidance. Repeated negative exercise experiences and comparisons with peers may further reduce their exercise motivation.28 Over time, these negative bodily experiences and social comparison pressures may be associated with lower willingness to participate in exercise, poorer emotional regulation, and lower resilience. Further longitudinal or mixed-methods studies, including qualitative research, are needed to clarify these potential explanations and underlying mechanisms.

Relationship Between PA, EI, and Resilience

Based on structural equation modeling, this study examined the structural relationships among PA, EI, and resilience in college students, controlling for demographic variables such as gender. The results indicated that PA was not only significantly and positively associated with resilience but also indirectly related to resilience via EI. These findings support the health promotion perspective of physical–psychological interaction, highlighting a close link between bodily behavior and psychological resources.

The results further showed that higher levels of PA were associated with higher resilience, consistent with previous evidence indicating that regular PA is related to improved coping ability and greater adaptation to adversity.29 From a psychophysiological perspective, exercise may function as a manageable stressor. Repeated exposure to exercise and recovery may be associated with stronger adaptive responses to stress, which may partly account for the observed association between PA and coping ability.30 For college students with poor physical fitness, although physical limitations may constrain exercise participation, PA of appropriate intensity remains positively associated with physical self-efficacy and resilience, reflecting the coordinated development of physical and psychological functioning.

This study also demonstrated that EI serves as a critical pathway linking bodily behavior and psychological adaptation. PA was significantly associated with EI, consistent with prior research showing that physically active individuals often exhibit better emotional regulation.31 According to the broaden-and-build theory, positive emotions can broaden cognitive processes and contribute to the development of enduring psychological resources.32 The present model suggests that the higher EI associated with greater PA may represent an important indirect pathway in the association between PA and resilience.33 These findings further support the proposed “PA–EI–resilience” pathway.34

Multi-Group Comparative Analysis Across Different Physical Fitness Levels

Using multi-group structural equation modeling, this study further revealed that although the overall logical chain of PA–EI–resilience was present, these structural relationships showed significant pathway heterogeneity among college students with different physical fitness characteristics. This finding refines the assumption of homogeneous psychological benefits of exercise in previous studies and suggests that physical fitness level may serve as an important boundary condition shaping the relationship between physical and psychological functioning.

In the sports rehabilitation group, the direct association between PA and resilience was non-significant, whereas the indirect pathway via EI was significant. This indicates that, for students with chronic illness, injury, or other physical limitations, higher levels of PA were not directly associated with greater resilience. Instead, the PA–resilience association in this group appeared to be mainly reflected through emotional processing.35

EI thus represented an important indirect pathway, highlighting the role of emotion perception and regulation in linking PA to resilience. Consistent with psychological compensation theory, physically disadvantaged groups may maintain overall system balance by developing stronger psychological resources when facing adversity related to limited physical functioning.36 Furthermore, university sports rehabilitation courses typically adopt adaptive physical education models emphasizing humanistic care, social support, and a low-competition environment. Such settings may be associated with greater emotional control and a more positive life perspective, which may in turn be associated with higher resilience. These findings suggest that interventions for this group should prioritize the mobilization of emotional resources in addition to the development of physical skills.

In the physical fitness enhancement group, the PA–resilience association was mainly direct, while the indirect association through EI was relatively weak and did not reach conventional statistical significance. Unlike the rehabilitation group, PA was directly and significantly associated with resilience. Although PA was also significantly associated with EI, the indirect pathway from PA to resilience through EI was not statistically significant, suggesting that the PA–resilience association in this group was more strongly reflected in the direct pathway. This pattern may reflect psychological challenges faced by students with poor physical fitness when engaging in PA. Previous studies have shown that students with obesity or lower fitness levels often experience higher body image anxiety and social pressure in PE contexts. For these students, exercise may be perceived more as a stressor than as a source of positive emotional experience.33 Consequently, the indirect association through EI may be relatively limited in this group, whereas the association between PA and resilience was more strongly reflected in the direct pathway. These findings indicate that while current enhancement courses may promote physical training and willpower development, they may be insufficient for fostering positive emotional experiences or broader psychological gains, resulting in weaker associations between physical and psychological factors.

In the general physical education group, the PA–EI–resilience mechanism appeared relatively stable, reflecting coordinated physical–psychological functioning. Among students with normal physical fitness, PA was both directly and positively associated with resilience and indirectly associated via EI, showing both significant direct and indirect associations. This suggests a dual role for PA in general students: PA may show both a direct association with resilience and an indirect association through EI-related emotional regulation and interpersonal functioning. The coexistence of direct and indirect pathways aligns with the embodied cognition perspective on physical–psychological interaction, indicating that, when physical functioning is intact, PA can be more readily transformed into psychological resources, representing a relatively ideal model for health promotion.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study used a cross-sectional design; therefore, temporal order and causal relationships among PA, EI, and resilience cannot be established. The mediation model should be interpreted as a statistical indirect association rather than evidence of a causal mechanism. Second, all variables were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias and common method bias. In addition, some potential explanations for the group differences, such as social pressure, body image concerns, and compensatory psychological adaptation, were not directly assessed. Future longitudinal, intervention, and qualitative studies are needed to further clarify these relationships.

Conclusion

This study revealed different patterns of physical and psychological characteristics among college students with varying physical fitness levels; students in the sports rehabilitation group had the lowest PA but showed higher resilience than those in the physical fitness enhancement group. PA was associated with resilience both directly and indirectly via EI, with distinct structural associations across groups: in the sports rehabilitation group, the association was mainly through EI; in the enhancement group, it was primarily direct; and in the general group, both associations were observed. These findings suggest that physical fitness may be associated with different patterns of association between PA and psychological outcomes. Universities should consider tailored strategies focusing on emotional support, exercise confidence, and psychological empowerment.
