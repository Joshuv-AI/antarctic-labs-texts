Automated assessment of coronal lower extremity alignment on long-leg radiographs using a deep-learning model: validation, efficiency gains, and superiority of an edge-based tibial joint-line definition
Authors: Wenzhong Jin, Xu Jiang, Yushun Tao, Yuqi Hu, Mengning Yan, Liangbin Gao, Kai Xie, Liao Wang, Jinwu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop, validate, and benchmark a fully automated deep learning (DL) system that simultaneously measures 15 coronal lower-limb alignment parameters on standing long-leg radiographs (LLRs) and localizes deformity, and to compare two tibial joint-line definitions for suitability in DL-based measurement.MethodsA retrospective set of 309 anteroposterior standing LLRs was split into training/validation/testing (60/20/20). External generalizability was assessed using 75 independent LLRs from a different scanner and patient cohort. YOLOv5 was used to detect multiple bony landmarks, followed by algorithmic calculation of 15 parameters (e.g., HKAA, mLDFA, mMPTA). Two tibial joint-line definitions were evaluated: Method 1 (line through medial/lateral lowest tibial plateau points) and Method 2 (line through most medial/lateral plateau edges). Accuracy, clinical failure rate (≥ 2° or ≥ 2%), and runtime were compared with expert consensus annotations. Bland-Altman analysis was added to assess measurement bias and clinical agreement.ResultsOn the external dataset, absolute errors for knee phenotype–related parameters ranged from 0.07° to 0.45°. Automated analysis of all 15 parameters took 24.3 ± 0.7 s, reducing time by 89–91% versus manual measurement (p < 0.001). Method 2 produced significantly smaller absolute errors than Method 1 for nearly all parameters (p ≤ 0.005). Clinically significant failure rates were low (0–4.7%) and were significantly lower than an attending physician’s for several key metrics on the external set. The distribution of varus/valgus/neutral alignment based on HKAA and extraarticular deformity locations were reported.ConclusionThis DL framework provides fast, comprehensive, and specialist-level coronal alignment assessment on LLRs. An edge-based tibial joint-line definition (Method 2) outperforms a lowest-point definition, improving precision and reliability for DL measurement pipelines, supporting clinically deployable orthopedic imaging AI. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate Method 2 against clinical outcomes including osteoarthritis progression and surgical results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09980-x.Keywords: Deep learning, Artificial intelligence, Long-leg radiography, Lower extremity alignment, Coronal alignment

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) represents a profound global health burden, characterized by pain, functional limitation, and substantial socioeconomic impact, affecting approximately 250 million individuals worldwide [1, 2]. The mechanical environment of the knee joint, heavily influenced by coronal plane alignment of the lower extremity, is a critical determinant in the initiation and progression of KOA [3, 4]. Varus or valgus malalignment leads to aberrant load distribution across the articular surfaces, accelerating cartilage degeneration. When conservative management fails to alleviate symptoms, surgical intervention becomes necessary. The contemporary surgical armamentarium for KOA is diverse, ranging from joint-preserving osteotomies for focal, extra-articular deformities to partial (unicompartmental) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for advanced intra-articular disease [5–8].

The selection of an optimal surgical strategy is increasingly guided by the concept of knee phenotypes—morphological classifications based on precise coronal alignment parameters [9–12]. Strategies for TKA, including mechanical alignment (MA), kinematic alignment (KA), and their variants, are predicated on restoring a patient-specific, functional alignment rather than a universal mechanical axis [9–12]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of standardized knee phenotyping, gender-based phenotype differences, and the impact of alignment on ligamentous laxity, underscoring the need for reliable alignment measurement tools [12–15]. Consequently, accurate, reproducible, and comprehensive quantification of coronal alignment parameters from weightbearing long-leg anteroposterior radiographs (LLRs) is no longer merely diagnostic but a cornerstone of personalized surgical planning and prognostic assessment.

The current clinical standard involves manual annotation of anatomical landmarks and subsequent geometric measurement by trained orthopaedic surgeons or radiologists. This process is inherently labor-intensive, time-consuming, and susceptible to intra- and inter-observer variability, introducing inconsistency into critical decision-making data. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), offers a transformative paradigm to automate this workflow, promising unparalleled consistency, dramatic gains in efficiency, and the potential to unlock insights from large-scale radiographic databases [16–19].

Although pioneering DL models have been developed for measuring parameters like the hip-knee-ankle angle (HKAA) [20–22], significant gaps persist that hinder clinical translation. First, most existing models offer a limited scope, automating only a handful of parameters (often just HKAA), whereas comprehensive assessment requires a suite of measurements including femoral and tibial mechanical angles, joint-line orientation, and the weight-bearing line ratio. Second, robust external validation—essential for demonstrating generalizability across different imaging devices and patient populations—is frequently lacking or reveals concerning performance degradation [23, 24]. Third, an unresolved methodological question pertains to the optimal definition of key anatomical constructs within a DL framework, especially the tibial joint line. This study aims to fill these gaps by developing a comprehensive DL model and validating the optimal tibial joint-line definition for automated measurement, which has not been systematically investigated previously.

This study has two primary objectives: first, to develop and rigorously validate a fully automated DL model capable of simultaneous landmark detection, 15 coronal alignment parameter calculation, and deformity localization; second, to compare the two tibial joint-line definitions in the DL pipeline. It is hypothesized that the comprehensive DL model achieves expert-level accuracy with high efficiency, and the edge-based joint-line definition (Method 2) shows superior precision compared with the lowest-point definition (Method 1).

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective, multicenter study was conducted following approval from the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Ethics Committee, which granted a waiver for the requirement of informed consent due to the anonymized nature of the retrospective data analysis.

Data acquisition and patient cohorts

Long‑leg anteroposterior radiographs (LLRs) were collected from three digital radiography systems across two tertiary orthopedic centers to ensure methodological rigor and assess generalizability.

Primary Development Dataset: This dataset was sourced from two scanners: Scanner 1 (SONIALVISION Safire Plus, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at Hospital A, with images obtained in 2018; and Scanner 2 (uDR 770i, United Imaging, Shanghai, China) at Hospital B, with images obtained between 2018 and 2021.

Independent External Validation Dataset: To rigorously test model generalizability, a separate dataset was acquired from a new scanner: Scanner 3 (Multitom Rax, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) installed at Hospital A, with images collected during the first half of 2022. This dataset was entirely withheld from the model development process.

Inclusion criteria comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) for whom standard, standing long-leg AP radiographs were available. The primary dataset included patients with radiographically confirmed medial-compartment knee osteoarthritis (KOA) and asymptomatic individuals without evidence of significant arthritis or malalignment to ensure the model was trained on anatomical variation. The external validation dataset followed a similar inclusion strategy.Stringent exclusion criteria were applied to all datasets to remove confounding factors: (1) presence of developmental dysplasia of the hip; (2) history of osteonecrosis of the femoral head; (3) prior surgery, malignancy, or significant trauma (e.g., fracture) affecting the lower extremities; (4) complete obliteration of the knee joint space precluding landmark identification; (5) radiographs deemed of insufficient technical quality for reliable assessment. The absolute number of patients excluded for each criterion is listed in Table S1.

A detailed flowchart of participant selection is provided in Fig. 1. The final analyzed cohort comprised 383 patients (766 lower extremities), with 136 male and 247 female participants. All analyses were performed at the lower‑extremity level. The primary dataset (309 LLRs, 618 extremities) was randomly split at the patient level into a training set (186 LLRs, 372 extremities, 60%), a validation set (62 LLRs, 124 extremities, 20%) for hyperparameter tuning, and a held‑out testing set (61 LLRs, 122 extremities, 20%). The independent external validation set consisted of 75 LLRs (150 extremities). Demographic and baseline alignment characteristics for all subsets are summarized in Table 1S. No additional exclusions were applied after final cohort assembly.

Ground-truth annotation protocol and parameter definitions

Establishing a reliable ground truth is paramount for supervised DL model development and validation. All radiographic annotations were performed using a custom‑built, standardized software interface. Two senior orthopaedic specialists (denoted SS‑A and SS‑B), each with over fifteen years of dedicated experience in musculoskeletal radiograph analysis, independently annotated all images. Their consensus mean values (SS‑M) served as the definitive ground truth for all subsequent analyses.

The annotation protocol required the manual placement of key anatomical landmarks and delineation of regions of interest (ROIs), as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Deformity classification

Based on the annotated parameters, each lower extremity was classified as having a deformity (varus or valgus) at the hip, knee, or ankle according to widely accepted normative ranges described by Paley et al. [22, 26] (e.g., HKAA normal: 180° ± 3°; mLDFA normal: 85°–90°). Deformity was explicitly defined as alignment outside these normal ranges, corresponding to varus or valgus malalignment. This classification was used to evaluate the model’s diagnostic performance in deformity localization.

Deep-learning model development

Model Architecture and Pipeline: A two-stage pipeline centered on the YOLOv5 object detection architecture was employed, renowned for its optimal balance of speed and accuracy. The overall pipeline is depicted in Fig. 3. The first stage utilizes YOLOv5 to detect the eleven key anatomical landmarks directly, treating each as a miniature “object” within the image. The detected landmark coordinates are then fed into a deterministic geometric calculation module (second stage) that computes all fifteen alignment parameters and executes the deformity classification rules.

Pipeline of our deep-learning based pipeline for automate anatomical parameters measurement. YOLOv5 algorithm is used to detect bony landmarks accurately and robustly. Then, parameters are measured according to predicted landmarks and final deformity assessment is conducted

Image Preprocessing and Augmentation: All input LLRs were resized and padded to a uniform dimension of 1024 × 1024 pixels. Global histogram equalization was applied to enhance contrast and normalize intensity variations across different scanners. To bolster model robustness and mitigate overfitting, an extensive data augmentation strategy was employed during training: random horizontal flips, translational shifts (± 10%), scaling (± 20%), color jittering (brightness, contrast, saturation), and the mosaic augmentation technique [27], which combines four training images into one.

Training Details: Transfer learning was initiated using YOLOv5 weights pre-trained on the large-scale Common Objects in Context (COCO) dataset [28, 29]. The model was optimized using stochastic gradient descent with a momentum of 0.937. The learning rate was warmed up from 0.01 to 0.1 over the first three epochs, then followed a linear decay schedule to 0.01 over the remaining epochs. Training was conducted with a batch size of 16 for 100 epochs on two NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs (32 GB memory each) using the PyTorch framework. The model checkpoint with the lowest loss on the validation set was selected for final evaluation.

Statistical analysis plan

Performance Metrics: Model performance was evaluated on the internal testing set and the independent external validation set using the following metrics:

Landmark Localization Error: The Euclidean distance (in mm, after DICOM‑based pixel‑spacing calibration) between the DL‑predicted landmark and the specialist annotation (SS‑A).

Parameter Measurement Error: Absolute error (|DL – SS‑M|) and mean signed error (DL – SS‑M) for each anatomical parameter.

Clinical Failure Rate: The proportion of measurements where the absolute error exceeded a clinically relevant threshold (≥ 2° for angles, ≥ 2% for WBLR) [21].

Agreement Statistics: Intra‑class correlation coefficients (ICC) using a two‑way mixed‑effects model for absolute agreement were calculated to assess intra‑observer (AP‑1 vs. AP‑2) and inter‑observer (SS‑A vs. SS‑B, AP‑1 vs. SS‑M, DL vs. SS‑M) reliability. ICC values > 0.90, 0.75–0.90, and < 0.75 were interpreted as excellent, good, and poor reliability, respectively. Bland-Altman analysis with 95% limits of agreement was added to evaluate measurement bias and clinical interchangeability.

Diagnostic Performance: For deformity detection, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated against the ground‑truth deformity classification derived from SS‑M.

Time Efficiency: The total time required for the DL model to process an image and output all measurements was recorded and compared to manual measurement times.

Comparative Analyses: To benchmark the DL model against clinical practice, an attending orthopaedic physician (AP) with six years of experience performed annotations on the testing set twice (AP‑1, AP‑2, two‑month interval) and on the external set once (AP‑1). Paired sample t‑tests were used to compare absolute errors between DL and AP. Chi‑square or Fisher’s exact tests compared failure rates. Paired t‑tests were also used to compare errors between Method 1 and Method 2 within each measurement source (DL and AP). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp.), with a two‑tailed p‑value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Sample size consideration

An a priori sample size calculation was performed based on preliminary data for HKAA absolute error from the testing set (DL: 0.15°±0.14°; AP: 0.21°±0.17°). To detect this difference with 80% power and a two-sided alpha of 0.05, a minimum of 106 lower extremities was required. Our external validation set of 150 extremities provided adequate statistical power for the primary comparative analyses.

Results

Cohort characteristics and measurement reliability

The baseline characteristics of the training, validation, testing, and external validation datasets are detailed in Table 1S. The cohorts exhibited expected variations in alignment parameters, with the external set showing a slightly more valgus overall alignment profile (mean HKAA 177.2°) compared to the primary datasets (~ 175°), underscoring its utility for testing generalizability. Excellent measurement reliability was established. As shown in Table 2S, intra-observer agreement for the attending physician (AP-1 vs. AP-2) was excellent (ICC ≥ 0.879) for all parameters. Inter-observer agreement between the two senior specialists (SS-A vs. SS-B) was also excellent (ICC ≥ 0.928). Most importantly, the agreement between the DL model predictions and the ground truth (SS-M) was excellent across all fifteen parameters (ICC ≥ 0.925), confirming the model’s ability to replicate expert consensus. Bland-Altman analyses for key parameters (HKAA, mLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA) in the external validation set are presented in Figure S1, demonstrating minimal bias and narrow 95% limits of agreement.

Precision of anatomical landmark detection

The DL model demonstrated high spatial precision in localizing the requisite anatomical landmarks. On the testing set, the mean Euclidean error between AI-predicted landmarks and specialist annotations ranged from 0.7 ± 0.4 mm for the medial tibial plateau point (MPTP) to 1.3 ± 0.6 mm for the lateral lowest tibial plateau point (LLPTP), as detailed in Table 3S. Errors for region-of-interest (ROI) localization were slightly larger, as expected, ranging from 3.6 mm (ankle) to 5.0 mm (hip). These sub-millimeter to low-millimeter level errors in landmark detection form the critical foundation for subsequent high-accuracy angular measurements.

Accuracy of automated parameter measurement

The primary results for parameter measurement accuracy are summarized in Table 1 (absolute errors) and Table 4S (mean signed errors). On the independent external validation dataset—the most stringent test of generalizability—the DL model achieved outstanding performance. Absolute errors for parameters central to knee-phenotype classification (HKAA, mLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA) ranged narrowly from 0.07° to 0.45°. Specifically, for HKAA, the gold-standard overall alignment parameter, the DL model’s absolute error was only 0.07° ± 0.06°, significantly lower than the AP’s error of 0.17° ± 0.13° (p < 0.001).

When benchmarked against the attending physician (AP), the DL model showed statistically superior or equivalent performance for the majority of parameters. It had significantly smaller absolute errors for HKAA, WBLR, and mLDFA on both the testing and external sets (p < 0.01). On the external set, its superiority extended to mLPFA, mMPTA, JLCA, KJLO, and WBLKJLt (p < 0.001). The AP maintained a small but significant advantage only for mLDTA and AJLO across both datasets, suggesting the talar dome landmarks used for ankle axis definition may present a unique challenge for the current model configuration.

Comparative analysis of tibial joint-line definitions

A key finding of this study was the consistent superiority of Method 2 (edge‑points) over Method 1 (lowest‑points). As evidenced in Table 1, for the DL model, almost all parameters derived from Method 2 (mMPTA2, JLCA2, KJLO2, WBLKJLt2) exhibited significantly smaller absolute errors than their Method 1 counterparts (p ≤ 0.005). This pattern was also observed for the AP’s manual measurements on the testing set for JLCA, mMPTA, KJLO, and WBLKJLt (p < 0.02). The average errors (Table 4S) further confirmed that Method 2 yielded measurements with a smaller bias relative to the ground truth.

Clinical failure rate analysis

The proportion of measurements exceeding the clinically significant error threshold (≥ 2° or ≥ 2%) is a critical safety metric. Results are presented in Table 2. The DL model’s failure rates were uniformly low (0% to 5.7% across all parameters/datasets). Importantly, on the external validation set, the DL model had a significantly lower failure rate than the AP for WBLR (0% vs. 10.7%, p < 0.001), JLCA1 (2.7% vs. 8.7%, p = 0.025), and KJLO1 (1.3% vs. 6.0%, p = 0.032). For the AP, Method 2 was associated with significantly lower failure rates than Method 1 for several parameters (e.g., JLCA, mMPTA on both sets, p < 0.05), whereas the DL model’s failure rate did not differ significantly between methods, remaining low for both.

Unparalleled efficiency gains

The DL model delivered a transformative reduction in analysis time. Manual measurement by a senior specialist required 220.1 ± 24.8 s per image on the external set, and the attending physician required 281.5 ± 31.7 s. In stark contrast, the DL model completed the full suite of fifteen measurements in 24.3 ± 0.7 s. This represents a 91% time saving compared to the AP and an 89% saving compared to the specialist (p < 0.001). This efficiency enables high-throughput analysis previously impractical in clinical or research settings.

Performance in deformity localization

The model’s ability to correctly identify the presence and location of coronal plane deformities is shown in Table 3. Diagnostic accuracy was high for major alignment parameters. For HKAA, accuracy was 99.2% (testing) and 98.0% (external). For mLDFA, accuracy was 95.1% and 96.7%, respectively. For mMPTA and JLCA, accuracies ranged from 78.7% to 96.7% across the two definitions and datasets, generally matching or exceeding the performance of the attending physician. This demonstrates the model’s utility not only for measurement but also for preliminary diagnostic categorization.

Discussion

This study successfully achieves its dual aims: first, by introducing a comprehensive and robust deep-learning framework that fully automates the complex assessment of coronal lower-extremity alignment with expert-level accuracy and revolutionary efficiency; and second, by resolving a pertinent methodological ambiguity through empirical evidence that identifies the edge-based definition of the tibial joint line as the superior choice for automated measurement systems. Our model’s performance, validated on a rigorously independent dataset, addresses key limitations of prior works and marks a significant advance toward clinically deployable AI in orthopedic radiology.

Advancing beyond prior deep-learning models

Earlier DL applications in this domain have been pioneering yet constrained. Models by Pei et al. [17], Tack et al. [18], and Nguyen et al. [19] were largely limited to measuring HKAA, providing a narrow view of alignment. Moreover, they were predominantly validated on internal test sets, leaving generalizability in question. Schock et al. [20] employed an external dataset but reported a wider error range for HKAA (-2.63° to 2.89°). Simon et al. [21] developed a more comprehensive model but reported high failure rates (1.4–39.8%) on external validation, indicating unreliable performance surpassing manual error margins. As detailed in Tables 4 and 5, our model demonstrates clear advancements: it measures a comprehensive panel of 15 parameters, maintains low absolute errors (0.07°–0.45°) and failure rates (0–4.7%) on a true external validation set, and does so with a speed unattainable by human experts. This combination of breadth, accuracy, robustness, and speed has not been previously achieved. Recent reviews on AI-assisted musculoskeletal imaging and standardized alignment analysis further support the clinical value of automated tools [30–34].

Several factors likely contribute to this enhanced performance. First, the quality of the training data is paramount; our ground truth was established by highly experienced specialists with excellent inter-rater agreement, providing a consistent learning target. Second, our use of YOLOv5 for direct landmark detection, rather than a segmentation-based approach, simplifies the learning objective. The model focuses solely on accurately locating specific points, a task for which modern object detectors are highly optimized, avoiding the computational overhead and potential propagation of errors from pixel-wise segmentation masks. Third, the extensive data augmentation strategy enhanced the model’s ability to generalize across variations in patient positioning, body habitus, and initial image contrast.

The superiority of the edge-based joint line definition (method 2)

The systematic comparison of tibial joint-line definitions yields a practically important conclusion. While Method 1 (lowest points) has a clear anatomical rationale tied to the articular surface, its reliable identification is notoriously challenging, especially in early osteoarthritis where cartilage loss is minimal or in knees with osteophyte formation that obscures the true nadir of the plateau. This difficulty is reflected in the literature and in our data by the typically lower inter-observer ICC for JLCA1. Method 2 (medial/lateral edges) utilizes points that are more consistently identifiable on radiographs for both human annotators and DL algorithms. They represent stable bony contours less susceptible to the ambiguities of articular cartilage visualization. Consequently, measurements derived from Method 2, such as mMPTA2 and JLCA2, exhibited significantly smaller errors and lower failure rates. Future prospective studies are needed to validate whether Method 2 improves correlation with clinical outcomes, including OA progression, postoperative function, and implant survival after osteotomy or TKA.

Clinical implications and practical utility

The clinical implications of this DL model are substantial for daily orthopedic practice. It can be integrated into preoperative planning for osteotomy and total knee arthroplasty, provide rapid alignment reports in outpatient clinics, and enable large‑scale knee phenotyping for epidemiologic research. The dramatic time saving makes detailed alignment analysis feasible in busy clinical settings. For research, this tool enables large‑scale phenotyping of knee osteoarthritis from existing radiographic databases, facilitating studies on the prevalence of different alignment phenotypes, their natural history, and their relationship to treatment outcomes. It also provides a standardized measurement method for multicenter trials.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be addressed. First, while our external validation used a different scanner and time period, all data originated from two academic centers. Broader validation across community hospitals, diverse geographic populations, and a wider array of imaging systems is essential to confirm universal robustness. Second, the model currently processes 2D radiographs. Coronal alignment is a 3D construct, and future integration with lateral radiographs or CT scans to account for sagittal and axial plane parameters would yield a more complete assessment. Third, the sub-optimal performance on mLDTA/AJLO points to a need for refining the ankle-joint modeling, perhaps by exploring alternative landmark definitions for the talar dome. Finally, as a purely imaging-based tool, it is one component of a holistic clinical decision that must incorporate patient symptoms, physical exam, and other diagnostic data. The logical next step is the development of an integrated diagnostic support system that fuses multimodal data—imaging, demographics, gait analysis—to provide comprehensive patient-specific recommendations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed and rigorously validated a deep-learning model that sets a new standard for the automated, comprehensive evaluation of coronal lower-extremity alignment. It delivers specialist-level measurement accuracy across a broad parameter set, coupled with a greater than 90% reduction in analysis time. Furthermore, this study provides definitive evidence that an edge-based definition of the tibial joint line is more reliable and accurate than a lowest-point-based definition when employed within an automated measurement pipeline. This framework represents a significant technological leap with immediate potential to enhance the precision, efficiency, and scalability of alignment assessment in both orthopedic clinical practice and epidemiological research.
