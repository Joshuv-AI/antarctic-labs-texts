Learning Locally, Revising Globally: Global Reviser for Federated Learning with Noisy Labels
Yuxin Tian 1 Mouxing Yang 1 Yuhao Zhou 1 Jian Wang 1 Qing Ye 1 Tongliang Liu 2 Gang Niu 3 Jiancheng Lv 1 1 Sichuan University, 2 University of Sydney, 3 Southeast University {cs.yuxintian}@outlook.com {lvjiancheng}@scu.edu.cn

Abstract
The success of most federated learning (FL) methods heavily depends on label quality, which is often inaccessible in real-world scenarios, such as medicine, leading to the federated label-noise (F-LN) problem. In this study, we observe that the global model of FL memorizes the noisy labels slowly. Based on the observations, we propose a novel approach dubbed Global Reviser for Federated Learning with Noisy Labels (FedGR) to enhance the label-noise robustness of FL. In brief, FedGR employs three novel modules to achieve noisy label sniffing and refining, local knowledge revising, and local model regularization. Specifically, the global model is adopted to infer local data proxies for global sample selection and refine incorrect labels. To maximize the utilization of local knowledge, we leverage the global model to revise the local exponential moving average (EMA) model of each client and distill it into the clients’ models. Additionally, we introduce a global-to-local representation regularization to mitigate the overfitting of noisy labels. Extensive experiments on three F-LNL benchmarks against seven baseline methods demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed FedGR.

Introduction

Federated learning (FL) aims to collaboratively train a global model across multiple clients without requiring data centralization, supporting numerous privacy-sensitive applications such as health-care
Kaissis et al. 2020
and recommendation system
Sun et al. 2024
.
Despite the promising performance of existing FL methods
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020b
, their success heavily relies on high-quality annotated data.
In practice, it is daunting and even impossible to precisely annotate all the clients’ data
Irvin et al. 2019
, thus inevitably leading to the so-called federated label-noise (F-LN) problem
Yang et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2022
.
Unlike the centralized label-noise problem
Han et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2020a
, F-LN is more challenging due to the noise heterogeneity.
More specifically, different clients would suffer from different noisy patterns (
e.g.
, label-noise ratios and types)
Wu et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2024
, which would prevent the direct application of existing centralized label-noise-oriented methods.
Therefore, it is highly expected to customize the approach to tackle the F-LN problem.

To address the F-LN problem, one straightforward solution for federated learning with noisy labels (F-LNL) is performing client-specific sample selection
Wang et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2024
by modeling the noisy pattern
Arazo et al. 2019
of each client.
However, the client-specific sample selection approaches lead to unreliable modeling results due to the data heterogeneity
Kim et al. 2022
;
Tam et al. 2023
and label-noise heterogeneity
Wu et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2024
, thereby achieving limited label-noise robustness.
To alleviate this issue, recent studies propose aggregating client-specific data proxies, such as class centers
Rolnick et al. 2017
and noise-related statistics
Li et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023
, for noise modeling and filtering from a global perspective.
Although achieving favorable results, these proxy-based approaches have an incentive to expose the information of clients
Yang et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2022
, thus struggling to find a balance between the effectiveness of noise modeling and the privacy protection requirement of FL.

Different from the existing studies that seek appropriate client-specific data proxies for noise modeling
Arazo et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023
, we observe that the global model could serve as an ideal auxiliary for noise modeling and sample selection.
Specifically, as shown in
Fig.

1
, the global model demonstrates less label-noise overfitting compared to the centrally trained model over long training epochs.
Thus, the desirable characteristic of the global model could be leveraged to improve the label-noise robustness of FL while alleviating the privacy exposure risks, which however has not been explored so far to the best of our knowledge.

Based on the above discussions and observations, in this paper, we propose a novel method dubbed Global Reviser for Federated Learning with Noisy Labels (FedGR).
The proposed FedGR consists of the sniffing-then-refining, global revised exponential moving average (EMA) distillation, and global representation regularization modules.
In detail, the sniffing-then-refining first computes the instance-wise selection criteria by resorting to the global model, then performs global noise filtering and label refinement.
After that, to leverage all the data of each client, the global revised EMA distillation module with revised EMA model parameters derived from the global model is employed.
Finally, global representation regularization is adopted to regularize the local model’s representation for further alleviating the overfitting issue of label errors during local model training.
The contributions and novelties of this work can be outlined as follows:

•

We provide an insightful observation that the global model of FL fits noisy labels slowly and learns underlying correct knowledge. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first studies to reveal such a characteristic of FL. This observation motivates us to propose an F-LNL approach with the support of the global model.

•

By effectively leveraging the characteristics of the global model, we propose a novel method called FedGR. FedGR first infers the selection criteria for the subsequent global noise modeling and then refines the noisy labels by resorting to the global model. Then, we leverage the global model to “revise” the local EMA model of each client and distill its knowledge to the local model, thereby maximizing the utilization of local knowledge across all clients. Finally, FedGR involves global-to-local representation regularization to diminish the impact of label errors further.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on three F-LNL datasets against seven baselines to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed FedGR. Furthermore, we benchmark these methods under diverse federated label-noise situations, including label-noise and data heterogeneity, making the study more reflective of real-world situations.

2
Related Work

2.1
Centralized Learning with Noisy Labels

The vanilla Cross Entropy (CE) loss has been proved to overfit label-noise data easily
Zhang et al. 2017
.
To address it, most of the centralized learning with noisy labels (C-LNL) studies leverage the
memorization effect

Arpit et al. 2017
to formulate robust training strategies for sample selection and noisy label correction.
For instance, Co-teaching
Han et al. 2018
and its improvements
Yu et al. 2019
utilize the small-loss trick to sieve noisy labels and adopt two peer networks to alleviate accumulated sample selection errors.
Another seminal work DivideMix
Li et al. 2020a
incorporates MixMatch
Berthelot et al. 2019
for noisy label refurbishment and obtains exceptional performance even under high label-noise rate.
The latest C-LNL research
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
also draws considerable inspiration from these earlier efforts.

However, it is undesirable for FL clients to adopt these C-LNL methods for label-noise robustness directly.
First, the data heterogeneity
Li et al. 2022
of FL makes the class-balanced assumption used by almost all the C-LNL approaches unattainable.
Furthermore, these methods induce sophisticated learning algorithms, such as two peer networks
Han et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020a
, which involve extra computational overhead for FL.
In contrast, FedGR performs sample sieving on the server instead of each client itself which mitigates the underlying impacts of the data heterogeneity.
Additionally, FedGR introduces a modest computational overhead (refer to the appendix) while achieving promising robustness against noisy labels (see
Section

4
).

2.2
Federated Learning with Noisy Labels

There is no doubt that low-quality labels would impair the performance and robustness of FL.
To alleviate this, the first research line is to adopt label noise modeling
Arazo et al. 2019
with noise-distinguishable information for sample selection or client detection.
For instance, FedNoiL
Wang et al. 2022
and FedFixer
Ji et al. 2024
adopt the confidence score and regularized loss
Cheng et al. 2021
for client-specific sample selection, respectively.
However, as the label-noise patterns of clients are heterogeneous, some methods aggregate the client’s data proxies, such as local intrinsic dimension
Xu et al. 2022
, prediction uncertainty
Lu et al. 2024
, and class-wise loss
Wu et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2024
for malicious client detection or the peer client’s model
Kim et al. 2022
;
Tam et al. 2023
, class centers
Yang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
, and the Gaussian Mixture Model
Li et al. 2024
for client-specific sample selection.
Additionally, recent studies adopt local regularization to achieve label-noise robustness, such as the mixup
Jiang et al. 2022
or local noise transition matrix estimation
Zhou and Wang 2024
;
Li et al. 2021
.

However, these F-LNL approaches are affected by defects of one kind or another.
Client-specific noise modeling
Arazo et al. 2019
based on memorization effect
Ji et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2022
is unreliable for the clients with difficult label-noise patterns.
And, detecting the clients
Jiang et al. 2022
with noisy labels is far enough, since they sacrifice somewhat useful data and thus do not achieve limited results.
Furthermore, the methods that gather client-specific data proxies may cause privacy leakage, as they transmit high-risk local information
Yang et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Tam et al. 2023
.
Unlike them, we observe that the global model fits noisy labels slowly and this motivates us to continuously leverage this characteristic to achieve label-noise robustness via global noise modeling, incorrect label refining, local knowledge revising and distillation, and client’s model regularization.
The extensive experiments confirm the effectiveness of the proposed FedGR (see
Section

4
).

3
Method

3.1
Problem Definition

The F-LN problem is practical and challenging, as label errors are inevitable.
To address it, the F-LNL approaches intend to collaboratively train an applicable global model with noisy labels.
Following
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2022
, a typical FL system maintains one central server for model aggregation and up to
K
clients which would train models on its local dataset
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\}^{n_{k}}_{i=1}
and upload the trained models via an unstable network.
The
\hat{y}_{i}
and
n_{k}
denote the one-hot vector of the given label and the size of the local dataset on client
k
, respectively.
Formally, the global objective
\mathcal{L}
of F-LNL could be the same as FL

\argmin_{\mathbf{w}_{g}}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}{a_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{k},\text{ and }\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}a_{k}=1\,,

(1)

which is the composition of each local objective
\mathcal{L}_{k}
.
The
a_{k}
and
\mathcal{S}
denote the weight of the
k
-th client’s objective function and the set of all clients, respectively.
The
\mathcal{L}_{k}
upon its local dataset
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
with loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
is

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[\ell\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l},\hat{y}_{i}\right)\right]

(2)

where the logit
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l}
output by backbone
f(\cdot)
and the head
h(\cdot)
is

\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l}=h\circ f(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w}_{k})\,.

(3)

In practice, the global model parameters
\mathbf{w}_{g}^{t}
at
t
-th communication round
1
1

1

We use communication round and round interchangeably.
is the importance weighted average of the local model parameters which are successfully received by the server due to the unreliable network and communication overhead:

\mathbf{w}^{t}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}\left(t\right)}a_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{t},\text{ and }\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}\left(t\right)}a_{k}=1

(4)

where
\mathcal{S}\left(t\right)\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is the successfully participated client set at round
t
.

Based on our observations, the global model of FL tends to overfit label-noise samples slowly.
We propose leveraging this characteristic to infer the selection criteria for the global noise modeling,
i.e.,
centralized sieving (CS), and perform label refinement.
Then, we introduce a global revised EMA distillation method to further utilize local knowledge, improving the performance of the global model.
Additionally, the global model can also act as a regularizer to prevent the local model from fitting incorrect labels.
Importantly, our approach focuses solely on improving local training without altering the FL model aggregation process.
To sum up, the local objective consists of the sniffing-then-refining objective
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{SR}
, the global revised EMA distillation
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, and the global representation regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{k}
:

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{SR}+\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{B}_{k}+\lambda_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{R}_{k}\,,

(5)

where
\lambda_{\mathcal{R}}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}
are the corresponding hyperparameters.
All of these components will be elaborated on in the following.

3.2
Sniffing-then-Refining

With the noisy labels, we divide the local learning on these given labels into two phases by following
Han et al. 2018
.
The first one is acquired for label-noise sniffing and global model warmup which is named label-noise sniffing, whilst the second one is used to refine the labels of the estimated noisy set, which is called label refining (LR).
To avoid the adverse impact of data and label-noise heterogeneity, we adopt a sample selection method named centralized sieving (CS) in the first phase, which sieves the noisy data on the server with privacy protection.
According to our observation, we put to use the global model along with these two phases for better sample selection and label refinement.

Phase I: Label-noise Sniffing

For an arbitrary FL system, the server cannot access whether a participated client is label-noise, how many label-noise samples it owns, and even which sample has the wrong label at the early training stage.
Therefore, at round
t
, the client
k
can only perform naive supervised learning with cross entropy
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
as loss function on its corresponding dataset
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}

\mathcal{L}_{k}^{SR}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s},\hat{y}_{i}\right)\right],\text{ if }t<\alpha

(6)

where the output logit
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s}
of the strong augmented image
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{s}
is

\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s}=h\circ f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{s};\mathbf{w}_{k}^{t}\right)\,.

(7)

To sniff and approximate the noise details of each client, we adopt label-noise sniffing for
\alpha
rounds and propose a global label-noise sieving method,
i.e.,
centralized sieving.
Notably, to sniff the label-noise situations of the whole FL system,
\mathcal{S}\left(t\right)
is randomly sampled without replacement in this stage, which is different from the usual FL setup
Xu et al. 2022
;
McMahan et al. 2017
.

Different from client-specific noise filtering, one straightforward idea is to perform sample sieving on the server.
More specifically, the successfully participated client
k
at round
t
can upload the instance-wise metrics as the selection criteria to the server.
After successfully receiving these metrics, the server could conduct the Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) for sample selection with an appropriate and considerable number of sample metrics which is much more than that on a single client.
In this paper, we consider the selection criteria that 1) is less affected by the overfitting of noisy labels and 2) is guaranteed that the server or peer client cannot derivative any private information from it.
Thus, we adopt the mean loss over the number of successfully participated rounds with the global model as the selection criteria.

Specifically, at
t
-th round, given the loss observations
L_{i}^{t}=\{\ell_{i}^{p}\}_{p=1}^{T_{k}}
of
i
-th sample at the
k
-th clients, where
k\in\mathcal{S}\left(t\right)
and
T_{k}
is the number of the successfully participated rounds of client
k
, the mean of these loss observations could be

\bar{\ell}_{i}=\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{p=1}^{T_{k}}\ell_{i}^{p}\,.

(8)

The latest loss observation is

\ell_{i}^{T_{k}}=\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{g},\hat{y}_{i}\right),\text{ where }\mathbf{p}_{i}^{g}=h\circ f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w}_{g}^{t-1}\right)\,.

(9)

The above procedures would be conducted before local training and the instance-wise metrics
\{\bar{\ell}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
would be uploaded along with the local model parameters to the server at the end of local training.
The server will utilize these distinguishable label-noise statistics for GMM selection.
Then, by sending GMM results back to clients, the server and the clients could access these label-noise details according to the GMM results.
In other words, the sample selection results such as
r_{k}
and which sample is label-noise would be sent back to the client
k
when it successfully participated once again in the following rounds.

Phase II: Label Refining

After sniffing the label-noise situation of each client at early
\alpha
rounds, the client could step into a label refining phase to refine the local dataset with the usual FL client sampling.
With the GMM results, the client
k
could divide its local dataset
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
into a clean set
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}
and a label-noise set
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{n}
.
Yet, such a splitting cannot be precisely and perfectly, especially for difficult label-noise patterns
Wu et al. 2023
.
In other words, some clean samples would be divided into the label-noise set.
Based on our observation, we then perform a label refinement to simultaneously relabel and furbish local dataset.
Then, the refined dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
on client
k
will be used for local training:

\mathcal{L}_{k}^{SR}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s},\tilde{y}_{i}\right)\right],\text{ if }t\geq\alpha

(10)

where
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s}
is the output logit of the strong augmented image
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{s}

\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s}=h\circ f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{s};\mathbf{w}_{k}^{t}\right)\,.

(11)

Here, we elaborate on the label refining strategy.
Following the pseudo strategy of
Sohn et al. 2020
, we first conduct pseudo labeling on client
k
at the beginning of round
t
with the global model:

\displaystyle\forall\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i}\right)\in\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k},

(12)

\displaystyle y_{i}^{pse}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{T}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{g,w}\right),&\max\left(\sigma\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{g,w}\right)\right)>\epsilon\\
&\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C},&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

where

\mathbf{p}_{i}^{g,w}=h\circ f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{w};\mathbf{w}_{g}^{t-1}\right)\,,

(13)

and

\mathcal{T}(\cdot)=\text{onehot}(\argmax(\cdot))\,.

(14)

The
\sigma(\cdot)
,
C
, and
\epsilon
refer to
softmax(\cdot)
function,
\mathcal{T}(\cdot)
, the number of classes, and the confidence threshold, respectively.
In this study, the confidence threshold
\epsilon
is set to 0.9.
Then, due to the ever-changing label-noise patterns, we argue that the label refinement should also be conditioned on the estimated label-noise ratio
r_{k}
and thus the refining strategy could be as follows:

\tilde{y}_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{y}_{i},&r_{k}<\beta\text{ and }\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}\\
&q_{i}\hat{y}_{i}+(1-q_{i})y_{i}^{pse},&r_{k}<\beta\text{ and }\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{n}\\
&y_{i}^{pse},&r_{k}\geq\beta\\
\end{aligned}\right.\,

(15)

where
\beta
is a hyperparameter which is set to 0.8.
Such a strategy is based on the following two considerations.
On the one hand, the splitting results of the client with easy label-noise patterns are relatively reliable and we refine the label-noise set guided by clean probability
q_{i}
of GMM, following
Li et al. 2020a
.
On the other hand, some clients would suffer from high label-noise (
r_{k}>0.8
) or challenging label-noise type (asymmetric) and we consider none of these given labels to be trustworthy and only use the pseudo labels as the refined labels.

To sum up, the training objective of Sniffing-then-Refining is

\mathcal{L}_{k}^{SR}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s},\hat{y}_{i}\right)\right],&t<\alpha\\

\end{aligned}\right.\,.

3.3
Global Revised EMA Distillation

Only using the clean set is far from enough and the knowledge carried by noisy data cannot be overlooked.
To maximize the usage of data of every client, we further perform global revised EMA distillation to improve the performance of the global model by distilling the local knowledge to the client’s model.
Firstly, we adopt a local EMA model as a local knowledge container.
Then, following our observation, we utilize the global model to both bootstrap and revise the local EMA model.

Firstly, to distill the relatively correct knowledge instead of the label-noise knowledge during local training, the activation of this distillation is conditioned on the learning status of the global model

\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&t<\delta\\
&\lambda_{\mathcal{B}},&t\geq\delta\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

where
\delta
is a hyperparameter.
Then, the local EMA model
\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t}
on client
k
can be bootstrapped with global model

\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t,1}=\mathbf{w}_{g}^{t-1},\text{ if }t=\delta\,.

(18)

Finally, we perform KL logit distillation to leverage these local knowledge via local training:

\mathcal{B}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[KL\left(\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}^{le,w}}{\tau},\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}^{l,s}}{\tau}\right)\right]\,,

(19)

where

\mathbf{p}_{i}^{le,w/s}=h\circ f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{w/s};\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t}\right)\,.

(20)

Apart from bootstrapping, we also resort to the global model “revising” the parameters of the local EMA model which could implicitly alleviate the negative impact of noisy labels, according to our observation.
The whole EMA revising procedure is visualized in
Fig.

3
.
To be specific, differing from vanilla EMA updating on client
k
at
m_{k}
-th train step with momentum decay
\gamma_{l}
:

\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t,m_{k}}=\gamma_{l}\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t,m_{k}-1}+(1-\gamma_{l})\mathbf{w}_{k}^{t,m_{k}}\,,

(21)

we employ the global model to update the local EMA weights with decay
\gamma_{g}
which is conditioned on the situations of label-noise ratio and label refinement.
In other words, if a client suffers from a high label-noise ratio and cannot re-label sufficient samples, its corresponding local EMA model may be unreliable.
Consequently, at round
t
,
\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t-1}
on client
k
can be updated before local training start with following:

\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t,1}=\gamma_{g}\mathbf{w}_{k,ema}^{t-1}+(1-\gamma_{g})\mathbf{w}_{g}^{t-1}

(22)

where the momentum decay on global model
\gamma_{g}
is

\gamma_{g}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\kappa,&r_{k}<\beta\text{ and }t\geq\delta\\
&0,&\left(r_{k}\geq\beta\text{ and }\frac{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}|}{|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}|}<\mu\right)\text{ or }t<\delta\\
\end{aligned}\right.\,.

(23)

The
{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}|}/{|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}|}
and
\mu
refers to the proportion of successfully refined samples and its corresponding hyperparameter which is set to 0.5 in this study.

3.4
Global Representation Regularization

In line with our observation, we also propose global representation regularization to prevent the overfitting of wrong labels during local training.
According to our observation, the representation of the global model
f(\cdot;\mathbf{w}_{g}^{\left(t-1\right)})
is less affected by label errors.
Therefore, we straightforwardly distill the representation of the global model to the client’s local model.
Unlike the vanilla distillation, we adopt an instance discriminative-like task as the goal of regularization, inspired by
Chen and He 2021
;
Sohn et al. 2020
;
Lubana et al. 2022
.
Specifically, the global representation of a weak augmented image should be consistent with the local representation of the strong augmented image.
Formally, the objective of regularization on
k
-th client at
t
-th round can be

\mathcal{R}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[KL\left(\frac{f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{w};\mathbf{w}_{g}^{t-1}\right)}{\tau},\frac{f\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{s};\mathbf{w}_{k}^{t}\right)}{\tau}\right)\right]\,.

(24)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{w/s}
,
KL
, and
\tau
denote the weak/strong augmented image, the KL loss function, and the temperature that is set to 0.5, respectively.

5
Conclusion

In this study, we observe that the global model of FL tends to overfit noise labels slowly.
Based on this, we propose FedGR, a label-noise robust FL approach by leveraging such a characteristic to compute the selection criteria for global noise modeling, refine the noisy labels, revise the client’s EMA model for subsequent local knowledge distillation, and regularize the local training of FL clients.
The results on three F-LNL benchmarks confirm the effectiveness of the proposed FedGR under intertwined F-LNL settings.

Currently, FedGR does not consider dynamic participation, whereby clients could join the FL system according to their willingness.
Additionally, the fairness of F-LNL, which examines whether clean clients compromise their performance to improve malicious clients, is not considered. Future work will focus on these aspects.

Implementation Details.

This study adopts a model consisting of a network backbone
f(\cdot)
and a linear classification head
h(\cdot)
for all experiments.
Due to the randomness of the data partition, the experiments in CIFAR-10/100 are carried out three times with various random seeds (1, 13, and 42) and the experiments in Clothing1M are carried out with seed 1.
The entire hyperparameter configurations FedGR uses are listed in

These hyperparameters are divided into three groups, namely the parameters for FL setups, the optimization configurations of the client’s local training, and the specific hyperparameters for the proposed FedGR.
Notably, all the baselines adopt the same optimization configurations.

Hyperparameters of Baselines.

Our code repository implements the typical centralized learning with noisy labels (C-LNL) approaches (FL-Coteaching
Han et al. 2018
, FL-DivideMix
Li et al. 2020a
).
For the robust label-noise F-LNL methods, we use their official implementations for reproduction of results.Some common hyperparameters, such as the optimization configurations, are shown in
Table

7
.
We will briefly describe the specific hyperparameters these baselines use by adopting the same mathematical notations used in their papers.

•

FedProx

Li et al. 2020b
is proposed to tackle the data heterogeneity
Li et al. 2022
by introducing a model parameter proximal term to the local learning objective with hyperparameter
\mu_{prox}
. In this study, we follow FedCorr
Xu et al. 2022
to set
\mu_{prox}=1
for all experiments.

•

FL-Coteaching.
Following the original settings
Han et al. 2018
, we let the decay of the client-specific sample drop rate be
R(T)=1-\tau\cdot\{T^{c}/T_{k},1\}
with
c=1
,
T_{k}=25
, where
T
denotes the
T
-th communication round and the client-specific noise level
\tau
is known to be the expectation of the uniform distribution.
Formally, we let
\tau=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{k}|\rho_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\rho_{min},\rho_{max}\right)\right]
for the experiments on CIFAR-10/100
Krizhevsky and Hinton 2009
. For Clothing1M
Xiao et al. 2015
, we set
\tau=0.39
, as it contains natural label noise in a ratio of 39.46%.

As we discussed in related work, directly adopting the C-LNL methods to the local training of the FL would introduce additional computational overhead.
Here, we provide an intuitive computational analysis, shown in
Table

8
.
In this comparison, the communication cost is split into two parts, “server
\rightarrow
client” and “client
\rightarrow
server”, while the computation cost is split into three other parts, “forward”, “backward”, and “other cost”.
The “total cos” aggregates “forward” and “backward” cost and “other cost” denotes the computation overhead induced by diverse label-noise robust F-LNL methods.

Discussions.

According to
Table

8
, both FL-Coteaching and FL-DivideMix, which are the typical C-LNL methods (Coteaching
Han et al. 2018
and DivideMix
Li et al. 2020a
) implemented under FL, increase the additional communication and computation overhead.
The label-noise robust FL methods, such as FedCorr
Xu et al. 2022
, FedNoro
Wu et al. 2023
, FedFixer
Ji et al. 2024
and FedGR, are still inevitable to introduce extra communication and computation overhead.
However, these extra costs are much less than the typical C-LNL methods implemented under FL.
Specifically, the transmitted information and the number of forward and backward passes are much less.
As for the proposed FedGR, though it slightly increases the communication burden due to
D>N
and
D>C
and requires one more forward pass compared against FedCorr
Xu et al. 2022
and FedNoRo
