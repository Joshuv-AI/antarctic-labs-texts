Mycobacterium bovis induces cellular oxidative stress via the host protein SH3PXD2B
Authors: Donghui Liu, Ting Zhang, Zhengzhong Xu, Chengkun Zheng, Xin’an Jiao, Xiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterium bovis (M. bovis) is a major pathogen that causes zoonotic tuberculosis. It is transmitted via aerosols, colonizes the lungs of the host and causes oxidative stress in macrophages, resulting in tissue damage. The mechanisms underlying the oxidative damage to cells induced by this infection have not yet been fully elucidated. This study investigated the effects of SH3PXD2B on the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant mechanisms during M. bovis infection. The results showed that SH3PXD2B expression was significantly upregulated after M. bovis infection, and promoted the production of cellular NOX-dependent ROS and the lipid peroxidation marker MDA. Additionally, overexpression of SH3PXD2B inhibited the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and reduced the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, SOD, and GPx. Conversely, knockdown of SH3PXD2B exerted the opposite effect and improved cell viability. In summary, SH3PXD2B orchestrates M. bovis-induced oxidative stress in macrophages through a dual mechanism: amplifying ROS production while simultaneously crippling the host’s antioxidant capacity. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into M. bovis pathogenesis and suggest that SH3PXD2B may be a potential molecular target for future research into disease resistance in cattle and the development of new therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05582-w.Keywords: Mycobacterium bovis, SH3PXD2B, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Bovine tuberculosis is a serious zoonotic disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium bovis (M. bovis). With the number of infected cattle continuing to rise, particularly in developing countries, the disease is inflicting significant economic losses on the livestock industry and posing a serious threat to public health [1, 2]. M. bovis is primarily transmitted via aerosols and can colonize the lungs after entering the body, with macrophages being one of its main host cells. After phagocytosing pathogenic microorganisms, macrophages generate large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through a process known as “respiratory burst”; this process is crucial for killing intracellular pathogens [3]. However, at high concentrations, ROS exhibit strong oxidative toxicity. As well as promoting lipid peroxidation, they can also damage macromolecules such as nucleic acids and amino acids through oxidation. This can directly impair organelles such as mitochondria, and induce various biological changes including apoptosis, necrosis, and pyroptosis [4]. Furthermore, while ROS have a killing effect on pathogenic microorganisms, M. bovis has evolved various mechanisms to evade the effects of ROS [5, 6]. M. bovis infection is often accompanied by cellular redox imbalance and host immune dysfunction: Upon infection, immune cells, particularly macrophages, rapidly produce excessive ROS at the infection site, creating an oxidative environment. If the host’s antioxidant capacity is insufficient, oxidative stress is triggered, leading to oxidative damage and even cell death [7]. This results in tissue damage and mycobacterial proliferation, thereby facilitating the survival of the mycobacteria and persistent infection [8].

SH3PXD2B has been reported to play a role in cellular ROS production. A study by Gianni et al. demonstrated that SH3PXD2B can recruit NADPH Oxidase 1 (NOX1) to specific sites within invasive pseudopods in colorectal cancer cell lines, and that cells simultaneously produce ROS and degrade the ECM in an SH3PXD2B-dependent manner [9]. Furthermore, additional studies have revealed that SH3PXD2B can interact with the NOXA1 protein [9, 10]. NOXA1 acts as an NOX activator and its primary function is to regulate NOX1-mediated ROS production in cells [11]. Despite its well-documented pro-oxidant role in cancer biology, the involvement of SH3PXD2B in macrophage redox homeostasis and antimicrobial immunity remains entirely unexplored. Therefore, this study further investigates the effects of SH3PXD2B on cellular ROS production and antioxidant mechanisms under M. bovis BCG infection conditions, with the aim of providing a research foundation for understanding the pathogenic mechanisms of M. bovis.

Materials and methods

Bacteria, cells and plasmids

M. bovis BCG (Pasteur), HEK293T cells were stored in our laboratory. Bomac cells were donated by Professor Guo Aizhen from Huazhong Agricultural University. M. bovis BCG was grown in Middlebrook 7H9 medium (BD Diagnostics, USA) supplemented with 10% albumin-dextrose-catalase (ADC) (Difco, USA). Bomac cells, HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Plasmid pCMV-SH3PXD2B (Myc-tag) and pCMV-p22phox-HA were constructed by this study.

Antibodies and compounds

The following antibodies and compounds were used in this study: Rabbit anti-SH3PXD2B polyclonal antibody (C-Term) (abs113628, Absin, China), Myc tag mouse monoclonal antibody (AF0033, Beyotime, China), HA tag mouse monoclonal antibody (AF5057, Beyotime, China), rabbit anti-Nrf2 polyclonal antibody(16396-1-AP, Proteintech, China)rabbit anti-Keap1 polyclonal antibody (10503-2-AP); mouse anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody (A2-F6, HUABio, China), Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated-goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (AS003, ABclonal, China)and HRP-conjugated-goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS014, ABclonal, China); NOX enzyme inhibitor Apocynin (TargetMol, USA) [12], Nrf2 pathway activator tert-Butylhydroquinone (TBHQ) (TargetMol, USA) [13].

SH3PXD2B overexpression and siRNA interference

Cells were plated in 24-well plates and grown to 80% confluence. For bovine SH3PXD2B overexpression, cells were transfected with pCMV-SH3PXD2B or pCMV-Myc (empty vector, EV) using Lip2000 Transfection Reagent (Biosharp, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 800 ng plasmid and 2 µL transfection reagent were individually diluted in 50 µL Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA). The two dilutions were combined, mixed well, and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The mixture was then added to the cells maintained in complete DMEM, and the culture medium was replaced with complete DMEM 12 h later. For siRNA interference, the 40 pmol siRNA or negative control (NC) siRNA was transfected with Lip2000 Transfection Reagent. The siRNA sequences used are listed in Table 1. The siRNAs were synthesized by Angel biotech (Suzhou, China).

BCG infection

Bomac cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 12 h before transfection. Cells were then transfected with 800 ng of pCMV-SH3PXD2B or empty vector, or 40 pmol of siRNA-SH3PXD2B or siRNA-NC (negative control), using Lip2000 transfection reagent. After 24 h, for inhibitor or activator treatment assays, cells were individually pretreated with the corresponding inhibitor or activator for 1 h prior to bacterial infection. Cells were then infected with BCG at an MOI of 10 for 4 h (BCG is fully dispersed by resuspension and vortexing in PBST). Subsequently, the medium was removed and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Then cells were maintained in complete DMEM containing gentamicin for 1 h. After washing, replace the culture medium and continue to cultivate. This time is considered as 0 h. At selected time points, Bomac cells were lysed in 500 µL PBS containing 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100. Tenfold serial dilutions of the lysate were performed, followed by plating on 7H10 agar plates to calculate the intracellular bacterial CFU. The intracellular viability of bacteria is calculated based on CFU of 0 h. And the cell viability was measured using the CCK8 kit (Beyotime, China) according to the instructions.

Total RNA was extracted from the Bomac cells using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China), and cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper). The synthesized cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until further use. qPCR was performed using Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) and run for 40 cycles on a Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 instrument (ThermoFisher, USA). Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from cells by lysis with ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein per sample were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (PALL, USA). According to the pre-stained protein molecular weight marker and the theoretical molecular weight of target proteins, the membranes were neatly cut horizontally between distinct protein bands at appropriate positions. Only the membrane regions corresponding to the target proteins were retained for subsequent blocking and antibody incubation. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies. The membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After membranes were washed three times, images of protein blots were obtained with a ChemiDoc XRS (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) using the Western ECL Substrate kit (Vazyme, China). The density of each band was normalized to that of β-actin.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Co-immunoprecipitation protocol was performed using a Myc-tag or HA-tag Protein IP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). HEK-293T cells were cultured in 6-well plates using DMEM with 10% FBS for 12 h prior to transfection. 4 µg of pCMV-SH3PXD2B or pCMV-p22phox-HA was transfected into cells using Lip2000 Transfection Reagent. Then, the cells were initially rinsed twice in PBS after 48 h. Following this, protein extraction from the cells was obtained by lysing them with ice-cold RIPA buffer, which was supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. A portion of the lysed cell protein sample was designated as the “Input group”, while the remaining sample was mixed with anti-Myc or anti-HA magnetic beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C with slow shaking. The magnetic beads were collected using a magnetic rack, after which the supernatant was discarded and washed three times with TBST. The beads were then collected after the final wash and resuspended in 1× SDS buffer, constituting the test group. The samples were boiled at 100℃ for 15 min and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blots.

Detection of intracellular ROS and MDA

The ROS was determined by a reactive oxygen species assay kit (Beyotime, China) with an oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe (DCFH-DA). The cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA (diluted in serum-free DMEM) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and use a multi-function fluorescence microplate reader to detect the cell fluorescence intensity (parameter settings: excitation wavelength of 488 nm, emission wavelength of 525 nm). For malondialdehyde (MDA) levels: Cell proteins were collected by lysing cells with Western and IP cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), and protein concentration was measured using a BCA kit. The MDA content in the extracted protein samples was determined according to the instructions of the MDA detection kit (Beyotime, China). All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Detection of intracellular CAT, SOD, GPx activity

Cell proteins were collected by lysing cells with Western and IP cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min on ice and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the cell-free supernatants. The BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to determine the concentration of the supernatants.The activity of Catalase (CAT), Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) were determined according to the instructions of the kit (Beyotime, China). All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data represent the mean of three independent biological experiments, with error bars denoting the standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns, P > 0.05; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001.

Results

SH3PXD2B promotes the production of cellular ROS and MDA

The expression level of the SH3PXD2B in Bomac cells following BCG infection was detected using a Western blot assay (Fig. 1A). And the grayscale analysis revealed that, compared with the uninfected group, the expression level of the SH3PXD2B significantly increased in the BCG-infected cells at 6 and 24 h post-infection (Fig. 1B). SH3PXD2B belongs to the p47phox protein superfamily. The p47phox and related proteins are involved in activating NOX in cells and play a crucial role in regulating cellular ROS [14]. Related studies have shown that, as a member of the p47phox superfamily, the SH3PXD2B participates in and regulates the process of guiding and localizing NOX1 to the membrane of podocytes and invading cells, thereby increasing local ROS concentrations [9, 15].

M. bovis infection upregulates SH3PXD2B, driving intracellular ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation. A Expression level of SH3PXD2B in Bomac cells during BCG infection. B Analysis of grayscale values for the relative expression level of SH3PXD2B. C The ROS level of Bomac with overexpression; D The ROS level of Bomac with knockdown. E The MDA content of Bomac with overexpression; F The MDA content of Bomac with knockdown

The ROS level in Bomac cells was detected by using the DCFH-DA probes, and the results showed a significant increase in cellular ROS levels after BCG infection (Fig. 1C, D). This indicates that macrophages generate large amounts of ROS when stimulated by BCG, which is consistent with relevant literature reports [16, 17]. Compared to the EV group, the ROS level in cells overexpressing SH3PXD2B was significantly higher. Additionally, under BCG infection conditions, the ROS level in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group was also significantly higher than that in the EV control group (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the level of cellular ROS in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group was significantly lower than that in the NC control group. Under BCG infection conditions, the level of cellular ROS in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group was significantly lower than in the NC control group (Fig. 1D).

MDA is primarily produced through the decomposition of polyunsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane when it is attacked by ROS and free radicals. It’s a significant indicator of cellular oxidative damage [18]. The MDA content of cells was then measured at different time points after BCG infection. The results indicated that at both 6 h and 24 h post-BCG infection, the MDA content in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group was significantly higher than that in the EV control group (Fig. 1E), This indicates that SH3PXD2B overexpression promotes MDA accumulation in Bomac cells during BCG infection. The MDA content in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group did not differ significantly from the control group at 6 h post-infection, but the MDA content in the cells at 24 h post-infection was significantly lower than that in the NC control group (Fig. 1F). This suggests that knocking down SH3PXD2B alleviates the accumulation of MDA in Bomac cells.

There is an interaction between bovine SH3PXD2B and p22phox

In the resting state, p47phox, along with p40 and p67phox, constitutes the cytoplasmic component of the NOX complex. It only binds to the membrane component p22phox protein when activated, thereby activating NOX and promoting ROS generation. In addition to p47phox, other proteins in this superfamily, such as NOXO1 and SH3PXD2A in this superfamily have also been reported to interact with p22phox [19, 20]. The SH3 domain is the primary domain in the SH3PXD2B protein that exerts regulatory functions. It acts as a docking site for signaling molecules, binding to downstream proteins and regulating their functions. Through sequence analysis, we discovered multiple binding motifs for the SH3 domain (PxxP) in the bovine p22phox protein sequence (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, molecular simulation docking was performed on the bovine SH3PXD2B and p22phox proteins. The results showed that hydrogen bonds could form between the SH3PXD2B protein and the p22phox protein at amino acid residues such as E140, K137, S153, and N154 (Fig. 2B).

To further confirm the interaction between the bovine SH3PXD2B and the p22phox, we amplified the CYBA gene, which encodes the p22phox protein, using Bomac cell cDNA as a template. We then cloned the amplified gene into the pCMV-HA vector to construct the eukaryotic expression plasmid pCMV-p22phox-HA. Then, the plasmids pCMV-p22phox-HA and pCMV-SH3PXD2B were co-transfected in the HEK293T cells. Meanwhile, the group transfected with each plasmid alone and the EV group served as controls for the Co-IP experiment. The results showed that using anti-Myc magnetic beads could pull down p22phox-HA in the co-transfection group. There were no corresponding bands in the pCMV-SH3PXD2B single transfection group or the EV control group (Fig. 2C). Similarly, SH3PXD2B-Myc could be successfully pulled down using anti-HA magnetic beads in the co-transfection group, with no corresponding bands observed in the pCMV-P22phox-HA single transfection group or the EV control group (Fig. 2D). These results confirm an interaction between bovine SH3PXD2B and the NOX structural subunit p22phox.

SH3PXD2B-mediated ROS generation is dependent on NOX activity

The p22phox protein is an important component of the NOX complex that participates in the assembly and function of the membrane components of some NOX complexes (NOX1, NOX2, NOX3, and NOX4), and plays a crucial role in NOX-dependent ROS generation [11, 21]. To verify whether the effect of SH3PXD2B on ROS levels in Bomac cells depends on NOX activity, we overexpressed SH3PXD2B in Bomac cells and treated them with the NOX enzyme inhibitor Apocynin (50 µM). ROS levels in the cells were measured 24 h after stimulation with BCG. The results showed that, with SH3PXD2B overexpression, ROS levels in the Apocynin-treated group were significantly lower than those in the untreated group. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in ROS levels between the SH3PXD2B overexpressing group and the EV group under Apocynin treatment (Fig. 3). These results indicate that the increase in cellular ROS mediated by SH3PXD2B during BCG infection is dependent on NOX activity.

SH3PXD2B actively inhibits the Keap1-Nrf2 antioxidant defense axis

The Nrf2 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in cellular antioxidant stress resistance. Under normal physiological conditions, Keap1 forms a complex with Nrf2, which is then ubiquitinated and sent to the proteasome for degradation. However, when cells undergo redox imbalance due to stimulation, ROS induce conformational changes in Keap1. This interferes with the ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2, thereby activating the pathway and inducing the transcription of a series of downstream cytoprotective genes to resist the oxidative stress damage caused by various stimuli to cells [22].

Therefore, the impact of SH3PXD2B overexpression on the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway was examined. Western blot results showed that the Nrf2 protein levels in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group were significantly lower than in the EV group, while the Keap1 protein expression levels were significantly higher. Furthermore, under BCG infection conditions, the Nrf2 expression levels were notably lower in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group than in the EV control group. Conversely, Keap1 protein expression levels were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 4A, B, C). In addition, qPCR results showed no significant difference in Nrf2 gene expression between the SH3PXD2B overexpression group and the EV group. This may be due to the changes in cellular protein levels have not yet affected the Nrf2 transcriptional regulation. Under BCG infection conditions, compared to the EV group, the transcription level of Nrf2 significantly decreased in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group (Fig. 4G, H). These results indicate that overexpression of SH3PXD2B inhibits the activation of the cellular Nrf2 signaling pathway.

Furthermore, we examined the impact of SH3PXD2B knockdown on the Nrf2 pathway in Bomac cells. Western blot results showed that the Nrf2 expression was significantly higher in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group than in the NC group, while Keap1 protein expression was significantly lower. Additionally, under BCG infection conditions, compared to the NC group, the Nrf2 expression in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group was also significantly increased, while the Keap1 expression was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 4D, E, F). qPCR results showed that under BCG infection conditions, the transcription level of Nrf2 in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group was significantly higher than that in the NC group, while the Keap1 transcription level was significantly lower than that in the NC group (Fig. 4I, J).

The transcription levels of downstream protective genes of the Nrf2 pathway, including NQO1 and HO-1 were also examined. The results showed that under BCG infection conditions, overexpression of SH3PXD2B significantly downregulated the transcription of genes such as NQO1 and HO-1 (Fig. 5A, B). Conversely, the transcription levels of NQO1 and HO-1 in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group were significantly higher than those in the NC group (Fig. 5C, D). Under the treatment of Nrf2 activator TBHQ (20 µM), there was no significant difference in the transcription levels of NQO1 and HO-1 between SH3PXD2B overexpression group and EV group (Fig. 5E, F). Collectively, these results indicate that SH3PXD2B inhibits the activation of the Nrf2 pathway during infection.

SH3PXD2B suppresses cellular antioxidant enzyme activity

To investigate whether SH3PXD2B affects the antioxidant enzyme activity of macrophages, we used a reagent kit to detect the activity of CAT, SOD, and GPx in Bomac cells. The results showed that the CAT, SOD, and GPx enzyme activities in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group were significantly lower than those in the EV group. Under BCG infection conditions, CAT, SOD, and GPx activities in the SH3PXD2B overexpression group were also significantly lower compared to the EV group (Fig. 6A, B, C). These results indicate that overexpression of SH3PXD2B inhibits cellular antioxidant enzyme activity.

SH3PXD2B attenuates the activities of key intracellular antioxidant enzymes. A CAT activity of Bomac cells with SH3PXD2B overexpressing; B SOD activity of Bomac cells with SH3PXD2B overexpressing; C GPx activity of Bomac cells with SH3PXD2B overexpressing. D CAT; activity of Bomac cells with SH3PXD2B knockdown; E SOD activity of Bomac cells with SH3PXD2B knockdown; F GPx activity of Bomac cells with SH3PXD2B knockdown

Further experiments were conducted using siRNA-transfected Bomac cells. The CAT and GPx enzyme activities in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group were significantly higher than in the NC group (Fig. 6D, F). However, in the absence of BCG infection, there was no significant difference in SOD enzyme activity between the SH3PXD2B knockdown group and the NC group (Fig. 6E). This may be because under basic cellular physiological conditions, the intrinsic SOD activity in cells is sufficient to maintain redox homeostasis. However, when cells are subjected to pathogen stimulation, the overall oxidative balance of cells is disrupted and the antioxidant protective effect of SH3PXD2B knockdown is fully manifested. These results indicate that knocking down SH3PXD2B enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thereby aiding cells in resisting oxidative stress.

SH3PXD2B promotes cell damage during BCG infection

Through the aforementioned experiments, we verified the impact of SH3PXD2B on cellular oxidative stress under BCG infection conditions. Furthermore, the extent of cellular oxidative damage caused by BCG infection was assessed by examining cell death when SH3PXD2B was overexpressed or knocked down. Cell viability assays at 3 days post-infection demonstrated that SH3PXD2B overexpression significantly exacerbated BCG-induced macrophage death compared to the EV controls (Fig. 7A). Conversely, the cell viability in the SH3PXD2B knockdown group was significantly higher compared to the NC control group (Fig. 7B). These findings suggest that SH3PXD2B exacerbates the cellular damage caused by BCG infection.

SH3PXD2B exacerbates the cellular damage caused by BCG infection. A The cell viability of Bomac with SH3PXD2B overexpression; B The cell viability of Bomac with SH3PXD2B knockdown

Meanwhile, we further investigated the effect of ROS mediated by SH3PXD2B on the intracellular survival of M. bovis BCG. Surprisingly, the colony counting results showed no significant difference in the intracellular activity rate between the SH3PXD2B overexpression group and the empty vector control group at 24 and 48 h (Fig. 8A). When siRNA was used to knock down SH3PXD2B, there was no significant difference in cellular viability between the SH3PXD2B knockdown group and the NC control group at 24 and 48 h (Fig. 8B). These results suggest that SH3PXD2B primarily contributes to host cell injury rather than direct bacterial control.

Discussion

Most proteins in p47phox protein superfamily have been confirmed to be involved in the activation process of NOX. For instance, p47phox is pivotal in activating NOX. Under activating conditions, it assembles with three other cytoplasmic proteins (p67phox, p40phox, and Rac2) to form an active NOX2 complex containing transmembrane proteins (p22phox and NADPH2). This complex has been proven to be an important pathway for ROS production in macrophages [21].Similarly, SH3PXD2B has also been proven to play a crucial role in cellular ROS production. It can guide and localize NOX1 to the foot structures, thereby regulating NOX1 function, and increasing the local ROS concentration [15].

Here, the overexpression of SH3PXD2B enhances the generation of ROS in Bomac cells under M. bovis infection conditions. Despite numerous studies indicating that ROS play a crucial role in the antibacterial activity of macrophages [11, 23], promoting the generation of ROS should aid macrophages in controlling the growth and survival of pathogenic microorganisms. However, overexpression of SH3PXD2B in Bomac cells did not reduce the intracellular survival of M. bovis BCG. This means that the ROS generation dependent on SH3PXD2B does not contribute significantly to the antibacterial activity of macrophages. This result may be caused by multiple factors: on the one hand, M. bovis can resist the ROS produced by macrophages and persist in the strongly oxidizing conditions within the cell thanks to its redox buffering system and various effector molecules. These molecules can neutralize the oxidative stress mediated by ROS in macrophages and protect the bacteria [6, 24]. On the other hand, the results of this study indicate that SH3PXD2B can interact with p22phox and mediate cellular ROS production in a NOX-dependent manner. Under normal physiological conditions, p22phox can binds to NADPH oxidase components in NOX1, NOX2, NOX3, and NOX4 and localize to corresponding membrane structures. However, the localization and functions of different NOXs in cellular structures are inconsistent. In macrophages, NOX2 is the predominant NOX located on the phagosomal membrane structure, and it is the main producer of ROS utilized by macrophages to kill bacteria [21]. However, Gianni et al. found through immunoprecipitation experiments that, as SH3PXD2B cannot interact with the p67phox protein (NOX2 activator) lacking the N-terminal PRR domain, it cannot mediate the function of NOX2 [9]. This implies that the ROS dependent on SH3PXD2B may be ineffective in killing pathogenic microorganisms within cells. In addition, the interaction between SH3PXD2B and p22phox was identified under ectopic expression conditions and may not fully reflect endogenous interactions in macrophages. The specific subcellular localization of NOX, p22phox, and SH3PXD2B in M. bovis-infected macrophages needs further investigation, to better elucidate the NOX activity and function mediated by SH3PXD2B. Moreover, further studies are also warranted to clarify which NOX isoform contributes to SH3PXD2B-mediated ROS production in macrophages upon M. bovis infection, as well as whether SH3PXD2B exerts synergistic or antagonistic effect on distinct NOX complexes. Furthermore, ROS measurement in this study relied on the DCFH-DA probe, which has limited specificity. It fails to distinguish different ROS species and may also respond to reactive nitrogen species and lipid peroxides, potentially introducing non-specific fluorescence interference. Future work will employ more specific probes and complementary assays to conduct in-depth research.

Additionally, high levels of ROS in cells can trigger oxidative stress. Excessive ROS can interact with various molecules, such as proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids, damaging or altering their functions and causing oxidative damage to cells [3, 23]. M. bovis triggers a progressive increase in ROS production by macrophages through continuous interaction with the host immune system, ultimately leading to cellular hyperoxidation and lipid peroxidation [25]. Under physiological conditions, high ROS levels are accompanied by increased antioxidant production, which maintain the redox balance within cells. However, in most cases of M. bovis infection, macrophages lose their primary antioxidant enzyme function in regulating lipid peroxidation accumulation. This leads to increased pathogen load and proliferation [25]. Activation of the Nrf2 pathway is crucial for cellular antioxidant defense. It can resist oxidative damage by transcriptionally activating multiple downstream cytoprotective genes (e.g. HO-1 and NQO1), thereby regulating cellular redox balance [26]. However, the results of this study indicate that high levels of SH3PXD2B expression inhibit Nrf2 pathway activation. During BCG infection, the activities of enzymes such as SOD, GPx, and CAT, which play an antioxidant role in cells, are also diminished by overexpressed SH3PXD2B. This results in damage to the antioxidant pathway of cells by BCG infection, exacerbating cellular oxidative stress (Fig. 9). Of note, all experiments in this study were performed using the attenuated M. bovis BCG strain, which cannot fully recapitulate the virulence characteristics of wild-type strains. Meanwhile, the exact type of cell death triggered by SH3PXD2B overexpression remains unclarified, and the detailed molecular mechanisms driving SH3PXD2B-associated cellular damage need further in-depth validation with wild-type strains in future work.

SH3PXD2B orchestrates M. bovis-induced oxidative damage in macrophages

In summary, our results confirm that SH3PXD2B is involved in the generation of ROS and the accumulating of lipid peroxide MDA in Bomac cells during M. bovis infection. It also impairs cellular antioxidant function by inhibiting the activation of the Nrf2 pathway activition and the activity of antioxidant enzymes. For M. bovis, this facilitates the induction of oxidative stress-induced damage and rupture of macrophages, which is also a key pathogenic strategy employed by M. bovis to escape from primary infected cells, trigger tissue caseous necrosis, and disseminate to adjacent tissues [27, 28]. This study provides a host-derived mechanism for M. bovis induced oxidative stress in host cells.

Conclusions

During M. bovis BCG infection, the expression of SH3PXD2B increases, thereby promoting the production of ROS via the NOX pathway. At the same time, it inhibits the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and the activity of cellular antioxidant enzymes, thereby disrupting the cellular redox balance and inducing oxidative stress in cells. These findings reveal the regulatory role of SH3PXD2B in redox imbalance, which may provide a foundation for further exploration of potential regulatory mechanisms related to cell damage induced by M. bovis.
