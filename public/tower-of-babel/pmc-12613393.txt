Exploring the role of cellular plasticity in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatosis and related molecular mechanisms
Authors: Merve Ercin, Selda Gezginci-Oktayoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn chronic diseases, hepatocytes may dedifferentiate and form a liver progenitor cell (LPC)-like population in response to long-term stress stimuli. We investigated the hypothesis that hepatocytes may dedifferentiate upon chronic high insulin stimulation and subsequently form adipocytes and/or fibroblast-like cells as a result of long-term fatty acid exposure.MethodsHepG2 cells were treated with prolonged high insulin followed by oleic acid (OA). The expression level of LPC, adipocyte and fibroblast marker genes was measured. Populations of cells carrying the LPC marker CD34, the fibroadipogenic progenitor cell marker PDGFR1α and the activated fibroblast marker FAPα were detected. Lipid accumulation and the existence of the adipocyte marker perilipin-A were shown. The relevant molecular mechanism was investigated by applying specific inhibitors and determining related protein levels.ResultsWith high insulin exposure, the number of CD34+ or PDGFR1α+ cells and the gene expression levels of LPC markers increased, whereas the gene expression of hepatocyte markers decreased. Lipid accumulation, adipogenesis and adipocyte marker gene expression levels and the density of Perilipin-A increased in cells treated with OA following high insulin. On the other hand, in cells treated with OA alone or OA following insulin, the expression levels of fibroblast marker genes and the FAPα+ cell population were increased. TLR4 and GSK3β inhibition reduced lipid accumulation whereas TLR4 and β-catenin prevented the increase in the FAPα+ cell population. Additionally, GSK3β and β-catenin levels increased in the nucleus in cells exposed to OA following high insulin.ConclusionLong-term high insulin stimulation is driving dedifferentiation of hepatocytes and causes the formation of fibroadipogenic progenitor cells. Long-term exposure of these cells to high fatty acids leads to adipogenesis, mainly via TLR4/GSK3β and fibrogenesis, via TLR4/β-catenin pathways.Graphical abstractThe findings and conclusions obtained from the present study are summarized below. (D) Signaling pathways in adipocyte-like and fibroblast-like cells formed by chronic OA stimulation following chronic high-level insulin administration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06922-4.Keywords: Adipogenesis, Fibrogenesis, Dedifferentiation, Hepatocyte, Insulin resistance, MAFLD, MASLD, TLR4/β-catenin, TLR4/GSK3β

Introduction

During the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), many significant morphological changes occur in hepatocytes. The morphological distinguishing feature of MAFLD is the accumulation of fat in hepatocytes [1]. Although hepatocytes exhibit an adipocyte-like morphology in liver sections from patients with MAFLD and MASLD, few studies have investigated the similarity of these cells with adipocytes at the molecular level. It was reported that after C57BL/6J mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) containing 42% fat for 24 weeks, the adipocyte markers aP2 and PPARγ increased in liver tissue [2]. Compared with that in control hepatocytes, albumin expression in steatotic hepatocytes was decreased. However, hepatic adiponectin and adiponectin receptor-2 expression are decreased by the upregulation of hepatic CD36. Furthermore, steatotic primary hepatocytes not only release proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-6, and IL-18 but are also able to activate macrophages when co-cultured. The overexpression of adipocyte-specific genes in hepatocytes, their secretory functions, and epithelial phenotype disorders caused functional changes as well as morphological changes in steatotic hepatocytes. A paper suggested that the adipogenic transformation of hepatocytes advances redox imbalance, while processes occurring in adipogenesis are activated in fat-laden hepatocytes in the liver, thus driving progression to MAFLD [3]. These findings suggest that the development of steatosis may overlap with the development of adipogenesis. Considering that preadipocytes are found in adipose tissue, where do the precursors of these adipocyte-like cells observed in the liver originate?

In its basic definition, dedifferentiation is the change in morphology and function of one or a certain group of cells as a result of reprogramming and the formation of another cell or group of cells. Special functions gained during the differentiation process are lost during the dedifferentiation, and the cell gains features such as self-renewal and the ability of stem/progenitor cells to differentiate. While this rarely occurs under certain physiological conditions, it mostly takes place under pathological conditions that cause cells to overfunction and force metabolism [4]. During both homeostasis and disease, regeneration of the liver parenchyma is achieved mainly by the proliferation of hepatocytes [5, 6]. The contribution of tissue-resident progenitor cells appears to be valid only when the normal hepatocyte response is exhausted in cases of severe chronic damage [7, 8]. However, the cellular mechanism of damage, which is determined by advanced fat accumulation in hepatocytes in the presence of important metabolic stress stimuli, such as chronic insulin resistance, remains unclear. Are the adipocyte-like hepatocytes that give rise to steatosis formed by fat accumulation in existing hepatocytes, adipogenesis of tissue-resident progenitor cells, or dedifferentiation and adipogenic differentiation?

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), the main source of type I collagen in the liver, play a crucial role in MASLD-associated fibrosis. Hepatocyte-derived factors such as cytokines, DAMPs, or extracellular vesicles and cytokines/chemokines released by Kupffer cells and macrophages trigger signaling cascades to convert inactive HSCs into active HSCs. As a result, activated HSCs increase the secretion of collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, leading to fibrotic scarring in the liver and ultimately chronic fibrosis [9]. Therefore, one of the potential strategies to prevent MASLD-associated fibrosis is to suppress the accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins, including collagen, or to directly inhibit HSC activation. However, the increase in the hepatic HSC pool during the development of MASLD and the cellular changes that contribute to this increase have not been fully elucidated. Is fibrosis developed during the development of MASLD due to the development of only resident HSCs, newly formed HSC-like cells originating from a population of progenitor cells, or both? Are there functional and structural differences between these HSC populations? The answers to these questions are very important for the development of MASLD treatment strategies.

In light of this information and related questions, the aim of this study was to investigate the cellular mechanisms of the development of steatosis in the pathogenesis of MAFLD and the development of fibrosis, which is the main driver of the pathogenesis of MASLD. In this context, first, insulin resistance was developed by applying high concentrations of insulin to human HepG2 cells, and the possibility that insulin resistance could transform hepatocytes into precursor cells was investigated. Second, whether exposure of these cells to OA causes adipocyte differentiation via adipogenesis and fibroblast differentiation via fibrogenesis and the molecular pathways involved in the associated molecular mechanism were investigated.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

HepG2 human hepatoma cells (ATCC, HB-8065; passage intervals 11–15) were incubated in complete medium (10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin and 2 mmol/L glutamine in Eagle's MEM) and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, humid environment with 5% carbon dioxide) [10]. In the first step, three different experimental groups were created: (1) Complete medium (control), (2) Low-level insulin that induces normal insulin signaling (1 nM) [11], (3) A high level of insulin can create insulin resistance. To determine the insulin concentration to be applied to the third group, insulin in the range of 1 nM–10 µM was applied to the cells for 24 h, and insulin concentrations that do not affect cell viability were determined via the MTT test [12]. Additionally, the changes in the levels of the molecular insulin signal indicator p-Akt (Thr308) were determined via western blotting. In the next step, the possibility of insulin application to induce dedifferentiation was tested at 24–96 h. The percentages of CD34+ cells, which is a marker of liver progenitor cells [13], were determined every 24 h via flow cytometry. Guided by these findings, after the application of the three different experimental conditions described above to HepG2 cells for 72 h, 10 nM OA, a steatosis inducer, was applied for 48 h. The OA concentration and application time were determined according to Oil Red O labeling and flow cytometric FSC/SSC analysis results. The in vitro experimental setup applied in this study is schematized in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Western blotting

After the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were obtained via the NE-PER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo), protein levels were measured via the Bradford method. The method was applied as detailed previously [12]. Transcription factor IIB (TFIIB) for the nuclear fraction was used as a loading control. The antibodies and application conditions are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Flow cytometry

To determine the CD34+ and PDGFR1α+ cell populations, the cells were suspended in staining buffer at a density of 106 cells/100 µl. Then, a FITC-conjugated mouse anti-human CD34 monoclonal antibody (BD Biosciences, 20 μL/test), a FITC-conjugated mouse IgG1 kappa isotype control antibody (BD Biosciences, 0.2 μg/test), a PE-conjugated mouse anti-human PDGFR1α monoclonal antibody (BioLegend, 1:20) and a PE-conjugated mouse IgG1 kappa isotype control antibody (BioLegend, 1:20) were used. After incubation at room temperature for 30 min in the dark, the cells were washed with staining buffer and analyzed with a FACS Calibur device (BD Biosciences) [12]. Additionally, the size and granularity of cells were determined by flow cytometric FSC/SSC analysis [14].

Oil Red O Staining

The intracytoplasmic lipid droplets were labeled with Oil Red O (ORO). Briefly, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After being washed twice with PBS, they were incubated with 0.3% Oil Red O solution for 15 min. After being washed five times with distilled water, the cells were imaged with a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope (Tokyo, Japan). To dissolve ORO, 100% isopropanol was applied for 15 min. Supernatants were transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance values were measured at a wavelength of 515 nm in a Biotek µquant microplate reader. To determine the total amount of cellular protein contained in each sample after staining, the wells were washed once with PBS and lysed with 2% SDS prepared in 0.1 M NaOH, after which the amount of total cellular protein was measured via the Bradford method. The relative lipid content of each sample was determined by normalizing the ORO absorbance values measured at 515 nm to the total protein amount [15].

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR)

At the end of 72 h of insulin administration (DD3), the gene expression levels of the liver progenitor cell markers AFP, KRT19, CDH2, SNAI1, Twist1, CD24, EpCAM, Nanog, Oct4, and Bex1 [16, 17] and the mature hepatocyte markers ALB and CYP3A7 [18] were measured via qRT‒PCR. Additionally, at the end of the second day of adipogenesis (A2), the gene expression levels of the preadipocyte markers KLF4 and UCP2; the early adipogenesis markers PPARG and CEBPA; and the mature adipocyte markers LPL, LEP and FABP4 were measured [4]. Furthermore, the expression levels of the active fibroblast genes ACTA2, FN1, Col1A, VIM, TGFβ1 and TIMP1 [19] were measured at the end of Day 2 (F2) of the differentiation protocol for fibroblast-like cells.

For this method, total RNA was isolated with TRIzol, and 1 µg of RNA was converted to cDNA with reverse transcriptase. For qPCR, 300 ng of cDNA, primer mixture and the GM SYBR qPCR Kit were used, and the method was applied via the Bio-Rad CFX-96 qPCR device according to the kit's instructions. The sequences of the primers used to determine the expression of each gene are given in Supplementary Table 2. HepG2 cells supplemented with complete medium were used as controls, and 18S rRNA was used as an internal control gene. The calculation was performed with the formula 2−(ΔΔCT) [12].

Matrigel invasion test

To demonstrate the invasive property of progenitor cells [20], the BD BioCoat tumor invasion system was used. After insulin treatment for 72 h, the cells were suspended in medium containing 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 1 × 105 cells were placed in each insert. The medium with FBS in the lower chamber served as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, noninvasive cells were removed from the inserts via cotton swabs, and the inserts were fixed in 10% formaldehyde in PBS for 20 min and covered with capping medium containing DAPI. The cells were counted via a fluorescence inverted microscope (Nikon) [21].

Immunofluorescence

At the end of the experimental period, the cells were fixed by treatment with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After the samples were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min, blocking buffer containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.01% saponin was added for 60 min at room temperature. The FITC-coupled perilipin-A antibody (Biorbyt) was prepared at a 1:1000 dilution in blocking buffer and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After the samples were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min, mounting medium containing DAPI was added. Imaging was performed on a confocal microscope (Leica, TCS-SPE), FITC and DAPI fluorescence intensities were measured in all areas, and the results were calculated in arbitrary units (AUs).

Investigation of related adipogenic and fibrogenic pathways via pharmacological inhibition

To investigate the molecular pathways that enable fibro-adipogenic cells, which are formed by the dedifferentiation of hepatocytes under the effect of insulin resistance, to differentiate into adipocyte-like cells through adipogenesis or into fibroblast-like cells through fibrogenesis, following 72 h of insulin application, the pharmacological inhibitor was applied for 2 h, and then 10 nM OA was added to the medium and applied together for 48 h. The TLR4 inhibitor Resatorvid (TAK-242), β-catenin inhibitor MSAB, CCR2 antagonist Succinate, and NFκB inhibitor Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium were obtained from MedChem Express. The GSK3β inhibitor BI-5521 was kindly provided by Boehringer Ingelheim via its open innovation platform OpnMe, available at https://opnme.com. To determine the inhibitor concentrations to be applied, concentrations that did not affect cell viability were determined by applying the dose ranges recommended by the manufacturer to the cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). Considering the results obtained and the effective doses published in the literature, the TLR4 inhibitor Resatorvid (TAK-242) 1 µM [22], the β-catenin inhibitor MSAB 5 µM [23], the CCR2 antagonist Succinate 10 nM [24], the NFκB inhibitor Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium 10 µM [25], and the GSK3β inhibitor BI-5521 were applied at a concentration of 50 nM.

Statistics

Each experiment was repeated at least three times, and the data were calculated as the means ± SEM. Two-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons with the Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied via the GraphPad Prism 4 program. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of insulin on cell viability and insulin signaling

After insulin was applied to HepG2 cells at concentrations ranging from 1 nM to 10 µM for 24 h, 1 µM insulin increased cell viability, whereas 10 µM insulin decreased it , according to the MTT test (Fig. 1A).

To investigate the effects of low (1 nM) and high (1 µM to 10 µM) insulin concentrations on insulin signaling in HepG2 cells, p-AKT (Thr308) and glucose uptake levels were measured 24 h after exposure. The findings obtained show that 1 nM insulin increases the active form of AKT, p-AKT (Thr308) and glucose uptake, which indicates that it stimulates insulin signaling, while 1 µM to 10 µM insulin suppresses it (Fig. 1B and C).

Dedifferentiatiating effect of insulin on hepatocytes

After 1 nM insulin, which stimulates the insulin signal, and 1–10 µM insulin, which suppresses this signal, were applied to HepG2 cells for 24 h to 96 h, the cells were stained with the liver progenitor cell marker CD34 antibody. Among the four different insulin concentrations applied to HepG2 cell populations, only 5 µM insulin was found to cause a significant increase in the CD34+ cell population at 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The expression levels of hepatocyte marker genes and progenitor cell marker genes were measured in the groups treated with insulin. Compared with those in the control group, the expression levels of the hepatocyte marker genes ALB and CYP3A7 were significantly higher but the expression levels of the progenitor cell marker genes AFP, KRT19, CDH2, SNAI1, Twist1, CD24, EpCAM, Nanog, OCT4 and BEX1 were significantly lower in the group administered 5 µM insulin (Fig. 2A and 2C). The fibroadipogenic progenitor cell marker PDGRF1α+ cell population increased significantly from 20% in the control group to 32% in the 5 µM insulin-treated group (Fig. 2B). An invasion test was performed to demonstrate the mobility feature of progenitor cells, and a significant increase in the number of invading cells was detected in cells treated with 5 µM insulin, which was consistent with the expression profile of epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) marker genes (Fig. 2D).

Effects of oleic acid on cell viability and steatosis

OA was applied to HepG2 cells at concentrations ranging from 1 to 1000 nM for 24 h. According to the results of the MTT test, there was a slight but significant decrease in the viability of cells treated with 1000 nM OA (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Additionally, the effects of OA on neutral fat accumulation were determined via the Oil Red O method. There was a significant increase in Oil Red O/µg protein levels in the group in which 10 nM OA was applied compared with the control group, and there was a significant decrease in the group in which 1000 nM OA was applied (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Since preserving cell viability and significantly inducing steatosis was possible with the application of 10 nM OA, it was deemed appropriate to continue the studies with this concentration.

Effects of oleic acid following high-concentration insulin exposure on the size and granularity of cells and neutral fat accumulation

After insulin was applied at a concentration of 5 µM to HepG2 cells for 72 h, the cells were exposed to 10 nM OA for 24 h to 72 h. All three OA application periods caused an increase in FSC/SSC (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although similar FSC/SSC values were determined as a result of the application of 10 nM OA for 24 or 48 h followed by the application of 5 µM insulin for 72 h, it was deemed appropriate to apply OA for 48 h to increase the stability of the differentiated phenotype.

Oleic acid exposure following high-concentration insulin drives hepatocytes to adipogenesis

Adipogenic changes were measured at the gene expression level as a result of the application of 5 µM insulin for 72 h followed by 10 nM OA for 48 h. Compared with those in the control group, the gene expression levels of the preadipocyte markers KLF4 and UCP2, the clonal expansion markers PPARG and CEBPA, and the lipid-loaded adipocyte markers LPL, LEP and FABP4 significantly increased after treatment with 5 µM insulin for 72 h and 10 nM OA for 48 h. While the adipogenic gene expression profile in the other experimental groups was generally similar to that in the control group, the application of 5 µM insulin for 72 h alone caused a slight but significant increase in PPARG and LPL gene expression (Fig. 3).

Oleic acid causes fibrogenic changes in HepG2 cells, and insulin resistance strengthens these changes

ACTA2, FN1, Col1A, VIM, TGFB1 and TIMP1 fibrogenic gene expression levels were significantly increased in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h following the application of 5 µM insulin for 72 h. In addition, ACTA2 gene expression was significantly increased in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h and in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h following 72 h of 1 nM insulin administration. FN1 gene expression was significantly increased in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h and in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h following 72 h of treatment with 1 nM insulin. Col1A gene expression was significantly increased in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h and in the group treated with 10 nM OA for 48 h following 72 h of treatment with 1 nM insulin (Fig. 6A). These findings suggested that OA alone could cause fibrogenic changes in HepG2 cells and that insulin resistance strengthened these changes.

Determination of the potential molecular pathways that may direct the development of steatosis and fibrosis

The TLR4 inhibitor Resatorvid (TAK-242), β-catenin inhibitor MSAB, CCR2 antagonist Succinate, NFκB inhibitor Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium, and GSK3β inhibitor BI-5521 were applied in combination with OA for 48 hours to cells that developed insulin resistance as a result of prolonged exposure to high insulin concentration. The degree of steatosis was assessed by measuring neutral fat accumulation with oil red O. As a result, the application of the TLR4 inhibitor Resatorvid (TAK-242) and the GSK3β inhibitor BI-5521 significantly suppressed steatosis compared with the application of 5 µM insulin + 10 nM OA (Fig. 7A). These findings indicate that the potential main pathway that may direct the development of steatosis following high-concentration insulin exposure involves TLR4 and GSK3β. Interestingly, the inhibition of β-catenin, CCR2, and NFκB caused a steatosis-like increase in neutral fat accumulation in hepatocytes (Fig. 7A).

On the other hand, in the same experimental set-up, the change in the active fibroblast-like cell population was evaluated by measuring the FAPα+ cell population. As a result, the application of the TLR4 inhibitor Resatorvid (TAK-242) and the β-catenin inhibitor MSAB significantly suppressed the FAPα+ cell population compared with that in the cells treated with 5 µM insulin + 10 nM OA (Fig. 7B). These findings indicate that the potential main pathway that may direct the development of fibrosis following high-concentration OA exposure involves TLR4 and β-catenin. Interestingly, CCR2 inhibition resulted in an increase in the FAPα+ cell population (Fig. 7B).

Identification of the downstream molecules that may direct the development of steatosis and fibrosis

Following chronic insulin and/or OA administration, changes in the protein levels of GSK3β and β-catenin, which are downstream molecules of TLR4 and insulin receptor-mediated signaling pathways, in the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were determined. Cytoplasmic p-GSK3β(Ser9) levels were high in the cells treated with 5 µM insulin and 10 nM OA, whereas they were low in the groups in which insulin and OA were applied together. Considering the potential of GSK3β to initiate adipogenesis by translocating to the nucleus, where it phosphorylates C/EBPβ, nuclear GSK3β levels were measured. The findings indicate that nuclear GSK3β levels were significantly increased in the 5 µM insulin group and increased in the 5 µM I + 10 nM OA group. This finding suggested the possibility that high-level chronic insulin stimulation causes GSK3β to translocate to the nucleus and direct adipogenesis in the presence of fatty acids. High levels of p-GSK3β (Ser9) indicate the inactivation of GSK3β and therefore the potential for β-catenin to be released and enter the nucleus, stimulating the expression of genes that cause fibrogenic changes among their target genes. To evaluate this hypothesis, β-catenin levels were determined in the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions. The findings indicate that the level of cytoplasmic β-catenin was lower in all the experimental groups than in the control group, whereas the level of nuclear β-catenin was significantly greater only in the 5 µM I + 10 nM OA group (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Although LPC-guided liver regeneration is an important pathway for repairing the parenchyma in chronic liver diseases, this pathway is not effective in advanced liver patients and may deviate in different directions [26]. The correlation between LPC counts and disease severity in chronic liver disease [27] suggests not only an increase in LPC in patients but also that LPCs are ineffectively differentiated into hepatocytes and may differentiate into cell types that cause the emergence of diseases. Hepatocytes can increase the LPC population by dedifferentiating in the context of long-term stress stimuli [28]. In light of this information, we investigated the hypothesis that prolonged insulin stimulation during the development of MAFLD and MASLD may dedifferentiate hepatocytes into LPC-like cells and these cells may differentiate into adipocytes and/or fibroblast-like cells as a result of long-term exposure to free fatty acids.

Hepatic cell culture models are indispensable for understanding the signaling pathways that regulate metabolic functions in the liver and are crucial for targeting disorders in pathological conditions. Undoubtedly, primary cells are superior to permanent cell lines because they are more similar to the in vivo state. However, the phenotype of these cells is unstable [29, 30]. The human hepatoma cell line HepG2 is often used to investigate insulin-dependent pathways [31], but the metabolic phenotype may differ from that of normal hepatocytes because these cells originate from tumor tissue. Acute insulin signaling in the mouse cell line AML12 and the human cell line THLE-2 derived from healthy liver cells was compared with that in the hepatoma cell line HepG2. The insulin response of AML12 and THLE-2 cells was impaired when the cells were cultured on the recommended growth medium. While THLE-2 cells carry low levels of insulin receptors, insulin receptor protein levels in HepG2 and AML12 cells are similar. As a result, it was determined that HepG2 cells were closer to the in vivo state despite their tumorigenic origin [11]. Considering these findings, HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells were used in our study, from which we constructed both in vitro insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis models.

Considering that insulin resistance is the basis of the pathogenesis of MAFLD and MASLD, we investigated the effects of insulin resistance on hepatocyte dedifferentiation at both the gene expression and protein levels. Among all the groups, there was a significant increase in the CD34+ and PDGFR1α+ populations of insulin-resistant cells that were treated with 5 μM insulin for 72 h. These findings indicate for the first time that chronic insulin resistance can cause hepatocyte dedifferentiation and the formation of fibroadipogenic progenitor-like cells. Although there is no direct study on this subject, a related study revealed that PGC1α, the coactivator of hepatocyte nuclear factor-4α (HNF4α), the main transcription factor involved in the acquisition of the hepatocyte phenotype, was suppressed by the application of insulin to human primary hepatocytes and HepG2 cells; thus, it was indirectly determined that insulin is a factor that causes the loss of the hepatocyte phenotype [32].

Oleic acid is frequently preferred for in vitro modeling of hepatocyte steatosis, which is the most prominent and common finding of MAFLD and MASLD [33, 34]. In our study, for the first time, we hypothesized that hepatocytes can form adipocyte-like cells via adipogenesis as a result of long-term exposure to free fatty acids following the differentiation of fibroadipogenic progenitor cells. In general, marker genes specific to preadipocytes, clonal expansion, and lipid-loaded adipocytes were significantly increased in cells treated with 10 nM OA following the administration of 5 μM insulin. The only exception was that PPARG and LPL gene expression levels were increased in the group treated with 5 μM insulin for 72 h. Since PPARG and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) are known to play important roles in lipid homeostasis, insulin resistance and adipogenesis [35], the increase in PPARG and LPL gene expression levels detected in the 5 μM insulin-treated group was associated with insulin resistance. Moreover, perilipin-A was evident in cells treated with 10 nM OA following the administration of 5 μM insulin and was localized around lipid vacuole-like round vesicles. This finding supports the formation of adipocyte-like cells by undergoing an adipogenesis-like process when fibroadipogenic progenitor cells, which are formed by the dedifferentiation of hepatocytes that develop insulin resistance, are exposed to chronic free fatty acids. Carr's team [36] reported that perilipin-A is mostly undetectable in normal liver tissue, moderately increased in MAFLD, and highest in MASLD-induced hepatic steatosis compared with the control, MAFLD, and HCV. Fujii’s team [37] reported that the expression patterns of perilipin-A and adipophilin in the livers of MASLD patients vary according to the size of lipid droplets. Members of the perilipin family, especially perilipin-A (1) and 2, increase with hepatic steatosis and are associated with the formation of lipid droplets [38]. These data indicate that adipogenesis and adipocyte-like cell formation are involved in the pathogenesis of MAFLD and MASLD.

HSCs are important effectors of progressive liver fibrosis and function to store retinyl esters in lipid droplets while being inactivated. When HSCs are activated, they differentiate into myofibroblasts and produce many fibrogenic proteins, including collagen, fibronectin, α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP1) [39]. In our study, we investigated for the first time the possibility that OA application to newly formed fibroadipogenic progenitor-like cells under the influence of chronic insulin resistance contributes to fibrosis. Our findings revealed that 10 nM OA followed by 5 μM insulin significantly increased the expression levels of the fibrogenic genes ACTA2, FN1, Col1A, VIM, TGFB1 and TIMP1. In addition, the frequency of the active fibroblast marker FAPα+ cell population was high in the same group. Surprisingly, the expression levels of the ACTA2, FN1 and Col1A genes increased significantly in the groups treated with 10 nM OA alone or following 1 nM insulin administration. In addition, increases in the FAPα+ cell population were greater in cells treated with OA alone or OA following the development of insulin resistance and lower when OA was applied to cells with normal insulin signaling, in line with changes in gene expression levels. These findings suggest that OA alone may cause fibrogenic changes in hepatocytes, whereas insulin signaling suppresses these changes. Although there are numerous studies on the fibrotic effects of OA on HSCs [40, 41], the present study suggests for the first time that hepatocytes are directly transdifferentiated into fibroblast-like cells or that fibroadipogenic cells formed by dedifferentiation of hepatocytes can differentiate into fibroblast-like cells and thus contribute to fibrosis.

To determine the molecular mechanisms that drive adipogenesis and fibrogenesis following hepatocyte dedifferentiation driven by insulin resistance, OA was administered together with inhibitors of TLR4, GSK3β, β-catenin, CCR2 or NFκB, which can be induced by insulin and fatty acids. As a result, TLR4 and GSK3β inhibition prevented lipid accumulation, and the inhibition of TLR4 and β-catenin inhibited the increase in the FAPα+ cell population. At this point, two different pathways stimulated by TLR4 could regulate the pathogenesis of MASLD. TLR4 is involved in dysregulated inflammatory and metabolic lipid pathways that alter the onset of chronic liver damage, fibrosis [42] and MAFLD [43, 44]. Decreased TLR4 expression prevents triglyceride accumulation by increasing fatty acid oxidation in the liver [43], whereas TLR4 agonists increase hepatic steatosis and inflammation [45, 46]. The finding that TLR4 is a common inhibitor of adipogenesis and fibrogenesis in dedifferentiated cells coincides with this information. The GSK3 inhibitor CHIR-99021 has been found to ameliorate steatosis and suppress hepatic lipid accumulation through the modulation of mitochondrial dysfunction in the hepatocytes of obese patients [47, 48]. In our study, BI-5521, a different GSK3β inhibitor, was shown for the first time to inhibit steatosis in dedifferentiated cells, and this finding is consistent with previous findings. The canonical Wnt pathway is normally inactive in physiologically healthy livers, but in some pathophysiological conditions, it becomes active during cell renewal [49]. Wnt signaling activates HSCs, increasing EMT and ECM accumulation [50]. Therefore, modulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling is considered a viable option for treating fibrosis and reducing progression to cirrhosis. In mice with MASLD treated with PRI-724, a selective CBP/β-catenin inhibitor, the expression of profibrotic genes was suppressed, and the expression of antifibrotic genes was stimulated; at the same time, a reduction in adipose tissue in the liver caused by MASLD was detected [51]. In our study, we showed for the first time that the β-catenin inhibitor MSAB suppressed fibrogenesis in dedifferentiated cells, whereas β-catenin inhibition had no effect on adipogenic processes.

When the molecular mechanism is examined in detail, the findings obtained at the protein level in our study indicate that the exposure of dedifferentiated cells to fatty acids may increase the expression of genes associated with adipogenesis by transporting active GSK3β to the nucleus. GSK3β can phosphorylate a variety of substrates, including transcription factors such as C/EBPβ, which regulate adipogenesis in the nucleus [52]. On the other hand, the level of β-catenin, one of the best-known mediators of GSK3β-mediated signaling, was found to increase in the nuclear fraction following fatty acid exposure in cells that dedifferentiate with insulin resistance. Despite the low levels of inactive GSK3β in this group, the translocation of β-catenin to the nucleus is surprising. This finding suggests that although GSK3β is active, the β-catenin degradation complex is inactive due to the inadequacy of the participation of related proteins in the complex. Wnt/β-catenin signaling is known to play a role in fibrogenesis in various tissues [53, 54]. Considering that β-catenin inhibition together with the exposure of cells that are dedifferentiated by insulin resistance to fatty acids suppresses the FAPα+ cell population, the nuclear translocation of β-catenin in fibroadipogenic progenitor-like cells may direct fibrogenesis.

Of course, the use of cell lines fails to capture the intricate in vivo environment, which includes interactions with various cell types, extracellular matrix components, and systemic factors that significantly influence insulin action and metabolic processes. This complexity is crucial for understanding the full scope of insulin's effects on liver cells. For this reason, in vivo validation of the findings obtained in the current study is essential.
