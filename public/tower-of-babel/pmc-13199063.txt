Extracellular vesicles from SB431542-conditioned human adipose-derived stem cells enhance in vitro and in vivo osteogenesis via a miR-20a-5p-BAMBI-BMP/canonical Wnt signaling axis
Authors: Julika Huber, Michael T Longaker, Natalina Quarto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Critical-sized bone defects resulting from tumors, trauma, or surgery present a significant clinical challenge due to its poor self-regenerating capabilities and limited therapeutic options. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have shown promise in bone regeneration, possibly mediated through their secretion of bioactive factors rather than direct engraftment and differentiation. MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) deliver micro-RNAs (miRs), proteins, and lipids that may modulate bone regeneration.Methods: EVs were isolated by differential ultracentrifugation from osteogenically induced human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs), treated with or without the small molecule SB431542, an inhibitor of TGFβ signaling (EV-hASCs[−/+]SB). EVs were characterized using western blot and nanoparticle tracking analysis. hASCs were cultured with EV-hASCs[−/+]SBs to assess bone regeneration in vitro. Bone regeneration was assessed in vivo in calvarial defects treated with (EV-hASCs[−/+]SB). EV “cargo” was analyzed for miR-content.Results: Enhanced osteogenesis is shown in vitro and in vivo upon treatment with EV-hASCs[+]SB. EV-hASCs[+]SB-enriched-miR-20a-5p was identified as a modulating factor in osteogenesis, silencing BAMBI and enhancing early downstream phosphorylation of SMAD1/5 and late activation of β-catenin in hASCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation, targets of bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) and canonical Wnt (cWnt) signaling pathways.Conclusion: EVs act as carriers for miR-mediated pro-osteogenic signaling, harnessing the therapeutic potential of MSCs in the context of bone tissue repair.Keywords: small molecule, adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells, extracellular vesicles microRNA, axis-signaling, osteogenesis

This study highlights the therapeutic potential of extracellular vesicles (EVs) as mediators of signaling in bone regeneration. We demonstrate that EVs derived from human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) treated with the TGFβ inhibitor SB431542 (EV-hASCs[+]SB) enhance osteogenesis both in vitro and in vivo by modulating bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) and canonical Wnt (cWnt) signaling, two key pathways central to bone regeneration. miR-20a-5p, enriched in EVs, silences BAMBI, triggers early activation of BMP signaling and subsequently drives cWnt signaling. The identification of miR-20a-5p as a key modulator in EV-mediated pro-osteogenic signaling advances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying osteogenesis in hASCs. Our findings provide support for more research into the development of targeted therapies combining the regenerative potential of hASCs without the risks associated with cell-based approaches.

Introduction

Bone defects can arise from various causes including trauma, congenital malformations, autoimmune conditions, infections, and surgery. Although bone has a large self-healing ability, some defects or fractures are too big to regenerate by itself. Bioactive/scaffold implantable materials, cell-types, intracellular, and extracellular molecular signaling pathways are the current tools employed to regenerate bone tissues.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and their osteogenic potential during remodeling processes play important roles in bone regeneration. Therefore, understanding in-depth molecular signaling pathways involved in their osteolineage programming is key for the development of bone implants, bone substitute materials, and cell-based scaffolds for bone regeneration.1,2 Earlier studies have proposed that MSC-derived paracrine factors represent the main mechanism of tissue regeneration after damage.3,4 It has been shown that MSCs secrete bioactive factors, which act directly by promoting intracellular signaling. Therefore, although stem cells per se hold great potential for both the repair and regeneration of different tissue types, including bone,5 the attention has shifted to cell-free therapies. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from stem cells/mesenchymal cells have demonstrated pro-regenerative ability in several tissues. Thus, there is emerging interest for EVs in tissue regeneration, for example, in bone, in the scientific biomedical community.6–8 EVs are biomolecular nanostructures and comprise a heterogenous group of vesicles secreted from cells, including exosomes and microvesicles.9,10 They range in size from 30 to 1000+ nm and play an important role in intercellular communication, by delivering microRNA (miR), messenger RNA (mRNA), proteins, and other biomolecules.9,10 EVs are non-teratogenic, less immunogenic, and migrate extensively and thus represent an appealing option for targeted drug delivery.10

Transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) holds a complex role in bone metabolism and can both enhance and repress bone formation.11–15 Active TGFβ binds to a receptor complex activating type I and II receptor kinases with consecutive phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and translocation to the nucleus to regulate gene transcription. Conversely, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) activity regulates and stimulates most essential steps during osteoblast differentiation and maturation.13,15,16 BMPs bind to homomeric type II receptors inducing a Smad-dependent (Smad 1, 5, and 8) and a Smad-independent (MAPK) signaling pathway.15

Senarath-Yapa et al.11 demonstrated enhanced bone regeneration upon inhibition of TGFβ-signaling with small molecule SB431542 by relieving Smad2/3-mediated repression of Runx2 and simultaneously increasing Smad1/5 activity, thus activating BMP signaling. Building on these findings, our study investigates the underlying mechanisms by which SB431542 enhances osteoinduction in the context of MSC-mediated bone regeneration, focusing on EV-mediated paracrine signaling mechanisms.

We demonstrate enhanced osteogenesis in vitro and in vivo upon treatment with EVs derived from hASCs exposed to SB431542 (EV-hASCs[+]SB). A key finding of this study is the identification of miR-20a-5p, enriched in EV-hASCs[+]SB, modulating osteogenesis by silencing BAMBI and enhancing downstream phosphorylation of SMAD1/5 and activation of β-catenin in hASCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation, targets of BMP and cWnt signaling pathways. Specifically, we observed early activation of BMP signaling and subsequent activation of cWnt-signaling, highlighting a temporal component of miR-20a-5p in regulating osteogenesis and supporting its role as an important mediator in bone regeneration. We demonstrate the mechanisms underlying SB431542-enhanced osteoinduction and uncover potential novel therapeutic targets that leverage MSC-based regenerative approaches without the risk associated with cell-based therapies.

Methods

Primary cell cultures

Human tissue collection and analysis were approved by the local ethics committee for human subjects research at Stanford University (IRB Protocol #2188). The requirement for informed consent was waived, as only otherwise discarded tissue was used and no identifiable patient information was included. Experiments were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable institutional guidelines. Human ASCs were obtained from elective liposuction surgeries of patients. Primary cell culture of hASCs was performed as previously described.17 Cells were plated in tissue culture dishes in DMEM-GlutaMAX medium with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin in incubators at 37 °C and 5% CO2. If applicable, cells were treated with 10 µm SB431542 (#S1067, Selleckchem.com) as previously described.11

Isolation of extracellular vesicles

For each round of EV isolation, hASCs from 3 patients were pooled. Cells were plated in 15 cm tissue culture dishes and cultured in osteogenic medium (alpha MEM, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate and 100 µg/mL ascorbic acid). The medium was changed after 2 and 4 days, and FBS was replaced with exosome-depleted FBS (#A2720803, Thermo Fisher Scientific/InVitrogen) on day 4. After 6 days of osteogenic differentiation, supernatant was collected, and EVs were isolated via differential ultracentrifugation as previously described.18 In total, 105 mL of cell supernatant was processed per experimental condition. In brief, the supernatant was centrifuged for 10 min at 300×g. The remaining pellet was discarded and the supernatant was recentrifuged for 10 min at 2000×g. This step was repeated first with centrifugation for 30 min at 10 000×g, followed by 70 mins at 100 000×g. Next, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS and recentrifuged for 70 min at 100 000×g. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4 °C. Isolated EVs were stored at −80 °C before further utilization. EVs isolated from hASCs with/without SB431542 are labeled EV-hASCs[−]SB and EV-hASCs[+]SB, respectively. Quantification of EV-hASCs[+/−]SB was performed via Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific/InVitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Characterization of EVs by capillary western blot

For characterization of EV-hASCs[+/−]SB, capillary western blot for CD9, TSG101, and CANX was performed on pooled samples of 3 patients each. CD81 and ALIX were analyzed in a separate run and are presented separately. Western blot was performed by Alpha Nano Tech LLC. In brief, the samples were lysed using 10× RIPA buffer. The protein concentration was adjusted to 112.5 μg/mL using 0.1× sample buffer (Protein Simple). Simple western reagents were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 5× fluorescent master mix was added to the samples at a 1:4 volume ratio. Samples were loaded on an assay plate and automatically loaded into the capillary. Samples were separated by size when migrating through a stacking and separation matrix. The samples were analyzed with the Jess Simple Western instrument by Protein Simple using a 20-120 kDa separation module with RePlex protocol and total protein staining.

Target proteins were identified using primary antibodies against CD9, CD81, TSG101, ALIX, and CANX and immunoprobed with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and chemiluminescent substrate. The signal was detected and quantitated.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

For characterization of size distribution and particle concentration, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) of EV-hASCs[+/−]SB was performed on pooled samples of 3 patients each by Alpha Nano Tech LLC. In brief, the Brownian motion of individual particles in suspension is tracked using a laser-illuminated chamber. Particle size is calculated based on the Stokes–Einstein equation. Data were acquired using ZetaVIEW (S/N 20-520) and the software ZetaView (version 8.05.12 SP1).

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on pooled samples of 3 patients each by Alpha Nano Tech LLC. Copper carbon formvar grids with 400 mesh were glow-discharged immediately before use. The grids were placed on the drop of sample and incubated for 10 min. Following the incubation, the grids were washed twice by floating on a drop of freshly filtered DI water for 30 s. The grids were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and imaged using Jeol JEM 1400 Flash TEM equipped with AMT NanoSprint 61 ActiveVu camera at 80 kV operating voltage.

Osteogenic differentiation assays

If applicable, cells were treated with SB431542 (10 µm) or with EV-hASCs[+/−]SB (20 µg/mL). For osteogenic differentiation assays, cells were cultured in osteogenic medium containing alpha MEM, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 100 µg/mL ascorbic acid for 21 days or until mineralization and bone nodule formation were observed. Where described, cells were treated with the following factors: noggin (# 3344, 150 ng/mL), BAMBI (#921, 300 ng/mL), sFrp1 (#5396), and DKK1 (#5439, each 250 ng/mL, R&D Systems, Inc.). Alizarin Red staining was performed as previously described.19 Experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA from cells was collected using TRIzol reagent (#15596026 Thermo Fisher Scientific/InVitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified and quantified RNA was treated with DNAse I to clear genomic DNA and reverse transcribed to ctDNA using random hexamer primers at 42 °C for 1 h. PCR was performed (94 °C for 5 min, 94 °C for 30 s, annealing for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min) for 30 cycles in total. The following primers were used: hSMAD6, hBAMBI, hID2, hGAPDH, hRUNX2, hBGLAP, hAXIN2, hMYC, and hCCND1. Primer sequences, amplicons, and annealing temperature are presented in Table S1. GAPDH was used as loading control. Experiments were performed at least 2 times in triplicates.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, cells were seeded on chambered glass slides before experiments were performed. Cells were washed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with PBST (0.25% Triton-100 in PBS) 2× each for 7 min and blocked for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with 5% BSA in PBS. Slides were incubated with primary antibody against Phospho-SMAD2/3 (Sr465, Ser467) (#44-244G, InVitrogen) at dilution 1:250 in 1% BSA/PBS or Phospho-Smad1/5 (Ser463/465) (41D10), (#9516T Cell Signaling) at dilution 1:500 in 1% BSA/PBS for 3 h at RT, followed by 5 washes, each 5 min with PBST. Cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#A32731 InVitrogen) at dilution 1:400 for 45 min, followed by 4 washes each 5 min with PBST and 1 wash with PBS. For immunofluorescent staining with β-catenin, slides were washed and fixed in 4% PFA for 20 min, washed 3× each 5 min in PBST and blocked in 10% normal donkey serum in PBST for 1 h at RT. Slides were incubated with primary antibody against active-β-catenin (#05-665, EMD Millipore) at dilution 1:100 in 2% donkey serum in PBST overnight at 4 °C. Slides were washed 5× each 5 min in PBST and incubated for 1 h with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (#A-11001, InVitrogen) at a dilution of 1:1000 in 2% donkey serum. Slides were washed 3× each 5 min in PBST and 2× each mins in PBS. For EV uptake assay, EV-hASCs[+/−]SB were isolated as described above and stained with fluorescent membrane labeling dye PKH67G kit (#PKH67GL, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Dyed EV-hASCs[+/−]SB and dyed control samples without EVs were incubated with hASCs in chambered slides for 24 h before they were washed, fixed with 4% PFA, and washed again. Slides were mounted with Fluoromount-G with Dapi. Laser scanning confocal microscopy was performed with a Leica SP8 microscope located in the Stanford University Cell Sciences Imaging Core Facility (RRID: SCR_017787). This microscope was funded, in part, by Award Number 1S10OD010580 from the National Center for Research Resources (NCRR). Experiments were performed at least 2 times in duplicates.

Animal husbandry and surgeries

Animals were cared for in accordance with the regulations by the Institutional Animal Care and the Use Committee of Stanford University (Protocol #APLAC_8397). C57/BL6 mice (Strain #000664) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Postnatal day 30 animals were undergoing calvarial defect surgery as previously described.20 In brief, following anesthesia, an incision was made along the midline of the skull. The pericranium was carefully removed. Using a trephine drill, 2-mm calvarial defects were created in both parietal bones taking care not to damage the underlying dura mater. EV-hASCs[+/−]SB were injected in a 2% sodium alginate gel (#PHR1471Supelco, Millipore Sigma). The gel was carefully inserted into the defect. An empty scaffold gel was fitted on the contralateral side. The incision was closed using a 6-0 nylon suture. Surgeries were performed in triplicate. Bone regeneration was analyzed via Micro-CT (micro-computed tomography) scanning on the day of surgery and on postoperative weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8. Skulls were harvested and processed for histology after 8 weeks.

Micro-CT scanning

Animals (n = 3 each) were anesthetized by 2% isoflurane and scanning was performed with a high-resolution Bruker SkyScan 1276 CMOS Micro CT. NRecon software (Bruker Corporation) was used for reconstruction. CTAn and CTVol (Bruker Corporation) were used for the creation of 3D images. The remaining defect area was determined using the Magic Wand Tool in Adobe Photoshop (Adobe Inc.). Quantification of bone regeneration was performed by measuring the defect area on both the treated and scaffold-only sides at each postoperative time point. The area of the remaining defect was normalized to its respective initial defect size to obtain the percentage of residual defect area. The difference in residual defect area between the scaffold-only and treated sides (scaffold% − treated%) was then calculated to determine the percentage of newly regenerated bone attributable to treatment. Finally, this value was subtracted from 100 to express the reduction in defect size relative to the scaffold-treated contralateral side.

Tissue harvesting and histology

Animals were sacrificed 8 weeks postoperatively. Animals (n = 3 each) were asphyxiated by CO2 and the skull was harvested. Skulls were washed in PBS, fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C and decalcified in 19% EDTA. Serial grade sucrose solutions (10%, 20%, 30%) were used for dehydration followed by cryo-embedding in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT-Tissue-Tek) at −80 °C. Specimens were sectioned in an anterior-posterior direction in 10 µm sections.

Representative slides encompassing the calvarial defect were stained by Movat’s Pentachrome according to standard procedures and analyzed using a digital slide scanner (Motic EasyScan One) and processed using Adobe Photoshop. Quantitative analysis of bone formation was performed on Movat’s Pentachrome-stained histological section using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). Color separation was performed using the Color Deconvolution plugin to distinguish mineralized bone. The deconvoluted areas were converted to 8-bit grayscale and thresholds were applied to isolate the yellow (mineralized bone) component. The area of mineralized bone was measured and presented as the difference between the defect area in EV-hASCs[−/+]SB-treated and scaffold-treated contralateral defects, normalized to the scaffold-treated area and expressed as a percentage.

BAMBI immunoblotting analysis

For BAMBI and GAPDH immunodetection 60 mg of protein cell lysates in RIPA buffer were resolved by a gradient NuPAGE 4%-12% bis-Tris-HCl sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel (Novex, Life Technologies). Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Bio-Rad, Inc.) and probed with specific rabbit anti-BAMBI antibody at 1:600 dilution (#ab203070, Abcam) and rabbit anti-GAPDH at 1:1000 dilution (#2118, Cell Signaling Technology). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit antibody at 1:10000 dilution (#7077S, Cell Signaling) was used. Immunoblotted proteins were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (#6883, Cell Signaling). Images of immune-positive bands were captured by a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). To assess loading control, membranes were stripped and re-probed with anti-GAPDH antibody described above.

miR sequencing

miR-sequencing was performed in duplicates on pooled samples of 3 patients each by Alpha Nano Tech LLC. RNA was isolated using Exosomal RNA isolation Kit (#58000, NorgenBiotek) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and quality were assessed using Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Libraries for small RNA sequencing were prepared using a standard protocol. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit v2 (Illumina Inc.). The size range specified for sequencing analysis was between 15 and 50 nucleotides, including miRs.

miR transfection assays

Cells were seeded to be sub-confluent and allowed to adhere overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Synthetic hsa-miR-20a-5p-mimic (mirVana miRNA mimic #4464066, miRBase Accession Number MI0000076), hsa-miR-20a-5p-inhibitor (mirVana miRNA inhibitor, #4464084, miRBase Accession Number MI0000076), and hsa-miR-1 (mirVana miRNA Inhibitor #4464076 Negative Control #1, Thermo Fisher Scientific/InVitrogen) were used for transfection. Lipid-based transfection reagent (Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent #13778100, Thermo Fisher) was utilized according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, transfection complexes were prepared by diluting miR and transfection reagent in serum-free media (Opti-MEM Medium, #31985062, Thermo Fisher Scientific/InVitrogen) and incubated at RT for 5 mins. The complexes were then added to the cells in complete media. After 48 h of transfection, cells were harvested for downstream analyses. Experiments were performed at least 2 times in triplicates.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 using an unpaired 2-tailed t-test or 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to assess differences among groups. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effective inhibition of TGFβ signaling pathway in hASCs by SB431542

Human adipose tissue was obtained via liposuction and hASCs isolated and cultured. Osteogenic induction was initiated, and cells were treated with or without small molecule SB431542, a TGFβ-inhibitor. After 6 days in osteogenic differentiation medium, supernatant was collected, EVs were isolated via differential ultracentrifugation and used for in vitro and in vivo analyses (Figure 1A).

(B) Confocal image of immunofluorescent staining for Dapi (blue color) and pSMAD2/3 (green color), downstream target of TGFβ signaling pathway, showing inhibition of pSMAD2/3 expression in SB431542-treated ASCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation. Scale bar value = 50 µm. Magnification at ×20. (D) RT-PCR gene expression analysis demonstrating downregulation of SMAD6 in SB431542-treated ASCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation. (E) Osteogenic assay showing increased mineralization and bone nodule formation in ASCs treated with SB431542 after 21 days of osteogenic induction. n = 3. Scale bar value = 250 µm.

TGFβ directly phosphorylates and activates SMAD2 and 3, which form complexes with SMAD4 and translocate to the nucleus to regulate gene transcription.21 SMAD6 expression is induced by TGFβ and acts as a negative feedback regulator on TGFβ-signaling.22,23 Effective TGFβ-inhibition is confirmed by significantly decreased phosphorylation of SMAD2/3 (Figure 1B and C) and diminished upregulation of SMAD6 in hASCs treated with SB431542 (Figure 1D).

Additionally, we demonstrate increased matrix mineralization and bone nodule formation in SB431542-treated hASCs in vitro after 21 days in osteogenic differentiation (Figure 1E).

Taken together, our data confirms inhibition of TGFβ signaling after treatment with SB431542 in hASCs.

Isolated EVs are taken up by recipient cells

To establish the presence of EVs, we performed capillary western blot analysis to identify EV-specific proteins and NTA and TEM to estimate particle size, concentration, and morphology. CD9 and CD81 are tetraspanin transmembrane proteins, and TSG101 and ALIX are part of the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT), which are commonly used as exosomal markers, a subpopulation of EVs. Calnexin (CANX) is a protein resident to the endoplasmic reticulum and is used as a negative control to assess for cellular contamination.24,25 In EV-hASCs[−/+]SB-preparations, we observed bands corresponding to the target proteins CD9 and CD81 at ∼32 and ∼28 kDa and TSG101 and ALIX at ∼53-54 and ∼102-103 kDa, slightly higher than the expected target values at ∼25, ∼25, ∼44, and ∼95 kDa, respectively (Figure 2A). This small discrepancy is likely due to posttranslational modifications. The bands were absent in the negative control for CANX, confirming specificity. A digital rendering of the blot (Figure 2B) illustrates the results.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis confirmed the presence of EVs in both samples revealing a median size of 160.1 nm (X10 94.4, X90 292.7) and 144.8 nm (X10 91.3, X90 247.0) in EV-hASCs[−]SB and EV-hASCs[+]SB, respectively, consistent with the expected size range for EVs (Figure 2C and D). The wide range of particle sizes reflects the heterogenous nature of the extracellular vesicle population in the sample, containing exosomes, microvesicles, and other vesicle subtypes. The particle concentration was 1.6e^10/mL and 2.7e^10/mL in in EV-hASCs[−/+]SB, respectively (Figure 2C).

Transmission electron microscopy analysis revealed the presence of round, membrane-bound vesicles with diameters ranging from approximately 50-500 nm, exhibiting the characteristic round, lipid-bilayer-enclosed vesicular morphology of EVs (Figure 2E). Together, these findings confirm that the isolated particles fulfill the minimal criteria for EVs as outlined by the MISEV2023 guidelines.24

Next, we investigated whether EVs were taken up by recipient hASCs. Isolated EVs were labeled with fluorescent dye PKH67, which specifically labels lipid membranes. hASCs were then cultured for 24 h with the labeled EVs, fixed, and imaged to visualize the uptake. Fluorescent signals corresponding to PKH67 were detected within the cytoplasm of recipient hASCs, confirming the internalization of the EV-hASCs[−/+]SB. Control experiments using dye-only samples showed no detectable fluorescence, demonstrating the specificity of the observed signal (Figure 2F).

Thus, these findings confirm the presence of EVs in our samples and indicate effective uptake of EVs into the recipient cells, suggesting their potential role in mediating intercellular communication.

EV-hASCs[+]SB display increased osteoinduction activity in vitro and in vivo

Our initial approach was to assess the in vitro osteogenic effect of EV-hASCs[−]SB and EV-hASCs[+] treatments on hASCs cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium for 21 days. Alizarin Red staining and quantification detected a significant increase of the extracellular matrix mineralization and bone nodule formation in EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated hASCs compared to EV-hASCs[−]SB-treated hASCs (Figure 3A and B). RT-PCR gene expression analysis revealed upregulation of early osteogenic marker RUNX2, at days 4 and 8 of osteogenic differentiation assay followed by upregulation of late osteogenic marker osteocalcin (BGLAP) at days 8 and 12 in EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated hASCs (Figure 3C).

These results sparked our interest in investigating the potential osteo-regenerative activity of EV-hASCs[+]SB in vivo. This was pursued by employing a mouse calvarial defect model.20,26 Therefore, 2-mm calvarial defects were created in the parietal bones of postnatal day 30 mice. EVs were suspended in alginate gel and delivered into the defect. As control, the contralateral side of the bone was treated with a scaffold alginate gel without EVs. Micro-CT images were taken on the day of surgery (day 0) and 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks postnatally to track bone regeneration (Figure 3D). The reduction of the EV-hASCs[+/-]SB-treated defect area was calculated as percentage relative to the scaffold-treated contralateral defect area. At 8 weeks postoperatively, minimal regeneration was observed in scaffold alone and EV-hASCs[−]SB-treated defects, whereas significantly increased bone formation is seen in EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated defects (Figure 3D and E). Representative histological sections of the defects, stained with Movat’s Pentachrome, show a narrower defect area in EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated defects compared to EV-hASCs[[−]SB- and scaffold-treated defects (Figure 3F, arrows). Additionally, EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated defects revealed faint, yellow-stained tissue, indicative of early-stage matrix deposition in soft tissue (Figure 3F, arrowheads). Quantitative analysis of Movat’s pentachrome-stained sections further confirmed these observations, demonstrating a significantly higher area fraction of mineralized bone in EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated defects compared with EV-hASCs[−]SB-treated defects (Figure 3G).

Taken together, these findings reveal that EV-hASCs[+]SB-treatment enhances osteogenesis both in vitro and in vivo and influences tissue organization and the regenerative environment in calvarial defects in vivo.

EV-hASCs[+]SB “cargo” is enriched with miR-20a-5p promoting activation of pro-osteogenic signaling and enhanced osteogenesis

To gain insights into mechanism(s) governing the enhanced osteoinduction activity observed both in vitro and in vitro, we analyzed the miR content of EV-hASCs[−/+]SB. For this analysis, miRs with a P-value cutoff of .05 and an FC cutoff of ±1 were selected. The heatmap illustrates the upregulated and downregulated miRs in EV-hASCs[+]SB and in EV-hASCs[−]SB meeting the exclusion criteria (Figure 4A). Among the upregulated miRs in EV-hASCs[+]SB was miR-20a-5p. This miR has been previously shown to silence BMP and activin membrane-bound inhibitor (BAMBI) in human dental pulp stem cells.27 Indeed, we confirmed decreased BAMBI expression both at RNA and protein level in hASCs treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB (Figure 4B and C).

Next, we investigated the status of endogenous activation of two pro-osteogenic signaling pathways, BMP and cWnt, in EV-hASCs[+]SB-treated and EV-hASCs[−]SB-treated hASCs, respectively. To this end, we performed RT-PCR gene expression analysis for specific genes known as targets of activated BMP and cWnt signaling pathways. Selected genes included ID2, a transcription factor regulated by active BMP/SMAD signaling, and AXIN2, CYCLIN D1, and C-MYC, targets of activated cWnt signaling.28–30 We observed increased expression of ID2 from day 4 on and increased expression of AXIN2 and C-MYC on days 8 and 12 and of CYCLIN D1 on day 8 of osteogenic induction (Figure 4D). These findings indicate an early activation of BMP signaling starting on day 4 followed by late activation of cWnt signaling in hASCs treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB as compared to cells treated with EV-hASCs[−]SB.

To provide compelling evidence on the role of miR-20a-5p for the endogenous activation of both BMP and cWnt signaling, we performed the analysis on hASCs transfected with a miR-20a-5p-mimic. As shown in Figure 4E (left panel), hASCs transfected with miR-20a-5p-mimic displayed an activation of both signaling pathways, mirroring the profile found in cells treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB, described above. Conversely, miR-20a-5p-inhibitor suppressed the EV-hASCs[+]SB-mediated activation of BMP and cWnt signaling (Figure 4E, middle panel). Transfection with miR-scramble as a reference control did not alter the activity elicited by EV-hASCs[+]SB on hASCs (Figure 4E, right panel).

Next, to assess whether miR-20a-5p-mimic could directly trigger an enhancement of hASCs osteogenic activity, we carried out an in vitro osteoinduction assay. Alizarin staining and its quantification (Figure 4F and G) revealed a more robust mineralization of the extracellular matrix in cells transfected with miR-20a-5p-mimic as compared to miR-20a-5p-inhibitor+EV-hASCs[+]SB. Moreover, cells transfected with miRs-scramble and treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB also displayed, similar to miR-20a-5p-mimic-transfected cells, an intense Alizarin staining (Figure 4F and G). This enhanced osteoinduction activity was further validated by RT-PCR analysis for RUNX2 and BGLAP (Figure 4H).

To further confirm the functional role of miR-20a-5p, we performed a rescue experiment by adding exogenous BAMBI protein to miR-20a-5p-mimic transfected cells (Figure S1A, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). This treatment inhibited activation of BMP signaling (Figure S1B and C, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) thereby, preventing cWnt signaling activation and osteogenic activity (Figure S1D-H, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Taken together these results suggest a direct role of miR-20a-5p in enhancing the osteogenic activity of hASCs through the BAMBI-BMP/cWnt signaling axis.

Our findings indicated a temporal sequence in the activation of two pro-osteogenic signaling pathways with early activation of BMP followed by a late activation of cWnt. The late activation of cWnt signaling relative to that of BMP signaling poses innate questions: Is active BMP signaling an upstream effector of late activation of cWnt signaling? Or is activation of cWnt signaling an autonomous/independent event? To comprehend the relationship between these two signaling pathways, we set up experiments with the same schematic as those presented above but in the presence of noggin an inhibitor of BMP signaling pathway.31,32 By adding exogenous noggin protein either to hASCs transfected with miR-20a-5p-mimic or in the presence of EV-hASCs[+]SB, we observed, in addition to suppression of ID2 expression upregulation, indicative of inactive BMP signaling, also a subsequent inactivation of cWnt signaling (Figure 4I), as confirmed by a sharp downregulation of AXIN2, CYCLIN D1, and C-MYC (Figure 4I). Furthermore, noggin treatment abrogated the osteogenic activity promoted by miR-20a-5p mimic and EV-hASCs[+]SB (Figure 4J and F, bottom).

Our findings were further supported by immunofluorescent staining for downstream effector of BMP signaling SMAD1/5 and cWnt signaling β-catenin performed in hASCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation (Figure 4K) We noticed increased fluorescence staining for phosphoSMAD1/5 on days 4 and 8 in hASCs treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB or miR1-scramble-transfected cells and treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB and miR-20a-5p-mimic-transfected cells, but not in cells treated with EV-hASCs[−]SB, noggin+EV-hASCs[+]SB or transfected with miR-20a-5p-inhibitor and treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB (Figure 4K). Additionally, we found increased signal for active β-catenin on day 8 in respective treatment conditions but no signal on day 4 (Figure 4L), thus confirming our previous transcriptomic findings.

To further validate the role of BMP signaling in modulating the downstream cWnt activation, we performed a set of experiments with cells treated with EV-hASCs[+]SB in the presence of sFrp1 and DKK1, inhibitors of cWnt signaling. The two inhibitors were applied in combination to ensure inhibition of the LRP5/6 and Frizzled receptor, as well as sequestration of Wnt ligands in the extracellular space. The treatment effectively inhibited cWnt signaling as assessed by downregulation of its target genes, decreased active β-catenin staining of cells and osteogenic activity (Figure S2A-E, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Conversely, it did not affect the activation of BMP signaling as indicated by sustained ID2 expression (Figure S2A, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Thus, experiments performed either with noggin, an inhibitor of BMP signaling, or sFrp1 and DKK1, inhibitors of cWnt signaling, strongly indicate a cross-talk between BMP and cWnt signaling, positioning active BMP signaling as an upstream effector and cWnt signaling as activation its downstream target.

Discussion

In the last decade EVs, including exosomes and micro-vesicles have emerged as an appealing “next-generation” cell-free therapeutic tool for translational medicine. Our group has previously shown that the small molecule SB431542, inhibitor of TGF-β signaling enhances the osteogenic potential of murine dura mater and osteoblast cells. Building on these findings, we interrogated whether SB431542 could elicit a similar effect on undifferentiated hASC, and the underlying mechanisms. We specifically focused on EVs secreted from hASCs conditioned with this small molecule. Herein, we report that SB431542-treated hASCs secrete EVs with a “cargo” enriched for miR-20a-5p. We demonstrate that this epigenetic factor enhances osteogenesis by suppressing BAMBI expression and thereby leading to activation of BMP signaling followed by late activation of cWnt signaling. Moreover, a gain-of-function experiment further validated a direct role of BAMBI in these events.

It has been reported that miR-20a-5p plays a role in multiple cellular processes, including osteogenesis, by modulating TGFβ-, MAPK-, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways.33–39 In the bone microenvironment, previous studies demonstrated that miR-20a-5p mediated induction of osteogenesis by silencing PPARγ, Bambi, and Crim1, negative regulators of BMP signaling.27,40–44 These findings were confirmed by transfecting hMSCs with a miR-20a-mimic and a reversal of respective expression levels upon miR-20a-inhibition.41 By employing a dual-luciferase reporter assay, Cen et al.27 confirmed BAMBI as a direct target of miR-20a-5p in human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs). Moreover, they demonstrated increased phosphorylation of Smad5 as well as of p38-MAPK in hDPSCs transfected with miR-20a-5p-mimic or upon silencing of BAMBI, respectively.

Several studies have reported crosstalk between p38-MAPK and cWnt signaling.45–49 In light of this knowledge, and since activation of p38 MAPK is critical to activate cWnt signaling,50 we cannot rule out the presence of an ongoing cross-talk between the two signaling pathways in our cell system.

Data garnered from our investigation further confirm the ability of miR-20a-5p to repress expression of BAMBI leading to increased activation of the BMP-downstream effector pSMAD1/5 as shown by our immunofluorescence staining in hASCs. Our study builds on this knowledge by showing the involvement of additional molecular mechanism(s) such as a late activation of cWnt following BMP signaling activation. Furthermore, we demonstrate that early activation of BMP signaling is crucial for subsequent activation of cWnt and enhancement of hASC’s osteogenic activity triggered by EVs derived from SB431542-conditioned hASCs. Moreover, experiments performed either in the presence of noggin, an inhibitor of BMP signaling, or sFrp1 and DKK1, inhibitors of cWnt signaling positioned the BMP active signaling upstream in the miR-20a-5p-triggered axis.

Crosstalk between BMP and cWnt signaling pathways regulates development and homeostasis at multiple levels in different systems.51,52 For instance, a finely tuned coordination of BMP and cWnt signaling at several stages is important to define cell-specific osteogenic fate.53

By unveiling a relay mechanism of BMP-cWnt signaling activation in hASCs via a miR-20a-5p-mediated axis, our study provides new insights into the role of this epigenetic factor in the context of hASC’s osteo-skeletal activity.

The importance of cWnt signaling in normal bone biology is well established.54–56 Remarkably, active cWnt signaling may elicit opposite biological activity in the context of osteogenic differentiation depending on the status of target cells (undifferentiated vs differentiated), as well as on the threshold levels of its activation and Wnt ligands. Enhanced active cWnt signaling keeps MSCs in an undifferentiated status preventing them to specify toward an osteogenic lineage, whereas it promotes osteogenesis in a pre-differentiated osteoblast.57 This knowledge points to a beneficial timing of cWnt signaling activation in our cell system. The late activation of cWnt signaling promotes robust osteogenic activity in hASCs, which perhaps would have been missed with an early cWnt signaling activation relative to BMP signaling.

In a translational setting, these findings support the potential use of EVs in bone-related diseases, acting as carriers for pro-osteogenic molecules, including miR-20a-5p, and thereby activating key osteogenic pathways. EVs could be harnessed as a targeted, cell-free therapeutic approach to enhance bone regeneration in diseases characterized by bone loss or impaired bone regeneration, including osteoporosis, fracture non-union, or traumatic bone defects.58–60 However, though the field of EV biology holds great promise for regenerative medicine, several challenges remain before clinical translation can be realized, including ensuring compliance with standardized protocols for isolation and characterization, large-scale and reproducible EV production and maintaining stability and bioactivity during storage.61

Despite the mechanistic insights provided by this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. While we evaluated the regenerative potential of EV-hASCs[−/+]SB in a calvarial bone defect model, the experiments were performed in healthy young animals and do not recapitulate the pathophysiological conditions present in disease-specific or compromised settings, such as osteoporosis or advanced age. In these contexts, impaired osteogenic differentiation, altered immune response, and changes in the bone microenvironment could significantly influence EV-induced responsiveness in recipient cells. Furthermore, systemic factors such as hormonal dysregulation or oxidative stress may affect EV biodistribution, uptake, and stability. While our current findings demonstrate a clear osteoinductive potential, additional studies are necessary to understand the role of EV-hASCs[−/+]SB in bone diseases.

Conclusions

We identified EV-enriched miR-20a-5p as a downstream mediator of SB431542-induced inhibition of TGFβ signaling in hASCs, acting via BAMBI on BMP and cWnt signaling pathways. These findings demonstrate the importance of EV-mediated signaling during osteogenic differentiation of hASCs in vitro and in mouse calvarial defect healing in vivo. Our study further highlights EVs as a valuable therapeutic tool as well as promising miR-carriers due to their low cytotoxicity, antigenicity, and suitability for long-term storage.62
