Reducing decisional conflict in COVID-19 vaccination in ethnocultural communities through sensemaking: a participatory action mixed-methods study
Authors: Denise Campbell-Scherer, Eliana Castillo, Yvonne Chiu, Thea Luig, Stephanie Fernandez, Mawien Akot, Irene Dormitorio, Zhewar Hama, Ali Mahdi, Nasreen Omar, Laura Sterling, Atiya Syeda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine how cultural health brokers, as trusted intermediaries between formal systems and diverse ethnocultural communities, help navigate decisional conflict and misinformation regarding COVID-19 vaccination and to identify how their work contributes to system resilience in crisis contexts.DesignA community-based participatory action sensemaking research project to capture the real-time work of cultural health brokers in helping people navigate decisional conflict for vaccination.Setting, participantsMulticultural Health Broker Cooperative in Edmonton, Alberta where brokers speak 54 languages and serve more than 10 000 people from diverse ethnolinguistic communities. 28 cultural health brokers (9 male; experience 4–25 years) contributed to data collection and analysis between 16 September 2021 and 16 December 2021.Data collection and analysisThe brokers captured real-time reflections and self-interpretations in the SenseMaker platform through a theoretically informed, codesigned, mixed-method data collection tool. The team engaged in 13 weekly, 90 minute, audio-recorded and transcribed sessions: seven focused on understanding and action planning and five reflecting on the SenseMaker data, the focus of the thematic analysis. Data were managed in NVivo (QSR International, Version 12, 2018).ResultsBrokers collected 277 narratives and conducted 13 sensemaking sessions. Understanding and purpose were identified in 68% of narratives as key to achieving coherence; 81% of narratives highlighted trust as crucial to what was needed for action; 62% of narratives reflected on a potential risk, with loss of trust a concern in 70% of them. A rich understanding of the sources of decisional conflict and misinformation was achieved and managed through outreach. There were four entwined components to navigation of the evolving complexity of COVID-19 vaccination: (1) building and sustaining trust; (2) strengthening relationships; (3) creating safe spaces for collective sensemaking and solution finding; and (4) leveraging cultural and social capital to address barriers. Through these mechanisms, brokers reduced decisional conflict and misinformation, supporting informed, values-congruent decisions.ConclusionsCultural health brokers, embedded within communities and linked to formal systems, play a critical role in crisis response by fostering trust, mobilising resources and enabling collective sensemaking. This study demonstrates how these intermediaries’ contextually and culturally attuned work provides a model for building system resilience for future crisis response.

Introduction

Aqoon la’aan waa iftiin la’aan

To be without knowledge is to be without light. ~Somali Proverb

COVID-19, an unprecedented global pandemic, illuminated how complex crises cause decisional conflict, intensify fraught discussions about risk and exacerbate existing social divisions and inequities.1 2 The Lancet Commission has highlighted that public policies failed to adequately address the profound inequities in the impact of COVID-19.3 The Commission’s key recommendations for the future emphasise the need for: protection of vulnerable groups; strengthened public health relationships with local communities and community organisations; health education for health promotion, disease prevention and emergency preparedness; and effective health communication strategies to combat misinformation. However, the Commission did not articulate how these goals could be operationalised in practice.3

The Illuminate Programme, a rapid-response, community-based participatory action research initiative, addresses this gap by capturing the real-time impact of the pandemic and the role of cultural health brokers’ response. Beginning in May 2020, cultural health brokers identified the multifaceted negative impacts of COVID-19 on people in vulnerable circumstances, and by July 2021, decisional conflict surrounding vaccination had emerged as a pressing concern. Brokers reached out to academic collaborators from prior community-based participatory research partnerships to request assistance in capturing their brokering work in the evolving crisis and generating insights to inform broader health policy discussions. Part one of the Illuminate Programme, the Illuminate Project, revealed how systemic inequities with deep roots that result in poor health amplified the multiple impacts of COVID-19 on members of ethnocultural communities in vulnerable circumstances.4 It also demonstrated the crucial role of cultural brokering practices in mitigating these impacts, and how leveraging social capital and community strengths was crucial to building resilience.

This paper presents part 2 of the Illuminate Programme, the Sensemaking Project, which examined how trusted intermediaries supported emergency response through timely, culturally and linguistically tailored information, education and outreach to help people and communities navigate decisional conflict and misinformation for COVID-19 vaccination. The study sought to understand how cultural brokers, embedded as intermediaries between systems and communities, engaged in sensemaking as a continuous process to establish situational awareness, to support understanding and enable collective action.5 This work directly informed brokers’ actions during the study period and contributed to broader regional working groups coordinating pandemic response. Application of sensemaking in a pragmatic, participatory approach is emerging as a valuable means of studying crises.4 6 Understanding the role of trusted intermediaries in sensemaking offers insights into how public health systems can create the networks and infrastructures that strengthen resilience for both ongoing pandemic recovery and future emergency preparedness.

Methods

We report our mixed-methods study according to the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study checklist.7

Setting and participants

This study was conducted in collaboration with clinician academic researchers from the University of Alberta (DC-S) and the University of Calgary (EC), and an academic researcher from the Physician Learning Program (TL) together with peer researchers from the Multicultural Health Broker Cooperative (MCHB) in Edmonton, Alberta. The MCHB is operated by cultural health brokers of immigrant and refugee backgrounds,8 9 with deep knowledge of culture, gender and power relations, and context of the migration experience.10 Cultural brokering is defined as ‘the act of bridging, linking, mediating between groups of persons of differing cultural backgrounds for the purpose of reducing conflict and producing change’.8 Brokers are embedded in communities, supporting migrant families through health education, holistic family support, community development and system advocacy.11 Collectively, the MCHB brokers speak 54 languages and serve more than 10 000 people from diverse ethnolinguistic communities. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, DC-S’s research team was engaged in a participatory action research collaboration with the MCHB focused on addressing clinical and social determinants of health to advance obesity and diabetes prevention and management in newcomer ethno-cultural communities.1012 The MCHB community partners’ were deeply concerned with the evolving crisis of COVID-19’s increased negative impacts in their communities and requested the help of the academic researchers in crafting a new research initiative to understand its evolving impacts; to address misinformation, decisional conflict and access concerns for COVID-19 vaccination; and, to study their efforts to work as intermediaries between community and formal systems to broker solutions. The partners’ stance was to understand and reduce decisional conflict around COVID-19 vaccination by addressing questions, fears and misinformation to support people in making informed choices congruent with their own values and preferences. Executive MCHB director and project co-lead (YC) invited cultural brokers who attended a co-operative meeting to discuss these concerns and who expressed interest in contributing to the response efforts to participate in the study. The demographics of consenting peer researchers for this project are in online supplemental appendix 1. In this project, we refer to brokers serving as peer researchers as intermediaries.

Patient and public involvement

The cultural brokers participated in all phases of the project, from inception, instrument design, data collection and analysis, knowledge translation, and acted as intermediaries between individuals, families, communities, religious and community service groups, and the formal systems of healthcare, social care and education.

Study design and theoretical considerations

The Illuminate Programme had three interconnected purposes: understanding real-world sensemaking of individuals and communities navigating the COVID-19 crisis; illuminating the role and function of cultural health brokers as intermediaries between community and formal systems to bridge cultural, linguistic and knowledge gaps; and, to understand a complex problem from multiple perspectives in order to guide action for impact. The overall aim of the programme was to help support ongoing pandemic response and recovery efforts, and future emergency planning, especially for ethnocultural communities.

The first purpose was addressed in the Illuminate Project.4 The Sensemaking Project presented here addressed the second purpose of the programme, with both projects addressing the third purpose. To achieve this, we used a partnership-based, integrated, mixed-methods approach13 with an action research design.14 Our methods are based on the principles of community-based and trauma-informed participatory action research.1518 We collected real-time data during the evolving crisis of the intermediaries’ experiences in understanding of what was happening and initiating activities in bridging between formal systems, community agencies and community leaders, as well as their experiences supporting individuals and families in COVID-19 response. This real-time information was collated to inform ongoing group reflection and understanding, or sensemaking. This process of navigating an evolving situation was done via individual and social sensemaking.5 19

Sensemaking in a crisis is informed by the Cynefin framework, which helps distinguish between simple, complicated, complex and chaotic contexts, thereby guiding decision-making and action under conditions of uncertainty.6 20 To deepen our analysis, our data collection instrument and interpretive approach includes a range of theoretical perspectives. Bourdieu’s concepts of fields, habitus and capital21 explore how structured social relations, embodied dispositions and the distribution of material and symbolic resources shape the possibilities for action in crisis settings. In parallel, Foucault’s notions of sovereign power, disciplinary power and biopower22 look at the multiple modalities through which authority is exercised, bodies are regulated and populations are governed during health emergencies, shaping both individual and collective health behaviours. Centola’s account of complex contagion23 highlights that behaviour change—such as vaccine uptake—rarely spreads through single exposures, but instead requires reinforcement from multiple trusted sources to gain traction. Complementing these perspectives, Antonovsky’s framework of salutogenesis24 emphasises the importance of sense of coherence—encompassing manageability, meaningfulness and comprehensibility—as a foundation for sustaining and fostering health during crisis. Collectively, these theoretical perspectives constitute a layered analytical frame that enables a more nuanced understanding of how individuals and systems negotiate meaning, exercise power and engage in health-related practices under conditions of profound uncertainty.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection for sensemaking (figure 1) involves: capturing real-time data of a dynamic social situation in flux; ongoing reflection, exploring entangled and evolving phenomena to understand; and, to inform meaning-making, decision-making and coordination of action to address complex problems.4 6 20 25 26

Data collection and sensemaking.

Data were composed of mixed-method data collected in the SenseMaker (Cognitive Edge) platform and reflections from the weekly 90 minute team sensemaking sessions between 16 September and 16 December 2021. We have described the mixed-method data collection using SenseMaker (Cognitive Edge) in detail previously.4 The data collection instrument (online supplemental appendix 2) uses a large number of micronarratives or reflections, rather than a small number of in-depth narratives. The intermediaries captured short reflections from their work as cultural brokers over the time period. They then self-interpreted their reflections, using questions created to categorise the narratives, which included multiple choice questions, a dyad and ternary plots. The reflections were entered via a mobile phone app or computer interface and took about 8–10 min to complete. The intermediaries were supported with technical questions and were adept at entry after a couple of narratives. Data collected from the instrument included qualitative data for the narratives, compositional data for the ternary plots, categorical data for the multiple-choice questions, and continuous data for the dyads. Data was stored securely on the Cognitive Edge servers.

The sensemaking sessions consisted of the entire team of intermediaries and academics engaged in a facilitated dialogue on the incoming reflections, which supported their own and their clients’ sensemaking and operational actions. The initial sessions focused on problem solving, planning and understanding of the evolving context. Brokers’ operational actions included: mobilising community members, organising information sessions, translating and disseminating culturally-tailored information, and providing pop-up vaccination clinics. The final five sessions focused on discussions of the captured SenseMaker data. Weekly sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data were managed in NVivo (QSR International, V.12, 2018). The final five sessions, together with all of the SenseMaker data, served as the main corpus of data for this study.

Two academic peer researchers (DC-S, EC) cross-coded inductively and deductively the final five sensemaking session transcripts. Multiple codes were applied to the transcripts, reflecting the entanglement of the complex processes throughout analysis. To enhance trustworthiness, we triangulated the sensemaking sessions’ data with the reflections collected in SenseMaker and we recorded our analytical and reflexive thinking. Following an iterative process of discussing patterns and abstractions, DC-S and EC constructed themes synthesising the relationships between the codes to address our research questions. These were explored with our peer researchers. This approach allowed us to provide contextually rich information describing the role of the intermediaries in bridging between community and formal systems, and to describe what happened in this real-world response to the pandemic. Integration of the expertise and tacit knowledge of our community partners was integral to the sensemaking process. This collaborative sensemaking was crucial to understanding the data about how understanding and enacting relationships achieved action. Reflexivity, the continuous process of being aware and transparent on how our own stance as researchers impacts the knowledge we are co-creating, was a constant feature of the action and analytical thinking and process throughout. Reflexivity and sensemaking also supported deepening understandings of our dual role as physicians actively supporting the brokers’ and communities’ understanding of the emerging medical questions. EC, as a vaccination and reproductive infectious diseases expert, and DC-S, as a family medicine expert, worked to keep abreast of the evolving clinical, therapeutic and public health response. This understanding was critical in our engagement with the brokers and supporting of community outreach when requested through town halls, zoom sessions, outreach to faith leaders and health system leaders.

Trust was the foundational component. It is supported by the intermediaries’ shared refugee and immigrant experience, and deep knowledge of the culture and countries of origin. Their status as trusted partners with the formal system and other service organisations comes from over 25 years of partnerships and ongoing work to build and maintain relationships and navigate challenges. This history facilitated their ability to mobilise community members to engage in information sessions built to address emerging community questions and areas of vaccine hesitancy. Trust enabled the intermediaries to create timely, culturally and linguistically tailored information in print and video form; organise community engagement opportunities using Zoom; host pop-up information and vaccination sessions in partnership with public health to remove barriers to access; and, mobilise natural communication channels with key community leaders to help people access resources, navigate decisional conflict and address misinformation about COVID-19 vaccination. In this sense, trust was not only a precondition for engagement but also the mechanism that allowed information and expertise to flow between communities and formal systems. The results highlight trust as crucial to creating the conditions for ontological security, creating safe spaces for engagement and sense-making to reduce decisional conflict.

Engagement in sensemaking and solution finding over time was conducted at multiple levels: with individuals, families, communities, service and religious organisations, with experts, and with the formal system. People’s willingness to engage in collective sensemaking, and to support the actions of the intermediaries in delivering solutions, was contingent on both trust and relationships. Leveraging of intermediaries’ social and cultural capital, closely intertwined with trust, was required for action. They drew from their prior experience navigating public health crises, such as the Ft. McMurray fires of 2016. This capital allowed them to mobilise people for action. At the heart of this process was a deliberate respect for each person’s worldview, enacted through a reflective, non-judgemental stance. This approach enabled intermediaries to surface and address deeply held fears, decisional conflicts and misinformation, creating the conditions for constructive sensemaking (figure 4). These results underscore the critical role of relationships as the infrastructure for sensemaking.

Leveraging of social and cultural capital was particularly evident in bridging across different fields—community, formal systems such as health and education, and religious groups. One example concerned temporary foreign workers whose employment was disrupted when the pandemic hit. Their precarious immigration status created stress: they wanted to be vaccinated but feared deportation. Intermediaries mobilised community organisations serving this group for outreach and brokered with the health system to ensure sensitive information was not collected during vaccination. This bridging capacity allowed access barriers to be mitigated in real time. Such examples illustrate that the power of intermediaries lay not only in transmitting information but in reconfiguring relationships between communities and institutions to enable action.

Insights for the formal system also emerged through intermediaries’ ongoing navigation of the crisis (box 2). They emphasised the need for proactive engagement between formal systems and communities; recognition of intermediaries’ crucial role and formal incorporation into bidirectional knowledge transfer; and greater understanding of how people’s histories and habitus shape their interpretations of government and health system actions in a pandemic. Intermediaries also highlighted their unique ability to mobilise communities by aligning formal expertise with local realities. One example was the recognition that vaccine mandates created tension between the need to protect people vs individual freedoms (box 2). It was apparent that people who had experienced oppressive government control in their home countries were concerned that vaccine mandates were a double-edged sword and that governments might get used to exercising this power, and in the future use it in more oppressive ways. Government and formal systems supporting community pop-up vaccination clinics, providing incentives and transportation support were well received. A key insight was the ability of intermediaries to bridge to medical experts—integrating them as intimate collaborators in trust-building efforts and embedding them in community information events and outreach. Through these processes, experts became familiar and trusted figures, thereby enhancing the legitimacy of formal knowledge in community sensemaking.

Taken together, these findings illustrate that intermediaries’ effectiveness lay not simply in delivering information or organising services, but in cultivating the social and cultural conditions that made action possible. By fostering trust, maintaining relationships and leveraging cultural capital, they created safe spaces in which uncertainty could be navigated and collective solutions forged. Their bridging role brings otherwise distant scientific expertise closer to communities, enabling its integration into local decision-making. In doing so, intermediaries helped sustain a sense of stability and control—key ingredients for people to make decisions under crisis conditions. These mechanisms resonate with broader theoretical accounts of how societies confront risk and uncertainty, themes elaborated in the following discussion.

Interpretation

The COVID-19 crisis has brought into sharp relief the need for advancing the integration of formal health systems and communities globally in order to combat the unwarranted variations in impact and outcome in vulnerable populations. The findings of this study highlight that there are intermediaries with existing relationships, solutions and infrastructure that can be enriched, leveraged and amplified to better meet emergency response needs in the community. Understanding how intermediaries work in contextually and culturally appropriate ways—by leveraging trust, bridging diverse fields and mobilising action through exaptation from prior crisis experience—emerges as a crucial element in strengthening system resilience for crisis response. This study was conducted during the real-time unfolding of the COVID-19 crisis. Our results demonstrate how the rapidly evolving context of the crisis threatened peoples’ sense of ontological security, creating decisional conflict around actions such as vaccination. Ontological security refers to a sense of trust, order and continuity in one’s experiences.27 28 The four components characterising the intermediaries’ role—trust, relational work, creation of safe spaces for collective sensemaking and solution finding, and the leveraging of social and cultural capital—together created an ecosystem to mitigate the threat to ontological security, enabling understanding and action.

COVID-19 was distinct from prior crises in that intermediaries’ and community members’ understanding of what was happening required active integration of medical content experts into the ongoing sensemaking process. Intermediaries’ ability to bridge to experts empowered communities’ navigation of the evolving landscape. This integration surfaced people’s decisional conflicts around pandemic measures such as vaccination, while also opening opportunities for dialogue, outreach and action. Concrete examples included supporting groups with specific needs (pregnant individuals, parents, people living with disabilities and seniors); partnering with religious leaders to reinforce consistent public health messaging; mobilising community and business leaders to facilitate pop-up vaccination clinics; working with schools to support parent communication around pediatric vaccination; and collaborating with community organisations to marshal shared resources for emergency supports.

Our findings align with prior scholarship on how human societies navigate risk and uncertainty. Lupton, in her seminal work Risk,1 27 highlights the importance of supporting peoples’ sensemaking to reduce decisional conflict and sustain ontological security—the stability and continuity required to act in times of crisis.28 29 Ontological security entails a sense of control, and to some extent a sense of governing one’s own fate.30 Familiarity, routines and trusting relationships help preserve this security when unpredictability impairs decision-making. Trust is the linchpin of ontological security in crisis31 and in our study, the foundation for intermediaries’ effectiveness. Giddens underscores the divide between communities’ internal expertise and the scientific expertise needed to interpret an evolving crisis.29 Humans’ rely most heavily on trusted figures within their social networks for sensemaking.5 19 Yet distant scientific experts—‘absent others’1 31 must be relationally proximate to be integrated into community decision making. Intermediaries’ bridging role achieved precisely this: embedding experts into community networks, rendering them familiar and trustworthy, and thereby legitimising their knowledge within local sensemaking processes. In this way, intermediaries not only facilitated individual decision-making but also strengthened collective resilience.

This study captured real-world data from members of ethnocultural populations in one Canadian city during the unfolding of the pandemic. While this provides valuable insight, further research across diverse international contexts will enrich understanding of how intermediaries operate and how formal systems might partner with them. Nevertheless, our findings suggest practical directions for pandemic preparedness: integrating intermediaries into public health strategy, investing in their capacity to bridge cultural and formal systems, and creating infrastructures for bidirectional knowledge flow. Doing so can expand opportunities for social sensemaking, reduce decisional conflict and ultimately enhance community and system resilience in navigating future emergencies.

Conclusions

The Sensemaking Project illuminates how cultural brokers, as trusted intermediaries, provided ongoing support for the pandemic response with timely, cultural and linguistically tailored information, education and outreach efforts. By creating safe spaces and bridging between community and health systems to support social sensemaking in the dynamic evolving context of the COVID-19 vaccination response, they helped to reduce decisional conflict and misinformation, supporting people to make informed, values-congruent personal decisions. We need to explicitly amplify and integrate the voices of intermediaries and their work into ongoing health system transformations. Understanding how intermediaries operate in contextually and culturally appropriate ways, leveraging trust across diverse fields they bridge, and mobilising action by exaptation from previous crisis experience is crucial to developing system resilience for future emergency response.
