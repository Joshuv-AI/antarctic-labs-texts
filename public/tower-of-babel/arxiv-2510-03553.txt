CCD-Bench: Probing Cultural Conflict in Large Language Model Decision-Making
Hasibur Rahman, Hanan Salam

Abstract
Although Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly implicated in interpersonal and societal decision-making, their ability to navigate explicit conflicts between legitimately different cultural value systems remains largely unexamined. Existing benchmarks predominantly target cultural knowledge (CulturalBench), value prediction (WorldValuesBench), or single-axis bias diagnostics (CDEval); none, however, evaluate how LLMs adjudicate when multiple culturally grounded values directly clash. We address this gap with CCD-Bench, a benchmark that assesses LLM decision-making under overt cross-cultural value conflict. CCD-Bench comprises 2,182 open-ended dilemmas spanning seven domains, each paired with exactly ten anonymized response options corresponding to the ten GLOBE cultural clusters, which represent the organizational behavior of 62 societies. These dilemmas are presented using a Stratified Latin Square to mitigate ordering effects. We evaluate 17 leading non-reasoning LLMs. LLMs disproportionately prefer Nordic Europe (mean 20.2%) and Germanic Europe (12.4%), while the options for Eastern Europe and the Middle East & North Africa are underrepresented (5.6–5.8%). Although 87.9% of rationales reference two or more GLOBE dimensions, this apparent pluralism is largely superficial: LLMs repeatedly recombine a narrow subset of Future Orientation and Performance Orientation, and rarely ground choices in Assertiveness or Gender Egalitarianism (both < 3 % <3\% ). Ordering effects are negligible (Cramér’s V < 0.10 V<0.10 ), and symmetrized KL divergence indicates LLMs clustering by developer lineage rather than geography. Taken together, these patterns suggest that contemporary alignment pipelines encourage a consensus-oriented, progress-centric worldview that underserves scenarios demanding explicit power negotiation, rights-based reasoning, or gender-aware analysis. CCD-Bench thus shifts evaluation from isolated bias detection to pluralistic decision making, revealing that current LLMs maintain Western-centric, consensus-oriented preferences even when confronted with ten equally valid, culturally diverse alternatives, and underscoring the need for alignment strategies that substantively engage with diverse worldviews.



Introduction

Large Language models (LLMs) have been actively explored as decision mediators in interpersonal and social contexts, including conflict resolution and moral judgment tasks
(
Tan et al. 2024
;
Pires et al. 2025
;
Koçak, Puranam, and Yeğin 2024
)
. Given that LLMs have become ubiquitous in everyday applications through conversational user interfaces, millions of people worldwide are engaging with LLMs in socially mediated roles. A seemingly mundane query, “How should I email my boss to request leave?”, demands not only factual accuracy but culturally astute counsel that respects prevailing norms and values.

However, the widespread adoption of LLMs exposes a pressing challenge: can LLMs align their recommendations with the full spectrum of global cultural values? A growing body of evidence shows that LLMs inherit salient cultural leanings from their training data
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Karinshak et al. 2024
;
Lu, Song, and Zhang 2025
;
AlKhamissi et al. 2024
)
. Users, therefore, risk receiving responses that, although coherent, may be culturally narrow or inconsistent. Consider the question “How do you perceive the sharing of responsibilities within the family?” a query that elicits fundamentally different yet equally legitimate responses across cultures, from merit-based distribution to age-based hierarchies to gender egalitarianism. While prompt-engineering strategies that foreground contextual cues offer partial relief
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Zhong, Yun, and Sun 2024
)
, a deeper dilemma emerges:

”How do LLMs adjudicate situations where legitimate cultural values, such as hierarchy vs egalitarianism, or group harmony vs personal initiative, directly collide?”
.

Following
AlKhamissi et al. 2024
, we adopt “cultural trends” rather than “biases” to describe LLM inclinations, as these patterns may not imply harm but reveal limitations in serving pluralistic contexts.

Leading benchmarks, such as MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
and ELO
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, prioritize factual accuracy and stylistic quality, thereby overlooking the cultural nuance and social intelligence required in real-world interactions. Early alignment paradigms foreground universal principles such as helpfulness, honesty, harmlessness (the “HHH” criteria)
(
Askell et al. 2021
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
, yet sidestep the pluralistic, culturally contingent nature of human values
(
Barbara Herrnstein Smith 1991
)
. Real decisions often hinge on reconciling legitimate but conflicting value systems, a challenge for which the “HHH” framework offers little guidance. In contrast to existing benchmarks
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Chiu et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
that simply record where a model sits on individual cultural axes, a current limitation in the field is that no prior benchmark explores such explicit cultural conflicts, where legitimate values directly clash, leaving a gap in understanding LLMs’ decision-making in pluralistic scenarios.

To address this gap, we introduce
CCD-Bench
, the
Culture-Conflict Decision Benchmark
. CCD-Bench probes how LLMs navigate value-plural dilemmas in which multiple worldviews are equally defensible. Our diagnostic suite evaluates the 17 most-used LLMs (excluding specialized reasoning models), embedding all culturally salient information directly in the prompt to isolate the LLMs’ intrinsic decision tendencies. CCD-Bench constitutes a paradigm shift in LLMs’ cultural evaluation. The benchmark comprises 2,182 dilemmas across seven domains: Arts, Education, Family, Wellness, Work, Scientific, and Lifestyle, adapted from
Wang et al. 2024
. Building on the insight that many LLMs already encode cultural frameworks
(
Karinshak et al. 2024
)
, we generate ten anonymized candidate answers for each dilemma via a state-of-the-art reasoning model, followed by human verification. Each answer embodies one of GLOBE’s cultural clusters: Anglo, Latin Europe, Nordic Europe, Germanic Europe, Eastern Europe, Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East and North Africa, Southern Asia, and Confucian Asia, representing 62 societies
(
House et al. 2004
)
. Rather than locating an LLM on isolated cultural dimensions, we observe how it behaves when confronted with competing imperatives. CCD-Bench embeds the cultural trade-off within the dilemma itself, offers ten culturally-diverse responses aligned with GLOBE cultural framework, and asks the LLM to select and justify one, thereby exposing its cultural reasoning processes.

We advance three contributions:

1.

We introduce CCD-Bench, the first benchmark for evaluating LLMs on navigating complex cultural value conflicts, comprising 2,182 dilemmas across seven domains with ten culturally distinct, anonymized responses per dilemma, drawn from GLOBE clusters.

2.

We develop a robust pipeline for generating and verifying culturally authentic alternatives, utilizing multi-cluster prompting, similarity pruning, human annotation, and a Stratified Latin Square presentation to facilitate a fair assessment of value pluralism.

3.

We evaluate 17 leading LLMs on CCD-Bench, revealing systematic patterns in how different models navigate cultural conflicts and the extent to which they default to particular cultural frameworks.

Related Work

Compelling evidence already shows that LLMs carry distinct cultural fingerprints. Prompting GPT-4 in English, for instance, yields analytic, independence-laden reasoning, whereas Chinese prompts evoke holistic, interdependent values
(
Lu, Song, and Zhang 2025
)
. Persona prompting can accentuate these leanings, but cannot erase them. Likewise,
CDEval
finds GPT-4 skewed toward low power distance and high individualism, while Baichuan-13B and Qwen-7B exhibit stronger collectivist tendencies
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Yet, its binary, one-dimension-at-a-time format flattens pluralism.
LLM-GLOBE
moves to open-ended prompts, uncovering tonal and rhetorical differences
(
Karinshak et al. 2024
)
, but confines itself to bilateral comparisons and still avoids explicit value collisions. A cross-cultural survey alignment study
(
AlKhamissi et al. 2024
)
demonstrates improvements through “Anthropological Prompting”, but falls short of testing what happens when legitimate values directly clash.

Other benchmarks further highlight cultural gaps in LLMs but similarly stop short of probing value conflicts. For instance,
CulturalBench

(
Chiu et al. 2024
)
features 1,227 human-written questions across 45 regions and 17 topics to assess cultural knowledge, with variants for different difficulty levels. However, it focuses on recall and ambiguity handling rather than decision-making amid conflicting norms.
WorldValuesBench

(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
draws from the World Values Survey to create millions of examples for predicting demographic-conditioned values, evaluating alignment via distribution metrics, but emphasizes prediction over resolution of conflicting perspectives.
BLEnD

(
Myung et al. 2024
)
tests everyday cultural knowledge with 52,600 QA pairs across 16 countries and 13 languages, revealing performance disparities for low-resource cultures, although it targets factual recall rather than value trade-offs. CAMeL
(
Naous et al. 2024
)
and its extensions detect biases in associations, particularly between Arab and Western entities, using fill-in-the-blank prompts and parallel data, but center on implicit biases instead of explicit dilemmas requiring justification.

Despite these contributions, a key limitation persists: no existing work systematically explores how LLMs adjudicate cultural value conflicts, where multiple legitimate worldviews collide in decision scenarios. This gap motivates CCD-Bench’s focus on pluralistic dilemmas with cultural conflicts.

Methodology

This section describes the methodology used to construct CCD-Bench. The benchmark itself consists of 2,182 cultural dilemmas with 10 culturally-aligned response alternatives each, while this methodology details the systematic process for generating, verifying, and structuring these materials.

Generation Module

We build upon CDEval’s question corpus as it represents the most comprehensive collection of culturally-relevant scenarios. These questions were specifically designed to elicit culture-dependent responses and have been empirically shown to produce divergent answers across different cultural contexts
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. By leveraging this validated foundation rather than generating questions de novo, we ensure our benchmark captures genuine cultural decision points while maintaining comparability with existing cultural AI evaluation efforts.

We begin with the 2,953 short–form cultural questions curated by
Wang et al. 2024
. From these, we select 2204 open-ended questions that can plausibly elicit divergent advice when viewed through different cultural lenses by removing questions that required binary options.
Each scenario is categorized into one of seven application domains: Arts, Education, Family, Wellness, Work, Scientific, and Lifestyle.

For every dilemma
q
, we generate a set of
10

culturally distinctive
answers
\{a_{c}\}_{c=1}^{10}
, one per GLOBE cluster
\mathcal{C}=\{
Anglo, Latin Europe, …, Confucian Asia
\}

(
House et al. 2004
)
.
A single call to a state-of-the-art reasoning model (OpenAI
o3-2025-04-16
) produces these alternatives in parallel by embedding fenced
<cultural identity>
directives directly in the prompt. The
<cultural identity>
contained the anonymized GLOBE results of each cultural cluster. A detailed prompt structure can be found in the public release. The reasoning model must return: (i) a
one–sentence answer
that omits explicit self-reference to culture and (ii) a
one–sentence rationale
that references GLOBE cultural values.
Based on the findings from
Balepur, Rudinger, and Boyd-Graber 2025
, we ask the LLM to provide the rationale to increase coherence with the specific
<cultural identity>
.

Similarity pruning and regeneration.

While our prompting should theoretically yield diverse responses based on
<cultural identity>
directives, redundancy emerged at the lexical level when generated responses of different cultures express similar pragmatic advice using overlapping vocabulary. As redundant answers reduce discriminative power, we therefore compute pairwise
MinHash Jaccard

(
Cann et al. 2025
)
similarity over 3-gram shingles for each 10-tuple. Any pair with similarity
\tau\!>\!0.70
triggers a regeneration of the higher-similarity answer with a maximum of two attempts. We selected
\tau=0.70
based on preliminary analysis showing that answer pairs above this threshold exhibited minimal semantic distinction when manually reviewed, while pairs below 0.70 maintained meaningful cultural perspectives.

Due to not meeting the threshold among 2,204 questions, 22 questions were removed. Across the final 2,182 dilemmas, the pair-wise
MinHash Jaccard
similarity between the ten alternatives has a mean of
M=0.123
with a standard deviation of
\sigma=0.109
(N =
\binom{10}{2}\times 2182
comparisons), confirming that regeneration successfully suppresses near-duplicate content while preserving lexical variety. Our similarity pruning specifically targets lexical redundancy while preserving semantic and cultural distinctiveness, as validated by our human verification step.

Option ordering.

Pezeshkpour and Hruschka 2024
demonstrated that the order of options in multiple-choice questions (MCQs) significantly impacts the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs). To neutralize serial-position effects, we employ a
10\times 10

Stratified Latin Square
, a domain-specific adaptation of the standard Latin square design, where independent Latin squares are generated and applied separately for each individual domain to ensure balanced option ordering within those subgroups, rather than globally across the entire corpus. Each cultural alternative appears exactly once in every ordinal slot (1–10) and precedes/follows every other alternative exactly once across the whole corpus, satisfying both row and column orthogonality. A detailed evaluation prompt structure can be found in the public release.

Verification Module

A human annotator reviews every candidate set. The annotator confirms: (i) each generated option would be viewed as
reasonable
within the target cluster, (ii) does not mention the cultural cluster directly, and (iii) is semantically distinct from the other nine options. Non-conforming options are regenerated until all criteria are met, with a maximum of two attempts. None of the earlier 2,182 dilemmas were removed at this step.

Evaluation Module

Our analytic pipeline decomposes evaluation logs into three layers: descriptive statistics, null-hypothesis significance tests, and robust similarity/diversity indices.

Descriptive layer.

For each evaluation file, we record (i) absolute frequency
f_{c}

\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{10})

H_{0}

(1)

<0.10

<0.30

\geq 0.50

We construct a

Taken together, the results suggest prevailing alignment pipelines embed a conciliatory, progress-centred worldview emblematic of affluent liberal democracies—functional in many settings yet inadequate to capture humanity’s full cultural breadth.

Do LLMs integrate multiple cultural dimensions or default to a single axis? (RQ3)

High pluralism.

Across the whole corpus (
N=37,092
rationales), 87.9% cite two or more dimensions, indicating that most LLMs
can
articulate multi-faceted justifications when prompted. Yet, the model spread is broad, ranging from a low of 68.6% (Meta-Llama 4 Maverick) to a peak of 98.6% (Anthropic Claude-4 Sonnet). 7 of the 17 systems exceed the 90% mark, whereas three systems remain below 80%. High pluralism is therefore common.

Pluralism
\,{\neq}\,
cultural breadth.

One might expect an LLM that distributes its selections broadly (i.e., exhibits high entropy) also to draw on more dimensions, but the data suggest otherwise. The Pearson correlation between entropy and pluralism is mildly
negative
(

command-r

highest

Which pairings dominate?

Two results stand out: first, a single pairing explains one-fifth of all plural rationales, with
Future Orientation (FO) + Performance Orientation (PO)
appearing 6,732 times (20.6% of all plural explanations), no other pairing reaches 16%, meaning LLMs overwhelmingly justify “multi-dimensional” decisions as “long-term planning to achieve goals”; second, collectivist modifiers dominate the next tier, splicing

Summing across all pairings shows the frequency with which each GLOBE dimension enters multi-dimensional rationales: PO (16.2%), FO (15.1%), IC (14.9%), IGC (13.5%), HO (13.0%), PD (12.5%), Uncertainty Avoidance (UA) (9.5%), Gender Egalitarianism (GE) (2.7%), and Assertiveness (AS) (2.6%). <

The expanded per-model breakdown shows that: (i)

LLMs integrate multiple cultural dimensions, but combinations are imbalanced: four mainstream values, FO, PO, and the two forms of Collectivism, dominate

Our generated corpus has a Latin-square balance score of
\approx 99.8\%
. Despite employing the Stratified Latin Square design to neutralize serial and residual ordering biases, these biases may persist if models exhibit systematic preferences for certain positions (e.g., favoring early or late options). In this section, we analyze the distribution of selections across the 10 positions for each cultural cluster, aggregated over all 17 models and 2,182 dilemmas (total valid selections: 36,977; expected per position under uniformity: 3,697.7). We quantify positional bias using Cramér’s
V
per cluster, interpreting values below 0.10 as negligible. Figure
3
visualizes the percentage of selections for each cluster-position pair.

Overall Positional Bias Levels.

All 10 clusters exhibit negligible positional bias, with Cramér’s
V
values ranging from 0.0510 (Nordic Europe) to 0.0877 (Eastern Europe), confirming no small, medium, or large effects across the board. This low bias underscores the effectiveness of our Latin Square design in mitigating ordering artifacts. A detailed breakdown can be found in the public release.

Mild Primacy Effect in Positions.

A modest yet discernible primacy tendency surfaces: Position 1 (P1) is chosen disproportionately in nine of the ten cultural clusters, 15.6% for Confucian Asia and 15.5% for Eastern Europe, for example, producing an aggregate deviation of 42.2 from the uniform baseline. Nordic Europe constitutes the sole exception, exhibiting a slight preference for P4 (12.7%) over P1 (11.4%). Choices in the later positions (P6–P10) remain evenly distributed, whereas P5 proves most balanced overall, with a deviation of only 8.6.

Cluster-Specific Variations.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
CCD-Bench
, a novel benchmark specifically designed to assess how contemporary LLMs navigate conflicts among legitimate cultural values. Evaluating 17 LLMs on 2,182 dilemmas, we observed a pronounced cultural trend toward Nordic and Germanic clusters, hallmarks of egalitarian, consensus-oriented reasoning, while perspectives rooted in Eastern Europe and the Middle East & North Africa remained markedly under‐represented. Despite high rates of multi-dimensional rationales, models relied on a narrow set of GLOBE dimensions, while rarely invoking Assertiveness or Gender Egalitarianism (
<
3% each), suggesting an aversion to confrontational or gender-focused reasoning and indicating limited true pluralism in reasoning. Ordering biases were negligible due to our Stratified Latin Square design, and model similarities clustered more by developer lineage than geographic provenance.

These results underscore the need for more nuanced approaches to cultural dynamics in LLMs if they are to operate credibly within an increasingly pluralistic world. Future research could extend CCD-Bench to multilingual prompts, incorporate dynamic user interactions, or explore mitigation strategies, such as targeted fine-tuning, to foster more diverse and adaptable decision-making.
