Latent profile analysis of mindfulness and experiential avoidance associated with social media use disorder
Authors: Mustafa Ercengiz, Samet Makas, Mustafa Bayraktar, Mehmet Şata, Erol Uğur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine mindfulness and experiential avoidance as profile indicators associated with social media use disorder through an individual-centered approach. Therefore, we examined how mindfulness and experiential avoidance co-occur with social media use disorder within a theoretically informed framework.MethodsThe study data were obtained from 826 participants, 348 (42%) male and 478 (58%) female. The participants’ ages varied from 18 to 25, with a mean age of 21.3 (df = 1.8). The associations between the variables were investigated using correlation analysis. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to find university student profiles.ResultsAs a result of the correlation analysis, mindfulness is negatively correlated with both social media disorder and experiential avoidance. Social media disorder is positively correlated experiential avoidance.ConclusionsThe four profiles obtained from LPA express the pattern in university students by examining social media use disorder, cognitive and emotional conscious awareness and experiential avoidance behavior together. Future research should examine whether this pattern can be observed among students in different developmental periods and education levels. The characteristics that differentiate individuals in the profiles from each other can be retested in similar samples using the repeatability feature of LPA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04961-0.

Introduction

In addition to the many conveniences it provides (fast and practical communication, online shopping), social media has an ever-increasing area of influence. Recent studies show that social media use is rapidly expanding [1–5]. 62.3% of the world’s population uses social media. The time spent on social media increased by 8 min compared to January 2023 and was recorded as 2.23 h per day in January 2024. 94.3% of internet users in the world use social media. Most adolescents spend most of their time online on social media, online music, and video platforms [6].

The rapid spread of social media use brings problems related to social media use [7–13]. One of these problems can be said to be social media use disorder [14–19]. Social media use disorder is associated with problems such as depression-anxiety-stress [20], social anxiety [21], low self-esteem and psychological well-being [22], and anhedonia [23]. However, is social media use disorder, which has attracted the attention of researchers, associated with the emergence of mental, behavioral, and social problems? Or is it related to specific existing personality profiles (neurotic, extrovert, etc.)? Some recent studies suggest that behavioral, psychological, and social problems are associated with patterns of positive or negative social media use. Research findings show that social media use disorder is associated with factors such as usage pattern [24], personality structure [25–26], and usage motivation [27]. In other words, there are complex relationships between social media use disorder and individual preferences and personal characteristics in the use of social media. Therefore, an individual-centered approach was needed instead of a variable-centered approach to investigate the existence of different subgroups of individuals regarding social media use disorder. In our study, we examined mindfulness and experiential avoidance as profile indicators to understand how different configurations of these variables are associated with social media use disorder through this approach. Therefore, we examined factors theoretically relevant to understanding social media use disorder-related profiles within a theoretically informed framework.

Theoretical basis of the research

Avoidance of negative internal experiences has been discussed across several psychological and clinical traditions [28–31]. However, the present study is grounded primarily in the Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) framework because ACT directly conceptualizes experiential avoidance and mindfulness as theoretically relevant constructs for understanding behavioral dysregulation [32–33]. In this study, these constructs are not treated as causal mechanisms or predictors; rather, they are examined as ACT-informed profile indicators that co-occur with social media use disorder (SMUD) in a cross-sectional context.

Within ACT, experiential avoidance refers to attempts to avoid contact with unwanted internal experiences and to alter the frequency, form, or situational triggers of these experiences [33]. ACT conceptualizes experiential avoidance as a process associated with psychological inflexibility and with several forms of psychological difficulty, including addictive or excessive technology-related behaviors [34–44]. Accordingly, the analytical model in the present study is based on an ACT-consistent rationale in which experiential avoidance and mindfulness are used to characterize latent patterns associated with SMUD.

Social media use may be associated with momentary isolation from the real environment and temporary distance from feelings and thoughts [45]. In particular, one of the most common uses is the behavior of briefly logging into social media from the phone at any time during the day and exiting a few minutes later. This behavior may help individuals temporarily distance themselves from experienced problems, relationship issues that need to be solved, daily responsibilities, and even complex moods [46]. Thus, the instantaneous feeling of relief may be associated with using social media for a more extended period and more frequently [47]. While the use of social media in this way may reinforce the individual’s avoidance behavior, it may also be associated with a decrease in the perception of control. Social media may function as one practical context for experiential avoidance. Therefore, it can be said that avoidance behavior is an important correlate of social media use disorder.

Mindfulness is defined as focusing on what is happening in the present moment in a non-judgmental and accepting manner [48]. According to ACT, mindfulness refers to experiencing internal and external events as they occur in the present rather than being dominated by past- or future-oriented evaluations [33]. Previous studies have shown that individuals with higher mindfulness tend to report lower levels of social media use disorder [49]. Individuals with lower mindfulness also tend to report more negative affect [50], whereas higher mindfulness has been associated with stronger emotion regulation skills [48, 51]. Although these findings are theoretically consistent with ACT, the present study does not test mindfulness as a causal protective mechanism. Instead, mindfulness is treated as an ACT-informed correlate and profile indicator that may help characterize different patterns of SMUD-related psychological functioning [52].

Experiential avoidance and mindfulness as correlates of social media use disorder

In ACT, experiential avoidance is defined as an individual’s avoidance of contact with internal experiences (sensation, feeling, behavioral tendency, emotion) and the effort to change the content or frequency of these experiences [53]. Considering that an individual experiencing experiential avoidance moves away from accepting the emotions and thoughts of the moment as they are in the face of challenging experiences [54], it can be said that individuals characterized by higher experiential avoidance may report more intensive social media use [41]. Accordingly, it can be seen that experiential avoidance can be considered in an associational rather than causal context in its relationship with social media use disorder. The results of increased social media use may help examine correlates of social media use disorder (SMUD). A related study observed that SMUD was associated with experiential avoidance behavior and that the duration of digital tool use had a mediating effect [55]. Some research findings revealed that greater social media use duration was associated with higher experiential avoidance behavior [41], and higher experiential avoidance behavior was associated with higher social media use disorder [41, 56–58]. In this context, the fact that social media use may provide individuals with instant emotional relief [59], may be linked to a feeling of distancing from individual problems [60, 61], and may relate to lower momentary stress levels [62] may be associated with experiential avoidance behavior.

Many studies examining the relationship between personality traits and SMUD have pointed out that personality traits and SMUD have significant relationships, but there is a lack of literature on personality profiles and SMUD [63, 64]. It is stated that individuals with an experiential avoidance (EA) profile use avoidance of negative experiences as a coping strategy, and this is associated with intensive technology and internet use [33]. Considering the relationship between experiential avoidance and personality traits [65], it can be said that EA can be considered a correlate of SMUD. Mindfulness skill involves an individual being aware of the moment and objectively evaluating their feelings and thoughts [66]. Through mindfulness studies, the person notices their feelings and takes action to reduce the adverse effects they create [67]. In this respect, mindfulness gains importance in realizing the feelings of tension and stress experienced by the individual in environments such as social media, where instant change is very rapid, and the positive aspects of experiences are often emphasized [68]. The relationship between social media use disorder and problems such as anxiety [69], depression [70], anxiety [71], eating disorders [72], loneliness and isolation [73] and negative body image [74] has been revealed. Therefore, emotional, thought, and behavioral problems associated with social media use disorder may be addressed through mindfulness skills [75–79].

Mindfulness is seen as a method that can be used to regulate emotions and overcome experiential avoidance [80]. Mindfulness appears to yield effective results in mindfulness-based therapy applications and ACT [81]. With experiential avoidance, a person experiences short-term relief by avoiding intrusive thoughts and experiences cognitively, emotionally, and behaviorally but develops problems instead of a coping strategy in the long term [82]. However, mindfulness is an important practice to overcome these problems [83–86]. In this respect, examining the relationship between the concepts of mindfulness and experiential avoidance can be inspiring for future applications.

Social media use is associated with a person’s emotional state and behaviors (such as spending more time and frequency on a mobile phone and feeling stressed when unable to access social media) [87]. Social media use disorder, like substance abuse, may be related to reward-system processes associated with addictive patterns [88]. However, since there has not been enough research on this subject, a clear conclusion cannot be drawn about the relationship between social media use disorder and other psychopathologies. Although there is a statistical relationship between mindfulness and experiential avoidance [80], it is thought that evaluating the relationship between social media use disorder and these concepts together is important in understanding the SMUD phenomenon in the context of cross-sectional associations and theoretical links.

Social media use disorder is associated with emotions and thoughts and may coincide with stronger negative emotions [89]. This may be associated with higher experiential avoidance behavior. People use social media more to avoid negative experiences [57]. However, maladaptive use behaviors may be lower among individuals with higher mindfulness [58]. However, it can be said that the question of whether mindfulness practice can help clarify the association between social media use disorder and experiential avoidance is critical.

Latent profile analysis (LPA)

The total score approach, frequently used in studies, ignores the heterogeneity of the study population [90]. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) is a statistical approach that examines groups of individuals with similar symptom patterns according to their severity levels. In this respect, the LPA approach treats each individual as a consistent unit as a person-centered statistical method. It aims to determine SMUD usage profiles based on several SMUD usage indicators. Compared with other classification models, LPA strictly preserves the number of categories and achieves more accurate classification using the maximum likelihood estimator [91].

LPA maximizes the heterogeneity between subgroups and homogeneity within subgroups, thus identifying unobserved subgroups (classes) within the sample [92]. Using the LPA approach, we can identify profiles of individuals with similar characteristics of SMUD use, thus determining the heterogeneity of different potential usage profiles of individuals experiencing SMUD and distinguishing typical combinations of social media usage patterns in individuals. For example, LPA can distinguish low-risk users from users with intense problems. In this way, it provides the opportunity to understand the impact of social media use on individuals in more depth. Due to these advantages, the LPA approach has been widely applied in subgroup classification studies such as sociology and psychology [93].

Methods

Participants

A sensitivity-based power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to assess the adequacy of the final sample size. Since the pairwise relationships among CAMS-R, SMDS-9, and MEAQ scores were examined using Pearson correlation analysis, the analysis was conducted with a two-way correlation test at α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a final sample size of N = 815. The results showed that the sample was sufficient to detect small relationships at r = .098. Considering that the observed correlations ranged from |r| = 0.399 to |r| = 0.458, the current sample appears to have sufficient statistical power to test the relationships examined in the study. Additionally, the minimum detectable effect size in the sensitivity analysis based on a one-way ANOVA for the four profile groups was Cohen’s f = 0.116. These findings indicate that the final analysis sample is sufficient for correlation and profile comparisons. While the study’s universe consists of university students, the sample consists of 826 university students selected by convenience sampling, one of the non-probability sampling methods. Using the appropriate sampling method for sample selection facilitates efficient and rapid data collection but may introduce some limitations concerning representativeness. Consequently, the objective was to mitigate generalizability concerns by extensively specifying the demographic distribution (e.g., age, gender). 58% (478) of the participants were female, whilst 42% (348) were male. The participants’ ages varied from 18 to 25, with a mean age of 21.3 (df = 1.8). Research data were collected via an online form. The researcher conducted the study by incorporating a statement that outlined the study’s goals, methodology, confidentiality, and principles of voluntary participation. In the amended versions, 25 respondents who either failed to complete the forms or submitted them inaccurately or negligently, together with five respondents who allegedly never utilized the Internet, were eliminated. Data gathered from 826 students were assessed and analyzed.

Given that the data were collected using self-report measures administered within the same online survey, the potential influence of common method variance was examined using Harman’s single-factor test. All items from the three study measures were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis. The first unrotated factor accounted for 16.79% of the total variance, which was well below the commonly used 50% threshold. This finding suggests that common method variance was unlikely to represent a dominant source of bias in the present data. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional and self-report nature of the study, common method variance cannot be completely ruled out.

Instruments

The social media disorder scale (SMDS-9)

The 9item Social Media Disorder Scale (SMDS9) [94]was created to assess the extent of social media use disorder among teenagers. Items (e.g., Have you persistently experienced an inability to concentrate on anything else until you could reaccess social media? ) assessed on an eight-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 7 (more than 40 times daily). The score obtained from the scale varies between 0 and 63 points. A high score obtained from the scale indicates a higher level of social media use disorder among adolescents. As a result of the confirmatory factor analysis, the goodness-of-fit indices of the scale were calculated as follows: χ²/df = 1.87, RMSEA = 0.066, CFI = 0.98, GFI = 0.98, IFI = 0.98, NFI = 0.96, RFI = 0.97, and SRMR = 0.039. A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was undertaken in the reliability and validity study for this research to substantiate the validity of the measurements derived from the assessment instrument. As a result of the analysis, the following goodness-of-fit indices were identified: RMSEA (90% CI) = 0.062 (0.050–0.074), SRMR = 0.073, CFI = 0.978, NNFI/TLI = 0.971, NFI = 0.972, and GFI = 0.983. For the reliability of the measurements, McDonald’s omega coefficient was calculated as 0.864, while Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.875. The analyses confirmed that the measurements obtained from the assessment tools provided reliable and valid results.

Multidimensional experiential avoidance questionnaire-30 (MEAQ)

The 30-item Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire (MEAQ) [95] was developed to assess individuals’ levels of experiential avoidance. The scale comprises six subdimensions: Behavioral Avoidance (items 1–5), Discomfort Intolerance (items 6–10), Procrastination (items 11–15), Distraction/Suppression (items 16–20), Repression/Denial (items 21–25), and Distress Endurance (items 26–30).

Each item (e.g., “I do not engage in activities that I believe will make me uncomfortable.“) is rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The scale is scored based on its six subdimensions, with only item 15 being reverse-scored. A total experiential avoidance score can also be obtained; however, this requires reverse scoring of the Distress Endurance subdimension. Higher scores indicate greater levels of experiential avoidance within the respective subdimension.

The fit indices obtained during scale development were as follows: CMIN = 812.519, df = 390, CMIN/df = 2.08, RMSEA = 0.054, SRMR = 0.068, CFI = 0.91, IFI = 0.91, and TLI = 0.90. To assess the validity of the scale, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted, yielding the following fit indices: RMSEA (90% CI) = 0.053 (0.050–0.057), SRMR = 0.063, CFI = 0.914, NNFI/TLI = 0.905, NFI = 0.881, and GFI = 0.957.

To assess the scale’s reliability, McDonald’s omega coefficient was calculated as 0.808, while Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.826. These results confirm that the assessment tool provides reliable and valid measurements of experiential avoidance.

Cognitive and affective mindfulness scale-revised (CAMS-R)

The 10-item CAMS-R [96] was developed to measure individuals’ levels of cognitive and emotional conscious awareness. The items (e.g., “It is easy for me to focus on what I am doing.“) are rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score on the scale ranges from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating a greater level of cognitive and emotional conscious awareness.

The scale’s fit indices were calculated as follows: GFI = 0.94 and CFI = 0.91. In this study, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to provide evidence for the validity of the measurements obtained from the scale. The results yielded the following values: RMSEA (90% CI) = 0.068 (0.057-0.078), SRMR = 0.070, CFI = 0.965, NNFI/TLI = 0.927, NFI = 0.937, and GFI = 0.980.

For reliability, McDonald’s omega coefficient was found to be 0.748, while Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.755. Based on these analyses, it was determined that the measurements obtained from the scale are both reliable and valid.

Data analysis

The following analyses were carried out by the study’s goals. First, descriptive statistics were used, such as mean and standard deviation for continuous data and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. The associations between the variables were investigated using correlation analysis.

McDonald’s omega and Cronbach’s alpha values were computed to evaluate reliability, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to validate the measures derived from the scales. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to find university student profiles. LPA is better than techniques like k-means clustering, which relies on distance-based algorithms because it predicts the likelihood that an individual belongs to a particular profile [97]. The LPA employed SMDS-9, CAMS-R, and MEAQ as indicators.

The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Sample-Size Adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SABIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR-LRT), and entropy were taken into consideration to estimate the LPA solutions.

Lastly, to ascertain whether the distributions of age and gender differed among the selected student profiles, difference analyses were performed. The software programs SPSS (version 25), JASP (version 0.19.1.0), and Mplus (version 8) were used for all analyses.

These transformations were not used as a basis for interpreting the substantive profile means. The LPA model estimation and the descriptive characteristics of the retained profiles were reported using the original total scores of the SMDS-9, CAMS-R, and MEAQ. The four-profile solution was retained because it remained theoretically interpretable and substantively consistent with the initial solution. The latent profile solutions were evaluated using multiple statistical and substantive criteria, including AIC, BIC, sample-size adjusted BIC, entropy, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test, the bootstrap likelihood ratio test, class size, parsimony, and theoretical interpretability. Although the information criteria generally decreased as the number of profiles increased, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test supported improvement up to the four-profile solution. Specifically, the four-profile model provided a significant improvement over the three-profile model, LMR p = .045, whereas the five-profile model did not significantly improve model fit over the four-profile model, LMR p = .068. The entropy value for the four-profile model was 0.713, indicating moderate classification precision. The four-profile solution was therefore retained because it provided the best balance between statistical fit, parsimony, classification quality, and theoretical interpretability. However, because one profile represented a relatively small subgroup of the sample, this profile was interpreted cautiously.

Although Profile 3 represented a relatively small subgroup of the sample (n = 21, 2.6%), it was retained because it showed a theoretically meaningful and empirically distinct pattern, characterized by the lowest mindfulness and the highest experiential avoidance scores. However, given its small size, this profile should be interpreted cautiously and considered preliminary until replicated in larger and independent samples. The four-profile solution was further interpreted by examining the mean scores of the Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale-Revised (CAMS-R), Social Media Disorder Scale (SMDS-9), and Multidimensional Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire-30 (MEAQ-30) across the latent profiles. Profile 1 was labelled the Mindful Low-Risk Profile because individuals in this group reported the highest level of mindfulness, as reflected by the highest CAMS-R scores, and the lowest level of social media use disorder, as indicated by the lowest SMDS-9 scores, together with the lowest MEAQ-30 scores. Profile 2 was labelled the Dysregulated High-Risk Profile, as this group showed low mindfulness, the highest level of social media use disorder, and elevated experiential avoidance. Profile 3 was labelled the Avoidant Vulnerable Profile, reflecting the lowest mindfulness and the highest experiential avoidance scores, although its level of social media use disorder was lower than that of Profile 2. Finally, Profile 4, which represented the largest proportion of the sample, was labelled the Moderate Adaptive Profile, as participants in this group showed moderate levels of mindfulness, experiential avoidance, and social media use disorder. Results and discussion

The current study utilized Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to identify heterogeneous subgroups of university students based on their levels of mindfulness, experiential avoidance, and social media use disorder (SMUD). Rather than relying on variable-centered approaches, this person-centered analytical strategy allowed for the identification of four distinct psychological profiles. To facilitate interpretation, these profiles were conceptualized as: Mindful and Adaptive Users (Profile 1), At-Risk Avoidant Users (Profile 2), Highly Detached/Avoidant Users (Profile 3), and Moderate Users (Profile 4). The findings are best understood through the lens of the Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) framework, which posits that psychological flexibility—anchored by mindfulness and acceptance—is crucial for adaptive functioning, whereas experiential avoidance represents a core vulnerability for behavioral dysregulation.

The group defined as " Mindful and Adaptive Users” (Profile 1) exhibited a pattern characterized by high conscious awareness, low experiential avoidance, and low social media use disorder. This finding parallels the literature [77, 99], which points to the protective role of conscious awareness against social media use disorder and related psychopathologies. In particular, the low level of experiential avoidance exhibited by this profile supports findings in the literature that associate it with a reduced tendency to avoid symptoms such as depression and anxiety [41, 58].

The current findings suggest that individuals who cognitively and emotionally accept the present moment may have developed a more critical awareness of idealized content in digital environments. In this context, the high level of awareness in individuals in Profile 1 may limit the need to use social media as an escape mechanism (experiential avoidance). However, this should be considered an indicator of an adaptive psychological profile rather than a causal process, given the cross-sectional data. In conclusion, individuals in this group may use social media in a more controlled and functional way that does not prevent the direct experience of the present moment. It is observed that they maintain this level.

In the “At-Risk Avoidant Users” (Profile 2) group, high levels of experiential avoidance and social media use disorder were observed despite moderate levels of conscious awareness. The pattern exhibited by this profile is consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the negative association between conscious awareness and both experiential avoidance [100, 101] and social media use disorder [102, 103]. Compared with Profile 1, the lower awareness and higher avoidance scores in this group suggest that a relative weakness in self-regulation capacity may increase the risk of social media use disorder.

The literature indicates that individuals with low levels of conscious awareness tend to spend more time on ephemeral content on social media [104]. The high social media use disorder exhibited by individuals in Profile 2, despite moderate awareness skills, may suggest that this group uses social media as a strategy to avoid negative internal experiences (anxiety, stress, etc.). The dominance of experiential avoidance in this group suggests that individuals turn to digital platforms rather than face challenging emotions. This supports the theoretical framework [100, 102]. Consequently, these findings suggest that Profile 2’s moderate level of awareness skills may be insufficient to protect against high levels of avoidance.

When Profile 2 is examined, it is noteworthy that the male ratio is higher compared to other profiles. Although studies in the literature show that social media use is more common among women [105–107], gender-based differences in usage motivations may explain this finding. It is stated that women use social media more for interpersonal communication, while men exhibit a more intensive, ‘disconnected’ use for purposes such as gaming and information tracking [108, 109]. In this context, the high male density in Profile 2 suggests that social media may be used as a tool focused on distancing from conscious awareness and experiential avoidance in this group.

The high social media use and experiential avoidance pattern exhibited by Profile 2 can be attributed to social media offering an easily accessible mechanism for escaping challenging internal experiences (stress, anxiety, etc.) [54]. Individuals may turn to instant rewards and content on digital platforms to distance themselves from the current reality. This provides a theoretical framework for why individuals in Profile 2 have lower awareness and higher avoidance scores than those in Profile 1.

The lack of a significant difference in age between the profiles may be due to the homogeneous nature of the sample (university students within a narrow age range). Therefore, future studies conducted in broader age ranges encompassing different developmental stages are important for the generalizability of these profiles.

The group defined as “Highly Detached/Avoidant Users” (Profile 3) constituted the riskiest profile within the sample, exhibiting the lowest cognitive and emotional awareness and the highest multidimensional experiential avoidance scores. Interestingly, the level of social media use disorder in this group remained at a “moderate” rather than “highest” level. The existing literature supports a negative relationship between mindful awareness and social media use disorder [58, 104] and the role of experiential avoidance in technology-focused addictions [40, 110–113]. However, the lower-than-expected level of social media use disorder in Profile 3 suggests that these individuals may have turned to alternative media outside of social media (e.g., gaming addiction or complete withdrawal) to meet their experiential avoidance needs, or that they exhibited a “mechanical detachment” rather than “overuse” on digital platforms.

Theoretically, mindful awareness allows individuals to remain present in the moment by accepting all experiences, positive or negative, thus reducing the need for avoidance strategies [83, 114]. The weak mindfulness skills of individuals in Profile 3 may be the reason for this. It may limit their capacity to cope with internal experiences that cause stress and anxiety [79]. In this context, the high level of experiential avoidance exhibited by this group can be considered as a defense mechanism against emotional difficulties. Consequently, the significant deficiency in self-regulation strategies of this profile indicates that they represent the most vulnerable group in terms of psychological health, even if they exhibit moderate levels of social media use disorder.

The “Moderate Users” (Profile 4) group exhibited moderate scores across all variables (mindfulness, experiential avoidance, and social media use disorder), mirroring the sample’s overall trend. This finding indicates that moderate mindfulness skills do not constitute a sufficient threshold to suppress experiential avoidance or eliminate the risk of social media use disorder. The negative (mindfulness- social media use disorder/avoidance) and positive (avoidance- social media use disorder) correlations identified in the current literature [41, 58, 83, 115–117] manifested as a balanced middle-ground pattern in Profile 4. This represents a spectrum where protective and risk-increasing factors balance each other in this group.

The four profiles obtained from LPA express the behavioral pattern in university students by examining social media use disorder, cognitive and emotional conscious awareness, and experiential avoidance behavior together. Future research is needed to determine whether this pattern can be observed in students across different developmental periods and education levels. The characteristics that differentiate individuals in the profiles from each other can be retested in similar samples using the repeatability feature of LPA. In addition, LPA, as a person-centered approach, can reveal complex subgroup characteristics and help understand individuals’ behavioral tendencies in depth [118]. Therefore, this analytical approach can make it easier for researchers and practitioners to explain distinct behavioral patterns regarding digital media use.

Clinical and educational implications

The identification of these profiles may offer preliminary practical implications for university psychological counseling centers. Rather than using the profiles as diagnostic categories, practitioners may consider them as heuristic patterns that can inform screening, psychoeducation, and prevention-oriented counseling for problematic digital behavior. For students whose patterns are characterized by lower mindfulness and higher experiential avoidance, ACT-informed counseling strategies may be relevant, particularly strategies that encourage present-moment awareness, cognitive defusion, acceptance of internal experiences, and more flexible responses to academic anxiety and social pressures. Because the present study is cross-sectional and does not test intervention mechanisms, these implications should be interpreted as theoretically informed suggestions that require further validation in longitudinal and intervention-based research.

Limitations

The study relied on convenience sampling and online self-report data, which may have introduced selection bias and common method variance. Although Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first unrotated factor accounted for only 16.79% of the total variance, suggesting that common method variance was not a dominant concern, this result does not eliminate the possibility of method-related bias. Participants who voluntarily completed the online survey may differ from the broader university student population in terms of digital engagement, psychological awareness, and willingness to disclose problematic social media use. Future studies should therefore employ probability-based or stratified sampling strategies, multi-informant assessments, behavioral indicators of social media use, and longitudinal designs to strengthen internal validity and reduce potential bias.

The four-profile solution should be interpreted cautiously because the entropy value indicated moderate rather than high classification precision, and one profile represented a relatively small subgroup of the sample. Therefore, profile membership should not be considered a definitive classification or psychological type. Rather, the profiles should be interpreted as probabilistic patterns of co-occurring mindfulness, experiential avoidance, and social media use disorder. Future studies should examine the stability and external validity of these profiles using larger samples, longitudinal designs, and independent behavioral or clinical indicators.
