New Medical Schools, Access to Physicians, and Health Outcomes: Evidence From Japan
Authors: Kisho Hoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe analyse the impact of the construction of medical schools and associated university hospitals in Japan during the 1970s, which was primarily driven by ‘One Prefecture, One Medical School Policy’ (OPOMS). We find that the number of physicians increased significantly in prefectures where a medical school was built, and this effect persisted for more than 20 years. Moreover, we uncover suggestive evidence that the establishment of university hospitals may lead to a reduction in the overall mortality rate, especially among the elderly. Our results also indicate that the improvement in health outcomes is likely attributed to access to high‐quality care resulting from the opening of university hospitals, rather than an overall increase in the utilisation of healthcare services.

Introduction

Reducing regional health inequality is a long‐standing challenge faced by both developing and developed countries. Numerous studies have documented a strong link between health disparities and spatial differences in healthcare access. 1 In response to this concern, governments have implemented supply‐side interventions for healthcare access. For example, in Canada, Quebec gave financial incentives for physicians to work in rural and isolated areas, attracting physicians from urban areas (Bolduc et al. (1996)). In the US, after WW2, the federal government provided subsidies for constructing non‐profit and local government hospitals in hospitals‐scarce areas, improving hospital access in rural areas (Chung et al. (2017)).

Despite the prevalence of such policies aiming to reduce spatial inequalities in healthcare access, convincing evidence of their effects on health outcomes is surprisingly limited. This issue can be attributed to two main reasons. First, the effects of supply‐side reforms to healthcare access remain unknown or ambiguous, since it is rare for these reforms to lead to quasi‐experimental variations in healthcare access. 2 Second, the lack of clean identification of the effect on healthcare access subsequently makes it difficult to estimate the effect of the intervention on health outcomes. 3

This study provides new evidence on the effectiveness of a large‐scale, supply‐side intervention by analysing the establishment of new medical schools in Japan. In the 1970s, the Japanese government's deregulation of medical school construction led 17 prefectures to establish their first medical school and an affiliated university hospital. This expansion, equivalent to roughly 25% of the prefectures with a medical school before the policy, occurred primarily under the ‘One Prefecture, One Medical School (OPOMS)’ policy. 4

We employ both an event study design and difference‐in‐differences to show the short and long‐term effects of the medical school and university hospital constructions on access to physicians and on health outcomes. We find that construction of the medical schools results in an increase of approximately 25% in the number of physicians, and this effect persists for more than 20 years. The increase in the number of physicians is mainly observed at the university hospital and spans many specialities, with treated prefectures closing their pre‐existing mid‐career physician‐density gap as cohorts mature.

Furthermore, we find suggestive evidence that the policy reduces mortality by approximately 2.7%, a decline driven primarily by a reduction in mortality among the elderly. Our analysis of the mechanisms suggests this improvement is not due to an overall increase in healthcare utilisation but is instead attributable to an increase in the quality of care following the establishment of the university hospital.

Our research contributes to several bodies of literature. First, we build on the literature examining the link between healthcare access and health outcomes in rural areas. While numerous studies find a negative correlation between physicians per capita and mortality rates (e.g., Finkelstein et al. (2021); Or et al. (2005); Robst (2001)), most do not analyse the causal impact of supply‐side interventions on health outcomes in rural settings. Notable exceptions include Gruber et al. (2014), who show that increased government funding for hospitals serving the poor and reduced co‐pays lowered infant mortality rates in Thailand, and McCloy (2022), who finds that the expansion of healthcare capital stock through Hill‐Burton funding reduced mortality rates in the US.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the positive health outcomes of an educational policy that opened a university hospital and increased the supply of young physicians by establishing a new medical school. The most closely related paper is Iizuka and Watanabe (2016), who find that a 2004 reform of Japan's medical internship system inadvertently reduced rural access to hospitals and experienced physicians, thereby increasing mortality rates. While their study documents the unintended negative consequences of a change in medical training, ours demonstrates the intended positive consequences of a policy designed to expand medical education. Our findings can therefore be viewed as complementary to theirs.

Our research is also consistent with numerous other studies examining the effects of distance to healthcare on health outcomes. This literature suggests that while the general effect of distance can be ambiguous, reducing travel time to hospitals that provide high‐quality care is crucial for lowering mortality from acute diseases. 5 This implies that supply‐side policies, such as establishing university hospitals that serve as centres for high‐quality care, can be an effective strategy for mitigating regional health disparities.

Next, our research contributes to the literature on policies designed to reduce geographical disparities in physician access. One stream of this literature evaluates whether policies that increase the overall supply of physicians successfully improve access in rural areas. For example, an active line of research analyses the effect of accepting more international medical graduates (IMGs) in the US. While there is no consensus, most studies suggest this policy has had limited success in mitigating urban‐rural disparities. 6

Our work also contributes to the literature on physician location choice, which has consistently found that non‐monetary factors—such as place of birth and, critically, location of training—are stronger drivers of practice location than financial incentives (Cooper et al. (1975); Steele and Rimlinger (1965); Falcettoni (2018); Kulka and McWeeny (2019)). Our primary contribution is to show that the influence of a training location on physician choice has a lasting impact, demonstrating that training locally fosters a durable attachment, not just a temporary ‘lock‐in’.

This finding is consistent with evidence from targeted educational programmes. For example, Magnus and Tollan (1993) found that after a medical school was established in rural northern Norway, 56% of its graduates remained to practice in rural areas. More broadly, our result aligns with studies showing that exposure to rural settings through education or upbringing is a powerful predictor of physicians choosing to practice in and remain in those areas (Rabinowitz et al. (1999); Matsumoto et al. (2008)). 7

Section 5 presents the results and conducts robustness checks. Section 6 explores the mechanism and discusses the external validity of our research. Section 7 concludes.

Background and Source of Variations

Medical School Construction 8

By the mid‐20th century, regional inequality in physician access was a major challenge in Japan. Physician density was consistently high in urban centres, while prefectures without a medical school suffered from a severe lack of physicians.

Prior to 1970, with the construction of new medical schools prohibited, the government's initial strategy was to expand enrollment at existing schools in response to increased demand after the 1961 introduction of National Health Insurance (NHI). 9 Between 1962 and 1969, total enrollment capacity increased from 2880 to 4040 10 , but this was deemed insufficient as the physician‐to‐population ratio remained low at just 1.14 per 1000 people.

To address this shortfall, the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MHW) set a goal in 1970 to raise the ratio to 1.5 physicians per 1000 population within 15 years. To achieve this, regulations on establishing medical schools were relaxed, leading to a wave of construction. Between 1970 and 1978, 17 private and one public medical school were founded, with three prefectures gaining their first medical school during this time. 11

From 1973 to 1981, the Japanese government undertook a nationwide initiative known as the “One Prefecture, One Medical School Policy (OPOMS)” to construct public medical schools in prefectures where no medical school had existed before. This landmark initiative established 16 public medical schools and affiliated university hospitals in prefectures that previously had none, ensuring that by 1981, every prefecture in Japan had at least one medical school. 12

In total, this period saw an increase of approximately 25% in the number of medical schools and university hospitals. With the policy's objective met, a moratorium on new medical school construction was introduced in 1981, which lasted until 2016.

Treatment Group and Control Group

Our research exploits the quasi‐random distribution of medical schools before 1970 to estimate the causal impact of their construction. We define the treatment group as the 17 prefectures that had no medical school prior to 1970 and established their first one during the expansion era. 13 For the control group, we selected 20 prefectures that already had at least one medical school before 1970 and did not add any new ones. Prefectures that had an existing medical school and also added another one are excluded from the analysis to ensure a clean comparison. 14 Figure 2 provides a map of the prefectures in our treatment and control groups.

Timeline of Medical School and University Hospital Construction and Career Path of Physicians

The Japanese government set a standardised timeline for all new medical school and university hospital construction. A new medical school was typically followed by the opening of its affiliated university hospital three to 4 years later. These new university hospitals—regarded as the most advanced medical centres in their respective prefectures—were primarily staffed by an initial inflow of established professors and physicians from prestigious urban institutions. 15

The first cohort of students graduated with medical licenses 6 years after the school's founding. These new graduates then began a mandatory 2‐year internship at their alma mater's university hospital, creating a second, sustained wave of physicians into these prefectures. After their internship, they became resident physicians, undertaking several years of clinical rotations and on‐the‐job training at the university hospital and other general hospitals within the same prefecture.

During this residency period, the university hospital largely controlled the physicians' career paths. In exchange for advanced training and board certification opportunities, residents were assigned placements by the university, which acted as a de facto dispatcher for medical talent within the prefecture. 16 After approximately 8 years of such rotations, physicians were considered fully trained and were finally free to choose their own place of work. Data

We utilise multiple data sources spanning the period from 1960 to 2006 at the prefecture level. Specifically, we digitise all survey data conducted between 1960 and 1996, excluding the data on GDP and population, which are used as control variables.

List of Universities

Our data on the establishment years and class sizes of medical schools and university hospitals come from the Zenkoku Daigaku Ichiran [List of Universities]. This annual survey is conducted by Japan's Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) and provides information on all universities and their affiliated facilities. From this source, we identify the founding years of each medical school and its university hospital, as well as the graduation year of the first student cohort.

Physicians

Survey of Physicians, Dentists, and Pharmacists

Our physician data are from the Ishi, Shikaishi, Yakuzaishi Chousa [Survey of Physicians, Dentists, and Pharmacists], a census of all physicians in Japan. The survey was conducted annually by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) until 1982 and biennially thereafter. It provides prefecture‐level counts of physicians by place of work (e.g., university hospital, clinic), by speciality (available since 1969), and by 5‐year age groups (available since 1976). We use this information to construct a prefecture‐level panel dataset spanning from 1960 to 2006. 17

Survey of Medical Institutions

Additionally, we use data from the Iryō Shisetsu Chosa [Survey of Medical Institutions], an annual MHLW survey that reports the number of full‐time hospital physicians at the prefecture level. We draw on this source to construct a panel dataset for the period from 1960 to 1999.

Health Outcomes

Our prefecture‐level health outcome data are from the Jinkou Doutai Toukei [Vital Statistics], conducted by the MHLW. From this source, we use the annual number of deaths by cause (e.g., all‐cause, cancer, stroke) and by 5‐year age groups. 18

A significant break in the time series for cause‐specific mortality occurred in 1995 when the coding system switched to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10). This change makes it impossible to consistently track specific causes of death across the pre‐ and post‐1995 period. 19 Therefore, our analysis of cause‐specific mortality rates is limited to the period from 1960 to 1994.

Hospitals, Beds, Patients, and Other Medical Staffs

Our data on medical infrastructure are from the Survey of Medical Institutions. From this source, we use prefecture‐level data from 1960 to 1999 on the annual number of hospitals, clinics, emergency hospitals, hospital beds, and nurses.

To measure patient volume, we use the Byōin Hōkoku [Hospital Report], an annual MHLW survey. This report provides the total number of outpatient and inpatient cases at the prefecture level for the same 1960 to 1999 period.

Control Variables

We control for demographic and economic factors potentially confounding the medical demand and supply. Demographic data are drawn from the Population Estimation Survey (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications). We define the children rate as the proportion of the population aged 0–4, and the elderly rate as the proportion of the population aged 65 and older. To account for regional economic conditions, we include real GDP per capita and real income per capita, obtained from the Annual Report on Prefectural Accounts (Cabinet Office).

Empirical Strategy

Our analysis focuses on estimating the effect of medical school and subsequent university hospital constructions on the number of physicians and health outcomes. The identification relies on the fact that certain prefectures established new medical schools due to the absence of existing medical schools. Exploiting the variation in the prefecture‐level distribution of medical schools before the deregulation period, we compare outcomes before and after the establishment of medical schools.

Our primary focus is on two key outcomes: the number of physicians per capita and mortality rates. 20 Esti is the dummy variable which takes 1 if no medical school existed before the deregulation period at prefecture i, and 1t=ti∗+k is a set of dummies which takes 1 if k periods have passed since the year of the establishment of a medical school at prefecture i.

We set the baseline period at k=−1, meaning that our coefficients of interest show the effects of the years relative to the year of a medical school construction compared to a year prior to its occurrence. For the number of physicians, we consider a time horizon of m=24 years. In terms of mortality outcomes, we examine the effects over a time span of m=19 years for all‐cause mortality and m=14 years for cause‐specific mortalities. In all cases, we can examine the long‐term effects of the medical school construction on outcomes.

We only control for a limited number of time‐varying observable demographic variables, including children rate, elderly rate, and GDP per capita, to avoid ‘bad controls’, and denote them by Xit. δt are time‐fixed effects that control for time‐specific components in the outcome variables common across prefectures. αi are prefecture fixed effects and control for unobservable time‐invariant components of outcomes for each prefecture. Finally, ϵit is a time‐varying error at the prefecture level. We cluster standard error at the prefecture level.

To estimate specifications that allow for pre‐trends when the outcome is the number of physicians, we employ the two‐step method proposed by Agha and Zeltzer (2022). First, we estimate a model that includes only the linear pre‐trend for the treatment group, as well as the interaction of 1t=ti∗+k and Esti for the post‐treatment periods, and fixed effects. In the second step, we residualise the outcome by the estimated pre‐trend and then re‐estimate Equation (1). This method enables us to remove the effect of the linear pre‐trend from the post‐treatment period and allows us to assess the nonlinear trends in the pretreatment period.

We apply this pre‐trend adjustment only to outcomes for which the institutional mechanism underlying pre‐period divergence is directly relevant: the total number of physicians and the number of physicians working in university hospitals. The rationale is that pre‐policy cohort expansion in the control group mechanically increases the flow of newly graduating physicians, who enter the labour market through university‐hospital internship/residency, implying that any cohort‐driven divergence should be most visible in the university‐hospital series and, by aggregation, in the total stock. By contrast, for physician counts in clinics and non‐university hospitals, the undetrended event‐study coefficients do not exhibit the same systematic pre‐period divergence attributable to this entry channel; we therefore report those outcomes without detrending.

The validity of the linear specification for the outcomes we detrend is supported by two diagnostics. First, the undetrended event‐study coefficients for total physicians and university‐hospital physicians (Figure A1) display a smooth, monotonic pre‐period drift that is well‐approximated by a linear trend. Second, Figure A2 documents a steady expansion in graduate cohorts in the control group prior to the policy, consistent with a linear supply‐side driver of the pre‐period divergence. We therefore model this divergence parametrically with a linear trend for the affected outcomes. 21

Discussion of Identification

The primary identification concern is the potential violation of the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA): the establishment of medical schools and university hospitals in the treatment group might have impacted the number of physicians and health outcomes in the control group. This concern may arise due to the influx of medical professors and physicians into the treatment group, even though the historical context suggests that the primary providers of physicians are not included in the control group. 23

Due to the unavailability of prefecture‐level data on the alma mater of all physicians, we are unable to directly assess the validity of the SUTVA assumption. Instead, we leverage data on the alma mater composition of physicians working at university hospitals for each prefecture in 1981. This information serves as a valuable proxy for measuring the flow of physicians from the control group, as the alma mater of physicians working at university hospitals has a significant influence on physician allocation and job placement, as discussed in Section 2.3.

Based on this information, we perform the following robustness check to verify the results of our analysis. First, we exclude two influential prefectures, Miyagi and Kyoto, from the control group, as they are members of former imperial universities and the main providers of physicians to the other prefectures. Furthermore, we exclude prefectures with university hospitals receiving more than 50% of physicians from the control group, except the two influential prefectures. Using the remaining prefectures, we re‐estimate the effect of the medical school construction on the number of physicians and health outcomes, and show that the results are robust in Appendix B. We perform this and other robustness checks discussed in Section 5.3.

Instead, the pattern is consistent with a crowding‐out effect, wherein the entry of new graduates induces incumbent physicians to relocate or retire. Robustness Checks

SUTVA Assumption

As discussed in Section 4.2, a potential threat to our identification strategy is the violation of the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) due to physician migration. If the establishment of new medical schools induces significant movement of physicians between the treatment and control groups, the independence of the potential outcomes is compromised. To address this, we impose restrictions on the sample to ensure the stability of the control group and the isolation of the treatment effect.

Our baseline robustness check imposes two restrictions based on historical and empirical patterns. First, regarding the control group, we exclude Miyagi and Kyoto. These prefectures host former Imperial Universities (Tohoku University and Kyoto University), which have historically served as the main providers of physicians to other prefectures. Figure A3 characterises the labour market structure of the remaining control group: Panels (a) and (b) indicate that the vast majority of physicians employed in the control group are alumni of their local medical schools, with minimal cross‐prefecture mobility. This suggests that the restricted control group consists of self‐sufficient medical markets.

Second, regarding the treatment group, we exclude prefectures that rely heavily on the control group for their physician supply to mitigate inward spillovers. Panel (c) shows that most treatment prefectures experienced only a modest influx (average dependency ratio ≈ 26%). Accordingly, our baseline robustness check excludes treatment prefectures where university hospitals receive more than 50% of their physicians from the control group.

To validate these baseline choices and address potential asymmetry in our exclusion rules, we conduct an additional robustness check using a symmetric, data‐driven specification. In this alternative specification, we replace the qualitative exclusions with strict quantitative thresholds for both groups. For the control group, we calculate the ‘export share’ of physicians (defined in Appendix B) and exclude any prefecture with an export share exceeding 1%. This quantitative rule captures the previously excluded prefectures (Miyagi and Kyoto) as well as other prefectures with significant export shares. For the treatment group, we apply a stricter threshold, excluding prefectures that import more than 40% of their physicians from the control group.

Figure A4 presents the event study estimates for both the baseline and the symmetric robustness checks. Panels (a) to (d) show that the effects on physician supply and mortality are statistically significant and quantitatively similar across both specifications. This stability confirms that our results are not driven by specific sample selection criteria or potential SUTVA violations.

Spillover Effects on Mortality

In the previous section, we conducted a robustness check to address concerns on the supply side. However, it is plausible that during the pre‐treatment period, individuals in the treatment group with severe conditions sought better medical care in prefectures within the control group due to the absence of a university hospital in their own prefecture. This scenario could lead to a reduction in the mortality rate for people in the treatment group, while potentially increasing the mortality rate for people in the control group due to crowding out.

On the other hand, in the post‐treatment period, people in the treatment group begin receiving medical care within their own prefectures, while some from the control group may seek medical care in prefectures from the treatment group or get better access to medical care in the control group due to the efflux of patients from the treatment group. This shift in seeking medical care could lead to a reduction in the mortality rate for people in the control group, while potentially increasing the mortality rate for people in the treatment group due to crowding out.

Since we do not observe cross‐prefecture patient flows, we cannot directly quantify these demand‐side spillovers or sign the resulting bias. In principle, changes in patient mobility could bias the estimated mortality effect in either direction. Under a plausible net‐flow pattern—treated residents travelling to control prefectures for high‐acuity care pre‐treatment and subsequently receiving care locally after the university hospital opens—spillovers would tend to attenuate treatment–control mortality contrasts towards zero.

Discussion

Mechanism

In this section, we examine the channels through which the establishment of a medical school and affiliated university hospital reduces mortality. The key empirical fact motivating the mechanism discussion is that mortality declines even though aggregate measures of health‐care quantity—such as the total number of beds, inpatients, and outpatients—do not exhibit persistent long‐run increases.

We organise the discussion around three margins: (i) access and utilisation volume, (ii) physician human capital, and (iii) institutional capital (technology intensity). The evidence below suggests that the mortality effect is unlikely to be explained by broad expansions in overall utilisation or by measurable improvements in physician human capital, and is most consistent with a reallocation towards high‐capital hospital environments enabled by the new university hospital and the accompanying physician supply.

Access to Facilities, Capacities, and Utilisation

First, we analyse the effect on the number of healthcare facilities and their aggregate capacity. Panels (a) to (c) of Figure 12 show no discernible change in the number of non‐university hospitals, clinics, or emergency hospitals associated with the entry of a medical school.

Taken together, these results suggest that the policy did not generate a sustained increase in aggregate health‐care capacity or utilisation at the prefecture level. Instead, the establishment of the university hospital induces a compositional shift: capacity at non‐university hospitals is substituted by capacity at the new university hospital. This compositional shift is central for interpreting the mortality effects: the relevant margin is not ‘more care overall’, but where care is delivered and what type of services are provided, consistent with a facility‐level reallocation across provider types.

From a welfare perspective, this substitution can be interpreted in two ways. If university‐hospital beds embody higher‐capital, higher‐technology production, the shift is consistent with an efficiency‐enhancing reorganisation of capacity rather than a pure quantity expansion (see Section 6.1.3). Alternatively, it could reflect displacement that weakened some incumbent providers (e.g., competition for staff or budgets). The stable number of non‐university hospitals and the absence of sustained declines in aggregate inpatient/outpatient utilisation suggest that the dominant margin is reallocation of capacity rather than a contraction in access.

Physician Human Capital

A potential explanation for the mortality decline is that the establishment of the medical school increases the quality of physicians in the region. As discussed in Section 2.3, graduates of medical university typically work at the affiliated university hospital. Therefore, if the new medical school provides superior medical education or selects higher‐ability students, the resulting difference in physician human capital could directly improve health outcomes.

We test this channel using national licensing exam pass rates from 1974 to 1999 as a proxy for physician quality. Figure A14 plots these pass rates by treatment status. The first new medical school in the treatment group was established in 1976, with the number of treated schools increasing until 1986. The Figure shows that after 1986, the ranges of exam pass rates for the treatment and control groups largely overlap, suggesting no persistent difference in performance.

Institutional Capital and Technological Intensity

Given limited evidence for quantity expansion or measurable improvements in physician human capital, we examine the role of institutional capital. University hospitals in Japan are functionally distinct from general hospitals, designated to handle high‐acuity care that requires advanced medical infrastructure. The observed shift in where care is delivered can be driven by a combination of reduced travel time/proximity, changes in referral patterns, and emergency transport/triage routing high‐acuity cases to advanced‐capability hospitals once they become locally available. Since university hospitals typically attract a higher‐risk case mix, a decline in aggregate mortality despite this potential negative selection strengthens the interpretation that technology‐ and system‐intensive hospital production functions played a substantial role.

While the estimates for stroke and cancer lack statistical precision, their signs and magnitudes are consistent with a channel operating through technology‐intensive acute care.

These stylized facts, together with the cause‐specific mortality pattern documented above, support the interpretation that the relevant ‘quality’ margin is not primarily physician test performance, but rather access to technology‐intensive hospital production functions. In this framework, the compositional shift documented in Section 6.1.1—substitution away from non‐university capacity towards the newly established university hospital—represents a shift from lower‐capital to higher‐capital environments capable of providing advanced, life‐saving interventions.

Furthermore, these findings offer a potential explanation for the role of the increased physician supply observed in Section 5.1. Advanced institutional capital typically requires high labour intensity to operate; a ventilator unit or a cardiovascular surgery team requires a depth of specialised staffing that general hospitals cannot support. This suggests a complementarity between the supply of physicians and the provision of advanced medical technology, where the influx of new physicians serves as the necessary labour input to activate high‐quality institutional capital.

We emphasise that this institutional‐capital interpretation is consistent with the evidence but not uniquely identified: we do not observe patient‐level pathways, referrals, or procedure‐level utilisation in our panel, so we cannot directly trace which clinical interventions drove the mortality decline. Nonetheless, the combination of limited long‐run changes in aggregate utilisation and clear institutional differences in technology intensity supports institutional capital as the most coherent mechanism given the available data.

Cost–Benefit Analysis

In this section, we analyse the social cost of establishing a medical school and compare it to the social benefits derived from reduced mortality. We anchor all monetary values in the year 2000.

Estimation of Social Costs

We estimate the social cost of medical school construction using the financial statements of Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University for 2016, the year its Faculty of Medicine was established for the first time in 35 years in Japan. We employ this newly established faculty as a proxy because comprehensive historical cost data for the 1970s are unavailable and its student cohort size—a primary determinant of operational scale—corresponds to the average admission capacity of the medical schools established under the OPOMS policy.

Although the university is a private institution, we treat its resource consumption as a proxy for the social cost of a public medical school. We assume capital infrastructure is publicly financed to reflect the OPOMS policy structure, necessitating an adjustment for the marginal cost of public funds. Because medical education and university‐hospital operations have become more capital‐ and technology‐intensive since the 1970s, the 2016 accounts likely overstate the resource cost of an OPOMS‐era institution of comparable cohort size; if so, the implied benefit–cost ratio should be interpreted as conservative (a lower bound).

We calculate the annual social cost as the sum of the opportunity cost of physical capital, the consumption of operational resources, potential induced healthcare expenditures, and the deadweight loss from public subsidies. Opportunity Cost of Physical Capital

Standard accounting depreciation often fails to capture the true economic cost of capital tied up in land and infrastructure. We therefore estimate the rental cost of the physical capital stock. According to the Balance Sheet for 2016, the university's tangible fixed assets—comprising land, newly constructed buildings, and medical equipment—totalled 29.84 billion JPY at the end of the establishment year (Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University (2016a)). To determine the annual social cost of these resources, we apply a social discount rate of 4%, based on the standard guidelines for the evaluation of public infrastructure projects in Japan (Ministry of Land et al. (2023)). This yields an annual opportunity cost of 1.19 billion JPY (nominal).

Operational Resource Consumption

We measure the flow of resources utilised in education and healthcare provision using the Statement of Activities. According to the Financial Summary in the 2016 Business Report, the total expenditures for education and medical activities amounted to 21.26 billion JPY (Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University (2016b)). This Figure captures the gross resource consumption of the university, including personnel expenses, research costs, and hospital running costs. Notably, this estimate is highly conservative as it includes the costs associated with the pre‐existing Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences.

Induced Healthcare Expenditures

To assess whether the establishment of university hospitals increased aggregate healthcare spending, we estimated the policy effect on health care expenditure using the same difference‐in‐differences specification defined in Equation (2), with prefecture‐level National Health Insurance payments as the outcome. The estimated coefficient was negative and statistically insignificant (−1.03 million JPY, t=−1.31, N=1158). While this suggests no statistically detectable increase in spending, to remain conservative, we incorporate a buffer for potential induced demand based on the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval, adding 0.85 billion JPY (real 2000 values) to the annual cost.

Marginal Cost of Public Funds (MCF)

Unlike national universities, Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University is a private institution primarily funded by user fees. However, it received government subsidies totalling 0.54 billion JPY in 2016 (Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University (2016c)). To ensure a fair comparison with the public nature of the OPOMS policy, we assume that the capital infrastructure and induced healthcare expenditures are financed through taxation. We apply a marginal cost of funds (MCF) multiplier of 1.3 (Poterba (1994)) to the sum of the annual capital opportunity cost (1.19 billion JPY), government subsidies (0.54 billion JPY), and the induced healthcare expenditures (0.85 billion JPY, adjusted to nominal 2016 values for consistency in calculation). This captures the deadweight loss associated with raising the necessary public funds.

Total Adjusted Social Cost

Summing these components yields the total social cost. To allow for comparison with our benefit estimates, we deflate the nominal 2016 figures to year 2000 prices using the GDP deflator (adjustment factor of 1.12). The induced healthcare cost is already expressed in real 2000 terms. Consequently, the operational and capital costs increase by the deflationary factor, and the total MCF adjustment accounts for the tax distortion on all publicly financed components. The resulting estimated annual social cost in 2000 prices is 26.84 billion JPY.

Estimation of Social Benefits and Results

We quantify social benefits based on the reduction in mortality. Because data limitations preclude the measurement of improvements in morbidity, our estimates represent a lower bound of the total welfare gain. The results indicate a mortality reduction of 20.2 per 100,000 population. Evaluated at the average population of treated prefectures (1.67 million), this corresponds to approximately 337 statistical lives saved annually.

To value these lives, we employ the age‐specific Value of Statistical Life (VSL) from Tanizawa et al. (2025). While standard welfare analyses typically apply a population‐wide average VSL which exceeds 1 billion JPY (Viscusi and Aldy (2003)), Tanizawa et al. (2025) estimate the VSL for the Japanese population aged over 60 at 340 million JPY (nominal). We use this age‐specific value to avoid overestimation given that mortality reductions are concentrated among the elderly. Converting this estimate from 2023 nominal values to year 2000 prices using the GDP deflator (adjustment factor of 1.03), we obtain a conservative VSL of 350 million JPY. The total estimated annual social benefit is 118.02 billion JPY.

Table 9 summarises the cost–benefit calculus. Even under these conservative assumptions—utilising the high costs of modern medical infrastructure and the age‐adjusted valuation of life for the elderly—the analysis demonstrates that the policy is socially efficient. The social benefits exceed the social costs by a factor of 4.4.

Conclusion

We have examined the impact of medical school construction and the opening of university hospitals on access to physicians and health outcomes. Our analysis reveals compelling evidence that the establishment of a medical school and university hospital leads to a significant increase in the overall number of physicians. This effect is primarily driven by physicians practising at the university hospital and has persisted for a period exceeding 20 years. As these cohorts mature, treated prefectures close their pre‐existing deficit in mid‐career physician density, indicating that the policy produced a durable expansion in local physician supply.

Furthermore, we find suggestive evidence that the establishment of a university hospital decreases mortality rates, particularly among the elderly. These benefits likely stem from improvements in the quality of care following the opening of the university hospital, rather than an overall increase in healthcare utilisation.

These findings likely possess external validity beyond the Japanese context. The strategy of using medical education to boost rural supply has precedents in Scandinavia (Magnus and Tollan 1993; Simoens and Hurst 2006), and regional licensing barriers in the United States and Canada create retention incentives comparable to the institutional frictions observed in our study.

Our analysis is subject to several limitations. First, the impact on the number of physicians may be biased by potential violations of the SUTVA assumption. Despite addressing this concern through multiple robustness checks, we are unable to directly assess it without individual‐level physician data. Second, the estimated mortality effect may be affected by demand‐side spillovers through cross‐prefecture patient mobility and crowding; without patient‐flow data, we cannot determine the direction of bias, though under plausible net‐flow patterns the estimates could be attenuated towards zero. Finally, we are unable to directly examine the mechanism behind the decline in mortality, although our findings suggest it is driven by the reallocation of care from non‐university to university hospitals, alongside quality improvements.

Based on our findings, we propose that the construction of medical schools and university hospitals represents an effective policy approach for increasing local physician supply and sustaining that supply into mid‐career stages. However, it is advisable to consider additional measures for effectively directing severe medical cases to university hospitals and fostering spillovers to non‐university hospitals to further enhance health outcomes.

Sample Selection and SUTVA Robustness

B.1 Sample Selection

To assess the validity of excluding Miyagi and Kyoto from the control group, we calculate the physician ‘export share’ for all candidate control prefectures using 1981 physician distribution data. The export share for prefecture i is defined as the mean proportion of physicians in other university hospitals (j≠i) who graduated from medical schools in prefecture i. We find that Miyagi and Kyoto are outliers with export shares above 4%. In contrast, the remaining control prefectures exhibit an average export share of 0.9%. In the symmetric robustness check discussed in the main text, we pair this quantitative control restriction (Export Share < 1%) with a stricter treatment restriction (Import Share < 40%).
