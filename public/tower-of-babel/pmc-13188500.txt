Developing a competency framework for medical laboratory scientists in Taiwan: a modified Delphi approach
Authors: Hsien-Li Huang, Chi-Wei Lee, Pi-Yueh Chang, Huey-Ling You, Ping Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical Laboratory Scientists (MLS) are essential members of the healthcare team, providing critical information for diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and disease prevention. With rapid advances in laboratory medicine, the scope of MLS practice has expanded, increasing the need for clearly defined competency expectations. Although competency frameworks have been developed in several countries, Taiwan currently lacks a comprehensive framework that is both internationally informed and contextually relevant to local healthcare practice.MethodsThis study was conducted from August 2022 to July 2023, employing a multi-stage mixed-methods approach to develop a competency framework for MLS in Taiwan. A structured literature review and expert focus group discussions were first undertaken to identify preliminary competency domains. A three-round modified Delphi survey involving 39 experts from teaching hospitals across different regions of Taiwan was subsequently conducted to achieve consensus. Iterative modifications were made between each round to refine competency indicators, and the final consensus rate was set at 75%.ResultsThe final framework comprises five core competencies—Technical Skills, Knowledge Base, Communication, Continuous Professional Development, and Professionalism—and 15 associated sub-competencies. Notably, three competency domain-level revisions were made during the Delphi process to reflect more observable and assessable behaviors. Additionally, six sub-competencies were revised for clarity and alignment with professional practice.ConclusionThis study successfully constructed a comprehensive and hierarchically structured competency framework for MLS in Taiwan. The framework is both internationally aligned and deeply localized, providing a robust foundation for curriculum development and competency-based assessments. This framework is expected to significantly support the ongoing modernization of MLS education and enhance the quality of laboratory services in Taiwan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09191-x.

Introduction

Medical Laboratory Scientists (MLS) play a critical role in modern healthcare systems, by supporting accurate diagnosis, therapeutic monitoring, and disease prevention. Their expertise in performing complex laboratory analyses, interpreting results, and ensuring quality assurance has a direct impact on patient care and public health outcomes [1]. In an era characterized by rapid technological advancements, increasing diagnostic complexity, and evolving healthcare demands, the responsibilities and required competencies of MLS are continuously expanding beyond traditional laboratory tasks to include quality management, interprofessional collaboration, digital competencies, and patient-centered care [2–4]. This evolution necessitates a clear and comprehensive definition of the competencies required for MLS to perform effectively and adapt to emerging healthcare challenges [5].

To ensure that health professions education remains responsive to evolving professional demands, the development and implementation of robust competency frameworks has become essential. This shift is globally recognized, exemplified by the emergence of Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) which aims to develop professionals who are proficient in local healthcare needs [6–8]. Several international organizations—including the International Federation of Biomedical Laboratory Science (IFBLS), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Medical Sciences Council of New Zealand, the Pathology Associations Council of Australia, and national bodies in Japan and Sweden—have established competency standards for MLS or public health laboratory professionals [9–14]. Additionally, the American Society for Clinical Pathology and the Canadian Society for Medical Laboratory Science offer competency-based certification examinations [15, 16]. The widespread adoption of ISO 15,189 accreditation has further reinforced the importance of strengthening laboratory workforce competencies [17]. However, these international frameworks often reflect the structure and demands of their respective healthcare systems and may not fully align with the unique regulatory, educational, and clinical environments of other regions [18].

In Taiwan, MLS qualifications and professional development are supported by national licensure examinations, structured postgraduate training programs, and competency assessment requirements associated with laboratory accreditation standards. These mechanisms primarily emphasize knowledge acquisition and task-based technical skills. In addition, the professional advancement framework proposed by the Taiwan Society of Laboratory Medicine outlines expectations related to technical performance, quality management, teaching, research, and administrative competencies. However, these expectations are often implemented in a fragmented manner, and competency domains and performance indicators are described broadly without a clearly integrated developmental structure. Consequently, there is a need to synthesize existing expectations into a structured, coherent, and consensus-based competency framework aligned with the characteristics of Taiwan’s healthcare system.

A well-defined competency framework can provide a structured blueprint for curriculum design, competency-based assessment, and continuing professional development in medical laboratory science. However, a nationally relevant and consensus-based competency framework for MLS in Taiwan remains lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a comprehensive and hierarchically structured competency framework for MLS in Taiwan.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a sequential three-phase mixed-methods design consisted of (1) a structured literature review to identify an initial pool of relevant competency domains and items, (2) a focus group discussion to refine these preliminary competencies and explore local contextual expectations, and (3) a modified Delphi study to achieve expert consensus on the finalized competency framework [19]. The research was conducted between August 1, 2022, and July 31, 2023, and was approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 202200030B0A3).

Phase 1: structured literature review

A structured literature review was conducted to inform the development of the preliminary competency framework. The review was conducted using comprehensive search and synthesis procedures informed by systematic review principles and was guided by the Population–Concept–Context framework commonly used in competency framework research. Electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, Airiti Library, and national thesis repositories, employing keywords such as “competence,” “competency,” “task,” “job,” “knowledge,” “skill,” “attitude,” “medical laboratory scientist,” “medical technologist,” and “clinical laboratory scientist.” were searched for publications up to December 2022. Additional grey literature was identified through Google advanced search, manual screening of reference lists, and targeted searches of websites of professional laboratory organizations.

Studies describing competency frameworks or professional role expectations for MLS or related laboratory professionals were eligible. After removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two investigators, followed by full-text review to determine eligibility. Key competency domains were extracted and thematically synthesized to generate preliminary competency structures that informed the focus group discussion and Delphi questionnaire development.

Finally, 18 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis [2, 9–16, 20–28]. Key competency concepts were extracted and conceptually synthesized to generate preliminary competency domains, which informed the focus group discussions and Delphi questionnaire design. Phase 2: focus group discussion

To refine the preliminary competency framework and enhance contextual relevance, a focus group discussion was conducted using purposive sampling to recruit expert MLS through professional networks, including the Taiwan Society of Laboratory Medicine (TSLM) and the national MLS association. Nine senior MLS with leadership roles in clinical laboratories and responsibilities in education and workforce development participated in online focus group discussion.

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [29]. Two investigators independently coded transcripts and organized codes into candidate competency domains through iterative comparison and consensus discussion. Findings were integrated with results from the literature review to develop the preliminary competency framework and inform the design of the Delphi questionnaire. The preliminary framework comprised five core competencies—Technical Skill (TS), Knowledge Base (KB), Team Resource Management (TRM), Continuous Professional Learning, Development and Quality Improvement (CPQ), and Professionalism and Accountability (PA)—and 14 sub-competencies. Phase 3: Delphi study

A three-round modified Delphi survey was conducted to validate and refine the proposed competency framework. Experts were recruited using stratified purposive sampling to ensure representation across geographic regions and institutional levels. A total of 41 experts were invited (Northern region: 17; Central: 10; Southern: 12; Eastern: 2), of whom 39 consented to participate (response rate: 95.1%). All participants completed the three Delphi rounds.

The questionnaire was developed based on findings from the literature review and focus group discussion, following established methodological guidance for competency framework development and Delphi survey design [30–32]. It included five proposed core competencies and fourteen sub-competencies rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very inappropriate to 5 = very appropriate), along with open-ended questions to capture qualitative feedback. Prior to Round 1, the questionnaire underwent content validation through expert review, and minor revisions were made to improve clarity and contextual relevance. The English versions of the Round 1–3 questionnaires are provided as Supplementary Files (Additional file 1–3).

Participants represented diverse clinical institutions, including medical centers and teaching hospitals, and were actively involved in laboratory education, workforce development, and professional governance. The panel size exceeded commonly recommended Delphi sample sizes of 15–30 participants, enhancing the diversity of expert perspectives and the robustness of consensus outcomes [33]. The survey was administered using both paper-based and electronic formats to maximize response participation. Controlled feedback was provided between rounds in the form of anonymized summaries of quantitative ratings and qualitative comments, enabling participants to reconsider their judgments. This iterative consensus-building process is consistent with established methodological recommendations for Delphi studies [19].

Consensus criteria and data analysis

Competencies were evaluated and retained, modified, or removed based on predefined statistical thresholds. Specifically, items with a mean score (M) < 3.5 were considered for elimination in subsequent rounds. High variability (standard deviation (SD) > 0.5) among expert opinions prompted further discussion and clarification by the research team for subsequent rounds. Consensus for each competency item was defined as a coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 25% (i.e., (100% - CV) ≥ 75%) [34]. The Delphi process was terminated once 95% of the competency indicators met these convergence criteria for consensus across all domains [35].

Results

Expert panel demographics and distribution

A total of 39 MLS experts participated in the Delphi study (Table 2), with 100% participation maintained across all Delphi rounds. The panel demonstrated near gender balance, comprising 20 females (51%) and 19 males (49%). Most participants held managerial positions (28/39, 72%). Years of professional experience were collected as total laboratory practice experience, which included both prior bench work and subsequent managerial or educational roles. Overall, 33 participants (85%) had more than 10 years of laboratory practice experience. All participants had prior hands-on experience in routine laboratory practice before assuming managerial or educational roles. Regarding educational background, 10 participants (26%) held doctoral degrees, 23 (59%) held master’s degrees, and 6 (15%) held bachelor’s degrees. Participants were primarily affiliated with medical centers (19/39, 49%) and regional hospitals (19/39, 49%), with one participant (2%) from a district hospital. Geographic representation included northern Taiwan (15/39, 38%), central (10/39, 26%), southern (12/39, 31%), and eastern regions (2/39, 5%).

Refinement and consensus on core competencies

The Delphi process refined five preliminary core competencies for MLS. The initial eight competency domains identified from the literature review were reorganized and consolidated into five broader domains based on thematic overlap and expert consensus on their relevance to MLS practice in Taiwan. Two domains—administrative management and teaching supervision—were excluded after focus group discussion, as they were considered role-specific responsibilities typically associated with senior or managerial positions rather than core competencies required of all MLS. In Round 1, TRM was replaced by COM. CPQ was refined to CPD. Finally, PA was simplified to PRO. By Round 3, all five core competencies achieved the predefined consensus criterion (5/5 items, 100%) (Table 3). On a 5-point Likert scale, mean scores ranged from 4.87 to 4.95, with consensus levels ranging from 92% to 95% across the five competencies. TS demonstrated the highest consensus (Mean = 4.95 ± 0.22; consensus level = 95%), followed by KB (Mean = 4.92 ± 0.27; consensus level = 95%) and COM (Mean = 4.90 ± 0.31; consensus level = 94%).

Refinement and consensus on sub-competencies

A total of 14 sub-competencies were evaluated through a three-round Delphi process. Following Round 1, six sub-competencies were revised based on expert feedback to improve conceptual clarity and contextual relevance to MLS practice in Taiwan. These refinements, along with structured feedback provided in subsequent rounds, contributed to progressively higher consensus levels. By Round 3, all sub-competencies reached the predefined consensus threshold (consensus level ≥ 75%), and twelve of the fourteen sub-competencies (12/14, 86%) achieved very high consensus levels (≥ 95%) (Table 4). Mean importance ratings ranged from 4.82 to 4.93 on a 5-point Likert scale, indicating strong expert agreement on the relevance of the proposed competency indicators. The highest consensus levels were observed for the sub-competencies validating and reporting laboratory results accurately and in a timely manner (Mean = 4.93 ± 0.10; consensus level = 98%), establishing and maintaining laboratory documentation, equipment, and resource management systems (Mean = 4.89 ± 0.17; consensus level = 97%), and effective communication with colleagues and interdisciplinary healthcare teams to improve laboratory service quality (Mean = 4.89 ± 0.17; consensus level = 97%).

Definition of core competency domains and sub-competencies

Based on consensus achieved through the Delphi process, a competency framework comprising five core domains and corresponding sub-competencies was finalized (Table 5). The TS domain covers competencies related to specimen handling, analytical procedures, quality assurance, and accurate result validation and reporting. The KB domain integrates theoretical understanding of laboratory medicine, application of health information systems, and principles of laboratory safety and risk management. Based on expert feedback during the Delphi process, safety-related competencies were integrated into the KB domain as foundational professional knowledge. In Taiwan, laboratory safety and risk management are typically taught and assessed as part of core professional knowledge and technical training. The COM domain emphasizes effective interdisciplinary collaboration as well as the ability to manage and utilize human resources in laboratory settings. The CPD domain reflects competencies related to lifelong learning, research literacy, clinical reasoning, and quality improvement initiatives. Finally, the PRO domain encompasses ethical conduct, legal responsibility, accountability, and commitment to professional standards.

Effective workflow and risk management are essential to ensure biosafety and minimize laboratory-related errors and hazards. Therefore, the following sub-competencies were identified.

Ÿ TS2: Performing laboratory analyses and preserving specimens

Ÿ TS3: Applying quality assurance principles to conduct laboratory quality control

Ÿ TS4: Validating and reporting laboratory results accurately and timely

Ÿ KB1: Applying knowledge of laboratory medicine to support patient care.

Ÿ KB2: Utilizing healthcare information systems or software for documentation, data retrieval, calculation, and analysis.

Ÿ KB3: Establishing, managing, and maintaining documentation, equipment, laboratory resources, and inventory.

Ÿ KB4: Maintaining and promoting safe laboratory practices

Ÿ COM2: Managing and utilizing human resources effectively in laboratory settings

Ÿ CPD2: Demonstrating logical reasoning, scientific inquiry, and research skills

Ÿ PRO1: Understanding and adhering to accreditation standards, assessment regulations, and national laws governing medical laboratories.

Ÿ PRO2: Demonstrating professional responsibility, ethical behavior, and integrity in medical laboratory practice.

Discussion

This study developed a comprehensive competency framework for MLS in Taiwan using a sequential mixed-methods design that incorporated a structured literature review, focus group discussions, and a modified Delphi process. The finalized framework comprises five core competencies—Technical Skill (TS), Knowledge Base (KB), Communication (COM), Continuous Professional Development (CPD), and Professionalism (PRO)—along with 15 sub-competencies. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on entry-level competencies [13, 14], this framework is intended to support MLS practice across diverse laboratory settings (e.g., medical centers, regional hospitals, and different laboratory disciplines) and throughout multiple stages of career development. Competency frameworks provide a structured foundation for curriculum design, competency-based assessment, and progressive professional development when operationalized through milestones and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) [36, 37].

Some competency items initially showed lower consensus, possibly reflecting differences between internationally emphasized competencies and current local practice patterns. However, because none of the items met the predefined exclusion threshold, all were retained for further consideration. The provision of aggregated quantitative ratings and anonymized qualitative feedback between Delphi rounds enabled participants to reconsider their judgments within a broader contextual perspective. This iterative consensus-building process facilitated convergence of expert opinions while reducing the likelihood of prematurely excluding competencies that may gain relevance as MLS roles continue to evolve.

During the Delphi process, experts recommended refinements to improve the conceptual clarity and contextual relevance of the competency framework. Technical Resource Management (TRM) was replaced by Communication, as the former was viewed as a training strategy rather than a core professional competency. Although language proficiency has been highlighted in some international frameworks, such as the Swedish model [14], experts in this study emphasized practical communication skills essential for maintaining laboratory service quality and ensuring patient safety within Taiwan’s multilingual and team-oriented healthcare environment. CPQ was simplified to CPD to align with internationally recognized terminology, emphasizing lifelong learning. Similarly, PA was streamlined to PRO, acknowledging that accountability is inherently embedded within professional conduct. In addition, several sub-competencies were refined to better reflect real-world laboratory practice, including clearer distinctions between pre-analytical and analytical responsibilities and greater emphasis on principle-based quality assurance and effective human resource utilization.

In addition to adjustments at the core domain level, six sub-competencies also underwent revision after the first Delphi round to enhance their accuracy, consistency, or alignment with professional practice. For instance, the original item “Collecting, preparing, and analyzing clinical specimens” was split into two distinct sub-competencies—“Collecting and preparing clinical specimens” and “Performing laboratory analyses and specimen preservation”—to delineate tasks during the pre-analytical and analytical phases. Other improvements included changing “Conducting laboratory quality control” to “Applying quality assurance principles to conduct laboratory quality control,” and modifying “Supervising and managing personnel” to “Managing and utilizing human resources effectively in laboratory settings.” Both revisions underscore an emphasis on principle-based practice and real-world applicability.

The competency framework developed in this study was designed to define competency domains and sub-competencies at a conceptual level rather than to specify detailed behavioral indicators. For example, the sub-competency “maintaining and promoting safe laboratory practices” encompasses the understanding and application of biosafety principles, risk identification, incident prevention, and compliance with laboratory quality and safety standards. Within competency-based education, such conceptual domains serve as a foundation for subsequent development of milestones, EPAs, and assessment strategies, providing a basis for translating competency expectations into observable professional performance.

Consistent with international MLS competency frameworks, the TS and KB domains emphasize laboratory techniques, quality assurance, and safety practices. Safety-related competencies were integrated into the KB domain to reflect the structure of MLS education and regulatory expectations in Taiwan, where laboratory safety and risk management are typically taught and assessed as core professional knowledge aligned with accreditation standards. The Communication domain similarly reflects the interdisciplinary and multilingual clinical environment in which MLS operate, highlighting collaboration with healthcare teams as a key determinant of service quality and patient safety.

This study also incorporated contextual elements relevant to Taiwan’s healthcare system and professional practice environment. The explicit inclusion of competencies related to ethics, regulatory compliance, and accountability within the PRO domain may reflect strengthened clinical laboratory governance and patient safety expectations. Experts also highlighted the importance of inventory and resource management, particularly in response to supply chain disruptions experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic [38]. Furthermore, competencies related to the application of health information systems were included within the Knowledge Base domain, reflecting Taiwan’s highly digitized healthcare infrastructure and the widespread implementation of the National Health Insurance system [39, 40]. The establishment of CPD as an independent competency domain is also consistent with national licensing requirements mandating continuing education for MLS, reinforcing the importance of lifelong learning in maintaining professional competence [37].

While the competency framework aligns with international models, it introduces localized refinements reflecting Taiwan’s regulatory context and evolving MLS roles. Several limitations should be considered. First, the framework primarily addresses competencies relevant to hospital-based laboratory practice and may not fully capture requirements in non-clinical or highly specialized settings. Second, limited representation from district hospitals may underrepresent perspectives from smaller institutions where MLS often assume broader responsibilities. Third, although the modified Delphi method provides a structured approach to consensus development, findings remain influenced by panel composition and expert judgment. In addition, emerging competency areas such as artificial intelligence, digital literacy, and global health perspectives were not defined as standalone domains and warrant further exploration in future research. Despite these limitations, the proposed framework provides a foundation for developing EPAs and milestones to support competency-based education and workforce development in medical laboratory science.

Conclusions

This study employed a multi-stage sequential mixed-methods design, culminating in a three-round Delphi process, to develop a comprehensive, hierarchically structured, and consensus-driven competency framework for MLS in Taiwan. This framework comprises five core competencies and fifteen sub-competencies. While broadly aligned with international competency models, it incorporates contextual adaptations reflecting Taiwan’s healthcare system and evolving professional expectations, including competencies related to health information systems and interprofessional communication. By providing a clear and contextually relevant framework, this study offers a foundation for curriculum development, competency-based assessment, and continuing professional development in medical laboratory science. The framework may also support future efforts to operationalize competency expectations through milestones and EPAs, thereby contributing to the advancement of competency-based education and laboratory practice in Taiwan.
