Construction of a novel G2/M checkpoint-related gene signature for prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma based on machine learning
Authors: Kunxiong Guo, Yucong Lin, Wanting Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is recognized as one of the most aggressive cancers worldwide. This study aims to discover novel biomarkers that can effectively stratify patients at high risk for HCC and facilitate personalized management.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes analysis was conducted to identify marker genes associated with HCC based on transcriptomic profiles from the TCGA-LIHC database. And then, we utilized the weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) to explore the correlation between various gene expression clustering modules based on the the identified differentially expressed genes above and clinical phenotypes. Subsequently, we integrated 10 machine learning algorithms into 117 combinations to establish the optimal model for predicting survival in HCC patients. Then, immune landscape was evaluated. Individuals with high G2/MR scores exhibited shorter survival and higher regulatory T cells infiltration compared to those with low G2/MR scores. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common cancers worldwide and poses a significant challenge to public health. In 2022, nearly 865,269 individuals were diagnosed with liver cancer worldwide, with HCC accounting for 75% to 85% of these cases [1]. Despite the implementation of a national hepatitis B virus (HBV) vaccination program for newborns in 1984, which resulted in a 35.9% reduction in the incidence of HCC among individuals under 30 years of age [2], there has been an increase in cases of steatohepatitis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis-related HCC. Furthermore, the parity between incidence and mortality rates, with approximately 830,000 deaths annually, highlights the poor prognosis associated with this malignancy [3]. Although a variety of treatment modalities are available for HCC patients—including surgical resection, percutaneous ablation, radiation therapy, liver transplantation, and immunotherapy—the relative 5-year survival rate remains unsatisfactory, at approximately 18% [4]. Therefore, it is necessary to gain a comprehensive understanding of the biological characteristics of HCC and to identify potential specific biomarkers that could enhance diagnosis and treatment strategies.

The maintenance of genomic stability is essential for the survival of an individual cell and crucial for cancer avoidance. The G2/M checkpoint is critical in preventing the transmission of DNA damage to the next generation of cells by arresting cell cycle, thereby maintaining genomic integrity and contributing to cancer avoidance [5, 6]. Dysfunction of the G2/M checkpoint allows for the proliferation of damaged cells and facilitating tumorigenesis. Chen X et al. has identified a distinct suppressive immune barrier structure in immune checkpoint inhibitors non-response HCC patients, which associated with elevated levels of G2/M checkpoint genes [7]. Nevertheless, the specific role and prognostic value of G2/M checkpoint in HCC remains not clear.

Machine-learning algorithms have proven effective in autonomously selecting pivotal genes from whole transcriptomic profiles to develop dependable cancer prognostic models. Through machine-learning algorithms, many prognostic models have been developed to help predicting clinical outcomes and guiding personalized treatment in lung adenocarcinoma, osteosarcoma and so on [8, 9].

In this study, utilizing RNA sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases, we used the weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) and differentially expressed genes analysis to identify the gene signature change between HCC tissue and adjacent normal tissue. Our results reveal a notable upregulation of genes associated with the G2/M checkpoint in HCC compared to adjacent normal tissue. Using 117 combinations based on 10 machine learning algorithms, we developed G2/M risk (G2/MR) models that effectively predict prognosis in HCC patients. Additionally, these genes involved in G2/MR model majorly distributed in regulatory T cell and epithelial cells in HCC tissue. Notably, deoxythymidylate kinase (DTYMK), a critical component of G2/M checkpoint signaling, was significantly overexpressed in HCC tissue compared to adjacent normal tissues. Knockdown of DTYMK notably inhibited the proliferation, invasion, migration, and clonogenicity of HCC cells. These findings suggested new potential biomarkers and therapeutic strategies for HCC treatment.

Methods

Data sources

We screened six HCC datasets (Table S1). Gene expression profiles and clinical data of TCGA-LIHC were collected from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://cancergenome.nih.gov/), including 370 cases of HCC and 50 controls. 213 samples of the ICGC LIRI-JP cohort were download from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC, https://dcc.icgc.org/). GSE14520, GSE47197, GSE54236, GSE149614 were retrospectively obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Clinical specimens

Paired fresh-frozen specimens of primary liver cancer tissues and adjacent non-tumor liver tissues were obtained from treatment-naive patients undergoing surgery at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. All cases were histologically confirmed as liver cancer through pathological examination. The basic clinical and pathological data of the HCC patients was listed in Table S2.

Identification of DEGs in TCGA-LIHC

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from TCGA-LIHC were identified utilizing ‘DESeq’ R package. The screening thresholds were set at |log2 FC| ≥ 1 and p value ≤ 0.05. We visualized the DEGs using the ggplot2 package in R, presenting the results as volcano plots. The criterion for upregulated DEGs was established as log2 FC ≥ 1 and p < 0.05, while log2 FC ≤ -1 and p < 0.05 was defined as downregulated DEGs.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA is performed to identify modules of highly correlated genes, summarize the interconnections between modules and associations with external sample traits [10]. In this study, WGCNA was constructed from the R packet ‘WGCNA’ to identify the most relevant HCC modules [11]. Specifically, we use the “WGCNA” software package to construct the correlation matrix for DEGs in TCGA-LIHC. The optimal soft threshold is selected to transform the correlation matrix into the adjacency matrix, and the topological overlap matrix (TOM) is generated from the adjacency matrix. Using the phase dissimilarity measure based on TOM and the average linkage hierarchical clustering, the genes with similar expression patterns are divided into gene modules.

Module and clinical feature correlation analysis

Pearson correlation coefficient was used to measure the correlation between different gene modules and clinical traits, and the module with the strongest correlation with HCC was selected as the key module for subsequent analysis. Genes were screened according to gene significance (GS) > 0.5 and module members (MM) > 0.7.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Genes were selected based on GS > 0.5 and MM > 0.7 and enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichrd (http://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/Enrichr) [12]. The false discovery rate (FDR) of gene enrichment is less than 0.05, which is considered to be statistically significant.

Construction of prognostic model based on machine learning

150 cases of TCGA-LIHC cohort were used as training cohort, 363 cases of TCGA-LIHC cohort and 220 cases of GSE14520 cohort were used as validation cohort. In the training cohort, 117 combinations of 10 machine learning algorithms were used [Mime: Building an Integrated Model Based on Machine Learning (https://github.com/l-magnificence/Mime)] for variable selection in a 10-fold cross-validation framework. For each model of these 10 machine learning algorithms, we calculated the C index of the training set and the validation set. Then, we ranked the prediction performance of the models according to the average C index. The most powerful algorithm combination among all algorithm combination was selected. We performed multivariate Cox regression analysis to further screen genes to construct G2/MR model, and used gene expression values and coefficients to establish a risk score for quantification. The scoring formula is as follows: Risk score = ∑i=1 Coef(i) × x(i). based on the scoring formula, we determined the risk score for each HCC patient in the TCGA-LIHC and GSE14520 microarray cohorts and divided them into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score [13]. To investigate whether there were significant differences in OS and RFS between the low-risk and high-risk groups, we used the “survminer” package and the “timeROC” package to perform receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of the risk score in predicting OS in HCC patients.

Tumor immune infiltration analysis

Based on Gene expression profiles from HCC patients, the relative proportions of 22 infiltrating immune cells in different groups were inferred to utilize the CIBERSORT algorithm [14]. The association among G2/MR scores and relative proportions of infiltrating immune cells was investigated through Pearson correlation analysis.

Cell culture and small interfering RNA transfection

Human liver cancer cell lines (Huh-7 and PLC/PRF/5) were obtained from China’s national cell line resource. Huh-7 and PLC/PRF/5 was grown in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. All cells were cultured in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

All siRNAs were transfected into the cells with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent. The sequences of small interfering RNAs were listed below: siDTYMK#1 5’-GCAAACUUCUGAGUUCCUATT-3’, siDTYMK#2 5’-GGAACAAGUGCCGUUAAUUTT-3.

Immunohistochemical staining of human HCC samples

Human HCC tissue slices were subjected to deparaffinization, antigen retrieval and blocking and were stained with anti-DTYMK (Proteintech, 15360-1-AP). at 4℃ overnight. After washed three times with TBST buffer, slides were treated with anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody and DAB reagents. And then hematoxylin solutions were used to stain the chromatin of cell nuclei. The mounted slices were analyzed under a microscopy.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s procedure. And then the total RNA was reversed transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). The TB Green Premix Ex Taq Kit (Takara, Japan) was used for RT-qPCR process. The primer’s sequence utilized were as follows: DTYMK, forward 5’-CCGGTTCCCGGAAAGATCAAC-3’, reverse 5’-TCCCAGCGATTTGCAGAAAAA-3’; GAPDH, forward 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’, reverse 5’-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’. ΔΔCt method was utilized to evaluate the RNA expression levels of specific genes.

Western blot analysis

The indicated proteins were detected using the following antibodies: anti-DTYMK (ab154867, abcam, 1:1000) and β-actin (BS6007M, Bioworld, 1:2500). Total proteins from cells or tissues were prepared, and the protein concentrations were determined. Total proteins were separated using 10% SDS‒PAGE and then electroblotted onto a PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked in PBST with 7% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, and then incubated membranes with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After that, membranes were washed with PBST and incubated with secondary antibodies (Proteintech, SA00001-1 and SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. Images were developed by chemiluminescence.

Cell cycle analysis via flow cytometry

Hub-7 and PLC/PRF/5 cells were transfected with anti- DTYMK siRNA for 24 h. The cells were collected and wash twice. After fixed by pre-chilled 70% cold ethanol for 2 h at -20 °C, fixed cells were then incubated with 50 µg/mL propidium iodide for 30 min at 4 °C. The data were collected using a flow cytometry.

Cell proliferation, colony formation, and migration and invasion assays

Hub-7 and PLC/PRF/5 cells were transfected with anti- DTYMK siRNA for 12 h. For accessing the cell proliferation, 1 × 104 cells were planted in the 48-well plates and cultured. The cell number were counted at time points of 24, 48, 96 and 120 h using hemocytometer. For the colony formation assay, after transfection with anti- DTYMK siRNA for 12 h, 1000 cells were seeded in the 6well plates and incubated for 8–10 days. These cells were then fixed with methanol and stained with 5% crystal violet solution and the colony number were counted for single visible clones. As for the cell migration assay, 1 × 105 cells were resuspended by 100 µL cultured medium with 0.05% FBS and then seeded in the upper chambers of Transwell (80 μm pore size). For the cell invasion assay, 1 × 105 cells suspended in 100 µL cultured medium with 0.05% FBS were added to the upper Transwell chambers coated with coagulated Matrigel. Cultured medium with 10% FBS were add in the bottom chamber. After 24–48 h, migrated or invasive cells were fixed and then stained with 5% crystal violet solution. Images were obtained under a under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized R software (version 4.3.1), along with relevant R packages and GraphPad Prism 10.1, to process, statistically analyze, and visualize the data. The unpaired Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons for normally distributed variables. Pearson correlation analysis was utilized for normally distributed variables, while Spearman’s correlation analysis was employed for non-normally distributed variables to assess correlations. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while ns represented not significance.

Results

Identification of DEGs

Figure 1 provides a detailed illustration of the cohort and research workflow, enhancing comprehension of the study methodology. To identify the genes involved in HCC progression, we selected TCGA-LIHC dataset to analyze the DEGs between HCC tissue and adjacent-normal tissue. Firstly, we normalized the raw data of expression profile from 420 samples and constructed a hierarchical cluster tree, which showed that no significant outliers were observed (Fig. 2.a). The principal component analysis of the dataset was carried out, and it showed that HCC tissues (n = 370) and adjacent-normal tissues (n = 50) cluster separately in the different spaces according to gene expression data (Fig. 2.b). Next, we used “DESeq” R package to identify the DEGs between HCC tissue and adjacent normal tissue. A total of 4447 DEGs, including 3117 up-regulated genes and 1330 down-regulated genes, were screened out at the threshold of p ≤ 0.05 and |log2 FC| > 1 and selected for subsequent analysis (Fig. 2.c) (Table S3).

Recognition of HCC-related gene modules by WGCNA

The expression data of the 4447 DEGs mentioned above were selected for gene co-expression network analysis. According to the characteristic of the co-expression network being scale-free, the optimal weighting coefficient β is selected, with a soft threshold of 6 determined as the β value. (Fig. 3.a). The co-representation network (Fig. 3.c) was constructed by dynamic tree cut method for module identification, and six modules were finally obtained (Fig. 3.d). Among them, magenta (Cor = 0.71, p = 1e-200) (Fig. 3.b and FigS1) module had the strongest correlation with HCC. The magenta module comprised 2914 coexpressed genes (Table S4), and 277 genes (Table S5) were screened as potential HCC-related genes by applying thresholds of MM > 0.7 and GS > 0.5.

Construction and validation of G2/MR score model based on machine learning algorithms

HALLMARK enrichment analyses were carried out to provide further insight into the functions and pathways of 277 HCC-related genes identified by WGCNA. The G2/M checkpoint was most significant pathway among all enriched HALLMARK pathways (FigS2). To develop a prognostic model based on 48 genes involved in the enriched G2/M checkpoint pathway, we used 150 cases of TCGA-LIHC as the training cohort, and 363 cases of TCGA-LIHC and 213 cases of ICGC LIRI-JP cohort as the validation cohort. We employed 117 combinations of 10 machine learning algorithms, including the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression algorithm (Lasso, “glmnet” R package), the Ridge regression algorithm (Ridge, “glmnet” R package), the stepwise Cox proportional hazards regression algorithm (Stepwise Cox, “Stepwise” R package), the CoxBoost algorithm (CoxBoost, “CoxBoost” R package), the random survival forest algorithm (RSF, “RandomForestSRC” R package), the elastic net regression algorithm (Enet, “glmnet” R package), the partial least squares regression to Cox model algorithm (plsRcox, “plsRcox” R package), the supervised principal component regression algorithm (SuperPC, “superpc” R package), the gradient boosting machine algorithm (GBM, “gbm” R package), and the survival support vector machine algorithm (survival-SVM, “survivalsvm” R package), to develop a prognostic prediction model for HCC, utilizing the 150 cases of TCGA-LIHC as the training cohort. Then, 117 prediction models were combined through a 10-fold cross-validation framework, and the C-index of all training and validation cohorts was calculated. Among the models constructed by 117 machine learning algorithms, StepCox[forward] + RSF and RSF models produced the highest average C-index, which was 0.76 (Fig. 4.a). Interestingly, we found that StepCox[forward] + RSF and RSF models provide the same prognostic model (G2/MR) contained five genes (STMN1, DTYMK, EZH2, PBK, and UCK2) which was calculated using the following formula: G2MRS = 0.164*STMN1 gene expression + 0.136*DTYMK gene expression + 0.169*EZH2 gene expression + 0.121*PBK gene expression + 0.131*UCK2 gene expression.

Utilizing the median G2/MR score as a threshold, all HCC patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. K-M survival analysis revealed that high-risk group exhibited a significantly reduced OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared with those in the low-risk group in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 4.b-e). To further examine the prognostic predictive capacity of G2/MR model, we performed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival in the four cohorts (Fig. 4.g-i). Additionally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that the G2/MR score could serve as an independent prognostic factor for OS (FigS3). Collectively, G2/MR score possessed the capability to serve as both a diagnostic and prognostic indicator for HCC.

Comparative analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells across the G2/MRhigh and G2/MRlow groups

Given the significant impact of the immune microenvironment on the progression of HCC, we aimed to elucidate the differences in the immune landscape between the G2/MRhigh group and the G2/MRlow group. CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm was used to quantify the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types. The immune infiltration levels of Tregs cells in the G2/MRhigh group were higher than the G2/MRlow group, while that of activated mast cell and NK cells in the G2/MRhigh group were lower than the G2/MRlow group (Fig. 5.a). Next, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between G2/MR scores and the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types. The results indicated that G2/MR scores were positively correlated with the Tregs cells (Fig. 5.b and c). CD4, CD25 and FoxP3 are the markers of Tregs cells. G2/MR scores were significantly correlated with the infiltration levels of Tregs cells and the expression levels of CD4, CD25, and FoxP3 (Fig. 5.c). The expression levels of CD4, CD25, and FoxP3 in the G2/MRhigh group were remarkably higher than the G2/MRlow group (Fig. 5.d).

Expression distribution of five G2/MR genes in a single-cell resolution

In order to characterize the expression patterns of the five G2/MR genes including STMN1, DTYMK, EZH2, PBK and UCK2 in the tumor microenvironment, the 10× genomics dataset GSE149614 and HCCDB database was used to explore the distribution and expression of these genes in a single-cell resolution. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) clustering revealed that the cells in HCC could be divided into nine clusters according to the expression of cell markers (Fig. 6.a and FigS4). Among them, the five G2/MR genes were mainly distributed in Tregs cells and epithelial cells, and the expression levels of STMN1 and DTYMK were higher than those of EZH2, PBK and UCK2 (Fig. 6.b-f and FigS5). Given the critical roles of Tregs cells in immune regulation and epithelial cells in tumor progression, the G2/MR scores might serve as indicators of the biological activities of Tregs and epithelial cells, ultimately correlating with the clinical outcomes of patients with HCC.

In vitro experiments validating the regulation of HCC phenotype by DTYMK

To investigate the role of the G2/MR genes in cancers, we first analyzed their expression in TCGA-LIHC, GSE14520, GSE47197, and GSE54236 datasets, and found that the expression levels of STMN1, DTYMK, EZH2, PBK and UCK2 were all higher in HCC compared to adjacent-normal tissues (FigS6). STMN1, EZH2, PBK, UCK2 have established roles in cancers, with well-documented biological functions and therapeutic targeting studies [15–18]. However, the role of DTYMK, a pivotal gene in pyrimidine metabolism [19], in the progression of HCC remains poorly defined. We explored the expression of DTYMK in pan-cancer using the online database TIMER2.0 [20] and found that DTYMK was highly expressed in almost all types of cancer, which suggested the significant role of DTYMK in cancers (Fig. 7.a). The immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of human HCC and adjacent tissues demonstrated the expression level of DTYMK in HCC was higher than that in adjacent tissue (Fig. 7.d). We next conducted cell cycle analysis via flow cytometry to demonstrate DTYMK’s role in the regulation of cell cycle. As shown in Fig. 7.b and c, knockdown of DTYMK significantly increased the proportion of cells in S-phage. To access the role of DTYMK in HCC, we transfected the anti-DTYMK small interfering RNA into two live cancer cell lines (Huh-7 and PLC/PRF/5). Knockdown of DTYMK was validated via reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot (FigS7). In both Huh-7 and PLC/PRF/5 cell lines, compared with the NC group, knockdown of DTYMK significantly reduced cancer cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion (Fig. 7. e-o).

Discussion

HCC is one of the most malignant cancers globally, and brings a huge burden on human health. Conventional therapies, typically including surgical resection, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, have not shown satisfactory outcomes in patients with HCC overall [1, 21, 22]. The lack of distinct clinical symptoms in the early stages of cancer often leads to patients being diagnosed at a more advanced stage of HCC, so the focus on HCC control has been improving access to early diagnosis and screening. Thus, developing new specific biomarkers and new therapeutics are imperative for clinical treatment and success.

In this study, we revealed the G2/M checkpoints were significantly upregulated in HCC and correlated with reduced survival in patients. We evaluated 117 combinations of machine learning algorithms to develop the G2/MR model, which was subsequently validated in four external cohorts and one internal cohort. Notably, genes involved in the G2/MR model were enriched in Treg cells and epithelial cells of HCC. DTYMK is a key gene in the G2/MR model and is associated with decreased survival rates. In vitro experiments also demonstrated the expression of DTYMK in human HCC and its role in cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and colony formation.

The aberrant activation of the G2/M checkpoint is intricately associated with the dysregulated cell cycle observed in cancer cell, resulting in genomic instability [5]. The current understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms associated with the G2/M checkpoint in the progression of HCC remains inadequate. It is essential to address this knowledge gap to develop targeted therapeutic strategies and identify reliable diagnostic biomarkers. Our study found that the G2/M checkpoint pathway were upregulated in HCC and correlated with reduced survival in patients. Importantly, single-cell sequencing analyses have demonstrated that genes involved in G2/MR models were particularly enriched in Tregs cells, suggesting that G2/M checkpoint pathway may facilitate tumor progression through a dual mechanism: it directly promotes the proliferation of tumor cells, as exemplified by the migration and invasion capabilities mediated by DTYMK; it also contributes to the establishment of a “cold tumor” microenvironment through immunosuppression mediated by Tregs cells.

The involvement of G2/M checkpoint in HCC progression suggest its potential value as specific prognostic biomarkers. This study has elucidated G2/M checkpoint pathway utilizing WGCNA and integrated 117 machine learning algorithm combinations to establish a prognostic model (G2/MR). G2/MR model evaluates only five genes (STMN1, DTYMK, EZH2, PBK, and UCK2) as minimal prognostic determinants, achieving consistent HCC outcome prediction. With only five genes requiring quantification, the G2/MR model offers cost-effective clinical deployment. Its robust performance in both training and validation cohorts (mean C-index = 0.76) suggests utility in resource-limited settings where complex genomic profiling is impractical. Compared with other published prognostic models [23–25], the G2M/R risk score was significantly related to the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-years survival in all included HCC cohorts, indicating the potential clinical application of G2M/R risk score in evaluate the prognosis of HCC patients.

Cancer cells exhibit an insatiable demand for nucleotide biosynthesis to sustain their uncontrolled proliferation. DTYMK, a pivotal enzyme in pyrimidine metabolism, catalyzes the ATP-dependent phosphorylation of deoxy-thymidine monophosphate (deoxy-TMP) to deoxy-thymidine diphosphate (deoxy-TDP) via both de novo and salvage pathways [26]. Elevated expression of DTYMK is significantly associated with aggressive tumor characteristics across various malignancies, including HCC, colorectal adenocarcinoma, and non-small cell lung cancer, where it serves as an independent prognostic marker for diminished survival and advanced disease progression (higher TNM stages, larger tumor sizes, and poorer histological differentiation) [27–29]. Mechanistically, the silencing of DTYMK disrupts PI3K/AKT signaling through downregulation the expression of CART, MAPKAPK2, AKT1, and NRF1, thereby effectively suppressing malignant behaviors [30]. Additionally, regulatory networks involving tumor-suppressive microRNAs further modulate DTYMK activity [31]. Our in vitro experiments using two liver cancer cell lines (Huh-7 and PLC/PRF/5) confirmed DTYMK’s crucial role in regulating proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation, suggesting DTYMK is a potential target and developing small inhibitor or inhibitory peptides targeting DTYMK may provide new therapeutic strategies in HCC. Interestingly, DTYMK inhibition has been shown to exhibit therapeutic synergy with conventional chemotherapeutic agents by compromising DNA damage repair [32]. Thus, combined with small inhibitor or inhibitory peptides targeting DTYMK, chemotherapy drugs such as 5’-FU, Doxorubicin and Cisplatin possibly presents more effective anti-tumor activity in HCC.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. We did not develop novel inhibitors that specifically target the G2/M checkpoint pathway or the critical gene KPNA2. The exact roles of these genes involved in the G2/M model are not yet clear in HCC. Furthermore, our analysis is based on publicly accessible datasets of HCC patients, which constrains the breadth of our findings. Given the prognostic potential of these markers, it is imperative to conduct further validation through prospective studies. Additionally, it is essential to evaluate the predictive capabilities of this diagnostic model in real-world clinical environments, particularly for patients undergoing immunotherapy or receiving treatments that rely on cytotoxic DNA-damaging mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our study developed a five-gene risk model through the application of WGCNA and machine learning algorithms, demonstrating its effectiveness in predicting clinical outcomes for patients with HCC. Notably, Tregs cells and epithelial cells exhibited elevated expression levels of genes involved in G2/MR models at a single-cell resolution, highlighting the significant role of immune suppression in the progression of HCC. In vitro investigations further elucidated the critical function of DTYMK in cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation. These findings underscore the potential of targeting the G2/M checkpoint or DTYMK as a strategy to enhance prognostic outcomes for HCC patients, thereby suggesting a promising avenue for the advancement of personalized medicine.
