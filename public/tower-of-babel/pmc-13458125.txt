Attention-Enhanced Hybrid Bidirectional LSTM and Temporal Convolutional Network for Early Detection of Lung Cancer in Low-Dose CT Scans
Authors: Benjamin Appiah Yeboah, Michael Asiedu Asare, Isaac Acquah, Kofi Ampomah Mensah, Mawusi Gbemavor-Assonhe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe developed and evaluated a lightweight, interpretable, and computationally efficient hybrid deep learning model for multiclass classification of early lung cancer in low-dose CT scans, potentially deployable in resource-limited healthcare environments.MethodsA novel hybrid architecture was developed that integrates Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) networks, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs), Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) blocks, and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME). The model employed depthwise separable convolutions to reduce computational complexity. A multi-stream feature extraction framework was implemented to enhance interpretability and capture spatial-temporal patterns. The model was trained and validated on the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset (version 2), containing 3,609 CT scan slices from 110 patients (1,097 original, 2,512 augmented), across three classes: normal, benign, and malignant. The dataset was split into training (60%), validation (30%), and testing (10%) subsets. Training was conducted using the AdamW optimizer for 16 epochs.ResultsThe model achieved 98.06% accuracy, 98.15% precision, 98.06% recall, and 98.04% F1-score, with a 99.88% AUC, a model size of 3.33 MB, and 279,561 parameters.ConclusionThe lightweight model achieves high diagnostic accuracy with computational efficiency, SHAP-based and LIME-based interpretability methods, enabling potential suitability for deployment in resource-constrained clinical settings.Keywords: lung cancer detection, low-dose computed tomography, hybrid deep learning, temporal convolutional networks, bidirectional LSTM, efficient channel attention, explainable artificial intelligence

1. Introduction

The rising global burden of lung cancer 1 has intensified the demand for effective early detection strategies within healthcare systems worldwide.2-4 Lung cancer accounts for approximately 12.4% of global cancer incidence and 18.7% of cancer-related mortality. 5 Diagnosis is challenging due to the disease’s heterogeneous nature, characterized by diverse histological subtypes and varying tumor growth patterns. Detecting the disease at its earliest stage offers a decisive advantage: it substantially improves patient survival and life expectancy by enabling timely intervention and preserving a broader range of treatment options.6,7 While tissue biopsies remain the definitive way to confirm lung cancer, their invasive nature poses procedural risks, particularly when lesions are located near the trachea or a major vessel.8-10

Modern diagnostic pathways adopt a multimodal imaging strategy, combining chest radiography (CXR), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with histopathological and molecular analyses.11,12 Among these modalities, low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) has become the gold standard for lung cancer screening, combining high sensitivity with minimal radiation exposure. This capability has enabled population-wide screening programs capable of reducing lung cancer mortality on a global scale.13,14 The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) demonstrated a 20% relative reduction in lung cancer mortality among high-risk individuals undergoing LDCT screening compared with chest radiography. 15 This benefit was later confirmed by the Dutch–Belgian NELSON trial, which reported a 24% reduction in mortality among men undergoing volume-based CT screening. 16

Despite these promising outcomes, a major limitation of LDCT screening is the high rate of false-positive findings. In the NLST, approximately 24.2% of LDCT scans were initially classified as positive. However, more than 96% of these findings were eventually identified as false positives. The majority of these false positives were small benign pulmonary nodules, such as granulomas from previous infections, intrapulmonary lymph nodes, or inflammatory lesions.15,17,18 Clinically, this is illustrated by a scenario where a high-risk patient whose LDCT reveals a 5 mm ground-glass opacity that is flagged for follow-up imaging, referred for a PET-CT, and ultimately undergoes a CT-guided biopsy, only to have the specimen confirm a benign granuloma. This cascade of unnecessary procedures, driven by a single ambiguous nodule, is repeated across thousands of patients annually, generating substantial patient anxiety and healthcare burden. 19 It is precisely this inability to reliably distinguish a malignant nodule from a benign mimic at the point of initial screening that has motivated the development of advanced computational approaches, including artificial intelligence and deep learning techniques, to improve nodule classification accuracy and reduce avoidable downstream investigations.

Integrating deep learning into LDCT imaging represents a viable evolutionary step toward precision diagnostics. These systems have demonstrated the potential to augment human performance while reducing interpretive variability and standardizing clinical decision-making.20,21 In principle, AI-enhanced screening could democratize early detection, making it a universal component of preventive medicine rather than a privilege of well-resourced healthcare systems. Promising results have emerged from controlled research environments, such as Shamrat et al.’s fine-tuned model, achieving a multiclass classification accuracy of 96.97%, 22 and hybrid deep learning rule-based systems improving sensitivity and specificity. 23

Despite rapid advances in AI-driven imaging, significant challenges persist across three interrelated dimensions. At the clinical level, AI-assisted LDCT interpretation remains hindered by false-positive rates often exceeding 20%, leading to unnecessary biopsies, follow-up scans, and patient anxiety. Inter-radiologist variability persists, undermining diagnostic confidence.13,24-26 This challenge is magnified when detecting very small nodules (<3 mm), as they are easily obscured by other anatomical structures and frequently missed. At the technical level, conventional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) struggle to capture the long-range spatial dependencies in CT scans that are critical for identifying subtle malignancies.27,28 While state-of-the-art models achieve strong results in controlled settings, they demand high-end GPUs, large memory capacities, and multi-second inference times, requirements beyond the infrastructure of many rural clinics and underfunded hospitals.29,30 At the trust level, most AI models function as “black boxes,” providing little transparency into their decision-making. A radiologist presented with a high-risk prediction, but no indication of which nodule characteristics drove the classification, is left without a principled basis for action. This opacity limits adoption in low-resource environments, where clinicians often work without AI specialists.31,32 Recent systematic reviews confirm that deployment across diverse healthcare contexts remains inconsistent despite marked algorithmic gains due to combined infrastructural and interpretability gaps.33-35

The motivation for the present work arises directly from this convergence of unmet needs. Existing models force a trade-off between accuracy, computational efficiency, and interpretability because none can be sacrificed in a clinically viable system. This study, therefore, aims to develop and evaluate a lightweight hybrid deep learning model that addresses all three dimensions simultaneously, with explicit optimization for deployment in resource-poor healthcare settings. The proposed architecture integrates Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) networks, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs), and Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) mechanisms, designed to detect subtle spatial patterns while remaining computationally inexpensive. To address the interpretability gap, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), which provide complementary case-specific and global feature attribution, are incorporated to highlight the nodule regions that drive each prediction in a form that non-specialist clinicians can meaningfully interrogate.

The scope of this work is limited to LDCT-based pulmonary nodule classification in adult populations using established benchmark datasets; it does not extend to other imaging modalities, pediatric cohorts, or prospective clinical validation. Our contributions are threefold:

2. Related Works

Recent research has explored numerous AI approaches for automated lung cancer detection and classification using histopathological imaging data. These approaches can broadly be categorized into CNN-based models, hybrid deep learning architectures, and transformer or attention-based frameworks.

2.1. CNN-Based Models

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly those leveraging transfer learning from large-scale pre-trained architectures, have become the dominant paradigm for automated lung cancer classification from histopathological images. Across multiple comparative evaluations, architectures such as VGG16, ResNet variants, DenseNet, and InceptionV3 have consistently demonstrated robust discriminative feature extraction even when training data are limited, with DenseNet achieving the highest reported accuracy of 97% among architectures evaluated on histopathology benchmarks, 36 and ResNet-50 attaining 96.83% on four-class lung tissue classification. 37 These results collectively establish that transfer learning substantially reduces the data and computational burden of training from scratch while preserving classification fidelity across the principal histological subtypes: lung adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and benign tissue. 38 Further work has extended this foundation toward clinical utility, with ensemble and computer-aided detection (CAD) approaches demonstrating that multi-model combinations can assist pathologists in real-world diagnostic workflows. 39

Classical machine learning pipelines have also been explored as alternatives to deep learning for early lung tumor detection. Notably, combinations of PCA-based feature extraction, decision tree classification, and SVM-based diagnosis report competitive accuracy (98.07%) with low false-positive rates, 40 though such approaches sacrifice the hierarchical feature learning capacity that makes CNNs effective on high-dimensional image data. Recent studies have built upon classical methods for early-stage detection and staging tasks. Notably, Gopalakrishnan et al. 41 developed a machine learning-based framework for predicting early-stage lung cancer. Similarly, Vinston Raja et al. 42 proposed machine learning techniques for accurately staging lung carcinoma from low-dose CT scans, focusing on minimizing radiation exposure while preserving classification performance. These studies underscore the ongoing importance of machine learning in early diagnostic processes. However, their reliance on handcrafted features limits their ability to capture complex spatial patterns compared to deep learning models.

Notwithstanding these advances, a consistent limitation across this body of work emerges: performance is reported under controlled benchmark conditions, with little attention to computational overhead, deployment feasibility in resource-constrained settings, or the interpretability of model predictions. CNN architectures optimized for accuracy on curated datasets demand substantial GPU resources and offer limited transparency into the spatial features driving classification decisions, precisely the properties that constrain clinical adoption beyond well-equipped research centers. This gap motivates the hybrid lightweight approach proposed in the present study, which targets accuracy, efficiency, and explainability concurrently.

2.2. Hybrid Deep Learning Architectures

Recognition of the limitations inherent to single-architecture models has driven a shift toward hybrid frameworks that combine complementary learning paradigms to capture richer feature representations. The dominant strategy pairs CNNs, which excel at extracting local spatial textures, with architectures that model longer-range dependencies. This has taken several forms in the literature: CNN-transformer hybrids that couple local convolutional feature extraction with global self-attention,43,44 CNN-recurrent hybrids that additionally encode sequential and contextual information through LSTM and self-attention mechanisms,45,46 and lighter combinations that substitute the final classification stage of a CNN with a traditional machine learning classifier, such as an SVM. 47 Across these configurations, the performance gains over single-architecture baselines are consistent and substantial: a ResNet-ViT hybrid achieved 99.31% accuracy on the LC25000 dataset 44 and an EfficientNet-based deep hybrid network improved over a conventional CNN baseline. 48

Recent research has investigated hybrid pipelines that combine advanced image processing techniques with machine learning to enhance staging and detection. Tamilvizhi et al. 49 proposed an integrated framework that includes innovative steps for image preprocessing, segmentation, and classification aimed at lung cancer detection and stage prediction. Their method improves feature quality before classification, leading to better diagnostic performance. However, these multi-stage pipelines introduce additional computational overhead and require careful parameter tuning, which limits their scalability and real-time deployment in clinical settings.

These studies collectively demonstrate that architectural hybridization effectively enhances classification accuracy. However, the field’s preoccupation with maximizing benchmark performance has come at a cost: most proposed hybrids are architecturally complex, parameter-heavy, and evaluated solely on accuracy metrics, with little consideration of inference speed, memory footprint, or suitability for deployment on standard hardware. Interpretability is similarly absent in the reviewed hybrid frameworks.

2.3. Transformer and Attention-Based Frameworks

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have become an alternative to CNNs for lung cancer image classification. Their core advantage is their use of self-attention mechanisms that model long-range spatial dependencies across an entire image, addressing a well-documented weakness of purely convolutional approaches. Empirical evidence for this advantage is strong: ViT-based classifiers have consistently matched or surpassed CNN baselines on standard histopathology benchmarks, with reported accuracies reaching 99.04% on the LC25000 dataset, 50 and demonstrations of cross-task versatility across both lung and colon cancer classification. 51 Custom ViT architectures further augmented with channel and spatial attention mechanisms have extended these gains to CT imaging, where pre-trained models have historically struggled with pixel-level feature discrimination, 52 while transformer-based U-Net variants with class-balanced ensemble strategies have been applied to the more demanding problem of multi-label lung disease classification from chest X-rays, partially redressing the localization bias inherent in conventional CNNs. 49

However, standard ViTs are data-hungry and parameter-intensive, limiting their utility in low-resource settings. One approach to addressing this tension is the development of lightweight hybrid architectures that maintain the global dependency modeling capabilities of self-attention while improving the parameter efficiency of convolutional operations. An example of this is the Lung Mobile ViT (LMVT), which integrates depthwise separable convolutions with multi-head self-attention and a Convolution Block Attention Module (CBAM) for feature selection. This combination achieves state-of-the-art results while using significantly fewer parameters. 53 This approach is significant because it indicates that the field is starting to recognize that accuracy and efficiency can coexist without being mutually exclusive. However, interpretability remains notably absent from the literature on Vision Transformers (ViTs) reviewed here. While attention maps are sometimes mentioned as a form of implicit explainability, they do not provide the spatially grounded, clinician-facing rationales necessary for safe clinical application. The proposed model in this study builds on the efficiency advancements made by works such as LMVT, while also addressing the interpretability gap through integrated explanations based on LIME and SHAP.

The original dataset of 1,097 CT scans was obtained from Mendeley Data 55 and exhibited significant class imbalance (Table 1). The dataset creators addressed this by applying targeted elastic transformations that mimic natural anatomical variations and differential augmentation rates: 10-fold for normal, 25-fold for benign, and 7-fold for malignant images. This systematic augmentation approach generated a final balanced dataset of 3,609 images while maintaining the anatomical realism necessary for effective model training (Table 1). The 110 cases vary in gender, age, educational accomplishment, residential area, and living status. While some of them are employees of the Iraqi ministries of Transport and Oil, others are farmers and gainers. Most of them come from the middle region of Iraq, particularly the provinces of Baghdad, Wasit, Diyala, Salahuddin, and Babylon.

All CT images were originally acquired using a Siemens SOMATOM scanner at 120 kV with a slice thickness of 1 mm. The imaging window parameters included a width ranging from 350 to 1200 Hounsfield Units (HU) and a center between 50 and 600 HU, with breath-hold at full inspiration during acquisition. De-identified DICOM-format images were converted into standard folders organized by diagnostic class for subsequent machine learning analysis.

All versions of the dataset lack patient-level identifiers, metadata, and slice-to-case mappings, necessitating treatment of each CT slice as an independent sample. This approach is consistent with previous studies using this dataset. While this limits our ability to account for intra-patient correlations or perform patient-level splitting, our evaluation protocol followed best practices for slice-level classification under these constraints.

3.1.1. Dataset Justification

We selected the IQ-OTH/NCCD Lung Cancer Augmented Dataset over the widely used LUNA16 benchmark for several methodological reasons. While LUNA16 contains 888 CT scans from the LIDC-IDRI database and serves as a standard for nodule detection, it lacks explicit diagnostic labels for benign and malignant classifications. Using LUNA16 for diagnostic classification would require additional preprocessing to derive labels from radiological annotations, potentially introducing label noise and bias.

The IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset provides high-resolution DICOM scans with direct class labels for benign, malignant, and normal, enabling immediate multiclass classification without intermediate label derivation. Additionally, its larger sample size of 3,609 augmented samples (from 1,097 originals) offers greater statistical power and reduces the risk of overfitting compared to LUNA16’s 888 scans. This combination of explicit labeling, larger sample size, and direct classification compatibility makes the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset more suitable for our end-to-end deep learning approach.

3.1.2. Dataset Splitting

The dataset was partitioned into training (60%), validation (30%), and testing (10%) subsets, comprising 2,165, 1,083, and 361 samples, respectively, to balance model optimization, facilitate hyperparameter tuning, and enable unbiased performance evaluation. The 1-sample validation set discrepancy (1,083 vs. expected 1,082.7) occurred because the malignant class, having an odd number of samples (1,201), required rounding during the 30% allocation (1,201 × 0.30 = 360.3). The fractional sample (0.3) was rounded up to maintain integer counts, allocating 361 samples to validation rather than 360, resulting in 1 additional sample in the validation set.

Cross-validation was not implemented because, without patient-level identifiers, slice-level k-fold cross-validation would substantially increase the likelihood of placing highly correlated slices from the same scan into both training and test folds. Repeated exposure to near-duplicate image content can artificially inflate performance estimates. To mitigate this risk, we used a single, fixed train/validation/test split, which reduces the frequency of potential information leakage compared to repeated resampling in k-fold procedures while subject to the dataset’s inherent correlation limitations.

Stratified sampling preserved class distributions across all subsets to address single-split variance constraints while maintaining statistical rigor. Bootstrap resampling with 2,000 iterations also generated robust confidence intervals and variance-aware performance estimates. This approach ensures conservative and reproducible generalization assessment despite the absence of patient-level data grouping.

The first layer, Bi-LSTM, captures both forward and backward contextual dependencies in the sequentially structured input, making it particularly effective for learning intricate patterns in CT and MRI images. This property makes it well-suited for lung cancer detection tasks.

Bi-LSTM is selected over alternative sequence models, such as Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) or standard LSTMs, due to its ability to retain long-range dependencies while mitigating vanishing gradient issues. 3.3. Experimental Setup

Model experiments were conducted using PyTorch 2.9.0+cu126 on a single NVIDIA GPU (Compute Unified Device Architecture, CUDA) for training and evaluation.

3.3.1. Model Training

The model was configured with an input size of 384, 64 LSTM units, and 64 TCN channels, with the output layer adapted to the number of target classes.

Training was conducted for up to 16 epochs using mini-batch gradient descent on a GPU-enabled device. To address class imbalance and prioritize clinically critical predictions, class weighting was incorporated into the loss function, assigning weights of [1.0, 1.3, 1.1] to the benign, malignant, and normal classes, respectively. This weighting scheme emphasizes the malignant class to prioritize diagnostic safety and minimize the risk of false negatives. Although the augmented dataset achieved class balance, an unequal class weighting strategy was used in the loss function to explicitly maximize sensitivity for malignant classifications.

A focal loss function was utilized to further mitigate class imbalance and hard-sample dominance, with a focusing parameter (γ) of 2.0 and the same class-specific weighting factors. This formulation encourages the model to focus more on difficult-to-classify examples, enhancing robustness in imbalanced classification settings.

Optimization was performed using the Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) technique combined with the AdamW optimizer. SAM enhances generalization by seeking parameter regions with uniformly low loss, thereby improving model robustness. The AdamW optimizer was initialized with a base learning rate scaled from a maximum learning rate of 5×10−4 , and a weight decay coefficient of 1×10−4 was applied to regularize the model and prevent overfitting. The SAM perturbation radius (ρ) was set to 0.05. A One-Cycle Learning Rate scheduling policy was employed to accelerate convergence and improve generalization. The learning rate was increased to a peak value of 5×10−4 during the initial 30% of training, followed by a cosine annealing strategy to gradually decrease it to a very small value. This dynamic adjustment of the learning rate helps the model escape shallow minima and converge more effectively. During each training iteration, SAM was applied in a two-step process involving an ascent step followed by a descent step, with gradients computed twice per batch. Training loss and accuracy were computed at the end of each epoch, alongside validation loss and accuracy evaluated on a held-out validation set under a no-gradient setting.

Model performance was monitored using validation loss, and the best-performing model was saved based on the lowest observed validation loss. Early stopping was implemented with a patience of 7 epochs to prevent overfitting, terminating training if no improvement in validation loss was observed over consecutive epochs. The overall training time was recorded, and all experiments were conducted under consistent computational settings to ensure reproducibility. The test set (n = 361) was kept entirely blind during model training, hyperparameter tuning, and validation. No performance metrics were computed on the test set until the final evaluation, ensuring an unbiased assessment of the model’s generalization ability.

3.3.2. Performance Evaluation and Interpretability

We implemented a comprehensive evaluation framework to assess model performance. We monitored loss and accuracy metrics across training and validation sets during training, with model checkpointing at each epoch. The optimal model was selected based on minimum validation loss to ensure robust generalization.

Performance evaluation employed multiple complementary metrics to address the multiclass classification challenge across normal, benign, and malignant categories. We calculated accuracy, Area Under the Curve (AUC), precision, recall, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score to provide a comprehensive assessment beyond simple classification accuracy. AUC quantified the model’s discriminative capacity between classes, while precision and recall evaluated the reliability and sensitivity of the predictions, respectively. The F1-score provided a balanced measure that addresses the precision-recall trade-off, particularly critical for malignant case detection, where false negatives carry significant clinical consequences. Specificity measures the model’s ability to correctly identify those without lung cancer. Positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) indicate the model’s accuracy in detecting the presence or absence of malignancy, considering prevalence. Due to the multiclass nature of the classification task, class-wise metrics were first computed using a one-versus-rest strategy. Macro-averaged metrics were reported to assess balanced performance across classes, whereas weighted averages were reported to reflect overall population-level performance while accounting for class frequencies.

Training dynamics were analyzed using loss and accuracy curves over 16 epochs to identify potential overfitting, underfitting, or data leakage. This temporal analysis provided insights into model convergence behavior and generalization capacity on unseen data.

For clinical interpretability, we implemented LIME to generate instance-specific explanations of model predictions and SHAP for a global understanding of the model’s behavior. LIME operates by perturbing input CT scans and training local surrogate models to approximate decision boundaries, providing clinically relevant explanations for individual predictions. SHAP works by systematically masking or replacing input features to observe changes in the model’s prediction.

3.4. Statistical Analysis

To ensure rigorous statistical evaluation, nonparametric bootstrapping with 2,000 resamples was employed to estimate 95% confidence intervals for key performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score) using NumPy (version 2.0.2) and SciPy (version 1.11.4) libraries.

Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d, calculated as the difference between the proposed model’s point estimate and each baseline’s reported value, divided by the standard deviation derived from the proposed model’s bootstrap distribution, because the baseline studies reported only point estimates without variance measures. Effect sizes were interpreted using an extended classification scheme to accommodate values exceeding the conventional 0.8 “large” threshold: negligible (d < 0.2), small (0.2–0.5), medium (0.5–0.8), large (0.8–1.2), very large (1.2–2.0), and huge (d ≥ 2.0).

A random seed of 42 was applied across all analyses to ensure reproducibility.

5. Discussion

The proposed model addresses key challenges in automated lung cancer classification by achieving strong and balanced performance across all diagnostic categories. The model demonstrates exceptional classification performance, as evidenced by its high F1-score, precision, and recall, and an AUC of 99.88%. These results reflect the model’s robust ability to discriminate between malignant, benign, and normal lung cancer cases.

The model’s excellent performance is rooted in its carefully optimized architecture, which unites Bi-LSTMs, TCNs, and ECA blocks, each serving a distinct role in enhancing feature extraction and classification. Batch normalization, applied at the input stage before the multi-stream feature extraction block, stabilizes the data distribution and ensures that the Bi-LSTM and TCN layers receive well-scaled inputs. This approach prevents instability in the model’s hidden layers and accelerates convergence, directly supporting gains across all metrics, including accuracy and F1-score. The Bi-LSTM module captures forward and backward temporal dependencies within CT scan sequences, enabling the recognition of complex structural relationships critical to lung cancer classification. This bidirectional processing enhances recall, improves the F1-score, and reduces false negatives, providing a vital safeguard in clinical contexts where missed detections can be fatal. To further stabilize sequential learning, layer normalization follows the Bi-LSTM, regulating internal memory states across time steps and ensuring efficient, reliable pattern learning that generalizes well to unseen data.

The TCN layers extend long-range dependency modeling while avoiding the vanishing gradient pitfalls of recurrent architectures. They efficiently capture multi-scale temporal features through depthwise separable convolutions, contributing to the model’s exceptional AUC (99.88%) and lightweight footprint (3.3 MB). These layers simultaneously enhance spatial feature learning, supporting high precision (98.15%) and minimizing false positives. The multi-stream feature extraction layer combines residual and convolutional pathways to broaden representational diversity and prevent over-reliance on a single processing route, resulting in balanced accuracy, recall, and F1-score. The ECA blocks refine these representations by adaptively reweighting channels based on learned dependencies, thereby amplifying the most clinically relevant patterns for robust, interpretable decision-making. This mechanism sharpens both precision and recall while preserving computational efficiency, as shown in Figure 7. The design’s effectiveness is evident in the steady decline of loss curves and the parallel improvement in accuracy curves shown in Figure 6A and B, with minimal divergence between the training and validation sets, providing clear evidence of strong generalization and resistance to overfitting. Key architectural features, including normalization layers, depthwise separable convolutions, residual and convolutional blocks, dropout in Bi-LSTM, dilated convolutions in TCN, and ECA attention mechanisms, work in concert to stabilize learning, reduce parameter complexity, expand receptive fields, and maintain feature richness. Collectively, these innovations create a predictive architecture that is both high-performing and computationally lean, making it well-suited for potential deployment in clinical environments.

The uniformly high performance across all diagnostic categories (malignant, benign, and normal) demonstrates the model’s methodological robustness (Table 2). The close alignment between macro- and weighted-average scores indicates balanced predictive strength not disproportionately influenced by class prevalence, addressing a common challenge in medical AI applications. This balance is critical in retrospective datasets, where class imbalance and absence of patient-level metadata can otherwise introduce subtle biases. The observed stability across metrics suggests that integrating Bi-LSTM, TCN, and ECA modules, combined with stratified sampling and bootstrap-based variance estimation, contributed to consistent feature learning and resilience against overfitting. Such methodological resilience positions the model for more reliable translation to heterogeneous clinical datasets.

To establish the clinical and technical significance of these results, we conducted comprehensive comparisons with state-of-the-art lung cancer classification approaches. The variation in reported metrics across studies reflects differences in methodological priorities: some emphasize raw accuracy, while others focus on interpretability or computational efficiency. Others focused on precision, F1-score, or recall to evaluate model efficiency in multiclass lung cancer classification, omitting AUC. Given these methodological variations, our comparative analysis employs five key criteria: diagnostic accuracy, model explainability, architectural robustness, computational efficiency, and clinical utility. 6. Limitations and Future Directions

Although the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset is high-quality and well-annotated, this study was limited to internal stratified testing; future research will pursue external validation using real-world, multi-institutional datasets and prospective clinical trials to ensure broader generalizability. Additionally, while the dataset creators applied elastic augmentation to address class imbalance and promote generalization, such synthetic transformations may introduce subtle distribution shifts; subsequent work will examine potential biases introduced by these techniques and evaluate model performance on raw, unaugmented datasets from diverse clinical contexts.

Interpretability and robustness also remain areas for enhancement. The Bi-LSTM-TCN architecture’s temporal modeling capacity, while effective in capturing sequential dependencies within CT scan data, may inadvertently overfit to temporal noise, particularly in scans of suboptimal quality. To address this, targeted sensitivity analyses will be conducted across varying scan resolutions, imaging modalities, and nodule types. Relatedly, since the primary objective of this work was to develop and validate an integrated lightweight architecture rather than to isolate the contribution of individual components, future work will conduct systematic ablation experiments to evaluate each component’s contribution.

Comparative effect-size estimates relied on Cohen’s d computed using only the proposed model’s bootstrap-derived standard deviation, as the baseline studies did not report variance estimates; this treats each baseline’s reported metric as a fixed-point value and does not account for variability arising from different data splits, training runs, or random initialization across studies. Future work will therefore pursue head-to-head reimplementation of baseline architectures on a shared test set to enable formal paired statistical comparisons rather than only effect-size approximation.

Beyond addressing these limitations, future work will explore multimodal extensions of the current framework by integrating imaging features with patient metadata and radiology reports and incorporating large language models (LLMs) to enhance contextual understanding and emulate clinician reasoning in lung cancer triage and longitudinal follow-up planning.

7. Conclusions

We proposed a lightweight, hybrid deep learning model that effectively classifies lung cancer into normal, benign, and malignant categories using CT scans. Leveraging Bi-LSTM, TCNs, ECA modules, and depthwise separable convolutions, the proposed model demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy and computational efficiency. On the held-out test set, the proposed hybrid deep learning model achieved 98.06% accuracy for early-stage lung cancer detection from LDCT scans. Performance metrics showed precision of 98.15%, recall of 98.06%, and an F1- score of 98.04% with excellent discriminative capability (AUC = 99.88%). It is well-suited for integration into low-resource clinical environments, mobile health solutions, and telemedicine platforms. Furthermore, the model bridges the critical gap between AI performance and clinical trust by emphasizing explainability through LIME and SHAP, ensuring that AI-driven predictions are accurate and transparent to healthcare professionals. This approach enhances early detection of lung cancer. Future work will focus on external validation using multi-institutional datasets, prospective clinical evaluation, clinician reader studies, usability studies, workflow assessment, robustness testing across diverse scanners and imaging protocols, sensitivity analyses, and integration of patient metadata and radiology reports to improve clinical generalizability.
