Modeling kidney fibrosis and tubular regeneration in iPSC-derived kidney organoids
Authors: Shengbing Li, Quincy Nlandu, Thierry P P van den Bosch, Carla C Baan, Rafael Kramann, Martin J Hoogduijn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney fibrosis is one of the pathological hallmarks of chronic kidney disease, likely contributing to the loss of kidney function. The mechanisms leading to kidney fibrosis and its reversibility is only partially understood, which hampers the development of therapeutic targets. Therefore, it is crucial to establish a robust human in vitro model that can be used to study kidney fibrosis and potential regeneration.MethodsHuman induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) were differentiated into kidney organoids. Fibrotic injury was induced by mimicking hypoxia (1% O2 48 h), inflammation (interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) 96 h) or a combination (hypoxia and IL-1β). Organoids were harvested at injury onset and up to 2 weeks post-injury. Fibrosis was assessed by mRNA and protein expression of fibronectin (FN1) and collagen type I, regeneration was evaluated through the presence of CD133+ and CD24+ progenitor cells and markers for differentiated kidney cell types.ResultsThe combination of hypoxia and IL-1β induced the strongest fibrotic response with significant upregulation of FN1 and collagen type I, and loss of tubular and glomerular markers. Over time, FN1 levels realigned with the control group, whereas collagen type I remained elevated. Tubular markers (Villin and ECAD) recovered to near-control levels, coinciding with increased CD133+ and CD24+ cell population and Ki67 expression. In contrast, PODXL+ glomerular structures showed limited recovery.ConclusionsWe present a reproducible human kidney organoid model that captures both fibrotic remodeling and tubular regeneration following clinically relevant injury. This platform offers a valuable tool for studying kidney-specific fibrosis dynamics and testing anti-fibrotic or pro-regenerative strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04866-6.Keywords: Kidney fibrosis, Kidney organoid, Ischemia reperfusion injury, Hypoxia, Inflammation, Kidney regeneration

Introduction

Kidney fibrosis is a hallmark of all chronic kidney diseases (CKD), globally affecting millions of people [1]. It is a pathological repair mechanism marked by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and the loss of renal parenchyma. Kidney fibrosis is induced by a range of insults, including inflammation, hypoxia among various others [2]. These common triggers also contribute to the development of fibrosis in transplanted kidneys, ultimately leading to allograft failure, just like in CKD [3]. Key driver cells in kidney fibrosis include ECM-producing mesenchymal cells and injured tubular epithelial cells (TECs), which initiate and shape fibrotic niches [4–6]. These TECs are highly sensitive to ischemia and toxic substances, which can induce TEC necrosis. This necrosis triggers an inflammatory response, creating a self-amplifying cycle known as necroinflammation which is another driver of fibrosis[7, 8].

However, these TECs also appear to be versatile, as the prevailing hypothesis suggests that injured TECs can recover either through the regeneration of the damaged cells themselves or by repopulation of the epithelium with progenitor-like TECs [9]. A number of studies have demonstrated that human kidneys contain a distinct population of progenitor-like cells that expand in response to injury and reconstitute the tubule. These cells, so called scattered tubular cells (STCs) are characterized by the expression of cell surface markers CD133 and CD24 [10]. STCs exhibit a primitive phenotype, lacking a defined apical brush border and failing to perform all functions of fully differentiated tubular cells, as reflected by their low expression of transporters such as megalin[11–16]. Despite this, the role of CD133+ CD24+ cells throughout the injury and recovery phase is not fully understood.

There are currently no approved therapies that are able to halt kidney fibrosis or therapies that are capable of stimulating regeneration during fibrosis [17]. Conventional experimental models have provided valuable insights in fibrosis progression and regeneration, but have not led to breakthroughs in therapy development. Animal models, particularly the unilateral ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) rodent model, are commonly used to study kidney fibrosis. However, these in vivo models exhibit significant differences in mechanistic physiology compared to the human kidney, which can obscure the behavior of human kidney cells during injury and repair. Human biopsies enable the observation of fibrosis and its clinical associations but are not suitable for mechanistic or intervention studies [18]. Precision-cut kidney slices preserve native tissue architecture and multicellular composition, providing an ex vivo platform for studying fibrosis. However, their limited viability and potential for contamination hinder their use in long-term studies of fibrosis progression or regeneration [19]. Thus, there is a need for alternative models that recapitulate the human kidney and support medium- to long-term experimental testing.

Human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) derived kidney organoids overcome several of these limitations of animal models, biopsy and precision-cut slices by combining human specific biology, multicellular complexity and 3D architecture in a single platform [20–22]. They contain several kidney specific cell types including tubular cells, podocytes and mesenchymal cells, enabling the study of cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions during injury and recovery. Kidney organoids have been used to model congenital diseases like Alport syndrome, as well as viral infections such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and Monkeypox [23–27]. Furthermore, these models have shown to be relevant for studying fibrosis in the context of COVID-induced injury and cisplatin-induced injury [24]. In addition to viral and toxin-induced injury, Kuppe et al. induced fibrosis in kidney organoids following stimulation with the inflammatory cytokine, interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) [5]. However, IRI remains a critical yet underutilized model in human fibrosis studies, despite being a major driver of kidney fibrosis in chronic kidney disease. Our study aims to establish a robust iPSC-derived kidney organoid fibrosis model using IRI as a clinically relevant injury, which we mimic through hypoxia and reoxygenation [28]. In addition, we will incorporate IL-1β to better capture the inflammatory component that arises during IRI. By combining these factors, we seek to evaluate fibrotic remodeling and tubular regeneration within a single human-relevant platform.

Methods

Human iPSC derivation

Human primary skin fibroblasts from healthy donors were reprogrammed into iPSCs using the multicistronic lentiviral vector encoding OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and C-MYC [29]. All procedures involving donors were conducted in alignment with the ethical guidelines approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Erasmus University Medical Center (MEC-2017-248) [30]. The iPSC lines were cultured on γ-irradiated mouse embryonic feeders at the beginning, and they were subsequently transitioned to feeder-free culture.

Human iPSC culture

Three cryopreserved human iPSC lines were thawed and cultured in StemFlex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, The Netherlands) supplemented with Revitacell solution (Gibco, The Netherlands) on Geltrex coated plates (1:100) (Gibco). Medium was refreshed with StemFlex 1 day after thawing and every 2 days thereafter. Cells were passaged in clumps using ReleSR (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) every 3–4 days after iPSC colonies reached around 85% confluency. The split ratio was 1:6 to 1:10. After 5 to 6 passages, cells were used for differentiation.

Kidney organoid differentiation

When iPSC colonies achieved a confluency of 85%, they were dissociated to single cells using TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 4 min. Single cells were resuspended in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) and subsequently spun down at 300 g for 4 min. These cells were then resuspended in StemFlex medium supplemented with RevitaCell solution and seeded at a density of 20,000 to 30,000 cells/cm2 on geltrex coated T25 flasks (Corning, New York, USA). Following seeding, cells were incubated overnight (18–24 h) at 37 °C, 5% CO2. The next day (day 0), the medium was switched to advanced RPMI 1640 (Gibco) containing 8 μm CHIR99021 (TOCRIS, Bristol, UK) and this medium was refreshed daily for an additional 2 days. After 72 h of CHIR99021 treatment, the medium was refreshed with advanced RPMI 1640 supplemented with 200 ng/mL recombinant human FGF9 (Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA), 1 μg/mL heparin sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 10 ng/mL activinA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). After 4 days of seeding (day 4), cells formed a tight monolayer. They were then dissociated by TrypLE and resuspended in advanced RPMI 1640 supplemented with 3 μm CHIR99021, 200 ng/mL FGF9 and 1 μg/mL heparin. Cells were added in a 96 well V-bottom plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a cell density of 500,000 cells in 150μL per well and centrifuged for 3 min at 300 g to aggregate cells into spheroids. V-bottom plates were incubated for 2 days. After 2 days, cell pellets were transferred to 6-well plate transwells (day 6). On day 7, medium was changed to advanced RPMI 1640 containing 200 ng/mL FGF9 and 1 μg/mL heparin, and was refreshed every other day for 4 days. On day 11, all factors were removed from the medium, and multiple renal vesicles were visible under a microscope at this stage. Medium was refreshed every other day until day 20, facilitating further maturation (Fig. 1).

A schematic overview of the kidney organoid culture process

Organoid fibrosis induction

This study tested 3 fibrosis-inducing conditions: hypoxia, inflammation through IL-1β and the combination of hypoxia and IL-1β. For the hypoxia condition, organoids were incubated at 1% O2 from day 16 to day 18 of differentiation. Subsequently, they were transferred to normoxia for 2 days prior to harvest. For the inflammation condition, organoids were refreshed with advanced RPMI 1640 containing 100 ng/mL IL-1β (Sigma-Aldrich) at day 16 for 96 h. Medium was refreshed at day 18. For the combined condition, organoids were cultured under hypoxic conditions (1% O2) from day 16 until day 18 in the presence of 100 ng/mL IL-1β. After that, the organoids were moved to normoxia for an additional 2 days in the presence of IL-1β. Organoids were harvested on day 20, 27 or 34.

Quantitative real-time PCR analyses (qPCR)

RNA of organoids were isolated by RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The quantity of RNA was measured using DeNovix DS-11 (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, US). cDNA were synthesized from 400 ng RNA in an end volume of 80μL through Moloney Murine Leukemia Virus Reverse Transcriptase Kit (M-MLV RT; Invitrogen, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), random primers (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA), and RNasin® Ribonuclease Inhibitor (Promega). qPCR were performed by TaqMan™ Gene Expression Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TaqMan™ Gene Expression Assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (Table 1). Gene expressions were calculated using 2 − ΔΔCT method expressed as a fold change, using GAPDH as housekeeping gene.

Immunohistochemistry and quantitative assessment

Kidney organoids were fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution for 30 min at room temperature. Organoids were then washed in DPBS twice, embedded in 1% Agarose LE (Roche, Germany) and placed in cassettes for processing and paraffin-embedding. Organoid sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin using HE600 according to manufactures instructions (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Arizona, USA). Immunohistochemistry was performed with an automated, validated and accredited staining system (Ventana Benchmark ULTRA, Ventana Medical Systems) using ultraview or optiview universal DAB detection Kit. In brief, following deparaffinization and heat-induced antigen retrieval the tissue samples were incubated with the antibody of interest (Table 2). Incubation was followed by haematoxylin II counter stain for 20 min and then a blue coloring reagent for 8 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Arizona, USA). Images were taken by NanoZoomer (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). Staining was quantified by determining the percentage of stained area by QuPath-v0.3.2 software.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (IF)

Co-staining of CD133/CD24 combined with Ki67, Villin1 and ECAD was performed by automated multiplex IF using the Ventana Benchmark Discovery (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Arizona, USA). In brief, following deparaffinization and heat-induced antigen retrieval with CC1 (#950-500, Ventana) for 32 min the tissue samples were incubated with CD133 was incubated for 32 min at 37 °C followed by OmniMap anti-rabbit HRP or OmniMap-mouse HRP (#760-4311/#760-4310, Ventana) and detection with R6G (#950-240, Ventana). An antibody denaturation step was performed with CC2 (#950-123, Ventana) at 100 °C for 20 min. Secondly, incubation with anti-Ki67 was performed for 32 min at 37 °C, followed by OmniMap anti-rabbit (#760-3211, Ventana) and detection with Red610 (#760-245, Ventana) for 8 min. An antibody denaturation step followed with CC2 at 100 °C for 20 min. Thirdly, anti-ECAD was incubated for 16 min at 37 °C, followed by OmniMap anti-mouse HRP (#760-4310, Ventana) and detection with Cy5 (#760-238, Ventana) for 8 min. An antibody denaturation step followed with CC2 at 100 °C for 20 min. Lastly, anti-Villin was incubated for 32 min at 37 °C, followed by OmniMap anti-rabbit HRP (#760-4311, Ventana) and detection with FAM (#760-243, Ventana) for 8 min. Finally, slides were washed in phosphate-buffered saline and mounted with Vectashield containing 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Vector laboratories, Peterborough, UK). Slides were imaged with Axioscan Zeiss.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay

Supernatants were harvested at the end of the experiment on day 20. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min before storage at − 80 °C. LDH activity was measured using the LDH-Glo cytotoxicity assay kit (Promega) following the manual. Briefly, cell supernatant was diluted 1:100 and 50µL of diluted supernatant was added to 50µL detection reagent and incubated for an hour. The Luminescence was measured on a CLARIOStar Plus plate reader.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed through GraphPad Prism 10 software (San Diego, CA, USA) for statistical significance. Depending on the data distribution, either one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Multiple comparisons were corrected using Tukey’s post-hoc test. Data represent at least 3 independent experiments and are presented as the mean with standard error of the mean (SEM). P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The combination of hypoxia and IL-1β upregulates the expression of fibrosis markers

To establish a kidney organoid fibrosis model that relates to the clinical settings, we treated kidney organoids with different injury conditions: hypoxia (1% O2), inflammation (IL-1β) or simultaneously with hypoxia and IL-1β to mimic IRI (Fig. 2A).

The mRNA expression of fibrosis marker FN1 in kidney organoids was significantly increased in the hypoxia+IL-1β group on day 20 compared with all other groups, whereas hypoxia or IL-1β alone had little effect (Fig. 2B). Similarly, COL1A1, was significantly upregulated in the hypoxia+IL-1β group in comparison with the control, and to either stimulus alone (Fig. 2C).

At the protein level, FN1 expression was significantly elevated in organoids exposed to the hypoxia alone and combined hypoxia+IL-1β injury compared with control. In contrast, organoids exposed to IL-1β only did not alter FN1 protein level (Fig. 2D). For collagen type I, no marked change was detected in either the hypoxia or IL-1β groups, whereas the combined hypoxia+IL-1β condition resulted in a significant increase compared to the control organoids (Fig. 2E).

Hypoxia and IL-1β combined injury increases collagen expression over time

To investigate the temporal dynamics of fibrosis marker expressions after combined hypoxia and IL-1β injury, kidney organoids were analyzed immediately after injury (day 20, T0) and following an additional 7 days (day 27, T1) or 14 days (day 34, T2) of culture without further stimulation (Fig. 3A).

FN1 expression, at both mRNA and protein levels, was significantly declined at T1 and T2 compared with T0, reaching levels similar to those of control organoids (Fig. 3B and D). In contrast, collagen type I exhibited a distinct pattern: COL1A1 mRNA remained at elevated levels at both T1 and T2 compared to the control, with T2 being significantly higher than T1. However, no significant elevation was observed between T0 and T2. Similarly, collagen type I protein expression was also significantly upregulated at T0 relative to control and remained stable through T1 and T2 (Fig. 3C and E).

Kidney tubular cells are injured during fibrosis but recover over time

To explore whether the hypoxia and IL-1β-induced fibrosis process affected nephron cell types, we assessed the expression of key podocyte and tubular markers. The podocyte marker PODXL was markedly downregulated at the mRNA level at T0 and remained low at both T1 and T2 compared with control. Despite this reduction, immunohistochemistry at T0 showed persistent PODXL protein expression, although glomerular structures exhibited abnormal staining patterns with dispersed, morphologically altered PODXL+ cells. By T1 and T2, the number of PODXL+ cells or fragments was markedly reduced compared to T0 (Fig. 4A and 4B).

The proximal tubule marker Villin and the distal tubule marker CDH1/ECAD were both significantly reduced at mRNA and protein levels at T0 compared with control. Over the subsequent extended culture period, both markers showed recovery: Villin expression returned to control levels by T1 and was maintained at T2, with a corresponding increase in protein expression. Moreover, CDH1 mRNA and ECAD protein were also significantly increased over time, reaching near-control levels by T2 (Fig. 4C–F).

Recovery of tubular marker expression was in line with cytotoxicity data, which showed elevated LDH release at T0 indicating acute damage, followed by a decrease to control levels at T1 and T2 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Fibroblasts, the main ECM-producing cell type in kidney fibrosis, are abundantly present in kidney organoids. Interestingly, PDGFRα, the marker for fibroblasts, showed a progressive increase from T0 to T1 and T2, reaching statistical significance at T2 compared to control (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Similarly, FAP mRNA expression was significantly upregulated starting from T0 and remained high levels throughout T1 and T2, indicating early and sustained fibroblast activation following combined hypoxia and IL-1β injury (Supplementary Fig. 2B). PDGFRα protein, initially reduced at T0 like other kidney structure cell types, increased by T2, indicating sustained fibroblast activation alongside partial recovery of nephron structures (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

CD133+ and CD24+ cells in kidney organoids

To evaluate tubular regeneration after injury, we quantified CD133 and CD24 mRNA expression in kidney organoids following hypoxia+IL-1β treatment. CD133 mRNA levels rose in a time-dependent manner, with the most pronounced increase observed at T2, despite only modest upward trends at T0 and T1 (Fig. 5A). In contrast, CD24 mRNA was significantly downregulated immediately after hypoxia+IL-1β exposure but recovered by T2, reaching levels above baseline (Fig. 5B).

IF staining was performed to localize the CD133+ and CD24+ cell populations within the kidney organoids. CD133, which is primarily expressed in tubular structures, was predominantly detected on the apical side of the tubules. The presence of CD133 was mainly observed in ECAD+ structures and was already evident in control organoids, though it became increasingly prominent after injury in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 5C).

In contrast, CD24 expression was not detectable at the protein level in control organoids, nor at time points T0 and T1 post-injury. However, by T2, CD24 expression became readily observable. Similar to CD133, CD24 localized within the tubules and was mainly confined to the apical membrane. However, its expression pattern was not as strongly associated with ECAD+ tubules as observed for CD133. Double-positive tubular structures for CD133 and CD24 were also observed, exhibiting distinct staining patterns for each marker (Supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, Ki67, a well-established proliferation marker, was upregulated following injury, with subsets of Ki67+ cells co-localizing with both CD133+ and CD24+ tubular cells. Together, these data demonstrate a coordinated and time-dependent activation of progenitor-like epithelial populations in injured kidney organoids, highlighting their potential role in tubular regeneration.

Discussion

Kidney organoids have been successfully utilized as a human model for diverse types of kidney diseases [23]. Here, we induced robust fibrogenesis in kidney organoids through stimulation with IL-1β and hypoxia, and thereby recapitulated key mechanisms driving kidney fibrosis. This injury model not only triggered ECM deposition across multiple compartments but also revealed the intrinsic regenerative capacity of tubular cells.

A key advantage of our model lies in its multicellular complexity, which recapitulates the interactions driving ECM generation and fibrotic remodeling. Previous work has shown that kidney organoids containing nephron epithelia and stromal cells can be spatially organized to mimic native kidney architecture [22, 31, 32]. In our system, interstitial fibroblasts, the principal producers of ECM, coexist with tubular epithelial and glomerular cells, each of which contributes to fibrogenesis. Upon injury induction, all of these populations exhibit a response, rather than a single cell type being targeted, thereby validating the model as a comprehensive platform for studying the coordinated mechanisms of kidney fibrosis.

In contrast to other fibrosis induction protocols [33–35], our results reveal that IL-1β is indispensable for eliciting a robust fibrotic response in kidney organoids. Whereas hypoxia exposure alone failed to induce significant ECM accumulation, the addition of IL-1β, a predominantly macrophage-derived proinflammatory cytokine, induced pronounced increases in collagen deposition and fibroblast activation [36–39]. Consistent with these findings, previous work demonstrated that hypoxia for 2 days does not alter stromal cell phenotype, whereas 6 days or more of low-oxygen culture elicits significant collagen upregulation and cellular morphological shifts [40]. Lemos et al. has shown that IL-1β triggers a MYC-dependent metabolic switch in murine kidney stromal cells, driving collagen synthesis and myofibroblast differentiation; our data extend these insights to a human organoid system [41]. Together, these observations underscore the central role of inflammatory mediators, beyond classic profibrotic factors like TGF-β, in kidney fibrogenesis modeling.

Within our kidney organoid model, interstitial fibroblasts exhibited pronounced activation following injury, driving substantial collagen and fibronectin deposition despite modest cell proliferation. The distinct temporal patterns of FN1 and collagen type I expression are particularly intriguing: FN1 shows a transient surge, consistent with its role in assembling a provisional matrix that facilitates cell adhesion and provides a scaffold for collagen deposition, after which its levels decline as the matrix matures. In contrast, collagen type I remains persistently elevated, reflecting deposition of structural collagen and progressive matrix stabilization, processes often driven by pro-fibrotic cues such as TGF-β and sustained myofibroblast activation [42, 43]. The data suggest that the initial loss of nephron structures is primarily a consequence of the acute injury and is more closely associated with the transient FN1 response, given that fibrotic changes persist and structures partially recover in subsequent weeks. However, our analysis focused on the endpoint of the 4-day injury (T0), and we did not examine the dynamics during the injury window itself. While we observed persistent fibrotic changes and partial recovery in the following weeks, other processes, such as necroinflammation driven by prominent cell death during the acute phase, may also contribute to injury in the initial 4 days [8].

A key observation in our model is the absence of glomerular recovery, which mirrors the in vivo situation where podocytes show limited regenerative capacity after severe injury. Moeller et al. suggests that podocyte numbers may be partly replenished in vivo, potentially through recruitment or transdifferentiation of parietal epithelial cells [44]. However, the robustness and physiological relevance of this process remain debated [44, 45]. In our system, podocyte structures disintegrated without reformation or the emergence of progenitor-like cells (CD133+ and CD24+), supporting the view that podocytes are highly vulnerable to detachment or death and lack meaningful regenerative potential. Therapeutically, this points toward dual strategies: preserving existing podocytes by stabilizing slit-diaphragm/cytoskeletal architecture and reducing injurious signaling, and promoting regeneration by testing interventions that enhance parietal epithelial cell-to-podocyte recruitment. Our organoid platform is well-suited to screen such protective and pro-regenerative therapies and to delineate the conditions under which glomerular repair can (or cannot) be achieved.

In contrast, kidney TECs displayed intrinsic regenerative capacity in the same model, a phenomenon also observed in human kidneys. Following injury, we observed a time-dependent emergence of CD133⁺ and CD24⁺ populations, markers of dedifferentiated STCs, known to mediate nephron repair in vivo [14, 46]. These STC-like cells, which to our knowledge are the first to be demonstrated in human kidney organoids under fibrotic stress, exhibited apical CD133+ and CD24+ expression. The appearance of these progenitor-like subsets correlated with structural recovery of tubular segments without exogenous growth factors, mirroring repair dynamics in adult human kidneys. Future work should employ lineage tracing to delineate STC subpopulations, identify the molecular cues driving their activation, and quantify their precise contribution to functional regeneration and overall organoid recovery.

Despite its strengths, the organoid injury model has limitations. Kidney organoids exhibit immature, fetal‐like transcriptional profiles and lack mature vasculature or an endogenous immune compartment [21, 23]. The absence of circulating immune cells precludes modeling macrophage–epithelial crosstalk beyond cytokine supplementation. Incorporation of autologous immune populations or microfluidic perfusion systems could enhance physiological relevance [47].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study established a robust human kidney organoid model that recapitulates both key drivers of tubulointerstitial fibrosis and the intrinsic regenerative responses of tubular epithelium. Our organoid model system provides a framework for mechanistic dissection of fibrogenic and regenerative pathways and for the preclinical evaluation of anti-fibrotic or pro-regenerative therapies.
