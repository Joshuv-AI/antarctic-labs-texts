Workplace ostracism and job performance: revisiting the limits of employee engagement in addressing workplace exclusion
Authors: Kailash Kumar Sahu, Ravi Kumar Mishra, Tushar Ranjan Sahoo, Shreya Kushwaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWorkplace ostracism represents a subtle but damaging form of workplace exclusion that can weaken employees’ psychological functioning and job-related outcomes. Drawing on self-regulation theory (SRT), this study examines the association between workplace ostracism and employee job performance and investigates whether employee engagement moderates this relationship. Data were collected from 477 employees working in IT, banking and insurance, BPO, and supply-chain/service roles and analysed using partial least square structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) through SmartPLS 4. The findings show that workplace ostracism is significantly and negatively associated with job performance, whereas employee engagement is significantly and positively associated with job performance. The model explained 24.10% of the variance in job performance. Contrary to the proposed moderation hypothesis, employee engagement did not significantly buffer the negative association between workplace ostracism and job performance. This suggests that engagement may enhance performance directly but may not operate as a sufficient protective condition against the adverse performance-related consequences of workplace exclusion. The study contributes to organizational behavior literature by clarifying the limits of employee engagement as a buffering resource and highlighting the need for organizations to directly address exclusionary workplace practices.Keywords: Self-Regulation Theory, Workplace Ostracism, Job Performance, Employee Engagement, PLS-SEM, Indian Service SectorSubject terms: Business and management, Business and management, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Workplace ostracism (WPO), defined as the perception of being ignored or excluded by colleagues or supervisors, is a pervasive yet often subtle workplace experience that can significantly shape employees’ psychological and behavioural outcomes1,2,3. It is particularly relevant in service-sector organizations, where interpersonal coordination, information sharing, and collaborative problem-solving are central to productivity and service quality4,5,6. Employees who experience ostracism may feel socially disconnected from the workplace, resulting in psychological stress, cognitive distraction, emotional exhaustion, and reduced access to work-related support. These conditions may weaken employees’ ability to focus on assigned tasks and sustain effective job performance (JP)1,5,7,8,6.

Prior research has consistently shown that WPO is associated with unfavourable job attitudes, withdrawal behaviour, reduced organizational citizenship behaviour (OCB), knowledge hiding, and counterproductive work behaviour1,9,6,10. However, much of this literature has focused either on psychological consequences or on indirect behavioural outcomes, while comparatively less attention has been paid to how ostracism is directly associated with JP in multi-sector service settings. From the perspective of SRT, ostracism may impair performance because excluded employees must devote cognitive and emotional resources to interpreting, managing, and coping with social rejection. As a result, fewer self-regulatory resources remain available for self-monitoring, attention control, goal pursuit, and task execution. Thus, WPO is not only a negative social experience but also a disruption to the self-regulatory processes through which employees maintain performance at work.

Employee engagement (EE) is another important construct in understanding JP. Engaged employees are characterized by higher levels of involvement, dedication, and psychological investment in their work roles. Previous studies have associated EE with improved JP, positive workplace attitudes, and better organizational outcomes11,12,5. Within an SRT framework, engagement can be understood as a motivational and attentional work state that may support goal-directed behaviour, persistence, and task focus. Accordingly, engaged employees may be more capable of maintaining work effort even when exposed to adverse workplace experiences. This provides the theoretical basis for examining whether EE can function as a boundary condition in the relationship between WPO and JP.

Existing studies suggest that WPO can reduce engagement and subsequently weaken service performance, indicating that engagement has often been examined as a mediating mechanism11,5. However, the possibility that EE may moderate the WPO–JP relationship remains less clearly established. The moderation argument requires separate attention because it asks whether employees who are already highly engaged are better able to preserve performance when facing exclusion. This study therefore extends prior work by examining EE not merely as an outcome of ostracism, but as a potential protective work-related state that may alter the strength of the association between WPO and JP.

The Indian service-sector context provides a meaningful setting for this inquiry. Service organizations in India often require intensive interpersonal coordination across teams, customers, supervisors, and digital work systems. In such contexts, perceived exclusion may restrict access to informal information, peer support, and collaborative opportunities, thereby making ostracism especially relevant for performance. Although studies have examined ostracism in hospitality, IT, and other service settings, evidence from Indian multi-sector service employees remains limited, particularly with respect to direct and moderated performance-related outcomes. Accordingly, this study addresses three gaps. First, it examines the direct association between WPO and JP among Indian service-sector employees. Second, it investigates the positive association between EE and JP. Third, it tests whether EE moderates the relationship between WPO and JP through an SRT-based explanation. By doing so, the study clarifies whether engagement operates only as a performance-enhancing work state or also as a buffering condition against the adverse performance-related implications of WPO.

Theoretical background

Self-regulation theory (SRT)

SRT explains how individuals set goals, monitor their progress, regulate emotional reactions, and adjust behaviour to achieve desired outcomes in response to personal standards and environmental demands13,14,15. Research has highlighted its relevance in adaptive goal pursuit across academic, health, and workplace contexts16,17. In organizational settings, effective self-regulation enables employees to maintain attention, control impulses, manage emotions, and persist in task-related behaviour despite social or situational pressures. WPO may disrupt these processes because excluded employees often devote cognitive and emotional energy to interpreting social rejection, managing negative emotions, and restoring a sense of belonging18,19,20. Such diversion of regulatory resources can weaken self-monitoring, reduce goal focus, and impair employees’ ability to sustain productive work behaviour. While employees with stronger self-regulatory capacity may better manage emotional responses and maintain goal-directed effort21, persistent ostracism can compromise self-control and increase stress, emotional exhaustion, and counterproductive work behaviours22,23,24.

Conceptual distinctiveness between employee engagement and self-regulation

Employee engagement is a positive work-related state marked by vigor, dedication, and absorption, through which employees invest physical, emotional, and cognitive energy in their work roles25,26. Self-regulation, however, is not treated in this study as a measured variable but as the theoretical process explaining how employees manage attention, emotions, and goal-directed behaviour under adverse workplace conditions. It involves self-monitoring, emotional control, motivation, and behavioural adjustment toward goals27. Thus, engagement reflects an employee’s motivational connection with work, whereas self-regulation explains the internal process through which such motivation may be sustained or disrupted. Prior studies suggest that self-regulatory processes, particularly emotion regulation, can support engagement by helping employees remain focused and immersed in work25,28,29.

WPO and JP

WPO has been shown to adversely affect employees’ psychological and work-related outcomes. In service-sector settings, where performance often depends on coordination, communication, and timely access to information, ostracism may be particularly harmful. Ostracized employees may experience psychological distress, emotional exhaustion, job tension, and reduced workplace belonging, all of which can weaken their ability to concentrate on assigned duties and interact effectively with customers and co-workers30,4,5. From the perspective of SRT, WPO can impair JP because employees are required to redirect cognitive and emotional resources toward understanding and coping with exclusion. This reduces the resources available for self-monitoring, attention control, persistence, and goal-directed task behaviour.

Prior studies further indicate that ostracism is associated with withdrawal behaviour, reduced OCB, knowledge hiding, and counterproductive work outcomes5;9,6. In the Indian context, particularly within the IT sector, WPO has also been linked with increased turnover intentions, which may create additional costs for organizations through loss of skilled employees and organizational knowledge31. However, despite growing evidence on the consequences of ostracism, empirical attention to its direct association with JP among Indian service-sector employees remains limited, especially across multiple service domains beyond a single industry. Therefore, examining this relationship is important for understanding how exclusionary workplace experiences translate into performance-related outcomes in service organizations. Accordingly, it is proposed that employees who experience higher levels of WPO are likely to report lower JP because exclusion disrupts their psychological focus, weakens access to social and informational resources, and interferes with self-regulated task execution.

H1: WPO is negatively related to employee JP.

Moderating influence of EE

EE is a positive work-related psychological state involving vigor, dedication, and absorption, through which employees invest physical, cognitive, and emotional energy in their work roles32,33,34. Engaged employees generally demonstrate higher commitment, productivity, customer orientation, and JP32,35,36. In the Indian service-sector context, meaningful work, empowerment, supportive culture, and leadership support have been found to strengthen engagement and improve work outcomes37,34. Thus, EE is expected to have a direct positive association with JP because engaged employees are more focused, persistent, and willing to invest discretionary effort in task completion.

From the perspective of SRT, engagement may also act as a potential boundary condition in the WPO–JP relationship. WPO creates psychological stress and diverts employees’ attention toward coping with exclusion, thereby weakening cognitive control, emotional regulation, and goal-directed behaviour5. Highly engaged employees may be better positioned to preserve task focus because their stronger dedication, absorption, and work involvement can support persistence even under adverse social conditions. In this sense, engagement may help employees maintain self-regulatory effort by keeping attention directed toward work goals rather than exclusion-related distress. Conversely, employees with low engagement may have fewer motivational resources to sustain performance when exposed to ostracism. Prior research has often examined engagement as a mediator through which ostracism affects service performance11,5. However, moderation is theoretically relevant because it examines whether the strength of the negative association between ostracism and performance differs across levels of engagement. Therefore, this study investigates engagement not only as a direct predictor of JP but also as a possible protective work-related state.

H2: EE is positively related to employee JP.

H3: EE significantly moderates the relationship between WPO and JP.

Methodology

Research design and sampling

This study adopted a cross-sectional correlational research design to examine the associations among WPO, EE, and JP. Data were collected from April 2022 to October 2022 using both online and in-person survey modes. The target population comprised full-time employees working in service-sector organizations in Madhya Pradesh, India. Four cities with relatively high concentrations of industrial and service-sector employment, i.e., Indore, Bhopal, Dewas, and Jabalpur, were treated as geographical strata. Respondents were drawn from IT, banking and insurance, BPO, and supply-chain/service roles.

Organizations were approached through professional networks, institutional contacts, and workplace gatekeepers. Employees were invited to participate voluntarily after being informed about the academic purpose of the study, anonymity of responses, confidentiality of data, and their right to withdraw at any stage. Questionnaires were distributed through printed forms during organizational visits and through online survey links shared with eligible employees. Within each city stratum, responses were screened for eligibility, and where excess responses were available, MS Excel random-number procedures were used during data screening and final case selection. Proportional allocation across strata was not strictly applied; however, efforts were made to obtain representation from major service-sector categories.

A total of 600 questionnaires were distributed/invited, out of which 528 responses were received. After the first screening for incomplete or ineligible responses, 497 responses were retained for detailed checking. Further exclusion of responses with missing values and unusable response patterns resulted in a final valid sample of 477 respondents, which was used for analysis and hypothesis testing. Thus, the final usable response rate was 79.50% (see Table 1).

Preliminary descriptive analysis was conducted to examine the response pattern and distributional properties of the measurement items. The mean values for EE items ranged from 2.862 to 3.562, indicating moderate levels of engagement among respondents. WPO items showed comparatively higher mean values, ranging from 3.499 to 3.656, suggesting that respondents reported noticeable experiences of exclusion in the workplace. JP items recorded lower mean values, ranging from 2.342 to 2.468. The standard deviation (STDEV) values across items indicated adequate variability in responses. Skewness values ranged from − 0.436 to 0.753, while excess kurtosis values ranged from − 1.246 to 0.232. These values fall within commonly accepted limits for assessing approximate normality in social science data, as skewness and kurtosis values within ± 2 are generally considered acceptable38,39. Therefore, the data were considered suitable for further multivariate analysis.

Research instruments

The study used previously validated measurement scales to assess WPO, EE, and JP. As the respondents belonged to Madhya Pradesh, where many participants are more familiar with Hindi, the original English questionnaire was translated into Hindi using the back-translation procedure recommended for cross-cultural survey research40,41. First, the questionnaire was translated from English to Hindi, and then an independent bilingual expert translated it back into English. The original and back-translated versions were compared to ensure conceptual equivalence rather than literal word-to-word similarity.

WPO was measured using the ten-item scale developed by Ferris et al.,18. Sample items included “Others left the area when I entered,” “My greetings have gone unanswered at work,” and “Others ignored me at work.” EE was measured using ten items derived from Shuck et al.,42, which capture employees’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioural investment in work. Sample items included “I feel a strong sense of belonging to my job,” “I am rarely distracted when I am working,” and “I do more than is expected of me.” The Shuck et al.,42 scale was selected because it captures cognitive, emotional, and behavioural dimensions of engagement, which aligned with the study’s focus. JP was assessed using nine items from Koopmans43. Sample items included “I managed to plan my work so that it was done on time” and “I started new tasks myself when my old ones were finished.” Self-reported JP was used because supervisor-rated or objective performance data were not uniformly accessible across participating organizations and sectors. Moreover, self-report measures are commonly used when studying individual work behaviour across heterogeneous occupational groups, especially where standardized organizational performance records are unavailable.

To ensure content clarity and contextual suitability, the translated questionnaire was reviewed by four subject experts in management and organizational behaviour. The experts examined the items for language clarity, conceptual relevance, and appropriateness for Indian service-sector employees. Minor wording changes were suggested and incorporated without altering the original meaning of the items. Thereafter, a pilot study was conducted with 50 respondents from the target population. The pilot participants did not suggest any major modifications, indicating that the items were understandable and contextually appropriate. All responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree.

Analysis and results

The study employed PLS-SEM using SmartPLS 4 because the objective was to explain variance in JP and assess the predictive relevance of the proposed model, rather than to compare competing covariance-based theoretical models. PLS-SEM is appropriate for prediction-oriented research, models involving interaction effects, and studies using survey data that may not strictly meet multivariate normality assumptions44,38. Although the model is relatively parsimonious, PLS-SEM was considered suitable because the study examines both direct and moderating relationships and focuses on the explanatory power of WPO and EE for JP. The analysis followed recommended procedures for evaluating measurement quality, structural paths, effect sizes, and predictive relevance (Hair et al., 2022;45).

Outer loading

Outer loadings were examined to assess indicator reliability, with values above 0.70 considered acceptable in PLS-SEM46. Indicators with lower loadings were not removed automatically; item deletion was considered only when it improved measurement quality without weakening the conceptual coverage of the construct. As shown in Table 1, most indicators met the recommended threshold. However, WPO10 and EE10 were excluded because their loadings were below 0.70 and their removal improved the reliability and validity estimates of the respective constructs47.

Reliability and validity measures

The reliability and convergent validity of the measurement model were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, rho_A, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.70 indicate satisfactory internal consistency, although it is considered a conservative measure48,49. CR and rho_A are regarded as more robust indicators, with threshold values exceeding 0.70 confirming adequate construct reliability46. Furthermore, convergent validity is established when AVE values exceed 0.50, indicating that constructs explain more than half of the variance of their indicators38. In this study, all constructs met these recommended thresholds, demonstrating strong internal consistency reliability and satisfactory convergent validity, thereby confirming the adequacy of the measurement model for further structural analysis (see Table 2). It is noteworthy that the reliability values for WPO were very high, with Cronbach’s alpha and CR exceeding 0.95. While such values confirm strong internal consistency, they may also indicate conceptual similarity or redundancy among items. However, WPO scale was retained because it is a widely validated measure developed by Ferris et al.,18, and the items represent closely related manifestations of being ignored or excluded at work. Therefore, the high reliability was interpreted as reflecting strong item consistency rather than measurement error, although this issue is acknowledged as a point for cautious interpretation.

Common method bias assessment

As all variables were collected from the same respondents at a single point in time, common method bias (CMB) was assessed using both procedural and statistical remedies. During data collection, respondents were assured of anonymity and confidentiality, informed that there were no right or wrong answers, and encouraged to respond honestly. The questionnaire also separated the measurement of key constructs to reduce evaluation apprehension and method-related response tendencies52. Statistically, Harman’s single-factor test was first applied. The first unrotated factor accounted for 38.2% of the total variance, which is below the commonly used 50% threshold, suggesting that a single factor did not dominate the data. In addition, CMB was assessed using the full collinearity approach based on inner variance inflation factor (VIF) values, as recommended in PLS-SEM53. A threshold of 3.3 is considered conservative for detecting potential CMB issues. The obtained VIF values for EE → JP (1.084), WPO → JP (1.006), and the interaction term (1.078) were all below this threshold. Thus, while CMB cannot be completely ruled out, the procedural and statistical checks suggest that it is unlikely to substantially bias the estimated relationships.

Preliminary analysis of sociodemographic differences in JP

A preliminary analysis was conducted to examine whether JP differed across selected sociodemographic categories. Independent samples t-test results showed no statistically significant difference in JP based on gender (t = 1.035, p > 0.05). Levene’s test confirmed that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was satisfied, indicating that the comparison between gender groups was appropriate. Further, one-way ANOVA results revealed no statistically significant differences in JP across age groups (F = 0.920, p > 0.05) or employment sectors (F = 0.714, p > 0.05). These findings suggest that JP was broadly comparable across gender, age, and sectoral categories in the present study. Therefore, these sociodemographic variables were not found to create meaningful group-based variation in JP.

Testing of hypothesis

The structural model was evaluated using path coefficients (β), STDEV, t-statistics, p-values, bootstrapped confidence intervals, effect sizes (f²), coefficient of determination (R²), and predictive relevance (Q²predict). Bootstrapping is commonly recommended in PLS-SEM for testing the statistical significance of structural relationships, particularly when assessing direct and interaction effects38,45. The model explained 24.10% of the variance in JP (R² = 0.241), indicating a moderate level of explanatory power for the endogenous construct. As presented in Table 5; Fig. 1, WPO showed a significant negative association with JP (β = -0.359, t = 5.479, p < 0.001), supporting H1. The confidence interval for this path did not include zero (LLCI = -0.486, ULCI = -0.234), further confirming the statistical significance of the relationship. The f² value of 0.169 indicates a moderate effect of WPO on JP. Similarly, EE showed a significant positive association with JP (β = 0.291, t = 7.025, p < 0.001), supporting H2. The confidence interval was fully above zero (LLCI = 0.210, ULCI = 0.373), and the f² value of 0.103 suggests a small-to-moderate effect.

Conditional effects

The conditional effects of EE on the relationship between WPO and JP were examined using the pick-a-point approach, which estimates the association between the predictor and outcome at different levels of the moderator, namely low, mean, and high levels of EE. As shown in Table 5; Fig. 2, the association between WPO and JP remained negative and statistically significant across all three levels of EE, with effects of β = -0.409 at low engagement, β = -0.359 at mean engagement, and β = -0.309 at high engagement. Although the negative coefficient appears slightly smaller at higher levels of EE, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously because the interaction term reported in Table 4 was not statistically significant. Therefore, the conditional effects do not provide sufficient evidence that EE meaningfully changes the strength of the relationship between WPO and JP. Rather, the results indicate that WPO remains negatively associated with JP regardless of whether employees report low, average, or high levels of engagement. This finding is consistent with the rejection of H3 and suggests the absence of a statistically supported moderating influence.

Model fit

The model fit was assessed using standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), dULS, dG, and normed fit index (NFI) values for both the saturated and estimated models. The SRMR value was 0.055 for both models, which is below the recommended cut-off of 0.08, indicating an acceptable level of approximate model fit and suggesting that the difference between the observed and model-implied correlations was within an acceptable range55,56. The dULS values of 1.133 and 1.137 and dG values of 2.168 and 2.170 indicate similar discrepancy levels across the saturated and estimated models, suggesting consistency between the measurement and structural specifications57. However, the NFI value was 0.745, which is below the commonly suggested threshold of 0.9058. This lower NFI may be due to the parsimonious nature of the model, limited number of endogenous constructs, and the sensitivity of NFI to model specification in PLS-SEM. Therefore, although SRMR supports acceptable model fit, the lower NFI is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Discussion

This study examined the associations among WPO, EE, and JP among employees working in selected service-sector organizations. Guided by SRT, the study sought to understand whether WPO is negatively associated with JP and whether EE functions as a direct performance-enhancing factor as well as a possible buffering condition. The findings provide support for the direct associations but do not support the hypothesized moderating role of EE.

The findings reveal a significant negative association between WPO and JP (β = -0.359, p < 0.001), thereby supporting H1. This result is consistent with prior literature suggesting that WPO undermines employees’ psychological functioning and behavioural outcomes. Empirical evidence indicates that ostracism is associated with emotional strain, cognitive distraction, adverse work behaviours, and reduced performance-related outcomes59. From the perspective of SRT, such exclusion may disrupt employees’ ability to monitor and regulate their behaviour effectively, as psychological resources are diverted toward interpreting and coping with social rejection rather than task accomplishment. Consequently, employees experiencing ostracism may find it more difficult to sustain attention, motivation, and goal-directed behaviour, which can be reflected in lower JP.

The study further confirms a significant positive association between EE and JP (β = 0.291, p < 0.001), supporting H2. This finding reinforces the well-established view that engaged employees demonstrate higher levels of vigor, dedication, and cognitive involvement, which are likely to support better performance outcomes60. Within the SRT framework, engagement can be understood as a motivational and attentional work state that supports self-monitoring, goal commitment, and persistence in task execution. Thus, the finding suggests that employees who are more engaged with their work are more likely to report higher JP. However, given the cross-sectional and self-reported nature of the data, this relationship should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of causality.

Contrary to expectations, the moderating effect of EE on the WPO–JP relationship was statistically insignificant (β = 0.050, p = 0.395), leading to the rejection of H3. Although the conditional-effects analysis showed that the negative association between WPO and JP was slightly weaker at higher levels of engagement, this pattern should not be overinterpreted because the interaction term was not significant. Therefore, the more appropriate interpretation is that EE did not significantly buffer the negative association between WPO and JP in this sample. This finding suggests that while engagement is positively associated with performance, it may not necessarily function as a protective condition when employees experience social exclusion at work.

One possible explanation for this result is that WPO directly threatens employees’ sense of belonging, social identity, and access to informal workplace support. These experiences may create emotional and cognitive demands that are not easily offset by a general state of work engagement. From an SRT perspective, ostracism may interfere with core self-regulatory processes such as emotional control, attention regulation, and goal maintenance. When employees devote regulatory resources to managing exclusion-related distress, fewer resources may remain available for productive task execution. This aligns with research indicating that ostracism can interfere with self-control mechanisms and increase maladaptive behavioural responses19. Thus, even employees who are generally engaged may still experience performance-related difficulties when exposed to exclusionary workplace experiences.

Another important finding concerns the weak negative association between WPO and EE observed in the correlation matrix. Although prior literature often suggests that WPO can reduce engagement61, the present sample showed only a limited relationship between these constructs. This may indicate that some employees remain psychologically invested in their work despite experiencing exclusion, possibly because of professional commitment, career concerns, role expectations, or limited employment alternatives. It may also suggest that ostracism and engagement are not simple opposites: an employee may feel ignored by colleagues yet continue to remain absorbed in work tasks. This weak association may partly explain why EE did not emerge as a significant moderator in the WPO-JP relationship.

These findings have relevance for contemporary service-sector workplaces, including hybrid and digitally mediated work arrangements where reduced interpersonal contact may increase the risk of perceived exclusion. However, the implications should be framed cautiously because the study did not directly test inclusion interventions, leadership practices, or psychological safety mechanisms. The results indicate that organizations should not assume that engagement-enhancing practices alone will reduce the performance-related implications of WPO. Instead, engagement initiatives may need to be complemented by direct efforts to identify and reduce exclusionary behaviours, strengthen communication, and promote inclusive interpersonal norms. Hence, the study contributes to organizational behaviour literature by showing that EE is positively associated with JP but did not significantly alter the negative association between WPO and JP in the present study.

Implications of the study

Theoretical implications

This study contributes to WPO literature by examining EE as a possible boundary condition in the relationship between WPO and JP. While prior research often positions EE as a potential buffering resource, the present findings show that engagement did not significantly moderate the negative association between WPO and JP in the study. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence that engagement cannot serve a restorative function; rather, they suggest that engagement may not always operate as a buffering resource under conditions of WPO. Existing literature also indicates that WPO undermines psychological safety and depletes emotional and cognitive resources, thereby impairing work outcomes62. Thus, the study refines theoretical understanding by distinguishing between performance-enhancing work states and resources that may directly restore employees’ sense of inclusion, belongingness, and emotional recovery. It calls for future theorization to incorporate stronger psychological constructs such as belongingness restoration, emotional recovery, and inclusion-based frameworks.

Research implications

The findings open several avenues for future research. First, given the non-significant moderating role of EE, future studies should explore alternative buffering mechanisms such as psychological resilience, emotional intelligence, and perceived inclusion climate. Prior evidence suggests that WPO disrupts basic psychological needs and motivation, leading to negative behavioral outcomes63. Second, future research should adopt multi-wave or longitudinal designs to capture the dynamic nature of ostracism, engagement, and performance over time. Third, future studies may use supervisor-rated or objective performance indicators to reduce reliance on self-reported performance. Fourth, future research may also examine mediation models involving EE, as prior studies have often conceptualized engagement as a mechanism through which ostracism affects work outcomes. Finally, cross-cultural investigations are necessary, as cultural contexts influence how employees perceive and respond to social exclusion.

Practical implications

The study highlights that organizations should be cautious in assuming that EE initiatives alone can reduce the adverse performance-related implications of WPO. Instead, organizations may need to pay direct attention to exclusionary behaviours and workplace interaction patterns. Research shows that WPO is associated with reduced psychological well-being, engagement, and organizational commitment, which may ultimately affect performance64. Therefore, organizations may consider inclusion-oriented policies, supportive communication practices, and mechanisms for identifying exclusionary behaviours at an early stage. Leadership development programs focusing on empathy, inclusiveness, and psychological safety may also be useful, although the present study did not directly test these interventions. Additionally, structured team-building initiatives and open communication platforms may help strengthen interpersonal connections. These suggestions should be interpreted as practical extensions of the findings rather than direct causal evidence from the present study.

Limitations of the study

The study is subject to certain limitations. First, its cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretation, particularly for the tested moderation relationship. Second, reliance on self-reported data, including self-rated job performance, may introduce social desirability, recall bias, and common method bias, although procedural and statistical checks were applied. Third, the sample was limited to selected service sectors and cities of Madhya Pradesh, India, which may restrict generalizability to other regions and industries. Fourth, although a good usable response rate was obtained, potential non-response bias cannot be fully ruled out. Finally, the NFI value was below the commonly recommended threshold, which is acknowledged as a model-fit limitation.

Conclusion

Workplace exclusion may appear silent, but its consequences for employee performance are clearly visible. This study examined the relationship between WPO, EE, and JP among service-sector employees in India. The findings show that WPO is negatively associated with JP, indicating that employees who experience exclusion at work may find it difficult to maintain focus, motivation, and task-related effectiveness. The study also confirms that EE is positively associated with JP, suggesting that engaged employees are more likely to invest energy, dedication, and attention in their work roles. However, EE did not significantly moderate the relationship between WPO and JP. This indicates that, in the present study, engagement may enhance performance directly but may not necessarily protect employees from the adverse performance-related implications of WPO. The study contributes to organizational behaviour literature by clarifying the limits of engagement as a buffering resource and highlighting the need to directly address exclusionary workplace experiences. Thus, the findings suggest that organizations should promote engagement while also developing inclusive work practices that reduce ostracism and strengthen interpersonal support.
