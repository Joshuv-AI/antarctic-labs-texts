Andes hantavirus – A primer
Authors: Nico Joel Halwe, Alina Tscherne, Florian Krammer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Andes hantavirus outbreak on a cruise ship unberthing from Ushuaia, Argentina, towards the Canary Islands and Europe has gained remarkable media attention in spring of 2026. Here, we want to spotlight the most important facts about Andes hantavirus and hantaviruses in general. We want to emphasize and inform about the worldwide circulation of diverse hantaviruses and the necessity to strengthen efforts in virus characterization and the development of effective countermeasures against hantaviruses in general.

Introduction to Andes hantavirus

Andes hantavirus (ANDV, species name Orthohantavirus andesense) belongs to the genus Orthohantavirus in the family of Hantaviridae, order Bunyavirales. ANDV is an enveloped virus with a negative-sense, single-stranded and triple-segmented RNA genome, assigned as large (L), medium (M), and small (S) segments1 (Fig. 1). The three segments encode for an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, L segment), the nucleocapsid protein (N, S segment), a non-structural protein (NSs, S segment), and a glycoprotein precursor (M segment), which subsequently undergoes cleavage at the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) into two mature glycoprotein subunits named Gn and Gc1. These glycoprotein subunits form heterodimeric tetramers that directly interact with their host cell receptor, for ANDV protocadherin-1 (PCDH1), for subsequent cellular entry and infection2. Just recently, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) of these GnGc tetramer structures revealed a prefusion conformation that allows pH-dependent activation for subsequent fusion3. ANDV circulates mainly in the long-tailed pygmy rice rat (Oligoryzomys longicaudatus), which is endemic in areas along the Andes mountains of Chilean and Argentinian Patagonia4. Spillover infections to humans from rodent hosts often occur after inhalation of dust aerosols containing elements of rodent feces, urine, saliva, or similar material1. Direct interaction with rodents (e.g., scratches, bites) can also lead to human infections. Thus, risk factors for exposure to hantaviruses include activities in rodent habitat, for example, forestry or agriculture, as well as cleaning activities and direct contact with rodent excreta, especially without appropriate personal protective equipment (e.g. gloves, face masks) (Fig. 1)5. ANDV infections are often symptomatic with a case-fatality ratio estimated at 21%–36%, depending on the geographic region, with the highest incidences in Patagonia6. However, the rate of asymptomatic infections is not completely clear and asymptomatic infections are likely also common. Symptom onset happens between week one and week six post infection; however, incubation periods of up to eight weeks have been reported in isolated cases. Infected individuals can experience fever, headache, body aches, respiratory symptoms, and general malaise. ANDV human-to-human transmission and the cruise ship outbreak

ANDV is the only hantavirus with confirmed, but limited, human-to-human (H2H) transmission so far10–12. Importantly, H2H-transmission of ANDV usually – but not always - requires prolonged and close contact. In one prospective study, ANDV-infected individuals demonstrated viral shedding until day 22 after the onset of symptoms7. The same study also observed substantially higher viral shedding in bodily fluids in the acute phase of infection up to 16 days post symptom onset, indicating a higher transmission potential during the prodromal phase of the infection7. Other speculations also suggest that ANDV-infected individuals are infectious as long as they display clinical symptoms. Household contacts of infected individuals experience the highest risk of getting infected. Moreover, sexual contact has been hypothesized as a further potential transmission route, especially as it was shown that ANDV can persist in human semen for over 71 months13. In late 2018/early 2019, a likely single rodent-to-human spillover of ANDV caused an outbreak in Epuyen, Argentina, involving 34 confirmed cases with 11 deaths10. Epidemiological outbreak investigations later confirmed H2H-transmission, mainly driven by “super-spreader”-individuals showing extraordinarily high viral loads during sampling10. The most recent example with highly suspected H2H-transmission is represented by the ANDV-outbreak on the cruise ship “MV Hondius”, which was on its way from Ushuaia, Argentina, to European waters14,15. As of June 4th 2026, 13 cases have been identified, three of which died due to the infection14. A recent report informed about a rapid progression of the illness, atypical pneumonia, and respiratory failure as predominant symptoms associated with the fatalities16. Extensive contact tracing measures were undertaken to identify individuals who might be affected or have had contact with infected individuals or those who left the ship before the outbreak was noticed. Considering an incubation period of up to 42 days (and potentially even longer) before symptom onset, these interventions needed to continue for an extended period of time.

Importance of rapid viral genome sequencing during outbreaks

The availability of viral sequences is of utmost importance in such an outbreak scenario to detect potential mutations that may indicate ongoing viral evolution and human adaptation. The University of Geneva was the first to publish the full genome of ANDV from one of the infected individuals17 already six days after the cruise ship outbreak was reported to the WHO, and further full genome sequences soon followed18. Preliminary reports have shown that the first two viral genome sequences of ANDV-infected individuals from this cruise ship were nearly identical, with only two synonymous nucleotide substitutions in the L-segment in affected cases across the outbreak cluster19. In addition, the sequences generated from the cruise ship outbreak so far were highly similar (~98% identity) to the 2018/2019 outbreak virus strain. These results do not indicate an extensive viral evolution of ANDV, nor do they suggest enhanced adaptation to or transmissibility in humans, and therefore do not suggest an upcoming pandemic scenario as experienced with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)15.

Hantavirus reservoirs around the world

It is noteworthy to mention that not only ANDV, but also several other species of hantaviruses around the globe are known to cause zoonotic infections in humans20. For example, several lineages of Puumala hantavirus (PUUV) are found in multiple countries in Europe21, Tula hantavirus (TULV) and Dobrava-Belgrade hantavirus (DOBV)22 can also be found in Europe, Hantaan hantavirus (HTNV)23 can be found in Asia, Seoul hantavirus (SEOV) circulates globally in the rat population, and Sin Nombre virus (SNV) is found in North America24. Similar to ANDV, all these diverse human pathogenic hantaviruses have their natural reservoir in rodent hosts that become persistently infected after exposure and initial infection. Hantaviruses have also been found in other reservoir species, including bats. Hantaviruses of Eurasian origin, assigned as Old World hantaviruses (OWHVs), persist in very common and widely distributed reservoir hosts, such as brown rats (SEOV), bank voles (PUUV), or yellow-necked field mice (DOBV, HTNV)22. OWHVs in Europe (e.g., PUUV) or Asia (e.g., HTNV, SEOV) lead to a usually less lethal hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS)25 as compared to the HCPS caused by New World hantaviruses (NWHVs), e.g., ANDV and SNV in the Americas. Nevertheless, reports have informed about over 10.000 annual cases of HFRS in Europe alone, most of them linked to an infection with PUUV22. Moreover, case-fatality ratios of ~0.1 – 0.5% for PUUV, ~5% for HTNV, and ~10% for DOBV have been reported1. Thus, considerable numbers of fatalities are attributable to hantaviruses other than ANDV6,22; yet public and media attention to these facts remains limited. Importantly, ANDV is the only known hantavirus capable of H2H transmission.

The urgent need to intensify hantavirus research

Numerous knowledge gaps in hantavirus research are yet to be filled and urgently need our attention to be prepared for potentially larger future outbreaks and to manage occurring zoonotic infections. First, although thousands of cases of HFRS or HCPS are recorded annually, neither licensed vaccines (with the exception of Asian hantaviruses) nor therapeutics are available to prevent or treat a hantavirus infection. Second, there is currently no animal model available that adequately mimics human HFRS disease. Rodent models typically develop an asymptomatic persistent infection after experimental inoculation with SNV, SEOV, HTNV, PUUV, or DOBV26. The only exception is ANDV, for which some strains induce HCPS-like disease in Syrian hamsters26. Third, the primary mechanism of ANDV transmission is still inconclusive. While inhalation of aerosolized rodent excreta is assumed to be one of the main modes of rodent-to-human transmission, the human-to-human transmission mechanism of ANDV is poorly understood, as it often, but not always, requires prolonged and close contact10. Last, there is currently no reverse genetics system available to generate infectious clones in vitro, since hantaviruses are very difficult to cultivate in cell culture in the laboratory. Development of a reverse genetics system would allow genetic modifications of hantaviruses to study specific viral protein functions associated with viral entry mechanisms, replication or transmission. Furthermore, an appropriate in vitro cultivation system would allow the generation of live-attenuated vaccine candidates against hantaviruses.

Conclusion and Outlook

The ANDV outbreak on the cruise ship with 13 cases so far has gained massive attention around the globe. In contrast, the worldwide circulation of diverse hantaviruses, such as SNV, HTNV, PUUV, and others, causing thousands of zoonotic spillover infections in humans per year, is usually overlooked. The recent ANDV outbreak shows that significant investments are needed to better understand hantavirus infection, pathogenicity, transmission, and molecular biology – including but not limited to ANDV. Furthermore, the development of vaccines and therapeutics to counteract hantavirus infections will be key to responding to future outbreaks and managing existing zoonotic infections. Unfortunately, the lack of adequate funding to address hantavirus-related questions remains a major impediment. Strengthening these efforts can limit the amount of future zoonotic spillover infections to humans and thereby reduce the risk of viral adaptation to humans - maybe stopping a potential pandemic before it even starts.
