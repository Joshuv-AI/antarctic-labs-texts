Characters of symmetric groups: sharp bounds on virtual degrees and the Witten zeta function
Lucas Teyssier, Paul Thévenin

Abstract
Resumo Ni pruvas precizajn barojn je la virtualaj gradoj enkondukitaj de Larsen kaj Shalev. Tio induktas plibonigitajn barojn je karakteroj de la simetria grupo. Ni sekve pliakrigas barojn de Liebeck kaj Shalev pri la zeto-funkcio de Witten. Nia ĉefa aplikaĵo estas karakterizo de la senfikspunktaj konjugklasoj kies asociata hazarda promenado miksiĝas post 2 paŝoj.



1
Introduction

1.1
Context

Representation theory has been used to solve various problems in different areas of mathematics. A striking example is the pioneering work of Diaconis and Shahshahani on a random process called the random transposition shuffle
[
12
]
. They used character estimates to prove a sharp phase transition – now known as the cutoff phenomenon – concerning the minimum number of random transpositions whose product yields an almost uniform random permutation. This was the starting point of the field of mixing times, whose goal is to understand how long it takes for a random process to approach stationarity.

Other techniques to study mixing times were developed in the following years, notably by Aldous and Diaconis
[
3
,
2
]
, and mixing properties of emblematic card shufflings were precisely understood, also for small decks of cards
[
4
]
. We refer to
[
32
]
for an introduction to mixing times and to
[
10
]
for the mathematics of card shuffling.

A natural way to generalize Diaconis and Shahshahani’s random transposition shuffle on the symmetric group
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is to replace the conjugacy class of transpositions – from which we pick uniform elements – by another conjugacy class.
In specific cases, a similar cutoff phenomenon was proved; for
k
-cycles (where
k>n/2
) in
[
39
]
; for
k
-cycles (where
k
is fixed) in
[
5
]
; for
k
-cycles (where
k=o(n)
) in
[
24
]
; and for all conjugacy classes whose support has size
o(n)
in
[
6
]
(the support of a permutation is the set of its non-fixed points). Most of these results rely on representation theory. However, interestingly, the proof of
[
5
]
follows the cycle structure of the permutation obtained after several multiplications via probabilistic arguments, and that of
[
6
]
, which was the first cutoff result for a large class of conjugacy classes, relies on a curvature argument.
In the companion paper
[
42
]
, we extend the cutoff result of
[
6
]
to all conjugacy classes with a number of fixed points of order
n
, also with respect to the
L^{2}
distance.

There has also been significant effort devoted to understanding conjugacy classes with full support (i.e. without fixed points). For the cycle types
[r^{n/r}]
Lulov
[
38
]
proved using the character bounds of Fomin and Lulov
[
17
]
that the mixing time is
3
when
r=2
and
2
when
r\geq 3
. Lulov and Pak later conjectured in
[
39
, Conjecture 4.1]
that the mixing time is at most 3 for all fixed-point free conjugacy classes (i.e., without fixed points), and Larsen and Shalev
[
29
]
proved that conjecture.

Motivated by the study of mixing times of Markov chains (among various other problems), uniform bounds on characters have been established and sharpened over the past few decades.
Roichman
[
43
]
proved bounds that led to the correct order of the mixing time of conjugacy class walks on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that have a number of fixed points of order
n
. This result was later strengthened by Müller and Schlage-Puchta
[
41
]
, who obtained results that are uniform over all conjugacy classes.

On the other hand, sharp bounds on characters of permutations with few fixed points were proved in the landmark paper
[
29
]
of Larsen and Shalev. They proved several conjectures, among which the Lulov–Pak conjecture as discussed earlier. The bounds that we obtain here build on this paper; see Section
1.2
for a more detailed description of their character bounds.
In the companion paper
[
42
]
we prove an optimal uniform character bound as well as the sharpest possible
exponential
character bounds for permutations with enough fixed points.

An important tool to estimate characters of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (see e.g. Theorem 3.10 in
[
40
]
), which is a key element in the proofs of
[
43
,
41
,
29
,
42
]
. On the other hand, other techniques have proved to be more powerful for certain diagrams. Féray and Śniady
[
14
]
found uniform bounds on characters that are especially good for diagrams that do not have a long first row or column, using a reformulation of Stanley’s conjectured character formula. More precisely,
[
15
]
proves Conjecture 3 from
[
44
]
, while the reformulation of Stanley’s formula appears as Theorem 2 in
[
14
]
.

In a different direction, Lifshitz and Marmor
[
37
]
established character bounds using hypercontractivity on symmetric groups (see also
[
16
,
26
]
).
They obtained uniform bounds on characters for permutations with at most a given number of cycles (instead of considering the full cycle structure, as done in the previously mentioned works). This allows them to consider permutations having up to
n/(\ln n)^{O(1)}
cycles, thus covering new cases.

Bounds on characters play a crucial role in the broader context of finite groups, and were notably used to prove a conjecture due to Ore
[
33
]
. General bounds on characters of finite classical groups were recently obtained by Guralnick, Larsen and Tiep
[
22
,
23
]
, and uniform bounds on characters for all finite quasisimple groups of Lie type were established by Larsen and Tiep
[
31
]
.

A canonical tool used to convert character bounds for a group
G
into practical estimates for applications is the Witten zeta function. It is defined as
\zeta(s)=\sum_{\alpha}(d_{\alpha})^{-s}
for
s>0
, where the sum is taken over all irreducible representations
\alpha
of
G
, and
d_{\alpha}
is the dimension of
\alpha
. Such a zeta function initially appeared in the computation of volumes of moduli spaces in Witten’s work
[
45
]
(see Equation (4.72) there). Liebeck and Shalev proved bounds for the symmetric group
[
34
]
and for Fuchsian groups
[
35
]
, which were useful for a variety of applications, including mixing times and covering numbers
[
29
,
28
,
27
]
. We refer to
[
29
]
,
[
31
]
, and the survey article
[
36
]
for further applications of character bounds.

Throughout the paper,
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denotes the symmetric group of degree
n
, and
\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
the set of its irreducible representations. For a permutation
\sigma
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
f_{i}(\sigma)
denotes the number of cycles of length
i
in
\sigma
. By extension, for a conjugacy class
{\mathcal{C}}
, we denote by
f_{i}({\mathcal{C}})
the number of cycles of length
i
in any permutation
\sigma\in{\mathcal{C}}
. In addition, we will use the notation
f=\max(f_{1},1)
. For convenience, we will use
\lambda
to simultaneously denote a representation, the associated integer partition, and the associated Young diagram (see Section
2.1
for definitions); and we may write
\lambda\vdash n
for
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
. If
E
is a finite set, we denote by
\Unif_{E}
the uniform probability measure on
E
.

1.2
The Larsen–Shalev character bounds

We describe here the results of Larsen and Shalev
[
29
]
, whose improvement is the main purpose of the paper.
We assume familiarity with the representation theory of symmetric groups and will follow the notation from the textbook
[
40
]
: we denote by
d_{\lambda}
the dimension of a representation
\lambda
,
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)
the character of
\lambda
evaluated at a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\frac{\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)}{d_{\lambda}}
the associated normalized character.

Larsen and Shalev obtained character bounds by introducing and studying new objects. One of them is called the virtual degree
D(\lambda)
of a representation
\lambda
. It is defined as

D(\lambda):=\frac{(n-1)!}{\prod_{i}a_{i}!b_{i}!},

(1.1)

where
a_{i}=\lambda_{i}-i
and
b_{i}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i
. Here
\lambda_{i}
(resp.
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
) denotes the size of the
i
-th row (resp.
i
-th column) of the Young diagram associated to
\lambda
(see Section
2.1
for precise definitions).
The virtual degree is a convenient substitute for the dimension
d_{\lambda}
, which allows for easier computations. See Section
2.3
for more details.

Another object introduced in
[
29
]
is the orbit growth exponent
E(\sigma)
. Let
n\geq 1
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Denote by
f_{i}(\sigma)
the number of cycles of length
i\geq 1
of
\sigma
, and for
k\geq 1
, set
\Sigma_{k}=\Sigma_{k}(\sigma):=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}if_{i}(\sigma)
. We define the orbit growth sequence
(e_{i})_{i\geq 1}
by
n^{e_{1}+\ldots+e_{i}}=\max\left(\Sigma_{i},1\right)
for all
i\geq 1
, and set

E(\sigma)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{e_{i}}{i}.

(1.2)

A key intermediate result that Larsen and Shalev proved is the following, which we will refer to as a theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

For any
n\geq 1
,
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
, we have the following character bound:

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq D(\lambda)^{E(\sigma)}.

(1.3)

This is proved via an elegant induction in the proof of their main theorem (for reference, the induction hypothesis is
[
29
, Eq. (17)]
), where the orbit growth exponent
E(\sigma)
naturally appears. They also showed that
D(\lambda)
cannot be much larger than
d_{\lambda}
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
29
]
, Theorem 2.2)
.

As
|\lambda|\to\infty
, we have

D(\lambda)\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+o(1)}.

(1.4)

Combining Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
and the fact that
E(\sigma)\leq 1
for all
\sigma
, they obtain the following character bound.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
29
]
, Theorem 1.1 (a))
.

As
n\to\infty
, we have uniformly over all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
,

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)+o(1)}.

(1.5)

The orbit growth exponent
E(\sigma)
defined in (
1.2
) looks complicated at first glance, but it is actually simple to compute and appears to lead to the best possible character bounds in different regimes such as in (
1.6
) below.

Let us give a few important examples of values of
E(\sigma)
. We always denote by
f_{1}
the number of fixed points of a permutation
\sigma
and set
f=\max(f_{1},1)
.

Example 1.4
.

(a)

Assume that
\sigma\sim[2^{n/2}]
, then
e_{2}=1
and
e_{i}=0
for
i\neq 2
, so
E(\sigma)=1/2
.

(b)

Assume that
\sigma
has no fixed point, then
e_{2}+e_{3}+\ldots=1
so
E(\sigma)\leq 1/2
.

(c)

More generally, for any
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
we have
e_{1}=\frac{\ln f}{\ln n}
, so
E(\sigma)\leq e_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(1-e_{1})=\frac{1+e_{1}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\ln f}{2\ln n}
, that is,
E(\sigma)-1\leq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}

(d)

Assume that
\sigma\sim[k,1^{n-k}]
, then
E(\sigma)=1/n
if
k=n
(i.e.
\sigma
is an
n
-cycle) and if
f_{1}\geq 1
,
E(\sigma)=e_{1}+\frac{e_{k}}{k}=e_{1}+\frac{1-e_{1}}{k}=\frac{\ln f}{\ln n}+\frac{\ln(n/f)}{k\ln n}
.

Combining Theorem
1.3
with Example
1.4
(b), Larsen and Shalev obtained the following bound as
n\to\infty
, uniform over fixed-point free permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and irreducible representations
\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
,

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{1/2+o(1)}.

(1.6)

This allowed them to prove the above-mentioned conjecture of Lulov and Pak, and even extend it (in Theorem 1.8) to the mixing time of conjugacy classes that have a small number of fixed points.

1.3
Main results

We present here our main contributions: the main result, Theorem
1.5
, which improves on Theorem
1.2
, provides uniform bounds on virtual degrees and allows us to obtain refined character bounds (Theorem
1.6
). Proposition
1.9
provides bounds on the Witten zeta function, which we apply together with Theorem
1.6
to characterize fixed-point free conjugacy classes that mix in 2 steps (Theorem
1.10
). We also deduce from our bounds that conjugacy classes with
o(\sqrt{n})
cycles mix fast (Theorem
1.11
), and finally that there is a cutoff whenever the conjugacy class has a large support and no short cycles (Theorem
1.13
).

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists a universal constant
C
such that, for every
n\geq 2
and any integer partition
\lambda\vdash n
, we have

D(\lambda)\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{C}{\ln n}}.

(1.7)

We will show in Section
4.3
that Theorem
1.5
is sharp for different shapes of diagrams.

Plugging this into Theorem
1.1
, we get the following character bound.

Theorem 1.6
.

For every
n\geq 2
, any permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and any integer partition
\lambda\vdash n
, we have

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln n}\right)E(\sigma)},

(1.8)

where
C
is the universal constant from Theorem
1.5
.

In Proposition
5.1
we prove that
E(\sigma)\leq\frac{\ln\left(\max(\cyc(\sigma),2)\right)}{\ln n}
, where
\cyc(\sigma)
is the number of cycles of
\sigma
. This allows us to deduce the following character bound, which improves on
[
29
, Theorem 1.4]
and
[
37
, Theorem 1.7]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, we have

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{\frac{\ln\cyc(\sigma)}{\ln n}+O\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)}.

(1.9)

We now define the Witten zeta function. For
n\geq 1
,
A\subset\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
and
s\geq 0
,

\zeta_{n}(A,s):=\sum_{\lambda\in A}\frac{1}{\left(d_{\lambda}\right)^{s}}.

(1.10)

Note that compared to the usual definition where the sum is over all irreducible representations, we add the subset of representations
A
on which we sum as a parameter of the function
\zeta_{n}
.

In
[
34
]
, Liebeck and Shalev proved that
\zeta_{n}(\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**},s)=O(n^{-s})
, if
s>0
is fixed and
n\to\infty
, where
\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}=\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\backslash\left\{[n],[1^{n}]\right\}=\left\{\lambda\vdash n\mid d_{\lambda}>1\right\}
. This was later improved by Hough
[
24
]
who showed that, for some large constant
c_{0}>0
, we have
\zeta_{n}(\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**},c_{0}/\ln n)=O(1)
.
We prove the following asymptotics for the Witten zeta function. Let
p(n)
be the number of integer partitions of the integer
n
, with the convention that
p(0)=1
. Denote the associated generating function by
\Phi
, that is
\Phi(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)x^{n}
for
x\in[0,1)
.

Theorem 1.8
.

For any
c>0
, we have

\zeta_{n}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}},c/\ln n):=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}}d_{\lambda}^{-c/\ln n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}2\Phi(e^{-c}).

(1.11)

We deduce the following convergence result, which could also be obtained as a consequence of Hough’s estimates.

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
(s_{n})
be a sequence of positive real numbers. We have

\zeta_{n}(\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**},s_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ if and only if }\hskip 10.00002pts_{n}\ln n\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty.

(1.12)

In Section
6
, we will also prove useful variants of Proposition
1.9
, for other subsets
A\subset\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, and when
s_{n}
is of order
\frac{1}{\ln n}
.

As an application of our bounds on characters and on the Witten zeta function, we are able to characterize which fixed-point free conjugacy classes mix in 2 steps. We recall that the convolution product of two measures
\mu,\nu
on a group
G
is defined by
\mu\ast\nu(g)=\sum_{x\in G}\mu(x)\nu(gx^{-1})
for
g\in G
. As such, if
S\subset G
, then
\Unif_{S}^{*t}
is the distribution of the simple random walk (started at the neutral element) on the Cayley graph
\Cay(G,S)
after
t
steps. Finally, the total variation distance between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on a finite set
E
is given by

\dtv(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in E}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|.

(1.13)

Theorem 1.10
.

For each
n\geq 2
let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, which is fixed-point free (i.e.
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=0
). Recall that
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})
denotes the number of transpositions of a permutation
\sigma\in{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
. Then we have

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ if and only if }\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptf_{2}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=o(n).

(1.14)

We also prove that conjugacy classes with few cycles mix fast.

Theorem 1.11
.

For each
n\geq 2
let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Assume that
\cyc(\sigma)=o\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
for
\sigma\in{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
. Then

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(1.15)

We believe that the only parts of the cycle structure that affect mixing times are the number of fixed points and the number of transpositions. We make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.12
.

For each
n\geq 2
let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Then

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ if and only if }\hskip 10.00002pt[f_{1}=o(\sqrt{n})\text{ and }f_{2}=o(n)],

(1.16)

where
f_{1}=f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})
and
f_{2}=f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})
.

As a third application, we finally show that conjugacy classes with a large support and no short cycles exhibit cutoff. If
{\mathcal{C}}
is a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
t\geq 0
, we denote by
\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{C}},t)

the set
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\mathfrak{A}_{n}
if
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
t
is odd, and
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
otherwise.

Theorem 1.13
.

For each
n\geq 2
let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}\neq\left\{\Id\right\}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Write
f=\max(f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}),1)
.
Assume that
f=o(n)
and
\min\left\{i\geq 2{\;:\;}f_{i}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\geq 1\right\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}(1-\varepsilon)\right\rfloor\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(\left\lceil\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}(1+\varepsilon)\right\rceil\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(1.17)

where
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t):=\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*t},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)},t)}\right)
for
t\geq 0
.

Remark 1.1
.

Our improved bounds are useful in several ways. They lead to sharp results for permutations with few fixed points or few cycles, as we saw in Theorem
1.10
and Theorem
1.11
. They also extend the applicability of the Larsen–Shalev character bounds (recalled as Theorem
1.3
) from
f=n^{1-\varepsilon(n)}
(where
\varepsilon(n)
is the non-explicit
o(1)
from Theorem
1.3
) to
f=o(n)
. In the regime
f=o(n)
, recalling Example
1.4
(c), this leads to the uniform bound

\frac{|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)|}{d_{\lambda}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}}.

(1.18)

The uniform constant
1/2
above cannot be improved, as it is sharp for fixed-point free involutions. In the follow-up paper
[
42
]
, we prove that (
1.18
) holds uniformly over all permutations and irreducible representations.

As a last application, we prove mixing results on products with fixed-point free involutions. The mixing of such products is strongly motivated by the theory of
random maps; the connection is detailed in Section
9
.

Theorem 1.14
.

For each
n\geq 2
even, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}
be a fixed-point free conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and denote by
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
the conjugacy class of fixed-point free involutions (that is, of cycle type
[2^{n/2}]
). Then

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}))}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ if and only if }\hskip 10.00002ptf_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=o(n),

(1.19)

where
{\mathfrak{E}}(({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}))
is the coset of
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
with sign
\sgn({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})\sgn({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})
.

This extends the result of
[
8
, Theorem 2.2]
. Our theorem implies in particular the convergence in distribution of the number of vertices in a certain model of random maps (see Theorem
9.2
), improving
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
and recovering the result of
[
7
, Theorem 3]
under a stronger assumption. Note that Theorem
1.14
is strictly stronger than Theorem
9.2
, and therefore is not contained in
[
7
, Theorem 3]
.

Structure of the paper

, we introduce

, and analyze

We prove Theorem

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Young diagrams, representations and integer partitions

We say that
\lambda:=[\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}]
is a
partition
of the integer
n
if
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
,
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
for all
i\leq k-1
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=n
. We write
\lambda\vdash n
if
\lambda
is a partition of
n
. It turns out that integer partitions of
n
are in bijection with irreducible representations of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, which makes them a good tool to study characters.

A common and useful way to code an integer partition (and thus a representation of

2.2
Hook lengths, hook products and the hook length formula

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. If

in
\lambda

u
, including

Let us now consider a subset of boxes

H(\lambda,E):=\prod_{u\in E}H(\lambda,u)

(2.1)

the

We can now state the hook length formula. Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram and

denote its number of boxes. A standard tableau of
\lambda
is a filling of
\lambda
with the numbers from 1 to

such that the numbers are increasing on each row and column. We denote by
\ST(\lambda)
the set of standard tableaux of
\lambda

d_{\lambda}:=|\ST(\lambda)|

The hook length formula was discovered by Frame, Robinson, and Thrall

(2.2)

Example 2.1
.

Consider again
\lambda=[3,2]

\frac{n!}{H(\lambda,\lambda)}=\frac{5!}{4\cdot 3\cdot 1\cdot 2\cdot 1}=\frac{120}{24}=5

2.4
Some elementary results

We collect here standard or elementary results that we will use throughout the paper. We give proofs for completeness.

Lemma 2.3
.

(a)

For any
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
we have
d_{\lambda}\leq\sqrt{n!}
.

(b)

For
n\geq 6
, we have
n!\leq(n/2)^{n}
.

(c)

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Denote by
s=s(\lambda)
the external hook of
\lambda
. Then
d_{s}=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}
.

Proof.

(a)

A classical formula from representation theory is the following: for any finite group
G
we have
|G|=\sum_{\rho\in\widehat{G}}d_{\rho}^{2}
, where
\widehat{G}
is the set of all irreducible representations of
G
. This identity represents for example the dimensions on both sides of the Fourier isomorphism (see
[
40
, Section 1.3]
). Hence, for any
\lambda\in\widehat{G}
of a finite group
G
we have

d_{\lambda}^{2}\leq\sum_{\rho\in\widehat{G}}d_{\rho}^{2}=|G|.

(2.3)

The result follows taking square roots on both sides, since for
n\geq 1
we have
|\mathfrak{S}_{n}|=n!
.

(b)

We proceed by induction. The result holds for
n=6
since
6!=720\leq 729=3^{6}=(6/2)^{6}
. Let now
n\geq 6
and assume that
n!\leq(n/2)^{n}
. Then

\frac{(n+1)!}{((n+1)/2)^{n+1}}=\frac{n!}{(n/2)^{n}}(n+1)\frac{2}{n+1}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}\leq 2\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}.

(2.4)

Since
\ln
is concave, we have
\ln(1+x)\geq x\ln 2
for
0\leq x\leq 1
, and therefore, we have for
n\geq 1

2\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}=2e^{-n\ln(1+1/n)}\leq 2e^{-n\frac{\ln 2}{n}}=1,

(2.5)

concluding the proof.

(c)

Recall that
d_{s}
is the number of standard tableaux of
s
. Observe that a standard tableau necessarily has 1 placed in the bottom-left corner. Furthermore, since
s
is a hook, the rest of the standard tableau is determined by which numbers we chose to place on the first row, for which there are
\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}
possibilities. ∎

3
Sliced hook products

We introduce here a new tool in the study of Young diagrams, which we call
sliced hook products
. Sliced hook products are defined in Section
3.1
. The idea is to replace the whole hook product of a diagram by a simpler product.

We study these sliced hook products in Section
3.2
and obtain precise approximations of (classical) hook products. The key result is the bound

d_{\lambda}\geq\binom{n}{|c|}d_{s}d_{c}e^{-6\sqrt{|c|}}

(3.1)

presented in Proposition
3.8
, which improves on
[
29
, Lemma 2.1]
. Here,
s
denotes the external hook of
\lambda
and
c=\lambda\backslash s
is the center of
\lambda
, as defined in Section
2.3
and drawn in Figure
5
.

In Section
3.3
, we introduce the
augmented dimension

d_{\lambda}^{+}
, which is hybrid between
d_{\lambda}
and
D(\lambda)
and well-suited for inductions, and consider some of its useful properties.

3.1
Definitions

Despite being elegant, the hook length formula may be tricky to use, because of how hard it is to estimate hook products. A powerful idea to approximate hook products is to take into account the contribution to hook lengths of only some boxes in the hook of a box, making the resulting hook product easier to compute and to use.

We first extend the definition of hook lengths to any set of boxes. We represent boxes by the coordinates of their top right corner in the plane
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. As such a box can be seen as an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and a set of boxes is a subset
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, see Figure
6
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let

S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
be a finite set of boxes, and let
u=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
(not necessarily in
S
). We define the hook length of
u
with respect to
S
by

H(S,u)=\left|\left\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S{\;:\;}[x=x^{\prime}\text{ and }y\leq y^{\prime}]\text{ or }[y=y^{\prime}\text{ and }x\leq x^{\prime}]\right\}\right|.

(3.2)

.

Let

More generally, if

(3.4)

We will refer to “slicing” for the procedure consisting of replacing

3.2
Bounds on sliced hook products

Let us define, for any Young diagrams
\mu\subset\lambda
and any set partition
P
of
\lambda
, the ratio

R_{P}(\lambda,\mu):=\frac{H(\lambda,\mu)}{H^{*P}(\lambda,\mu)}.

(3.5)

If
P
is the
ab\delta
-(resp.
\lambda_{1}
-,
\lambda^{1}
-)slicing, we denote the corresponding ratio by
R_{ab\delta}
(resp.
R_{\lambda_{1}}
,
R_{\lambda^{1}}
).

We start with rewriting the ratio, in the case of a slicing with respect to the first row.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\mu,\lambda
are two Young diagrams such that
\mu\subset\lambda
, then we have:

(i)

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{H(\lambda,\mu_{1})}{H(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})}
;

(ii)

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\mu)\leq R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)
.

Proof.

Let us first prove (i). Since the first row of
\lambda
does not appear in the hook products of boxes on the second row and above, we have
H(\lambda,\mu_{\geq 2})=H(\lambda_{\geq 2},\mu_{\geq 2})
. Therefore,

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{H(\lambda,\mu_{1})H(\lambda,\mu_{\geq 2})}{H(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})H(\lambda_{\geq 2},\mu_{\geq 2})}=\frac{H(\lambda,\mu_{1})}{H(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})}.

(3.6)

Now we prove (ii). For each
u\in\lambda
, we have
H_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,u)\leq H(\lambda,u)
, i.e.
\frac{H_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,u)}{H(\lambda,u)}\leq 1
. Using (i), we get that

\begin{split}\frac{R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\mu)}{R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)}&=\frac{H(\lambda,\mu_{1})/H(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{1})/H(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}=\frac{H(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})/H(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{1})/H(\lambda,\mu_{1})}=\frac{H(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}\backslash\mu_{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{1}\backslash\mu_{1})}\\
&=\prod_{u\in\lambda_{1}\backslash\mu_{1}}\frac{H(\lambda_{1},u)}{H(\lambda,u)}\leq 1,\end{split}

(3.7)

where
\lambda_{1}\backslash\mu_{1}
denotes the set of boxes that are in
\lambda_{1}
but not in
\mu_{1}
, that is, the set of boxes whose top right corner is in
\left\{(i,1){\;:\;}\mu_{1}+1\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}\right\}
.
∎

Definition 3.5
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
u=(u_{i})_{1\leq i\leq p},v=(v_{i})_{1\leq i\leq p}
be two
p
-tuples of real numbers. We say that
v
majorizes
u
and write
u\preceq v
if, for all
1\leq\ell\leq p
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}u_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}v_{i}.

(3.8)

and

\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}.

(3.9)

We write
u\prec v
if, in addition, they are not equal.

The following lemma is a classical inequality which we will use several times.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
u=(u_{i})_{1\leq i\leq p},v=(v_{i})_{1\leq i\leq p}
be two (weakly) decreasing
p
-tuples of positive real numbers such that
v\succeq u
. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}.

(3.10)

Proof.

By Karamata’s inequality, applied to the convex function
-\ln
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{p}-\ln u_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}-\ln v_{i}
. Multiplying by
-1
and taking the exponential concludes the proof.
∎

Given two integers
0\leq k\leq n
, we write

n^{\downarrow k}:=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\prod_{0\leq i\leq k-1}(n-i)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}=k!\binom{n}{k}.

(3.11)

Let us now prove various bounds on
\lambda_{1}
-slicings.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mu\subset\lambda
be (non-empty) Young diagrams. Recall the notation
r=\lambda_{\geq 2}
.

(a)

Recall from Definition
2.2
that
u_{\leq k}(\lambda)=\lambda_{\geq 2}\cap\lambda^{\prime}_{\leq k}
is the set of boxes of
\lambda
that are in the first
k
columns but are not on the first row, as represented in Figure
5
. We have

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\frac{\left(\lambda_{1}+\left\lceil\frac{u_{\leq\mu_{1}}(\lambda)}{\mu_{1}}\right\rceil\right)^{\downarrow\mu_{1}}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\mu_{1}}}.

(3.12)

(b)

We have

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq\binom{\lambda_{1}+\left\lceil\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}}\right\rceil}{\lambda_{1}}.

(3.13)

(c)

If
r\leq\lambda_{1}
, then

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq 1+\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}-r+1}.

(3.14)

(d)

Finally, we have

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq e^{3\sqrt{r}}.

(3.15)

Proof.

(a)

First observe that, as a consequence of Lemma
3.6
, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{\mu_{1}}(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\mu_{1}}\left(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+\frac{u_{\leq\mu_{1}}(\lambda)}{\mu_{1}}\right).

(3.16)

This implies that, setting
m:=\left\lceil\frac{u_{\leq\mu_{1}}(\lambda)}{\mu_{1}}\right\rceil
, we have by Lemma
3.4
(i):

\begin{split}R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\mu)&=\frac{H(\lambda,\mu_{1})}{H(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})}=\prod_{i=1}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}}{\lambda_{1}-(i-1)}\\
&\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+m}{\lambda_{1}-(i-1)}=\frac{(\lambda_{1}+m)^{\downarrow\mu_{1}}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\mu_{1}}}.\end{split}

(3.17)

This concludes the proof of (a).

(b)

In the case
\mu=\lambda
, we have
\frac{u_{\leq\mu_{1}}(\lambda)}{\mu_{1}}=\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}}
. We therefore have directly, by (a):

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq\frac{(\lambda_{1}+m)^{\downarrow\lambda_{1}}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\lambda_{1}}}=\binom{\lambda_{1}+m}{\lambda_{1}}.

(3.18)

(c)

Denote
\mu=[\lambda_{1},n-\lambda_{1}]
. This diagram has the same number of boxes on (and over) the first row as
\lambda
, but has only two rows. Recall that
u_{i}(\lambda)
denotes the number of boxes in the
i
-th column of
\lambda
which are above the first row, i.e.
u_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-1
. By definition of
R_{\lambda_{1}}
, and since
H(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})=\lambda_{1}!=\mu_{1}!=H(\mu_{1},\mu_{1})
, we have

\frac{R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)}{R_{\mu_{1}}(\mu,\mu)}=\frac{H(\lambda,\lambda_{1})/H(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}{H(\mu,\mu_{1})/H(\mu_{1},\mu_{1})}=\frac{H(\lambda,\lambda_{1})}{H(\mu,\mu_{1})}=\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}(\lambda)}{\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}(\mu)}.

(3.19)

Moreover, the sequences
(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}(\lambda))_{1\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}}
and
(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}(\mu))_{1\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}}
are decreasing and satisfy
(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}(\lambda))_{1\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}}\succeq(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}(\mu))_{1\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}}
. We deduce from Lemma
3.6
that the right hand side of (
3.19
) is upper bounded by 1, i.e. that
R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq R_{\mu_{1}}(\mu,\mu)
. We conclude that

\begin{split}R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq R_{\mu_{1}}(\mu,\mu)&=\frac{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+1)\right)(\lambda_{1}-r)!}{\lambda_{1}!}\\
&=\frac{(\lambda_{1}+1)^{\downarrow r}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow r}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}+1}{\lambda_{1}-r+1}=1+\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}-r+1}.\end{split}

(3.20)

(d)

We split the proof according to the value of
r
.

•

If
r<\lambda_{1}
, then starting from (c) we have

R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}+1}{\lambda_{1}-r+1}\leq\frac{r+1}{r-r+1}=r+1\leq e^{\sqrt{r}},

(3.21)

and thus
R_{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda,\lambda)\leq e^{3\sqrt{r}}
.

•

Assume now that
r\geq\lambda_{1}
. Let
n=|\lambda|
. Note that here again
\mu=\lambda
, so that
\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}
and
u_{\leq\mu_{1}}=r
. Hence,
m=\left\lceil\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}}\right\rceil
, and since
r\geq\lambda_{1}
we have
m\geq 1
. Hence,
m=\left\lceil\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}}\right\rceil\leq\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}}+1\leq 2\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}}
, so that
\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{2r}{m}
.
First assume that
\lambda_{1}\leq m
. Using that
p^{\downarrow p}=p!\geq(p/e)^{p}
for all
p\geq 0
, we therefore have, using
m\leq 2r/\lambda_{1}
in the last inequality,

\binom{\lambda_{1}+m}{\lambda_{1}}=\frac{(\lambda_{1}+m)^{\downarrow\lambda_{1}}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\lambda_{1}}}\leq\frac{(2m)^{\lambda_{1}}}{(\lambda_{1}/e)^{\lambda_{1}}}=\left(\frac{2em}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\lambda_{1}}\leq\left(\frac{4er}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)^{\lambda_{1}}.

(3.22)

Now observe that we can rewrite
\left(\frac{4er}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)^{\lambda_{1}}=\left(\left(\frac{2\sqrt{er}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{2}
. Furthermore, for
T>0
the function
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}\mapsto(T/x)^{x}
is maximal at
x=T/e
, so we get, with
T=2\sqrt{er}
,

\binom{\lambda_{1}+m}{\lambda_{1}}\leq\left(e^{T/e}\right)^{2}=e^{\frac{4\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{e}}}\leq e^{3\sqrt{r}}.

(3.23)

The case
m\leq\lambda_{1}
is proved the same way by symmetry, since
\binom{\lambda_{1}+m}{\lambda_{1}}=\binom{\lambda_{1}+m}{m}
. The result follows by (b). This concludes the proof of (d). ∎

We can now use these results to bound classical hook products and obtain a crucial inequality involving
d_{\lambda},d_{s}
and
d_{c}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\lambda
be a (non-empty) integer partition.
We have

\frac{d_{\lambda}}{\binom{n}{c}d_{s}d_{c}}\geq e^{-6\sqrt{c}},

(3.24)

where we recall that
n=|\lambda|
,
c=\lambda^{\geq 2}
, and
s=n-c=\lambda^{1}
.

Proof.

First, we have

\begin{split}H(\lambda,\lambda)&=sH(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})H(\lambda,c)\\
&=s\cdot\lambda_{a}^{1}!\cdot\lambda_{b}^{1}!\frac{H(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})}{H(\lambda_{a}^{1},\lambda_{a}^{1})}\frac{H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})}{H(\lambda_{b}^{1},\lambda_{b}^{1})}H(c,c)\\
&=\frac{s!}{\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{b}^{1}}}\frac{H(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})}{H(\lambda_{a}^{1},\lambda_{a}^{1})}\frac{H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})}{H(\lambda_{b}^{1},\lambda_{b}^{1})}H(c,c).\end{split}

(3.25)

We deduce from the hook length formula and Lemma
2.3
that

\begin{split}\frac{d_{\lambda}}{\binom{n}{c}}=\frac{c!s!}{n!}\frac{n!}{H(\lambda,\lambda)}&=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{b}^{1}}\frac{H(\lambda_{a}^{1},\lambda_{a}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})}\frac{H(\lambda_{b}^{1},\lambda_{b}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})}\frac{c!}{H(c,c)}\\
&=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{b}^{1}}\frac{H(\lambda_{a}^{1},\lambda_{a}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})}\frac{H(\lambda_{b}^{1},\lambda_{b}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})}d_{c}\\
&=\frac{H(\lambda_{a}^{1},\lambda_{a}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})}\frac{H(\lambda_{b}^{1},\lambda_{b}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})}d_{s}d_{c}.\end{split}

(3.26)

Moreover, by Proposition
3.7
(d) applied twice respectively to the first row and the first column of the diagrams
\lambda^{\prime}_{\geq 2}
and
\lambda_{\geq 2}
, we get

\frac{H(\lambda_{a}^{1},\lambda_{a}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{a}^{1})}\frac{H(\lambda_{b}^{1},\lambda_{b}^{1})}{H(\lambda,\lambda_{b}^{1})}\geq e^{-3\sqrt{c}}e^{-3\sqrt{c}}=e^{-6\sqrt{c}}.

(3.27)

Plugging this into (
3.26
) concludes the proof.
∎

3.3
Virtual degrees and augmented dimensions

If
P
is a set partition of a diagram
\lambda\vdash n
, we can associate to it a notion of
P
-dimension via the analog of the hook length formula:

d_{\lambda}^{*P}:=\frac{n!}{H^{*P}(\lambda,\lambda)}.

(3.28)

The virtual degree, defined in (
1.1
), and
ab\delta
-dimension are closely related: we have

D(\lambda)=\frac{d_{\lambda}^{*ab\delta}}{n}.

(3.29)

We may therefore refer to
ab\delta
-sliced hook lengths and products as
virtual
hook lengths and products.

Let us now define a last notion of dimension, which will prove to be very convenient in the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
We define the
augmented dimension

d_{\lambda}^{+}
of a Young diagram
\lambda
by

d^{+}_{\lambda}:=\frac{n!}{H^{+}(\lambda,\lambda)},

(3.30)

where

H^{+}(\lambda,\lambda)=\left(\prod_{i}s_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{i}a_{i}!b_{i}!\right).

(3.31)

(Here
s_{i}
denotes the hook started at
\delta_{i}
and we recall that
a_{i}=\lambda_{a}^{i}
and
b_{i}=\lambda_{b}^{i}
.)

The augmented dimension
d_{\lambda}^{+}
is hybrid between
d_{\lambda}
and
D(\lambda)
: the on-diagonal augmented hook lengths are the usual (non-sliced) hook lengths, while the off-diagonal augmented hook lengths are the virtual hook lengths.

Note that

By definition we have

\frac{D(\lambda)}{d_{\lambda}^{+}}=\frac{\frac{(n-1)!}{\prod_{i}a_{i}!b_{i}!}}{\frac{n!}{\left(\prod_{i}a_{i}!b_{i}!\right)\left(\prod_{i}s_{i}\right)}}=\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}.

(3.32)

One advantage of using
d_{\lambda}^{+}
is the following product identity (which we prove later on, in Lemma
4.7
):

d_{\lambda}^{+}=\binom{n}{c}d_{s}^{+}d_{c}^{+}.

(3.33)

This will be convenient when bounding
d_{\lambda}^{+}
by induction on the size of the center
c
of
\lambda
.

4
Sharp bounds on virtual degrees

The aim of this section is to use the results of the previous sections to prove Theorem
1.5
.
In light of (
3.32
), which rewrites as

D(\lambda)=\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}d_{\lambda}^{+},

(4.1)

it is enough to prove the two following statements.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{diag}>0
such that, for every
n\geq 2
and every diagram
\lambda\vdash n
, we have

\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\leq d_{\lambda}^{C_{diag}/\ln n}.

(4.2)

Proposition 4.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{aug}>0
such that, for every
n\geq 2
and every diagram
\lambda\vdash n
, we have

d_{\lambda}^{+}\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+C_{aug}/\ln n}.

(4.3)

Intuitively, Proposition
4.1
states that the virtual degree
D(\lambda)
and the augmented dimension
d_{\lambda}^{+}
are close to each other, and Proposition
4.2
states that
d_{\lambda}^{+}
is close to the usual notion of dimension,
d_{\lambda}
.

We prove Proposition
4.1
in Section
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
in Section
4.2
. Finally, in Section
4.3
we provide examples that show that Theorem
1.5
is sharp up to the value of the constant.

4.1
Proof of Proposition
4.1

Let us first give two general upper bounds on
\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
such that
c=c(\lambda)\geq 1
.
Then we have

(a)

\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\leq(c/\delta(c))^{\delta(c)},

(4.4)

where
c=\lambda^{\geq 2}
is the center of
\lambda
and
\delta(c):=\delta(\lambda)-1
is the diagonal length of
c
.

(b)

Furthermore,

\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\leq e^{\sqrt{c}\ln c}.

(4.5)

Proof.

(a)

First, since
s_{1}\leq n
, we have

\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\leq\prod_{i\geq 2}s_{i}.

(4.6)

Moreover, by concavity of the logarithm, we have (using that
\delta(c)=\delta(\lambda)-1
)

\prod_{i\geq 2}s_{i}=\exp\left(\sum_{2\leq i\leq\delta(\lambda)}\ln(s_{i})\right)\leq\exp\left(\sum_{2\leq i\leq\delta(\lambda)}\ln\left(\frac{c}{\delta(c)}\right)\right)=(c/\delta(c))^{\delta(c)},

(4.7)

concluding the proof of (a).

(b)

We have
\delta(c)\leq\sqrt{c}
(since the square of side length
\delta(c)
is included in the diagram
c
), and
s_{i}\leq c
for each
i\geq 2
. It therefore follows that

\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\leq\prod_{i\geq 2}s_{i}\leq c^{\delta(c)}\leq c^{\sqrt{c}}=e^{\sqrt{c}\ln c}.\qed

(4.8)

In Proposition
3.8
, we showed that
d_{\lambda}\geq\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{b}^{1}}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\binom{n}{c}d_{c}
. Depending on the size of
c
, only some of the terms in the lower bound will be useful, here we give simple lower bounds on some of these terms.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
such that
c\geq 1
. Then

(a)

\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{b}^{1}}\geq s-1.

(4.9)

(b)

\binom{n}{c}\geq\max\left((n/c)^{c},(n/s)^{s}\right).

(4.10)

Proof.

(a)

Since
c\geq 1
, we have
1\leq\lambda_{b}^{1}\leq s-2
. We therefore have

\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{b}^{1}}\geq\binom{s-1}{1}=s-1.

(4.11)

(b)

This bound is a classical bound on binomial coefficients. We have

\binom{n}{c}=\frac{n^{\downarrow c}}{c!}\geq(n/c)^{c},

(4.12)

and since
\binom{n}{c}=\binom{n}{s}
, we also have
\binom{n}{c}\geq(n/s)^{s}
by symmetry, concluding the proof. ∎

We finally give a lower bound on
d_{\lambda}
which depends only on the diagonal length of
\lambda
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Then

d_{\lambda}\geq\left(\frac{\delta}{2e}\right)^{\delta(\delta-1)},

(4.13)

where
\delta
is the diagonal length of
\lambda
. In particular, if
n\geq 8e^{6}
and
\delta\geq n^{1/3}
, we have

d_{\lambda}\geq e^{n^{2/3}/2}.

(4.14)

Proof.

Since
d_{\lambda}
is the number of standard tableaux of
\lambda
, and
\lambda
contains the square
\mu:=\left[\delta^{\delta}\right]
, we have
d_{\lambda}\geq d_{\mu}
.
Moreover, since the hook lengths in
\mu
are at most
2\delta
, by the hook length formula we have

d_{\mu}=\frac{(\delta^{2})!}{H(\mu,\mu)}\geq\frac{(\delta^{2}/e)^{\delta^{2}}}{(2\delta)^{\delta^{2}}}=\left(\frac{\delta}{2e}\right)^{\delta^{2}}.

(4.15)

Since
d_{\mu}\geq 1
, we deduce that
d_{\mu}\geq\left(\frac{\delta}{2e}\right)^{\delta(\delta-1)}
.

Assume now that
n\geq 8e^{6}
and
\delta\geq n^{1/3}
. Then we have
\delta\geq\left(8e^{6}\right)^{1/3}=2e^{2}
so
\delta/(2e)\geq e
, and we also have
\delta(\delta-1)\geq\delta^{2}/2
since
\delta\geq 2
, so
(\delta/(2e))^{\delta(\delta-1)}\geq e^{\delta^{2}/2}\geq e^{n^{2/3}/2}
.
∎

We now prove Proposition
4.1
for small values of
n
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
n_{0}\geq 2
, for every
2\leq n\leq n_{0}
and every
\lambda\vdash n
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq d_{\lambda}^{C/\ln n},

(4.16)

where
C=\frac{n_{0}(\ln n_{0})^{2}}{\ln 2}
.

Proof.

Let
n_{0}\geq 2
,
2\leq n\leq n_{0}
and
\lambda\vdash n
. If
\lambda
is flat (horizontal or vertical), then
d_{\lambda}=1
and
\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}=1
so the result holds. Assume now that
\lambda
is not flat.
Then
d_{\lambda}\geq 2
so

\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq\prod_{1\leq i\leq\delta(\lambda)}s_{i}\leq n_{0}^{n_{0}}=2^{\frac{1}{\ln 2}n_{0}\ln n_{0}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{\ln 2}n_{0}\ln n_{0}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{\ln 2}(n_{0}\ln n_{0})\frac{\ln n_{0}}{\ln n}}=d_{\lambda}^{C/\ln n}.\qed

(4.17)

We now have all the tools to prove Proposition
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Set
n_{0}=\left\lfloor e^{55}\right\rfloor
and
C_{diag}=e^{64}
. By Lemma
4.6
the result holds for all
2\leq n\leq n_{0}
, since
\frac{n_{0}(\ln n_{0})^{2}}{\ln 2}\leq e^{55}(55^{2})/\ln 2\leq C_{diag}
.

Assume now that
n>n_{0}
. We will prove that for such
n
the bound
\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\leq d_{\lambda}^{C/\ln n}
holds with
C=500
, which is enough since
C\leq C_{diag}
.

We split the proof into several cases, depending on how large
c
is.

•

If
c\in\left\{0,1\right\}
then
\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq\frac{n}{n}=1
so the result holds.

•

Assume that
2\leq c\leq n^{8/9}
. First, by Proposition
3.8
, Lemma
4.4
(b), and since
n/c\geq n^{1/9}
, we have

d_{\lambda}\geq\binom{n}{c}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\geq(n/c)^{c}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\geq n^{c/9}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}.

(4.18)

Moreover, since
c\geq 2
and
n\geq e^{55}
, we have
e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\geq e^{-5c}\geq n^{-c/11}
, and therefore
n^{c/9}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\geq n^{c(1/9-1/11)}\geq n^{c/500}
.

On the other hand, we have
\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq e^{\sqrt{c}\ln c}
by Lemma
4.3
(b). We deduce, using that
\ln c\leq\sqrt{c}
for all
c\geq 1
so that
\sqrt{c}\ln c\leq c
, that

\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq e^{\sqrt{c}\ln c}\leq e^{c}=\left(n^{c/500}\right)^{C/\ln n}\leq d_{\lambda}^{C/\ln n}.

(4.19)

•

Assume that
n^{8/9}\leq c\leq n-n^{2/3}
. Then

\frac{n}{c}\geq\frac{n}{n-n^{2/3}}=\frac{1}{1-n^{-1/3}}\geq\frac{1}{e^{-n^{-1/3}}}=e^{n^{-1/3}}.

(4.20)

Moreover, since
n\geq 12^{18}
, we have
12\sqrt{c}\leq 12\sqrt{n}=n^{5/9}(12n^{-1/18})\leq n^{5/9}
. We deduce, using again Proposition
3.8
and Lemma
4.4
(b) for the first inequality, that

d_{\lambda}\geq(n/c)^{c}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\geq\left(e^{n^{-1/3}}\right)^{n^{8/9}}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}=e^{n^{5/9}}e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\geq e^{n^{5/9}/2}.

(4.21)

We conclude using Lemma
4.3
(b) (and that
c\leq n
) that

\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq e^{\sqrt{c}\ln c}\leq e^{\sqrt{n}\ln n}\leq e^{500\frac{n^{5/9}}{2\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{500/\ln n}=d_{\lambda}^{C/\ln n}.

(4.22)

•

Assume now that
c\geq n-n^{2/3}
, i.e.
s\leq n^{2/3}
. Then we have
\delta(\lambda)\geq n^{1/3}
, and since
n\geq e^{55}
we also have
n\geq 8e^{6}
. Therefore by Lemma
4.5
we have
d_{\lambda}\geq e^{n^{2/3}/2}
. We conclude, proceeding as in the previous case, that

\frac{1}{n}\prod_{i}s_{i}\leq e^{\sqrt{c}\ln c}\leq e^{\sqrt{n}\ln n}\leq e^{500\frac{n^{2/3}}{2\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{500/\ln n}\leq d_{\lambda}^{C/\ln n}.

(4.23)

This concludes the proof.
∎

4.2
Proof of Proposition
4.2

Our aim is to show that
\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}}
is small. We will proceed by induction, slicing the external hook
s=\lambda^{1}
.

First, we recall from Proposition
3.8
that the dimension of a diagram
d_{\lambda}
and the dimension of its center
d_{c}
are related as follows: we have
d_{\lambda}\geq e^{-6\sqrt{c}}\binom{n}{c}d_{s}d_{c}
, that is, recalling from Lemma
2.3
that
d_{s}=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}
,

\frac{d_{c}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq\frac{e^{6\sqrt{c}}}{\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}\binom{n}{s}}.

(4.24)

Remark 4.1
.

We also always have
d_{\lambda}\leq\binom{n}{c}d_{s}d_{c}
. Indeed, by first choosing which numbers from 1 to
n
we place in
c
, we obtain
|\ST(\lambda)|\leq\binom{n}{c}|\ST(s)||\ST(c)|
. This argument was written by Diaconis and Shahshahani in their proof of cutoff for random transpositions (with
r=\lambda_{\geq 2}
in place of
c
), see
[
13
, Fact 1 in Chapter 3D, p. 39-40]
. One can therefore see Proposition
3.8
as a quantified version of the intuitive approximation
d_{\lambda}\approx\binom{n}{c}d_{s}d_{c}
, for large values of
s
and
c
.

Comparing the augmented dimensions
d_{\lambda}^{+}
and
d_{c}^{+}
is much simpler by design, since the hook products involved there are already sliced by definition. Also, by definition of
d_{s}^{+}
we have
d_{s}^{+}=\frac{s!}{s\lambda_{a}^{1}!\lambda_{b}^{1}!}=\frac{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}!\lambda_{b}^{1}!}=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}
, and we recall from Lemma
2.3
that
d_{s}=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}
, so that

d_{s}^{+}=d_{s}=\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}.

(4.25)

In the next lemma we show that the aforementioned intuitive approximation for dimensions actually becomes an equality when it comes to augmented dimensions.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Then

d_{\lambda}^{+}=\binom{n}{s}d_{s}^{+}d_{c}^{+}.

(4.26)

In other words, we have

\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{c}^{+}}=\binom{n}{s}{\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}}.

(4.27)

Proof.

By definition we have

H^{+}(\lambda,\lambda)=s\lambda_{a}^{1}!\lambda_{b}^{1}!H^{+}(c,c).

(4.28)

We therefore get

\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{c}^{+}}=\frac{n!/H^{+}(\lambda,\lambda)}{c!/H^{+}(c,c)}=\frac{n^{\downarrow s}}{s\lambda_{a}^{1}!\lambda_{b}^{1}!}=\frac{n^{\downarrow s}}{s!}\frac{(s-1)!}{\lambda_{a}^{1}!\lambda_{b}^{1}!}=\binom{n}{s}{\binom{s-1}{\lambda_{a}^{1}}}.\qed

(4.29)

Combining (
4.24
) and Lemma
4.7
, we obtain the following bound.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Then

\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq e^{6\sqrt{c}}\frac{d_{c}^{+}}{d_{c}}.

(4.30)

We now fix constants for the rest of this section. We set

n_{0}=\left\lfloor e^{51}\right\rfloor,\hskip 10.00002ptc_{0}=10^{8},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptC=C_{aug}=e^{61}.

(4.31)

The next lemma, whose proof we omit, collects some bounds which we will use just after.

Lemma 4.9
.

The following hold.

(i)

For all

, we have

(

)

\sqrt{n}\leq\frac{n}{8}
,

(

)

,

(

)

(

(

We first check that our result holds for small values of

.

Let
n

(4.33)

We now consider small values of
c
, assuming that
n
is large enough.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
n\geq n_{0}
and

d_{\lambda}^{+}\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+C/\ln n}.

(4.34)

If

\lambda_{a}^{1}\geq 1
and
\lambda_{b}^{1}\geq 1
so
d_{\lambda}\geq d_{s}\geq\binom{s-1}{1}=s-1\geq\sqrt{n}
(since
n\geq n_{0}\geq 3
and
c\leq c_{0}
). Then by Corollary
4.8
and Lemma
4.9
(
iii_{2}
),

d_{\lambda}^{+}\leq d_{\lambda}e^{6\sqrt{c}}\frac{d_{c}^{+}}{d_{c}}\leq d_{\lambda}e^{6\sqrt{c}}d_{c}^{+}\leq d_{\lambda}e^{6\sqrt{c_{0}}}c_{0}!\leq d_{\lambda}\sqrt{n}^{\frac{12\sqrt{c_{0}}\ln(c_{0}!)}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+C/\ln n}.\qed

(4.35)

The next result concerns large values of
n
and specific values of
s
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
n\geq n_{0}
and
s
such that
\sqrt{n}\leq s\leq n/8
. Then

\left(\frac{es}{n}\right)^{s/2}\leq e^{-\frac{\sqrt{n}\ln n}{8}}.

(4.36)

Proof.

First note that since
n\geq n_{0}
, we have
\sqrt{n}\leq n/8
by Lemma
4.9
(
i_{1}
). We define

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}f:&\left[\sqrt{n},n/8\right]&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}\\
&x&\longmapsto&\left(\frac{ex}{n}\right)^{x/2}\end{array}

(4.37)

and observe that
g:=\ln\circ f
is convex (since
g^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2x}>0
for
x\in\left[\sqrt{n},n/8\right]
). Hence,
g
reaches its maximum value when
x
is either minimal or maximal and therefore the same holds also for
f
. Moreover,
e^{2}\leq 8
and
n\geq 4\sqrt{n}\ln n
(by Lemma
4.9
(
i_{2}
)) so we have

\frac{f(n/8)}{f(\sqrt{n})}=\frac{(e/8)^{n/16}}{(e/\sqrt{n})^{{\sqrt{n}/2}}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}^{\sqrt{n}/2}}{e^{n/16}}=\frac{n^{\sqrt{n}/4}}{e^{n/16}}\leq 1.

(4.38)

We deduce that
f
is maximal at
\sqrt{n}
, and conclude that

\max_{\sqrt{n}\leq s\leq n/8}f(s)\leq f(\sqrt{n})=(e/\sqrt{n})^{\sqrt{n}/2}\leq(n^{-1/4})^{\sqrt{n}/2}=e^{-\frac{\sqrt{n}\ln n}{8}}.\qed

(4.39)

We now have all the tools to prove Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

We proceed by (strong) induction on
n
. Our recurrence hypothesis is

H(n):\text{\textquotedblleft}d_{\lambda}^{+}\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+C/\ln n}\text{ for every }\lambda\vdash n\text{\textquotedblright}.

(4.40)

By Lemma
4.10
,
H(n)
holds for all
2\leq n\leq n_{0}
, which initializes the induction.

Let now
n>n_{0}
and assume that
H(m)
holds for every
m\leq n-1
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
.

The case
0\leq c\leq c_{0}
follows from Lemma
4.11
. Since, in addition,
s\geq\sqrt{n}
for every diagram, we can assume from now on that
c_{0}\leq c\leq n-\sqrt{n}
.

By Corollary
4.8
,

\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{C}{\ln n}}}=\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}}d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{C}{\ln n}}\leq e^{6\sqrt{c}}\frac{d_{c}^{+}}{d_{c}}d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{C}{\ln n}}=e^{6\sqrt{c}}\frac{d_{c}^{+}}{d_{c}^{1+\frac{C}{\ln c}}}\left(\frac{d_{c}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{C/\ln c}d_{\lambda}^{\frac{C}{\ln c}-\frac{C}{\ln n}}.

(4.41)

Applying the induction hypothesis to
c
, we deduce that

\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{C}{\ln n}}}\leq e^{6\sqrt{c}}\left(\frac{d_{c}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{C/\ln c}d_{\lambda}^{\frac{C}{\ln c}-\frac{C}{\ln n}}.

(4.42)

By (
4.24
) and using that
d_{\lambda}\leq\binom{n}{c}d_{c}d_{s}
(as explained in Remark
4.1
), we deduce that

\begin{split}\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{C}{\ln n}}}&\leq e^{6\sqrt{c}}\left(\frac{e^{6\sqrt{c}}}{d_{s}\binom{n}{s}}\right)^{\frac{C}{\ln c}}\left(\binom{n}{c}d_{c}d_{s}\right)^{\frac{C}{\ln c}-\frac{C}{\ln n}}\\
&=e^{6\sqrt{c}\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln c}\right)}\left(d_{s}\binom{n}{c}\right)^{-C/\ln n}d_{c}^{\frac{C}{\ln c}-\frac{C}{\ln n}}\\
&=\left(\frac{e^{6\sqrt{c}(1+\frac{\ln c}{C})}}{\left(d_{s}\binom{n}{c}\right)^{\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\right)^{C/\ln c}\\
&\leq\left(e^{6\sqrt{c}(1+\frac{\ln c}{C})}\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\right)^{C/\ln c}\end{split}

(4.43)

To show our result, it is therefore enough to prove that

e^{6\sqrt{c}(1+\frac{\ln c}{C})}\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq 1.

(4.44)

We split the proof of (
4.44
) depending on the value of
c
.

n-\sqrt{n}\geq c\geq 7n/8

In this case, we have
\sqrt{n}\leq s\leq n/8
. Then

1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln n-\ln c}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln(\frac{c+s}{c})}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln(1+\frac{s}{c})}{\ln n}\leq\frac{s}{c\ln n}.

(4.45)

In addition, by Lemma
2.3
,
d_{c}\leq\sqrt{c!}\leq c^{c/2}\leq n^{c/2}
, so that

d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq e^{s/2}.

(4.46)

On the other hand, since
1-s/n\geq 1/2
by assumption, we have

\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}=1+\frac{\ln(c/n)}{\ln n}=1+\frac{\ln(1-s/n)}{\ln n}\geq 1-\frac{\ln 2}{\ln n},

(4.47)

and in particular
\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}\geq 1/2
by Lemma
4.9
(
i_{3}
).

Recall also from Lemma
4.4
(b) that
\binom{n}{c}=\binom{n}{s}\geq(n/s)^{s}
. Therefore, by (
4.46
) and Lemma
4.12
,

\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq\left(\frac{s}{n}\right)^{s/2}e^{s/2}=\left(\frac{es}{n}\right)^{s/2}\leq e^{-\frac{\sqrt{n}\ln n}{8}}.

(4.48)

We conclude, using that
\ln c\leq\ln n
and
\sqrt{c}\leq\sqrt{n}
, and by Lemma
4.9
(
i_{4}
) (which we can apply since
C\geq 10^{3}
), that

e^{6\sqrt{c}(1+\frac{\ln c}{C})}\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq e^{\sqrt{n}\left(6+\frac{6\ln n}{C}-\frac{\ln n}{8}\right)}\leq 1.

(4.49)

c_{0}\leq c\leq 7n/8
Since
c\geq c_{0}\geq 6
, we have by Lemma
2.3
that
d_{c}\leq\sqrt{c!}\leq(c/2)^{c/2}
.
Therefore,

\begin{split}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}=d_{c}^{\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\leq\left((c/2)^{c/2}\right)^{\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}&=\left((c/n)^{c/2}\right)^{\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\left(n^{c/2}\right)^{\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\left((1/2)^{c/2}\right)^{\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\\
&=\left((c/n)^{c/2}\right)^{\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}(n/c)^{c/2}\,\cdot\,2^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\\
&=\left(\frac{n}{c}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\right)}2^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}.\end{split}

(4.50)

On the other hand we still have

\binom{n}{c}\geq(n/c)^{c}

and

\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}=1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}
, so

\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq\left(\frac{n}{c}\right)^{-c\left(1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\right)},

(4.51)

and overall we get

\begin{split}\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}&\leq\left(\frac{n}{c}\right)^{-c\left(1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\right)}\left(\frac{n}{c}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\right)}2^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\\
&=\left(\frac{c}{n}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\right)}2^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}.\end{split}

(4.52)

Observe that both factors are clearly
\leq 1
.
Now, if
c\geq\sqrt{n}
we have
1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\geq 1/2
so

\left(\frac{c}{n}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\right)}\leq\left(\frac{c}{n}\right)^{\frac{c}{4}}\leq(7/8)^{c/4}.

(4.53)

On the other hand, if
c\leq\sqrt{n}
, we have
\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}\geq 1/2
so

2^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\ln(n/c)}{\ln n}}\leq 2^{-\frac{c}{4}}\leq(7/8)^{c/4},

(4.54)

so we always have

\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq(7/8)^{c/4}.

(4.55)

Hence, we get (using Lemma
4.9
(ii) and the fact that
C\geq 1
) that

e^{6\sqrt{c}(1+\frac{\ln c}{C})}\binom{n}{c}^{-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}d_{c}^{1-\frac{\ln c}{\ln n}}\leq e^{6\sqrt{c}\left(1+\frac{\ln c}{C}\right)-\frac{c}{4}\ln(8/7)}\leq 1.

(4.56)

This concludes the proof of (
4.44
), and hence of the proposition.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By (
3.32
), Proposition
4.1
, and Proposition
4.2
, we have

\frac{D(\lambda)}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{D(\lambda)}{d_{\lambda}^{+}}\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{\prod_{i}s_{i}}{n}\frac{d_{\lambda}^{+}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{C_{diag}/\ln n}d_{\lambda}^{C_{aug}/\ln n},

(4.57)

i.e.
D(\lambda)\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+(C_{diag}+C_{aug})/\ln n}
. Moreover, since we used the constants
C_{diag}=e^{64}
and
C_{aug}=e^{61}
in the previous proofs, the bound
D(\lambda)\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+C/\ln n}
holds with e.g.
C=e^{65}
.
∎

4.3
Sharpness of the bound

We consider here two examples, namely square-shaped diagrams and almost-flat diagrams, which show sharpness of Theorem
1.5
. In other words, Theorem
1.5
provides the best possible asymptotic bound on virtual degrees that is uniform over all irreducible characters.
For square-shaped diagrams
\lambda=[p^{p}]
, we show that
D(\lambda)=d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{4\ln 2}{\ln n}(1+o(1))}
where
n=p^{2}
, and for
\lambda=[n-2,2]
, we show that
D(\lambda)=d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{\ln 2}{2\ln n}(1+o(1))}
.
We did not optimize the value of the constant
C
in Theorem
1.5
, but we conjecture that as
n\to\infty
we have
D(\lambda)\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+\frac{4\ln 2+o(1)}{\ln n}}
uniformly for any
\lambda\vdash n
, and that the constant
4\ln 2
is optimal.

4.3.1
Square-shaped diagrams

For all
n\geq 1
such that
n
is a perfect square (i.e.
p:=\sqrt{n}\in\mathbb{N}
), let
\lambda_{n}=[p^{p}]=[p,\ldots,p]
be the square-shaped Young diagram (see Figure
12
).

Figure 12:
The Young diagram
\lambda_{25}

Let us compute
d_{\lambda_{n}}
. We have

H(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n})=\prod_{i,j=1}^{p}(i+j-1)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\frac{(i+p-1)!}{(i-1)!}=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{2p-1}j!}{(\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}i!)^{2}}=\frac{G(2p+1)}{G(p+1)^{2}},

(4.58)

where
G
denotes Barnes’
G
-function.

In particular, the asymptotic expansion of its logarithm is known (see e.g.
[
1
, Lemma
5.1
]
): as
z\rightarrow\infty
,

\ln G(1+z)=\frac{z^{2}}{2}\ln z-\frac{3z^{2}}{4}+O(z).

(4.59)

This provides

\begin{split}\ln H(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n})&=\ln G(2p+1)-2\ln G(p+1)\\
&=\frac{(2p)^{2}}{2}\ln(2p)-\frac{3(2p)^{2}}{4}-2\frac{p^{2}}{2}\ln p+2\frac{3p^{2}}{4}+O(p)\\
&=2p^{2}\ln p+2p^{2}\ln 2-3p^{2}-p^{2}\ln p+\frac{3}{2}p^{2}+O(p)\\
&=p^{2}\ln p+(2\ln 2-\frac{3}{2})p^{2}+O(p).\end{split}

(4.60)

Thus, we get using Stirling’s approximation:

\begin{split}\ln d_{\lambda_{n}}&=\ln n!-\ln H(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n})\\
&=n\ln n-n+O(\ln n)-p^{2}\ln p-(2\ln 2-\frac{3}{2})p^{2}+O(p)\\
&=2p^{2}\ln p-p^{2}-p^{2}\ln p-(2\ln 2-\frac{3}{2})p^{2}+O(p)\\
&=p^{2}\ln p+(\frac{1}{2}-2\ln 2)p^{2}+O(p).\end{split}

(4.61)

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}D(\lambda_{n})=\frac{(n-1)!}{\prod a_{i}!\prod b_{i}!}=\frac{(n-1)!}{(\prod_{i=1}^{p-1}i!)^{2}}=\frac{(n-1)!}{G(1+p)^{2}}.\end{split}

(4.62)

Using again Stirling’s formula along with (
4.59
), we get that

\begin{split}\ln D(\lambda_{n})&=\ln((n-1)!)-2\ln G(p+1)\\
&=n\ln n-n+O(\ln n)-2\frac{p^{2}}{2}\ln p+2\frac{3p^{2}}{4}+O(p)\\
&=2p^{2}\ln p-p^{2}-p^{2}\ln p+\frac{3}{2}p^{2}+O(p)\\
&=p^{2}\ln p+\frac{1}{2}p^{2}+O(p).\end{split}

(4.63)

We deduce that

\begin{split}\ln D(\lambda_{n})-\ln d_{\lambda_{n}}=\left(2\ln 2\right)p^{2}+O(p)=\frac{4\ln 2}{\ln n}\ln d_{\lambda_{n}}(1+o(1)).\end{split}

(4.64)

Hence, as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

D(\lambda_{n})=d_{\lambda_{n}}^{1+\frac{4\ln 2}{\ln n}(1+o(1))}.

(4.65)

4.3.2
Almost-flat diagrams

The second case that we consider is the case of a diagram
\mu_{n}
of size
n
with two rows, one of length
n-2
and the second of length
2
(see Figure
13
).

Figure 13:
The Young diagram
\mu_{10}

In this case, we can also compute

H(\mu_{n},\mu_{n})=(n-4)!*(n-2)*(n-1)*2=2\frac{n!}{n(n-3)},

(4.66)

so that

d_{\mu_{n}}=\frac{n(n-3)}{2}.

(4.67)

On the other hand, we have

D(\mu_{n})=\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-3)!}=(n-1)(n-2).

(4.68)

Hence, we have

\ln\left(\frac{D(\mu_{n})}{d_{\mu_{n}}}\right)=\ln\left(\frac{2(n-1)(n-2)}{n(n-3)}\right)=\ln 2+o(1)=\frac{\ln 2}{2\ln n}\ln d_{\mu_{n}}(1+o(1)).

(4.69)

We conclude that

D(\mu_{n})=d_{\mu_{n}}^{1+\frac{\ln 2}{2\ln n}(1+o(1))}.

(4.70)

5
Character bounds in terms of the number of cycles

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.7
, which provides bounds on characters in terms of the number of cycles of the permutation
\sigma
.

To prove bounds on characters depending on the number of cycles of permutations, Larsen and Shalev
[
29
]
introduced the notion of cycle growth sequence. The cycle growth sequence
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is defined by

n^{b_{k}}=\max(F_{k}(\sigma),1)
, where
F_{k}=f_{1}+\ldots+f_{k}
denotes the number of cycles of
\sigma
of length at most
k
, and Larsen and Shalev set

B(\sigma)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{b_{k}}{k(k+1)}.

(5.1)

They showed that asymptotically
E(\sigma)\leq B(\sigma)+o(1)
, and that if
\cyc(\sigma)\leq n^{\alpha}
then
B(\sigma)\leq\alpha
. Plugging these bounds into Theorem
1.3
, they deduced in
[
29
, Theorem 1.4]
that if
\cyc(\sigma)\leq n^{\alpha}
then

|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq d_{\lambda}^{\alpha+o(1)},

(5.2)

improving on their bound from
[
30
]
.

Remark 5.1
.

The
o(1)
term above has two components: one coming from the bound
D(\lambda)\leq d_{\lambda}^{1+o(1)}
(Theorem
1.3
) and one from the bound
E(\sigma)\leq B(\sigma)+o(1)
. The bounds at the end of the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
29
]
actually show that
E(\sigma)\leq B(\sigma)+O\left(\frac{\ln\ln n}{\ln n}\right)
. Combining this observation with Theorem
1.6
would give the bound
|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq d_{\lambda}^{\alpha+O\left(\frac{\ln\ln n}{\ln n}\right)}
if
\cyc(\sigma)\leq n^{\alpha}
, which is another formulation of the character bound from Lifshitz and Marmor
[
37
, Theorem 1.7]
. The first aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.7
, which provides a sharper bound.

An equivalent formulation (and a more direct proof) of the bound
E(\sigma)\leq B(\sigma)+o(1)
is given in
[
27
, Lemma 2.8]
: fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha>0
, then as
n\to\infty
, for all
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
satisfying
\cyc(\sigma)\leq n^{\alpha}
we have
E(\sigma)\leq\alpha+\varepsilon
.

In Proposition
5.1
(and Corollary
5.2
), we show the following stronger result: if
2\leq\cyc(\sigma)\leq n^{\alpha}
then
E(\sigma)\leq\alpha
. The proof relies on a convexity argument and a telescoping product. Combining it with Theorem
1.6
, it allows us to extend the character bounds of Lifshitz and Marmor
[
37
, Theorem 1.7]
– which hold when
\cyc(\sigma)\leq\frac{n}{(\ln n)^{C}}
for some constant
C>0
– to permutations with
\cyc(\sigma)=o(n)
. This is the content of Theorem
1.7
.

5.1
A simple bound on orbit growth exponents

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. We begin by proving the following surprisingly simple bound on
E(\sigma)
that depends only on the number of cycles
\cyc(\sigma)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then

E(\sigma)\leq\frac{\ln\left(\max(\cyc(\sigma),2)\right)}{\ln n}.

(5.3)

We first present a simple consequence of Proposition
5.1
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Assume that

\cyc(\sigma)\geq 2
, and let
0<\alpha\leq 1
such that
\cyc(\sigma)=n^{\alpha}
. Then

E(\sigma)\leq\alpha.

(5.4)

Proof.

Since
\cyc(\sigma)\geq 2
, from Proposition
5.1
we have

E(\sigma)\leq\frac{\ln\cyc(\sigma)}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln n^{\alpha}}{\ln n}=\alpha
.
∎

We now build the tools that will be needed to prove Proposition
5.1
.

Let
\imin:=\imin(\sigma)=\min\left\{i\geq 1{\;:\;}f_{i}\geq 1\right\}
be the length of the smallest cycle in
\sigma
.
We can rewrite the definition of the orbit growth sequence in terms of
\imin
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then

e_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ for }i<\imin\\
\frac{\ln\left(\imin f_{\imin}\right)}{\ln n}&\text{ for }i=\imin\\
\frac{\ln(\Sigma_{i}/\Sigma_{i-1})}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln(1+if_{i}/\Sigma_{i-1})}{\ln n}&\text{ for }\imin<i\leq n.\end{cases}

(5.5)

Proof.

By definition of
\imin
we have
f_{i}=0
for
i<\imin
, so for
i<\imin
we have
\Sigma_{i}=0
and therefore
e_{i}=0
. It follows that
n^{e_{\imin}}=n^{e_{1}+\ldots+e_{\imin}}=\Sigma_{\imin}=\imin f_{\imin}
, and we deduce the formula for
e_{\imin}
taking logarithms.

Let now
i>\imin
. Then
\Sigma_{i-1}\geq\Sigma_{\imin}\geq 1
and therefore

\Sigma_{i}=n^{e_{1}+\ldots+e_{i}}=n^{e_{1}+\ldots+e_{i-1}}n^{e_{i}}=\Sigma_{i-1}n^{e_{i}}.

(5.6)

We then obtain the first equality for
e_{i}
by dividing both sides by
\Sigma_{i-1}
and taking the logarithm, and the second equality by rewriting
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma_{i-1}+if_{i}
.
∎

We can now prove a general simple bound on
E(\sigma)
. We recall that
F_{i}=f_{1}+\ldots+f_{i}
is the number of cycles of
\sigma
of length at most
i
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then

E(\sigma)\leq\frac{1}{\imin}\frac{\ln\left(\imin\cyc(\sigma)\right)}{\ln n}.

(5.7)

Proof.

Let
i>\imin
. By Lemma
5.3
, and using that
(1+x)^{a}\leq 1+ax
for
x\geq 0
and
0\leq a\leq 1
, we have

\frac{\imin}{i}e_{i}=\frac{\ln\left(\left(1+\frac{if_{i}}{\Sigma_{i-1}}\right)^{\imin/i}\right)}{\ln n}\leq\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac{\imin f_{i}}{\Sigma_{i-1}}\right)}{\ln n}.

(5.8)

Moreover since
f_{j}=0
for
1\leq j<\imin
, we have
\Sigma_{i-1}\geq\imin F_{i-1}
, and therefore

\frac{\imin}{i}e_{i}\leq\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac{f_{i}}{F_{i-1}}\right)}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln\left(F_{i}/F_{i-1}\right)}{\ln n}.

(5.9)

We deduce, recalling from Lemma
5.3
that
e_{\imin}=\frac{\ln\left(\imin f_{\imin}\right)}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln\left(\imin F_{\imin}\right)}{\ln n}
, that

\begin{split}E(\sigma)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{e_{i}}{i}&=\frac{e_{\imin}}{\imin}+\frac{1}{\imin}\sum_{i>\imin}\frac{\imin}{i}e_{i}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\imin\ln n}\left(\ln\left(\imin F_{\imin}\right)+\sum_{\imin<i\leq n}\ln\left(\frac{F_{i}}{F_{i-1}}\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\imin\ln n}\ln\left(\imin F_{\imin}\prod_{\imin<i\leq n}\frac{F_{i}}{F_{i-1}}\right)\\
&=\frac{\ln(\imin F_{n})}{\imin\ln n},\end{split}

(5.10)

which concludes the proof since
F_{n}=\cyc(\sigma)
.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
5.1
.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

First, if
\imin(\sigma)=1
, this follows immediately from Proposition
5.4
.

Second, if
\cyc(\sigma)=1
then
\sigma
is an
n
-cycle; in this case we have
E(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n}\leq\frac{\ln 2}{\ln n}
and the result also holds.

Assume finally that
\imin\geq 2
and
\cyc(\sigma)\geq 2
. Since
x\mapsto\frac{\ln x}{x-1}
is decreasing on
[2,\infty)
, we have

\ln\cyc(\sigma)\geq\ln 2=\frac{\ln 2}{2-1}\geq\frac{\ln\imin}{\imin-1}.

(5.11)

Therefore
(\imin-1)\ln\cyc(\sigma)\geq\ln\imin
, i.e.
\imin\ln\cyc(\sigma)\geq\ln\imin+\ln\cyc(\sigma)
, which rewrites as

\ln\cyc(\sigma)\geq\frac{1}{\imin}\ln\left(\imin\cyc(\sigma)\right).

(5.12)

Dividing both sides by
\ln n
, and applying Proposition
5.4
, we conclude that

\frac{\ln\left(\cyc(\sigma)\right)}{\ln n}\geq\frac{1}{\imin}\frac{\ln\left(\imin\cyc(\sigma)\right)}{\ln n}\geq E(\sigma).\qed

(5.13)

5.2
Character bound

Combining Theorem
1.6
and Proposition
5.1
, we can prove Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

By Theorem
1.6
and Proposition
5.1
, we have (since
E(\sigma)\leq 1
)

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln n}\right)}\leq d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)+\frac{C}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{\frac{\ln\left(\max(\cyc(\sigma),2)\right)}{\ln n}+\frac{C}{\ln n}}.

(5.14)

This allows us to conclude since
\ln\left(\max(\cyc(\sigma),2)\right)\leq\ln\left(2\cyc(\sigma)\right)\leq\ln\left(\cyc(\sigma)\right)+1
.
∎

6
Bounds on the Witten zeta function

Recall from (
1.10
) our definition of the Witten zeta function: for
n\geq 1
,
A\subseteq\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
, and
s\geq 0
,

\zeta_{n}(A,s):=\sum_{\lambda\in A}\frac{1}{(d_{\lambda})^{s}}.

(6.1)

For
k\in[0,\infty)
, define

\Lambda^{n}(k)=\left\{\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq n-k\right\},

(6.2)

where we recall that
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
respectively denote the length of the first row and column.

Liebeck and Shalev
[
34
, Proposition 2.5]
proved that if
s>0
and
k
are fixed, then as
n\to\infty
we have the bound

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(k),s)=O\left(n^{-ks}\right).

(6.3)

This bound is very convenient. It was used for example by Larsen and Shalev
[
29
]
in combination with the Diaconis–Shahshahani upper bound lemma (
[
13
, Lemma 1, page 24 in Chapter 3B]
) to derive mixing time results from character bounds.

For some applications that we will consider, we want asymptotic bounds on
\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(k),s_{n})
where the argument
s_{n}
is not fixed, but tends to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
instead. Bounds of this kind were proved by Hough
[
24
, Proposition 4]
, which we realized only after writing the first version of this paper. We note that Proposition
1.9
could also be derived from the estimates of Hough.

The goal of this section is to further refine these estimates. Our strategy is similar to that of
[
34
]
, except that we consider the sums
\sum_{\lambda\in A}d_{\lambda}^{-s}
associated to a more efficient decomposition into subsets
A
of Young diagrams. More precisely, Liebeck and Shalev split the diagrams
\lambda
according to whether
n-\lambda_{1}
is larger than
n/3
; we replace
n/3
by
n^{2/3}
, which is more suited for our purposes. On the other hand, Hough’s proofs were different, and relied on the Larsen–Shalev bound on virtual degrees (recalled in Theorem
1.2
).

Our arguments are elementary and we give self-contained proofs.

6.1
Elementary bounds on dimensions

We start with elementary lower bounds on dimensions.

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists
n_{0}\geq 1
such that the following holds. Let
n\geq n_{0}
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Denote
r=r(\lambda):=n-\lambda_{1}
.

(a)

If
\lambda_{1}\geq n-n^{2/3}
, then
n^{r}\geq d_{\lambda}\geq n^{r}\frac{e^{-3r^{2}/n}}{r!}\geq n^{r/6}
.

(b)

If
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}
, then
d_{\lambda}\geq e^{c_{0}r}
, where
c_{0}>0
is a small universal constant.

Proof.

(a)

We have
H(\lambda,\lambda)\geq H(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})=(n-r)!
. We deduce from the hook length formula that
d_{\lambda}\leq n!/(n-r)!\leq n^{r}
.
Conversely, the number of standard tableaux of
\lambda
is at least the number of standard tableaux of
\lambda
with
1,2,\ldots,r
placed first on the first row (we have
\lambda_{1}\geq r
by assumption), which is equal to
\binom{n-r}{r}d_{\lambda_{\geq 2}}
. We deduce that, for
n
large enough, uniformly in
\lambda\vdash n
with
r\leq n^{2/3}
,

d_{\lambda}\geq\binom{n-r}{r}=\frac{1}{r!}(n-r)^{\downarrow r}\geq\frac{1}{r!}(n-2r)^{r}\geq\frac{1}{r!}n^{r}e^{-3r^{2}/n}.

(6.4)

Finally,
3r/n\leq 1
so
e^{-3r^{2}/n}\geq e^{-r}\geq n^{-r/6}
, and
n^{r}/r!\geq n^{r}/r^{r}\geq n^{r}/(n^{2/3})^{r}=n^{r/3}
. Therefore, the right hand side of (
6.4
) is larger than
n^{r/3}n^{-r/6}=n^{r/6}
.

(b)

Assume first that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}<n/10
. Then, since every hook length in
\lambda
is at most
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1}^{\prime}-1\leq 2\lambda_{1}
, we have
H(\lambda,\lambda)\leq(2\lambda_{1})^{n}\leq(n/5)^{n}
. Since
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
, we deduce from the hook length formula that

d_{\lambda}=\frac{n!}{H(\lambda,\lambda)}\geq\frac{(n/e)^{n}}{(n/5)^{n}}=(5/e)^{n}\geq(5/e)^{r}.

(6.5)

Now assume that
\lambda_{1}\geq n/10
and
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}
. We may assume that
n\geq 3
. Since the case
\lambda=[n]
is trivial, we may also assume that
r\geq 1
. Set
p:=\max(\left\lfloor r/40\right\rfloor,1)
. Then
\lambda
contains a subdiagram
\mu
which has
2p
boxes on the first row and
p
boxes over the first row.
Then, using that the skew diagram
d_{\mu\backslash[p]}
consists of two disconnected parts of size
p
, we obtain, counting the number of standard tableaux that

d_{\lambda}\geq d_{\mu}\geq d_{\mu\backslash[p]}=\binom{2p}{p}d_{\mu_{\geq 2}}\geq\binom{2p}{p}\geq e^{p/2}\geq e^{r/160},

(6.6)

which concludes the proof.
∎

6.2
Asymptotics of the Witten zeta function

In this section we prove Theorem
1.8
and Proposition
1.9
. Recall that
p(n)
denotes the number of integer partitions of the integer
n
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
c>0
be fixed. We have

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(n^{2/3}),c/\ln n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(6.7)

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
(b), with the same constant
c_{0}
, we have as
n\to\infty
:

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(n^{2/3}),c/\ln n)=\sum_{r\geq n^{2/3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}\\
r(\lambda)=r\text{ and }\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(d_{\lambda})^{-c/\ln n}\leq\sum_{r\geq n^{2/3}}p(r)e^{-c_{0}rc/\ln n}.

(6.8)

Therefore, for
n
large enough, we have:

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(n^{2/3}),c/\ln n)\leq\sum_{r\geq n^{2/3}}p(n)e^{-c_{0}n^{2/3}c/\ln n}\leq np(n)e^{-n^{0.6}}.

(6.9)

Hence, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, using that
p(n)=e^{O(\sqrt{n})}
, we get:

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(n^{2/3}),c/\ln n)\leq e^{O(\sqrt{n})}e^{-n^{0.6}}\to 0.\qed

(6.10)

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
c>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be fixed. There exists
r_{0}=r_{0}(c,\varepsilon)
such that, for
n
large enough, we have

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(r_{0}),c/\ln n)\leq\varepsilon.

(6.11)

Proof.

Let
R\geq 1
. Let
n\geq 3
, which is assumed to be large in the whole proof. By Lemmas
6.2
and
6.1
(a), we have:

\begin{split}\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(R),c/\ln n)&=\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(R),c/\ln n)-\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(n^{2/3}),c/\ln n)+o(1)\\
&=\sum_{R\leq r<n^{2/3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}r(\lambda)=r\end{subarray}}(d_{\lambda})^{-c/\ln n}+o(1)\\
&\leq\sum_{R\leq r<n^{2/3}}p(r)\left(n^{-r/6}\right)^{c/\ln n}+o(1)\\
&\leq\sum_{r=R}^{\infty}p(r)e^{-rc/6}+o(1).\end{split}

(6.12)

The last sum is finite since
p(r)=e^{O(\sqrt{r})}
. Furthermore,
\sum\limits_{r=R}^{\infty}p(r)e^{-rc/6}\rightarrow 0
as
R\to\infty
, and the desired result follows.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Fix
c,r\geq 0
. As
n\to\infty
, we have

e^{-cr}\leq\min_{\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}r(\lambda)=r}d_{\lambda}^{-c/\ln n}\leq\max_{\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}r(\lambda)=r}d_{\lambda}^{-c/\ln n}\leq e^{-cr}+o(1).

(6.13)

Proof.

Since
r
is fixed,
r\leq n^{2/3}
for
n
large enough. We have
(n^{r})^{-c/\ln n}=e^{-cr}
and, as
n\to\infty
,
(e^{-3r^{2}/n}/r!)^{-c/\ln n}=1+o(1)
, and hence
(n^{r}e^{-3r^{2}/n}/r!)^{-c/\ln n}=e^{-cr}(1+o(1))=e^{-cr}+o(1)
. The result then follows from Lemma
6.1
(a).
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
c>0
and let
k,R
be two fixed integers such that
0\leq k\leq R
. We have

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(k),c/\ln n)-\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(R+1),c/\ln n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\sum_{r=k}^{R}p(r)e^{-cr}.

(6.14)

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
6.4
, since the sum has a bounded number of terms.
∎

For
k\geq 0
and
x\in[0,1)
, we set

\Phi_{\geq k}(x)=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}p(r)x^{r}.

(6.15)

For
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
, we set

\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(k)=\left\{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}{\;:\;}\max(\lambda_{1},\lambda^{\prime}_{1})\leq n-k\right\}.

(6.16)

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
c>0
and
k\geq 0
be fixed. Then

\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{n}(\Lambda_{\textup{sym}}^{n}(k),c/\ln n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\Phi_{\geq k}(e^{-c}).

(6.17)

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
6.3
, there exists
r_{0}=r_{0}(c,\varepsilon)
such that, for
n
large enough, we have
\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(r_{0}),c/\ln n)\leq\varepsilon
. Up to taking
r_{0}
larger we also have
\sum_{r=r_{0}}^{\infty}p(r)e^{-cr}\leq\varepsilon
, i.e.
\Phi(e^{-c})-\sum_{r=0}^{r_{0}-1}p(r)e^{-cr}\leq\varepsilon
. We may also assume that
r_{0}\geq k
. Since a diagram and its conjugate have the same dimension, by symmetry we have for
n
large

\begin{split}\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(k)\backslash\Lambda^{n}(r_{0}),c/\ln n)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(k)\backslash\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(r_{0})}d_{\lambda}^{-c/\ln n}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(k)}d_{\lambda}^{-c/\ln n}\\
&\leq\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(k)\backslash\Lambda^{n}(r_{0}),c/\ln n)+\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}(r_{0}),c/\ln n),\end{split}

(6.18)

where we have used that, if
\lambda_{1}\geq n-r_{0}
, then necessarily
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, using also Lemma
6.5
, we have for
n
large enough:

\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(k)}d_{\lambda}^{-c/\ln n}-\Phi_{\geq k}(e^{-c})\right|\leq 3\varepsilon

(6.19)

This concludes the proof.
∎

The asymptotic results for the Witten zeta function immediately follow, as we now see.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

This is the case
k=0
of Proposition
6.6
, noting that
\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(0)
is the set of all partitions of
n
.
∎

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Let
(s_{n})_{n\geq 3}
be a sequence of positive real numbers. Then

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{sym}}^{n}(k),s_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ if and only if }\hskip 10.00002pts_{n}\ln n\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty.

(6.20)

Proof.

Let
k\geq 1
. By Proposition
6.6
, for any
c>0
we have

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(k),c/\ln n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}2\Phi_{\geq k}(e^{-c}).

(6.21)

Since, for
n

6.3
Asymptotics at larger scales

In Proposition
1.9
, we showed that if
(s_{n})_{n\geq 3}
is such that
s_{n}\ln n\to\infty
, then
\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),s_{n})\to 0
. We now quantify the speed of convergence to 0. This will be useful later, in the proof of Theorem
9.2
in which we investigate the number of vertices of some random maps.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Let
t>0
and
u>0
. Then

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),t+u)\leq\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),t)(n-1)^{-u}.

(6.22)

Proof.

Since
\min\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1)}d_{\lambda}=n-1
, we have

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),t+u)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1)}d_{\lambda}^{-(t+u)}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1)}d_{\lambda}^{-t}(n-1)^{-u}=\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),t)(n-1)^{-u}.\qed

(6.23)

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
(s_{n})_{n\geq 3}
be a sequence of positive real numbers. Assume that
s_{n}\ln n\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Then, as
n\to\infty
, we have:

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),s_{n})\sim 2(n-1)^{-s_{n}}.

(6.24)

Proof.

First observe that

\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),s_{n})\geq d_{[n-1,1]}^{-s_{n}}+d_{[2,1^{n-2}]}^{-s_{n}}=2(n-1)^{-s_{n}},

(6.25)

so we only have to provide an upper bound to
\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),s_{n})
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
c>0
be large enough so that
\Phi_{\geq 1}(e^{-c})\leq(1+\varepsilon)e^{-c}
. Applying Lemma
6.8
with
t=c/\ln n
and
u=s_{n}-c/\ln n
, we get that

\begin{split}\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),s_{n})&\leq\zeta_{n}(\Lambda^{n}_{\textup{sym}}(1),c/\ln n)(n-1)^{-s_{n}+c/\ln n}\\
&=(2\Phi_{\geq 1}(e^{-c})+o(1))(e^{c}+o(1))(n-1)^{-s_{n}}\\
&\leq(2(1+\varepsilon)+o(1))(n-1)^{-s_{n}}.\end{split}

(6.26)

This concludes the proof since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary.
∎

7
Products of two conjugacy invariant random permutations

7.1
The Diaconis–Shahshahani upper bound lemma

Let
n\geq 1
and
t\geq 1
. Let
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{t})
be a family of conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Let
\sgn(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\sgn\left({\mathcal{C}}_{i}\right)
and let
{\mathfrak{E}}\left(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}\right)
be the coset of
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with sign
\sgn(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})
. Note in particular that, for
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, we have

\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}(\sigma)=\frac{1+\sgn(\sigma)\sgn(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}{n!}.

(7.1)

Let
(\sigma_{i})_{1\leq i\leq t}
be independent variables such that
\sigma_{i}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq t
. We want to understand when the product
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{t}
is close to uniform in
{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})
. In other words we want to control the quantity

\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}):=\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\cdots*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\;,\;\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}\left(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}\right)}\right),

(7.2)

and in particular understand under which conditions it is close to 0.

The Diaconis–Shahshahani upper bound lemma (see
[
13
, Lemma 1 on Page 24 in Chapter 3B]
) can also be written for products of elements in different conjugacy classes, see
[
29
, Lemma 6.6]
. Different versions are also written in
[
20
,
8
,
24
]
. A detailed and clear proof when
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}})
was given by Hough
[
24
, Section 2]
.

We state here a general version that also takes into account the signs of the conjugacy classes.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
t\geq 1
. Let
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{t})
be a family of conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Then

4\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{**}}\left(d_{\lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}}_{i})\right)^{2}.

(7.3)

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[
24
, Equation (4)]
. We have

\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\left|\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\cdots*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}(\sigma)-\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}\left(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}\right)}(\sigma)\right|.

(7.4)

Observe that the Fourier coefficients of
\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}\left(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}\right)}
evaluated at the trivial representation
[n]
and the sign representation
[1^{n}]
are

\widehat{\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}}([n])=1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}}([1^{n}])=\sgn(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}),

(7.5)

and
\widehat{\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}}(\lambda)=0
for
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{**}
.
Hence, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on (
7.4
) and then the Plancherel identity, we obtain

\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{**}}\left(\ch^{\lambda}(\Id)\right)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left(\frac{\ch^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}}_{i})}{\ch^{\lambda}(\Id)}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}}_{i}))^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(7.6)

The result follows.
∎

7.2
A sufficient condition for a product of two permutations to be close to uniform

We are particularly interested in the case of
t=2
conjugacy classes. We will write results with asymptotic notation (as
n\to\infty
); writing also
f(n)=\omega_{+}(g(n))
if
g(n)=o(f(n))
and
f(n)>0
(for
n
large enough).

Plugging the bounds from Theorem
1.6
into Lemma
7.1
immediately gives the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
be conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and denote
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2})
. Then

4\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2(E({\mathcal{C}}_{1})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2})-1)+O(1/\ln n)}.

(7.7)

Using our bounds on the Witten zeta function we deduce the following sufficient condition for
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}
to be asymptotically close to uniform.

Proposition 7.3
.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
be two conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, and denote
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})
. Assume that as
n\to\infty
we have

E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n).

(7.8)

Then

\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(7.9)

Proof.

Plugging the assumption
E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)
into Lemma
7.2
, and Proposition
6.7
, we obtain

4\delta(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)}=o(1).\qed

7.3
Some character bounds

We gather here some bounds on
E({\mathcal{C}})
, that we will later use in applications in combination with Proposition
7.3
.

Proposition 7.4
.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.

(a)

Assume that
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=O(1)
and
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=o(n)
. Then

E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{2}-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right).

(7.11)

(b)

Assume that
\cyc({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=o(\sqrt{n})
. Then

E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{2}-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right).

(7.12)

(c)

Assume that
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=o(n)
. Then

E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right).

(7.13)

Proof.

In this proof we write
e_{i},f_{i}
for
e_{i}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}),f_{i}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})
, and
f=\max(f_{1},1)
.

(a)

Since
f=O(1)
and
f_{2}=o(n)
, we have
e_{1}=\frac{\ln f}{\ln n}=O\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)
and

e_{2}\leq e_{1}+e_{2}=\frac{\ln(\max(1,f_{1}+2f_{2}))}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln o(n)}{\ln n}=1-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right),

(7.14)

so

E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{6}\left(1-e_{2}\right)+\frac{2}{3}e_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)=\frac{1}{2}-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right).

(7.15)

(b)

This follows from Proposition
5.1
, since
\frac{\ln o(\sqrt{n})}{\ln n}=\frac{1}{2}-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)
.

(c)

We have

E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\leq e_{1}+\frac{1-e_{1}}{2}=1-\frac{1-e_{1}}{2}=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ln f}{\ln n}\right)=1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n},

(7.16)

and by assumption
f=o(n)
so
\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln\omega_{+}(1)}{\ln n}=\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)
. The result follows. ∎

8
Characterization of fixed-point free conjugacy classes that mix in two steps

8.1
Random walks and fixed-point free conjugacy classes

Let
({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})
be a sequence of fixed-point free conjugacy classes (that is, such that
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=0
). We consider the sequence of random walks on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, with respective increment measures
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}
. In this section we restrict to families
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{t})
where all
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
are identical (to some conjugacy class
{\mathcal{C}}
). We write
{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}},t)
for
{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})
and
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)
for
\delta({\mathcal{C}})
, that is

\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{C}},t)=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\mathfrak{A}_{n}&\text{ if }{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\mathfrak{A}_{n}\text{ and }t\text{ is odd}\\
\mathfrak{A}_{n}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

(8.1)

is the coset of
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
on which the walk is supported after
t
steps, and

\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)=\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*t},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)},t)}\right)

(8.2)

for
t\geq 0
.

Note that after 1 step the walk (started at
\Id
) is concentrated on
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
so
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(1)=1-o(1)
and the walk cannot have mixed yet. Larsen and Shalev proved in
[
29
]
that the mixing time of such a sequence of walks is 2 or 3, i.e. (in light of what precedes) that
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(3)\to 0
. This settled a conjecture due to Lulov and Pak (
[
39
, Conjecture 4.1]
).

A natural refinement is then to understand for which conjugacy classes the mixing time is 2 and for which ones it is 3; in other words to understand when we have
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(2)\to 0
. The goal of this section is to prove such a characterization, Theorem
1.10
.

8.2
Bounds on the tail of the number of fixed points

A way to prove a lower bound on the total variation distance between two measures
\mu
and
\nu
is to find a good
splitting
event
A
, which is an event
A
such that
\mu(A)
is large and
\nu(A)
is small. For random transpositions, Diaconis and Shahshahani considered the number of fixed points. Here we also consider the number of fixed points but in a different way. We show that if the supports of permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
have order
n
transpositions and
x_{1}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}
,
x_{2}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}
are independent, then in the product permutation
x_{1}x_{2}
many transpositions compensate, leading to a significantly higher probability to have many fixed points than for a uniform permutation.
There are strong hints that in this case the distribution of the number of fixed points is asymptotically not
\Poiss(1)
such as asymptotic bounds on the second moment in
[
25
, Section 3]
, but this is not enough to conclude the non-convergence in distribution to
\Poiss(1)
, and in this section we prove the non-convergence.

For
n\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
we set

E_{m}=E_{m}^{(n)}=\left\{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}{\;:\;}f_{1}(\sigma)\geq m\right\}.

(8.3)

For
n\geq 1
we set
X=X_{n}\sim\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
,
Y=Y_{n}\sim\Unif_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, and
Z=Z_{n}\sim\Poiss(1)
.

We recall a classical fact on the distribution of the number of fixed points of random permutations, usually attributed to Goncharov.

Lemma 8.1

(
[
21
]
)
.

We have

\dtv\left({\mathcal{L}}(f_{1}(Y_{n})),\Poiss(1)\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(8.4)

There are many proofs of this result and the convergence is very fast, see the discussions in
[
11
]
and
[
19
]
.
Here we will only need rough bounds on the tails for the uniform distribution on the alternating group
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let us fix
m\geq 0
. Then there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(m)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have

\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}(E_{m})\leq\frac{3}{m!}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\Unif_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}(E_{m})\leq\frac{3}{m!}.

(8.5)

Proof.

We prove the result for
\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}(E_{m})
; the same arguments apply to
\Unif_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}(E_{m})
, up to replacing
X_{n}
by a variable that is uniform on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
.

First, since
|{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}|=2|{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}|
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n})\geq m)\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(Y_{n})\geq m)
for all
n
. Moreover, by Lemma
8.1
, for
n
large enough we have

2{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(Y_{n})\geq m)\leq e{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{n}\geq m)=\sum_{j\geq m}\frac{1}{j!}\leq\frac{3}{m!}.

(8.6)

This concludes the proof since
{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n})\geq m)=\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}(E_{m})
.
∎

Let us now show that the cancellations of transpositions after two steps lead to many fixed points.
The use of consecutive transpositions in the next proof is inspired by the arguments with unseparated pairs in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1/2
and
m\geq 2
even. There exists
n_{1}=n_{1}(\alpha,m)
such that for every
n\geq n_{1}
and every conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\min(f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}),f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}))\geq\alpha n
, we have

\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}}(E_{m})\geq\frac{C(m,\alpha)}{(m/2)!},

(8.7)

where
C(m,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in[\alpha,1/2]}e^{-2\alpha\gamma}(2\alpha\gamma)^{m/2}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\alpha^{2}}(2\alpha^{2})^{m/2}
.

Proof.

First, observe that

C(m,\alpha):=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in[\alpha,1/2]}e^{-2\alpha\gamma}(2\alpha\gamma)^{m/2}=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\delta\in[2\alpha^{2},\alpha]}e^{-\delta}\delta^{m/2}.

(8.8)

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\ni\delta\mapsto e^{-\delta}\delta^{m/2}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Since
m/2\geq\delta
by assumption, for
\delta\in[2\alpha^{2},\alpha]
we have
g^{\prime}(\delta)=(m/2-\delta)e^{-\delta}\delta^{m/2-1}\geq 0
.
g
is therefore increasing on
[2\alpha^{2},\alpha]
, which implies that
C(m,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\alpha^{2}}(2\alpha^{2})^{m/2}
.

We prove the result by contradiction. Fix
m\geq 2
even, and assume that there exists a sequence
({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})_{n\geq 1}
with
\min(f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}),f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}))\geq\alpha n
such that, for infinitely many values of
n
,
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}}(E_{m})<C(m,\alpha)/(m/2)!
.
Up to extracting a subsequence, one can also assume that there exists
\gamma\in[\alpha,1/2]
such that
\frac{f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})}{n}\rightarrow\gamma
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
q:=2\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha n}{2}\right\rfloor
and let
x_{1}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}
,
x_{2}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}}
be independent. Since
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})\geq q
, one can assume without loss of generality that

x_{1}:=(1\;\;2)(3\;\;4)\cdots(2q-1\;\;2q)\sigma,

(8.9)

where
\sigma
is a permutation of the integers from
2q+1
to
n
.

We denote by
N_{n}
the (random) number of transpositions
(2i-1\;\;2i)
for
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,q\right\}
which are also transpositions of
x_{2}
.
We claim that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

N_{n}\overset{(d)}{\rightarrow}\Poiss(2\alpha\gamma).

(8.10)

Assuming (
8.10
), observe that
f_{1}(x_{1}x_{2})\geq 2N_{n}
, since each transposition common to
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
corresponds to two fixed points of
x_{1}x_{2}
. Hence, one gets, for all fixed
m\geq 1
:

\mathbb{P}(f_{1}(x_{1}x_{2})\geq m)\geq\mathbb{P}(N_{n}\geq m/2)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{P}(\Poiss(2\alpha\gamma)\geq m/2)\geq e^{-2\alpha\gamma}\frac{(2\alpha\gamma)^{m/2}}{(m/2)!},

(8.11)

so, for
n
large enough (depending on
m
and
\alpha
), we get

\mathbb{P}(f_{1}(x_{1}x_{2})\geq m)\geq\frac{e^{-2\alpha\gamma}}{2}\frac{(2\alpha\gamma)^{m/2}}{(m/2)!}\geq\frac{C(m,\alpha)}{(m/2)!}.

(8.12)

We now turn to the proof of (
8.10
). For
r\geq 1
, let
M_{r}^{(n)}:=\mathbb{E}[(N_{n})^{\downarrow r}]
be the
r
-th factorial moment of
N_{n}
. For any
r\geq 1
fixed, we get by symmetry

M_{r}^{(n)}=q^{\downarrow r}\mathbb{P}\left((1\;\;2),\ldots,(2r-1\;\;2r)\text{ appear in }x_{2}\right).

(8.13)

Now recall that the cardinality of a conjugacy class
{\mathcal{C}}
of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is
\frac{n!}{\prod_{k\geq 1}k^{f_{k}({\mathcal{C}})}(f_{k}({\mathcal{C}}))!}
. We thus get, for
n
large enough so that
q\geq r
:

\begin{split}\frac{M_{r}^{(n)}}{q^{\downarrow r}}&=\frac{(n-2r)!}{2^{f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})-r}(f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})-r)!\prod_{k\geq 3}k^{f_{k}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})}f_{k}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})!}\left(\frac{n!}{\prod_{k\geq 2}k^{f_{k}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})}f_{k}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})!}\right)^{-1}\\
&=2^{r}\frac{f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})^{\downarrow r}}{n^{\downarrow 2r}}\\
&\sim 2^{r}n^{-2r}f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})^{r}\\
&\sim\left(\frac{2\gamma}{n}\right)^{r}.\end{split}

(8.14)

Plugging it into (
8.13
) and using that
q\sim\alpha n
, one gets

M_{r}^{(n)}\sim(\alpha n)^{r}\left(\frac{2\gamma}{n}\right)^{r}=(2\alpha\gamma)^{r}.

(8.15)

One recognizes the
r
-th factorial moment of
\Poiss(2\alpha\gamma)
. Since the Poisson distributions are characterized by their moments, this shows (
8.10
), and thus concludes the proof.
∎

We can now use this result to prove that
E_{m}
is a splitting event.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
0<\alpha<1/2
. There exists
c=c(\alpha)>0
,
m=m(\alpha)\geq 1
and
n_{2}=n_{2}(\alpha)
such that for every

, and every conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}(E_{m(\alpha)})-\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}(E_{m(\alpha)})\geq c(\alpha),

(8.16)

where
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2})
. In particular

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}\right)\geq c(\alpha).

(8.17)

Proof.

First observe that for any even integer
m\geq 2
, we have, setting
\beta=e^{-2\alpha^{2}}\alpha^{2}
,

\begin{split}m!\left(\frac{e^{-2\alpha^{2}}}{2}\frac{(2\alpha^{2})^{m/2}}{(m/2)!}\right)=m^{\downarrow(m/2)}\frac{e^{-2\alpha^{2}}}{2}(2\alpha^{2})^{m/2}\geq\beta^{m/2}m^{\downarrow(m/2)}&\geq\beta^{m/2}(m/2)^{m/2}\\
&=\left(\beta m/2\right)^{m/2}.\end{split}

(8.18)

We now fix
m(\alpha):=2\left\lceil\frac{5}{\beta}\right\rceil
. Then

m(\alpha)!\frac{C(m(\alpha),\alpha)}{(m(\alpha)/2)!}\geq\left(\frac{\beta m(\alpha)}{2}\right)^{m(\alpha)/2}\geq\frac{\beta m(\alpha)}{2}\geq 5,

(8.19)

where
C(m,\alpha)
is the same constant as in Lemma

.
We deduce from Lemma
8.3
that for
n
large enough

\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}(E_{m(\alpha)})\geq\frac{5}{m(\alpha)!}.

(8.20)

We conclude using Lemma
8.2
, that for
n
large enough

\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}(E_{m(\alpha)})-\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}})}(E_{m(\alpha)})\geq\frac{5}{m(\alpha)!}-\frac{3}{m(\alpha)!}=\frac{2}{m(\alpha)!}=:c(\alpha)>0.\qed

(8.21)

Proof of Theorem
1.10

.

Proof of Theorem

.

First, by Proposition

9
Application to random surfaces

The convergence in distribution of the product of two permutations that are uniform in their conjugacy classes has an interesting application to random maps, which was first spotted by Gamburd
[
20
]
(see also Chmutov and Pittel
[
8
]
and Budzinski, Curien and Petri
[
7
]
). The main idea is to code a map by two permutations
\alpha,\beta
, where
\alpha
codes the polygon structure of the faces of the map, and
\beta
the way these polygons are glued together. More precisely, consider a collection of
n
oriented polygons with various numbers of sides, the total number of sides being an even number, say
N
. Let
n_{j}
be the number of polygons with
j
sides, so that
\sum_{j}n_{j}=n
and
\sum_{j}jn_{j}=N
. Labeling the sides of the polygons by
\{1,\ldots,N\}
uniformly at random, we define a permutation
\alpha
of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
by
\alpha(i)=j
if the side labeled
j
immediately follows the side labeled
i
around a polygon in counterclockwise order. Hence, the cycle structure of
\alpha
codes the number of sides of the polygons. We now build a surface
M_{N}
from it by gluing these
N
oriented sides by pairs, uniformly at random. This gluing can be seen as a uniform fixed-point free involution
\beta
of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
, where
\beta(i)=j
(and thus
\beta(j)=i
) if the sides labeled
i
and
j
are glued together. One can then check that each cycle of the product
\alpha\beta
corresponds to a vertex of the resulting surface, and that the number of orbits of the subgroup generated by
\alpha
and
\beta
is the number of connected components of
M_{N}
.

Since the labels are random, the permutation
\alpha
is uniform in the conjugacy class corresponding to the
n_{j}
’s, and
\beta
is a uniform fixed-point free involution,
\alpha
and
\beta
being independent.

Both
[
20
]
and
[
8
]
rely on this permutation point of view and on representation theory. Gamburd’s result is for regular graphs (with degree
d\geq 3
, i.e. when
n_{d}=N/d
and
n_{j}=0
for
j\neq d
) and relies on the character estimates of Fomin and Lulov
[
17
]
. They show that, if
\alpha\sim[m^{N/m}]
for some fixed
m\geq 3
, then the permutation
\alpha\beta
is close to being uniform (for the total variation distance).
Chmutov and Pittel then used the more general bounds of Larsen and Shalev
[
29
]
to prove that
\alpha\beta
is close to uniform in total variation as soon as
n_{1}=n_{2}=0
, that is when all cycles of
\alpha
have length at least 3.

Extending Gamburd’s results, Chmutov and Pittel obtain the following (
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
). Here and in what follows,
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
denotes the law of a random variable
X
.

Theorem 9.1

(
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

Assume that
n_{1}=n_{2}=0
, that is, the polygons all have at least
3
sides. Let
V_{N}
be the number of vertices of
M_{N}
, and
{\mathcal{L}}(V_{N})
its distribution. Then:

\dtv({\mathcal{L}}(V_{N}),{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{C}_{N}))=O(N^{-1}),

(9.1)

uniformly for all sequences
(n_{j})_{j\geq 3}
. Here,
\widetilde{C}_{N}
denotes the number of cycles in a uniform permutation of
{\mathfrak{A}}_{N}
(resp.
{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{N}
) if the classes
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same sign (resp. opposite signs).

They deduce from this theorem a local central limit theorem for
V_{N}
, see
[
8
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Using a probabilistic approach and relying on the peeling process of a random map, Budzinski, Curien and Petri
[
7
, Theorem 3]
show that
\dtv({\mathcal{L}}(V_{N}),{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{C}_{N}))\rightarrow 0
, under the weaker assumptions that
n_{1}=o(\sqrt{n})
and
n_{2}=o(n)
.

Since our results sharpen the Larsen–Shalev character bounds, it is no surprise that we can improve on the results of Gamburd and Chmutov–Pittel following the same strategy. It turns out that the improved bounds on the Witten zeta function are also needed, and we use Proposition
8.4
to prove Theorem
1.14
. Its proof is an adaptation of that of Theorem
1.10
.

Proof of Theorem
1.14
.

First assume that
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=o(n)
. Then, by Proposition
7.4
(a), we have
E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=1/2-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)
. Furthermore, we have
E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=1/2
, so
E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)
. We conclude using Proposition
7.3
that
\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})}\right)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
On the other hand, if
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=o(n)
does not hold, then there exists
0<a\leq 1/2
such that
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})\geq an
along a subsequence, and therefore (since
f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=n/2\geq an
), by Proposition
8.4
the convergence does not hold.
∎

In terms of random maps, Theorem
1.14
immediately implies the following theorem.

Theorem 9.2
.

Consider for all
N
a sequence
(n_{j}^{(N)})_{1\leq j\leq N}
such that
\sum_{j}jn_{j}^{(N)}=N
. Assume that
n_{1}^{(N)}=0
for all
N
, and that

. Let
V_{N}

, and
\widetilde{C}_{N}
be the number of cycles in a uniform permutation of

(resp.
{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{N}
) if the conjugacy classes of
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same sign (resp. opposite signs).
Then, as

:

\dtv({\mathcal{L}}(V_{N}),{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{C}_{N}))\rightarrow 0.

(9.2)

This provides an algebraic proof of
[

, in the case where
n_{1}^{(N)}=0
for all

. Combining Theorem

with
[

, we immediately find that, under the assumptions of Theorem

Applying our bounds carefully allows us to obtain in addition a local central limit theorem when

does not grow too fast.

In the next theorem,
C_{N}
denotes the (random) number of cycles in a uniform permutation in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
. It is known that
\mathbb{E}[C_{N}]=\ln N+O(1)
, and
\Var(C_{N})=\ln N+O(1)
.

Theorem 9.3
.

Assume that
n_{1}^{(N)}=0
for all
N
, and that
n_{2}^{(N)}=o\left(\frac{N}{(\ln N)^{3}}\right)
. Fix
a>0
. Then, uniformly for all integers
\ell
satisfying

\frac{\ell-\mathbb{E}[C_{N}]}{\sqrt{\Var(C_{N})}}\in[-a,a]

(9.3)

and the admissibility condition that
N-\ell
is even (resp. odd) if
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same sign (resp. opposite signs),
we have

\mathbb{P}(V_{N}=\ell)=\frac{\left(2+o(1)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\ell-\mathbb{E}[C_{N}]\right)^{2}}{2\Var(C_{N})}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\Var(C_{N})}},

(9.4)

where we recall that
n=\sum_{j}n_{j}
is the number of polygons.

Proof.

Observe that, if
n_{2}^{(N)}=0
for all
N
large enough, then the result immediately follows from
[
8
, Theorem 3.2]
. Therefore, it is enough to prove our result in the case where
n_{2}^{(N)}\geq 1
for all
N
.
Consider
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)}
such that
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)})=0
and
2f_{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)})=\frac{N}{\gamma_{N}}\geq 2
, where
\gamma_{N}=\omega_{+}((\ln N)^{3})
and the notation
f_{i}
is defined above (
1.2
). Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(N)}
be the conjugacy class of fixed-point free involutions.
Write
e_{i}
for
e_{i}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)})
. We have
e_{1}=0
and
e_{2}=\frac{\ln(N/\gamma_{N})}{\ln N}=1-\frac{\ln\gamma_{N}}{\ln N}
, so

E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)})=\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{e_{i}}{i}\leq\frac{e_{2}}{2}+\frac{1-e_{2}}{3}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1-e_{2}}{6}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{\ln\gamma_{N}}{\ln N}.

(9.5)

Since
E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(N)})=\frac{1}{2}
, and
\gamma_{N}\to\infty
we get:

s_{N}:=-2(E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(N)})-1)\geq\frac{1}{3}\frac{\ln\gamma_{N}}{\ln N}=\omega_{+}(1/\ln N).

(9.6)

By Proposition
6.9
, we therefore get that, for any sequence
t_{N}=O(1/\ln N)
, as
N\to\infty
,

\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2\left(E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(N)})-1\right)+t_{N}}\sim 2(N-1)^{-s_{N}+t_{N}}=O\left(\exp\left(-\frac{1}{3}\ln\gamma_{N}\right)\right)=O(\gamma_{N}^{-1/3}).

(9.7)

Plugging this into Lemma
7.2
, and using that
\gamma_{N}=\omega_{+}((\ln N)^{3})
, we get:

\dtv({\mathcal{L}}(V_{N}),{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{C}_{N}))\leq\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(N)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(N)}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(N)})}\right)=O(\gamma_{N}^{-1/6})=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln N}}\right).

(9.8)

Finally, by
[
8
, Equation (3.5)]
, uniformly for any admissible
\ell
satisfying (
9.3
), we have

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{C}_{N}=\ell)=\frac{(2+o(1))\exp\left(-\frac{(\ell-\mathbb{E}[C_{N}])^{2}}{2\Var(C_{N})}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\Var(C_{N})}}\geq(1+o(1))\frac{c_{a}}{\sqrt{\ln N}},

(9.9)

where
c_{a}=2\frac{\exp(-a^{2}/2)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}>0
depends only on
a
, and in particular, for any such
\ell
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{C}_{N}=\ell)=\omega_{+}\left(\dtv({\mathcal{L}}(V_{N}),{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{C}_{N}))\right)
. This implies that, uniformly for any such
\ell
, we have
\mathbb{P}(V_{N}=\ell)=(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{C}_{N}=\ell)
, which, in view of the equality in (
9.9
), concludes the proof.
∎

Obtaining a local central limit theorem in the general case
n_{2}^{(N)}=o(N)
would require a better control on the speed of convergence in Theorem
9.2
.

Let us finally mention that
[
7
, Proposition 14]
states that the surface
M_{N}
is connected with probability tending to
1
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

10
Further mixing time estimates

This last section consists of the proofs of Theorems
1.11
and
1.13
about mixing time estimates for specific conjugacy classes, and complementary results about products of two permutations.

10.1
Products of two permutations with constraints on cycle types

10.1.1
Mixing time of permutations with few cycles

We prove here Theorem
1.11
, which states that conjugacy classes of permutations with
o(\sqrt{n})
cycles mix in 2 steps.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

This is the same proof as for Theorem
1.10
: by Proposition
7.4
(b), we have
2E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)
, and it follows from Proposition
7.3
that
\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
.
∎

Remark 10.1
.

The condition
\cyc({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=o(\sqrt{n})
is not a characterization. For instance if
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}\sim[3^{n/3}]
, we have
\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
, while
\cyc({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\asymp n
. However, the condition
\cyc({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})=o(\sqrt{n})
is sharp. For example, if
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
is the conjugacy class of cycles of length
k=n-\lfloor\alpha\sqrt{n}\rfloor
for some fixed
\alpha>0
, one can show that the number of fixed points of permutations taken according to
\sigma\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2}
is asymptotically
\Poiss(1+\alpha^{2})
, and therefore (looking at the probability to have 0 fixed point) that
\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*2},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\right)+o(1)\geq e^{-1}-e^{-(1+\alpha^{2})}>0
.

10.1.2
Products of long cycles with other permutations

The characters of long cycles are very small: if
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is an
n
-cycle, then
E(\sigma)=1/n
and therefore by Theorem
1.6
we have
\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{O(1/n)}
. Actually, by the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, if
\sigma
is an
n
-cycle we have
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=(-1)^{i}
if
\lambda=[n-i,1^{i}]
for some
0\leq i\leq n-1
, and
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=0
for other diagrams, and therefore for all diagrams we have
\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq 1
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
is the conjugacy class of
n
-cycles, the
L^{2}
distance to stationarity then rewrites as

\mathrm{d}_{L^{2}}\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}}^{*t},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)},t)}\right)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}d_{[n-i,1^{i}]}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{d_{[n-i,1^{i}]}}\right)^{2t}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}d_{[n-i,1^{i}]}^{2(1-t)}}.

(10.1)

The bound above hints that
n
-cycles mix very fast: they need hardly more than one step to mix. Although it does not make sense in terms of random walks to consider times that are not integers, if we replace
t
by
1+\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)
in (
10.1
), then the right hand side tends to
0
.

Another way to see that
n
-cycles mix well is to show that they can compensate the irregularities of other cycle types. This is the content of the next theorem.

Theorem 10.1
.

For each
n\geq 2
let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}
denote the conjugacy class of
n
-cycles, and let
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Denote
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})
. Assume that
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=o(n)
.
Then we have

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(10.2)

Proof.

First we have
E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=1/n=O(1/\ln n)
, and by Proposition
7.4
(c) we have
E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)
. We deduce that
E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})+E({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})=1-\omega_{+}(1/\ln n)
, and conclude by Proposition
7.3
that
\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})}\right)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 10.2
.

The same arguments apply for conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}
such that
E({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=O(1/\ln n)
, for instance if
\cyc({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)})=O(1)
.

Proposition 10.2
.

For each
n\geq 2
let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}
denote the conjugacy class of
n
-cycles, and let
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Denote
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)}=({\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})
. Assume that there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that for
n
large enough we have
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})\geq\gamma n
. Then

\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}*\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\not\rightarrow}0.

(10.3)

Proof.

In this proof we drop the dependence on
n
from the notation of most variables, and we denote
F=f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)})
.
Let
\tau_{1}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{(n)}}
and
\tau_{2}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}}
be independent variables.
Denote by
\tau^{*}
a deterministic permutation of
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{(n)}
whose fixed points are
\{1,\ldots,F\}
. By conjugacy invariance of
\tau_{1}
we have

f_{1}(\tau_{1}\circ\tau_{2})\overset{(d)}{=}f_{1}(\tau_{1}\circ\tau^{*}),

(10.4)

where
\overset{(d)}{=}
denotes the equality in distribution.
Since
\tau_{1}
is a uniformly random
n
-cycle, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}\circ\tau^{*}(i)=i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }1\leq i\leq F,\\
\frac{1}{n-1}&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(10.5)

In particular, let
\sigma
be distributed as
\tau_{1}\circ\tau_{2}
and fix
\beta<\gamma
. We have, for any
n
large enough so that
\gamma n\geq\beta n+1
:

\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(\sigma)]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{1}\circ\tau_{2}(i)=i\right)=F\cdot 0+(n-F)\cdot\frac{1}{n-1}=1-\frac{F-1}{n-1}\leq 1-\beta.

(10.6)

Hence, setting
h_{K}:x\mapsto x\mathds{1}_{x\leq K}
for
K\geq 1
, we have for any
K\geq 1

\mathbb{E}[h_{K}(f_{1}(\sigma))]\leq\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(\sigma)]\leq 1-\beta.

(10.7)

On the other hand, fix an integer
K_{0}:=K_{0}(\beta)
large enough such that
\sum_{k=0}^{K_{0}}k\frac{e^{-1}}{k!}\geq 1-\beta/3
. Such a
K_{0}
exists, since
\sum_{k\geq 0}k\frac{e^{-1}}{k!}=1
.
Now, let
\Pi\sim\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})}
be a uniformly random permutation in
{\mathfrak{E}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}^{(n)})
. Since
f_{1}(\Pi)
converges in distribution to
\Poiss(1)
, for any fixed
k\geq 0
, we have
\mathbb{P}(f_{1}(\Pi)=k)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\frac{e^{-1}}{k!}
. Therefore, for
n
large enough:

\mathbb{E}[h_{K_{0}}(f_{1}(\Pi))]\geq\sum_{k=0}^{K_{0}}k\frac{e^{-1}}{k!}-\frac{\beta}{6}\geq 1-\frac{\beta}{2}.

(10.8)

By (
10.7
), this implies that, for
n
large enough (recall that
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
denotes the law of a random variable
X
):

\mathbb{E}[h_{K_{0}}(f_{1}(\Pi))]-\mathbb{E}[h_{K_{0}}(f_{1}(\sigma))]\geq\frac{\beta}{2}.

(10.9)

Letting
\mu:={\mathcal{L}}(f_{1}(\Pi))
and
\nu:={\mathcal{L}}(f_{1}(\sigma))
be the laws of
f_{1}(\Pi)
and
f_{1}(\sigma)
, we get

\frac{\beta}{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K_{0}}k(\mu(k)-\nu(k))\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K_{0}}K_{0}\max_{0\leq k\leq K_{0}}(\mu(k)-\nu(k))=(K_{0}+1)K_{0}\max_{0\leq k\leq K_{0}}(\mu(k)-\nu(k)).

(10.10)

In particular, there exists
k\leq K_{0}
for which

{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(\Pi)=k)-{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(\sigma)=k)\geq\frac{\beta}{2K_{0}(K_{0}+1)},

(10.11)

which concludes the proof.
∎

10.2
Cutoff for conjugacy classes with large support and no short cycles

Here we prove Theorem
1.13
, that is, cutoff for conjugacy classes with no short cycles.

Let us first prove another bound on
E(\sigma)
, this time depending on the length of the smallest non-trivial cycle of
\sigma
.

For
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\{\Id\}
, let
\ibis:=\ibis(\sigma)=\min\left\{i\geq 2{\;:\;}f_{i}\geq 1\right\}
be the length of the smallest non-trivial cycle of
\sigma
. We also denote by
\cyc_{\geq i}(\sigma)
the number of cycles of
\sigma
of length at least
i
.

Proposition 10.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\left\{\Id\right\}
with at least one fixed point. Then

E(\sigma)-1\leq-\frac{\ln(n/f_{1})}{\ln n}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ibis}\right).

(10.12)

Proof.

For convenience, in this proof, set
\ell=\ibis(\sigma)
. We define, for
i\geq\ell
,

G_{i}=f_{1}+\ell\sum_{\ell\leq j\leq i}f_{j}.

(10.13)

We have by definition of the orbit growth sequence that
e_{1}=\frac{\ln f_{1}}{\ln n}
, that
e_{i}=0
for
2\leq i<\ell
,
and that
e_{\ell}=\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac{\ell f_{\ell}}{f_{1}}\right)}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{G_{\ell}}{f_{1}}\right)}{\ln n}
. Then, using that
(1+x)^{a}\leq 1+ax
for [
x\geq 0
and
0\leq a\leq 1
], we get for
i>\ell
:

\frac{\ell}{i}e_{i}=\frac{\ln\left(\left(1+\frac{if_{i}}{\Sigma_{i-1}}\right)^{\ell/i}\right)}{\ln n}\leq\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac{\ell f_{i}}{\Sigma_{i-1}}\right)}{\ln n}\leq\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac{\ell f_{i}}{G_{i-1}}\right)}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln\left(G_{i}/G_{i-1}\right)}{\ln n},

(10.14)

where we used in the last inequality that

\Sigma_{i-1}=f_{1}+\sum_{\ell\leq j\leq i-1}jf_{j}\geq f_{1}+\ell\sum_{\ell\leq j\leq i-1}f_{j}=G_{i-1}.

(10.15)

The terms then compensate as in the proof of Proposition
5.4
: we have

\begin{split}E(\sigma)=e_{1}+\frac{e_{\ell}}{\ell}+\sum_{i>\ell}\frac{e_{i}}{i}=\frac{\ln f_{1}}{\ln n}+\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{\ln(G_{\ell}/f_{1})}{\ln n}+\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i>\ell}\frac{\ell}{i}e_{i}\leq\frac{\ln f_{1}}{\ln n}+\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{\ln(G_{n}/f_{1})}{\ln n}.\end{split}

(10.16)

We conclude that

E(\sigma)\leq\frac{\ln f_{1}}{\ln n}+\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{\ln(n/f_{1})}{\ln n}=\frac{\ln f_{1}}{\ln n}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell}\right)+\frac{1}{\ell}=1-\frac{\ln(n/f_{1})}{\ln n}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell}\right).\qed

(10.17)

Remark 10.3
.

If
\sigma
consists only of fixed points and
\ibis
-cycles, then
E(\sigma)=e_{1}+\frac{1-e_{1}}{\ibis}=e_{1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ibis}\right)+\frac{1}{\ibis}
. The bound from Proposition
10.3
is therefore sharp in this case.

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.13
.

Proof of Theorem
1.13
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
First assume that
f_{1}=0
. Recall that
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(0)=1-o(1)
since the walk starts from the identity permutation, and
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(1)=1-o(1)
since the walk after 1 step is concentrated on
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}
. The result to prove is therefore that
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(2)\to 0
.

In this case we have
\imin=\min\left\{i\geq 2{\;:\;}f_{i}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\geq 1\right\}\to\infty
by assumption, so by Proposition
5.4
we have
E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\leq\frac{1}{\imin}=o(1)
.
It follows directly from Proposition
7.3
that
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(2)\to 0
.

From now on we assume that
f_{1}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\geq 1
.
The lower bound is a classical counting argument that can be done with coupon collector methods. We briefly recall it. A second moment argument shows that, if one multiplies
\left\lfloor\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}(1-\varepsilon)\right\rfloor
permutations (i.i.d., uniform in a conjugacy class) that have support size
n-f
, then, with probability
1-o(1)
, at least
n^{\varepsilon}/2
are fixed points of all these permutations and therefore are fixed points of their product. Since uniform permutations (also on
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
and
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
) have less than
\ln n
fixed points with probability
1-o(1)
, we deduce that
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}(1-\varepsilon)\right\rfloor\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1
.

Let us now show the cutoff upper bound. The proof is similar to that of Theorem
1.10
.
Set for the rest of the proof
t:=\left\lceil\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}(1+\varepsilon)\right\rceil
, and
\ell=\ibis=\min\left\{i\geq 2{\;:\;}f_{i}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})\geq 1\right\}
.

Let
C
be the constant appearing in Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
. First, by Proposition
10.3
, and the facts that
\ell\to\infty
, and
f=o(n)
, we have

\begin{split}\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln n}\right)E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})-1&=\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln n}\right)\left(E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})-1\right)+\frac{C}{\ln n}\\
&\leq-\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln n}\right)\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell}\right)+\frac{C}{\ln n}\\
&=-\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}(1+o(1)).\end{split}

(10.18)

In particular, it is negative for
n
large enough. We therefore have as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

\begin{split}1+t\left(\left(1+\frac{C}{\ln n}\right)E({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})-1\right)&\leq 1+\left((1+\varepsilon)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}\right)\left(-\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}(1+o(1))\right)\\
&=-\varepsilon+o(1).\end{split}

(10.19)

We deduce from Lemma
7.1
, Theorem
1.6
, and bounds on the Witten zeta function (Proposition
6.7
), that

4\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(t)^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}\left(d_{\lambda}|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}}^{(n)})|^{t}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{-2\varepsilon+o(1)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(10.20)

which concludes the proof.
∎
