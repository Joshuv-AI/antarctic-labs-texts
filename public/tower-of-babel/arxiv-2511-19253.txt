MAESTRO: Multi-Agent Environment Shaping through Task and Reward Optimization
Boyuan Wu

Abstract
Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) is fundamentally constrained by two design bottlenecks: the manual engineering of dense reward functions and the difficulty of constructing curricula that prevent local optima in high-dimensional state spaces. Existing solutions typically rely on static, hand-crafted heuristics that struggle to generalize. While Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently been applied to decision-making, most approaches utilize the “LLM-as-Agent” paradigm—placing the model directly in the control loop—which incurs prohibitive inference latency and token costs unsuitable for real-time systems. In this work, we propose MAESTRO (Multi-Agent Environment Shaping through Task and Reward Optimization), a framework that shifts the role of the LLM from real-time agent to high-level architect . MAESTRO treats the training process as a dual-loop optimization problem where the LLM dynamically designs both the task and the solving guidance. The system integrates two generative components: (i) a semantic curriculum generator that synthesizes diverse, context-rich scenarios based on agent performance, and (ii) an automated reward synthesizer that writes executable Python reward functions to guide agents through the evolving curriculum. These components optimize the training of a standard off-policy MARL backbone (MADDPG) without requiring LLM inference during execution. We validate MAESTRO on the challenging domain of real-world Traffic Signal Control (Hangzhou network, 16 intersections), conducting a controlled ablation study comparing LLM-generated curriculum with template rewards (baseline) against our full system combining LLM curriculum and LLM-generated rewards. Our results demonstrate that using LLMs as architects for both task generation and reward shaping yields improved mean performance and risk-adjusted returns. Specifically, across four independent seeds, the full system achieves + 4.0 % +4.0\% improvement in mean performance (163.26 vs 156.93) and 2.2 × \times better risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe Ratio 1.53 vs 0.70) compared to the alternative curriculum baseline. These findings suggest that LLM-generated reward shaping with constrained generation provides measurable performance gains for production deployment in cooperative multi-agent systems. Our code is available at: https://github.com/BrianWu1010/MAESTRO.git .

Introduction

Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) holds immense promise for solving complex decentralized control problems, from warehouse logistics to sensor network coordination. However, scaling MARL to realistic, non-stationary environments is persistently hindered by two fundamental design bottlenecks:
curriculum design
and
reward engineering
.

The first bottleneck, exploration, is often addressed by **Curriculum Learning (CL)**, which gradually increases task difficulty. However, constructing effective curricula requires deep domain expertise to ensure tasks are meaningfully diverse and effective
[
7
]
. The second bottleneck concerns reward specification: the efficacy of MARL is brittle with respect to reward specification. Standard dense rewards are often misaligned with global objectives
[
11
]
, while sparse global rewards fail to provide sufficient learning signal. Handcrafting reward functions that generalize across dynamic regimes remains a labor-intensive and error-prone process.

Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a potential remedy by encoding rich semantic priors that can automate these design processes. However, the prevailing integration paradigm—
LLM-as-Agent
—typically inserts the model directly into the control loop to select actions
[
3
,
2
,
13
]
. While this yields strong zero-shot generalization, it incurs prohibitive inference latency and token costs, rendering it impractical for real-time control systems requiring sub-second decision making. Furthermore, this approach underutilizes the LLM’s capacity for high-level reasoning, relegating it to per-step tactical decisions.

Related Work

2.1
MARL for Decentralized Control

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) is widely applied to cooperative control problems where agents share a global goal but make local decisions based on partial observations. TSC, a challenging instance of a Dec-POMDP, popularized the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm
[
5
]
, where agents learn a joint value function during training but act independently in deployment. Early applications of CTDE to traffic, such as CoLight
[
10
]
and MPLight
[
1
]
, focused on modeling inter-agent dependencies using mechanisms like graph attention and phase-pressure signals. These approaches assume a
static, manually designed reward function
and a fixed environment setup. Our work maintains the stability and real-time capability of the CTDE backbone (MADDPG) but allows the reward function itself and the training environment to evolve dynamically via LLM-generated shaping signals, addressing a critical generalization gap.

2.2
Automated Curriculum and Environment Generation

The difficulty of exploration in high-dimensional MARL motivates

Automatic Curriculum Learning (ACL)
, which autonomously sequences tasks
to maximize agent learning progress
[
8
]
. Methods like
POET
[
9
]
and ALP-GMM
[
8
]
demonstrate
the power of generating diverse environments to force agents to acquire
generalizable skills. In the context of TSC, curriculum generation has
traditionally been limited to heuristic, numeric adjustments of traffic
parameters, such as flow rates and phase durations
[
11
]
.
These numeric methods lack the expressiveness to generate complex, semantically
meaningful scenarios (e.g., “a peak-hour commuter pattern with a major
bottleneck on the eastern artery”). Our approach moves beyond numeric scaling
to a language-informed ACL, where an LLM generates

semantically rich traffic contexts
conditioned on a scalar difficulty
signal, significantly expanding the scope of environment shaping.

2.3
LLMs for Reward and Policy Generation

The integration of Large Language Models into RL systems is broadly categorized into two paradigms:

LLM-as-Agent.

In this setting, the LLM directly selects actions or high-level plans, often via chain-of-thought reasoning. TrafficGPT
[
13
]
and LLM-TSCS
[
3
]
exemplify this for traffic control. While providing strong zero-shot behavior, these methods are bottlenecked by the LLM’s high inference latency and token cost, making them ill-suited for real-time control applications with strict timing constraints.

LLM-as-Teacher (Offline Guidance).

This paradigm leverages the LLM’s
reasoning capabilities during the training phase to shape the learning process.
Recent work like Eureka
[
6
]
and Code-as-Policies
[
4
]

demonstrated that LLMs can generate effective policy code or, critically,

executable reward functions
for single-agent or robotic skill acquisition.
These reward functions act as powerful, automatically generated priors.

MAESTRO
builds upon and substantially extends the
LLM-as-Teacher

direction for the cooperative MARL domain. We introduce two key novelties:
(i) the generative process produces executable Python reward functions for a

decentralized, cooperative MARL system
, which is significantly more complex
than the single-agent setting; and (ii) we explicitly

couple reward generation with a performance-driven, LLM-generated curriculum
,
ensuring the reward logic evolves in synergy with the increasing task difficulty.
This coupling is essential for the stability and generalization of MARL policies
in non-stationary environments.

Preliminaries

3.1
Contextual MARL Formulation and Objectives

We model multi-intersection TSC as a
contextual Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP)
, augmented with the MAESTRO training framework. The environment is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{C}=\langle\mathcal{C},\mathcal{I},\mathcal{S},\{A_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{O_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\mathcal{T}_{C},\mathcal{R}_{C},\gamma\rangle,

where:

•

\mathcal{C}
denotes the
curriculum context space
(generated by the LLM Architect), which parameterizes traffic flows, route patterns, and demand distributions.

•

\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,N\}
is the set of traffic-signal agents (intersections).

•

\mathcal{S}
is the global state;
O_{i}
and
A_{i}
are the observation and action spaces for agent
i
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{C}
is the
context-dependent transition function
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{C}
is the
context-dependent reward function
(synthesized by the LLM Architect), and
\gamma
is the discount factor.

At the start of each training stage, a context
C\in\mathcal{C}
is sampled and the LLM Architect produces the corresponding reward function
\mathcal{R}_{C}
. Agents then interact with the environment under the joint policy
\pi_{C}
, generating a trajectory
\tau
. The objective is to maximize the expected cumulative return over all curriculum contexts:

\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{C\sim p(\mathcal{C}),\;\tau\sim\mathcal{T}_{C}(\cdot\mid\pi_{C})}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}\mathcal{R}_{C}(s_{t},a_{t})\right].

(1)

We adopt
MADDPG

[
5
]
as the
MARL Learner backbone
within the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm. This design preserves stability during cooperative learning while maintaining efficient, decentralized execution at test time—cleanly separating the LLM’s offline design role from the agents’ real-time control.

3.2
MARL Learner Specification

Simulation Setup.

Experiments use the CityFlow microscopic simulator
[
12
]
on the real-world
Hangzhou (HZ) network
with
|\mathcal{I}|=16
signalized intersections. Each episode spans 360 timesteps (1s per step). The curriculum context
C
configures dynamic elements such as route distributions, vehicle arrival patterns, and vehicle parameters. Full parameter ranges are provided in the appendix.

Observations and Actions.

Each agent
i
receives a structured observation vector
o_{i}^{t}
comprising local queue lengths, inflow/outflow rates, current phase, elapsed phase duration, lane occupancy, and short-horizon traffic history. This feature design follows established MARL–TSC practice and is detailed in the appendix. Agent actions correspond to selecting one of the
|P_{i}|=4
feasible signal phases at each timestep, consistent with intersection geometry.

Traffic Metrics.

Performance is evaluated using standard TSC metrics:
mean travel time
,
delay
,
queue length
, and
throughput
. In MAESTRO, these metrics serve two purposes: (i) they drive
difficulty adjustment
for the curriculum generator, and (ii) they are combined into the
composite reward signal

\mathcal{R}_{C}
used during agent training.

4.3
LLM-Generated Reward Shaping (MAESTRO – A7)

The full
MAESTRO
configuration (A7) extends the A2 curriculum baseline by
introducing LLM-generated auxiliary rewards that evolve with the curriculum.

Reward Generation and Validation.

At regeneration events (triggered by
\Delta d\geq 0.15
, 20 episodes since the
last generation, or a stagnation-induced difficulty increase), GPT-4o-mini is
prompted with the current difficulty, aggregated statistics, and a description of
the learning goal. The LLM generates a short Python reward function

r_{\mathrm{LLM},t}
. A three-stage validation pipeline ensures safety:

•

Syntax Check:
abstract syntax tree validation,

•

Sandboxed Execution:
sample rollouts to confirm runtime behavior,

•

Safety Check:
range bounding and finite-value verification.

If three consecutive generations fail, the system reverts to environment-only
rewards (behaving as A2) until the next regeneration trigger.

Scaling and Difficulty-Dependent Weighting.

Because LLM-generated rewards can differ in scale, we apply a fixed scaling factor
of
170.0
to match typical environment reward magnitudes. A difficulty-dependent
weight

w(d)=w_{\min}+\frac{d-d_{\min}}{d_{\max}-d_{\min}}(w_{\max}-w_{\min}),

with
w_{\min}=0.1
,
w_{\max}=0.5
,
d_{\min}=0.3
, and
d_{\max}=1.0
,
controls the auxiliary contribution. This ensures low influence at easy
difficulties and stronger guidance in challenging regimes.

Combined Reward.

The per-timestep reward used for MADDPG updates is:

r_{t}=\begin{cases}r_{\mathrm{env},t},&\text{A2, A8 (Curriculum Baselines)},\\[3.0pt]
(1-w(d))\,r_{\mathrm{env},t}+w(d)\cdot 170.0\cdot r_{\mathrm{LLM},t},&\text{A7 (Full MAESTRO)}.\end{cases}

Here,
r_{\mathrm{env},t}
is the CityFlow reward and
r_{\mathrm{LLM},t}
is the
auxiliary shaping signal generated by the Architect.

Experimental Design and Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate the
MAESTRO
framework by quantifying the contribution of LLM-generated reward shaping and adaptive curriculum design to the performance and robustness of cooperative MARL. Our goals are twofold: (1) isolate the effect of LLM-generated reward functions relative to template-based rewards under an adaptive curriculum, and (2) demonstrate the stability and learning efficiency of the full MAESTRO system in a complex, non-stationary environment.

Experimental Conditions.

We evaluate three configurations forming a controlled ablation (detailed in §
4
):

•

A2 (Curriculum Baseline):
Uses the
llm_adaptive
curriculum with a fixed template-based environment reward.

•

A8 (Alternative Curriculum):
Uses an alternative
llm
curriculum implementation and the same template-based reward.

•

A7 (Full MAESTRO):
Uses the A2 curriculum but replaces template rewards with dynamic LLM-generated reward functions, scaled by 170.0 and weighted by a difficulty-adaptive factor
w\in[0.1,0.5]
.

All experiments use identical hyperparameters, network architecture, agent design, and simulation settings.

Key Comparisons.

This design supports two central comparisons:
(1)
A2 vs. A7
isolates the contribution of LLM-generated reward shaping within the same curriculum framework;
(2)
A2 vs. A8
evaluates two LLM curriculum strategies while keeping the reward design fixed.
We train all three configurations for 200 episodes using four seeds, prioritizing deeper runs to strengthen statistical power and attribute performance differences to specific LLM components.

5.1
Environment and Curriculum Setting

All experiments use the Hangzhou (HZ) traffic network with 16 signalized intersections (§
3.2
). Each episode spans 360 steps. We report standard traffic-signal-control metrics: mean delay, queue length, throughput, and cumulative return.

The adaptive curriculum maintains a scalar difficulty
d
updated from a rolling five-episode window. When the windowed return exceeds 173.25 (105% of a target 165.0) for two consecutive windows,
d
increases by 5%. When it falls below 156.75 (95% of target),
d
decreases by 10%. If unchanged for 15 episodes,
d
increases by 5% to escape stagnation. The difficulty is clipped to
[0.3,1.0]
. Difficulty monotonicity with respect to task hardness (queue length and delay) is empirically verified under a fixed-time control policy. Because difficulty updates are performance-driven, curriculum trajectories differ by seed.

In the full
MAESTRO
system (A7), difficulty changes and stagnation events also trigger LLM reward regeneration (§
4.3
), whereas A2 and A8 apply identical curriculum logic but omit reward regeneration.

5.2
Metrics

We evaluate results along three dimensions:

•

Traffic Efficiency and Final Performance:
Episode return, mean delay, queue length, and throughput measured over episodes 180–199.

•

Learning Efficiency and Stability:
Episodes required to reach the target performance (165.0), time to reach 90% of final return, and stability measured by variance and standard deviation of returns in episodes 180–199, supplemented with a risk-adjusted metric (Sharpe Ratio).

•

Curriculum Behavior:
Per-seed difficulty trajectories, counts of difficulty adjustments, and final difficulty reached.

5.3
Experimental Protocol

Each configuration (A2, A8, A7) is trained with four seeds (200, 300, 400, 500). Training runs for 200 episodes (72,000 environment steps). Evaluation episodes occur every two training episodes, with deterministic policies (no exploration noise) to measure true policy quality. Hyperparameters follow standard MADDPG practice, and all simulation parameters (network topology, demand, and CityFlow settings) remain fixed.

Reproducibility is enforced by seeding PyTorch, NumPy, Python RNG, and CuDNN. In A7, LLM-generated reward code is processed through a deterministic validation and post-processing pipeline, ensuring that identical seeds lead to identical code acceptance decisions.

Statistical analyses operate on seed-level aggregates. For each configuration and seed, we compute mean return over episodes 180–199 as the summary of final performance. Pairwise comparisons use the Mann–Whitney U test (
\alpha=0.05
, Bonferroni corrected). Effect sizes use Cohen’s
d
, and uncertainty is expressed via 95% bootstrap confidence intervals computed from 10,000 resamples. We avoid pooling episodes across seeds to preserve independence and capture between-seed variance.

Discussion and Future Work

7.1
Key Findings and Methodological Implications

Generative Shaping Improves Stability.

A core finding of this study is the substantial stability gain introduced by MAESTRO’s LLM-generated reward shaping (A7). While the alternative curriculum (A8) attains the highest mean return, A7 exhibits a
2.6
\times
reduction in variance relative to A2
(CV 0.65% vs. 1.69%) and
3.9
\times
improvement over A8
(CV 2.55%), yielding the strongest risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe Ratio 1.76). These results support the broader methodological claim that repositioning the LLM as an
Architect
—tasked with producing constrained, executable shaping signals—offers a principled route to stabilizing cooperative MARL. For safety-critical applications such as traffic control, this increased consistency is of practical significance.

Curriculum Design as the Primary Driver.

The most pronounced performance gains arise from modifying the curriculum-generation strategy (A2
\rightarrow
A8). This highlights that
task selection and progression
remain the dominant levers in determining MARL efficiency and asymptotic performance. The ability of the LLM to synthesize semantically rich and diverse scenarios enables more informative training distributions than manually designed or template-based curricula.

Modest Return Gains, Large Domain Impact.

Although aggregate episode-return improvements appear modest (1–2%), domain-specific metrics reveal large operational benefits:
20–28% reductions in delay
and
6–9% improvements in throughput
. These findings reinforce the importance of evaluating MARL systems not only through abstract learning metrics but also through domain-relevant indicators that capture real-world utility.

7.2
Limitations and Future Work

Generalization Beyond Traffic Control.

Experiments are conducted exclusively in the Hangzhou TSC environment. Future work should investigate MAESTRO in broader cooperative MARL domains—such as multi-robot coordination, distributed resource allocation, and networked systems—to assess whether the LLM-as-Architect paradigm generalizes to structurally different environments.

Dependence on Model and Prompt Design.

The framework relies on a specific model (GPT-4o-mini) and a fixed prompt configuration. Systematic prompt optimization, prompt diversification, or incorporating few-shot demonstrations may yield higher-quality generative outputs. Evaluating performance across different LLM families would help clarify the robustness of MAESTRO to model choice.

Richer Curriculum and Reward Structures.

An important direction is expanding the expressiveness of the curriculum space and reward generator. Integrating LLM-informed priors into policy or value initialization, incorporating temporal abstractions, or conditioning curriculum generation on uncertainty estimates could further enhance sample efficiency and generalization.

Safety and Interpretability of Generated Rewards.

Although MAESTRO employs syntactic, runtime, and safety checks for generated reward code, subtle misalignment or specification errors remain possible. Future work should explore formal verification of LLM-generated code, human-in-the-loop oversight, and runtime safety filters to ensure alignment with operational and ethical constraints, especially for real-world deployment scenarios.
