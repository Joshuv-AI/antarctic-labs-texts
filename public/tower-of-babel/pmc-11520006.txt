Characterizing Treatment Credibility, Treatment Expectancy, and Symptom Expectancy in Patients Before Nonoperative Treatment for Degenerative Meniscal Tears
Authors: Leslie J Bisson, Nomi S Weiss-Laxer, Jeffrey N Katz, Mohammad N Haider, Kyle B Flikkema, Jeffrey M Lackner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The role of nonspecific factors, such as treatment credibility, treatment expectancy, and symptom expectancy, may shape the outcomes of the nonoperative treatment of degenerative meniscal tears (DMTs).Purpose/Hypothesis:The purpose of this study was to characterize treatment credibility and expectancy, symptom expectancy, and patient and clinical correlates of these factors among participants before undergoing 4 nonoperative treatment programs for DMTs. It was hypothesized that (1) treatment credibility scores would be similar across patient and baseline clinical subgroups and (2) treatment expectancy and symptom expectancy scores would be lower in older patients and among those reporting a longer pain duration or greater pain intensity.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:In 126 participants, validated scales were administered before treatment to assess the credibility of the assigned treatment (possible score of 1-9) as well as the expectations of treatment outcomes (0-100) and symptom improvement (1-5). Patient and clinical characteristics associated with these variables were examined.Results:On average, participants (mean age, 58 years [range, 45-75 years]; 51% female; 94% White) expected the treatment to improve their symptoms by a mean of 68% and expected their symptoms to be “very likely” to improve. Participants with a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2 had lower mean treatment credibility (6.09 ± 1.81) and treatment expectancy (64.53 ± 25.16) scores on bivariate analyses than those with a BMI <30 kg/m2 (6.73 ± 1.69 [P = .045] and 72.86 ± 19.20 [P = .039], respectively). Participants reporting a pain duration ≥3 months also had lower mean treatment expectancy (63.85 ± 23.83) and symptom expectancy (3.70 ± 0.85) scores compared to those reporting a pain duration <3 months (75.52 ± 18.93 [P = .003] and 4.16 ± 0.67 [P = .001], respectively).Conclusion:Most patients about to undergo physical therapy for DMTs considered it to be between “somewhat” and “very” credible and believed that their symptoms, on average, were “very likely” to improve. Although not supported by most evidence, patients with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 believed physical therapy to be less credible and had lower treatment and symptom expectations, and those with symptoms present for ≥3 months had lower treatment and symptom expectations as well. Whether these nonspecific factors influence outcomes should be considered for further research.Keywords: knee, meniscus, treatment credibility, treatment expectancy, symptom expectancy, nonoperative treatment, meniscal tear

The syndrome of knee pain, osteoarthritis, and degenerative meniscal tears (DMTs) is common among middle-aged and older adults and is associated with disability, high costs, and loss of work.1,2,6,8,16 Up to half of adults in the population aged >50 years have a meniscal tear on magnetic resonance imaging, and those with tears are no more likely to have knee pain than those without tears. 6 Over the last 2 decades, the first-line treatment of DMTs has evolved from arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (APM) to nonoperative therapy (typically physical therapy [PT] and exercises) for most patients, with APM reserved for those who do not improve.11,21,25,29,30 During this evolution, the treatment of DMTs has focused on addressing disease-specific factors such as meniscal tear morphology if APM is performed or muscle weakness and joint effusion when PT is employed.

In general, health research has indicated that the efficacy of treatment approaches may be caused by technical factors acting through physiological mechanisms as well as nonspecific factors not clearly related to the pathoanatomy.7,20,24,31 Specific factors include theory-based interventions believed to induce a proposed therapeutic effect, such as the belief (largely disproved but widely held nonetheless) that the mechanical symptoms of a meniscal tear arise from interactions between torn meniscal tissue and surrounding structures in the joint. 19 Nonspecific factors are those with generic effects that come about simply by initiating treatment such as the general effect of clinical attention, “bedside manner,” treatment expectations, and contextual factors that emerge as practitioners interact with patients. Nonspecific factors apply to all treatment methods; among the most powerful are patients’ beliefs about a treatment option's credibility and its expected therapeutic benefit. 24

Treatment credibility is the extent to which patients believe that the rationale for the treatment that they will undergo is credible; credibility reflects how believable, convincing, and logical the patient perceives the treatment to be. Treatment expectancy is the extent to which patients believe the treatment that they will undergo will relieve or improve the symptom(s) for which they are seeking care. Symptom expectancy is the extent to which patients expect specific symptoms will be improved after their treatment ends.20,24,31 In the medical literature, stronger treatment credibility has been positively associated with having an increased likelihood of achieving clinically meaningful changes.20,24,31 To the best of our knowledge, the orthopaedic literature has yet to distinguish between treatment and symptom expectancies explicitly. Kuhn et al 15 investigated treatment credibility and found that patients with atraumatic, symptomatic, and full-thickness rotator cuff tears are more likely to benefit from PT if the patient actually believes that rehabilitation will be effective. Further, Mahomed et al 17 found that patient expectations of pain relief in total joint arthroplasty predicted the functional outcomes of patients.

Despite their association with outcomes in several orthopaedic conditions, patient expectations have not been well described or differentiated in patients with DMTs who are about to undergo nonoperative management. We were unable to find studies that described the association between treatment credibility and orthopaedic outcomes in this patient population. In addition, we did not find literature describing whether patient and baseline clinical characteristics are associated with treatment credibility and expectancy in patients with DMTs. Measuring these nonspecific factors and determining whether they vary according to patient and baseline clinical characteristics are important first steps in understanding their potential influence on patients with DMTs.

In a cohort of patients with DMTs about to undergo nonoperative management and enrolled in a randomized clinical trial, this study aimed to (1) quantify treatment credibility, treatment expectancy, and symptom expectancy and (2) identify patient (age, sex, race, insurance) and baseline clinical (body mass index [BMI], pain duration and intensity, Kellgren-Lawrence [KL] grade 13 ) characteristics associated with these nonspecific factors. Based on prior studies in other conditions, we hypothesized that (1) treatment credibility scores would be similar across patient and baseline clinical subgroups and (2) treatment expectancy and symptom expectancy scores would be lower in older patients and among those reporting a longer duration and/or greater intensity of pain.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This is a secondary analysis of the Treatment of Meniscal Tears in Osteoarthritis (TeMPO) trial 27 conducted at one of the study sites in Buffalo, New York. TeMPO is a multicenter randomized controlled trial designed to examine the comparative efficacy of 4 PT treatment programs. The 4 treatment programs lasted 3 months and included the following: (1) home exercises, focused on lower extremity muscle strengthening and stretching; (2) an enriched home program with adherence optimization (text messages 3 times a week during the intervention period to encourage compliance with home exercises); (3) a home program with adherence optimization and in-clinic placebo PT (ultrasound with no energy emitted, massage of nonjoint areas); and (4) a home program, adherence optimization, in-clinic physical therapist–delivered exercises (strengthening, stretching, neuromuscular training), and manual therapy. All patients completed questionnaires as part of the TeMPO protocol. A subset of patients completed an additional survey, the meniscal tear version of the Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire (CEQ-MT), between February 2021 and July 2022. We modified this instrument for patients with meniscal tears by altering all the questions to refer to a “reduction in knee symptoms” as opposed to the more generic original “improving your functioning.”3,26 Participants completed surveys within 2 weeks of randomization. Participants completed the CEQ-MT and TeMPO questionnaires after being told which PT treatment arm they would be randomized to and after being provided the rationale for the treatment but before implementing the full scope of therapy. This was done to limit the extent to which treatment responses would confound the effect of nonspecific factors. Data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted at Mass General Brigham.9,10 This study was approved by Mass General Brigham's institutional review board, and all patients provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Outcome Measures

We measured treatment credibility and treatment expectancy using a modified version of the CEQ-MT based on a psychometrically validated measure. 5 We quantified treatment credibility by taking the mean score of the first 3 items of the CEQ-MT to create an overall credibility rating (9-point scale). We quantified treatment expectancy by using the following CEQ-MT question, asking patients to rate their response on a scale from 0% to 100% (in 10-point increments): “By the end of treatment, how much improvement in knee symptoms do you think will occur?” We assessed symptom expectancy using the Musculoskeletal Outcomes Data Evaluation and Management System (MODEMS), which examines pretreatment expectations for the relief of knee symptoms and their impact on activities of living (eg, housework, sleep).23,28 Each MODEMS item had a lowest possible score of 1 (not likely) and highest possible score of 5 (extremely likely). We calculated the overall MODEMS symptom expectancy score by taking the mean score of the first 5 items.

Respondents reported their patient information (age, sex, self-identified race, income, insurance). Baseline clinical information was either directly measured at the time of enrollment (BMI), collected from questionnaires (time from onset of knee symptoms to completion of surveys, severity of knee symptoms when walking on level ground during the last week [none, mild, moderate, severe, extreme]), or obtained from initial radiographs (KL grade).

Statistical Analysis

We performed univariate analyses for patient, clinical, and nonspecific factors for the entire sample. We also conducted a correlation analysis to ascertain the strength of the relationships among the expectancy and credibility scores. We dichotomized several patient variables (age ≥60 years, sex, White race, and insurance) and clinical variables (BMI, KL grade, pain severity, and pain duration). We assessed the association of these binary variables and the continuous nonspecific factors using an independent-samples t test. Subsequently, we fit 3 mixed-effects models predicting nonspecific factors (treatment credibility, treatment expectancy, and symptom expectancy) using the following binary variables: age (≥60/<60 years), sex (male/female), race (White/non-White), BMI (≥30/<30 kg/m2), education (high school/some college or higher), insurance (private/other), KL grade (3/0-2), pain severity (mild/moderate or higher), and pain duration (≥3/<3 months). Variables for the multivariate models were selected based on statistical significance on bivariate analyses. We also included factors identified as important correlates of expectancy in the scientific literature. We included a random-effects intercept in each model. We considered a P value of <.05 to be statistically significant and performed all analyses using SPSS (Version 29; IBM).

The cohort included 126 patients (Table 1) with a mean age of 58 years (range, 45-75), was 51% female, and was 94% White. The overall survey completion rate was 93%.

Quantifying Nonspecific Factors

The mean treatment credibility score was 6.47 ± 1.78 (range, 1-9), corresponding to a response of “somewhat” to “very” credible. Regarding treatment expectancy, participants stated that, on average, they would experience 67.98% ± 22.00% improvement by the end of their treatment (range, 10% to 90%). The mean symptom expectancy score was 3.92 ± 0.80 (range, 1-5), corresponding to “very likely” to improve. Expectancy and credibility items were moderately correlated with each other (see correlation matrix in Appendix Table A1).

Associations With Patient or Clinical Correlates

On bivariate analyses (Table 2), we did not observe associations between age, sex, race, education, insurance, KL classification and pain severity and the treatment credibility, treatment expectancy, and symptom expectancy variables. Participants with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 had a lower mean treatment credibility score than those with a BMI <30 kg/m2 (6.09 ± 1.81 vs 6.73 ± 1.69, respectively; P = .045). Similarly, participants with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 had a lower mean treatment expectancy score (64.53 ± 25.16 vs 72.86 ± 19.20, respectively; P = .039). Participants reporting a pain duration ≥3 months also had lower mean treatment expectancy (63.85 ± 23.83) and symptom expectancy (3.70 ± 0.85) scores compared to those reporting pain for <3 months (75.52 ± 18.93 [P = .003] and 4.16 ± 0.67 [P = .001], respectively).

Discussion

This study assessed treatment credibility, treatment expectancy, and symptom expectancy in patients before they began PT for meniscal tears. We assessed treatment credibility and expectancy because these constructs have been associated with improved treatment outcomes in a range of medical and musculoskeletal conditions.15,17 Further, these constructs are potentially modifiable. 18 We hypothesized that (1) treatment credibility scores would be similar across patient and baseline clinical subgroups and (2) treatment expectancy and symptom expectancy scores would be lower in older patients and those with a longer duration or greater intensity of pain. Our first hypothesis was rejected, as we found that in the multivariate models, treatment credibility was moderately 4 lower for patients with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Our second hypothesis was partially accepted in which we found that patients reporting a longer pain duration (≥3 months) had lower treatment expectancy and symptom expectancy scores. We also found that a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 was associated with lower treatment and symptom expectancy. In our models, older age and greater pain intensity were not associated with treatment or symptom expectancy.

In general, our findings suggest that the credibility of PT for those about to undergo the treatment of DMTs was between “somewhat” and “very” credible and suggest more pessimism about expectations for success in patients who were obese and those who reported a longer duration of symptoms (≥3 months). These findings are surprising, given the large evidence base supporting PT as the first-line treatment for meniscal tears published in high-impact journals.11,25,29,30 The PT protocol utilized in the present study resulted from convening a panel of international experts in the nonoperative treatment of DMTs and was based on an extensive review of the literature, followed by a review and funding of the trial by the National Institutes of Health. 27 It incorporated the highest evidence available when the trial started and is still used by the 2 main recruiting centers. These factors should make it highly credible. Effectively educating patients regarding the evidence supporting PT may present an opportunity to increase the credibility and therapeutic value of PT above and beyond its specific effects. 18

Second, there is minimal support for obese patients having worse results with PT in the setting of meniscal tears. Kemp et al 14 systematically reviewed prospective studies of PT for meniscal tears to identify the predictors of success and generally found no effect of BMI category on outcomes. Finally, regarding pain, some studies have found a higher crossover to early surgery with greater pain12,22 as well as with a shorter pain duration, although pain duration was analyzed as <1 or >1 year in a prior study. 12 Our findings suggest a disconnect between patients believing that they will respond better if they have a shorter duration of pain and the results of studies showing that there are higher failure rates after the nonoperative management of DMTs with shorter pain durations. This knowledge may be useful in designing educational programs and materials in combination with nonoperative management and may also help providers to identify patients who believe that the course of treatment lacks credibility or who have unrealistic expectations of treatment outcomes. Encouragingly, others have found that expectations can be modified with educational classes before surgery. 18

Limitations

We note several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and comprised participants at only one study site. Further, our study population was 94% White; therefore, our findings cannot necessarily be generalized to non-White populations. Last, while the TeMPO trial emphasized equipoise between all treatment arms and patients completed the CEQ-MT and MODEMS surveys at a time designed to minimize any treatment response, it is possible that there was an early response that affected some survey answers.

Conclusion

We found that PT for DMTs to be considered between “somewhat” and “very” credible by patients about to undergo that treatment, that patients expected the treatment to improve their symptoms by a mean of 68%, and that patients believed that their symptoms were, on average, “very likely” to improve. Although not supported by most evidence, patients with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 believed PT to be less credible and had lower treatment and symptom expectations, and those with pain present for ≥3 months had lower treatment and symptom expectations as well. Whether these nonspecific factors influence outcomes in the TeMPO trial will be a focus of further research.
