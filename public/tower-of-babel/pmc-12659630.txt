Incidence and antimicrobial resistance of non-typhoidal Salmonella enterica in stool samples from children under five in peri-urban Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso
Authors: Alimatou Héma, Ben I. Soulama, Jean Sawadogo, Amidou Z. Ouédraogo, Denise Hien, Issa Nébié, Sophie Houard, Alphonse Ouédraogo, Alfred B. Tiono, Mahamoudou Sanou, Sodiomon B. Sirima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) causes significant illness worldwide, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Poor sanitation, limited healthcare, and rising antimicrobial resistance (AMR) worsen its impact on children under five. This study estimated the incidence and described AMR patterns of NTS among children under five in peri-urban Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso.MethodsA 12-month longitudinal cohort (ShigaPlexIM baseline study) was conducted in peri-urban Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso. Between December 2020 and March 2021, 750 children under five were enrolled and followed individually for 12 months, with study activities spanning 16 months in total. Stool samples were collected during diarrheal episodes and scheduled visits. Salmonella spp. was identified by stool culture, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method per CLSI 2020 guidelines. Incidence rates were calculated using survival analysis over child-months of follow-up.ResultsOf 750 children enrolled, 680 (90.7%) completed follow-up, contributing 7,992.9 child-months. Salmonella was isolated from 97 of 2,401 (4.0%) stool samples. After excluding 33 isolates (29 prevalent cases and 4 typhoidal cases), 64 incident NTS cases were identified, resulting in an incidence rate of 8.0 per 1,000 child-months (95% CI: 6.3–10.2). The highest incidence was observed among children aged 12–23 months (10.3, 95% CI: 6.7–15.6) and in males (8.7, 95% CI: 6.3–12.1); however, sex differences were not significant (IRR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.49–1.15, p = 0.184). Incidence in diarrheal stools was 43.7 per 1,000 child-months (95% CI: 23.5–81.2). All isolates were fully susceptible to imipenem, ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, and aztreonam. High susceptibility (≥ 96.7%) was observed for ceftazidime, cefotaxime, chloramphenicol, cefepime, and tobramycin. Moderate resistance was found for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (4.4%), ampicillin (5.6%), nalidixic acid (5.6%), and streptomycin (6.7%). Resistance to tetracycline was highest at 74.4%. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was detected in 4.4% of isolates.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a substantial burden of NTS infections in children under five in peri-urban Burkina Faso. Although MDR prevalence was low, high tetracycline resistance indicates widespread circulation of resistant strains. Continuous AMR surveillance is essential to guide treatment and preserve antibiotic efficacy.

Background

Diarrheal diseases remain a significant global public health challenge and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among children under five years of age [1]. Among the key bacterial causes, Salmonella spp. are a major contributor to this burden, primarily causing an acute diarrheal illness [2]. With Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica being the predominant pathogenic species, transmission occurs mainly through the fecal-oral route via contaminated food, water, and direct contact [3–5]. This risk is disproportionately higher in low-resource settings, where factors like inadequate sanitation, unsafe drinking water, and poor hygiene practices amplify transmission [6–8].

Globally, non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) is estimated to cause 93.8 million cases of acute gastroenteritis and 155,000 deaths annually [9]. The burden of NTS is not evenly distributed, with the highest incidence rates found in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [10–12]. While high-income regions report lower incidence rates, SSA bears a disproportionate burden, where Salmonella enterica contributed to over 230,000 foodborne deaths in 2019 alone [10–12]. In Burkina Faso, recent evidence confirms substantial Salmonella contamination across multiple sources, with pooled prevalence rates of 4.6% in humans, 20.1% in animals, and 16.8% in food products [13].

The economic impact in Burkina Faso is staggering; foodborne diseases caused by NTS and other enteric pathogens were estimated to cost the nation 391 million international dollars in 2017, with children under five bearing up to 75% of this burden [14]. Studies from Burkina Faso have documented concerning AMR patterns, including high levels of contamination in ready-to-eat foods [15, 16] and street foods [3], with many isolates showing resistance to commonly used antibiotics. Furthermore, research has demonstrated genetic links between Salmonella strains from animal sources and human clinical isolates, highlighting the importance of zoonotic transmission in this setting [13, 17].

While NTS gastroenteritis is often self-limiting, it can progress to life-threatening invasive NTS (iNTS) disease, particularly in vulnerable populations such as young children, and those with malaria or malnutrition [18, 19]. The severity of the NTS problem in Burkina Faso is starkly illustrated by the data on its invasive form. A study in Nanoro reported an adjusted iNTS incidence rate of 4,138 per 100,000 person-years in children under five, a rate substantially higher than that of severe malaria in the same population [18]. This indicates that the community burden of NTS, which begins as gastroenteritis, is exceptionally high and can lead to severe outcomes.

Compounding this high disease burden is the escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). The rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains complicates treatment for severe cases and poses a significant public health risk. MDR is defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, with resistance to first-line antibiotics (ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) being common in Salmonella isolates from the region [20]. For severe or invasive infections, treatment guidelines in Burkina Faso, aligning with WHO recommendations, advise the use of third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone) or fluoroquinolones. However, without routine surveillance of isolates from gastroenteritis cases, it is difficult to monitor the emergence of resistance to these critical drugs.

This research aims to address this critical knowledge gap. By investigating the incidence and AMR profiles of Salmonella isolated from stool samples of children under five with diarrhea, this study will provide crucial data to inform public health strategies, guide clinical management, and contribute to the fight against antibiotic-resistant Salmonella in Burkina Faso.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in the Nongr-Massom health district of Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso, encompassing the peri-urban neighborhoods of Polesgo and Nioko 2. These areas are characterized by overcrowding, poor sanitation, and limited access to clean water [21].

ShigaPlexIM baseline study participants

Detailed methods on the ShigaPlexIM baseline study (formerly ShigOraVax study) have been previously published [22]. Briefly, this research was part of a multisite, prospective longitudinal cohort study under the ShigaPlexIM consortium. The present analysis uses data from the Burkina Faso site, 750 children under five years of age were enrolled between December 2020 and March 2021, with each child followed for a period of 12 months. The total study duration was 16 months (December 2020 to March 2022), accounting for the enrollment phase followed by individual follow-up.

Recruitment was community-based. Households with eligible children were identified by Community Health Workers. To ensure equitable representation, quota targets were set (approximately 300 children from Polesgo and 450 from Nioko 2), and a random selection was performed when volunteers exceeded quotas. Eligible participants were permanent residents without plans to relocate and without specific exclusion criteria (known immunodeficiency, major congenital anomalies, etc.). Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians.

For this analysis, the “exposure” was defined as the time each child spent residing in the study area during the follow-up period. The primary “outcome” for this analysis was a stool culture-confirmed Salmonella infection.

Follow-up and data collection.

After enrolment (designated as Month 0), children were actively monitored through scheduled visits at baseline (Month 0), Month 6, and Month 12 (or a closure visit), in addition to passive surveillance for diarrheal episodes. Stool samples were collected routinely during these scheduled visits and additionally during any diarrheal illness.

A total of 2,401 stool samples were collected and cultured for Salmonella. This total comprises:750 non-diarrheal samples at baseline (Month 0),735 non-diarrheal samples at the Month 6 follow-up,680 non-diarrheal samples at the Month 12 follow-up, and.236 samples collected during diarrheal illness episodes identified through passive surveillance.

Salmonella case definition

A Salmonella case was defined as any stool sample diarrheal or non-diarrheal that yielded a culture-confirmed Salmonella isolate. A new episode was defined as a positive specimen occurring after at least seven consecutive Salmonella-negative days. Consequently, each faecal specimen meeting this ≥ 7-day gap criterion and testing positive for Salmonella was considered a distinct episode of infection.

Culture and identification of Salmonella isolates

Stool samples were collected from participants at the Kossodo Medical Center (CMA) and the Polesgo Health and Social Promotion Center (CSPS). Samples were placed in Cary-Blair transport medium and transported at 2–8 °C to the GRAS laboratory for processing.

Salmonella isolation was performed using conventional stool culture techniques as previously described [23, 24]. Briefly, stool samples were inoculated onto MacConkey Agar and Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) Agar (Liofilchem®, Italy) and incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h.

Presumptive Salmonella isolates were screened using Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) Agar, Sulfur Indole Motility (SIM) Medium, and Lysine Iron Agar (LIA) (Becton Dickinson, USA), along with a urease production test. Final identification was confirmed using the API 20E system (bioMérieux, France). Partial serogrouping was performed using Bio-Rad antisera (Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Quality control for media and identification procedures was performed using reference strains Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar typhimurium ATCC® 14,028 and Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar enteritidis ATCC® 13,076.

Serotyping of Salmonella isolates was performed using a slide agglutination test according to the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme [25], following the manufacturer’s protocol for Bio-Rad antisera (Marnes-la-Coquette, France). The procedure aimed to identify the somatic (O) and flagellar (H) antigens to determine the serogroup and, where possible, the serovar. The process was conducted systematically in three main stages:Auto-agglutination and Omni-O check: each isolate was first tested for auto-agglutination in sterile saline (0.85% NaCl). Only non-autoagglutinating strains were processed further. A positive reaction was followed by testing with the corresponding monovalent O antisera to determine the specific O antigen group (e.g., O:4,5 for Group B; O:9 for Group D; O:6,7,8 for Group C). Isolates negative with these polyvalent O antisera were tested for the Vi antigen. Vi-positive strains were heated at 100 °C for 30 min to remove the capsule and then retested.H Antigen identification: for isolates with a determined O group, flagellar (H) antigens were characterized. For biphasic strains, phase inversion was performed using the Sven Gard method to express the second phase, which was then identified.

However, comprehensive serotyping was not achieved for all isolates due to limitations in the full range of H antisera required to identify all potential flagellar phases.

While the majority of isolates could not be fully serotyped, their classification as non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) is supported by several factors: biochemical profiling confirmed all isolates as Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica; the clinical context of community-acquired diarrhea in young children is inconsistent with systemic typhoidal infection; and partial serogrouping of the typed isolates revealed only NTS-associated serogroups (B, D, E). This epidemiological and laboratory evidence makes it highly probable that the untyped isolates represent NTS, which predominates in this population.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Müeller-Hinton agar (Liofilchem®, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy). A total of 16 antibiotics (Liofilchem®, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy) were tested. The antibiotic panel was selected based on their clinical relevance for treating salmonellosis and for surveillance of key resistance phenotypes as per national and international guidelines. The antibiotics included: monobactam: aztreonam (30 µg); penicillins: ampicillin (20 µg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (30 µg); cephalosporins: cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg); aminoglycosides: streptomycin (10 µg), tobramycin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg); carbapenem: imipenem (10 µg); quinolones: nalidixic acid (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg); and others: tetracycline (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (25 µg).

A 0.5 McFarland standard suspension of each isolate was prepared and inoculated on the surface of Müeller-Hinton agar. Antibiotic disks were applied using sterile tweezers. Plates were incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h. The diameters of inhibition zones were measured using a Vernier calliper. Interpretation of susceptibility was performed according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2020 guidelines.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories.

Quality control for performance testing of media, stains, reagents and identification kits was ensured using reference strains Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC® 14,028 and Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar enteritidis ATCC® 13,076.

Quality control for susceptibility testing was ensured using reference strains Escherichia coli ATCC® 25,922, E. coli ATCC® 35,218, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC® 27,853.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including proportions, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize baseline characteristics. The incidence of Salmonella infection was estimated using survival analysis techniques, specifically Kaplan–Meier estimates. A prevalent case was defined as any child in whom Salmonella was isolated from a stool sample collected at enrolment (time zero) or after a first incident case during follow-up. These cases were excluded from the incidence analysis to ensure that only new (incident) infections were considered.

Data were analysed using Stata version 19 MP (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). The primary outcome was the first episode of Salmonella infection, defined as a positive stool culture. Time-at-risk was measured in child-months, with total exposure time aggregated across the cohort to compute incidence rates. Incidence was calculated as the number of first-episode cases divided by the total number of child-months at risk, and expressed as episodes per child-month. Antimicrobial resistance rates were calculated as the number of resistant isolates divided by the total number of isolates tested. Resistance was interpreted based on phenotypic methods in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Data visualization was conducted using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [26], with resistance profiles of Salmonella isolates analysed using the AMR package in R.

Results

Distribution of Salmonella species

Of the 97 Salmonella isolates obtained from stool samples, seven identified as Salmonella serogroup A (a typhoidal serovar) were excluded from this Non-Typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) analysis. Of the 90 remaining isolates, the majority (73.3%, n=66) were identified only to the genus level as not typed Salmonella (Salmonella spp.), due to limitations in available antisera for full serogrouping (Fig. 1). Among the isolates that were partially or fully typed, serogroups B and E were the most frequently identified, each accounting for 7.8% (n=7) of all isolates, followed by group D (4.4%, n=4). A small number of isolates were identified as specific serotypes: Salmonella typhimurium, Salmonella enteritidis, and Salmonella Paratyphi B, each representing approximately 1% (n=1) of all isolates.

Distribution of Salmonella spp. by age and gender

The age group 12 - 23 months showed the highest number (n=27) of Salmonella isolations among the 90 cases. The distribution patterns by age and sex are illustrated in Fig. 2. Salmonella isolates were more frequently detected in male children compared to females. Salmonella Paratyphi B and Salmonella typhimurium were isolated in infants aged 0 to 11 months, while Salmonella enteritidis was identified in a child aged 24 to 35 months. Serogroups B, D, and E were found across all age groups. The not typed Salmonella spp. was the most common and was found more frequently in children aged 12–35 months. Although a tendency toward variation in species distribution according to age group was observed, sex differences were not significant (IRR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.49-1.15, p=0.184).

Overall incidence of non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) among children under five

Out of 2,401 stool samples collected and cultured from 680 children who completed the one-year follow-up, Salmonella was isolated from 97 samples. Of these, a total of 33 were excluded from the incidence analysis: 29 prevalent or non-incident cases (20 at enrolment; 9 occurring after a first incident case) and 4 incident cases of typhoidal Salmonella serogroup A. Of seven total serogroup A isolates identified, three were already excluded as prevalent cases, leaving four incident cases additionally removed to maintain the NTS focus. After exclusions, 64 incident NTS infections were recorded over 7,992.9 child-months of follow-up, yielding an overall incidence rate of 8.0 per 1,000 child-months (95% CI: 6.3–10.2). Sex-specific incidence was 7.3 (95% CI: 5.0–10.5) in females and 8.7 (95% CI: 6.3–12.1) in males per 1,000 child-months, with no statistically significant difference. The highest incidence occurred in children aged 12–23 months (10.3 per 1,000 child-months; 95% CI: 6.7–15.6) and the lowest in those aged 48–59 months (5.6 per 1,000 child-months; 95% CI: 2.5–12.4). Incidence was substantially higher in diarrheal cases (43.7 per 1,000 child-months) than non-diarrheal cases (6.4 per 1,000 child-months). Poisson regression confirmed diarrhea as a significant risk factor (IRR = 2.98; 95% CI: 1.78–4.98; p < 0.001). Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of Salmonella strains

Overall Salmonella resistance profiles

All 90 NTS isolates were tested for antimicrobial susceptibility. Overall, the Salmonella isolates exhibited high susceptibility to most of the antibiotics tested, particularly β-lactams such as aztreonam, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, carbapenem, as well as third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins as well as fluoroquinolones. Complete susceptibility (100%) was observed for aztreonam, imipenem, ciprofloxacin, and ceftriaxone. Aminoglycosides were also highly effective, with susceptibility rates of 98.9% for tobramycin, 96.7% for gentamicin, and 93.3% for streptomycin. In contrast, a high prevalence of resistance was observed for tetracycline, with (74.4% (67/90)) of isolates classified as resistant. Antimicrobial resistance profiles by diarrhoea status

Resistance patterns differed slightly by diarrhoea status. Among the 13 Salmonella isolates from diarrheal samples, 76.9% (10/13) were resistant to tetracycline, compared to 74.0% (57/77) in asymptomatic cases. Resistance to ampicillin was observed in 15.4% (2/13) of diarrheal isolates and 3.9% (3/77) of asymptomatic isolates. Similarly, resistance to gentamicin (7.7% (1/13) vs 2.6% (2/77)), nalidixic acid (7.7% (1/13) vs 5.2% (4/77)), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (15.4%(2/13) vs 2.6%(2/77) was higher in diarrheal samples. Prevalence of multidrug resistance Salmonellaspp

Among the 90 Salmonella isolates, four were identified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), representing an overall prevalence of 4.4% (4/90). These included two isolates from asymptomatic cases (2.6%; 2/77) and two isolates from diarrheal cases (15.4%; 2/13). The four MDR isolates were found in both male (n=1, age 24–35 months) and female (n=3, age groups 0–11, 12–23, and 48–60 months) children. No isolates met the criteria for extensively drug-resistant (XDR) or pandrug-resistant (PDR) profiles.

The MDR isolates exhibited four distinct resistance phenotypes, ranging from resistance to three to six antibiotics. One MDR isolate, identified as Salmonella typhimurium, exhibited resistance to ampicillin, streptomycin, tetracycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. The remaining three MDR isolates (classified as Salmonella spp.) showed diverse resistance patterns, including combinations of gentamicin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, ampicillin, nalidixic acid, cefepime, and tetracycline.

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the burden of Salmonella infections and AMR patterns among children under five years of age in peri-urban communities of Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso. The overall incidence rate of 8.0 per 1,000 child-months confirms that Salmonella remains a significant enteric pathogen in this high-risk population, with notably high incidence rates observed among children aged 12–23 months.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, which have identified children under two as particularly vulnerable to non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) infections [27, 28]. This increased vulnerability can be attributed to several factors, such as a decrease in maternal antibodies, the introduction of complementary foods, and increased environmental exposure during early childhood mobility [29–31].

The high incidence of NTS in diarrhoeal stools (43.7 per 1,000 child-months) further highlights its role as a major pathogen responsible for diarrhoea in this context, even though a significant proportion of cases also involved asymptomatic children. The nearly threefold higher risk (IRR= 2.98) of NTS infection during diarrheal episodes underscores the clinical importance of this pathogen in childhood gastroenteritis.

There was a notable presence of asymptomatic intestinal carriage among children under five in this cohort. Asymptomatic carriers may play a critical role in the silent transmission of Salmonella within households and communities, particularly in settings with suboptimal hygiene and sanitation [32, 33]. Intestinal carriage of non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) is not only a reservoir for ongoing environmental contamination but also a recognized risk factor for the development of invasive NTS (iNTS) disease, especially in immunocompromised individuals or those with underlying conditions such as malnutrition, malaria, or HIV infection [4, 34, 35]. Invasive NTS disease can lead to severe outcomes, including septicemia, meningitis, and death, particularly in children under five and other vulnerable populations. The presence of asymptomatic carriers thus poses a dual threat: perpetuating community transmission and increasing the risk of invasive complications [34].

The antimicrobial susceptibility profile revealed encouragingly high effectiveness for key antibiotics, including ciprofloxacin, imipenem, aztreonam, and third-generation cephalosporins (all 100% susceptible). A particularly notable finding in our study is the high prevalence of resistance to tetracycline among NTS isolates (74.4%), affecting both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases. This widespread resistance across both clinical presentations suggests that tetracycline-resistant Salmonella strains are circulating extensively in the community. The near-equal resistance rates between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals point toward environmental or agricultural sources of transmission, such as contaminated food products or water supplies, where tetracycline use is common in livestock. This aligns with previous evidence linking high tetracycline resistance in enteric bacteria to unregulated antibiotic use in animal husbandry in low-resource settings [36, 37]. Although tetracycline is not a first-line treatment for salmonellosis in children [38], the high level of resistance raises broader public health concerns. about antimicrobial pressure in the community and potential co-selection for resistance to other antibiotic classes through mobile genetic elements [36].

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was observed in 4.4% of NTS isolates. While this is relatively low compared to MDR rates reported in other African settings [39, 40], the presence of MDR strains remains concerning [41]. These strains have the potential to cause invasive disease and limit therapeutic options, especially in young children and immunocompromised populations [42]. Importantly, no extensively drug-resistant (XDR) or pan-drug-resistant (PDR) strains were identified, suggesting these resistance patterns may not yet be widespread in this setting. Our study has several strengths, including its prospective cohort design with active and passive surveillance over a one-year period. However, this manuscript focused on reporting the incidence rates and antimicrobial resistance patterns of NTS. Future research should investigate the behavioural, environmental, and household-level factors contributing to transmission. Furthermore, some limitations should be acknowledged, including potential underestimation of burden through culture-based methods, inability to fully serotype the majority of isolates (70.1%), and lack of molecular resistance profiling to identify underlying genetic mechanisms.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable evidence for policymakers and clinicians. The relatively low MDR rate (4.4%) presents an opportunity to reinforce rational antibiotic use and prevent further resistance development. At the same time, the high tetracycline resistance and detection of asymptomatic carriers underscore the need for integrated approaches combining hygiene promotion, AMR surveillance, and community education.

To address these gaps, we plan to perform whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of the Salmonella isolates in future work. WGS will allow for high-resolution serotype identification, detection of AMR genes and mobile genetic elements, and a deeper understanding of phylogenetic relationships between strains. This approach will strengthen molecular surveillance, contribute to regional epidemiological mapping, and support the development of targeted public health interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant burden of Salmonella infections and a concerning prevalence of antimicrobial resistance among young children in peri-urban Burkina Faso. A key finding is the very high resistance to tetracycline (74.4%), which signals substantial antimicrobial pressure in the community.

The low rate of multidrug resistance to key antibiotics like cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones is encouraging for current treatment options. Collectively, these results highlight the urgency of reinforcing AMR surveillance systems, improving diagnostic capacity, and promoting rational antibiotic use. Future work should integrate WGS to elucidate transmission dynamics and resistance mechanisms, guiding targeted interventions to reduce the burden ofSalmonella and AMR in this vulnerable population.
