Entropy Regularization as Robustness under Bayesian Drift Uncertainty
Andy Au

Abstract
We study entropy-regularized mean-variance portfolio optimization under Bayesian drift uncertainty. Gaussian policies remain optimal under partial information, the value function is quadratic in wealth, and belief-dependent coefficients admit closed-form solutions. The mean control is identical to deterministic Bayesian Markowitz feedback; entropy regularization affects only the policy variance. Additionally, this variance does not affect information gain, and instead provides belief-dependent robustness. Notably, optimal policy variance increases with posterior conviction | m t | |m_{t}| , forcing greater action randomization when mean position is most aggressive.

Introduction

Portfolio optimization under parameter uncertainty is a fundamental problem in finance. The classical mean-variance framework of
8
produces clean solutions when parameters are known, but real markets have unknown drift. The difficulty of estimating expected returns
(
9
)
leads to unstable allocations when one naively plugs in estimated means.

Two recent lines of work address this from different angles:

1.

Bayesian learning:

3
solve the continuous-time Markowitz problem when drift is unknown. Using Kalman–Bucy filtering, they reduce the problem to a semilinear PDE in the belief state and obtain closed-form solutions for Gaussian priors. The optimal control is deterministic.

2.

Entropy-regularized control:

11
study mean-variance optimization with known drift but replace deterministic controls with stochastic policies. Adding an entropy bonus yields tractable Gaussian solutions and connects to relaxed stochastic control. The problem closes through Riccati ODEs.

These two lines sit within broader literature. Optimal investment under partial information on drift was started by
7
and extended to different utility by
2
and
1
;
3
is the mean-variance instance. Entropy-regularized control in RL traces to
13
in discrete time and
5
(Soft Actor–Critic) in practice;
11
provides the continuous-time theory. Our finding that entropy regularization acts as a robustness mechanism connects to the robust portfolio optimization literature
(
4
;
6
)
, where agents hedge against model misspecification. Our robustness comes from the entropy penalty rather than explicit minimax over a model set.

This paper considers the intersection: entropy-regularized control when drift is unknown. The interaction produces behavior absent from either model individually. In this framework, entropy regularization and Bayesian learning are orthogonal, yet the combination creates a counterintuitive belief-dependent policy variance.

2
Problem Formulation

2.1
Market Model

Consider a market with a risk-free asset earning rate
r
and a risky asset with price dynamics

\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_{t},

where
\sigma>0
is known and
\mu
is unknown. We parameterize the unknown drift by the Sharpe ratio
\rho:=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The Sharpe ratio
\rho
is independent of
W
and has Gaussian prior:
\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},P_{0})
.

We work under the physical measure and model the excess drift
\mu-r
as constant but unknown. The investor’s uncertainty is captured by the prior on
\rho
.

2.2
Bayesian Filtering

The investor observes prices but not
\rho
directly. Define the
whitened
cumulative excess return

Y_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\frac{dS_{s}}{S_{s}}-r\,ds\right),

which satisfies
dY_{t}=\rho\,dt+dW_{t}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(Y_{s}:s\leq t)
be the observation filtration, and define the posterior moments

m_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[\rho\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}],\qquad P_{t}:=\Var(\rho\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}).

Proposition 2.2

(Posterior dynamics)
.

The posterior remains Gaussian:
\rho\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t},P_{t})
, with dynamics

\displaystyle dm_{t}

\displaystyle=P_{t}\,d\widehat{W}_{t},

(1)

\displaystyle dP_{t}

\displaystyle=-P_{t}^{2}\,dt,

(2)

where the innovation
d\widehat{W}_{t}:=dY_{t}-m_{t}\,dt
is a Brownian motion in
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. The posterior variance has closed-form
P_{t}=\frac{P_{0}}{1+P_{0}t}
.

This result follows from standard Kalman–Bucy filtering. Three features stand out:

1.

P_{t}
is deterministic (uncertainty decays at rate
O(\frac{1}{t})
regardless of observations).

2.

m_{t}
is a martingale converging a.s. to
\rho
.

3.

Posterior dynamics (
1
)–(
2
) depend on the observation process
Y
, not the control policy
\pi
. Learning is policy independent.

2.3
Wealth Dynamics and Bilinear Structure

Let
X_{t}:=e^{-rt}\bar{X}_{t}
be discounted wealth and
u_{t}
the discounted dollar position in the risky asset. Using the innovation decomposition
dW_{t}=d\widehat{W}_{t}-(\rho-m_{t})\,dt
:

dX_{t}=\sigma u_{t}(m_{t}\,dt+d\widehat{W}_{t}).

(3)

The drift
\sigma m_{t}u_{t}
is bilinear, a product of belief and control. This is the break from classical LQG, where drift is affine in state and control separately. The bilinear structure has two consequences: it prevents the HJB equation from closing into Riccati ODEs (see Proposition
4.1
), and thus requires the exponential substitution of Section
4.2
.

Under the observation filtration, the pair
(X_{t},m_{t})
is Markov, with
P_{t}
as a deterministic time-dependent parameter. Through filtering, we have gone from partial information to a fully observed Markov control problem.

2.4
Entropy-Regularized Objective

Following
11
, we replace the pointwise control
u_{t}
with a stochastic policy
\pi_{t}
, a probability distribution over positions.

Definition 2.3

(Admissible policy)
.

An admissible policy is a progressively measurable family
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of probability densities on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\pi_{t}(u)\,du\,dt\right]<\infty
.

We work at the generator level, where the controlled dynamics depend on
\pi
only through its moments; this is the relaxed control formulation of
11
.

The entropy-regularized value function is

V(t,x,m,P)=\inf_{\pi}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X_{T}-w)^{2}+\tau\int_{t}^{T}H(\pi_{s})\,ds\;\Big|\;X_{t}=x,m_{t}=m,P_{t}=P\right],

(4)

where
w\in\mathbb{R}
is the target wealth level,
\tau>0
is the entropy weight, and
H(\pi):=\int\pi(u)\log\pi(u)\,du
is the negative of differential entropy. Following the classical embedding of
12
,
w
plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier for the mean constraint
\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]=z
, and sweeping
w
traces the efficient frontier.

The entropy term penalizes concentrated policies. As
\tau\to 0
, we recover the deterministic Bayesian Markowitz problem of
3
.

3
HJB Equation and Optimal Policy

3.1
Generator

Under policy
\pi_{t}
with mean
\bar{u}
and variance
\varsigma^{2}
, the generator of any smooth
\varphi(t,x,m,P)
is

\mathcal{L}^{\pi}\varphi=\sigma\bar{u}m\,\varphi_{x}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\bar{u}^{2}+\varsigma^{2})\varphi_{xx}+\sigma\bar{u}P\,\varphi_{xm}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}\varphi_{mm}-P^{2}\varphi_{P}.

(5)

The policy only enters through its first two moments. The
\varphi_{xx}
term involves
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[u^{2}]=\bar{u}^{2}+\varsigma^{2}
because
(dX)^{2}=\sigma^{2}u^{2}\,dt
, and averaging over
\pi
retains both moments. The mixed term
\varphi_{xm}
involves
\bar{u}
only, because
dX\,dm=\sigma uP_{t}\,dt
is linear in
u
, so averaging over
\pi
only sees the mean. Randomizing the control introduces additional diffusion in wealth that is conditionally independent of the innovation
d\widehat{W}_{t}
, contributing to
(dX)^{2}
but not to the covariance
dX\,dm
.

3.2
HJB Equation

Since
(X_{t},m_{t},P_{t})
is Markov under the observation filtration (with
P_{t}
deterministic), the dynamic programming principle applies. The HJB equation is

\partial_{t}V+\inf_{\pi}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{\pi}V+\tau H(\pi)\right\}=0,\qquad V(T,x,m,P)=(x-w)^{2}.

(6)

3.3
Gaussian Optimality

Lemma 3.1

(Optimal policy is Gaussian)
.

Suppose
V\in C^{1,2}
with
V_{xx}>0
. The optimal policy is
\pi^{*}=\mathcal{N}(\bar{u}^{*},\varsigma^{*2})
with

\bar{u}^{*}=-\frac{mV_{x}+PV_{xm}}{\sigma V_{xx}},\qquad\varsigma^{*2}=\frac{\tau}{\sigma^{2}V_{xx}}.

(7)

Proof.

The
\pi
-dependent terms in
\mathcal{L}^{\pi}V+\tau H(\pi)
are

\Phi(\pi):=\sigma\bar{u}(mV_{x}+PV_{xm})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\bar{u}^{2}+\varsigma^{2})V_{xx}+\tau H(\pi).

Taking the first variation under
\int\pi=1
gives

\log\pi^{*}(u)+1+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\sigma u(mV_{x}+PV_{xm})+\frac{\sigma^{2}u^{2}}{2}V_{xx}\right)=\text{const}.

The exponent is quadratic in
u
, so
\pi^{*}
is Gaussian. Completing the square gives the stated mean and variance.

\square

Remark 3.2

(Mean-variance separation)
.

The optimal policy separates cleanly: the mean
\bar{u}^{*}
depends on beliefs and value gradients, while the variance
\varsigma^{*2}
depends only on the curvature
V_{xx}
and the entropy weight
\tau
. The coupling term
PV_{xm}
in the mean is new relative to
11
and reflects the influence of drift uncertainty on the optimal position. Notably, the mean control is independent of
\tau
; entropy regularization affects only the policy variance, not the optimal mean action.

3.4
Reduced HJB

Substituting the Gaussian optimizer into (
6
):

0=V_{t}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}V_{mm}-P^{2}V_{P}-\frac{(mV_{x}+PV_{xm})^{2}}{2V_{xx}}-\frac{\tau}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{2\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}V_{xx}}\right),

(8)

with terminal condition
V(T,x,m,P)=(x-w)^{2}
.

4
Value Function Structure

In classical LQG and
11
, the value function is polynomial
in the state and the HJB closes into Riccati ODEs. The bilinear drift

\sigma m_{t}u_{t}
prevents this here; the nonlinear term

\frac{(mV_{x}+PV_{xm})^{2}}{V_{xx}}
increases the degree in
m
faster
than the linear PDE terms can cancel.

Proposition 4.1

(Polynomial impossibility)
.

No polynomial ansatz in
(x,m)
of finite degree satisfies the reduced HJB (
8
). (See Appendix
A
for the full proof.)

Intuitively, the nonlinear term
\frac{G^{2}}{V_{xx}}
squares the belief-weighted gradient, so each attempt to cap the degree in
m
at a finite level is defeated by the HJB nonlinearity.

We therefore seek a solution quadratic in wealth but nonpolynomial in belief.

4.1
Quadratic-in-Wealth Ansatz

The terminal condition is quadratic in just
x
. Following
3
, we seek a solution of the form

V(t,x,m,P)=A(t,m,P)x^{2}+B(t,m,P)x+C(t,m,P),

(9)

where coefficients depend on beliefs but need not be polynomial in
m
. We verify below that this ansatz satisfies the HJB.

Proposition 4.2

(Coefficient system)
.

The ansatz (
9
) satisfies (
8
) if and only if
(A,B,C)
solve

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A_{t}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}A_{mm}-P^{2}A_{P}-\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{A},

(10)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=B_{t}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}B_{mm}-P^{2}B_{P}-\frac{\Gamma\Lambda}{A},

(11)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=C_{t}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}C_{mm}-P^{2}C_{P}-\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{4A}-\frac{\tau}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}A}\right),

(12)

where
\Gamma:=mA+PA_{m}
and
\Lambda:=mB+PB_{m}
, with terminal conditions
A(T)=1
,
B(T)=-2w
,
C(T)=w^{2}
.

The system is triangular: (
10
) is closed in
A
; given
A
, (
11
) is linear in
B
; given
(A,B)
, (
12
) is linear in
C
.

Proposition 4.3

(
w
-separation)
.

Within the system (
10
)–(
12
): (i)
A
is independent of
w
; (ii)
B=-2wA
; (iii) writing
C=w^{2}A+D
, the function
D
is independent of
w
and satisfies

0=D_{t}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}D_{mm}-P^{2}D_{P}-\frac{\tau}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}A}\right),\qquad D(T)=0.

(13)

So the problem reduces to solving (
10
) for
A
, then (
13
) for
D
.

4.2
Closed-form Solution

Since
P_{t}=\frac{P_{0}}{1+P_{0}t}
is deterministic, we can write
A(t,m):=A(t,m,P_{t})
along the characteristic
dP=-P^{2}\,dt
, reducing the coefficient system to two dimensions
(t,m)
. Following
3
, Section 4.3.2, the exponential substitution
A=e^{R}
transforms (
10
) into a semilinear PDE which admits a quadratic solution
R(t,m)=\alpha(t)m^{2}+\gamma(t)
.

Proposition 4.4

(Closed-form solution)
.

For Gaussian priors,
A(t,m)=e^{\alpha(t)m^{2}+\gamma(t)}
with

\alpha(t)=-\frac{(1+P_{0}t)(T-t)}{1+P_{0}(2T-t)},

(14)

and

\gamma(t)=\int_{t}^{T}P_{s}^{2}\alpha(s)\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{(1+P_{0}t)(1+P_{0}(2T-t))}{(1+P_{0}T)^{2}}\right).

(15)

Proof sketch.

With
A=e^{\alpha m^{2}+\gamma}
and
P=P_{t}
deterministic, the
A
-PDE reduces to matching coefficients:

\displaystyle m^{2}\text{-terms:}

\displaystyle\quad\alpha^{\prime}=1+4P_{t}\alpha+2P_{t}^{2}\alpha^{2},\quad\alpha(T)=0,

\displaystyle m^{0}\text{-terms:}

\displaystyle\quad\gamma^{\prime}=-P_{t}^{2}\alpha,\quad\gamma(T)=0.

The
\alpha
-equation is a Riccati ODE solved by the stated formula. Integrating
\gamma^{\prime}=-P_{t}^{2}\alpha
from
t
to
T
with terminal condition
\gamma(T)=0
gives
\gamma(t)=\int_{t}^{T}P_{s}^{2}\alpha(s)\,ds
. The closed-form follows by direct integration.

\square

Remark 4.5

(
\tau
independence of
A
)
.

The entropy weight
\tau
does not appear in
A
or
\alpha
; it enters in the
D
equation only. This is because
A
determines the value function curvature
V_{xx}=2A
, but
A
itself is determined by the underlying Bayesian Markowitz problem, not by the choice to randomize. Since
\alpha(t)<0
and
\gamma(t)<0
for
t<T
, we have
A(t,m)<1
whenever

. Drift uncertainty reduces the value function curvature
V_{xx}=2A
relative
to the terminal condition.

The formula for
\alpha
becomes more interpretable when written in terms of current posterior variance:

\alpha(t)=-\frac{T-t}{1+2P_{t}(T-t)},

which makes the dependence on time-to-go and current uncertainty explicit.

4.3
The Entropy Premium

Proposition
4.3
reduced the problem to solving (
13
) for
D
. Since
P_{t}
is deterministic, we work along the characteristic and write
\tilde{D}(t,m):=D(t,m,P_{t})
. Using
\frac{dP_{t}}{dt}=-P_{t}^{2}
, the PDE becomes

0=\tilde{D}_{t}+\frac{P_{t}^{2}}{2}\tilde{D}_{mm}-\frac{\tau}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}A(t,m)}\right),\qquad\tilde{D}(T,m)=0.

(16)

Substituting
A=e^{\alpha m^{2}+\gamma}
, the source term becomes

\log\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}A}\right)=\log\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}}-\alpha(t)m^{2}-\gamma(t),

which is quadratic in
m
. This motivates the ansatz
\tilde{D}(t,m)=\eta(t)m^{2}+\zeta(t)
.

Proposition 4.6

(Entropy premium)
.

The function
D(t,m,P_{t})=\eta(t)m^{2}+\zeta(t)
where

\displaystyle\eta(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\int_{t}^{T}\alpha(s)\,ds,

(17)

\displaystyle\zeta(t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\left[\log\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}}-\gamma(s)\right]-P_{s}^{2}\,\eta(s)\right)ds.

(18)

Since
\alpha(t)<0
for
t<T
, we have
\eta(t)<0
for
t<T
.

Proof.

Substituting
\tilde{D}=\eta m^{2}+\zeta
into (
16
) and matching coefficients:

\displaystyle m^{2}\text{-terms:}

\displaystyle\quad\eta^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\tau}{2}\alpha(t),\quad\eta(T)=0,

\displaystyle m^{0}\text{-terms:}

\displaystyle\quad\zeta^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\tau}{2}\left[\log\frac{\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}}-\gamma(t)\right]-P_{t}^{2}\,\eta(t),\quad\zeta(T)=0.

Integrating backward from
T
gives (
17
)–(
18
).

\square

The complete value function is thus

V(t,x,m)=e^{\alpha(t)m^{2}+\gamma(t)}(x-w)^{2}+\eta(t)m^{2}+\zeta(t).

(19)

Remark 4.7

(Policy independence of entropy premium)
.

The entropy premium
D=\eta m^{2}+\zeta
is independent of wealth

. Since the optimal policy (

) depends only on
V_{x}
and

, which involve only

, the function

does not affect optimal behavior. It captures the expected cost of entropy regularization, not a guide to action.

Using Lemma

with

\bar{u}^{*}=-\frac{m(1+2P_{t}\alpha)}{\sigma}(x-w),\qquad\varsigma^{*2}=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}A}.

The mean control is linear in tracking error

with belief-dependent gain. The policy variance depends on

Remark 4.8

(Verification)
.

The candidate value function (

) is a classical solution to the HJB equation (

). Standard verification arguments (see

5
Limiting Cases

We verify consistency with existing results in two limits.

5.1
Known Drift:
P_{0}\to 0

When
P_{0}\to 0
, the posterior collapses:
P_{t}\equiv 0
and
m_{t}\equiv m_{0}=:\rho
. From (
14
),
\alpha(t)\to-(T-t)
. From (
15
),
\gamma(t)\to 0
since
P_{s}\to 0
. Thus
A(t)\to e^{-(T-t)\rho^{2}}
.

The reduced HJB becomes

0=V_{t}-\frac{\rho^{2}V_{x}^{2}}{2V_{xx}}-\frac{\tau}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{2\pi\tau}{\sigma^{2}V_{xx}}\right),

and the optimal policy is

\bar{u}^{*}=-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}(x-w),\qquad\varsigma^{*2}=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}}e^{\rho^{2}(T-t)},

recovering

, Theorem 1.

5.2
Deterministic Control:
\tau\to 0

When
\tau\to 0
, the policy variance
\varsigma^{*2}=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}A}\to 0
. The policy collapses to deterministic feedback:

u^{*}=\bar{u}^{*}=-\frac{m(1+2P_{t}\alpha)}{\sigma}(x-w).

This matches the deterministic feedback control in
3
, and the framework consistently interpolates between the two known cases.

6
Belief-dependent Policy Variance

Sections
3
–
4
established that entropy regularization leaves the mean control and value function curvature unchanged; it enters only through the policy variance and the additive entropy premium
D
. Particularly, the posterior dynamics (
1
)–(
2
) are functions of the observation process alone, so randomizing the control policy cannot accelerate learning about
\rho
. The mean action is identical to
3
; entropy regularization adds only a policy variance and an additive value correction.

Then why entropy regularization? We now turn to the structure of the policy variance, which is the primary contribution of the combined model.

6.1
Conviction Driven Variance

Recall from (
20
) that the optimal policy variance is
\varsigma^{*2}(t,m)=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}A(t,m)}
with
A=e^{\alpha(t)m^{2}+\gamma(t)}
.

Proposition 6.1

(Belief-dependent variance)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
with
\tau>0
and
T<\infty
, the optimal policy variance
\varsigma^{*2}(t,m)
is: symmetric in
m
, minimized at
m=0
, and strictly increasing in
|m|
for
|m|>0
at each fixed
t<T
.

Stronger posterior conviction (larger
|m|
, the magnitude of the drift estimate, not posterior precision
P_{t}
) implies greater policy randomization.

Proof.

From (
20
),

\varsigma^{*2}(t,m)=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}}\,e^{-\alpha(t)m^{2}-\gamma(t)}.

This depends on
m
only through
m^{2}
, giving symmetry. Differentiating in
m
:

\frac{\partial\,\varsigma^{*2}}{\partial m}=-2\alpha(t)\,m\,\varsigma^{*2}(t,m).

By Proposition
4.4
,
\alpha(t)<0
for
t<T
, so
-2\alpha(t)>0
. The derivative has the same sign as
m
; negative for
m<0
, zero at
m=0
, positive for
m>0
. Hence
\varsigma^{*2}
is minimized at
m=0
and strictly increasing in
|m|
for
|m|>0
.

\square

6.3
Economic Interpretation

When
|m|
is large, the certainty-equivalent mean control
\bar{u}^{*}\propto m(x-w)
prescribes an aggressive position. And structurally:

|m|\text{ large}\;\Rightarrow\;|\bar{u}^{*}|\text{ large}\;\Rightarrow\;A(t,m)\text{ small (}\alpha<0\text{)}\;\Rightarrow\;\varsigma^{*2}=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}A}\text{ large.}

Entropy regularization forces greater action randomization when the mean position is most aggressive; softening the consequences of acting too strongly on a posterior that may be wrong. This is a robustness mechanism, not an information seeking one. We use robustness here in the informal sense; entropy regularization spreads probability mass across actions when our model prescribes aggressive positions, softening exposure to model error. A formal connection to minimax robustness with KL-ball duality
(
6
)
lies beyond the present scope.

7
Conclusion

We solved the entropy-regularized mean-variance problem under Bayesian drift uncertainty, obtaining closed-form solutions consistent with both
3
and
11
as limiting cases. The principal finding is that entropy regularization, though orthogonal to learning in this framework, generates a belief-dependent policy variance that increases with posterior conviction. This acts as a robustness mechanism against aggressive positioning under model risk rather than an information-seeking tool.

Our model is minimal: one risky asset, Gaussian prior, constant volatility, no transaction costs, and no position constraints. These simplifications are what allow us to obtain closed-form solutions, however, they limit direct applicability.

Several extensions are then natural. In the multi-asset setting, all the structure remains, as shown by
3
. As noted by
11
the Gaussian optimal policy means that model-free RL algorithms can be restricted to learn efficiently. Non-Gaussian priors would break the closed-form filtering and require numerical PDE / particle filtering methods; what survives remains an open question.

Proof of Polynomial Impossibility

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Fix

and suppose
V(t,\cdot,\cdot,P)
is polynomial of finite degree in
