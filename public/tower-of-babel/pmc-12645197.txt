Cerebral cavernous malformation with secondary bacterial infection: illustrative case
Authors: Kantha Siddhanth Gujjari, Ameen Farsakh, Frederick P Mariajoseph, Bi Yi Chen, Amitha Thomas, Beena Kumar, Ahmad Badran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSecondary infection of cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) is exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature. While CCMs are typically sporadic or familial vascular malformations, there is growing evidence that local or systemic infections may influence their pathogenesis or complicate existing lesions.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report the case of a 5-year-old boy who presented with clinical features of meningitis and was found to have a right frontal multilobulated cystic mass with appearances concerning for abscess or neoplasm. The patient underwent resection, which confirmed a cavernous malformation with superimposed abscess formation. Cultures identified Streptococcus pneumoniae, and subsequent investigations revealed multiple CCMs as well as a pathogenic KRIT1 gene mutation. The child made a complete recovery with targeted antibiotic therapy.LESSONSThis is the first documented case of a CCM secondarily infected by S. pneumoniae. It emphasizes the importance of considering infection in patients with known or suspected CCMs presenting with systemic or intracranial infection, particularly when imaging is atypical. Cavernous malformations, or cavernomas, are abnormal clusters of capillaries that can cause hemorrhage, result in surrounding hemosiderin staining.1 Approximately 80% of cavernomas occur sporadically, while the remaining cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.2,3 The incidence of cavernomas is estimated to be between 0.15 and 0.56 per 100,000 individuals.1,2 Cavernomas associated with bacterial infections are extremely rare, with only 4 cases documented in the literature to date; of these, only 2 are considered to be cavernomas with definitive secondary bacterial infection.4–7 Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most commonly implicated organism in bacterial meningitis in children older than 1 month of age and adults.8 However, no cases have been reported in the literature linking S. pneumoniae to secondary hematogenous infection of an underlying cavernous malformation. Secondary hematogenous infection is believed to occur when bacteria enter the bloodstream, triggering activation of the complement and coagulation systems, which releases inflammatory mediators that facilitate bacterial crossing of the blood-brain barrier.8 Timely recognition and diagnosis are crucial to prevent the high morbidity associated with this condition.8

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation and Investigations

A previously healthy 5-year-old boy of Southeast Asian birth origin presented to the emergency department with a 2-week history of fevers, rhinorrhoea, sore throat, and retro-orbital headache. He was alert and showed no neurological deficits, leading to a provisional diagnosis of a viral illness with associated viral conjunctivitis, after which he was discharged.

The following day, he returned with worsening headaches, photophobia, neck stiffness, and fever of 38.1°C. At this stage, the patient was alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15 without any focal neurological deficit on cranial nerve or peripheral neurological examination. His other vital signs were also normal for his age (oxygen saturation 94% on room air, respiratory rate 24 breaths per minute, heart rate 91 beats per minute, blood pressure 111/81 mm Hg). There were no skin rashes or stigmata of systemic infection.

Intravenous cefotaxime was initiated for suspected meningitis. Oral midazolam and inhaled nitrous oxide were administered for sedation to facilitate a lumbar puncture. However, the procedure was abandoned due to an anaphylactic reaction to cefotaxime, requiring intramuscular adrenaline. The patient subsequently underwent a noncontrast brain CT that revealed a 53 × 42 × 42–mm multicystic right frontal lesion with calcification and vasogenic edema, with initial differential diagnoses of supratentorial ependymoma or another glial tumor. The neurosurgical team was subsequently consulted.

Immediately following the CT scan, the patient’s condition deteriorated; he became drowsy, with a GCS score of 11 (eyes: 3, verbal: 2, motor: 6). His pupils were equal and reactive bilaterally at 4 mm, and there was full range of eye movements without ophthalmoplegia. There was no motor weakness in upper and lower limbs bilaterally, with sensation also intact throughout. He was meningitic with photophobia and neck stiffness, hence not tolerating bedside fundoscopy. His serum white blood cell count was 41.3 × 109/L with a neutrophil count of 37.17 × 109/L. Concern for a cerebral abscess led to intubation and further neuroimaging in the form of MRI, following intravenous administration of dexamethasone and prophylactic levetiracetam. CT and MRI (Figs. 1 and 2) revealed a large, multilobulated right frontal mass (40 × 53 × 49 mm) with central nodular enhancement and a ring-enhancing cystic component with diffusion restriction, significant edema, and areas of increased susceptibility. Three smaller nonenhancing foci suggested multiple cavernomas with recent hemorrhage. However, because of the atypical enhancement and diffusion restriction, the possibility of atypical abscesses, such as tuberculous abscess or neurocysticercosis, was considered. Empirical intravenous moxifloxacin, vancomycin, and metronidazole were initiated in consultation with a pediatric infectious diseases team.

Operative Management

The patient underwent a stereotactic craniotomy and resection of the right frontal lesion (Fig. 3). Postdurotomy, the swollen frontal lobes were decompressed by draining CSF from the optico-carotid cistern. Brain relaxation allowed for a corticotomy to access the lesion, which was heterogeneous with cystic and solid components, including purulent fluid. The lesion showed hemosiderin staining and vascular components, with the right frontal horn of the lateral ventricle breached. A Bactiseal antibiotic-impregnated external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed. Intraoperative blood cultures returned positive for gram-positive cocci, later identified as S. pneumoniae. Postoperative MRI confirmed complete lesion evacuation (Fig. 2F), and the patient was extubated without new neurological deficits.

Heterogeneous appearance of the lesion, with regions of calcification and a solid capsule encasing the cystic lesion. The capsule was circumferentially dissected and removed. The cystic components of the lesion were decompressed of purulent fluid before this image was taken.

Following microbiology results, the infectious disease team adjusted antibiotics to moxifloxacin monotherapy. The immunological workup showed no underlying immunodeficiency, and the EVD was removed without complications. Histopathology confirmed a cavernoma (Fig. 4) with a superimposed abscess formation (Fig. 5), and genetic testing as an outpatient later identified a KRIT1 mutation and G6PD deficiency. Postoperative moxifloxacin was continued for 6 weeks before antibiotic cessation.

Postoperative Follow-Up

The patient was followed up with repeat MRI and clinical review at 8 weeks postoperatively, followed with a plan for 12-monthly surveillance MRI studies to monitor the remaining cavernomas. At time of the 8-week clinical review, the patient’s regular oral levetiracetam 250 mg twice daily was ceased as he did not have any further seizures. Ophthalmological evaluation ruled out retinal vascular malformations, and the patient made a full recovery, remaining asymptomatic from his other cavernomas.

Discussion

Observations

Unique Features of the Presenting Case

This case represents the first documented instance in the literature of a cavernous malformation with secondary hematogenous infection by S. pneumoniae, the second reported case of secondary infection of a cavernoma in a patient with a KRIT1 mutation, and the second instance involving a pediatric patient with a cavernoma and secondary infection. Overall, it is the fifth reported case of a cavernoma with secondary infection.

The patient was found to have an underlying genetic mutation associated with his multiple cavernomas. One of the cavernomas was associated with a superimposed infection. There was an acute onset of infective symptoms, in keeping with the clinical manifestations of a right frontal cerebral abscess and meningitis. The underlying genetic condition and presence of multiple other cavernomas suggest that the cavernoma was a primary condition rather than a secondary cavernoma following an intracranial infection. This link was further supported on histopathological and microbiological testing of the operative samples, as demonstrated on the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stains.

Histopathology Insights

Histological analysis in our case revealed dense infiltration by neutrophils, histiocytes, and lymphocytes, consistent with an acute inflammatory response. This demonstrates that systemic infection can convert a previously silent cerebral cavernous malformation into a symptomatic abscess. Mechanistically, KRIT1-mutated endothelial cells exhibit impaired junctional integrity, increased vascular permeability, and heightened susceptibility to oxidative stress.2,3 Systemic infection triggers cytokine release, complement activation, and immune cell recruitment, further destabilizing the blood-brain barrier and facilitating bacterial invasion.2,3,8

Comparison With Reported Cases

To our knowledge, there are 4 other reports of cavernous malformation with superimposed bacterial or fungal infection. House et al. reported on the case of a 40-year-old female who underwent multiple craniotomies for cavernoma resection and secondary abscess drainage.6 While purulent fluid was present during the initial surgery, no cultures were obtained, and subsequent cultures were negative until a third surgery when Mycoplasma hominis was identified.6 Because of the delayed culture results, it is unclear whether the infection was due to secondary hematogenous spread or a postoperative wound infection. Sun et al. described a 60-year-old male who presented with intermittent headaches and right leg weakness, with a history of a left temporal abscess 3 years prior that was managed with antibiotics alone as he declined surgery at the time.7 Imaging showed hemorrhage and an expanded abscess in the same area, and resection revealed an abscess with Aspergillus fumigatus within a cavernous malformation.7 The lack of cavernoma evidence on initial imaging and later hemorrhage suggests this may have been an abscess with secondary cavernoma formation rather than an infected cavernoma, differing etiologically from the present case. Another case of secondary mucormycosis infection of a cavernoma was recently described in a 22-year-old female who was immunocompetent.9

Borsaru and Naidoo described an 18-month-old female who initially presented with a focal seizure and a right frontal cavernous malformation with subacute hemorrhage, managed conservatively.5 Following a brief gastroenteritis episode complicated by Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, she presented multiple times with irritability and drowsiness. Imaging eventually showed a multiloculated, rim-enhancing lesion consistent with an abscess. Craniotomy confirmed a cavernous malformation and S. aureus abscess, representing an underlying cavernoma with secondary infection in a pediatric patient, similar to the current case.5 Recently, Sanders et al. reported on the case of a 7-year-old child with a right deep-seated cavernous malformation secondarily infected by Streptococcus pyogenes who made a good recovery following surgical aspiration of the infected collection and antibiotics.10

Amoah et al. reported a 16-year-old female who initially presented with fever and meningismus, with CSF findings suggestive of bacterial meningitis.4 Initial imaging revealed a left cerebellar lesion, later confirmed as a cavernoma with turbid fluid in the capsule and positive Campylobacter fetus cultures from intraoperative samples, CSF, and blood.4 Follow-up MRI identified a spinal cord cavernoma, and genetic testing confirmed a KRIT1 mutation, illustrating another case of cavernoma with secondary infection in a KRIT1 mutation patient, analogous to our report.4

Lessons

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

Emerging evidence suggests that the pathogenesis of cavernomas may be influenced not only by genetic mutations, such as those in KRIT1, but also by inflammatory and immune-mediated mechanisms.1–3 In patients with KRIT1 mutations, endothelial cell integrity is compromised due to dysregulation of the cavernoma complex, leading to increased vascular permeability, defective intercellular junctions, and a predisposition to hemorrhage.2,3 These fragile vascular channels lack mature blood-brain barrier architecture, making them more susceptible to infiltration by circulating pathogens during systemic infection.8 In the context of bacteremia, inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, alongside complement activation, further disrupt the blood-brain barrier, allowing immune cells and bacteria like S. pneumoniae to translocate into neural tissue.8 The resulting localized immune response, dominated by neutrophils, histiocytes, and lymphocytes—as seen histologically in this case—can convert a previously asymptomatic cavernoma into a symptomatic abscess.4,5 This case adds to a growing body of literature, suggesting that systemic infection and immune dysregulation may not only complicate cavernomas but also contribute to their evolution and clinical manifestation.

Clinical Relevance

Secondary infection of a cavernoma is exceedingly rare. This case illustrates this unusual condition in a patient with a genetic predisposition (KRIT1 mutation). Together with a few similar cases reported in the literature, it suggests that secondary infections of cavernomas should be considered when there is clinical suspicion of CNS infection in a patient known to have a cavernoma, even if imaging findings are not typical for infection or cavernoma.
