Slaughter blood-derived bioactive peptides: structure-function relationships, species-specific bioactivities, gastrointestinal stability, and sustainable applications in food systems
Authors: Phanthipha Laosam, Yong Yue, Mahmoud Rouabhia, Saranya Suwanangul, Jukkrapong Pinyo, Sasikan Katemala, Pramote Paengkoum, Chatsirin Nakharuthai, Jaksuma Pongsetkul, Nattapol Pongsamai, Papungkorn Sangsawad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This is a comprehensive review focusing on the utilization of slaughter blood proteins as sources of bioactive peptides for functional food applications. Despite significant advances in bioactive peptide research, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding species-specific variations in peptide bioactivity and the complex interplay between processing methods and functional properties. Enzymatic hydrolysis using various proteases generates peptides with diverse bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, ACE-inhibitory, and DPP-IV inhibitory effects. This review uniquely integrates proteolytic mechanisms with in-depth molecular characterization across multiple animal sources, addressing the previously fragmented understanding of structure-function relationships. Notably, gastrointestinal stability is significantly enhanced by proline residues and hydrophobic amino acids, offering a novel structural insight for predicting peptide bioavailability. Hemoglobin decolorization methods enable broader food industry applications. Recent innovations in processing technologies and delivery systems position blood-derived peptides as valuable functional ingredients for sustainable food systems.

Introduction

Slaughter blood, a major byproduct of meat processing operations worldwide, represents an abundant yet significantly underutilized source of high-quality protein. Protein hydrolysates derived from blood contain bioactive peptides with immense potential for sustainable valorization (Demaman Oro et al., 2025). Annually, millions of liters of blood are generated from animal slaughter worldwide (Gupta et al., 2024). Blood from various species comprises approximately 20% protein content, making it a protein-rich byproduct with considerable commercial value (Toldrà et al., 2019). The protein composition includes both cellular fraction proteins (primarily hemoglobin) and plasma proteins (albumins, globulins, and fibrinogen), each offering unique functional properties valuable for food applications. Plasma proteins demonstrate excellent gelation properties, emulsifying capacity, and water-holding abilities that can enhance textural quality and oxidative stability of food products (Lynch et al., 2017).

In food applications, slaughter blood proteins are utilized as antioxidant agents that enhance product shelf life by preventing lipid oxidation (Demaman Oro et al., 2025). Studies have demonstrated their ability to scavenge free radicals and exhibit metal-chelating properties (Kumari et al., 2022). Blood-derived peptides possess antimicrobial properties that can inhibit the growth of pathogenic microorganisms and spoilage bacteria, making them useful as natural preservatives in processed foods (Wessely-Szponder et al., 2021). Antihypertensive activity, primarily through the inhibition of Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE), has been one of the most extensively studied properties of blood-derived peptides (Laosam et al., 2024). Moreover, blood-derived peptides have exhibited distinctive anti-inflammatory properties, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Cont et al., 2024). The pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries have shown increasing interest in blood components, particularly their potential in wound healing applications and as ingredients in natural cosmetic formulations (Frazier et al., 2020; Nair et al., 2022; Wessely-Szponder et al., 2021).

Despite substantial research efforts, significant knowledge gaps persist in understanding the intricate relationships between species-specific protein compositions and resulting bioactive peptide profiles. Peptides must maintain their structural integrity and functional properties throughout the complex journey from ingestion to their site of action. The effectiveness of these peptides depends on their ability to survive the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal tract, including exposure to various digestive enzymes, pH changes, and mechanical stresses. For instance, peptides with antihypertensive or antioxidant properties must retain their bioactive sequences to interact with target receptors or neutralize free radicals effectively. Studies have shown that peptides containing specific amino acid sequences (such as proline-rich segments) or those with particular structural features (including cyclic configurations) demonstrate enhanced resistance to digestive degradation, leading to improved bioavailability and therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, some blood-derived peptides act as precursors that only become active after partial digestion, releasing their bioactive fragments through controlled enzymatic breakdown. This sequential activation mechanism highlights the importance of understanding not only the initial peptide structure but also its behavior throughout the digestive process. Therefore, assessing peptide stability during digestion through parameters such as IC50 values and activity retention rates is essential for predicting their potential health benefits and developing effective delivery strategies to ensure they reach their intended targets in an active form (Chen et al., 2024). However, the molecular mechanisms governing peptide stability and their predictive modeling remain largely fragmented across different animal sources, limiting the development of standardized processing protocols.

The therapeutic potential of bioactive peptides has garnered significant attention in recent years, yet their clinical application faces substantial challenges due to complex biological barriers affecting their systemic bioavailability. The journey of a peptide from ingestion to its therapeutic target involves navigating multiple physiological obstacles that can compromise its efficacy. Initially, peptides encounter the harsh gastric environment, where exposure to low pH (1.5–3.5) and digestive proteases can lead to structural degradation and loss of biological activity (L. Liu et al. 2024a, b). Those peptides surviving gastric conditions must then overcome the selective permeability of the intestinal epithelium, where their molecular sizes and physicochemical properties often impede efficient absorption (Nicze et al., 2024). The presence of tight junctions between enterocytes restricts paracellular transport, while the hydrophilic nature of many peptides limits their transcellular passage. Even after successful intestinal absorption, peptides face rapid enzymatic degradation in the bloodstream by plasma peptidases, significantly reducing their half-life and therapeutic window (Ashique et al., 2024). These sequential barriers collectively contribute to the notably low bioavailability of most peptides, often less than 1–2% of the administered dose, necessitating the development of innovative delivery strategies to enhance their therapeutic potential (Ding et al., 2024). Current literature lacks comprehensive comparative analyses of how these bioavailability challenges vary across different blood sources and processing methods, creating critical gaps in optimization strategies.

Considerable research has focused on the stability and bioavailability of blood-derived ingredients during food processing and digestion. Studies indicate that careful selection of processing parameters can preserve the bioactivity of blood proteins and peptides while ensuring food safety. Emerging encapsulation technologies offer solutions for protecting bioactive compounds during processing and storage, with demonstrated stability improvements of up to 80% over unprotected ingredients (Zabot et al., 2022). The sustainability aspect of blood protein utilization in food applications aligns with the growing industry emphasis on circular economy principles. Efficient utilization of slaughter blood not only reduces waste but also contributes to more sustainable food production systems. Economic analyses suggest that proper utilization of blood, especially blood proteins, could add significant value to meat processing operations while providing the food industry with cost-effective functional ingredients. Nevertheless, the integration of species-specific optimization with sustainable processing technologies remains an underexplored frontier that could revolutionize blood protein valorization. This review explores recent advances in blood protein utilization in food applications, examining processing technologies of protein hydrolysate on functional properties, bioactivity, and current challenges. Uniquely, this comprehensive analysis addresses the fragmented understanding by systematically comparing structure-function relationships across multiple animal sources while identifying novel processing-bioactivity correlations. As the global food industry seeks sustainable protein sources and their hydrolysate, blood proteins represent a promising resource worthy of continued research and development focus.

Physicochemical properties of slaughter blood

Blood composition varies significantly among animal species but generally follows a consistent structural organization of cellular components suspended in plasma. The cellular fraction, primarily consisting of red blood cells (erythrocytes), typically accounts for 30–50% of total blood volume and contains the oxygen-carrying protein hemoglobin, which imparts blood’s characteristic red coloration (Zaleski, 2024). White blood cells (leukocytes) and platelets constitute smaller portions of the cellular fraction but play crucial roles in immune defense and hemostatic mechanisms, respectively. The plasma fraction, comprising 50–70% of blood volume, represents a complex aqueous solution containing a diverse array of proteins. The predominant plasma protein components include albumins (55–62% of total plasma protein), globulins (38–44%), and fibrinogen (1–5%). Additional plasma constituents encompass electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride), nutrients (glucose, amino acids, lipids), hormones, enzymes, metabolic waste products, and various bioactive compounds, all maintained within specific physiological concentration ranges to support optimal biological functions.

Red blood cells from different animal species exhibit distinct morphological characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 1A-C. Porcine erythrocytes typically appear as biconcave discs with a diameter of 6–8 μm, while chicken and duck erythrocytes are notably larger (10–12 μm). These structural differences significantly influence their oxygen-carrying capacity and functional properties (Sorapukdee & Narunatsopanon, 2017; Zheng et al., 2024). Hemoglobin, the primary component of red blood cells, is responsible for both the characteristic red coloration and oxygen transport function of blood. As demonstrated in Fig. 1D, hemoglobin exhibits a quaternary structure consisting of four polypeptide chains (two α-globin and two β-globin chains) arranged in a tetrahedral configuration. Each polypeptide chain contains a heme prosthetic group (Fig. 1E), which features a centrally coordinated iron atom (Fe²⁺) within a porphyrin ring structure. This sophisticated molecular architecture enables highly efficient oxygen binding and transport throughout the circulatory system (Sorapukdee & Narunatsopanon, 2017; Zheng et al., 2024). In contrast, the plasma fraction represents a protein-rich fluid containing albumins, globulins, and fibrinogen, along with various other solutes including lipids, carbohydrates, hormones, and essential minerals (Toldrà et al., 2019). The total protein content of whole blood typically ranges from 17 to 22%, with considerable variation depending on the animal species (Toldrà et al., 2019).

Species-specific variations in blood protein content have been extensively documented in the literature. Bovine blood contains 17–19% total protein, with plasma proteins accounting for 6–8% and hemoglobin comprising 10–12% of the total content (Supriyanta et al., 2021). Porcine blood demonstrates similar protein levels, with total protein content ranging from 18 to 19% (Fu et al., 2019). Conversely, avian blood, particularly from ducks, exhibits lower total protein content, generally ranging between 12 and 13% (Jamdar & Harikumar, 2016).

While the majority of research has traditionally focused on bovine and porcine blood due to their abundance in the meat processing industry, increasing scientific attention is being directed toward small ruminant blood, particularly from sheep and goats. As presented in Table 1, total protein content varies considerably among species, with bovine and porcine blood containing the highest concentrations (7.9–8.7 and 7.7–8.9 g/100 mL, respectively), while goat blood demonstrates notably lower protein content (6.8–7.2 g/100 mL). These compositional variations significantly influence the potential applications of blood-derived products across different industrial sectors. Specifically, sheep blood has been reported to contain total protein levels ranging from 6.9 to 7.2 g/100 mL, with albumin concentrations of 2.7–2.9 g/100 mL and globulin levels of 3.2–3.5 g/100 mL (Fanta et al., 2024).

Animal blood in human food applications

Animal blood has been historically utilized in various culinary traditions worldwide, serving as a primary ingredient and functional food component. Porcine blood is extensively used in European cuisine, particularly in traditional products like black pudding (UK), blutwurst (Germany), and morcilla (Spain), where it functions as both a protein source and a binding agent, typically comprising 20–60% of the final product (Bohrer, 2024). Bovine blood and its fractions have found widespread application in the food industry, with plasma proteins being utilized as natural emulsifiers and binding agents in processed meat products, capable of improving texture and water-holding capacity at concentrations of 1–2% (Gupta et al., 2024). In Asian cuisines, duck and chicken blood are commonly used in traditional dishes, with duck blood curd being particularly popular in Chinese cuisine. The functional properties of blood proteins, particularly their gel-forming ability (optimal at pH 6.0–7.0) and emulsifying capacity (200–400 mL oil/g protein), make them valuable ingredients in food formulations (Toldrà et al., 2019). Modern food applications have expanded to include blood protein fractions as natural colorants (hemoglobin-based, with coloring efficiency of 100–150 color units/mg), protein enrichment ingredients (with protein content of 80–95%), and functional ingredients in specialty products (Cheng et al., 2024).

Recent developments in food technology have led to innovative applications of animal blood components in human food systems, focusing on functionality and nutrition. Blood cell fractions are being transformed into microencapsulated iron supplements, providing bioavailable heme iron with 2–3 times higher absorption rates than non-heme sources (Hu et al., 2023). The food industry has also developed sophisticated processing methods to minimize the sensory impact of blood ingredients, including spray-drying techniques that produce neutral-flavored protein ingredients with shelf life exceeding 12 months when stored below 25 °C and relative humidity < 60% (Cheng et al., 2024). Additionally, enzymatic modification of blood proteins has enabled the production of bioactive peptides with various functional properties, including antioxidant activity (IC50 values ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 mg/mL) and ACE-inhibitory effects, allowing for their application in functional foods and nutraceuticals. Contemporary food safety protocols have established strict processing guidelines, including heat treatment (typically 72 °C for 15 min) and quality control measures to ensure the safe utilization of blood ingredients in food applications.

Bioactive peptides from slaughter blood

Peptide synthesis and characterization

Slaughter blood, a by-product of meat production, is rich in proteins that can be broken down into smaller bioactive peptides. Two primary methods used for peptide production from slaughter blood are enzymatic hydrolysis and chemical hydrolysis. Both methods are essential in transforming large protein molecules into bioactive peptides. As shown in Fig. 1F, the systematic workflow for bioactive peptide production involves eight critical steps.

Enzymatic hydrolysis is the most common method to produce peptides from slaughter blood. This process involves using proteolytic enzymes like trypsin, pepsin, and papain to cleave protein molecules into smaller peptide chains. Enzymatic hydrolysis is advantageous because it is highly specific, allowing for the production of peptides with targeted bioactivities. The mild conditions under which enzymatic hydrolysis occurs help preserve the bioactive properties of peptides, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities. Chemical hydrolysis, on the other hand, uses acids or alkalis to break down proteins into peptides. This method is faster than enzymatic hydrolysis but can lead to non-specific cleavage, resulting in a mixture of peptides with varying bioactivities (Cruz-Casas et al., 2021). While chemical hydrolysis is less precise, it is useful when large quantities of peptides are required for industrial applications (Garcés-Rimón et al., 2022). However, care must be taken during chemical hydrolysis to avoid excessive degradation of the bioactive peptides, which may reduce their functional properties.

Hemoglobin decolorization methods and mechanisms

Hemoglobin decolorization encompasses several approaches that reduce or eliminate the characteristic red color of blood, enabling broader use of blood-derived products in applications where color limits acceptance. Enzymatic methods rely on two complementary actions: proteases such as alcalase or papain cleave the globin chains to release and expose the heme group, while heme-degrading enzymes (e.g., heme oxygenase) open the porphyrin ring, converting heme to biliverdin (green), bilirubin (yellow) and ultimately colorless metabolites. Combinations that first cleave the protein and then act on the liberated heme are particularly effective, with efficiency governed by enzyme specificity, pH, temperature and hydrolysis time. Physical and solvent-based methods—including acetone, ethanol or acidified-acetone extraction and membrane separation—instead isolate the colored heme from the protein fraction on the basis of differential solubility, molecular weight or charge, without altering its chemical structure, though they often require multiple extraction cycles and subsequent solvent removal.

Chemical oxidative decolorization is among the most effective approaches and acts by destroying the chromophore itself (Fig. 2A). The principal food-grade oxidants are hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), peracetic acid (PAA) and ozone (O3); stronger agents such as sodium hypochlorite and benzoyl peroxide are also effective but less favoured owing to chlorinated or benzoic-acid residue concerns. Mechanistically, oxidation begins with conversion of the heme iron from Fe2+ to Fe3+, followed by the methene bridges and vulnerable double bonds of the porphyrin, causing ring opening and fragmentation into colorless compounds and collapse of the conjugated system responsible for visible-light absorption. Decolorization efficiency depends on oxidant concentration, temperature, pH and exposure time, and is enhanced for H2O2 when catalysed by trace metals or peroxidases.

Despite the technological advantages of oxidative decolorization, the food-grade safety, residual chemical load, and nutritional integrity of the resulting protein product require explicit consideration. H2O2, PAA, and O3 are all recognised food-processing aids, including 21 CFR 184.1366 (H2O2 for decolorization of beef feet, tripe, and instant tea), 21 CFR 173.370 (PAA at ≤ 220 ppm for meat and poultry), and 21 CFR 173.368 (O3 for raw and processed meat). Residue control is well established. H2O2 is fully degraded to water and oxygen by food-grade catalase (EC 1.11.1.6), with Sakai et al. (2022) reporting residual H2O2 below detection in catalase-quenched soy protein isolate, and 184.1366(d) requiring residual H2O2 to be removed during processing. PAA decomposes spontaneously to acetic acid, water, and oxygen, leaving residues below regulatory thresholds at ≤ 200 ppm and ≤ 30 min contact. O3 is self-quenching (aqueous half-life of minutes at neutral pH), though bromate and aldehyde by-products require monitoring when halides or unsaturated lipids are present. Sakai et al. (2022) reported no significant difference (p > 0.05) in INFOGEST-released amino acids, including oxidation-sensitive Met, Trp, Lys, and His, between treated and untreated soy protein, because in plant proteins the color arises from Maillard melanoidins and phenolics, which the oxidant removes preferentially rather than attacking peptide bonds; treatment also raised solubility 1.77-fold and improved emulsifying and foaming properties. In blood, by contrast, the color resides in the haem–porphyrin, so decolorization necessarily exposes the globin and plasma proteins to oxidative conditions: the direct reaction of H2O2 with hemoglobin can induce α-globin crosslinking and residue oxidation (Vallelian et al., 2008). Protein quality is therefore preserved only under mild, dose-controlled conditions with prompt catalase quenching. For PAA and O3 within recommended dosages, amino-acid profiles remain within ± 5% of controls, although O3 above ~ 5 mg L⁻¹ progressively decreases cysteine and methionine and is not recommended for sulphur-rich blood proteins. As a residue-free alternative, enzymatic and pH-mediated decolorization of hemoglobin hydrolysates—exploiting preferential release of histidine-rich, haem-binding peptides—removes color while retaining protein functionality (Li et al., 2023). Because the ACE- and DPP-IV-inhibitory peptides released from blood proteins depend on primary sequence rather than tertiary fold, mild decolorization is not expected to diminish their potency; antioxidant peptides, however, draw activity from oxidation-sensitive residues (Cys, Met, Trp, Tyr, His) and should be verified experimentally after decolorization.

Proteases in the proteolysis reactions to generate peptides from slaughter blood

Enzymatic hydrolysis is the most common method to produce peptides. This process involves proteolytic enzymes like alcalase, chymotrypsin, trypsin, pepsin, penicillopepsin and papain to cleave protein molecules into smaller peptide chains. Proteases involved in proteolysis reactions to generate peptides from slaughter blood are indicated in Fig. 2B. Alcalase (subtilisin) employs a serine protease mechanism utilizing a catalytic triad of Ser-His-Asp. The reaction proceeds through a nucleophilic attack by the serine hydroxyl group on the peptide bond of carbonyl carbon, forming a tetrahedral intermediate stabilized by an oxyanion hole (Contesini et al., 2018). This intermediate collapses, releasing the N-terminal fragment and forming an acyl-enzyme complex, which is subsequently hydrolyzed to release the C-terminal fragment and regenerate the enzyme. This mechanism allows for relatively rapid hydrolysis rates and broad substrate specificity. Alcalase demonstrates remarkable versatility in producing peptides with impressive antioxidant capabilities. The enzyme also yields peptides with significant angiotensin I-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory activity. Additionally, alcalase-generated peptides often exhibit strong metal binding properties, which can enhance mineral bioavailability or serve chelating functions in different applications. The hydrolysis products show promising antidiabetic effects through mechanisms like DPP-IV inhibition, helping regulate glucose metabolism. Many alcalase-derived peptides demonstrate anti-inflammatory activity by modulating key inflammatory pathways, while others exhibit antimicrobial properties against various pathogens, potentially serving as natural preservatives (Tacias-Pascacio et al., 2020).

Trypsin functions as a serine protease similar to alcalase, but with highly specific substrate recognition, exclusively cleaving peptide bonds on the C-terminal side of positively charged amino acids (lysine and arginine) due to a negatively charged aspartate residue in its specificity pocket that forms strong ionic interactions with these basic residues, often generating peptides from bovine globulins, whole plasma, and bovine albumin with C-terminal basic residues that can enhance certain bioactivities such as ACE inhibition. Chymotrypsin shares the serine protease mechanism with trypsin but has fundamentally different substrate specificity. Its hydrophobic binding pocket accommodates bulky aromatic side chains, causing it to cleave preferentially after phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan residues. Secondary preferences include leucine and methionine. This specificity for aromatic residues results in peptides that often exhibit strong antioxidant properties, as aromatic amino acids are effective electron donors. Antioxidant activities were demonstrated in porcine red blood corpuscles, porcine plasma, and porcine defibrinated blood plasma. Moreover, chymotrypsin functions optimally in ACE inhibition in defibrinated plasma.

Papain operates through a cysteine protease mechanism involving a catalytic dyad of Cys-His. The active site of the cysteine thiol group becomes deprotonated by the nearby histidine, generating a highly nucleophilic thiolate anion that attacks the peptide bond carbonyl. The resulting thioacyl-enzyme intermediate undergoes hydrolysis to release the C-terminal fragment (Tacias-Pascacio et al., 2020). The activity of papain is highly dependent on maintaining the reduced state of its active site cysteine, making it sensitive to oxidizing environments. Papain has been used to produce antioxidant activity peptide by poultry hemoglobin (Cui et al., 2025). In addition, papain catalyzed whole plasma shown ACE inhibition (Merz et al., 2015). Papain-derived bioactive peptides have shown remarkable abilities to interact with human metabolism, offering promising potential in modulating insulin resistance and the mTOR pathway (Bhullar & Wu, 2020). They also work by inhibiting key enzymes involved in metabolic disorders, by blocking angiotensin-converting enzyme to help regulate blood pressure, or by inhibiting DPP-IV to improve glucose metabolism in diabetes management (Tacias-Pascacio et al., 2020).

In addition, a popular enzyme such as neutrase functions as a metalloprotease, coordinating a zinc ion in its active site that activates a water molecule, which then serves as the nucleophile for peptide bond hydrolysis. The zinc ion polarizes the peptide bond carbonyl while simultaneously positioning the water molecule for attack. This metal-assisted hydrolysis mechanism is distinct from the covalent intermediates formed in serine and cysteine proteases and is typically more sensitive to metal chelators and pH fluctuations. A study by Laosam et al. (2024) demonstrated that the duck blood hydrolysate peptide WMHVR exhibited the highest antioxidant and ACE inhibitory activities by binding to the S1, S2, and S ′ pockets through van der Waals and hydrogen bonding interactions.

Moreover, exopeptidases and endopeptidases such as flavorzyme, being a complex enzyme mixture, contains multiple proteases with diverse mechanisms. Its endopeptidases typically operate through serine or metalloprotease mechanisms, while its exopeptidases progressively remove amino acids from peptide termini through various catalytic strategies (Merz et al., 2015). Pepsin utilizes an aspartic protease mechanism, employing two catalytic aspartate residues that activate a water molecule for nucleophilic attack on the peptide bond (Boon et al., 2020). Unlike serine or cysteine proteases, pepsin does not form covalent enzyme-substrate intermediates. Instead, one aspartate residue acts as a general base to activate water, while the other functions as a general acid to protonate the leaving group. Pepsin exhibits optimal activity under highly acidic conditions, mimicking the stomach environment and preferentially cleaves at hydrophobic and aromatic amino acid residues, particularly between Phe-Phe, Phe-Tyr, and Leu-Val bonds.

Peptide characterization

Peptide characterization through size exclusion chromatography (SEC), plays a key role in analyzing and fractionating peptides derived from slaughter blood. SEC analysis typically employs columns packed with cross-linked dextran gels (such as Sephadex G-25, G-50, or G-75) or specialized HPLC columns with defined pore sizes, offering separation ranges from 100 Da to 100 kDa (Kan et al., 2025). Bovine blood hydrolysates generally yield peptide fractions ranging from 0.5 to 30 kDa, with bioactive peptides predominantly found in the 1–5 kDa range, while porcine blood peptides show similar distribution patterns but with notably higher concentrations in the 2–7 kDa range (Esfahan et al., 2021). Small ruminant blood (sheep and goat) hydrolysates typically produce peptide fractions with molecular weights between 0.3 and 15 kDa, with antihypertensive and antioxidant peptides predominantly found in fractions below 3 kDa (Hou et al., 2019; Kumari et al., 2024). Poultry blood (chicken and duck) peptides often exhibit narrower molecular weight distributions, typically ranging from 0.5 to 10 kDa, with bioactive fractions most commonly found in the 1–3 kDa range (Zheng et al., 2024).

The molecular weight characterization of peptides often employs complementary techniques to validate SEC findings and provide more detailed size distribution profiles. Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) has become an essential tool for precise molecular weight determination, capable of analyzing peptides across a wide mass range (500 − 20,000 Da) with high accuracy (± 0.1%) (Strupat., 2005). Small ruminant blood peptides show unique mass spectral patterns, with sheep blood peptides often exhibiting clusters in the 700–2000 Da range and goat blood peptides showing distinctive distributions between 900 and 2500 Da (Alvites et al., 2021).

Purification techniques

Once peptides are produced through hydrolysis, purification is critical to ensure their efficacy and bioactivity. Techniques like ultrafiltration, membrane separation, and chromatography isolate peptides with specific molecular weights and bioactive properties. Purification is essential for enhancing the bioactivity of peptides and concentrating the purified peptides to levels required for their intended applications. For example, purified peptides derived from bovine blood have shown enhanced antioxidant activity, making them suitable for functional foods and therapeutic formulations to reduce oxidative stress (Laosam et al., 2024). Without adequate purification, the efficacy of these bioactive peptides may be limited due to the presence of unwanted proteins or larger peptides that lack the desired functionality. The purification of peptides following hydrolysis is a critical step in obtaining bioactive peptides with specific functionalities. The complex peptide mixtures obtained from blood protein hydrolysates require various separation techniques to isolate peptides of interest. The most employed techniques include membrane filtration, various forms of chromatography, and electrophoresis, each offering distinct advantages and limitations in peptide purification, as shown in Fig. 1F. The three primary purification processing methods, membrane filtration emerges as the most efficient with recovery rates of 80–95%, while chromatographic methods demonstrate moderate to high efficiency (60–90%) with enhanced selectivity, making them especially valuable processing through ion exchange chromatography, poultry blood via RP-HPLC, and complex peptide mixtures requiring multiple purification steps; electrophoresis, despite having the lowest recovery rates (40–70%), provides superior resolution and proves particularly beneficial for analytical characterization, small-scale purification, and species-specific peptide mapping (Table 2) (Teixeira et al., 2019). Each technique offers distinct advantages and limitations in terms of selectivity, scalability, and cost-effectiveness. The selection of appropriate purification methods depends on factors such as the desired peptide characteristics, intended application, and scale of production.

Membrane filtration:

technique employs a series of membranes with specific molecular weight cut-offs (MWCO), typically ranging from 1 to 100 kDa, to achieve targeted peptide fractions (Alavi & Ciftci, 2023). The efficiency of membrane filtration is significantly influenced by operational parameters, including transmembrane pressure (typically 2–4 bar for ultrafiltration), cross-flow velocity (1–3 m/s), temperature (usually maintained between 20 and 40 °C), and pH of the peptide solution, which must be optimized based on the specific blood source and desired peptide characteristics (Peles et al., 2022). Modern membrane filtration systems often incorporate ceramic or specialized polymeric membranes that offer enhanced resistance to fouling and improved separation efficiency, which is particularly important when processing blood proteins prone to membrane clogging. The process typically achieves 80–95% recovery of bioactive peptides, with the specific recovery rate varying depending on the blood source and target peptide size range.

Chromatography:

techniques play a vital role in the purification and isolation of bioactive peptides with different methods employed based on the specific characteristics of peptides. Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) is often used as an initial separation step, particularly effective due to their complex peptide profiles, typically employing Sephadex G-25 or G-50 columns to fractionate peptides based on molecular size (Strupat., 2005). Ion Exchange Chromatography (IEC) has shown particular efficacy in separating peptides, where the unique charge distributions of their peptides enable efficient separation using both cation and anion exchange resins, with optimal separation achieved at pH 7–8 for basic peptides and pH 4–5 for acidic peptides. Reverse-Phase High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (RP-HPLC) serves as the final purification step, proving especially valuable for isolating bioactive peptides utilizing C18 or C8 columns with acetonitrile-water gradients containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (Abdelhedi & Nasri, 2019). Modern chromatographic applications have incorporated automated systems with enhanced resolution and throughput, such as Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) for larger-scale separations, achieving peptide purities exceeding 95% while maintaining bioactivity (Lynch et al., 2017). The optimization of chromatographic conditions for each species considers factors such as mobile phase composition, flow rate (typically 0.5-2.0 mL/min for analytical scales), and column temperature (usually maintained between 25 and 30 °C), with recent advances including the use of monolithic columns for improved separation efficiency and reduced analysis time. Recovery rates vary significantly depending on the chromatographic method and blood source, ranging from 60 to 90% for individual techniques, with higher recoveries achieved through optimized multi-step chromatographic processes (Ramos-de‐la‐Peña et al., 2019).

In vitro and in vivo bioactive properties of blood-derived peptides

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant properties of peptides derived from sheep and goat blood have emerged as a significant area of research due to their potential applications in food preservation and health promotion. Bovine blood-derived peptides have demonstrated remarkable antioxidant activities, with hydrolysates containing specific peptide sequences such as VNFKLLSHSLLVTLASHL showing significant DPPH radical scavenging activity (IC50 values ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 mg/mL) and metal chelating properties (Xing et al., 2019). EVGK from duck plasma protein exhibited the highest Fe2+ chelating ability (16.35%), and RCLQ presented the highest FRAP (0.62 TE/mg) and DPPH (95.12%) (Yang et al., 2020). Chicken blood peptides (AEDKKLIQ) have shown distinctive antioxidant mechanisms, with certain fractions exhibiting superior metal chelating abilities (chelating rates of 65–80% at 1.0 mg/mL) and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity (IC50 values of 0.15–0.3 mg/mL) (Wang et al., 2024c). While three peptides released from sheep plasma WEEPAM, SLHFME, and HCTTFMI (Size of 761.84, 762.87, and 852.03 Da) demonstrated antioxidant peptides (Glutathione, DPPH and superoxide radical scavenging) (Jingyun et al., 2024). Moreover, the studies encompass various blood sources with peptide sequences ranging from simple such as WVRQAPGKGL from pig plasma (Zhan et al., 2022). All studies except one donkey blood study in mice and the chicken blood cell study in rats were performed in vitro (Ma et al., 2022, Wongngam et al., 2022), with biological activities primarily focusing on antioxidant properties as displayed in Table 2.

The antioxidant activities of blood-derived peptides primarily operate through two major mechanisms: hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) and single electron transfer (SET), with the efficiency of these mechanisms largely dependent on the peptide sequence and amino acid composition. In the HAT mechanism, antioxidant peptides directly donate hydrogen atoms to free radicals, converting them into non-reactive species through the reaction: P-H (peptide) + ROO• → P• + ROOH, the resulting peptide radical (P•) is relatively stable due to resonance delocalization (Zou et al., 2016). This mechanism is particularly effective for peptides containing amino acids with easily abstractable hydrogen atoms, such as tyrosine, tryptophan, histidine, and cysteine. These peptides are attributed mainly to their unique amino acid composition, particularly the presence of hydrophobic amino acids (leucine, valine, phenylalanine) and electron-donating residues such as tyrosine and tryptophan (Zhu et al., 2024). The SET mechanism, conversely, involves the transfer of a single electron to reduce radicals, metal ions, or carbonyls Pan et al. (2021) mentioned that this mechanism is favored by peptides containing amino acids with aromatic rings (phenylalanine, tyrosine) or sulfur-containing residues (methionine, cysteine), which can readily donate electrons while maintaining stability through electronic delocalization. The efficiency of SET mechanisms has been demonstrated in peptides containing His-His or Tyr-Phe sequences, showing electron-donating capacity values of 20–35 µmol Trolox equivalents per gram of peptide (Athira et al., 2021).

Anti-inflammatory properties

Anti-inflammatory peptides exert their effects through multiple molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways (Table 2), primarily targeting key inflammatory mediators and transcription factors. The primary mechanism involves the modulation of the Nuclear Factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, where bioactive peptides can inhibit the phosphorylation and degradation of IκB-α, thereby preventing NF-κB translocation to the nucleus and subsequent pro-inflammatory gene expression (Saleh et al., 2021). Blood peptides also demonstrate significant effects on reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Chakrabarti et al., 2014). A bovine plasma hydrolysate containing the peptides AGATGVTISGAG, YSRRHPEYAVS, Q(K)AW and L(I)I(I)VR showed ACE-inhibitory activity at an IC50 of 0.18 mg/mL expressed on a total-protein basis (Gómez Sampedro & Zapata Montoya, 2014); as a mass-based value for a crude hydrolysate, this cannot be ranked directly against the molar (µM) IC50 values of the purified peptides described below. Bovine blood, from the same species, has been extensively studied, with hemoglobin-derived peptides showing IC50 values ranging from 0.1 to 100 µM. In comparison, the bovine hemoglobin peptide LSFPTTKTYFPHF showed moderate activity at 96.43 ± 6.17 µM (Wang et al., 2017). Notably, the chicken blood cell-derived peptide VSKRLNGDA was tested in vivo using a rat model, demonstrating ACE-inhibitory activity at 26.46 µM (Wongngam et al., 2022). Small ruminant blood (sheep and goat) has yielded interesting results, with peptides showing unique structural features enhancing their stability and bioavailability. For instance, blood peptides containing the sequence KTYFPHFDLSH have demonstrated both ACE-inhibitory activity (IC50 = 0.3 µM) and good resistance to gastrointestinal digestion (Lafarga et al., 2015). Finally, the purified muscovy duck plasma peptide VALSSLRP exhibited strong ACE-inhibitory activity (91.67 ± 0.73% inhibition at 1 mg/mL; IC50 = 0.039 mg/mL) (Zhu et al., 2022).

It should be emphasised that the potency values compiled in Table 2 and discussed above are not directly comparable, because they are expressed on different bases. Crude and fractionated hydrolysates are reported per total protein mass (mg mL⁻¹) — e.g., the bovine plasma hydrolysate at 0.18 mg mL⁻¹ (Gómez Sampedro & Zapata Montoya, 2014) — whereas purified, sequence-identified peptides are usually reported on a molar basis (µM), e.g., LRVSV (0.15 µM), VVYPWTQRF (0.7 µM), KTYFPHFDLSH (0.3 µM) and VSKRLNGDA (26.46 µM). A small mg mL⁻¹ value does not imply higher intrinsic potency than a larger µM value: an individual active sequence within a hydrolysate is present at a molar concentration two to three orders of magnitude below that implied by the bulk protein mass. The comparison is complicated further by entries reported as percentage inhibition at a fixed dose, or as a mass-based IC50 even for a purified peptide (e.g., VALSSLRP, 91.67% at 1 mg/mL; IC50 = 0.039 mg/mL, Zhu et al., 2022). Quantitative ranking should therefore be restricted to entries expressed on the same basis, with cross-basis comparisons treated qualitatively only.

Although human clinical data for slaughter-blood-derived peptides are not much available, clinical efficacy has been established for analogous food-protein-derived antihypertensive peptides. A recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials reported a significant blood-pressure-lowering effect of orally administered food-protein-derived peptides (Liao et al., 2021), and milk-derived lactotripeptides (IPP and VPP) produced a consistent reduction in systolic blood pressure across controlled trials in European and Asian populations (Cicero et al., 2013). These findings provide proof of concept that orally delivered ACE-inhibitory peptides can lower blood pressure in humans, and underline the need for dedicated clinical evaluation of blood-derived peptide preparations.

Type 2 diabetes management

Dipeptidyl Peptidase IV (DPP-IV) is a serine protease that plays a crucial role in glucose metabolism by rapidly degrading incretin hormones, particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). These incretins stimulate insulin secretion, suppress glucagon release, and regulate gastric emptying, making DPP-IV inhibition a valuable therapeutic target for type 2 diabetes management. DPP-IV inhibitors prevent the degradation of incretin hormones, thereby prolonging their beneficial effects on glucose homeostasis. Figure 5 illustrates the mechanism of action and amino acid characteristics of DPP-IV inhibitory peptides. Figure 5A shows GLP-1 effects on the pancreas and kidney, where food breakdown triggers GLP-1 release from the intestine; when GLP-1 is cleaved (inactivated) by DPP-IV, its beneficial actions are lost, leading to decreased sodium excretion, increased albuminuria and proteinuria, increased tubular reabsorption, and decreased insulin release. In contrast, DPP-IV inhibitors block GLP-1 degradation, keeping GLP-1 active and resulting in improved glycemic control, reduced albuminuria, lower hypoglycemia risk, anti-fibrotic effects, and enhanced insulin release. Blood-derived peptides have emerged as promising natural DPP-IV inhibitors, offering potential advantages in terms of safety and bioavailability. These peptides typically contain 2–15 amino acids, with structures often featuring proline or alanine at specific positions that facilitate binding to the DPP-IV active site (Mu et al., 2024). The DPP-IV inhibitory activity of blood-derived peptides varies among species and is influenced by both peptide sequence and processing conditions (Table 2). Bovine blood has been extensively studied, with hemoglobin-derived peptides showing IC50 values ranging from 80 to 500 µM. Notable sequences include AAATP (IC50 = 100 µM) and LPVPQ (IC50 = 150 µM), which demonstrate significant DPP-IV inhibitory activity (Nongonierma & FitzGerald, 2016). Porcine blood peptides have shown comparable effectiveness, with sequences such as GPAGPKG exhibiting IC50 values around 250 µM (Stožinić et al., 2025). Studies on small ruminant blood (sheep and goat) have revealed unique peptide sequences with dual functionality, showing DPP-IV inhibitory activity and antioxidant properties. For instance, peptides from sheep blood containing the sequence VAGTWY have demonstrated DPP-IV inhibition (IC50 = 180 µM) while also showing free radical scavenging activity (Lafarga & Hayes, 2014).

Allergic properties

Regarding allergic properties of blood-derived peptides, there has been limited but important research in this area. Blood proteins, particularly from certain species, can potentially trigger allergic reactions in sensitive individuals. Several studies have investigated the allergenic potential of blood-derived products, with particular focus on proteins like serum albumin, which is known to be allergenic in some populations. For example, research by Pongprayoon et al. (2025) examined the allergenic potential of serum albumin from equine, caprine, and leporine to albumins from human, bovine and porcine finding that while the intact protein can trigger allergic responses in sensitive individuals, albumin may not be suitable for albumin therapy for humans. Therefore it is possible to use them to monitor the osmotic pressure among their species, but the allergenic response must be seriously considered. However, it’s worth noting that some small peptides may still retain allergenic potential, particularly those containing specific sequential epitopes. The degree of hydrolysis appears critical in determining the final allergenic potential of blood-derived peptides, with more extensive hydrolysis generally associated with reduced allergenicity.

Other bioactivities

Beyond the well-established antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, ACE inhibitory, and DPP-IV inhibitory activities, blood-derived peptides exhibit several other promising bioactivities that warrant further investigation. Antimicrobial peptides derived from slaughter blood, particularly from bovine and porcine sources, have shown significant activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria (López-Pedrouso et al., 2023; Silveira et al., 2021). Immunomodulatory peptides from blood proteins have demonstrated the ability to enhance immune system function, with studies showing increased lymphocyte proliferation and modulation of cytokine production (Ashaolu et al., 2024). Additionally, emerging research has identified novel activities, including anticancer properties (with certain peptides showing inhibition of cancer cell proliferation), wound healing capabilities, and cognitive enhancement effects through blood-brain barrier penetrating peptides (Lynch et al., 2017). Recent studies have also revealed the potential of blood-derived peptides in tissue engineering applications, mainly as bioactive components in scaffolds and wound dressing materials, showing enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation rates of up to 200% compared to control materials (Frazier et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2024a, b; Wessely-Szponder et al. 2021). Additionally, recent work by Gao et al. (2021) revealed that modified blood peptides could effectively cross the blood-brain barrier, opening new possibilities for treating neurological conditions. The field has also witnessed innovative approaches in delivery systems and modification strategies. Ejazi et al. (2023) developed a novel nanocarrier system for blood-derived peptides that enhanced their bioavailability by 300% compared to conventional delivery methods. Research has demonstrated that strategic modifications of blood-derived peptides can significantly improve their pharmacokinetic profiles and target specificity. For instance, Dan et al. (2020) successfully developed PEGylated blood peptides that demonstrated extended half-lives and improved tissue distribution in phase I clinical trials, opening new possibilities for peptide-based therapeutics.

Bioavailability of bioactive peptides during digestion and blood circulation

Bioactive peptides (BAPs) derived from food sources have significantly contributed to health promotion and disease management. While their potential is promising, the journey from laboratory success to practical therapeutic application faces several challenges. The effectiveness of these peptides is intricately linked to their effectiveness after digestion (Sinha et al., 2021). Certain bioactive peptides exert local effects in the gastrointestinal tract through direct interactions with receptors and cellular targets in the gastrointestinal tract, whereby systemic absorption is not required. The gastrointestinal tract environment maintains structural integrity and biological activity of these peptides, as intestinal barrier crossing is not required. Specific antimicrobial peptides can directly inhibit pathogenic bacteria in the gut lumen, while nutrient absorption is modulated and other peptides influence gut hormone secretion through direct contact with intestinal cells. Furthermore, immune responses are triggered through interactions with immune cells in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), and gut microbiota composition is influenced by various peptides without systemic absorption being necessitated. Through these localized mechanisms, therapeutic effects are achieved directly within the gastrointestinal environment, where the peptides’ biological functions are preserved and their targeted activities are facilitated without the need for extensive bioavailability considerations.

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract presents complex challenges for orally administered BAPs. Unlike traditional small-molecule drugs, peptides must navigate through multiple digestive enzymes while maintaining their structural integrity to reach their intended targets. The process involves exposure to various proteases and peptidases, each specializing in breaking down specific amino acid combinations. This digestive gauntlet includes interactions of pepsin in the stomach acidic environment, followed by encounters with trypsin, chymotrypsin, and various other enzymes in the intestinal phase (Lajis, 2020).

The complexity of peptide transport and modification in the GI system is comprehensively illustrated in Fig. 6. BAPs must navigate through four critical sequential stages before reaching their target organs. Initially, in the gastric environment, BAPs encounter pepsin under acidic conditions (pH 1.5–3.5), where primary structural modifications occur through proteolytic cleavage (Xu et al., 2019). Under these conditions, peptide bonds can undergo acid-catalyzed hydrolysis, potentially fragmenting longer peptides into shorter sequences (Verma et al., 2021). The presence of pepsin in the stomach introduces additional complexity, as this enzyme preferentially cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine). The second stage involves intestinal proteases, particularly pancreatic enzymes, which further modify peptide structures under different pH conditions. As peptides enter the small intestine, they encounter a more alkaline environment (pH 6.8–7.2) and a complex mixture of pancreatic enzymes. The pancreatic proteases trypsin, chymotrypsin, and carboxypeptidases exhibit distinct cleavage patterns: trypsin targets lysine and arginine residues, chymotrypsin preferentially cleaves after aromatic amino acids, and carboxypeptidases remove amino acids from the C-terminus (Whitcomb & Lowe, 2007). This is followed by the crucial brush border and epithelial protease stage, where peptides interact with the complex cellular architecture of the intestinal epithelium. This stage involves multiple transport mechanisms, including transcellular and paracellular pathways, significantly affecting peptide bioavailability (Chakrabarti et al., 2018). Finally, peptides that successfully cross the intestinal barrier face additional modifications from blood plasma proteases before potentially reaching their target organs. Throughout this journey, various GI factors, including pH variations, enzymatic activities, and electrostatic interactions, create significant barriers to the absorption and bioavailability of hydrophilic BAPs.

Gastrointestinal stability and proline role

The structural characteristics of bioactive peptides play a pivotal role in determining their resistance to gastrointestinal degradation. Amino acid sequences containing proline residues, particularly when positioned at the N-terminus or as part of a Pro-Pro sequence, demonstrate enhanced stability against aminopeptidases (Malde et al., 2019). The cyclic architecture unique to proline results in spatial blocking that restricts enzyme binding and subsequent peptide bond cleavage. C. Wang et al., 2025 interestingly mentioned that proline stands apart from other amino acids due to its distinctive molecular structure featuring a pyrrolidine ring, which creates a constrained secondary amino group. This unique architecture leads to several key functional characteristics when proline is incorporated into peptide sequences. The rigid nature of the pyrrolidine ring significantly restricts rotation around the α-carbon and nitrogen bonds within peptide structures, creating defined bends in peptide chains where the side chain forms a cyclic connection back to the backbone amide. Additionally, proline exhibits a remarkable ability to switch between cis and trans configurations due to the proximity of its nitrogen atom to the peptide bond. This conformational flexibility helps protect proline-containing peptides from enzymatic breakdown, which explains why proline is frequently found near the N-terminus of bioactive peptides and hormones. As the only natural secondary imino acid, proline undergoes unique metabolic processes that differentiate it from standard amino acids. Following its oxidation to pyrroline-5-carboxylate or glutamic-γ-semialdehyde, proline can participate in various metabolic pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid and urea cycles. Through these mechanisms, proline serves as an important cellular signaling molecule, influencing mitochondrial activity, cell growth and survival, and other essential biological processes. However, the relationship between peptide length and bioactivity represents a critical yet complex aspect of bioactive peptide research. While proline-containing peptides often demonstrate enhanced stability against digestive enzymes, peptide length itself fundamentally influences both stability and bioactivity through multiple mechanisms. Shorter peptides (typically 2–6 amino acids) generally exhibit superior intestinal absorption and bioavailability, allowing them to more readily reach systemic circulation and target tissues.

Additionally, peptides rich in hydrophobic amino acids often show improved stability, as demonstrated in a comprehensive study by (Ahmed et al., 2022), which found that peptides containing leucine, isoleucine, and valine residues exhibited greater resistance to gastric digestion compared to those dominated by polar amino acids (Fig. 6). Studies using simulated gastric fluid (SGF) have shown that peptides containing hydrophobic amino acids often exhibit greater stability (Wang et al., 2023). The relationship between peptide length and molecular weight influences peptide during digestion. Medium-length peptides (7–15 amino acids) containing specific structural motifs, such as cyclic configurations or strategic disulfide bonds, have demonstrated superior stability during gastrointestinal transit (Ahmed et al., 2022). Chemical modifications, including N-terminal acetylation and C-terminal amidation, have emerged as effective strategies for enhancing peptide stability. A landmark study by Xu et al. (2019) documented that such modifications could increase the half-life of bioactive peptides in simulated gastric fluid by up to 300% while maintaining their biological activity. Branched-chain amino acids and specific secondary structures, particularly β-sheets, have also been correlated with increased resistance to proteolytic degradation.

Recent studies on bovine and duck blood-derived peptides have demonstrated the complexity of peptide stability throughout the digestive process. Notably, when these peptides undergo simulated GI digestion, they can be either fragmented into smaller peptides or remain intact, potentially affecting their bioactivities as DPP-IV inhibitors, ACE inhibitors, or antioxidants (Boonkong et al., 2024). Similarly, Laosam et al. (2024) shows the production and stability assessment of duck blood peptides, where the process begins with heated blood treatment followed by enzymatic hydrolysis and scaling up to pilot-scale production. The resulting soluble hydrolysate powder (SHP) undergoes in vitro GI digestion tests and blood plasma digestion simulations. Crucially, these studies reveal that while some peptides maintain their bioactivities after digestion, others undergo structural modifications that can enhance or diminish their functional properties.

Absorption and blood circulation

The absorption of peptides across the intestinal epithelium represents a critical step in their bioavailability. Several transport mechanisms are involved, including transcellular transport through peptide transporters (PEPT1 and PEPT2), paracellular transport through tight junctions, and transcytosis (Xu et al., 2019). Studies using Caco-2 cell monolayers have shown that peptides with molecular weights below 1000 Da generally exhibit better absorption rates, typically ranging from 5 to 15% of the initial concentration. Once in circulation, peptides face further challenges from plasma peptidases, which can significantly affect their half-life. Blood-derived peptides often show enhanced circulation stability compared to other sources, possibly due to structural similarities with endogenous blood proteins. Bioactive peptides undergo various metabolic transformations during circulation that can affect their biological activity. Plasma aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases continuously modify circulating peptides, potentially creating new bioactive sequences or inactivating existing ones. The half-life of blood-derived peptides in circulation varies significantly, ranging from several minutes to several hours, depending on their sequence and structure (Ahmed et al., 2022). Factors influencing stability include specific amino acids (particularly proline), secondary structure elements, and post-translational modifications.

Additionally, interaction with plasma proteins, mainly with albumin, can protect peptides from enzymatic degradation and extend their circulation time. Understanding these complex changes has led to several strategic approaches for enhancing peptide stability and bioavailability. These include structural modifications such as N-terminal acetylation or C-terminal amidation, incorporating D-amino acids, and developing novel delivery systems (Evans et al., 2020). Recent advances in proteomics and peptidomics have enabled better tracking of peptide modifications throughout the digestive and circulatory systems, leading to a more informed design of bioactive peptides with enhanced stability and targeted activity.

Factors influencing peptide stability

The stability of blood-derived peptides varies significantly depending on their structure, size, and the environmental conditions they encounter. Peptide stability is influenced by several factors, including pH, temperature, storage conditions, and the presence of proteolytic enzymes. In general, smaller peptides are more vulnerable to degradation than larger ones, though this can be mitigated by specific structural features. Temperature significantly impacts peptide stability, with higher temperatures accelerating degradation through various mechanisms including peptide bond hydrolysis and oxidation of susceptible amino acids like methionine and cysteine. pH also plays a crucial role, with most blood-derived peptides showing optimal stability in the pH range of 6–8, while extreme pH conditions can accelerate degradation.

The stability of peptides directly impacts the shelf-life, efficacy, and overall quality of products containing them. In food applications, peptide degradation can lead to reduced bioactivity, altered sensory properties (development of bitterness), and diminished functional properties like emulsification or gelling capacity. In pharmaceutical or nutraceutical formulations, peptide instability can result in decreased therapeutic efficacy and potentially unpredictable biological effects. Several strategies have been developed to enhance peptide stability in products, including microencapsulation techniques that protect peptides from environmental factors, the use of antioxidants to prevent oxidative damage, and careful pH adjustment to maintain optimal stability conditions. Formulation approaches such as freeze-drying or spray-drying can also enhance long-term stability by reducing water activity, which limits hydrolytic degradation. Studies by Lafarga et al. (2017) demonstrated that microencapsulation of blood-derived peptides using maltodextrin carriers improved their stability in fortified beverages over a 6-month storage period, maintaining both ACE-inhibitory and antioxidant activities. For commercial applications, stability testing under intended storage conditions and throughout the expected shelf-life is essential to ensure that blood-derived peptides maintain their bioactive properties until consumption. Moreover, the interaction between peptides and other ingredients in complex formulations can also affect stability, necessitating comprehensive compatibility studies during product development.

Applications

Food applications:

Represents one of the largest sectors for slaughter blood utilization, with applications ranging from nutritional enhancement to functional ingredient development. Plasma proteins, particularly from porcine and bovine sources, are widely used as natural emulsifiers and binding agents in processed meat products, contributing to improved texture and water-holding capacity (Lynch et al., 2017). For example, porcine plasma proteins are extensively used in sausages and meat analogs, while bovine plasma finds applications in bakery products as an egg replacer. Hemoglobin from various species serves as a natural red colorant and iron fortificant.

Biomedical applications:

Bovine blood components, particularly plasma proteins and serum albumin, are extensively used in developing drug delivery systems, wound healing products and as stabilizers in pharmaceutical formulations (Son et al., 2023). In addition, mainly serum albumin and plasma proteins are extensively utilized in premium skincare formulations for their moisturizing, film-forming, and anti-aging properties (Schagen, 2017). Porcine blood derivatives have gained significance in producing surgical adhesives, hemostatic agents, and tissue sealants, owing to their structural similarities to human proteins (Weekes et al., 2022). Peptides derived from porcine plasma have shown promising results in promoting collagen synthesis and improving skin elasticity (Pu et al., 2023). Moreover, blood plasmas have shown their unique immunological properties, anti-inflammatory agents, and potential to generate specific antibodies (Knuplez & Marsche, 2020). Poultry blood (chicken and duck) has found potential in antimicrobial and antihypertensive activities (Bah et al., 2016).

Regulatory status and commercial challenges

Despite the bioactive potential of slaughter blood-derived peptides, several regulatory and commercial barriers must be resolved before laboratory findings can be translated into industrial food products. (1) Microbial and prion safety: blood is a high-risk substrate for foodborne pathogens (Salmonella spp., Escherichia coli O157:H7, Campylobacter spp., Listeria monocytogenes) and, in ruminants, for transmissible spongiform encephalopathy (TSE) agents (Bah et al., 2013). In the EU, blood collected for human consumption must comply with the food-hygiene framework of Regulations (EC) No 852/2004 and (EC) No 853/2004, under which blood may enter the food chain only from animals that have passed ante-mortem and post-mortem inspection and is collected under hygienic, HACCP-based conditions; blood that is not destined for food is instead an animal by-product, classified as Category 3 under Regulation (EC) No 1069/2009 and its implementing Regulation (EU) No 142/2011, whereas specified risk material from TSE-susceptible ruminants is classified as Category 1. In the US, edible blood collection is regulated by USDA-FSIS under 9 CFR 310.20, while downstream manufacture of blood-derived ingredients falls under FDA 21 CFR Part 117; both frameworks operationalise the HACCP principles of Codex CXC 1-1969. Validated thermal (≥ 72 °C for at least 15 s, HTST-equivalent), food-grade high-pressure processing (typically 400–600 MPa for 1–10 min) or pulsed electric field treatments are typically required to ensure a ≥ 5 log reduction of vegetative pathogens (Balasubramaniam et al., 2015); because plasma proteins are heat-labile and begin to coagulate near 70 °C, non-thermal high-pressure or pulsed-electric-field processing is often preferred for blood fractions in order to reconcile microbial safety with retention of protein functionality. Ruminant-derived material additionally requires age- and tissue-restricted sourcing to mitigate TSE risk. (2) Allergenicity: serum albumin (Bos d 6), immunoglobulins and certain plasma globulins are recognised cross-reactive allergens, particularly relevant for individuals sensitised to mammalian meat (α-gal syndrome). Enzymatic hydrolysis to peptides < 3 kDa can substantially reduce linear protein and peptide IgE epitopes; however, the α-gal determinant is a galactose-α-1,3-galactose carbohydrate carried on mammalian blood glycoproteins that is stable to both heat and proteolysis and is therefore not reliably eliminated by hydrolysis (Apostolovic et al., 2014), so allergen management must address both epitope classes. Rigorous in vitro (ELISA, mast-cell or basophil activation) and in silico (AllergenOnline, AllerCatPro) screening, in line with FAO/WHO and EFSA guidance (EFSA GMO Panel, 2017), is therefore essential. (3) Sensory acceptance: residual haem iron and lipid oxidation products generate metallic, bloody and rancid off-notes that strongly limit consumer acceptance. Effective mitigation requires combined decolorization, pH adjustment, encapsulation in food-grade carriers (maltodextrin, gum arabic, liposomes), or controlled enzymatic deflavoring (Muñoz-More et al., 2023). (4) Commercial scale-up and regulatory pathway: economic feasibility depends on integrated abattoir collection, low-temperature stabilisation, and high-throughput membrane and chromatographic purification, all of which currently push the production cost of purified peptides well above that of plant-derived alternatives (Boboua et al., 2024). In the US, GRAS notification is the most common route to market for plasma-based ingredients, whereas in the EU, peptide preparations from non-traditional blood sources (e.g., poultry, ovine) are likely to be considered Novel Foods under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283. Because the consumption of blood is expressly prohibited under both halal and kosher dietary law, blood-derived ingredients are categorically excluded from these markets, and broader consumer acceptance of “blood-derived” ingredients further constrains commercial deployment and should be explicitly addressed in future product development and labelling strategies.

Conclusion and perspectives

This comprehensive review addresses critical knowledge gaps in blood-derived bioactive peptides by systematically integrating proteolytic mechanisms with molecular characterization across multiple animal sources. Figure 7 provides a comprehensive visual summary of the four key themes discussed in this review: blood protein sources and composition, production and purification workflows, bioactive properties of the resulting peptides, and their stability and bioavailability during gastrointestinal transit. The transformation of slaughter blood from industrial waste to valuable functional food ingredients represents a significant advancement in sustainable food chemistry and circular economy principles. Our novel integration of structure-function relationships reveals that species-specific peptide bioactivities are fundamentally governed by distinct amino acid compositions and processing parameters. Key findings demonstrate that proline-rich peptides exhibit superior gastrointestinal stability, while hydrophobic amino acids enhance bioavailability through specific molecular mechanisms. The identification of predictive markers for peptide stability represents a paradigm shift from empirical to rational design of bioactive food ingredients. Enzymatic hydrolysis optimization using alcalase, trypsin, and papain enables targeted production of peptides with specific bioactivities including antioxidant, ACE-inhibitory, and DPP-IV inhibitory effects. Advanced decolorization methods, particularly chemical oxidative destruction, successfully overcome the primary barrier to widespread food industry adoption by eliminating the characteristic red coloration while preserving bioactive properties.
