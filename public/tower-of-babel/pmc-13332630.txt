Long-Term Follow-Up After Dual Transcatheter Valve-in-Valve Implantation Under VA-ECMO Support
Authors: Raghuram Palaparti, Max Lenz, Varius Dannenberg, Gregor Heitzinger, Caglayan Demirel, Anna Bartunek, Christian Nitsche, Luliana Coti, Irene Lang, Philipp E Bartko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiogenic shock due to simultaneous degeneration of multiple prosthetic valves is rare and is associated with prohibitive surgical risk.Case SummaryA 73-year-old woman with 3 prior sternotomies presented with acute decompensated heart failure, which rapidly progressed to cardiogenic shock after atrial fibrillation. Multimodality imaging demonstrated severe dysfunction of both aortic and mitral bioprostheses, with biventricular dysfunction. Refractory shock necessitated venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Given extreme surgical risk, a transcatheter strategy was pursued. Sequential transfemoral aortic and transseptal mitral valve-in-valve implantations were successfully performed under ECMO support. Hemodynamics improved immediately, allowing ECMO explantation. At the 3-year follow-up, both valves demonstrated durable function with sustained clinical recovery.DiscussionThe increasing integration of mechanical circulatory support with rapidly evolving transcatheter valve technologies is enabling a new paradigm of complex and high-risk structural heart interventions.Take-Home MessageDual transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation is feasible and effective in selected patients on mechanical circulatory support.Key words: cardiogenic shock, VA-ECMO, valve-in-valve TAVR, valve-in-valve TMVR

Clinical Presentation, Multimodality Imaging, and Procedural Strategy in a Patient With Cardiogenic Shock Due to Simultaneous Degeneration of Aortic and Mitral Bioprostheses

Stabilization with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation enabled sequential transfemoral aortic and transseptal mitral valve-in-valve implantation, resulting in rapid hemodynamic recovery and durable valve function at 3-year follow-up.

ECMO = extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; ICU = intensive care unit; TAVI = transcatheter aortic valve implantation; TMVR = transcatheter mitral valve replacement; VA-ECMO = venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; ViV = valve-in-valve.

History of Presentation

A 73-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnea and orthopnea culminating in acute decompensated heart failure with pulmonary edema. On admission, her blood pressure was 100/60 mm Hg, with a paced rhythm at approximately 100 beats/min on electrocardiogram. She was hypoxemic and tachypneic, requiring supplemental oxygen. During hospitalization, she developed atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response followed by abrupt hemodynamic deterioration with systolic blood pressure falling to 60 mm Hg, requiring escalating vasoactive support and ultimately mechanical circulatory support (MCS). Physical examination revealed cool extremities, elevated jugular venous pressure, diffuse pulmonary crackles, a holosystolic murmur, and peripheral edema. Despite medical therapy, she progressed to refractory cardiogenic shock.

Past Medical History

The patient had hypertension, hyperlipidemia, bicuspid aortic valve disease, Factor V Leiden mutation with prior deep venous thrombosis on chronic anticoagulation, and a history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia type II. She had undergone 3 prior sternotomies, including surgical aortic valve replacement (2004), mitral valve repair with a 30-mm annuloplasty ring (2011), and redo surgery with aortic root replacement and implantation of a 21-mm Mosaic Ultra aortic bioprosthesis (internal diameter: 17 mm) and a 29-mm Mosaic mitral bioprosthesis (internal diameter: 24 mm) in 2013. A leadless ventricular pacemaker had been implanted for tachy-brady syndrome. Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included acute coronary syndrome, tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy, acute prosthetic valvular dysfunction, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, and primary arrhythmic shock. Given severe pulmonary edema and the patient's extensive valve history, acute prosthetic valve dysfunction was considered as leading diagnosis.

Investigations

Electrocardiography showed paced rhythm with intermittent atrial fibrillation. Chest radiography revealed severe pulmonary congestion with bilateral pleural effusions. Transthoracic echocardiography showed moderately reduced left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction: 40%), severe pulmonary hypertension, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and right ventricular dysfunction. Both surgical prosthetic valves appeared severely dysfunctional. Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated severe stenosis of the surgical aortic bioprosthesis with a peak/mean gradient of 79/49 mm Hg and moderate aortic regurgitation. The mitral bioprosthesis exhibited markedly restricted leaflet motion with severe stenosis and a peak/mean gradient of 43/30 mm Hg. Three-dimensional imaging demonstrated thickened and immobile mitral prosthetic leaflets. Layered thrombus was identified on the interatrial septum and within the left atrial appendage, conferring elevated embolic risk (Figure 1). Despite maximal medical therapy, serum lactate rose progressively, peaking at 12.3 mmol/L, confirming refractory cardiogenic shock. After stabilization on MCS, multidetector computed tomography was performed for procedural planning and demonstrated suitable valve dimensions, favorable coronary heights and valve-to-coronary distances, and low predicted risk of left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction (neo-LVOT area: 454 mm2).

Management

Given refractory shock despite maximal ventilatory and vasoactive support, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was initiated via percutaneous femoral cannulation using heparin-coated cannulas (venous drainage: 23-F from right femoral access, arterial return: 19-F in left femoral access [Maquet] and a 6-F distal limb perfusion cannula [Terumo]). Hemodynamic stabilization was rapid, with improvement in end-organ perfusion. Anticoagulation was achieved with argatroban given prior heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. After multidisciplinary heart team review, redo surgical intervention was deemed prohibitive. A combined dual transcatheter valve-in-valve strategy (transcatheter aortic valve replacement [TAVR] and transcatheter mitral valve replacement [TMVR]) under ECMO support was selected.

Procedure

On day 7 of hospitalization, the patient underwent sequential transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation under general anesthesia with cerebral embolic protection. Ultrasound-guided right brachial arterial access and femoral arterial and venous access were obtained. A Sentinel cerebral protection system (Boston Scientific) was placed via right brachial access. A 16-F DrySeal sheath (Gore) and a 16-F eSheath (Edwards Lifesciences) were placed in the right femoral artery and left femoral vein, respectively. Temporary pacing was performed using a 5-F balloon-tipped Pacel catheter (Abbott) via another venous access site. The degenerated aortic prosthesis was crossed using an Amplatzer left (AL1) diagnostic catheter (Cordis) and a straight-tip hydrophilic guidewire (Terumo), which was exchanged with an angulated 5-F pigtail catheter. A Safari XS guidewire (Boston Scientific) was positioned in the left ventricle. Given the small internal diameter of the degenerated surgical aortic prosthesis (17 mm), a self-expanding supra-annular transcatheter heart valve was selected to optimize postprocedural hemodynamics.

The transfemoral valve-in-valve TAVR procedure was performed first to relieve severe outflow obstruction and improve systemic forward flow, allowing reassessment of hemodynamics before proceeding to the more complex transseptal valve-in-valve TMVR. At the time of the intervention, the patient had achieved partial hemodynamic stabilization on VA-ECMO with pulsatility, indicating meaningful native left ventricular ejection despite ongoing MCS. Consequently, reduction or temporary cessation of ECMO flow alone was insufficient to suppress native cardiac output during valve deployment. Rapid/fast ventricular pacing was therefore used to transiently reduce stroke volume and facilitate stable positioning of the transcatheter valve while ECMO flow was modulated to maintain systemic perfusion. A 23-mm Evolut PRO+ transcatheter heart valve (Medtronic) was deployed during fast ventricular pacing with deliberate ECMO flow modulation (baseline: 2.5-3.0 L/min, reduced to ∼1.0 L/min during deployment). Postdilatation was performed with an 18-mm True dilatation balloon (Bard Peripheral Vascular) to optimize prosthesis expansion (Figure 2, Video 1, Case Summary Video).

Transseptal access for valve-in-valve TMVR was obtained under echocardiographic guidance. The interatrial septum was dilated with a 12-mm Armada noncompliant balloon (Abbott Vascular). Two Safari XS wires were positioned in the left ventricle for additional support. A 26-mm Sapien 3 transcatheter heart valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was deployed within the degenerated surgical mitral bioprosthesis during rapid pacing with brief ECMO flow interruption (flow clamped to 0 L/min) to facilitate stable prosthesis positioning. Transient cardiac stunning occurred immediately after mitral valve implantation, which was managed with increased ECMO flows (up to 3.5 L/min) to maintain systemic perfusion. Native ventricular contractility and arterial pulsatility gradually recovered over the following minutes without additional intervention. Intraprocedural echocardiography confirmed excellent valve function with low gradients and no paravalvular regurgitation or LVOT obstruction (Figure 3, Video 2, Case Summary Video). There was no significant shunt across the iatrogenic atrial septal defect, and it was not closed. Arterial access was closed with 1 ProGlide device (Abbott Vascular) and an 8-F AngioSeal device (Terumo). Femoral venous access was closed with 1 ProGlide and a figure-of-8 suture.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient demonstrated immediate hemodynamic improvement, allowing VA-ECMO explantation the following day. Her intensive care unit course was complicated by Enterococcus faecalis bacteremia, Pseudomonas-associated groin wound infection, shock liver, acute kidney injury, hospital-acquired pneumonia, and critical illness polyneuropathy. She was discharged on hospital day 37 after rehabilitation. At the 1-, 2-, and 3-year follow-ups, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated durable valve function without significant stenosis or regurgitation. At 3 years, the mean gradients were 12 mm Hg across the aortic valve-in-valve prosthesis and 5 mm Hg across the mitral valve-in-valve prosthesis, without significant regurgitation. Left ventricular function improved, with an ejection fraction of 55%. The patient remained clinically stable with no recurrent heart failure. Discussion

Current guidelines support transcatheter valve-in-valve therapy for degenerated surgical bioprostheses in patients at high or prohibitive surgical risk.1,2 However, the evidence is largely derived from elective settings and does not specifically address patients presenting with cardiogenic shock or requiring MCS.3 Our patient met the criteria for advanced cardiogenic shock with progressive hypoperfusion.4 This case highlights the role of VA-ECMO in enabling transcatheter intervention.5 Careful ECMO flow modulation was essential to balance systemic perfusion against left ventricular loading during valve deployment, underscoring the need for close coordination among the interventional, anesthesia, and perfusion teams.6

Although thrombus was identified on the interatrial septum and within the left atrial appendage, a transseptal mitral valve-in-valve approach was considered feasible after multidisciplinary heart team discussion. A transapical strategy was avoided because of the patient's extreme surgical risk (3 prior sternotomies and cardiogenic shock on VA-ECMO) and her history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia requiring argatroban. In addition, treatment of the degenerated small surgical aortic prosthesis required transfemoral valve-in-valve TAVR with a supra-annular self-expanding valve, favoring a fully percutaneous strategy.

Simultaneous degeneration of aortic and mitral prosthetic valves is uncommon but is increasingly encountered. Reports of complex transcatheter heart valve interventions under ECMO support remain limited.7, 8, 9 This case provides 3-year follow-up data supporting the feasibility and durability of this strategy. Analogous to complex high-risk indicated percutaneous coronary intervention, integration of MCS with evolving transcatheter valve technologies is giving rise to an emerging field of complex, high-risk structural heart interventions.10

Conclusions

In patients presenting with cardiogenic shock due to degeneration of multiple prosthetic valves, transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation under VA-ECMO support can provide a lifesaving and durable therapeutic option. Careful patient selection, detailed imaging assessment, and real-time ECMO flow modulation are essential to optimize outcomes.
