Multi-omics analysis of deep brain stimulation associated with brain-gut axis modulation and symptom amelioration in a Parkinson’s disease mouse model
Authors: Ye Song, Changxin Yang, Jie Tu, Jiaojiao Dong, Xiao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying PD-associated brain-gut dysfunction through multi-omics analyses and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of combined Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) and Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) interventions. Transcriptomic and 16S rRNA datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Sequence Read Archive (SRA) were integrated and analyzed using DESeq2, limma, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and PICRUSt2 to identify disrupted pathways and microbial functional features. In the 1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD mouse model, four groups (Normal, MPTP, MPTP + DBS, and MPTP + DBS+BCI) were assessed using behavioral testing, Local Field Potentials (LFP) recordings, molecular assays, and histological analysis. The findings revealed synaptic damage and metabolic pathway disruptions in PD brains, accompanied by reduced abundance of Short-Chain Fatty Acid (SCFA)-producing gut microbes. Combined DBS and BCI markedly improved motor deficits, suppressed aberrant β oscillations, restored gut barrier integrity and microbial homeostasis, and reduced pathological α-synuclein (αSyn) aggregation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that DBS + BCI is associated with improvements across neural, microbial and inflammatory readouts, supporting a correlative brain-gut-immune framework. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with an increasing global incidence that has become a major public health concern [1]. Its hallmark pathology is the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), leading to motor impairments such as bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity [2]. Although traditionally regarded as a disorder confined to central neurodegeneration, growing evidence indicates that PD represents a multifaceted syndrome involving brain-gut-immune dysregulation [3]. Non-motor symptoms, including constipation and gastrointestinal dysmotility, often precede motor manifestations by years, suggesting the gut may serve as an early site of disease initiation [4].

As a central regulator of the gut-brain axis, the intestinal microbiota shapes neuro-immune-metabolic homeostasis through metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), aromatic compounds, and neurotransmitter precursors [5]. Numerous studies have shown profound gut dysbiosis in PD, characterized by reduced SCFAs, epithelial barrier impairment, and immune activation. These alterations may propagate to the brain via vagal pathways, inflammatory cascades, or exosomal signaling, ultimately triggering neurodegeneration [6]. From this perspective, exploring how gut microbiota influence transcriptional and neural circuit states has emerged as a critical frontier in PD research [7].

The “Braak hypothesis” provides a classic framework supporting a peripheral origin of PD, proposing that pathological α-synuclein (αSyn) aggregation begins in the enteric nervous system (ENS) and ascends via the vagus nerve to the brainstem and midbrain [8, 9]. Histological evidence has identified αSyn inclusions in peripheral tissues, including the ENS and vagus nerve of PD patients [10]. Animal studies further demonstrate that intestinal injection of αSyn fibrils induces central pathology [11]. Collectively, these findings suggest the gut is not only an early reservoir of pathology but also a pivotal driver of disease progression.

Advances in neuromodulation have transformed PD therapy, with deep brain stimulation (DBS) now widely applied in advanced stages [12]. DBS of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) not only corrects aberrant neural rhythms but also modulates synaptic protein expression, axonal projections, and circuit remodeling, thereby enhancing plasticity and delaying neurodegeneration [13, 14]. Recent studies reveal that DBS may also influence peripheral immunity and microbial balance through vagal pathways [15]. Flow cytometry and transcriptomics indicate that DBS decreases pro-inflammatory Th17 cells, increases regulatory T-cell (Treg) phenotypes, and reduces central inflammation [16, 17]. Clinically, some patients experience alleviation of constipation and improved gastrointestinal function after DBS, suggesting a distal regulatory effect [18]. Nonetheless, its impact on gut barrier integrity and microbial metabolism remains incompletely defined, limiting extension from local to systemic interventions [19].

Brain-computer interface (BCI) technologies represent the next generation of adaptive neuromodulation, offering closed-loop feedback and self-adjusting capabilities. BCI has shown promise in disorders such as epilepsy and consciousness impairment [20]. In PD, BCI can modulate DBS parameters based on pathological local field potentials (LFP) signals, particularly β-band oscillations, thereby refining neural discharge patterns. Preclinical and early clinical studies suggest that DBS combined with BCI intervention enhances synaptic synchrony, plasticity, and neuronal excitability [21]. Moreover, BCI may influence cortical-brainstem-vagus-gut pathways, enabling immune modulation akin to vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) and thereby regulating inflammation and microbial metabolism [22]. However, its role in brain-gut-immune interactions remains poorly characterized, with limited integrative multi-omics evidence [23].

To address this gap, we integrated transcriptomic datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE20141, GSE49036) with gut microbiome profiles from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (PRJEB30615), combined with 1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced mouse models and neuromodulation experiments. Using robust rank aggregation (RRA) and limma differential expression analysis, PICRUSt2 functional prediction, random forest modeling, and cross-modal Spearman correlation networks, we identified gut-brain interaction pathways linking key bacterial taxa to central genes. These findings were further validated by qPCR, LFP recordings, Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), and 16S sequencing, assessing changes in gut barrier function, neuronal architecture, and inflammatory states. Together, these results enabled the construction of a “neurotransmitter-microbiota-immune axis” model. This study provides new insights into systemic imbalances underlying PD and expands the conceptual framework for DBS combined with BCI intervention as a systemic, rather than solely local, therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Transcriptomic differential analysis and pathway enrichment in human brain tissues

Two publicly available human brain transcriptome datasets, GSE20141 and GSE49036, were retrieved from the GEO database. GSE20141 contains RNA expression profiles of prefrontal cortex samples from PD patients and controls, generated using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. GSE49036, obtained with the Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 platform, likewise includes brain tissue samples from PD and control subjects. Raw CEL files and annotation data were downloaded via the GEOquery package and uniformly transformed into log₂ expression matrices. To harmonize data across platforms and reduce probe redundancy, expression values from multiple probes mapping to the same gene were averaged to represent that gene. The resulting datasets were merged into a single expression matrix for downstream analysis. Given the platform heterogeneity, potential batch effects were corrected using the ComBat function in the sva package. Data consistency before and after correction was assessed by principal component analysis (PCA) and density distribution plots. Successful correction was defined as the absence of dataset-specific clustering along principal components and convergence of expression density curves. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified independently using the limma package and the RRA algorithm. In limma, a design matrix specified group assignments, and linear modeling followed by eBayes estimation was used to calculate differential expression. Genes with |log₂FC| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significant. In RRA, genes from each dataset were ranked by P value, and concordance of rankings across datasets was assessed, with significant shared up- or downregulated genes determined by RRA P values. The intersection of DEGs from both methods was designated as the candidate gene set. Their expression patterns were visualized with boxplots using ggplot2, while heatmaps and volcano plots were generated to confirm reproducibility and stability across datasets.

Functional enrichment was performed with the clusterProfiler package. Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process (BP) enrichment used parameters: ontology = “BP”, pAdjustMethod = “BH”, qvalueCutoff = 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment (species = Homo sapiens) was also conducted, followed by KEGG Mapper validation to assess expression changes in pathways linked to fatty acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor (PPAR) signaling, thereby revealing signatures of metabolic dysregulation. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was further applied to the entire expression profile using the fgsea package, ranking genes by log₂FC and evaluating GO-BP and KEGG-defined gene sets for global enrichment trends in the PD group (Figure S1).

Gut microbiota 16S rRNA sequencing and multidimensional microecological analysis

To systematically investigate gut microbiota alterations associated with PD and their responses to DBS combined with BCI intervention, we first conducted large-scale screening of microbial differences using public datasets, followed by validation in a mouse model to assess intervention efficacy and genus-level shifts.

Public dataset mining and analysis

Paired-end 16S rRNA V4 region sequencing data (2 × 250 bp) from PD patients (n = 156) and healthy controls (HC, n = 100) were retrieved from the European SRA project PRJEB30615. Raw FASTQ files were processed using fastp v0.23.4 for adapter trimming and quality filtering (Phred ≥ 20). Reads were denoised, merged, chimera-filtered, and converted into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 within QIIME2 v2023.2. Low-abundance ASVs (total count < 10) were removed to reduce noise. Taxonomic classification to the genus level was performed with the SILVA v138 database.

Alpha diversity (Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson, and InvSimpson indices) was calculated using the “phyloseq” and “vegan” R packages, with group differences tested by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity was assessed via Bray-Curtis distances, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), PERMANOVA (Adonis), and β-dispersion tests. Differential taxa were identified with DESeq2 and STAMP v2.1.3 (FDR < 0.05), while LefSe was applied for linear discriminant analysis to highlight PD-specific taxa. Phylogenetic cladograms were constructed with GraPhlAn and iTOL to illustrate evolutionary divergence (Figure S2).

Mouse model validation

On day 14 after MPTP induction, fresh fecal samples were collected from MPTP and MPTP + DBS+BCI groups under stress-free conditions, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. DNA was extracted using the TIANGEN Fecal DNA Kit (DP328, Tiangen Biotech, China) and quality-checked with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (A260/A280 = 1.8-2.0, intact bands required). The 16S V3-V4 region was amplified using primers 341 F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp). Processing followed the same pipeline as the human dataset, with ASV construction in QIIME2 and annotation with SILVA v138. Low-abundance ASVs (< 10 total reads) were filtered, and normalization was performed by rarefying to the minimum sequencing depth. Rarefaction curves confirmed adequate sequencing coverage, and underperforming samples were excluded. Alpha diversity indices (Shannon_e, Shannon_q = 2, Shannon_q = 10, Simpson, ACE, and Chao1) were calculated in R v4.3.1 using “phyloseq” and “vegan,” with group comparisons by Wilcoxon tests. Beta diversity was evaluated using PERMANOVA and betadisper. Genus-level relative abundance was visualized by stacked bar plots. Differential genera were identified using MetagenomeSeq (fitZig model) and DESeq2 v1.40.1, with forest plots displaying log2 fold changes and 95% confidence intervals.

Functional prediction and diagnostic modeling

To infer the functional potential of the microbial community based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing data, we utilized PICRUSt2 (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2). ASVs were normalized by copy number, and predicted metagenomes were mapped to KEGG Orthology (KO) and MetaCyc pathways.

To identify potential microbial biomarkers distinguishing PD from HC, we constructed a Random Forest (RF) classification model using the randomForest package in R. To mitigate overfitting and evaluate model robustness, we implemented a rigorous cross-validation strategy. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. Within the training set, feature selection and hyperparameter optimization were conducted using 10-fold cross-validation. The generalization capability of the final model was then assessed on the independent test set. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using the Area Under the Curve (AUC), specificity, and sensitivity metrics via the pROC package.

Analysis of gut microbiota-mediated regulation of neural pathways in PD

This study aimed to evaluate the potential role of the gut-brain axis in PD by integrating microbial functional pathways, abundances of SCFA-producing taxa, and aberrant signaling pathways in PD-affected brain regions. We combined 16S rRNA sequencing with transcriptomic datasets (GSE20141 and GSE49036) from the GEO database. Using the Hmisc package in R, we performed Spearman correlation analyses between microbial abundances and brain regional GO pathway scores, thereby constructing a cross-tissue correlation network. GO terms were further mapped to KEGG Level 2 functional categories using a keyword-based approach, enabling the establishment of microbiota-pathway associations and ensuring functional integration across data modalities. The resulting cross-modal functional network was visualized with the igraph and ggraph packages, and statistical analyses with heatmap visualization revealed potential links between microbial metabolic functions (e.g., energy metabolism, transport and catabolism) and neural processes (e.g., synaptic vesicle dynamics, axon terminal signaling) (Figure S3).

Animal experiment design and interventions

Male SPF-grade C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, 20–22 g) were obtained from the institutional animal facility. All experimental protocols were approved by the local ethics committee of Harbin Institute of Technology (2025-09-02) and complied with the Regulations for the Administration of Laboratory Animals of the People’s Republic of China. Mice were housed under barrier conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50%-60% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water, and body weight and health were monitored throughout the study.

Animals were randomly assigned to four groups: Normal, MPTP, MPTP + DBS, and MPTP + DBS + BCI (n = 15 per group). Except for the Normal group, all mice received intraperitoneal MPTP injections (30 mg/kg) for 5 consecutive days to induce the PD model. On day 7, mice in the MPTP + DBS and MPTP + DBS + BCI groups underwent electrode implantation in the STN (coordinates: AP -2.0 mm, ML + 1.5 mm, DV -4.8 mm). Electrodes were connected to a multichannel stimulator, with stimulation parameters set to 130 Hz, 60 µs pulse width, and 100 µA current, applied for 2 h/day over 7 days. The BCI system analyzed LFP-derived PSD to detect aberrant β-band synchronization (13–30 Hz) and dynamically adjusted stimulation frequency (100–130 Hz) in a closed-loop manner. Behavioral assessments were conducted after intervention, followed by the collection of brain and colonic tissues for further analysis.

CatWalk gait analysis

The CatWalk XT system (Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands) was employed to quantitatively assess gait parameters. The testing corridor was maintained under dim and quiet conditions to encourage voluntary crossing. After acclimation, each mouse completed three uninterrupted runs on the testing day. The system automatically recorded stride length and stand duration of fore- and hindlimbs. Data were analyzed using CatWalk XT software (v10.6), and mean values from three runs per animal were used for statistical comparisons.

Constant-speed rotarod and pole descent tests

Motor performance was evaluated using a rotarod test following one week of training. Mice were placed on a 3 cm-diameter rod rotating from 5 to 50 rpm over 5 min, with latency to fall recorded across three trials. After training, a pole descent test was performed, in which mice descended a 75 cm-long, 1 cm-diameter pole; descent time was recorded across three trials.

Open-field test

Spontaneous locomotor activity was assessed in a 50 × 50 × 40 cm plexiglass arena. Mice were habituated for 10 min, then allowed free exploration for an additional 10 min. Movements were tracked with an infrared camera and ANY-maze software (Stoelting, USA). Parameters extracted included total distance traveled, average speed, number of center entries, and distance traveled in the center zone (25 × 25 cm). These metrics were used to evaluate locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior.

LFP acquisition and β-band analysis

In anesthetized and stereotaxically fixed mice, stainless-steel electrodes (outer diameter ~ 0.3 mm) were bilaterally implanted into the STN according to coordinates from the Paxinos and Franklin mouse brain atlas. LFPs were continuously recorded at a 1 kHz sampling rate using a multichannel neural signal recording system (e.g., Plexon or Neuralynx).

All signals were band-pass filtered between 1 and 100 Hz to eliminate electromyographic artifacts and DC drift. Power spectral density (PSD) was estimated using Welch’s method (2 s window, 50% overlap), with β-band activity defined as 13–30 Hz. To minimize inter-individual baseline variability, β power was normalized to the total power within 1–100 Hz and expressed as a percentage. For experiments involving DBS, LFPs were analyzed before, during, and after stimulation to assess β-band synchrony and pathological oscillatory activity.

Closed-loop BCI system architecture and stimulation strategy

We established a LFP-based closed-loop BCI system to implement adaptive DBS modulation. The system comprises three core modules: signal acquisition, feature extraction/decision logic, and stimulation modulation. (1) Signal Acquisition: Real-time LFP signals were acquired from STN-implanted electrodes (sampling rate: 1 kHz) and band-pass filtered (1–100 Hz) to remove artifacts. (2) Feature Extraction and Decision Logic: PSD was calculated using a sliding window approach (500 ms window, 50% overlap). The normalized power within the β-band (13–30 Hz) served as the control variable. The decision logic utilized a threshold-trigger mechanism: the trigger threshold was set at the 75th percentile of the baseline β-power recorded during the resting state. (3) Output Modulation: When real-time β-power exceeded the threshold for > 1 s, the system automatically triggered high-frequency stimulation (130 Hz). Conversely, when β-power fell below the threshold, stimulation reverted to a low-frequency maintenance mode (60 Hz) or ceased. This closed-loop architecture embodies the real-time feedback characteristic of BCI systems, targeting pathological oscillations precisely to optimize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential side effects from continuous stimulation.

Heart rate variability (HRV)

HRV recordings were obtained in awake, restrained mice under stable ambient conditions (22–24 °C) to minimize autonomic fluctuations. Pulse wave signals were collected for 10 min at 500 Hz using a non-invasive tail-cuff photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor (e.g., ADInstruments MLT1020/D). Peak detection and pulse-to-pulse interval (PPI) calculation were performed using a customized algorithm or built-in preprocessing modules, with artifacts removed prior to generating RR interval sequences. HRV parameters were analyzed with Kubios HRV Premium software (University of Eastern Finland), employing standard time-domain methods with default filter thresholds. The primary indices included: standard deviation of NN intervals (SDNN), reflecting overall autonomic variability, and root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), reflecting parasympathetic activity.

Tissue collection and processing

On day 14 after intervention, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p.). Following confirmation of anesthesia, cardiac perfusion was performed with ~ 20 mL ice-cold 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove blood, followed by 10–15 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for fixation of brain and peripheral tissues. The whole brain and colon were rapidly dissected and transferred to cold PBS. Under a dissecting microscope, the SNc and caudate-putamen (CP) were isolated and divided into hemispheres. The left hemisphere was post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, then processed for histology: paraffin embedding following graded ethanol dehydration and xylene clearing, or OCT embedding after 30% sucrose cryoprotection for frozen sections used in Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF). The right hemisphere was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent protein (Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay [RIPA] lysis) and RNA (TRIzol extraction) analyses, with portions fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M PBS, 4 °C, 24 h) for TEM.

Colon samples were taken from the mid-segment (~ 4 cm distal to the cecum), rinsed thoroughly with PBS, and flattened to record natural length as a functional parameter. Tissues were then divided: one part fixed in 4% PFA for paraffin embedding and histological staining, and the other snap-frozen for molecular analyses. All procedures were performed under cold conditions and with minimal delay to preserve morphological and molecular integrity.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen brain Sect. (20 μm) were mounted on collagen-coated slides and stored at − 20 °C. Prior to staining, sections were air-dried at room temperature for 30 min. To assess dopaminergic neuronal activity, sections from the substantia nigra (SN) were subjected to double immunofluorescence staining for tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and the immediate early gene c-Fos. Following permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocking in 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-TH and mouse anti-c-Fos primary antibodies. After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (10 min). Images were acquired with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope, with 3–5 representative fields captured per animal. TH⁺, c-Fos⁺, and TH⁺/c-Fos⁺ double-positive cells were manually quantified using ImageJ, and the ratio of double-positive to total TH⁺ neurons was calculated as an indicator of dopaminergic neuronal activation.

To evaluate PD-related protein aggregation, sections from the CP and SN were processed for dual staining of αSyn and phosphorylated Ser129 α-synuclein (pSer129-αSyn). Antigen retrieval and blocking were performed as described above, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with rabbit anti-αSyn and mouse anti-pSer129-αSyn antibodies. After secondary antibody incubation, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. To aid in anatomical localization, rapid Nissl staining was performed, and the resulting purple-blue Nissl signals were superimposed on immunofluorescence images. Protein aggregates were analyzed using ImageJ or Fiji software to quantify number, mean area, and density, thereby assessing αSyn aggregation and spatial distribution under different treatments.

Frozen colonic tissue Sections (10 μm) were used to examine epithelial barrier structure and mucosal immune phenotypes. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-ZO-1, rabbit anti-CLDN5, mouse anti-CD86, and mouse anti-CD206 antibodies, followed by Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500). After DAPI nuclear counterstaining, images were captured by confocal microscopy. ZO-1 and CLDN5 expression was assessed based on signal continuity and localization, while CD86⁺ and CD206⁺ macrophages were quantified to calculate the M1/M2 polarization ratio, reflecting intestinal immune homeostasis. All image acquisition and analysis were performed under blinded conditions, with careful control for spectral overlap and cross-channel interference. Antibody details are provided in Table S1.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Tissues were homogenized on ice in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, #89900) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (10 µL buffer per mg tissue). Homogenates were rotated for 30 min at 4 °C and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatants were collected for protein quantification (Pierce™ Bicinchoninic Acid [BCA] Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher, #23225). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg per sample) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (NuPAGE™ 10% Bis-Tris Gel, Invitrogen, #NP0301BOX) and transferred to Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.45 μm; Millipore, #IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with BSA (Sigma, #A9647) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TH, Dopamine Transporter (DAT), Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1 (TRPV1), Heat Shock Cognate 70 (HSC70), ZO-1, and CLDN5 (Table S1). After PBS-T (0.05% Tween-20) washes, membranes were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074S or #7076S). Bands were visualized using ECL substrate (SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS, Thermo Fisher, #34580) and quantified by ImageJ, with β-actin serving as the loading control. Results were expressed as the target protein/β-actin ratio.

For analysis of αSyn solubility, SN tissues were lysed in T-PER™ reagent (Thermo Fisher, #78510) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was collected as the soluble fraction, while the pellet was resuspended in 1% SDS buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, #L4509) and sonicated to extract insoluble proteins. Both fractions were quantified, loaded equally onto SDS-PAGE, and probed with anti-αSyn antibodies. The ratio of insoluble to total αSyn signal intensity was used to evaluate aggregation levels across treatment groups. Details of antibodies and key reagents are listed in Table S1.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Homogenization was performed with a Precellys Evolution tissue grinder (Bertin Instruments) and steel beads to ensure RNA integrity. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and only samples with an A260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were included for further analysis. One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, RR037A) in a final reaction volume of 10 µL. Quantitative PCR was carried out using the SYBR Green method with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL SYBR Mix, 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template, and 7.2 µL RNase-free water. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Specificity of amplification was confirmed by melt-curve analysis. Target genes included Snca, Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and Interleukin-10 (IL-10), with β-actin serving as the internal control. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) and validated for amplification efficiency using gradient PCR. Relative expression levels were calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt method, and all experiments were performed in triplicate wells. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay

To quantify αSyn protein expression in brain regions under different experimental conditions, a commercial ELISA kit (Abcam, ab260057) was used to analyze homogenates of the CP and midbrain. Tissues were homogenized in PBS containing protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min to remove debris. Supernatants were collected, and total protein concentrations were determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227) for normalization of αSyn levels. ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Standards and samples were analyzed in duplicate; 100 µL of reaction solution was added per well and incubated at room temperature for 2 h, followed by sequential addition of the detection antibody and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Color development was achieved with TMB substrate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Epoch microplate reader. The standard curve was generated using a log-log regression model.

H&E staining and inflammation scoring

Colon tissues were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. H&E staining was performed following standard procedures. Sections underwent deparaffinization with xylene, rehydration through graded ethanol, nuclear staining with hematoxylin, cytoplasmic counterstaining with eosin, dehydration, and mounting. Images were acquired using a Leica DM3000 light microscope. For each animal, three representative sections from different regions were selected, and three random high-power fields (100×) per section were analyzed. Inflammation was evaluated using the Dieleman scoring system, which assesses crypt architecture, epithelial integrity, and inflammatory cell infiltration (each scored 0–3; total score 0–12). Scoring was performed in a blinded manner by two independent observers. Discrepancies greater than one point were adjudicated by a third evaluator. The average score per animal was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, and multiple group comparisons were analyzed with one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Nonparametric data were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Correlations were assessed using Spearman or Pearson tests. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Significance levels were defined as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

Multi-omics analysis of public databases reveals brain-gut interaction imbalance in PD

To systematically characterize transcriptional alterations in PD brain tissue, we performed an integrative analysis of two publicly available GEO datasets (GSE20141 and GSE49036). Following batch-effect correction, differential expression profiling, key gene identification, and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted.

Prior to correction, visualization indicated systematic deviations between the two datasets, with PCA displaying clear separation based on batch effects (Figure S4A-C). After adjustment with the Combat algorithm, expression distributions were normalized, and sample clustering was no longer biased by dataset origin, indicating effective removal of batch effects.

Subsequently, RRA and limma-based differential expression analyses were applied to the combined expression matrix. A total of 147 DEGs were consistently identified across both strategies (Fig. 1A), exhibiting reproducible expression trends across datasets. These DEGs likely represent central molecular determinants of PD pathogenesis.

Differential Gene Expression Screening and Functional Enrichment Analysis. Note: (A) Venn diagram showing the intersection of DEGs identified by RRA and limma methods; (B) boxplot depicting the expression distribution of intersecting candidate genes; (C) GO enrichment map illustrating BP networks; (D) KEGG enrichment map highlighting significantly enriched metabolic pathways; (E-F) GSEA plots evaluating overall enrichment trends of GO BP

Among them, several neural regulatory genes emerged as key candidates. Notably, Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), SLC18A2/SLC8A1 (neurotransmitter transporters), and SV2B (a synaptic vesicle protein) were significantly downregulated in PD, while Interleukin-6 Receptor (IL6R) and P2RX7 (a purinergic receptor) were upregulated (Fig. 1B). These genes are implicated in synaptic homeostasis, inflammatory signaling, and neurotransmitter cycling, forming a potential molecular basis for neurodegenerative processes in PD.

GO enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated genes were concentrated in processes essential for neuronal network integrity, including synapse assembly, chemical synaptic transmission, and cell junction organization (Fig. 1C), underscoring synaptic and connectivity impairment as defining pathological features of PD.

KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated profound metabolic dysregulation, with enrichment in fatty acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and the PPAR signaling pathway. DEGs such as ELOVL6, MCAT, ACSL6, FABP7, and ME3 pointed to disrupted lipid synthesis and energy metabolism. Additional enrichment of glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis (EXT2, B3GALT6) and amino sugar metabolism (MPI, GNPD2) highlighted widespread perturbations in carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 1D). These metabolic alterations may not solely arise from neuronal dysfunction but could also be influenced by microbial metabolites—including SCFAs and aromatic compounds—supporting a potential role for the gut microbiota in shaping PD pathology.

GSEA provided a genome-wide perspective, revealing broad negative enrichment of synapse-related pathways (e.g., synaptic assembly, trans-synaptic signaling), whereas non-neuronal processes such as cell adhesion and cellular component organization exhibited partial activation (Fig. 1E-F).

Collectively, these multi-omics findings reveal pronounced synaptic structural and functional disruption in PD brains, accompanied by metabolic pathway imbalance. The data support the concept that cross-talk between neuronal metabolism, immune signaling, and gut-derived metabolites underlies brain-gut axis dysfunction, representing a critical pathogenic mechanism in PD.

Multidimensional data mining reveals gut microbiota dysbiosis and microbiota-brain interactions in PD

To systematically characterize gut microbial imbalance in PD, we performed an integrative analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing data from the SRA project PRJEB30615, comprising PD patients (n = 156) and HC (n = 100). The analysis focused on microbial composition, phylogenetic structure, and functional network remodeling, and further explored potential interactions with brain signaling.

Alpha-diversity assessment showed no significant differences in richness indices (Richness, Chao1, ACE) or diversity indices (Shannon, InvSimpson) between groups, although the Simpson index was significantly elevated in PD, indicating an increased dominance of certain taxa (Figure S5A). Rarefaction curves approached saturation, confirming sufficient sequencing depth (Figure S5B). PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distances revealed distinct community segregation between PD and HC (Figure S5C), with PERMANOVA confirming statistical significance (Adonis R² = 0.03, p = 0.000167). Moreover, PD samples exhibited greater β-diversity dispersion (p = 0.001), suggesting increased instability of microbial structure.

At the genus level, Venn analysis identified 72 shared core genera, with PD-specific genera (n = 58) markedly exceeding those in HC (n = 30), indicating enhanced disease-specific microbiota features (Figure S5D). DESeq2 detected 83 differentially abundant genera, with 60 enriched and 23 depleted in PD (Figure S5E), suggesting a shift toward a pathogenic microbial profile.

Overall community structure remained broadly similar, but key genera shifted: Streptococcus and Anaerobutyricum were slightly elevated in PD, while Blautia and Lacticaseibacillus declined, implicating disrupted metabolic and immunoregulatory functions (Fig. 2A-B). Forest plots confirmed significant reductions in beneficial taxa such as Fournierella, alongside notable increases in conditionally pathogenic or metabolically active genera, including Bifidobacterium, Dyssomobacter, and Actinomyces (adjusted p < 0.001, 95% CI excluding zero). The abnormal expansion of Bifidobacterium may reflect compensatory proliferation or functional drift, while elevated Dyssomobacter and Actinomyces suggest inflammation and barrier impairment, consistent with a pathogenic ecological shift (Fig. 2C).

Phylogenetic cladograms revealed distinct taxonomic clustering: HC-dominant taxa (e.g., Ruminococcaceae, Eubacteriaceae) localized centrally, whereas PD-enriched families (e.g., Enterococcaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae) expanded peripherally, reflecting phylogenetic dispersion (Fig. 2D). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) identified discriminative taxa (Fig. 2E), including declines in Blautia and Ruminococcaceae—microbes critical for immune homeostasis, energy metabolism, and neuronal health—underscoring niche and metabolic divergence.

Functional prediction using PICRUSt2 indicated potential shifts of 16 key pathways in PD, including neurodegenerative disease, energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and immune-related processes (adjusted p < 0.05; Figure S6A). A random forest model integrating genera and predicted pathway variables identified Fournierella, Massilimassilia, and circulatory system pathways as top contributors (Figure S6B). The model achieved an AUC of 1.00 in the current dataset (Figure S6C), suggesting high accuracy as a potential microbial biomarker for PD. However, given the limited sample size, this high classification performance may reflect specific dataset characteristics, warranting further validation in larger, independent cohorts to confirm its generalizability. Network analysis further revealed SCFA-producing taxa such as Blautia tightly linked to digestive-excretory functions, while Falcatimonas and Rahnella connected simultaneously with immune, neurological, and metabolic pathways, suggesting their roles as functional hubs (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these multidimensional analyses demonstrate that PD-associated dysbiosis extends beyond taxonomic imbalance to encompass metabolic reprogramming and phylogenetic divergence, potentially reflecting key associations within gut-brain signaling in disease onset and progression.

Gut microbiota-mediated regulation of neural signaling pathways in PD

To further validate the neuro-gut axis, we integrated microbial functional pathways, the abundance of SCFA-associated dominant genera, and aberrant signaling pathways in PD-affected brain regions. A cross-tissue Spearman correlation analysis was performed to uncover potential interactions (Fig. 3A).

Cross-tissue spearman correlation reveals gut-brain interaction mechanisms. Note: (A) Workflow of integrative cross-tissue analysis, including 16S microbial abundance, SCFA-producing genera, and brain-region functional pathways assessed by Spearman correlation; (B) cross-modal functional network integrating 16S and GEO pathway annotations, showing interactions among co-linear pathways and functional modules; (C) network of associations between dominant genera and key brain-region genes; (D) heatmap of Spearman correlations between genera and brain-region DEGs

The “Cross-modal Functional Network” map illustrated the correspondence between microbial functions and neural signaling pathways. Gut metabolic functions, such as energy metabolism and transport/catabolism, were strongly linked to neural processes, including synaptic vesicle activity, exocytosis, and axon terminal signaling, suggesting a cross-tissue association pattern consistent with microbiota-brain coupling. Several functional nodes were bridged through structures such as the synaptic vesicle membrane (Fig. 3B).

We then constructed a “Microbiota-Gene Network” to examine associations between dominant taxa (e.g., Fournierella, Blautia, Lactococcus) and key brain-region genes. For instance, Fournierella correlated positively with mitochondrial metabolism and translation-related genes such as COQ9 and RPLP0, while Lactococcus was significantly associated with axonal transport and glucose transporter genes, including TUBA1B and SLC2A1, forming a robust interaction network that supports a microbiota-mediated link between metabolism and synaptic activity (Fig. 3C).

To quantify these relationships, we generated a “Microbes vs. Gene Correlation (Spearman)” heatmap. The strongest association was observed between Lactococcus and IL6R, suggesting a potential role in PD pathogenesis via inflammatory signaling. Blautia exhibited broad positive correlations with genes related to synaptic function, inflammation, and molecular transport, highlighting it as a potential central regulator (Fig. 3D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that alterations in PD-related gut microbiota are closely associated with signaling, energy metabolism, and synaptic structure in the brain. The correlation networks reveal possible cross-tissue association patterns and provide a foundation for exploring microbiota-based interventions in PD.

DBS combined with BCI intervention significantly improves motor function, neural oscillatory activity, and autonomic regulation in a PD model

To comprehensively assess the therapeutic efficacy of DBS combined with BCI intervention in an MPTP-induced PD mouse model, four experimental groups were established: Normal, MPTP, MPTP + DBS, and MPTP + DBS+BCI (Fig. 4A). Specifically, the closed-loop BCI system continuously monitored STN LFPs and modulated stimulation frequency (HFS vs. LFS) based on real-time beta-band (13–30 Hz) PSD, enabling adaptive therapeutic intervention.

DBS Combined with BCI interventions improve motor function in the MPTP-Induced PD mouse model. Note: (A) Schematic of experimental design and closed-loop BCI strategy. The left panel illustrates the MPTP model induction, grouping (Normal, MPTP, MPTP + DBS, MPTP + DBS+BCI), and behavioral testing timeline. The right panel details the closed-loop BCI architecture: Local Field Potentials (LFPs) are acquired from the STN in real-time and filtered (1–100 Hz). Beta-band power (13–30 Hz) is extracted via Power Spectral Density (PSD) analysis. A control algorithm compares real-time beta power against a pre-set threshold (75% of baseline). High-Frequency Stimulation (HFS, 130 Hz) is triggered when beta power exceeds the threshold; otherwise, the system reverts to Low-Frequency Stimulation (LFS, 60 Hz) or standby mode, enabling adaptive modulation; (B) CatWalk gait analysis assessing stride length and support time; (C) Rotarod test evaluating balance duration; (D) Pole test measuring time to descend; (E) Open-field tracking plots; (F-I) Quantitative open-field analyses of total distance traveled, distance moved within the central area, average speed, and frequency of center entries. Data are presented as n = 15; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

At the behavioral level, gait performance was first evaluated using the CatWalk automated analysis system (Fig. 4B). Compared with the Normal group, MPTP mice exhibited shorter stride length and prolonged stance duration, indicating impaired motor coordination. DBS treatment partially alleviated these deficits, whereas the DBS combined with BCI intervention produced a more pronounced improvement, with stride length and stance duration approaching normal levels, highlighting a synergistic effect on gait stabilization.

In the rotarod test (Fig. 4C), MPTP mice showed reduced latency to fall relative to controls, consistent with vestibulo-cerebellar dysfunction. DBS treatment modestly improved balance without reaching statistical significance, while the combined intervention significantly enhanced performance compared with both MPTP and MPTP + DBS groups, underscoring its superiority in postural and coordination control. Similarly, in the pole test (Fig. 4D), the combined intervention markedly shortened descent time, reflecting improved initiation of movement.

Spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were further evaluated in the open-field test. MPTP mice displayed restricted movement patterns (Fig. 4E), with significant reductions in total distance traveled (Fig. 4F), average speed (Fig. 4H), central zone entries (Fig. 4I), and distance moved within the central area (Fig. 4G), relative to controls. While DBS partially improved some parameters, the combined intervention significantly outperformed both MPTP and DBS groups across all metrics, indicating robust enhancement of locomotor activity and exploratory behavior.

At the electrophysiological level, LFPs were recorded in the midbrain to assess β-band oscillations under resting conditions (Figure S7A). MPTP mice exhibited elevated β power, reflecting heightened pathological synchrony. DBS reduced β oscillations to some extent, whereas combined intervention restored β power more effectively, approximating control levels. This suggests that BCI augments DBS in attenuating aberrant β rhythms and re-establishing neural network synchrony.

Autonomic dysfunction, another key non-motor feature of PD, was examined via HRV. Time-domain measures included SDNN (overall variability) and RMSSD (parasympathetic tone) (Figures S7B-C). Both indices were significantly reduced in MPTP mice. Combined intervention significantly improved HRV compared with both MPTP and DBS groups, demonstrating its capacity to restore autonomic regulation.

In summary, DBS combined with BCI intervention confers superior benefits over DBS alone, improving motor behavior, electrophysiological synchrony, and autonomic stability. These findings suggest that BCI enhances the therapeutic scope of DBS, providing a promising strategy for multi-system regulation in PD.

DBS combined with BCI preserves the integrity of dopaminergic neurons and synaptic structures

To investigate the effects of DBS combined with BCI on dopaminergic neurons and neural activity in a PD mouse model (Fig. 5A), MPTP-induced PD mice were divided into four groups: Normal, MPTP, MPTP + DBS, and MPTP + DBS+BCI. Stimulation electrodes were bilaterally implanted into the STN, and brain tissues were collected on day 14 for histological and protein-level analyses. TEM of the SN (Fig. 5B) revealed that the MPTP group exhibited disrupted presynaptic membranes, disorganized synaptic vesicles, blurred synaptic clefts, and a marked reduction in synaptic density. Partial structural improvement was observed in the MPTP + DBS group, while the MPTP + DBS+BCI group showed well-aligned vesicles, enhanced structural continuity, and significantly increased synaptic density. TH IHC (Fig. 5C) demonstrated a sharp reduction of TH-positive neurons in the MPTP group, indicating severe dopaminergic system impairment. Mild improvement was observed in the MPTP + DBS group, whereas the MPTP + DBS+BCI group exhibited a robust increase in TH expression, with neuronal counts approaching those of the Normal group.

For WB, n = 5; for all other experiments, n = 15. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Nissl staining (Fig. 5D) further confirmed a pronounced neuronal loss and morphological collapse in the MPTP group. In contrast, the MPTP + DBS+BCI group showed a substantial restoration of neuronal numbers and more intact, well-defined somata, suggesting a protective effect of the intervention.

Confocal immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5E) revealed high expression of TH (green) and c-fos (red) in the SNc of the Normal group, consistent with active dopaminergic neuronal function. In the MPTP group, both TH and c-fos were markedly reduced, indicating functional impairment. Compared with MPTP mice, the MPTP + DBS group exhibited notable upregulation of TH and c-fos, and combined BCI intervention further amplified this improvement, suggesting that DBS with BCI partially restores dopaminergic neuronal activity and promotes neural activation.

WB analysis (Fig. 5F) showed that proteins related to dopaminergic function and neuronal homeostasis, including TRPV1, HSC70, DAT, and TH, were substantially downregulated in the MPTP group, with TH and DAT most severely reduced. DBS treatment significantly upregulated all four proteins, and their expression was further enhanced by the addition of BCI.

DBS combined with BCI intervention effectively eliminates αSyn aggregation in the midbrain

To systematically evaluate the impact of DBS combined with BCI intervention on the pathological features of PD, particularly its regulation of abnormal αSyn aggregation, we employed an MPTP-induced PD mouse model. On day 14 of treatment, tissues from the CP and SN were collected for comprehensive pathological and molecular analyses (Fig. 6A).

Laser confocal immunofluorescence revealed αSyn (red) and its pathological phosphorylated form, pSer129-αSyn (green), in CP and SN regions, with Nissl staining (blue) marking neuronal structures. The MPTP group displayed extensive punctate and aggregated signals, whereas the intervention groups showed progressively reduced accumulation. Strikingly, pathological aggregates were nearly absent in the MPTP + DBS+BCI group (Fig. 6B).

WB analysis corroborated these findings (Fig. 6C-D). In midbrain soluble and insoluble fractions, αSyn accumulation was markedly elevated in the MPTP group, indicating aggravated pathology. DBS alone partially reduced insoluble αSyn, while DBS combined with BCI intervention restored levels close to those of controls. Densitometric analysis further demonstrated that insoluble αSyn decreased significantly with DBS and declined even more with the combined strategy. The ratio of insoluble to total αSyn was also substantially reduced, underscoring the ability of DBS + BCI to clear aggregated protein and alleviate neurotoxicity.

To determine whether these changes originated from transcriptional regulation, RT-qPCR was performed on CP and midbrain tissues (Fig. 6E). MPTP administration elevated αSyn transcript levels, whereas DBS + BCI markedly suppressed expression, suggesting transcriptional involvement in the observed pathology.

Consistent results were obtained from ELISA, which quantified αSyn protein in CP and midbrain homogenates (Fig. 6F). Levels were significantly higher in the MPTP group compared with controls, but decreased most prominently following DBS + BCI intervention. Collectively, these data demonstrate that DBS combined with BCI not only reduces αSyn transcription but also eliminates aggregated forms and lowers overall protein burden. This multifaceted effect significantly mitigates pathological protein accumulation in PD models, offering a promising strategy to mitigate neurotoxicity-induced pathology.

DBS combined with BCI intervention alleviates peripheral inflammation in the colonic tissue of PD model mice

To systematically evaluate the regulatory effects of DBS combined with BCI intervention on peripheral pathology in a PD model, colonic tissues were collected on day 14 after MPTP administration and subjected to multidimensional assessments, including structural morphology, permeability, motility, and inflammatory factor expression (Fig. 7A). Physiological structure: MPTP-treated mice exhibited significant weight loss, whereas this trend was mitigated in the combined intervention group (Figure S8A). Colonic shortening induced by MPTP was markedly reversed following treatment (Figure S8B). FITC-dextran permeability assays revealed elevated serum FITC levels in the MPTP group, which returned to baseline after intervention (Figure S8C). Gastrointestinal transit, assessed by Evans blue gavage and fecal output within 24 h, showed delayed gastric emptying and intestinal propulsion in MPTP mice, while DBS combined with BCI intervention promoted functional improvement (Figure S8D). Total fecal output was reduced in the MPTP group but significantly improved after intervention (Figure S8E).

Barrier function: Immunofluorescence staining showed that MPTP disrupted ZO-1 and CLDN5 distribution, whereas the combined intervention restored their continuity (Fig. 7B-C). WB further confirmed downregulation of ZO-1 and CLDN5 protein levels in the MPTP group and their robust upregulation following intervention (Fig. 7D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MPTP induces marked disruption of colonic barrier integrity and pro-inflammatory activation, whereas DBS combined with BCI intervention effectively ameliorates gastrointestinal dysfunction and peripheral inflammation in PD model mice, thereby offering a potential strategy to interrupt central-peripheral pathological feedback.

DBS combined with BCI ameliorates gut microbiota homeostasis

To investigate the regulatory effects of DBS combined with BCI intervention on gut microbiota diversity and composition in advanced PD, fecal samples from MPTP mice were collected after 14 days of treatment. 16S rRNA sequencing was then performed (Fig. 8A).

(B) α-diversity indices including Simpson index, Shannon evenness (Shannon_e), and Shannon index at q = 2 and q = 10 (Shannon_2, Shannon_10). Analysis of α-diversity revealed that the DBS + BCI group exhibited a significantly lower Simpson index compared with the MPTP group, indicating reduced dominance and more balanced community distribution. The Shannon evenness index (Shannon_e) was markedly increased, reflecting improved microbial uniformity. Likewise, weighted Shannon indices at q = 2 (Shannon_2) and q = 10 (Shannon_10) were both significantly higher in the DBS + BCI group (Fig. 8B), suggesting that the intervention enhanced not only evenness but also the contribution of rare and low-abundance taxa. Rarefaction curves (Fig. 8C) reached saturation at sequencing depths of ~ 50,000 reads, confirming adequate coverage and reliability.

At the genus level, Akkermansia was markedly elevated in the MPTP group, consistent with its compensatory expansion under mucin-degrading and proinflammatory conditions. Desulfovibrio (a hydrogen sulfide-producing genus) and Adlercreutzia were also more abundant in the MPTP group, potentially linked to oxidative stress and metabolic imbalance. In contrast, Lactococcus was significantly enriched in the DBS + BCI group, suggesting a protective role in lactate metabolism and mucosal repair (Fig. 8D). Differential abundance analysis using the MetagenomeSeq model with 95% confidence intervals identified Christensenella and Prevotella as significantly enriched in the DBS + BCI group, whereas Akkermansia and Staphylococcus were enriched in the MPTP group (Fig. 8E). Boxplots further validated these compositional differences. Collectively, these findings indicate that DBS combined with BCI intervention suppresses potentially harmful taxa while enriching beneficial genera, thereby promoting the re-establishment of gut microbial homeostasis (Fig. 8F).

Discussion

This study integrates analyses from multi-omics public databases, mouse models of PD, and multidimensional intervention strategies to elucidate the disrupted brain-gut-immune crosstalk in PD and to validate the multilayered neuroprotective effects of DBS combined with BCI interventions.

Although the importance of the gut-brain axis in PD has gained increasing recognition, the mechanisms by which microbial metabolites modulate central nervous system function across tissues remain poorly defined [24]. Previous investigations have largely relied on descriptive observations, such as altered bacterial abundances or reduced SCFAs, without establishing integrative multi-omics frameworks linking microbial functional shifts to molecular abnormalities in the brain [25]. To address this gap, we constructed a cross-tissue correlation network from gut microbiota to brain transcriptomes, revealing that microbial metabolites could be linked to PD-relevant pathways through effects on synaptic activity, inflammatory signaling, and neurotrophic pathways.

Transcriptomic profiling demonstrated that DEGs in PD brains were significantly enriched in pathways related to synaptic vesicle trafficking, neurotransmitter release, mitochondrial energy metabolism, and inflammation, underscoring the coexistence of neuronal dysfunction and chronic neuroinflammation in disease pathology. Notably, aberrant expression of genes such as SLC18A2, SLC8A1, and P2RX7 implicates dysregulated dopamine transport, calcium signaling, and cytokine-mediated inflammatory amplification. Concurrently, SCFA-producing genera, including Roseburia, Blautia, and Faecalibacterium, were markedly depleted in PD patients, accompanied by reduced activity in carbon metabolism and butyrate biosynthesis pathways. Cross-tissue correlation analyses further revealed strong associations between these taxa and brain-region genes in critical pathways, suggesting the presence of a microbiota-metabolite-neuron functional axis. It is worth noting that our transcriptomic analysis utilized human cortical data, while the intervention focused on the mouse midbrain. Although region-specific differences exist, fundamental pathological processes in PD, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic impairment, and neuroinflammation, are conserved across brain regions and species. By targeting these shared molecular networks, we aimed to identify systemic therapeutic targets relevant to the generalized neurodegenerative state of PD, rather than region-restricted alterations.

Among microbial metabolites, butyrate emerged as a central mediator. Previous studies have shown that butyrate activates GPR41/GPR43 receptors, thereby modulating epithelial barrier integrity, immune homeostasis, and vagal signaling, ultimately influencing central inflammation and neuroplasticity [26]. In addition, butyrate functions as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor to induce BDNF expression, conferring neuroprotective and regenerative effects [27]. Consistent with these findings, our study identified decreased abundances of butyrate-producing genera such as Roseburia, which correlated with suppressed expression of downstream BDNF-related genes, providing indirect, correlative support consistent with a butyrate-related mechanism of neuronal function.

By establishing a three-layer interaction model spanning gut microbiota, metabolic function, and brain transcriptional signaling, this work extends systems-level understanding of PD pathophysiology and offers a mechanistic rationale for microbiota-targeted interventions. In particular, the identified “metabolism-synapse-inflammation” axis highlights how microbial dysbiosis, via SCFA depletion, may be associated with pro-inflammatory pathway activation and neuronal dysfunction.

In animal models, we evaluated the efficacy of DBS combined with BCI interventions on motor function in PD mice. Behavioral assessments, including CatWalk, rotarod, and open-field tests, consistently demonstrated that the combined intervention produced greater improvements in motor coordination, spontaneous activity, and anxiety-like behavior than DBS alone. These findings highlight a synergistic effect between BCI and DBS, extending beyond conventional approaches focused solely on neural stimulation and opening new avenues for BCI-assisted neuromodulation. Specifically, our system functions as a closed-loop interface that decodes real-time β-band (13–30 Hz) power spectral density from STN LFP signals. Unlike standard adaptive DBS (aDBS) which typically uses simple threshold-based switching, our BCI module incorporates a dynamic decision logic that continuously modulates stimulation frequency (100–130 Hz) proportional to the detected pathological synchrony. The novelty of this study lies not merely in the closed-loop control of motor circuits but in exploring how this precise neural modulation exerts cascading effects via the brain-gut axis to improve peripheral microbiota and immune status. This extension from “central closed-loop modulation” to “systemic remodeling” distinguishes our work from traditional aDBS studies.

Electrophysiological monitoring of LFP revealed pronounced β-band abnormalities in MPTP-treated mice, indicative of enhanced pathological synchrony. Combined treatment markedly suppressed aberrant β power and restored physiological oscillations. HRV analysis further indicated effective restoration of autonomic function. Together, these results underscore that DBS combined with BCI interventions extend beyond motor regulation to encompass multidimensional modulation of neural oscillatory activity and autonomic balance.

At the mechanistic level, we examined the intervention’s effects on αSyn pathology. Immunofluorescence and WB analyses consistently showed that MPTP induction caused substantial accumulation of insoluble αSyn in the midbrain and CP, whereas combined treatment significantly reduced both expression and aggregation. Complementary qPCR and ELISA results demonstrated that DBS + BCI attenuated αSyn burden by downregulating transcription and total protein levels, providing convergent evidence of symptom amelioration.

We also explored central-peripheral interactions contributing to PD progression. In colonic tissues, MPTP mice exhibited barrier disruption (ZO-1/CLDN5 downregulation), pro-inflammatory activation (TNF-α/IL-1β upregulation), and increased M1 macrophage polarization, indicative of systemic inflammatory imbalance. Combined treatment restored epithelial integrity, suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines, and elevated anti-inflammatory IL-10, suggesting that BCI-mediated modulation extends beyond central circuits to include neuro-immune-barrier interactions that alleviate peripheral pathology.

Further 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed that DBS + BCI reshaped gut microbiota composition, enhancing microbial diversity and structure. Beneficial taxa such as Akkermansia and Lactobacillus were enriched, while potentially harmful Desulfovibrio decreased. Improvements in the Shannon diversity index and low-abundance taxa contribution indicated positive impacts on intestinal homeostasis. These convergent results across behavioral, electrophysiological, pathological, and microbial domains reinforce the “gut-brain-immune axis” as a critical framework for intervention.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the integration of human cortical transcriptomics with mouse midbrain DBS interventions involves cross-species and cross-region extrapolation. While we focused on conserved pathways (e.g., inflammation, synaptic function) relevant to general PD pathology, region-specific transcriptional signatures may differ, and findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, the MPTP mouse model represents an acute neurotoxic insult. While it recapitulates key features like dopaminergic loss, it does not fully mimic the chronic, progressive pathology of human PD. Consequently, observed benefits reflect symptom amelioration and acute pathology mitigation rather than long-term disease modification. Third, our multi-omics integration provides correlational insights; causal relationships between microbial shifts and neuroprotection require further mechanistic validation. Fourth, functional analysis relied on PICRUSt2 predictions, which infer potential rather than actual metabolic activity. Future studies integrating metagenomics and metabolomics are needed. Finally, regarding the machine learning model, the high AUC (1.00) suggests potential overfitting due to the sample size relative to feature dimensions, despite our feature selection efforts. Validation in independent, larger cohorts is necessary to confirm the generalizability of the identified microbial biomarkers.

In conclusion, through integrated multi-omics, animal experimentation, and neuromodulation, this study systematically delineates the disrupted gut-brain-immune interplay in PD and demonstrates that DBS combined with BCI intervention exerts broad regulatory effects on motor function, neural synchrony, αSyn aggregation, peripheral inflammation, and microbiota homeostasis. For the first time in PD research, we propose a “metabolism-inflammation-neural” interaction axis, clarifying the gut microbiome’s potential role as a bridge in central neuropathology. This study provides a robust theoretical foundation and potential translational path for advancing mechanistic insights and systemic intervention strategies in PD.
