QueryIPI : Query-agnostic Indirect Prompt Injection on Coding Agents
Yuchong Xie 1 ∗ Zesen Liu 1 ∗ Mingyu Luo 2 Zhixiang Zhang 1 Kaikai Zhang 1 Zongjie Li 1 Ping Chen 2 Shuai Wang 1 Dongdong She 1 † 1 The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology 2 Fudan University

Abstract
Coding agents in modern IDEs orchestrate powerful tools and system-level actions, creating a high-stakes attack surface. Prior works on Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) are mainly query-specific, rendering attacks unstable and with a relatively low success rate. We argue that a query-agnostic attack represents a far more severe threat to real-world LLM coding agents. However, it is fundamentally challenging, as the static query-agonistic payload must compete for the agent’s attention against diverse user query and other contextual prompts. Our key insight is that this challenge can be circumvented by exploiting a common vulnerability of LLM coding agents: the leakage of the agent’s internal prompt. Access to the prompt transforms the attack from an infeasible black-box search into a constrained, white-box optimization problem. Using this, we introduce QueryIPI , the first query-agnostic IPI method for realistic coding agents. QueryIPI uses the tool description as its malicious input channel and employs an iterative, prompt-based search to systematically refine this description. This method leverages the agent’s internal prompt in two key phases: initial seed generation, to create a description aligned with the agent’s role and security guardrails, and iterative reflection, to analyze failures and systematically rewrite the description to bypass those defenses. We evaluate QueryIPI on five simulated realistic coding agents, achieving an average success rate of 70%, 82%, and 87% with 2, 4, and 8 training samples, respectively, while the baseline achieves an average success rate of only 50%. Crucially, we demonstrate that the malicious tool descriptions generated in simulation successfully transfer to and compromise their real-world counterparts, proving that internal-prompt-aware, query-agnostic IPI is a practical and effective threat to contemporary coding agents.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) powered coding agents have been widely deployed in major IDEs, including VS Code Copilot
(
cop 2025
)
, Cursor
(
Cursor
)
, and Windsurf
(
win 2025
)
. Through protocols like the Model Context Protocol (MCP)
(
Anthropic 2024
)
, these agents evolved from simple code generators to powerful code development platforms that can interact with various external tools. This capability to interact with external tools brings coding agents a central and critical position in modern IDEs. With system-level operations and privileged context, coding agents can directly modify code and environments, creating a high-stake attack surface.

Indirect prompt injection (IPI) has been shown to pose a significant security threat to LLM agents
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
wunderwuzzi 2025
;
Debenedetti et al. 2024
;
Greshake et al. 2023
)
, especially the popular coding agents widely used in practice.
Prior IPI research is mainly query-specific, as existing methods bind the attack to specific invoked tools in an ad-hoc fashion. One line of work embeds the attack payload in the invoked tool’s output
(
Debenedetti et al. 2024
;
Zhan et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Zhan et al. 2025
)
. While another line of work embeds the attack payload within the tool’s description
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Labs 2025a
;
Labs 2025b
)
, where the existing attack paradigm is designed to trigger only when the user’s query directs the agent to use the
specific
invoked tool or a related category of functions. In both scenarios, the attack’s success hinges on the user’s query, rendering it unstable and with a relatively low success rate.

We argue that a far more potent and realistic threat model is the query-agnostic attack. By definition, such an attack is designed to
trigger reliably and execute its malicious payload regardless of the user’s specific input
. As visualized in Figure
1
, this transforms the threat from an unstable, low-probability event (left panel) into a stable and highly reproducible attack (right panel). But achieving this level of robustness is fundamentally challenging. The fundamental challenge in achieving robustness stems from the LLM’s attentional bias
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
. For instance, attacks via tool returns are often effective because the model’s attention is naturally focused on processing the tool’s output. However, injecting through a tool description, for example, to establish a connection with a malicious MCP server, is substantially more difficult. Experimental evidence confirms this, showing that prompts designed for tool-return IPI attacks have a near-zero success rate in a tool-description setting
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. When injecting via a tool description, the injected content faces significant interference from the user query, the system prompt (including embedded defenses
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Hines et al. 2024
;
san 2023
;
ins 2023
)
), and other competing tools. As a query-agnostic attack, the injected content cannot leverage the user’s query to direct the model’s focus, making it exceptionally challenging to overcome this divided attention.

We observe that this challenge can be circumvented by a common vulnerability: the leakage of the agent’s internal prompt. These prompts are often exposed through various channels, including direct prompt injection surfaces
(
Xie et al. 2025
;
x1xhlol 2025
)
or via white-box access to open-source agents
(
Team 2025
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
. Once the internal prompt is known, an attacker can analyze the agent’s internal instructions, including its operational logic and prescribed safety guardrails. This knowledge turns the black-box search for an effective attack into a more constrained, white-box optimization problem. Instead of blindly guessing, the attacker can craft a payload that targets explicit weaknesses in the agent’s logic, thereby reducing the search space and increasing the stability and transferability of the attack.

We present
QueryIPI
, the first query-agnostic IPI method designed for realistic coding agents. To achieve this query-agnostic behavior,
QueryIPI
leverages the tool description as its malicious input channel. Our approach employs an iterative, prompt-based search method to systematically refine this malicious description until it reliably triggers the desired payload. This method critically leverages the agent’s internal prompt in two key phases: initial seed generation and iterative reflection. Specifically, during initial seed generation,
QueryIPI
conditions the generation process on the internal prompt to create a seed description that aligns with the agent’s prescribed role, tool-use patterns, and embedded security guardrails. Then, during the reflection phase of each iteration, it explicitly uses the internal prompt to analyze failures and rewrite the description, enabling it to craft reformulations that systematically target and bypass the agent’s internal defenses.

To automate our experiments, we simulated five realistic coding agents: Cursor
(
Cursor
)
, Windsurf
(
win 2025
)
, Cline
(
Team 2025
)
, Copilot
(
cop 2025
)
, and Trae
(
tra 2025
)
. Because our attack does not depend on the output of a tool’s invocation, our evaluation can focus solely on the agent’s initial response to a user query, without requiring actual tool interaction. Our method proves highly effective. On five simulated realistic coding agents,
QueryIPI
achieves an average success rate of 70%, 82%, and 87% with 2, 4, and 8 training samples, respectively, while the baseline achieves an average success rate of only 50%.
Crucially, we also demonstrate that the malicious tool descriptions generated through our simulated environment successfully transfer to and compromise the actual, real-world versions of these coding agents.

In summary, our contributions are:

•

We are the first to define and formalize the query-agnostic IPI attack, a novel threat model for LLM agents.

•

We propose
QueryIPI
, an automated method that leverages the agent’s internal prompt to systematically generate these attacks.

•

We successfully execute our attack on five simulated coding agents and demonstrate its transferability to real-world coding agents, confirming its practical threat.

Related Work – IPI Attack

Indirect Prompt Injection is a significant security threat where an LLM is compromised by malicious instructions embedded in external data sources it consumes
(
Greshake et al. 2023
)
.
While this threat applies broadly to various LLM-integrated applications, our work concentrates on the more complex and autonomous paradigm of LLM agents.

The most common line of work explores IPI against agents through malicious content embedded in tool outputs or retrieved documents.
Several benchmarks have been developed specifically to evaluate LLM agents in this context. AgentDojo
(
Debenedetti et al. 2024
)
and Injecagent
(
Zhan et al. 2024
)
provide environments where an agent’s tools can return poisoned results. The vwa-adv benchmark
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
extends this agent-centric paradigm to multimodal scenarios.
Building on these environments, various attack strategies against agents have been proposed.
AGENTVIGIL
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
introduced a generic black-box red-teaming method, but its success still relies on the agent selecting the compromised tool.
Other works have specialized this attack vector for specific agent domains, such as web agents that process poisoned content (AdvAgent
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
) or agents that handle structured data (StruPhantom
(
Feng and Pan 2025
)
).
Adaptive attacks against agents
(
Zhan et al. 2025
)
also fits this model. A common thread unites all these approaches:
the user must issue a query that compels the agent to invoke the specific tool or retrieve the document containing the payload, thus rendering the attack opportunistic
.

A second line of work injects malicious content into an agent’s tool descriptions, for example, to establish a connection with a malicious MCP server, or other metadata
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Labs 2025a
;
Labs 2025b
)
.
This vector presents a distinct challenge, as research shows that naively porting attack techniques from the tool-output setting yields very low success rates
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. The threat model in these works is query-specific: the attack paradigms are designed to trigger only when the user’s query directs the agent to use the specific compromised tool or a related category of functions. A recent work in this area is ToolHijacker
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
, which also crafts malicious tool descriptions. However, this retrieval-based tool selection mechanism is not applicable to realistic coding agent settings.

Threat Model of Query-Agnostic IPI Attack.
We model an agent,
\mathcal{A}
, as a system defined by its system prompt,
\mathcal{P}_{\text{agent}}
, and a toolset,
\mathcal{T}
, with a specific instance denoted as
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}_{\text{agent}},\mathcal{T}}
. In a benign scenario, this agent takes a user query,
\mathcal{Q}
, as input to produce an intended behavior,
\mathcal{B}^{B}
, and a corresponding response,
\mathcal{R}^{B}
, which we express as
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}_{\text{agent}},\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{Q})=(\mathcal{B}^{B},\mathcal{R}^{B})
. Our threat model considers a query-agnostic attack where an attacker, who has full knowledge of internal prompt, including
\mathcal{P}_{\text{agent}}
and
\mathcal{T}
, and can observe the agent’s outputs
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{R})
, crafts and injects a malicious tool,
t_{mal}
, into the agent’s environment. This action creates a compromised toolset,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}\cup{t_{mal}}
, effectively altering the agent’s instance. The attack is successful if, for any arbitrary user query
\mathcal{Q}
, this compromised agent,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}_{\text{agent}},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
, is manipulated into performing a predetermined malicious action,
a_{\text{mal}}
, as part of its behavior
\mathcal{B}^{T}
. This malicious outcome is represented by the equation
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}_{\text{agent}},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{Q})=(\mathcal{B}^{T},\mathcal{R}^{T})
, where the execution of the payload is triggered regardless of the user’s query. We will discuss the scenario where the attacker does not have complete knowledge of the internal prompt in the

.

4.1
Mutation Function

The Mutation function, central to our iterative procedure in Algorithm
1
, generates new candidate descriptions (
D_{\text{var}}
) from the current best-performing one (
d_{\text{cand}}
). This task is performed by a dedicated "Mutation LLM," which takes the agent’s internal system prompt, the malicious action
a_{\text{mal}}
, the number of variants to generate (
N
), the seed description
d_{\text{cand}}
, and interaction history as input. It outputs a set of new descriptions along with its reasoning for the modifications.

We employ a two-phase prompting strategy for the Mutation LLM. The complete system prompt is presented in
Section

B.1
. The first phase,
Initial Seed Generation
, is executed at the beginning of the optimization process when no interaction history is available.

In this phase, the Mutation LLM is prompted to perform a deep analysis of the agent’s internal prompt, focusing on its decision-making logic, safety constraints, and specified approval-seeking triggers. A crucial secondary objective of this analysis is to identify the internal tools and calling conventions relevant to the malicious action, which ensures that subsequent mutations are more targeted and functionally viable. Based on this analysis, the LLM is tasked with crafting a description that emulates the agent’s native style by matching the tone, vocabulary, and technical specificity of the agent’s existing toolset. This mimicry is a strategic choice designed to make the malicious tool appear as a trusted, internal component, thus reducing its overt maliciousness and increasing the likelihood of bypassing the agent’s initial safety filters.

The second phase,
Reflective Optimization
, is used in subsequent generations. The Mutation LLM is provided with the tool descriptions from the past
X
iterations, their scores, and a sample of
Y
agent responses for each. The specific values for these hyperparameters are detailed in
Section

5
. We deliberately withhold the user queries that produced these responses to force the model to infer general failure modes rather than overfitting to specific queries.

Based on this history, the Mutation LLM addresses two primary failure cases by leveraging its understanding of the agent’s internal prompt to guide the modifications. ❶ If the agent’s responses are irrelevant to the malicious action, the model is prompted to increase the description’s visibility. In addition to applying established strategies, the model also performs a targeted analysis of the agent’s internal prompt. For instance, it might assess the prompt’s complexity and make the tool’s purpose more explicit to ensure it is not overlooked among numerous other instructions. ❷ If the agent considered the tool but refused the action, the model is instructed to analyze the refusal by cross-referencing it with the agent’s internal prompt. This allows it to pinpoint the exact safety rule that was triggered, enabling a targeted refinement of the description to bypass that specific constraint.

4.2
Scoring Function

The Scoring Function is designed to quantitatively assess the extent to which a given malicious tool description succeeds in causing the target agent to perform a predetermined malicious action. For each candidate tool description provided as input, the scoring function first injects it into the target agent. Subsequently, it systematically feeds all user queries from our training dataset to this modified agent and collects the corresponding responses for evaluation.

The evaluation’s core is a specialized "Judge LLM". For each user query, the Judge LLM receives four distinct inputs: the user query itself, the predetermined malicious action, the malicious tool description being evaluated, and the target agent’s response to that query. Based on these inputs, the Judge LLM produces a fine-grained score ranging from 0 to 100. The total score for a given tool description, which serves as the final return value of the Scoring Function, is the sum of the individual scores returned by the Judge LLM across all user queries.

In designing our scoring mechanism, we deliberately moved away from a binary success/failure classification (e.g., 0 or 1) as seen in methodologies like PAIR
(
Chao et al. 2025
)
. Instead, we adopted a more granular, continuous scoring approach, similar in spirit to the one used in TAP
(
Mehrotra et al. 2024
)
, to capture nuanced outcomes. In this task, a score of 0 indicates that the agent’s response for a given user query is completely irrelevant to the predetermined malicious action. Conversely, a score of 100 signifies a successful attack, where the response executes the predetermined malicious action. Intermediate scores represent states where the agent was influenced by the malicious tool description but the action was not fully completed, or where the agent’s defense mechanisms were triggered. To ensure the Judge LLM’s accuracy, we calibrated it using human feedback. Annotators ranked a set of generated agent outputs, and we then adjusted the prompt of Judge LLM to ensure its scoring rank order closely matched these human-provided rankings.

5.1
Experiment Setup

Experiment Target.

Automating experiments on real-world coding agents is challenging due to their reliance on graphical user interfaces (GUIs). Therefore, for our experiments, we construct simulated agents based on the internal prompts, including system prompts and tool definitions, of several real-world coding agents, namely Cursor
(
Cursor
)
, Windsurf
(
win 2025
)
, Cline
(
Team 2025
)
, Copilot
(
cop 2025
)
, and Trae
(
tra 2025
)
, which were leaked in
Xie et al. 2025
. These simulated agents utilize Claude 4 Sonnet as their backend large language model (LLM). This approach ensures our experimental environment closely mirrors that of real-world agents. To validate that our attacks are practically viable beyond this simulated setting, we also conduct transferability experiments on actual coding agents, with results presented in
Section

5.4
. Our attack objective is to induce the agent to execute malicious commands via its command execution tool. We target this tool due to its universality in coding agents and the high-severity risk it poses. Since this tool is often protected by safeguards, it provides a realistic and challenging environment to test our attack’s effectiveness.

Datasets.

We employ two datasets in our evaluation.
➀ The first is the Command Injection Payload List
†
†

http://github.com/payloadbox/command-injection-payload-list
, an open-source collection of command injection payloads.
The commands featured in these payloads are commonly used by attackers for malicious purposes. From this collection, we selected 10 distinct commands to serve as the basis for our malicious payloads.
➁ The second is LMSYS-Chat-1M
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
, a large-scale real-world dialogue dataset which is collected from real-world conversations.
We randomly sample user queries from this dataset for the training and testing phases of our experiments.

Metrics.

We measure the Attack Success Rate (ASR), defined as the percentage of test cases resulting in the successful execution of a malicious command. To calculate this, for each of the 10 malicious command selected, we randomly sample 10 distinct user queries from the LMSYS-Chat-1M dataset, creating a total of 100 unique test cases (10 commands × 10 queries). The ASR is the percentage of successful executions across these 100 total test cases.

Compared Baseline.

We compare our method against TIPExploit
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
, hereafter referred to as TIP, as our primary baseline. TIP demonstrates a systematic exploitation workflow (TEW) on LLM-based agentic systems and operates by using a handcrafted tool description to inject malicious payloads. We focus on its
RCE-1
setting, which injects these payloads solely through the tool description channel. To implement this baseline, we manually adapted the handcrafted descriptions proposed by TIP to suit each of the 10 malicious commands used in our evaluation.

Parameter Settings.

To ensure the stability and reproducibility of our results, we carefully configured the parameters for our experiments. The temperature for both the target coding agent and the judge LLM was set to 0. In contrast, the temperature for the mutator LLM was set to 1 to foster creativity and generate diverse attack tool descriptions. For the hyperparameters of our attack generation algorithm, we set the number of generations to 20. In each generation, the mutator produces two new variants of the attack tool descriptions.

QueryIPI

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
QueryIPI
, the first query-agnostic indirect prompt injection attack on coding agents. By leveraging leaked internal prompts in an iterative search,
QueryIPI
systematically crafts malicious tool descriptions that bypass agent defenses. Our method demonstrates high efficacy in simulated environments and, critically, successfully transfers to compromise real-world coding agents. This work establishes query-agnostic IPI as a practical, deterministic threat, highlighting the severe security risk of exposed internal prompts.

7
Limitations

Our method’s effectiveness is predicated on the availability of a powerful backend LLM for generation and reflection. This introduces a potential limitation, as modern, state-of-the-art LLMs are heavily aligned to refuse requests for generating malicious content. An LLM’s safety guardrails could, in principle, hinder our attack generation process. However, we contend that this is not a fundamental barrier in the current threat landscape. The extensive and successful research into "jailbreaking" techniques
(
Wei et al. 2023
;
Zou et al. 2023
)
has repeatedly demonstrated that LLM safety alignments can be bypassed with sophisticated prompting. An adversary can therefore leverage these publicly known jailbreaking methods to ensure the backend LLM’s cooperation, making our proposed attack practical and realistic despite the presence of safety filters.

8
Ethical considerations

The research in this paper introduces
QueryIPI
, a novel attack method, and thus carries an inherent dual-use risk where malicious actors could adopt our methodology to compromise AI agents. However, we firmly believe that the benefits of responsible disclosure to the defensive community outweigh this risk. Our primary goal is to alert developers and AI safety researchers to this practical and stealthy threat vector, following the principle that understanding offense is crucial for building robust defense. To mitigate harm, we have conducted all experiments in controlled, simulated environments, are committed to sharing our findings with affected vendors prior to widespread publication, and have deliberately avoided releasing ready-to-use attack tools or the specific malicious payloads generated by our system. Our ultimate aim is to catalyze the development of more secure and resilient AI systems, not to equip attackers.

Definition of
\mathcal{D}

We define
\mathcal{D}
as the search space of candidate tool descriptions.
Each element
d\in\mathcal{D}
is a structured tuple:

d=\langle\texttt{tool\_name},\texttt{description},\texttt{args}\rangle,

where
\texttt{tool\_name}\in\Sigma^{*}
is a textual identifier (a sequence of tokens from vocabulary
\Sigma
);

\texttt{description}\in\Sigma^{*}
specifies the natural-language functionality of the tool, which may embed the payload command while maintaining a benign surface form;

\texttt{args}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{m}
denotes an optional list of arguments, with
m
being the maximum number of supported arguments.

Hence, the overall search space can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\big\{\langle\texttt{tool\_name},\texttt{description},\texttt{args}\rangle\;\mid\;

\displaystyle\texttt{tool\_name},\texttt{description}\in\Sigma^{*},\texttt{args}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{m}\big\}.

In practice, we constrain
\mathcal{D}
by initializing seeds with content aligned with the extracted safety mechanisms
\mathcal{S}

and incorporating the payload command
\mathcal{C}_{\text{payload}}
.
