Not just splitting hairs—monitoring environmental chemical exposures, their biological effects and their social determinants with hair shafts and follicles: a narrative review
Authors: Marie Robert, Alba Iglesias-González, Rawan Nour, Ke Zhou, Brice M.R. Appenzeller, Martine Bellanger, Pierre-Alexandre Vidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Humans encounter a wide range of natural and synthetic chemicals throughout their lifespan and these exposures have a complex impact on health outcomes. Epidemiological studies provide compelling evidence linking chemical exposure to neurological disorders and various cancers. However, establishing causality between specific pollutants, or their mixtures, and health outcomes is challenging due to long disease latencies. Functional biomarkers in exposed subjects provide rapid predictions of the impact of environmental exposures. Notably, genome alterations, including DNA breaks and epigenetic modifications, are hallmark features of cancers, premature aging, and neurological diseases. Biomonitoring studies show that individual chemical exposures are specific. These differences highlight disparities across socioeconomic groups. They also present opportunities to associate specific environmental factors and disease development, as well as socioeconomic determinants of exposures and their consequences. This review focuses on biomonitoring environmental chemical exposures and their effects with hair and hair follicles. Pollutants detected in the hair matrix reflect cumulative internal exposures over specific time periods, while biomarkers can be analyzed in plucked hair follicles. These mini organs contain proliferating and stem/progenitor cells, which are critical to understanding the onset of various diseases. We propose an integrated approach that combines socioeconomic indicators, chemical analyses in hair, and biological assessments of matching hair follicles to evaluate the exposome and its effects on the genome. We discuss the state of the art of these approaches, their current limitations, and the perspectives they offer.

Introduction

Environmental chemical exposures play a significant role in shaping health outcomes by contributing to the risks of various diseases. For instance, epidemiological studies have established associations between pesticide exposure and several health conditions [1–3], including cancers in adults [4–9] and children [10–13], neurological disorders [14–18], respiratory diseases [19], endocrine disorders such as thyroid dysfunctions [20, 21] and diabetes [22, 23]. Emerging evidence also suggests a link between pesticide exposure and depression [24]. Beyond their substantial impact on health, environmental chemical exposures impose a large economic burden. Our 2017 study estimated that preventable exposures to neurotoxicants, air pollution, and endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) may account for over 10% of the global gross domestic product (GDP) [25]. The World Bank reported that, in 2019, the estimated monetary cost of health damage from air pollution alone amounted to 6.1% of global GDP [26].

Chemical exposures are diverse and context-dependent. Pesticides, for example, are extensively used worldwide [27, 28]. They include organic and inorganic herbicides, insecticides and fungicides from various chemical classes such as organophosphates (OPs), carbamates, neonicotinoids, pyrethroids, and organochlorines (OCs). Industrial activities further contribute to environmental contamination with dioxins, brominated diphenyl ethers, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), flame retardants (FRs), and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs). Some chemicals are resistant to degradation and persist in soil, air and biota and remain detectable years after being banned in most countries [29]. This highlights the need for a comprehensive approach to monitoring the chemical exposome in human populations rather than limiting epidemiological studies to chemicals currently used [30].

Environmental pollution does not affect all individuals equally; children are particularly susceptible to toxicants due to their unique behaviors, higher surface area to volume ratio, and immature metabolic systems [31]. The ESTEBAN and PestiRiv studies in France highlighted that children exhibit higher exposure levels to various pollutants compared to adults [32, 33]. Furthermore, exposure to pesticides and other pollutants during early life stages may lead to long-term health effects that manifest later in life [3], a concept coined the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) [34, 35].

Socioeconomic status (SES) also plays a critical role in shaping exposure levels and disparities [36]. Lower SES populations often reside in areas with higher environmental pollutant concentrations and may have limited access to resources that mitigate exposure. According to The Lancet Commission on Pollution and Health [37], approximately 92% of the deaths related to pollution occur in low or middle income-countries. The report also highlights that—overall—underserved minorities are more at risk of pollution-related diseases. Conversely, higher SES groups might experience increased exposure to certain pollutants through different pathways, such as dietary choices [38, 39]. These contrasting exposure sources across SES groups highlight the complex relationship between social determinants and environmental health risks.

Biomonitoring is essential to assess individual exposure to environmental pollutants. It consists of measuring the concentration of pollutants and their metabolites in human biological samples such as tissue, blood, urine, and hair [40, 41]. These concentrations reflect exposures from different sources, such as diet, housing, and the occupational environment. As described in the next section, chemical concentration in matrices significantly correlates with exposure levels. Despite the availability of detailed health outcome data for large cohorts [42, 43], evaluating the impact of the chemical exposome on health remains challenging. The long latencies of most diseases complicate association analyses. Moreover, replicating the complex mixtures of pollutants and relevant internal doses in cell culture systems and animal models is difficult, if not impossible. Functional biomarkers in exposed individuals are therefore important as they provide rapid approaches to predict the impact of environmental exposures on health. Classic sources of cells to assess these biomarkers include blood and buccal cells which are terminally differentiated and quiescent. More recently, we and others have established that plucked hair follicles are an interesting source of cells for environmental toxicology studies [44–48]. Plucking hair is less invasive than blood draws, hence more acceptable—especially for children—and cell types in follicles are relevant to assess genomic alterations.

In this narrative review, we present an integrated approach that combines (1) chemical profiling of pollutants in hair, (2) examination of social conditions influencing exposure and (3) genomic analysis of hair follicles, to explore the interplay between context, contamination and biology. This review begins by exploring hair as a unique biomonitoring matrix for chemical exposures, setting the stage for its integration with the biological and social dimensions of the exposome.

Biomonitoring human chemical exposures with hair

Understanding the human chemical exposome for personalized risk assessment requires comprehensive monitoring of individual exposures. This includes capturing mixture effects and improving the assessment of causal links between exposures and health outcomes [30]. This process remains challenging due to the multiplicity of exposure pathways (e.g., water, soil, dust, food, air) and the rapid metabolization and excretion of many pollutants, in particular through urine [2, 49, 50]. In this context, hair analysis enables noninvasive and practical bio-sampling, especially for children and adolescents, to quantify internal exposures to a wide range of pollutants [51–53].

Hair sample collection, storage and analyses

Hair samples are collected by cutting hair strands [54–56]. Hair samples are stable—there is no excretion or metabolization within hair. They can be kept for extended periods of time [54, 56]. Washing steps with water and organic solvents are needed to remove chemicals possibly deposited on hair surface from external exposures [51, 57, 58]. Multi-residue analytical methods have been developed for the detection of multiple biomarkers [51, 53, 59, 60] and it is now possible to quantify in a single sample a broad range of chemicals, including pesticides from different classes and other pollutants such as bisphenols, PFAS, and FRs. We stress that the concentration of chemicals detected in hair corresponds to the chronic exposure of the individual to these pollutants. Hair analysis is representative of the time window corresponding to hair length, based on the consensus that hair grows 1 cm per month [56, 61]. While multiple factors influence individual hair growth rate, including race/ethnicity [62] (up to ~ 30% differences) and age [63] (~ 10% slower growth with age), hair analysis results can nevertheless be contextualized and temporal information such as seasonal effects and exposure habits can be assessed via segmental hair analysis [43].

The first step for chemical analyses in hair consists in matrix de-structuration by alkaline hydrolysis or pulverization, depending on the target chemicals analyzed. This step improves extraction from the matrix, conducted with solvents adapted to the compound’s physicochemical properties. Internal standards are added during this step for chemical quantification by mass spectrometry-based targeted or non-targeted methods.

Validation of hair analysis to document chemical exposures

The major route of incorporation of chemicals into the hair matrix is via the blood. Chemical concentrations in hair therefore reflect cumulative averages of plasma levels, provided that an adequate decontamination protocol is used to remove chemicals adsorbed on hair surface (see below). This has been validated in animal studies [51, 52]. Experiments with rats exposed to cocktails of pesticides found overall good correlations between chemical concentration in hair and both (1) the exposure dose and (2) the chemical concentration in plasma [49, 64]. Hair analysis is particularly valuable for fast-elimination compounds as it gives access to averaged concentration and is therefore not affected by the high variability observed in fluids [65]. In addition, inclusion of pharmacokinetic data to the statistical model improves the precision of concentration predictions [66]. In humans also, good hair-blood correlations are found, e.g., for FRs and pesticide exposures [52].

Hair vs. other matrices

Concentration of chemicals in hair represents the average exposure of an individual over time windows corresponding to the hair length analyzed. As these windows typically cover weeks to months, the concentration of the detected chemicals is not affected by the precise moment of collection. In contrast, body fluids provide snapshots of recent exposures due to the fast clearance of contaminants in these matrices and the repeated exposure on the short-term for some chemicals [49, 65]. Hence, repeated measures of urinary biomarkers tend to have a high variability, which strongly limits the possibility to reliably classify individuals based on urine analysis [50, 65, 67–69]. The type of chemicals detectable in the different matrices differs. In contrast to bodily fluids in which mostly metabolites (urine) or parent compounds (blood) can be detected, hair is suitable for the detection of both parent chemicals and metabolites from various chemical families, including persistent and non-persistent pollutants [40, 51, 52, 70–73]. A greater number of chemicals are therefore typically detected in hair than in urine [49, 74]. There is a clear advantage in being able to detect parent compounds when the goal is to determine sources of exposures because metabolites are often non-specific, i.e., multiple chemicals can be converted into the same metabolite. Hydrophilic contaminants are rapidly eliminated in urine and detoxification systems increase hydrophilicity of non-polar chemicals for their excretion by the renal system. Yet, hair analysis captures both hydrophobic and hydrophilic compounds [74], indicating that many hydrophilic organic pollutants (or their metabolites) do remain long enough in circulation for their incorporation in hairs.

Limitation of hairs for monitoring environmental chemical exposures

Evidently, scalp hair analysis cannot be done with individuals who are bald or have extremely short hair. In specific contexts, participant refusal may stem from cultural hair taboos, as detailed below. In the absence of hair from the head, hair collected from other body parts can be used for the analysis of chemicals. While no data is available for pollutants yet, the use of non-head hairs is often considered for the analysis of various medical and illicit drugs or alcohol metabolites [56, 75].

The risk of external contamination, namely the deposition of chemicals from air or dust on hair surface, has often been mentioned as a limitation to hair analysis, based on the assumption that distinction cannot be made with chemicals incorporated from blood (considered representative of the internal dose). To mitigate this risk, decontamination procedures are usually performed before the extraction step, in order to remove chemicals possibly deposited on hair surface [52, 57]. This approach is now broadly accepted in the field and described in detail in the literature [52, 57, 61, 76, 77]. It is also mentioned in the “Consensus on general recommendations for hair testing” released by the Society of Hair Testing [56]. Moreover, the analysis of metabolites instead of (or in parallel to) the parent compounds can also help to address the possible bias due to external contamination. Indeed, metabolites of chemical pollutants, which represent a significant part of the compounds targeted for analysis from hair extracts, are only produced by biological metabolization. Their detection therefore strongly suggests incorporation from blood and not external deposition [53].

Generally speaking, external deposition of chemicals should not be considered a major bias in exposure assessment, as the presence of chemicals in human environments (air, dust) cannot be disconnected from their transfer to the human body. In other words, if chemicals are sufficiently concentrated in the subject’s direct environment, leading to detectable levels deposited on hair surface, it is extremely likely that the subject was actually exposed to these chemicals, primarily through breathing, and via dermal contact. Alternative approaches based on personal wearable devices such as wristband samplers are increasingly used to assess exposure to volatile of semi-volatile chemicals [45, 78–81]. These samplers collect externally deposited chemicals. Validation work for this approach show that chemicals present in the direct environment are indicative of the level of exposure [81, 82], although exact internal doses cannot be calculated from passive samplers (nor from any biomonitoring method for that matter). To our knowledge, no study has yet combined hair analysis with wristband samplers. This combined approach could help clarify exposure routes: associations between hair and wristband detections would indicate skin absorption and/or inhalation as primary routes, whereas a lack of association would suggest other exposure pathways such as oral exposure via nutrition.

Hair characteristics can influence both the accumulation of pollutants and the analytical processes to quantify them. For example, hair pigmentation influences the accumulation of medical drugs and substances of abuse, likely due to the high affinity of some chemicals for melanin [83–87]. Less is known about the impact of hair pigmentation on the bioaccumulation of organic contaminants. Animal studies suggest that the accumulation of some—but not all—organic contaminants may depend on hair pigmentation [88] and some organic contaminants were found to accumulate more in black than in white human hairs [89]. The effect of pigmentation on hair accumulation is however likely the exception rather than the rule [49, 90, 91]. Washing habits are not considered to affect the concentration of chemicals in hair, but “aggressive” cosmetic treatments (ex: perming, bleaching, dyeing…) need to be considered in the interpretation of the results. The latter is much less relevant for studies focused on children.

Despite some limitations, hair remains an attractive matrix for assessing chronic exposure. Hair analysis has clear advantages when compared with fluids such as urine and blood due to hair stability, ease of sampling, non-invasiveness and the ability to better (albeit not perfectly) describe human chronic chemical exposure. Consequently, hair has gained increasing acceptance in human biomonitoring and epidemiological research. It has been well established as a biomonitoring matrix for over 30 years and has been used for numerous studies, including those by Iglesias-González et al. (2022) [92] and Peng et al. (2021) [93], which investigated exposure to persistent and non-persistent organic chemicals in children and adults, respectively. Having outlined the strengths and weaknesses of hair-based chemical measurement, we next consider the social dimensions of exposure, focusing on how SES shapes both exposure patterns and participation in biomonitoring studies.

Socioeconomic disparities in environmental exposures

Evidence of SES-related disparities in exposure levels

A substantial body of evidence shows that SES significantly influences exposure to environmental chemicals, spanning air pollutants, EDCs, heavy metals, and pesticides. Socially disadvantaged populations are more likely to live in areas burdened by polluted environments and in inadequate housing. Studies have reported poorer indoor air quality and elevated pesticides (often legacy OCs and contemporary pyrethroids) in lower-SES households [94–97]. For example, social housing in Canada tends to have high indoor pesticide use, leading to exposure disparities in low-income communities [98, 99]. Repeated exposures to a vast array of environmental pollutants have also been measured in low-SES communities in the U.S., both in urban and rural settings [100–104]. A recurrent and alarming finding from these studies is that the majority of children in poor rural communities are repeatedly exposed to neurotoxic OPs.

Environmental risk is not exclusive to low-SES groups. Wealthier populations are also exposed to harmful chemicals [105–107]. EDCs—such as PCBs, FR, phthalates, and bisphenol A (BPA)—vary by SES. Among Swedish adolescents, those born to mothers from high-income countries exhibited higher levels of PCBs, FRs, and PFAS compared to those from lower-income countries [107]. Also, pregnant women with higher SES have elevated pesticide levels [38, 39], likely due in part to greater fruit consumption [38]. Accordingly, replacing conventional produces with organic options has been found to reduce pesticide metabolites in women [73, 108].

These SES-dependent exposure patterns highlight the importance of biomonitoring—inclusive of internal dose measurements—to accurately capture differential chemical burdens across populations.

Impact of SES on participation and engagement in hair collection

Overall, hair collection is widely accepted in environmental health studies. In the U.S., about 72% of parents agreed to hair sampling for their children in a tobacco exposure study, with refusals primarily due to lack of time or interest [48]. In Western France, we conducted a survey and found a 76% acceptance rate for hair procuration among parents or guardians of school-aged children.

However, participants with higher SES are consistently overrepresented [73, 109]. In our survey, 68% of respondents held ‘advantaged occupations’ and 45% had at least a master’s degree. A U.S. study found that among 'economically marginalized families’, children were significantly more likely to provide hair samples if they came from households earning ≥ US $20,000 annually [110]. Adapting study designs can reduce this SES participation gap. For example, introducing a peer 'buddy' recruitment system substantially improved inclusion among low SES immigrant population in Belgium’s FLEHS biomonitoring project [111]. Similarly, a U.S. community-partnered study reported that a high proportion (82%) of low-income adults provided hair samples when recruitment was relationship-based, flexible, and emphasized personal benefits [112]. Finally, insights from the DEMOCOPHES project—a pan-European biomonitoring initiative—emphasize the effectiveness of school-based recruitment, culturally tailored communication, consistent interviewer training, and reminder strategies in promoting equity and participation rates to 75% across SES groups [113, 114].

These findings underscore that, although hair collection is widely accepted, participation can be limited by structural and cultural barriers—including mistrust, limited awareness, logistical constraints, and beliefs around hair. In some cultures, hair holds symbolic significance and cutting it may be taboo, reducing consent among specific groups [115]. Without deliberate strategies to address these multifaceted barriers, biomonitoring studies may result in skewed representation and underestimated exposures in vulnerable populations.

Implications for public health Interventions

The WHO Europe’s second assessment on environmental health inequalities highlights a critical data gap: “unequal environmental conditions, risk exposures and related health outcomes affect citizens daily”, yet data disaggregated by SES remain scarce [116]. Without identifying which populations face disproportionate exposures, public health interventions risk missing those most in need—or even widening disparities. Consequently, the WHO now emphasizes disaggregated environmental health monitoring to quantify exposure disparities linked to SES, using indicators such as indoor air quality, pesticide residues, and chemical concentrations in hair samples [117]. In this context, biomonitoring through hair sampling (described above) is a powerful approach. Unlike environmental proxies, hair captures integrated internal exposure over time, enabling measurements of cumulative chemical burdens across SES strata.

To complement monitoring efforts, public policy should prioritize equity while addressing exposures across diverse socioeconomic groups. Regulatory actions—such as restricting indoor pesticide use in social housing [99], enforcing safer lead remediation methods, and mandating non-toxic building materials—can benefit all populations and also help close exposure gaps affecting the most vulnerable. Moreover, community-based interventions that integrate biomonitoring with culturally tailored communication, such as school- or clinic-based hair collection followed by educational support, can improve long-term engagement. These approaches align with participatory frameworks used in health promotion initiatives across diverse settings [80, 118]. Integrating equity-sensitive biomonitoring, community-based approaches, and regulatory action is essential to reduce SES-related exposure disparities in environmental exposures [119].

Hair follicle biomarkers for environmental toxicology

As detailed above, individual environmental chemical exposures are unique, as are their presumed impacts on health. Since chronic diseases linked to pesticides and other pollutants have long latencies and multifactorial etiologies, immediate biological measures are essential to evaluate the true, individualized impact of pollutants. Here, we discuss plucked hair follicles as a source of biological material with attractive properties to evaluate biological effects linked to chemical exposures in diverse SES contexts.

Hair anatomy

Hairs consist of a shaft, the keratinized and visible part of the hair, and the follicle (also called ‘hair root’), deeply inserted in the skin [120–122]. Hair shafts are composed of tightly compacted (dead) cells originating from the follicle. The anatomy of hair follicles is presented in Fig. 1. It has been extensively studied, due to the societal interest in hair loss treatments and the potential of follicular cells in regenerative medicine. Hair follicles are composed of concentric layers of cells from two main structures (the outer and inner root sheaths), which are typically present in plucked hair follicles [123]. The bulb is the bottom part of the follicle. At its base is the hair matrix which interacts with the dermal papilla. The matrix contains undifferentiated keratinocytes which actively proliferate over long periods of time to form the shaft of growing hair, and to produce follicular cell layers [124].

The skin is densely vascularized with blood and lymphatic vessels. The hair papilla surrounds capillaries that provide nutrients to the growing hair but also exposes cells from the bulb to substances in the bloodstream such as drugs, pesticides and other environmental pollutants. These eventually accumulate in the hair shaft [125, 126]. Another network of blood capillaries irrigate the upper part of the follicle, between the sebaceous gland and the skin [127]. Hence, follicular cells distal to the dermal papilla are also expected to come in contact with circulating chemicals. In mice, lymphatic vessels surround the bulge and connect neighboring hair follicles [128, 129]. Depletion of lymphatic vessels blocks the growth of hair follicles [129], indicating that these anatomical structures are important for follicle development and/or cycling, likely in relation to follicular stem cells. It is however unknown if and how lymphatics contribute to chemical exposures of hairs.

Follicular stem cells are essential to the renewal of the follicle at the beginning of each new growth cycle [130]. Importantly, stem cells are present in plucked hair follicles [131, 132]. Hence, hair collection may be a convenient source of cells with stem/progenitor characteristics for population-wide studies. Procuration of stem cells from other sources is comparatively more invasive (e.g., bone marrow aspirates for hematopoietic stem cells, liposuction for adipose stem cells) or restricted to specific contexts (e.g., birth for placenta, loss of deciduous teeth in children for dental pulp). Building on the anatomical insights, we then examine how plucked hair follicles can be used to detect biological effects such as DNA damage and epigenetic alterations arising from environmental exposures.

Feasibility and acceptability of hair follicle procuration

Procuration of hair follicles is simply done by plucking small bundles of 3–5 hairs with a flat tweezer. Follicles are immediately fixed in paraformaldehyde, then can be stored at least 6 months in a phosphate saline buffer [80]. Follicles are taken from the temporal region of the scalp. This site was preferred over eyebrow hairs by community partners [45]. Beyond acceptability, scalp hairs have a longer anagen phase than other terminal hairs. The majority of plucked hairs from the scalp are therefore in the anagen growth phase [122], limiting variability.

Overall, acceptability for this biosampling approach seems high, although consent rates are often not published. Here, we focus on participation of children, a sensitive age group for which data is available. In the PACE5 community-based participatory research study on pesticide exposure among U.S. children, 83% of the participants agreed to hair follicle collection [48]. In contrast, the ongoing PESTIFOL study in western France which combines analyses of hair and hair follicles from schoolchildren has a lower participation rate of about 50%. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in study design and outreach methods. Specifically, PACE5 leveraged community partners to facilitate enrollment, whereas PESTIFOL relied primarily on general advertisements distributed through schools, which may have been less effective in engaging families.

Detection of DNA damage in hair follicles

DNA damage is a pathological characteristic of neurological diseases [133–135] and a hallmark of cancers [136, 137]. The types of DNA lesions depend on the genomic insults. Among these, DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are particularly deleterious. DSB repair syndromes are characterized by neurodevelopmental defects [138] and error-prone repair of DSBs can lead to chromosomal translocations driving certain types of cancers such as hematologic malignancies, which are associated with pesticides exposures [4–6, 10–12, 139]. The genotoxic effects of environmental chemical exposures can be assessed by measuring DNA damage in the exposed population [140–150].

Exfoliated buccal cells and peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) are often used in environmental toxicology to evaluate genotoxicity [150–153]. One limitation of PBLs and buccal samples is that the majority of cells are terminally differentiated. In contrast, the bulb of anagen hair follicles is a site of intense cell proliferation, making this mini-organ a valuable source of cells to study DNA lesions. Proliferating cells are particularly vulnerable to DNA damage which can interfere with DNA replication. Diverse types of DNA lesions, including DNA adducts, DNA cross-links, oxidative lesions, and single-strand breaks can indeed be converted into highly toxic DSBs in the replicative process [154–156]. Accordingly, there is a long tradition of studying radiation responses with hair follicles in animal models [157, 158].

Only very few studies have quantified DNA damage in human hair follicles (Table 1). In a clinical trial with patients treated with Olaparib, a poly(adenosine diphosphate [ADP]–ribose) polymerase inhibitor, the amount of DSBs was evaluated in plucked eyebrows by staining follicles for phosphorylated H2AX (γH2AX) [159]. The number of γH2AX damage foci strongly increased shortly after initiation of the treatment. Bio-dosimetry of ionizing radiation exposures has also been validated with human and animal hair samples [44–46, 160]. Redon et al. [160] compared plucked follicles and PBLs measurements after full-body irradiation in non-human primates. While radiation-induced damage rapidly decreased post-irradiation in PBLs, DSB foci remained stable longer in follicles. This difference was interpreted as differential repair rates or lower clearance of heavily damaged cells in hairs than in blood. A relative stability of DSB indicators in follicles is an advantage to monitor genotoxic effects.

Biological effects of radiofrequency radiation (RFR) emitted from mobile phones were also evaluated with hair follicles from human volunteers [161]. DNA damage levels, measured by comet assay, increased with inferred RFR exposure, supporting the view that RFRs may induce genetic damage [166]. This study illustrates the potential of follicular cells to study dimensions of the exposome beyond chemical pollutants.

Hair follicle culture protocols are well established [167]. Using cultures of hair follicles derived from skin punch biopsies, the group of Ralf Paus showed clear effects of ionizing radiation and chemotherapy on follicular cells, leading to reduced proliferation, increased apoptosis and elevated levels of oxidative DNA damage [162, 163]. The approach is very powerful and practical when residual skin tissue is available (e.g., following cosmetic surgery procedures). It would however be challenging to implement for larger cohorts of participants in non-clinical settings, such as children.

The PACE5 study focused on pesticide exposures in children. It combined exposure assessment with silicone wristbands and cholinesterase assays, and DSB measurements in plucked hair follicles [45, 80]. Localization of the 53BP1 DNA repair factor was quantified. This protein forms distinct microscopic foci at break sites [168, 169] (Fig. 2A), similar to (and largely overlapping with) γH2AX and other repair proteins. In the absence of DNA damage, cell nuclei have pan-nuclear 53BP1 signals, enabling a precise quantification of cells without damage. Study participants from farmworker households had significantly higher levels of follicular DSBs than urban, non-farmworker, participants. Moreover, children for whom OPs were detected in the wristband had an average 30% higher DSB count compared to children without OP detection. Acetylcholinesterase depression, indicative of OP exposure, correlated with follicular DNA damage. The results point to a link between OP exposure and DSB accumulation in epithelial cells, consistent with the known DNA-damaging effects of these insecticides [109, 170–173].

Since the original publication, we have quantified DNA damage in the sheath region of the follicles and found the same difference between groups (Fig. 2B-D), as well as a significant correlation between sites of measure (Fig. 2D). This is important because, while the majority of plucked follicles were cellularized in the sheath region, a subset (corresponding to follicles breaking above the papilla) had no bulb.

A notable limitation of the PACE5 study, along with several other studies investigating the association between environmental pollution and DNA damage, is the insufficient consideration of SES. In PACE5, the participants had relatively homogeneous SES, limiting our ability to associate SES indicators to exposures and biological effects. Other studies often lack comprehensive SES information. However, emerging evidence shows that SES is a determinant of DNA damage in children. For instance, the ORGANIKO children’s health study found that oxidative DNA damage was associated with pyrethroid exposures. Parental education–a proxy for SES–was a good predictor of urinary pyrethroid levels [109]. Similarly, results from the PROMESA children cohort suggest that DNA damage in children is influenced by SES, among other components of the exposome [174].

Epigenetic analyses

Epigenetic changes, including DNA methylation and histone modifications can lead to the misregulation of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, thereby promoting cancer initiation [175]. These epigenetic defects may underlie windows of susceptibility for cancers and other diseases during fetal development, childhood and adolescence, whereby “early life environment has widespread consequences for later health” [176]. Growing evidence, mostly from preclinical studies, points towards epigenetic changes mediated by environmental exposures, including chemicals, radiation, lifestyle factors like alcohol consumption, nutrition and smoking, and SES [177, 178]. As such, studying the impact of the environmental chemical exposures on the epigenome is a priority. In humans, most of epigenetic studies were conducted on epidemiological cohorts using blood [178, 179] or urine samples [180]. While, to our knowledge, no epigenetic analysis has been performed with human hair follicles in the context of environmental toxicology, DNA methylation and histone modifications can be quantified in plucked follicles (Table 1), revealing age-dependent changes in DNA methylation in humans [47] and cattle [181], and stress-related effects [165]. Epigenetic analyses from hair follicles have also been used with infants, showing that the maternal nutrition status during pregnancy influences the epigenome of their children [164].

Epigenetic changes have profound effects on gene expression and Kudlova et al. have shown with a mouse model that gene expression analysis can easily be quantified with plucked hair follicles [46]. Plucked human hair follicles have also been validated as a non-invasive source of tissue enabling highly reproducible transcriptomic analyses [182, 183].

Having covered the feasibility and relevance of hair follicle biomarkers, we conclude by integrating chemical, biological, and social measures—outlining a future research framework that supports equitable and mechanistic exposome studies.

Integrative perspectives and future directions

Chemical exposome in hairs and biological effects in follicles

Hair follicles are surrogate tissues for measuring biological effects of human exposures, such as DNA damage and epigenetic alterations. Combining biomonitoring of pesticides and other pollutants in hair shafts, and biological outcomes in follicles—two minimally invasive procedures—could be very informative. This combined approach anchors exposure-effect pathways within a single biological matrix and across diverse populations to deepen mechanistic understanding.

While temporality of the chemical and biological readouts is distinct, with weeks/months of organic compound accumulation in hair vs. snapshots of biological data at the time of collection in follicles, it is important to consider that chemical hair analysis captures chronic internal exposures to pollutants. In most cases, exposure to pollutants via the lived environment and diet, among other sources, remains stable over short periods of time. Additionally, some biological effects such as epigenetic changes tend to be long-lasting if not permanent. We therefore anticipate that meaningful knowledge can be derived from this orthogonal approach.

Multiple environmental pollutants including pesticides, flame retardants, phthalates, and PFAS are known or suspected to be endocrine disruptors [184]. These compounds can be measured in hair [51, 53, 185]. Interestingly, hair follicles are responsive to various hormones, including estrogens and androgens [186, 187]. Combining chemical analyses with hormone receptor binding and signaling assays in follicles could be used to further our understanding of endocrine disruptors in physiological contexts.

Environmental damage to stem cells

An attractive perspective is to perform targeted biological analyses specifically in stem cells from hair follicles. Through multiplex analyses including human follicular stem cell markers, specific genetic alterations can, in principle, be quantified in the stem/progenitor population.

Stem cells of the skin and other tissues can accumulate mutations that will in turn lead to uncontrolled and niche-independent growth. This phenomenon happens during tissue regeneration or in response to viral infections, radiation, and metabolic changes [188, 189]. Since stem cells have unlimited growth potential, it is thought that they require only few changes to acquire a malignant phenotype [190, 191]. Dysfunctional stem cells have indeed been proposed to be at the origin of multiple malignancies and accelerated aging [192]. It is therefore important to understand which types of (environmental) insults lead to (epi)genetic alterations in stem cells. Our current understanding of how follicular stem cells respond to DNA damaging agents is incomplete, yet it is clear that these cells have a DNA damage response that is distinct from non-stem cells. For instance, murine hair bulge stem cells have an increased resistance to cell death induced by DNA damage thanks to enhanced non-homologous end-joining activity [193]. This mechanism of repair is error-prone, allowing accumulation of mutations over the long term.

Generalizability of biological measurements in hair follicles

An important open question is to what extent DNA damage levels and other (epi)genetic alterations in hair follicles represent systemic effects. This question of generalizability can actually be extended to other biosampling approaches based on exfoliated buccal cells, blood, etc. While the hallmark study by Fong et al. [159] clearly showed that systemic exposure to a DNA damaging agent causes DNA damage in hair follicles, additional studies comparing biological readouts in follicular cells and in other organs are needed. In the context of primary cancer prevention, direct comparisons between hair follicles and other epithelial tissues are granted and we anticipate an important knowledge transfer from clinical studies using follicles as tissue surrogates to benefit environmental toxicology. Animal studies with controlled systemic administration of pollutants—including genotoxic agents and endocrine disruptors with known epigenetic effects—will also help clarify the level of correspondence between (epi)genetic changes in hair follicles and other tissues.

Social-chemical-biological interactions in the exposome

Environmental exposures are “socially determined” [194]. Further research is needed to examine how these exposures vary across different SES at both micro (i.e., individual and household), and macro (i.e., community and area) levels [195, 196]. Integrating social, chemical, and biological data can directly identify key health determinants, some of which are modifiable through lifestyle changes or exposure reduction. Recognizing these factors supports the identification of preventable risk factors and protective measures, particularly in vulnerable populations such as children [25]. A deeper understanding of the social to chemical exposome, viewed through the lens of the social determinants of health, can inform public policy and guide the development of effective interventions. However, most environmental epidemiological studies have yet to fully integrate social factors as “overarching determinants of health” [197–199]. To address this gap, our ongoing PESTIFOL study [200] investigates SES-mediated disparities in environmental exposures and their biological consequences, using the integrative approach outlined above (Fig. 3).

Understanding health trajectories requires exploring how social environments, chemical exposures, and internal biological responses intertwine over time. The Human Early-Life Exposome (HELIX) cohort [201], which followed mother-child pairs from six European cohorts, has linked prenatal and childhood exposures—including pollutants, air quality, and social stressors—to molecular signatures from multi-omics analyses [202]. The multi-layer omics approach included profiling of DNA methylation, gene expression, and serum metabolites. This study revealed over a thousand strong exposome–omics profiles associations, clearly demonstrating that social contexts shape both chemical burden and downstream biological effects.

Drawing upon this paradigm, the ongoing PESTIFOL study, targeting approximately 150 schoolchildren, focuses on the social–chemical–biological interplay (Fig. 3). While smaller in scale, PESTIFOL’s depth allows us to directly connect individual SES (e.g., parental education, occupation, household characteristics) and neighborhood SES [195, 196], with internalized pollutant measurements in hair, and with biological effects observed in matching hair follicles. The following analytic framework can be applied to studies with comprehensive exposome assessment such as PESTIFOL: (1) a validation of the constructs of the exposome model—specifically the social and chemical factors—using structural equation modeling (SEM) [203]. This approach enables to include chemical exposures as latent variables, previously identified from a principal component analysis (PCA) of chemical concentrations, and social exposure as another latent variable derived from multiple SES indicators. Both latent variables are tested as predictors of biological outcomes (e.g., DNA damage) with direct and indirect mediating effects. All regression models estimated simultaneously within the SEM analysis are adjusted for confounders. (2) With the model constructs validated in the SEM, the decomposition of the total effect of SES into natural direct effects through chemical exposures and natural indirect effects can be done via mediation analysis by inverse odds ratio weighting. (3) Finally, the DNA damage risk attributable to SES, specifically the proportion of DNA damage mediated through individual chemical substances and mixtures can be estimated [204, 205]. Ultimately, the goal is to map the pathway Social environment → Chemical exposure → Molecular changes in follicular cells, to advance exposome science and to inform more equitable public health strategies [197–199].

Conclusion

Combining minimally invasive hair-based biomonitoring with biological readouts from hair follicles and detailed socioeconomic and environmental data offers a powerful approach to characterize the exposome. Embedding these tools within cohorts—with attention to biologically sensitive endpoints—can strengthen our understanding of how social and environmental factors interact to influence health. This integrated framework will help develop targeted prevention strategies and improve the relevance of environmental research for population at increased risk, such as children.
