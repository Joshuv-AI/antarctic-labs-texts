When Direct LDL Cholesterol Assays Fail: A Case Series of Analytically Implausible Results
Authors: Sanghyeun Park, Joonsang Yu, Sollip Kim, Woochang Lee, Sail Chun
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) is a key biomarker for cardiovascular risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making. Therefore, the analytical integrity of LDL-C measurements is essential for accurate clinical interpretation [1]. Direct homogeneous LDL-C assays are widely implemented because they are convenient, automated, and independent of triglyceride-based calculations [2]. However, their analytical performance can be compromised in the presence of abnormal or disease-related lipoprotein profiles [3, 4]. The frequency of such profiles in routine practice, as well as the optimal laboratory response, remains insufficiently defined.

We investigated 16 representative cases of analytically implausible direct LDL-C results identified in our laboratory over the past 2 yrs (December 2023–October 2025). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Korea (2025-1439), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data. In our institution, lipid profiles are measured using dedicated reagents for the LDL-C assay on either the Cobas 8000 platform (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), primarily for inpatient testing, or the AU5800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), mainly for outpatient testing. Across these platforms, unexpected patterns occurred in approximately 1% of nearly 400,000 lipid profile tests performed over 1 yr (unpublished data). Analytically implausible results were defined as discordant lipid fractions that violated established stoichiometric relationships (e.g., LDL-C plus HDL cholesterol or even LDL-C alone exceeding total cholesterol), as well as abnormal reaction-curve patterns associated with implausibly low or immeasurable LDL-C values. When instrument-assigned or laboratory information system-generated flags were triggered, specimens were reanalyzed on the alternative platform to determine whether the anomalous findings would resolve on a different analytical system. Although both platforms employ homogeneous enzymatic LDL-C assays, their reaction mechanisms differ substantially: the Cobas method suppresses non-LDL lipoproteins using selective surfactants, whereas the AU5800 removes non-LDL cholesterol through a preliminary enzymatic consumption step before quantifying LDL-C in a secondary reaction. Dilution studies were additionally performed to assess the concentration-dependent nonlinearity or interference. The clinical information for each case was reviewed, and the final LDL-C interpretations were determined by the attending clinical pathologist based on integrated laboratory and clinical findings. For the available cases, LDL subfraction analysis (Quantimetrix, Redondo Beach, CA, USA) was performed to characterize the underlying dyslipidemia pattern.

Most patients had hepatobiliary or pancreatic disorders, predominantly cholestatic conditions, such as alcoholic hepatitis, biliary obstruction, or associated cancers. These individuals consistently had markedly elevated alkaline phosphatase, γ-glutamyltransferase, and total bilirubin levels. Across all cases, LDL-C values derived from the Cobas 8000 were substantially lower than those from the AU5800, with a mean inter-assay difference of –304.7±194.7 mg/dL. However, LDL-C was not measured using a reference measurement procedure in this routine clinical setting, precluding the determination of the platform that produced results closer to the true LDL-C concentration. Dilutional repeat testing reproduced the initial results, suggesting assay susceptibility to abnormal lipoproteins rather than nonspecific inhibitory effects [3, 5]. LDL subfraction analysis, available for the six cases, consistently revealed cholestatic patterns with markedly increased very-low-density and intermediate-density lipoprotein fractions, broad or blunted LDL distribution, and relative suppression of normal LDL peaks (Fig. 1). Four patients who underwent follow-up lipid testing after clinical improvement showed resolution of analyzer flags, with LDL-C values measured on the AU5800 no longer being analytically implausible (Table 1).

The analytically implausible LDL-C patterns observed here align with cholestatic dyslipidemia, in which lipoprotein-X (Lp-X)—a phospholipid- and free-cholesterol-rich, apolipoprotein B-negative particle—reportedly interferes with homogeneous assays by mimicking LDL or evading assay-selective steps [5–7]. Nevertheless, as we did not directly measure Lp-X in this case series, its involvement remains putative. Other particles, such as remnant-rich lipoproteins or paraprotein-associated complexes, may similarly compromise assay selectivity and generate platform-dependent LDL-C values [5–8].

The pronounced platform discordance we observed is supported by recent Korean standardization data showing that LDL-C exhibits substantial method-dependent non-selectivity when sample composition deviates from normative assumptions, particularly in the presence of triglyceride-rich or structurally abnormal lipoproteins [9, 10]. These observations indicate that the discrepancies in our cases are attributable to matrix effects inherent to homogeneous LDL-C assays.

Given these vulnerabilities, when direct LDL-C assays fail and yield analytically implausible values, laboratories can enhance result reliability by taking several practical verification steps, such as examining the reaction curve, assessing potential interferences or preanalytical issues, and comparing results from an alternative platform when feasible. Reviewing the patient’s clinical context and, when appropriate, performing LDL subfraction analysis may help elucidate the underlying cause of the assay failure. Follow-up testing after clinical condition improvement can be informative in determining whether the analytic anomaly resolves in parallel, providing further insight into the nature of the interference.
