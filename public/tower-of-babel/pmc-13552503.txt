DPAS-Graph: adaptive spatial-feature relation learning for spatial RNA-to-protein prediction and virtual protein profiling
Authors: Mingyuan Xu, Zhixin Dong, Bisheng Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaired spatial multi-omics provides a supervised basis for learning RNA–protein correspondence in situ, but predicting protein abundance from spatial transcriptomic data alone remains challenging across tissue contexts and protein panels. Here, we present DPAS-Graph, an adaptive relation-learning framework for spatial RNA-to-protein prediction. Rather than directly merging spatial proximity and transcriptomic similarity as fixed graph priors, DPAS-Graph represents them as two relation channels on a shared edge support and updates their contributions during representation learning for protein prediction. Its Niche-Coupled Field Encoder combines layer-wise edge-relation modeling, intra-branch relation refinement, and cross-branch residual correction to learn spot representations for protein abundance prediction. In a leave-one-dataset-out benchmark across seven paired spatial multi-omics datasets, DPAS-Graph achieved lower aggregate prediction errors and improved spot-level agreement of protein expression profiles, with gains mainly reflected in error-based metrics and PCC-Spot. Spatial autocorrelation and protein-derived domain agreement analyses were further used to characterize the spatial behavior of the predicted protein maps. When applied to external RNA-only spatial sections, DPAS-Graph generated qualitatively interpretable marker-level virtual protein maps, illustrating its use as a complementary tool for protein-level interpretation of transcriptomics-only spatial data.

Introduction

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) measures local RNA expression while preserving tissue spatial context, thereby supporting the study of tissue architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and local microenvironments [1–3]. However, RNA abundance alone does not fully characterize cellular functional states, because messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels are only partially coupled and are shaped by multiple layers of posttranscriptional and posttranslational regulation [4–7]. In contrast, antibody-based protein markers, including antibody-derived tag readouts, often provide more direct information about cell phenotype, immune state, and selected signaling activities [8–10]. Recent advances in spatial multi-omics, including spatially resolved joint RNA–protein profiling technologies such as spatial CITE-seq and spatial molecular imaging, provide paired RNA and protein measurements for learning the correspondence between RNA expression and protein abundance in tissue context [11–13]. Nevertheless, such experiments remain constrained by cost, throughput, and accessibility, which limits their broad application across tissues, cohorts, and marker panels [14, 15]. Therefore, predicting protein abundance from spatial transcriptomic data constitutes a well-defined computational task in spatial multi-omics analysis. This task can support completion of protein measurements in paired spatial multi-omics datasets and provide a complementary route for marker-level protein interpretation in RNA-only spatial transcriptomic data.

Existing work relevant to this task can be grouped into two lines: cross-modal protein prediction and spatial relational modeling. On the one hand, paired single-cell multi-omics methods learn RNA–protein correspondence through joint modeling, prediction, or imputation. Representative methods include TotalVI, cTP-net, sciPENN, and scLinear [16–19]. These methods show that paired RNA–protein measurements can support cross-modal representation learning, protein abundance prediction, and missing protein imputation. However, because they are mainly designed for single-cell or nonspatial paired data, spatial neighborhood structure is usually not part of their core modeling assumptions. On the other hand, many spatial omics methods model neighborhood relationships between spots or cells by incorporating spatial proximity, expression similarity, or graph propagation mechanisms into spatial representation learning and spatial multi-omics prediction [20–26]. Recent work has also explored dynamic graph-attention mechanisms for spatial-context-aware transcriptomic representation learning [27]. Among these methods, DGAT and STProtein provide relevant references for protein prediction in spatial settings [28, 29].

In spatial RNA-to-protein prediction, RNA expression and spatial coordinates provide two sources of inter-spot relationships. Neighborhoods defined by spatial coordinates reflect local tissue context among physically adjacent spots, whereas similarity relationships defined by RNA expression capture molecular resemblance between spots with similar transcriptomic states [20–23]. At tissue boundaries, in mixed-cellular regions or within heterogeneous microenvironments, these relationships may not contribute equally to local information propagation. Therefore, representing spatial proximity and RNA expression similarity through a single fixed relation in advance may limit the model’s ability to adjust their propagation effects across representation layers. Based on this consideration, we represent spatial proximity and RNA expression similarity as two relation-weight channels on a shared edge support, and focus on how these channels can be layer-wise reweighted, intra-branch refined, and cross-branch coordinated under the protein prediction objective.

Accordingly, we propose DPAS-Graph, short for Dual-graph Protein prediction with Adaptive Spatial-feature learning, an adaptive relation-learning framework for spatial RNA-to-protein prediction. DPAS-Graph treats spots as nodes and represents spatial proximity and transcriptomic similarity as two relation-weight channels on a shared edge support, corresponding, respectively, to tissue-coordinate-defined proximity and RNA-expression-derived similarity. Rather than representing these relationships as a single fixed graph relation in advance, DPAS-Graph adaptively adjusts the propagation effects of the two relation channels during multilayer representation learning and coordinates their contributions to spot representation updates. In this way, the model learns spot representations optimized for protein abundance prediction. During training, DPAS-Graph uses protein abundance prediction as the primary task and introduces an auxiliary RNA reconstruction branch as a regularizer tied to RNA-expression structure.

Materials and methods

DPAS-Graph is designed for cross-dataset spatial RNA-to-protein prediction. As illustrated in Fig. 1, its workflow consists of shared edge support construction, relation-coordinated encoding, and protein abundance prediction. For each spatial section, the model takes spot-level RNA expression and spatial coordinates as input. Spatial coordinates define physical-proximity candidate edges, whereas RNA expression defines transcriptomic-similarity candidate edges. Edges from the two sources are unified into a shared edge support, with each shared edge assigned an initial spatial relation weight and an initial feature-similarity relation weight. These node features, edges, and relation weights are passed to the Niche-Coupled Field Encoder, which updates spot representations through multilayer propagation, recalibrates the effective weights of the two relation channels, and coordinates messages from the spatial and feature-similarity branches. The resulting spot representations are used to predict protein abundance, with an auxiliary RNA reconstruction branch introduced during training as a regularizer associated with RNA-expression structure.

Overall framework of DPAS-Graph, in which, for each spatial section, spot-level RNA expression and spatial coordinates define transcriptomic-similarity and physical-proximity candidate relations, respectively; the candidate edges from the two sources are unified into a shared edge support, with each shared edge carrying an initial feature-similarity relation weight and an initial spatial-proximity relation weight; the Niche-Coupled Field Encoder updates and coordinates the two relation channels to learn spot representations for protein abundance prediction; and the encoded representations are then passed to a protein abundance head for the main RNA-to-protein prediction task and an mRNA reconstruction head for auxiliary reconstruction, while paired protein measurements are used as supervision during training.

Benchmark evaluation procedure and evaluation metrics

We evaluated DPAS-Graph on seven paired spatial multi-omics datasets under a leave-one-dataset-out setting. In each fold, one dataset was used as the held-out test section, and the remaining datasets were used for training. During training, the model used RNA expression, spatial coordinates, and paired protein measurements from the training sections. During testing, the model received only the RNA expression matrix and spatial coordinate matrix of the held-out section, whereas the paired protein matrix was used only for evaluation. Details of the dataset sources, preprocessing, RNA feature harmonization, protein target-panel alignment, and the LoDO split and repetition protocol are provided in Supplementary Material S1, whereas graph-construction details are provided in Supplementary Material S2.

To ensure consistent comparison, DPAS-Graph and all baseline methods used the same data splits, RNA input space, protein prediction targets, and evaluation protocol. External RNA-only spatial sections did not contain paired protein measurements and were therefore used only for virtual protein map visualization, rather than being included in the quantitative benchmark.

Model performance was first evaluated using protein prediction error and correlation metrics, including RMSE-Global, MAE-Global, PCC-Pro, PCC-Spot, RMSE-Pro, and SP-Pro. RMSE-Global and MAE-Global measure overall prediction error across all spots and proteins. RMSE-Pro measures the average protein-wise prediction error. PCC-Pro and SP-Pro measure protein-wise Pearson and Spearman correlations between predicted and measured spatial abundance profiles, respectively. PCC-Spot measures, for each spot, the correlation between the predicted and measured protein expression profiles across the target protein panel. To complement global error and correlation metrics, which do not explicitly assess spatial structure, we further computed Moran’s I, Geary’s C, residual Moran’s I, and protein-derived domain-level agreement metrics as spatial diagnostic endpoints. These metrics were used to characterize spatial autocorrelation, residual spatial structure, and agreement between predicted-protein-derived and measured-protein-derived domains. Detailed definitions, computation procedures, and complete results are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Application to external transcriptomics-only spatial sections

To examine whether DPAS-Graph can be transferred to RNA-only spatial transcriptomic data, we further applied it to two external spatial sections, including one human lung cancer section (Human Lung Cancer) and one human tonsil section (esvq52_nluss5) [32, 33]. Because neither section contains paired protein measurements, this part does not provide quantitative validation at the protein level, but instead provides a qualitative application demonstration of the virtual protein maps generated by the model on external spatial transcriptomic data [4–6]. Details of the external dataset sources, preprocessing procedures, and projection onto the benchmark RNA feature space are provided in Supplementary Sections S1.2 and S1.3.

As shown in Fig. 5a, the virtual protein maps generated by DPAS-Graph in the Human Lung Cancer section first show clear regional differentiation rather than nearly uniform outputs. For KRT5, the predicted protein signal preserves the broad high- and low-signal partition suggested by its cognate RNA across the main tissue area, indicating that the model can capture spatial variation related to epithelial/basal-like states in an external tumor section [34]. The predicted ACTA2 map, by contrast, exhibits a more continuous structural pattern that is consistent with the coarse tissue compartmentation in this section [35]. By comparison, MS4A1 and CD3E do not constitute the dominant signals in this lung cancer slice, but their predicted maps still retain spatial heterogeneity instead of collapsing into an unstructured background. Taken together, these results suggest that DPAS-Graph can generate virtual protein landscapes with recognizable regional structure and biological relevance on an external RNA-only lung cancer section.

As shown in Fig. 5b, the virtual protein maps generated by DPAS-Graph in the Human Tonsil Section display a more typical form of immune-associated spatial heterogeneity. In particular, the predicted MS4A1 and CD3E maps both show clear spatial differentiation, but their distributions do not simply overlap, instead suggesting an organized arrangement of distinct immune-related regions within the section [36, 37]. Discussion and conclusion

Paired spatial multi-omics provides direct supervision for learning RNA–protein correspondence in tissue context, yet cross-dataset RNA-to-protein prediction remains challenging because protein abundance reflects transcriptomic state, local tissue context, and dataset-specific measurement conditions [6, 11, 15]. In this study, we developed DPAS-Graph as an adaptive relation-learning framework for spatial RNA-to-protein prediction. Across a leave-one-dataset-out benchmark comprising seven paired spatial multi-omics datasets, DPAS-Graph achieved favorable aggregate performance in global prediction error, protein-wise prediction error, and spot-level protein-profile concordance. These findings support the utility of coordinating spatial proximity and RNA-expression similarity during representation learning for protein abundance prediction from spatial transcriptomic inputs.

The ablation results suggest that the predictive benefit of DPAS-Graph depends not only on which inter-spot relations are included, but also on how their contributions are updated and coordinated. Spatial coordinates and RNA expression provide physical-proximity and transcriptomic-similarity relations, respectively, whereas the methodological contribution of DPAS-Graph lies in placing these relations within a unified representation-learning process and adaptively regulating their propagation through layer-wise reweighting, intra-branch refinement, and cross-branch correction. The lower performance of the single-channel, static dual-channel, and mechanism-ablated variants is consistent with this interpretation (Table 4). Model rankings nevertheless varied across evaluation dimensions. RMSE and MAE quantify numerical prediction error, PCC-Spot measures spot-level protein-profile concordance, and PCC-Pro and SP-Pro evaluate protein-wise Pearson and Spearman correlations across spatial locations. This metric dependence explains why DPAS-Graph performed more favorably on error-based metrics and PCC-Spot, whereas TotalVI achieved higher PCC-Pro and SP-Pro. Complementary analyses based on Moran’s I, Geary’s C, residual Moran’s I, and protein-derived domain agreement further characterized the spatial statistical behavior of the predicted protein maps (Supplementary Material S4). Together, these analyses show that model performance is metric dependent and that spatial fidelity cannot be inferred from global prediction accuracy alone.

Beyond benchmark evaluation on paired datasets, DPAS-Graph provides a route for extending RNA-to-protein prediction to RNA-only ST sections. This setting is practically relevant because many spatial transcriptomic datasets contain spatially resolved RNA measurements but lack paired protein readouts, partly owing to differences in platform availability, assay complexity, and experimental cost [1, 14, 15]. By transferring the learned RNA–protein correspondence to such sections, DPAS-Graph can generate marker-level virtual protein maps that complement transcriptome-level interpretation and support hypothesis generation for subsequent experimental validation or region-focused analysis. Because the external sections evaluated in this study do not contain paired protein measurements, these results should be viewed as a qualitative application demonstration rather than an independent assessment of prediction accuracy. To support traceability and reproducibility, the corresponding dataset identifiers, acquisition sources, preprocessing procedures, and inference workflow are documented in Supplementary Material S1 and the Data and code availability section.

Applying DPAS-Graph across datasets from different sources requires consideration of variation in the RNA feature space, protein target panel, and spatial representation. In the current benchmark, harmonizing gene identities, feature order, and expression scale was necessary to establish a common RNA input space for cross-dataset training and comparison; however, gene filtering, expression normalization, and shared-gene harmonization can alter transcriptomic distances between spots and consequently affect the feature-side neighbor set and its initial relation weights [1, 15, 38]. Harmonizing the original antibody panels was likewise necessary to define a common supervised output across the leave-one-dataset-out folds; however, differences in panel composition, missing targets, protein dynamic ranges, and tissue-specific marker expression can change prediction difficulty and the interpretation of protein-wise evaluation metrics. A consistent spatial-neighborhood definition supports comparison across the evaluated sections; nevertheless, spatial resolution, spot density, tissue scale, coordinate processing, neighborhood size, and distance metric can alter the physical meaning of an edge, the spatial edge support, and the resulting message-propagation behavior [20–23]. Despite these sources of variation, the preprocessing and harmonization workflow used in this study provides a practical way to bring paired spatial RNA–protein datasets from different sources into a consistent input and target space. When additional datasets are included, the same workflow can be retained, while the shared gene set and protein target panel are redefined according to the available measurements. Evaluation across a wider range of platforms and spatial resolutions would further assess the generalizability of DPAS-Graph. Because the current objective primarily optimizes protein abundance prediction rather than spatial-structure-sensitive endpoints, future extensions could also incorporate multiscale neighborhoods, region-level constraints, or spatial regularization. In addition, the relatively high peak reserved GPU memory of the current implementation motivates the use of sparse edge operations, edge sampling, block-wise inference, and lightweight graph encoders. Overall, DPAS-Graph provides a task-driven relation-learning framework for cross-dataset spatial RNA-to-protein prediction and a complementary route for marker-level virtual protein profiling in RNA-only spatial transcriptomic data.
