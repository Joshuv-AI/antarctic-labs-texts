Chemotherapy in a dog with neutropenia and marginal zone lymphoma
Authors: Keiko KODAMA, Akihisa SUWA, Mika TANABE, Kazuhito KODAMA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 12-year-old Beagle dog with generalized lymphadenopathy and persistent neutropenia was diagnosed with advanced marginal zone lymphoma based on cytology, histopathology, and polymerase chain reaction for antigen receptor rearrangement analysis. Bone marrow examination revealed hypocellularity predominantly affecting the myeloid lineage, while normal morphology and maturation were preserved across hematopoietic lineages, with no evidence of myelophthisis or fibrosis. Chemotherapy was initiated using a modified and dose-reduced multi-agent protocol, followed by L-asparaginase and nimustine; full-dose chemotherapy was also subsequently tolerated without serious adverse events. Two complete remissions were achieved during the treatment course. Normalization of the neutrophil count during tumor progression was presumed to reflect increased neutrophil demand associated with disease progression, indicating that compensatory hematopoietic capacity was preserved. This case illustrates that, in dogs with persistent neutropenia and myeloid hypoplasia, chemotherapy may be considered when bone marrow evaluation confirms preserved hematopoietic maturation and careful monitoring is performed.Keywords: bone marrow examination, chemotherapy, dog, lymphoma, neutropenia

Lymphoma is a common neoplasm in dogs, accounting for approximately 7–24% of all tumors [18]. Of these, 60–70% are B-cell lymphomas, and when the B-cell lymphoma is of the large cell type, nearly 90% are classified as either diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) or marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) [14, 22, 24]. MZL is a relatively common lymphoma in dogs, comprising approximately 5%–15% of all lymphomas [3, 14, 23]. It is primarily an indolent lymphoma that arises in lymph nodes, with a survival time that can reach 2–3 years [3, 13]. However, advanced-stage MZL has been reported to exhibit biological behavior similar to that of DLBCL [5, 15, 21].

For the treatment of intermediate- and high-grade lymphoma in dogs, multi-agent chemotherapy is the first-line therapy, with cyclophosphamide, hydroxydaunorubicin (doxorubicin), vincristine, and prednisolone (CHOP)-based chemotherapy widely used as a standard protocol [18]. Anticancer agents typically inhibit cell division and induce apoptosis by interfering with processes involved in the cell cycle [7]. As a result, they target actively proliferating tumor cells but also affect normal rapidly dividing cells such as hematopoietic stem cells, making myelosuppression one of the most common and representative adverse events during chemotherapy in dogs [4, 9, 12, 25].

The reference interval (RI) for the neutrophil count in dogs varies among laboratories, but neutropenia is generally defined as a neutrophil count of <3,000/μL [16]. The main causes of neutropenia in dogs and cats include decreased or ineffective production of neutrophils in the bone marrow (e.g., myelophthisis, lymphoproliferative disease), drug-induced suppression (e.g., anticancer agents, immunosuppressants), exposure to toxins, infections, and other causes (e.g., bone marrow hypoplasia/aplasia, immune-mediated disease) [11]. Neutropenia is often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, with clinical signs typically absent unless there is peripheral neutrophil consumption [11]. A study involving 232 dogs with neutropenia found that only 7 (3%) were diagnosed with primary bone marrow disorders, including 1 case of leukemia (0.4%) and 5 cases of myeloid hypoplasia (2.1%) [1].

To our knowledge, reports describing chemotherapy in dogs with persistent neutropenia associated with myeloid hypoplasia are limited. This report describes the clinical course, treatment response, and adverse events in a dog with MZL and persistent neutropenia, in which bone marrow examination revealed myeloid hypoplasia without evidence of myelophthisis.

A 12-year-old, 9.9-kg castrated male Beagle dog was presented on Day 0 with a 1-week history of persistent swelling in the mandibular lymph node. Physical examination revealed generalized lymphadenopathy, with the left mandibular lymph node being the most prominent, measuring 50 × 45 × 23 mm. Fine-needle aspiration cytology of the enlarged left superficial cervical lymph node revealed a population consisting of 13% small, 50% intermediate, and 37% large lymphocytes, with intermediate to large lymphocytes predominating (Fig. 1A). These lymphocytes exhibited round to irregular nuclei, and some showed marked nuclear atypia, such as cloverleaf or lobulated nuclei. Mitotic figures were occasionally observed, with 15 mitoses identified in 15 high-power fields at 500× magnification. Additionally, the polymerase chain reaction for antigen receptor rearrangement was positive for the immunoglobulin heavy chain gene and negative for the T-cell receptor gamma gene. Based on these findings, a board-certified pathologist (Diplomate of the American College of Veterinary Pathologists) diagnosed high-grade B-cell lymphoma.

On Day 6, a complete blood count (CBC), serum biochemistry, urinalysis, thoracic and abdominal radiographs, and echocardiography were performed. The CBC revealed mild neutropenia (2,810/μL; RI, 3,000–11,500/μL), while other parameters were within normal limits. Abdominal ultrasonography showed mild splenomegaly with multiple hypoechoic lesions (5–10 mm on the long axis) and enlargement of the colic lymph node (11.3 × 6.3 mm). L-asparaginase (L-asp) (Leunase Injection 5000; Kyowa Kirin, Tokyo, Japan) was administered at 400 IU/kg subcutaneously, and prednisolone (Prednisolone Injection; Tamura, Saitama, Japan) was administered at 2 mg/kg subcutaneously. Thereafter, the dog continued to receive oral prednisolone (PREDNINE Tablets 5 mg; Shionogi, Osaka, Japan) at 1.5 mg/kg once daily and oral lansoprazole (Lansoprazole OD Tablets 15 mg; Sawai, Osaka, Japan) at 0.75 mg/kg once daily. The treatment response was evaluated based on the Response Evaluation Criteria for Peripheral Nodal Lymphoma in Dogs (v1.0) [19]. A neutrophil count of 3,000/μL was used as the cutoff value for chemotherapy administration. On Day 14, partial remission was achieved based on the reduction in lymph node size, but chemotherapy was deferred because of continued neutropenia (2,540/μL), and CBCs were monitored every few days. Because the neutrophil count did not recover and progressive disease was observed on Day 44, bone marrow aspiration, bone marrow core biopsy, and excisional biopsy of the right superficial cervical lymph node were performed simultaneously on Day 49. Bone marrow samples were obtained from the left proximal humerus and were evaluated by the same board-certified veterinary pathologist. The CBC and myelogram findings are shown in Tables 1 and 2. Bone marrow cytology revealed a hypocellular marrow, with overall cellularity ranging from 10% to 30%. Megakaryocytes were sparsely distributed and mostly morphologically normal, although a few exhibited separated nuclei. Granulocytic and erythroid cell lineages appeared morphologically and maturationally normal (Fig. 1B), with a normal myeloid-to-erythroid ratio of 1.17 (RI, 0.90–1.76). Core biopsy revealed 10% cellularity with predominantly fatty marrow (Fig. 1C), although active granulopoiesis and erythropoiesis were noted in hematopoietic foci. No fibrosis or neoplastic infiltration was observed. A peripheral blood smear showed no abnormalities in red blood cells or platelets; however, the leukocyte density was reduced, and mild neutropenia was observed. Lymphocytes were primarily medium-sized and lacked atypia. Based on these findings, the same pathologist who evaluated the initial lymph node cytology diagnosed persistent relative neutropenia due to myeloid hypoplasia, with no evidence of neoplastic infiltration, fibrosis, or other marrow-suppressive factors. Histopathological examination of the lymph node raised suspicion of advanced MZL based on the growth pattern of tumor cells and polymerase chain reaction for antigen receptor rearrangement results (Fig. 1D). The presence of some large lymphocytes suggested possible transformation into DLBCL.

Despite persistent neutropenia (1,500–3,000/μL), the tumor continued to progress. After a discussion with the owner, chemotherapy was initiated using a further modified and dose-reduced version of the modified University of Wisconsin–Madison 25-week protocol [6]. L-asp was administered on Day 56, dose-reduced VCR (Oncovin for Injection 1 mg; Nihon Kayaku, Tokyo, Japan) was administered at 0.375 mg/m2 intravenously on Day 63, and dose-reduced CPM (Endoxan for Injection 100 mg; Shionogi) was administered at 150 mg/m2 intravenously on Day 70. Complete remission (CR) was achieved on Day 77. Subsequently, VCR was used in place of DOX, and chemotherapy continued with the dose-reduction protocol. On Day 147, bilateral mandibular lymph node enlargement recurred. The VCR and CPM doses were increased to 0.5 mg/m2 and 200 mg/m2, respectively, and DOX (Doxorubicin Hydrochloride Injection 10 mg Sandoz; Sandoz, Tokyo, Japan) was added at 1 mg/kg intravenously. However, tumor control was not achieved. On Day 224, the chemotherapy was switched to nimustine (ACNU) (Nidran for Injection 25 mg; Daiichi Sankyo, Tokyo, Japan) at 25 mg/m2 intravenously. CR was achieved again on Day 231 (7 days after initial ACNU administration), although the neutrophil count reached its nadir during the treatment period (440/μL). The dog remained afebrile, and the neutrophil count recovered to 2,250/μL within 7 days. However, the second CR lasted only 14 days. On Day 252, ulceration of the left hard palate and swelling below the left eye were observed, and cytology suggested squamous cell carcinoma. The lesion progressed rapidly, and with concurrent lymphoma progression, palliative care was initiated at the owner’s request after Day 272. The patient died at home on Day 327.

Bone marrow examination in the present case demonstrated hypocellularity predominantly affecting the myeloid lineage, while granulocytic and erythroid cells showed normal morphology and maturation. In addition, active hematopoiesis was observed in hematopoietic foci, and no evidence of myelophthisis, fibrosis, or neoplastic infiltration was identified. These findings indicate that the bone marrow was functionally preserved despite reduced cellularity. Chemotherapy was administered under close monitoring and resulted in two episodes of CR without serious adverse events. This case suggests that, even in dogs with myeloid hypoplasia without myelophthisis, chemotherapy may be safely performed when bone marrow status is appropriately evaluated.

Hypoplastic marrow is defined as having 5–25% cellularity within the hematopoietic space, while aplastic marrow is defined as having less than 5% cellularity. Such marrow states can result from insufficient numbers of hematopoietic stem cells, abnormalities in the hematopoietic microenvironment, or abnormal humoral or cellular control of hematopoiesis. These factors are interrelated, and the specific defect in a given disorder is usually unknown [8]. Persistent neutropenia in dogs can result from a wide range of conditions, including infectious, inflammatory, immune-mediated, drug-related, and primary bone marrow disorders [1, 11]. In the present case, there was no clinical or laboratory evidence of infection, inflammation, toxin exposure, or drug-induced suppression prior to chemotherapy. Moreover, the absence of dysplastic changes, normal maturation across hematopoietic lineages, and lack of other cytopenias made immune-mediated neutropenia unlikely [1, 2, 10]. Because no definitive etiology could be identified and the clinical course did not fully correspond to a specific bone marrow disease entity, the marrow findings were interpreted as representing a condition characterized by myeloid hypoplasia. In this case, the term “myeloid hypoplasia” is used to describe the morphological and functional state of the bone marrow at the time of evaluation, rather than to imply a specific underlying disease. During the later stages of the disease, the neutrophil count transiently increased into the normal range, reaching a maximum of 10,900/μL. This finding suggests that increased neutrophil demand, possibly attributable to inflammation or tissue necrosis associated with lymphoma progression and oral lesions suspicious for squamous cell carcinoma, elicited an appropriate compensatory response from the bone marrow [11]. These observations indicate that, although the baseline neutrophil count was low, the bone marrow retained the capacity to respond to hematopoietic stress.

In dogs with advanced lymphoma, when neutropenia or thrombocytopenia presumed to result from myelophthisis is observed prior to chemotherapy, the administration of chemotherapy is expected to restore hematopoiesis by eliminating neoplastic cells [18]. In this case, mild neutropenia was present before treatment, and therapy was initiated with L-asp. However, neutropenia persisted despite the patient showing a partial remission to treatment; therefore, lymph node excisional biopsy and marrow evaluation were performed. In early-stage MZL, mitotic activity is known to be almost completely absent within areas of neoplastic proliferation [20]. However, in this case, mitotic figures were occasionally observed in the initial cytology. Several cytologic and imaging findings, including the predominance of medium to large lymphocytes with marked nuclear atypia and the presence of splenic and mesenteric lymph node involvement on abdominal ultrasonography, further suggest that the patient may have already been in an advanced stage at the time of initial presentation. The subsequent excisional biopsy of the lymph node corroborated the cytologic and imaging findings, demonstrating frequent mitotic figures and a growth pattern consistent with advanced-stage MZL. The slight difference in the observed frequency of mitotic figures between the initial cytology and the excisional biopsy specimen is likely attributable to the differences in the type of material examined.

In this case, the bone marrow was confirmed to exhibit myeloid hypoplasia with maintained hematopoietic function. However, because of the lack of veterinary information on chemotherapy protocols for patients with this hematologic background, treatment was continued with dose-reduced drugs. In CHOP-based chemotherapy, febrile neutropenia is commonly observed during the initial cycle or first administration of drugs [4, 17]. While CHOP-based chemotherapy induces remission in 80–90% of dogs, with a median survival of 10–12 months and 20–25% surviving beyond 2 years [18], the durations of the first and second CRs in this case were 70 and 14 days, respectively. This suggests suboptimal efficacy compared with standard protocols. A previous report indicated that dogs who developed chemotherapy-induced neutropenia had significantly longer remission durations than those who did not [25]. In this case, resuming full-dose chemotherapy did not result in severe myelosuppression; however, whether standard dosing at the initiation of treatment would have resulted in a longer CR remains speculative.

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single case, and the effectiveness of similar treatment in other dogs with comparable pathology remains unknown. Second, although a modified University of Wisconsin–Madison 25-week protocol was planned, the exclusion of DOX in the early part of treatment may have influenced the treatment outcomes. Third, because the dog initially presented with a low neutrophil count, it was difficult to accurately assess the impact of chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression. The marked neutropenia observed after ACNU administration could not be ruled out as being related to the underlying condition.

In dogs with lymphoma and persistent neutropenia, bone marrow evaluation is recommended to assess for underlying conditions. Even in cases of myeloid hypoplasia, if hematopoiesis is normally maintained across all lineages, the bone marrow may still respond appropriately to chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression. Therefore, treatment should be considered in such cases, provided that thorough evaluation and close monitoring are performed.
