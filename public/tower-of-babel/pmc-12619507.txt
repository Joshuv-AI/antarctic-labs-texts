Implementing liquid biopsy NGS in stage III/IV NSCLC: clinical utility assessment from a real-world Chinese cohort
Authors: Xinxin Yang, Sihang Gao, Ran Ju, Xiaoyong Ji, Shuzhe Deng, Chenxu Guo, Yunjing Hou, Xiaoxi Pan, Lijiao Lin, Li Qu, Meixuan Wan, Shuo Yang, Guangyu Shan, Hongxue Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCell-free circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) has demonstrated the potential to guide the personalized treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Furthermore, clinical treatment-response data guided by ctDNA remain scarce. This study systematically evaluates an NGS platform’s detection accuracy and clinical utility in a large Chinese NSCLC cohort, establishing therapeutic-response evidence.MethodsWe first established the cutoff and quality control parameters for the NGS test using clinical plasma samples from NSCLC patients, with ddPCR serving as the reference standard. The performance of the assay was then robustly validated in an independent cohort of 522 samples. Finally, we analyzed the driver and resistance mutations for their distribution, concordance with tissue samples, and therapeutic relevance.ResultsThrough analysis of plasma samples using ROC and downsample methods, we established a 0.2% detection threshold and identified > 1400x mean effective depth as the critical QC metric; under these parameters, ddPCR validation of 17 specific sites in the 522-sample cohort demonstrated > 80% positive percentage agreement (PPA) and > 95% negative percentage agreement (NPA), confirming strong NGS-ddPCR concordance. The 21-gene panel detected mutations in 73.75% of patients, with 45.59% harboring NCCN-recommended targetable mutations. In the tissue-plasma concordance analysis, stage-specific performance was observed: Stage III showed 28.57% PPA (2/7) versus 99.20% NPA (124/125), while Stage IV demonstrated 99.20% PPA (124/125) and 99.46% NPA (183/184). Clinically relevant plasma-specific mutations were identified throughout, and pooled data demonstrated equivalent targeted therapy response rates between this ctDNA-based NGS and National Medical Products Administration (NMPA)-approved tissue-based assays.ConclusionsThis study highlights the feasibility of ctDNA-based NGS in clinical practice, offering valuable and clinically relevant mutational genomic profiling for stage III/IV NSCLC in Chinese patients.Trial registrationThis study is registered with Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2000041034, on December 16, 2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15227-0.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Circulating tumor DNA, Next-generation sequencing, Genetic alterations

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. The advent of precision medicine, however, has transformed the management of this disease, as targeted therapies are often the most efficacious options for patients harboring actionable genetic alterations [3]. The National Comprehensive Cancer (NCCN) guidelines recommend testing for drivers mutations and resistance mutations in key biomarkers such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) activating mutations, V-Raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B (BRAF) V600E and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) G12C. These tests are essential for personalizing NSCLC treatment selection and monitoring [4].

For decades, Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) has been the primary material for molecular profiling [5]. FFPE samples are easy to handle, store, and, importantly, have a high concentration of tumor cells, which improves detection sensitivity [6, 7]. Currently, the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) has approved several tests for detecting mutations in tissue samples from patients with NSCLC, including the Cobas EGFR Mutation Test v2 (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc.). However, FFPE samples also have notable limitations: they are impractical for about 30% of patients and, more critically, they cannot fully capture the complex landscape of tumor heterogeneity—both intratumoral heterogeneity due to limited sampling and spatial heterogeneity between metastatic sites [8, 9]. Moreover, as mutations evolve under targeted therapy, invasive tissue re-biopsies are often required for detection [10].

In contrast, plasma biopsy, which is minimally invasive and better at overcoming tissue heterogeneity, has been widely adopted in clinical practice [8]. The NCCN guidelines suggest that cell-free DNA (cfDNA) may be used to detect actionable mutations with similar importance to tissue biopsy, through tissue biopsy may still be required if a liquid biopsy test is negative [11]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS), a method that enables comprehensive analysis of multiple biomarkers simultaneously, is increasingly used in ctDNA-based assays and has proven to be a valuable clinical tool [12, 13].

We retrospectively analyzed a cohort of 522 Chinese patients diagnosed with stage III or IV NSCLC to explore the feasibility of a ctDNA-based NGS assay. We conducted a comparative analysis of results from ctDNA-based NGS assays, ddPCR and tissue-based assays. Additionally, we present response rates from 85 patients, comparing therapy guided by the ctDNA-based NGS assay to that based on other methods.

Methods

Patients’ enrollment and samples collection

Between January 2021 and March 2023, patients aged 18 or older, diagnosed with stage III or IV NSCLC at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (Harbin, China), were enrolled in our cohort. Participants were required to provide 14–20 mL of peripheral blood samples, with some were also asked to submit concurrent FFPE samples. Exclusion criteria included: (1) insufficient cfDNA yield (a minimum of 20 ng required for library preparation) following extraction; (2) a diagnosis of a second malignant tumor; (3) prior transplant surgery; and (4) prior receipt of allogeneic blood transfusion or immunotherapy potentially introducing foreign DNA. Detailed clinicopathological data, including age, gender, clinical stage and histology, were collected from medical records. Response rates were assessed using modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria (version 1.1). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient for the use of their samples and clinical characteristics. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital No. 2024-235-SJ.

Plasma samples were collected in 10 mL Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes (Streck, La Vista, NE) and stored at room temperature for processing within one week. A two-step centrifugation protocol was used to separate plasma and buffy coat. Plasma and FFPE tissue samples were stored at −80 ℃ until DNA isolation. cfDNA was isolated from 4 mL plasma using the Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Beijing USCI Medical Devices Co., Ltd., TQ003, China) and stored in 52 µL of elution buffer provided with the kit. Genomic DNA (gDNA) for genetic testing was extracted from tumor tissue using the QIAmp FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, 56404, Germany). For the Cobas EGFR Mutation Test, DNA extraction was performed using the Cobas DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., Tucson, AZ). The concentration of gDNA for Cobas EGFR Mutation Test was measured using NanoDrop Onec (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while the concentration of cfDNA and gDNA for other tests were measured using Qubit dsDNA High-Sensitivity (HS) assay kit (Invitrogen, Q32854, USA).

Library preparation, target region capturing, and sequencing

Next generation sequencing libraries were generated from over 20ng of cfDNA using USCI UgenDX Lung Cancer kit, an NGS platform assay. The libraries were then hybridized to a targeted 21-gene panel, as illustrated in Fig. 1, which was designed for the detection of single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (InDels), structure variants (SVs), and copy number variants (CNVs) in key NSCLC driver genes including EGFR, BRAF, KRAS, etc. The gene list of our panel was supplied in Table S1. Then, the libraries were then sequenced on the USCISEQ-200 analyzers (Beijing USCI Medical Devices Co., Ltd., China) to generate 100 bp pair-end reads.

Droplet digital PCR

Specific assays for SNVs, InDels were performed using ddPCR (Bio-Rad) to detect EGFR, BRAF and KRAS mutations. The reaction mixture consisted of 10 µL ddPCR Supermix for Probes (Bio-Rad), 1 µL of Primers & Probe (FAM labeled + HEX labeled + Primers), DNA template (20 ng of input), and nuclease-free water to a total volume of 20uL. For each InDel and SNV, one probe was labeled with FAM and the opposite was labeled with HEX. The ddPCR amplification of target region was performed using C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). After amplification, samples were loaded into the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad). The final evaluation was performed by QuantaSoft software (version 1.7.4.0917, Bio-Rad).

Tumor tissue molecular testing

Fluorescence quantitative PCR was employed to detect alterations in EGFR and BRAF in tumor tissue samples, utilizing AmoyDx EGFR Mutations Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics, 8.0120201W012, China) and AmoyDx BRAF Mutations Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics, 8.0120301 × 024, China). The mutations included in this study were EGFR L858R, 19Del, T790M, G719S and BRAF V600E. The results were confirmed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

For the Cobas EGFR Mutation Test, extracted genomic DNA from tumor tissue was subjected to real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based EGFR mutation tests using the Cobas EGFR Mutation Test v2 (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc.), as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Additionally, we sent tissue samples to Beijing BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. for Sanger sequencing to detect KRAS G12C.

Bioinformatic analysis

The raw sequencing reads were performed using Trimmomatic (version 0.39), and then were mapped to the human reference genome assembly Human Genome Build 19 (Hg19)/GRCh37 for sequence alignment by Burrows-Wheeler aligner (version 0.7.10). Duplicated reads were marked using Picard (Version 1.124). Local alignment optimization, variant calling and annotation were using Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK, version 3.2), VarScan (version 2.4.1) and ANNOVAR (version 20180416). Mutations were retained using the following criteria, a 0.2% variant positivity cutoff with local depth > 1000x and sample average depth > 1400x. InDels and SNVs with population frequency over 0.1% in the databases of ExAC, 1000 Genomes and dbSNP were filtered. All statistical analyses and graphics were performed by R (version 4.0.2).

Results

Patients’ demographics

A total of 522 plasma samples from patients with histologically confirmed stage III/IV NSCLC were analyzed. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1, with detailed patient characteristics being provided in Table S2. The cohort consisted of 233 females and 289 males, with a median age of 61 (range: 35–82). Most patients (395/522, 75.67%) were in clinical stage IV. Sufficient ctDNA (≥ 20ng) for analysis was obtained from all 522 plasma samples. Among these patients, 150 had paired tissue samples that were obtained within 30 days of blood collection. In addition, we collected 75 samples with post-treatment efficacy evaluation data from other cohorts. The study design is drawn in Fig. 2.

CtDNA NGS panel analytical validation

Previous studies on NGS performance have focused on large panels and tissue samples, with minimal research addressing small panels and ctDNA samples. Here, we conducted a preliminary performance evaluation of our 21-gene NGS panel for plasma samples using ddPCR as the gold standard. Utilizing 312 plasma samples from patients with NSCLC sent to USCI for UgenDX Lung Cancer testing, we established SNVs and InDels detection thresholds via ROC analysis. Limited ddPCR probes availability confined our assessment to 17 specific sites across five genes: EGFR 19del, L858R, T790M, G719S; BRAF V600E; KRAS G12C, G12A, G12D, G12S, G13C, G13D; PIK3CA E542K, E545K, H1047R, H1047L; NRAS Q61K, Q61R. Our validation strategy mandated ddPCR confirmation for all NGS-positive sites across these plasma samples, remaining sites were prioritized evenly based on residual sample volume. This yielded validation of 768 SNVs and 241 InDels, with ROC analysis determining a 0.2% detection threshold for both variant types (Fig. 3A, B).

Downsample analysis of 15 high-depth samples identified 1400x as the critical minimum mean effective depth for NGS quality control. This process involved two sequential steps: First, reducing sample depths incrementally (600x, 800x, 1000x, 1200x, 1400x, 1600x, 1800x, 2000x) established that per-site depths exceeding 1000x ensured 100% detection of positive variants (Fig. 3C). Second, we confirmed that achieving a mean effective depth of 1400x per sample guaranteed all sites met this 1000x minimum requirement (Fig. 3D).

Applying the 0.2% threshold and 1400x effective average depth QC criteria to the 522-patient cohort generated NGS variant calls. Subsequent ddPCR validation (same 17 sites) demonstrated > 80% PPA and > 95% NPA across all mutations (Table 2; Fig. 3E). Discordant results occurred at 15 of 1307 tested sites, comprising six EGFR 19del, three EGFR L858R, four EGFR T790M, one KRAS G12D, and one PIK3CA E545K variant. Specifically, seven of these discordant sites were not detected by NGS, while seven were falsely detected by NGS. Notably, the validation effort was primarily focused on 17 specific sites across five genes due to constraints in ddPCR probe availability and sample volume. This approach excluded the verification of complex alterations like gene fusions, thereby limiting the generalizability of the performance data to the full panel, particularly for variants with inherently lower plasma shedding.

Distribution of genomic alterations in NSCLC

We analyzed NGS data from 522 samples using established thresholds and QC criteria, including a 0.2% variant positivity cutoff and > 1400x effective average depth requirement, with all samples meeting QC standards. Our 21-gene panel (Fig. 1) encompasses all NCCN-recommended NSCLC therapeutic targets and other relevant genes. Among the cohort, 385 patients (73.75%) harbored ≥ 1 genomic alteration, and NCCN-recommended target mutations were detected in 45.59% of patients. TP53 emerged as the most frequently mutated gene (35.2%), followed by EGFR (34.1%), PIK3CA (8.8%), KRAS (8.4%), MET (6.5%), ALK (3.6%), BRAF (3.1%), NTRK1 (2.9%), PTEN (2.9%), and FGFR1 (2.7%). EGFR and KRAS mutations exhibited mutual exclusivity. Among the cohort, EGFR and KRAS mutations were identified in 178 (34.1%) and 44 (8.4%) patients, respectively. However, only 5 cases showed co-occurrence (P < 0.01), demonstrating that a KRAS mutation (targetable only in G12C cases) rarely coincides with a concurrent EGFR mutation, which is consistent with their known mutually exclusive pattern (Fig. 4A).

We delineated driver and resistance mutations to provide a clearer, more detailed view of patient mutation profiles (Fig. 4B). We observed that 100% of patients harboring EGFR resistance mutations also carried corresponding sensitizing mutations. Driver mutations, including EGFR 19del, EGFR L858R, ALK/ROS1/RET fusions, MET amplification, and ERBB2 20ins, rarely co-occurred in the same patient. Focusing on EGFR mutations (34.1% prevalence), categorization showed common driver mutations dominated (83.14%), comprising exon 19 deletions (44.94%) and L858R substitutions (38.20%). Other actionable alterations included exon 20 insertions (2.81%) and uncommon sensitizing variants (6.74%) such as G719X, S768I, and L861Q (Fig. 4C).

To investigate the resistance landscape, we analyzed the 123 patients with classic sensitizing mutations (19del/L858R). The distribution of acquired resistance mutations (T790M or C797S) across different disease courses is detailed in Table 3. In brief, 18 patients harbored such mutations, and their occurrence was highly correlated with the clinical treatment history: the vast majority (16/18) were identified in patients who had developed resistance to prior TKIs. Fisher’s exact test confirmed a significant association between resistance mutations and the patient’s disease course (P < 0.001).

Concordance between tissue and plasma

We next conducted a retrospective analysis to assess the consistency between tissue and plasma samples. In the first part, paired tissue samples were utilized to interrogate six actionable mutations: EGFR 19del, L858R, T790M; BRAF V600E; KRAS G12C; and the rare EGFR G719S. Specific mutations within the EGFR and BRAF genes were detected using the AmoyDx EGFR Mutations Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics, 8.0120201W012, China) and the AmoyDx BRAF Mutations Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics, 8.0120301 × 024, China), both approved by the NMPA. Additionally, Sanger sequencing was used to detect the KRAS G12C mutation. A total of 57 paired samples were analyzed, including 33 positive sites and 303 negative sites based on tissue results (Fig. 5A). At the site level, the concordance for EGFR 19del, L858R, T790M, G719S, BRAF V600E and KRAS G12C was 96.49% (55/57), 89.47% (51/57), 98.25% (56/57), 100% (57/57), 100% (57/57), and 100% (57/57), respectively. Nine sites showed discrepancies, including two for EGFR 19del, six for EGFR L858R and one for EGFR T790M. The sensitivity and specificity of the NGS were 78.79% and 99.35%, respectively.

We further categorized the samples by clinical stage, with 22 samples from Stage III and 35 from Stage IV. Among the nine inconsistent sites, six were from Stage III samples, and three were from Stage IV samples. The PPA for Stage III and Stage IV samples were 28.57% (2/7) and 92.31% (24/26), respectively, while NPA for Stage III and Stage IV samples were 99.20% (124/125) and 99.46% (183/184), respectively. These results are summarized in Fig. 5B. Given the notably lower sensitivity in Stage III disease, we further analyzed the potential influence of tumor substage on ctDNA detection (Table 4). Among the 7 tissue-positive sites in Stage III, 6 were from stage IIIA, only 2 of which were plasma-positive; the single tissue-positive site in stage IIIB was plasma-negative, while no tissue-positive sites were observed in stage IIIC. Although these findings suggest particularly low plasma detection sensitivity in stage IIIA, the limited number of tissue-positive cases precludes meaningful statistical comparison across IIIA, IIIB, and IIIC substages. For completeness, we also analyzed Stage IV substages and found 13 tissue-positive sites in both IVA and IVB. The two ctDNA false negatives in Stage IV both occurred in the IVA subgroup, supporting the trend of slightly lower ctDNA shed in earlier substages.

In the second part, we compared results from tissue samples tested by Cobas EGFR Mutation Test v2 (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc.) with ctDNA-based NGS in 95 paired samples. These samples were also classified by clinical stage to evaluate potential differences in tissue-plasma concordance between Stage III and Stage IV. For Stage III samples, only one positive site was detected, which was insufficient to observe any meaningful differences. In this analysis, Cobas detected 25 positive and 70 negative samples. Only two discordant sites were observed, each from a distinct sample, and both were identified in plasma (Fig. 5C).

We then focused on plasma-specific mutations in EGFR identified in four patients: 02260P, 02287P, 02034P, and 02468P. To verify these mutations, we compared NGS results with those from ddPCR (Table 5). Notably, patient 02468P had an EGFR L858R mutation detected by NGS, but ddPCR showed a negative result, suggesting a possible false positive. However, the remaining plasma-specific mutations were consistent between NGS and ddPCR. We failed to obtain follow-up treatment data for patient 02468P but successfully tracked patients 02034P (Fig. 5D) and 02287P (Fig. 5E). In patient 02034P, who carried a plasma-unique EGFR L858R mutation (9.4% variant frequency), targeted therapy resulted in a partial response (PR) per RECIST 1.1. The sum of the longest diameters of the target lesions decreased from 34.1 mm at baseline to 25.4 mm after six months of treatment, corresponding to a reduction of 25.5%. Patient 02287P was initially found to harbor an EGFR 19del mutation in tissue. After developing resistance to first-generation EGFR-TKI plus radiotherapy following 1.5 years (+ 620 days) of treatment, concurrent tissue and plasma testing at + 696 days revealed a plasma EGFR T790M mutation, which explained the resistance. The patient was subsequently switched to osimertinib, achieved a confirmed PR, and remained progression-free through + 1214 days of follow-up.

ctDNA-based NGS-directed therapy and response rates

In order to further investigate the relationship between EGFR-activating mutations (including EGFR 19del, L858R, and T790M) identified by ctDNA-based NGS and treatment responses after an appropriate period of targeted therapy, we collected the efficacy information of 85 patients. Of the 85 samples, 10 were sourced from the cohort of the present study, while the remaining 75 originated from the research projects we carried out in other hospitals. Notably, the detection methods employed across these different sources were kept consistent. All relevant information has been presented in Table S3. All 85 patients were found, via ctDNA-based NGS, to carry EGFR-activating mutations and subsequently underwent targeted therapy. Specifically, the objective response rate (ORR) among patients treated with Icotinib was 85.19% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 67.52–94.08%), for those on Gefitinib it was 83.33% (95% CI: 43.65–96.99%), and for patients receiving Osimertinib, the ORR stood at 78.85% (95% CI: 65.97–87.76%) (Fig. 6).

Pooled response rates for patients identified by ctDNA-based NGS were compared with those obtained using other methods. No statistically significant difference was found between ctDNA-based NGS and ddPCR or between ctDNA-based NGS and tissue-based qPCR. PR, partial response; CR, complete response; ORR, objective response rate

We also compared the ORR of ctDNA-based NGS with that of other detection methods. Among them, the detection methods of the tissue-based Cobas EGFR Mutation Test v2 and AmoyDx EGFR Mutations Detection Kit have both been approved by the NMPA. Our results demonstrated that ORR to ctDNA-guided targeted therapy was marginally lower than those guided by tissue-based testing, though without statistical significance.

Discussion

With the accumulation of research findings, ctDNA testing has gained increasing attention. In the genetic testing for patients with NSCLC, the 2024 V5 NCCN NSCLC guidelines revised the statement in Footnote II from “If there is insufficient tissue to allow testing for all of Complete genotyping for EGFR, KRAS, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, NTRK1/2/3, MET, RET, and ERBB2 (HER2) via repeat biopsy and/or plasma testing should be done” to “Complete genotyping for EGFR, KRAS, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, NTRK1/2/3, MET, RET, and ERBB2 (HER2) via biopsy and/or plasma testing“ [14]. This elevates plasma testing from a salvage solution to an equivalent option. However, the implementation of ctDNA-based NGS in clinical practice requires rigorous validation of its analytical performance and a clear understanding of its clinical utility, especially in specific populations such as Chinese patients with NSCLC. Our study adds to this body of evidence by validating a targeted NGS panel and assessing its performance in a real-world cohort.

A key finding of our study is the critical importance of establishing platform-specific analytical parameters. Previous NGS validation studies predominantly focused on tissue samples and large panels [15, 16], with limited research establishing cutoffs and quality control parameters for plasma samples and small panels. The 0.2% cutoff and a mean effective depth of >1400x, determined using ddPCR as a reference standard, were essential to achieving a robust performance with >80% PPA and >95% NPA for the verified sites. This underscores that stringent, empirically derived quality control metrics are fundamental for reliable mutation detection in the low VAF range typical of ctDNA, and that performance cannot be extrapolated from tissue-based assays or larger panels.

Our data further highlights a fundamental constraint of liquid biopsy: its dependence on ctDNA shed into the bloodstream. The stark contrast in sensitivity between Stage III and Stage IV disease, with the former showing a PPA of only 28.57%, is a sobering reminder that tumor burden and possibly earlier substage significantly impact detection. This stage-dependent performance has direct implications for clinical practice, suggesting that negative plasma results in earlier-stage patients should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmatory tissue testing. Future studies with larger cohorts will allow for a more powerful substage-specific analysis to delineate the impact of disease stage on ctDNA detection. Furthermore, the application of larger gene panels could provide a more accurate estimation of tumor content, thereby enabling a deeper investigation into the relationship between disease stage and ctDNA shed.

Conversely, the clinical utility of plasma-specific mutations detected in our cohort deserves emphasis. The cases where plasma-unique mutations (e.g., T790M at resistance) were later verified and successfully guided therapy change underscore a distinct advantage of liquid biopsy: its ability to capture spatially heterogeneous or emerging resistant clones that may be missed by a single tissue biopsy. This supports the use of ctDNA not just as a complementary tool at diagnosis, but as a dynamic monitoring system for managing disease evolution and therapy resistance [17, 18].

Regarding the clinical utility of ctDNA-guided therapy, the marginally inferior, though not statistically significant, ORR compared to tissue-based testing is an important observation. We interpret this finding with caution, as the observed discrepancy is likely multifactorial. On one hand, the presence of detectable ctDNA itself is a known indicator of higher tumor burden and more advanced disease [19], which inherently selects for a patient cohort with a less favorable prognosis [18, 20]. On the other hand, we cannot rule out the potential influence of confounding factors, such as differences in the specific EGFR-TKI agents administered between the comparison groups. Therefore, future studies with larger, prospectively stratified cohorts are warranted to isolate the individual contribution of the detection method from other clinical variables.

Our study has several limitations. First, the cohort of patients with matched blood and FFPE samples was not actively selected but constituted a convenience sample based on practical and ethical constraints, which may introduce a potential source of selection bias. Second, as previously noted, patients progressing on EGFR-TKI therapy demonstrate higher propensity for genetic testing, potentially rendering our cohort non-representative of a random cross-section of Chinese patients with stage III/IV NSCLC. Additionally, during validation, limited ddPCR probe availability and residual sample volume restricted cutoff determination and verification to 17 specific sites across only 5 genes. These excluded complex mutations (e.g., SVs), which exhibit lower plasma detection rates than SNVs/InDels, thus limiting generalizability to full panel performance. Finally, constrained paired tissue samples (particularly stage III cases with targetable alterations) and limited cases with plasma-specific or tissue- specific mutations precluded robust comparative analyses. Expanded datasets would enable more precise assessments.

Conclusions

We validated the performance of ctDNA NGS. At our established parameters (0.2% cutoff, mean effective depth ≥ 1400x) using ddPCR as the gold standard, PPA exceeded 80% and NPA surpassed 95%. Additionally, our cohort study in Chinese patients with stage III/IV NSCLC demonstrated that plasma-unique mutations could guide treatment decisions, though ctDNA testing cannot fully replace tissue biopsy.
