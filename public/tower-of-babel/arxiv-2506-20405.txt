Controlling Casimir Effect in Networks


Abstract
Networks have played a pivotal role in physics, mathematics, and biology, propelling the recent revolution in artificial intelligence. This paper studies quantum field theories on networks and proposes a novel junction condition on the node. The junction condition is consistent with energy conservation in the sense that the total energy flow into the node is zero. As an application, we explore the Casimir effect on networks. Remarkably, the Casimir force on one edge can be changed from attractive to repulsive by adjusting the lengths of the other edges, providing a straightforward way to control the Casimir effect. We begin by discussing the Casimir effect for ( 1 + 1 ) (1+1) -dimensional free massless scalars on a simple network. We then extend this discussion to various types of networks and higher dimensions. Finally, we offer brief comments on some open questions.

Everything in the world is interconnected. Regardless of the type of particle—photons, electrons, protons—there exists universal gravitation between them. No matter how far apart the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen pairs are separated, quantum entanglement persists between them

As a result, studying quantum mechanics within networks is a crucial area of research that holds both theoretical and practical significance. This paper will begin by exploring the Casimir effect in the networks.

Casimir effect originates from changes in vacuum zero-point energy induced by boundaries or spacetime topology

In this article, we propose an alternative method for conveniently controlling the strength and direction of the Casimir force. We demonstrate that by changing the lengths of certain edges in a network, we can turn the Casimir force from attractive to repulsive at one edge. Refer to Fig.
1

Let’s summarize our key results. We derive the junction condition for quantum field theory at the network node
N
and confirm it ensures the conservation of current, expressed as:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}|_{N}=0,

(1)

where
J_{i}
represents the current flowing from edge
E_{i}
to node
N
, encompassing all types of current, such as energy and electric current. Refer to Fig.
2
for the network containing
n
edges connected by a single node. We first examine the Casimir effect for a one-dimensional massless scalar field on the simplest network with three edges connected at a single node. We impose the junction condition at the node and either a Neumann boundary condition (NBC) or a Dirichlet boundary condition (DBC) at the outer boundaries. Our results show that, for both boundary conditions, the Casimir force on one edge decreases as the lengths of the other edges increase. Specifically, for DBC, the Casimir force is always attractive, while for NBC, it can turn repulsive depending on the lengths of the edges. We explore more complex networks with additional nodes and loops, finding similar results. Finally, we extend our analysis to higher dimensions, where we again observe a repulsive Casimir force for NBC, but the force no longer changes signs. Instead, it approaches a non-zero limit when the lengths of the other edges become infinity.

Section 4 and Section 5 generalize the discussions to other types of networks and higher dimensions, respectively. Finally, we conclude with some discussions in Section 6. The appendix discusses the network Casimir effect by applying the Green-function method.

2
Quantum field theories on networks

The key aspect of developing a field theory on a network is determining the appropriate junction condition on the nodes. In this section, we apply the variational principle to derive this condition and confirm that it is consistent with the current conservation equation (
1
). To demonstrate these ideas, we will focus on a massless free scalar field in the simplest network within
1+1
dimensions. The generalizations to more general field theories, networks, and higher dimensions are straightforward.

Consider a network of
n
edges
E
connected at a single node
N
in a
(1+1)
-dimensional flat spacetime. See Fig.
2
. The action of a massless free scalar field is given by

\displaystyle I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{E_{i}}dtdx_{i}\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi_{i}\partial_{\nu}\phi_{i}\Big),

(2)

where
\eta^{\mu\nu}=\text{diag(-1,1)}
is the metric,
\phi_{i}
labels the scalar field on the edge
E_{i}
, and the edges
E_{i}
intersect at the node
N
with
x_{i}=0
. Note that
x_{i}
is the one-dimensional space coordinate on the edge
E_{i}
. Please don’t confuse it with three-dimensional space coordinates, i.e.,
x_{i}=(x,y,z)
. By varying the action, we derive the equation of motion (EOM) in the bulk

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x_{i}}^{2})\phi_{i}=0,

(3)

and the boundary terms on the node

\displaystyle\delta I|_{N}=\int_{N}dt\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{n_{i}}\phi_{i}\delta\phi_{i}\Big)=0,

(4)

where
\partial_{n_{i}}=\partial_{x_{i}}
and
n_{i}
is the normal vector pointing from node
N
to edge
E_{i}
. For a well-defined variational principle, we need
\delta I|_{N}
to vanish. Naturally, we also require continuity of the scalar fields at the node

\displaystyle\phi_{i}|_{N}=\phi(t),

(5)

some arbitrary function
\phi(t)
. Then (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\delta I|_{N}=\int_{N}dt\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{n_{i}}\phi_{i}\Big)\delta\phi=0,

(6)

which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{n_{i}}\phi_{i}|_{N}=0.

(7)

In summary, we propose the following junction conditions at the node

\displaystyle\text{junction conditions}:\ \phi_{i}|_{N}=\phi_{j}|_{N},\ \ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{n_{i}}\phi_{i}|_{N}=0.

(8)

Two important points need to be addressed. First, the junction condition in equation (
8
) provides
n
independent constraints, which is the correct number needed to define a theory on
n
edges. To clarify, for a half-line (one boundary), one boundary condition is sufficient. Therefore, for a network with
n
edges,
n
independent conditions are necessary. We will confirm that the junction condition is adequate for determining the Casimir effect on networks in the upcoming sections. Second, the junction condition is consistent with energy conservation at the nodes, as expressed in equation (
1
). The energy flow density for a scalar field is given by
J=T_{xt}=\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{x}\phi
. Using equations (
5
) and (
7
), we can show that the total energy flowing out of a node is zero

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}|_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{t}\phi_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\phi_{i}|_{N}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{n_{i}}\phi_{i})\partial_{t}\phi|_{N}=0.

(9)

Figure 3:
Simplest network: three edges linked by one node. We impose the junction condition (
8
) on the node (red point), while Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions on the blue and yellow endpoints of the edges in the left and right figures, respectively. Here
x_{i}
is the space coordinate on edge
E_{i}
, which vanishes on the node (red point)
N:x_{i}=0
. The length of edge
E_{i}
is
L_{i}
.

3
Casimir effect on simplest network

This section investigates the Casimir effect on the simplest network with three edges connected by one node, as shown in Fig.
3
. We impose the junction condition (
8
) on the node (redpoint), while Dirichlet boundary condition (DBC) and Neumann boundary condition (NBC) on the blue and yellow outer endpoints in the left and right figures, respectively. We find the Casimir effect is weaker than that of a strip for both DBC and NBC. With DBC, the Casimir energy is consistently negative, indicating an attractive force. In contrast, for NBC, the Casimir energy can switch signs by adjusting the edge lengths, allowing for control over the Casimir effect and enabling a shift from attraction to repulsion.

3.1
DBC

We first consider DBC on the outer boundaries
B
of networks

\displaystyle{\text{Dirichlet BC}}:\ \phi_{i}|_{B}=0,

(10)

where
B
is labeled by blue points in Fig.
3
.

Solving the EOM (
3
), we get the general solution for the scalar on edge
E_{i}

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=\Big(a_{i}\sin(kx_{i})+b_{i}\cos(kx_{i})\Big)e^{-i\omega t},

(11)

where
k=\omega
. To obey the continuous condition (
5
), the frequency
\omega
on different edges must take the same value. Imposing DBC (
10
) on the endpoint
x_{i}=L_{i}
, we can simplify (
11
) to be

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=a_{i}\sin\Big(k(x_{i}-L_{i})\Big)e^{-i\omega t},

(12)

where
L_{i}
is the length of edge
E_{i}
. Substitute (
12
) into the junction condition (
8
), we get

\displaystyle a_{1}\sin(kL_{1})=a_{2}\sin(kL_{2})=a_{3}\sin(kL_{3}),

(13)

and

\displaystyle a_{1}\cos(kL_{1})+a_{2}\cos(kL_{2})+a_{3}\cos(kL_{3})=0.

(14)

Dividing (
14
) by (
13
) gives the constraint on the spectrum

\displaystyle\Delta(k)=\cot(kL_{1})+\cot(kL_{2})+\cot(kL_{3})=0.

(15)

The following outlines the standard method for deriving the Casimir effect using the energy spectrum. We will use a straightforward mode-summing approach and will discuss the Green-function method in the appendix. The key formula we use is

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}}{2}-\sum_{m}\frac{p_{m}}{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{\omega}{2}d\ln\Delta(\omega),

(16)

where
\omega_{n}
and
p_{m}
are the zeros and poles of the spectrum function
\Delta(\omega)
(
15
), respectively. In our case, the poles read

\displaystyle p_{m}=\frac{m\pi}{L_{1}},\frac{m\pi}{L_{2}},\frac{m\pi}{L_{3}},

(17)

with
m
being positive integers. Then, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\frac{p_{m}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{L_{3}})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m=-\frac{\pi}{24}(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{L_{3}}),

(18)

where we have used
\zeta(-1)=\sum m=-1/12
. The contour integral of (
16
) is performed along the right semi-circle with an infinite radius and the imaginary axis. As in the strip Casimir effect scenario, the integral around the infinite semi-circle vanishes with appropriate regularizations, leaving only the contribution from the imaginary axis. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{\omega}{2}d\ln\Delta(w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}-\frac{i\omega\Delta^{\prime}(i\omega)}{2\pi\Delta(i\omega)}d\omega

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega\left(L_{1}\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+L_{2}\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+L_{3}\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}{2\pi\left(\coth\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}d\omega,

(19)

where the integral converges. From (
16
,
18
,
3.1
), we finally obtain the Casimir energy for the simplest network with DBC endpoints (left figure of Fig.
3
),

\displaystyle W_{\text{DBC}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}}{2}=-\frac{\pi}{24}(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{L_{3}})

(20)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega\left(L_{1}\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+L_{2}\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+L_{3}\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}{2\pi\left(\coth\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}d\omega.

Let us test the above formula in the symmetric case where
L_{1}=L_{2}=L_{3}=L
. In this scenario, the Casimir energy (
20
) becomes
W_{DBC}=-\pi/(16L)
. On the other hand, the spectrum can be obtained exactly for
L_{i}=L
. From the constraints (
13
,
14
), we obtain three independent solutions: one from
\cos(kL)=0
with
a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}
, and two from
\sin(kL)=0
resulting in
a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=0
. These solutions contribute to the Casimir energy

\displaystyle W_{\text{DBC}}|_{L_{i}=L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi}{2L}(\frac{1}{2}+m)+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n\pi}{2L}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2L}\zeta\left(-1,\frac{1}{2}\right)+2\frac{\pi}{2L}\zeta(-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{16L},

(22)

which agrees with (
20
). Above, we have used
\zeta\left(-1,\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(\frac{1}{2}+m)=1/24
.

Let’s look at a different scenario with
L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty
. In this case, the Casimir energy (
20
) becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty}W_{\text{DBC}}=-\frac{\text{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}{4\pi L_{1}}\approx-\frac{0.029}{L_{1}}>-\frac{\pi}{24L_{1}}.

(23)

This result shows that the Casimir energy does not vanish, even when the network length is infinite. Additionally, the magnitude of this energy is smaller than that of a strip. These observations can be understood naturally. As waves on edge
E_{1}
approach the node, they undergo reflection and transmission. Consequently, edge
E_{1}
acts like a semi-transmissive strip, making the Casimir effect weaker but nonvanishing.

Now let us discuss the Casimir force

\displaystyle F^{i}=-\frac{\partial W}{\partial L_{i}},

(24)

which is the experimentally measurable physical quantity. By integration by parts, we rewrite the Casimir energy (
20
) as

\displaystyle W_{\text{DBC}}=\frac{-\pi}{24}(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{L_{3}})-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega}{2\pi}d\log\left(\frac{\coth\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{3}\omega\right)}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ =\frac{-\pi}{24}(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{L_{3}})+\int_{0}^{\infty}\log\left(\frac{\coth\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{3}\omega\right)}{3}\right)\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}.

(25)

We include the factor of 3 in the logarithm to ensure the integral converges as
\omega
approaches infinity. Substituting this expression into the formula for the Casimir force yields

\displaystyle F^{i}_{\text{DBC}}=-\frac{\pi}{24L_{i}^{2}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega\text{csch}^{2}\left(L_{i}\omega\right)}{2\pi\left(\coth\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\coth\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}d\omega.

(26)

Comparing the Casimir energy (
20
) with the force (
26
), we observe an interesting relation

\displaystyle W=F^{1}L_{1}+F^{2}L_{2}+F^{3}L_{3}.

(27)

Let us explain the physical origin of this novel relation. In a (1+1)- dimensional conformal field theory (CFT), the stress-energy tensor must be traceless
T^{\mu}_{\ \mu}=-T_{tt}+T_{xx}=0
, resulting in equal energy density
T_{tt}
and pressure
T_{xx}=F
along the edges. Thus, the total energy can be expressed as the sum of forces multiplied by their respective lengths.

Let us discuss some typical cases. For
L_{i}=L
, the force (
26
) can be calculated exactly. We get

\displaystyle F^{i}_{\text{DBC}}|_{L_{i}=L}=-\frac{\pi}{48L^{2}},

(28)

which matches the Casimir energy (
21
) since we have three edges involved. As we let
L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty
, the force becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty}F^{1}_{\text{DBC}}=-\frac{\text{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}{4\pi L_{1}^{2}},

(29)

which agrees with (
23
). Now, if we set
L_{2}=L_{3}=0
, the integral from equation (
26
) becomes zero, and the force on edge
E_{1}
simplifies to that of a strip

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{2}=L_{3}\to 0}F^{1}_{\text{DBC}}=-\frac{\pi}{24L_{1}^{2}}.

(30)

This result is expected because the network becomes a strip when
L_{2}=L_{3}=0
.

3.2
NBC

Let’s go on to explore the network with NBC

\displaystyle{\text{Neumann BC}}:\ \partial_{x_{i}}\phi_{i}|_{B}=0,

(31)

where
B
refers to the outer endpoints of the edges (marked as yellow points in Fig.
3
). The calculations for NBC follow a similar process as those for DBC. Below are the key points and main results for NBC.

To satisfy NBC at the outer endpoints, we make the following ansatz of scalars

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=a_{i}\cos\Big(k(x_{i}-L_{i})\Big)e^{-i\omega t}.

(32)

Then the junction condition (
8
) on the node gives

\displaystyle a_{1}\cos(kL_{1})=a_{2}\cos(kL_{2})=a_{3}\cos(kL_{3}),

(33)

and

\displaystyle a_{1}\sin(kL_{1})+a_{2}\sin(kL_{2})+a_{3}\sin(kL_{3})=0.

(34)

From the above two equations, we get the spectrum for NBC

\displaystyle\Delta(k)=\tan(kL_{1})+\tan(kL_{2})+\tan(kL_{3})=0,

(35)

which is similar to the spectrum for DBC, except that we replace
\cot
with
\tan
. Therefore, when calculating the Casimir effect, we substitute
coth
and
csch
with
\tanh
and
sech
. Additionally, the poles of
\Delta(k)
shift to

\displaystyle p_{m}=\frac{(\frac{1}{2}+m)\pi}{L_{1}},\frac{(\frac{1}{2}+m)\pi}{L_{2}},\frac{(\frac{1}{2}+m)\pi}{L_{3}}.

(36)

Taking these modifications into account and following the methodology in Section 3.1, we obtain the Casimir energy

\displaystyle W_{\text{NBC}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{48}\left(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}+\frac{1}{L_{3}}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega\left(L_{1}\text{sech}^{2}\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+L_{2}\text{sech}^{2}\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+L_{3}\text{sech}^{2}\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}{2\pi\left(\tanh\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\tanh\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\tanh\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}d\omega,

and the Casimir force for NBC

\displaystyle F^{i}_{\text{NBC}}=\frac{\pi}{48L_{i}^{2}}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega\text{sech}^{2}\left(L_{i}\omega\right)}{2\pi\left(\tanh\left(L_{1}\omega\right)+\tanh\left(L_{2}\omega\right)+\tanh\left(L_{3}\omega\right)\right)}d\omega.

(38)

The above Casimir energy and force for NBC obey the conformal relation (
27
), reinforcing the validity of our findings.

To gain a deeper understanding of the Casimir effect for NBC, let us examine some typical examples. Surprisingly, the Casimir effect disappears for
L_{i}=L

\displaystyle W_{\text{NBC}}|_{L_{i}=L}=F^{i}_{\text{NBC}}|_{L_{i}=L}=0.

(39)

We can confirm it through spectrum analysis. From the spectrum (
96
) with
L_{i}=L
, we get one independent solution with
\sin(kL)=0,a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}
, and other two independent solutions with
\cos(kL)=0,a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=0
. Summing these modes gives us a zero Casimir effect for NBC

\displaystyle W_{\text{NBC}}|_{L_{i}=L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi}{2L}(\frac{1}{2}+m)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n\pi}{2L}

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{L}\zeta\left(-1,\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2L}\zeta(-1)=0.

Now, as
L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty
, we find

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty}W_{\text{NBC}}=-\frac{\text{Li}_{2}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)}{4\pi L_{1}}\approx\frac{0.025}{L_{1}},

(41)

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{2}=L_{3}\to\infty}F^{1}_{\text{NBC}}=-\frac{\text{Li}_{2}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)}{4\pi L_{1}^{2}}\approx\frac{0.025}{L_{1}^{2}}.

(42)

On the other hand, as
L_{2}=L_{3}\to 0

\displaystyle\lim_{L_{2}=L_{3}\to 0}F^{1}_{\text{NBC}}=-\frac{\pi}{24L_{1}^{2}},

(43)

which aligns with the Casimir force for a strip. From our findings in the two limits, we see that the Casimir force can change signs under NBC.

4.1
Tree network

This subsection examines the Casimir effect on a network with five edges linked by two nodes, as illustrated in Fig.

) on the nodes (red points), while either the DBC (

) or NBC (

) is applied to the outer endpoints of the edges (black points).

Let us first study the case of DBC. We make the following ansatzes for the scalars on each edge

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=a_{i}\sin\Big(k(x_{i}-L_{i})\Big)e^{-i\omega t},\ \text{for i=1,2,}

(44)

\displaystyle\phi_{3}=\Big(a_{3}\sin(kx_{3})+b_{3}\cos(kx_{3})\Big)e^{-i\omega t},

(45)

\displaystyle\phi_{j}=a_{i}\sin(kx_{j})e^{-i\omega t},\ \text{for j=4,5},

(46)

where

. One can easily see that the above ansatzs obey DBC (

) on the outer endpoints of Fig.

(x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=0)

\displaystyle a_{1}\sin(kL_{1})-a_{2}\sin(kL_{2})=0,

(47)

\displaystyle a_{2}\sin(kL_{2})+b_{3}=0,

(48)

\displaystyle a_{1}\cos(kL_{1})+a_{2}\cos(kL_{2})+a_{3}=0.

(49)

Similarly, the junction condition (
8

(x_{3}=L_{3},x_{4}=L_{4},x_{5}=L_{5})
gives

\displaystyle\Big(a_{3}\sin(kL_{3})+b_{3}\cos(kL_{3})\Big)-a_{4}\sin(kL_{4})=0,

6
Conclusions and Discussions

This paper examines the Casimir effect in networks, utilizing a free massless scalar field as an illustrative example. We derive the junction condition at network nodes, ensuring it aligns with current conservation—meaning the total currents entering a node equal zero. We discover that the Casimir force on one edge can be switched from attractive to repulsive by adjusting the lengths of other edges, assuming we apply Neumann boundary conditions (NBC) on the outer edges. It provides a practical means of controlling the Casimir effect, with potential applications in nanotechnology. Additionally, we find that the Casimir amplitude for any edge is smaller than that of a strip of the same length. This occurs because waves can move between edges, causing the node to act like a semi-transparent boundary, which reduces the Casimir amplitude. We first examine the simplest network in 1+1 dimensions and later extend our findings to more complex networks and higher dimensions. In higher dimensions, we also observe a repulsive Casimir force for NBC. However, unlike in lower dimensions, the force does not change sign when we adjust the edge lengths; instead, there is a minimum value for the Casimir force on one edge when the other edges are of infinite length.

Many interesting problems are worthy of exploration. Let us list just a few of them.

•

This paper focuses on scalar fields and planar networks. It would be interesting to extend the study to include different types of fields, like Dirac fermions and Maxwell fields, as well as 3D networks. Considering the temperature effect
[
8
,
21
]
is also interesting.

•

Exploring other quantum effects, such as entanglement and tunneling in networks, could provide valuable insights into these phenomena. Additionally, solving the Schrödinger equation in the network is also intriguing, especially since the junction condition for wave functions may alter energy levels and introduce new phenomena.

•

Recently, one of the authors proposed a fundamental bound of the Casimir effect
[
22
,
23
]
. As we discussed above, the Casimir effect in the network becomes smaller than that of a strip. It is intriguing to explore the bounds of the Casimir effect in a network with
n
nodes and
p
edges.

•

Finally, using realistic 3D devices to simulate 1D networks experimentally could help validate our predictions. The Casimir effect in networks made of thin tubes is another interesting area to investigate.
