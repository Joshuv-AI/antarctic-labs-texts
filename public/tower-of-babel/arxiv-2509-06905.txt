Yet another exponential Hopfield model
Linda Albanese, Andrea Alessandrelli, Adriano Barra, Peter Sollich

Abstract
We propose and analyze a new variation of the so-called exponential Hopfield model , a recently introduced family of associative neural networks with unprecedented storage capacity. Our construction is based on a cost function defined through exponentials of standard quadratic loss functions, which naturally favors configurations corresponding to perfect recall. Despite not being a mean-field system, the model admits a tractable mathematical analysis of its dynamics and retrieval properties that agree with those for the original exponential model introduced by Ramsauer and coworkers. By means of a signal-to-noise approach, we demonstrate that stored patterns remain stable fixed points of the zero-temperature dynamics up to an exponentially large number of patterns in the system size. We further quantify the basins of attraction of the retrieved memories, showing that while enlarging their radius reduces the overall load, the storage capacity nonetheless retains its exponential scaling. An independent derivation within the perfect recall regime confirms these results and provides an estimate of the relevant prefactors. Our findings thus complement and extend previous studies on exponential Hopfield networks, establishing that even under robustness constraints these models preserve their exceptional storage capabilities. Beyond their theoretical interest, such networks point towards principled mechanisms for massively scalable associative memory, with potential implications for both neuroscience-inspired computation and high-capacity machine learning architectures.

2.1
Patterns as fixed points

By relying upon signal-to-noise techniques, our primary goal here is to estimate the storage capacity of this network and its tolerance against (quenched) noise. To obtain this information, the first question we address is the stability of a neural configuration. We start the neural dynamics by providing as initial condition exactly one of the patterns, and study how its stability changes as a function of the number of stored patterns
P
. Then we study the robustness against perturbations by blurring the initial condition (i.e. the input provided to the network) for its noiseless dynamics, as this provides an indication about the amplitude of the basins of attractions and, thus, the fault tolerance of the network.

A natural starting point, in signal-to-noise approaches, is to evaluate the effective field acting on the
i
-th neuron. To do this we exploit the definition of the Mattis magnetization
m_{\mu}
and split off the contribution of the
i
-th, namely

m_{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}\Rightarrow Nm_{\mu}=\xi_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}.

(4)

We can then recast the cost function (
3
) as

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})&=&-Ne^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}+\xi_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}}=-Ne^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}e^{\xi_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}}.\end{array}

(5)

Since
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
, we have the relation

e^{\xi_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}}=e^{\xi_{i}^{\mu}}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{i}+1}{2}\right)-e^{-\xi_{i}^{\mu}}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{i}-1}{2}\right)

(6)

and can write the cost function

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})&=&-Ne^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\left[e^{\xi_{i}^{\mu}}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{i}+1}{2}\right)-e^{-\xi_{i}^{\mu}}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{i}-1}{2}\right)\right]\\
\\
&=&-Ne^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\sigma_{i}-Ne^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\cosh(\xi_{i}^{\mu}),\end{array}

(7)

in terms of hyperbolic sine and cosine functions.
The prefactor of
-\sigma_{i}
in the first term is naturally identified as the post-synaptic field
h_{i}
acting on neuron
\sigma_{i}
. We therefore define
for
i=1,\ldots,N

\begin{array}[]{lll}h_{i}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi}):=e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu}),\\
\\
\mathcal{C}_{i}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi}):=e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\cosh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\,,\end{array}

(8)

where
\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}
indicates the neural configuration
\bm{\sigma}
without
\sigma_{i}
. In terms of these quantities we have simply

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})&=&-N\left[\,h_{i}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi})\sigma_{i}+\,\mathcal{C}_{i}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi})\right].\end{array}

(9)

In this way we have rewritten the cost function in terms of the internal fields, or more specifically as a sum of a
\sigma_{i}
-independent term and one that is linear in
\sigma_{i}
.

From (
9
) we can easily compute the cost of the spin flip
\sigma_{i}\to-\sigma_{i}
, i.e. the difference between (
9
) evaluated at
-\sigma_{i}
and at
\sigma_{i}
. We call this quantity
\Delta E
as in physical terms it is an energy change and have explicitly

\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta E=\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i},-\sigma_{i}|\bm{\xi})-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i},\sigma_{i}|\bm{\xi})&=&2Nh_{i}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi})\sigma_{i}\\
\\
&=&Ne^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\sigma_{i}.\end{array}

(10)

The zero temperature neural dynamics is such that it always reduces the energy, so that if
\Delta E>0
we have
\sigma_{i}^{(n+1)}=\sigma_{i}^{(n)}
; while if
\Delta E<0
we have
\sigma_{i}^{(n+1)}=-\sigma_{i}^{(n)}
, giving overall

\sigma_{i}^{(n+1)}=\sigma_{i}^{(n)}\mathrm{sign}\left[h_{i}(\bm{\sigma}_{\backslash i}^{(n)}|\bm{\xi})\sigma_{i}^{(n)}\right]=\sigma_{i}^{(n)}\mathrm{sign}\left[e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{(n)}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\sigma_{i}^{(n)}\right].

(11)

According to (
11

\Delta E=2Nh_{i}(\bm{\xi}^{1}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi})\xi_{i}^{1}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\xi^{1}_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\xi^{1}_{i}>0.

(12)

We now exploit the Central Limit Theorem (in the limit of large
N
and

) to approximate the distribution of the quantity
h_{i}(\bm{\xi}^{1}_{\backslash i}|\bm{\xi})\xi_{i}^{1}
appearing in inequality (

) as Gaussian,
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sqrt{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}^{2}}\;)
, with moments given by

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mu_{1}=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\xi^{1}_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\xi^{1}_{i}\right]=e^{-1}\sinh(1),\\

\mu_{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\xi^{1}_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\xi^{1}_{i}\right)^{2}\right]=e^{-2}\sinh^{2}(1)+e^{-2}\sinh^{2}(1)P\left(\dfrac{1+e^{-4}}{2}\right)^{N-1}\end{array}

This happens with probability

m_{1}^{(1)}=\displaystyle\int\dfrac{dz\,e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathrm{sign}\left(\mu_{1}+z\sqrt{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}^{2}}\right)=\mathrm{erf}\left(\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{2(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}^{2})}}\right)\,.

(36)

For the explicit values of (
33
) and (

) we find

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mu_{1}=&e^{-1}\sinh(1)\left[\dfrac{(1+r)+(1-r)e^{-2}}{2}\right]^{N-1},\\
\\
\mu_{2}=&e^{-2}\sinh^{2}(1)\left[\dfrac{(1+r)+(1-r)e^{-2}}{2}\right]^{2(N-1)}+e^{-2}\sinh^{2}(1)P\left(\dfrac{1+e^{-4}}{2}\right)^{N-1}.\end{array}

(37)

Inserting these into (
36
) gives

m_{1}^{(1)}=\mathrm{erf}\left\{\left[2P\left(\dfrac{2(1+e^{-4})}{[(1+r)+(1-r)e^{-2}]^{2}}\right)^{N-1}\right]^{-1/2}\right\}.

(38)

Equivalently, if we set

\displaystyle P=\gamma\left(\dfrac{[(1+r)+(1-r)e^{-2}]^{2}}{2(1+e^{-4})}\right)^{N-1},

(39)

then we can write (
38
) as

\begin{array}[]{lll}m_{1}^{(1)}=\mathrm{erf}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\right).\end{array}

(40)

We thus still find an exponential storage capacity guaranteeing pattern recall from corrupted initial conditions, as long as

r>\dfrac{\sqrt{2(1+e^{4})}}{e^{2}-1}-\dfrac{1+e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}\approx 0.337438,

(41)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle d<\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2(1+e^{4})}}{e^{2}-1}-\dfrac{1+e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}\right)\approx 0.331281.

(42)

Note that if

, we recover the previous maximal storage defined in (

). Conversely, if the network needs to handle corrupted patterns as input, its maximal storage capacity must decrease: the greater the noise in the input, the lower the maximal storage capacity. We emphasize that the storage capacity nonetheless remains exponential as shown by (

Explicitly, perfect recall after one parallel neural update is obtained when
\gamma
defined in (

P=\dfrac{1}{\ln(N)}\left(\dfrac{[(1+r)+(1-r)e^{-2}]^{2}}{2(1+e^{-4})}\right)^{N-1}.

(43)

Put differently, for this number of patterns, initial network states with a Mattis magnetization greater than
r

2.3
The
perfect recall
regime

In order to corroborate our findings so far regarding the exponential storage capacity of the Hopfield model studied here, we provide a further independent argument based on considering its equilibrium statistics.

Let us start from the parallel neural dynamics update rule (
11
), which for large
n
will generate samples from a zero temperature (
\beta\to\infty
) Boltzmann distribution, or from one of its ergodic sectors. Separating off the contribution from pattern
\mu=1
, the update rule reads

\sigma_{i}^{(n+1)}=\sigma_{i}^{(n)}\>\mathrm{sign}\left[e^{-\xi_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}^{(n)}+N(m_{1}^{(n)}-1)}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{1})\sigma_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu>1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma^{(n)}_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\sigma_{i}^{(n)}\right].

(44)

In equilibrium the statistics of
\sigma_{i}^{(n+1)}
are identical to those of
\sigma_{i}^{(n)}
by construction, so dropping the iteration superscripts we have the equality between equilibrium averages

\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle=\left\langle\sigma_{i}\>\mathrm{sign}\left[e^{-\xi_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}+N(m_{1}-1)}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{1})\sigma_{i}+e^{-N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu>1}^{P}e^{\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}}\sinh(\xi_{i}^{\mu})\sigma_{i}\right]\right\rangle\ .

(45)

Setting
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}=\xi_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}
, Eq. (
45
) can, after multiplication by
\xi_{i}^{1}
, be written in the compact form

\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{i}\rangle=\langle\mathrm{sign}(c\,e^{-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}}+z_{i})\rangle

(46)

where

\displaystyle c=

\displaystyle e^{N(m_{1}-1)},

(47)

\displaystyle z_{i}=

\displaystyle e^{-N}\sum_{\mu>1}^{P}\dfrac{\sinh(\xi_{i}^{1}\xi_{i}^{\mu})}{\sinh(1)}\prod\limits_{j\neq i}\exp\left[\xi_{j}^{1}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\tilde{\sigma}_{j}\right],

(48)

with
z_{i}
playing the role of a noise. Note that as we can explicitly write
c\,e^{-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}}=e^{-N+\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{\sigma}_{j}}
, the quantity being averaged on the r.h.s. of (
46
) is a function only of the
\tilde{\sigma}_{j}
with
j\neq i
.

The identity (
46
) is exact with the
z_{i}
as defined, i.e. being functions of the
\tilde{\sigma}_{j}
. As an approximation one could treat the
z_{i}
as quenched variables, as in the signal-to-noise approach in Sec.
2.1
. However, already the systematic interaction term in Eq. (
46
) is not of simple mean-field type in the sense that one could treat
m_{1}
as a fixed order parameter. This is because, even if in (
46
) just one of the
\tilde{\sigma}_{j}
changes sign – corresponding to a single spin flip during Monte Carlo dynamics – this will change the systematic interaction term
ce^{-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}}
by a substantial amount, specifically by a factor
e^{2}
or
e^{-2}
. Provided that
z_{i}<0
, the quantity being averaged on the r.h.s. of (
46
) can therefore easily change sign.
This implies that correlations between spins will not generically be weak even for large
N
, so that mean-field approximations do not apply.

Nonetheless, the regime of perfect recall (
m_{1}\to 1
) can be demarcated relatively easily as it corresponds to
c=1
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}=1
for all
i
. This is indeed a solution of (
46
) provided that
z_{i}>-e^{-1}
for all
i
. Taking the
z_{i}
to be independent and identically distributed variables and requiring the probability of
all

z_{i}
being above
-e^{-1}
to be close to unity gives the condition

[1-\mathbb{P}(z<-e^{-1})]^{N}=1-\delta

(49)

with some small
\delta
, or equivalently

\mathbb{P}(z<-e^{-1})=\delta/N.

(50)

Calculating the moments of the
z_{i}
from the relevant quenched averages as in Eqs. (
13
)), we find that they have zero mean and
variance
s^{2}=e^{-2}P[(1+e^{-4})/2]^{N-1}=e^{-2}\gamma
with
\gamma
as defined in (
39
) (for
r=1
).
The constraint (
50
) thus translates into

\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{erfc}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}se}\right)=\dfrac{\delta}{N}.

(51)

Given the smallness of the r.h.s., one can use the asymptotic form of the complementary error function to simplify this to

\frac{se}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1/(2s^{2}e^{2})}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}}e^{-1/(2\gamma)}=\delta/N,

(52)

with asymptotic (large N) solution

\gamma=\frac{1}{2\ln(N/\delta)}\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad P\equiv P^{*}(N)=\frac{1}{2\ln(N/\delta)}\left(\frac{2}{1+e^{-4}}\right)^{N-1}.

(53)

This result is essentially identical to the estimate (
18
) for the storage capacity provided by the signal-to-noise analysis, the difference being only in logarithmic factors. The choice of
\delta
is essentially arbitrary but one could choose e.g.
\delta=N^{-a}
with
a>0
so that the probability that recall will fail vanishes as
N\to 0
. This would give
[2(1+a)\ln N]^{-1}
as the prefactor in (
53
), illustrating the weak dependence on the specific choice of
\delta
. We note finally that the independence of the
z_{i}
assumed above is a simplification. This can be removed by a more elaborate calculation along the lines shown in the Appendix but again only modifies the prefactor in (
53
).

3
Conclusions

Recently, since the explosion of social media and the digital era, several major corporations have been struggling with massive data storage, exploring new frontiers for data handling such as cooling data centers under the ocean
Oceano1
;
Oceano2
. In this context, beyond empirical approaches, a theoretical solution lies in crafting neural networks with massive storage capacity. In this research field we may locate the so-called
exponential Hopfield models
, namely modern Hebbian networks that – if equipped with
N
neurons – can store an exponential (in
N
) number of patterns
P
. Such models have appeared in rapid succession in the recent literature, using both binary
ExpHop1
and real-valued neurons
ExpHop2
;
LucibelloMezard
.

In these short notes we have introduced and studied another cost function for an exponential Hopfield model: based on the (extensive) standard loss functions
\mathcal{L}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2N}||\bm{\xi}^{\mu}-\bm{\sigma}||^{2}
, one per pattern, we defined the overall cost function of the network
as
-N\sum_{\mu}^{P}\exp\left(-N\mathcal{L}^{\mu}\right)
, using the exponentials of the
L_{2}
-loss functions rather than these loss function themselves. As the exponent can be written as
-N\mathcal{L}^{\mu}=N(m_{\mu}-1)
in terms of the Mattis magnetizations
m_{\mu}
, the underlying motivation for this choice of cost function is that minimizing it should force one of the
m_{\mu}
towards unity:
this defines the
perfect recall regime
that we mainly focussed on.

The main reward of this variation on a theme is that the resulting network can store an exponential number of patterns
3
3

3

There are logarithmic corrections to this,
as in all the other exponential models
ExpHop1
;
ExpHop2
and also in the standard reference
Bovier
.
. Even if we insist on enlarging their basins of attraction to facilitate the reconstruction of patterns from noisy inputs, the storage capacity remains exponential, though of course the relevant exponent decreases.

In future work it will be interesting to deepen the role of replica symmetry breaking in the network: very preliminary results seem to suggest that this does not play a major role, at least within the retrieval region

KanterSompo
and the sleepy Hopfield model – or dreaming neural network –
AlbertDreaming
, where dreaming destroys the spin glass region
AlbertD2

. A similar phenomenon occurs in conventional spin glasses. Here the solution for the pairwise scenario captured by the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
