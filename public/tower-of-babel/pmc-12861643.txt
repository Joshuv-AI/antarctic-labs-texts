My fibromyalgia journey
Authors: Helen Robinson
Published: 2025

My journey with fibromyalgia began in late 2013. I began experiencing chronic pain, particularly in my neck and shoulders, and a general feeling of being unwell.

I went to my GP on many occasions and explained my symptoms. As a result, I was sent for X-rays and blood tests, which all came back as normal. I was then sent for physiotherapy as my pain spread to my neck, shoulders and legs. Physiotherapy was unbearable – it was extremely painful during the sessions and, in the days that followed, only seemed to exacerbate the pain.

I continued to go to my GP, explaining my symptoms, and each time I explained that I knew something was wrong with me. On one particular visit, I again explained all my symptoms, and the doctor turned to me and said, ‘I don’t know what more you want me to do for you’, she just sat looking at me wanting me to provide an answer as she said nothing. The consultation ended and I was left in tears, and I vowed I would never go to the doctors again.

Over time my pain and fatigue continued, and I became depressed and anxious, I felt like I was losing my mind and left wondering ‘why wasn’t I believed’.

I knew that there was something wrong with me, but nobody believed me, and my needs were not being met, therefore I re-engaged with the doctors in an act of desperation. I was seen by a new GP at the practice. She took time to listen to me, prescribed me medication and she said that I should go back to see her in a couple of weeks to see if there had been any improvement in my symptoms. Sadly, there was no improvement, and I was then prescribed an alternative medication; again I went away for a couple of weeks as before, to see if things improved. Unfortunately, I suffered multiple side effects. Therefore, I went back to my GP and was prescribed another medication, and this was the first time I was told I may have ‘fibromyalgia’; I was advised not to search for fFibromyalgia’ on the internet, however I was not given any further information/leaflets. Subsequently, I was left wondering and unsure of what to do, or how to manage this condition.

After this appointment, I was referred to a specialist pain centre. While waiting for this appointment, I again went to my GP regarding the pain, and they increased my medication – something I later realised should never have happened. At this point, I was also prescribed two further medications to help manage the pain. One of those medications helped my mood, but the pain continued.

After years of suffering constant pain, feeling depressed and anxious, my life changed dramatically. I had to give up my job, which was something I loved to do, but the pain and fatigue were so unbearable that I spent most of my days in bed, yet was unable to sleep due to the pain. I rarely left the house.

I was later treated by a specialist pain centre and my fibromyalgia journey changed. I was finally understood, I was taken seriously, and most importantly I felt that I was being listened to. During my care at this pain centre, I was prescribed multiple different medications. Unfortunately, the side effects of each of these medications forced me to stop taking them. I also attended a pain management programme and found the advice on pacing yourself, gentle exercise and relaxation techniques helpful.

Once I was discharged from the specialist pain centre, I was back in the care of my GP. This again became troublesome due to the lack of continuity of care. It also became particularly tiring because every time I saw a new doctor; I would have to explain my symptoms repeatedly, as they were unaware of the effects of fibromyalgia. I also found fibromyalgia became the excuse for every ailment I went to the doctors with, even if it was a separate matter with no linked symptoms to fibromyalgia.

I had, for many years, thought that one of my medications was causing some issues. I had gained approximately 4 stone in weight, had chronic sweating, I was pre-diabetic, and had high liver function, and my pain was still bad. I questioned this several times with my GP and I was asked ‘Why was I against it?’. After doing my own research on this medication, I found that the amount I was prescribed was usually for somebody suffering with severe depression and although it was able to be prescribed for fibromyalgia, my prescribed dosage exceeded what was recommended. Therefore, I decided to come off this medication and in the months following this, I suffered horrendous withdrawal symptomsl however, I lost the weight I had gained (without dieting), my blood sugar levels reduced, my liver function was back to normal and my sweating improved dramatically, and my overall pain remained the same as when I was on this medication.

My journey with fibromyalgia has been one of mixed blessings. I have been fortunate to work alongside doctors and researchers as a patient participant in fibromyalgia studies. However, it has also been a lonely journey due to the lack of understanding, and the delay in diagnosis. I also found that there is a general assumption with fibromyalgia that because you look well, there’s nothing wrong with you.

I live with fibromyalgia every day, and I try to manage it with painkillers, alternative therapies and with the people who know me best, such as my husband and my daughters.

I know one day a specific treatment will be found for fibromyalgia that targets the specific symptoms. And through education and communication, I hope that people who present with fibromyalgia symptoms will be heard, understood, and given the correct treatment.
