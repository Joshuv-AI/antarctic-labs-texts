A Finite-Sample Analysis of Distributionally Robust Average-Reward Reinforcement Learning
Zachary Roch, Chi Zhang, George K. Atia, Yue Wang

Abstract
Robust reinforcement learning (RL) under the average-reward criterion is crucial for long-term decision making under potential environment mismatches, yet its finite-sample complexity study remains largely unexplored. Existing works offer algorithms with asymptotic guarantees, but the absence of finite-sample analysis hinders its principled understanding and practical deployment, especially in data-limited settings. We close this gap by proposing Robust Halpern Iteration (RHI) , the first algorithm with provable finite-sample complexity guarantee. Under standard uncertainty sets – including contamination sets and ℓ p \ell_{p} -norm balls – RHI attains an ε \varepsilon -optimal policy with near-optimal sample complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( S ​ A ​ ℋ 2 ε 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{S\,A\,\mathcal{H}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right) , where S S and A A denote the numbers of states and actions, and ℋ \mathcal{H} is the robust optimal bias span. This result gives the first polynomial sample complexity guarantee for robust average-reward RL. Moreover, our RHI’s independence from prior knowledge distinguishes it from many previous average-reward RL studies. Our work thus constitutes a significant advancement in enhancing the practical applicability of robust average-reward methods to complex, real-world problems.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) aims to optimize an agent’s performance by identifying a policy that maximizes cumulative rewards, according to a specified criterion, while interacting with an environment. Despite remarkable success in applications such as board games
(
Silver et al. 2016
;
Zha et al. 2021
)
and video games
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022a
)
, standard RL often experiences significant performance degradation when deployed in practical settings. This phenomenon, known as the "Sim-to-Real" gap
(
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Peng et al. 2018
;
Tobin et al. 2017
)
, arises from discrepancies between the training environment (simulation) and the deployment environment (real world). In simulation-based applications like games, where these environments are typically identical, standard RL performs well. However, in many real-world scenarios, factors such as modeling errors, environmental perturbations, and potential adversarial attacks introduce model mismatches
(
Henderson et al. 2018
;
Rajeswaran et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
. These discrepancies can lead to suboptimal policies and poor performance, undermining the reliability of RL in practical applications.

To address this reliability challenge, the framework of (distributionally) robust RL was introduced
(
Bagnell et al. 2001
;
Nilim and El Ghaoui 2004
;
Iyengar 2005
)
. It differs from standard RL by considering a set of possible environment transition dynamics (an uncertainty set) instead of assuming a single, fixed model. The goal in distributionally robust RL is typically to optimize performance under the worst-case scenario across all models within this set. This approach provides performance guarantees across the range of uncertain environments considered, making the resulting policy more resilient to model mismatches and enhancing its generalizability.

Beyond the challenge of robustness, the choice of reward criterion significantly influences the RL problem formulation and solution. The discounted-reward criterion is the most extensively studied, offering elegant mathematical properties. However, its inherent focus on short-term rewards, due to the exponential decay factor, can sometimes lead to suboptimal long-term performance
(
Schwartz 1993
;
Seijen and Sutton 2014
;
Tsitsiklis and Roy 1997
;
Abounadi et al. 2001
)
. In contrast, many practical applications, including queuing control, financial portfolio optimization, and communication networks
(
Kober et al. 2013
;
Lu et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Moody and Saffell 2001
;
Charpentier et al. 2021
;
Masoudi 2021
;
Li and Hai 2024
)
, necessitate evaluating policies based on their long-term average performance. This highlights the importance of optimizing the long-term average reward, particularly in environments subject to uncertainty, motivating our focus in this paper on robust RL under the average-reward criterion.

Robust RL under the average-reward criterion, despite its practical significance, presents considerable challenges compared to its discounted-reward counterpart, primarily due to its dependence on the limiting behavior of underlying stochastic processes. Consequently, this area remains relatively understudied. Recent work by
(
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Grand-Clement et al. 2023
)
further underscores these inherent difficulties, highlighting issues such as the non-contractive nature of the associated Bellman operator, the high dimensionality of the solution space, and the instability of common iteration-based algorithms. A critical gap persists in this domain: existing studies on robust average-reward RL are predominantly asymptotic, leaving its crucial finite-sample properties largely unexplored.

A natural and promising avenue to address this gap and enable finite-sample analysis is to reduce average-reward problems to their discounted counterparts and leverage the substantial body of work on robust discounted-reward RL, adapting techniques from non-robust reduction studies
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Zurek and Chen 2023
)
. This "reduction" approach is theoretically grounded in the convergence of the robust discounted value function to the average-reward value function as the discount factor approaches
1
, given mild assumptions
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
. However, while these reduction methods show promise, it is unknown how to concretely implement it and if the reduction can result in near-optimal sample complexity for robust RL. Moreover, in the non-robust studies, implementations of such a reduction typically rely on prior knowledge of certain underlying characteristics (related to e.g., the optimal bias span
(
Zurek and Chen 2023
)
or the mixing time
(
Wang et al. 2023c
)
) of the underlying Markov Decision Process (MDP), which is unrealistic in most real-world scenarios.

In this paper, we propose a direct approach, Robust Halpern Iteration (RHI), which enables practical implementations and near-optimal sample complexity. Our contributions are summarized as follows.

Analysis of limitations in reduction-based approaches.
We first propose a reduction-based framework, which reduces the robust average-reward RL problem to a robust discounted RL problem. We then analyze its key limitations, focusing on information requirements and resulting sample complexity. We highlight two primary challenges:
(1) Ensuring the discounted solution is near-optimal for the average-reward case often requires a discount factor extremely close to 1. When coupled with existing sample complexity bounds for robust discounted MDPs, this requirement can impose a sub-optimal sample complexity for the average-reward problem, creating a challenging trade-off between optimality guarantees and computational feasibility.
(2) Similarly to non-robust settings, reduction approaches aiming for better sample complexity typically require knowledge of quantities like the optimal robust span (defined later). This requirement for prior knowledge is generally impractical, limiting the applicability of such methods.

Design and analysis of robust Halpern iteration (RHI).
Motivated by the challenges of reduction approaches, we design and analyze RHI, a direct algorithm for robust average-reward RL inspired by Halpern Iteration in the optimization literature
(
Halpern 1967
;
Lieder 2021
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
. Key technical contributions include introducing a quotient space to manage the high dimensionality of the robust Bellman equation’s solution space and developing a model-free

Related work

Robust RL with average reward.
Studies on robust RL with average reward are relatively limited. Early research focused on dynamic programming (DP) methods in robust Average-reward MDPs (AMDPs). These investigations, initiated by
(
Tewari and Bartlett 2007
)
for specific finite-interval uncertainty sets, were subsequently extended to more general uncertainty models in works such as
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Grand-Clement et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
. These foundational studies were instrumental in revealing the fundamental structure of robust AMDPs and illustrating their connections to robust Discounted MDPs (DMDPs). As an alternative method,
(
Chatterjee et al. 2023
)
recently proposed a game-theoretic approach for finding the optimal policy. Building on the understanding of robust AMDP structures, the focus also extended to learning algorithms, where
(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
introduced a model-free algorithm with asymptotic convergence guarantees.

However, all of these aforementioned approaches – whether planning-based, game-theoretic, or model-free learning – focus on asymptotic convergence only, leaving finite-sample complexity analyses largely unaddressed. One of the few results concerning finite-sample complexity stems from
(
Grand-Clément and Petrik 2024
)
, which developed a reduction-based framework centered on the Blackwell optimal factor. As discussed further in
Section

4
, this method, unfortunately, leads to an exponentially large sample complexity. More recently,
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
also provided a finite-sample analysis for robust average-reward RL; however, they focus on policy evaluation problems (i.e., estimating the robust average reward for a fixed policy), rather than finding an optimal policy.

Consequently, obtaining efficient, polynomial-time finite-sample guarantees for learning optimal policies in robust AMDPs remains an open and critical challenge, which this paper aims to address.

Robust RL with discounted rewards.

Robust DMDPs were first studied in foundational works such as
(
Iyengar 2005
;
Nilim and El Ghaoui 2004
;
Bagnell et al. 2001
;
Wiesemann et al. 2013
;
Lim et al. 2013
)
. These initial investigations typically assumed a fully known uncertainty set and developed solutions based on robust DP. Since then, extensive theoretical research has significantly adapted and extended these concepts to various learning paradigms where the uncertainty set or the nominal model might be unknown or learned from data.
Prominent research directions include analyses in settings with generative models
(
Yang et al. 2022
;
Panaganti and Kalathil 2022
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023d
;
Liang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2022b
;
Wang et al. 2023e
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2023f
;
Kumar et al. 2023
;
Derman et al. 2021
)
, investigations into offline learning from fixed datasets
(
Shi and Chi 2022
;
Liu and Xu 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Wang et al. 2024d
)
, and developments within online learning frameworks involving exploration
(
Wang and Zou 2021
;
Lu et al. 2024
)
. A key focus across these diverse settings is often to provide rigorous finite-sample complexity guarantees or convergence rates, characterized under different assumptions regarding the structure of the uncertainty set and the nature of data access.

Non-robust RL with average reward.

The study of non-robust AMDPs originated with foundational model-based DP techniques, such as Policy Iteration and Value Iteration, which assume a known model
(
Puterman 2014
;
Bertsekas 2011
)
. Subsequently, research shifted towards model-free RL algorithms. These include adaptations of Q-learning and SARSA, like RVI Q-learning
(
Abounadi et al. 2001
;
Wan et al. 2021
;
Wan and Sutton 2022
)
, designed to learn optimal policies directly from interaction data without requiring explicit model knowledge
(
Dewanto et al. 2020
)
.

Beyond asymptotic convergence, sample complexity for achieving near-optimal policies in AMDPs is extensively studied. A significant body of work is based on the reduction framework, which transforms the AMDP into a DMDP using a carefully chosen discount factor. However, selecting an appropriate discount factor typically requires prior knowledge of crucial MDP parameters, such as the span of the bias function
(
Zurek and Chen 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Zurek and Chen 2024
;
Sapronov and Yudin 2024
;
Jin and Sidford 2021
)
or various mixing time constants
(
Wang et al. 2023c
;
Wang et al. 2023g
)
. Notable progress has been made under such assumptions, for instance,
(
Zurek and Chen 2023
;
Sapronov and Yudin 2024
)
demonstrate that if the bias span is known and used to set the reduction factor, the resulting sample complexity matches the minimax optimal rate for weakly communicating MDPs
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
.
Alongside reduction-based methods, direct approaches that do not involve conversion to DMDPs, but still require prior knowledge, have also been recently developed
(
Zhang and Xie 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
)
. Recognizing that the prerequisite of prior knowledge can be restrictive and impractical, and that estimating these parameters accurately is challenging
(
Tuynman et al. 2024
)
, another line of research investigates AMDPs without prior knowledge, achieving sub-optimal sample complexity
(
Lee et al. 2025
;
Jin et al. 2024a
;
Lee and Ryu 2025
;
Tuynman et al. 2024
)
.

Extending these diverse frameworks and insights to robust AMDPs is, however, particularly challenging. This difficulty stems from the greater complexity inherent in the robust average-reward paradigm, including issues such as the non-linearity of the robust Bellman operator and a more intricate, high-dimensional solution space for the robust Bellman equation
(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
.

First attempt: a reduction framework for robust AMDPs

Although recent works have designed algorithms with asymptotic convergence guarantee
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
, the finite sample analysis of robust AMDPs remains largely under-explored.
To analyze the sample complexity, a natural starting point is to leverage insights from the extensive literature on robust RL under the discounted-reward criterion.
Such approaches are based on the following result.

Lemma 4.1
.

(
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
)
Assume that the robust AMDP satisfies Assumption
3.1
, then

\lim_{\gamma\to 1}(1-\gamma)\,V^{\pi}_{\gamma,\mathcal{P}}\;=\;g^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}},\quad\lim_{\gamma\to 1}\max_{\pi}(1-\gamma)\,V^{\pi}_{\gamma,\mathcal{P}}\;=\;g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}.

(12)

The results imply that, under the assumption, the performance of discounted robust RL asymptotically converges to that of the average-reward formulation as the discount factor approaches 1. Hence, a robust AMDP can be approximately solved via its corresponding robust DMDP with a sufficiently large discount factor. In particular,
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
proves that there exists a discount factor
\gamma_{\mathrm{reduction}}
such that an
\epsilon
-optimal policy for the robust DMDP with discount
\gamma_{\mathrm{reduction}}
is also
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
-optimal for the robust AMDP, and thus the robust AMDP can be reduced to a DMDP. We note that a similar reduction idea has also been explored for non-robust AMDPs
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023c
;
Wang et al. 2023g
;
Zurek and Chen 2023
;
Sapronov and Yudin 2024
)
. These works show that, with a suitable reduction factor depending on the optimal span (
H\triangleq\textbf{Sp}(h^{\pi^{\star}}_{\mathsf{P}})
), the non-robust AMDP can be solved through the corresponding DMDP, with a (near-)optimal sample complexity.

Although promising and straightforward, there is no study on the choice of
\gamma_{\mathrm{reduction}}
and consequential sample complexity for robust AMDPs. In the following result, we close this gap by providing a concrete choice of the reduction factor.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, let
\gamma_{\mathrm{reduction}}=1-\frac{\epsilon}{\mathcal{H}}
.
This implies that any
\epsilon_{\gamma}
-optimal policy

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,\gamma_{\mathrm{reduction}})

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
-optimal policy for the associated robust AMDP:

\displaystyle g_{\mathcal{P}}^{\pi^{*}}-g_{\mathcal{P}}^{\hat{\pi}_{\gamma}}\leq\left(8+\frac{5\epsilon_{\gamma}}{\mathcal{H}}\right)\epsilon.

Based on
Theorem

4.2

(

;

)

\gamma_{bw}
when the nominal kernels are rational, i.e.,
\mathsf{P}^{a}_{s}=n_{s,a}/m_{s,a}
with
n_{s,a},m_{s,a}\in\mathbb{N}
:
\gamma_{bw}\;\leq\;1-\frac{C}{S^{S}m^{S^{2}}}
,
where
m
is the smallest denominator among all kernel entries. However, coupling this bound with existing sample-complexity results for robust DMDPs even yields exponential sample complexity for robust AMDPs.

We conjecture that, the high complexity is not due to our loose bound in the reduction method
Theorem

4.2
. A similar framework is also developed under non-robust AMDPs
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, and it is shown that the large complexity is due to the loose bounds for DMDPs
(
Wang et al. 2023c
;
Wang et al. 2023g
;
Zurek and Chen 2023
;
Sapronov and Yudin 2024
)
. Thus, it is still unclear whether the reduction approach can achieve better or near-optimal complexity in robust AMDPs.

The second limitation of the reduction approach is that, implementing it
requires prior knowledge
of the optimal robust span
\mathcal{H}
. Note that
\mathcal{H}
is defined over the whole uncertainty set, thus it can be impractical to assume that it is known. Moreover, estimating such a parameter can also be challenging, even in the non-robust case
(
Zurek and Chen 2023
;
Tuynman et al. 2024
)
. Consequently, even if the reduction approach can achieve near-optimal complexity, the accompanying information requirements could render the approach impractical.

5
Robust Halpern iteration (RHI) for robust AMDPs

Identifying issues of the reduction framework as discussed above, a natural question arises:

Can we design an algorithm that solves robust AMDPs with near-optimal sample complexity without requiring any additional assumptions or prior knowledge?

In this section, we answer this question affirmatively by designing our robust Halpern Iteration (RHI) algorithm. We will show later that, our RHI algorithm does not require any prior information of robust AMDP, and achieves a near-optimal sample complexity.

As discussed in
Section

3
, finding the optimal policy for a robust AMDP is equivalent to solving the corresponding robust Bellman equation (
10
):
Q=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g^{*}}(Q)=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)-g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)=r+\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}(\max_{\mathcal{A}}Q)
. However, solving this equation is highly challenging. Firstly, the equation has two unknown variables:
Q
and
g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}
; Since
g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}
is unknown, the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}}
is not readily feasible. Moreover, the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g}
is a non-expansion, invalidating the previous methods for discounted settings. Finally, the non-linear structure of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g}
(compared to linear structure of non-robust operator) further results in a complicated solution space to the Bellman equation
(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
. In the following, we address these challenges sequentially, and propose our RHI algorithm.

We first claim that, even if we do not know the value of
g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}
, we can still obtain the optimal policy through a proximal equation. Our claim is based on the following result, where we show that a near-optimal policy can be identified by approximating the solution to the robust Bellman equation (
10
) w.r.t. the Span semi-norm.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
and
\pi
be the greedy policy w.r.t.
Q
, i.e.,
\pi(s)\in\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s,a).
Then, for every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, it holds that

\displaystyle 0\leq g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}-g^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\leq\textbf{Sp}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}}(Q)-Q)=\textbf{Sp}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)-Q).

(13)

The result thus implies that, to obtain the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, exactly solving (
10
) is not necessary; Instead, it suffices to find a weaker solution
Q
such that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}}(Q)-Q=ce
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
and the all-one vector
e=(1,...,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
(note that the solution
Q
to (
10
) also satisfies the equation with
c=0
). Moreover, we can show that the above equation, and hence finding the optimal policy, are further equivalent to solving the proximal equation that only contains one variable:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)-Q=ce,\text{ for some }c\in\mathbb{R}.

(14)

Since it is sufficient to find an arbitrary solution to (
14
), inspired by previous studies of non-robust AMDPs
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
, we instead consider the embedded equation in the quotient space w.r.t. identical vectors. Namely, we define a relation between two vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
:
v\sim w
if
v-w=ce
for some
c
, which can be directly verified to be an equivalence relation. We thus construct the quotient space
E\triangleq\mathbb{R}^{SA}/\sim
, and the embedded equation of (
14
) on
E
becomes:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)]=[Q],\text{ where }[\cdot]\text{ denotes the equivalence class of }\cdot.

(15)

So far, we have shown that solving a robust AMDP is equivalent to solving the equation (
15
) in the quotient space
E
. Notably, this equation only contains one variable and has a much easier structure.

However, in contrast to the discounted operator, the robust Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}
is not a contraction but only a non-expansion, even in the quotient space
E
. This invalidates the previous approaches for the discounted setting, which utilize the Banach-Picard iteration to find the unique fixed point of the discounted contracted operator. To address this issue and find a solution to the non-expansion equation (
14
), we adopt the Halpern iteration
(
Halpern 1967
)
from the stochastic approximation area. Namely, to solve an equation
x=T(x)
for a non-expansion operator
x
, the Halpern iteration recursively updates the algorithms through
x^{k+1}=(1-\beta_{k+1})x^{0}+\beta_{k+1}T(x^{k})
, which is a convex combination between
T(x^{k})
and the initialization
x^{0}
. Halpern iteration has been studied in optimization areas
(
Halpern 1967
;
Sabach and Shtern 2017
;
Lieder 2021
;
Park and Ryu 2022
;
Contreras and Cominetti 2023
)
and more recently in non-robust RL
(
Lee et al. 2025
;
Lee and Ryu 2025
)
.

Based on the Halpern iteration, we can similarly develop our RHI algorithm, whose update rule is
[Q^{k+1}]=[(1-\beta_{k+1})Q^{0}+\beta_{k+1}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)]
. We show in the following result that it will converge to some solution to (
15
), and hence find an optimal policy, when the robust AMDP is known.

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider the exact robust Halpern iteration
[Q^{k+1}]=[(1-\beta_{k+1})Q^{0}+\beta_{k+1}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)]
, with
\beta_{k}=\frac{k}{k+2}
. Set
\pi_{k}
to be the greedy policy w.r.t.
Q^{k}
. Then

\displaystyle\textbf{Sp}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{k})-Q^{k})\rightarrow 0,\text{ and }g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}-g^{\pi_{k}}\to 0,\text{ as }k\to\infty.

(16)

This result hence implies the asymptotic convergence of our RHI algorithm, when we exactly know the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
. However, in the learning setting where we do not know the worst-case kernel, we only have access to samples from the nominal kernel. Based on this, we propose a data-driven variant of our RHI algorithm, and develop sample complexity analysis.

However, estimating the robust Bellman operator with nominal samples is challenging. Note that the robust Bellman operator captures the dynamics under the worst-case transition kernel, which is generally different from the nominal kernel. To address this issue, a multi-level Monte-Carlo (MLMC) approach was introduced in previous works
(
Liu et al. 2022b
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
. However, MLMC generally results in an infinitely large sample complexity, and only guarantees asymptotic convergence, hence it cannot be applied.

To effectively estimate the robust Bellman operator while maintaining a traceable sample complexity, we propose a recursive sampling technique, inspired by
(
Lee et al. 2025
;
Jin et al. 2024b
)
. Namely, we utilize the nominal samples to estimate the difference between two steps:
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{k})-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{k-1})
. This sampling scheme allows us to re-use the samples from previous steps, and hence improves sample efficiency. Based on this technique, we design a concrete sampling subroutines,
R-SAMPLE
, for two types of uncertainty set: contamination model (
4
) and
\ell_{p}
-norm model (
5
). With this, we further propose our RHI algorithm in
Algorithm

1
. In our algorithm, we utilize the sampling scheme to estimate the difference between two steps, and then re-use the estimation
T^{k-1}
of the Bellman operator for the previous step to construct the estimation
T^{k}
for the current step.

We then derive the sample complexity analysis for our RHI algorithm.

Theorem 5.3

(Performance of RHI)
.

Consider a robust AMDP defined by contamination or
\ell_{p}
-norm, satisfying
3.1
. Set the step sizes
c_{k}=5(k+2)\ln^{2}(k+2)
and
\beta_{k}=k/(k+2)
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the output policy
\pi^{n}
is
\epsilon
-optimal:

\displaystyle g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}-g^{\pi^{n}}_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\leq\epsilon,

(17)

as long as the total iteration number
n
exceeds
\frac{\mathcal{H}}{\epsilon}
, resulting in the total sample complexity of

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{SA\mathcal{H}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(18)

This result is the first finite sample complexity guarantee for robust AMDPs, indicating the sample efficiency of our algorithm to find an
\epsilon
-optimal policy using
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{SA\mathcal{H}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
samples. In contrast to
Section

4
, our algorithm does not require any prior knowledge of
\mathcal{H}.

The minimax optimal sample complexity for
non-robust
AMDPs is shown to be
\tilde{\Omega}\left(\frac{SAH}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

(
Wang et al. 2022
)
. Noting that non-robust AMDPs are special cases of robust ones, we conjugate that the minimax optimal complexity for robust ones is
\tilde{\Omega}\left(\frac{SA\mathcal{H}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
. Thus, our sample complexity result matches this conjugated optimal complexity in all terms except for
\mathcal{H}
, and is hence near-optimal. We also highlight that, the minimax optimal complexity for non-robust AMDPs is obtained only with prior knowledge
(
Zurek and Chen 2023
;
Sapronov and Yudin 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023c
;
Wang et al. 2023g
)
. When there is no such knowledge, non-robust algorithms also are sub-optimal
(
Jin et al. 2024a
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
. We leave it as future research to investigate the minimax lower bound for robust AMDPs, if it is achievable without any prior knowledge, and if it can be extended to other uncertainty sets.

7
Conclusion

Robust reinforcement learning under the average-reward criterion suffers from the significant challenge of developing efficient algorithms with finite-sample guarantees, thus hindering its application in data-limited environments. This generally resulted from complexity of the problem setting and the limitations of prior approaches, which often relied on asymptotic analyses, yielded exponential sample complexity, or required impractical prior knowledge.
Therefore, we
introduced Robust Halpern Iteration (RHI), a novel model-free algorithm for finding near-optimal policies in robust AMDPs. A key advantage of RHI is its ability to bypass the need for prior knowledge of specific MDP parameters–a common prerequisite for many previous methods. We theoretically established that RHI achieves a polynomial sample complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{SA\mathcal{H}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
to find an
\epsilon
-optimal policy, under the contamination and
\ell_{p}
-norm uncertainty sets. Our result is the first polynomial and near-optimal sample complexity guarantee for learning optimal policies in robust AMDPs, enhancing the applicability of average-reward robust RL in those data-intensive and real-world applications.

.

Assume that the robust-AMDP satisfies

\displaystyle g_{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}

Appendix B
Sampling algorithm

In this section, we present a method to approximate the robust Bellman operator
T^{k}\approx\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{k})
by sampling from the nominal kernel
P_{0}
.

Denote
\hat{P}_{s,a}^{k}
the worst-case kernel at iteration
k
for a state-action pair
(s,a)
, and define
h^{k}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s,a)
. If we are able to sample from the worst-case kernel, the robust Bellman operator can be expressed as:

\displaystyle T^{k}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\frac{1}{m^{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{k}}h^{k}(\hat{s}_{j}),

(66)

where
\{\hat{s}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m^{k}}\sim\hat{P}_{s,a}^{k}

\hat{P}_{s,a}^{k}

P_{0}
at each iteration in RHI. To address this limitation,
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
provide a feasible solution by introducing a penalty term to the Bellman operator on the nominal kernel. This allows us to express the robust Bellman operator equivalently as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{k})=r+P_{0}\max_{\mathcal{A}}Q^{k}-R\kappa(\max_{\mathcal{A}}Q^{k}),

(67)

P_{0}

(68)

where
\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m^{k}}\sim P_{0}(\cdot|s,a)
.
To facilitate the sampling process, we define the difference terms
d^{k}=h^{k}-h^{k-1}
and
K(h^{k})=\kappa(h^{k})-\kappa(h^{k-1})
. By adding it to the previous estimate

\displaystyle T^{k}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\frac{1}{m^{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{k}}d^{k}(s_{j})-RK(h^{k}).

This implies that
\sigma_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(h^{k})=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\hat{P}^{k}_{s,a}}[h^{k}(s^{\prime})].
At each iteration and for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S\times A},
let
D^{i}
be the sampling process on the nominal kernel
P_{0}.
With this formulation, at each iteration
k
,
h^{k}
is computed under its respective worst case kernel via the introduction of a penalty from
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
. With a slight abuse of notation, we can compactly denote this difference between iterations
i\leq k
as
d^{i}=h^{i}-h^{i-1}
or more formally for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(h^{i})-\sigma_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(h^{i-1})=P_{0}d^{i}-R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(h^{i})+R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(h^{i-1}),

(73)

where the
R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(\cdot)
terms come from
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
. This implies that
D^{i}(s,a)\approx\sigma_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(h^{i})=\min_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}P^{\top}h^{i}.
Since
h^{-1}=0
by the initialization of RHI, we have the robust Bellman operator as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{k})(s,a)

\displaystyle=r(s,a)+\sigma_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(h^{k}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=r(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\hat{P}^{k}_{s,a}}[h^{k}(s^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=r(s,a)-R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(h^{k},s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P_{0}(s^{\prime}|s,a)h(s^{\prime}),

(74)

where the final equality holds by adapting Theorem 8 from
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
for the infinite-horizon average reward case considering only uncertainty in the transitions (reward uncertainty is
0
).
This equality between the robust Bellman operator and the non-robust Bellman operator implies that we need a way to bound the sampling error from the nominal kernel. To show this difference, we further define
R_{s,a}K(h^{i})\triangleq R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(h^{i})-R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(h^{i-1})
. Recall that

T^{k}=r+\sum^{k}_{i=0}\big(D^{i}-RK(h^{i})\big)

and that, at iteration
k
and state-action pair
(s,a),
we sample the next state for
j=1,2,\dots,m_{k}
times, i.e.
s^{s,a}_{k,j}\sim P_{0}(\cdot|s,a).
This gives us an empirical estimate for the given state-action pair
D^{k}(s,a)=\frac{1}{m^{k}}\sum^{m_{k}}_{j=1}d^{k}(s^{s,a}_{k,j})-R_{s,a}K(h^{k},s,a).
Due to initialization of RHI,
T^{-1}=r
and
h^{-1}=0.
Then for each
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},
we can subtract (
74
) from
T^{k}
to find via telescoping that,

\displaystyle T^{k}(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{k})(s,a)

\displaystyle=\bigg(r(s,a)+\sum^{k}_{i=0}\big(D^{i}(s,a)-R_{s,a}K(h^{i},s,a)\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\bigg(r(s,a)-R_{s,a}\kappa_{q}(h^{k},s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P_{0}(s^{\prime}|s,a)h^{k}(s^{\prime})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Big(D^{i}(s,a)-P_{0}d^{i}(s^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\zeta^{i}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{k}(s,a).

(75)

This holds due to
\sum^{k}_{i=0}-RK(h^{i})=-R\kappa_{q}(h^{k})
which cancels out perfectly with
R\kappa_{q}(h^{k})
from
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{k}).
With this, we first estimate
\mathbb{P}(\|\varsigma^{k}(s,a)\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon),\,\forall(s,a)
by adapting the arguments of the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality as done in
(
Lee et al. 2025
)
.
Now consider the filtration
\mathcal{F}^{k}=\sigma(\{D^{i}\}^{k}_{i=0}).
Since
h^{k},\ d^{k},\text{ and }m_{k}
are
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
-measurable, it immediately follows that
\mathbb{E}[\zeta^{k}(s,a)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=0
for all
(s,a)
during sampling due to the equivalence between the robust and non-robust Bellman operators
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
. This subsequently implies that the sequence
\{\varsigma^{k}(s,a)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a
\mathcal{F}^{k}
-martingale. Using Markov’s inequality and the tower property of conditional expectation yields that for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S\times A}
and
\lambda>0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\varsigma^{k}(s,a)\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\lambda\epsilon}\mathbb{E}[\text{exp}(\lambda\varsigma^{k}(s,a))]

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\big[\text{exp}(\lambda\varsigma^{k-1}(s,a))\mathbb{E}[\text{exp}(\lambda\zeta^{k}(s,a))|\mathcal{F}^{k-1}]\big].

(76)

Since the penalty terms cancel out when telescoping, we can show that conditionally on
\mathcal{F}^{k-1},
the terms
\zeta^{k}_{j}=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\bigg(d^{k}(s^{s,a}_{k,j})-P_{0}d^{k}(s,a)\bigg)
are bounded such that
|\zeta^{k}_{j}|\leq\frac{\|d^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}}{m_{k}}.
This is due to each term being an independent random variable such that
d^{k}(s^{s,a}_{k,j})\in[\min_{s^{\prime}}d^{k}(s^{\prime}),\ \max_{s^{\prime}}d^{k}(s^{\prime})]
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\zeta^{k}(s,a)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=0.
Given this, Hoeffding’s Lemma yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{exp}\lambda\zeta^{k}(s,a)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\displaystyle\Pi}_{j=1}^{m_{k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\text{exp}(\lambda\zeta^{k}_{j})|\mathcal{F}^{k-1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\text{exp}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}_{\text{sp}}/m_{k}\bigg).

(77)

Using (
C
) and (
C
) along with
m_{k}\geq\alpha c_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}_{\text{sp}}/\epsilon^{2},
we can use induction to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{exp}(\lambda\varsigma^{k}(s,a))]\leq\text{exp}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\epsilon^{2}\sum^{k}_{i=0}c_{i}^{-1}/\alpha\bigg).

(78)

Notice that due to the inclusion of the penalty term
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
, the distribution over the sampling trajectories
(\hat{P}^{k}_{s,a}D^{k}_{s,a})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
reduces to the probability distribution over the sampling trajectories
(D^{k}_{s,a})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
from the nominal kernel. Combining this with
\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}c_{i}^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{2},
we have
\mathbb{P}(\varsigma^{k}(s,a)\geq\epsilon)\leq\text{exp}(-\lambda\epsilon+\frac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}\epsilon^{2}/\alpha).
Taking
\lambda=2\alpha/\epsilon
we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\varsigma^{k}(s,a)\geq\epsilon)\leq\text{exp}(-\alpha)=\frac{\delta}{2|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|(n+1)}.

Synonymously, we can find the same bound for
\mathbb{P}(\varsigma^{k}(s,a)\leq-\epsilon)
s.t.
\mathbb{P}(|\varsigma^{k}(s,a)|\geq\epsilon)\leq\delta/(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|(n+1)).
If we apply a union bound over all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S\times A}
we find
\mathbb{P}(\|\varsigma^{k}\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon)\leq\delta/(n+1).

A second union bound over all iterations
k
yields
\mathbb{P}\big(\bigcup^{n}_{k=0}\{\|\varsigma^{k}\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon\}\big)\leq\delta,
Finally, simply taking the complement of this event completes the proof.

∎

Due to our novel addition of the penalty term from
(
Kumar et al. 2023

into the recursive sampling technique used by
(
Lee et al. 2025

, though some modifications are needed to show the robust formulation of it. We proceed with this below.

Going forward we select a concrete choice for sequences

and
\beta_{k}=k/(k+2).
To facilitate this analysis, We fix the solution to the robust Bellman equation as

See that with this formulation and the introduction of the penalty term,

is the solution of both the non-robust and the robust Bellman equations as the penalty cancels out via telescoping.

We also maintain the same notation that RHI uses to generate the sequences and assume that

\displaystyle\|T^{k}(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{k})\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\quad\forall k=0,1,\dots,n\text{, and }(s,a)\in\mathcal{S\times A,}

(79)

which holds with probability
(1-\delta)
by
Proposition

C.2
. See that, due to the equality between the robust and non-robust Bellman operators
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
combined with the cancellation of the penalty term when telescoping, our sampling for

implies that
\|D^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\|d^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}.
We can combine this fact with the nonexpansivity of the map

to imply that

\displaystyle\|T^{k}-T^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}=\|D^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\|d^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}=\|h^{k}-h^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\|Q^{k}_{\mathcal{P}}-Q^{k-1}_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\text{sp}}.

(80)

Before proving our main theorem, we present two preliminary lemmas from

Lemma C.3
.

Assume (
79
) and that the sequences
c_{k}=5(k+2)\ln^{2}(k+2)
and
\beta_{k}=k/(k+2)
hold. Then,

\displaystyle\|Q^{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon k,\quad\forall\ k=0,1,\dots,n.

(81)

Proof.

At iteration
k,

Q^{k}=(1-\beta_{k})Q^{0}+\beta_{k}T^{k-1}
with
\beta_{k}=\frac{k}{k+2}
we have

\displaystyle\|Q^{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta_{k})\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\beta_{k}\|T^{k-1}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{k+2}\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{k}{k+2}\|T^{k-1}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}.

(82)

Recall the proof of
Proposition

C.2
, we see that the regularization term cancels out during the sampling process with the error stemming from the sampling of the nominal kernel. Looking at the term on the right, by using the invariance of
\|\cdot\|_{\text{sp}}
by the addition of constants and the nonexpansivity of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}
for the given seminorm, we can then apply the triangle inequality along with the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{\text{sp}}\leq 2\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and the bound in (
79
) to obtain,

\displaystyle\|T^{k-1}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}=\|T^{k-1}-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{*})\|_{\text{sp}}\leq 2\epsilon+\|Q^{k-1}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}.

(83)

We can then plug this back into (
C
) to get

\displaystyle\|Q^{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\frac{2}{k+2}\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{k}{k+2}\big(2\epsilon+\|Q^{k-1}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}\big).

If we let
\omega_{k}=(k+1)(k+2)\|Q^{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}
then we have
\omega_{k}\leq\omega_{0}(k+1)+2\epsilon k(k+1)+\omega_{k-1}.

Then by using induction we get,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{0}\sum^{k}_{i=1}(i+1)+2\epsilon\sum^{k}_{i=1}i(i+1)+\omega_{0}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\frac{1}{2}(k+1)(k+2)+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon k(k+1)(k+2),

before finally dividing by
(k+1)(k+2)
to get the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.4
.

Assume (
79
) and that the sequences
c_{k}=5(k+2)\ln^{2}(k+2)
and
\beta_{k}=k/(k+2)
hold. If
\rho_{k}=2\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon k,
then for all
k=1,2,\dots,n
we have

\displaystyle\|Q^{k}-Q^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\frac{2}{k(k+1)}\sum^{k}_{i=1}\rho_{i+2}.

Proof.

Using the iterations of
Q^{k}=\frac{2}{k+2}Q^{0}+\frac{k}{k+2}T^{k-1}
and
Q^{k-1}=\frac{2}{k+1}Q^{0}+\frac{k-1}{k+1}T^{k-2}
we can subtract the two to find

\displaystyle Q^{k}-Q^{k-1}=\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\big(T^{k-1}-Q^{0}\big)+\frac{k-1}{k+1}\big(T^{k-1}-T^{k-2}\big).

(84)

If we use
\|Q^{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon k
from
Lemma

C.3
and equation (
83
) we have

\displaystyle\|T^{k-1}-Q^{0}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\|T^{k-1}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\|Q^{*}-Q^{0}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{k+2}.

Substituting this into (
84
) and using (
80
) yields

\displaystyle\|Q^{k}-Q^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\rho_{k+2}+\frac{k-1}{k+1}\|Q^{k-1}-Q^{k-2}\|_{\text{sp}}.

By letting
\tilde{\omega}_{k}=k(k+1)\|Q^{k}-Q^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}
and seeing that with recursion,
\tilde{\omega}_{k-1}\rightarrow 0,
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k}{k+2}\rho_{k+2}+k(k-1)\|Q^{k-1}-Q^{k-2}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k}{k+2}\rho_{k+2}+\tilde{\omega}_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho_{k+2}+\tilde{\omega}_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum^{k}_{i=1}\rho_{i+2}

\displaystyle\|Q^{k}-Q^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{k(k+1)}\sum^{k}_{i=1}\rho_{i+2},

where the final inequality comes from dividing both sides by
k(k+1).
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem C.5

(Robust Halpern Iteration Performance)
.

Assume that the robust-AMDP satisfies
3.1
and that the sequences
c_{k}=5(k+2)\ln^{2}(k+2)
and
\beta_{k}=k/(k+2)
. With probability of at least
(1-\delta)
we have for all
s\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}-g^{\pi,n}_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\leq\|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{n})-Q^{n}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\epsilon,

(85)

which obtains a sample and time complexity of the order

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigg(\ |\mathcal{S}|\ |\mathcal{A}|\ \Big(\frac{\mathcal{H}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\Big)\bigg),

where
L=\ln\Big(\frac{2\ |\mathcal{S}|\ |\mathcal{A}|\ (n+1)}{\delta}\Big)\log^{3}(n+2).

Proof.

See that in
Proposition

C.2
, we have with probability at least
(1-\delta)
that
\|T^{k}-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{k})\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and for all
k=0,1,\dots,n.
Since we can equivalently show the
sa
-rectangular
\ell_{p}
robust Bellman operator as the regularized non-robust Bellman operator, then the following analysis occurs w.r.t. the worst case kernel
\hat{P}^{k}_{s,a}
for each
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.
With this, the recursion of
Q^{n}=\frac{2}{n+2}Q^{0}+\frac{n}{n+2}T^{n-1}
implies that for each
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n})-Q^{n}=\frac{2}{n+2}\underbrace{\Big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n})-Q^{0}\Big)}_{\textbf{Term 1}}+\frac{n}{n+2}\underbrace{\Big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n})-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n-1})\Big)}_{\textbf{Term 2}}+\frac{n}{n+2}\underbrace{\Big(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n-1})-T^{n-1}\Big)}_{\textbf{Term 3}}.

(86)

Term 1:

Let
\rho_{n}=2\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon n
and see that
Q^{*}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P},g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}}(Q)
for all
\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}
and
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.
Using this with the invariance of
\|\cdot\|_{\text{sp}}
by additive constants, the triangle inequality, the nonexpansivity of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(\cdot)
under the span seminorm, and
Lemma

C.3
yield

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n})-Q^{0}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q^{n}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\|Q^{*}-Q^{0}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n},\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Term 2:

Using a similar method with
Lemma

C.4
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n})-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n-1})\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle=\|Q^{n}-Q^{n-1}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\rho_{i+2},\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Term 3:

Using
Proposition

C.2
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n-1})-T^{n-1}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\epsilon,\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

To combine the terms in equation (
86
), we can use these estimates, apply the triangle inequality,
Proposition

C.2
and the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{\text{sp}}\leq 2\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
to derive

\displaystyle g^{*}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}-g^{\pi,n}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{a}_{s}}(Q^{n})-Q^{n}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n+2}\rho_{n}+\frac{n}{n+2}\bigg[\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\rho_{i+2}+\bigg]+\frac{n}{n+2}(2\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{n+2}\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{4\epsilon n}{3(n+2)}+\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)}\bigg[\sum^{n}_{i=1}\Big(2\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon(i+2)\Big)\bigg]+2\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{n+2}\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{4\epsilon n}{3(n+2)}+\frac{4n}{(n+1)(n+2)}\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+\frac{2\epsilon(n^{2}+5n)}{3(n+2)(n+1)}+2\epsilon

Which proves our initial claim. In order to estimate the complexity, we look at when
k\geq 1.
Using the fact that
\|d^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}\leq\|Q^{k}-Q^{k-1}\|_{\text{sp}}
in (

) and

C.4

\displaystyle\|d^{k}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{k(k+1)}\sum^{k}_{i=1}\rho_{i+2}

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{k=0}m_{k}

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)+\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum^{n}_{k=0}c_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)+\frac{10\alpha}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln^{2}(2)\|Q^{0}\|^{2}_{\text{sp}}+\frac{5\alpha}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum^{n}_{k=1}(k+2)\ln^{2}(k+2)\Big(\frac{4}{k+1}\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}+2\epsilon\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)+\frac{10\alpha}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln^{2}(2)\|Q^{0}\|^{2}_{\text{sp}}+\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{240\alpha}{\epsilon^{2}(k+1)}\ln^{2}(k+2)\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\text{sp}}^{2}+40\alpha\sum^{n}_{k=1}(k+2)\ln^{2}(k+2)

\displaystyle=O\Big(\alpha\|Q^{0}\|_{\text{sp}}^{2}/\epsilon^{2}+\alpha\ln^{3}(n+2)\|Q^{0}-Q^{*}\|^{2}_{\text{sp}}/\epsilon^{2}+\alpha n^{2}\ln^{2}(n+2)\Big),

(87)

where the penultimate line uses
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
and

) by

Moreover, set

and

, then we complete the proof.
