Box Singularity Conditions in Box Diagrams of Decay Processes
Chao-Wei Shen, Ming-Yang Duan, Jia-Jun Wu

Abstract
Since 1959, singularities within single-loop diagrams have been studied. They are believed to significantly influence our understanding of experimental observables. In this study, we explore the singularities that arise from box diagrams in the decay process, which can be classified into two distinct categories. A comprehensive analysis of box singularities has been conducted, wherein we have derived and presented specific conditional formulae to ascertain the occurrence of singularities, along with the related physical scenarios.



I
Introduction

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the fundamental theory of strong interaction.
The basic fields of QCD are quark field, antiquark field and gluon field, and gluons are exchanged to carry the strong interaction.
A prominent feature of QCD is color confinement, which states that only the color singlet states can be detected experimentally.
Therefore, the colorless hadrons are the smallest visible states, and a better understanding of the hadron states, including both the internal structures and the spectrum, is one of the most valid ways to comprehend QCD and remains a significant challenge in particle physics.
On the other hand, difficulties exist in the low energy region in QCD, which is around a few hundreds of MeV, since nonperturbative dynamics dominate here, and it is still a mystery.
Different from the perturbation calculation, the loop diagrams that reflect nonperturbative properties may play an important role in the amplitude of a reaction.
Thus, it is of great significance to investigate the loop diagrams.

Seeking for various hadrons from the experimental observations, such as decay widths and cross sections, is a crucial task in hadron physics.
As a general rule, the peak structure observed in the experimental invariant mass spectrum is recognized as a resonance.
However, in reality, it may also be generated by some other mechanisms.
In Ref.
[
1
]
, the authors proved that there are two types of singularities in the three-point loop diagrams: two-body threshold cusps and triangle singularities (TS).
Both of them can be reflected in the invariant mass spectrum, with the latter corresponding to the peak structure where the TS occurs.
Actually, other kinds of loop diagrams can also exhibit similar or different singularities.
Therefore, it is of great significance to distinguish whether the peak structure observed in an experiment originates from a usual resonance or a pure kinematic singularity.

The singularity of hadron loop diagrams was first studied in Ref.
[
2

in 1959.
In that paper, L. D. Landau investigated the essence of singularity in the case of single loops and typically provided the specific contributions of TS.
Nevertheless, at that time, it was impossible to find an actual process that met the rigorous kinematic conditions of TS.

II
The loop integral and triangle singularity

In loop calculations, in addition to the usual ultraviolet and infrared divergence, some singularities can lead to a new divergence.
This divergence will result in a peak-like structure in the invariant mass spectra of certain final states, which can be mixed with the peaks generated by resonances.
Since this singularity position is independent of the interaction term and only depends on the kinematic effect of all involved particles in the loop, it is useful to extract the position of such singularity in phase space.

We start with the simplest single loop diagram of the
i\to f_{1}+f_{2}
decay containing three intermediate particles to explore the integral structure that leads to singularity.
Such a triangle loop diagram is shown in Fig.
1
, where the convention of notation and numeration are given.
The corresponding scalar loop integral writes:

\displaystyle I_{2}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q^{2}-m_{1}^{2}+i\varepsilon)[(P-q)^{2}-m_{2}^{2}+i\varepsilon]}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{\left[(P-q-p_{f_{1}})^{2}-m_{3}^{2}+i\varepsilon\right]},

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{2}\equiv

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{4}qI_{2},

(1)

where
m_{i}(i=1,2,3)
is the mass of the
i
-th intermediate particle.
Please note that small quantities such as
\varepsilon
are taken to be
0^{+}
unless otherwise specified.
It should be noted that the interaction vertex functions in the loop integration hereinafter are neglected, as they do not affect the existence of the singularity we are concerned about, although such vertexes will affect the strength of singularity.Furthermore, such a triangle loop diagram is symmetric, telling that changing the direction of
q_{3}
is equivalent.

Figure 1:
The triangle loop diagram.

In the rest frame of the initial particle, i.e.
P=(M,\vec{0})
, there are a total of six poles for the integral of
q^{0}
in Eq.(
1
).
Three of them,
-\omega_{1}+i\varepsilon
,
M-\omega_{2}+i\varepsilon
and
E_{f_{2}}-\omega_{3}+i\varepsilon
, are located above the real axis of the complex energy plane, while the other three,
\omega_{1}-i\varepsilon
,
M+\omega_{2}-i\varepsilon
, and
E_{f_{2}}+\omega_{3}-i\varepsilon
are located below the real axis.
Here
\omega_{i}=\sqrt{\vec{q}_{i}^{2}+m_{i}^{2}}
is the on-shell energy of the
i
-th intermediate particle in the loop, which is a function of
\vec{q}_{i}
, and
E_{f_{i}}
is the energy of final particle
f_{i}
.
Then we can obtain the integral of
q^{0}
by calculating the residues of the poles as follows,

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{2}=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{3}\vec{q}\ [{\rm Res}(I_{2},\omega_{1}-i\varepsilon)+{\rm Res}(I_{2},M+\omega_{2}-i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle+{\rm Res}(I_{2},E_{f_{2}}+\omega_{3}-i\varepsilon)],

(2)

where
{\rm Res}(I,Z)
represents the residue of the function
I
at pole
Z
.

The integral variable can be decomposed as
{\rm d}^{3}\vec{q}\equiv q^{2}{\rm d}q{\rm d}\cos\theta{\rm d}\phi
.
We define
\vec{p}_{f_{2}}=(0,0,p_{f_{2}})
with (
p_{f_{2}}\geq 0
), so only two variables are left, i.e.
{\rm d}^{3}\vec{q}=2\pi q^{2}{\rm d}q{\rm d}\cos\theta
.
The integral interval of
q
is
[0,\infty)
and that of
\cos\theta
is
[-1,1]
.
Then we have
\omega_{1,2}(q)=\sqrt{q^{2}+m_{1,2}^{2}}
,
\omega_{3}(q,\theta)=\sqrt{p_{f_{2}}^{2}+q^{2}+m_{3}^{2}-2p_{f_{2}}q\cos\theta}
.
Besides, the singularity from the poles of these two variables in all residues is first-order, which is determined by the propagator of intermediate particles.
For such first-order poles, there are only two possible divergences after integration.
In the first case, the pole position is exactly on the boundary of the integration interval, such as
\int_{0}^{\text{a}ny}x^{-1}dx\to\infty
.
The second scenario is that the integral path is pinched between two poles, where the real parts of these two poles are the same, with one on the upper edge of the real axis and the other on the lower edge.

For the integral
{\cal I}_{2}
, it can be found that singularity only occurs at the residue of pole
\omega_{1}-i\varepsilon
.
Then
{\cal I}_{2}
is written as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{2}=

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{3}\vec{q}\Big[\frac{f(\vec{q})}{(M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{(E_{f_{2}}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\,\theta)+i\varepsilon)}+h(\vec{q})\Big],

(3)

where
f(\vec{q})
and
h(\vec{q})
are general polynomials and do not produce singularity.
They do not affect the singularity position and divergence, as they become a constant when the integral variables approach the singularity position.
Therefore, we can just focus on the two denominator terms.
The first term
1/(M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon)
has a pole at
q^{+}_{\rm on}=q_{\rm on}+i\varepsilon
, where
q_{\rm on}=\sqrt{\lambda(M^{2},m_{1}^{2},m_{2}^{2})}/(2M)
is the on-shell momentum of particles
1
and
2
in the rest frame of the initial particle and
\lambda(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}-2\alpha\beta-2\alpha\gamma-2\beta\gamma
is the Käll
\rm\acute{e}
n function.
To make the integral path pinched between two poles, the other term
1/(E_{f_{2}}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon)
should have a pole
q^{-}_{\rm on}=q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon^{\prime}
after integrating
\cos\theta
.
It, which is a function of
q
and
\cos\theta
, can be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{f_{2}}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\,\theta)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{f_{2}}-\sqrt{m^{2}_{1}+q^{2}}-\sqrt{p_{f_{2}}^{2}+q^{2}+m_{3}^{2}-2p_{f_{2}}q\cos\theta}+i\varepsilon}.

(4)

It can be seen that it only contains the first-order pole of
\cos\theta
.
To generate singularity,
\cos\theta
of the pole position should be located at the boundary of the integration interval, which means
\cos\theta=-1
or 1.
Therefore, the denominator of Eq.(
4
) has to be zero when
q=q^{-}_{\rm on}
and
\cos\theta=-1
or
1
, then we have:

\displaystyle E_{f_{2}}-\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})-\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\,\pi\,{\rm or}\,0)=0,

(5)

\displaystyle i\varepsilon+i\varepsilon^{\prime}\left(\frac{q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}-\frac{\mp p_{f_{2}}-q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\,\pi\,{\rm or}\,0)}\right)=0.

(6)

To make Eq.(
6
) hold, the value of the term in the bracket needs to be negative, which means that the value of
\cos\theta
can only be taken as
1
, i.e.
\theta=0
.
Therefore, the singularity conditions of the triangle loop diagram are:

\displaystyle E_{f_{2}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})+\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\,0),

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}<\frac{p_{f_{2}}-q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\,0)}=v_{3}.

(7)

From the physical perspective,
\cos\theta=1
means that all involved particles are moving on the same line, and
v_{1}<v_{3}
indicates that the intermediate particle 3 and particle 1 are moving in the same direction, and particle 3 moves faster than particle 1, which tells that particle 3 can always catch up with particle 1.
Thus, a classical collision occurs at the singularity point for the triangle loop case.
The condition for this triangle singularity has already been discussed in Ref.
[
12
]
, and here we provide an alternative mathematical method as shown in Eq.(
6
), which will later be used to study the conditions for box singularity.

III
Two diagrams of the 3-body decay with box single loop

In the previous section, we investigated triangle singularity by analyzing the algebraic properties of the loop integral and obtained a corresponding physical interpretation.
Now we turn to our goal of this paper to explore the singularities in the box loop diagram.
Unlike triangle diagrams that are symmetric, box diagrams have different structures.
There are two different types of box diagrams as shown in Fig.
2
.
Although the loop integrals in these two cases are mathematically identical, the conditions that generate the singularities are different.

Figure 2:
Two types of box diagrams. The notation of particles and momentum is shown in (b).

In the subsequent calculations, we take the momentum notation
k_{2}\equiv p_{f_{3}}
and
k_{1}\equiv p_{f_{2}}+p_{f_{3}}
as given in Fig.
2
.
Then the loop integral
{\cal I}_{3}
of the box diagram writes:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{3}\equiv

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm d}^{4}q}{(q^{2}-m_{1}^{2}+i\varepsilon)[(P-q)^{2}-m_{2}^{2}+i\varepsilon]}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{\left[(k_{1}-q)^{2}-m_{3}^{2}+i\varepsilon\right]\left[(k_{2}-q)^{2}-m_{4}^{2}+i\varepsilon\right]}.

(8)

There are a total of eight poles for the integral of
q^{0}
:

\displaystyle q^{0}=

\displaystyle\pm\left(\omega_{1}-i\varepsilon\right),\ M\pm\left(\omega_{2}-i\varepsilon\right),

\displaystyle k_{1}^{0}\pm\left(\omega_{3}-i\varepsilon\right),\ k_{2}^{0}\pm\left(\omega_{4}-i\varepsilon\right).

(9)

For the case of Fig.
2
, we take the poles below the real axis to apply the residue theorem.
It can be found that there are a total of three terms in the denominator that could be zero, and they all come from the residue of pole
\omega_{1}-i\varepsilon
, which means that only one term
{\cal I}_{a}
in
{\cal I}_{3}
plays a role when discussing the singularity.
Here, for convenience, we define the three terms that can be zero as:

\displaystyle a=

\displaystyle M-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+i\varepsilon,

\displaystyle b=

\displaystyle k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}-\omega_{3}+i\varepsilon,

\displaystyle c=

\displaystyle k_{2}^{0}-\omega_{1}-\omega_{4}+i\varepsilon.

(10)

When these three terms are equal to zero, they correspond to the three cuts as shown in Fig.
3
.
Then we only need to focus on
{\cal I}_{a}
to extract the location of the singularity, and it can be written as:

{\cal I}_{a}=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{q}}{abc}.

(11)

Figure 3:
The relevant cuts for the two types of box diagrams.

For Fig.
2
, the situation is more complicated.
There are four terms in the denominator that can be zero, and they are not independent of each other.
These four terms are:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}=

\displaystyle M-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+i\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\tilde{b}=

\displaystyle M-k_{2}^{0}-\omega_{2}-\omega_{4}+i\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\tilde{c}=

\displaystyle k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}-\omega_{3}+i\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}=

\displaystyle k_{1}^{0}-k_{2}^{0}-\omega_{3}-\omega_{4}+i\varepsilon.

(12)

Whether we choose the poles above or below the real axis to apply the residue theorem, there are always two residues that contain such terms.
It is easy to see that
\tilde{a}+\tilde{d}=\tilde{b}+\tilde{c}
, which means that when any three of them are zero, the remaining one is also zero.
These four terms correspond to the four cuts as given in Fig.
3
.
We choose the residues of poles
M-\omega_{2}+i\varepsilon
and
k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{3}+i\varepsilon
for subsequent calculations.
Then
{\cal I}_{b}
, which actually makes a difference to the singularity, is written as:

{\cal I}_{b}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\vec{q}}\frac{\tilde{a}+\tilde{d}}{\tilde{a}\tilde{b}\tilde{c}\tilde{d}}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\vec{q}}\frac{\tilde{b}+\tilde{c}}{\tilde{a}\tilde{b}\tilde{c}\tilde{d}}.

(13)

The detailed calculation of Eq.(
13
) can be found in Appendix
A
.

In summary, we obtain two explicit formulas for exploring singularities for the two types of box diagrams.

IV
Singularity conditions in the case of Fig.
2

In this section, we start from Eq.(
11
) in Fig.
2
to discuss the conditions for singularity and related properties.

We assume that in the rest frame of the initial particle,
\vec{k}_{2}
is along the
\hat{z}
-axis, and
\vec{k}_{1}
is in the
\hat{x}-\hat{z}
plane, that is,
\vec{k}_{1}=k_{1}(\sin\theta_{1},0,\cos\theta_{1})
and
\vec{k}_{2}=k_{2}(0,0,1)
, where
\cos\theta_{1}=\left(m_{f_{2}}^{2}-m_{f_{2}f_{3}}^{2}-m_{f_{3}}^{2}+2k_{1}^{0}k_{2}^{0}\right)/(2k_{1}k_{2})
with
k_{1,\,2}\geq 0
and
m_{f_{2}f_{3}}
being the invariant mass of particles
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
.
The loop integral momentum is defined as
\vec{q}=q(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)
, and all energies are functions of the integral variables
q
,
\cos\theta
and
\phi
, written as:

\displaystyle\omega_{1,2}(q)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{q^{2}+m_{1,2}^{2}},

\displaystyle\omega_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)=

\displaystyle\big(q^{2}+k^{2}_{1}+m_{3}^{2}-2qk_{1}\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta

\displaystyle-2qk_{1}\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta\cos\phi\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\omega_{4}(q,\theta)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{q^{2}+k^{2}_{2}+m_{4}^{2}-2qk_{2}\cos\theta}.

(14)

Then Eq.(
11
) becomes,

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{a}=

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{q^{2}\mathrm{d}{q}}{M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\int^{\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\cos\theta}}{k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{4}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\phi}}{k^{0}_{1}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon}.

(15)

It should be noted that when
\theta_{1}=0
or
\pi
, the variable
\phi
does not appear in the integrand, but Eq.(
15
) still holds.
We will discuss this case in the second subsection.
Here we first consider the case where
\theta_{1}\neq 0
and
\pi
, and the integral related to
\phi
can be written as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{\phi}=

\displaystyle\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int^{\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\phi\left(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)+\omega_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)\right)}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\phi}\left((k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon\right)}.

(16)

We found that only when divergence occurs at the boundary of
\cos\phi
, it diverges after integrating
\phi
.
If the integral concerning
\phi
does not diverge, then the divergent singularity is, at most, a TS.
In other words, it tells that
\phi
should take the boundary value
0
or
\pi
when the box singularity happens.
This corresponds to the physical situation where all the internal and external particles involved are in the same
\hat{x}-\hat{z}
plane.
In addition, we need to complete the integration of
{\cal I}_{\phi}
around the singular points as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{0}_{\phi}=

\displaystyle\int^{\epsilon}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\phi\left(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)+\omega_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)\right)}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\phi}\left((k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{3}(q,\theta,0)+i\varepsilon}},

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\pi}_{\phi}=

\displaystyle\int^{\pi}_{\pi-\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\phi\left(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)+\omega_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)\right)}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\phi}\left((k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{3}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{3}(q,\theta,\pi)+i\varepsilon}}.

(17)

Thus, to extract the singularity condition of Eq.(
11
), we only need to consider the following integral,

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{a}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}q\ {\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}}{M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon},

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{(\cos\theta-X(q)+i\varepsilon)\sqrt{\cos(\theta_{1}\mp\theta)-Y(q)+i\varepsilon}},

(18)

where

\displaystyle X(q)\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{m_{4}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}-m_{f_{3}}^{2}+2k_{2}^{0}\sqrt{q^{2}+m_{1}^{2}}}{2k_{2}q},

\displaystyle Y(q)\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{m_{3}^{2}-m_{f_{2}f_{3}}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}+2k_{1}^{0}\sqrt{q^{2}+m_{1}^{2}}}{2k_{1}q},

(19)

and the “
\mp
” sign in the formulas and explanations hereinafter corresponds to the cases where
\phi=0
and
\pi
, respectively.

The pole of
1/(M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon)
is
q_{\rm on}+i\varepsilon
.
Similar to the case of triangle singularity, we know that
q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
also needs to be a pole of
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
, i.e.
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)=\infty
.
Then we turn to the integral of
\cos\theta
in
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
.
The first denominator of
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
contains only one first-order pole, that is,
\cos\theta=X(q)-i\varepsilon
, and further derivation is required for the second denominator.

In order to give a explicit solution for
\cos(\theta_{1}\mp\theta)-Y(q)+i\varepsilon=0
, we define the following two functions:

\displaystyle Y^{+}_{0}(q)\equiv

\displaystyle Y(q)\cos\theta_{1}+\sqrt{1-Y^{2}(q)}\sin\theta_{1},

\displaystyle Y^{-}_{0}(q)\equiv

\displaystyle Y(q)\cos\theta_{1}-\sqrt{1-Y^{2}(q)}\sin\theta_{1}.

(20)

After the derivation given in Appendix
B
, we obtain the solutions as:

\displaystyle\cos\theta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y^{+}_{0}(q)+i\varepsilon_{0},&\phi=0\,\&\,\theta_{1}>\theta>0\\
Y^{-}_{0}(q)-i\varepsilon_{0},&\phi=0\,\&\,\theta_{1}<\theta<\pi\\
Y^{+}_{0}(q)-i\varepsilon_{\pi},&\phi=\pi\,\&\,\pi-\theta_{1}>\theta>0\\
Y^{-}_{0}(q)+i\varepsilon_{\pi},&\phi=\pi\,\&\,\pi-\theta_{1}<\theta<\pi\end{array}\right.,

where the imaginary parts are:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\sin\theta}{|\sin(\theta_{1}-\theta)|}>0,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\pi}=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\sin\theta}{|\sin(\theta_{1}+\theta)|}>0.

(25)

Furthermore, for the case where
\cos\theta
is at the boundary, there are special solutions that correspond to triangle singularities rather than box singularities.
Their specific forms and related discussions are also given in Appendix
B
.

Then there are only two situations that make
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
diverge at
q=q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
.
Firstly, the momentum directions of the three intermediate particles
1
,
3
, and
4
are different in the rest frame of the mother particle.
Moreover, in the rest frame of the final particles
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
, the directions of particles 1 and 4 are also different.
In this case, the singularity happens when the integral path of
\cos\theta
is pinched between the two poles from two denominators of
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
.
Since the singularity generated here has nothing to do with triangle singularity, we refer to such singularity as the general box singularity.
Secondly, all three final particles are moving on the same line, i.e.
\theta_{1}=0
or
\pi
.
In this case, the entire integration is independent of the variable
\phi
.
We will later find that even if the two
\cos\theta
poles of
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
can be pinched when
\theta_{1}=\pi
,
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
is still not divergent at
q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
.
Thus, the possible singularity only arises from boundary divergence, which results in all particles moving on the same line.
We will discuss these two cases in detail in the subsequent subsections.

IV.1
The general box singularity

In this subsection, we consider the case that the integral path of
\cos\theta
is pinched between the two poles from the two denominators of
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
in Eq.(
18
).
Since we already have the pole
X(q)-i\varepsilon
of the first denominator, which lies below the real
\cos\theta
-axis, another
\cos\theta
pole from the second denominator should be above the real axis, which are the solutions with positive imaginary parts in Eq.(
IV
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\cos\theta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y^{+}_{0}(q)+i\varepsilon_{0},&\phi=0\,\&\,\theta_{1}>\theta>0\\
Y^{-}_{0}(q)+i\varepsilon_{\pi},&\phi=\pi\,\&\,\pi-\theta_{1}<\theta<\pi\end{array}\right..

Therefore, for the cases of
\phi=0
and
\pi
, there are restrictions
\theta_{1}>\theta
and
\theta_{1}+\theta>\pi
respectively.
Notably, these two restrictions
\theta_{1}>\theta
for
\phi=0
and
\theta_{1}+\theta>\pi
for
\phi=\pi
can be reduced to the same constraint
\vec{v}_{3\perp 1}\cdot\vec{v}_{4\perp 1}<0
, where
\vec{v}_{3\perp 1}
and
\vec{v}_{4\perp 1}
are the velocities of intermediate particles 3 and 4 perpendicular to the direction of particle
1
.
For convenience, we take
v_{3\perp 1}>0>v_{4\perp 1}
in the following calculation.
Besides, we can understand this condition from a physical perspective that particles
3
and
4
move in opposite directions perpendicular to particle 1.

For simplicity, we define:

\displaystyle Y_{0}(q)=\cos\theta_{\text{on}}\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y^{+}_{0}(q),&\phi=0\\
Y^{-}_{0}(q),&\phi=\pi\end{array}\right..

Then the two poles of the integrand are
X(q)-i\varepsilon
and
Y_{0}(q)+i\varepsilon
, and we can write
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
in the form as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{(\cos\theta-X(q)+i\varepsilon)\sqrt{\cos\theta-Y_{0}(q)-i\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{X(q)-Y_{0}(q)-i\varepsilon}}.

(30)

It can be seen that
{\cal I}^{\prime}_{a}
is divergent only when

X(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-Y_{0}(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-i\varepsilon^{\prime}=0.

(31)

We find that the real part of Eq.(
31
) naturally holds when all the intermediate particles are on-shell, i.e.
{\rm Re}[X(q_{\rm on})]={\rm Re}[Y_{0}(q_{\rm on})]
.
So, we only need to focus on the imaginary part of Eq.(
31
).
It is clear that imaginary part of
X(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-Y_{0}(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)
should be positive to hold Eq.(
31
).
By first-order expansion of
\varepsilon
, we get:

\frac{{\rm d}X}{{\rm d}q}\Big|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}-\frac{{\rm d}Y_{0}}{{\rm d}Y}\frac{{\rm d}Y}{{\rm d}q}\Big|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}<0.

(32)

It results in (the detailed derivation can be found in Appendix
C
):

-\frac{v_{1}-v_{4\parallel 1}}{v_{4\perp 1}}+\frac{v_{1}-v_{3\parallel 1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}<0.

(33)

where

\displaystyle v_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})},

\displaystyle v_{3\perp 1}=

\displaystyle|\vec{v}_{3\perp 1}|=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{k_{1}\sin(\theta_{1}-\theta_{\rm on})}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on},0)},&\phi=0\\
\frac{k_{1}\sin(\theta_{1}+\theta_{\rm on}-\pi)}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on},\pi)},&\phi=\pi\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle v_{4\perp 1}=

\displaystyle-|\vec{v}_{4\perp 1}|=-\frac{k_{2}\sin\theta_{\rm on}}{\omega_{4}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on})}.

(35)

The signs of
v_{3\parallel 1}
and
v_{4\parallel 1}
are undetermined and they are defined as:

\displaystyle v_{3\parallel 1}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{k_{1}\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{\rm on})-q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on},0)},&\phi=0\\
\frac{k_{1}\cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{\rm on})-q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on},\pi)},&\phi=\pi\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle v_{4\parallel 1}=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{2}\cos\theta_{\rm on}-q_{\rm on}}{\omega_{4}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on})}.

(38)

Eq.(
33
) can be explained physically.
Assume that the lifetimes of intermediate particles
2
,
3
and
4
are
t_{2}
,
t_{3}
and
t_{4}
, respectively.
For particle 4 to catch up with particle 1 and collide as shown in Fig.
4
, two conditions must be met:

\displaystyle v_{3\perp 1}t_{3}=

\displaystyle-v_{4\perp 1}t_{4},

\displaystyle v_{2}t_{2}+v_{3\parallel 1}t_{3}+v_{4\parallel 1}t_{4}=

\displaystyle v_{1}(t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{4}).

(39)

Since
v_{1}
is positive and
v_{2}
is negative, we have:

\displaystyle(v_{1}-v_{2})t_{2}=

\displaystyle v_{3\parallel 1}t_{3}+v_{4\parallel 1}t_{4}-v_{1}(t_{3}+t_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{4\perp 1}}\right)v_{3\perp 1}t_{3}>0.

(40)

It is exactly the same as Eq.(
33
).
Therefore, the physical explanation of Eq.(
33
) is that particle 4 can catch up with particle 1 to collide, which is consistent with the statement of Coleman-Norton theorem in Ref.
[
39
]
.

In summary, for the case where the integral path of
\cos\theta
is pinched between two poles, we obtain the following constraints:

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{3\perp 1}

\displaystyle\cdot\vec{v}_{4\perp 1}<0,

\displaystyle\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{4\perp 1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}<0,

(41)

and all intermediate particles are on-shell.
The corresponding physical situation is that particle 4 can catch up with particle 1 to collide in real space-time.

For the case of

or
\pi

In this subsection, we study the situation where all final particles move in the same straight line, i.e.
\theta_{1}=0
or

Since the integral variable

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{a}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}q\ {\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}}{M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon},

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{(k_{2}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{4}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta\tilde{f}(q,\theta)}{(\cos\theta-X(q)+i\varepsilon)(\pm\cos\theta-Y(q)+i\varepsilon)},

(43)

where
\omega_{3}(q,\theta)=\sqrt{q^{2}+m_{3}^{2}+k_{1}^{2}\pm 2qk_{1}\cos\theta}

The “
\pm
” sign in Eq.(

) corresponds to
\theta_{1}=0
and
\pi
respectively,
X(q)
and

are defined in Eq.(

), and

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(q,\theta)=\frac{(k_{2}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)+\omega_{4}(q,\theta))(k_{1}^{0}-\omega_{1}(q)+\omega_{3}(q,\theta))}{4q^{2}k_{1}k_{2}}

(44)

is just a regular function without any singularity, which we will drop when studying the divergence conditions.

Since
q=q_{\rm on}+i\varepsilon
is a zero point in the denominator of Eq.(

), in order to make
{\cal I}_{a}
diverge, we need to ensure that
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}
diverges at
q=q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
after integrating
\cos\theta
.

\cos\theta
poles, or there exists an endpoint divergence.

We first prove that
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}
cannot diverge at
q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
due to the integral path being pinched between the two
\cos\theta

, as the poles from both denominators of Eq.(

\cos\theta

For

In this case, even if the two

.
The derivation is also provided in Appendix

.

Therefore, only the case of endpoint divergence can make
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}
diverge at
q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
, i.e.
\theta=0
or
\pi
.
At this point, all particles are moving on the same line.
Obviously, there are four possibilities for
\theta_{1}
and

.

IV.2.1
\theta_{1}=\theta=\pi

For
\theta_{1}=\theta=\pi
,
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}
can be calculated as:

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{(\cos\theta-(X(q)-i\varepsilon_{X}))(\cos\theta+(Y(q)-i\varepsilon_{Y}))}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\log(-1-(X(q)-i\varepsilon_{X}))-\log(-1+(Y(q)-i\varepsilon_{Y}))}{Y(q)+X(q)-i(\varepsilon_{X}+\varepsilon_{Y})}.

(45)

We first check the divergence condition when
q=q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon
, and there are:

\displaystyle X(q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle X(q_{\text{on}})-i\varepsilon\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}X(q)}{q}\right|_{q\to q_{\text{on}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X(q_{\text{on}})-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\left(\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{2}/k^{0}_{2}}-X(q_{\text{on}})\right),

\displaystyle Y(q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle Y(q_{\text{on}})-i\varepsilon\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Y(q)}{q}\right|_{q\to q_{\text{on}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y(q_{\text{on}})-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\left(\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{1}/k^{0}_{1}}-Y(q_{\text{on}})\right).

(46)

In this case, we have
X(q_{\text{on}})=-1
and
Y(q_{\text{on}})=1
for the condition of all intermediate particle on the mass shell, then the first
\log
term in Eq.(
45
) is:

\displaystyle(-1-(X(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-i\varepsilon_{X}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\left(\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{2}/k^{0}_{2}}+1\right)+i\varepsilon_{X}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0.

(47)

It does not diverge at
q=q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon
.
For the second
\log
term, we have:

\displaystyle(-1+(Y(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-i\varepsilon_{Y}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\left(\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{1}/k^{0}_{1}}-1\right)-i\varepsilon_{Y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\frac{v_{3}-v_{1}}{k_{1}/\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}-i\varepsilon_{Y},

(48)

where
v_{1}=-q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}<0
and
v_{3}=(q_{\rm on}-k_{1})/\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)
.
When
v_{3}<v_{1}<0
, Eq.(
48
) can be zero, telling that
q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon
can be a pole of
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}
.
For the denominator of Eq.(
45
), we have:

\displaystyle Y(q)+X(q)-i(\varepsilon_{X}+\varepsilon_{Y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\left(\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{2}/k^{0}_{2}}+\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{1}/k^{0}_{1}}\right)-i(\varepsilon_{Y}+\varepsilon_{X})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 0,

(49)

which will not have a pole of
q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon
.

In summary, we find that divergence occurs only in the
\log\left(-1+(Y(q)-i\varepsilon_{Y})\right)
term with
v_{3}<v_{1}<0
such that
\left(-i\varepsilon\frac{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}{q_{\text{on}}k_{1}}(v_{3}-v_{1})-i\varepsilon_{Y})\right)\to 0
.
Then
{\cal I}_{a}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{a}\propto

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}q\frac{\log(q-q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon_{1})-\log(q-q_{\text{on}}+i\varepsilon)}{(q-q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon_{2})(q-q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon_{3})}.

2
,
4
,
f_{1}
, and
f_{3}
are moving along the
\hat{z}
-axis, while particles

,

and

are moving in the opposite direction, as shown in Fig.

, which indicates that particle 3 can collide with particle 1 and generate particles

and

.

,
2

.

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{a}\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\log(-1-X(q)+i\varepsilon_{X})-\log(-1-X(q)+i\varepsilon_{X}+\delta)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\propto

To sum up, the physical condition for box singularity with all particles on the same line is that all intermediate particles are on-shell, and particles 3 or 4 can “catch up” with particle 1, that is, the velocities of particle 3 or 4 must be larger than that particle 1 and in the same direction.
An interesting situation is that particle 3 can catch up with particle 1 but particle 4 cannot collide with particle 1, that is, the decay of
3\to 4+f_{2}
occurs before particle 3 catches up with particle 1, so particle 4 cannot collide with particle 1 in real space-time.
The classical collision does not occur, which seems to be inconsistent with the description of Coleman-Norton theorem.
In fact, one can not determine the lifetime of the intermediate particles in a Feynman diagram, even if they are on-shell, so the above scenario is just one possibility.
Another plausible scenario is that particle 3 can live long enough to surpass particle 1 before decaying into particle 4 and
f_{2}
.
Then, since particle 4 is moving slower than particle 1, particle 1 will “catch up” with particle 4, and such a process can still be regarded as a classical collision.
Therefore, regarding the statement of Coleman-Norton theorem, we interpret it as “classical collisions” should be possible when Landau pole exists.

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{b_{1}}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{q^{2}\mathrm{d}{q}}{M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\cos\theta}}{k^{0}_{1}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\phi}}{k^{0}_{1}-k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)-\omega_{4}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon}.

Likewise,

These cases will be discussed separately in the second subsection, and here we only consider the case of

Similar to Eqs.(

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{b}_{\phi}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\phi}}{k^{0}_{1}-k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)-\omega_{4}(q,\theta,\phi)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(k^{0}_{1}-k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{3}(q,\theta))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{4}(q,\theta,0{\rm\,or\,}\pi)+i\varepsilon}}.

It is important to determine the singularity of

The infinitesimal imaginary part

IV

Firstly, we consider the sign of the imaginary part of
\cos\theta_{\rm on}

We denote the square of the denominator in Eq. (

\displaystyle f_{b}(\cos\theta,q)\equiv

\displaystyle(k^{0}_{1}-k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{3}(q,\theta))^{2}-\omega^{2}_{4}(q,\theta,0{\rm\,or\,}\pi).

(61)

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{b}(Z+i\varepsilon^{\prime},q)+i\varepsilon=

\displaystyle f_{b}(Z,q)+\left.\frac{\partial f_{b}}{\partial\cos\theta}\right|_{\cos\theta=Z}i\varepsilon^{\prime}+i\varepsilon,

(62)

where

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial f_{b}}{\partial\cos\theta}\right|_{\cos\theta=Z}=

\displaystyle\frac{2q\omega_{4}(q,\theta_{\rm on},0{\rm\,or\,}\pi)}{\sin\theta_{\rm on}}(v_{3\perp 1}-v_{4\perp 1}).

(63)

Here,
v_{3\perp 1}

are the velocities of intermediate particles 3 and 4 perpendicular to the direction of particle 1,

\displaystyle v_{3\perp 1}=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{2}\sin\theta_{\rm on}}{\omega_{3}(q,\theta_{\rm on})},

\displaystyle v_{4\perp 1}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{-k_{2}\sin(\theta_{\rm on}-\theta_{1})}{\omega_{4}(q,\theta_{\rm on},0)},&\phi=0\\
\frac{-k_{2}\sin(\theta_{\rm on}+\theta_{1})}{\omega_{4}(q,\theta_{\rm on},\pi)},&\phi=\pi\end{array}\right..

Here we take
v_{3\perp 1}>0
, and the sign of
v_{4\perp 1}
is undetermined.
It is evident that the sign of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
in Eq. (

), which is the imaginary part of
\cos\theta_{\rm on}
, is determined by the sign of
\frac{\partial f_{b}}{\partial\cos\theta}
, so we have:

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{\rm on}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Z_{1}(q)-i\varepsilon_{0},&v_{3\perp 1}>v_{4\perp 1}\\
Z_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon_{0},&0<v_{3\perp 1}<v_{4\perp 1}\end{array}\right..

Then
{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{b_{1}}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{q^{2}\mathrm{d}{q}{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}}{M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon},

(68)

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\cos\theta}}{(\cos\theta-Y(q)+i\varepsilon)\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos\theta_{\rm on}}}.

(69)

Y(q)
is given in Eq. (

It is obvious that

This is quite similar to

As discussed before, for

So, we only need to consider the case where the integral path of

In the second subsection, we will discuss the cases of
\theta_{1}=0
and
\pi
one by one.

V.1
The general box singularity

In this subsection, we only consider that the singularity is generated when the integral path of
\cos\theta
is pinched between two poles.
The pole of the first denominator of Eq. (
69
) locates below the real axis of
\cos\theta
, while the imaginary part of the pole of the second denominator can be positive or negative as shown in Eq. (
V
).
Only the solution with a positive imaginary part in Eq.(
V
) is what we need, and the corresponding divergence condition of
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}
is
0<v_{3\perp 1}<v_{4\perp 1}
.

Then for
{\cal\tilde{I}}_{b}^{\prime}
, we have:

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}_{b}^{\prime}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\cos\theta}}{(\cos\theta-Y(q)+i\varepsilon)\sqrt{\cos\theta-Z_{2}(q)-i\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{Y(q)-Z_{2}(q)-i\varepsilon}}.

(70)

Thus, the divergence of the integral
{\cal\tilde{I}}_{b}^{\prime}
comes from the
Y(q)-Z_{2}(q)-i\varepsilon
term.
It means that
{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
is divergent when

Y(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-Z_{2}(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)-i\varepsilon^{\prime}=0.

(71)

The real part of Eq.(
71
) is satisfied when the particles are on-shell, and for the imaginary part to also be valid, it requires

\displaystyle 0>

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}(Y-Z_{2})}{{\rm d}q}\Big|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}=\frac{{\rm d}Y}{{\rm d}q}\Big|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}-\frac{{\rm d}Z_{2}}{{\rm d}q}\Big|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}.

(72)

We use the definition of
f_{b}
in Eq.(
61
) to calculate
\frac{{\rm d}Z_{2}}{{\rm d}q}
, that is

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}Z_{2}}{{\rm d}q}=-\frac{\partial f_{b}(Z_{2},q)/\partial q}{\partial f_{b}(Z_{2},q)/\partial Z_{2}},

(73)

where
f_{b}(Z_{2}(q),q)=0
.
It is straightforward to obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{b}(Z_{2},q)}{\partial q}=2\omega_{4}(q,\theta_{\rm on},\phi=0{\rm\,or\,}\pi)(v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{4\parallel 1}),

(74)

where

\displaystyle v_{3\parallel 1}=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{1}\cos\theta_{\rm on}-q}{\omega_{3}(q,\theta_{\rm on})},

\displaystyle v_{4\parallel 1}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{q-k_{2}\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{\rm on})}{\omega_{4}(q,\theta_{\rm on},0)},&\phi=0\\
\frac{q-k_{2}\cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{\rm on})}{\omega_{4}(q,\theta_{\rm on},\pi)},&\phi=\pi\end{array}\right..

So we can obtain the singularity condition based on Eqs. (
72
,
73
) as follows,

\displaystyle 0>

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\theta_{\rm on})}{k_{1}q_{\rm on}}\frac{v_{3\perp 1}v_{4\perp 1}}{v_{4\perp 1}-v_{3\perp 1}}\left(\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{4\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}\right),

(77)

which is equivalent to

\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{4\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}<0.

(78)

Here
v_{1}=q_{\rm on}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})
and the definitions of
v_{3\perp 1}
,
v_{4\perp 1}
,
v_{3\parallel 1}
and
v_{4\parallel 1}
are given in Eqs.(
V
) and (
V.1
) with
q=q_{\rm on}
.
Similar to the case of Fig.
2
, we can physically explain Eq.(
78
).
Assume that the lifetimes of particles 2, 3, and 4 are
t_{2}
,
t_{3}
, and
t_{4}
, respectively.
For particle 3 and particle 4 to collide, we have
v_{3\perp 1}t_{3}=v_{4\perp 1}t_{4}
and
v_{2}t_{2}+v_{3\parallel 1}t_{3}=v_{1}(t_{2}+t_{3}-t_{4})+v_{4\parallel 1}t_{4}
.
Since
v_{1}
is positive,
v_{2}
is negative and
t_{2}
is larger than zero, we have:

\displaystyle(v_{1}-v_{2})t_{2}=

\displaystyle v_{3\parallel 1}t_{3}-v_{4\parallel 1}t_{4}-v_{1}(t_{3}-t_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{4\perp 1}})v_{3\perp 1}t_{3}>0.

(79)

It can be seen that Eq.(
79
) is exactly the same as Eq.(
78
).
So the physical explanation of Eq.(
78
) is that particle 4 can collide with particle 3, and this physical process is vividly illustrated in Fig.
10
.

The diagram of the internal particle trajectories in real space-time when box singularity happens in the case of Fig.

.

Therefore, the singularity conditions of
{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
are:

\displaystyle v_{4\perp 1}>v_{3\perp 1}>0,

(80)

\displaystyle\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{4\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{1}}{v_{3\perp 1}}<0.

(81)

Then the singularity conditions for
{\cal I}_{b_{2}}
can be easily obtained by exchanging particle indices, that is,

\displaystyle v_{3\perp 2}>v_{4\perp 2}>0,

(82)

\displaystyle\frac{v_{3\parallel 2}-v_{2}}{v_{3\perp 2}}-\frac{v_{4\parallel 2}-v_{2}}{v_{4\perp 2}}<0.

(83)

where the value of
v_{2}
is set to be positive.
However, we need to write this condition in the same framework, that is,
v_{1}
is positive and
v_{2}
is negative.
So we have:

\displaystyle v_{3\perp 1}>v_{4\perp 1}>0,

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{2}}{v_{4\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{2}}{v_{3\perp 1}}<0.

(85)

Similarly, there are
v_{3\perp 1}t_{3}=v_{4\perp 1}t_{4}
and
v_{2}(t_{1}+t_{4}-t_{3})+v_{3\parallel 1}t_{3}=v_{1}t_{1}+v_{4\parallel 1}t_{4}
to make particle 3 and 4 collide in real space-time.
Then there are:

\displaystyle(v_{1}-v_{2})t_{1}=

\displaystyle v_{3\parallel 1}t_{3}-v_{4\parallel 1}t_{4}-v_{2}(t_{3}-t_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\frac{v_{3\parallel 1}-v_{2}}{v_{3\perp 1}}-\frac{v_{4\parallel 1}-v_{2}}{v_{4\perp 1}})v_{3\perp 1}t_{3}>0,

(86)

which is the same as Eq.(
85
).
Therefore, the physical interpretation of Eq.(
85
) is that particles 4 and 3 can actually collide.
The corresponding physical process is also shown in Fig.
10
, which is the same as the case of
v_{4\perp 1}>v_{3\perp 1}
.

To sum up, the physical manifestation of singularity is that particle 4 can collide with particle 3 in real space-time.

V.2
For the case of
\theta_{1}=0
or
\pi

In this subsection, we aim to obtain the singularity condition when
\theta_{1}=0
or
\pi
.
At this point, variable
\phi
no longer appears in the integration, and correspondingly, the energy of particle 4 becomes

\displaystyle\omega^{\mp}_{4}(q,\theta)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{q^{2}+k^{2}_{2}+m_{4}^{2}\mp 2qk_{2}\cos\theta},

(87)

where
\mp
correspond to
\theta_{1}=0
and
\pi
, respectively.

{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
can be simplified as follows,

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{b_{1}}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{2\pi q^{2}\mathrm{d}{q}{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}}{M-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{2}(q)+i\varepsilon},

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\cos\theta}}{k^{0}_{1}-\omega_{1}(q)-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{k^{0}_{1}-k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)-\omega^{\mp}_{4}(q,\theta)+i\varepsilon}.

(88)

The pole of the denominator of
{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
is
q=q_{\rm on}+i\varepsilon
, so it requires
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon)\to\infty
to make

diverge.

We then analyze the integral
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}
, whose two denominators each produce a first-order pole.
As mentioned before, there are two ways for
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}
to diverge.
The pole of the first denominator of
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}
is
\cos\theta=Y(q)-i\varepsilon
, so for the case where the two poles are pinched, we only need the imaginary part of the pole of the second denominator to be positive.
We can obtain the imaginary part of the second denominator at the pole with
\cos\theta=Z_{b}+i\varepsilon_{Z}
is as follows,

\displaystyle q\left(\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{3}}\pm\frac{k_{2}}{\omega^{\mp}_{4}}\right)\varepsilon_{Z}+\varepsilon.

(89)

It is easy to find that
\varepsilon_{Z}
is positive only when
\theta_{1}=\pi
and
\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q,\theta)}<\frac{k_{2}}{\omega_{4}(q,\theta)}
when such term to be zero.
We denote the solution under this condition as
Z_{b}(q)
, and then there is:

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\cos\theta}}{(\cos\theta-Y(q)+i\varepsilon)(\cos\theta-Z_{b}(q)-i\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Y(q)-Z_{b}(q)-i\varepsilon}.

(90)

Although we do not provide the explicit expression for
Z_{b}(q)
, we still need to confirm the sign of the following equation,

\displaystyle\text{Im}[Y(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon^{\prime})-Z_{b}(q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon^{\prime})]-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{\prime}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}{\left(Y(q)-Z_{b}(q)\right)}}{\mathrm{d}q}\right|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}-\varepsilon.

(91)

Through calculations similar to Eq.(
72
), we obtain
\mathrm{d}{Z_{b}(q)}/\mathrm{d}{q}
as:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}Z_{b}}{{\rm d}q}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial f_{b}(Z_{b},q)/\partial q}{\partial f_{b}(Z_{b},q)/\partial Z_{b}},

(92)

where
f_{b}(\cos\theta,q)\equiv k^{0}_{1}-k^{0}_{2}-\omega_{3}(q,\theta)-\omega^{+}_{4}(q,\theta)
and
f_{b}(Z_{b}(q),q)=0
is satisfied.
Then we have:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}{(Y(q)-Z_{b}(q))}}{\mathrm{d}{q}}\right|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\left(-\frac{\frac{\partial\omega_{1}}{\partial q}}{\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial\cos\theta}}+\frac{-\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial q}\frac{\partial\omega^{+}_{4}}{\partial\cos\theta}+\frac{\partial\omega^{+}_{4}}{\partial q}\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial\cos\theta}}{\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial\cos\theta}\left(\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial\cos\theta}+\frac{\partial\omega^{+}_{4}}{\partial\cos\theta}\right)}\right)\right|_{\cos\theta\to Z_{b}(q),\,q\to q_{\rm on}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\omega_{3}}{k_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{1}}+\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}k_{1}-\omega_{3}k_{2}}\right)\right|_{\cos\theta\to Z_{b}(q),\,q\to q_{\rm on}}>0.

(93)

Therefore, Eq.(
91
) is always negative, which means that
q_{\rm on}-i\varepsilon
cannot be a pole of
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}
.
In other words, the only way for
{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
to diverge in this case is for the pole to be
\cos\theta=\pm 1
.
The corresponding physical situation is that all particles move on the same line.
There are four different cases where
\theta
and
\theta_{1}
are equal to 0 or
\pi
, and each of these will be discussed in the next four subsections.

V.2.1
\theta_{1}=\theta=\pi

For
\theta_{1}=\theta=\pi
,
{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}
can be calculated as:

\displaystyle{\cal\tilde{I}}^{\prime}_{b}\propto

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{(\cos\theta-(Y(q)-i\varepsilon_{Y}))(\cos\theta-(Z_{b}(q)+i\varepsilon_{Z}))}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\log(-1-(Y(q)-i\varepsilon_{Y}))-\log(-1-(Z_{b}(q)+i\varepsilon_{Z}))}{Y(q)-Z_{b}(q)-i(\varepsilon_{Y}+\varepsilon_{Z})},

(94)

where
\varepsilon_{Y}>0
and the sign of
\varepsilon_{Z}
is undetermined.
Then with
Y(q_{\text{on}})=-1
and
Z_{b}(q_{\text{on}})=-1

\displaystyle Y(q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle Y(q_{\text{on}})-i\varepsilon\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Y(q)}{\mathrm{d}{q}}\right|_{q\to q_{\text{on}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\text{on}}}\left(\frac{q_{\text{on}}/\omega_{1}(q_{\rm on})}{k_{1}/k^{0}_{1}}+1\right),

(95)

\displaystyle Z_{b}(q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle Z_{b}(q_{\text{on}})-i\varepsilon\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{b}(q)}{\mathrm{d}{q}}\right|_{q\to q_{\text{on}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1-\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\rm on}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}-\frac{k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{q_{\rm on}+k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}+\frac{q_{\rm on}-k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}\right),

(96)

We find that the imaginary part of

cannot be the pole of
\log(-1-(Y(q)-i\varepsilon_{Y}))

While for the

\displaystyle-1-(Z_{b}(q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon)+i\varepsilon_{Z})=i\varepsilon\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{b}(q)}{\mathrm{d}{q}}\right|_{q\to q_{\rm on}}-i\varepsilon_{Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\varepsilon_{Z}+\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\rm on}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}-\frac{k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{q_{\rm on}+k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}+\frac{q_{\rm on}-k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\varepsilon_{Z}+\frac{i\varepsilon}{q_{\rm on}}\left(\frac{k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}-\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}\right)^{-1}(v_{4}-v_{3}).

(97)

Since the sign of
\varepsilon_{Z}
is the same as that of
\frac{k_{2}}{\omega^{+}_{4}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}-\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{3}(q_{\rm on},\pi)}

\varepsilon_{Z}>0

Thus, when
\varepsilon_{Z}<0
,

cannot be a pole of the two
\log
terms in
\tilde{\cal I}_{b}^{\prime}
.
Through the discussions similar to those in Sec.
IV.2.4
, we can conclude that

is not a pole of the denominator of Eq. (

In short,

v_{4}>v_{3}>0

The complete integral
{\cal I}_{b_{1}}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{b_{1}}\propto

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}q\frac{\log(q-q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon_{1})-\log(q-q_{\text{on}}+i\varepsilon)}{(q-q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon_{2})(q-q_{\text{on}}-i\varepsilon_{3})}.

V.2.3

In this case, we have:

VI
Mass condition formulae for generating singularity

In the previous sections, we provided the singularity conditions based on velocity relations in various cases of box diagrams, which can be understood from Coleman-Norton theorem.
However, the conditions expressed in terms of velocity relations cannot be directly applied to determine whether a singularity exists in a particular box diagram.
The best way is to express the conditions as relations between masses, which can effectively determine whether there is Landau singularity in a given box diagram.

For the triangle singularity, the authors provide a formula that relates the masses of the involved particles in Ref.
[
37
]
.
We do the same here.
For a triangle loop diagram, if all the intermediate particles are on-shell and all move in the same straight line, then there is a mass relation:

\displaystyle 4M^{2}m_{3}^{2}=

\displaystyle(m^{2}_{1}-m^{2}_{2}+m^{2}_{f_{1}}-m_{f_{2}}^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle-\left(\zeta(M,m_{1},m_{2})-\zeta(M,m_{f_{1}},m_{f_{2}})\right)^{2}.

(102)

Here we define
\zeta(a,b,c)=\sqrt{\lambda(a^{2},b^{2},c^{2})}
for simplicity.
In addition, some conditions need to be met to ensure that all intermediate particles can be on-shell, such as
M>m_{1}+m_{2}
,
m_{2}>m_{f_{1}}+m_{3}
, and
m_{f_{2}}>m_{1}+m_{3}
, which we refer to as the on-shell condition.
Although these conditions are necessary for the singularity, they are not the focus of our work, and they are just obvious and simple comparisons of particle masses.
So in the subsequent discussions, we assume that these on-shell conditions are valid and will not provide specific details.
In Sec.
II
, we have derived that the condition for triangle singularity is
v_{1}<v_{3}
, so we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta(M,m_{f_{1}},m_{f_{2}})}{m^{2}_{i}-m^{2}_{f_{1}}+m^{2}_{f_{2}}}

\displaystyle>\frac{\zeta(M,m_{1},m_{2})}{m^{2}_{i}-m^{2}_{2}+m^{2}_{1}}.

(103)

According to Eqs.(
102
) and (
103

Summary

In this article, we delve into the issue of box singularities in three-body decay processes with box diagrams.
We conduct a thorough analysis to identify the various kinematic conditions that may give rise to singularities.
We conduct a thorough analysis to identify the various kinematic conditions that may give rise to singularities.
From a physical standpoint, we establish two primary criteria that must be satisfied: first, all four internal particles must be on-shell; and second, the conditions must allow for classical collisions, in accordance with the Coleman-Norton theorem.

We emphasize that in scenarios where particles do not move in a straight line, specific velocity constraints are applicable. For Fig.
