The Online Landscape of Brazilian Butt Lift Information: Readability, Quality, and Traffic Analysis
Authors: Elisa Bertulla, Edoardo Raposio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Brazilian Butt Lift (BBL) has become increasingly popular yet remains one of the highest-risk aesthetic procedures. As patients frequently use online sources to understand medical procedures, clear and reliable web-based information is essential.ObjectivesThis study evaluates the readability, understandability, quality, and visibility of online BBL resources.MethodsA Google search for “Brazilian Butt Lift Procedure” was conducted using Startpage to reduce bias. The first 10 eligible English-language websites were analyzed. Extracted text was evaluated using 6 readability indices. Understandability was assessed with the Patient Educational Material Assessment Tool (PEMAT), overall quality with the JAMA (Journal of the American Medical Association) Benchmark Criteria. Website visibility was measured using SpyFu estimates of monthly organic traffic. Spearman's correlation was used to explore the relationship between readability and traffic.ResultsReadability for all websites exceeded the recommended sixth-grade level (mean readability score 21.68), indicating complex content. Sixty percentage of the websites scored 83% in PEMAT due to long sentences and limited explanation of technical terms; the remaining 40% websites met all understandability criteria. Journal of the American Medical Association assessment revealed deficiencies in transparency, with few sites providing references, author credentials, or publication dates. The correlation between readability and organic traffic was weakly positive (ρ = .176), indicating that frequently visited websites were not necessarily easier to read.ConclusionsIn this review, online BBL information was generally difficult to understand and often lacks essential quality indicators. The weak relationship between readability and traffic suggests that popular websites may not provide accessible content. Improving linguistic clarity, structure, and transparency is needed to ensure patients receive reliable, comprehensible guidance on this high-risk procedure.Level of Evidence: 5 (Therapeutic) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

The Brazilian butt lift (BBL) is an increasingly popular cosmetic procedure in recent years.1,2 Despite its name, a BBL does not remove excess skin or reposition sagging skin.3,4 Rather, a BBL is an augmentation of the lower back, hips, posterior thighs, and gluteal region.5,6 The procedure begins with liposuction, which refines the overall silhouette, followed by fat transfer to these areas to enhance volume and contour.7-10 Despite its increasing popularity, the BBL is considered one of the highest risk aesthetic procedures, largely due to the risk of intravascular fat injection, resulting in fat embolism and death.11-15

With the growing digitalization of health information, the internet has become a primary source for patients use to understand complex procedures, evaluate risks and benefits, and making healthcare decisions.16,17 According to European data from 2025, approximately 94% of individuals reported using the internet within the last 3 months.18

The concept of health literacy, referring to people's ability to obtain, understand, and use health information and services in order to make appropriate health decisions is a major determinant of patients' autonomy.18 Research shows that low health literacy drives health disparities, worsens outcomes, makes it harder to get care, and increases mortality.19,20 For this reason, it is crucial that online health information should be written so that it is clear and accessible to its audience.21 Guidelines set forth by the National Institutes of Health and by the American Medical Association specify that consumer medical information should be written at a fourth- to sixth-grade level.22,23 It is also important that such information is of high quality and accurate. Already previous studies published in the medical literature have assessed readability of contents and found out that much of the information is frequently too difficult for the average patient.24,25 This study evaluates the readability, understandability, quality, and visibility of online BBL resources.

METHODS

A web search for “Brazilian Butt Lift Procedure” was performed using Google (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA) the largest internet search engine, in November 2025.

Location, cookies, and user account information were disabled during the search to avoid inadvertent bias in the results, using Startpage (Startpage BV, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Websites were excluded from the study if they were sponsored, duplicates, unrelated, non-English, or lacked sufficient information for a complete review. Scientific articles and journals were also excluded from the study as they are generally inaccessible to the public or do not serve as patient-oriented resources. YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok videos were similarly excluded as they fell outside the scope of this analysis.

Ethical approval was not required for this study as it involved analysis of publicly available online data and did not include human participants or patient-level information.

According to their order of appearance, the first 10 records were selected. The text from each website was extracted and compiled into Microsoft Word (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and were edited to exclude images, videos, figures, captions, advertisements, references, links, disclaimers, and acknowledgements.

A readability analysis was performed using OhToolBox,26 applying 7 standardized readability scores: Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level, Gunning Fog Index, Coleman–Liau Index, Automated Readability Index, and Sample Measure of Gobbledygook Index (SMOG). Reading scores are calculated based on the average sentence length (in words) and word length (in syllables) used throughout the text. The readability scores, except for the Flesch Reading Ease, correspond to a school grade level: the higher the number, the more difficult the text is to read.27 These tools have been widely applied to systematically evaluate the readability and accessibility of written materials across diverse professional domains.28

Because the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating easier readability, the scale was inverted to align directionally with the other readability indices (where higher scores reflect more difficult text). In addition to the primary readability indices, the percentage of complex words (defined as words with ≥3 syllables) was reported as a descriptive measure of lexical complexity. Variability among readability indices was quantified using standard deviation, reflecting differences in underlying metrics despite directional harmonization.

The resulting score for each of the content was tabulated on Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365, 2025) and an average readability score for each website was provided.

Subsequently, a qualitative assessment was conducted using the JAMA (Journal of American Medical Association) Benchmark29,30 and an analysis of understandability using the PEMAT (Patient Educational Material Assessment Tool). PEMAT evaluates understandability and actionability of health materials, with higher scores indicating better quality.31 Only the “Understandability” section of the PEMAT was used in this study, as the website content did not allow for assessment of “Actionability.” The JAMA Benchmark criteria assess transparency based on 4 indicators (authorship, attribution, currency, and disclosure), with higher scores indicating higher quality and reliability.29,30 Finally, website visibility was assessed using SpyFu (SpyFu, Inc., Scottsdale, AZ). For each website, we extracted the estimated organic monthly traffic (corresponding to SpyFu's “Estimated Monthly Search Engine Optimization (SEO) Clicks”) and the number of organic keywords. Paid traffic metrics and total inbound clicks were not included, as they do not reflect natural user engagement. SpyFu did not consistently provide domain authority or backlink data across all websites; therefore, these metrics were excluded from the analysis. For each website, SpyFu identifies all Google search queries in which the domain appears and reports its position in the search engine results page (SERP). (For example, a keyword marked as “Rank: 5” indicates that the website occupies the fifth organic result on Google for that search term).

Statistical analysis was performed using inferential and descriptive and methods. Given the small sample size (n = 10) and the non-normal distribution of traffic values, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between average readability scores and estimated organic monthly traffic. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize readability scores and estimated organic monthly traffic across websites. Additional qualitative assessments, including JAMA benchmarks and PEMAT comprehensibility scores, were reported descriptively to provide a comprehensive overview of website quality, but were not included in the statistical correlation.

All analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA).

Two websites were produced by private clinics (20%), 2 by private physician practices (20%), 2 by national health services (20%), 1 by a scientific society (10%), 1 by a university hospital (1%), 1 by a certification board (10%), and 1 was an educational website (10%).

Only 2 websites were close to the recommended reading level, slightly exceeding it, according to a single metric (FKG 7.3 and 8.10).

Regarding the assessment of understandability using the PEMAT tool (percentage scores, with higher values indicating better understandability), 6 of the analyzed texts (60%) received the same PEMAT score of 83%, as they were lacking in the same specific items—namely, sentence length and adequate explanation of technical terms. These texts were still well structured: possessed of appropriate sequential screenshots and heading titles, clear elucidation of the procedural step with prompts addressing expected questions and presented instructions of a postoperative nature. The remaining 4 websites (40%) fulfilled all PEMAT requirements, using simple and accessible language, shorter sentences, and clear explanations of all technical concepts, thereby making the information more immediate and easier to understand for the general public. Regarding the qualitative assessment using the JAMA Benchmark Criteria (4 criteria scored 0-1; total score range 0-4, with higher values indicating higher transparency and quality), only 1 website (10%) achieved the maximum score across all sections (4/4). Two websites (20%) obtained a score of 0/4, 3 (30%) a score of 1/4, 2 (20%) a score of 2/4, and 2 (20%) a score of 3/4. This highlights that, in most websites lacked references, author's name and credentials, and the publication or update date, and in some cases, an explicit disclosure of conflicts of interest was also absent.

DISCUSSION

One important social determinant of health is health literacy, which is the capacity to obtain, comprehend, and apply health information. Lower levels of health literacy are associated with poorer health outcomes, less access to care, and greater difficulty navigating healthcare services, thereby contributing to the widening of health disparities among different social groups.32,33

The increasing digitalization of health information offers new opportunities but also risks of creating a greater divide for those with limited digital skills or low health literacy. People with low health literacy are more likely to be exposed to low quality information because they tend to rely on less authoritative sources, such as social media and blogs.34

BBL is a very common procedure, so it is important that the online information is clear, and understandable.

In this study, we evaluated the readability, understandability, overall quality, and SEO performance of 10 websites providing information about BBL. Most of the websites were based in the USA, with the remaining 2 located in the UK, and 1 in Canada. The websites represented a variety of sources, including private clinics, individual physician practices, national health services, a scientific society, a university hospital, a certification board, and an educational portal. This diversity reflects the heterogeneous nature of online health information providers.

A previous study35 has evaluated the readability and understandability of online BBL information. Our study revisits this topic 5 years later to examine potential changes and further adds a qualitative analysis using the JAMA Benchmark Criteria and a website traffic analysis to explore whether the most visited sites are also the most comprehensible. Similarly to Oleck et al,35 our results also show a readability level exceeding the grade 6 reading level recommended by the National Institutes of Health36 and the American Medical Association.37

Only 2 websites were close to the recommended reading level, slightly exceeding it, according to a single metric (FKG 7.3 and 8.10).

These findings are in line with observations in other surgical and medical fields, where the readability of online materials reaching between grade 9 and grade 14—corresponding to high school or college-level reading—which represents a barrier to patient comprehension and active engagement.38-40

Factors contributing to the difficulty of these texts include technical and complex language, such as the use of medical terminology, long sentences, and complex grammatical structures; longer materials, lack of clear sectioning, bullet points, and headings, which reduces accessibility; and the absence of images, diagrams, or videos that could facilitate understanding, particularly disadvantaging individuals with low health literacy.16,17,41

The assessment of understandability using the PEMAT tool revealed that approximately half of the websites scored 83%, indicating minor shortcomings in sentence length and the explanation of technical terms. Despite these limitations, these websites were generally well structured, providing clear headings, step-by-step procedural explanations, anticipatory guidance for common questions, and understandable postoperative instructions. The remaining websites fulfilled all PEMAT criteria, using accessible language, shorter sentences, and clear explanations of technical concepts, thus facilitating comprehension for the general public. In summary, the assessment highlights that even comprehensive and informative texts can benefit from clearer language and immediate definitions of technical terms, in order to make the information fully accessible to all readers.

The qualitative assessment using the JAMA Benchmark Criteria indicated that the most websites lacked references, author credentials, publication dates, and, in some cases, explicit disclosure of conflicts of interest. This underscores persistent gaps in transparency and accountability across health-related websites, with potential implications for the perceived credibility of its content.

The correlation between average readability scores and estimated organic monthly traffic was weakly positive (Spearman's ρ = 0.176, P = .63), indicating that, overall, websites with higher readability scores (more complex text) tended to have slightly higher traffic, although the relationship is weak and not statistically significant.

Site-specific analysis revealed distinct patterns. Two websites (managed by a certification board and an educational portal) showed alignment between text complexity and traffic; 5 websites exhibited higher traffic and lower readability (more difficult to read), suggesting that the websites with the highest visibility were also those with more difficult-to-read content, which may pose a potential issue for patient comprehension. In contrast, 3 sites—including 2 runs by physicians and one by private clinic—had higher readability relative to their traffic. This could reflect their low traffic, or it may indicate that more readable content does not necessarily correspond to higher visibility.

These observations suggest that while readability influences comprehension, a weak correlation with traffic indicates that popularity does not necessarily correspond to easier-to-read content. While our analysis did not find a strong correlation between readability and organic traffic, it is generally recognized that more readable content can enhance user engagement and accessibility, potentially contributing to better SEO performance and wider reach.42-44

Overall, our findings emphasize the need for balancing content complexity and accessibility.

While some websites provide detailed and technically precise information, overly complex text may limit comprehension for the general public. Conversely, simpler and properly structured content improves comprehension, but it does not necessarily lead to a greater number of visitors. Moreover, adherence to established quality criteria, such as JAMA benchmarks, remains limited, which indicates that many websites could benefit from improved transparency regarding authorship, sources, and potential conflicts of interest. These findings highlight the need for ongoing work to enhance both the clarity and reliability of medical information available online, especially for the public who need accessible and trustworthy resources.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the small number of websites analyzed and the use of only English-language content may not be entirely representative of all online content on this topic. In addition, only Google was used to identify websites, whereas patients may also access other search engines and lower-ranked sites that were not included. Third, the estimated organic traffic reflects general search engine activity and does not allow differentiation of user type; therefore, it cannot be assumed that all visits originated from patients, as traffic may also include healthcare professionals, students, or other nonpatient user. Fourth, the qualitative assessment using the JAMA Benchmark Criteria naturally suffers from subjectivity due of the nature of human-based assessment although it is conducted in a structured framework. Finally, the cross-sectional design of this study only captures a snapshot of website content and visibility at a single point in time. Websites are frequently updated, and traffic patterns may change over time, which may affect the reproducibility of the results.

CONCLUSIONS

In this sample of 10 websites, online information about the Brazilian Butt Lift remains difficult for the general public to understand, with readability levels consistently exceeding recommended standards. Although some websites were well structured, technical language and insufficient explanation of key terms limited overall comprehensibility. Quality assessment further revealed frequent gaps in transparency, including missing references, author credentials, and publication dates. The weak correlation between readability and organic traffic indicates that highly visited websites are not necessarily more accessible, potentially exposing patients to complex information that may hinder informed decision-making. Overall, these findings highlight the need for clearer language, improved structure, and greater adherence to quality standards to ensure that online BBL resources are both accurate and accessible to patients seeking reliable guidance about this procedure.
