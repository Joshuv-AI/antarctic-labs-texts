Abstract
. A metric measure space ( X , μ ) (X,\mu) is 1-regular if 0 < lim r → 0 μ ⁡ ( B ⁡ ( x , r ) ) r < ∞ 0<\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{r}<\infty for μ \mu -a.e x ∈ X x\in X . We give a complete geometric characterisation of the rectifiable and purely unrectifiable part of a 1-regular measure in terms of its tangent spaces. A special instance of a 1-regular metric measure space is a 1-uniform space ( Y , ν ) (Y,\nu) , which satisfies ν ⁡ ( B ⁡ ( y , r ) ) = r \nu(B(y,r))=r for all y ∈ Y y\in Y and r > 0 r>0 . We prove that there are exactly three 1-uniform metric measure spaces.



1.
Introduction

Geometric measure theory began with the pioneering work of Besicovitch.
One of the most striking, yet simplest to state results is the following
[
4
]
.
If
\mu
is a Borel measure on the plane for which

(1.1)

\Theta^{1}(\mu,x):=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{2r}

exists and is positive and finite
\mu
-a.e, then
\mu
is 1-rectifiable.
That is,
\mu
almost all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be covered by countably many Lipschitz images of
\mathbb{R}
.
Besicovitch’s argument was extended to 1-dimensional measures in higher dimension Euclidean spaces by
[
12
]
, but question about higher dimensional measures, known as the
Besicovitch problem
remained open for many years.
Partial results due to
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
proved the
n
-rectifiability of
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{E}
whenever

(1.2)

\Theta^{n}(\mu,x):=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{(2r)^{n}}

equals 1 almost everywhere.
Further,
[
10
]
proved that if
\Theta^{s}(\mu,x)
exists and is positive and finite
\mu
-a.e, then
s
must be an integer.
It was not until 50 years after Besicovitch’s work that
[
13
]
positively resolved the Besicovitch problem.

In this paper we consider the Besicovitch problem in arbitrary metric spaces.
To set the stage, we recall another result of
[
10
]
which states that, if
\Theta^{s}(\mu,x)
is positive and finite in any metric space, then
s\geq 1
.
Further, for
s>1
, if
\mathbb{R}
is considered with the metric
d(x,y)=\sqrt[s]{|x-y|}
, then 1-dimensional Lebesgue measure satisfies
\mathcal{L}^{1}(B(x,r))=2r^{s}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
.
However, this metric space is purely 1-unrectifiable.
That is,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)=0
for every 1-rectifiable
\gamma\subset\sqrt[s]{\mathbb{R}}
.
In particular,
\sqrt[s]{\mathbb{R}}
is not
n
-rectifiable for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, for general metric spaces, the remaining question is the validity of the 1-dimensional Besicovitch problem.

The argument of Besicovitch is of a combinatorial nature and in fact proves the rectifiability of measures satisfying (
1.1
) in any uniformly convex Banach space.
Moreover, the argument applies to any metric space with a reasonable notion of “strict convexity” such as the existence of a
unique
geodesic connecting any two points in the space.
However, the argument does not give any insight to the problem in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.
A related question is the Besicovitch
\tfrac{1}{2}
conjecture
[
14
,
8
]
.
However, even a solution of the most general form of this conjecture would not imply the rectifiability of measures satisfying (
1.1
).

One could reasonably expect that the 1-dimensional Besicovitch problem holds in any metric space.
The fact that any connected compact set of finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
measure is 1-rectifiable often allows for different and significantly simpler arguments for the case of 1-dimensional sets.
This is particularly evident in the proof of the Besicovitch problem in Euclidean spaces.
However, the expectation is in fact false.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
e_{i}
be the standard basis of
\ell_{1}
.
The set

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{i}s_{i}e_{i}:s_{i}\in\{0,1\},\ s_{i}=0\text{ for sufficiently large }i\right\}\subset\ell_{1}

equipped with
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is purely 1-unrectifiable and satisfies

(1.3)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(x,r))=r\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{S},\ r>0.

A measure satisfying (
1.3
) is called
1-uniform
.
It is a very special example of a measure that satisfies (
1.1
).
Measures that satisfy (
1.1
) are called
1-regular
.

Naturally, one now wonders if there are any other 1-uniform metric measure spaces.
Using
\mathcal{S}
one can construct another 1-uniform space by placing a circle
\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z}
at those points in
\mathcal{S}
with
s_{i}=0
for all
i<0
(see
Definition

3.4
).
We denote this space by
\mathcal{T}
.
These two spaces, together with
\mathbb{R}
, are the only 1-uniform metric measure spaces.

Theorem 1.2
.

A 1-uniform metric measure space is isometric to a scaled copy of
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{T}
.

It is interesting to observe that all of these spaces are equipped with a constant multiple of Hausdorff measure.
Also, any two points can be exchanged by an isometry of the space.

With
Theorem

1.2
in hand, we prove a structure theorem for 1-regular metric measure spaces in terms of their tangent spaces.
Tangent
measures
were introduced by Preiss in his solution of the Besicovitch problem in Euclidean space.
Roughly speaking, a tangent measure is a weak* limit of a Borel measure under dilations of the form
y\mapsto(y-x)/r_{i}
as
r_{i}\to 0
, normalised by some scaling factor.
The set of all tangent measures to a measure
\mu
at
x
is denoted by
\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x)
.
Tangent measures naturally generalise to the concept of a tangent
space
of a metric measure space.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a 1-regular metric measure space.
There exists a decomposition
X=E\cup S
such that for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
,
\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x)=\{\mathbb{R}\}
and, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in S
,
\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x)=\{\lambda\mathcal{S}:\lambda>0\}
.
Moreover,
\mu|_{E}
is 1-rectifiable and
S
is purely 1-unrectifiable.

The moreover part of
Theorem

1.3
is given by work of the author
[
2
]
which proves a characterisation of
n
-rectifiable metric spaces in terms of flat tangent spaces.
The rectifiability statement also follows from joint work with Valentine
[
3
]
, which proves the 1-rectifiability of metric measures spaces with connected tangents.
Tangent spaces of 1-regular spaces are 1-uniform.
Therefore, to prove
Theorem

1.3
we must show that
\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x)
consists of
either

\mathbb{R}
or
\mathcal{S}
, and not a combination of the two and
\mathcal{T}
.

Theorem

1.3
allows us to solve the 1-dimensional Besicovitch problem in various classes of metric spaces.
We refer the reader to
Section

7
for the relevant definitions.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X
be one of the following:

(1)

A doubling geodesic metric space.

(2)

A uniformly convex Banach space.

(3)

The Heisenberg group equipped with any homogeneous norm.

Then any 1-regular measure on
X
is 1-rectifiable.

Theorem

1.4
will follow from
Theorem

1.3
by showing that
\mathcal{S}
does not isometrically embed into any such
X
.

The article is structured as follows.
In
Section

2
we give various preliminary lemmas that we will use throughout the argument.
We also recall the definition of a tangent space and several results related to them.
In
Section

3
we show that
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
are purely 1-unrectifiable and 1-uniform.
In
Section

4
we adapt the notion of a Besicovitch
circle pair
from
[
4
]
so that the definition is suitable for working in an arbitrary metric space.
We then deduce several structural consequences of the existence of a Besicovitch pair in a 1-uniform metric measure space.
Since we consider metric spaces without any notion of strict convexity, our arguments completely diverge from those of Besicovitch.

Section

5
contains our classification of 1-uniform spaces using the results on Besicovitch pairs.
Finally,
Section

6
contains the proof of
Theorem

1.3
and
Section

7
the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

For notational simplicity, from this point on we will not use the “1-” prefix and simply refer to a rectifiable, purely unrectifiable, regular or uniform metric measure space.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Geometric properties of metric spaces

Throughout this article,
(X,d)
will denote a complete metric space and a
measure
will refer to a
\sigma
-finite Borel regular (outer) measure.
For
\lambda>0
, let
\lambda X
denote the metric space
(X,\lambda d)
.
For
x\in X
and
r\geq 0
, let
B(x,r)
be the
open
ball of radius
r
and
C(x,r)
the
closure
of
B(x,r)
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a measure
\mu
on
X
we define the
density
of
\mu
to be

\Theta^{1}(\mu,x):=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{2r}

whenever the limit exists.
Note that, in the case that the limit exists, we can use either open or closed balls or
C(x,r)
and obtain an equivalent definition and value of
\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)
.
The measure
\mu
is
regular
if
\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)
exists
\mu
-a.e.
Further,
\mu
is
uniform
if
\mu(B(x,r))=r
for every
x\in X
and
r\geq 0
.

The (1-dimensional) Hausdorff measure is defined by

\mathcal{H}^{1}(A):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{diam}A_{i}:A\subset\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}A_{i}\right\}

for any Borel subset
A
of a metric space
X
.
Recall that there is a natural relationship between Hausdorff measure and density: if
\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)\leq\lambda
for all
x
is a Borel set
A
then
\mu(A)\leq 2\lambda\mathcal{H}^{1}(A)
; if
\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)\geq\lambda
for all
x\in A
then
\mu(A)\geq\lambda\mathcal{H}^{1}(A)
.
See
[
5
, Section 2.10.19]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
S
of a complete metric space
X
is
rectifiable
if there exist countably many
A_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
and Lipschitz
\gamma_{i}\colon A_{i}\to X
such that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(S\setminus\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}(A_{i})\right)=0.

By a result of Kirchheim
[
7
, Lemma 4]
, for any
\epsilon>0
an equivalent definition is obtained if each
\gamma_{i}
is
(1+\epsilon)
-biLipschitz.
If
X
is a Banach space, by linearly extending each
\gamma_{i}
on the complement of each
A_{i}
, we get an equivalent definition if we require each
A_{i}=\mathbb{R}
.

The following result of Kirchheim
[
7
, Theorem 9]
shows that rectifiable sets are regular.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X
be a rectifiable metric space with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)<\infty
.
Then

\Theta^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1},x)=1

for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in X
.

Regular measures on a metric space are related in the following way.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
\mu,\nu
two measures on
X
such that

0<\Theta^{1}(\nu,x)\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)<\infty

for every
x
in some Borel
B\subset X
.
Then
\mu|_{B}\ll\nu|_{B}
and the Radon-Nikodym derivative is given by

\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}=\frac{\Theta^{1}(\mu,)}{\Theta^{1}(\nu,)}

Proof.

Since both
\mu|_{B}\ll\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{B}
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{B}\ll\nu|_{B}
,
\mu|_{B}\ll\nu|_{B}
.
To prove the statement about the Radon-Nikodym derivatives, it suffices to suppose that both
\mu
and
\nu
are finite.

To this end, first note that
\nu
is asymptotically doubling.
That is,

\frac{\nu(B(x,2r))}{\nu(B(x,r))}<\infty

for
\nu
-a.e.
x\in X
and all sufficiently small
r>0
.
Therefore the Lebesgue differentiation theorem holds for
\nu
integrable functions on
X

[
6
, Section 3.4]
, such as
d\mu/d\nu
.
By applying it we obtain

\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}(x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{\nu(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}d\nu=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{\nu(B(x,r))}=\frac{\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)}{\Theta^{1}(\nu,x)}

for
\nu
-a.e.
x\in X
, as required.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)<\infty
for which
\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)=1
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in X
.
Suppose that
R\subset X
is rectifiable and has finite
\mu
measure.
Then
\mu|_{R}=\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)<\infty
, by
[
5
, Section 2.10.19]
,
\Theta^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{X\setminus R},x)=0
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in R
.
Since
\mu\ll\mathcal{H}^{1}
,
\Theta^{1}(\mu|_{X\setminus R},x)=0
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in R
and so
\Theta^{1}(\mu|_{R},x)=1
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in R
.
By
Theorem

2.3
,
\Theta^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{R},x)=1
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in R
, and so the result follows from applying
Lemma

2.4
to the
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-full measure set where
\Theta^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{R},x)=\Theta^{1}(\mu|_{R},x)=1
.
∎

The following result is standard.

Lemma 2.6
.

Any uniform metric measure space is
proper
.
That is, closed and bounded sets are compact.

Proof.

We show that any ball
B(x,r)
can be covered by at most 5 closed balls of radius
r/2
.
Inductively, this implies that any ball is totally bounded.
Therefore, any closed and bounded set, being complete and totally bounded, is compact.

Let
x_{i}
,
i\in I
be a maximal
r/2
separated subset of
B(x,r)
.
Then
B(x,r)\subset\cup_{i\in I}B(x_{i},r/2)
.
Moreover the
B(x_{i},r/4)
are disjoint subsets of
B(x,5r/4)
and so

|I|r/4=\sum_{i\in I}\tilde{\mu}(B(x,r/4))=\tilde{\mu}(\cup_{i\in I}B(x,r/4))\leq\tilde{\mu}(B(x,5r/4))=5r/4.

Therefore
|I|\leq 5
.
∎

We will inductively construct functions using the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(X,d),(Y,\rho)
be two metric spaces,
S_{1}\subset S_{2}\subset S_{3}\subset\ldots
an increasing sequence of subsets of
X
and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}\colon S_{n}\to Y
such that, for every
m\leq n
,
f_{n}|_{S_{m}}=f_{m}
.
If each
f_{n}
is 1-Lipschitz/injective/an isometry, then so is

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\colon\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}S_{n}\to Y

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=f_{n}(x)\text{ whenever }x\in S_{n}.

Proof.

If
x,y\in S
then there exists an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x,y\in S_{n}
.
Then, if each
f_{n}
is 1-Lipschitz,

\rho(f(x),f(y))=\rho(f_{n}(x),f_{n}(y))\leq d(x,y)

and hence
f
is 1-Lipschitz.

If each
f_{n}
is an isometry then the same argument with the inequality replaced by an equality shows that
f
is an isometry.
A similar argument proves the result when each
f_{n}
is injective.
∎

2.2.
Tangents of metric measure spaces

[
13
]
introduced the notion of a
tangent measure
of a measure
\mu
on Euclidean space as one step the solution of the classical Besicovitch problem.
In
[
2
]
, these ideas were extended to define
tangent spaces
of a metric measure space.
We begin by recalling several results and constructions from
[
2
]
.

Definition 2.8

(Definition 2.12
[
2
]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
x\in X
.
For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
, and
L,r>0
, define

F^{L,r}_{x}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\left\{\int g\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu):g\colon X\to[-1,1],\ L\text{-Lipschitz},\ \operatorname{spt}g\subset B(x,r)\right\}.

Define
F_{x}(\mu,\nu)
to be the infimum, over all
0<\epsilon<1/2
, for which

F_{x}^{1/\epsilon,1/\epsilon}(\mu,\nu)<\epsilon.

If no such
0<\epsilon<1/2
exists, set
F_{x}(\mu,\nu)=1/2
.

Definition 2.9

(Definition 4.9
[
2
]
)
.

A
pointed
metric measure space
(X,d,\mu,x)
consists of a metric measure space and a distinguished point
x\in X
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,x)
and
(Y,\rho,\nu,y)
be pointed metric measure spaces.
Define

d_{*}((X,d,\mu,x),(Y,\rho,\nu,y))

to be the infimum, over all pointed metric spaces
(Z,z)
and all isometric embeddings
(X,x),(Y,y)\to(Z,z)
of
F_{z}(\mu,\nu)
.

Note that
F_{z}^{L,r}
is increasing in both
L
and
r
.
By
[
2
, Corollary 4.13]
,
d_{*}
is a complete and separable metric on
\mathbb{M}_{*}
, the equivalence classes of all pointed metric measure spaces under the relation

(X,\mu,x)\sim(Y,\nu,y)\text{ if }(\operatorname{spt}\mu\cup\{x\},\mu,x)\text{ is isometric to }(\operatorname{spt}\nu\cup\{y\},\nu,y).

Since an element of
\mathbb{M}_{*}
is determined by the support of the measure alone, we will often omit the metric space from notation.
Similarly, we will only specify an explicit metric when necessary.

Given
(\mu,d,x)\in\mathbb{M}_{*}
with
x\in\operatorname{spt}\mu
and
r>0
, let

T_{r}(\mu,d,x):=\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu(B(x,r))},\frac{d}{r},x\right)

Definition 2.10

(Definition 5.1
[
2
]
)
.

A
(\nu,\rho,y)\in\mathbb{M}_{*}
is a
tangent measure
of
(\mu,d,x)\in\mathbb{M}_{*}
if there exist
r_{k}\to 0
such that

d_{*}(T_{r_{k}}(\mu,d,x),(\nu,\rho,y))\to 0.

The set of all tangent measures of
(\mu,d,x)
will be denoted by
\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,d,x)
.

The tangent spaces of asymptotically doubling measures are compact metric spaces.

Proposition 2.11

(Proposition 5.3
[
2
]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
be asymptotically doubling.
For
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\operatorname{spt}\mu
and every
r_{0}>0
,

(2.1)

\{T_{r}(\mu,x):0<r<r_{0}\}\text{ is pre-compact.}

In particular, for any
x\in\operatorname{spt}\mu
satisfying (
2.1
),
\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x)
is a non-empty compact metric space when equipped with
d_{*}
and

(2.2)

\forall\delta>0,\ \exists r_{x}>0\text{ s.t. }d_{*}(T_{r}(\mu,x),\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x))\leq\delta\ \forall 0<r<r_{x}.

By the Lebesgue density theorem, tangents also pass to subsets.

Lemma 2.12

(
[
2
]
Corollary 5.7)
.

Let
(\mu,d,x)\in\mathbb{M}_{*}
and let
S\subset\operatorname{spt}\mu
be
\mu
-measurable.
Then for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in S
,

\operatorname{Tan}(\mu|_{S},x)=\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x).

A near identical argument to
[
2
, Lemma 5.6]
gives the following.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space and suppose, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
,

0<\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)<\infty.

Then for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
, every
(\nu,y)\in\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,x)
and every

,

\frac{\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)}{\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)}\leq\frac{\nu(B(z,r))}{r}\leq\frac{\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)}{\Theta^{1}(\mu,x)}.

In particular, if

Tangents of rectifiable sets are isometric to
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 2.14

(Theorem 1.1
[
2
]
)
.

If
E\subset X
is 1-rectifiable, then for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in E
,
\operatorname{Tan}(E,\mathcal{H}^{1},x)=\{(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{H}^{1}/2)\}
.

In
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
the converse statement is proven (in any dimension).
In fact, it suffices that the tangent spaces are supported on bi-Lipschitz images of Euclidean space.
For 1-dimensional metric spaces, it suffices that all tangent measures are supported on connected metric spaces
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
.
We record the following corollary.

Theorem 2.15
.

Suppose that for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in X
,
\Theta^{1}_{*}(\mathcal{H}^{1},x)>0
and

\operatorname{Tan}(X,\mathcal{H}^{1},x)=\{(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{H}^{1}/2)\}.

Then
X
is 1-rectifiable.

We require an adaptation of Preiss’s classical “tangents of tangents are tangents”.

Lemma 2.16
.

For
(X,d,\mu,x)\in\mathbb{M}_{*}
suppose that

\Psi:=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{\mu(B(x,r+\epsilon))}>0.

For any
(Y,\rho,\nu,y)\in\operatorname{Tan}(X,d,\mu,x)
and
s>0
, there exists
\Phi
satisfying
\Psi\leq\Phi\leq 1
such that

\Phi T_{s}(\nu,\rho,y):=\left(\frac{\Phi\nu}{\nu(B(y,s))},\frac{\rho}{s},y\right)\in\operatorname{Tan}(X,d,\mu,x).

In particular, if
x
satisfies (
2.1
),

\operatorname{Tan}(\nu,\rho,y)\subset[1,\Psi^{-1}]\operatorname{Tan}(\mu,d,x).

Proof.

Let
r_{i}\to 0
such that
T_{r_{i}}(\mu,d,x)\to(\nu,\rho,y)
.
Then

\left(\frac{\mu(B(x,r_{i}s))}{\nu(B(y,s))\mu(B(x,r_{i}))}\frac{\mu}{\mu(B(x,r_{i}s))},\frac{d}{r_{i}s},x\right)\to T_{s}(\nu,\rho,y)

For any
\epsilon>0
,

1\geq\frac{\mu(B(x,r_{i}s))}{\nu(B(y,s))\mu(B(x,r_{i}))}\geq\frac{\mu(B(x,r_{i}))}{\mu(B(x,r_{i}+\epsilon))}

for sufficiently large
i
.
Therefore, by passing to a further subsequence, we may suppose that the inner quantity converges to some
\Phi
satisfying the required condition.

For any
s_{i}\to 0
, there exists

3.
A purely unrectifiable uniform metric measure space

We will consider the following subset of binary sequences with the following metric and measure.

Definition 3.1
.

Let

\mathcal{S}=\{s\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\exists\ n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ s.t. }s_{i}=0\ \forall\ i\geq n\}

equipped with the metric

d(s,t)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{i}|s_{i}-t_{i}|

and 1-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{1}
.

Note that the metric is well defined on
\mathcal{S}
and that
\mathcal{S}
is isometric to the set in
Theorem

1.1
in the introduction.
All distinguished points in
\mathcal{S}
are equivalent: for any
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
there exists an isometry of
\mathcal{S}
exchanging
x
and
y
.

We first show that
\mathcal{S}
is uniform.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{S}
and
r\geq 0
,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(x,r))=r
.

Proof.

For any
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the map

\displaystyle T\colon\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto t_{i}+s_{i}\mod 2

is an isometry that maps
s
to
0
.
Since
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is preserved by isometries, it suffices to prove that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(0,r))=r
for each
r>0
.

To see this, for any
r>0
we may identify
r
with its binary representation
r\in\mathcal{S}
, chosen such that
r
does not have a recurring 1, and let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
be the largest integer with
r_{n}\neq 0
.
For each
i\leq n
let

r^{j}=\sum_{i=j}^{n}2^{i}r_{i}.

Also let

C=\{t\in\mathcal{S}:\exists m\text{ s.t. }t_{i}=1\ \forall i\leq m\}.

Then
C
is countable and so
\mathcal{H}^{1}(C)=0
.

Fix
m\leq n
.
We will estimate the Hausdorff measure of
B(0,r)\setminus C
from above by covering it by sets of diameter at most
2^{m}
.
First, for any

p=(p_{m},p_{m+1},\ldots,p_{n-2},p_{n-1})\in\{0,1\}^{n-m}

let

S(p)=\{t\in B(0,r):t_{i}=p_{i}\ \forall\ m\leq i<n\}.

Then for any
s,t\in S(p)
,

d(s,t)\leq\sum_{i<m}2^{i}|s_{i}-t_{i}|\leq 2^{m},

so that each
S(p)
has diameter at most
2^{m}
.
Moreover, if
d(0,s)<r^{n}
, then
s_{i}=0
for each
i\geq n
, so that

B(0,r^{n})\subset\bigcup\{S(p):p\in\{0,1\}^{n-m}\}.

Second, for any
i<n
with
r_{i}=1
let

S_{i}=\{s\in B(0,r):s_{j}=r_{j}\ \forall\ i<j\leq n,s_{i}=0\}.

If
s\not\in C
and
d(0,s)\geq r^{n}
then
s_{n}=r_{n}=1
.
Also, since
r
does not have a recurring 1, if
d(0,s)\leq r
then
s_{i}\leq r_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, so that if
d(0,s)<r
then
s_{i}<r_{i}
for some
i<n
.
Therefore, we may cover
(B(0,r)\setminus C)\setminus B(0,r^{n})
by
\cup\{S_{i}:i<n,\ r_{i}=1\}
.
Finally, notice that the diameter of
S_{i}
is at most
2^{i}
.

By combining these observations,
B(0,r)
is covered by

\bigcup\{S(p):p\in\{0,1\}^{n-m}\}\cup\bigcup\{s_{i}:i<n,\ r_{i}=1\}.

Since each of these sets have diameter at most
2^{m}
, with
m\leq n
chosen arbitrarily, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(0,r))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=1}^{2^{n-m}}2^{m}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<n\\
r_{i}=1\end{subarray}}2^{i}

\displaystyle=2^{n}+\sum_{i<n}2^{i}r_{i}=r.

To see the other inequality, note that the map

\displaystyle B(0,r)

\displaystyle\to[0,r)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{i}s_{i}

is 1-Lipschitz and injective, so that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(0,r))\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}([0,r))=r
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(0,r))=r
.
∎

Next we show that
S
is purely unrectifiable.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\gamma\colon A\subset\mathbb{R}\to S
be biLipschitz.
Then
\mathcal{L}^{1}(A)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be
L
-biLipschitz.
For any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we can cover
S
by
2^{j}
sets of the form
T_{j}(x_{1}),T_{j}(x_{2}),\ldots,T_{j}(x_{2^{j}})
of diameter
2^{-j}
.
These sets are separated by distance at least
2^{-j}
.
Therefore, the sets
A_{j}^{i}=\gamma^{-1}(T_{j}(x_{i}))
have diameter at most
L\cdot 2^{-j}
and are separated by distance at least
2^{-j}/L
.
Since
A\subset\cup_{i}A_{j}^{i}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
A
cannot have any density points and so must have measure zero.
∎

This lemma establishes
Theorem

1.1
.

Next we consider the following space.

Definition 3.4
.

Let

\mathcal{T}=\{(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}:\exists\ n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ s.t. }s_{i}=0\ \forall i\geq n\}

equipped with the metric

\mathcal{H}^{1}/2
.

As is the case for
\mathcal{S}
, all distinguished points in
\mathcal{T}
are equivalent.

The set
\mathcal{T}
is 1-rectifiable, since it is a countable union of circles
\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
Also
\mathcal{H}^{1}
restricted to
\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z}
is simply Lebesgue measure.
The space
\mathcal{T}
is also uniform.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{T}
and
r\geq 0
,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(x,r))=2r
.

Proof.

Let
(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
r>0
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be maximal with
n<r
and let
r^{\prime}=r-n
, so that
0<r^{\prime}\leq 1
.
Then, if
d((x,s),(y,t))<r
,

d(x,y)+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{i}|s_{i}-t_{i}|<r^{\prime}+n,

so that

(3.1)

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{i}|s_{i}-t_{i}|\leq n,

and, if
eq.

3.1
is an equality, then
d(x,y)<r^{\prime}
.
Note that there is only one value of
t
, which we denote by
t^{*}
, that makes
eq.

3.1
an equality, and it is such that
|s-t|
is the binary representation of
n
.
That is,

(3.2)

B((x,s),r)\subset\bigcup\{\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\{t\}:\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{i}|s_{i}-t_{i}|<n\}\cup B(x,r^{\prime})\times\{t^{*}\}.

A direct calculation shows that the reverse inequality, and hence equality, holds.
Also note that this decomposition is disjoint.

Since the values of
t
that satisfy
eq.

3.1
as a strict inequality correspond to the binary representations of those integers
0\leq m<n
,
Since
eq.

3.2
is a disjoint decomposition, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}(B((x,s),r))

\displaystyle=n\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}/2\mathbb{Z})+B(x,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=n2+2r^{\prime}=2r.

∎

Definition 3.6
.

In what follows we write
\mathbb{R}
for the real line equipped with the measure
\mathcal{H}^{1}/2
, so that
\mathbb{R}
is uniform.

Define

\mathcal{U}=\{\mathbb{R},T_{r}\mathcal{T},T_{r}\mathcal{S}:r>0\}.

If
X
is uniform, then so is
\mathcal{T}_{r}X
for all

and so the elements of

are all uniform spaces.
The content of the next two sections shows that
\mathcal{U}
contains all 1-uniform spaces.

4.
Besicovitch pairs

The work of
[
4
]
relies on finding a certain configuration of points called a Besicovitch pair.
Besicovitch pairs also feature in the work of
[
14
,
8
]
.
We will also make heavy use of Besicovitch pairs, but we require a slightly different formulation than
[
4
]
(but the two are equivalent in Euclidean space) in order to work in an arbitrary metric space.

Definition 4.1
.

A pair of points
a,b
in a metric space is a
Besicovitch pair
if for any
x\in B(a,d(a,b))
and any
y\in B(b,d(a,b))
,

d(x,y)\geq d(a,b).

Of particular importance is the relationship between Besicovitch pairs and connectivity of the space.
In
[
4
]
, Besicovitch pairs are found as a consequence of a set of finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
measure being purely unrectifiable.
In the presence of a uniform measure, we will find them as a consequence of a ball being disconnected.

We emphasise that, although the definition of a Besicovitch pair is motivated by the approach in
[
4
]
, those techniques strongly rely on the strict convexity of the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is not true in general.
As a result, our method is entirely different.

First we prove several metric properties of a uniform metric measure space.
Recall that
C(x,r)
is the closure of the open ball
B(x,r)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a uniform metric measure space,
x\in X
and
r>0
.

(1)

If
C(x,r)
is open then there exists a
y\not\in C(x,r)
with
d(x,y)=r
.

(2)

If
U\subset X
is open and
F\subset U
is a full measure subset.
Then the closure of
F
equals the closure of
U
.

(3)

If
B(x,r)
contains disjoint balls
B(y_{1},r_{1})
and
B(y_{2},r_{2})
with
r=r_{1}+r_{2}
and
r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0
then

C(x,r)=C(y_{1},r_{1})\cup C(y_{2},r_{2}).

(4)

If
y,z\in X
with
B(y,d(y,z))\cap B(x,d(x,y))=\emptyset
and
d(x,z)>d(x,y)
, then

d(x,z)=d(x,y)+d(y,z).

(5)

There can be at most one point
y\in X
with
d(x,y)=r
for which there exists a
\delta>0
such that

B(x,r)\cap B(y,\delta)=\emptyset.

Proof.

To prove
item

1
, for each
\epsilon>0

\mu(B(x,r+\epsilon))=r+\epsilon>r=\mu(C(x,r))

and so there exists a
y\in B(x,r+\epsilon)\setminus C(x,r)
.
Since this is true for all
\epsilon>0
and since
C(b,r+1)
is compact, there exists a
y\in C(x,r+1)\setminus C(b,r)
with
d(x,y)=r
, as required.

Since
U
minus the closure of
F
is open,
if it contains a point it must contain an open ball, which necessarily has positive measure.
This is impossible since
F
is a full measure subset of
U
.
Therefore, the closure of
U
is contained in the closure of
F
.
Since
F\subset U
, this proves
item

2
.

To prove
item

3
, observe that, since
\mu
is uniform and
B(y_{1},r_{1})
and
B(y_{2},r_{2})
are disjoint subsets of
B(x,r)
,

\mu(B(x,r)\setminus(B(x,r_{1})\cup B(y,r_{2})))=r-r_{1}-r_{2}=0.

Thus,
item

2
gives the result.

To prove
item

4
, observe that for any
0<r<d(y,z)
,
B(y,r)
and
B(x,d(x,y))
are disjoint subsets of
B(x,d(x,y)+r)
, and so

\displaystyle B(x,d(x,y)+d(y,z))

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{0<r<d(y,z)}C(x,d(x,y)+r)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{0<r<d(y,z)}C(x,d(x,y))\cup C(y,r)

\displaystyle=C(x,d(x,y))\cup B(y,d(y,z))

using
item

3
for the second equality.
Therefore, since
d(x,z)>d(x,y)
,
z\not\in B(x,d(x,y)+d(y,z))

and so
d(x,z)\geq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
.
Therefore, by the triangle inequality,
d(x,z)=d(x,y)+d(y,z)
.

Finally, suppose for a contradiction to
item

5
, that there exist two points
y_{1}\neq y_{2}
and
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
such that

B(x,r)\cap B(y_{i},\delta_{i})=\emptyset

for
i=1,2
and set
\delta=\min(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},d(y_{1},y_{2})/2)
.
Then
B(y_{1},\delta),B(y_{2},\delta)
and
B(x,r)
are disjoint subsets of
B(x,r+\delta)
and so

2\delta+r=\mu(B(y_{1},\delta)\cup B(y_{2},\delta)\cup B(x,r))\leq\mu(B(x,r+\delta))=r+\delta,

a contradiction.
∎

First we establish the existence of Besicovitch pairs in disconnected balls.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a proper metric space that satisfies
Lemma

4.2

item

3
and suppose that, for some
x\in X
and
r>0
,
C(x,r)
is disconnected.
Then there exists a Besicovitch pair
a,b
with
a\in C(x,r)
and
d(a,b)\leq r
.

Proof.

Let
C(x,r)=A\cup B
be a decomposition into disjoint closed sets with
x\in A
and
B\neq\emptyset
.
Since
B
is a closed subset of
C(x,r)
(which is compact since
X
is proper), there exists a closest point
y
of
B
to
x
.
In particular,
B(x,d(x,y))\subset A
and, since
A
is closed,
d(y,A)>0
.
Therefore,

B(y,d(y,A)/2)\cap B(x,d(x,y))\subset B(y,d(y,A)/2)\cap A=\emptyset.

By
Lemma

4.2

item

3
,

C(x,d(x,y)+d(y,A)/2)=C(x,d(x,y))\cup C(y,d(y,A)/2)\\
=(C(x,d(x,y))\cap A)\cup(C(x,d(x,y))\cap B)\cup C(y,d(y,A)/2)

and so

is open since it contains a ball of radius

\min(d(y,A)/2,d(A,B))>0

around each of its points.
Since
A
is a closed subset of
C(x,r)
,
Y
is also compact.

To conclude, since
Y
is compact with closed complement

, we may find
a\in Y
and
b\in Y^{c}
with

.
This implies that
a,b
is a Besicovitch pair.
Indeed, if
x\in B(a,d(a,b))
and

then necessarily we have

and hence

.
By construction we have

, as required.
∎

allows us to define the following mapping between the components of a Besicovitch pair.

.

be a uniform metric measure space and

, by the definition of a Besicovitch pair.

.

.

To prove

.

.

Theorem 4.8

5.
Classifying 1-uniform metric measure spaces

The relationship between connected compact sets of finite Hausdorff measure and Lipschitz curves is well known.
We will use the following, see, for example,
[
1
, Theorems 4.4.20, 4.2.1, 4.4.2]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a compact connected metric space with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)<\infty
.
Then for any
x,y\in X
there exists an injective Lipschitz curve
\gamma_{xy}\colon[0,L]\to S
with
\gamma_{xy}(0)=x
,
\gamma_{xy}(L)=y
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{xy})
minimal amongst all curves joining
x
to
y
.

Moreover, we may suppose that
\gamma_{xy}
is
parametrised by arc-length
.
That is,
\gamma_{xy}
is 1-Lipschitz and, for any
s\leq t\in[0,L]
,

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{xy}([s,t]))=t-s.

We will be interested in
parametrisations
of Lipschitz curves.
For convenience, we will refer to both the function
\gamma\colon I\to X
or its image
\gamma(I)
by simply
\gamma
.

The previous theorem is often used to show that a compact connected metric space of finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
measure is the image of a Lipschitz curve.
We prove a variant for uniform metric measure spaces.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(K,d)
be a (non-empty) compact connected metric space with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(K)<\infty
.
Suppose that, for any injective
\gamma\colon I\to K
that is parametrised by arc-length, and any
[c-r,c+r]\subset I
,

C(\gamma(c),r)=\gamma([c-r,c+r]).

Then
K
is the image of a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\colon I\to K
.
On any proper subinterval,
\gamma
is injective and parametrised by arc-length.

Proof.

If
K
consists of a single point, we are done.
Otherwise, let
x,y
be distinct points in
K
.
By
Theorem

5.1
there exists an injective Lipschitz
\gamma_{1}\colon I_{1}\ \to K
of positive length joining
x
to
y
that is parametrised by arc-length.

We now proceed to inductively construct an increasing sequence of compact intervals
I_{n}
and sequence of Lipschitz curves

\gamma_{n}\colon I_{n}\to K

each of which are parametrised by arc-length, are equal on their common domain, and satisfy

(5.1)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{n})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\max\{d(z,\gamma_{i}):z\in K\}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
suppose that we have defined such
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
.
If
\gamma_{n}
is the whole of
K
then we stop and define all subsequent
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{n}
.
Note that
eq.

5.1
is satisfied.
Otherwise, let
z_{n}\in K\setminus\gamma_{n}
satisfy

(5.2)

d(z_{n},\gamma_{n})=\max\{d(z,\gamma_{n}):z\in K\}>0,

which exists by the compactness of
K
and
\gamma_{n}
.
Also, for
e,e^{\prime}
the end points of
\gamma_{n}
, choose
y\in\{e,e^{\prime}\}
such that

(5.3)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{yz})\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{ez}),\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{e^{\prime}z}).

Observe that
\gamma_{n}\cap\gamma_{yz}=\{y\}
.
Indeed, by applying
Lemma

5.3
with
c=(\gamma_{n}^{-1}(e)+\gamma_{n}^{-1}(e^{\prime}))/2
and
r=|\gamma_{n}^{-1}(e)-\gamma_{n}^{-1}(e^{\prime})|/2
,
B(\gamma_{n}(c),r)=\gamma_{n}
.
Therefore, since
\gamma_{n}
is injective, if
\gamma_{n}\cap\gamma_{yz}
contains a point other than
y
, it must also contain the other end point,
y^{\prime}
, of
\gamma_{n}
, and then continue to join
y^{\prime}
to
z
.
This would mean that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{yz})>\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{y^{\prime}z})
, which contradicts
eq.

5.3
.

Construct an injective 1-Lipschitz curve
\gamma_{n+1}
by concatenating
\gamma_{n}
and
\gamma_{yz}
in the compatible way.
Note that, since
\gamma_{n}\cap\gamma_{yz}
is a single point, and hence a set of measure zero,
\gamma_{n+1}
is parametrised by arc-length.
Since both
\gamma_{n}
and
\gamma_{yz}
are subsets of
K
, so is
\gamma_{n+1}
.
Also, since
\gamma_{yz}
is parametrised by arc-length,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{yz})\geq d(y,z)=d(z_{n},\gamma_{n})
.
By combining
eq.

5.2
and the induction hypothesis
eq.

5.1
on
\gamma_{n}
, we see that
eq.

5.1
is true for
\gamma_{n+1}
.
This completes the inductive step for
\gamma_{n+1}
.

Observe that, since each
\gamma_{n}
is parametrised by arc-length,

(5.4)

\operatorname{length}(I_{n})=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{n})\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(C(x,r)).

Since the
I_{n}
are increasing,
I=\cup_{n}I_{n}
is an interval of finite length and by
Lemma

2.7
we obtain an injective
\gamma\colon I\to K
defined by
\gamma(t)=\gamma_{n}(t)
whenever
t\in I_{n}
.
It is parametrised by arc-length.
By
eq.

5.4
, the closure of
\gamma
is the whole of
K
.
However, the closure of
\gamma
is simply the unique extension of
\gamma
to the closure
\bar{I}
of
I
.
By translating
I
, we may suppose
\bar{I}=[-l,l]
for some
l>0
.
Since
[-l,l]\setminus I
is at most
\pm l
, this curve is parametrised by arc-length and injective on
I
.

Now suppose
c\in(-l,l)
, so that there exists an
r>0
such that
[c-r,c+r]\subset(-l,l)
.
For any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
,
\gamma
restricted to
[-l+\epsilon,c+r]
is injective and parametrised by arc-length.
Therefore, by hypothesis,
\gamma(-l+\epsilon)\not\in B(\gamma(c),r)
.
Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary,
\gamma(l)\not\in B(\gamma(c),r)
and hence
\gamma(l)\neq\gamma(c)
.
Similarly,
l
cannot be mapped to the same point as
c
, and so
\gamma
is injective on any subinterval.
∎

The uniform condition imposes strong geometric constraints on a Lipschitz curve.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a uniform metric measure space.

(1)

For any injective
\gamma\colon I\to X
that is parametrised by arc-length and any
[c-r,c+r]\subset I
,

C(\gamma(c),r)=\gamma([c-r,c+r])

and
d(\gamma(c),\gamma(c\pm r))=r
.

(2)

For any surjective
\gamma\colon I\to C(x,r)
that is parametrised by arc-length, after possibly translating
I
,
I=[-r,r]
and either
\gamma(r)=\gamma(-r)
or
\gamma(0)=x
.

Proof.

By multiplying
\mu
by a constant, we may suppose that
\mu(B(x,r))=2r
for each
x\in X
and
r>0
.
Observe that
\gamma(I)
is contained within an open ball
B
, that
\mu|_{B}(B(x,r))=2r
for all
x\in B
and all sufficiently small
r>0
and that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(B)<\infty
.
Therefore, by
Corollary

2.5
,
\mu|_{\gamma(I)}=\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\gamma(I)}
.

Note that for any
[c-r,c+r]\subset I
,
\gamma|_{[c-r,c+r]}
is also parametrised by arc-length.
Therefore,

\mu(\gamma((c-r,c+r)))=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma((c-r,c+r))=2r.

Also, since
\gamma
is 1-Lipschitz,
\gamma((c-r,c+r))\subset B(\gamma(c),r)
.
Thus, by
Lemma

4.2

item

2
,
C(\gamma(c),r)=\gamma([c-r,c+r])
.

Note that, for any
[c-r,c+r]\subset[-l,l]
, since
\gamma
is 1-Lipschitz,
d(\gamma(c),\gamma(c\pm r))\leq r
.
Also, for any
\epsilon>0
, since
\gamma
is injective on
[c-(r-\epsilon),c+(r-\epsilon)]
,

\gamma(c\pm r)\not\in\gamma([c-(r-\epsilon),c+(r-\epsilon)])=C(\gamma(s),r-\epsilon),

so that
d(\gamma(c),\gamma(c\pm r))\geq r-\epsilon
.
Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary,
d(\gamma(c),\gamma(c\pm r))=r
, as required.

Further, if
\gamma\colon I\to C(x,r)
is surjective and parametrised by arc-length, then

2r=\mu(\gamma(I))=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma(I))=\operatorname{length}(I)

and so by translating, we may suppose that
I=[-r,r]
.
If
\gamma(r)\neq\gamma(-r)
then
\gamma
is injective and so we must have
d(x,\gamma(\pm r))=r
.

Indeed, if
d(x,\gamma(r))<r
then there exists an
\epsilon>0
such that
B(\gamma(r),\epsilon)\subset B(x,r)
and such that
C(\gamma(\pm r),\epsilon)
are disjoint.
By the first part of the lemma,
d(\gamma(r),\gamma(0))=r
and
\gamma([r-\epsilon,r+\epsilon])=C(\gamma(0),r-\epsilon)
, and so by the triangle inequality,
\gamma([r-\epsilon,r+\epsilon])
and
B(\gamma(r),\epsilon)
are disjoint.
Therefore,
B(\gamma(r),\epsilon)=\gamma((r-\epsilon,r])
.
By hypothesis,
\mu(B(\gamma(r),\epsilon))=2r
but, since
\gamma
is parametrised by arc-length,
\mu(\gamma((r-\epsilon,r]))=r
, a contradiction.
Thus
d(\gamma(\pm),x)=r
.
Since
\gamma
is 1-Lipschitz, the only possibility is
x=\gamma(0)
.
∎

We begin our classification of uniform metric measure spaces.

Theorem 5.4
.

A connected uniform metric measure space is isometric to
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Fix a point
x_{0}\in X
.
First note that, since
X
is connected, for any
r>0
,
C(x_{0},r)
is also connected.
Indeed, if
C(x_{0},r)
is disconnected, then by
Proposition

4.3
, there exists a Besicovitch pair
a,b\in C(x_{0},r)
.
By
Lemma

4.5

item

2
,
C(a,d(a,b))
is a non-trivial open and closed subset of
X
, a contradiction.

Now, for any
r>0
,
Lemma

5.2
and
Lemma

5.3

item

2
give a surjective Lipschitz
\gamma_{r}\colon[-r,r]\to C(x_{0},r)
that is parametrised by arc-length and injective except possibly at the end points.
Note, this means that
\gamma_{r/2}
cannot map
{-r/2,r/2}
to the same point, since
\gamma_{r}
does not contain a closed loop of measure
r
.
Therefore,
\gamma_{r/2}(0)=x
by
Lemma

5.3

item

2
.
Also, by
Lemma

5.3

item

1
,

\gamma^{r/4}:=\gamma_{r/2}|_{[-r/4,r/4]}\colon[-r/4,r/4]\to C(x_{0},r/4)

Note that, for any

,

is an isometry and so equals

Therefore, by pre-composing by multiplication by

if necessary, the

agree on their common domain.
Thus, by

\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[-n,n]\to\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C(x_{0},n)=X,

∎

A similar type of argument handles the case that a component of a Besicovitch pair is connected.

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that
a,b
is a Besicovitch pair in a uniform metric measure space and that

Then
C(a,d(a,b))
is isometric to

.

Let
d=d(a,b)
.
By
Lemma

5.2
,
C(a,d)
is the image of a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\colon[-d,d]\to C(a,d)
that is parametrised by arc-length and injective except possibly at the end points.
First observe that necessarily,
\gamma(-d)=\gamma(d)
.
Indeed, if
\gamma(-d)\neq\gamma(d)
then by
Lemma

5.3

item

1
,
\gamma(0)=a
.
However, by
Lemma

4.5

item

2
, for any

.
By applying

item

\gamma(-d)=\gamma(d)

Thus, we may periodically extend

to a 1-Lipschitz function defined on

with period

.
Moreover, for any

,

The final lemma we require to complete the classification is to show that the existence of a single Besicovitch pair implies that all points form a Besicovitch pair.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a uniform metric measure space and

a Besicovitch pair and

.
Then there exists a

Identify

with its isometric image in

given by

4.8
.
With this identification,

is contained in an isometric copy

of

, with a corresponding isometric copy

of

,

The statement about the sequences

We now proceed to prove the classification of uniform metric measure spaces.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a uniform metric measure space.

Fix

.
We first show that there exists a Besicovitch pair

For any integer

5.6

.

Theorem 5.8

(X,d,\mu)

Suppose that

is isometric to a scaled copy of
\mathcal{S}
.

By

, there exist

and

is a Besicovitch pair.
Let

7.
Some solutions to the 1-dimensional Besicovitch problem

We now prove
Theorem

1.4
by showing that
\mathcal{S}
does not isometrically embed into any
X
in the statement.
In fact we will show that, for sufficiently large
n
, the following metric space does not isometrically embed.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let

\mathcal{S}_{n}:=\{s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}\}

with the metric
\rho(0,i)=2^{i}
and
\rho(i,j)=\rho(i,0)+\rho(0,j)
for each
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
.

Once we have shown this, a compactness argument then implies that
\mathcal{S}_{n}
does not
(1+\epsilon)
-bi-Lipschitz embed, for some
\epsilon>0
(alternatively, this follows by adding quantitative details to each argument below).
Consequently, the set
S
in
Theorem

1.3
must have measure zero, completing the proof.

7.1.
Doubling geodesic metric spaces

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, a metric space
(X,d)
is
N
-doubling
if any ball is covered by
N
balls of half the radius.
A metric space is
geodesic
if for any two points
x,y\in X
there exists an arc-length parametrised
\gamma\colon[0,d(x,y)]\to X
such that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)=d(x,y)
.
For such a
\gamma
,
|s-t|=d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))
for each
s,t\in[0,d(x,y)]
.

Suppose that
X
is geodesic and
N
-doubling and that, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
isometrically embeds into
X
.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
let
\gamma_{i}
be a geodesic joining
s_{0}
to
s_{i}
.
Then for any
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
and
0\leq t_{i}\leq d(s_{0},s_{i})
,

d(\gamma_{i}(t_{i}),\gamma_{j}(t_{j}))=2^{t_{i}}+2^{t_{j}}.

In particular,
\gamma_{i}(2)\in B(s_{0},2)
and

d(\gamma_{i}(2),\gamma_{j}(2))=4

for each
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
.
Therefore,
B(s_{0},2)
is not contained within
n
balls of radius
1
and hence
n\leq N
.

7.2.
Uniformly convex Banach spaces

A Banach space
(X,\|\cdot\|)
is
strictly convex
if, for every
x,y\in X
,
\|x+y\|=\|x\|+\|y\|
implies that
x
and
y
are co-linear.
Observe that
S_{3}
does not isometrically embed into a strictly convex Banach space.
Indeed, after translating so that
s_{0}=0
, such an embedding would require all
s_{i}
to be co-linear, which violates the definition of
\rho
.

Further,
X
is
uniformly convex
if for every
0<\epsilon\leq 2
there exists
\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that
\|x-y\|\geq\epsilon
implies
\|x+y\|\leq 2(1-\delta)
.
Uniform convexity is a quantitative notion of strict convexity.
Given a
modulus of convexity

\delta(\epsilon)
, the fact that
S_{4}
does not isometrically embed into any (4-dimensional) strictly convex Banach space, there exists some
\eta>0
such that
S_{4}
does not
(1+\eta)
-bi-Lipschitz embed into a uniformly convex Banach space with modulus of convexity
\delta(\epsilon)
.

7.3.
Doubling metric spaces with dilations

A metric space
(X,d)
is equipped with
dilations
if, for every
c\in X
and
\lambda>0
, there exists a map
\delta_{\lambda}\colon X\to X
with
f(c)=c
such that

d(\delta_{\lambda}(x),\delta_{\lambda}(y))=\lambda d(x,y)

for all
x,y\in X

and
\delta_{\lambda^{-}1}=\delta_{\lambda}^{-1}
.
Suppose also that there exist
0<\eta<1
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any
x\in X
and
m,n\geq M
,

(7.1)

d(\delta_{2^{m}}x,\delta_{2^{n}}x)\leq\eta(2^{m}+2^{n})d(c,x).

Now suppose that
X
is
N
-doubling, let
l\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
0<2^{-l}<1-\eta
and set
n=M+N^{l+1}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{S}_{n}
isometrically embeds into
X
and let
\delta_{\lambda}
be the dilations centred on
s_{0}
.
Cover
B(s_{0},1)
by balls
B_{1},\ldots,B_{N^{l+1}}
of diameter at most
2^{-l}
.
Then there exists some
B_{i}
that contains two points
\delta_{2^{-m}}(s_{m})
and
\delta_{2^{-m}}(s_{m})
with
m,n\geq M
.
By the triangle inequality and the properties of dilations, in particular (
7.1
),

\displaystyle d(s_{m},s_{n})

\displaystyle=d(\delta_{2^{m}}(\delta_{2^{-m}}(s_{m})),\delta_{2^{n}}(\delta_{2^{-n}}(s_{n})))

\displaystyle\leq d(\delta_{2^{m}}(\delta_{2^{-m}}(s_{m})),\delta_{2^{m}}(\delta_{2^{-n}}(s_{n})))

\displaystyle\quad+d(\delta_{2^{m}}(\delta_{2^{-n}}(s_{n})),\delta_{2^{n}}(\delta_{2^{-n}}(s_{n})))

\displaystyle\leq 2^{m}2^{-l}+\eta(2^{m}+2^{n})\leq(\eta+2^{-l})(2^{m}+2^{n}).

Since
\eta+2^{-l}<1
, this is impossible.

An example of a space with dilations satisfying (
7.1
) is the
Heisenberg group

\mathbb{H}

[
6
, Definition 14.2.8]
.
This is the set
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the group law

(x,y,t)\star(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+2(xy^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y)).

Note that 0 is the identity and
(x,y,t)^{-1}=(-x,-y,-t)
.
For
\lambda>0
, define

\delta_{\lambda}(x,y,t)=(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda^{2}z).

A
homogeneous norm
on
\mathbb{H}
is a function
\|\cdot\|\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\|a^{-1}\|=\|a\|
,

\|a^{-1}\star b\|\leq\|a\|+\|b\|

and

\|\delta_{\lambda}(a)^{-1}\star\delta_{\lambda}(b)\|=\lambda\|a\star b^{-1}\|

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{H}
and
\lambda>0
.
That is,
d(a,b)=\|a^{-1}\star b\|
defines a metric with respect to which each
\delta_{\lambda}
is a dilation centred on 0.
Since this metric is invariant under the group action,
\mathbb{H}
has dilations centred on all points.
To see (
7.1
), let
(x,y,t)\in\mathbb{H}
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and set
\lambda=2^{m}
and
\mu=2^{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle\|\delta_{\lambda}(a)^{-1}\delta_{\mu}(a)\|

\displaystyle=\|(\mu-\lambda)x,(\mu-\lambda)y,(\mu^{2}-\lambda^{2})t\|

(7.2)

\displaystyle=|\mu-\lambda|\|x,y,\tfrac{\mu+\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}t\|.

The topology with respect to
\|\cdot\|
agrees with the Euclidean topology.
Since
|\tfrac{\mu+\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}|\leq 4
, there exists
C>0
such that the final expression in (
7.2
) is at most
C|\mu-\lambda|
for all
(x,y,t)\in B(0,1)
.
