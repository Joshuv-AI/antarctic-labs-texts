Rapid photoacoustic trace gas sensor for online end-tidal CO monitoring in exhaled breath
Authors: Xiao Geng, Tianbai Zhao, Qiwen Yu, Xiu Yang, Lei Liu, Yize Liang, Xukun Yin, Huailiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnline detection of exhaled breath biomarkers has attracted increasing attention because of its noninvasive, real-time, and point-of-care diagnostic potential. End-tidal carbon monoxide (ETCO) is an important breath biomarker associated with smoking behavior, neonatal hemolysis, and cardiovascular diseases. Here, we report a differential photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) sensor for rapid and highly sensitive online ETCO monitoring. A compact 3D-printed dual-channel differential resonant photoacoustic cell (DPAC), with an effective sample volume of only 2.5 mL, was developed to enhance acoustic signal collection while suppressing common-mode noise. The optimized DPAC exhibited an acoustic resonance frequency of 3726.94 Hz, corresponding to a laser current modulation frequency of 1863.47 Hz for second-harmonic detection, with a quality factor of 26. A quantum cascade laser targeting the CO absorption line at 2176.1 cm−1 was used as the excitation source. The sensor exhibited excellent linearity over the concentration range of 0–1000 ppm, with an R2 value exceeding 0.9979, enabling both ambient-level and highly elevated CO measurements on a single platform. A detection limit of 30.6 ppb was achieved at a lock-in time constant of 1 s. Breath measurements from five smokers and five non-smokers showed clear discrimination between the two groups. The smokers exhibited an average ETCO concentration of 21.4 ppm, with all measured values higher than 13.2 ppm, whereas the non-smokers showed an average ETCO concentration of 3.3 ppm, with a maximum value below 3.8 ppm. The proposed PAS sensor combines ppb-level sensitivity and a wide dynamic range, providing a promising platform for real-time breath analysis and preliminary ETCO monitoring.Keywords: Photoacoustic spectroscopy, End-tidal carbon monoxide, Exhaled breath analysis, 3D printing photoacoustic cell, Quantum cascade laser

Introduction

Online exhaled breath analysis has gained increasing attention in medical diagnostics, personalized medicine, environmental exposure assessment, and health monitoring because of its noninvasive nature, simple sampling procedure, and capability for real-time analysis [1], [2], [3]. Variations in the chemical composition of exhaled breath can reflect metabolic activity, pulmonary gas exchange, and physiological or pathological states. Because exhaled breath contains both endogenous and exogenous compounds, the analysis of breath biomarkers provides valuable information on individual metabolic processes, environmental exposure, and intake pathways [4], [5], [6].

Carbon monoxide (CO) in exhaled breath is an important biomarker with both endogenous and exogenous origins. Endogenous CO is mainly produced through heme degradation catalyzed by heme oxygenase, and exhaled CO has been evaluated as a noninvasive indicator related to endogenous heme metabolism and oxidative or inflammatory processes [7]. Exogenous CO mainly originates from environmental exposure and tobacco smoke. Therefore, breath CO measurement has been widely used as an objective indicator of recent smoking exposure and smoking-status assessment [8]. In addition, a recent clinical study involving 717 patients with acute myocardial infarction reported that elevated expiratory CO levels were associated with increased one-year all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events [9], [10]. These findings suggest that exhaled CO may provide complementary information for smoking-status assessment and cardiovascular risk evaluation, although it should be interpreted together with established clinical factors. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is widely used in breath analysis because of its high selectivity and strong compound identification capability. Real-time mass spectrometry techniques, such as selected ion flow tube mass spectrometry (SIFT-MS) and proton transfer reaction mass spectrometry (PTR-MS), have enabled online breath monitoring with high temporal resolution [11]. However, these methods often require complex instrumentation and careful sample handling. Sample degradation, adsorption loss, humidity effects, and delayed response may limit their applicability for real-time breath monitoring. Moreover, exhaled breath is highly humid and close to body temperature, which further complicates mass spectrometric analysis. Isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) offers high precision and stability but suffers from high cost, bulky instrumentation, and complex sample preparation [12].

Optical gas sensing techniques have attracted considerable interest for breath analysis because of their high sensitivity, high selectivity, and potential for compact and real-time detection. Non-dispersive infrared spectroscopy (NDIR) is inexpensive and easy to operate, making it suitable for routine diagnostics. However, its limited spectral resolution and susceptibility to environmental interference can reduce measurement accuracy [13], [14]. Tunable diode laser absorption spectroscopy (TDLAS) provides high selectivity by targeting specific molecular absorption lines. To enhance sensitivity, TDLAS systems often employ multipass absorption cells, such as White or Herriott cells, with optical path lengths ranging from several centimeters to tens of meters according to the Beer-Lambert law [15], [16], [17], [18]. Nevertheless, large-volume multipass cells may increase the gas exchange time, slow the sensor response, and make the system more sensitive to environmental disturbances.

Photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) has emerged as a powerful technique for trace gas detection because of its high sensitivity, wide dynamic range, compact sampling volume, and immunity to optical background interference. These advantages make PAS particularly attractive for online breath analysis, where rapid response and high sensitivity are simultaneously required. Recent advances in PAS have further improved its performance through dual-mode resonant detection, frequency-division multiplexing, frequency tracking combined with WMS−2f detection, and optimized photoacoustic cell structures, including spherical resonators, differential Helmholtz cells, T-type resonant cells, and topology-optimized resonators [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. PAS has been widely used in gas sensing because it converts molecular optical absorption into an acoustic signal through non-radiative relaxation, enabling sensitive detection with compact sampling volumes and low optical-background interference. Its applicability has been demonstrated in a wide range of scenarios. In breath analysis, Mao et al. developed a PAS-based system for simultaneous detection of H2, CH4, and CO2 for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth assessment [25]. Popa et al. applied CO2-laser PAS to ammonia breath testing for Helicobacter pylori and observed increased breath ammonia levels in infected subjects [26]. For environmental monitoring, Wang et al. reported a miniature mid-infrared PAS sensor for detecting dissolved CO2 in seawater, demonstrating the potential of PAS for marine carbon-cycle monitoring [27]. Cavity-enhanced MEMS-based PAS has also been used for ppt-level N2O detection, highlighting the potential of PAS for greenhouse-gas monitoring [28]. In addition, quartz-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy (QEPAS) has been applied to hazardous gas leak detection, such as H2S detection in methane-based gas leaks, showing its suitability for industrial safety and workplace monitoring [29]. These studies demonstrate that PAS is a versatile platform for biomedical, environmental, industrial, and safety-related gas sensing applications.

For CO detection, PAS systems commonly operate in three spectral regions: the second overtone band near 1.6 μm, the first overtone band near 2.3 μm, and the fundamental absorption band near 4.6 μm in the mid-infrared region [30]. In the 1.6 μm region, the CO absorption line strength is weak, and achieving sub-ppm detection is challenging even when an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA) is used to boost the optical power to the watt level [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. The absorption line strength near 2.3 μm is approximately two orders of magnitude stronger than that near 1.6 μm, allowing ppm-level CO detection without an EDFA [36], [37], [38], [39]. However, the weak overtone transitions in the near-infrared region remain insufficient for reliable breath CO detection in healthy non-smokers, whose exhaled CO concentration is typically below 6 ppm.

In contrast, the fundamental absorption band of CO in the mid-infrared region provides much stronger absorption and is therefore highly favorable for sensitive breath CO detection. At room temperature, the fundamental rovibrational band of CO is centered near 2143 cm−1, with the R-branch transitions located at higher wavenumbers and the P-branch transitions at lower wavenumbers. The most prominent room-temperature absorption lines are mainly distributed within approximately 2100–2180 cm−1[40], [41], [42]. In 2020, Maurin et al. reported a QEPAS sensor for CO detection with a detection limit of 20 ppbv[43]. Sun et al. developed a QEPAS sensor based on a custom T-shaped tuning fork and a 4.61 μm quantum cascade laser (QCL), achieving a detection limit of 10 ppb for CO in sulfur hexafluoride with an averaging time of 10 s [19]. However, the response time was approximately 3 min, which is too slow to accurately capture the peak CO concentration at the end of exhalation. During a complete exhalation process, the CO concentration rapidly rises from the ambient background level, reaches a peak in the end-tidal alveolar gas, and then decreases rapidly. This peak concentration is closely related to the blood carboxyhemoglobin level and is a key indicator for clinical assessment. Therefore, an insufficient response speed may lead to inaccurate ETCO measurements and even false-negative results, limiting the practical use of the sensor in clinical screening, smoking cessation guidance, and health monitoring. In this work, we report a compact differential PAS sensor for online end-tidal CO monitoring in exhaled breath. The system was designed to meet the key requirements of breath CO analysis, including sufficient sensitivity for low-ppm ETCO detection, small sampling volume, reduced acoustic noise, and rapid gas exchange for tracking the late-exhalation plateau. A 2.5 mL dual-channel differential photoacoustic cell was developed and combined with a 4.6 μm QCL targeting the CO absorption line at 2176.1 cm−1. Under optimized conditions, the sensor achieved a 1σ detection limit of 30.6 ppb, a wide linear response from 0 to 1000 ppm, and measured T90 response and recovery times of 1.2 s and 1.3 s, respectively. These results demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed sensor for real-time breath CO analysis and preliminary ETCO assessment.

Absorption line and laser selection

To better evaluate potential spectral interference from major breath constituents, the absorbance spectra of 10 ppm CO, 5% CO2, 1% H2O, and 1 ppm N2O were simulated using the HITRAN database over the wavenumber range of 2170–2180 cm−1. The optical path length and pressure were set to 1 cm and 1 atm, respectively. A CO2 concentration of 5% was selected to represent the typical CO2 level in exhaled breath, while 1 ppm N2O was used to evaluate possible interference from trace N2O under breath-relevant conditions. As shown in Fig. 1, CO exhibits distinct absorption lines in this spectral region, whereas CO2 shows no obvious absorption feature around the selected CO line at 2176.1 cm−1. Although H2O and N2O exhibit absorption features in this region, their simulated absorbance near 2176.1 cm−1 is much weaker than the CO absorption signal used for breath CO monitoring. Therefore, the CO line at 2176.1 cm−1 was selected for subsequent measurements because it provides strong CO absorption and low spectral interference from the major breath matrix components.

Simulated absorbance spectra of 10 ppm CO, 1% H2O, 5% CO2 and 1 ppm N2O in the range of 2170–2180 cm−1. The optical path length and pressure were set to 1 cm and 1 atm, respectively.

A QCL was selected as the excitation source to target this CO absorption line. The optical power of the QCL was measured using a power meter (3 A, Ophir). The maximum output power of the QCL was 26 mW. The current and temperature tuning coefficients of the laser were experimentally determined to be −0.049 cm−1/mA and −0.403 cm−1/°C, respectively. For the subsequent measurements, the laser temperature and injection current were set to 29.8 °C and 90 mA, respectively.

Experimental setup

A schematic of the photoacoustic CO sensing system is shown in Fig. 2. Synthetic zero air, consisting of approximately 79% N2 and 21% O2 with negligible target-gas impurities, was used as the background gas. A certified gas mixture containing 10,000 ppm CO in air was diluted with zero air to generate CO samples at different concentrations. The gas preparation unit consisted of three flow meters (IX500, Shaanxi Yidu). Two flow meters independently regulated the flow rates of the zero air and the CO standard gas. The two gas streams were combined through a three-way connector and subsequently passed through a third flow meter, which monitored the total flow rate of the resulting gas mixture. By adjusting the dilution ratio, CO mixtures with controlled and stable concentrations were generated to simulate exhaled breath containing different CO levels. To reproduce the high-humidity condition of human exhaled breath, a tube humidifier (PDMSXA−1000, Perm Select) was placed upstream of the photoacoustic cell inlet to maintain the gas humidity at a saturation level. The prepared CO gas mixture was passed through the humidifier before entering the DPAC. The gas flowing out of the humidifier was considered to be close to saturated humidity under the laboratory conditions. This humidified gas matrix was used in the subsequent calibration and breath-simulation measurements, so that the operating condition of the sensor was closer to that of real exhaled breath. This configuration ensured stable gas delivery and improved the accuracy and repeatability of the measurements.

The pressure (626D Baratron, MKS) and flow rate of the sensing system were maintained at 93 kPa and 220 sccm, respectively, using pressure-reducing valves (PRV), a pump (N813.5, KNF), and a needle valve. The effective sample volume of the DPAC was 2.5 mL. At the operating flow rate of 220 sccm, the theoretical one-volume gas residence time of the cell was calculated to be approximately 0.68 s. This value only represents the estimated gas residence time inside the DPAC. The complete system response also depends on the gas transport through the tubing, valves, and breath-sampling interface, as well as the dynamic filtering of the lock-in amplifier. After the sensor performance had been evaluated using laboratory-prepared CO mixtures, the gas preparation unit was replaced with a breath-sampling interface. Exhaled breath was then introduced directly into the photoacoustic cell through the sampling interface, enabling real-time monitoring of the CO concentration during exhalation.

To reduce the fabrication cost, a differential photoacoustic cell (DPAC) was designed and manufactured from acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) using 3D printing. The DPAC consists of two geometrically identical acoustic channels. The cell has an effective sample volume of 2.5 mL and a total mass of 32.4 g. The compact gas-chamber design shortens the gas exchange time and thereby improves the temporal response of the sensor. The internal structure comprises two differential acoustic channels, two buffer chambers, one gas inlet, and one gas outlet. Each acoustic channel contains a cylindrical cavity with a length of 40 mm and an inner radius of 3 mm. Acoustic finite-element simulation was performed and the DPAC used in this work follows a differential photoacoustic cell configuration similar to our previously reported compact differential photoacoustic exhaled gas sensor [5], but its dimensions were optimized for the present CO sensing system. To clarify the hybrid structure of the DPAC, a simplified internal schematic was added as an inset in Fig. 2, showing the 3D-printed ABS body, inserted stainless-steel resonator tubes, buffer chambers, gas inlet/outlet, microphones, and differential preamplifier. The simulated acoustic pressure distribution indicated that the maximum acoustic pressure was located near the central region of each acoustic channel. ABS was used mainly for rapid prototyping of the compact DPAC. To reduce possible adsorption, outgassing, and humidity-related effects from the printed polymer surface, stainless-steel tubes were inserted into the two acoustic channels and served as the main acoustic resonators. Before measurements, the gas path was purged with humidified background gas until a stable baseline was obtained. The main body of the DPAC was fabricated from ABS using 3D printing to enable low-cost and rapid prototype integration. It should be noted that the 3D-printed ABS structure was mainly used as the integrated support body, including the gas inlet/outlet, buffer chambers, channel housing, and microphone mounting positions. Stainless-steel tubes were inserted into the two acoustic channels and served as the main resonator sections. This hybrid design combines the low-cost and flexible integration capability of 3D printing with the smoother inner surfaces, better dimensional accuracy, and improved channel-to-channel consistency of stainless-steel tubes. A cylindrical buffer chamber, with both a length and radius of 10 mm, was located at each end of the resonator. Standard-precision UV-grade fused-silica windows (OW22-UV, LBTEK) were mounted on the outer sides of the buffer chambers, opposite the acoustic channels, and sealed using cover plates. A high-sensitivity electret condenser microphone (EM258, Primo) was installed in each channel to detect the photoacoustic signals generated within the corresponding resonator. Each microphone had dimensions of 6.0 mm × 2.7 mm and a nominal sensitivity of −32 ± 2 dB. The two microphones exhibited closely matched frequency responses near the acoustic resonance frequency of approximately 3.73 kHz, which is essential for effective common-mode noise suppression in differential detection. It should be noted that the use of stainless-steel tubes in the present prototype does not exclude the possibility of fully 3D-printed acoustic resonators. Recent studies have shown that 3D-printed or additively manufactured resonator structures can provide effective acoustic enhancement in PAS sensors when the fabrication accuracy and surface roughness are properly controlled. For example, 3D-printed dual-tube resonators, miniaturized Y-type resonant photoacoustic cells, and compact spiral-architected resonator structures have been demonstrated for sensitive trace-gas detection [44], [45], [46], [47]. Therefore, fully 3D-printed or surface-treated resonator structures will be considered in future designs to further reduce fabrication cost, simplify assembly, and improve device integration.

The acoustic signals from the two microphones were processed by a custom-made differential preamplifier circuit before being sent to the lock-in amplifier. The two microphone signals were first AC-coupled and amplified through two identical preamplification channels. Then, the two amplified signals were subtracted using a differential amplifier to suppress common-mode acoustic and environmental noise. The output of the custom-made circuit was therefore an amplified differential photoacoustic signal, which was subsequently fed into the lock-in amplifier (SR830, Stanford Research Systems) for phase-sensitive demodulation. In this configuration, the differential subtraction and preamplification were performed before lock-in detection, while the lock-in amplifier was used only for demodulation and filtering. Owing to the geometrically symmetric configuration of the photoacoustic cell, common-mode disturbances, including flow-induced noise and environmental acoustic noise, produced nearly identical amplitudes and phases in the two microphone channels in the absence of optical excitation. These common-mode noise components were therefore effectively suppressed by differential subtraction. The lock-in amplifier time constant was set to 1 s with a filter slope of 12 dB/oct, corresponding to an equivalent noise bandwidth of 0.25 Hz. The temperature of the distributed-feedback quantum cascade laser (DFB-QCL) was controlled using a benchtop temperature controller (TED200C, Thorlabs) and maintained at 29.8 °C during the experiments. To implement second-harmonic wavelength modulation and improve the detection sensitivity, the injection current of the DFB-QCL was controlled using a current driver (ILX3220, Newport).

A sawtooth waveform and a sinusoidal modulation waveform were generated using a function generator (33500B, Agilent) and combined using a custom-built summing circuit before being applied to the laser current driver. The sawtooth waveform provided slow wavelength scanning across the selected CO absorption line, whereas the sinusoidal waveform produced high-frequency wavelength modulation. The sawtooth period determined the duration of one complete spectral scan. The sinusoidal modulation frequency of the laser current was denoted as f1/2. In second-harmonic wavelength modulation detection, the photoacoustic signal is mainly generated at 2f1/2. Therefore, f1/2 was set to one-half of the acoustic resonance frequency of the photoacoustic cell, so that the second-harmonic photoacoustic signal at 2f1/2 coincided with the acoustic resonance frequency. The peak-to-peak amplitude of the sinusoidal waveform determined the laser wavelength-modulation depth. All calibration and breath measurements were performed close to atmospheric pressure. The sample pressure was monitored during the experiment to ensure stable operating conditions. Since online exhaled-breath monitoring is normally performed under near-atmospheric pressure, the present work focused on sensor performance under this condition.

Results and discussion

Photoacoustic resonance frequency

The resonance frequency of the photoacoustic cell can be approximately estimated using f=v/2L, where (v) is the speed of sound in the gas and (L) is the length of the acoustic resonator. At standard atmospheric pressure and a temperature of 23 °C, the speed of sound in N2 is ∼340 m/s. However, the speed of sound in exhaled breath varies with its composition, temperature, and humidity. Therefore, experimental frequency scanning and calibration are required to determine the actual resonance frequency of the photoacoustic cell under the operating conditions. By substituting the relevant gas properties and resonator dimensions into the theoretical model, the quality factor of the acoustic resonator in N2 was estimated to be 23. After the gas pressure and flow rate had been stabilized, the prepared gas mixtures were introduced into the photoacoustic cell. The emission wavenumber of the QCL was tuned to the selected CO absorption line at 2176.1 cm−1. To characterize the acoustic response of the photoacoustic cell, the sinusoidal modulation frequency was scanned around one-half of the expected acoustic resonance frequency with a step size of 0.1 Hz. Because the photoacoustic signal was detected at the second harmonic, the corresponding acoustic excitation frequency was twice the applied modulation frequency. The resulting photoacoustic signal amplitudes were recorded as a function of the acoustic excitation frequency to obtain the resonance response curve of the cell. The interference from major breath matrix components was experimentally evaluated after determining the resonance frequency. First, the influence of CO2 was tested by adding 5% CO2 to the CO gas mixture. Compared with the CO signal obtained without CO2, the signal obtained in the presence of 5% CO2 showed no obvious change. This result is consistent with the HITRAN-based spectral simulation in Fig. 1, confirming that CO2 does not introduce significant spectral interference near 2176.1 cm−1.

Water vapor may affect PAS-based CO detection through two mechanisms: direct spectral absorption and modification of the vibrational-to-translational relaxation process. To evaluate direct H2O spectral interference experimentally, saturated humidified gas without CO was introduced into the DPAC under the same laser, flow, pressure, and lock-in settings. The measured signal remained close to the baseline noise level, indicating that water vapor alone did not produce an obvious CO-equivalent photoacoustic signal at 2176.1 cm−1. In addition, humidity-dependent measurements were performed during system optimization. The CO photoacoustic signal under near-saturated humidity was ∼ three times higher than that under dry conditions, which can be attributed to more efficient vibrational-to-translational relaxation of CO in the presence of water vapor. Since human exhaled breath is highly humid and close to saturation, both calibration and breath measurements were performed under near-saturated humidity conditions. Therefore, the humidity effect was included as part of the operating gas matrix rather than treated as an uncontrolled external interference.

Assessment of sensing performance

To evaluate the sensitivity of the sensing system, CO mixtures in synthetic zero air with concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 ppm were sequentially introduced into the photoacoustic cell. After the photoacoustic signal had stabilized at each concentration, the signal amplitude was continuously recorded for 100 s, as shown in Fig. 4(a). To determine the detection limit, the baseline noise was evaluated under CO-free background gas using the same flow rate, pressure, laser operating parameters, and lock-in amplifier settings as those used for the calibration measurement. The baseline signal was continuously recorded for 100 s, and the standard deviation of the noise was calculated to be 1.12 μV. At a CO concentration of 500 ppm, the mean photoacoustic signal amplitude was ∼ 18,300 μV. Therefore, the signal-to-noise ratio was calculated to be 1.63 × 104. The 1σ detection limit was then determined to be 30.6 ppb. This confirms that the sensing performance was stable and predictable over the tested concentration range. The sensor was operated in a fixed-wavelength on-peak WMS−2f mode for the performance evaluation. To evaluate the linearity and dynamic range of the sensor, CO mixtures in synthetic zero air with concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 ppm were subsequently introduced into the photoacoustic cell. After the signal had reached a steady state at each concentration, the mean steady-state photoacoustic signal amplitude was recorded in Fig. 4(b) and used to construct the calibration curve. To more clearly illustrate the dynamic response of the proposed sensor, a T90 step-response test was performed, as shown in Fig. 5. The response T90 was defined as the time required for the normalized PA signal to increase from 10% to 90% of the final steady-state value after the concentration step was introduced, while the recovery T90 was defined as the time required for the signal to decrease from 90% to 10% after the concentration step was removed. The measured response and recovery T90 values were 1.2 s and 1.3 s, respectively.

T90 step-response test of the proposed PAS sensor.

The baseline stability was further evaluated by continuously recording the CO-free humidified background signal for ∼ 3700 s under the same operating conditions. As shown in Fig. 6, the raw baseline signal showed no obvious monotonic drift. Allan-Werle deviation analysis was then performed after converting the baseline fluctuation into equivalent CO concentration using the calibration coefficient. The Allan-Werle deviation is expressed in ppb, and the 1 s value was close to the 1σ LOD of 30.6 ppb. A slight short-term variation within the first tens of seconds was observed, which may be related to residual flow fluctuation and lock-in filtering. With increasing integration time, the Allan-Werle deviation generally decreased and reached a minimum value of approximately 1.8 ppb at an integration time of approximately 520 s. At longer integration times, the deviation no longer decreased consistently because slow instrumental and environmental fluctuations gradually became dominant. It should be noted that the optimal long-term averaged detection limit is mainly relevant to stationary gas measurements, whereas the 1 s detection limit is more representative of the dynamic response required for online end-tidal CO monitoring. However, this fluctuation was much smaller than the ppm-level CO concentrations measured in breath samples.

To better clarify the novelty and performance of the proposed sensor, a quantitative comparison with representative optical-based CO and breath CO sensors is provided in Table 1. BF-PAS is beat-frequency photoacoustic spectroscopy technology. PTS is photothermal spectroscopy gas sensing. TDLAS-based breath CO sensors can provide fast time resolution, while QEPAS and QEPAS/PTS sensors have demonstrated ppb-level detection limits. However, detection sensitivity alone does not fully determine the suitability of a sensor for online ETCO monitoring. For this application, sampling volume, gas-exchange capability, measurement mode, and the ability to continuously record the breath profile are also important. Compared with systems relying on larger absorption cells, sampling bags, or long averaging times, the present sensor uses a 2.5 mL differential photoacoustic cell and directly records the dynamic CO profile during exhalation. The achieved 1σ detection limit of 30.6 ppb is sufficiently lower than the ppm-level CO concentrations observed in both smokers and non-smokers, while the measured T90 response and recovery times of 1.2 s and 1.3 s are short enough for extracting the late-exhalation plateau under the adopted 15 s exhalation protocol. Thus, the strength of the proposed sensor lies in the combination of compact volume, differential noise suppression, adequate sensitivity, and online breath-profile monitoring capability.

End-tidal CO measurements in smokers and non-smokers

Reducing smoking prevalence remains an important public health objective, as reflected in the Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030), which aims to reduce the smoking prevalence among individuals aged 15 years and older to 20% by 2030. Exhaled CO is widely used as a noninvasive biomarker for objectively assessing recent tobacco smoke exposure and verifying self-reported smoking status in smoking cessation practice. The sensing method developed in this work enables rapid and quantitative measurement of end-tidal CO, providing a potential analytical approach for smoking-status assessment and longitudinal monitoring during smoking cessation.

During breath measurement, a simple sampling interface was used to introduce exhaled breath into the DPAC. The interface consisted of a disposable mouthpiece, a short sampling tube, and a gas outlet/bypass. The volunteer exhaled through the mouthpiece after a 3 s breath-hold. A downstream pump and needle valve continuously drew a small portion of the exhaled breath into the DPAC at a controlled flow rate of 220 sccm, while the excess breath was vented through the outlet of the sampling interface. Therefore, the exhaled breath was not forced directly through the photoacoustic cell by the subject. Instead, the sensor sampled the breath flow at a constant flow rate, which reduced pressure and flow disturbances in the DPAC and enabled real-time monitoring of the CO concentration during exhalation. A standardized breath-sampling protocol was adopted to improve measurement repeatability. Each volunteer was instructed to hold their breath for 3 s, exhale continuously for 15 s, and rest for 10 s before the subsequent measurement. The 3 s breath-hold was selected to improve the repeatability of late-exhaled gas sampling while avoiding discomfort caused by a longer breath-hold. The 15 s exhalation period was selected based on preliminary measurements, which showed that the CO signal generally increased after the onset of exhalation and reached a relatively stable plateau during the late-exhalation stage. This duration was therefore sufficient to allow the displacement of anatomical dead-space gas and the arrival of late-exhaled breath at the photoacoustic cell. For each exhalation trace, the CO signal was first converted into concentration using the calibration equation. The baseline level was calculated from the pre-exhalation period. The onset of exhalation was identified when the CO signal increased above the baseline noise level. The end-tidal region was operationally defined as the late-exhalation plateau from 10 s to 15 s after the onset of exhalation. The ETCO value for each exhalation was calculated as the average CO concentration over this fixed plateau window rather than the maximum concentration. A total acquisition period of 2 min was used for each volunteer, during which four independent exhalation measurements were recorded. The mean value of the four measurements was used for subsequent analysis to reduce random variability and minimize the influence of uncontrolled physiological and experimental factors. The measured T90 response and recovery times were approximately 1.2 s and 1.3 s, respectively, which are much shorter than the 15 s exhalation duration and therefore sufficient for extracting the late-exhalation plateau from 10 s to 15 s after the onset of exhalation.

Using this protocol, end-tidal CO measurements were performed on five smokers and five non-smokers. Fig. 7 shows the real-time photoacoustic CO signals recorded from the ten volunteers. For all participants, the signal remained close to the baseline before exhalation, increased rapidly after the onset of exhalation, and subsequently approached a plateau, typically between approximately 10–17 s. The signal then decreased rapidly after exhalation ceased. After the end of exhalation, the CO signal decreased rapidly from the late-exhalation plateau to the baseline. The decreasing time was defined as the time required for the CO signal to decrease from the plateau level to near the baseline after exhalation stopped. Based on the recorded breath profiles, the decreasing time was approximately 4–5 s for different volunteers. This value is longer than the measured whole-system T90 recovery time of ∼ 1.3 s, indicating that the sensor response was sufficiently fast to follow the decrease of CO concentration during the breath-sampling process. This temporal profile is consistent with the progressive displacement of anatomical dead-space air by alveolar breath and indicates that the plateau region can be used to determine the end-tidal CO level. The different rising slopes in Fig. 7 are mainly attributed to individual exhalation dynamics, including exhalation flow rate, lung volume, exhalation strength, and the time required to flush the sampling interface and anatomical dead-space gas. Since the DPAC sampling flow and lock-in parameters were kept constant, these slope differences do not indicate different sensor response times. Therefore, the ETCO value was extracted from the late-exhalation plateau region rather than from the rising slope.

Real-time photoacoustic CO signals measured from five smokers and five non-smokers using the standardized breath-sampling protocol.

A clear difference was observed between the smoker and non-smoker groups. The photoacoustic signals from the smokers were substantially higher than those from the non-smokers throughout the plateau region. The smoker group also exhibited greater interindividual variability, which may be associated with differences in recent cigarette consumption, smoking history, inhalation behavior, and the elapsed time since the last cigarette. In contrast, the signals obtained from the non-smokers remained close to the baseline and showed relatively small variations. Although the rise time and plateau duration differed among individuals, the complete exhalation profiles were captured without apparent temporal distortion, demonstrating that the sensor response is sufficient to resolve rapid variations in exhaled CO.

As shown in Fig. 8, the mean end-tidal CO concentration obtained from four repeated measurements is presented for each volunteer. Volunteers 1–5 correspond to the five non-smokers, whereas volunteers 6–10 correspond to the five smokers. The maximum concentration measured among the non-smokers was 3.79 ppm, whereas the minimum concentration measured among the smokers was 13.16 ppm, resulting in a clear separation between the two groups. The mean concentration was 21.39 ppm for the smoker group and 3.25 ppm for the non-smoker group. The standard-deviation error bars were not included in Fig. 8 because they were barely visible on the ppm-level concentration scale. The maximum relative standard deviation (RSD) of the four repeated measurements among all volunteers was 1%, and the largest relative difference among repeated measurements was 1.5%, indicating good repeatability of the breath-sampling and ETCO extraction procedure. Under the present experimental conditions, the substantially higher CO concentrations observed in the smokers demonstrate the capability of the proposed sensor to differentiate smokers from non-smokers. Nevertheless, because only ten volunteers were included, these results should be regarded as a preliminary demonstration rather than the establishment of a general diagnostic threshold. This breath test should be regarded as a preliminary proof-of-concept demonstration rather than clinical validation.

Mean end-tidal CO concentrations obtained from four repeated measurements for five smokers and five non-smokers. Volunteers 1–5 correspond to non-smokers, and volunteers 6–10 correspond to smokers.

Exhaled CO is primarily indicative of recent tobacco smoke exposure and may provide complementary information for the objective assessment of smoking status. The proposed sensing system therefore has potential applications in smoking cessation clinics for monitoring recent smoking behavior and evaluating temporal changes in end-tidal CO during cessation. Further studies involving larger cohorts, standardized assessments of tobacco dependence, and longitudinal follow-up are required to clarify the relationships among end-tidal CO concentration, dependence severity, abstinence status, and smoking relapse. A previous study involving 717 patients with myocardial infarction reported that an exhaled CO concentration above 11 ppm was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality within one year. The one-year mortality rates were 16.5% and 5.2% in the high- and low-CO groups, respectively. Patients with elevated exhaled CO levels also exhibited higher incidences of major adverse cardiovascular events and in-hospital complications. These findings suggest that exhaled CO measurement may provide complementary information for cardiovascular risk assessment when interpreted together with smoking history and established clinical risk factors. However, exhaled CO should be regarded as an adjunctive biomarker rather than an independent diagnostic or prognostic indicator, and its clinical value requires further validation in larger and more diverse populations.

It should be emphasized that the present volunteer test was designed to verify the feasibility of real-time ETCO measurement using the proposed PAS sensor. The small cohort size does not allow the establishment of diagnostic thresholds or clinical decision criteria. Larger-scale studies involving more diverse populations, standardized smoking-history records, clinical reference methods, and longitudinal follow-up are required before the sensor can be used for clinical decision-making. It should also be noted that no simultaneous CO2 measurement was performed in the present study. Therefore, the late-exhalation plateau selected from the CO signal could not be independently confirmed by a CO2 plateau. The extracted CO concentration should therefore be regarded as an estimated end-tidal CO value obtained under a standardized breathing protocol. Future work will integrate simultaneous CO2 monitoring or capnography to more accurately identify the true end-tidal alveolar region and improve the reliability of ETCO extraction.

Conclusions

A compact differential photoacoustic spectroscopy sensor was developed for rapid and highly sensitive measurement of end-tidal carbon monoxide in exhaled breath. A 3D-printed dual-channel differential resonant photoacoustic cell with an effective sample volume of 2.5 mL was employed to enhance the acoustic response, suppress common-mode noise, and facilitate rapid gas exchange. The selected CO absorption line at 2176.1 cm−1 was excited using a distributed-feedback quantum cascade laser. The experimentally determined acoustic resonance frequency and quality factor of the photoacoustic cell were 3726.94 Hz and 26, respectively. Under the optimized operating conditions, the sensor achieved a detection limit of 30.6 ppb at a lock-in amplifier time constant of 1 s. The compact sampling volume provided a theoretical one-volume gas residence time of approximately 0.68 s, and the measured response and recovery T90 values were 1.2 s and 1.3 s, respectively, enabling second-scale tracking of exhaled CO variations.

Using second-harmonic wavelength modulation, the sensor response was evaluated with CO mixtures prepared in synthetic zero air. A linear response was obtained over the concentration range of 0–1000 ppm, with a coefficient of determination of R2= 0.9979. The feasibility of the sensor for breath analysis was evaluated using five smokers and five non-smokers. Four repeated measurements were performed for each volunteer over a total acquisition period of 2 min. The smoker group exhibited a mean end-tidal CO concentration of 21.39 ppm, with a minimum measured value of 13.16 ppm, whereas the non-smoker group showed a mean concentration of 3.25 ppm and a maximum value of 3.79 ppm. The clear separation observed between the two groups demonstrates the capability of the proposed sensor to distinguish smokers from non-smokers under the present experimental conditions.

The present prototype still has several limitations. In the DPAC, the stainless-steel tubes act as the main acoustic resonator sections and primary gas-contact channels, whereas the 3D-printed ABS body mainly provides mechanical support and structural integration. Although this hybrid design reduces direct interaction between humid breath samples and the polymer surface, long-term polymer aging, humidity cycling, surface contamination, and sealing degradation may still influence the acoustic response, baseline stability, and calibration accuracy. Therefore, accelerated aging tests, humidity-cycling experiments, and repeated-use evaluations will be required before practical deployment. In addition, the sensitivity is mainly limited by the available QCL optical power, residual microphone and electronic noise, acoustic losses in the compact resonator, imperfect matching between the two differential channels, and possible window or wall background signals. The sensitivity can be further improved by increasing the excitation optical power, optimizing the resonator geometry, improving channel-to-channel matching, using lower-noise microphones and preamplifiers, and reducing background absorption. Future miniaturization will focus on integrating the QCL source, compact optical module, laser driver, temperature controller, embedded lock-in demodulation circuit, and breath-sampling unit into a more compact platform. Combined with automatic baseline correction, humidity/pressure compensation, and embedded data-processing algorithms, these improvements may support the development of a portable or handheld point-of-care breath CO analyzer.

Overall, these results demonstrate the technical feasibility of the proposed sensor for real-time breath CO analysis. However, the current volunteer cohort was limited in size, and the breath experiment should be regarded as a preliminary proof-of-principle demonstration rather than a clinical validation. Further studies involving larger and more diverse populations, standardized assessments of tobacco dependence, and longitudinal measurements are required to establish clinically applicable thresholds and clarify the relationships among smoking behavior, end-tidal CO concentration, and cardiovascular risk.
