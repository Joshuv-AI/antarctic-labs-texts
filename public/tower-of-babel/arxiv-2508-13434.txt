EventTSF : Event-Aware Non-Stationary Time Series Forecasting
Yunfeng Ge, Ming Jin, Yiji Zhao, Hongyan Li

Abstract
Time series forecasting plays a vital role in critical domains like energy and transportation, where non-stationary dynamics are deeply intertwined with events in other modalities such as texts. However, incorporating natural language–based external events to improve non-stationary forecasting remains largely unexplored, as most approaches still rely on a single modality, resulting in limited contextual knowledge and model underperformance. Enabling fine-grained multimodal interactions between temporal and textual data is challenged by three fundamental issues: (1) the difficulty of fine-grained synchronization between time-varying discrete textual events and continuous time series; (2) the inherent temporal uncertainty introduced by textual semantics; and (3) the misalignment between textual event embeddings and multi-resolution temporal patterns. In this work, we address these challenges by introducing event-aware non-stationary time series forecasting ( EventTSF ), an autoregressive generation framework that integrates historical time series with textual events to make subsequent forecasts. Specifically, EventTSF uses autoregressive diffusion with flow matching at each step to capture nuanced temporal-event interactions. To handle event-induced uncertainty, flow matching timesteps are adaptively controlled according to event semantic signals. The underlying denoiser employs a multimodal U-shaped diffusion transformer that efficiently fuses temporal and textual modalities across different resolutions. Extensive experiments on 8 synthetic and real-world datasets show that EventTSF outperforms 12 baselines across diverse event-aware non-stationary time series forecasting scenarios, achieving substantial improvements of 10.7 % 10.7\% higher forecasting accuracy and 1.13 × 1.13\times faster training efficiency.



Introduction

Time series forecasting is critical in domains like energy, transportation, and meteorology
(
Liang et al. 2024b
)
, where accurate forecasting enables effective decision-making and resource management.
However, real-world forecasting faces persistent challenges from non-stationarity and distribution shifts
(
Box and Jenkins 1976
;
Liu et al. 2023d
)
, where time series distribution changes over time. While recent advances, including normalization techniques e.g., RevIN
(
Kim et al. 2021
)
, model decomposition e.g., Koopa
(
Liu et al. 2023c
)
, and online learning e.g., OneNet
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
, have partially addressed these issues, they typically operate on single modalities, overlooking that the change is entangled with external events from other modalities, particularly texts. This leaves valuable contextual knowledge largely unexploited, resulting in suboptimal forecasting performance, shown in Figure
1
(a). The distinct distributions triggered by event-type, shown in Figure
1
(b), highlight the need for event-aware forecasting.

Recent studies have actively explored multimodal integration for time series forecasting with large language models (LLMs) via prompt-based paradigms and agentic interactions
(
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2025
)
.
These methods exhibit notable limitations in handling non-stationarity emerging from temporal dynamics and complex events interaction because: (1) existing methods utilize general (static) textual contextual information for predictive enhancement
(
Zhang et al. 2025c
)
, yet exhibit constrained capability in capturing fine-grained event-induced shifts; (2) LLM tokenization suffers from representation issues
(
Singh and Strouse 2024
)
and produces deterministic outputs, inadequately modeling event-induced uncertainty. Recent advances in flow matching models
(
Liu et al. 2025c
)
and autoregressive generation
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
provide promising foundations to address these challenges.

While recent advances make fine-grained event-aware forecasting possible, three challenges remain: ❶
Temporal-event synchronization
. Existing methods
(
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Narasimhan et al. 2024
)
rely on static metatext context, which makes it difficult to synchronize time-varying discrete events with continuous time series and neglects crucial temporal interactions like cascading and lagged dependencies.
❷
Event-conditional uncertainty modeling
. Current time series diffusion approaches
(
Ye, Xu, and Gui 2025
;
Yuan and Qiao 2024
)
treat sampling parameters as hyperparameters, neglecting how event semantics dynamically alter temporal distributions and influence uncertainty variations across event types.
❸
Cross-modal representation alignment
. Textual event embeddings and time series patterns often misalign, for instance, “rainfall” affects traffic all day while “post-holiday rush” targets peak periods.

To address these challenges, we propose
EventTSF
, an autoregressive diffusion architecture for event-aware non-stationary time series forecasting.

EventTSF
integrates historical time series and asynchronous textual events for subsequent forecasts. Specifically, it adopts an
autoregressive diffusion architecture
where it maintains learnable temporal priors at each time step, integrating event information via flow matching to align time series with asynchronous event embeddings autoregressively.
To mitigate event-induced uncertainty, we propose an
event-controlled flow matching timesteps
mechanism, which replaces fixed sampling timesteps with adaptive ones. Event-conditioned networks dynamically allocate more timesteps to enhance precision when critical events occur.
Additionally, we design a
multimodal U-shaped diffusion transformer
to align event semantic embeddings and multiple temporal resolutions. It aligns textual event semantics and multi-resolution temporal patterns through diffusion transformers with multilevel up/down-sampling, handling multi-resolution event impacts from coarse-grained patterns to fine-grained fluctuations.

Paradigm reformulation

•

Innovated methodology
. We propose a Multimodal U-shaped Diffusion Transformer that precisely aligns event representation and multiple-resolution time series.

•

Superior performance
.
EventTSF
achieves
47\%
accuracy gains on dominant multimodal non-stationary datasets and
1.13\times
improvement in training efficiency.

Definition

Given a univariate time series
\mathbf{X}_{1:L}=\{x_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
of length
L
, we define a set of
N
chronologically ordered fine-grained textual event embeddings
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{s},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{N}\}
. Each event embedding
\mathbf{c}_{s}
corresponds to a non-overlapping consistent time segment
\mathbf{x}_{s}=\{x_{l}\}_{l=i_{s}}^{j_{s}}\subset\mathbf{X}_{1:L}
, where
i_{s}
and
j_{s}
denote the start and end timestamps of event segment
s
. The multimodal time series datasets is defined as
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{c}_{s})\}_{s=1}^{N}
, satisfying the constraints: (1)
\bigcup_{s=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{s}\subseteq\mathbf{X}_{1:L}
(complete coverage), and (2)
\mathbf{x}_{s}\cap\mathbf{x}_{s^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for all
s\neq s^{\prime}
(non-overlapping segments).

We address event-aware non-stationary time series forecasting, where the underlying temporal distribution shifts over time across different event categories,

P(\mathcal{X}\mid\mathbf{c}_{i})\neq P(\mathcal{X}\mid\mathbf{c}_{j}),\quad\text{if}\quad\mathbf{c}_{i}\neq\mathbf{c}_{j}.

Given
p
historical time series and event pairs
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{l}\}_{l=s-p+1}^{s}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{c}}=\{\mathbf{c}_{l}\}_{l=s-p+1}^{s}
, and
q
future event descriptions
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{c}}=\{\mathbf{c}_{l}\}_{l=s+1}^{s+q}
, the objective is to forecast the following
q
future time series predictions
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{c}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{c}})
, where
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathbf{x}}
is the estimated
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{x}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{l}\}_{s+1}^{s+q}
;
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
denotes an event-aware forecasting model that captures non-stationary dynamics conditioned on external events.

Methodology

We introduce
EventTSF
, an architecture designed for event-aware non-stationary time series forecasting by blending multiple data sources. It combines (1) autoregressive diffusion, (2) event-controlled flow matching, and (3) a U-shaped diffusion transformer to address event and time series synchronization, event-induced temporal uncertainty, and cross-modal representation misalignment, respectively.

Multimodal Autoregressive Diffusion Architecture

Exogenous events exert influence on real-world time series forecasting. For example, traffic demand often surges after major sports games or public celebrations; network bandwidth usage can spike during highly anticipated live broadcasts; product sales rise due to planned promotional campaigns; and residential electricity demand amidst extreme heat warnings, as shown in Figure
2
.
To synchronize non-stationary time series with dynamic event information, we propose a multimodal autoregressive diffusion architecture.

During the training stage, learnable prior state
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}
is introduced at each autoregressive time step
s
, serving as historical context. The model integrates noisy time series, diffusion timesteps, prior state, and textual event embeddings from OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3
. Then, flow matching is applied to align the input with event conditions. This procedure autoregressively models temporal and event dynamics across all time steps in a global view.
Mathematically, at each autoregressive time step
s
and diffusion timestep
t
, the model conditions on latent state
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}
and event representation
\mathbf{c}_{s}
to learn the velocity of flow trajectory
\mathbf{v}_{s}^{t}
using a flow matching denoiser
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
.
The independence of
\mathbf{v}_{s}^{t}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{s}
enables using
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
as a unified network, thereby improving efficiency.
This design enables fine-grained synchronization between evolving temporal patterns and event semantics.
The training algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

As illustrated in the right part of Figure
2
, during the inference stage, the architecture leverages the historical time series
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and corresponding textual events
\mathcal{H}_{c}
to compute the historical state
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
without noise. During forecasting, the historical state
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
serves as the initial state
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}
; the model takes noise input
\mathbf{n}_{s}
and the exogenous event
\mathbf{c}_{s}
to generate prediction
\mathbf{x}_{s}
via the learned flow matching denoiser
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\mathbf{Z}_{s-1},\mathbf{n}_{s},\mathbf{c}_{s},t)
, coupled with a
T
-step
ODE
solver. The model autoregressively incorporates dynamic event descriptions, enabling flexible event-aware forecasting under evolving contexts. See Algorithm
2
for details.

Flow Matching with Event-controlled Timesteps

We use the flow matching
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
to train textual events and time series jointly. At each time step
s
and diffusion timestep
t\in[0,1]
, flow matching generates data trajectories from a prior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{0})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
to the target distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{1})
. An optimal transport path, realized through Rectified Flow
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
, guides this transformation process:

\mathbf{x}_{s}^{t}=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{s}^{0}+t\mathbf{x}_{s}^{1}.

(1)

The instantaneous velocity vector field along this path
\mathbf{v}_{s}^{t}
with respect to diffusion timestep
t
is:

\mathbf{v}_{s}^{t}=\frac{d\mathbf{x}_{s}^{t}}{dt}=\mathbf{x}_{s}^{1}-\mathbf{x}_{s}^{0}.

(2)

A neural network
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{t},\mathbf{Z}_{s-1},\mathbf{c}_{s},t)
approximates the target vector field
\mathbf{v}_{s}^{t}
. To enhance generation quality,
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}
is designed to integrate the conditional information
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
. In our settings, this conditional information includes the historical state
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}
of the time series and the textual condition
\mathbf{c}_{s}
.
During sampling, the model generates
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{s}^{1}
by first sampling noise
\mathbf{x}_{s}^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and then solving the learned velocity field
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}
via a numerical
ODE
solver over
T
intervals from
t=0
to
t=1
.

Event-controlled Timesteps
.
Non-stationary time series are subject to the uncertainty introduced by exogenous events. Instead of treating the total number of diffusion sampling intervals
T
as a fixed hyperparameter, we propose flow matching with event-controlled timesteps where sampling timesteps are adaptively controlled by the event information to mitigate this uncertainty. We define this as a learnable function conditioned on the textual event embedding
\mathbf{c}_{s}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{T}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\text{Linear}(\mathbf{c}_{s})),

(3)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T+\delta_{T}},

where
\text{Linear}(\cdot)
is a learnable linear layer that maps event embeddings to scalars, followed by sigmoid activation
\sigma(\cdot)
. This process enables the model to adaptively control the number of sampling steps based on event semantics, where a larger
\delta_{T}
corresponds to finer sampling, ensuring higher precision. Event-controlled timesteps improve the flexibility and robustness of processing event-time series interactions.

Multimodal U-shaped Diffusion Transformer

Textual events and non-stationary temporal dynamics often exhibit representational misalignment, especially when events have multi-resolution impacts ranging from coarse-grained trends to fine-grained surges. To address this challenge, we propose the Multimodal U-shaped Diffusion Transformer (M-U-DiT), inspired by the U-Net model
(
Ronneberger, Fischer, and Brox 2015
)
. M-U-DiT leverages down-sampling, up-sampling, and skip connections to bridge textual event semantics with multi-resolution temporal components.
M-U-DiT takes current noisy input
\mathbf{x}_{s}^{t}
(or
\mathbf{n}_{s}^{t}
during sampling), previous latent state
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}
, flow matching timestep
t
, and textual condition
\mathbf{c}_{s}
, then outputs estimated velocity field and state
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{s}
.

Input
. Input processing begins with cross-attention that integrates historical information
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}^{t}
at diffusion timestep
t
and current noisy observation
\mathbf{x}_{s}^{t}
, producing enriched representation
\mathbf{x}_{e}^{(0)}
as input to the stacked M-U-DiT.
Meanwhile, diffusion timestep
t
and textual condition
\mathbf{c}_{s}
are embedded and fused to form conditioning vectors
\mathbf{g}_{*}=\phi_{*}(t)+\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{c}_{s})
, where
\phi_{*}
and
\Phi_{*}
are embedding layers. To match feature dimensions across different M-U-DiT components, global vectors following this formulation
\mathbf{g}_{e}
,
\mathbf{g}_{b}
, and
\mathbf{g}_{d}
, are used for the encoder, bottleneck layer, and decoder, respectively.

M-U-DiT
. We denote the
l
-th encoder layer as
\epsilon_{e}^{(l)}
, the single bottleneck layer as
\epsilon_{b}
, and the
l
-th decoder layer as
\epsilon_{d}^{(l)}
, where
l\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. Down-sampling and up-sampling operations, denoted as
Down-sampling
and
Up-sampling
, are applied in the encoder and decoder layers.

M-U-DiT takes the temporal features
\mathbf{x}_{e}^{(0)}
and global condition
\mathbf{g}_{*}
as input, and processes them through the down-sampling encoder, a bottleneck layer, and the up-sampling decoder.
The computation at the
l
-th down-sampling encoder layer is defined as follows:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{e}^{(l)}=\epsilon_{e}(\mathbf{x}_{e}^{(l-1)},\mathbf{g}_{e}),

(4)

\mathbf{x}_{e}^{(l)}=\begin{cases}\textit{Down-sampling }\left(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{e}^{(l)}\right),&l<M\\
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{e}^{(l)}&l=M\end{cases}.

(5)

The output of the
M
-th encoding layer
\mathbf{x}_{e}^{(M)}
is fed to the bottleneck layer as
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{b}
. The bottleneck computation is
\mathbf{x}_{b}=\epsilon_{b}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{b},\textbf{g}_{b})
.
Following this, the bottleneck output
\mathbf{x}_{b}
initializes the decoder as
\mathbf{x}_{d}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x}_{b}
. The decoding computation at the
l
-th up-sampling decoder layer is defined as follows:

\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{d}^{(l)}=\begin{cases}\textit{Up-sampling}\left(\mathbf{x}_{d}^{(l-1)}\right),&l>1\\
\mathbf{x}_{d}^{(l-1)}&l=1\end{cases},

(6)

\mathbf{x}_{d}^{(l)}=\epsilon_{d}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{d}^{(l)}+\mathbf{x}_{e}^{(l)},\mathbf{g}_{d}).

(7)

Output
. The final decoder layer output
\mathbf{x}_{d}^{(M)}
predicts the velocity field. Updated latent state is derived via cross-attention between output and original latent state
\mathbf{Z}_{s-1}^{t}
.

\Delta_{\nu}\textit{J-FTSD}

when incorporating versus excluding textual event inputs.

evaluates alignment between real and generated joint distributions of time series and associated metadata
(
Narasimhan et al. 2024
)

N

\mathcal{D}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{c}_{\text{noise}})\}

Conclusion

This work pioneers the integration of external multimodal knowledge to tackle non-stationarity in time series forecasting.
EventTSF
captures fine-grained temporal–textual interactions via an autoregressive diffusion architecture with event-controlled timesteps and a U-shaped denoiser.
This groundbreaking architecture addresses challenges in event synchronization and event-induced uncertainty. The Multimodal U-shaped Diffusion Transformer enables alignment of event representations and time series.
Extensive experiments on 12 baselines show its effectiveness. Success in modeling cross-modal non-stationary dynamics opens up a direction for non-stationary time series analysis.

Appendix A
Detailed Dataset Descriptions

Eight datasets are utilized to evaluate
EventTSF
, including one synthetic dataset and seven real-world datasets. Statistical descriptions of the multimodal event-aware datasets are supplied in Table
3
. The event embedding space for each dataset is shown in Figure
10
.
All datasets are publicly available, and the construction methods are described as follows:

Synthetic Dataset.

To evaluate the event-aware capabilities of
EventTSF
, we construct a synthetic dataset to simulate how events manifest as shifts in time series patterns. Specifically, this dataset is composed of four distinct waveform patterns: sinusoidal, triangular, sawtooth, and near-square waves. Each waveform is perturbed with varying noise levels to emulate realistic signal variability. Each waveform segment is annotated with a corresponding event description, such as “sine wave,” “triangle wave with noise,” or “sawtooth wave with spikes.”
The textual component is obtained by first prompting GPT-4o to describe each waveform segment, followed by embedding generation using OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3
.
The generated time series segments and their descriptive embeddings are combined to form a comprehensive synthetic dataset, enabling evaluation of
EventTSF
’s ability to be aware of event transitions and adapt its forecasts accordingly.
As shown in Table
3
, the Synthetic Dataset comprises a moderate number of samples with a balanced distribution across waveform types. The average description length is 2.14 words, indicating that each annotation primarily captures the waveform category.
As shown in Figure
10
, the t-SNE visualization of the text embeddings reveals a clear separation among categories. Overall, the synthetic dataset effectively simulates real-world scenarios of time series and textual events.

Atmospheric Physics–Weather Events Dataset.

This dataset is derived from atmospheric physics measurements recorded at the Beutenberg weather station in Jena, Germany. Associated natural language descriptions are provided by a local meteorological forecast service. These descriptions cover various aspects of the weather, including cloud conditions such as broken clouds, partly sunny skies, and passing clouds, as well as perceived comfort levels and relative humidity.
These descriptions are then embedded into vector representations using OpenAI’s text embedding. The resulting textual embeddings are aligned to the same granularity as the atmospheric measurements.
For the time series modality, we adopt Vapor Pressure Deficit as the primary signal, as it quantitatively measures air dryness and strongly correlates with weather-related textual attributes.
As shown in Table
3
, the Atmospheric Physics–Weather Events Dataset contains the second largest number of samples and exhibits the finest sampling rate with 10-minute intervals. The average event description length is 3.64 words. Since the event descriptions include both discrete and continuous variables, the event type distribution cannot be statistically summarized in categorical terms.
As illustrated in Figure
10
, despite the presence of both discrete and continuous attributes in the event descriptions, the t-SNE visualization reveals distinct and well-separated clusters, indicating latent semantic structures within the data. Overall, this dataset effectively integrates fine-grained time series signals with correlated textual event descriptions, providing a robust multimodal dataset resource for event-aware forecasting.

Figure 8:
Visualization of the Electricity–Extreme Weather Events dataset. Though a distinguished event type, similar temporal dynamics can be observed in this dataset.

Traffic–Public Events Dataset.

We reconstruct the Traffic–Public Events Dataset based on the methodology proposed in
(
Liang et al. 2024a
)
, where regional traffic flow is notably affected by public events, such as sports games and city-wide celebrations. Specifically, we collect green taxi drop-off data in the vicinity of the Barclays Center in New York City, spanning January to June 2015, and aggregate it at an hourly resolution.
In parallel, we collect the official schedules of major public events held at the Barclays Center during the same period. Each textual event is aligned with its corresponding time series slice, during which traffic patterns are expected to be influenced by the associated event.
For time series slices that do not correspond to any public events, which represent regular days, we assign a plain textual embedding to preserve alignment between the textual and time series modalities.
As shown in Table
3
, the Traffic–Public Events Dataset contains the smallest number of samples among all datasets. The average event description length is 4.21 words, primarily due to the length of the whole event name. Since the events belong to an open set and are not constrained by a predefined category list, the event type distribution is not statistically summarized.
As depicted in Figure
10
, despite variations in textual embeddings, the t-SNE visualization reveals several well-defined clusters in the center, indicating that the latent representations of event-related segments exhibit meaningful separability.

A case study of a textual event from the Traffic-News Events dataset. The original news event descriptions are often overly verbose.

Temperature–Rainfall Events Dataset
(
Lee et al. 2025
)
.

The Temperature-Rainfall Dataset includes binary rainfall event labels and hourly temperature time series data collected over five years across several major urban cities, including New York, San Francisco, and Houston.
As shown in Table
3
, since the dataset captures only the binary presence or absence of rainfall, the textual event descriptions are limited to a single token: either true or false. These labels are directly encoded as textual event embeddings.
Consequently, as illustrated in Figure
10
, the t-SNE visualization shows that the embeddings for the three cities, including New York, San Francisco, and Houston, form two distinct clusters, corresponding to the binary event categories.

Electricity–Extreme Weather Events Dataset
(
Liu et al. 2023a
)
.

The Electricity–Weather Events Dataset is a multimodal dataset that records electricity load data in conjunction with four event types: low temperature event, high temperature event, high humidity event, and no event.
As shown in Table
3
, this dataset contains the largest number of timestamps among all datasets, covering a six-year period. Notably, over half of the records belong to the no-event category, indicating a sparsity of key events.
As illustrated in Figure
10
, the textual embeddings form well-separated clusters in the event embedding space, clearly reflecting the four predefined event types.
However, despite the clear separation observed in the textual embedding space, the average electricity load curves under different event conditions remain nearly indistinguishable, as shown in Figure
8
. This indicates a weak correlation between the recorded events and the electricity load time series, which limits the effectiveness of textual events for event-aware forecasting tasks.

Traffic-News Events Dataset
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
.

The Traffic–News Events Dataset
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
consists of traffic load records paired with textual news events that capture a broad range of external factors, including holidays, weather conditions, and other relevant occurrences.
As shown in Table
3
, this dataset contains the longest textual descriptions among all datasets, with an average length of 280 words per event. The textual event embeddings are generated using OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3
model.
The event embedding space is visualized in Figure
10
. Despite the richness of the textual content, the embeddings exhibit weakly differentiated clusters, suggesting that long-form news descriptions may not clearly represent the core semantics of the associated events. This lack of semantic compactness in the representations poses challenges for models trying to leverage the textual event to improve forecasting performance.

Appendix B
Analysis of the Datasets for RQ2

To evaluate the contribution of the textual modality to forecasting performance, we adopt the
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}\textit{J-FTSD}
metric to quantify predictability. However, two event-aware datasets, namely the Electricity–Extreme Weather Events Dataset and the Traffic–News Events Dataset, show unsatisfactory results. These results can be attributed to intrinsic limitations in the quality and alignment of the textual event data.
For this Electricity–Extreme Weather Events Dataset, event annotations occur infrequently. As shown in Table
3
, 62.9% of the time steps are labeled as “no event,” significantly reducing the availability of informative supervision. Moreover, as illustrated in Figure
8
, even when events are present, the corresponding time series patterns remain highly similar across different event types. This weak correlation between recorded events and temporal dynamics limits the event-aware forecasting.
Another dataset under consideration is the Traffic–News Events Dataset, which suffers from excessive redundancy in its textual modality.
A large portion of the event descriptions, accounting for 87.3 percent, consists of background news reports as shown in Figure
9
.
These background reports hinders precise alignment between events and the corresponding time series slices.
Furthermore, the pretrained
text-embedding-3
model from OpenAI fails to produce semantically compact clusters for these background news events as shown in Figure
10
. This indicates a lack of structure in the embedding space and further exacerbating alignment challenges.
Taken together, these findings highlight that the informativeness and interrelation of textual events are pivotal for event-aware forecasting. Simply incorporating textual data is insufficient. Our results advocate increased efforts in dataset construction and curation strategies for event-aware time series datasets.

Appendix C
Training Details

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS with 64GB of system RAM. The software environment are based on Python 3.11.10 and PyTorch 2.5.0 with CUDA 12.1.1. Complete software and package specifications are provided in the code repository for reproducibility.
Models were trained for a maximum of
1000
epochs with a batch size of
64
. Early stopping was applied based on validation loss, evaluated every
5
epochs with a patience of
5
. Input time series were standardized using z-score normalization. A dynamic dropout strategy was adopted, in which the dropout rate decreased linearly from
0.6
to
0.05
throughout training.
We employed the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate
2\times 10^{-4}
and a weight decay of
1\times 10^{-3}
. The optimizer was configured with
\beta_{1}=0.9
and
\beta_{2}=0.999
, and the learning rate followed a OneCycleLR schedule. The learning rate was warmed up from
1\times 10^{-5}
to the peak over the first 20% of training steps. It then linearly decayed to a final value of
1\times 10^{-7}
over the remaining steps.
The flow matching training procedure used
100
sampling steps, while the sampling procedure employed
50
sampling steps. The model architecture consists of
3
encoder layers,
1
bottleneck layer, and
3
decoder layers. Both downsampling and upsampling rates were set to
2
. The historical state dimension was
48
, the hidden dimension was
256
, the textual embedding dimension was
128
, and the fully connected layer dimension was
1024
.

More Related Work

In the financial domain, events have been shown to enhance forecasting tasks by leveraging deep learning techniques to extract sentiment, descriptive, and semantic features from textual event data. These methods can be classified into four lines, including ❶ deep learning methods
(
Xu et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2018
;
Emami et al. 2023
)
, ❷ reinforcement learning methods
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, ❸ generative methods
(
Duan et al. 2022
)
, and ❹ large language model based methods
(
Liu and Jia 2025
;
Xiao et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025d
)
.
Deep learning methods, such as REST
(
Xu et al. 2021
)
utilizes a long short-term memory network to model the impact of newly emerged events on stock price fluctuations, while
(
Emami et al. 2023
)
introduces a modality-aligned transformer to encode financial time series and event semantics jointly.
Reinforcement learning methods, such as DeepTrader
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, incorporate macroeconomic events into trading strategies through reward shaping and policy adaptation. Generative methods, such as FactorVAE
(
Duan et al. 2022
)
disentangle latent event factors to improve forecasting.
Recently, LLM-based frameworks have emerged as powerful tools, including prompt adaptation
(
Liu and Jia 2025
)
and retrieval-augmented generation
(
Xiao et al. 2025
)
, aiming to construct particular features for future dynamics estimation.
However, these studies assume that events have already occurred, and thus focus on learning latent factors conditioned on those events. In contrast, our method (
EventTSF
) targets the event-known forecasting setting. This setting commonly arises in real-world scenarios, such as scheduled sports competitions that impact future traffic patterns or anticipated promotional sales that drive abrupt changes in demand. By conditioning on the known, future scheduled event,
EventTSF
models how time series dynamics evolve in a causally plausible and temporally aligned manner, tackling a forecasting challenge thst is both practically critical and largely underexplored.
