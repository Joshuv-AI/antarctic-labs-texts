Strange and pseudo-differentiable functions with applications to prime partitions
Anji Dong, Nicolas Robles, Alexandru Zaharescu, Dirk Zeindler

Abstract
. Let 𝔭 ℙ r ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n) denote the number of partitions of n n into r r -full primes. We use the Hardy-Littlewood circle method to find the asymptotic of 𝔭 ℙ r ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n) as n → ∞ n\to\infty . This extends previous results in the literature of partitions into primes. We also show an analogue result involving convolutions of von Mangoldt functions and the zeros of the Riemann zeta-function. To handle the resulting non-principal major arcs we introduce the definition of strange functions and pseudo-differentiability.

Primary: 11P55, 11P82, 26A24. Secondary: 11L07, 11L20, 11M26.

Keywords and phrases
: weights associated to partitions, pseudo-differentiable functions, strange functions, inclusion-exclusion, Hardy-Littlewood circle method, exponential sums, von Mangoldt function, zeros of the zeta function.

1.
Introduction and results

1.1.
Previous and current results

A partition of a positive integer
n
is a non-decreasing sequence of positive integers whose sum is equal to
n
. Let
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{N}}(n)
denote the restricted partition function that counts partitions of
n
lying within
\mathbb{N}
. A classical result of Hardy and Ramanujan
[
15
]
states that

\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{N}}(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}\exp\bigg(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\bigg)\quad\textnormal{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Let
\mathbb{P}
denote the set of primes. Partition functions that count partitions of
n
lying within
\mathbb{P}
have also been studied by several authors such as Bateman and Erdös
[
2
,
3
]
, Browkin
[
7
]
, Kerawala
[
16
]
, Roth and Szekeres
[
17
]
, as well as Yang
[
23
]
. In 2008 Vaughan
[
20
]
was able to simplify and improve most of the literature on prime partitions. Moreover, in 2021, Gafni
[
14
]
proved an asymptotic formula for partitions with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{k}=\{p^{k}\,;\,p\in\mathbb{P}\}
for fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Explicitly, Gafni established

\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{k}}(n)\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}\exp\bigg(\mathfrak{c}_{2}\frac{n^{\frac{1}{k+1}}}{(\log n)^{\frac{k}{k+1}}}(1+o(1))\bigg)n^{-\frac{2k+1}{2k+2}}(\log n)^{-\frac{k}{2k+2}}\quad\textnormal{as}\quad n\to\infty,

where
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
are positive constants that can be made explicit and that depend only on
k
. Vaughan’s result is the special case
k=1
. For
r\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{r}=\{p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{r}\,;\,p_{i}\in\mathbb{P}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq r\}
denote the set of
r
-full primes. In this note we propose to find the asymptotic for
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)
as
n\to\infty
. Due to the close proximity of the characteristic function of the primes
\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{P}}
to the von Mangoldt function
\Lambda
, we also show how to adapt the asymptotic of
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)
to that of
\mathfrak{p}_{\Lambda^{*r}}(n)
where
\Lambda^{*r}=\Lambda*\Lambda*\cdots*\Lambda
exactly
r
times. Our results are as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\log\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)=2n^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(Q(n)\big)^{-1}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log n}\bigg)\bigg),

where the term

is given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle Q(n):=\left(\frac{\log n+\log\log n-\log 2-\log P_{r}(\log\log n-\log 2)-\log\zeta(2)}{2\zeta(2)P_{r}\left(\log\log n-\log 2\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and
P_{r}
is a polynomial of degree

with leading coefficient
r
.

The polynomial
P_{r}
can be explicitly determined and its formula is given in (
8.3
) with the special cases
r=1,2,3,4
stated in Corollary
8.3
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a polynomial
\widetilde{P}_{r}
of degree
r-1
and leading coefficient
1
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\log\mathfrak{p}_{\Lambda^{*r}}(n)=2n^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\zeta(2)\widetilde{P}_{r}\left(\frac{\log n}{2}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{(\log\log n)^{r-1}}{\log n}\right)\right).

Likewise, the polynomial
\widetilde{P}_{r}
can also be explicitly determined and its formula is given in (

) with the special cases

It is worth remarking some recent results in the literature of weighted partitions following the work of Vaughan in
[
21
]
. The partitions for
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{k}}(n)
where

\mathcal{N}_{k}=\{x^{k}\,;\,x\in\mathbb{N}\}

were studied by Gafni in

This was later generalized in
[

by Berndt, Malik and Zaharescu to
\mathcal{N}_{k,a,b}=\{x^{k}\equiv a\operatorname{mod}b\,;\,x\in\mathbb{N}\}
for fixed

and
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
with
(a,b)=1

Let
\mathcal{N}_{f}=\{f(x)\,;\,x\in\mathbb{N}\}
where
f
is a polynomial of degree
d\geq 2
.
The associated partitions were studied in
[
12
]
when
\gcd(\mathcal{N}_{f})=1
. Chromatic partitions associated to the generalized divisor function were established in
[
4

. Furthermore, partitions

and

by arithmetic functions such as the Möbius and Liouville functions were studied in
[

and
[

[

[

.

The technique to extract the asymptotic formula from the generating series is that of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method
[
19
]
. As we will elaborate, the idea is to divide a circle of radius
\rho<1
into three distinct types of arcs: the
principal
major arc, the non-principal major arcs, and the minor arcs. Each of these arcs requires its own set of techniques. The main term in the asymptotic for
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)
will come from the principal major arcs whereas the other two arcs will give rise to the error terms.

The technology to deal with the minor arcs requires precise bounds on exponential sums twisted by suitable arithmetic functions such as

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\operatorname{e}(\alpha n)\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\operatorname{e}(x)=\exp(2\pi ix)

and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. In our case, these functions will be
f=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
, the characteristic function of
r
-full primes, and
f=\Lambda^{*r}
.

The bounds must hold in the ‘minor arc regime’. This regime consists of all
\alpha
that cannot be well approximated by a rational number
\frac{a}{q}
with
q
small,
see (
2.6
) – (
2.8
) for the exact requirement.
The given bounds are indeed valid on the whole circle, but in some cases they are worse than trivial.

The handling of the minor arcs is for the most part arithmetic in nature.

On the other hand, the principal arcs are mostly handled analytically, usually by the method of contour integration or the method of moments. In addition, one also heavily employs the method of steepest descent.

Interestingly, the non-principal major arcs are a mixture and necessitate arithmetico-analytic techniques such as exponential sums in arithmetic progressions, Siegel-Walfisz estimates, and, in our case, pseudo-differentiability and strange functions.

By leveraging our previous results on exponential sums
[
11
]
twisted by
\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
and
\Lambda^{*r}
we are able to bound the minor arcs associated to
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{\Lambda^{*r}}
. Furthermore, by using a Hankel contour over the zero-free region of the Riemann zeta-function we will be able to extract the main term of the asymptotic for
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
. In the case of
\mathfrak{p}_{\Lambda^{*r}}
we will employ a contour that explicitly shows the connection to the zeros of the Riemann zeta-function. By far, the longest and most difficult part of the paper will deal with the non-principal major arcs. We elaborate on this point in the next section.

1.2.
Technology on pseudo-differentiable functions

To show that the non-principal major arcs do not contribute to the main term,
we require a result similar to the Siegel-Walfisz theorem for primes in an arithmetic progression.
Explicitly, for
q,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
(q,\ell)=1
we have to study the sums

(1.5)

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\\
p_{1}\cdots p_{r}\leq t\\
p_{1}\ldots p_{r}\equiv\ell\bmod q\end{subarray}}1\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell):=\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}\cdots n_{r}\leq t\\
n_{1}\ldots n_{r}\equiv\ell\bmod q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Lambda(n_{i}),

and our main task is to show that we can write

(1.6)

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)=D_{r}(t)+O\left(t(\log t)^{-C}\right)\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)=\widetilde{D}_{r}(t)+O\left(t(\log t)^{-C}\right),

where
C\geq 1
is arbitrarily large,
D_{r}(t)
and
\widetilde{D}_{r}(t)
are differentiable with respect to
t
and
D_{r}^{\prime}(t)
and
\widetilde{D}^{\prime}_{r}(t)
can be bounded suitably. Observe that

(1.7)

\displaystyle A_{1}(t;q,\ell)=\pi(t;q,\ell)=\operatorname{card}\,\{p\leq t\,;\,p\equiv\ell\bmod q\}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{1}(t;q,\ell)=\psi(t;q,\ell),

and thus the case
r=1
corresponds to the classical the Siegel-Walfisz theorem.
The proof of this theorem can be found for example in
[
10
,
\mathsection
20-22]
and requires the analytic continuation of the Dirichlet
L
-function.
To use the same argument for
r>1
as for the Siegel-Walfisz theorem, an analytic continuation of the function

\mathfrak{F}_{r}(s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)\frac{\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{P}}^{*r}(n)}{n^{s}}

is needed for
\real(s)>s_{0}
for some
s_{0}
.
However, as far as the authors are aware, such an analytical continuation is not available and would also require a substantial amount of work to be established.
Thus an alternative argument is needed. The most natural one is to combine induction over
r
, the hyperbola method and Abel summation.
In particular, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{2}(t;q,\ell)=

\displaystyle\frac{\big(\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})\big)^{2}}{\varphi(q)}-2\sum_{p|q}\frac{\operatorname{Li}(t/p)}{\varphi(q)}+\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{t\pi(u)}{u^{2}\log(t/u)}\,du+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right).

Unfortunately, two problems occur in this induction argument.
First, explicit expressions for
r\geq 3
are not easy to achieve and are also quite complicated.
Second, for
r\geq 2
the function
A_{r}(t;q,\ell)
is only once continuously differentiable, but we require for the induction argument higher derivatives.
Our solution to these problems is to introduce pseudo-differentiable functions, see Section
5.2
.
This simplifies the resulting expressions substantially and allows to carry over the desirable strong error term arising from the Siegel-Walfisz theorem.

3.
Useful lemmas

We state in this section some auxiliary lemmas we will need later.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
a\geq 0
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\geq 0
be given.
Further let
\gamma=\gamma_{1}+i\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\gamma_{1}>0
and
\gamma_{2}\ll\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}
for some
A>0
.
We then have as
\gamma_{1}\to 0

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}t^{\lambda}\exp(-\gamma t)dt=\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}}{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b}}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\gamma^{\lambda+1}}+O\bigg(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{a+1}}{\gamma_{1}^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b+1}}\bigg).

Proof.

We split the interval
[2,\infty]
into intervals
[2,d],[d,u]
and
[u,\infty]
with

\displaystyle d=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A+1+|b|}}\quad\text{and}\quad u=\frac{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}.

We first look at the integral over
[2,d]
.
We bound each term in the integral trivially and get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{2}^{d}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}t^{\lambda}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq(\log\log d)^{a}(\log d)^{|b|}d^{\lambda}\int_{2}^{d}1\,dt\ll(\log\log d)^{a}(\log d)^{|b|}d^{\lambda+1}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{|b|}}{\gamma_{1}^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{(A+1+|b|)(\lambda+1)}}\ll\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}}{\gamma^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{\lambda+1}}.

Next we look at the integral over
[u,\infty]
.
Observe that

(\log\log t)^{a}(\log t)^{|b|}t^{\lambda}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}t)

is monotonically decaying in the interval
[u,\infty]
for
\gamma_{1}
small enough.
Thus

\displaystyle\left|\int_{u}^{\infty}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}t^{\lambda}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}u\right)(\log\log u)^{a}(\log u)^{|b|}u^{\lambda}\int_{u}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}t\right)dt

\displaystyle\ll(\log\log u)^{a}(\log u)^{|b|}u^{\lambda}\frac{\exp(-\gamma_{1}u)}{\gamma_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{|b|}}{\gamma_{1}^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2\lambda}}\exp(-\gamma_{1}u)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{|b|}}{\gamma_{1}^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2\lambda}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{C}},

where
C>0
can be chosen arbitrarily.
We have used on the last line that
\gamma_{1}u=\log^{2}(1/\gamma_{1})

and thus
\exp(-\gamma_{1}t)\leq\exp(-C\log(1/\gamma_{1}))
for any
C>0
for
\gamma_{1}
small enough.

The remaining task is to look at the integral over
[d,u]
.
Notice that
t\in[d,u]
implies
\log(t\gamma_{1})=O(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))
and so

\displaystyle\log t

\displaystyle=\log(1/\gamma_{1})\left(1+\frac{\log(\gamma_{1}t)}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\right)=\log(1/\gamma_{1})\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\log\log t

\displaystyle=\log\big(\log(1/\gamma_{1})+\log(\gamma_{1}t)\big)=\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})+\log\left(1+\frac{\log(\gamma_{1}t)}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\right).

We therefore have for
t\in[d,u]
that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}}{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b}}+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{a+1}}{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b+1}}\right).

Furthermore, similar estimates as the ones above as well as the variable substitution
z=\gamma t
yield

\displaystyle\int_{d}^{u}t^{\lambda}e^{-\gamma t}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\lambda}e^{-\gamma t}dt+O\left(d^{\lambda+1}+u^{\lambda}\frac{\exp(-\gamma_{1}u)}{\gamma_{1}}\right)

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{\lambda+1}}\varointclockwise_{\mathcal{L}}z^{\lambda}e^{-z}dz+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{\lambda+1}}\right),

where
\mathcal{L}
is the curve
\mathcal{L}(x)=xe^{i\beta}
with

and
\beta
is the argument of
\gamma
.
Since
|\beta|<\frac{\pi}{2}
, the last integral exists and is equal to
\Gamma(\lambda+1)
.
Inserting (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) into the integral over
[d,u]
gives

\displaystyle\int_{d}^{u}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}t^{\lambda}e^{-\gamma t}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}}{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b}}\int_{d}^{u}t^{\lambda}e^{-\gamma t}dt+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{a+1}}{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\lambda}e^{-\gamma_{1}t}dt\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{a}}{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b}}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\gamma^{\lambda+1}}+O\bigg(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{a+1}}{\gamma_{1}^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{b+1}}\bigg).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}
be given.
We have for
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as
t\to\infty

(3.3)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(\log u)^{d}(\log\log(t/u))^{a}}{u(\log(t/u))^{b}}\,du

\displaystyle=c_{a,b,d}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}+\widetilde{P}(\log\log t)}{(d+1)2^{d+1}(\log t)^{b-d-1}}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b-d}}\right)

with
\widetilde{P}
a polynomial of degree
a-1
and
c_{a,b,d}={}_{2}F_{1}(b,1+d;2+d,\frac{1}{2})
,
where
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the ordinary hypergeometric function.
Further,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(\log\log(t/u))^{a}}{(u\log u)(\log(t/u))^{b}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\log t)^{a+1}+P(\log\log t)}{(\log t)^{b}}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b+1}}\right),

where
P
is a polynomial of degree at most
a
.
Finally, if
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\geq 2
then

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(\log\log(t/u))^{a}}{u(\log u)^{d}(\log(t/u))^{b}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(d-1)(\log 2)^{d-1}}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{\log t}\right).

Recall, the hypergeometric function is defined for
|z|<1
by the power series

(3.6)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1};z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}(a_{2})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

where
(q)_{n}=q(q+1)\cdots(q+n-1)
is the rising Pochhammer symbol.
We require for the proof of Theorem
5.4
the two cases

(3.7)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(0,1+d;2+d;\tfrac{1}{2})=1\ \text{ and }\ {}_{2}F_{1}(b,1;2;\tfrac{1}{2})=\frac{2}{1-b}(1-2^{b-1}).

The proof of Lemma
3.2

below gives more than (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).
Indeed, we can give an explicit expressions for the
O(\cdot)
term as a convergent power series involving

\log\log t
and
\log t
(for
t\geq e^{2}
).
However, the expression for the
O(\cdot)
term is a little bit involved and (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are sufficient for our purposes.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

We begin with some preparations.
Observe that we have for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1\}
that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(\log u)^{m}}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log t)^{m+1}}{(m+1)2^{m+1}}-\frac{(\log 2)^{m+1}}{m+1}\ \text{ and }

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{du}{u\log u}

\displaystyle=\log\log\sqrt{t}-\log\log 2=\log\log t-\log\log 4.

Further, let
f
be a meromorphic function such that

f(x)=\sum_{j=-\ell}^{\infty}c_{j}x^{j}
is convergent for
0<|x|<1
for some
\ell\geq 0
and
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
0<|\frac{\log u}{\log t}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we can use dominated convergence and get with (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) that for all
c\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle J_{f}(t):=

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(\log u)^{c}}{u}f\left(\frac{\log u}{\log t}\right)\,du=\sum_{j=-\ell}^{\infty}c_{j}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{1}{u}\frac{(\log u)^{c+j}}{(\log t)^{j}}\,du

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle c_{-c-1}(\log t)^{c+1}\left(\log\log t-\log\log 4\right)

(3.10)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\neq-c-1}^{\infty}c_{j}\left(\frac{(\log t)^{c+1}}{2^{c+1}(c+j+1)2^{j}}-\frac{(\log 2)^{j+c+1}}{(j+c+1)(\log t)^{j}}\right).

Further, observe

\displaystyle\frac{(\log\log(t/u))^{a}}{(\log(t/u))^{b}}=\frac{(\log\big(\log t-\log u\big))^{a}}{(\log t-\log u)^{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(\log\log t-\log(1-\frac{\log u}{\log t})\big)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}(1-\frac{\log u}{\log t})^{b}}

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\log t)^{b}}\sum_{k=0}^{a}\binom{a}{k}\big(\log\log t\big)^{a-k}(-1)^{k}f_{k}\left(\frac{\log u}{\log t}\right)

with
f_{k}(x)=\frac{\left(\log(1-x)\right)^{k}}{(1-x)^{b}}
.
Each
f_{k}
is an analytic function for
|x|<1
and thus

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(\log u)^{c}(\log\log(t/u))^{a}}{u(\log(t/u))^{b}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\log t)^{b}}\sum_{k=0}^{a}\binom{a}{k}(\log\log t)^{a-k}(-1)^{k}J_{f_{k}}(t).

We are now able to prove the lemma.
We first prove (
3.3
) and we thus choose
c=d\geq 0
in (
3.10
).
Since each
f_{k}
is holomorphic and
c\geq 0
, we get that
c_{-c-1}=0
for each
k
.
Thus the order of magnitude of each
J_{f_{k}}(t)
in (
3.12
) is
(\log t)^{c+1}
.
Also, inserting (
3.10
) into (
3.12
) shows that the integral in (
3.3
) has the required form and that
the leading term comes from
J_{f_{0}}(t)
.
It only remains to determine the leading coefficient.
Using the Taylor series of
f_{0}(x)=(1-x)^{-b}
gives

(3.13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{c+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{-b}{j}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(c+j+1)2^{j}}=\frac{1}{2^{d+1}(d+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b)_{j}}{j!}\frac{(d+1)_{j}}{(d+2)_{j}}\frac{1}{2^{j}}.

This completes the proof of (
3.3
).
Next we prove (
3.4
).
Here choose
c=-1
and observe that
f_{0}(0)=1
and
f_{k}(0)=0
for
1\leq k\leq b
.
This implies

\displaystyle J_{f_{0}}(t)=\log\log t+O(1)\ \text{ and }\ I_{f_{k}}(t)\ll 1\ \text{ for }1\leq k\leq a.

Inserting this into (
3.12
) completes the prove of (
3.4
).
Finally, to prove (
3.5
), we choose
c=-d
and thus
c\leq-2
.
Therefore

(3.14)

\displaystyle J_{f}(t)=\,

\displaystyle-\sum_{j\neq-c-1}^{\infty}c_{j}\left(\frac{(\log 2)^{j+c+1}}{(j+c+1)(\log t)^{j}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}\right)

and

\displaystyle J_{f_{0}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\log 2)^{c+1}}{(c+1)}+O\left(\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}\right)=\frac{1}{(d-1)(\log 2)^{d-1}}+O\left(\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}\right)\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle J_{f_{k}}(t)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}\ \text{ for }1\leq k\leq a.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Exponential sums and the minor arcs

In this section, we establish an upper bound for
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
and
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}
on the minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
,
see Lemma
4.4
.
For this, we require for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
an upper bound for the exponential sums

(4.1)

\displaystyle S_{r}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\in\mathbb{P}\\
p_{1}\cdots p_{r}\leq X\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\alpha p_{1}\cdots p_{r})\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}\cdots n_{r}\leq X\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\alpha n_{1}\cdots n_{r})\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Lambda(n_{i}).

In the sum
S_{r}(\alpha,X)
, each
p_{j}
runs over all primes.
Thus if a natural number
n
has the form
n=p^{r}
then
n
occurs once in this sum.
On the other hand, if
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{r}
with all
p_{j}
distinct then
n
occurs
r!
times.
To simplify the notation of sums as in (
4.1
), we will work with a vector notation.
Specifically, for
1\leq s\leq r
we employ the vectorized notation

(4.2)

\displaystyle\textbf{p}_{s}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}),\quad\textbf{n}_{s}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s})\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{s}):=\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}.

Thus we can write
S_{r}(\alpha,X)
and
\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)
in (
4.1
) as

(4.3)

\displaystyle S_{r}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{p}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}\\
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r}))\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)=\mathop{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{n}_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\\
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{n}_{r})\operatorname{e}(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})).

Further, in sums (
4.3
) we drop the notation
\textbf{p}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}
and
\textbf{n}_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and just write

(4.4)

\displaystyle S_{r}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r}))\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)=\mathop{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{n}_{r})\operatorname{e}(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})).

The index of the vector
\textbf{p}_{r}
and
\textbf{n}_{r}
shows over which set we take the sum.
If a symbol with index is not bold, for instance
p_{r}
and
n_{r}
, then we sum always just over
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{N}
.

Exponential sums have been studied by many authors and bounds are well known for
S_{1}(\alpha,X)
and
\widetilde{S}_{1}(\alpha,X)
.
The bound for
S_{1}(\alpha,X)
was established by Vinogradov
[
22
]
and the proof was later simplified by Vaughan
[
18
]
.
A much more powerful version was established by the authors in
[
11
]
.
Explicitly we have the following.

Theorem 4.1

(Generalized version of Vinogradov’s lemma,
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
as well as
\Upsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}},\quad(a,q)=1.

Then for any
X\geq 2
, one has

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X\max\{1,\sqrt{\Upsilon}\}}{\sqrt{q}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{X}\sqrt{q}\bigg)(\log X)^{3},

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{1}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X\max\{1,\sqrt{\Upsilon}\}}{\sqrt{q}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{X}\sqrt{q}\bigg)(\log X)^{4}.

Note that for
\Upsilon=1
, we recover Vinogradov’s lemma.
Theorem
4.1
turns out to be very powerful for induction arguments.
In particular, the authors were able to establish in
[
11
]
to following bound
for
S_{r}(\alpha,X)
and
\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Upsilon>0
such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}},\quad(a,q)=1.

We then have for
X\geq 2
and
q\leq X

(4.6)

\displaystyle S_{r}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll(\log X)^{3}\left(Xq^{-\frac{1}{2r}}\max\{1,\Upsilon^{\frac{1}{2r}}\}+X^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+X^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}q^{\frac{1}{2r}}\right),

(4.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll(\log X)^{3+r}\left(Xq^{-\frac{1}{2r}}\max\{1,\Upsilon^{\frac{1}{2r}}\}+X^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+X^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}q^{\frac{1}{2r}}\right).

We now can use Theorem
4.2
to establish an upper bound for
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
and
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}

on the minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
(
2.8
).
Using the definition of
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
in (
2.3
) and that
\rho=e^{-1/X}
, we can write

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{\textbf{p}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}}e^{-j\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})/X}\operatorname{e}(j\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\alpha).

We have for all
\beta>0
that

\displaystyle e^{-\beta j/X}=\int_{\beta}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}dy.

Thus we can rewrite the sum infinite over primes as the following combined sum

\displaystyle\sum_{\textbf{p}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}}e^{-\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p})j/X}\operatorname{e}(j\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}\sum_{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\leq y}\operatorname{e}(j\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\alpha)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}S_{r}(j\alpha,y)dy,

with
S_{r}(\alpha,X)
is as in (
4.3
).
Since
|S_{r}(j\alpha,X)|\leq S_{r}(0,X)\ll X(\log\log X)^{r-1}
and
using Lemma
3.1
with
\gamma=j/X
, we get for
j\leq X
for the coarse but useful bound

\displaystyle\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}S_{r}(j\alpha,X)\,dy

\displaystyle\ll\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}y(\log\log y)^{r-1}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}dy\ll(\log\log X)^{r-1}\bigg(\frac{X}{j}\bigg).

Let
J\leq X
be a parameter at our disposal to be chosen later. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}S_{r}(j\alpha,X)\,dy\ll(\log\log X)^{r-1}\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\frac{X}{j}\ll\frac{X^{1+\epsilon}}{J}.

We can summarize this by saying that for any
J\leq X
we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j}\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}S_{r}(j\alpha,X)\,dy+O\bigg(\frac{X^{1+\epsilon}}{J}\bigg).

Using the definition of (
2.4
), we get, with a similar computation, that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j}\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)\,dy+O\bigg(\frac{X^{1+\epsilon}}{J}\bigg)

with
\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)
as in (
4.1
).
Note that (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) hold for all
\alpha
and not just those in the minor arcs.
Combining these two inequalities with Theorem
4.2
, we can obtain the following bounds.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
be as in (
2.8
) and
A>0
.
We then have for
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle\ll X(\log X)^{3-\frac{A}{2r}}\ \text{ and }\ \Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\ll X(\log X)^{3+r-\frac{A}{2r}}.

Proof.

The proofs for
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
and
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}
are (almost) identical and we thus give it only for
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
. Fix
J
be a parameter of our choice define the
y
-integral in (
4.8
) by

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(X,j):=\int_{2^{r}}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}S_{r}(j\alpha,y)dy.

For each
j\leq J
, we employ Dirichlet’s theorem (see
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
) to choose
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
(a,q)=1
, both maybe dependent on
j
, such that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\bigg|j\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\bigg|\leq q^{-1}X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad q<X(\log X)^{-A}.

Since
q^{-1}X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}\leq q^{-2}
, Theorem
4.2
implies

(4.13)

\displaystyle S_{r}(j\alpha,y)\ll(\log y)^{3}\left(yq^{-\frac{1}{2r}}+y^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+y^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}q^{\frac{1}{2r}}\right).

Inserting this into
\mathfrak{I}(X,j)
and using Lemma
3.1
with
\gamma=j/X
, we obtain

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(X,j)\ll\left(\log\frac{X}{j}\right)^{3}\bigg(\frac{X}{jq^{\frac{1}{2r}}}+\left(\frac{X}{j}\right)^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+\left(\frac{X}{j}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}q^{\frac{1}{2r}}\bigg),

We now have to bound the terms involving
q
.
We use for
q^{\frac{1}{2r}}
that
q<X(\log X)^{-A}
.
For the term
q^{-\frac{1}{2r}}
, we use that
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
.
First, we divide (
4.12
) by
j
and then define
a_{j}:=a/(a,j)
and
q_{j}:=jq/(a,j)
.
The definition of
\delta_{q}
in (
2.6
) then implies

(4.15)

\displaystyle\bigg|\alpha-\frac{a_{j}}{q_{j}}\bigg|\leq\delta_{q_{j}}.

Since
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
, the definition of
\mathfrak{m}
implies that
jq\geq q_{j}>Q
, where
Q=(\log X)^{A}
.
Thus

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(X,j)\ll\left(\log X\right)^{3}\bigg(\frac{X}{j^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}(\log X)^{\frac{A}{2r}}}+\left(\frac{X}{j}\right)^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+\frac{X}{j^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}(\log X)^{\frac{A}{2r}}}\bigg).

Inserting this into (
4.8
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\mathfrak{I}(X,j)}{j}\ll\,

\displaystyle(\log X)^{3}\bigg(\frac{X}{(\log X)^{\frac{A}{2r}}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j^{\frac{4r-1}{2r}}}+X^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j^{\frac{5+4r}{3+2r}}}\bigg).

Since the power of
j
in both sums is strictly larger than one we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\mathfrak{I}(X,j)}{j}+O\bigg(\frac{X^{1+\epsilon}}{J}\bigg)\ll X(\log X)^{3-\frac{A}{2r}}+X^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}(\log X)^{3}+O\bigg(\frac{X^{1+\epsilon}}{J}\bigg).

The second term is lower than the first term since
\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}<1
.
The result now follows by choosing
J=\sqrt{X}
.
∎

The inequalities in Lemma
4.3
hold for all
A>0
.
However, they are only meaningful if these upper bounds are lower than
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)
and
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho)
. Theorem
8.1
(or Corollary
7.5
) implies

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)\sim\frac{\zeta(2)rX(\log\log X)^{r-1}}{\log X}\ \text{ and }\ \Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho)\sim\frac{\zeta(2)X(\log X)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}.

Thus we require for
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
that
3-\frac{A}{2r}<-1
,
and for
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}
that
3+r-\frac{A}{2r}<r-1
.
Both inequalities are equivalent to
A>8r
, which is exactly the condition in (
2.8
).
We immediately get

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
and
A
be as in (
2.8
).
Then there exists a
\rho_{0}
such that for all
\rho\geq\rho_{0}
and all
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\leq\frac{3}{4}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho),\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{Re}(\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\leq\frac{3}{4}\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho).

5.
Pseudo-differentiable functions

The aim of this section is to provide the necessary framework to establish upper bounds for the real parts of
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}
and of
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}
on the non-principal major arcs.

5.1.
Motivation

To establish an upper bound for the real part of
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}
and of
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}
on the non-principal major arcs
\mathfrak{M}(q,a)
with
2\leq q\leq Q
, we have to study for
q,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
(q,\ell)=1
the expression
A_{r}(t;q,\ell)
and
\widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)
with

(5.1)

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\\
p_{1}\cdots p_{r}\leq t\\
p_{1}\ldots p_{r}\equiv\ell\bmod q\end{subarray}}1\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell):=\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}\cdots n_{r}\leq t\\
n_{1}\ldots n_{r}\equiv\ell\bmod q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Lambda(n_{i})

and
A_{r}(t):=A_{r}(t;1,0)
and
\widetilde{A}_{r}(t):=A_{r}(t;1,0)
.
More precisely, we have to show that we can write

(5.2)

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)=D_{r}(t)+O\left(t(\log t)^{-C}\right)\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)=\widetilde{D}_{r}(t)+O\left(t(\log t)^{-C}\right),

where
C\geq 1
is arbitrarily large,

are differentiable with respect to

Obverse that we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle A_{1}(t;q,\ell)=\pi(t;q,\ell)=\operatorname{card}\,\{p\leq t\,;\,p\equiv\ell\bmod q\}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{1}(t;q,\ell)=\psi(t;q,\ell),

where
\pi(t;q,\ell)
denotes the number of primes less than or equal to

which are congruent to

mod

and where

is the second Chebyshev function.
The Siegel-Walfisz theorem then states that for every natural number
A\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a positive constant

such that as

and
\ell,q\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies
(\ell,q)=1
and

, we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\pi(t;q,\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Li}(t)}{\varphi(q)}+O\bigg(t\exp\bigg(-\frac{C_{A}}{2}(\log t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)\bigg)\ \text{ and }

(5.5)

\displaystyle\psi(t;q,\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{\varphi(q)}+O\bigg(t\exp\bigg(-\frac{C_{A}}{2}(\log t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)\bigg),

where
\operatorname{Li}(t)
is the logarithmic integral and
\varphi(q)
is Euler’s totient function.
We do not require the Siegel-Walfisz theorem in full strength.
We will use it only in the form

(5.6)

\displaystyle\pi(t;q,\ell)=\frac{\operatorname{Li}(t)}{\varphi(q)}+O\big(t(\log t)^{-C}\big)\ \text{ and }\ \psi(t;q,\ell)=\frac{t}{\varphi(q)}+O\big(t(\log t)^{-C}\big)

In particular, the implicit constant in the

.
Thus (

.
Further, we have for

Theorem 5.1

(

)
.

We have as

that

\displaystyle A_{2}(t;q,\ell)=\,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}(\log\log u+M+O((\log u)^{-C})\left(\operatorname{Li}(t/u)-\frac{t}{u\log(t/u)}\right)du

(5.7)

\displaystyle-\frac{\operatorname{Li}^{2}(\sqrt{t})}{\varphi(q)}+\frac{2\sqrt{t}}{\varphi(q)}\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})\left({\log\log t-\log 2+M}\right)+O\left(t(\log t)^{-C}\right),

where
C>0
is arbitrarily large.

Theorem

implies, with a small computation, that (

[

.

Unfortunately, explicit expressions like (

and are also quite complicated.
Also, the argument to obtain the Siegel-Walfisz theorem does not seem to work in this situation. We are thus using induction over
r
.
So we need an approach that is compatible with an induction argument and that can also be implemented.
If we analyze the proof carefully in the cases
r=1
and

and

and

5.2.
Definition of pseudo-differentiable functions

We have to consider functions, where the leading term has the form
\frac{tP_{a}(\log\log t)}{(\log t)^{b}}
with
P_{a}
a polynomial, but the function itself is not a rational function in
\log t
and
\log\log t
.
An example is the logarithmic integral
\operatorname{Li}
.
Furthermore, we are dealing with functions that are approximately smooth, but all derivatives have a small, non-differentiable error term. For this type of functions we introduce the following class.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
g
be a smooth function on
[2,\infty]
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then a function
f
on
[2,\infty]
is called
N
times pseudo-differentiable with respect to
g
if there exists a sequence
\{f^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{N}
of
C^{1}
-functions such that as
t\to\infty

(5.8)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=f^{(0)}(t)+O\left(g(t)\right),

and for all
0\leq n\leq N-1

(5.9)

\displaystyle(f^{(n)})^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=f^{(n+1)}(t)+O(g^{(n+1)}(t)),

where
(f^{(n)})^{\prime}(t)
is the derivative of
f^{(n)}(t)
and
g^{(n)}(t)
is the
n
th derivative of
g
.
We will call
f^{(n)}
the
n
th pseudo-derivative of
f
with respect to
g
.
If a function
f
is infinitely often pseudo-differentiable with respect to
g

then we call the function strange with respect to
g
.

The implicit constant in
O(\cdot)
in (
5.9
) may depend on
n
.
To simplify the notation, we will omit ‘
with respect to
g
’ when the function
g
is clear from the context.
Note that the sequence
\{f^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is in general not unique.
However, if
f
is a smooth function, then
f
is automatically strange with respect to any function
g
and we always choose the
n
-th derivative of
f
for
f^{(n)}
in this case.
Furthermore, we will only work with functions on
[2,\infty]
.
Therefore, we will no longer state this and assume that the reader is aware of that.

In this paper, we are mainly interested in strange functions with respect to

t(\log t)^{-C}
with
C\geq 1
.
Lemma
6.3
(or a direct computation) shows that the derivatives of
t(\log t)^{-C}
are

(5.10)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{(\log t)^{C}},\ \text{ and }\ \left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right)^{(n)}\asymp\frac{1}{t^{n-1}(\log t)^{C+1}}\ \text{ for }n\geq 2,

where
f\asymp g
denotes that there exist constants
M\geq m>0
such that

\displaystyle mf(t)\leq g(t)\leq Mf(t).

Examples of strange functions with respect to
t(\log t)^{-C}
are

(5.11)

\displaystyle\pi(t;q,a)\ \text{ and }\ \int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u;q,a)\frac{t\operatorname{Li}(t/u)}{u^{2}}\,du.

The Siegel-Walfisz theorem immediately gives that
\pi(t;q,a)
is strange.
The second one requires more work, see Lemma
6.9
.

The main purpose of introducing pseudo-differential functions is to establish an asymptotic expression for
A_{r}(t)
similar to the expressions for
A_{1}(t)
and
A_{2}(t)
.
A natural question at this point is: how many pseudo-derivatives do we need?
To determine the behavior of
A_{r}(t)
for
r\geq 3
, we use induction over
r
.
In particular, we use the Abel summation formula to give in Lemma
6.1
an expression for
A_{r}(t)
, which requires the first pseudo-derivative of
A_{r-1}(t)
.
This implies that we need
r-2
pseudo-derivatives of
A_{2}(t)
to be able to determine the asymptotic behavior of
A_{r}(t)
.
Since
r\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary, we require that
A_{2}(t)
be strange.
Explicitly, we will show

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
r\geq 1
be given.
Further, as
t\to\infty
, assume that
\ell
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\leq(\log t)^{A}
for some

and
(q,\ell)=1

Then the function

is strange with respect to
t(\log t)^{-C}
for all
C\geq C_{0}

for some
C_{0}=C_{0}(r)>0
.
Further,

(5.12)

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)=A_{r}^{(0)}(t;q)+O(t(\log t)^{-C}),

where

is independent of
\ell
and the implicit constant in

can be chosen independent of
q
and
\ell
. Furthermore, we have for
n=0
and

\displaystyle A_{r}^{(n)}(t;q)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{\varphi(q)}\frac{t^{1-n}(\log\log t)^{r-1}}{\log t}+O\left(\frac{t^{1-n}(\log\log q)(\log\log t)^{r-2}}{\varphi(q)\log t}\right),

and for

\displaystyle A^{(n)}_{r}(t;q)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{r(n-2)!}{\varphi(q)}\frac{(\log\log t)^{r-1}}{t^{n-1}(\log t)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log q)(\log\log t)^{r-2}}{\varphi(q)t^{n-1}(\log t)^{2}}\right).

We have in Theorem

The expressions in (

We expect for

and

\displaystyle A_{r}^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{1-n}}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{P_{r,n,k}(\log\log t)}{(\log t)^{1+k}}+O\left(t^{1-n}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a-1}}{(\log t)^{2+K}}\right)

\displaystyle A_{r}^{(n)}(t)

5.3.
Basic properties of pseudo-differential functions

Pseudo-differential functions share many properties with smooth functions, but require usually some additional assumptions.
Examples are the chain rule and product rule.

Lemma 5.5

(Chain rule for strange functions)
.

Let
\varphi:[2,\infty]\to[2,\infty]
a smooth function
with
\varphi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
and let
f
be strange with respect to
g
.
Additionally, suppose that for any
n\geq k\geq 1
and any sequence
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n-k+1}
of non-negative integers satisfying

(5.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-k+1}m_{j}=k\ \text{ and }\ \sum_{j=1}^{n-k+1}jm_{j}=n,

the following condition holds:

(5.21)

\displaystyle g^{(k)}(\varphi(t))\prod_{j=1}^{n-k+1}(\varphi^{(j)}(t))^{m_{j}}=O(g(\varphi(t))^{(n)}).

Then
f(\varphi)
is strange with respect to
g(\varphi)
.
Furthermore,

(5.22)

\displaystyle\big(f(\varphi(t))\big)^{(1)}=f^{(1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)

and for
n\in\mathbb{N}

(5.23)

\displaystyle\big(f(\varphi(t))\big)^{(n)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f^{(k)}(\varphi(t))B_{n,k}\big(\varphi^{(1)}(t),\varphi^{(2)}(t),\ldots,\varphi^{(n-k+1)}(t)\big),

where
B_{n,k}
are the Bell polynomials.

Thus we see that the chain rule(s) for strange functions in (
5.22
) and (
5.23
) have the same form as for smooth functions.
Recall that the Bell polynomials are given by

\displaystyle B_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1})=\sum\frac{n!}{m_{1}!m_{2}!\cdots m_{n-k+1}!}\prod_{j=1}^{n-k+1}\left(\frac{x_{j}}{j!}\right)^{m_{j}},

where the sum is taken over all sequences
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n-k+1}
fulfilling (
5.20
).
In particular, we have

(5.24)

\displaystyle B_{n,1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{n}\ \text{ and }\ B_{n,n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=B_{n,n}(x_{1})=x_{1}^{n}.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction. We start with
n=1
.
By assumption, we have

\displaystyle f(t)=f^{(0)}(t)+O\left(g(t)\right).

Since
\varphi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, we immediately get

\displaystyle f(\varphi(t))=f^{(0)}(\varphi(t))+O\left(g(\varphi(t))\right).

Since
f^{(0)}
is
C^{1}
, the chain rule and that
f
is strange imply

\displaystyle\big(f^{(0)}(\varphi(t))\big)^{\prime}=(f^{(0)})^{\prime}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\big(f^{(1)}(\varphi(t))+O(g^{(1)}(\varphi(t)))\big)\varphi^{\prime}(t)

(5.25)

\displaystyle=f^{(1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)+O\big(\big(g(\varphi(t))\big)^{\prime}\big).

This implies that (
5.23
) holds for
n=1
.
Suppose now (
5.23
) holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
f
is strange, the chain rule gives for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
with the same computation as in (
5.25
) that

(5.26)

\displaystyle\big(f^{(k)}(\varphi(t))\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=f^{(k+1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)+O\big(g^{(k+1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)\big).

Thus applying the product to (
5.23
) and inserting (
5.26
) gives

\displaystyle\big(\big(f(\varphi(t))\big)^{(n)}\big)^{\prime}=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}f^{(k+1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)B_{n,k}\big(\varphi^{(1)}(t),\varphi^{(2)}(t),\ldots,\varphi^{(n-k+1)}(t)\big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n}f^{(k)}(\varphi(t))\big(B_{n,k}\big(\varphi^{(1)}(t),\varphi^{(2)}(t),\ldots,\varphi^{(n-k+1)}(t)\big)\big)^{\prime}

(5.27)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n}O(g^{(k+1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t))B_{n,k}\big(\varphi^{(1)}(t),\varphi^{(2)}(t),\ldots,\varphi^{(n-k+1)}(t)\big).

The first two lines have exactly the same form as for the ordinary chain rule.
By leveraging the combinatorial properties of the Bell polynomials, we are able to combine these two lines and obtain equation (
5.23
) with
n+1
.
Finally, assumption (
5.21
) and the definition of the Bell polynomials show that

\displaystyle g^{(k+1)}(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)B_{n,k}\big(\varphi^{(1)}(t),\varphi^{(2)}(t),\ldots,\varphi^{(n-k+1)}(t)\big)\ll g(\varphi(t))^{(n+1)}.

Inserting this into (
5.27
) completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.6

(Product rule for strange functions)
.

Let
f
be a strange function with respect to
g
. Further, let
h
and
m
be a smooth functions such that for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(5.28)

\displaystyle g^{(k)}(t)h^{(\ell)}(t)\ll m^{(k+\ell)}(t).

Then
fh
is strange with respect to the function
m
and

(5.29)

\displaystyle(fh)^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}f^{(k)}(t)h^{(n-k)}(t).

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction.
We have

(5.30)

\displaystyle f(t)h(t)=\big(f^{(0)}(t)+O(g(t))\big)h(t)=f^{(0)}(t)h(t)+O(g(t)h(t)).

Since
f^{(0)}(t)
is differentiable by assumption,
f^{(0)}(t)h(t)
is also differentiable.
Thus
f(t)h(t)
is
0
times pseudo-differentiable and (
5.29
) hold for
n=0
.

Suppose that
f(t)h(t)
is
n
times pseudo-differentiable and
(
5.29
) holds
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then
(fh)^{(n)}(t)
is differentiable and the induction assumption implies

\displaystyle\big((fh)^{(n)}\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}f^{(k)}h^{(n-k)}\bigg)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left((f^{(k)})^{\prime}h^{(n-k)}+f^{(k)}h^{(n-k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(f^{(k+1)}h^{(n-k)}+f^{(k)}h^{(n-k+1)}+O(g^{(k+1)}h^{(n-k)})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}f^{(k)}h^{(n+1-k)}+O(m^{(n+1)}),

where we used on the last line assumption (
5.28
).
This completes the proof.
∎

In many cases it is possible to choose
m=gh
in Lemma
5.6
.
However, there are examples where we cannot choose
m=gh
.
One example, which will appear in our calculations, is
g(t)=h(t)=\sqrt{t}(\log t)^{-1}
.
We have in this case

\displaystyle g^{(1)}(t)h^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4t(\log t)^{2}}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{t(\log t)^{3}}\bigg),

(5.31)

\displaystyle\big(g(t)h(t)\big)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{t(\log t)^{3}}+\frac{24}{t(\log t)^{4}}.

Thus
g^{(1)}(t)h^{(1)}(t)\neq O\big((g(t)h(t))^{(2)}\big)
.
In this situation, we can choose
m(t)=t(\log t)^{-1}
.

6.

Theorems

We therefore only give the full details for the proof of the Theorem

The proof is a bit long and thus we will break it down into several smaller parts.
In the first step, in Section

Finally, in Section

6.1.
Recurrence expression for

and

To simplify the notation of sums as in (

),
we will work with a vectorized notation.
We write for

\displaystyle\textbf{p}_{s}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}),\quad\textbf{n}_{s}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}),\quad\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{s}):=\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_{i},\ \text{ and }\ \Lambda(\textbf{n}_{s}):=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\Lambda(n_{i}).

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{p}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}\\
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\equiv\ell\bmod q\}}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)=\mathop{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{n}_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\\
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{n}_{r})\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\equiv\ell\bmod q\}},

where
\mathds{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
is the indicator function.
Further, we drop the

and

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{J}\equiv\ell\bmod q\}}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)=\mathop{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\leq t\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{n}_{r})\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\equiv\ell\bmod q\}}.

The index of the vector
\textbf{p}_{r}
and
\textbf{n}_{r}
shows over which set we take the sum.
If a symbol with index is not bold, for instance
p_{r}
and
n_{r}
, then we sum always just over
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{N}
.

We begin with the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

\displaystyle A_{r}(t;q,\ell)=

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})A^{(0)}_{r-1}(\sqrt{t})}{\varphi(q)}-\sum_{p|q}A_{r-1}^{(0)}(t/p;q)-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\\
(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1}),q)>1\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Li}\left(\frac{t}{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u)\frac{tA_{r-1}^{(1)}(t/u;q)}{u^{2}}\,du+\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{tA_{r-1}(u)}{u^{2}\log(t/u)}\,du+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right),

where the implicit constant in

can be chosen independently of
q
and
\ell
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
r\geq 2
and suppose that Theorem
5.4
holds for all

.
Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{r}(t;q,\ell)=

\displaystyle+\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\psi(u)\frac{t\widetilde{A}_{r-1}^{(1)}(t/u;q)}{u^{2}}\,du+\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{t\widetilde{A}_{r-1}(u)}{u^{2}}\,du+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right),

Proof of Lemma

.

.

Observe that

\displaystyle p_{1}\cdots p_{r}\equiv\ell\bmod{q}\ \Longrightarrow(p_{1}\cdots p_{r},q)=1.

(6.7)

\displaystyle A_{r,3}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\leq t\\
p_{r}\leq\sqrt{t},\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\equiv\ell\bmod q\}}.

We first look at

.
Equation (

) implies that only those

.
Denote by

.
Then

(p_{r},q)=1\end{subarray}}A_{r-1}(\sqrt{t};q,\overline{p_{r}}\ell).

By assumption,
A_{r-1}(\sqrt{t};q,\overline{p_{r}}\ell)
is strange.
Further, we can write

\displaystyle A_{r-1}(t;q,\overline{p_{r}}\ell)=A_{r-1}^{(0)}(t;q)+O(\sqrt{t}(\log\sqrt{t})^{-C-1}),

where

\overline{p_{r}}\ell
.
The prime number theorem, (

\displaystyle=\left(A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t},q)+O\left(\sqrt{t}(\log\sqrt{t})^{-C}\right)\right)\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{r}\leq\sqrt{t}\\
(p_{r},q)=1\end{subarray}}1\Big)

\displaystyle=\left(A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t},q)+O\left(\sqrt{t}(\log\sqrt{t})^{-C}\right)\right)\left(\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})+O(\sqrt{t}(\log\sqrt{t})^{-C})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t},q)+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log\sqrt{t})^{C+1}}\right).

Next, we look at
A_{r,1}(t)
.
Using the definition and assumptions on

, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{r,1}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{r}\leq\sqrt{t}\\

(p_{r},q)=1\end{subarray}}\left(A_{r-1}^{(0)}(t/p_{r};q)+O\left(\frac{(t/p_{r})}{(\log(t/p_{r}))^{C+1}}\right)\right).

As above

.

Inserting this estimate in the above expression for

\displaystyle A_{r,1}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{t}}A_{r-1}^{(0)}(t/p;q)-\sum_{p|q}A_{r-1}^{(0)}(t/p;q)+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right).

Only the first sum in (

Since

is a differentiable function, we apply Abel’s summation formula.
We use the sequence

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{t}}A_{r-1}^{(0)}(t/p;q)

\displaystyle=\pi(\sqrt{t})A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t};q)+\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u)\frac{t(A_{r-1}^{(0)})^{\prime}(t/u;q)}{u^{2}}\,du.

Equation (

) and the prime number theorem imply

(6.12)

\displaystyle\pi(\sqrt{t})A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t};q)=\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t};q)+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right).

Inserting that

is strange into the integral in (

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u)\frac{t(A_{r-1}^{(0)})^{\prime}(t/u;q)}{u^{2}}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u)\frac{tA_{r-1}^{(1)}(t/u;q)}{u^{2}}\,du+O\bigg(\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{t\pi(u)}{u^{2}(\log(t/u))^{C+1}}\,du\bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u)\frac{tA_{r-1}^{(1)}(t/u;q)}{u^{2}}\,du+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right).

Combining everything, i.e. inserting (

) and (

) into (

(6.14)

\displaystyle A_{r,1}(t)=\,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\sqrt{t};q)-\sum_{p|q}A_{r-1}^{(0)}(\tfrac{t}{q};q)+\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\pi(u)\frac{tA_{r-1}^{(1)}(\tfrac{t}{u};q)}{u^{2}}\,du+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{C}}\right).

It remains to look at
A_{r,2}(t)
.
We denote by
\overline{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}
the multiplicative inverse of
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})
modulo
q
.
Using the definition of
\pi(t;q,\ell)
and the Siegel-Walfisz theorem, we get

\displaystyle A_{r,2}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\\
(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1}),q)=1\end{subarray}}\bigg(\sum_{p_{r}\leq\sqrt{t}}\mathds{1}_{\{p_{r}\equiv\overline{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}\ell\bmod q\}}\bigg)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\\
(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1}),q)=1\end{subarray}}\pi(\sqrt{t};q,\overline{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\\
(\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1}),q)=1\end{subarray}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\operatorname{Li}\left(\frac{t}{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}\right)+O\bigg(\frac{t}{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\big(\log(t/\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1}))\big)^{C+1}}\bigg)\bigg).

Since

, we have
\log(t/\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1}))\geq\frac{1}{2}\log t

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\\

\displaystyle A_{r,2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Li}\left(\frac{t}{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}\right)-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\\

\displaystyle b_{n}:=\operatorname{card}\{(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r-1})\,;\,\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})=n\}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})\leq\sqrt{t}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Li}\left(\frac{t}{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r-1})}\right)

Inserting (
6.17

,

and

∎

6.2.
Derivatives of some smooth functions

In this section we study the properties of functions involving
t^{r}(\log\log t)^{a}(\log t)^{-b}
and their derivatives.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}
be given and
P_{a}(x)
be a polynomial of degree
a
.
Further, let

(6.19)

\displaystyle D(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{tP_{a}(\log\log t)}{(\log t)^{b}}.

Then, as
t\to\infty
,

(6.20)

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{a}(\log\log t)}{(\log t)^{b}}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b+1}}\right),

and for
n\geq 2

(6.21)

\displaystyle D^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-2)!(-1)^{n}(-bP_{a}+P_{a}^{\prime})(\log\log t)}{t^{n-1}(\log t)^{b+1}}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{t^{n-1}(\log t)^{b+2}}\right).

Proof.

If
a=b=0
then
D_{t}(t)=t
and the lemma clearly holds in this case.
We thus can assume that
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
.
We have for
r\in\mathbb{R}

(6.22)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{t^{r}(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}=\frac{t^{r-1}(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}\big(r-b(\log t)^{-1}+a(\log(t)\log\log t)^{-1}\big).

Therefore the leading term in (
6.22
) is different in the cases
r=0
and
r\neq 0
.
Inserting
r=1
into (
6.22
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{t(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}=\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}\big(1-b(\log t)^{-1}+a(\log(t)\log\log t)^{-1}\big).

This completes the proof of (
6.20
).
We next look at (
6.21
) in the case
n=2
.
Inserting
r=0
in (
6.22
) gives

(6.23)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b}}=t^{-1}\frac{(\log\log t)^{a}}{(\log t)^{b+1}}\big(-b+a(\log\log t)^{-1}\big).

The cases
n\geq 3
then follow by induction.
∎

Furthermore, we need

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
D(t)
be as in Lemma
6.3
.
We then have for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

(6.24)

\displaystyle\big(D(\sqrt{t})\big)^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-n)}\frac{P_{a}(\log\log\sqrt{t})}{t^{n-1/2}(\log\sqrt{t})^{b}}\left(1+O\left((\log t)^{-1}\right)\right),

(6.25)

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t}))^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-n)}\frac{1}{t^{n-1/2}\log\sqrt{t}}\left(1+O\left((\log t)^{-1}\right)\right),

(6.26)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{(\log\sqrt{t})^{C}}\right)^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-n)}\frac{1}{t^{n-1/2}(\log\sqrt{t})^{C}}\left(1+O\left((\log t)^{-1}\right)\right).

Proof.

For the proof of (
6.24
), (
6.25
) and (
6.26
) we require

(6.27)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{t^{r+\frac{1}{2}}(\log\log\sqrt{t})^{a}}{(\log\sqrt{t})^{b}}=\frac{t^{r-\frac{1}{2}}(\log\log\sqrt{t})^{a}}{(\log\sqrt{t})^{b}}\Big(r+\frac{1}{2}+O\left((\log t)^{-1}\right)\Big)

and the observation

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)\cdot\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\ldots.

The proof of the remaining formulas follows by a combination of induction with the equations (
6.24
), (
6.25
) and (
6.26
).
This computation is straightforward and we thus omit the details.
∎

An immediate consequence of this lemma is the identity

(6.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}-k\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}-(n-k)\right)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }n=0\text{ or }n=1,\\
0&n\geq 2.\end{cases}

Indeed, we have

(6.29)

\displaystyle\frac{4t}{(\log t)^{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\log\sqrt{t}}\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\log\sqrt{t}}.

Computing the
n
th derivative and inserting (
6.26
) immediately gives (
6.28
).

The purpose of this subsection is to study the functions occurring in Lemma

Our objective is twofold: first, to demonstrate that all of these functions are strange, and second, to establish necessary asymptotics for them.
All strange functions in this subsection originate from

or

We therefore assume for the rest of this section that

is a strange function with respect to

Further, we assume there exists

and for each

of degree

\displaystyle D(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{tP_{a,0}(\log\log t)}{(\log t)^{b}}+O\left(\frac{t(\log\log t)^{a-1}}{(\log t)^{b+1}}\right),

\displaystyle D^{(n)}(t)

Lemma 6.5

Let

is strange with respect to
\sqrt{t}(\log\sqrt{t})^{-C}

Further, we have for all

\displaystyle\big(D(\sqrt{t})\big)^{(n)}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-n)}\frac{P_{a,1}(\log\log\sqrt{t})}{(\log\sqrt{t})^{b}}t^{\frac{1}{2}-n}+O\bigg(\frac{(\log\log t)^{a-1}}{t^{\frac{n-1}{2}}(\log\sqrt{t})^{b+1}}\bigg).

Proof.

To show that
D(\sqrt{t})
is strange, we apply the chain rule with

and
\varphi(t)=\sqrt{t}
.
Let
n\geq k\geq 1

Observe that for

\displaystyle(t^{\frac{1}{2}})^{(j)}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-j)}t^{\frac{1}{2}-j},\ \text{ and }\ g^{(k)}(\varphi(t))\ll\begin{cases}(\log t)^{-C}&\text{if }k=1,\\
t^{\frac{1-k}{2}}(\log t)^{-C-1}&\text{if }k\geq 2.\end{cases}

Since
k=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k+1}m_{j}
and
n=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k+1}jm_{j}
, we get

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n-k+1}(\varphi^{(j)}(t))^{m_{j}}

\displaystyle\ll\prod_{j=1}^{n-k+1}(t^{\frac{1}{2}-j})^{m_{j}}=t^{\frac{k}{2}-n}.

Combining these computations gives

(6.35)

\displaystyle g^{(k)}(\varphi(t))\prod_{j=1}^{n-k+1}(\varphi^{(j)}(t))^{m_{j}}\ll\begin{cases}t^{\frac{1}{2}-n}(\log t)^{-C}&\text{if }k=1,\\
t^{\frac{1}{2}-n}(\log t)^{-C-1}&\text{if }k\geq 2.\end{cases}

Furthermore, (
6.26
) gives

Lemma 7.1
.

Let

with

Moreover let

and
(\ell,q)=1

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\equiv\ell\bmod q}\exp(-\gamma\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})),

(7.2)

where

is the Euler totient function.

Proof.

We begin with

.
First, we apply Abel’s summation to

For this define

(7.3)

\displaystyle a_{n}=\operatorname{card}\{\textbf{p}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{r}\,:\,n=\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})\ \text{ and }\ n\equiv\ell\operatorname{mod}q\}.

Then

as in (

.
Then (

and thus

\displaystyle U_{r}(\gamma,\ell,q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}f(n)

\displaystyle=A_{r}(t;q,\ell)f(t)\big|_{t=2}^{\infty}-\int_{2}^{\infty}A_{r}(t;q,\ell)f^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}A_{r}(t;q,\ell)\exp(-\gamma t)dt.

Inserting that

is strange by Theorem

, we see that

\displaystyle U_{r}(\gamma,\ell,q)=\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}A_{r}^{(0)}(t;q)\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}t(\log t)^{-C}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{2\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2A+2}}\ll\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}.

This then implies that

\displaystyle U_{r}(\gamma,\ell,q)=\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}A_{r}^{(0)}(t;q)\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}\right).

give

\displaystyle\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}A_{r}^{(0)}(t;q)\exp(-\gamma t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{\infty}(A_{r}^{(0)}(t;q))^{\prime}\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O(1)

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{\infty}A_{r}^{(1)}(t;q)\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\int_{2}^{\infty}(\log t)^{-C}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r}{\varphi(q)}\frac{(\log\log t)^{r-1}}{\log t}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log q)(\log\log t)^{r-2}}{\varphi(q)\log t}\right)\right)e^{-\gamma t}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{\varphi(q)}\frac{(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{r-1}}{\gamma\log(1/\gamma_{1})}+O\left(\frac{(\log\log q)(\log\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{r-2}}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\right).

Combining everything finally completes the proof.

The computations for

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{n}=\operatorname{card}\{\textbf{n}_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\,:\,n=\mathcal{J}(\textbf{n}_{r})\ \text{ and }\ n\equiv\ell\operatorname{mod}q\}

Lemma 7.3

\displaystyle S_{2}

\mathcal{J}(\textbf{p}_{r})=\ell\operatorname{mod}q_{j}

We look next at the inner sum of

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\sqrt{X}}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\

8.1.
The method of contour integration

In Corollary

we have determined the asymptotic behaviour of

and

and

and

We obtain this via contour integration.
We begin with
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}
. We have

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
\rho=e^{-1/X}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exists a polynomial
P_{r}
of degree
r-1
and leading coefficient

such that we have for all any

and as
\rho\to 1^{-}

(8.1)

\displaystyle\bigg(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}P_{r}(\log\log X)}{\log X}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log X}\bigg)\bigg),

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}^{(m)}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}P_{r}(\log\log X)}{\log X}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log X}\bigg)\bigg).

We can explicitly compute the polynomial
P_{r}
.
The proof of Theorem
8.1
below gives

(8.3)

\displaystyle P_{r}(y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{r}\binom{r}{n}(-1)^{r-n}(\mathcal{D}(1))^{r-n}\sum_{h=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{h}\binom{n}{h}\binom{h}{k}(-1)^{k}\operatorname{Im}[(i\pi)^{n-h}]y^{h-k}\Gamma^{(k)}(1),

where

(8.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(s):=\log\big((s-1)\zeta(s)\big)-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(s)\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(s)=\sum_{j\geq 2}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{p^{js}}.

Here
\log
denotes the principal branch of the logarithm and
\log((s-1)\zeta(s))
is holomorphic in the standard zero-free region of
\zeta
.
Furthermore, for any
\delta>0
we have that
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(s)
converges absolutely and uniformly for
\real(s)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\delta
.
This shows that the expression

is well-defined.

First we prove an auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
0<a\leq 1
be given and
F:[0,a]\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function.
We then have for all
h\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

(8.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}X^{-u}(-\log u)^{h}F(u)\,du=\frac{F(0)}{\log X}\sum_{k=0}^{h}\binom{h}{k}(\log\log X)^{h-k}(-1)^{k}\Gamma^{(k)}(1)+O\left(\frac{(\log\log X)^{h}}{(\log X)^{2}}\right),

where
\Gamma^{(n)}
is the
n
th derivative of the Gamma function.

Proof.

Expanding
F
around
0
and inserting it into the integral give

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}X^{-u}(-\log u)^{h}F(u)\,du

\displaystyle=F(0)\int_{0}^{a}X^{-u}(-\log u)^{h}\,du+O\bigg(\int_{0}^{a}X^{-u}u(\log u)^{h}\,du\bigg).

We first look at the main term.
The variable substitution
t=u\log X
gives

\displaystyle F(0)\int_{0}^{a}X^{-u}(-\log u)^{h}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{F(0)}{\log X}\int_{0}^{a\log X}e^{-t}\left(-\log\left(\frac{t}{\log X}\right)\right)^{h}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{F(0)}{\log X}\sum_{k=0}^{h}\binom{h}{k}(\log\log X)^{h-k}(-1)^{k}\int_{0}^{a\log X}e^{-t}(\log t)^{k}\,dt.

Further

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a\log X}e^{-t}(\log t)^{k}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}(\log t)^{k}\,dt-\int_{a\log X}^{\infty}e^{-t}(\log t)^{k}\,dt=\Gamma^{(k)}(1)+O(X^{-a/2}).

Similarly, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}X^{-u}u(\log u)^{h}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\log X)^{2}}\int_{0}^{a\log X}te^{-t}\left(\log\left(\frac{t}{\log X}\right)\right)^{h}\,dt

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(\log\log X)^{h}}{(\log X)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}te^{-t}(\log t)^{h}\,dt\ll\frac{(\log\log X)^{h}}{(\log X)^{2}}.

Combining everything completes the proof.
∎

We only used one derivative of
F
in this proof.
By using more derivatives of

, we can get more terms in the expansion (

).
With this we can actually get more terms in the expansion in the Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

As before, we use here also the vector notation of sums, see (

).
Using the definition of

The Cahen-Mellin formula allows us to write the above expression as the following complex integral

Now we split the singular part of the prime zeta function by writing

\log

\displaystyle(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s))^{r}=\sum_{n=0}^{r}\binom{r}{n}(-\log(s-1))^{n}(\mathcal{D}(s))^{r-n}

\displaystyle\bigg(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)

The task is therefore to calculate the contribution of the binomial components.
A typical term in (

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(-\frac{1}{2})}X^{s}\zeta(s+1)\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{r}\Gamma(s+m)ds.

A direct estimate then gives (

).
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle X^{s+m}=X^{m+1}e^{(s-1)\log X}=X^{m+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\log X)^{\ell}}{k!}(s-1)^{\ell}.

Inserting this expression into the computation of the residue at
s=1
gives the main term in (
8.20
).
It remains to show that the sum over the non-trivial zeros is of lower order. However, this computation is straightforward and we thus omit the details.
∎

Corollary 8.5
.

We have

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=1,\quad\widetilde{P}_{2}(y)=y+\bigg(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(2)}{\zeta(2)}-3\gamma\bigg),

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{3}(y)

8.3.
The saddle point method and proofs of main theorems

We are now in a position to conclude the proof of the main theorems of Section
1
.

Theorem 8.8
.

Let
\Psi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(z)
and
\Psi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(z)
be as in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).
Further, let
\rho
and
\widetilde{\rho}
be the solutions of the equations

\displaystyle n=\rho\,\Phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)\ \text{ and }\ n=\widetilde{\rho}\,\Phi^{\prime}_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\widetilde{\rho}).

We then have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)=\frac{\rho^{-n}\Psi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)}{(2\pi\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r},(2)}(\rho))^{\frac{1}{2}}}(1+O(n^{-\frac{1}{6}}))\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{p}_{\Lambda^{*r}}(n)=\frac{(\widetilde{\rho})^{-n}\Psi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\widetilde{\rho})}{(2\pi\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r},(2)}(\widetilde{\rho}))^{\frac{1}{2}}}(1+O(n^{-\frac{1}{6}}))

as
n\to\infty
with
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r},(2)}(\rho)=(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho})^{2}\,\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)
and
\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r},(2)}(\rho)=(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho})^{2}\,\Phi_{\Lambda^{*r}}(\rho)
.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem uses the saddle-point method, which is fairly standard and well-known.
We will therefore only give a brief overview and only for
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)
.
The reader can find a complete analogous proof with all the details in
[
9
, Section 7]
.

Recall that
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)
is given by the integral

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)=\rho^{-n}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Psi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha)d\alpha=\rho^{-n}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\big(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha))d\alpha.

Recall, we have defined
\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
in (
2.8
) as

\mathfrak{M}(1,0)=\{\alpha\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}];|\alpha|\leq X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}\},

with
A>8r
arbitrary, but fixed.
The first step is to show that the integral over
[-\pi,\pi]\setminus\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
does not contribute to the main term.
Combining Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
7.6
immediately get

(8.45)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\leq\frac{3}{4}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)

for
\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]\setminus\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
.
Inserting this it the integral immediately gives

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\setminus\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}\exp(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha)d\alpha\bigg|\ll n^{-B}\exp\left(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)\right),

where
B\geq 1
can be chosen arbitrarily large.
It thus remains to consider the integral over
\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
.
We now spilt
\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
into the three regions

\displaystyle I_{1}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(1,0);\,|\alpha|\leq\eta\},\ I_{2}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(1,0);\,\eta<|\alpha|\leq\beta\}\ \text{ and }\ I_{3}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(1,0);\,\beta<|\alpha|\}

with
\eta=X^{-17/12}
and
\beta=(9\pi X)^{-1}
.
The next step is to show that the integrals over
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
are of lower order.
Using that
(1+r)^{-1}\leq 1-2r
for
0\leq r\leq 1/2
, we obtain for
\alpha\in I_{3}
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\alpha X}\right)=\frac{1}{1+4\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}X^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1+4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}X^{2}}\leq 1-2\pi^{2}\beta^{2}X^{2}=\frac{79}{81}.

Combining this with Corollary
7.5
, we get for
X
large enough and all
\alpha\in I_{3}
that

\operatorname{Re}\big(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)\leq\frac{80}{81}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)

As above, this shows that the integral over
I_{3}
is also of lower order.
For our next task we look at the integral over
I_{2}
.
Here we use the Taylor approximation of
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))
since it is more precise in the central region than Corollary
7.5
.
We have

(8.46)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)+2\pi i\alpha a_{n}-2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}b_{n}+4\pi^{3}R(\rho,\alpha)

with
a_{n}:=\rho\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}^{\prime}(\rho)
,
b_{n}:=\rho\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}^{\prime}(\rho)+\rho^{2}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)
and

\max\{|\real R(\rho,\alpha)|,|\operatorname{Im}R(\rho,\alpha)|\}\leq|\alpha|^{3}c_{n},

where
c_{n}:=\rho\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}^{\prime}(\rho)+\rho^{2}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)+\rho^{3}\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)
.
Theorem
8.1
implies that

\displaystyle b_{n}\sim 2\frac{\zeta(2)rX^{3}(\log\log X)^{r-1}}{\log X}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{n}\sim 6\frac{\zeta(2)rX^{4}(\log\log X)^{r-1}}{\log X}.

Let
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{5}
be arbitrary. Since
\alpha\in I_{2}
(and thus
X^{-17/12}\leq|\alpha|\leq(9\pi X)^{-1}
), we get for
X
large enough that

\displaystyle\real(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)-2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}b_{n}+4\pi^{3}|\alpha|^{3}c_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)-2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}\frac{\zeta(2)rX^{3}(\log\log X)^{r-1}}{\log X}\big((2-\epsilon)-(12+\epsilon)\pi|\alpha|X\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)-\frac{(6-10\epsilon)2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}{9}\frac{\zeta(2)rX^{3}(\log\log X)^{r-1}}{\log X}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{{\mathbb{P}_{r}}}(\rho)-\frac{8\pi^{2}X^{1/6}}{9}\frac{\zeta(2)r(\log\log X)^{r-1}}{\log X}.

Equation (
8.30
) implies that
X^{1/6}\asymp(\frac{n\log n}{\log\log n})^{1/12}
.
This implies that for
n
large

\displaystyle\left|\int_{I_{2}}\exp(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha)d\alpha\right|\leq\exp(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho)-n^{-1/13})\ll n^{-B}\exp(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho))

for any constant
B\geq 1
.
The last step is to show that the integral over
I_{1}
gives the main term.
The main idea is to approximate the integrand by a Gaussian.
These arguments are completely standard and straightforward and we thus omit the details.
∎

9.
Conclusion and future work

A natural question to ask is how to find partitions with respect to powerful
r
-primes, i.e.
m=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}
.
This will necessitate three main ingredients. For the principal major arcs, we will need to deal with integrands of the form
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\alpha_{1}(s-m))\cdots\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\alpha_{r}(s-m))
in (
8.8
). In addition, for the minor arcs we will need to bound exponential sums of the form (
1.4
) with
f(n)
being the characteristic function of prescribed powerful
r
-primes. Lastly, for the non-principal major arcs the technique of pseudo-differentiable and strange functions discussed in Section
5
will have to be enlarged to accommodate this new extension. This will be the subject of future research.
