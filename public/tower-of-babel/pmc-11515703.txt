Seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among healthy blood donors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Joyeuse Ukwishaka, Cyril Fotabong Mela, Jerry Brown Njoh Aseneh, Malak Ettaj, Dieudonné Ilboudo, Celestin Danwang, Sekou Samadoulougou, Fati Kirakoya-Samadoulougou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe development of a potent immune response and antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 is important for herd immunity. The serological response may be due to a previous infection or vaccination. Healthy blood donors could represent and provide information on the immune status of the general population. Therefore, we estimated the global and regional prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among healthy blood donors.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and ProQuest from December 2019 to January 2023. After critical appraisal and quality assessment, a qualitative synthesis of the identified relevant articles was performed. The random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. Funnel plots and Egger’s test were used to assess publication bias. Sensitivity analysis was performed, and heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistics.ResultsA total of 70 peer-reviewed articles were selected that together included 2,454,192 blood donors. The global estimated pooled prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among healthy blood donors was 10.3% (95% CI: 4.6 – 18.0%, n = 70). The highest seroprevalence was observed in Asia (17.7%), followed by Africa (16.1%). The seroprevalence in studies conducted before the introduction of the vaccine was 6.1%, whereas those of studies conducted after vaccines were available was 27.6%. High seroprevalence was observed in studies that measured antibodies against the S protein of the virus (15.2%), while lower (12.5%) in those that measured antibodies against the N protein. A high seroprevalence was observed in studies that only measured IgG antibodies (16.3%), and it was 5.9% in studies that measured total antibodies.ConclusionThe prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among healthy blood donors varies, potentially indicating geographical disparities in transmission and vaccination rates. To enhance community resilience, addressing these differences through inclusive health policies and adaptable public health measures is crucial. Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome, also called coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), is caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). The initial detection of this virus was in Wuhan, China, in December 2019 [1, 2]. The disease spread globally within months, claiming lives, and challenging health systems [1]. Given the alarming spread and severity of the disease, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared a pandemic on March 11, 2020 [3]. The common symptoms of the initial strain of COVID-19 were cough and fever [4, 5]. Others included muscle soreness, fatigue, and shortness of breath [4]. However, there were cases confirmed with COVID-19 that were asymptomatic [6–9]. The existence of asymptomatic infections presents an undetectable transmission risk within population in the same vicinity [10].

As of 28 April 2024, over 775 million COVID-19 cases and seven million deaths had been reported globally [11]. Several policies and strategic responses have been designed and implemented to fight the pandemic, such as social distancing, limited population movement (lockdowns), hand sanitation, the use of face-masks and finally vaccination to mitigate the transmission risk [12]. Several waves of the pandemic have been experienced, with the most recent being due to the uplifting of restrictions imposed at the height of the pandemic [13]. However, there was a corresponding increase in vaccine uptake in the general population in some countries [14]. Consequently, the prevalence and deaths related to COVID-19 have continued to decline since its peak in January 2022 [15]. As a result the WHO declared that COVID-19 was no longer considered a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHIEC) on 5th May 2023 [16].

On an individual basis, nearly all immunocompetent persons develop an adaptive immune response following SARS-CoV-2 infection [17, 18]. This immune response results from serum antibodies (Immunoglobulins G and M) that are present even in asymptomatic COVID-19 cases. The length of time there are sufficient antibodies to provide immunity varies in time after the initial infection [18]. Similarly, COVID-19 vaccines have been constructed to elicit an immune response [19]. Available antibody tests for SARS-CoV-2 measure either immunoglobulin M (IgM), immunoglobulin G (IgG), or immunoglobulin A (IgA), and some measure all of them (total antibodies). Presence of antibodies can result from prior SARS-CoV-2 infection and/or a history of vaccination [20].

Serological tests are designed to detect antibodies against particular viral proteins. SARS-CoV-2 has several antigens, with the Spike (S) protein of the surface of the virus and Nucleocapsid (N) protein of the viral core being the main target proteins for testing, as they both provide an immunogenic response [21, 22]. The virus binds to the host by protein S, and protein N is responsible for viral replication, especially during the early phases of infection [21–23]. When detecting SARS-CoV-2 antibodies that are the result of a previous infection, either N or S proteins provide a good immunogenic response, but a test that combines both greatly provides a greater sensitivity and specificity [22].

Serological screening or measurement of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies is important in determining the cumulative prevalence and to estimate seroconversion of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the general population [24]. Developing a potent immune response and antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 is important to attain herd immunity [25]. This process provides additional information on the dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 antibody response and inform public health authorities and policy-makers of the prevalence of antibodies to fight the pandemic in the general population [26, 27].

Information on the prevalence of specific serum antibodies (IgG and IgM) against SARS-CoV-2 would inform the immune status of the general population and therefore the herd immunity which in turn would indicate the susceptibility of the population to new waves of infection [28]. Healthy blood donors can be used as a proxy for the general population [29]; as such using estimates from these blood donors can give an impression of the global prevalence of antibodies against SARS-CoV-2. Specifically, we sought to determine the global and regional seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among asymptomatic individuals who donated blood.

Methods

Study design

This was a systematic review and meta-analysis. We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [30]. The protocol of this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023410442) before data extraction.

Search strategy

We used the Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome (PECO) framework to develop the research question and to structure our search strategy. For Population (P), we focused on healthy blood donors, Exposure (E) pertained to the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, there was no specific Comparison (C), and the Outcome (O) was the proportion of healthy blood donors with detectable SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. The formulated PECO research question guiding our search was: What proportion of healthy blood donors have detectable SARS-CoV-2 antibodies? We conducted a literature search on January 12, 2023, using three databases: PubMed, SCOPUS, and ProQuest Central. Table S1 presents the research strategy. Inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed journal articles of observational studies that measured the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among healthy/asymptomatic blood donors. Articles published in any language were included. All articles from December 1, 2019, when the first case of COVID-19 was documented, were included in the study.

Data management and selection criteria

Rayyan online software was used to compile the results of the search and remove duplicates. We included peer-reviewed observational studies, specifically cross-sectional and cohort studies. Our particular interest was studies that precisely documented the serum SARS-CoV-2 antibody status within our specific population of healthy blood donors, offering crucial insights into the prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. In contrast, certain studies were systematically excluded. This exclusion involved review articles, case studies, and case series because of their inherent limitations in providing the comprehensive data necessary for computing the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. In addition, studies lacking detailed records of this crucial information among healthy/ asymptomatic blood donors were purposefully omitted from our analysis.

Five independent reviewers (UJ, MC, JBNA, EM, ID) were involved in the screening of the articles, based on titles and abstracts. Thereafter the final selection of articles to be included, following a full text assessment, was also conducted by the same five reviewers. Any discordance and disagreement were addressed through discussion until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction and quality assessment

An Excel spreadsheet was used to summarize the data. Prevalence data considered the total number of asymptomatic or healthy blood donors positive for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among the total number of asymptomatic or healthy blood donors tested. Additionally, data on the author’s name, year of publication, article title, country, study design, sample size, vaccination period, serological assay used, serological methods, target protein, and the class of measured immunoglobulins were included in the database. Data extraction from individual studies was primarily carried out by five independent reviewers (UJ, MC, JBNA, EM, ID) from March 29, 2023, to April 20, 2023. These data were jointly reviewed, verified, and fused into the summary table of the selected studies. (Table 1).

Characteristics of the studies included in the meta-analysis

We used the National Institutes of Health (NIH) quality assessment tool for cohort and cross-sectional studies to assess the methodological quality and risk of bias of the included articles [31]. Five reviewers (UJ, MC, JBNA, EM, ID) independently used 14 items of the components of the NIH tool for the evaluation of the studies, and a consensus was reached through discussions. Each item was scored as 0 if the condition was not met or 1 if it was met. Table S2 provides more details on the NIH quality assessment questions.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis with random effects using R/Studio was performed to estimate the pooled prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and their 95% confidence interval (CI) among asymptomatic or healthy blood donors. Data were transformed using a Double Arcsine Transformation as described by Freeman-Tukey [32]. The transformed data were then back-transformed to ensure proportions between 0 and 100%. This process aimed to stabilize variances and avoid continuity correction for zero counts.

The calculated proportion for each study and the pooled seroprevalence with 95% CI were presented graphically by a forest plot. Analysis of the subgroups was performed to estimate the pooled prevalence for each group and to identify possible explanations for heterogeneity. The region (country), type of study, vaccination period, serological method, serological assay used, target protein, and class of measured Ig were considered for subgroup analysis. We conducted meta-regression and evaluated the heterogeneity across studies using I2 statistics, which indicate the proportion of the total variation among the studies attributed to heterogeneity rather than sampling error. R2 was reported to denote the proportion of variance explained by the covariates.

We performed a sensitivity analysis to investigate the influence of each individual study on the pooled seroprevalence estimate. This involved systematically removing each study and assessing whether it caused significant fluctuations in the overall pooled seroprevalence estimate. We assessed publication bias by visually observing the symmetry of the funnel plot and confirmed/refuted this by Egger’s test. To determine any temporal shifts in the strength and direction of the overall association estimate, we performed a cumulative meta-analysis using a random effect approach. The studies were initially categorized on the basis on their publication year and subsequently, they were incrementally incorporated into the analysis in chronological sequence. The pooled seroprevalence estimates were recalculated with the inclusion of each successive study.

Results

Identification and selection of studies

The PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1) summarizes the literature search and inclusion process. Initially, 765 records were retrieved from PubMed, 545 from Scopus, and 72 from ProQuest Central, totaling 1382 records. After removing duplicates, 1222 records were retained for title and abstract screening. Subsequently, 99 records were selected for full-text review. A Google Scholar search revealed nine studies, and hand searching the reference list of relevant papers revealed four more articles. Following an eligibility assessment, 70 articles encompassing 2,454,192 participants were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (see Table 1).

PRISMA Flowchart

Characteristics and quality of the included studies

The majority of studies were conducted in Asia (n = 26, 37.1%), followed by 17 (24.3%) studies in Europe, 10 (14.3%) in North America, seven (10%) in Central and South America, seven (10%) in Africa, and three (4.3%) in Oceania. Among the 70 studies, 52 (74.3%) were conducted before the vaccine was introduced, while 18 (25.7%) were conducted after availability of vaccines. Different serological assays were used to measure the seroprevalence in the participants, including the Abbot assay, which was used in 14 (20%) studies, Ortho diagnostic in 11 (15.7%) studies, Elecsys in eight (11.4%) studies, Wantai in six (8.6%) studies and other assays were used in eight (11% studies). The remaining 23 (32.9%) studies did not specify the type of assay used.

Regarding the serological measurement method that was used, 25 (35.7%) studies used enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA), 14 (20%) used chemiluminescence microparticle immunoassay (CMIA), 12 (17.1%) used electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA), three (4.3%) used a combination of more than one method, six (8.6) used another method, and 10 (14.3%) did not specify which serological measurement method used.

Among the 70 studies, 28 (40%) measured antibodies targeting the spike (S) protein of the surface of the virus, 16 (22.9%) measured antibodies against the nucleocapsid (N) protein of the viral core, 10 (14.3%) measured antibodies targeting both S and N proteins, and 16 (22.9%) studies did not specify the target antigen. Total antibodies were measured in 34 (48.6%) of the 70 studies, 34 (48.6%) measured only IgG, and two (2.8%) did not specify the type of antibodies measured.

As presented earlier, 2,454,192 healthy/asymptomatic blood donors were tested in the studies. These were included in 68 (97.2%) cross-sectional studies, and two (2.8%) cohort studies. Almost half of the studies (n = 32,5.7%) were published in 2022 with 30 (42.9%) published in 2021 and the remaining eight (11.4%) in 2020.

Seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in healthy blood donors

The seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among asymptomatic/healthy blood donors ranged from 0.0% to 69.1%. The pooled seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in healthy blood donors was 10.3% (95% CI: 4.6 – 18.0%, n = 70) (see Fig. 2).

Global SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in healthy blood donors

Subgroup analysis

The results of the subgroup analysis are presented in Table 3. The corresponding figures are provided in the online supplementary materials. Cross-sectional studies dominated the pooled seroprevalence, and cohort studies were less represented. The seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in healthy blood donors was 3.6% (95% CI: 2.7 – 4.6%, n = 2) in cohort studies, whereas it was 10.6% (95% CI: 4.7 – 18.4%, n = 68) in cross-sectional studies. Subgroup analysis

A high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 was observed in Asia (17.7%, 95% CI: 8.5 – 29.3%, n = 26), followed by Africa (16.1%, 95% CI: 6.8 – 28.3%, n = 7) and South and Central America (11.8%, 95% CI: 5.6 – 19.9%, n = 7). Low seroprevalence was observed in Oceania (0.8%, 95% CI: 0.1 – 2.0%, n = 3), followed by Europe (4.1%, 95% CI: 2.1 – 6.7%, n = 17) and North America (6.6%, 95% CI: 0.0 – 28.6%, n = 10). Figure S2.

The seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in studies conducted before the introduction of the vaccine was 6.1% (95% CI: 4.6 – 7.7%, n = 52), while that of studies conducted after the vaccine was 27.6% (95% CI: 12.4 – 46.2% n = 18). Figure S3.

The observed seroprevalence was comparable in studies that used Elecsys (13.2%, 95% CI: 6.3–22.3%, n = 8) and Abbot (11.3%, 95% CI: 4.0–21.8%, n = 14). It was, however, higher in studies that used Ortho (18.3%, 95% CI: 2.3–44.5%, n = 11) and in studies that used other types of assays (12.3%, 95% CI: 3.0–26.6%, n = 8). There was a lower observed seroprevalence in the studies that used Wantai (1.1%, 95% CI: 0.6–1.8%, n = 6) (see figure S4).

The observed seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 differed depending on the serological method that was used. Methods that used the ECLIA method reported the highest levels (20.5%, 95% CI: 7.9 – 37.0%, n = 12), followed by studies that used CMIA (13.1%, 95% CI: 7.9 – 19.3%, n = 14) and those that used ELISA (9.5%, 95% CI: 5.3 – 14.7%, n = 25). Studies that combined more than one measurement method to determine the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 reported the lowest seroprevalence of 0.8% (95% CI: 0.0 – 3.4% n = 3) (see Figure S5).

High seroprevalence was observed in studies that measured antibodies against the S protein of the virus (15.2%, 95% CI: 10.6 – 20.5%, n = 28), while it was 12.5% (95% CI: 5.3 – 22.1%, n = 16) in those that measured antibodies against the N protein. The seroprevalence was 6.0% (95% CI: 0.0 – 31.2% n = 10) in studies that measured both S and N proteins, and it was 4.5% (95% CI: 2.5 – 7.1% n = 16) in studies that did not specify the targeted viral antigen (Figure S6). A high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 was observed in studies that only measured IgG antibodies (16.3%, 95% CI: 9.9 – 23.9%, n = 34) and was low in studies that measured total antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 (5.9%, 95% CI: 0.7 – 15.8%, n = 34). Figure S7.

The meta-regression analysis showed that the region (continent) where the study was conducted explained 17.0% of the total heterogeneity (99.9%), the vaccination period explained 25.1%, serological assay explained 10.0%, serological method explained 12.5%, target protein explained 8.8%, and the Ig class explained 10.3% of the total heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

No single study has shown a dominant influence on pooled seroprevalence. The cumulative random effect meta-analysis revealed consistent direction and magnitude of the pooled estimates over the years, with no noticeable variations. The funnel plot shows a publication bias where studies with large sample sizes and studies with low seroprevalence were generally published (Fig. 3). Egger test for publication bias was statistically significant at < 0.001.

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies among healthy/asymptomatic blood donors globally. Seventy studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. The pooled global prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies was 10.3% (95% CI: 4.6 – 18.0%, n = 70).

Other meta-analyses have been conducted to investigate the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in the general population [33–37]. The meta-analyses by Bergeri and Bobrovitz [35, 36] conducted in 2020 and 2021 included 605 and 459 records, respectively. Bergeri found a pooled global estimate of the population-wide seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 of 59.2%, and Bobrovitz found a median seroprevalence of 4.5%. These two meta-analyses were conducted in the general population and included both primary studies and other unpublished records, including Google news, media reports, WHO, nongovernmental organizations, and expert sero-survey recommendation reports. These meta-analyses should have overcome the issue of publication bias by including more records from different sources, but their results are not comparable to the results of our study because the methodologies used in study selection and analysis are different.

On the other hand, a systematic review and meta-analysis among healthcare providers [38] found an overall seroprevalence of 8.7%, but this estimate does not represent the general population as it was inclusively done in healthcare providers. A previous meta-analysis by Hassan that investigated the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in blood donors included 28 studies and found a seroprevalence of 10% [39]. This seroprevalence is comparable to the 10.3% found in our study, although the study by Hassan included fewer studies and was conducted earlier in 2022 when the cumulative seroprevalence was still low.

A high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 was observed in Asia (17.7%), followed by Africa (16.1%). These findings do not align with the findings of a meta-analysis among healthcare providers [38] where a high seroprevalence was observed in studies conducted in North America (12.7%), followed by Europe (8.5%), and Africa (8.2%), with Asia having the lowest seroprevalence (4%). The differences observed at the regional level are possibly related to the duration of the disease, the rate and transmission of infection, and vaccination coverage. Other factors that could explain the regional variability of seroprevalence can be the region-specific public health status, environmental factors including the climate, and local resources [33, 40, 41].

The seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in studies conducted before the introduction of the vaccine was 6.1%, while that of studies conducted after the vaccine was 27.6%. These findings suggest that vaccination improved the increase in immunity compared with natural infection and that high vaccination coverage confers promising herd immunity to the general population. It was previously documented that natural acquired immunity after SARS-CoV-2 lasts for some months but wanes over time [42, 43]; however, it appears to be protective in terms of reinfection and against serious SARS-CoV-2 outcomes and is comparable to two mRNA doses of vaccines [42–46]. The booster doses of vaccines confer most protective immunity comparable with hybrid immunity, which is acquired through vaccination in a previously infected person [42, 47–49].

There are different SARS-CoV-2 serological tests that use different measurement methods. The SARS-CoV-2 immunoglobulin standard developed by the WHO compares tests that use the same isotype or target protein and was mainly developed to assess antibodies established by vaccines [50, 51]. There are important differences in the comparability of the serological tests for both natural and vaccine-induced immunity, mainly depending on the time that has elapsed from infection or the vaccine, but this time difference is less significant when comparing the IgG assays [51]. These differences apply to the quantitative measurement of different antibody tests [51]. The studies included in this meta-analysis used both qualitative and quantitative measurements of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, and we focused on qualitative measurements. In addition, variation in antigens used by manufacturers, varying measurement ranges used in each study, dilution techniques, measured samples, and period of measurement could have additionally limited the comparability of these serological tests.

Considering the type of serological assay used, the seroprevalence was higher in studies that used Ortho and was comparable in studies that used Elecsys and Abbot. Etienne showed that Abbot, Euroimmun, Liaison, and Wantai serological tests demonstrated good sensitivity in detecting SARS-CoV-2 antibodies [52]. Kundi showed a similarity between Roche, Abbott, and Liaison serological assays [53]. Seroprevalence estimates can vary considerably based on the assay used, principally in settings with low true seroprevalence, and the risk of biased estimates can be lessened with appropriate adjustment in addition to using multiple tests [54, 55]. Multiple testing involves parallel testing (testing the same sample on multiple assays concomitantly) and sequential testing (one test followed by a second or more other tests for confirmation), which are plausibly effective in ruling out false positive results [54]. Moreover, it was found that viral mutation and differences in antibody dynamics in particular populations, specifically in sub-Saharan Africa and in young people, can affect the assay performance [54, 56, 58–60].

Furthermore, we found that SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence was high in studies that used the ECLIA method, followed by that of studies that used CMIA and ELISA. Studies that combined more than one measurement method to determine the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 found the lowest seroprevalence. These findings differed from those of a meta-analysis of studies conducted in children [37] in 2022, where the highest seroprevalence was observed in studies that used ELISA and the lowest in studies that used LFIA, and a meta-analysis of studies conducted in the general population [33] in which the ELISA group revealed a higher seroprevalence. In a study by Kundi, they tested the performance of seven serological assays and found that ELISA detecting IgG and IgM against S protein was comparable to CLIA detecting total antibodies, but ELISA detecting anti-S IgG had a better accuracy beyond three weeks of infection and would be good in seroprevalence studies [53].

In this meta-analysis, high seroprevalence was observed in studies that measured antibodies against the S protein of the virus (15.2%), while it was 12.5% in those that measured antibodies against the N protein. The seroprevalence was 6.0% in studies that measured both S and N proteins. Either N or S proteins provide a good immunogenic response, but a test that evaluates antibodies against both proteins greatly provides a greater sensitivity and specificity [22].

A high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 was observed in studies that only measured IgG antibodies (16.3%), and it was 5.9% in studies that measured total antibodies to SARS-CoV-2. Masaki showed that pan Ig, IgG, and combination assays are all considered suitable for epidemiological and clinical use [61]. The difference found in our study could have resulted from the fact that some studies that tested total antibodies also tested two proteins and/or used multiple tests that could have diminished the number of false positive tests via sequential testing.

The evidence elicited in this meta-analysis has a public health implication that regions with low seroprevalence should uphold their vaccination coverage as a measure to attain herd immunity and control of the pandemic. The strengths of this study are that it pooled the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 from a relatively high number of studies, and using statistical techniques, it was able to explain some of the reasons behind the observed heterogeneity. The study also permits us to visualize the measure of seropositive status globally and regionally.

However, the results are subject to a few limitations. First, we noticed a publication bias where studies with a large sample size and studies with low seroprevalence were generally published. Second, the majority of the included studies did not adequately stratify the prevalence based on sociodemographic characteristics such as age and gender; therefore, these factors could not be incorporated in the subgroup analysis. Third, almost all studies included in our meta-analysis were cross-sectional studies; some of such studies may have overestimated the seroprevalence, especially if they were performed after a COVID-19 wave. Finally, the variability in the sample size of the included studies could have affected the heterogeneity observed between studies.

This study provides vital insights with implications for policymakers and public health practitioners. Evidence-based booster vaccination strategies informed by seroprevalence trends and waning immunity issues are recommended. Targeted vaccination approaches prioritizing vulnerable groups and areas with low seroprevalence are essential. It is advisable to mitigate socioeconomic disparities through inclusive health policies combined with adaptable public health measures given local seroprevalence rates. Reinforcing seroprevalence surveillance for outbreak management and continued investment in research to monitor trends are crucial. These measures collectively contribute to informed policy decisions to build community resilience during the post-PHEIC phase of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Conclusion

The prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 estimated in our meta-analysis denotes that most of the global population remains susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection. The disparities observed across regions underline the multifactorial nature of the disease, which may be influenced by factors such as disease duration, transmission and vaccination rates, and region-specific factors. Post-vaccine prevalence was notably higher. Varied serological tests influenced the results, emphasizing the necessity of standardized testing methods. Moving forward, there is a need to reinforce seroprevalence surveillance, invest in ongoing research, implement inclusive health policies, and adopt public health measures. These initiatives are crucial for building a resilient global community in the post-PHEIC phase of the COVID-19 pandemic.
