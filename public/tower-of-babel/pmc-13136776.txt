Ciprofol May Represent a Useful Sedative Option for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy
Authors: Takeshi Yamamura, Masanao Nakamura, Hiroki Kawashima
Published: 2026

Sedation during gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy has been reported to have positive effects for both patients and endoscopists. It improves patient compliance, reduces pain, discomfort, and anxiety and increases patient satisfaction [1, 2]. Consequently, the higher willingness to undergo repeat examinations is expected to improve the detection of GI diseases. From the perspective of endoscopists, sedation has also been reported to improve procedural quality and enhance endoscopist satisfaction [3]. When sedation is administered safely, its benefits are substantial.

The sedatives commonly used in GI endoscopy include midazolam, diazepam, flunitrazepam, propofol, dexmedetomidine, and remimazolam. In the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES) guidelines for sedation [4], midazolam is weakly proposed among benzodiazepine drugs for its sedative effects and patient satisfaction. Although midazolam is the most commonly used agent in Japan, it has undesirable drawbacks, including paradoxical agitation and a relatively long half‐life, which can result in prolonged recovery times. Propofol, an intravenous anesthetic agent targeting GABAA receptors, is considered a useful sedative for GI endoscopy because it is not associated with higher adverse event rates than those of midazolam and offers rapid recovery and shorter post‐procedure confinement. The JGES guidelines for sedation [4] state that propofol may be used by physicians who have undergone sedation training and only for patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists‐Physical Status (ASA‐PS) classification I or II. Furthermore, propofol is associated with adverse effects, such as hypoxia, bradycardia, and significant injection pain [5].

Recently, remimazolam has become available as a sedative agent for GI endoscopy. Remimazolam, a novel ester‐based benzodiazepine, is rapidly metabolized into inactive compounds by nonspecific tissue esterases with a significantly reduced risk of resedation. Remimazolam has a shorter duration of action than midazolam, resulting in faster recovery. A meta‐analysis comparing it with midazolam for sedative GI endoscopy demonstrated that remimazolam was associated with higher procedural success rates, lower rescue medication requirements, shorter recall times, and fewer adverse events [6]. Furthermore, remimazolam has a fast onset and short duration comparable to that of propofol. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing remimazolam and propofol for upper GI endoscopy sedation [7], the incidence of oxygen reserve depletion was significantly higher in the propofol group. Remimazolam also required less additional dosing during the procedures and was associated with a lower incidence of nausea. The satisfaction levels reported by both endoscopists and patients were comparable, suggesting that remimazolam may be a highly suitable agent for GI endoscopy sedation. Therefore, remimazolam is expected to be increasingly used for sedation in GI endoscopy in the foreseeable future.

Ciprofol is a structural analog of propofol. Although it acts on GABAA receptors similar to propofol, it has several important differences. First, Ciprofol is four to five times more potent than propofol. The incorporation of an R‐chiral center and a cyclopropyl group improves its pharmacological and physicochemical properties. Consequently, it may provide more effective sedation than propofol. Injection pain is related to the concentration of drugs in the outer aqueous phase of an emulsion. Ciprofol exhibited higher liposolubility and lower concentrations in the outer aqueous phase of the injection emulsion. Furthermore, at equivalent concentrations and doses, ciprofol‐injected emulsions exhibited higher lipophilicity and lower drug concentrations in the outer aqueous phase than propofol emulsions. These properties are thought to reduce injection pain with ciprofol compared to propofol. A phase III RCT comparing ciprofol with propofol demonstrated non‐inferiority in procedural success rates for gastroscopy and colonoscopy. Moreover, adverse events such as respiratory depression, apnea, and hypoxemia occurred less frequently with ciprofol, and injection pain was markedly reduced (4.9% vs. 52.4% with propofol) [8]. Additionally, a single‐center RCT targeting upper GI procedures demonstrated a lower incidence of severe swallowing dysfunction with ciprofol. Ciprofol was also associated with less hemodynamic instability, reduced respiratory depression, and less injection pain [5]. A meta‐analysis comparing ciprofol and propofol for anesthesia in patients undergoing GI endoscopies also demonstrated its non‐inferiority to propofol in patient sedation. It exhibited a favorable safety profile, with a lower incidence of injection pain and a potential reduction in the risk of hypotension [9]. Ciprofol is approved in China and widely used for sedation in GI endoscopy. Clinical trials are ongoing in the United States and Europe, and its potential for expansion into the global market is attracting considerable attention.

This multicenter, prospective RCT enrolled overweight patients (body mass indices ≥ 23 kg/m2 according to Asia–Pacific criteria) and evaluated the total incidence of hypoxia and severe hypoxia during sedation, with propofol or ciprofol [10]. Participants underwent combined esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy under sedation with either agent. The primary endpoints were total incidence of hypoxia (75% ≤ SpO2 < 90% lasting < 60s) and severe hypoxia (SpO2 < 75% for any duration or 75% ≤ SpO2 < 90% lasting ≥ 60s), which were defined and analyzed separately. Primary endpoint results showed that the total incidence of the composite endpoint of hypoxia and severe hypoxia was significantly lower in patients sedated with ciprofol compared with those sedated using propofol (12.45% vs. 17.77%, p = 0.018). Specifically, for severe hypoxia, the use of ciprofol was associated with a significantly lower incidence than that of propofol (4.35% vs. 7.62%, p = 0.028). The procedural success rate was 100% in both groups, with no significant differences in the insertion or procedure times. However, body movements occurred significantly less frequently with ciprofol (7.91% vs. 13.28%, p = 0.005). The overall incidence of sedation‐related adverse events was significantly lower with ciprofol than with propofol (61.66% vs. 71.48%, p < 0.001), which was mainly driven by reductions in hypotension and prolonged apnea (> 60 s). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of other events such as hypertension or bradycardia. As previously reported, recovery time was longer in the ciprofol group. The longer recovery time may represent a trade‐off for reduced respiratory depression and enhanced safety. Regarding injection pain, a notable concern with propofol, ciprofol caused significantly less pain (2.57% vs. 13.28%, p < 0.001). Although the overall incidence of injection pain in this study was lower than that reported in previous studies, this is likely attributable to the concomitant administration of sufentanil before sedation. Based on these findings, ciprofol appears to be a particularly suitable sedative for overweight patients and other populations at an elevated risk of hypoxia.

The limitations of this study include the exclusion of patients with severe obesity and restriction to esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy, thereby excluding procedures exceeding 30 min. Consequently, the efficacy and safety of ciprofol in severely obese populations and during prolonged endoscopic procedures require further investigation. Another limitation is that the propofol dose in this study was set at 1.5 mg/kg based on total body weight (TBW). Considering the pharmacokinetic characteristics of propofol, TBW‐based dosing may result in relatively higher effective exposure, particularly among overweight patients. Therefore, some guidelines recommend determining the dosage based on lean body weight or adjusted body weight. Consequently, administration of a relatively higher propofol dose may have influenced the incidence of respiratory events by propofol in this study.

Sedation plays an important role in GI endoscopy by alleviating patient discomfort, improving both physician and patient satisfaction, and enhancing procedural quality and outcomes, leading to an increased demand. However, challenges remain, including sedation‐related adverse events and the need for additional space and staff for post‐procedural recovery. Sedation‐related incidents can result in substantial patient harm, underscoring the importance of effective preventive strategies. Patients with obesity are particularly susceptible to airway obstruction during sedation and, therefore, are at an increased risk of hypoxia. Given the growing global prevalence of overweight and obesity, this study provides clinically important evidence for the selection of safer sedation agents for GI endoscopy in this high‐risk population.

Propofol has long been used for sedation during GI endoscopy, with evidence supporting its use across a wide range of procedures, including upper and lower endoscopy as well as endoscopic ultrasonography and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. Its safety has been demonstrated in clinical trials involving older adult patients. When administered under appropriate monitoring, propofol does not increase complication rates, is associated with a low interruption rate, even during prolonged procedures, and is therefore considered a beneficial sedative agent. However, to ensure patient safety, its use should be limited to physicians trained in airway management. Therefore, non‐anesthesiologists should undergo appropriate sedation training before administering propofol. Unlike Europe and the United States, Japan currently lacks a standardized educational system or formal guidelines for propofol use by non‐anesthetists. Furthermore, its use is restricted to selected patients, such as those classified as ASA‐PS I or II [4]. Therefore, there is a strong need to develop alternative agents that can overcome these restrictions. Remimazolam and ciprofol, which have demonstrated comparable efficacy to propofol and are associated with fewer sedation‐related adverse events, have the potential to become key agents for GI endoscopic sedation. Further evidence, including head‐to‐head comparisons and large‐scale RCTs, is warranted.

An RCT conducted by Renlong et al. demonstrated the efficacy and safety of ciprofol in routine GI endoscopy. Therefore, ciprofol may represent a promising alternative for sedation in endoscopic procedures.
