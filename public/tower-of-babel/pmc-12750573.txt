Cancer treatment shared decision-making and coping during the COVID-19 pandemic (CAN-DECIDE): a mixed-methods study
Authors: Rebecca A Aslakson, Rachel Siden, Laura M Holdsworth, Kathryn Colborn, Mae Verano, Bonnie Odelia Wong, Fabian Johnston, Richard Nudotor, Amn Siddiqi, Elizabeth Rickerson, Caroline Hollahan, Bridget Fahy, Isaac Lira, Brittany Waterman, Kemba Johnson, Shelley Fuld Nasso, Holly Tabor, Lidia Schapira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe hypothesized that cancer patients, their family members, and cancer and palliative care practitioners were experiencing significant COVID-related stress and changes to cancer care and sought to characterize contributing factors.MethodsWe completed a mixed methods study to explore cancer care-related experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic of cancer patients, family members, and cancer and palliative care practitioners. Participants were recruited from an ongoing clinical trial.ResultsPatients were pursuing curative-intent surgeries for upper gastrointestinal cancers. Between October 2020 and July 2021, we collected 491 surveys from 181 cancer patients and 26 family caregivers and conducted 71 in-depth interviews with 48 cancer patients, 6 family caregivers, and 19 practitioners. Patient-reported quality-of-life measurements were not associated with a corresponding COVID surge. Patients and caregivers were generally satisfied with their care, trusted providers, and were often unaware of pandemic-related changes. In contrast, practitioners reported significant pandemic-related changes to cancer care delivery with associated practitioner anxiety, fatigue, and moral distress. All felt that visitor restrictions negatively impacted care.ConclusionsOur data suggest that cancer patients coped well overall despite pandemic-related disruptions whereas cancer care clinicians experienced significant stress. These findings can inform future disaster-preparedness planning and policy-setting in institutions providing cancer care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01944-7.Keywords: Burnout, COVID-19, Cancer, Quality of life, Cancer care, Palliative care, COVID

Introduction

COVID-19 disproportionately impacted populations with underlying conditions, such as cancer [1–4]. During the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic, health care systems intermittently delayed or deferred cancer care, [2, 4] increased telehealth care delivery, [5, 6] and enacted new visitor restrictions [7]. Patients may also have had delayed cancer screenings or treatments [8]. Stay-at-home orders [9] and social distancing also isolated people from social supports such as family, faith communities, and cancer support groups [10]. Emerging data have also suggested that delays to potentially life-prolonging cancer treatments, fear of the virus itself, and/or the impact of social distancing measures could have negatively impacted the health and well-being of cancer patients [2, 11–14]. Moreover, as the COVID pandemic emerged in 2020, cancer care protocols were often adapted by clinicians and professional groups with variable involvement of patient advocates. We conducted a mixed methods study to explore the COVID-19 pandemic-related experiences and coping of cancer patients, their family caregivers, and their cancer and palliative care clinicians. We hypothesized that delays to potentially life-saving and life-prolonging cancer treatments, fear of the virus, and the impact of social distancing would have a negative impact on the health and well-being of cancer patients and family caregivers and the resilience of cancer and palliative practitioners. We also explored the engagement of cancer patients and their family caregivers in the process of cancer treatment decision-making during the earliest stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in circumstances of rapidly evolving, pandemic-related changes to cancer treatments.

Methods

Patient participants in this Cancer Treatment Shared Decision-Making and Coping during the COVID-19 Pandemic (CAN-DECIDE) study either previously participated, or were concurrently participating, in a parent clinical trial (NCT03611309), “A Multicenter, Randomized Controlled Trial of Perioperative Palliative Care Surrounding Cancer Surgery for Patients and Their Family Members” (PERIOP-PC) [15]. Patients were pursuing curative-intent surgeries for upper-GI cancers, including: esophageal, gastric, pancreatic, gall bladder, and liver cancers. PERIOP-PC participants were recruited in-person or over the phone from the outpatient clinic of their cancer surgeon. Once enrolled in PERIOP-PC, participants were randomized to either surgeon-alone or surgeon-palliative care team co-management during the perioperative period and participated in the trial for six months following their cancer surgery. For each patient participant, one family caregiver was also approached for trial participation. Patients from both intervention and control arms of PERIOP-PC were recruited to CAN-DECIDE. Patient and family caregiver participants who had previously completed PERIOP-PC were also invited to participate in CAN-DECIDE. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and consenting procedures were in accordance with the consenting processes prescribed by each site’s Institutional Review Board.

This mixed-methods study collected data through surveys completed by patients and caregivers and qualitative interviews with patients, caregivers, and cancer practitioners. The surveys collected data on patient-reported outcomes across six months to evaluate changes to patient and caregiver quality of life while the patient was receiving cancer care during pandemic. We conducted qualitative interviews to supplement the quantitative data by illuminating the context and participants’ lived experiences with cancer care during the pandemic. The interviews with cancer practitioners aimed to understand what changes to the delivery of cancer care were occurring at their institution during the pandemic, and interviews with patients and caregivers aimed to understand their experiences receiving cancer care during the pandemic and explore how they felt that their care had been impacted. Quantitative and qualitative data occurred concurrently and were integrated by reviewing the quantitative and qualitative results following analysis.

Quantitative data collection and analysis

The CAN-DECIDE survey was developed through investigator engagement with: (1) PERIOP-PC researchers, (2) cancer and palliative care practitioners, and (3) patient and family caregiver stakeholders. Survey domains included: COVID-19 experiences, COVID-19 coping, cancer coping, telehealth, and pandemic-related delays and changes to cancer treatments. Initial surveys with items for each domain were developed by core study team members (RA, MV) and distributed to four PERIOP-PC patient/family and clinician partners for feedback and cognitive interviewing to inform the final CAN-DECIDE survey. Participants who were on-trial for PERIOP-PC completed their CAN-DECIDE surveys at the same five time points as PERIOP-PC survey completion: pre-operative, one-week post-operative, and one-, three-, and six-months following cancer surgery. Participants who had previously completed PERIOP-PC and were re-enrolled to CAN-DECIDE completed up to three surveys at three-month intervals. Surveys were completed either online, over the phone, or on paper. Scores were summarized using means and standard deviations as well as medians and interquartile ranges. For categorical responses, frequencies and percentages, and figures for Likert-type items were created. Survey responses were summarized, including multiple surveys from some participants. Although some of these responses could have been correlated at the individual level, results were assumed to be independent and were summarized over all time points. Longitudinal trajectories of patient quality-of-life (QOL) scores were summarized graphically using cubic smoothing splines to estimate averages in scores over calendar time by site. QOL outcomes included the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy: Palliative Care [16, 17] (FACIT-Pal), Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy—General [17, 18] (FACT-G), and Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy: Spiritual Well-Being 12 Item Scale [19, 20] (FACIT-Sp12) scores, and patients’ self-reported physical and mental health scores from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29 [21, 22] (PROMIS-29) measures.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

Cancer patients and family caregivers were selected for in-depth interviews for maximum variation by geographic sites, cancer treatment phase, and treatment phase in relation to the pandemic (e.g. receiving cancer surgery prior to or following March 2020). [23] Patient and caregiver interviews were semi-structured with probes that addressed: (1) cancer coping during the pandemic; (2) pandemic-related changes to cancer treatments; and (3) telehealth (See Appendix A for complete topic guide). Patient interviews were conducted separately by three qualitative-trained researchers (LMH, RS, MV) who aligned interview practices, and results were reviewed regularly for quality by an experienced qualitative health services researcher (LMH).

Cancer practitioners were selected for variation across geographic sites and by clinical specialty – surgical, medical, and radiation oncologists, and palliative care practitioners. Practitioner interviews were semi-structured with probes that addressed: (1) perception of patient pandemic-related cancer coping; (2) approaches to pandemic-related cancer treatment changes; and (3) telehealth (See Appendix B for complete topic guide). Practitioner interviews were conducted by one researcher (BOW) who had a medical background in addition to qualitative training. All interviews were conducted via telephone over Zoom, audio recorded, and transcribed for analysis. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached. [24] Transcripts were uploaded into NVivo (released March 2020) for analysis using both deductive and inductive approaches. Coding frameworks with a priori codes were initially developed from the topic guides and used by three researchers who independently coded the same transcript and met to check for coding consistency and to discuss emergent codes not captured in the codebook; this process was repeated on two more transcripts. Where different codes were applied to the same interview response, researchers aligned understanding of code meanings through iterative discussions. Emergent codes were discussed and when salient, integrated into the codebook. Researchers then independently coded transcripts, annotated transcripts with analytic thoughts and notes, and met longitudinally for discussion. Coded data were analyzed using a matrix approach to identify patterns and check for negative evidence. [25] Separate matrices were created for the patient/family caregiver and practitioner groups to identify themes specific to each group.

Caregiver demographics characteristics

More than half of patient surveys (N = 245/422; 58%) documented that the respondent received some form of cancer treatment during the two months prior to the survey with 51% documenting having received cancer surgery, 55% having received an infusion chemotherapeutic, 20% having received an oral chemotherapeutic, and 18% having received radiation therapy. Treatments were not mutually exclusive with some patients documenting multiple types of cancer treatments during the previous two months. Only 13 surveys (5.3%) documented a patient-perceived, pandemic-related cancer treatment delay, cancellation, or change. Only 2 patients noted participating in the decision about the cancer treatment change. Another 6 patient surveys (2.5%) communicated uncertainty about whether or not a COVID-related cancer treatment delay or change had occurred. Only one patient survey noted a pandemic-related cancer treatment cancellation; the patient noted no involvement in that decision-making. No family caregivers noted cancer treatments being delayed, cancelled, or changed due to the pandemic though 3 (8%) reported being “unsure” whether a COVID-related cancer treatment change possibly occurred.

Over three quarters of patient surveys (329; 78%) documented that they were “coping well with the COVID-19 pandemic” and 33% noted being anxious about the pandemic. Moreover, 82% of patient surveys (N = 343) noted coping well with their cancer diagnosis and treatment, although, in 41% (N = 171), the patient noted being anxious about it. In general, respondents documented being more anxious about their cancer diagnosis and care, as compared to that for COVID-19; on a scale of 0 to 100 with 0 indicating maximal anxiety about COVID and 100 indicating maximal anxiety about cancer, the respondent mean was 68 (SD 29) with a median of 75 (IQR 50,95) (Fig. 1). Over three-quarters of family caregiver surveys (79%; N = 52) noted that they were coping well with the patient’s care during the pandemic and only 28% (N = 18) documented that the cancer patient was anxious about the pandemic. Another 74% of family caregiver surveys noted that the cancer patient was coping well with their cancer diagnosis and care, although, 47% (N = 31) noted that the cancer patient had also been anxious about it (Fig. 2).

Likert type items asked to the family caregiver

Over 60% of patient surveys (N = 256/421) indicated having had a telehealth visit for cancer care within the prior two months with 69% (N = 176/255) noting that they “liked telehealth visits for my cancer care.” Amongst patient surveys, 32% (N = 82/255) preferred telehealth over in-person visits for their cancer care, 26% (N = 66) preferred in-person visits over telehealth, and 42% (N = 107) were neutral between the two (Fig. 1). Just over half (53%; N = 35) of family caregiver surveys indicated that the cancer patient had a telehealth visit for cancer care within the prior two months, with 60% of family caregivers (N = 21/26) noting that they “liked telehealth visits for my family member’s cancer care.” Amongst family caregiver surveys, 20% (N = 6) preferred telehealth over in-person visits for their family member’s cancer care, 56% (N = 19) preferred in-person visits over telehealth, and 24% (N = 8) were neutral between the two (Fig. 2).

The longitudinal trajectories of patient QOL scores (measured by FACIT-Pal and FACT-G scores), patient spiritual well-being (measured by FACIT-Sp12), and patients’ self-reported physical and mental health scores (measured from the PROMIS 29 score) varied heavily both between and within institutions and across the study period (Figs. 3 & 4). The cubic smoothing splines display this variability, but there did not appear to be any obvious trends, especially related to the pandemic surge occurring during this period.

Interviews were conducted between August 18th, 2020 and July 1 st, 2021 (Tables 4 & 5). Telehealth

All parties reported near-universal, pandemic-related increases in telehealth and different consequent benefits, limitations, and preferences. Every practitioner and most patients reported using telehealth video visits as part of cancer care with most using telehealth visits (via smart phone or computer) for the first time. A commonly reported benefit of telehealth was the convenience to patients, particularly in eliminating travel and relieving patient anxiety about potential COVID-19 exposure. Some patients indicated that they preferred telehealth visits if given the choice, and a few patients described a desire to continue with telehealth for cancer care even after the pandemic was over. Both patients and practitioners described telehealth-related technological difficulties with some patients unable to use telehealth due to unfamiliarity with related software or not having access to a computer, smart phone, and/or internet connection. Both patients and practitioners noted that telehealth, as it had been implemented at the time of the study, did not allow for an adequate physical exam or for vitals to be taken during a visit. Practitioners preferred in-person visits for patients who were symptomatic, frail, or actively receiving cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy.

Decision-making and changes to cancer care during the pandemic

Very few interviewed patients recalled having discussions with their cancer practitioner teams about pandemic-related changes for cancer clinical care and most were unaware of whether the pandemic may have changed their cancer clinical care. When treatments were delayed or changed, patients attributed it to hospital policies or did not know a specific reason. Patients who experienced delays or changes were also still generally satisfied with how the delay was handled by their institution and cancer care team; they noted trusting that their cancer care team members were making the best decisions possible related to their oncologic care during the pandemic.

“I never felt as though I was being deprived of help because of the pandemic. I felt as though I got good medical treatment even with the pandemic going on.” (Patient 33)

Many patients had strong concerns about potential COVID-19 exposure when coming to in-person visits, but very few reported that they wished to delay a cancer treatment because of those fears.

Cancer and palliative care practitioners reported confusion surrounding pandemic-related changes to their ability to schedule or conduct office visits, treatments, or surgeries and further lack of clarity as to who could or should make these decisions.

“In terms of who makes those decisions, there's a general guideline or guidance, but I guess on a more ground level, we just make that decision and that discretion, it's up to us [...] who to see in person and who not.” (Medical Oncologist 4)

Some practitioners reported that while they had very little control over institutional policies that limited visitors or reduced the allowable number of patient visits, it was the practitioner’s responsibility to prioritize and triage their patients receiving care and to contact patients if a delay or treatment change was necessary. Differing from data reported by cancer patients and family caregivers, cancer practitioners noted that some patients requested their own delay or cancellation out of concern that in-person visits would expose them to COVID-19. Very few practitioners reported involving patients in decision-making around pandemic-related changes to cancer treatments. All practitioners particularly noted being tasked to reduce patient volume, particularly for surgeries, due to the pandemic. Approaches for reducing surgical volume varied by institution with some designed by hospital authorities and others deploying a triage system with choices made by the surgeons themselves. Reduction of surgical volume was typically through postponing elective procedures and/or utilization of non-surgical treatments, such as preoperative chemotherapy, prior to an actual surgery. Methods reported by practitioners for reducing the number of in-person clinic appointments included changes to: treatment modality (e.g. administering oral rather than intravenous chemotherapy); treatment frequency (e.g. extending the time between treatments); and/or treatment duration (e.g. shortening the duration of radiation protocols). In describing these pandemic-related treatment changes, cancer practitioners expressed that while some would not have taken place outside of the pandemic, all offered treatments remained consistent with current standards of care; some practitioners even felt that these changes were an improvement to how cancer care was done before the pandemic.

“Our cancer co-decisions, none of it was wrong. All of it’s defensible, all of it was reasonable. All of the patients got a standard of care, but was it the standard of care that we are proud of delivering? No.” (Medical Oncologist 3)

Visitor restrictions

Patients strongly reported being negatively impacted by pandemic-related, restrictive visitor policies. Some patients reported visitor restrictions forcing them to attend all cancer care visits alone and remarked that these visitor policy restrictions were especially detrimental during the perioperative period, with some expressing anxiety about potentially dying alone in the hospital.

“He couldn't visit at all through my whole recovery. I was in there for seven days. I didn't have any visitors. I have to tell you it was a very emotional drop-off because it was one of those surgeries where you don't even know if you're going to come home” (Patient 15).

Cancer and palliative care practitioners similarly described that visitor restrictions negatively impacted cancer care, particularly that the absence of family caregivers prevented optimal bi-directional sharing of valuable health information between patients, family caregivers, and cancer and practitioners. This theme is explored further in our qualitative paper on the impact visitor restrictions to our participants [26].

Coping during the pandemic

Cancer and palliative care practitioners and patients coped with stresses and pandemic-related gaps in cancer care in different ways. While many patients described being negatively affected by visitor restrictions, very few patients described the pandemic as having a negative impact on their actual clinical cancer care and treatments. Some patient and family caregiver respondents felt that the pandemic did not impact their cancer clinical care at all. Others described attitudes of acceptance (beliefs that their care was still the best it could have been given the circumstances) or satisfaction (beliefs that their care was as-good or better than they would have received pre-pandemic). Some expressed difficulties coping with symptom management, stress or anxiety related to their cancer or COVID-19 risk, or feeling more isolated or unable to do their usual activities. However, some still expressed having no problems with coping, and even described the pandemic as having a positive effect because it forced them to focus on their cancer recovery:

“This [the pandemic] is probably the best thing that could've happened to me [...] stay home, do what you can, don't push it too hard and recover and focus on your recovery” (Patient 20).

These experiences and emotions were also not mutually exclusive with respondents describing simultaneous feelings of difficulty and ease with their cancer and pandemic coping.

In contrast to cancer patients and family caregivers, cancer and palliative care practitioners described many pandemic-related difficulties, including feeling “moral injury” (a feeling of distress when clinicians’ feel that health system obligations or policies prevent them from prioritizing over the patient’s best interest [27]) related to making “life or death” decisions for their patients in a context of pandemic-related uncertainty and with little oversight or guidance (Surgical Oncologist 1).

"There was a brief period where we had to convince ourselves, for every time we wouldn't give chemo or some other aggressive invasive thing, it was like we had to convince ourselves that it was the right thing to do that" (Medical Oncologist 6).

While all practitioners agreed that they maintained standard of care for their patients throughout the pandemic, they noted frequent ethical or moral concerns about pandemic restrictions causing them to quickly pivot and/or make different decisions and recommendations than those they would normally provide. Practitioners repeatedly noting that pandemic restrictions caused them to provide different types or timings of care that they would have otherwise. Many cancer and palliative care practitioners also reported that the pandemic created more work, more anxiety and emotional fatigue, and more “burnout” and stress.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Through both quantitative and qualitative data, cancer patients and family caregivers and cancer and palliative care practitioners described many pandemic-related changes to cancer care, particularly involving novel utilization of telehealth for medical care delivery and adapting to pandemic-related visitor restrictions. During a November 2020 through February 2021 COVID surge that was prevalent across all study sites, patient quality of life, physical functioning, and/or mental functioning varied within and between study sites without appearance of surge-related increases or decreases. Further complicating data analysis during this surge was the concurrent and initial introduction of the first COVID vaccines, which may have also influenced survey responses. Through both quantitative and qualitative data, patients and family caregivers reported patients receiving many cancer treatments but rare perception of any pandemic-related treatment delays, changes, or cancellations. And while patients did experience significantly stressful events, most notably from the impact of visitor restrictions, [26] the quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate that patients and family caregivers coped well overall. These findings are in stark contrast to those from cancer and palliative care practitioners who reported in the interviews about widespread pandemic-related changes to cancer treatments and significant distress amongst practitioners about having to personally interpret and navigate pandemic-related cancer treatment restrictions and changes.

Dissonant pandemic experiences between patients and their cancer and palliative care practitioners

While any cancer diagnosis is stressful and cancer and/or cancer treatment presence likely increased vulnerability for severe COVID-19 infection, most patients were reassured that their cancer teams gave them excellent and safe care throughout these early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, based on our data, cancer patients and their family caregivers were not particularly concerned about the COVID pandemic particularly as compared with concern surrounding their cancer diagnosis and treatment. One feature of the pandemic that may have supported patients and caregivers was the rapid deployment of telehealth during the pandemic. In a systematic review of telehealth services in cancer care between 2020–2021, authors Murphy et al. found that many studies reported patients and caregivers having high satisfaction with telehealth for how it provided continuity of care in a time of disruption [28].

In contrast to this, cancer and palliative care practitioners described a period early in the COVID-19 pandemic that was beset with personal and professional challenges and worries. These findings of dissonant experiences reinforce those found in other studies [29] and may relate to multiple complex and co-occurring stresses, particularly for practitioners. Many hospitals increased surge capacity by reducing elective or semi-elective surgical procedures, which led to complex and varying guidelines for surgery scheduling; surgical oncologists noted particular difficulty interpreting and implementing these guidelines in day-to-day practice. While cancer and palliative care practitioners largely felt that treatment decisions met adjusted standards of care, they also noted that the choice and timing of cancer treatments, particularly surrounding surgery and pre-operative chemotherapy, deviated from usual pre-pandemic practice. Practitioners also reported their own stress due to enforcing widely unpopular visitor restriction policies as well as due to worrying about their own virus exposure at work and potentially transmitting work-acquired COVID-19 infections to their family members. Practitioners also noted that clinical care took longer during the pandemic, particularly in having to communicate separately with patients and families due to visitor restrictions. In conclusion, our data support that cancer patients and their family caregivers felt well-cared-for by their cancer teams, even despite an on-going and rapidly changing COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, cancer patients and family caregivers were generally unaware of pandemic-related cancer treatment changes and the frenetic, and at times traumatic, experiences described by their cancer and palliative care practitioners in adapting to those changes. Finally, we suspect that these dissonant experiences and the stresses described by cancer and palliative care practitioners likely contributed to significant emotionally and physical burnout amongst those practitioners.

Limitations

The pandemic has disproportionately affected minority populations in various ways, including within healthcare [30]. Our study participants were majority white, non-Hispanic, and college-educated, and thus, may yield findings not generalizable to other populations. Additionally, due to visitor restrictions and limited access to family caregivers during in-person study enrollment, relatively few family caregivers were recruited and fewer were interviewed. This might affect the generalizability of these findings. Recall bias is a known limitation of qualitative research and is an important consideration. Strengths of this study for mitigating recall bias include conducting timely interviews (selecting patients for interviews that were actively in the cancer treatment phase being sampled for), comparing interview data to the longitudinal survey data, and in how the pandemic itself created prominent memories in participants.

Conclusion

During the earliest stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, cancer patients and their family caregivers experienced some delays to care and significant stressors such as visitor restrictions, but generally noted good coping with both the pandemic as well as their cancer diagnosis and on-going treatments. Cancer patients also reported receiving multiple cancer treatments throughout the pandemic but very few were aware of any pandemic-related treatment changes. These experiences were in stark contrast to those of cancer and palliative care practitioners who noted frequent and widespread pandemic-related changes to cancer treatments along with significant related practitioner anxiety, fatigue, and moral distress.

Practice implications

These findings from the COVID-19 pandemic can inform future disaster-preparedness for institutions providing cancer care. Globally, new COVID-19 variants continue to emerge, [31, 32] and the probability of future pandemic events is increasing due to the impacts to pathogens exacerbated by globalization and climate change [33]. At the local level, cancer care can also be disrupted by natural disasters, [34] and healthcare institutions can be strained during other outbreaks of communicable diseases [35]. Our data suggest that in times when emergency measures are implemented, cancer patients and families have a high level of trust in their cancer practitioners and may cope well with some disruptions to care, such as moving visits to telehealth. However, disruptions due to visitor restrictions were negatively felt by all parties—patients, family caregivers, and providers. Visitor restriction policies were not unique to the COVID-19 pandemic and have been implemented in healthcare settings during other outbreaks of communicable disease [36]. Previous literature has highlighted ways that patients and families can be negatively impacted by being deprived of a caregiver and called for greater consideration of this impact when developing new visitor restriction policies [37, 38]. Lastly, cancer practitioners experienced moral distress and burnout in periods where they felt that they had to make triage decisions on their own. When patient volume needs to be limited, cancer care centers and hospital systems may wish to more rapidly develop and implement robust guides for navigating decision-making so as to better support front-line cancer practitioners.
