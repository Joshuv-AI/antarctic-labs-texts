Activation of the cGAS-STING Pathway by NETs Promotes Inflammatory Injury in Myocardial Ischemia-Reperfusion
Authors: Jie Jiang, Haonan Wu, Lingfeng Zhou, Sijia Wu, Yingying Li, Danli Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) is a major complication of reperfusion therapy, driven by excessive sterile inflammation. During this process, neutrophils release abundant neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which exacerbate tissue damage, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood.ObjectiveThis study tested the hypothesis that NETs activate the cGAS-STING pathway via their double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) skeleton, thereby driving inflammatory injury in MIRI.MethodsA rat MIRI model (30-min ischemia/2-h reperfusion) and H9c2 cardiomyocyte hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model were employed. NETs were isolated from PMA-stimulated neutrophils. Pharmacological inhibitors (DNase I, RU.521, C-176), siRNA-mediated cGAS knockdown, and plasmid-mediated cGAS overexpression were used to interrogate pathway engagement. Evaluated endpoints included infarct size, myocardial enzymes, NETs markers, cGAS-STING pathway protein phosphorylation, inflammatory cytokines, and reactive oxygen species.ResultsIn MIRI rats, NETs markers and cGAS-STING activation were markedly elevated, while DNase I, RU.521, or C-176 significantly reduced infarct size and inflammatory responses. In vitro, NETs directly activated the cGAS-STING/TBK1/NF-κB axis in H/R-treated cardiomyocytes, promoting cytokine release and ROS production—effects abolished by DNase I or cGAS knockdown, but potentiated by cGAS overexpression.ConclusionNETs-derived dsDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway in cardiomyocytes, driving downstream inflammatory responses that worsen MIRI. cGAS serves as a critical molecular node in this pathogenic axis, representing a promising therapeutic target for MIRI.

Introduction

Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) is a paradoxical pathological phenomenon in which restoration of blood flow to ischemic myocardium exacerbates tissue damage, representing a major determinant of poor prognosis in patients with acute myocardial infarction. Although revascularization procedures including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) effectively re-establish blood supply and rescue ischemic myocardium,1 they can themselves trigger MIRI, leading to enlarged infarct size and elevated risk of heart failure, with persistently high clinical incidence. Given the limited efficacy of anti-inflammatory strategies targeting individual downstream cytokines—such as anti-TNF-α antibodies and IL-1 receptor antagonists—in MIRI clinical trials,2 elucidating the molecular basis of MIRI and identifying actionable therapeutic targets remain clinically important.

Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulated inflammation lies at the core of MIRI pathology.3,4 Neutrophils, as major inflammatory effectors, rapidly infiltrate the reperfused myocardium and release reactive oxygen species (ROS), proteases, and cytokines, collectively exacerbating tissue damage.5 Recent studies have revealed that, beyond their classical functions, neutrophils can extrude reticular structures known as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Multiple studies have demonstrated that NETs are extensively produced during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (I/R), where they directly injure cardiomyocytes and activate immune cascades through their proteases, histones, and DNA components.6 Clinical studies have shown that in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients, culprit-site levels of dsDNA (529.8 ng/mL vs 403.7 ng/mL in peripheral blood) and citrullinated histone H3 (331.6 ng/mL vs 235.3 ng/mL) are significantly elevated, with MCP-1 levels positively correlated with NETs markers (r5 = 0.48).7 Elevated NETs levels have also been associated with poorer prognosis and increased risk of in-stent restenosis following acute myocardial infarction. Furthermore, coronary dsDNA ≥0.39 μg/mL independently predicts in-hospital major adverse cardiac events (OR = 46.26, 95% CI: 4.775–448.21, P < 0.01), with a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 53%, outperforming conventional markers such as troponin T and CK-MB.8 Therefore, interfering with NET formation or function may represent a novel strategy to attenuate inflammatory injury in MIRI.

NETs can be sensed by the cytosolic DNA sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS). Upon internalization into target cells—likely through endocytosis9—NETs translocate to the cytosol, where their DNA backbone directly activates cGAS.10 Activated cGAS then catalyzes the production of the second messenger cGAMP,11–13 which subsequently binds and activates stimulator of interferon genes (STING).14 Importantly, multiple independent studies15,16 have confirmed that cardiomyocytes express functional cGAS and STING, and that hypoxia/reoxygenation injury induces their phosphorylation and downstream signaling activation, indicating that cardiomyocytes possess the intrinsic machinery to sense cytosolic DNA and initiate innate immune responses upon ischemic injury. We therefore hypothesize that dsDNA released from NETs may engage the cardiomyocyte cGAS-STING pathway, representing a potential molecular link between neutrophil activation and myocardial inflammatory injury.

To dissect the causal role of the NETs–cGAS–STING axis in MIRI, this study employed complementary pharmacological and genetic approaches. DNase I was used to degrade the NETs DNA skeleton, allowing assessment of the specific contribution of dsDNA. RU.521,17 a selective cGAS inhibitor, competitively blocks cGAS–DNA binding and its enzymatic activity, while C-17618 irreversibly inhibits STING palmitoylation, preventing its Golgi trafficking and downstream signaling. These pharmacological tools were combined with siRNA-mediated cGAS knockdown and plasmid-driven cGAS overexpression to enable comprehensive interrogation of the pathway.

Based on this strategy, we investigated the role of the NETs–cGAS–STING axis in MIRI pathogenesis by integrating in vivo rat MIRI models with in vitro H/R-exposed H9c2 cardiomyocytes, utilizing molecular, cellular, and histopathological approaches to uncover mechanistic insights and identify potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Cell Lines

For the acquisition of experimental animals, male Sprague‑Dawley rats (body weight range: 180–220 g), free of specific pathogens, were obtained commercially from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (license No. SCXK (Beijing)2024‑0001). Housing of the animals occurred within the SPF facility of the Institute of Basic Theory for Chinese Medicine (China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences) under controlled environmental parameters: ambient temperature 22–26 °C, relative humidity 50–60%, and a 12‑h light/dark schedule, with food and water supplied ad libitum. Review and approval of the experimental protocol were provided by the Animal Ethics Committee of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (approval No. ERCCACMS21‑2406‑04). All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The ARRIVE Essential 10 checklist is provided as Supplementary Table 1.

The H9c2 cardiomyoblast cell line was obtained from HySigen Biosciences Inc. (catalog No. TCR-G607), a qualified commercial repository. Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Animal Model Establishment and Grouping

Myocardial ischemia‑reperfusion injury (MIRI) was induced as previously described by our group.19 Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (0.1 mL/100 g body weight) and endotracheally intubated, then connected to an animal ventilator. After anesthesia and endotracheal intubation, rats were ventilated. The heart was visualized through a left thoracotomy at the third and fourth intercostal spaces. A 5‑0 silk ligature was placed around the left anterior descending (LAD) artery and secured. Following a 30 min occlusion period, the knot was loosened to restore blood flow for 2 hours, thereby establishing reperfusion. For the sham‑operated animals, the suture was passed around the artery but not tied.

A total of 40 rats were equally divided into five groups (n = 8/group): (a) sham‑operated control (Sham); (b) MIRI model (Model); (c) DNase I group (100 μg/kg DNase I intraperitoneally 1 h before MIRI induction); (d) RU.521 group (2.5 mg/kg RU.521, catalog No. 312334, MedChemExpress, USA, in saline, i.p. 1 h prior to MIRI); (e) C‑176 group (6.7 mg/kg C‑176, catalog No. 240389, MedChemExpress, USA, in saline, i.p. 1 h before MIRI).

At the end of the experiments, rats were euthanized by an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (same concentration and route) for blood and tissue collection.

Neutrophil Isolation, Identification, and NETs Preparation

Percoll density gradient centrifugation was used to isolate neutrophils from whole blood of SD rats.20 Briefly, blood was diluted with dilution solution (catalog No. R1017, Solarbio, China) and layered onto a discontinuous Percoll gradient (82% and 69%, catalog No. 17-0891-01, Cytiva, USA). Centrifuged at 710 × g for 30 min (RT), the neutrophil band was collected and PBS‑washed, and subjected to red blood cell lysis (catalog No. R1010, Solarbio, China). The resulting purified neutrophils (>80% by Wright-Giemsa staining; >95% viable by trypan blue exclusion) were used for subsequent experiments.

Purity was assessed by Wright‑Giemsa staining (catalog No. G1020, Solarbio, China). Air‑dried smears were stained for 2 min, subsequently, an equal amount of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) was introduced and mixed for an additional 5 min. Smears were washed, examined microscopically, and purity >80% was confirmed. Viability was measured by mixing equal volumes of cell suspension and trypan blue (catalog No. C0011, Beyotime, China); after 3 min, viable cells were counted (>95%).

To induce neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), isolated neutrophils were adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL and seeded into 24‑well plates (8 × 104 cells/well). Sytox Green (catalog No. S7020, Invitrogen, USA) was added (final 2.5 μM). A blank control and a PMA‑stimulated group (50 nM PMA, catalog No. P1585, Sigma‑Aldrich, USA) were included. Cells were kept at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 4 h. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed typical NETs web‑like structures only in the PMA‑treated group.

Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 450×g for 10 min at 4°C. The cleared supernatant was then subjected to a second spin at 18,000×g for 15 min (4°C). The resulting pellet, containing NETs, was gently resuspended in ice‑cold PBS. Subsequently, the dsDNA content was quantified with a PicoGreen kit (catalog No. P7589, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Freshly prepared NETs were used immediately.

Cell Model Establishment and Grouping

H9c2 Cell Culture and NETs-H/R Injury Model

H9c2 cells were maintained in high‑glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with glucose‑ and serum‑free DMEM (D6540, Solarbio), and cells were placed in a hypoxic chamber (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) for 16 hours. Reoxygenation was achieved by returning the cells to complete medium under normoxic conditions (95% air, 5% CO2) for 2 h.

siRNA Transfection

Specific siRNAs were custom‑synthesized (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The siRNA sequences were: cGAS‑siRNA‑1 (GGACAAAUUGAGAUUGAAATT, UUUCAAUCUCAAUUUGUCCTT), cGAS‑siRNA‑2 (GAAUCGAGCUAGAAGAAUATT, UAUUCUUCUAGCUCGAUUCTT), cGAS‑siRNA‑3 (CGUGUGAAGUUCAAGAGAATT, UUCUCUUGAACUUCACACGTT). To transfect, H9c2 cells (1 × 105/well in 24‑well plates) were cultured for 24 h to ~50% confluence, then exposed to a mixture of 50 nM siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 (catalog No. L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were each diluted in Opti‑MEM reduced‑serum medium (catalog No. 31985070, Gibco, USA), mixed equally, incubated 20 min (RT) to form complexes, then added to cells. Six hours post‑transfection, fresh medium was added, and cells were incubated for a further 24–48 h. Knockdown efficiency was evaluated by RT‑qPCR for cGAS mRNA and by Western blot for cGAS protein. Efficiency >70% was considered acceptable.

Plasmid Overexpression

A rat cGAS overexpression plasmid (catalog No. 115442, Genomeditech, China) was used. H9c2 cells were placed into 24‑well plates (2 × 105 cells/well, 500 μL/well) of H9c2‑specific medium. At ~80% confluence (24 h), Cells were rinsed twice with PBS and subsequently maintained in 450 μL antibiotic‑free DMEM. Lipofectamine 3000 (0.75 μL; catalog No. L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and plasmid DNA (2 μg/mL) were diluted separately in 25 μL of Opti‑MEM (catalog No. 31985070, Gibco, USA), with P3000 (1 μL) added to the DNA solution. Equal volumes were then combined and incubated. Equal volumes were mixed, incubated 15 min (RT) to form complexes, then added to wells (50 μL/well). Six hours post‑transfection, fresh medium was added, and cells were allowed to grow for another 24–48 h. Validation of overexpression was carried out by RT‑qPCR (mRNA), Western blot (protein), and immunofluorescence (protein localization).

Experimental Grouping and Intervention in vitro

To examine how NETs affect H/R‑treated cardiomyocytes and the underlying molecular pathways, H9c2 cells were allocated into the following groups.

NETs intervention: Control (normal culture), H/R (16 h hypoxia/2 h reoxygenation), H/R+NETs (exogenous NETs during reoxygenation). Optimal NETs concentration (500, 1000, 1500 ng/mL) was determined by CCK‑8 (viability) and ELISA (IL‑6, LDH). For DNase I co‑treatment, DNase I was added 30 min before NETs. Optimal DNase I concentration (10–80 μg/mL) was determined by PicoGreen dsDNA assay.

cGAS knockdown: H/R+si‑NC, H/R+NETs+si‑NC, H/R+NETs+si‑cGAS.

cGAS overexpression: H/R+vector, H/R+NETs+vector, H/R+NETs+cGAS‑OE.

After treatments, cells and supernatants were collected.

Detection Indicators and Methods

Myocardial Infarct Size

At reperfusion end, hearts were excised, rinsed, frozen at −20 °C for 20 min, and cut from apex into 2‑mm slices. Slices were stained with 2% TTC staining solution (catalog No. 240001001, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C for 15 min (dark). Infarct area (gray‑white) vs non‑infarct area (red) was photographed. Infarct size (%) = (infarct area/total area) × 100 (Image‑Pro Plus).

Myocardial Enzyme Assay

Serum or cell culture supernatant was used to measure creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase isoenzyme MB (CK-MB), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (catalog No. 7080, Hitachi, Japan). All assay kits were obtained from ZhongSheng BeiKong Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China), with catalog numbers 241441 for CK, 241221 for CK-MB, and 230991 for LDH. Serum samples were used to assess myocardial injury in rats, while culture supernatants were used to evaluate H9c2 cell membrane integrity.

Immunofluorescence Double Staining (TSA) for NETs

For NETs detection, after deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections were quenched with 3% H2O2 (15 min, RT, dark), washed with PBS, then blocked with 3% BSA (30 min, RT). Primary anti‑CitH3 (1:1000, catalog No. FN240918, Fine Biotech, China) was applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing, HRP‑conjugated universal antibody (1:1000) was added (50 min, RT, dark), followed by TYR‑480 (10 min) and washes. Microwave stripping in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 15 min) was performed, then blocking was repeated. Anti‑MPO (1:100, catalog No. ab208670, Abcam, UK) was applied overnight, followed by HRP‑secondary (1:1000) and TYR‑570. DAPI counterstained nuclei. Yellow co‑localization was analyzed with Image‑Pro Plus.

Measurement of Serum dsDNA

A PicoGreen dsDNA fluorescence quantification kit (catalog No. P7589, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to measure serum dsDNA. Into 96‑well plates, 100 µL of diluted serum samples and 100 µL of diluted PicoGreen working solution were added, along with a series of standards (ranging from 1 µg/mL to 250 pg/mL). After a 5‑min incubation at room temperature in the dark, fluorescence intensity was read at excitation/emission wavelengths of 480/520 nm. Concentrations of dsDNA were derived by interpolation from the standard curve.

Detection of Inflammatory Cytokines

For immunohistochemistry, after dewaxing, rehydration, and microwave antigen retrieval (EDTA solution), endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2, followed by 5% BSA blocking. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IL‑1β, IL‑6, and TNF‑α. Subsequently, HRP‑labeled secondary antibodies were applied, and color was developed with DAB, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were acquired.

ELISA: TNF‑α (catalog No. 091145003132020912, Jianglai Biotechnology, China), IL‑1β (catalog No. 091145003208840912, Jianglai Biotechnology, China), and IL‑6 (catalog No. 091145003208960912, Jianglai Biotechnology, China) in rat serum, myocardial homogenates (ischemic tissue, 1:9 in ice‑cold PBS, homogenized, centrifuged at 3000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), or cell supernatants were measured per manufacturer’s instructions. Concentrations were derived from standard curves.

Detection of ROS Levels

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the H2DCFDA fluorescent probe (catalog No. BL714A, Lanjieke Technology, China). Following experimental treatments, H9c2 cells were incubated with 10 μM of the probe for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, then washed twice. Fluorescence was measured (ex/em 488/525 nm) or visualized under a fluorescence microscope. Results are presented as relative fluorescence intensity.

Immunofluorescence Staining of Myocardial Tissue

For immunofluorescence staining, dewaxed, rehydrated, and antigen‑retrieved sections were first treated with 3% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase, then blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min at RT. Primary antibodies against cGAS (1:200, catalog No. EPR26492‑84, Abcam, UK) and STING (1:500, catalog No. EPR13130, Abcam, UK) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted. Images were captured under a fluorescence microscope.

Western Blot

Protein was extracted from myocardial tissue or H9c2 cells and quantified using a BCA assay kit (catalog No. 03842500, Epizyme Biomedical Technology, China). Aliquots containing 20 μg of protein were loaded and separated by SDS‑PAGE (10% running gel, 5% stacking gel), then electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (0.22 μm, catalog No. 0000204509, Millipore, USA). To block non‑specific binding, membranes were incubated with 5% non‑fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti‑cGAS (1:2000, catalog No. 00151718, Proteintech, USA), rabbit anti‑STING (1:3000, catalog No. 00127828, Proteintech, USA), rabbit anti‑p‑STING (1:3000, catalog No. CS2106A, Cohesion, UK), rabbit anti‑TBK1 (1:5000, catalog No. 3504S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), rabbit anti‑p‑TBK1 (1:5000, catalog No. 5483S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti‑NF‑κB p65 (1:1000, catalog No. H680065024, Proteintech), rabbit anti‑p‑p65 (1:1000, catalog No. 3033S, Cell Signaling Technology), and rabbit anti‑β‑actin (1:10000, catalog No. AC240905002, Servicebio, China). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, catalog No. 20001400, Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (catalog No. YK378688, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged. Band intensities were analyzed with Image Lab software, and relative protein expression was normalized to β‑actin.

Total RNA was isolated from H9c2 cells using an RNA extraction kit (catalog No. A0112A, Tiangen Biotech, China). RNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0) were measured with a spectrophotometer. The thermal cycling program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15s and 60 °C for 60s. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and figure preparations were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The investigators performing animal surgeries, drug administrations, and outcome assessments were blinded to group allocation. For multi-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by post-hoc pairwise tests. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test prior to ANOVA. When variances were homogeneous, the least significant difference (LSD) method was used for post-hoc comparisons; when variances were heterogeneous, Dunnett’s T3 test was applied instead. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Measurements derived from the same animal are inherently correlated; however, one-way ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc corrections is the standard analytical approach for balanced multi-group designs in this context. All bar graphs display individual data points overlaid to facilitate visualization of data distribution.

Serum myocardial enzyme analysis revealed markedly higher levels of CK, CK‑MB, AST, LDH, cTnI, and TNNT2 in the MIRI group (P < 0.01). Further assessment of NETs formation and inflammatory responses showed that, compared with the sham-operated group, the MIRI model group had significantly higher serum and myocardial levels of the NET-associated markers CitH3, MPO, and dsDNA (P < 0.01), as well as the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (P < 0.01; Figure 2A–C). Immunofluorescence double staining detected only weak CitH3 (green) and MPO (red) signals in the sham group, with almost no co-localization. In the MIRI group, fluorescence intensities of both markers were enhanced in the infarct zone, and co-localized regions (yellow) were markedly increased, indicating extensive NET formation. DNase I intervention reduced all these signals (Figure 2D). Immunohistochemical analysis showed markedly higher integrated optical density and positive area percentages of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the MIRI infarct zone (P < 0.01). These elevations were substantially reversed by DNase I treatment (P < 0.05, P < 0.01; Figure 2E). NETs Exacerbate Inflammatory Responses in Cardiomyocytes via cGAS‑STING Pathway Activation

Neutrophils were collected from SD rat blood by Percoll density gradient centrifugation as described.20 Wright-Giemsa staining revealed light-red cytoplasm and purple, lobulated nuclei, confirming neutrophil purity >80%. To establish a stable H/R model, hypoxia duration was first screened under a fixed reoxygenation time of 2 h. CCK-8 results showed that cell viability decreased in a time-dependent manner with increasing hypoxia duration (P < 0.05, P < 0.01; Figure 3C). Intracellular IL-6 levels started to rise significantly after 10 h of hypoxia (P < 0.01) and peaked at 16 h. LDH release increased markedly after 16 h of hypoxia (P < 0.01; Figure 3D). Further optimization of reoxygenation time under fixed 16 h hypoxia showed that both IL-6 expression and LDH release were significantly higher at 2 h reoxygenation than at 4, 6, or 8 h (P < 0.01; Figure 3E). Therefore, 16 h hypoxia/2 h reoxygenation was used for subsequent experiments.

The optimal NETs concentration was assessed by treating H9c2 cells with 500, 1000, or 1500 ng/mL NETs after H/R. Concentrations of 1000 ng/mL and 1500 ng/mL significantly elevated ROS, LDH, and IL-6 levels in H/R-exposed cardiomyocytes (P < 0.01; Figure 3F), whereas 1000 ng/mL did not substantially reduce cell viability; thus, 1000 ng/mL was chosen. Exogenous NETs (1000 ng/mL) were then added to the H/R model to create the NETs‑H/R injury model. Compared with the H/R group, the H/R+NETs group showed significantly lower cell viability (P < 0.05), increased LDH release (P < 0.01), elevated intracellular ROS production (P < 0.01), and higher levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (P < 0.05, P < 0.01). Degradation of the NETs DNA skeleton with DNase I significantly ameliorated all these parameters (P < 0.05, P < 0.01; Figure 3G), indicating that NETs exacerbate H/R-induced cardiomyocyte injury via their DNA skeleton.

Discussion

Sterile inflammation is a key component of MIRI pathogenesis, and its magnitude and persistence directly influence the degree of myocardial damage and subsequent repair.21 The inflammatory response induced by ischemia-reperfusion injury is characterized by excessive recruitment of neutrophils and pro-inflammatory monocytes to the infarct zone. Neutrophils are already activated during the ischemic phase; upon reperfusion, their excessive accumulation and activation lead to the release of NETs, which in turn promote ROS production and the secretion of inflammatory mediators and chemokines, further recruiting immune cells to amplify the inflammatory response, impairing myocardial perfusion, and consequently exacerbating MIRI—ultimately establishing a vicious cycle of myocardial injury.22,23

In line with previous reports demonstrating that NETs burden correlates with infarct size and left ventricular dysfunction in STEMI patients,7 and that DNase I-mediated NETs degradation attenuates myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury in murine models,22,23 our rat MIRI model exhibited significantly elevated myocardial and serum levels of the NET markers CitH3, MPO, and dsDNA, which correlated positively with the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Notably, degradation of the NETs DNA skeleton with DNase I markedly reduced these inflammatory parameters and ameliorated myocardial injury. These data not only confirm abundant NET formation during MIRI but also directly demonstrate that NETs actively drive the inflammatory response. In vitro, exogenous NETs stimulation in H/R-treated cardiomyocytes upregulated cGAS and STING expression at both mRNA and protein levels, enhanced STING phosphorylation, and activated downstream TBK1/NF-κB signaling, accompanied by elevated inflammatory cytokine release and ROS production—effects that were abrogated by DNase I pretreatment. These findings indicate that dsDNA released from NETs is directly recognized by cardiomyocyte cGAS, activating the cGAS-STING pathway and thereby coupling extracellular inflammatory stimuli with intracellular innate immune responses. Collectively, our results position the NETs–cGAS-STING axis as a critical upstream initiator of inflammatory injury in MIRI.

The cGAS-STING pathway, as a cytosolic DNA-sensing cascade, plays an indispensable role in initiating innate immune responses.24 Upon recognition of aberrant cytosolic DNA, cGAS catalyzes the synthesis of the second messenger cGAMP, which subsequently binds to and activates STING. Activated STING then recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and initiates downstream signaling cascades that converge on two major transcriptional hubs: the TBK1-IRF3 axis,25 responsible for type I interferon production, and the TBK1-IKK-NF-κB axis, which drives the transcriptional activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines.26 Given that sterile inflammation is a hallmark of MIRI pathophysiology and NF-κB serves as a central transcriptional hub in the inflammatory response, the present study focused on the NF-κB arm of cGAS-STING signaling. Mechanistically, STING activation promotes the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 via the IKK complex (IKKα/IKKβ), leading to the transcriptional upregulation of genes encoding IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. In inflammatory diseases, cGAS recognizes NET-derived dsDNA, activates STING and its downstream NF-κB signaling, triggering the production of these pro-inflammatory cytokines that amplify the inflammatory response.27–29 Accumulating evidence indicates that during MIRI, DNA released from injured cardiomyocytes and infiltrating immune cells activates the cGAS-STING pathway, amplifying both local and systemic inflammation and exacerbating myocardial tissue injury.30,31

Pharmacological inhibition with RU.521 (a cGAS inhibitor) and C-176 (a STING inhibitor) in MIRI rats significantly reduced infarct size and tissue damage, and lowered serum and myocardial inflammatory cytokine levels. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that RU.521 reduces infarct size in murine MIRI models and that STING inhibition improves post-infarction cardiac function, further supporting a close association between cGAS-STING activation and MIRI severity. Genetic manipulation of cGAS expression further supported its necessity in NETs-induced inflammatory signaling. cGAS knockdown significantly attenuated NETs-induced upregulation of downstream pathway components and cytokines, indicating that cGAS plays a key role in STING-TBK1-NF-κB axis activation. Conversely, in cGAS-overexpressing cells, the H/R+NETs group showed significantly enhanced pathway activation and cytokine levels compared with the H/R group. These loss-of-function and gain-of-function results collectively support cGAS as a critical molecular node in NETs-driven inflammatory responses in cardiomyocytes. Notably, even with cGAS knockdown or pharmacological inhibition, NETs still induced a residual inflammatory response (though not statistically significant), suggesting that combination therapies targeting cGAS-STING alongside other DNA/damage-sensing pathways (such as TLR9 and AIM2) may achieve more optimal therapeutic outcomes.

In H/R-treated cardiomyocytes, exogenous NETs increased cGAS and STING mRNA and protein levels, and enhanced STING phosphorylation; DNase I pretreatment abolished these effects, further supporting the essential role of the dsDNA component in pathway activation.32 Indeed, NETs treatment significantly elevated the p-TBK1/TBK1 and p-p65/p65 ratios in cardiomyocytes, accompanied by substantial release of the NF-κB downstream cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, indicating that the TBK1-NF-κB axis is a predominant effector pathway in this model. This observation aligns with the sterile-inflammation-dominated nature of MIRI and provides a clear direction for future targeted interventions. Notably, the present study did not assess IRF3 phosphorylation or type I interferon expression; whether this pathway arm contributes to NETs-induced cardiomyocyte injury remains to be determined in future investigations.

Targeting cGAS offers the advantage of acting at the most upstream level of the signaling cascade, blocking cGAMP production at the source and thereby simultaneously inhibiting all downstream effector pathways (including both the NF-κB and IRF3 arms), potentially achieving broader suppressive effects. However, as a general sensor for dsDNA from various sources, long-term cGAS inhibition may interfere with immune surveillance against pathogen-derived or mitochondrial DNA. In contrast, STING targeting acts at a more focused node, primarily blocking cGAMP-mediated signaling while preserving upstream DNA recognition by cGAS, which may confer a better safety profile. However, STING exhibits structural differences across species, and the selectivity and specificity of its inhibitors require more careful evaluation. In the present study, RU.521 and C-176 showed comparable efficacy in reducing infarct size, suggesting that blocking any critical node of this signaling axis may yield similar therapeutic benefits in acute inflammatory intervention. Which strategy offers advantages in terms of long-term safety, therapeutic window, and combination therapy remains to be determined through systematic head-to-head comparative studies.

Of note, current therapeutic strategies are pivoting from targeting apoptosis alone towards blocking multiple independent, non-apoptotic regulated cell death pathways, as MIRI injury is driven by overlapping death networks including necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis.33,34 Our finding that NETs activate the cGAS-STING/NF-κB axis—which is known to prime the NLRP3 inflammasome and promote IL-1β/TNF-α release—suggests that this pathway may also contribute to pyroptosis35 and necroptosis36 in MIRI. However, the partial rescue observed with DNase I and STING inhibitors implies the involvement of parallel death pathways, such as TLR-mediated signals. Thus, while targeting cGAS-STING holds promise, future studies are warranted to dissect its specific contributions to each death modality and to explore combinatorial strategies that simultaneously intercept multiple cell death pathways.

Study Limitations and Future Perspectives

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the observation that neither DNase I, RU.521, nor C-176 completely eliminated inflammatory injury suggests that the pathogenic effects of NETs are not exclusively mediated by the cGAS-STING pathway, and that parallel pathways are likely involved. Beyond providing dsDNA to activate cGAS, NET components—including histones that directly activate NF-κB via TLR437 and granule proteins such as MPO and neutrophil elastase that damage cell membranes and induce oxidative stress38,39—may contribute independently to tissue injury. Moreover, extracellular dsDNA may engage other DNA sensors such as TLR9 and AIM2.40 These parallel mechanisms may partially compensate for cGAS-STING blockade, thereby limiting the efficacy of single-target strategies. Second, while our in vitro data demonstrate cardiomyocyte-autonomous cGAS-STING activation, the in vivo context likely involves complex interactions between cardiomyocytes and immune cells, with paracrine and immune amplification loops potentially contributing to the overall inflammatory response.41 Third, the clinical translation of therapeutic strategies targeting NETs or the cGAS-STING pathway is confronted with several objective challenges, including increased susceptibility to infection due to immunosuppression, potential impairment of cardiac repair, a narrow hyper-acute therapeutic window, and the presence of redundant parallel damage pathways. Nevertheless, these challenges are not insurmountable Emerging nanoparticle-based delivery systems—such as NET-targeted DNase I-conjugated nanoparticles (TNP-DN1) or neutrophil-targeted PAD4 inhibitor nanoplatforms42–45—hold promise for dissociating therapeutic efficacy from off-target immunosuppressive effects by enabling spatiotemporally controlled intervention precisely within the hyper-acute reperfusion window.

Future studies should systematically dissect the relative contributions of parallel DNA-sensing pathways—including TLR9 and AIM2—to NETs-induced myocardial inflammation, ideally through combinatorial pharmacological or genetic blockade. Investigations using extended reperfusion windows and conditional knockout models are warranted to clarify the long-term consequences of cGAS-STING inhibition on cardiac remodeling and to distinguish cardiomyocyte-autonomous effects from immune-mediated amplification loops.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, neutrophils release abundant NETs, which activate the cGAS-STING pathway in cardiomyocytes, trigger downstream NF-κB signaling, and drive the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and reactive oxygen species, ultimately exacerbating myocardial inflammatory injury. Inhibiting excessive NET formation and intervening in cGAS-STING pathway activation may represent potential therapeutic targets for MIRI.
