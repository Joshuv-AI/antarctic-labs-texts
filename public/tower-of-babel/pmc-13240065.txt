The Biosynthesis and Functions of Flavonoids: Recent Advances From Studies Across Land Plant Diversity
Authors: Kevin M. Davies, Nick W. Albert, Rebecca M. Yorker, Kathy E. Schwinn, Yanfei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Over the last decade there have been significant advances in genome sequencing and model species development for ferns, lycophytes, and the bryophyte lineages—mosses, liverworts, and hornworts. This has facilitated research on the biosynthesis and function of flavonoids in these non‐seed land‐plant lineages. Most studies have been on the liverwort model species Marchantia polymorpha (Marchantia). There has been extensive characterisation of biosynthetic and regulatory genes of the Marchantia flavonoid pathway, including generation of loss‐of‐function mutant lines to examine flavonoid contribution to tolerance of abiotic stresses and pathogen infection. Notably, the red pigments of liverworts were shown to be a new class of flavonoids, named ‘auronidins’. There are relatively few studies on mosses, lycophytes, or ferns. Yet these lineages also contain distinct red pigment structures not found in seed plants. They also contain novel enzymatic activities, unique horizontal gene transfer events, and expanded gene families for proteins such as the polyphenol oxidases. Additionally, the hornworts have been shown to have lost the flavonoid pathway during lineage‐specific evolution. Indeed, evidence suggests that aspects of flavonoid biosynthesis may have been lost and regained on multiple occasions, in different lineages, during land plant evolution. In this review, we summarise recent advances in understanding of flavonoid biosynthesis in non‐seed plants and examine how this informs theories of the evolution of the flavonoid pathway across the land‐plant lineages.

Introduction

Flavonoids are small, specialised metabolites produced as part of the phenylpropanoid pathway that contribute to many aspects of plant interactions with the abiotic and biotic environment. Reflecting this, the flavonoid pathway is one of the best‐characterised aspects of plant metabolism. There are more than ten thousand articles published each year on the flavonoid pathway of seed plants, including the bioactivities of flavonoids in the human diet. However, flavonoids are present in almost all land‐plant lineages, not only seed plants. Thus, the biosynthetic pathway is thought to have arisen early during land‐plant evolution. Yet it is only recently that more research attention has been paid to the biosynthesis, regulation, and function of flavonoids in non‐seed plants. These studies are uncovering both conserved and divergent aspects of the pathway across land‐plant lineages. The data from across land plants challenge previously accepted views on flavonoid evolution and cellular function. This is part of an increased recognition of the genetic diversity and distinctness of bryophytes (Dong et al. 2025). In this article, we highlight some of these recent discoveries and their implications for our understanding of the evolution of flavonoid biosynthesis and regulation and the function of flavonoids in abiotic and biotic stress tolerance.

Non‐seed plant lineages include vascular lineages such as lycophytes and monilophytes (ferns and allies), as well as the bryophytes, which represent a monophyletic lineage of non‐vascular plants (Panstruga et al. 2025). Flavonoid‐related studies on the lycophytes and monilophytes are generally lacking, except for some limited data on ferns. However, there are now several genome sequences and developing model species for ferns that should assist future research (e.g., Li et al. 2018; Marchant et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2025; Song et al. 2025). Most genetic and functional data on the flavonoid pathway in non‐seed plants is available on bryophytes. Bryophytes, comprised of the hornwort, liverwort, and moss plant groups, are important study subjects for several reasons. As the sister lineage to vascular plants, separating soon after the emergence of land plants 515–475 million years ago (Bowman 2022), they are key in comparative evolutionary studies. They are non‐tracheophytes with plant forms and lifecycles distinct to those of seed plants; also, the haploid gametophyte is dominant in bryophyte lifecycles. Liverworts and mosses have the largest habitat distribution of all land plants, being the prevailing flora in polar regions. In particular, the Sphagnum bogs of the far north latitudes are key ecosystems in relation to terrestrial carbon storage (Limpens et al. 2011). There are many bryophyte species that have adapted to varied extreme environments, not only in the polar regions. These include numerous species classified as ‘resurrection plants’ that can survive long periods of desiccation (Marks et al. 2025). While most tracheophytes lost desiccation tolerance in their vegetative tissues, with the development of desiccation‐tolerant seeds, many bryophytes retained vegetative desiccation tolerance during evolution (Zhang et al. 2024). Additionally, the flavonoid pathway evolved under a different mix of evolutionary pressures in bryophytes than in seed plants, as bryophytes lack many of the organs of seed plants in which flavonoids have well‐characterised key functions—such as pollen, seed, and roots. Moreover, the hornworts are the only land plants known to lack the flavonoid pathway (Schafran et al. 2025).

During the last twenty years, Marchantia polymorpha (Marchantia hereafter) has been established as a powerful model system for studies of liverworts (Bowman et al. 2022). Correspondingly, most genetic, biochemical, and molecular studies on flavonoids in non‐seed plants have been conducted on Marchantia. Indeed, a research priority is to examine flavonoid biosynthesis and function in other bryophytes, such as the moss model species Physcomitrium patens (Rensing et al. 2020) and, given the great diversity across the moss lineage, diverged genera such as Sphagnum (Healey et al. 2023) and Ceratodon purpureus (Kollar et al. 2021). The recent publication of 123 additional bryophyte sequences will greatly assist sequence‐based analyses (Dong et al. 2025).

Most studies on flavonoids in bryophytes fall into two types: phylogenetic analyses to identify candidate orthologous flavonoid biosynthetic and regulatory genes (Bowman et al. 2017; Cheng et al. 2018; Piatkowski et al. 2020; Davies et al. 2022) or chemical characterisation (Asakawa et al. 2013). Complementing these, there are a small number of recent publications on flavonoid functionality supported by loss‐of‐function or gain‐of‐function plant lines, or in vitro activity characterisation of biosynthetic enzymes (Han et al. 2014; Albert et al. 2018; Clayton et al. 2018; Berland et al. 2019; Li et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2020; Furudate et al. 2023). The physiological studies are restricted to Marchantia, while the enzymatic activity studies are on the closely related thalloid liverworts Marchantia emarginata, Marchantia paleacea, and Plagiochasma appendiculatum. Despite the importance of P. patens as a model for moss biology studies, there are few similar studies to date on that species. Most notable are the analysis of knockout mutants for the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic enzyme CYP98 (Xin et al. 2023) and a study on the evolution of the biosynthetic steps to flavones and flavonols (Li et al. 2020). There is also a lack of data on potential flavonoid functions in sporophytes of bryophytes or gametophytes of ferns and lycophytes. Many bryophytes have deeply pigmented sporophytes (Figure 1), and there is evidence for conserved production of colourless flavonoids for abiotic stress tolerance in pollen and spores (Xue et al. 2023). Overall, for non‐seed plants, while numbers and quality of genome sequence resources are rapidly expanding, functional studies remain scarce.

Proposing what the flavonoid biosynthetic and regulatory toolkit looked like in the Last Common Ancestor (LCA) of land plants is complicated by the emerging picture of extensive lineage‐specific gene losses and gains during embryophyte evolution. What was previously viewed as the canonical flavonoid pathway should be revisited: perhaps only the early phenylpropanoid genes and the biosynthetic genes for flavones were present in the LCA. For example, the otherwise conserved gene for the second flavonoid‐specific enzyme, CHALCONE ISOMERASE (CHI), is absent in mosses and in almost all hornwort species examined (Cheng et al. 2018; Schafran et al. 2025). The hornwort example, however, also illustrates the potential pitfalls of developing proposals from data for a few model species, such as Arabidopsis, Marchantia, P. patens, and the hornwort model genus Anthoceros. An examination across the hornwort diversity, with ten whole‐genome sequences representing all hornwort families and most of the genera, found a single canonical CHI orthologue (Schafran et al. 2025). This was in the single species of the monotypic hornwort class Leiosporocerotopsida, which diverged from other hornworts >300 million years ago. This supports the proposal that CHI was inherited by all land‐plant lineages but lost in most hornworts and mosses. In contrast, genes encoding FLAVONE SYNTHASE (FNS) activities may have arisen independently in several different lineages (Li et al. 2020). Enzymes have arisen, probably from a common ancestral gene, that can have FNS, FLAVANONE 2‐HYDROXYLASE, FLAVANONE 3‐HYDROXYLASE, or FLAVONOL SYNTHASE activities, or a combination of these. Additionally, FNS enzymes have arisen independently from two enzyme classes in angiosperms: the 2‐oxoglutarate‐dependent dioxygenases and cytochrome P450 mono‐oxygenases (Martens and Mithöfer 2005).

A useful dataset for helping to discern between ancestral and derived states for the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as the absence of flavonoids in hornworts, would be from comparisons with the Zygnematophycean algae, the sister lineage of land plants. This would require chemical identification of specialised metabolites in Zygnematophytes, especially those with terrestrial lifestyles, followed by characterisation of the associated biosynthetic pathways. Such studies could also indicate which functions, such as screening harmful light or modifying cell‐wall properties, evolved early on in plant evolution prior to the split of tracheophytes and bryophytes. The genome sequences of several Zygnematophycean species have been interrogated for phenylpropanoid pathway genes, and strong candidates for the core steps prior to the flavonoid‐specific branch have been identified (Fürst‐Jansen et al. 2020; Jiao et al. 2020; Piatkowski et al. 2020; de Vries and Feussner 2024). There have also been some initial metabolomic studies on algal species that have found the production a range of flavonoids (Jiao et al. 2020), and transcriptomics studies on algal species that have light‐induced red/purple pigmentation (Remias et al. 2012; Davies et al. 2022; Busch et al. 2024). However, challenges include the difficulty of structural identification of cell‐wall‐bound pigments and the lack of good Zygnematophycean model species (Carrillo‐Carrasco et al. 2025). The data published to date suggest that the phenylpropanoid pathway is not specifically a land plant innovation but rather found in different lineages within the green lineage (de Vries et al. 2021; de Vries and Feussner 2024). However, additional studies are required to help determine which pathway components have a common origin and which arose independently on multiple occasions, including potential independent Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) events.

Comparison of Marchantia and Arabidopsis does indicate that one section of flavonoid biosynthesis, function, and regulation is conserved across most land‐plant lineages and may have been inherited from the LCA: a UV RESISTANCE LOCUS8 (UVR8)‐regulated gene module for tolerance of UVB‐light exposure through production of vacuolar‐located flavone‐ or flavonol‐glycosides. Both the regulatory module of UVR8/REPRESSOR OF UV‐B PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS1 (RUP1)/ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5) and the core flavonoid biosynthetic genes to the direct flavone precursors (flavanones) are strongly conserved (Clayton et al. 2018; Ferreyra et al. 2021; Martínez‐Abaigar and Núñez‐Olivera 2022). There is a caveat associated with drawing wide conclusions from studies of only two species, because comparative data, such as for loss‐of‐function mutants, are lacking for other plant lineages. Notwithstanding, gene expression data support a function of flavonoids and/or other small phenolics in UVB‐tolerance for mosses (Wolf et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2021). Hornworts, which lack any flavonoids, do not appear to be unusually sensitive to increased UV‐B light exposure (Soriano et al. 2018, and the current authors’ unpublished studies). The UV‐B light screening could come from constitutively produced phenolics found in the cell wall, such as cinnamic acids and lignans. Indeed, it is possible that hornworts evolved to emphasise biosynthesis of cell‐wall localised phenolics, as hornwort genome sequences lack genes for UDP‐GLYCOSYLTRANSFERASES (UGTs) that contain the Plant Secondary Product Glycosyltransferase (PSPG) motif (Caygill and Dolan 2023; Schafran et al. 2025). In other land plants, these UGTs add glycosyl groups to phenolics to increase water solubility and facilitate accumulation in the vacuole.

Flavonoids and Abiotic Stress Tolerance Across Land Plant Lineages

There are extensive data accumulated over many years on the functions of flavonoids in tolerance of environmental stressors, both abiotic and biotic. Yet, apart from flavones/flavonols and UVB‐tolerance, few consistent examples across land plants are emerging for flavonoid function and the associated biosynthetic pathway evolution. Although some functions may be conserved—such as screening of Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR) by red pigments (Landi et al. 2015; Davies et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2024)—the compounds responsible and associated genes may have evolved independently on multiple occasions. Non‐seed plants show diverse patterns of red pigmentation (Figure 1), with both developmentally and environmentally regulated production. Red flavonoid pigments have been associated with a great array of patterns of production and proposed associated functions, serving as a good exemplar of the current state of knowledge. The anthocyanin pigments have been identified as contributing positively to tolerance of excess PAR, cold, drought, nutrient imbalance, and pathogen attack. However, almost all such studies have been on flowering plants (angiosperms). Until recently, the studies on non‐angiosperms commonly involved observations of visible phenotypes and assignment of function by comparison to data from angiosperms. For example, liverwort, moss, and fern species may produce red pigmentation during the bright but cold days of autumn, and this has been presumed to have a similar function to the autumnal production of anthocyanins in leaves (e.g., Hooijmaijers and Gould 2007; Bonnett et al. 2010), but functional assays are lacking. There has been much recent debate on the function(s) of anthocyanins during autumn colouration of the leaves of deciduous plants (Renner and Zohner 2019; Agati et al. 2021; Hughes et al. 2022). Two of the main theories that have emerged are: antioxidant activity for reducing free radical accumulation, and signalling to biological organisms. These may not be relevant when considering the insoluble cell wall‐bound pigments of mosses and liverworts (Zhou et al. 2024; Jibran et al. 2024). The need for experimental data on the functions of red pigments in non‐angiosperms is further emphasised by the recent studies highlighting that the flavonoid pigment structures and associated cellular properties may be quite different between tracheophyte plants and bryophytes.

So, what are some of the confirmed functions of red pigments in bryophytes? The pigments for which most data are available are the auronidins of liverworts. Auronidins are generally cell‐wall located, where they form polymers that alter wall properties: the presence of auronidins increases wall resistance to enzymatic digestion (Jibran et al. 2024) and reduces pathogen infection (Carella et al. 2019). Auronidins also contribute to general abiotic stress tolerance, perhaps through screening PAR and being a sink for excess cellular carbon (Zhou et al. 2024). A comparison of the stress responses and associated plant fitness of wild‐type Marchantia with plant lines carrying a mutation that prevents production of auronidin pigments confirmed the importance of the pigments for stress‐tolerance capabilities (Zhou et al. 2024). Under experimental conditions generating the largest stress response, nutrient imbalance combined with excess PAR, plants with induction of auronidin production were able to survive and re‐grow when the stress was removed, but the mutant plants lacking auronidin production died. When many different potential stressors were compared, the ones which most strongly induced auronidin production were those that could result in an excess of carbon supply over nitrogen availability. These observations on the liverwort model species support research on angiosperms that indicate a key function of flavonoid pigment induction could be as a sink for excess carbon, such as when normal photosynthetic pathways are perturbed (Lo Piccolo et al. 2020; Davies et al. 2022; Kitao et al. 2024).

Whether auronidins provide the same function in other liverwort species has yet to be tested. Nor is it clear if the red pigments of mosses, 3‐deoxyanthocyanins and sphagnorubins, provide similar cellular benefits, or if hornworts are disadvantaged by an absence of red pigmentation. Observational data do support a shared function for red pigments across mosses and liverworts, as many species have stress‐induced red pigmentation when strong PAR is present in conjunction with a secondary stressor, such as cold, drought, or nutrient imbalance (Waterman et al. 2018; Tan et al. 2023; Krishnamoorthi et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024). There are strikingly strong pigment phenotypes among some of the liverworts that grow directly on rock surfaces open to full sunlight (Figure 1), although whether these are the same auronidin structures as found in Marchantia is not known. The red pigmentation from 3‐deoxyanthocyanins of the water fern Azolla is also particularly strong when days are cold but bright (Cohen et al. 2002a,b; Cannavò et al. 2023). However, direct experimental evidence on red pigment function is lacking outside angiosperms and Marchantia.

A possibility we propose is that all land‐plant lineages (except perhaps hornworts) evolved a red pigment that could screen PAR and sequester excess carbon, responding to the evolutionary driver of photoprotection common across land plants, but lineage‐specific evolution led to different red flavonoid structures that have additional advantageous properties. In the case of the cell‐wall‐bound auronidins and sphagnorubins, the additional advantage could come through replicating some of the cell‐wall‐modifying properties of lignins for increasing the wall resistance to enzymatic digestion. As with anthocyanins, there may also be specific functions that have evolved in only a few species. For example, some moss species use red pigmentation in the sporophyte to help attract flies for aiding spore dispersal (Marino et al. 2009; deRegnier et al. 2025), and 3‐deoxyanthocyanins in Azolla may be both part of the signalling pathway to the symbiotic cyanobacterium Nostoc, and anti‐feedants (Cohen et al. 2002a,b; Güngör et al. 2024).

Reassessment of the Biosynthetic Gene Types Involved in Flavonoid Biosynthesis

The availability of genome sequences for non‐seed plants and initial functional studies has revealed that some of the classes of biosynthetic genes prominent in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis in non‐seed plants differ from those characterised from angiosperms. There are differences in both individual biosynthetic activities and in the gene families that have undergone extensive duplication and neofunctionalisation. However, a caveat is that some of the proteins identified in bryophytes may also have similar previously uncharacterised functions in angiosperms. Davies and André (2023) and Davies et al. (2024) examined the number of genes in major biosynthetic gene families for model species of each of the seven major land‐plant lineages. While many major biosynthetic enzyme families, such as the Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, 2‐oxogluturate‐dependent dioxygenases, and UGTs, generally followed the pattern of increasing gene number from bryophytes to lycophytes to seed plants, there were some marked variations among the different lineages. Notably, the Aldo–keto reductase, Polyketide synthase, Dirigent, and Polyphenol Oxidase (PPO) families are largest in bryophytes and form a much larger proportion of the total gene numbers of those species. Hornworts show additional variations, such as the complete absence of the PSGP‐UGT gene family. Davies and André (2023) examined a single species for each lineage, and Davies et al. (2024) added an additional liverwort and moss species. Here, we examine a single enzyme group, PPOs, using some of the bryophyte whole‐genome sequences published since those analyses.

PPOs are copper‐containing enzymes widely distributed in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes, which may have monooxygenase and hydroxylase activities on monophenols and/or o‐diphenols as substrates (Meitil et al. 2025). The PPO gene family is one of the most expanded biosynthetic gene families in bryophytes relative to tracheophytes (Davies et al. 2024). This is well illustrated by two of the leading plant model species: Arabidopsis has no PPO genes, while Marchantia has 71. Our analysis of selected genomes across the liverwort phylogeny did not indicate any general association of PPO gene family number and phylogenetic grouping, except perhaps small gene families in the Haplomitriopsida, the sister group of the other liverworts, comprised of the genera Haplomitrium, Treubia, and Apotreubia. For 19 other species examined, the PPO gene family numbers ranged from four (Riccia fluitans) to 71 (Marchantia). The moss species examined showed a similar variation in gene family size, from five in Sphagnum fallax to 19 in Pohlia nutans. This trend is mirrored in hornworts, where PPO numbers across genomes examined for four species ranged from 10 in Leiosporoceros dussii to 41 in Anthoceros punctatus. This suggests significant gene losses and gains during evolution within each individual lineage of bryophytes.

Few functions have been assigned to the PPOs of bryophytes, but the gene numbers and structural variety suggest a range of functionalities. The only well‐characterised activity so far is for biosynthesis of the flavonoid aurones, part of the auronidin biosynthetic route. PPOs are also responsible for aurone biosynthesis in angiosperms, but this seems to be from convergent evolution. The AUREUSIDIN SYNTHASE (AUS) enzymes of angiosperms are located to either the vacuole or chloroplast, while the Marchantia enzyme is thought to be located in the cell wall (Furudate et al. 2023). Indeed, in silico analysis predicts that many Marchantia PPO genes are cell wall‐located, yet this is not known for angiosperms. P. patens also has PPOs potentially targeted to the cell wall and released extracellularly. Based on loss‐of‐function mutant studies, the P. patens extracellular PPOs may have functions in modifying the local environment around bryophytes (Richter et al. 2012). On a much larger scale, phenolic release and oxidative enzymes play significant roles in formation and maintenance of Sphagnum bogs (McGivern et al. 2024). Until recently, almost all PPOs reported contain a C‐terminal ‘shield’ domain that blocks the active site until it is cleaved, typically upon delivery to the desired site of activity. The only known exceptions were secreted fungal PPOs having o‐methoxy phenolase activity (PPO type‐1(f) in the classification of Meitil et al. (2025). However, analysis of the Marchantia genome revealed 24 PPO Type‐1 genes that lack this C‐domain—hereafter referred to as short‐form PPOs (Table 1). Similar genes are found in other liverwort species, hornworts, and mosses. Notably, some liverworts, such as Riccia fluitans and Riccia sorocarpa, contain only the short‐form PPOs (Table 1). Recent work has also suggested that plant PPOs may not have arisen solely through vertical inheritance in eukaryotes. Phylogenetic analyses of PPO sequences from bacteria and eukaryotes indicate origin of some plant PPOs via bacterial–plant horizontal gene transfer early during land plant evolution (Llorente et al. 2016). Our results are consistent with this hypothesis, as the short‐form Marchantia PPOs cluster more closely with bacterial and fungal sequences than with other plant PPOs (Figure 2). Further analyses that, including a broader sampling of bryophyte PPOs would help to clarify this pattern. If this is correct, the large expansion and diversity of PPOs in bryophytes such as Marchantia, including the presence of short‐form PPOs, could reflect not only gene duplication within lineages but also subsequent diversification following the relatively early evolutionary acquisition of an ancestral bacterial gene.

Non‐Enzymatic Proteins Have Key Roles in Flavonoid Biosynthesis

Recent comparative genomic and biosynthetic studies on bryophytes and angiosperms have suggested a greater role for non‐enzymatic proteins in plant specialised metabolism than previously recognised. This has coincided with new functional studies on what have been termed “accessory” proteins (reviewed in Dastmalchi 2021). Here we highlight three gene families, for DIRIGENT proteins (DIRs), CHI and CHALCONE ISOMERASE‐LIKE (CHIL), and PATHOGENESIS RELATED‐10 proteins (PR10).

Dirigent Proteins and Phenylpropanoid Biosynthesis

Throughout land plant evolution, the phenylpropanoid pathway has diversified as adaptations arose in response to the challenges and opportunities of different ecological niches. One of the most abundant molecules in nature is lignin, providing strength and rigidity to plant cell walls for structural support, waterproofing, and tolerance against pathogens and pests. As non‐tracheophytes, lignin is lacking within bryophytes, although auronidins or other uncharacterised phenolic polymers could replicate some lignin functions (Carella et al. 2019; Jibran et al. 2024). However, lignans are present in some bryophytes. These are small dimeric phenolics of sporadic occurrence in angiosperms that share some precursors and biosynthetic commonalities with lignin, notably phenoxy radical coupling reactions in which bonds are formed between monolignol radicals via oxidation by laccases or peroxidases (Paniagua 2017; Hano et al. 2021; Meng et al. 2023). In angiosperms, lignans can have anti‐microbial, anti‐viral, or anti‐fungal properties. Lignans and/or neolignans are abundant and constitutive in hornworts and have been reported for some moss and liverwort species (Kulshrestha et al. 2022), but studies of associated functions are lacking. Neither have biosynthetic pathways to lignans been elucidated for bryophytes. However, in angiosperms cell wall‐localised DIR proteins are key for lignan formation and may well contribute to lignan biosynthesis in bryophytes.

Although DIRs were first characterised in association with lignans and lignin, it is becoming clear that they have varied functions in plant specialised metabolism. Based on protein structures, it has been predicted that all DIRs bind and stabilize quinone methide intermediates, while guiding regio‐ and/or stereo‐specificity (Meng et al. 2023). Known functions include a role in the biosynthesis of sesquiterpene‐derived phenolic (+)‐gossypol (Paniagua et al. 2017; Meng et al. 2023) and of the strigolactone hormones (Homma et al. 2024). The first DIRs characterised were proposed to be non‐enzymatic, rather directing the regio‐ and stereo‐selectivity of the phenoxy coupling reactions to ensure that a single target product is consistently formed (Paniagua et al. 2017). More recently characterised functions indicate more classical enzymatic activities (Gasper et al. 2016). Moreover, genomic and biosynthetic studies are suggesting the DIR gene family may have greatly expanded functions in bryophyte specialised metabolism, including for flavonoids and other phenylpropanoids (Davies et al. 2024). Gene expression data suggest DIR functions in auronidin and bibenzyl biosynthesis (Berland et al. 2019; Carella et al. 2019; Romani et al. 2020), but any biosynthetic reactions are uncharacterised. For lignans in angiosperms, DIRs do not have classical enzymatic function. They may also have analogous functions in phenoxy coupling reactions during lignin formation. Related to this, a function in ring‐formation and polymerization of auronidin in liverworts is plausible based on the predicted biosynthetic reactions. Given the large and structurally diverse DIR families found in bryophytes, it is probable that they have diverse functions in specialised metabolism.

Chalcone Isomerase and Chalcone Isomerase‐Like

Two proteins associated with flavonoid biosynthesis are CHI and CHIL, which both evolved from Fatty Acid Binding Proteins (FABPs). CHIL is found in all land‐plant lineages examined, and CHI in all except mosses and most hornwort species. Based on sequence conservation and intron‐exon patterns, all land plant CHIL and CHI genes appear to be derived from genes that neofunctionalised before the land plant LCA (Schafran et al. 2025). However, it is only recently that CHI and CHIL functions have been further elucidated. No catalytic activity is assigned to CHIL. Rather, it assists CHALCONE SYNTHASE (CHS), which conducts the first committed step of flavonoid biosynthesis to produce naringenin chalcone (NC). CHS belongs to a large family of polyketide synthases in land plants, which have neofunctionalised in different lineages and species to catalyse numerous different reactions in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. CHIL binding to CHS can rectify CHS's promiscuous activity to direct production of the target product (Waki et al. 2021; Wolf‐Saxon et al. 2023). Given the ubiquity of CHIL genes and the similar reduced flavonoid phenotypes in chil mutants of both Marchantia and angiosperms (Clayton et al. 2018; Miyahara et al. 2018), CHIL assistance of the CHS reactions may be a character that arose early during land plant evolution.

Questions remain around the evolution and function of CHI. NC can spontaneously cyclise to the flavanone naringenin, and this occurs readily in vitro, particularly in alkaline conditions (Zhou et al. 2025). It was thought that the incomplete loss of flavonoids observed in some chi mutants could reflect spontaneous cyclisation in planta, however, these phenotypes could be generated by activity of previously unidentified CHI gene family members (Miyahara et al. 2018). However, CHI greatly accelerates the reaction and directs stereochemically defined intramolecular cyclisation into biologically active (2S)‐flavanones (Jez and Noel 2002). A conserved function of CHI in land plants is further supported by the observation that chi mutations in either Marchantia or several angiosperm species reduce flavonoid production to minimal amounts (Clayton et al. 2018; Miyahara et al. 2018). Yet mosses have an efficient flavonoid biosynthetic pathway while lacking CHI genes (Cheng et al. 2018). Moreover, recent studies have discovered that the enzymatic activity of CHI is not required for effective function of the protein. Sugimoto et al. (2024) showed that tomato and maize CHI genes with mutations removing catalytic activity could fully complement the null chi mutants transparent testa5 of Arabidopsis and anthocyanin‐free of tomato. This suggests that the primary role of CHI may be to exclude the reactive NC from contact with the cytoplasm, providing an environment where it may spontaneously convert into naringenin, and to channel metabolites between other biosynthetic enzymes. Both CHI and CHIL probably evolved from non‐enzymatic FABPs, suggesting CHI enzymatic activity is a secondary activity that evolved to further enhance naringenin formation.

Pathogenesis Related‐10 Proteins

PR proteins are a diverse group of proteins that are often up‐regulated in response to pathogen infection and abiotic stresses. The PR10 subfamily includes PcPR10 of parsley (Petroselinum crispum), the pollen allergen gene Bet v1 of birch (Betula spp.), and allergen genes of apple (Malus domestica) and strawberry (Fragaria annanasa) fruit (Liu and Ekramoddoullah 2006; Fernandes et al. 2013; Dastmalchi 2021; Morris et al. 2021). Some PR10 members are associated with specialised plant metabolism, including flavonoid biosynthesis (Muñoz et al. 2010; Casañal et al. 2013; Fernandes et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2018; Morris et al. 2021). How PR10 proteins function has only recently been revealed. Some PR10 proteins catalyse biosynthetic reactions (e.g., Chen et al. 2018), but others may guide pathway stereochemistry and product selectivity (Morris et al. 2021). Recent research on Marchantia has revealed a function for PR10 proteins in flavonoid biosynthesis that is conserved between liverworts and angiosperms, involving an ability to bind small metabolites that may be key to all PR10 functions (Zhou et al. 2025). These proposals are based on comparing data on a Marchantia PR10 (MpPr10.5) with those on strawberry PR10 (FraA). Silencing FraA gene activity results in reduction of anthocyanin production in strawberry fruit (Muñoz et al. 2010; Casañal et al. 2013). Similarly, mutation of MpPR10.5 greatly reduces auronidin and flavone content of Marchantia plants (Zhou et al. 2025). FraA has been shown to bind a variety of small metabolites, including flavonoids, with differing affinities (Casañal et al. 2013). In Marchantia, flavone and auronidin biosynthesis share a single flavonoid intermediate, NC, indicating that MpPR10.5 acts on this compound. Indeed, MpPR10.5 protein binds NC in vitro and prevents spontaneous self‐cyclisation (Zhou et al. 2025). Thus, a key function of PR10 proteins, potentially conserved across land plants, is to promote flavonoid biosynthesis by selectively binding unstable intermediates to protect them from degradation or undesirable non‐enzymatic conversions, and perhaps facilitating their transport to the next enzymes in biosynthetic pathways.

The emerging functions of ‘accessory' proteins such as CHI, CHIL, and PR10 suggest they could contribute to a metabolon operating for flavonoid biosynthesis: a multi–enzyme complex ‘channelling' substrates between enzymes to direct the desired biosynthetic outcome and fine‐tuning metabolic flux. The existence of protein complexes channelling intermediates from one enzyme to the next is a long‐standing idea for flavonoid biosynthesis (Stafford 1974; Hrazdina and Wagner 1985; Winkel 2004). However, it has been debated whether observations of binding between flavonoid proteins, both in vitro and in planta (e.g., Nakayama et al. 2019; Zhang and Fernie 2020; Tian et al. 2022), correspond to a functional metabolon. Recently, the operation of such metabolons has been characterised in detail for other specialised metabolite pathways (e.g., Laursen et al. 2016; Mucha et al. 2019). Given the contribution of accessory proteins to flavonoid biosynthesis across the land plants, it seems plausible that metabolons are a well‐conserved and key component of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway.

Studies to date have yet to fully reveal whether all these functions are conserved in bryophytes. As mentioned earlier, experimental evidence shows the UVR8/RUP1/HY5 system is strongly conserved in Marchantia (Clayton et al. 2018), and the corresponding genes are present in mosses and hornworts. However, the same study found no evidence for an equivalent to the angiosperm SG7 R2R3MYB direct regulator of flavone/flavonol biosynthesis in Marchantia. Thus, MYB regulation of those genes may be a character acquired after the separation of tracheophyte and bryophyte lineages, or lost in the bryophyte ancestor. HY5 can be a direct activator of flavonoid biosynthetic genes (Naik et al. 2022), and perhaps that is the ancestral state. However, further research is required to determine what the direct activators of FNS and the associated UGT genes of liverworts and mosses are.

Although the studies of Clayton et al. (2018) failed to identify an R2R3MYB activator of flavone biosynthesis in Marchantia, two R2R3MYB genes (MpMYB02 and MpMYB14) do fall basal to a clade of R2R3MYBs containing the phenylpropanoid‐related R2R3MYB genes identified in Arabidopsis (e.g., SG4, SG5, SG6, SG7, SG22). It was found that MpMYB02 and MpMYB14 are required for production of bibenzyls and auronidin, respectively (Albert et al. 2018; Kubo et al. 2018; Berland et al. 2019). Thus, R2R3MYB activation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis may be a character inherited by all extant land plants from the LCA. Orthologues of MpMYB02 and MpMYB14 are found throughout the liverwort phylogeny (Figure 3), including Haplomitrium mnioides, indicating it is ancestral in the liverwort lineage. The genome sequences of some species, such as Treubia lacunosa, lack good candidate sequences for one or other of the genes, but this may reflect incomplete genome sequences. Expanded gene families for the R2R3MYBs that regulate flavonoid biosynthesis are typical for angiosperms, but this may be much rarer in liverworts. We analysed >50 liverwort transcriptomes or whole genome sequences for both thalloid and leafy liverwort species: the majority of liverwort species (ca. 85%) belong to the leafy Jungermanniopsida class that have stem and leaf structure resembling mosses, while species in the two other phylogenetic classes grow as a flattened leafless thallus (Haplomitriopsida and Marchantiopsida, the simple and complex thalloid forms) (Huang et al. 2026). Our analyses (Figure 4) found only a few species with multiple candidate sequences in the MYB02 and MYB14 clades. For example, H. mnioides (simple thalloid) has two MYB14 clade genes, and Pallavicinia ambigua (leafy) has two MYB14 and three MYB02 candidates. It would be interesting to determine whether the members of the gene families have distinct expression domains, as is common for angiosperm R2R3MYBs. The bHLH subclass IIIf that contains the MBW flavonoid‐regulatory genes of seed plants is present in all land plants (Kongsted and Glover 2023) except hornworts (Schafran et al. 2025), with four IIIf bHLH genes in P. patens and one in Marchantia (MpbHLH12). Whether the MYB, bHLH, and WDR genes of Marchantia form a functional MBW complex for flavonoid regulation has not been determined. As yet, there are no functional data on the regulators of flavonoid biosynthesis in mosses. Although bHLH and MYB gene families are also found in algae, there is no data on whether they are involved in regulating phenolic biosynthetic pathways. Nor is there evidence for R2R3MYB‐ or R3MYB‐repressor functions for flavonoid biosynthesis in liverworts or mosses. There is, however, evidence for MpWRKY10 repressing auronidin production in Marchantia thallus tissues (Krishnamoorthi et al. 2024). Hornwort genomes do not contain any R2R3MYBs of these sub‐groups, or any that form a clade with the flavonoid‐related bHLH12 genes. Thus, these may have been lost during lineage evolution along with the flavonoid biosynthetic genes.

Details about what might control the transcription or activity of the direct transcriptionalregulators of flavonoid pathway (i.e., R2R3MYB, bHLH, HY5) across land plants are starting to emerge. In addition to the UVR8 regulatory module, the TOPLESS protein may be a conserved regulatory feature. TOPLESS is a mediator of transcriptional repression that can have a general role in plants for mediating growth and other characteristics in response to environmental or developmental signals. TOPLESS has been linked to flavonoid regulation in both Arabidopsis (Sun et al. 2023) and P. patens (Causier et al. 2023). In Arabidopsis, TOPLESS has been shown to interact with the EAR domain that is found in R2R3MYB repressors of SG4 (Plant et al. 2021), such as AtMYB4, which represses flavonoid biosynthesis. In P. patens, plant lines with reduced activity of the PpTOPLESS gene have significantly increased flavonoid pathway activity and red/brown colour (Causier et al. 2023). The best characterised role of TOPLESS is in the auxin response pathway, in which it interacts with AUX/IAA and JAZ proteins for auxin and jasmonic acid/12‐oxo‐phytodienoic acid‐mediated repression of gene expression (Leydon et al. 2021; Plant et al. 2021). Whether TOPLESS is a conserved regulator of the flavonoid pathway across land plants is an important question worthy of further research.

Concluding Comments

In the context of other recent findings, we propose that aspects of flavonoid biosynthesis were lost and regained on multiple occasions during land plant evolution. For red pigments, we hypothesise that different structures arose in different lineages with unique functionalities in addition to the ability to screen potentially harmful visible light. Even within angiosperms, red pigmentation traits may be more flexible than often assumed; for example, betalains repeatedly replace anthocyanins in some families in the order Caryophyllales (Davies et al. 2018; Pucker et al. 2024), and carotenoids may replace anthocyanin functions in Cucurbitaceae (Choudhary et al. 2026). An open question is whether the loss of red pigmentation in hornworts limits associated stress tolerance against harmful visible light such that it places significant ecological constraints on available habitats. We suggest that the data on red pigments reflect a more dynamic pattern of evolution of specialised metabolism across land plants than previously accepted. This fits with recent proposals of the bryophyte and vascular plant lineages, both undergoing large gene losses from a relatively complex LCA, then diversification again during speciation in each lineage (Dong et al. 2025). The findings also have implications for reconstructing the genetic ‘toolkit’ of specialised metabolism in early land plants and the LCA.
