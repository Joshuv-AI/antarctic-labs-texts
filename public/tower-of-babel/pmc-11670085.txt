Engineering bacteria for cancer immunotherapy by inhibiting IDO activity and reprogramming CD8+ T cell response
Authors: Heng Wang, Fang Xu, Chenlu Yao, Huaxing Dai, Jialu Xu, Bingbing Wu, Bo Tian, Xiaolin Shi, Chao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis research addresses the limitations of current indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) inhibitors in anticancer therapy by developing a genetically engineered high tryptophan-expressing CB (L-Trp CB) strain. This strain selectively colonizes tumors and inhibits IDO activity through butyrate production, preventing tryptophan catabolism and kynurenine accumulation. Additionally, it enhances CD8+ T cell activation and energy metabolism in the tumor microenvironment. The study demonstrates that L-Trp CB restores CD8+ T cell function and significantly delays tumor growth in various animal models with minimal side effects. This approach offers a promising synthetic biology-based strategy to improve tumor immunotherapy by targeting IDO activity and reprogramming CD8+ T cell responses.

T cells play a crucial role in the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy (1). Mounting evidence has indicated that T cell responses depend heavily on metabolite availability (2). Carbohydrates, lipids, amino acids, and cofactors play crucial roles in generating a wide range of T cell responses by supplying energy, contributing to biomass, and importantly facilitating signaling processes (2). Studies have shown that metabolites not only serve as nutrients but also as “signal 4,” which dictates T cell activation (3, 4). However, cancer cells often undergo metabolic adaptations in the tumor microenvironment (TME), resulting in a metabolically stressful environment that hampers sufficient T cell activation (5). If activated T cells are deficient in specific metabolites, their response may be impaired, leading to weak T cell antigen receptor (TCR) signaling and abnormal epigenetic profiles (3). Thus, how to effectively provide metabolic signals to the tumor microenvironment is pivotal for supporting tumor antigen-experienced T cell responses.

Among the metabolites, tryptophan is an important nutrient for T cell responses and activity (6, 7). It acts as a regulator that is essential for supporting rapid T cell proliferation and responses (8). However, the enzymatic activity of indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase (IDO) in tumor cells depletes tryptophan levels, leading to the accumulation of metabolites such as kynurenine, which inhibits effector T cells and establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports cancer cell proliferation and survival (9). While IDO inhibitors have shown promise in preclinical models, clinical trials combining IDO inhibitors with PD-1 blockade have faced significant challenges (10). One of the primary challenges associated with IDO inhibitors is the potential for off-target effects (11). IDO is involved in various physiological processes beyond immune regulation, including neurotransmitter metabolism and tissue repair. Inhibiting IDO may inadvertently disrupt these processes, leading to unwanted side effects (11). Additionally, due to their chemical structure, traditional IDO inhibitors may exhibit toxicity by affecting multiple pathways (11). How to optimize the therapeutic potential of IDO inhibitors while mitigating their limitations is essential to develop improved immunotherapy modalities and regimens.

The past decade has witnessed the essential role of the microbiota in the response to cancer immunotherapy (12). Targeting the tumor microenvironment with live bacteria is promising as an immunotherapeutic strategy in preclinical and clinical trials (13). With the development of synthetic biology, an increasing number of studies have used bacteria to deliver payloads into tumors (14). Bacteria can be genetically engineered with desirable functions, such as regulating the tumor microenvironment, releasing drugs, and secreting immunostimulatory molecules (15). Clostridium butyricum (CB), a spore-forming anaerobic bacterium, is a probiotics commonly found in the intestines of animals and humans (16). One of the notable abilities of CB is to produce butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid that can regulate adaptive immune cells (17). More importantly, studies have shown that butyrate can significantly suppress indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) expression (18). Therefore, targeting tumor IDO using engineered bacteria is a potential strategy to address current challenges of IDO inhibitors.

Inspired by this, we genetically engineered CB by overexpressing tryptophan structural genes to achieve the continuous release of tryptophan and butyrate. Engineered CB can strictly colonize tumors following systemic administration. The butyrate can inhibit IDO activities only within the tumor, while the constant production of tryptophan by engineered CB in the tumor microenvironment was able to reprogram CD8+ T cell metabolism and promote effector phenotypes of CD8+ T cells. In multiple mouse and rabbit tumor models, engineered CB has been shown to restore the proportion and function of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, inhibit tumor growth, and synergize with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) to induce robust antitumor immunity.

Results

Engineering CB with a Tryptophan-Secreting System.

To obtain a high-abundance tryptophan CB strain, we amplified the genes encoding enzymes involved in tryptophan synthesis through an overexpression vector (without the trp operator) (Fig. 1A). The utilization of a strong promoter was one of the key factors for high gene expression. We first cloned the strong promoter into the pMTL82151 vector to obtain the expression plasmid pMTL82151-thl, and the cloned trpEDCBA genes were subsequently ligated into pMTL82151-thl to obtain the plasmid pMTL82151-trpEDCBA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The successful insertion of the gene into the plasmid was confirmed by detecting the gene products via electrophoretic identification (Fig. 1B). As the plasmid carried the chloramphenicol gene, the monoclones that formed in the medium containing chloramphenicol were selected for further culture, which indicated that the recombinant plasmid was successfully transferred into the probiotic strain CB (Clostridium butyricum), termed “L-Trp CB.” Quantitative PCR confirmed that L-Trp CB expressed high RNA levels of tryptophan biosynthetic enzyme-encoding genes (Fig. 1C). To confirm that the expression of the genes enabled the secretion of tryptophan in CB strains, the bacteria were cultured in thioglycolate media supplemented with tryptophan. Despite the environment containing enough tryptophan, L-Trp CB still secreted more tryptophan after 24 h. In contrast, the tryptophan concentration decreased in the culture medium of native CB. This result indicated that the mRNA was actively and continually expressed without being regulated by the product tryptophan due to the lack of a trp operator in the engineered CB (Fig. 1D). Overexpression of the trpEDCBA gene in engineered bacteria did not affect the production of butyrate in CB (Fig. 1E). Additionally, under transmission electron microscopy (TEM), the L-Trp CB exhibited a rod-shaped morphology similar to that of CB (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), showing no significant differences in size or zeta potential (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C) or in the rate of proliferation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). In summary, we successfully obtained an engineered CB that overexpressed tryptophan.

Studies have shown that butyrate inhibits the expression of IDO1 in cells by decreasing intracellular STAT1 levels and inhibiting HDAC activity (18) (Fig. 1F). First, we confirmed that butyrate led to a reduced level of IDO in various kinds of tumor cells (CT26, 4T1, and B16 cells) in a concentration-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C). This suggested that butyrate could serve as an IDO inhibitor to prevent tryptophan (Trp) depletion. Next, we cultured tumor cells in the presence of conditioned supernatants from CB or L-Trp CB. We observed that both CB and L-Trp CB metabolites were able to induce IDO downregulation in various tumor cells (Fig. 1 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D–F). To confirm that butyrate prevents tryptophan from being consumed by tumor cells, we determined the Trp levels in the culture media of tumor cells coincubated with CB metabolites. Compared to that in the untreated group, the tryptophan consumption of tumor cells in the group treated with CB metabolites was lower (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). These data suggested that the butyrate produced by CB could serve as a potential IDO inhibitor to prevent Trp depletion by cancer cells.

Furthermore, we investigated the effect of the metabolites of L-Trp CB on T cell function. Many studies have reported that Tryptophan, as well as some tryptophan metabolites, could potentially influence and regulate the function of CD8+ T cells and underline mechanism (19–22). First, we directly incubated naïve CD8+ T cells with different concentrations of tryptophan in vitro, showing that the levels of Gran B+, IFN-γ+, Ki67+, and TNF-α+ were up-regulated in the CD8+ T cells along with upregulation of tryptophan concentration (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). In addition, upon TCR/CD28 costimulation, tryptophan is essential for mTOR signaling pathway activation in T cells, which supports T cell survival and proliferation (23, 24). In our study, naïve CD8+ T cells were preactivated in vitro with anti-CD3/CD28 and IL-2. The CD8+ T cells were then incubated with bacterial supernatants from CB or L-Trp CB. We observed that L-Trp CB metabolites led to a significant increase in the proliferation and frequency of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells compared to CB metabolite treatment (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S6), indicating that tryptophan is essential for supporting activated CD8+ T cell proliferation and response.

Following TCR engagement, naive T cells rapidly switch from low metabolic activity by fatty acid oxidation to aerobic glycolysis, leading to rapid ATP and cytokine production (25). First, we directly incubated butyrate and/or tryptophan with CD8+ T cells. We observed that there was no significant difference in the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) of T cells treated with the butyrate compared to the untreated T cells. However, the addition of tryptophan significantly boosted the energy metabolism of the T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Considering that tryptophan is a precursor of various metabolites that directly affect metabolism (26), we hypothesized that exposure to metabolites from L-Trp CB would support changes in the cellular metabolism of T cells. Respiratory and extracellular flux analyses were performed using a Seahorse XF96 metabolic analyzer to analyze the treated T cells. We observed that the OCR and ECAR of T cells were both greater in the L-Trp CB supernatant treatment group than in the control group (Fig. 1 J and K). Specifically, L-Trp CB-derived metabolites promoted oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis in T cells. The phenotype of T cells with high glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation activity is often described as an “effector phenotype” (27). These findings suggested that metabolites of L-Trp CB altered intracellular energy metabolism to boost and maintain T cells in an effector phenotype (Fig. 1 J and K). In brief, the large amount of tryptophan secreted by L-Trp CB could regulate the metabolism of T cells to foster effector immune function after stimulation.

We further investigated an in vitro model in the presence of cancer cells to simulate the tumor microenvironment. We activated T cells with anti-CD3/anti-CD28, IL-2, and CB or L-Trp CB supernatants and then cocultured them with tumor cells (CT26 cells) in a Transwell system (Fig. 1L). Tumor cell coincubation resulted in exhaustion and anergy of T cells, which was partly due to the IDO activity that depletes tryptophan. In contrast, the L-Trp CB metabolites could partly reinvigorate the CD8+ T cell population, function, and proliferation compared to CB metabolites and controls (Fig. 1 M and N and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). These results indicated that L-Trp CB metabolites containing tryptophan and butyrate can reverse T cell dysfunction in a metabolically stressful environment.

In Vivo Biodistribution and Therapeutic Efficacy of L-Trp CB in Multiple Mouse Tumor Models.

We selected CT26 and 4T1 cells as mouse tumor models to assess the ability of L-Trp CB/CB to colonize tumors in vivo. The lux-CB/lux-L-Trp CB strain, harboring a constitutively expressed bacterial luciferase reporter gene (lux), was intravenously injected into CT26 or 4T1 tumor-bearing mice once the tumor reached a size of 100 to 200 mm3. The IVIS imaging system was used to record the signals of lux at different time points postinjection. Notably, after 36 h, a strong bioluminescence signal was observed at the tumor location in both tumor models; Moreover, the signal increased over time for at least 7 d and the signal gradually was diminished and disappeared over time (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B), suggesting that the living L-Trp CB/CB colonized and proliferated at the tumor site for a relatively long time. After 1 wk, the mice were killed, and various organs were collected for bioluminescence imaging. There was no detectable bioluminescence signal in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney, except for the tumor (Fig. 2B). This result verified that lux-CB/lux-L-Trp CB exhibited specific exclusive colonization and survival ability within the tumor over time following I.V. injection. This finding also suggests that bacterial accumulation in tumors is not solely attributed to the hypoxic environment but also to the inherently immune suppressive tumor microenvironment, which hinders bacterial clearance. To rule out the possibility that lux expression was decreased in CB in vivo, the major organs, including the liver, spleen, kidney, heart, lung, and tumor, were homogenized, diluted, and spread on reinforced Clostridium medium (RCM) agar plates containing kanamycin on day 7. After incubating at 37 °C for 24 to 36 h, we determined the bacterial colony counts using a colony counter. As expected, only the tumor plates had significant colony-forming unit (CFU) values (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D), further indicating the high selectivity of L-Trp CB/CB colonization exclusively within tumors.

These findings led us to examine whether intravenous L-Trp CB could inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 2D). We established subcutaneous CT26 and 4T1 tumor models in mice. After the tumors (CT26-Luc and 4T1-Luc cells) had grown to approximately 100 mm3, one intravenous injection (5 × 107 CFU) of L-Trp CB or native CB was administered to the mice on day 7. Although native CB had a minimal effect on tumor growth, L-Trp CB led to a significant delay in tumor growth, as evidenced by reduced tumor bioluminescence signals (Fig. 2E) and tumor sizes (Fig. 2 F, G, J, and K). Moreover, there was a significant increase in overall survival (Fig. 2 H and L). Although there was a slight decrease in mouse weight after the injection, no significant weight loss was observed over time (Fig. 2 I and M). In addition, H&E staining of major organs, routine blood indices, and inflammatory cytokines revealed no significant differences between L-Trp CB-treated mice and control mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), indicating good tolerance of L-Trp CB injection to these mice. A similar result was also observed in a more aggressive B16F10 tumor mouse model (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) and a VX2 tumor New Zealand rabbit model (Fig. 2 N–Q). These results suggested that L-Trp CB robustly induces a potent antitumor effect against solid tumors.

We further investigated whether immune response induced by L-Trp CB could give memory in a CT26 tumor rechallenge model. Compared with the untreated mice, the mice receiving L-Trp CB delayed the secondary tumor growth and improved survival (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), suggesting that L-Trp CB could provide an immune response that could give memory to protect mice from tumor rechallenge.

L-Trp CB Treatment Increases the Proportion and Function of Tumor-Infiltrating CD8+ T Cells.

We reasoned that the antitumor effect of L-Trp CB was due to its ability to reprogram the T cell metabolic phenotype by providing Trp and inhibiting IDO activity. To elucidate the mechanism underlying the antitumor effect of L-Trp CB on tumor-infiltrating T cells, we carried out flow cytometry and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq, 10× Genomics platform) analyses to characterize CT26 tumors after L-Trp CB treatment. Utilizing the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) algorithm for visual clustering analysis, we defined the major T cell phenotypic clusters: naive CD8+ T cells (CD8 Tnaive), CD8+ effector T cells (CD8 Tef), CD8+ memory T cells (CD8 Tm), CD8+ exhausted T cells (CD8 Tex), naive CD4+ T cells (CD4 Tnaive), CD4+ Treg cells (CD4 Treg), and CD4+ effector T cells (CD4 Tef) (Fig. 3A). Following L-Trp CB treatment, there was a striking increase in the frequency of all CD8+ T cells (including Tef, Tm, and Tex) within the tumor (Fig. 3B) and a decrease in naive CD8+ T cells and all CD4+ T cells (including Tnaive, Tef, and Treg) (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence staining of the tumors revealed prominent infiltration of CD8+ T cells after L-Trp CB treatment (Fig. 3C). The results of the flow cytometry analysis of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells among the CD3+ T cells were consistent with the results of the scRNA-seq analysis (Fig. 3 D and E). These results suggested that L-Trp CB significantly increased the proportion of infiltrating CD8+ T cells but not that of CD4+ T cells in tumors (Fig. 3 D and E). We also measured Treg cells in CD4+ T cells by flow cytometry analysis, revealing no difference in Treg percentage and function between CB and L-Trp CB treatment, which was consistent with the results of scRNA-seq analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B). We then further analyzed L-Trp CB-induced alterations in the phenotype of CD8+ T cells. Single-cell analysis and flow cytometry analysis revealed significantly increased levels of immune activation-related genes (e.g., Gzmb, Ifng, Mki67, Tnf, and Pdcd1) and corresponding proteins (Gran B, IFN-γ, Ki67, TNF-a, and PD-1) in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3 F–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S13A). Additionally, other functional and signal-regulating genes were more highly expressed in the CD8+ T cells of mice receiving L-Trp CB than in those of the controls (Fig. 3K and SI Appendix, Fig. S13B). These findings collectively indicated that L-Trp CB increased the number and function of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GESA) revealed significantly enriched pathways related to CD8+ T cells, including pathways related to natural killer cell-mediated immunity, immune effector processes, the Fc receptor signaling pathway, activation of the innate immune response, T cell chemotaxis, and innate immune response activating signal transduction (Fig. 3L and SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis revealed significantly enriched pathways in CD8+ T cells, including p53 signaling, T cell receptor signaling, and chemokine signaling pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B).

To clarify the chemotaxis signatures of L-Trp-regulated T cells, we used Fingolimod hydrochloride (FTY720) to inhibit T cell migration. FTY720 injection significantly increased the tumor size and reduced the survival rate in mice receiving L-Trp CB (SI Appendix, Fig. S14C), suggesting the administration of L-Trp CB promoted the trafficking of CD8+ T cells from peripheral lymphoid organs.

The mTOR pathway integrates signals from nutrients, energy status, and growth factors to regulate T cell activation and differentiation (28). mTOR signaling controls metabolic reprogramming, influencing glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis, and other metabolic processes (29). T cell activation by TCR/CD28 costimulation activates the mTOR signaling pathway, which is a central regulator of cellular metabolism, growth, proliferation, and survival in T cells (3). Studies have shown that depletion of tryptophan can lead to reduced mTOR activation due to decreased availability of this critical amino acid (30). In addition, mTOR signaling is also heavily influenced by SCFAs such as butyrate (31). By performing a gene set enrichment analysis of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, we showed significant upregulation of the MTORC1 signaling pathway in CD8+ T cells after L-Trp CB treatment compared with that in the control group, further revealing that L-Trp CB could improve CD8+ T cell function through the mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 3M). We further verified that L-Trp CB promoted the upregulation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway in CD8+ T cells through Western blotting, showing that phosphorylated expression of S6 and 4EBP1 was significantly up-regulated after treatment with L-Trp metabolites (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). To investigate the impact of L-Trp CB on different CD8+ T cell subtypes, pseudotime analysis was conducted, revealing four differentiation states: naive state 1, effector state 2, memory state 3, and exhaustion state 4 (Fig. 3N). These states started in naive state 1 and then differentiated into effector, memory, and exhaustion states. This finding is consistent with the high abundance of effector and exhaustion genes on CD8+ T cells in the L-Trp CB subgroup. A similar effect was also observed for 4T1 tumors after L-Trp CB treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S16).

We also examined the proportion and function of myeloid cells in tumors of mice after treatments, as these myeloid cells can modulate CD8+ T cell function. The results revealed that although CB or L-Trp CB treatment could increase the proportion of M1-macrophages and neutrophils while declined the proportion of M2-macrophages (CD206+) compared to untreated tumor, no essential difference was observed between CB and L-Trp CB treatments (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). It may be due to the fact that neutrophils were recruited and M1-macrophages were activated by the tumor-infiltrating bacteria, thus to clear away tumor-infiltrating bacteria (32, 33), but the high-level L-Trp had the insignificant influence on these myeloid cells within tumor based on these results.

L-Trp CB Directly Activates CD8+ T Cells Independent of CD4+ T Cells.

Given that T cells were strongly activated after L-Trp CB treatment, we depleted T cells in vivo to investigate whether the anticancer immune response triggered by L-Trp CB was associated with T cell–mediated immunity. Anti-CD4 or anti-CD8a antibodies were administered to the mice on days 3 and 6 to deplete the corresponding T cells. Flow cytometric analysis of the ratio of CD4+ to CD8+ T cells in the serum confirmed that the corresponding T cell subsets were almost completely depleted (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Following successful T cell depletion, L-Trp CB was intravenously administered to the tumor-bearing mice. Interestingly, tumor inhibition was completely restored by depletion of CD8+ T cells; counterintuitively, there was essentially no difference in tumor growth in CD4+ T cell–depleted mice (Fig. 4A).

These findings prompted us to hypothesize that CD8+ T cells are activated by L-Trp CB directly without the help of CD4+ cells. Typically, helper CD4+ T cells secrete various cytokines that can assist in the activation, proliferation, and function of CD8+ T cells (34). However, using single-cell RNA sequencing to characterize tumor-infiltrating CD4+ T cells, we found that the immune effector process and mTORC1 signaling pathway in CD4+ T cells were not significantly up-regulated after L-Trp CB treatment (Fig. 4B), which led to unchanged IL-2 release compared with that in untreated mice (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). KEGG functional enrichment analysis also revealed no significantly enriched pathways in CD4+ T cells, including the chemokine signaling pathway, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and T cell receptor signaling pathway (SI Appendix, Fig. S19B). In addition to IL-2, gene dot plots and violin plots showed that other cytokines released by CD4+ T cells were negligible compared to those released by untreated tumors (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S19 C and D). CD4+ T cell–associated cytokines were also validated by a LEGENDplex™ Multi-Analyte Assay kit at the protein level (SI Appendix, Fig. S19 A and D). In contrast to those in the CD8+ T cell cluster, the expression of other function- and signal-regulating genes in the CD4+ T cells of mice receiving L-Trp CB decreased compared with that in the controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S19E). These data support the notion that L-Trp CB treatment did not affect CD4+ T cells. To further confirm these results, we examined the IL-2 pathway in CD8+ T cells. IL-2-induced activation of CD8+ T cells was associated with three main downstream signaling pathways (the PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and JAK-STAT pathways) (Fig. 4D). Notably, the volcano plot and gene expression scatterplot revealed no significant changes in the relevant pathway genes in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S20 and Table S3). These data indicated that CD8+ T cells were not activated in an IL-2-dependent manner.

To further investigate whether L-Trp CB directly activates CD8+ T cells, we utilized fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to sort non-Treg-CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from lymph node T cell populations. Sorted non-Treg-CD4+ or CD8+ T cells were cocultured with CT26 cells in a transwell system to simulate the tumor microenvironment in the presence of L-Trp CB metabolites. A similar effect was also observed in vitro. Compared to CB metabolites, L-Trp CB metabolites did not increase the function or proliferation of CD4+ T cells, as indicated by the levels of IL-2+, IFN-γ+, Ki67+, and TNF-a+ in CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4 F–I and SI Appendix, Fig. S21A). However, the Gran B+, IFN-γ+, Ki67+, and TNF-α+ levels were increased in the CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4 J–M and SI Appendix, Fig. S21B). These results showed once again that L-Trp CB treatment can directly enhance the immune function of CD8+ T cells independent of CD4+ T cells. These findings highlighted that CD8+ T cells, rather than CD4+ T cells, are required for L-Trp CB-induced antitumor immunity.

L-Trp CB Modulates CD8+ T Cell Metabolism in the TME.

We next questioned how L-Trp CB can directly activate CD8+ T cells without the help provided by CD4+ T cells. First, we evaluated the antitumor effects of heat-killed L-Trp CB. In tumor-bearing mice, heat-killed L-Trp CB alone did not significantly alter tumor growth, suggesting that L-Trp-producing CB is required for optimal CD8+ T cell activation (Fig. 5A). These findings bolstered the hypothesis that L-Trp CB could suppress tumor growth by reprogramming CD8+ T cell metabolism. We determined the levels of tryptophan, kynurenine, and butyrate in collected tumor tissues after L-Trp CB colonization in various tumors. Increased levels of tryptophan and butyrate and reduced levels of kynurenine were observed in the collected tumor tissues of mice receiving L-Trp CB compared to those of the controls (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S22A). In addition, immunohistochemical staining and gene expression scatterplots revealed a decrease in IDO expression in tumor tissues from mice treated with L-Trp CB compared with that in control tissues (HK L-Trp CB) (Fig. 5 C and D and SI Appendix, Table S3), suggesting metabolic rewiring of tumors in mice receiving L-Trp CB. Moreover, glucose level was unchanged while glutamine level increased in tumor tissues collected from mice receiving L-Trp CB compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S22B). A previous study showed that cancer cells mainly rely on glutamine for survival and proliferation (35). It suggested that the L-Trp CB restored the tumor-killing ability of CD8+ T cells thus prevented the tumor from consuming glutamine. However, direct injection of free Trp did not affect tumor growth, probably due to IDO activity and the accumulation of kynurenine (SI Appendix, Fig. S23), indicating the importance of butyrate in inhibiting IDO activity.

We hypothesized that the large amounts of tryptophan and butyrate secreted by L-Trp CB in tumors affect the function of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells by reprogramming their metabolism. Strikingly, single-cell RNA-seq of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells from control and L-Trp CB-treated mice revealed that the genes whose expression was most significantly up-regulated were associated with glycolysis (e.g., Eno3, Ldha, and Gapdh), the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) (e.g., Gpd2), and oxidative phosphorylation (e.g., Atp5a1, Ndufb4, and Cox5b) (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S24A and Table S3). Dot plot analysis further indicated increased numbers and expression of related genes (Fig. 5F). GSEA reaffirmed that L-Trp CB promoted glycolysis and OXPHOS metabolic pathways in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5G). Notably, this effect was not significantly apparent in tumor-infiltrating non-Treg CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5G and SI Appendix, Fig. S24B and Table S3). In addition, we isolated CD8+/CD4+ non-Treg T cells to validate the OXPHOS and glycolysis changes. The results showed that the OXPHOS and glycolysis of non-Treg CD4+ T cells treated with L-Trp CB were not changed significantly, while the OXPHOS and glycolysis of CD8+ T cells were significantly increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S25). These data strongly explain why L-Trp CB directly activates CD8+ T cells independent of CD4+ T cells—the presence of Trp and butyrate was more essential for supporting tumor antigen-activated CD8+ T cell proliferation and responses than for stimulating CD4+ T cells by modulating the metabolism of CD8+ T cells within the TME.

We previously showed that L-Trp CB promoted all CD8+ effector T cells (Tef), CD8+ memory T cells (Tm), and CD8+ exhausted T cells (Tex) within the tumor (Fig. 3B). Further analysis revealed that among all the CD8+ T cell subtypes, the metabolic changes in CD8+ Tef cells, which secrete effector factors and play a major role in eliminating cancer cells, were most pronounced (Fig. 5 H and J and SI Appendix, Fig. S24C and Table S3). However, the metabolism of other CD8+ T cell subsets was not significantly up-regulated (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). The metabolic demands of CD8+ effector T cells are the highest because they need to produce energy, biosynthetic precursors, and effector molecules to carry out their functions. Our data suggested that L-Trp CB could support the rapid proliferation and effector functions of effector CD8+ T cells within tumors. Overall, we investigated how L-Trp CB directly activated tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells by reprogramming metabolism and elucidated the importance of Trp-related metabolism for tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cell antitumor activity.

Therapeutic Efficacy of L-Trp CB in Combination with PD-L1 Blockade.

As L-Trp CB can significantly activate CD8+ T cells within tumors, we hypothesized that coblocking PD-L1 with antibodies may induce a synergistic antitumor effect. To test this hypothesis, we established a CT26-luc tumor model in BALB/c mice. CB or L-Trp CB was intravenously injected once, and the anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody was intratumorally injected three times at 1-d intervals (Fig. 6A). As expected, L-Trp CB had synergistic effects on controlling tumor growth (Fig. 6 B–D); in two instances, combination therapy led to complete tumor clearance (Fig. 6 B–D) and significant improvement in overall survival (Fig. 6E). Importantly, the combination therapy also showed good tolerance in mice, as evidenced by nonsignificant variations in mouse body weight (Fig. 6F). Analysis of T cell immune components in tumors revealed that L-Trp CB+anti-PD-L1 treatment was sufficient to increase the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into tumors and increase the frequency of Ki67+IFN-γ+Gran B+TNF-a+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6 G–K and SI Appendix, Fig. S27 A–E), indicating robust antitumor immune activity. All these data suggested that L-Trp CB exhibited a strong synergistic effect with PD-L1 blockade in inducing potent antitumor immunity.

Discussion

With the development of synthetic biology, bacteria can be further modified to enhance their safety and antitumor activity (36). Engineered bacteria have been reported to express molecules such as L-arginine, PD-L1/CTLA-4 nanobodies, melanoma antigens, and stimulators of interferon genes (STING) agonists that regulate antitumor immune responses, providing a promising avenue for cancer treatment (37–40). For example, in clinical trials, an EcN strain (SYNB1891) was generated with an engineered dual trophic deficiency strategy; this strain produced interferon gene stimulator (STING) agonist, which up-regulated immune activation genes (40). However, intravenous live bacterial administration for cancer therapy also has issues associated with low bacterial survival and poor tumor accumulation, leading to detrimental side effects (41). Specialized anaerobic bacteria, such as Clostridium spp., cannot survive in an oxygenated environment, thus enhancing their specificity for tumor-targeted colonization (42). Clostridium butyricum is a strictly anaerobic bacterium that is well known to act as a probiotic to modulate the immune system but also produces butyrate (43). The abundant vasculature and hypoxic environment in tumors allow CB to actively colonize tumors.

Here, we developed an engineered bacterium, “L-Trp CB,” that can provide two metabolic signals within the TME: tryptophan and butyrate. Both can provide discrete signals that dictate T cell activation. In addition, butyrate not only serves as a metabolic signal but also inhibits IDO activity, which prevents tryptophan catabolism and kynurenine accumulation. We observed that butyrate and tryptophan metabolites secreted by L-Trp CB significantly restored the proportion and function of CD8+ T cells in vitro and in vivo, leading to significantly delayed tumor growth, improved survival, and limited side effects (Fig. 7). We further demonstrated the synergistic effect of combining PD-L1 blockade with L-Trp CB and the ability of these combinations to generate potent T cell antitumor immunity in multiple tumor models, indicating the broad application and clinical promise of our metabolite-targeting CB.

In addition, we were interested in exploring how L-Trp CB directly promotes CD8+ T cell activation. We then explained the phenomenon in which L-Trp CB drove antitumor immunity in CD8+ T cells from a metabolic perspective. Many metabolites can affect TCR signaling. Among them, mTOR serves as a central nexus for metabolic signaling in T cell activation, receiving inputs from diverse metabolic pathways and delivering crucial signals that orchestrate metabolic adaptations following T cell activation. Most metabolite-specific signals that alter T cell fate are involved in the mTOR pathway. For example, amino acids such as tryptophan are key for sustaining mTORC1 activity in CD8+ T cells (3, 44, 45). The absence of tryptophan inhibited the mTORC pathway, leading to insufficient energy for infiltrating T cells (46). In addition, butyrate can increase the function of mTOR as a central cellular metabolic sensor (3, 31). In our study, single-cell sequencing analysis and GSEA revealed upregulation of the MTORC1 signaling pathway in CD8+ T cells by L-Trp CB. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that L-Trp CB promoted energy metabolism, such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, in CD8+ T cells. Taken together, these findings support the notion that butyrate and tryptophan secreted by L-Trp CB increase glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in CD8+ T cells by promoting mTOR activity, which simultaneously corresponds to an increase in CD8+ T cell effector function (47). In line with our results, the associated energy metabolism of CD4+ T cells was not altered, suggesting the direct role of L-Trp CB in activating CD8+ T cells.

We further investigated the effect of the metabolites of L-Trp CB on myeloid cells, indicating our strategy could promote M1-macrophages and neutrophils accumulation while avoid M2-macrophages frequency within tumor. We cannot rule out the possibility that myeloid cells modulate CD8+ T cell function within tumor. Nevertheless, the detailed effect of L-Trp CB on myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment would be studied in the future.

Unexpectedly, we observed a decrease in the proportion of CD4+ T cells caused by L-Trp CB by single-cell sequencing and flow cytometry analysis. This further indicated that the L-Trp CB-mediated increase in antitumor efficacy was primarily driven by CD8+ T cells rather than CD4+ T cells. Although a previous study showed that butyrate can promote mTOR activation in CD4+ T cells (48), we did not observe that L-Trp CB treatment resulted in IL-2 secretion or TCR signaling activation in the CD4+ T cell population. Moreover, in the CD8+ T cell population, no significant changes in the expression of genes related to IL-2-mediated downstream signaling pathways were observed, indicating that L-Trp CB directly up-regulated functional phenotypes associated with CD8+ T cells. This finding was also verified via T cell depletion experiments. This effect may be due to butyrate, as previously reported, which preferentially induces Treg cell differentiation in the CD4+ T cell subset rather than in the T helper 1 (TH1), TH2, and Th17 cell subsets (49). However, the underlying reason requires further exploration.

In summary, we developed a high tryptophan-producing L-Trp CB for cancer immunotherapy. This research addresses the limitations of current IDO inhibitors in anticancer therapy by developing a genetically engineered high tryptophan-expressing CB (L-Trp CB) strain. This strain selectively colonizes tumors and inhibits IDO activity through butyrate production, preventing tryptophan catabolism and kynurenine accumulation. Additionally, it enhances CD8+ T cell activation and energy metabolism in the tumor microenvironment. The study demonstrates that L-Trp CB restores CD8+ T cell function and significantly delays tumor growth in various animal models with minimal side effects. This approach offers a promising synthetic biology-based strategy to improve tumor immunotherapy by targeting IDO activity and reprogramming CD8+ T cell responses.

Methods

Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix. Briefly, the Clostridium-Escherichia coli shuttle overexpression vector pMTL82151 was used to produce large amounts of tryptophan on Clostridium butyricum. Pthl was cloned and inserted into the vector pMTL82151, and the plasmid pMTL82151-thl was obtained. Then, the structural gene sequences on the E. coli tryptophan manipulator capable of expressing tryptophan biosynthetic enzymes (trp E, D, C, B, and A) were synthesized to produce tryptophan by expressing various synthases in the tryptophan biosynthetic pathway. Clostridium butyricum (BNCC337239) was purchased from the BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC). The Clostridium shuttle vector “pMTL82151” (V004418) was obtained from NovoPro. PMV306-G13-lux and E. coli CA434 were purchased from BioSci.
