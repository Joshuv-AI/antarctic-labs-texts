Immune context governs stage-specific responses to depleting anti-PD-1 antibody in type 1 diabetes
Authors: Yujia Zhai, Shuyun Dong, Lauren C Naatz, Tianxiao Zhang, Xiangyang Ye, Dean R Tantin, Tallulah Andrews, Mingnan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYA depleting anti-PD-1 antibody (D-αPD-1) exhibits stage-dependent effects: delaying hyperglycemia onset in a murine early insulitis (E-insulitis) model but accelerating disease in late insulitis (L-insulitis). Compared with E-insulitis, L-insulitis pancreatic islets show elevated immune cell infiltration, a pronounced Teff/Treg imbalance, and enrichment of proinflammatory macrophages—features that partially mirror human type 1 diabetes (T1D) pathology. D-αPD-1 treatment reduces PD-1+ T cells during E-insulitis but unexpectedly expands this population during L-insulitis, inconsistent with its intended depleting function. Islet macrophages, particularly the Mac3 subset, are the primary mediators of therapeutic outcomes in L-insulitis, showing enhanced islet accumulation, increased Fcγ receptor (FcγR) expression, and activation of IFN-α/γ and TNF-α response following D-αPD-1 treatment. Importantly, macrophage depletion neutralizes the hyperglycemia-accelerating effect of D-αPD-1 in L-insulitis. Collectively, proinflammatory macrophages and their interactions with D-αPD-1 critically determine antibody’s therapeutic outcomes in T1D, highlighting the importance of considering macrophage-centered immune context when designing and applying such therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

The FDA approval of teplizumab marks a significant therapeutic advance for type 1 diabetes (T1D); however, the disorder remains without a cure or a reliable strategy for prevention.1–5 Current immunosuppressive therapies remain constrained by modest efficacy and long-term lymphopenia and immune deficiency.6,7 An emerging strategy to address these limitations is the targeted depletion of PD-1+ cells, primarily effector lymphocytes8–10 that drive T1D and other autoimmune diseases.11–15 PD-1+ cells infiltrate pancreatic islets14,16–18 and secrete pathogenic cytokines in patients.12,13 Importantly, the PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade, expanding both the number and activity of PD-1+ cells,19 worsens autoimmune diseases in animal models and humans.20–22 In contrast, selective depletion of PD-1+ cells delays hyperglycemia onset in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice.22,23 The PD-1+ cell-centered therapeutic strategy offers two major advantages: it targets both pathogenic T and B cells,8,24,25 thereby broadening therapeutic coverage, while sparing naive (PD-1−) cells to preserve lymphocyte repertoires and protective immunity.22 To facilitate clinical translation of this strategy, we developed a depleting anti-PD-1 antibody (D-αPD-1).26 Antibody-based approaches are more clinically viable27 than PD-1 immunotoxins previously used to demonstrate the benefit of PD-1+ cell depletion.22,23 antibody-based approaches are more clinically viable.

Antibodies possess favorable pharmacokinetic and translational properties, such as long plasma half-life, appealing safety, scalability, and industrial feasibility, which underpin their broad medical applications.28,29 In T1D, antibodies are investigated not only as therapeutic agents but also as targeting moieties and accessory components of biomaterials.5,30–39 Teplizumab, a notable example, delays disease onset or preserves C-peptides in at-risk or newly diagnosed individuals.32,33 More antibodies have shown variable efficacy in preclinical and clinical studies, including those targeting immune cells (e.g., anti-CD20 antibody)37,38 or cytokines (e.g., anti-IL-21 and anti-IL17 antibodies).34–36 Beyond direct therapeutic applications, antibodies are frequently used as experimental tools to dissect the roles of immune cells and molecules in T1D pathogenesis. Furthermore, antibodies are increasingly utilized as biomaterials to enhance drug delivery and function.28,31,39 For example, anti-addressin antibodies have been used to modify poly(ethylene glycol)-poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PEG-PLGA) nanoparticles that enhance the delivery of immunosuppressive agents to lymph nodes (LNs),40 and antibodies have also been embedded in hydrogels that protect transplanted islets.39,41,42 Collectively, antibodies represent a versatile therapeutic and engineering platform in T1D and other autoimmune diseases.

One important observation from both preclinical and clinical studies is that disease stages profoundly influence therapeutic outcome in T1D. Clinically, T1D progression is classified into stages 1–3 based on autoantibody counts, glucose tolerance results, and glycemic status.43 In the NOD mouse model for T1D, two distinct pre-hyperglycemic phases are recognized: the initial or early insulitis (E-insulitis) phase, typically observed in 4–6 week-old females, and the established or late insulitis (L-insulitis) phase, present in 10-week-old or older females prior to hyperglycemia onset.44–46 The impact of disease stage is evident in therapeutic testing. For example, female NOD mice treated with a tyrosine kinase 2 inhibitor, BMS-986165, responded favorably during E-insulitis but not during L-insulitis.47 Similarly, Fms-like tyrosine kinase-3 ligand (Flt3-L) delayed hyperglycemia onset in E-insulitis mice but accelerated disease progression in L-insulitis.48 Human studies mirror this stage-dependent pattern: teplizumab and the combination therapy of anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) and G-CSF more effectively preserve β-cell function when administered to patients at stages 1–2 or during the transition between stages 2 and 3.49,50 While differential therapeutic outcomes may be partially attributed to residual β-cell mass, a stronger factor could be the evolving immune environment in which these therapies act. Yet systematic characterization of this dynamic immune context—using comprehensive approaches such as scRNA-seq or mass cytometry—remains lacking.

T1D progression is characterized by dynamic shifts in the pancreatic islet immune milieu.44–46 As disease advances, islets exhibit increased T cell infiltration and activation,45,51,52 heightened dendritic cell (DC) migration,44 and intensified interactions between T cells and antigen-presenting cells.44 Notably, the islets of recent-onset T1D patients harbor myeloid cells as well as lymphocytes,53,54 mirroring observations in NOD mice. Beyond these general immune features, the abundance and spatial distribution of cognate antigens in islets are particularly relevant to antibody efficacy. Of specific relevance to D-αPD-1, PD-1+ cells have been found within islets1,14,16–18; however, their temporal dynamics over disease courses have not been systematically mapped. Another critical factor to antibody activity is the presence of Fcγ receptor (FcγR)-expressing cells in islets—including DCs, macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells—that mediate effector functions.55 Among FcγR+ cells, macrophages are the earliest ones to accumulate in islets during T1D progression,55 in which they engulf β-cells, present autoantigens, and orchestrate local inflammation.56–58 DCs transport diabetogenic antigens out to pancreatic draining LNs and prime T cells there.57 NK cells, though less studied in T1D, have also been suggested to contribute to disease progression.59 Collectively, these findings illustrate that the islet immune environment evolves in both cellular composition and functional state, and that robust animal models are available to probe these changes. However, it remains underexplored how these shifting immune landscapes influence antibody efficacy—or conversely, how antibody treatments remodel the landscapes. Addressing these questions could reveal foundational principles to guide the rational design and application of antibody-based therapeutics or biomaterials in T1D.

Here, inspired by the observation that D-αPD-1 treatment yields opposite outcomes depending on T1D stages, we investigated how pancreatic islet immune environments shape responses to antibody therapy. We found that the progression from E- to L-insulitis is featured by higher PD-1+ Teff/Treg ratios and enrichment of inflammatory macrophages in islets. In E-insulitis, D-αPD-1-depleted PD-1+ T cells reduced macrophage infiltration and suppressed inflammatory signaling, producing therapeutic benefit. In L-insulitis, however, the antibody expanded PD-1+ T cells and enhanced macrophage infiltration, FcγR expression, and activation of interferon and TNF pathways. These macrophages also upregulated cytokines and chemokines that promote T cell recruitment and proliferation. Importantly, macrophage depletions abolished the antibody’s T1D-accelerating effect in L-insulitis. A macrophage subset, termed Mac-3, emerged as a key driver of pathogenic responses. Together, these findings underscore the importance of disease stage, immune context, and macrophages in guiding rational antibody design and application in T1D.

RESULTS

D-αPD-1 delays hyperglycemia onset in E-insulitis mice but accelerates it in L-insulitis mice

Depleting PD-1+ cells has been shown to delay or reverse disease progression in animal models of T1D and EAE.22,23 D-αPD-1 was developed and validated for its depleting capacity.26 To examine whether the treatment of D-αPD-1 delays or prevents T1D progression, we treated 10- to13-week-old female NOD mice with weekly injection of the antibody. Female NOD mice within this age range are not hyperglycemic (Figure S1) but exhibited established or late-stage insulitis.44–46 We thus referred to them as L-insulitis mice. Their blood glucose measurement (Figure 1), glucose tolerance test results (Figure 1A), and C-peptide values (Figure 1B) indicate significant β-cell loss and dysglycemia in L-insulitis mice, consistent with features observed in stage 2 T1D patients.43 Strikingly, within two weeks of initiating D-αPD-1 treatment, all L-insulitis mice developed hyperglycemia (Figure 1C). The median T1D-free survival of these mice was only one week following antibody treatment. In contrast, PBS-treated L-insulitis mice had a median survival of ten weeks, indicating that D-αPD-1 treatment accelerated T1D progression. This outcome was somewhat unexpected, as the treatment of PD-1-specific immunotoxins delays hyperglycemia onset in L-insulitis mice.22

Given that both T1D patients and animal models exhibit disease-stage-dependent responses to therapeutics,48–50 we next evaluated whether D-αPD-1 has a different treatment outcome in 4–7-week-old female NOD mice, which represent the initial or E-insulitis. Female NOD mice at this age are neither hyperglycemic (Figure S1) nor dysglycemic (Figures 1A and 1B), but they are predisposed to develop hyperglycemia later, analogous to stage 1 T1D in patients.43 Weekly D-αPD-1 treatment significantly delayed the onset of hyperglycemia when initiated in E-insulitis mice (Figure 1D). Notably, the D-αPD-1 group had not reached its median T1D-free survival by 21 weeks after treatment initiation, whereas the PBS-treated group reached a median survival of 13 weeks post-treatment. Thus, D-αPD-1 treatment conferred at least a nine-week delay in disease onset.

Insulitis progression increases the absolute numbers of pancreatic islet-infiltrating immune cells while keeping their relative composition

We compared the immune environments between E- and L-insulitis islets, which is important because D-αPD-1 operates through an immune-centered mechanism,26 and its divergent effects may stem from distinct immune milieus of the islets. Prior studies have characterized immune environments across stages of murine T1D45,46,51,52; however, these characterizations are rather limited relative to the enormous complexity and multidimensionality of immune milieus. More importantly, datasets of these studies are not directly suited to explain therapeutic outcomes of antibodies, since that was not the primary focus of the studies. To address this gap, we employed both scRNA-seq and flow cytometry to profile the immune features potentially relevant to D-αPD-1’s activity. Our scRNA-seq analysis pipeline was designed to produce two immune features: changes in immune cell abundance and activated pathways (Figure 2A). These two features could lead to identifying responsive cell populations to D-αPD-1. This analysis pipeline is embedded with a novel cell annotation approach (Figure 2B). Barcode annotation was performed using a reference-based barcode labeling (ReBaLa) approach,60,61 rather than approaches relying on limited gene sets or off-the-shelf annotation programs. We used scRNA-seq results of defined immune cell types as our reference.62 ReBaLa results were cross-validated with unsupervised clustering, following a principle that each cluster was assigned to a single cell type based on the dominant label within a given cluster (Figure S2). Detailed scRNA-seq procedures are provided in the methods.

scRNA-seq revealed that B cells, T cells, and macrophages are the dominant immune populations in both E- and L-insulitis islets (Figure 2C). In E-insulitis islets, B cells, T cells, and macrophages accounted for 36%, 38%, and 11% of CD45+ cells, respectively. In L-insulitis islets, these proportions were 40%, 37%, and 13% (Figure 2D, left). Flow cytometry confirmed the dominance of these three cell types (Figure 2D, right). NK cells, NKT cells, and DCs were found as minor populations, collectively comprising less than 15% of CD45+ cells. Thus, the relative composition of major immune populations did not differ substantially between stages. In contrast, the absolute numbers of infiltrated immune cells increased markedly from E- to L-insulitis: CD45+ cells rose from 285 to 854 per islet. All dominant immune cell types showed substantial amplification: B cells increased by 3.4-fold, T cells by 2.9-fold, and macrophages by 3.5-fold (Figure 2E, left). These increases in immune cell infiltration were independently confirmed by flow cytometric analysis (Figure 2E, right). It is noteworthy that the increased immune cell numbers per islet are not due to size change of islets, as the sizes of islets are indistinguishable between E- and L-insulitis mice (Figure S3).

One common indicator of immune activation is the increased ratios of effector T cells (Teff) to regulatory T cells (Treg).63,64 Indeed, the CD4 Teff/Treg ratio rose from 3.6 in E-insulitis to 6.6 in L-insulitis, while the CD8 Teff/Treg ratio rose from 0.8 to 1.8 (Figure 2F). To determine whether similar changes occur in humans, we analyzed published scRNA-seq datasets of pancreatic immune cells from T1D patients at stages 0 (single autoantibody detected), 2 (two autoantibodies and dysglycemia), and 3 (hyperglycemia). In humans, the CD4 Teff/Treg ratio increased from 4.7 at stage 0 to 6.4 at stages 2 and further to 15.2 at stage 3 (Figure 2G). There were not sufficient human CD8 Teff numbers for ratio analysis.

In summary, although the relative composition of in-islet immune cell types remains stable, overall immune cell infiltration and the Teff/Treg imbalance intensify markedly during disease progression. Notably, these changes in NOD mice mirror those observed in human T1D patients.

L-insulitis features higher PD-1+ Teff/Treg ratios alongside proinflammatory macrophages

PD-1+ cells were compared between E- and L-insulitis, as these cells are critical to D-αPD-1’s use. According to scRNA-seq analysis, the vast majority of Pdcd1 (encoding PD-1)-expressing barcodes (~90%) can be mapped to T cell clusters (Figure 3A), indicating that PD-1+ cells in both insulitis are primarily in the T cell compartment. This observation guided us to focus subsequent analyses on PD-1+ T cells. To identify PD-1+ T cells more reliably, we used a 17-gene set positively correlated with Pdcd1 expression in T cells as the criterion instead of using Pdcd1 alone. Thus, we could address a known limitation of scRNA-seq—low mRNA capture efficiency and incomplete detection of expressed genes within each cell.65,66 Details of this 17-gene set approach are provided in the methods. As an illustration, in PBS-treated E-insulitis islets, Pdcd1 alone identified 371 PD-1+ barcodes, whereas the gene set approach identified an additional 135 barcodes (Figure 3B). Beyond PBS-treated E-insulitis islets, the gene set approach also detected greater numbers of PD-1+ T cells than the Pdcd1-only approach in the other three islet samples, PBS-treated L-insulitis islets, D-αPD-1-treated E-insulitis islets, and D-αPD-1-treated L-insulitis islets (on average 42% more). Importantly, both scRNA-seq and flow cytometry results showed similar fractions of PD-1+ cells within the T cell compartment (Figure 3C).

We examined PD-1+ fractions in nine major subtypes of T cells in pancreatic islets: CD4 and CD8 Teff, naive T cells (Tn), memory T cells (Tmem), exhausted T cells (Tex), and Treg (Figure 3D). The five subtypes having the highest PD-1+ cell fractions were CD8 Tex, CD8 Teff, CD4 Tex, Tregs, and CD4 Teff. In PBS-treated E-insulitis islets, each of these subsets contained >75% PD-1+ cells (Figure 3E). Their PD-1+ cell-rich nature was consistent across all four samples used in this study (Figure S4). Quantitative analysis showed that PD-1+ CD4 and CD8 Teff cells expanded approximately fourfold from E- to L-insulitis when quantified as cell numbers per islet, whereas PD-1+ Tregs also increased, but more modestly (~1.8-fold, Figure 3F). These disproportionate increases raised the PD-1+ Teff/Treg ratio. From E- to L-insulitis, the CD4 Teff/Treg ratio increased from 3.2 to 5.3, and the CD8 Teff/Treg ratio increased from 0.8 to 1.8 (Figure 3G). A similar trend was observed in humans: the CD4 PD-1+ Teff/Treg ratio rose progressively across T1D stages (Figure 3H). Beyond absolute numbers, there are also evident shifts in the relative ranking of PD-1+ T cell subsets. For example, from the E- to L-insulitis, PD-1+ CD8 Teff rose from the 4th to the 2nd most abundant subset, whereas PD-1+ Tregs dropped from 2nd to 4th (Figure 3I).

Another immune determinant relevant to D-αPD-1 is FcγR+ immune cell populations, including macrophages, NK cells, and DCs. Compared with E-insulitis, macrophages—but not NK cells or DCs—in L-insulitis exhibited significant upregulation of the IFN-α RESPONSE and IFN-γ RESPONSE pathways (Figure 3J; Table 1). These findings of murine macrophages were mirrored by human macrophages in pancreatic islets: macrophages from stage 2 T1D patients (versus stage 0) upregulated six pathways, including TNF-α SIGNALING VIA NFKB, IFN-α RESPONSE, and IFN-γ RESPONSE (Table 1). These inflammatory signatures contribute to β-cell dysfunction and destruction in T1D.67–69 Furthermore, macrophages at both stages expressed all four common FcγRs (I, IIb, III, and IV), but transcripts of FcγRIII and FcγR IV were further elevated in L-insulitis (Figure 3K). These FcγRs mediate immune effector functions, including cytokine release, oxidative burst, and antibody-dependent phagocytosis.26 In contrast, NK cells decreased FcγRIIb expression and increased FcγRIII expression in L-insulitis relative to E-insulitis. DCs, by contrast, showed only weak expression of FcγRIIb and FcγRIII in E-insulitis, which was further reduced in L-insulitis (Figure 3K).

In summary, compared with E-insulitis, L-insulitis islets contain more PD-1+ T cells, display higher PD-1+ Teff/Treg ratios, and harbor macrophages showing stronger inflammatory features and elevated FcγR expression. Additionally, these skewed Teff/Treg balances and macrophage inflammatory signatures in mice parallel immune changes observed in the islets of human T1D patients.

D-αPD-1 reduces PD-1+ T cells in E-insulitis but expands them in L-insulitis

D-αPD-1 treatment increased PD-1+ T cells by 2.1-fold in L-insulitis islets relative to PBS controls. In contrast, the antibody treatment reduced PD-1+ T cells by 2.7-fold in E-insulitis islets compared with PBS controls. Specifically, D-αPD-1 amplified PD-1+ cells across the five most PD-1+-enriched T cell subtypes in L-insulitis, most prominently in CD4 and CD8 Teff, which increased 2.4- and 3.6-fold, respectively (Figure 4A). By contrast, in E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 reduced PD-1+ cell numbers in all five T cell subtypes. PD-1+ CD8 Tex reduced one to zero cells per islet, and PD-1+ CD4 Tex also showed a dramatic decline of 4.6-fold (Figure 4A). Consistent with these changes in PD-1+ T cells, D-αPD-1 treatment promoted the proliferation of PD-1+ Teff in L-insulitis, as indicated by increased Ki67 expression (Figures 4B and 4C) and bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation (Figure S5). Specifically, the fractions of Ki67+ PD-1+ CD4 and CD8 Teff increased from 21.6%–35.0% and 33%–58.4%, respectively. In contrast, the treatment did not affect proliferation of PD-1+ Teff cells in E-insulitis (Figures 4B, 4C, and S5). In agreement with the above-described changes of PD-1+ T cells, D-αPD-1 expanded the total numbers of these five T cell subtypes in L-insulitis but reduced them in E-insulitis (Figure 4D).

CD4 Teff consist of four subsets with distinct roles in T1D pathogenesis: Th1, Th2, Th17, and T follicular helper (TFH) cells.59 We next examined how D-αPD-1 affected their abundance. In L-insulitis, D-αPD-1 treatment expanded Th2 and Th17 most dramatically (4.7- and 4.3-fold, respectively), whereas TFH remained largely unaffected. In E-insulitis, however, Th17 showed only minor changes after antibody treatment, whereas TFH and Th1 were reduced most substantially by the antibody (Figure 4E). Furthermore, we assessed and compared PD-1 expression on these four subsets. PD-1+ TFH and Th1 cells exhibited higher Pdcd1 transcript levels than PD-1+ Th17 cells (Figure 4F). Our findings in PD-1 expression and prior reports70,71 may explain why D-αPD-1 effectively depletes PD-1hi TFH and Th1 cells but has limited efficacy against PD-1lo Th17 cells in E-insulitis. However, the D-αPD-1-induced expansion of CD4 Teff subsets during L-insulitis cannot be explained solely by the depleting function of the antibody.

Last, we examined TCF1hi and TCF1lo CD8 Teff, which play different roles in T1D progression: TCF1hi cells possess stem-like properties, whereas TCF1lo cells, which are terminally differentiated, are recognized as the primary mediator in T1D autoimmunity.11 Both CD8 Teff subsets express PD-1, but neither is exhausted. In L-insulitis, D-αPD-1 expanded TCF1lo cells by 1.5-fold while decreasing TCF1hi cells by 2.9-fold (Figure 4G). In E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 was able to reduce TCF1lo and TCF1hi CD8 Teff by 1.2- and 2.3-fold, respectively (Figure 4G). These trends of CD8 Teff in L- and E-insulitis were confirmed by flow cytometry analysis (Figures 4H and 4I).

In summary, in contrast to its effects during E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 broadly promoted proliferation and expansion of PD-1+ T cells during L-insulitis. The mechanisms underlying the proliferation and expansion are intriguingly contrary to the presumed depleting function of D-αPD-1, which warrants further investigation from a broader immunological perspective.

Macrophages in pancreatic islets are primary responders to D-αPD-1 treatment

Because D-αPD-1’s depleting function alone does not fully explain its effects on PD-1+ cells in L-insulitis mice, we extended our investigation on the antibody’s impact on broad immunity. D-αPD-1 treatment led to divergent immunological changes in L- and E-insulitis mice according to scRNA-seq data. First, D-αPD-1-treated islets harbored 2.4-fold more immune cells than PBS-treated controls in L-insulitis mice (2,037 versus 854 per islet). In contrast, in E-insulitis, D-αPD-1-treated islets contained 3.0-fold fewer immune cells (96 versus 285 cells per islet; Figure 5A). These opposing effects were validated by flow cytometry (Figure 5B). Second, D-αPD-1 had distinct effects on individual immune cell types. In L-insulitis, the most pronounced increases occurred in NK cells (6.3-fold), NK-T cells (3.4-fold), and macrophages (3.3-fold), whereas DCs showed the least change (Figure 5C). In contrast, E-insulitis showed dramatic reductions in DCs (8.5-fold) and NK T cells (6.7-fold). Macrophages and NK cells were also markedly reduced (4.0-fold each), whereas T and B cells were less affected (Figure 5C). Together, these data indicate that macrophages, NK cells, and NKT cells are primary responders to D-αPD-1. Notably, macrophages may be the most important responder, since NK and NKT cells only constituted a much smaller fraction of the immune population compared with macrophages (1% versus 10%). One additional observation is that D-αPD-1 further polarized the already distinct immune states in L- and E-insulitis islets. For example, the macrophage number difference between the two stages increased from approximately 3.4-fold before treatment (32 versus 110 macrophages per islet) to a striking 45-fold after treatment (8 versus 365 macrophages per islet).

In addition to cell number changes, we also examined whether D-αPD-1 altered molecular pathways and functions of immune cells by analyzing their differentially expressed genes (DEGs) caused by antibody treatment. Totally, fourteen cell types were examined, including five major immune cell types, B cells, macrophages, NK-T cells, NK cells, and DCs, and nine T cell subtypes, CD4 and CD8 Teff, CD4 and CD8 Tn, CD4 and CD8 Tmem, CD4 and CD8 Tex, and Treg. The examination identified significant pathway activations in seven cell types and subtypes: macrophages, CD4 Teff, B cells, CD8 Teff, CD4 Tmem, Treg, and CD8 Tmem (Figure 5D).

Macrophages were the strongest responder to D-αPD-1 treatment. In L-insulitis, 14 pathways were activated, including seven with gene ratios >0.2 (Table 2). In contrast, five pathways were altered in E-insulitis, each with modest gene ratios (0.02–0.09), reflecting weaker responses compared with those in L-insulitis macrophages (Table 2).

D-αPD-1 treatment amplifies activation and proinflammatory cytokine production in L-insulitis macrophages

The IFN-α RESPONSE and IFN-γ RESPONSE pathways showed distinct activation patterns (Table 1) and responded differently to D-αPD-1 treatment in L- and E-macrophages (Table 2). We therefore dissected these two pathways in greater detail using module scores that summarize the expressions of all their hallmark genes.72 Each macrophage (barcode) was assigned a module score, enabling visualization and comparison of pathway-specific gene expression across cells. First, L-insulitis macrophages had markedly higher IFN-α and IFN-γ module scores than E-insulitis macrophages (0.14 versus 0.09; 0.25 versus 0.22; Figure 6A). Next, in L-insulitis, D-αPD-1 significantly increased average IFN-α (from 0.14 to 0.20) and IFN-γ pathways scores (from 0.25 to 0.32; Figure 6B). As a comparison, in E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 only elevated IFN-α scores (from 0.09 to 0.15) but not IFN-γ scores (Figure 6C). Together, these data indicate that L-insulitis macrophages, which are already primed with elevated interferon responses (Table 1), undergo a pronounced amplification of IFN signaling following D-αPD-1 treatment.

First, the number of CD80+CD86+ macrophages increased from 0.5 to 4.5 cells per islet in D-αPD-1-treated L-insulitis mice compared with PBS-treated controls (Figure 6D); the fraction of CD80+CD86+ macrophages increased from 11.5% to 21.6% (Figure S6A). These data suggest a greater presence of activated, immunostimulatory macrophages in the L-insulitis islet environment after antibody treatment. Second, the number of IL-1β+ macrophages was 1.3 and 0.1 cells per islet in D-αPD-1- and PBS-treated mice, respectively, indicating the boost of the macrophages that promote T cell proliferation and differentiation (Figure 6E). Consistently, the fraction of IL-1β+ macrophages was higher in D-αPD-1-treated mice, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure S6B). Finally, the number of CXCL9+ macrophages was 1.4 and 0.1 cells per islet in D-αPD-1- and PBS-treated mice, respectively, signaling macrophages’ enhanced capacity for immune cell recruitment (Figure 6F). The fraction of CXCL9+ macrophages was also elevated after D-αPD-1 treatment (Figure S6C). In contrast, D-αPD-1 treatment did not increase CD80+CD86+, IL-1β+, or CXCL9+ macrophages in E-insulitis mice. Indeed, macrophages with these activated phenotypes were rare in E-insulitis islets compared with L-insulitis islets (Figures 6D–6F).

Macrophages are required for D-αPD-1-induced acceleration of T1D in L-insulitis mice

Given the strong association between macrophage infiltration and activation in pancreatic islets and the D-αPD-1’s T1D-accelerating effect in L-insulitis mice, we tested whether macrophages were functionally required for the T1D- accelerating effect. To this end, we first confirmed that clodronate liposomes effectively reduced macrophages in islets and pancreatic draining LN (pLN, Figure 7A). Then, we pretreated L-insulitis mice before administering D-αPD-1 to them. Notably, the pre-treatment of clodronate liposomes markedly attenuated the disease-accelerating effect of D-αPD-1 (Figure 7B). The median T1D-free survival of liposome-pretreated mice was extended to five weeks after initiation of D-αPD-1 treatment; two mice of this group remained T1D free 8 weeks after the treatment. On the other hand, PBS-pretreated mice exhibited a median T1D-free survival of only one week. These results indicate that macrophages critically support D-αPD-1-induced T1D acceleration.

To further illustrate macrophages’ roles, we also investigated their FcγR expression in responses to D-αPD-1 treatment as the expression levels govern antibody-FcγR interactions and inflammatory signaling. Among four FcγRs—FcγRI, FcγRII, FcγRIII, and FcγRIV—expressed by mice, L-insulitis macrophages exhibited higher baseline expression of FcγRIII and FcγRIV compared with E-insulitis macrophages (Figure 3K). In L-insulitis, D-αPD-1 treatment significantly increased the number of FcγRI+, FcγRIII+, and FcγRIV+ macrophages relative to PBS controls: FcγRI+ cells increased from 30 to 182 per islet; FcγRIII+ cells from 24 to 149 per islet; FcγRIV+ cells from 14 to 119 per islet (Figure 7C). Moreover, D-αPD-1 treatment also elevated the expression levels of these receptors on the receptor-positive cells (Figure 7D). In contrast, in E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 reduced the number of FcγRI+, FcγRIII+, and FcγRIV+ macrophages (Figure 7E). Among FcγR-positive macrophages, expression levels of FcγRI and FcγRIII were unchanged, whereas FcγRIV expression was decreased following D-αPD-1 treatment (Figure 7F). Importantly, FcγRIII and FcγRIV were already expressed at higher baseline levels in L-insulitis compared with E-insulitis (Figure 3K). Their further upregulation following D-αPD-1 treatment in L-insulitis suggests that antibody engagement may establish a positive-feedback loop that reinforces macrophage proinflammatory activation and contributes to disease acceleration.

The Mac-3 subset dominates L-insulitis macrophage responses to D-αPD-1

Islet-infiltrating macrophages are composed of functionally distinct subsets.46 We, thus, investigated how these subsets contributed to macrophage responses to D-αPD-1. We classified islet macrophages using ReBaLa and transcriptional references for five subsets: Mac-1 (Apoe), Mac-2 (Atf3), Mac-3 (Cxcl9), Mac-4 (Prdx1), and Mac-5 (Stmn1)46 (Figures 8A and S7). We first examined how D-αPD-1 treatment would impact the cell numbers of these subsets. In L-insulitis, D-αPD-1 markedly expanded all subsets (Figure 8B). Among them, Mac-3 and Mac-1 showed the strongest augmentation, increasing by 4.3- and 4.2-fold. Notably, Mac-3 reached the highest absolute abundance after treatment, with an average of 193 cells per islet. These findings indicate robust expansion or recruitment of proinflammatory macrophage subsets into an already inflamed environment in L-insulitis islets (Figure 2E). In contrast, in E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 diminished all subsets. The treatment reduced Mac-4 and Mac-5 to zero cells per islets. The drastic reductions were observed in Mac-3 and Mac-2, whose numbers declined by 4.5- and 4.3-fold, respectively (Figure 8C). From another perspective, the relative abundance of Mac-3 increased from 41.0 % to 53.0 % among all macrophages after D-αPD-1 treatment in L-insulitis; the relative abundance of Mac-3 decreased in E-insulitis (Figure 8D).

For the first feature, IFN-α and IFN-γ module scores were significantly higher in L-insulitis Mac-3 than in E-insulitis Mac-3 (Figure 9A). In E-insulitis, D-αPD-1 elevated IFN-α scores but did not affect IFN-γ scores (Figure 9C). This responsive pattern of Mac-3 mirrored that observed in total macrophages (Figures 6B and 6C). DISCUSSION

Here, we investigated how D-αPD-1 exerted stage-dependent effects on T1D progression. The pancreatic islet immune microenvironment dramatically evolves from E- to L-insulitis, which likely contributes to the stage-dependent effects. Consistent with this notion, D-αPD-1 reduces immune cell numbers, including PD-1+ T cells, in E-insulitis islets but expands them in L-insulitis islets. Mechanistically, macrophages—particularly the Mac-3 subset—appear to be the primary mediator for the effect induced by D-αPD-1. These cells exhibited activation of inflammatory pathways, upregulation of FcγR, and higher cytokine expression. Importantly, diminishing macrophages attenuated the hyperglycemia-accelerating effects of D-αPD-1 in L-insulitis mice. The conceptual and translational significance of these findings is discussed from the following three perspectives.

A systems-level investigation framework to interrogate stage-dependent therapeutic responses

The first conceptual advance is the design and implementation of an innovative and complementary analytical framework to investigate T1D stage-dependent responses. Divergent responses to immunotherapies of T1D are increasingly recognized in both patients and preclinical models.47–50,73 However, mechanistic explanations for these responses have remained speculative or relied primarily on reductionist approaches centered on individual immune cell types.47,48 For example, the differential effects of Flt3-ligand were attributed to the stage-specific abundance of IGRP-specific CD8+ T cells.48 While informative, such single-cell-type-focused explanations have their limitations since they are formulated without a system-level examination of immune composition and functional states. On the other hand, therapeutic outcomes are more likely to emerge from interactions among multiple immune and non-immune cell populations and the signaling pathways of these cells, which was discovered for teplizumab and other experimental drugs.59

Our study adopted a global and innovative scRNA-seq analysis framework, including ReBaLa, that explicitly integrates disease stages, treatments, and the complexity of immune responses into considerations (Figures 2A and 2B). This framework enabled systematic interrogation of immune cell abundance and pathway activation across all major immune populations and finer subsets present in pancreatic islets. Importantly, beyond the intuitive focus on PD-1+ T cell compartments, we also examined non-intuitive cell types, including macrophages, NK cells, and DCs. This analysis framework is essential for uncovering the dominant role of macrophages—particularly the Mac-3 subset—in mediating the divergent effects of D-αPD-1. In contrast, a single-cell-type-focused approach would likely have emphasized PD-1+ T cells, overlooked macrophages, and consequently misattributed treatment outcomes. Indeed, benefits of such comprehensive methodologies have been demonstrated in a study on how spontaneous and blocking anti-PD-1 antibody-induced T1D are differently driven by immune mechanisms.74

The benefits of using this global scRNA-seq analysis framework were expanded through integration with targeted, hypothesis-driven approaches. Flow cytometry independently confirmed scRNA-seq-derived changes in immune cell abundance and macrophage activation states (Figures 4, 5, and 6). Most importantly, macrophage depletion experiments directly demonstrated that macrophages are functionally required for D-αPD-1-induced disease acceleration in L-insulitis mice, transforming transcriptomic observations into definitive mechanistic evidence (Figure 7B). Together, this integrated investigative framework highlights the power of combining unbiased transcriptomic profiling with mechanistic validation to dissect stage-dependent therapeutic responses in autoimmune disease.

Macrophages as key mediators of stage-dependent effects of D-αPD-1

The second conceptual contribution is the identification and establishment of macrophages as key mediators of the stage-dependent effects. Macrophages are well-established effector cells of antibody-based therapies.55,75,76 Meanwhile, accumulating evidence implicates them in the initiation and progression of T1D,56,77–80 a disease largely viewed as T cell-driven.59 Macrophages contribute to β-cell dysfunction and destruction through antigen presentation and cytokine-mediated signaling.56,81 Despite this knowledge, a critical gap remains regarding how macrophages interact with therapeutic antibodies across distinct disease stages and immune microenvironments of T1D, and how these interactions ultimately shape treatment efficacy. Our findings provide a mechanistic framework that begins to address this gap (Figure 10).

A model for how macrophages mediate D-αPD-1-caused T1D acceleration

(Upper) As T1D progresses from E- to L-insulitis, macrophages increase in abundance and acquire a proinflammatory state within pancreatic islets. (Lower) In L-insulitis islets, D-αPD-1 engages both PD-1+ cells and macrophages. The dual engagement further potentiates inflammatory programs in macrophages. The engagement leads to elimination of PD-1+ cells, thereby reducing PD-1+ cell-mediated attack on β cells. However, hyper-activated, inflammatory macrophages also directly damage β cells. In addition, they recruit and stimulate the proliferation of immune cells, including PD-1+ effector T cells, ultimately expanding β cell destruction.

According to our model (Figure 10), macrophages are already abundant within islets and transcriptionally primed toward a proinflammatory state in L-insulitis prior to D-αPD-1 treatment, evidenced by elevated IFN-γ and IFN-α response signatures and higher expression of activating FcγRs (Figures 3J, 3K, and 6A). These findings are consistent with previous discoveries that macrophage phenotypes transition from homeostatic and tissue-remodeling programs in E-insulitis to IFN-responsive, inflammatory states in advanced insulitis.46 Importantly, this inflammatory trajectory is mirrored in pancreatic macrophages in stage 2 T1D patients (Figures 3J and 3K).

Upon exposure to D-αPD-1, these proinflammatory macrophages further upregulate their inflammatory programs, including IFN-α, IFN-γ, TNF-α/NFκB, and complement pathways (Figure 6B; Table 2), while simultaneously increasing both the abundance and FcγR expression of FcγR+ macrophages (Figures 7C and 7D). These changes create a positive-feedback loop in which the engagement between D-αPD-1 and FcγR enhances macrophage activation, which in turn promotes further immune recruitment, inflammation, and FcγR upregulation. The downstream consequences of macrophage hyperactivation are multifaceted. First, activated macrophages facilitate PD-1+ cell depletion that is intended for D-αPD-1 usage, which should have a protective effect on β cells and islets. Second, the macrophages directly damage β cells through inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β.56,81,82 Third, macrophages secrete and utilize chemokines and cytokines such as CXCL9 and IL-1β to recruit and stimulate effector T cells and other immune populations. The recruitment and stimulation promote proliferation and activation of these cells within islets and consequently β cell destruction (Figures 6D–6F).59 In summary, these processes create a hyperinflammatory niche in which D-αPD-1 simultaneously depletes PD-1+ cells yet paradoxically induces immune stimulation and accelerates β-cell destruction and hyperglycemia in L-insulitis.

The mechanistic model is supported by functional evidence. Depletion of macrophages markedly attenuated the disease-accelerating effect of D-αPD-1 in L-insulitis mice (Figures 7A and 7B), establishing macrophages as a key mediator linking antibody treatment to pathological outcome. In addition, the report that activation of cytocidal macrophages rapidly accelerates T1D in NOD mice reinforces the pathogenic implication of the cell activation.81 As a further support of this model, in E-insulitis— characterized by fewer and less inflammatory macrophages—D-αPD-1 primarily executes its intended depleting function, reducing PD-1+ T cell populations without inducing excessive pathogenic effects of macrophages (Figure 4A).

Translational implications for PD-1+ cell-directed therapies

Results of this study reinforced a previously proposed strategy: ameliorating autoimmune diseases through PD-1+ cell depletion.22,23 In E-insulitis, or more broadly in a permissive immune environment, the application of D-αPD-1 effectively reduces PD-1+ cells and induces immune suppression. Consistent with this strategy, PD-1+ cell-directed therapies alleviate experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE)83 and pemphigus vulgaris84 and prevent heart transplantation rejection.83 Notably, a chimeric antibody, consisting of an anti-human PD-1 variable domain fused to a mouse IgG2a constant domain, prevented EAE progression in mice expressing human PD-1.83 This therapeutic benefit was observed when the antibody was administered at early disease stages (EAE scores 0 or 0.5), corresponding to a relatively mild inflammatory environment likely analogous to E-insulitis. Collectively, these findings indicate that depletion of PD-1+ cells through antibodies can be efficacious for T1D, provided that the immune context is permissive.

Compared with other antibody-based therapeutic strategies for T1D, PD-1+ cell depletion and the D-αPD-1 therapy remain in early stages of development, making the insights gained here particularly valuable. We observed that D-αPD-1 substantially reduced Th1 and TFH cells but had a limited impact on Th17 cells in E-insulitis (Figure 4E). The antibody also diminished both TCF1hi and TCF1lo CD8+ Teff cells (Figure 4G). Furthermore, E-insulitis macrophages and Mac-3 only exhibited modest or unaltered inflammatory signals and lower FcγR expression following D-αPD-1 treatment. Collectively, these features delineate an immune landscape in which D-αPD-1 delays hyperglycemia onset. Characterizing this landscape is critical for predicting when PD-1+ cell depletion is likely to yield favorable therapeutic outcomes. Future studies incorporating broader immune profiling—including blood, spleen, and lymphoid tissues—will be essential to construct a more comprehensive immune landscape and a reliable predictive framework.

In summary, this study reveals that the therapeutic outcome of D-αPD-1 in T1D is dictated not solely by PD-1+ T cell depletion but by the immune context in which the antibody functions. Macrophages are a central determinant of the therapeutic outcomes of D-αPD-1. Conceptually, this work underscores the necessity of immune stage stratification and systems-level analysis in immunotherapy and antibody-based therapy design. Translationally, it provides a framework for predicting therapeutic efficacy, mitigating adverse outcomes, and rationally optimizing PD-1+ cell-directed therapies for T1D.

Experimental procedures with mice

D-αPD-1 treatment and monitoring of diabetes onset

E-insulitis or L-insulitis mice were intravenously injected with D-αPD-1 (7.5 mg/kg body weight [BW]) or PBS once weekly until diabetes onset was confirmed. The design and production of D-αPD-1 have been described in the previous publication.26 Blood glucose levels were measured with tail-tip blood samples twice weekly using a CONTOUR NEXT EZ Blood Glucose Monitoring System (Ascensia Diabetes Care). Diabetes onset was defined as two consecutive blood glucose readings exceeding 250 mg/dL. Blood glucose levels were monitored until the median T1D-free survival had been reached for the PBS control group.

Effects of D-αPD-1 on immune cells in pancreatic islets

E- and L-insulitis mice were treated intravenously on days 0 and 2 with either D-αPD-1 (7.5 mg/kg BW) or PBS. Mice with blood glucose levels exceeding 250 mg/dL prior to the second dose were excluded from receiving the second injection. On day 3, mice were euthanized, and pancreatic islets were isolated for downstream scRNA-seq and flow cytometry analyses.

Determination of T1D stage

For the intravenous glucose tolerance test (IVGTT), mice were fasted for 4 h prior to receiving an intravenous injection of glucose (1 g/kg BW) in PBS. Blood samples were collected at predetermined time points following glucose administration: 3, 5, 10, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. Blood glucose levels were measured using a CONTOUR NEXT EZ system. Rest glucose levels in E- and L-insulitis mice were measured separately using the same system. Serum C-peptide levels were quantified using a mouse C-peptide ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum samples were diluted 20-fold prior to measurement, and C-peptide concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and expressed as ng/mL. T1D stage (normoglycemia, dysglycemia, or hyperglycemia) was determined based on fasting glucose levels, C-peptide concentrations, and IVGTT responses.

Macrophage depletion during D-αPD-1 treatment

To assess the effect of clodronate liposomes, L-insulitis mice received intravenous injections of the liposomes (100 μL per mouse; 5 μg/μL) or PBS on days 0, 2, and 4. Mice were euthanized, and pancreatic islets and pancreatic LNs (pLNs) were isolated for flow cytometry analysis to quantify macrophage abundance. To examine macrophages’ contributions to D-αPD-1 effects, L-insulitis mice received intravenous injections of clodronate liposomes (100 μL per mouse; 5 μg/μL) or PBS on days −2, 0, 3, and 5. D-αPD-1 (7.5 mg/kg) was administered intravenously on days 0, 3, and 5. Blood glucose levels were monitored three times per week starting on day 0 until the median T1D-free survival was reached for each group.

Preparation for suspended islet cells (scRNA-seq and flow cytometry)

Islet isolation and dissociation were performed with modifications to established protocols.85 Briefly, the pancreas was isolated after being perfused with collagenase IV (Worthington Biochemical) through the bile duct. Isolated perfused pancreas will be digested for 25 min at 37°C and washed for 3 times in cold Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS). Intact islet will then be handpicked under a stereomicroscope and dispersed into a single-cell suspension using a nonenzymatic cell dissociation buffer (Gibco) for 15 min at 37°C. After 2 rounds of washing with cold HBSS to remove the residual reagent, final suspensions were immediately processed for either flow cytometry or cell sorting for scRNA-seq analysis.

Procedures in scRNA-seq study

Sequencing

scRNA-seq was performed on CD45+ immune cells isolated from pancreatic islets of E- and L-insulitis mice following PBS or D-αPD-1 treatment. Islets from five to eight mice were pooled together to generate one scRNA-seq sample for one treatment. Cells were stained with anti-CD45 antibody and DAPI prior to flow cytometric sorting. Sorted live CD45+DAPI− cells were subsequently stained with TotalSeq anti-mouse hashtag antibodies for sample multiplexing. The cells were submitted to the High-Throughput Genomics and Cancer Bioinformatics Shared Resource at the University of Utah for library preparation and sequencing, where single-cell partitioning, lysis, and RNA capture were performed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. Libraries were prepared using the Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Gene Expression Library Prep Kit v3.1 according to 10x Genomics protocol. Sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit v1.5 with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. Raw sequencing data were processed with the 10X Genomics Cloud analysis pipeline. Gene expression reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) and converted into unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices using Cell Ranger count (v.8.0.0). Hashtag oligonucleotide reads were captured as feature barcodes, and cell barcodes were demultiplexed and assigned to their sample of origin based on their feature barcodes.

Data clustering and annotation through ReBaLa

The cell count matrices were loaded into the Seurat package (v.5) environment using R (v.4.3.2). The UMI barcodes from different samples were merged using the “Merge” function. The merged data underwent quality control, normalization (NormalizeData), identification of highly variable genes (FindVariableFeatures), scaling (ScaleData), and dimensional reduction through principal-component analysis (PCA). Barcodes (cells) were processed with the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions and subsequently visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). The numbers of CD45+ cells (unique barcodes) used for downstream analysis are as follows: E-insulitis (PBS), 2,280 cells (equivalent to cells from 8 islets, representing 8 mice); E-insulitis (D-αPD-1), 903 cells (equivalent to cells from 9.4 islets, representing 8 mice); L-insulitis (PBS), 8,882 cells (equivalent to cells from 10.4 islets, representing 5 mice); and L-insulitis (D-αPD-1), 9,574 cells (equivalent to cells from 4.7 islets, representing 8 mice). For each sample, only ~10% of the total CD45+ cells submitted were used for library preparation, with a maximum of 10,000 cells per library. Accordingly, the islet numbers reported above are post hoc estimates based on the proportion of cells incorporated into each library.

CD45+ cells (barcodes) were annotated with certain cell types using the ReBaLa method in combination with unsupervised clustering. Gene expression datasets of annotated immune cells from pancreas and spleen (CellXGene, Mouse Cell Atlas) were used as reference atlases.46,86,87 Seurat’s FindTransferAnchors and TransferData functions were used to transfer reference cell labels to our dataset. These labels were cross-referenced with unsupervised clustering results to validate their consistency. To increase resolution and capture subtle immune populations,88 we applied high-resolution clustering, generating 90 clusters (Figure S2A, left). Each cluster was assigned a predominant immune cell identity based on the majority label from reference-based annotation (Figure S2A, right). Specifically, clusters lacking a dominant cell identity (no single label exceeded 80%) were considered ambiguous and removed from downstream analysis: one cluster example was presented at the bottom of the left panel (Figure S2A). In contrast, clusters in which more than 80% of the cells shared the same predicted cell type were annotated to that type and preserved for downstream analysis: one cluster example was presented at the bottom of the right panel (Figure S2A). Using this integrated approach, we removed 9 clusters (Figure S2B, blue barcodes indicate removed clusters) and preserved 81 out of 90 total clusters (Figure S2C). These 81 clusters were assigned to major immune cell populations (Figure S2D).

By using ReBaLa, T cells were further classified into nine subtypes: CD4 and CD8 Teff, CD4 and CD8 Tn, CD4 and CD8 Tmem, CD4 and CD8 Tex, and Treg. CD4 and CD8 Teff were further subset into separate objects for further sub-annotation. Four CD4 Teff subsets—Th1, Th2, Th17, and TFH—were defined by canonical transcription factor and cytokine expression patterns. Four CD4 Teff subsets— TCF1hi and TCF1lo— were distinguished by TCF1 expression. On the other hand, macrophages were then subdivided into five subsets [Mac-1 (Apoe), Mac-2 (Atf3), Mac-3 (Cxcl9), Mac-4 (Prdx1), and Mac-5 (Stmn1)] using ReBaLa, based on previously reported macrophage subsets in the pancreatic islets of NOD mice.46

Identification of PD-1+ T cells using the gene set approach

Our first step is to identify a set of genes whose expression is positively correlated with Pdcd1. To this end, merged T cell barcodes were divided into Pdcd1+ (Pdcd1 expression > 0) and Pdcd1− (expression = 0) groups. A total of 665 DEGs were identified between these two T cell populations using Seurat’s FindMarkers function with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Four stringent criteria were then applied to narrow down to genes highly correlated with Pdcd1: (1) log2 fold change > 1 between Pdcd1+ and Pdcd1− cells; (2) expression detectable in more than 50% of Pdcd1+ cells; (3) expression detectable in less than 50% of Pdcd1− cells; and (4) expression primarily restricted to T cells. Using these criteria, a set of 17 genes was identified (Table S6). We next used the AUCell package to quantify the expression of this Pdcd1-correlated, 17-gene set, yielding a composite AUCell score for each T cell barcode. The score was used to classify T cells as PD-1+ or PD-1−. To determine a biologically meaningful AUCell score cutoff for PD-1+ T cell designation, we evaluated a range of thresholds and selected the cutoff that aligned the fraction of PD-1+ CD8 T cells inferred from scRNA-seq data with that measured by flow cytometry in the L-insulitis (PBS) sample.

Pathway enrichment analysis

DEGs between L- and E-insulitis (PBS), and between D-αPD-1 and PBS-treated groups were identified within each immune population using Seurat’s FindMarkers. The DEG analysis was carried out using the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum test. DEGs should have p adjusted value < 0.05, FDR < 0.05. Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB (retrieved via the msigdbr R package) were used for pathway analysis.89,90 The Hallmark gene sets databases were utilized for the pathway enrichment analysis based on the upregulated and downregulated DEGs. Pathway enrichment analysis conducted with the “ClusterProfiler” function in the Bioconductor package. In those tables related to pathway analysis (Tables 1, 2, and S1–S5), gene count means the number of DEGs shown in a certain cell population that is found in certain pathways, and gene ratio is the ratio between the DEG count and the number of total genes included in certain pathways. Adjusted p value was generated from Benjamini-Hochberg correction based on the raw p value calculated by the hypergeometric distribution.

Determination of IFN pathway module score

To specifically evaluate the activation of IFN pathways, a module score was calculated for each barcode. The gene sets used to calculate module score were obtained from the MsigDB hallmark database: Hallmark IFN-α RESPONSE and IFN-γ RESPONSE gene sets from MSigDB were used, consisting of 100 and 205 genes, respectively. The module score for each barcode was generated using the AddModuleScore function from the Seurat package. Statistical significance was determined using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, and the adjusted p values were computed using Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Analysis of human scRNA-seq data

scRNA-seq data for islet cells of T1D patients were downloaded from the PANC-DB database.91,92 Patients represented varied disease development stages were selected: stage 0, one autoantibody; stage 2, two autoantibodies with dysglycemia; and stage 3: more than two autoantibodies with hyperglycemia. Human islet cells were annotated using ReBaLa and human immune cell references obtained from the CellXGene database.93

Procedures of flow cytometry

Immune cell analysis

Single cells isolated from pancreatic islets were resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer and stained with fluorophore-conjugated surface antibodies, including BV510 anti-CD19, BV605 anti-CD3, BV650 anti-F4/80, BV750 anti-MHC class II, FITC anti-CD11b, PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-CD45, PE anti-NKp46, PE/Cy7 anti-CD64, and APC/Cy7 anti-CD11c, at 4°C for 30 min. Cells were then washed, stained with DAPI to exclude dead cells, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Macrophage and intracellular cytokine analysis

Single cells collected from pancreatic islets were cultured in complete RPMI medium with addition of Brefeldin A (1X) and Monensin (1X) at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h to inhibit cytokine secretion and allow intracellular cytokine accumulation. Cells were then collected, washed, and resuspended in PBS, followed by staining with ViaKrome 808 Fixable Viability Dye at room temperature for 15 min. Cells were subsequently incubated with an Fc receptor blocking reagent followed by surface staining using antibodies against PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-CD45, PE/Cy7 anti-CD64, BV650 anti-F4/80, BV395 anti-CD86, and BV605 anti-CD80 for 30 min at 4°C. After washing, cells were fixed and permeabilized using fixation/permeabilization buffer for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed and stained with antibodies against intracellular markers (PE anti-CXCL9 and APC anti-proIl-1β) for 30 min at room temperature. After final washes, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

T cell proliferation analysis

Mice received daily BrdU treatment (1 mg in 100 μL per mouse, intraperitoneally) from day −4 to −1 prior to euthanasia. Islet cells were collected, resuspended in PBS, and stained with ViaKrome 808 Fixable Viability Dye at room temperature for 15 min. Cells were then stained with antibodies against surface markers (PerCP/Cy5.5 anti- BV605 anti-CD3, CD45, BV510 anti-CD4, Spark UV387 anti-CD8, Pacific blue anti-CD62L, BV785 anti-CD44, and APC anti-PD-1) for 30 min at 4°C. Following washing, cells were resuspended in BrdU staining buffer and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Cells were washed twice with permeabilization buffer, followed by incubation with DNase I work solution at 37°C for 1 h in the dark to expose incorporated BrdU. Cells were washed twice with permeabilization buffer and stained with antibodies against intracellular markers (Pe/Cy7 anti-BrdU, FITC anti-Ki-67, and PE anti-TCF1) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After two additional washes with permeabilization buffer, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Gating strategy in a flow cytometry study

The gating strategies for immune cell subsets, PD-1+ cells, macrophages, intracellular cytokines, and proliferating cells and TCF1 expression are shown in Figures S8–S12.

Statistical analysis

Diabetes-free survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and statistical significance was assessed with the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Blood glucose curves were plotted in GraphPad Prism, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each individual mouse. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Two-group comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p values are shown in the figures unless differences were not significant (NS). Unless otherwise indicated, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For scRNA-seq analyses, differential gene expression and module score comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. DEGs were identified using FDR-adjusted p values < 0.05. Gene enrichment analyses were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks insulin-producing cells in the pancreas. Despite decades of research, only one drug, teplizumab, has recently been approved to delay disease progression. In this study, we identified a potential new therapeutic strategy using an antibody, depleting anti-PD-1 antibody (D-aPD-1), that removes specific immune cells carrying the PD-1 marker. When given during the early stages of disease—before diabetes symptoms appear in mice—the treatment delayed the onset of high blood sugar. These findings suggest that selectively targeting PD-1+ immune cells could become a promising approach for preventing or slowing T1D.

Equally important, this study provides cellular- and transcriptomic-level insight into why antibody therapeutics produce opposing outcomes at different stages of autoimmune disease. We investigated stage-dependent responses using an scRNA-seq analysis pipeline with innovative barcode assignment and PD-1+ T cell identification, complemented by flow cytometry and targeted cell depletion. These integrated approaches enabled comprehensive profiling of immune landscapes across distinct disease stages and their interplays with antibody treatment. Although T1D is commonly considered a T cell-driven disease, our analysis revealed macrophages as one key mediator for the opposing effects antibody therapies. Our analyses of the immune environments demonstrate crucial parallels between murine and human T1D stages. These similarities, together with other findings of this study suggest possible stage-specific biomarkers stratifying T1D patients, including macrophage abundance and their inflammatory status, as well as PD-1+ Teff/Treg ratios. Given the dynamic and heterogenous immune environments of T1D patients, such biomarkers may provide invaluable guidance for personalizing antibody-based treatments. Together, these findings underscore the importance of designing antibody-based interventions with careful consideration of disease stage and immune context, thereby offering a pathway achieving safer and more effective treatment outcomes for T1D.
