Background

Surgery for older adults is becoming increasingly common due to the ageing global population.1 These patients are more vulnerable to perioperative complications because of their low physiological reserve, altered pharmacokinetics and multiple comorbidities, particularly in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia.1 2 To enhance surgical outcomes and reduce healthcare costs, it is crucial to address modifiable risk factors, such as preoperative anaemia.3 4

Among the elderly, anaemia is a serious yet often overlooked public health concern associated with increased rates of morbidity, mortality, slower recovery and prolonged hospital stays.5 6 Over 40% of individuals aged 80 years and older, and 17%–25% of those over 65 worldwide, experience preoperative anaemia, with higher prevalence in low-income and middle-income countries.7 The 40%–50% prevalence among elderly patients who underwent surgery is primarily attributed to perioperative blood loss, chronic illnesses and nutritional deficiencies.8

Anaemia during the perioperative phase is associated with increased intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, prolonged hospitalisation, infections and poor wound healing.9 It also exacerbates haemodynamic instability and tissue hypoxia during surgery, particularly in patients with renal or cardiovascular comorbidities.10 Despite their ongoing prevalence, blood transfusions carry risks because of potential immunomodulation and fluid overload.11 The implementation of preventative and corrective interventions, such as iron therapy, blood conservation methods and restrictive transfusion protocols, remains limited in low-resource settings.12 13

The purpose of this study is to evaluate how preoperative anaemia affects the immediate postoperative outcomes of elderly patients who underwent surgery in Ethiopia. The findings will inform evidence-based perioperative policies to improve surgical care for this population, maximise resource use and facilitate recovery.

Methods

Study design, period and settings

This prospective cohort study was conducted from 1 December 2024 to 29 March 2025 at two comprehensive specialised hospitals in Ethiopia.

Study population

Patients who underwent emergency or elective surgery during the study period were classified as elderly, defined as individuals aged 65 years or older.

Eligibility criteria

A total of 224 patients aged 65 years and older, who underwent emergency or elective surgery in general, orthopaedic, urological or gynaecological fields, were included in the study. Individuals with terminal diseases, severe cognitive impairments or active bleeding disorders were excluded.

Adding a 15% non-response rate, the final sample size was 112 in each group, totaling 224 participants.

Finally, study participants were selected using a consecutive sampling technique from two comparable comprehensive hospitals—Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and Debre Markos Specialized Hospital—which offer similar surgical services. On average, each hospital handles 28 elderly patients monthly undergoing both emergency and elective procedures. A total of 224 patients were included in the study, with 112 selected from each hospital.

Study endpoints

Perioperative blood transfusions during surgery were the primary outcome variable (yes/no). The following pre-specified secondary outcomes were also reported as yes/no: hospital mortality, postoperative complications, prolonged hospital stay (defined as over 7 days), postoperative ICU admission and poor postoperative recovery. Lower scores indicate worse results, and the validated 15-item Quality of Recovery (QoR-15) score was used to assess recovery quality.

Using the WHO criteria for preoperative anaemia status (no anaemia: Hb>13 g/L in men, ≥ 12 g/L in women), the independent variables included age, sex, body mass index and anaemia status. Additional clinical considerations comprised surgical specialty, comorbidities, anaesthesia type, expected intraoperative blood loss, American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS) classification, haemodynamic status on admission and urgency of the procedure.

Operational definitions

Elderly

This typically refers to individuals aged 65 years or older. Those aged 65–74 are often referred to as the ‘early elderly’, whereas individuals aged 75 and older are classified as the ‘late elderly’.15

Postoperative blood transfusion requirement

This was defined as the receipt of at least one unit of whole or packed red blood cells at any time from the start of surgery until hospital discharge.16

Postoperative intensive care unit admission

This was defined as admission to the ICU after surgery for critical care management for any reason.17

Prolonged hospital stay

This referred to a hospital stay longer than 7 days.18

Hospital mortality

This referred to death occurring during the same hospital stay in which the surgery was performed.19

Postoperative complications

These referred to the occurrence of one or more cardiac and/or non-cardiac complications during the postoperative period until discharge.20

Poor postoperative recovery

This was assessed using the validated QoR-15 score, where lower scores indicate worse recovery based on a score below the mean.21

Data collection

Using structured, pretested and verified tools, data were prospectively gathered at Debre Tabor and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals from 1 December 2024 to 29 March 2025. To identify eligible patients aged 65 years and older undergoing elective or emergency surgery, trained nurses and anaesthetists checked operating rooms, critical care units and surgical wards daily. Sociodemographic, clinical and perioperative data were collected from laboratory databases, anaesthesia logs and medical records after obtaining informed consent. Trained interviewers administered the QoR-15 questionnaire 24 hours post-surgery. All study materials were anonymised and securely stored, with supervisors conducting real-time data quality checks.

Data collection procedures and quality assurance

Thorough quality assurance procedures were implemented to ensure the validity, reliability and integrity of the data. To minimise inter-observer variability, supervisors, hospital staff and data collectors underwent 2 days of standardised training on ethics, data abstraction and study procedures. The QoR-15 questionnaire and case report forms were pretested on 24 elderly patients who underwent surgery, with minor modifications made for improved clarity. Senior clinicians validated haemoglobin levels, ASA-PS classification and comorbidities, and daily supervision ensured real-time verification. EpiData 4.6 was used for double data entry, and SPSS version 27 was employed for validation and data cleaning. All anonymised data were stored in a secure location with restricted access.

Handling of quantitative variables in the analysis

The normality of the following quantitative data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms: age, haemoglobin, operation length, expected blood loss, hospital stay and QoR-15 scores. Medians with IQRs were used to present non-normally distributed data, whereas means±SD were used for normally distributed data. Age was analysed both continuously and in subgroups (65–74, 75–84 and ≥85 years). Haemoglobin levels before surgery were monitored and categorised using WHO guidelines (anaemia: Hb <13 g/L in men, <12 g/L in women). The continuous and categorical Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) was employed, comparing groups with a score of ≥5 versus <5. Poor recovery was defined by continuously analysing QoR-15 scores, which were dichotomised at the mean. For comparisons across subgroups, blood loss and operation duration were also categorised.

Statistical analysis

EpiData 4.6 was used for data entry, and SPSS version 27 was used for analysis. Histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test were employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Results were summarised as means±SD or medians (IQR), with frequencies (%) representing categorical variables. Bivariate comparisons between anaemic and non-anaemic groups were conducted using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to analyse binary outcomes—such as perioperative transfusion, ICU admission, prolonged hospital stay, poor recovery and in-hospital mortality—while controlling for age, sex, ASA-PS classification, CCI, type and urgency of surgery, anaesthesia type, estimated blood loss and preoperative haemoglobin level. The models included variables that were clinically relevant and those with a p-value of <0.20 in univariable analyses. Complete-case analysis was applied to manage missing data (<5%). This study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology criteria (online supplemental A).

Patient and public involvement in this study

None.

Results

Baseline sociodemographic profile of the study participants

Of the 224 elderly patients who underwent surgery, the mean age was 73.3±8.3 years, with 55.7% being male (112 with anaemia and 112 without anaemia). The majority of the patients (59.5%) were married, and most were aged between 65 and 74. Additionally, 39.9% had no formal education. A higher percentage of the patients in the anaemic group lived in rural areas compared with their non-anaemic counterparts (63.2% vs 56.1%), resulting in an absolute risk difference of 7.1% (table 1).

Impact of preoperative anaemia on immediate postoperative outcomes

A correlation was noted between preoperative anaemia and poorer immediate postoperative outcomes. Severe complications were more common in the anaemic group, including hypotension (14.0% vs 7.9%; absolute risk difference 6.1%), acute respiratory distress syndrome (4.4% vs 0.9%; absolute risk difference 3.5%), sepsis (6.1% vs 1.8%; absolute risk difference 4.3%), surgical site infection (12.3% vs 8.8%; absolute risk difference 3.5%), delirium (7.0% vs 5.3%; absolute risk difference 1.7%) and wound dehiscence (3.5% vs 1.8%; absolute risk difference 1.7%). Additionally, the anaemic group experienced higher rates of unexpected ICU admissions (17.5% vs 2.6%; p<0.001; absolute risk difference 14.9%) and overall complications (31.6% vs 19.3%; p=0.002; absolute risk difference 12.3%). In terms of hospital stay duration, 30.7% of patients in the non-anaemic group stayed longer than 7 days compared with only 9.6% of patients in the anaemic group (6 vs 5 days; p<0.001), leading to an absolute risk difference of –21.1%. The in-hospital death rate was also higher in the anaemic group (7.9% vs 1.8%; p=0.03; absolute risk difference 6.1%), and they reported a worse quality of recovery (QoR-15; 112.7±13.7 vs 128.2±13.1; p<0.001) (table 3).

Perioperative transfusion rates

Perioperative transfusion rates were significantly higher in the anaemic group than in the non-anaemic group (42.3% vs 18.4%; p<0.001; absolute risk difference 24.0%). This included transfusions during surgery (31.5% vs 8.1%; p<0.001; absolute risk difference 23.4%) and after surgery (17.1% vs 9.9%; p=0.007; absolute risk difference 7.2%). Furthermore, the anaemic group more frequently required larger transfusions of ≥3 units (10.5% vs 2.6%; absolute risk difference 8.0%) (figure 1).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis by surgical type

Subgroup analyses by surgical specialty (gynaecological, urological, orthopaedic and abdominal) assessed whether consistent relationships exist between preoperative anaemia and postoperative outcomes, even after multivariable adjustment for surgery type. Patients with substantial blood loss (>1000 mL) or very low haemoglobin (<8 g/L) were excluded from sensitivity analyses to evaluate robustness. The risks of perioperative transfusion, ICU admission and poor recovery were consistently higher in the anaemic group across all surgical categories, with the most significant impacts observed in abdominal and gynaecological surgeries. These results highlight the overall susceptibility of the anaemic group to unfavourable postoperative outcomes and suggest that the increased risk is not solely due to extreme cases or specific surgical specialties (table 4).

Postoperative complications

Preoperative anaemia was identified as an independent predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes through multivariable analysis. The anaemic group was more likely to be admitted to the ICU (RR=3.89; 95% CI 1.21 to 12.5; p<0.001), receive perioperative transfusions (RR=4.76; 95% CI 3.2 to 7.08; p<0.001), experience poor recovery quality (RR=2.87; 95% CI 1.54 to 5.35; p<0.001), have extended hospital stays (RR=2.14; 95% CI 1.15 to 3.98; p<0.001) and face a higher risk of in-hospital mortality (RR=3.60; 95% CI 2.40 to 5.40; p=0.015) (table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated 224 elderly patients who underwent surgery, with 112 having preoperative anaemia and the other 112 not. Although both groups had comparable sociodemographic characteristics, the anaemic group exhibited higher ASA-PS classifications, underwent more emergency surgeries and had greater comorbidity burdens. Furthermore, they experienced significantly higher rates of transfusions, ICU hospitalisations, surgical complications, longer hospital stays and poorer recovery and increased in-hospital mortality rates. These findings underscore the importance of early identification and treatment of anaemia during surgery to improve surgical outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Our findings support the well-established link between preoperative anaemia and poor surgical outcomes. The anaemic group as compared with non-anemic group required substantially higher transfusions both intraoperatively (31.5% vs 8.1%; p<0.001) and postoperatively (17.1% vs 9.9%; p=0.007), with an overall transfusion rate of (42.3% VS 18.4%;p<0.001). Furthermore, the anaemic group was more likely to receive larger transfusion volumes (≥3 units) (10.5% vs 2.6%). These findings indicate that preoperative anaemia significantly impacts perioperative treatment.

Furthermore, the study included subgroup analyses stratified by surgical specialty (gynaecological, urological, orthopaedic and abdominal) to determine whether preoperative anaemia consistently predicted unfavourable postoperative outcomes, even after multivariable adjustment for surgical type.

Across all surgical types, particularly in gynaecological and abdominal procedures, the results indicated that the anaemic group was more likely to experience poor recovery, ICU hospitalisation and perioperative transfusion. Sensitivity analysis validated these patterns, revealing that significant blood loss or high haemoglobin levels were not the only factors contributing to these relationships. This trend may reflect the decreased physiological reserve of the anaemic group, which increases their susceptibility to complications, blood loss and perioperative stress, regardless of the surgical specialty.

Preoperative anaemia independently predicted several negative outcomes beyond the need for transfusions. Multivariable regression analysis revealed significantly increased odds of perioperative transfusion (RR=4.76), ICU admission (RR=3.89), poor recovery quality (RR=2.87), prolonged hospitalisation (RR=2.14) and in-hospital mortality (RR=3.60). These findings align with earlier research results indicating that preoperative anaemia is associated with higher rates of morbidity and mortality in surgical populations (Musallam et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2017).22 However, our transfusion rates were higher than those of studies that implemented anaemia management strategies, such as Froessler et al, which reported a 30% reduction with intravenous iron therapy.23 These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in transfusion thresholds, surgical complexity or the limited preoperative optimisation in our setting.

Conversely, our rates were lower than those observed in major cardiovascular surgeries or high-risk trauma (Rehn M. et al., 2019),24 25 suggesting that transfusion needs are significantly influenced by surgical urgency and physiological stress. Consistent with data from low-resource settings, such as Madagascar and Congo, where routine anaemia screening and optimisation are often inadequate, patients with anaemia had approximately a fourfold increased risk of postoperative ICU hospitalisation.26 Ethiopian data indicated a six-fold increase in ICU admissions among patients with anaema who underwent emergency surgery, highlighting that the association was much stronger in emergency procedures.27 The diminished cardiopulmonary and renal reserves in older adults may exacerbate the negative effects of anaemia through compromised oxygen supply and multi-organ failure.28 29

The anaemic group recorded a 3.6-fold higher in-hospital mortality rate, exceeding the pooled estimates of 1.5–2.5 reported in various prior meta-analyses. Several factors contribute to this increased mortality rate, including a higher proportion of emergency surgeries, greater comorbidity and high ASA-PS classifications in the anaemic group, as well as resource constraints and limited preoperative anaemia optimisation.30 31 Additionally, age-related physiological deterioration and a lack of perioperative resources are likely to exacerbate this risk. Even modest anaemia is clinically significant, especially as older patients struggle to compensate for their reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. Previous studies have shown that poor tissue oxygenation can lead to organ dysfunction and multi-organ failure, both of which are major causes of mortality.32 33

Additionally, the anaemic group faced a significantly higher risk of prolonged hospital stays (RR=2.14), consistent with research on general and orthopaedic surgery.34 35 This global pattern emphasises the impact of anaemia on recovery and its burden on the health system, even though the processes may differ depending on the setting (eg, infection vs delayed mobilisation). Economic assessments from Canada and Germany indicate that the costs associated with extended hospital admissions due to anaemia are greater than those linked to other modifiable risk factors. This highlights an under-used opportunity for intervention.31 36

Ultimately, the anaemic group experienced a significantly lower QoR (RR=2.87). This finding aligns with results of previous research on major orthopaedic and abdominal procedures, which indicates a correlation between anaemia and delayed recovery in both function and cognition.37 38 Mechanistically, chronic tissue hypoxia, reduced metabolic reserve and dysregulated inflammation (high interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein levels) may prolong recovery, as confirmed by functional MRI studies that show delayed cerebral reactivation in patients with anaemia.39 40 These results suggest that the consequences of anaemia extend beyond the short-term postoperative phase, contributing to long-term morbidity.

In summary, preoperative anaemia in older patients is a strong and independent predictor of poor surgical outcomes. It significantly increases the risks of mortality, transfusion, ICU admission, inadequate recovery and prolonged hospital stays. To enhance outcomes and reduce medical costs, perioperative optimisation techniques should prioritise the early detection and treatment of anaemia.

Clinical Implications

This study offers convincing proof that preoperative anaemia is a reliable, independent indicator of poor surgical outcomes in elderly populations who underwent surgery. It is imperative that preoperative anaemia screening and optimisation be done because patients with anaemia have much higher rates of intraoperative and postoperative transfusions, ICU admissions, extended hospital stays, poor recovery quality and in-hospital mortality. Strengths and limitations of the study

This prospective cohort study is one of the few conducted in Ethiopia to evaluate how preoperative anaemia affects outcomes in older patients who underwent surgery. The use of the QoR-15 questionnaire, a well-defined geriatric cohort, and explicit eligibility criteria all contributed to the methodological rigour. Key factors such as surgical urgency, ASA-PS status and comorbidities were considered through multivariable analyses. Generalisability was enhanced by including both elective and emergency surgeries, and selection bias was minimised through successive sampling. However, the study’s limited scope—conducted at only two hospitals—restricts its national representativeness. Additionally, unmeasured confounding factors, such as nutritional deficiencies or undiagnosed conditions, cannot be ruled out. Variability in outcomes may also have arisen from institutional differences in perioperative management, compounded by the lack of long-term follow-up, which limited the investigation of late complications.

Future research directions

Future studies should involve large, multi-centre, longitudinal cohorts to assess how preoperative anaemia impacts the quality of life, cognitive function and long-term functioning of older populations who underwent surgery. Additionally, the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of anaemia care techniques, such as intravenous iron and erythropoietin, should be evaluated in low-resource settings through randomised controlled trials. The challenges and facilitators of routine anaemia screening and treatment should also be examined using implementation science methodologies. This approach will generate evidence for the development of context-specific perioperative care guidelines for low-income countries.

Conclusion

This multi-centre prospective cohort study found that preoperative anaemia was independently associated with increased risks of in-hospital mortality, prolonged hospitalisation, ICU admission, perioperative transfusion and poorer short-term recovery. These observational results recognise the limitations of causal inference in an observational design and emphasise the need for prompt detection and treatment of anaemia in resource-limited settings.
