A Decision‐Aid Improves Clinician Practice and Associated Patient Compression Stocking Adherence
Authors: Laila Bar, Susan Brandis, Rachel Wenke, Darryn Marks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdherence to compression stocking wear is essential for reducing venous leg ulcer recurrence, yet adherence rates remain low. This study examined the relationship between a novel digital clinical decision‐aid (the Personalized and Multidimensional Compression Assessment and Intervention [PAMCAI]), clinician practice, and patient adherence with compression stocking use, by embedding a prospective comparative cohort study within a pilot randomized controlled trial. The clinical notes of occupational therapists using PAMCAI were compared with those not using PAMCAI on their documentation of adherence and frequency of wear, identification of patient‐specific barriers and attempts to address barriers. The relationship between higher documentation scores and patient adherence was also explored. Thirty‐six medical records from 18 patients demonstrated significantly higher documentation scores when PAMCAI was used for all three categories (p < 0.001). Improvements in adherence were positively associated with documentation quality in the targeted categories (p < 0.001). These findings indicate that PAMCAI supports practice changes that translate to improved patient adherence, highlighting the potential value of clinician‐focused decision‐aids in venous leg ulcer care.

Introduction

Patient adherence to compression stockings is important for the long‐term management and prevention of recurring venous leg ulcers (VLUs)—wounds in the skin of the lower legs [1]. However, adherence remains low, leading to adverse health and economic consequences [2, 3, 4]. Many barriers to adherence have been documented [5], but efforts to address this low adherence have had limited success [6].

Recommendations for personalized and multi‐modal interventions to improve stocking adherence [6, 7] informed the development of a clinical decision‐aid grounded in behaviour change theory [8], named the Personalized and Multidimensional Compression Assessment and Intervention (PAMCAI) [9]. Delivered via a mobile health application on an iPad, PAMCAI includes a questionnaire designed to identify patient‐specific barriers to adherence and prompt the selection of tailored strategies to improve adherence. PAMCAI guides clinicians through a sequential, step‐by‐step process, with a customized template to document each clinical consultation.

Recognizing that adherence can be influenced at multiple stages within a patient's healthcare journey [10], behaviour change theory suggests that some patients may lack the capability or motivation to fully engage in their treatment, making it problematic to place sole responsibility for adherence on the patient [11]. Instead, clinicians may play a vital role in driving patient adherence to compression therapy [12, 13]. Recognizing this, PAMCAI focuses on influencing clinician practice, empowering them to create conditions that positively impact patient outcomes, rather than targeting patients directly. Although decision‐aids have previously been applied in broader VLU management [14], shown to positively influence clinician behaviour in practice [15, 16], and to support the broader role of clinicians in shaping patient health outcomes [17], these concepts have largely been examined in isolation. There is limited evidence regarding the effectiveness of decision‐aids in changing clinicians' practice in the context of compression stocking adherence. In addition, little is known about how clinician behaviour during consultations may influence patient adherence outcomes. Addressing these gaps is important for understanding whether interventions targeting clinician behaviour can translate into improved adherence to compression therapy.

This research addresses two questions, explored across two phases: How effective is a clinical decision‐aid (PAMCAI) in changing clinicians' practice?To what extent are changes in clinicians' practice associated with improvements in patients' adherence to wearing compression stockings?

Methods

Study Design

A prospective comparative cohort study was conducted alongside a previously published pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the efficacy and economic impact of the PAMCAI decision aid upon patient adherence with compression stockings. This pilot found that PAMCAI is likely efficacious and cost effective compared with usual care in the setting of VLUs [9]. The current study (Figure 1) sought to understand potential mechanisms behind these results, in particular how clinician care changed when using the PAMCAI decision aid (Phase 1), and whether those changes in practice were linked to changes in patients' stocking adherence (Phase 2) seen in the pilot study.

Overview of the study design.

Ethical Approval was granted through the Gold Coast Hospital and Health Service (GCHHS) Human Research Ethics Committee and Bond University Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2020/QGC/62843 and SSA/2020/QGC/62843). Informed consent was obtained from all clinicians and patients involved in the research. The current study was reported in accordance with both the Revised Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) guidelines [18], and the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely‐collected health Data (RECORD) guidelines [19].

Patient Population

The patient population has been described in detail in the published pilot RCT [9]. In summary, 20 adults with a history of VLU attending two occupational therapy vascular outpatient clinics within GCHHS between 2021 and 2023 were recruited; two patients were lost to follow‐up. Eligibility was based on documented history of VLU in referrals or clinical records and low adherence to previously prescribed compression stockings, determined using the Adhesig Screen [20].

Clinicians

Four clinicians participated, with a mean of 11 years of experience as registered occupational therapists (range: 2–28 years) and a mean of 3.4 years of experience working in vascular services (range: < 1–10 years). Three clinicians contributed consultation notes both with and without PAMCAI, whereas one clinician contributed only a single consultation in which PAMCAI was used, as a result of routine rotation across clinical services.

Study Design

The study was conducted in two phases. Phase 1 examined whether use of PAMCAI influenced clinician practice, operationalized through scores derived from coded clinician documentation. Phase 2 examined whether clinician practice (coded clinician documentation scores) was associated with patient adherence to compression stockings (using scores derived from the pilot study), irrespective of PAMCAI.

Measurement of Clinician Practice

To measure clinician practice in Phase 1, clinician documentation (clinical notes) was used as a proxy record of what occurred during clinical consultations [21]. Direct observation was beyond the scope of this pragmatic study, and the use of clinical notes also offered advantages including reduced intrusiveness, minimized social desirability bias and greater efficiency [22].

Occupational therapists typically record consultations using SOAP note‐based templates that organize narrative text under standardized headings [23], and this process continued for all patients in the study. In the group receiving PAMCAI, a research‐specific template was used that included prompts relating to compression stocking adherence, documentation of barriers and proposed strategies to address them.

In the absence of a gold standard tool to assess the comprehensiveness of clinical documentation, and consistent with other studies that have developed custom tools to assess intervention effects on documentation quality [24], a documentation rating tool was developed for this research to evaluate the extent to which documentation addressed key categories. This involved creation of relevant documentation categories and a scoring system to grade how comprehensively each category was addressed. Iteratively developed coding systems were used to systematically categorize data from the clinical notes [25, 26, 27]. The development of this rating tool was informed by the research questions, clinical relevance, and data availability and was further shaped by clinical expertise, increasingly acknowledged as a valuable complement to evidence‐based practice [28]. It was hypothesized that clinical care (reflected through documentation comprehensiveness) would improve for items 1–3, since PAMCAI prompts clinicians to address these items. In contrast, the fourth category (VLU) functioned as a control, as VLU status should be routinely documented as standard in every consultation, regardless of PAMCAI use. Its inclusion served to identify documentation bias, that is, the possible widespread improvement in documentation resulting from clinician awareness of the study [29].

A five‐point Likert scale (0–4) was developed to assess documentation comprehensiveness for each category [28, 30]. A score of 0 denotes ‘no’ mention, whereas 1 represents a ‘brief’ mention. A score of 2 indicates ‘some’ detail, 3 reflects ‘good’ detail and 4 signifies an ‘excellent’ level of detail. The scoring key and illustrative examples are available in File S1.

Baseline and 6‐month follow‐up clinical notes were analyzed for 18 patients (9 with and 9 without the use of PAMCAI), yielding 36 consultation records in total, as each record was considered an independent observation of practice with or without exposure to PAMCAI [28, 30]. The de‐identified data were coded and transcribed into dedicated spreadsheets. Two occupational therapy researchers, the lead investigator and an impartial assessor not affiliated with the project, independently reviewed and scored these blinded datasets. In instances of score disparities, consensus was achieved through discussion.

Measurement of Patient Adherence With Compression Stockings

Patient adherence was measured using Question 10 of the Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) [14], which rates compression therapy use over the past 4 weeks on a 0–3 scale, with higher scores indicating greater adherence. This method has been described in detail in a separate publication [9].

Data Analysis

Phase 1

Initial and final Likert scores for each of the four categories were recorded, and central tendency measures, including median Likert scores and interquartile ranges, were calculated. Group comparisons between consultations using PAMCAI and usual care were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS (version 29.0.1.0) and Microsoft Excel, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Phase 2

Inferential analyses using Somers' D correlation were conducted to assess the strength and direction of the relationship between the clinician documentation score (reflecting on the comprehensiveness of care) and changes in patient adherence with compression stockings. A total of 36 clinical notes were analyzed, representing 18 patients seen at two timepoints. Documentation scores were reported separately across the four categories.

Mann–Whitney U revealed significant between‐group differences for the three targeted documentation categories: Adherence/frequency of wear, identification of barriers and attempts to address barriers (p < 0.001). Discussion

This is the first study to empirically investigate the impact of a novel clinical decision‐aid (PAMCAI) on clinician practice and track its subsequent influence on patient stocking adherence within the context of VLU care. We found that clinical care across three targeted categories (adherence/frequency of wear, barriers to adherence and attempts to address these barriers) was significantly more comprehensive when using PAMCAI compared to usual care (p < 0.001). This suggests that the clinical decision‐aid (PAMCAI) influenced clinician behaviour by directing attention towards key adherence‐related elements within the consultation, prompting clinicians to create conditions more conducive to patient adherence. Clinical decision‐aids have previously been described as mechanisms capable of inducing positive shifts in consultation practices [31, 32], reinforcing the potential of PAMCAI to improve clinician practice in this context. The lack of impact on the documentation about VLU across both study arms strengthens confidence in these findings as this indicates they are unlikely to simply be attributable to clinician hypervigilance within the research environment.

Secondly, we have demonstrated that when clinicians are more attentive to stocking adherence‐related factors (by using PAMCAI in their consultation) patient adherence to compression stocking wear is significantly better (p < 0.001). This finding supports the proposition that changes in clinician practice are linked to subsequent changes in patient behaviour. Importantly, the use of Somers' D enabled examination of this relationship in a directional manner, consistent with the study's conceptual model in which clinician practice acts as a predictor of patient adherence outcomes, rather than as a reciprocal association.

The comprehensive structure of PAMCAI appears to have directed clinicians' attention to key adherence factors that may be overlooked in routine care, shaping their consultation in multiple ways. The patient‐clinician relationship is widely recognized as a major influence on healthcare outcomes, often through its impact on patient adherence to medical advice. Meta‐analyses [33, 34] have demonstrated consistent associations between stronger patient–clinician relationships and improved health outcomes, although definitions and measurement approaches varied substantially across studies. Components commonly associated with these relationships, including communication style [35, 36], motivational interviewing [37], patient‐centred care [38], and the use of written materials [35, 38, 39], extend beyond the interpersonal dynamic itself. Notably, many of these variables have been tested as controlled variables in intervention studies and are similarly embedded within the structure of PAMCAI [9].

The findings also align with previous research suggesting that interventions targeting clinician behaviour may be more effective in influencing patient behaviour than those aimed directly at patients [34]. PAMCAI was intentionally designed as a clinician‐facing tool, recognizing that patient‐facing decision‐aids may be challenging to implement in complex, chronic care contexts [8]. Supporting this approach, Kelley et al. [34] noted that clinician‐focused interventions may be more efficient given the higher patient‐to‐clinician ratio and the broader influence clinicians exert across patient populations. Furthermore, while interventions that target patients directly need to be simple and brief to be feasible, adherence—particularly in chronic conditions—is complex and often requires more nuanced and multi‐faceted approaches to be effective [40].

Several limitations of this study are recognized. It is possible that therapists' awareness of the research influenced both their practice and their documentation, despite the inclusion of a mitigating baseline indicator (VLU documentation), particularly if they anticipated that their documentation might be reviewed. In this study, this awareness was unavoidable due to the presence of an iPad to deliver PAMCAI. Furthermore, improving clinical documentation accuracy and completeness may have a positive impact on patient outcomes by encouraging clinicians to capture essential details, ensuring comprehensive care and informing future clinical decisions [41, 42, 43]. Although documentation records offer valuable insights, they may not fully capture the entirety of the care provided, including nuanced conversations between clinicians and patients [44]. Alternative methods, such as audio or video recording, were considered but excluded due to being technically challenging, costly and posing potential risks to patient privacy. In addition, the iterative approach used to identify documentation categories may limit the generalizability of findings. In particular, the baseline comparator category, which was not expected to change with the intervention, may have benefited from more systematic pre‐study validation to confirm its stability and independence from intervention effects. Additionally, a further limitation of this study is the relatively small number of clinicians and patients included. Although the findings provide preliminary insights into the relationship between clinician practice and patient adherence, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results, and the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings may be limited.

Conclusion

The clinical decision‐aid (PAMCAI) significantly improved clinicians' care provision in relation to compression stockings compared with clinicians not using PAMCAI. Furthermore, this improved care was positively correlated with higher patient adherence to compression stockings. Improved clinician practice appears to be the mechanism by which PAMCAI improves patient adherence to compression stockings. Further research is warranted to validate these findings in larger and more diverse settings.
