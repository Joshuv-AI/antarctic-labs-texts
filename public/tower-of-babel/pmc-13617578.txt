ST-ConMa: a multimodal foundation framework for spatial transcriptomics via image–gene contrastive and matching learning
Authors: Taelim Yeom, Dongha Choi, Hyunju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatial transcriptomics (ST) enables the spatial mapping of gene expression by combining tissue images with spot-level gene expression profiles, allowing for the analysis of gene distribution and interactions between adjacent regions. Although numerous deep learning models have been developed for ST, most existing approaches are task-specific, and multimodal foundation models with broad knowledge of ST remain scarce. While a few ST multimodal models currently exist, they have been either trained on low-resolution tissue images or developed for a specific platform, thereby limiting their generalizability when applied to diverse ST data. To address this gap, we propose ST-ConMa, a multimodal foundation model pretrained on large-scale spot-level tissue images paired with corresponding gene expression profiles using contrastive and matching learning. While conventional task-specific and foundation models generate modality-specific embeddings for images and genes separately, our model jointly learns image–gene fusion representations using a multimodal encoder equipped with cross-attention and a matching objective. Through extensive evaluations on histopathology image classification benchmarks and domain-specific tasks including gene expression prediction and spatial clustering, we demonstrate that ST-ConMa learns high-quality multimodal representations and consistently outperforms existing methods. These results indicate that ST-ConMa serves as a generalizable backbone for diverse ST approaches.

Introduction

Recent advances in sequencing technologies have shifted research paradigms in the life sciences from bulk RNA sequencing, which measures average gene expression at the tissue or cell-line level, to single-cell RNA sequencing [1, 2], enabling precise profiling at the individual cell-level. This shift has allowed researchers to identify heterogeneous cell types and key subtypes involved in disease progression, thereby providing a deeper understanding of pathophysiology and laying the groundwork for precision medicine [3, 4]. A similar transition toward higher resolution has occurred in histopathology, where traditional whole-slide imaging combined with bulk-level gene profiling is being complemented by spatial transcriptomics (ST) [5, 6], a technique that captures gene expression together with spatial context at spot-level resolution. This advancement enables detailed analysis of the tissue microenvironment and interactions between spatially adjacent regions.

With advances in deep learning, various models have been developed for ST and actively applied to key tasks such as tissue alignment [7, 8], cell-type deconvolution [9, 10], spatial clustering [11, 12], and gene expression prediction [13, 14]. However, despite ongoing progress in model development, the lack of large-scale ST data limits most deep learning–based ST approaches to task-specific applications. Such task-specific approaches can typically be applied only to a single disease or tissue type and therefore fail to acquire and exploit generalizable knowledge across diverse ST datasets. Recently, to overcome this limitation and facilitate the development of ST multimodal foundation models, several large-scale datasets containing paired spot-level tissue images and corresponding gene expression profiles have been released. Among these, HEST-1K [15], STimage-1K4M [16], and ST-Bank [17] are representative examples, collectively providing millions of spot-level image–gene pairs across diverse species, tissues, and platforms. These datasets lay the groundwork for training multimodal foundation models that can learn generalizable representations for ST.

Building on such datasets, recent efforts have adopted Vision–Language Model (VLM) frameworks—such as CLIP [18] and ALIGN [19], which align image and text representations via contrastive learning using dual-encoder architectures—to develop multimodal foundation models for ST. Among these, OmiCLIP [17] was pretrained on ST-Bank following the CLIP framework. However, OmiCLIP exhibits several limitations when applied to downstream tasks. Its dual-encoder architecture lacks an explicit cross-modal fusion module, which prevents the generation of integrated image–gene fusion representations and limits the ability to capture fine-grained interactions between tissue images and gene features. Additionally, the use of a broad gene set rather than a predefined gene set shared across datasets, as well as the low-resolution patch images used during pretraining, may further constrain the transferability and generalizability of its learned representations. Similarly, UMPIRE [20], another recently developed model trained following the CLIP framework with a predefined gene set, relies exclusively on Visium platform data for pretraining, limiting the scale and diversity of its training data (691K pairs). Furthermore, like OmiCLIP, UMPIRE adopts a dual-encoder architecture and is therefore unable to generate fusion representations.

To address the lack of multimodal foundation models in ST and to provide a domain-specific pretrained backbone with a fusion module, we propose ST-ConMa—a multimodal foundation model for Spatial Transcriptomics, pretrained on over 981K spot-level image–gene pairs using Contrastive and Matching objectives. Our architecture is inspired by VLMs and consists of three key components: gene, image, and multimodal encoders. The gene encoder enables the model to capture gene–gene interactions and generate informative gene representations. The image encoder processes spot images to generate corresponding image representations. The multimodal encoder integrates information from both modalities, enabling the model to learn cross-modal relationships between image and gene features. During pretraining, the model is jointly optimized for three objectives: image–gene contrastive (IGC), image–image contrastive (IIC), and image–gene matching (IGM) losses. The IGC loss promotes cross-modal alignment between image and gene representations, while the IIC loss aligns different augmented views of the same image to encourage consistency of image representations [21]. IGM loss follows the Image–Text Matching objective [22] commonly used in VLMs such as ALBEF [23] and FLAVA [24], and drives the multimodal encoder to learn a fused representation. The combination of these objectives and the architecture enables the model to learn robust intra-modality representations and to capture inter-modality relationships effectively.

Pretraining dataset and preprocessing

For pretraining, we used Homo sapiens samples from the HEST-1K dataset. The gene name formats in this dataset were inconsistent across samples, with some using Ensembl gene IDs and others including prefixes such as genome assembly references like “GRCh38.” To ensure consistent gene naming, all Ensembl gene IDs were converted to gene symbols using MyGene.info [25], and then prefixes were removed. Within each sample, duplicated gene names were discarded. We then selected 586 samples that contained at least 10000 genes overlapping with the common gene vocabulary derived from CELLxGENE [26]. From these samples, we extracted the common set of 7816 shared genes. Subsequently, we extracted only spots containing at least one nonzero expression value among the common gene set, each with its corresponding spot image. For the input to the gene encoder, gene names were arranged in a space-separated sentence consisting of the top 100 highly expressed genes (HEGs) for each spot, ordered from the highest to the lowest expression values. In total, after excluding three datasets reserved for evaluation in the gene expression prediction task—where true expression values are used as targets—534 samples remained for pretraining, amounting to 981K image–gene pairs. These samples encompass data generated using multiple ST platforms, including Visium, Visium HD, and Spatial Transcriptomics (Figure S1).

Image encoder

We adopt PathoDuet [27] as the image encoder, which is based on the ViT-Base/16 [28] architecture and captures both local and global features of histopathology images. Each spot image is processed to generate three distinct views: one unaugmented view and two augmented views. The unaugmented view is used to ensure stable alignment with the gene representation, while the two augmented views are employed for IIC learning. The augmented views are generated using random cropping (up to 50%) along with other transformations (Table S1).

Gene encoder

We adopt the Pythia-410M [29] model, fine-tuned on over 57 million single cells using the Cell2Sentence [30] framework, as the gene encoder. In this framework, gene expression profiles are represented as rank-ordered gene name sentences rather than raw expression values, which provides more robustness to batch effects. Moreover, the language model tokenizer enables processing of any gene without vocabulary expansion. These properties make the encoder particularly well-suited for ST, where data originate from heterogeneous platforms, gene panels, and donors. Following common practice in vision–language pretraining, where the earlier layers of the language backbone feed the contrastive objective [23, 31], we use the first 12 of the 24 Pythia-410M layers, reducing GPU memory and avoiding an excessive capacity gap with the image encoder. Pretraining details

We used four NVIDIA V100 32GB GPUs for pretraining. During pretraining, each GPU processed 32 spots, and gradient accumulation was applied for four steps, resulting in an effective batch size of 512. We employed the AdamW [34] optimizer with a weight decay of 0.01 and applied a cosine learning rate scheduler with a 1-epoch linear warmup. The model comprises 395.9M trainable parameters and was trained for 12 epochs. Training took ∼26 h in total, with peak GPU memory usage of about 31GB (Table S2). The same GPU configuration was also used for all downstream tasks.

Downstream evaluation

Histopathology image representation evaluation

We evaluated the quality of the image representations learned by ST-ConMa using k-means clustering and linear probing on four histopathology image classification datasets: CAMELYON17-WILDS [35], NCT-CRC-HE-100K [36], WSSS4LUAD [37], and MHIST [38] (Note S1). We compared ST-ConMa with OmiCLIP, a multimodal foundation model for ST; PathoDuet, the image encoder used to initialize ST-ConMa; and an ablation model, ST-ConMa w/o IIC, which was pretrained without the IIC objective.

For clustering evaluation, k-means was applied directly to the representations extracted from the frozen image encoders. The number of clusters was fixed to the number of ground-truth classes in each dataset, and clustering performance was assessed using Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) (Note S2). Clustering was performed on the official test split when available. For WSSS4LUAD, whose evaluation splits provide pixel-level annotations rather than the single-class image-level labels required by our protocol, we instead used the single-labeled images from the training split. The number of k-means initializations was set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n_{\mathrm{init}}=50$\end{document}.

For linear probing, all encoder parameters were frozen and only a single linear classification layer was optimized. Macro-F1 and balanced accuracy were used as evaluation metrics. Hyperparameters were selected using the available validation split or cross-validation performed exclusively on the training data, and final performance was evaluated on the held-out test data. For WSSS4LUAD, performance was estimated using five-fold cross-validation. All linear-probing experiments were repeated with five random seeds, and the mean and standard deviation are reported. Detailed dataset preprocessing, hyperparameter search spaces, and optimization settings are provided in Note S3.

Gene expression prediction

We evaluated gene expression prediction on three ST benchmark datasets: HER2ST [13], comprising HER2-positive breast cancer samples; cSCC [39], comprising cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma samples; and HLT [40], comprising normal human liver tissue samples. After restricting the expression profiles to the 7816-gene vocabulary used during pretraining, spots with zero expression across all retained genes were excluded. Prediction performance was evaluated separately on 250 HEGs and 250 highly variable genes (HVGs). Detailed dataset characteristics and preprocessing procedures are provided in Notes S4 and S5.

We compared ST-ConMa with six gene expression prediction methods: HisToGene [41], Hist2ST [14], THItoGene [42], BLEEP [43], mclSTExp [44], and OmiCLIP [17]. The same evaluation folds and target gene sets were used for all methods. Detailed descriptions of the baseline methods are provided in Note S6.

For HER2ST and cSCC, we adopted leave-one-patient-out cross-validation. In each fold, all tissue sections and replicates from one patient were held out for testing, ensuring that no spot from the test patient was included in model training and reference-set construction. Because sections from the same patient may share patient-specific histological and molecular characteristics, this protocol provides a stringent assessment of generalization to unseen patients. For HLT, which comprises four tissue sections from a single donor, we instead employed leave-one-out cross-validation, with one section held out for testing in each fold and the remaining three sections used for training. An example of the data-splitting procedures is illustrated in Figure S2.

For downstream adaptation, ST-ConMa was fine-tuned using image–gene pairs from the training fold. The training procedure followed that used during pretraining, except that the IIC objective was excluded, such that the model was optimized using the IGC and IGM objectives. Image augmentation was applied during gene expression fine-tuning, but resizing was disabled to preserve precise alignment between spot-centered images and their corresponding gene profiles (Table S3). The complete optimization settings and implementation details are provided in Note S7 and Table S4.

For each test image, gene expression was inferred using a similarity-weighted combination of the expression profiles from the reference set. We additionally evaluated ST-ConMa in a zero-shot setting. In this setting, all encoder parameters were kept fixed at their pretrained values, and no gradient-based optimization was performed. Multimodal representation evaluation

To examine whether the multimodal encoder produces more discriminative representations than either modality alone, we performed linear probing on the DLPFC dataset [45]. The dataset consists of 12 tissue sections from three donors, each annotated with either five or seven spatial domains corresponding to cortical layers and white matter. The sections form six biological replicate pairs (Note S8).

Representations were extracted from the frozen image, gene, and multimodal encoders, and only a single linear classifier was trained on top of each representation. To assess the effectiveness of the multimodal encoder, we additionally evaluated a simple concatenation of the image and gene representations.

We employed replicate-pair-wise six-fold cross-validation. In each fold, one biological replicate pair, comprising two tissue sections, was held out for testing, while the remaining 10 sections were used for classifier training and validation. Performance was evaluated using balanced accuracy, and the complete procedure was repeated with five random seeds. We report the mean and standard deviation across the five runs. Detailed data splitting, optimization, early-stopping, and training settings are provided in Note S9.

Spatial clustering

We further evaluated spatial clustering performance on the DLPFC dataset and the human breast cancer dataset [46]. The DLPFC dataset shares the same characteristics as the one used in the previous section, whereas the human breast cancer dataset consists of a single tissue section annotated with 20 spatial domains. Detailed dataset characteristics are provided in Note S10.

For each tissue section, ST-ConMa was adapted using the corresponding image–gene pairs, following the same adaptation protocol as in gene expression prediction, except that image augmentation was excluded. After adaptation, spot-level fusion representations were extracted from the multimodal encoder and incorporated into the STAIG framework. Specifically, the ST-ConMa fusion representations replaced the default ResNet50-based image representations used for edge augmentation, and were also used to generate the pseudo-labels employed for negative-spot adjustment (Note S11).

We compared the resulting method with six spatial clustering baselines: SpaGCN [11], STAGATE [12], DeepST [47], GraphST [48], MuCoST [49], and STAIG [50]. For all methods, the number of clusters was fixed to the number of ground-truth spatial domains in each dataset: five or seven clusters for each DLPFC tissue section, and 20 clusters for the human breast cancer tissue section. To evaluate the learned spot representations without post-processing effects, label-refinement procedures were disabled where applicable. Clustering performance was assessed using ARI and NMI (Note S2). Detailed descriptions of all baseline methods are provided in Note S12.

Biological characterization of spatial domains

To investigate the biological characteristics of the spatial domains identified by ST-ConMa, we performed differential gene expression analysis for each domain by comparing spots assigned to the target domain with spots assigned to all remaining domains. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the resulting P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Significantly upregulated genes were subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Detailed procedures, including the fold-change and P-value thresholds, gene-set libraries, and enrichment settings, are provided in Note S13.

Results

Image–image contrastive loss improves image representation quality

We first examined whether incorporating the image encoder-specific IIC objective preserves and enhances image representation quality during image–gene alignment. To this end, we compared clustering and linear-probing performance on four histopathology datasets against OmiCLIP, PathoDuet, and the w/o IIC ablation. In the clustering evaluation, ST-ConMa consistently demonstrates superior performance across all four datasets (Fig. 2). It achieves the highest ARI on all datasets and the highest NMI on three datasets, with MHIST being the only exception.

UMAP visualization of image representations from OmiCLIP, PathoDuet, ST-ConMa without the IIC objective (w/o IIC), and ST-ConMa on the (a) CAMELYON17-WILDS, (b) NCT-CRC-HE-100K, (c) WSSS4LUAD, and (d) MHIST datasets, with ARI and NMI reported for each model (best in bold, second-best underlined).

On the CAMELYON17-WILDS dataset, ST-ConMa yields substantial improvements over PathoDuet, achieving relative increases of 172.6% in ARI and 127.5% in NMI (Fig. 2a). The ablation variant also shows improvements, although these gains are markedly smaller than those obtained when the IIC objective is included. Compared with OmiCLIP, ST-ConMa achieves ARI and NMI scores that are 161.6% and 144.7% higher, respectively. The advantage of the IIC objective is also evident under the linear-probing setting (Table S5).

On NCT-CRC-HE-100K, ST-ConMa again achieves the best clustering performance (Fig. 2b). In contrast to CAMELYON17-WILDS, the ablation variant exhibits performance degradation compared with PathoDuet, with relative drops of 8.8% in ARI and 3.0% in NMI, whereas including the IIC objective yields relative gains of 3.9% and 0.9%, respectively. OmiCLIP shows the lowest performance among all evaluated models, with ST-ConMa achieving 45.4% higher ARI and 30.3% higher NMI. Consistent trends are observed under the linear probing setting, where ST-ConMa achieves 15.0% higher Macro-F1 and 15.7% higher balanced accuracy than OmiCLIP.

For WSSS4LUAD, ST-ConMa consistently outperforms PathoDuet across all evaluation metrics, achieving relative gains of 16.6% in ARI and 29.5% in NMI (Fig. 2c). Similar trends are observed under the linear-probing setting. In the case of MHIST, ST-ConMa attains the highest ARI, while the ablation variant achieves the highest NMI (Fig. 2d). Notably, under linear probing, Macro-F1 and balanced accuracy decrease compared with PathoDuet. However, when a nonlinear MLP classifier is used instead, performance recovers, suggesting that multimodal pretraining produces image representations that are semantically well-clustered but not linearly separable on this dataset.

Across all datasets, the UMAP visualizations further corroborate the quantitative results. While OmiCLIP and the ablation variant exhibit substantial overlap in representations of critical tissue distinctions, such as Tumor and Normal, ST-ConMa consistently produces more clearly separated clusters, indicating superior representational separability. We further evaluated ablation variants without the other pretraining objectives using clustering and linear probing, as reported in Figure S3 and Table S6. Taken together, these results suggest that catastrophic forgetting in pretrained image encoders during image–gene alignment can be effectively mitigated by the IIC objective, thereby preserving and enhancing image representation quality.

We compared it against six baselines under both fine-tuning and zero-shot settings, focusing on generalization to patients unseen during training. Across all three benchmarks, ST-ConMa achieves the best performance on 10 of the 12 evaluated metrics (Table 1). For HER2ST, ST-ConMa (zero-shot) ranks third for HEGs and second for HVGs, while for cSCC it ranks fourth for both gene subsets. In contrast, OmiCLIP exhibits substantially weaker zero-shot performance on these datasets. On HER2ST, it yields negative PCC values, indicating that its predictions are inversely correlated with the ground-truth. On cSCC, although positive correlations are observed, the PCC scores remain ∼34.9% and 29.4% lower than those achieved by ST-ConMa (zero-shot) for HEGs and HVGs, respectively. These results indicate that the pretrained weights of ST-ConMa generalize effectively to unseen cancer-related datasets, suggesting that the IGC objective effectively aligns image and gene representations during the pretraining stage.

This strong generalization capability is further reflected under full fine-tuning. Relative to OmiCLIP, ST-ConMa achieves 6.6% and 14.3% higher values on HER2ST for the HEGs and HVGs. A similar pattern is observed for cSCC, where ST-ConMa attains 13.6% and 10.3% higher values, respectively. For normal human liver dataset (HLT), the performance margin is even more pronounced, with relative improvements of 65.8% and 1.6%.

We further examined whether performance depends critically on the size of the reference set by reducing it to 25%, 50%, and 75% of the training spots (Figures S4–S6). Although the full reference set yields the best performance across all three datasets, using only 25% still produces comparable PCC values on both the HEG and HVG sets, indicating that the retrieval-based prediction is robust to reference set size. To assess the statistical significance of the performance differences, we conducted pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests between ST-ConMa and each baseline model using the average gene-wise PCC and RMSE scores. ST-ConMa outperformed the baselines in 63 of the 68 comparisons, and 60 of these improvements were statistically significant (P <.05; Tables S7 and S8).

ST-ConMa effectively captures biologically relevant gene signatures

Beyond the overall gene expression prediction results, we analyze a subset of disease- and tissue-related genes curated from GeneCards [51] to assess whether the model accurately captures biologically relevant gene signatures (Note S14). On this subset, ST-ConMa achieves the best performance on 9 out of the 12 evaluation metrics (Table S9). Consistent with the results obtained using all 250 genes, ST-ConMa (zero-shot) again attains the lowest RMSE on the cSCC dataset, further demonstrating robust generalization to biologically meaningful gene subsets.

For the HER2ST dataset, we observe consistently higher PCC values for clinically important genes, including CD24, HSP90AB1, GNAS, and ERBB2, compared with other models (Table S10). Among these, ERBB2 (HER2) is a well-established clinical biomarker in breast cancer [52], and CD24 has been reported to support HER2 expression and contribute to resistance to lapatinib [53]. Multimodal encoder effectively integrates image and gene features

We then investigated whether the multimodal encoder yields more discriminative representations than either modality alone. Using the DLPFC dataset, we performed linear probing on representations from the frozen image, gene, and multimodal encoders, along with a simple concatenation of the two as a baseline. The fusion representations produced by the multimodal encoder achieve the highest balanced accuracy on 8 of the 12 sections, and the second-highest on two more (Table 2). A notable observation is that pretraining substantially improves the biological relevance of representations produced by both the image and gene encoders. On average, pretraining leads to a relative improvement of 55.7% for the image encoder and 49.9% for the gene encoder. These results indicate that aligning gene and image representations during pretraining enables the encoders to capture complementary information, leading to representations that better distinguish cortical layers.

As shown in Fig. 4, the model employing our fusion representation consistently outperforms the other six baseline models in terms of ARI and NMI. On the DLPFC dataset, ST-ConMa achieves an average ARI of 0.5970 and NMI of 0.6763 (Fig. 4a). These values are 12.9% and 5.1% higher than those of STAIG (second-best), which achieved 0.5286 and 0.6434, respectively.

Fusion representation of ST-ConMa enables fine-grained domain analysis

To assess whether the spatial clustering results of our model on the human breast cancer dataset are biologically meaningful, we conducted a more detailed analysis of a representative region. In the following analysis, we refer to the spatial regions identified by the STAIG framework with our fusion representations as domains, while clusters denote the groups obtained by applying k-means clustering directly to the fusion representations produced by the multimodal encoder. Specifically, we focused on Domain 8, which is broadly distributed across the tissue and largely overlaps with Clusters 14 (sage) and 19 (dark green) (Fig. 5a, 5b). Differential expression analysis comparing Domain 8 with the other domains identifies 670 genes that are significantly upregulated in this region (Fig. 5c). Among the top-ranked genes, several immune-related genes—including IGHG3, IGLC2, IGKC, C3, and IGHG1—are prominently enriched (Fig. 5e). These are immunoglobulin-related genes predominantly expressed by B cells and plasma cells, and C3 is a central component of the complement system, collectively indicating that this domain corresponds to an immune-activated region.

Analysis of Domain 8 identified in the spatial clustering results of ST-ConMa, showing (a) the fusion representations and clustering predictions generated by ST-ConMa for the human breast cancer dataset, (b) the spatial distribution of Domain 8, (c) a volcano plot of DEGs between Domain 8 and the remaining domains, (d) enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in Domain 8, (e) violin plots of the top five genes upregulated in Domain 8, (f) further partitioning of Domain 8 into finer subdomains, and (g) enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in the center/right subdomain.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses further support this interpretation (Fig. 5d). In the GO Biological Process category, pathways related to antigen receptor–mediated signaling and the B cell receptor signaling pathway are significantly enriched, while the Molecular Function category highlights MHC class II–related activities. KEGG pathway analysis reveals significant enrichment of Th1/Th2 and Th17 cell differentiation pathways, reflecting active interactions between T cells and B cells. Collectively, these enrichment patterns support the interpretation of Domain 8 as a spatially structured, immune-activated niche.

We further examined the subdivision of Domain 8 into two distinct clusters (Clusters 14 and 19) (Fig. 5f). The left lower region exhibits significant upregulation of IGHA1 and CD79A (Figure S9), suggesting a humoral immune niche enriched with antibody-secreting plasma cells and B cells. In contrast, the center/right region shows significant upregulation of genes including BGN, DCN, VCAN, and COL1A1, which are well-established markers associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [55, 56]. Enrichment analyses of this region reveal significant enrichment of pathways related to collagen-containing extracellular matrix and metalloendopeptidase inhibitor activity (Fig. 5g), indicating more pronounced extracellular matrix remodeling.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the left lower region corresponds to a humoral immune niche, whereas the center/right region represents a CAF-dominant immune–stromal niche. More broadly, these results demonstrate that leveraging our fusion representation enables fine-grained dissection of heterogeneous domains, facilitating detailed characterization of distinct microenvironments and potentially revealing novel clinically relevant niches.

Discussion and conclusion

In this work, we propose ST-ConMa, a multimodal foundation model and pretraining framework designed to leverage large-scale spot-level image–gene pairs for ST. By jointly learning image, gene, and fused multimodal representations through contrastive and matching objectives, ST-ConMa provides a unified backbone for a wide range of ST-specific tasks.

Although ST-ConMa adopts contrastive and matching objectives from established VLM frameworks, a key distinction of ST-ConMa is its explicit learning of fused image–gene representations for ST. Existing ST multimodal foundation models such as OmiCLIP and UMPIRE mainly align image and gene representations using dual encoders, whereas ST-ConMa further employs a multimodal encoder to capture interactions between histological image patches and gene features. In addition, the IIC objective helps preserve pathology-relevant visual information during multimodal pretraining. Together, these design choices provide complementary image, gene, and fused multimodal representations that can be transferred to diverse tasks.

Across extensive evaluations, ST-ConMa demonstrates strong and consistent performance. The IIC objective is shown to be essential for preserving and enhancing image encoder performance during multimodal pretraining. In gene expression prediction, the model exhibits robust generalization to unseen patients, particularly under zero-shot settings on cancer-related datasets, suggesting that the learned image–gene alignment is not overly dependent on dataset-specific fine-tuning. In spatial clustering, the fusion representation enables fine-grained characterization of tissue heterogeneity, identifying spatial domains that correspond to known biological structures, including humoral immune niches and CAF-dominant immune–stromal niches.

While the pretraining dataset comprises diverse tissues and conditions, the total number of samples remains limited in terms of inter-sample variability, which may restrict generalization to rare tissue types or disease contexts. As ST datasets continue to grow in both scale and diversity, we anticipate that the effectiveness and generalizability of ST-ConMa will further improve.

Taken together, our findings suggest that ST-ConMa serves as a robust multimodal backbone for ST. By enabling effective integration of histopathology images and gene features, it provides a scalable foundation for the development of future ST-specific approaches and for the discovery of biologically and clinically relevant tissue microenvironments.
