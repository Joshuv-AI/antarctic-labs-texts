Structural Fluctuation in Homodimeric Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetases Induces Half-of-the-Sites Activity
Authors: Yoshino Okamoto, Takunori Yasuda, Rikuri Morita, Yasuteru Shigeta, Ryuhei Harada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Enzymatic activity is regulated by various mechanisms to ensure biologically proper functions. Notable instances of such regulation in homodimeric enzymes include “all-of-the-sites activity” and “half-of-the-sites activity”. The difference in these activities lies in whether one or both of the subunits are simultaneously active. Owing to its uniqueness, the mechanism of half-of-the-sites activity has been widely investigated. Consequently, structural asymmetry derived from cooperative motion is considered to induce half-of-the-sites activity. In contrast, recent investigations have suggested that subunit-intrinsic properties or structural fluctuation also induces structural asymmetry. Hence, the mechanism underlying half-of-the-sites activity has not been completely elucidated. Additionally, most previous studies have focused only on half-of-the-sites activity. Therefore, by comparing the structural and dynamical properties of two representative homodimers exhibiting all-of-the-sites and half-of-the-sites activities, respectively, we attempted to elucidate the mechanism of half-of-the-sites activity. Specifically, all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were applied to lysyl-tRNA synthetase and tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase. Our analysis revealed that structural fluctuation is sufficient to induce structural asymmetry in addition to the well-established factor of cooperative motion. Considering that structural fluctuation is a common characteristic of any enzyme, it could be a general factor in half-of-the-sites activity.

Introduction

In cells, enzymes must function only for the appropriate times, locations, and quantity. Hence, enzymatic activity is highly regulated by various factors.1 The binding of specific activation proteins or post-translational modifications is a well-established factor in the regulation of enzymatic activity.2,3 Beyond the regulation imposed by external factors, self (homo) oligomerization is also recognized as a factor in the regulation of enzymatic activity.4,5

Among homo-oligomers, homodimers have been extensively investigated because they are the most common type of homo-oligomers (54% of homo-oligomers deposited in the Brenda database).4 Consequently, the regulation of the homodimeric activity has been stoichiometrically classified into all-of-the-sites or half-of-the-sites activities. The difference in these activities lies in whether one or both the subunits are simultaneously active.6−8 Based on these classifications, at first glance, half-of-the-sites activity appears to have a disadvantage compared to all-of-the-sites activity in decreasing enzyme efficiency. However, previous studies have reported its advantages in enzyme efficiency, such as promoting the dissociation of products9 and compensating for entropy by the empty subunit.10 As a result of these studies, half-of-the-sites activity is now widely considered to be one of the strategies used by enzymes to maximize enzyme efficiency depending on their surrounding environmental conditions.7

Owing to its uniqueness, the mechanisms that induce half-of-the-sites activity have been extensively studied, despite the limited prevalence of enzymes exhibiting this type of activity (1% of dimers deposited in the Protein Data Bank, PDB).6 For instance, a biochemical and crystallographic study on the homodimeric protease caspase 9 demonstrated that structural asymmetry in the catalytic domains leads to half-of-the-sites activity.11 In another instance, tRNA-(N1G37) methyltransferase (TrmD) showed that the collapse of structural asymmetry by a point mutation led to conversion from half-of-the-sites activity to all-of-the-sites activity.12 Consequently, half-of-the-sites activity is considered to be derived from structural asymmetry between the two subunits.6

Regarding factors that induce structural asymmetry, various studies have focused on the cooperative motion of enzymes.13−16 For instance, the Koshland–Nemethy–Filmer (KNF) model is well established as a theoretical model, in which ligand binding for one subunit causes allosteric structural changes in other subunits.17,18 Along with theoretical models, experimental or computational studies on several enzymes have reported that cooperative motion drives structural asymmetry.10,19 In contrast, recent investigations suggest that subunit-intrinsic properties or structural fluctuation also induce structural asymmetry.13,20 As described above, various factors that induce structural asymmetry have been proposed from different perspectives and the underlying mechanism has not been completely elucidated. Additionally, most previous investigations focused only on enzymes exhibiting half-of-the-sites activity,10−12 and quantitative comparison with enzymes exhibiting all-of-the-sites activity has not yet been conducted.

In this study, by comparing the structural and dynamical properties of enzymes exhibiting all-of-the-sites or half-of-the-sites activities, we attempted to elucidate the mechanism underlying half-of-the-sites activity. Herein, as model enzymes, we focused on homodimeric lysyl-tRNA synthetase (LysU) and homodimeric tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase (TyrRS), both of which belong to the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase family and attach amino acids to their cognate tRNAs.21 The LysU dimer exhibited all-of-the-sites activity upon binding to the first substrate (lysine).22 In contrast, the TyrRS dimer exhibited half-of-the-sites activity upon first substrate (tyrosine) binding, suggesting structural asymmetry in the apo form.23−25 We performed all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of these two dimers in the apo form and confirmed their differences in the structural symmetry of the binding pockets. Subsequently, we compared the structural features of the LysU and TyrRS dimers to reveal the mechanism underlying this structural difference. To further investigate this mechanism, we performed MD simulations of the monomer of each dimer. Our computational approaches and results provide a new basis for half-of-the-sites activity.

Methods

System Preparation of Initial Structures of LysU and TyrRS Dimers

Each system of two dimers in the apo form was prepared to perform all-atom MD simulations. The initial structure of the LysU dimer in Escherichia coli was modeled using AlphaFold2 (AF2)26,27 (UniProt ID: P0A8N5)28 because of missing residues in the available PDB structures. Structural prediction using AF2 generated five models. These models were relaxed using the Amber force field29 and ranked based on the ipTM score.30 The top-ranked structure was used as the initial structure for all-atom MD simulations. The initial structure of the TyrRS dimer was modeled using the X-ray crystal structure in E. coli deposited in the PDB (PDB ID: 1X8X).31 Although this crystal structure missed the carboxyl-terminal domains (residue number 323–424), we adopted this structure because these regions were reported not to affect activity by previous studies.24 The ligand (tyrosine) in the X-ray crystal structure was removed using PyMOL (PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5 Schrödinger, LLC.). Both LysU and TyrRS dimeric structures were modeled with their symmetric structures, i.e., the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of all atoms between their subunits was 0.19 and 0.0074 Å for the LysU and TyrRS dimers, respectively. Then, the initial structures of both dimers were solvated using the TIP3P water model32 with sodium ions for charge neutralization in cubic boxes, that is, 130 × 130 × 130 Å3 and 150 × 150 × 150 Å3 for the LysU and TyrRS dimers, respectively. Finally, the total number of atoms in the LysU and TyrRS dimers was 206188 and 323129, respectively.

Preparation of Initial Structures of LysU and TyrRS Monomers

Both LysU and TyrRS monomers in the apo form were also prepared to perform all-atom MD simulations. Each initial monomeric structure was modeled by using one of the two subunits in each initial dimeric structure. The structures of both monomers were then solvated using the TIP3P water model32 with sodium ions for charge neutralization in cubic boxes, that is, 130 × 130 × 130 Å3 and 100 × 100 × 100 Å3 for the LysU and TyrRS monomers, respectively. The total numbers of atoms in the LysU and TyrRS monomers were 208301 and 93385, respectively.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations of Each System

For each system, MD simulations were performed using the GPU version of the GROMACS 2020.6 package.33,34 The AMBER 14SBonlysc force field35 was used as the force field parameter. To increase the MD time step to 2.0 fs, the chemical bonds of each system and water molecules were treated using the LINCS36 and SETTLE37 algorithms, respectively. A modified Berendsen thermostat38 and a Parrinello–Rahman barostat39,40 were adopted to control the temperature and pressure of each system, respectively. The electrostatic interactions were evaluated with the particle mesh Ewald method41 with a 10 Å real-space cutoff. The cutoff for van der Waals interactions was 10 Å.

The following relaxation processes were considered for each system: First, the energy of each system was minimized in 10000 steps. Second, a 10 ps MD simulation under NVT (T = 300 K) was started from each minimized system. Third, a 10 ps MD simulation under NPT (T = 300 K and P = 1 bar) was started from the final snapshot of the previous process. The final snapshot of each MD simulation under NPT was used to initiate a 1000 ns MD simulation under NPT. The snapshots from the first 400 ns were discarded for equilibration, and those from the remaining 600 ns were used for analysis. To obtain statistically reliable trajectories, multiple MD trials were independently performed by changing their initial velocities at random (six and 12 MD trials for each dimer and monomer, respectively). In each MD simulation, snapshots were recorded every 50 ps, resulting in 12000 snapshots per MD trial.

Calculating Structural Fluctuation of the Binding Pockets

We constructed the subunit-wise distribution of Rg for both dimers using 12 trajectories (two subunits × six MD trials) to quantitatively evaluate the magnitude of their structural fluctuation. To obtain stationary distributions, Markov state models (MSMs) were built from all trajectories of each system using the DeepTime program.45 Here, six MD trials × 600 ns were considered to build the MSM for each dimer. The detailed procedure for building the MSMs is provided in the Supporting Information. For each dimer, the magnitude of structural fluctuation was evaluated by using the coefficient of variation (CV) defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean.

Estimation of Degree of Cooperative Motion

The degree of asymmetric cooperative motion in the TyrRS dimer was estimated by generating pseudo distributions per degree and searching for the most suitable degree to reproduce the computational distribution calculated from the actual MD simulations.

The degree of the asymmetric cooperative motion was defined as ξ ranging from 0 to 1. Here, ξ = 0 means that the subunits always show uncooperative motion: they take independent states. In contrast, ξ = 1 means the subunits always show asymmetric cooperative motion: they are forced to always take in different states mutually. The degree of ξ was set in increments of 0.05.

The pseudo distributions at each ξ were generated using a Monte Carlo-based approach. First, in preparation for this approach, the subunit-wise population along Rg of the binding pocket obtained from the actual MD simulation was divided by states using the SciPy46 and pomegranate program.47 Then, a Monte Carlo step (MCS) was repeated for each ξ. This MCS was based on the twice sampling of Rg values from the subunit-wise population according to its probability distribution. The first sampling was performed, and the state to which the sample data belong was recorded. Subsequently, a second sampling was performed. Herein, asymmetric cooperative motion was considered based on the ξ value. Specifically, with a probability of ξ, this sampling was performed from different states from that recorded in the first sampling, and with a probability of 1 – ξ, this sampling was performed independently from the first sampling. After twice sampling, the difference of the two Rg values in the first and second sampling was calculated as ΔRg.

We repeated this MCS as many times as the snapshot of the actual MD simulations and generated a ΔRg distribution (pseudo distribution) for each ξ. Measuring Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation (RMSF)

To investigate the structural fluctuation at the residue level in each system, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) for the Cα atoms of each subunit was measured based on each average structure calculated from overall trajectories of the dimer subunits (two subunits × six MD trials) and the monomer subunits (one subunit × 12 MD trials) of each system with MDAnalysis.42,43

Result and Discussion

Difference in Structural Symmetry between LysU and TyrRS Dimers

For the first analysis, to investigate the structural symmetry of both dimers, Rg of the binding pocket of each subunit (Figure 1a,b) was measured for each dimer because the size of the binding pockets should affect their binding affinities.50 Indeed, previous studies on half-of-the-sites activity have focused on the binding pockets of target proteins.11,12,19

The Rg distributions exhibited different patterns between both dimers, as shown in the representative trials in Figure 2a,b. Specifically, the distributions in the LysU dimer sufficiently overlapped among the subunits, indicating symmetric structures. In contrast, those in the TyrRS dimer were split into subunits, indicating asymmetric structures. These structural features were well common to almost all trials (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information).

Next, the difference in the distribution patterns between the dimers was quantitatively evaluated by using their Dasy. For instance, the values of Dasy of the representative trials in the LysU and TyrRS dimers were 0.29 and 0.56, respectively, indicating that the subunit distributions in the TyrRS dimer were about twice as asymmetric as those in the LysU dimer (Figure 2a,b). Besides, the averages of Dasy over the six MD trials were 0.26 ± 0.18 and 0.62 ± 0.24, for the LysU and TyrRS dimer, respectively (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). These values quantitatively supported the difference in structural symmetry between the dimers. The highly populated subunit structures of the binding pockets at the peaks of the representative distributions in the LysU dimer were similar to each other (RMSD of Cα atoms of the binding pockets = 0.97 Å), whereas those in the TyrRS dimer were different from each other (RMSD of Cα atoms of the binding pockets = 2.98 Å) (Figure 2c,d).

In summary, the binding pockets in the LysU dimer tend to possess symmetric structures, whereas those in the TyrRS dimer tend to possess asymmetric structures, as suggested in previous studies.24

Difference in Configurational Variety between LysU and TyrRS Dimers

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the difference in structural symmetry between the two dimers, their configurational variety of Rg in both binding pockets was compared by their coefficient of variation (CV). For both dimers, their CV values were calculated using subunit-wise Rg distributions obtained from the 12 trajectories (two subunits × six MD trials).

As shown in Figure 3, the Rg distribution of the TyrRS dimer was broader than that of the LysU dimer, and the CV value of the TyrRS dimer was about three times higher than that of the LysU dimer. This result means that the configurational variety of the binding pockets in the LysU dimer was low, whereas that in the TyrRS dimer was high. This difference may be related to the difference in structural symmetry between these two dimers.

How Much Cooperative Motion Contributes to Structural Asymmetry

As previous studies have reported, positive and negative cooperative motions can induce symmetric and asymmetric structures, respectively. Specifically, the former (latter) motion means that the subunits in homo-oligomers transition toward the same (different) states.51 Hence, we investigated the cooperative motion of each dimer to reveal the origin of the difference in the structural symmetry between these two dimers.

First, we focused on the LysU dimer, and its cooperative motion seemed to not contribute to symmetric structures in this dimer. This is because that the Rg distribution exhibited a sharp peak, indicating only one highly populated structural state (Figure 3a). In contrast, cooperative motion seems to contribute to the asymmetric structures in the TyrRS dimer because the Rg distribution exhibited broad coverage, suggesting multiple structural states (Figure 3b). For these reasons, we estimated the contribution of cooperative motion, especially asymmetric cooperative motion, which induces asymmetric structures in the TyrRS dimer. First, based on subunit-wise Rg distributions, we identified three structural states in the TyrRS dimer and divided the distribution by state (Figure 4a). Then, by applying the Monte Carlo-based approach, we generated multiple pseudo ΔRg distributions with various degrees of asymmetric cooperative motion (ξ) and searched for the most suitable ξ to reproduce the actual ΔRg distribution obtained from the MD simulations using KL divergence.48,49 As the most suitable ξ, Figure 4b,c showed that a 15% contribution rate of asymmetric cooperative motion was needed to reproduce the actual ΔRg distribution in the TyrRS dimer.

This result indicates that the asymmetric structures in the TyrRS dimer are mostly induced via uncooperative motion (contribution rate of 85%) rather than asymmetric cooperative motion (contribution rate of 15%). Namely, the Rg distributions in both the LysU and TyrRS dimers are mainly derived from their uncooperative motion.

Role of Configurational Variety to Structural Symmetry

In summary, the LysU dimer, which exhibits symmetric structures, has a low configurational variety of binding pockets through uncooperative motions. On the other hand, the TyrRS dimer, which exhibits asymmetric structures, has a high configurational variety through uncooperative motions. Given that both dimers exhibit little cooperative motion, the difference in structural symmetry between the LysU and TyrRS was not derived from the difference in their cooperative motion. Herein, the higher configurational variety in each subunit can increase the number of asymmetric structures because of the structural combination of the two subunits. Therefore, we conclude that the difference in configurational variety is a primary factor that leads to the difference in the structural symmetry between these two dimers.

Origin of Difference in Configurational Variety

To identify the origin of the difference in configurational variety between these two dimers, as shown in Figure 3, we investigated the subunit-intrinsic structural fluctuation of the LysU and TyrRS. Here, multiple (12) 1000 ns MD simulations of each of the LysU and TyrRS monomers were independently performed. As a quantitative measure, the RMSF of the residues defining Rg of the binding pocket of each subunit was compared between the monomer and dimer of each of the LysU and TyrRS.

In the LysU, the RMSF of the monomer was much higher than that of the dimer (Figure 5a), indicating that dimerization suppressed the fluctuation of the binding pockets in the LysU. In contrast, the RMSF of the TyrRS showed no differences between the monomer and the dimer (Figure 5b), indicating that the fluctuation of the binding pockets in the TyrRS was hardly affected by dimerization and maintained the high subunit-intrinsic fluctuation. Indeed, the average reduction in the RMSF of the binding pocket by dimerization was greater in the LysU (2.2 Å ± 0.85) than in the TyrRS (0.49 Å ± 0.57). These results suggest that the difference in the configurational variety of their binding pockets between these two dimers becomes more pronounced after dimerization, rather than in each monomeric state. Indeed, the comparison of the distributions of Rg in the binding pockets via CV also showed that the structural fluctuation in the LysU decreased by half from the monomer to dimer, whereas that in the TyrRS remained persistently high in both the monomer and dimer (compare Figure 3 with Figure S2 in the Supporting Information).

Subsequently, we investigated why the structural fluctuation of the LysU was altered after dimerization, but the TyrRS did not. We focused on their interfaces and calculated the proportion of the interface area for each dimer by using the following measure:The solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of the interface areas is defined as SASAinterface = SASAA + SASAB – SASAdimer, where SASAA and SASAB represent SASA of subunit A alone and subunit B alone, respectively, and SASAdimer represents SASA of the whole dimer. SASAmonomer represents the average SASA of the two subunits of each dimer. Here, the average of SASApopulation over all snapshots in the LysU dimer was about twice as high as that in the TyrRS dimer (36% ± 0.011 and 20% ± 0.013 in the LysU and TyrRS dimers, respectively). Additionally, the RMSF of the LysU dimer decreased overall, whereas that of the TyrRS dimer was almost unchanged (Figure S3 in the Supporting Information). These results suggest that the smaller interface area in the TyrRS dimer compared with the LysU dimer provides less structural constraint by the opposite subunit, resulting in the difference in structural fluctuation between these dimers.

In summary, we have concluded that the higher structural fluctuation in the TyrRS dimer derived from the smaller interface area causes the higher configurational variety of the binding pockets compared with the LysU dimer.

Mechanism of Half-of-the-Sites Activity

In this section, we summarize the structural mechanism leading to half-of-the-sites activity in the TyrRS dimer, revealed by comparing its structural and dynamical properties to those of the LysU dimer exhibiting all-of-the-sites activity. Furthermore, the application of this mechanism to other homo-oligomeric enzymes is discussed.

In the TyrRS dimer, the subunit-intrinsic high structural fluctuation was maintained after dimerization due to the small interface area. This leads to asymmetric structures via uncooperative motion, possibly resulting in half-of-the-sites activity. In other words, the high structural fluctuation seems to be the most important factor in inducing structural asymmetry of the binding pockets in the TyrRS dimer.

As structural fluctuation is not a characteristic confined to specific enzymes but is a common characteristic of any enzyme, the high fluctuation might be a universal factor in inducing half-of-the-sites activity. Indeed, SARS-CoV-2 chymotrypsin-like protease (3CLpro), which suggests half-of-the-sites activity, has flexible loop regions in its binding pockets and structural asymmetry.52 While our study revealed the importance of structural fluctuation, it also found that the structural asymmetry of the binding pockets in the TyrRS dimer was partly (15%) derived from cooperative motion. Considering our results, in conjunction with many previous studies on cooperativity,13−16 half-of-the-sites activity in homo-oligomeric enzymes seems to be induced by the balance of cooperative motion and structural fluctuation.

Conclusions

In the present study, by computationally comparing the structural and dynamical properties of enzymes exhibiting all-of-the-sites or half-of-the-sites activity, we attempted to elucidate the mechanism underlying half-of-the-sites activity. For this purpose, we performed all-atom MD simulations of the apo forms of the LysU and TyrRS dimers exhibiting all-of-the-sites activity and half-of-the-sites activity upon first substrate binding, respectively. Our comparative analysis between the LysU and TyrRS dimers revealed the mechanism of a difference in their structural symmetry. Specifically, compared with the LysU dimer, the smaller interface area in the TyrRS dimer provides less structural constraint on each subunit, leading to higher structural fluctuation. Furthermore, this higher structural fluctuation ultimately induces greater structural asymmetry through the uncooperative motion of each subunit.

In conclusion, our comparison between the enzymes exhibiting all-of-the-sites or half-of-the-sites activity confirmed that structural fluctuation is one of the important factors inducing structural asymmetry in addition to well-established factor cooperativity. In the future, our computational approaches and results will shed light on the regulation of the activity of other oligomeric enzymes.
