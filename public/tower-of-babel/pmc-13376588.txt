The importance of seed characteristics in distinguishing the rare and common plant species in Astragalus (Fabaceae)
Authors: Iraj Rahimi, Pejman Tahmasebi, Esmaeil Asadi, Ali Abbasi, Alireza Monfared
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the key important traits responsible for the rarity of plant species is crucial for effective conservation management. This study investigated the functional traits underlying rarity in several Astragalus species by comparing seed morphology, germination, dispersal, and pre-dispersal predation across 12 congeneric rare and common shrub and herbaceous species in Iranian steppe habitats, while controlling for phylogenetic relatedness using Phylogenetic Generalized Least Squares. Our analyses revealed that seed mass showed no consistent relationship with rarity after accounting for phylogenetic constraints, with both rare and common species exhibiting variable mass patterns. Our results also showed that rare species were typically constrained by a combination of low seed output, high proportional seed predation (~ 65%), and limited dispersal capacity (exclusive reliance on abiotic vectors) after controlling for phylogenetic relatedness. These limitations are compounded by poor seedling competitiveness (low Vigor Index II and biomass), creating demographic bottlenecks. In contrast, common species, despite experiencing even higher proportional seed damage (75%), compensate through high fecundity, biotic dispersal, and rapid germination (16.7% faster than rares), ensuring population resilience. In general, rarity emerged from synergistic deficiencies—low seed numbers, high predation impact, and limited dispersal—rather than single-trait limitations whereas abundance reflects compensatory trait combinations. These findings contradict the assumption of a universal rarity indicator and highlight the need for conservation strategies to integrate species-specific trait interactions across life stages. Mitigating demographic bottlenecks should be a priority, particularly for species lacking compensatory adaptations such as high fecundity or biotic dispersal.

Introduction

Understanding the underlying mechanisms behind the pattern of rarity is of significant practical importance for the development of effective conservation and management strategies1. Because among a set of ecologically similar conditions, rare species are likely to have a greater extinction risk than common species2, 3.

In the context of population and community ecology, scientists tend to define a list of inherent biological and ecological attributes associated with rarity4, 5. These life history traits include low growth rates and reproductive efforts6, low colonization abilities and demographic performance, dispersal limitation, and competitive vigor5, 7, 8. There are also several studies8, 9, 10, 11 that compared simple traits between rare and common species such as seed predation, short duration of flowering, seed and flower size and number, and pollination constraints. Although these studies have attempted to reveal rare and common species differences, our understanding of the processes is still limited and no consensus exists yet on the causes of rarity in plant species.

Seed morphological traits, in particular seed size and number, are expected to explain rarity through their functional impacts on germination, dispersal, and persistence processes8, 11. For example, if a plant species has the ability to produce a greater number of seeds, it is more likely to have a higher chance of successfully colonizing and establishing itself in new areas3, 12. Only a few researchers13, 14 have studied differences in seed size and number and dispersal ability between rare and congeneric common species, and the results were always inconsistent. It is generally accepted that larger seeds have a greater probability of winning in competition against smaller seeds because of their competitive superiority14. Several studies reported a positive correlation between seed size and seedling success in competitive environments and showed that larger seeds have higher germination rates (GR) than smaller seeds5, 14, 15. In contrast, small seeds are superior colonists due to their greater number and hence competitively superior with respect to dispersal16. In this context, dispersal is viewed as a fundamental life-history attribute influencing demography and colonization, and it has been indicated that species with low dispersal ability have significantly smaller geographic distributions than species with high dispersal ability17, 18. Despite this, our understanding of the role of seed dispersal in the causes of rarity remains limited.

Seed dormancy break and the timing of germination are crucial components of a plant’s life history strategy19, regulating the distribution and abundance of a plant species. A few studies showed that common species generally germinated in higher percentages and consequently were distributed over wider ranges of conditions than rare species5, 13. This was observed by Mattana et al. (2010) for an endemic Centranthus amazon compared to its widespread congener Centranthus ruber20. Furthermore, Zhang et al. (2020) showed that the faster germination of Triadica sebifera potentially facilitates the invasion of the introduced population compared to those from the native, endemic congeneric population5. However, other studies reported that rare species were apparently well adapted to germinate in a wider range of conditions, and this was clearly not a cause for their rarity. For example, Paulů et al. (2017) showed that rare species had higher proportions of germinated seeds than those of their common congeners14. There are also other studies that found no differences in germination; for example, Hewitt et al. (2015) showed a substantially similar range of germination for one rare and three common plant species of Melaleuca13. These contrasting results show that more comparative analyses of seed germination experiments of congeneric rare and common plant species are needed to understand whether germination requirements are associated with rarity.

Seed predation also has the potential to drive a rare plant to extinction. For example, Combs et al. (2011) and Combs et al. (2013) found sporadic population-level decline in narrow endemic species Astragalus sinuatus due to pre-dispersal predation, compared to its sympatric and widespread congeners A. leibergii and A. pursuit9, 21. However, equal predation in rare-common pairs has also been reported22, 23. These contrasting studies limit our ability to predict pre-dispersal predation effects on plant rarity.

As the largest genus of plants, Astragalus species are represented by approximately 3000 species worldwide and around 680 species in Iran24, most of which are rare, endemic, and narrowly distributed and categorized as IUCN threatened species25. Comparative studies on rare and common congeneric species of this genus where confounding factors such as differences in phylogeny and ecology are controlled are much less common, especially controlling for site-specific factors7. Moreover, previous research encountered several shortcomings, either only comparing a limited number of species (one rare against one common species) or only considering a limited number of plant traits. These resulted in limited knowledge and contrasting information on the causes of rarity. To fill these gaps, by controlling for site-specific and phylogenetic factors, several important life-history traits including seed morphological traits, germination, dispersal, and pre-dispersal predation of 12 congeneric rare and common species of the Astragalus genus were compared. Although some Astragalus species can exhibit clonal growth26, all species studied here are strictly aclonal, ensuring that seed reproduction is the primary driver of population dynamics and that our trait comparisons directly reflect sexual reproductive success. Accordingly, the main goal was to define either the key important trait or a set of trait combinations that explain rarity and to determine whether two groups of rare and common species could be differentiated by these traits. By analyzing trait-demography relationships across life stages, we sought to determine whether rarity arises from species-specific trait syndromes or broader ecological patterns, thereby informing targeted conservation strategies.

Materials and methods

Study area and sampling method

The study area is located in the steppe rangelands of (32° 30′N, 50°26′E, area of 800 ha), a part of the Shayda National Protected Area (Registered by the Department of Environment, IRI, in 2005), with an annual precipitation of 600 mm, being mostly in winter and early spring with annual average temperature is 12 °C. The elevation varies between 2200 and 3000 m a.s.l. The natural vegetation is characterized by the mosaics of grass species such as Bromus tomentelus, and Agropyron repens, and shrubs such as Astragalus spp. Six rare species of Astragalus holopsilus Bunge., A. heterophyllus Podlech, A. caraganae Fisch, A. camphylanthus Boiss, A. cephalanthus DC, A. cemerinus Beck. and six common species of A. effusus Bunge., A. curvirostris Boiss, A. angustiflorus K.Koch, A. verus Olivier, A. susianus Boiss. and A. rhodosemius Boiss. (Table 1) occurred in this area were selected for the study. In this study, we defined rarity as species exhibiting restricted population sizes and limited geographic distributions27. Based on IUCN criteria for geographic range and population thresholds28, we classified species into rare and common categories according to their local population density (individuals/ha). Rare species were characterized by low population densities (Table 1), restricted geographic ranges (< 100 km²), and occurrence in less than 1% of surveyed sites10, consistent with definitions of narrow endemism. These classifications were validated through (1) taxonomic verification using Flora Iranica (1986–2005), (2) conservation status assessments from the Red Data Book of Iran29, and (3) regional distribution patterns24. In contrast, common species exhibited population densities exceeding 100 individuals/ha and widespread distribution across central Iranian steppe ecosystems24.

In May 2018, we selected three 200-hectare homogeneous sites (maximum distance between sites: 2000 m) independent of environmental variables such as topography and land-use history, where all 12 Astragalus species occurred. Subsequently, 20 healthy and vigorous individuals of each species were carefully selected from well-lit environments offering optimal conditions, completely devoid of shading. These individuals were tagged for further surveys from July to September in 2018 and 2019. The local abundance (using line transect sampling), and numbers of inflorescences and flowers were estimated for each species during peak growing seasons (Table 1). Flowers and pods were collected at maturity in May and July of 2018 and 2019 and the samples were transferred to the laboratory for further analysis. For the three shrub species (A. verus, A. susianus, and A. rhodosemius), approximately 10% of pods (rough estimate of total pods) were collected in August and September of 2018 and 2019.

Flowers, seed and pod trait measurements

In the laboratory, we measured key morphological traits of seeds and pods associated with persistence and dispersal (Table 2). For each individual, 20 intact, undamaged seeds/pods were randomly selected. In addition, several key flower traits including leaflet number, shape and width, calyx length and teeth, corolla length, keel petal length, and wing petal length were measured to ensure that our results reflect trait-specific differences rather than phylogenetic relatedness (Table 2). These traits were selected based on their established utility for phylogenetic correction in Astragalus genus studies30, 31, 32, 33. Seed/pod mass was measured to ± 0.1 mg (reported as means with CV), while SmartGrain software34 and calipers (± 0.01 mm) quantified area, length, width, and shape (width-to-length ratio35). We also counted total seed/pod production and predation-damaged specimens (identified by larval frass or exit holes9) of each individual Asragalus plant. Dispersal syndromes (Table 1) were assessed by examining: (1) wind-dispersal adaptations (absent except balloon-like pods in A. cemerinus, A. camphylanthus, and A. cephalanthus11), (2) epizoochorous features, and (3) documented syndromes (endozoochory/myrmecochory in A. angustiflorus, A. verus, A. susianus, and A. rhodosemius; ballistic dispersal in A. angustiflorus36). Soil seed bank persistence followed by37, with only A. camphylanthus showing long-term persistence (Table 1).

Germination experiment

A germination experiment was conducted to evaluate seed germination patterns across all studied Astragalus species. Using seeds collected from the same populations in 2019, we first confirmed ≥ 98% viability through tetrazolium chloride testing of 20 randomly selected seeds per species. The experiments were performed separately for each species under controlled conditions in Germinator chambers (Jal Teb Lab Equipment, model JG-400 L). Five replicates × 20 seeds per species were placed in 9 cm diameter Petri dishes on double-layered moistened filter paper and subjected to 12 h/12 h light/dark cycles at alternating 20/10°C temperatures, with light periods providing 30 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PAR from fluorescent lamps. Over the 30-day monitoring period, we tracked germination events and subsequently excised radicles and shoots from cotyledons for drying at 75 ± 2 °C for 24 h.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the role of seed, pod, and germination traits (Tables 2 and 3) in distinguishing rare and common Astragalus species, we employed Phylogenetic Generalized Least Squares (PGLS) models, treating each trait from Tables 2, 3 and 4 as response variables. Previous phylogenetic studies on these Astragalus species38, 39, 40, 41 have established preliminary evolutionary relationships, particularly demonstrating close associations between shrub species. To ensure that observed trait differences reflect ecological adaptations rather than phylogenetic constraints, we conducted a preliminary phylogenetic analyses to verify that observed functional differences between rare and common species represent genuine ecological adaptations rather than phylogenetic artifacts. First, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using different morphological pod and flower traits (Table 2) via Gower distance matrix and UPGMA clustering with 1,000 bootstraps (R packages: ape, cluster). The tree was then integrated with seed germination, morphology and Pre-Dispersal Predation (Tables 2 and 3) in PGLS analyses testing rarity (Rare/Common), growth form (Shrub/Forb) and year effects. Each of these traits was analyzed separately to isolate their ecological contributions while controlling for phylogenetic inertia using Pagel’s λ. Traits with strong phylogenetic signals (Pagel’s λ > 0.7, p < 0.05) were excluded from ecological interpretations, while those showing both significant rarity effects and moderate phylogenetic signal (0.2 < Pagel’s λ < 0.7) and without signal ( (0.2 < Pagel’s λ ) were interpreted as ecologically conserved adaptations. Data transformations (log₁₀ for mass, arcsine square root for proportions) ensured normality, and The PGLS analysis was performed using the nlme package in R.

Results

Seed morphological traits

The analysis of seed mass across 12 Astragalus species (6 rare and 6 common) using Phylogenetic Generalized Least Squares (PGLS) showed that seed mass did not differ significantly between rare and common species when accounting for phylogenetic relatedness (p = 0.08, Pagel’s λ = 0.78), indicating that rarity status alone does not predict seed mass variation (Fig. 1) However, phylogenetically corrected pairwise comparisons (p < 0.05) identified significant interspecific differences in seed mass, showing that while some common shrubs (e.g., A. effusus) tended to have higher seed mass than rare herbs (e.g., A. heterophyllus), this pattern was not consistent across all species (Fig. 1), with certain rare species (e.g., A. camphylanthus) overlapping in mass with common congeners. Importantly, seed mass was significantly influenced by life form (shrub vs. herb, p = 0.003) and its interaction with rarity (p < 0.001), suggesting that ecological strategies related to growth form may play a more substantial role than rarity in determining seed mass variation.

For seed numbers, PGLS showed highly significant effects of rarity (F = 49, p = 0.000), life form (F = 85, p = 0.000), year (F = 120, p = 0.000), and their interaction (F = 20, p = 0.000). The production of more seeds by common species than their rare congeners remained evident and was one of the few traits where nearly all common species consistently outperformed their rare counterparts, producing significantly more seeds across both herbaceous and shrub life forms. This pattern remained evident even after accounting for phylogenetic constraints (Pagel’s λ = 0.15, p = 0.20). Seed mass variability (CV) showed significant phylogenetic signal (Pagel’s λ = 0.68, p = 0.03), with common shrub species (e.g., A. effusus) exhibiting higher variability than rare herbs (e.g., A. heterophyllus) (Table 4). Seed shape (Pagel’s λ = 0.61, p = 0.08) and length (Pagel’s λ = 0.58, p = 0.06) were less influenced by phylogeny, though rare herbs like A. holopsilus had more elongated seeds compared to common shrubs(Table 4). These two traits reflected interspecific variation independent of rarity. Furthermore, no significant differences were detected between rare and common species in other seed morphological traits, including seed area size, perimeter, width, and circularity.

Pre-dispersal seed predation

The PGLS analysis, accounting for phylogenetic influences (Pagel’s λ = 0.65, p = 0.02), showed that larval seed damage (number of dameged seeds) varied significantly with rarity (F = 579, p < 0.001; phylogenetically corrected), life form (F = 366, p < 0.001), year (F = 46, p < 0.01), and their interactions (Fig. 2). This analysis showed significantly higher numbers of larval seed predation in common species compared to rare congeners. Common shrubs like A. verus sustained the heaviest damage, exceeding predation rates in rare species (e.g., A. camphylanthus) by an order of magnitude. However, common species exhibited significantly lower proportions of intact seeds (42.3% ± 5.2) compared to rare congeners (58.7% ± 6.8; F = 136, p < 0.001), even after accounting for phylogenetic relationships (Pagel’s λ = 0.54, p = 0.03). This pattern was particularly pronounced in shrub species, where common shrubs (e.g., A. verus) showed < 40% intact seeds versus > 60% in rare shrubs (e.g., A. cephalanthus). Herbaceous species displayed less divergence (commons: 48.1% ± 4.3; rares: 55.2% ± 5.1), suggesting growth-form mediated responses. For herbaceous species, > 80% of common species’ seeds were predated versus ~ 60% in rare species. Among shrubs, common species showed higher damage proportions except in A. camphylanthus (rare), where predation exceeded congeners. Despite higher proportional seed damage in common species (Fig. 2), their greater absolute seed production (PGLS: F = 49, p < 0.001; Pagel’s λ = 0.65) ensures a larger residual seed pool available for recruitment. In contrast, rare species faced compounded limitations: lower seed output (e.g., A. cemerinus: 167 ± 19 seeds/plant vs. A. verus: 6,775 ± 566) exacerbates the impact of equivalent predation pressure, creating a Allee effect that perpetuates rarity. Pod numbers damaged was strongly phylogenetically constrained (Pagel’s λ = 0.82, p = 0.008) with life form × year interactions (p < 0.001), showing no residual rarity effects after phylogenetic correction.

Seed germination and establishment

Our analysis showed significant differences in germination and early seedling traits between rare and abundant species, with particularly striking contrasts among herbaceous plants (Table 5). Abundant herbaceous species demonstrated superior performance in initial germination characteristics, achieving 27.3% higher germination percentages (0.28 ± 0.03 vs. 0.22 ± 0.01; p < 0.05) and 16.7% faster germination rates (172.25 ± 28.93 vs. 147.58 ± 24.04) compared to their rare counterparts, with these differences showing minimal phylogenetic constraint (Pagel’s λ = 0.32–0.42). For shrubs, the pattern was similar but less pronounced, with abundant individuals showing 15.8% higher germination percentages (0.22 ± 0.03 vs. 0.19 ± 0.02) and 19.9% faster germination rates (155.09 ± 32.72 vs. 129.44 ± 23.79). The most remarkable reversal occurred in later-stage traits, where rare herbaceous species exhibited substantially greater vigor (Vigor Index II: 6.46 ± 0.88 vs. 2.46 ± 0.28; +162.6%) and biomass accumulation (dry seedling weight: 39.14 ± 5.72 mg vs. 10.18 ± 1.14 mg; +284.5%), despite strong phylogenetic signals for these traits (Pagel’s λ = 0.71–0.75). In contrast, shrub species showed an opposite pattern for dry weight, with abundant shrubs maintaining 55.8% greater biomass (18.00 ± 2.09 mg vs. 11.55 ± 0.74 mg). Germination uniformity displayed growth-form specific responses, being 21.4% higher in abundant herbs (107.94 ± 17.94 vs. 88.88 ± 11.51) but showing no significant difference in shrubs, with moderate phylogenetic influence (Pagel’s λ = 0.62). Temporal patterns revealed abundant herbs reached 50% germination (T50) 57.6% faster (133.13 ± 10.52 h vs. 209.81 ± 18.30 h), while shrubs showed no significant difference, with strong phylogenetic constraint (Pagel’s λ = 0.65).

Discussion

This study presents a multifaceted analysis of seed morphological traits, germination characteristics, and pre-dispersal predation patterns across 12 Astragalus species occupying similar ecological habitats. Contrary to initial expectations, our findings showed complex relationships between rarity and reproductive traits that challenge simplistic ecological generalizations, while providing insights into the mechanisms underlying species abundance patterns.

The most surprising finding concerns seed mass, which showed no consistent differentiation between rare and common congeners. Both rare (A. caraganae, A. cemerinus) and common (A. angustiflorus, A. rhodosemius) species produced heavier seeds, while other species across the rarity spectrum (A. holopsilus, A. effusus) produced lighter seeds. This aligns with previous studies6, 14 demonstrating that seed mass variations primarily occur at higher taxonomic levels42, making it a poor predictor of rarity among closely related species. In contrast, seed number emerged as a robust differentiator, with common species producing significantly more seeds, supporting findings by43, 44. This pattern was particularly pronounced in shrubs, where common species exhibited exceptional fecundity (A. rhodosemius), suggesting reproductive output may be more critical for shrub abundance than herbaceous species.

The predation results revealed that while common species experienced significantly higher proportional seed damage (75% vs. 65% in rares), their absolute reproductive output compensates for these losses. For instance, A. verus maintains ∼160 intact seeds despite 60% predation, whereas A. cemerinus produced only 20 seeds. This creates a demographic Allee effect where rare species face compounded limitations from both low fecundity and predation pressure. These findings partially support Martin and Meinke (2012) who reported 60–80% seed losses in rare Astragalus45, while contrasting with8, 20 who found rare species more vulnerable to predators. Our results align more closely with Rymer (2006), demonstrating greater absolute (though not relative) predation in common species46. The study revealed some differences between herbaceous and shrub species in coping mechanisms. Common herbaceous species (A. effusus, A. angustiflorus) compensate for predation likley through higher germination percentages and potential long-distance dispersal, while common shrubs (A. verus, A. rhodosemius) rely on massive seed production. Rare species, particularly A. holopsilus (herb) and A. cemerinus (shrub), lack such compensatory mechanisms, showing low competitive ability at seedling stages and minimal dispersal capacity. This explains their vulnerability despite similar proportional predation rates.

Our sudy also showed insights into how germination traits differentiate rare and abundant species, with particular emphasis on distinguishing ecological adaptations from phylogenetic constraints. The most ecologically informative traits for rarity classification are those with low to moderate phylogenetic signals (Pagel’s λ < 0.7), including germination percentage (λ = 0.32), germination rate (Pagel’s λ = 0.42), and germination uniformity (Pagel’s λ = 0.62). These traits show consistent, biologically meaningful patterns where abundant herbaceous species outperform rare ones by substantial margins (27.3% higher germination percentages, 16.7% faster germination rates, and 21.4% greater germination uniformity), demonstrating their utility as reliable indicators of rarity status. In contrast, traits with strong phylogenetic constraints (Pagel’s λ ≥ 0.7) such as Vigor Index II (Pagel’s λ = 0.75) and dry seedling weight (Pagel’s λ = 0.71), while showing dramatic differences (rare herbs exhibiting 163% higher Vigor Index II and 285% greater biomass), are less suitable for general rarity classification due to their evolutionary conservatism, though they may reveal lineage-specific adaptations. Shrubs present a more complex case, showing weaker rarity-related differences (15.8–19.9% in germination traits) and stronger phylogenetic influences overall, suggesting their rarity status may be determined by factors beyond regeneration characteristics. The superior germination performance of abundant species directly contributes to their ecological success through multiple mechanisms13, 14, 47, 48. Higher germination percentages (27.3% greater in abundant herbs) ensure more efficient use of seed banks and greater seedling vigor, increasing population sizes. Faster germination rates (16.7% quicker in abundant species) provide a competitive advantage by allowing earlier access to light, water, and nutrients, particularly in seasonal environments [13, Khanna et al., 2014; 40, 41]. Greater germination uniformity (21.4% more synchronized in abundant herbs) reduces variability in seedling emergence times, promoting cohort survival and minimizing predation risks. These traits may collectively enhance recruitment success, allowing abundant species to maintain larger populations, colonize available niches more effectively, and outcompete slower-germinating species49. The combination of higher germination success, rapid establishment, and synchronized emergence may create a positive feedback loop that reinforces their numerical dominance in ecosystems.

Several traits in our study, particularly those with strong phylogenetic signals (Pagel’s λ ≥ 0.7; e.g., Vigor Index II [Pagel’s λ = 0.75] and dry seedling weight [Pagel’s λ = 0.71]), proved unreliable for distinguishing rare and abundant species because their variation primarily reflected evolutionary history rather than ecological adaptations50. These phylogenetically constrained traits showed lineage-specific patterns (e.g., the exceptional Vigor Index II in rare herbs) that could misleadingly appear as ecological adaptations to rarity if phylogenetic non-independence is ignored. This emphasizes the critical need to account for phylogenetic signals in trait-based ecological studies51, as failing to do so may lead to spurious conclusions about rarity mechanisms. Our findings align with recent work demonstrating that high-λ traits often correlate poorly with contemporary ecological gradients52. For conservation applications, we may recommend prioritizing low-λ traits (e.g., germination percentage) that more reliably reflect ecological strategies independent of phylogenetic history.

Although not a primary focus of our investigation, the data collected from Table 1 reveal intriguing patterns in dispersal-related traits that warrant discussion. Contrary to theoretical expectations (Koffel et al., 2022), seed size showed no consistent correlation with rarity status. Both rare (e.g., A. caraganae, A. camphylanthus) and common (e.g., A. angustiflorus, A. rhodosemius) species exhibited larger seed morphologies, while other species across the rarity spectrum produced smaller seeds. This lack of clear differentiation challenges conventional assumptions that seed size directly influences soil seed bank incorporation or dispersal efficiency. However, examination of dispersal syndromes revealed more pronounced ecological differentiation. Rare species depended exclusively on abiotic dispersal mechanisms (wind, gravity), consistent with findings by Espeland and Emam (2011) regarding the limitations of passive dispersal strategies53. In contrast, common species demonstrated facultative or obligate biotic dispersal, primarily through endozoochory (A. verus, A. effusus) and myrmecochory. This pattern partially contradicts Ibanez et al. (2021), who reported no consistent relationship between dispersal syndrome and species abundance18. The prevalence of endozoochory among common congeners36 suggests that animal-mediated dispersal may enhance metapopulation dynamics and colonization capacity, potentially compensating for higher predation pressures through improved spatial escape. These emergent findings, while ancillary to our primary objectives, provide valuable insights into the complex interplay between dispersal ecology and rarity. The dissociation between seed morphology and dispersal efficiency, coupled with the clear dichotomy in dispersal vectors, implies that dispersal modality rather than seed size per se may represent a more critical determinant of abundance patterns in this system. This observation aligns with emerging frameworks that emphasize dispersal vector availability as a key driver of plant population dynamics (Thomson et al., 2018). Future studies should explicitly test whether the observed dispersal syndrome patterns represent a general phenomenon across other plant lineages with mixed reproductive strategies. Long-term monitoring of both seed rain and seed predation is required for understanding the ecological and evolutionary implications of species regeneration strategies in a plant communities.

In overall, our integrated analysis revealed that rarity in Astragalus species may emerge from complex interactions across multiple biological scales, where no single trait but rather synergistic combinations of life-history characteristics determine abundance patterns. While seed production differences appear initially diagnostic (common shrubs like A. verus producing 20-fold more seeds than rare A. cemerinus), their ecological significance only manifests when combined with predation data and establishment traits. The 65–75% seed predation affects species differentially - devastating for rare species with low baseline fecundity (e.g., only 7 intact seeds remaining in A. cemerinus) but tolerable for common species through compensatory mechanisms. These include either massive seed output (common shrubs) or superior dispersal/establishment (common herbs), is likley to create what we may term the “compensation threshold” of population viability.

Phylogenetic analyses revealed evolutionary constraints on certain traits (Pagel’s λ ≥ 0.7 for Vigor Index II, seedling biomass), while others (germination parameters, Pagel’s λ = 0.32–0.62) show ecological lability. This duality may explain why some species groups (particularly shrubs) exhibit conserved rarity syndromes across related taxa, while others (especially herbs) display more flexible responses to ecological pressures. Rare species are likley to rely on abiotic dispersal (wind/gravity), whereas common species employ biotic vectors (endozoochory/myrmechory), supporting the spatial escape hypothesis for abundant species. This pattern held despite some rare and common species sharing similar seed morphologies, indicating dispersal mode outweighs size in colonization capacity. The case studies of A. holopsilus (rare herb) and A. cemerinus (rare shrub) exemplify how different trait combinations create rarity. The herb shows a “triple jeopardy” of low seed output (53 ± 13), high predation (70%), and poor establishment (low VI/VII), while the shrub suffers primarily from reproductive limitation (20 ± 4 pods) without compensatory dispersal. Conversely, common species like A. verus overcome 60% predation through exceptional fecundity (404 ± 94 pods), and A. effusus compensates via long-distance dispersal. These findings necessitate a paradigm shift in rarity assessment from single-trait to syndrome-based evaluation and from ecological snapshots to phylogenetic-ecological integrations. Future research must expand to pollination ecology, intraspecific variation, and demographic modeling to fully unravel rarity determinants. Our results suggest that for Astragalus, rarity often represents the accumulation of subcritical limitations across multiple life stages rather than catastrophic single-factor constraints a nuance with profound implications for conservation prioritization and population recovery strategies.

Conclusion

In a comparative approach, many studies highlighted the differences between rare and common species by controlling the phylogenetical aspects of plant species and defined several traits responsible to the rarity. In this study, a key trait that distinguished the rare and abundant species was not observed. Although, both pre-dispersal seed loss to insect herbivores and lower seed production by rare species acted as important mechanisms limiting both individual fitness and population density for the rare species, there are still many equivocal evidences to be defined them as the key trait. It is not uncommon to find variability in trait responses between rare and common species, as well as within each group. The findings that some rare species possessed traits typically associated with common species, such as seed dormancy, high GP and GR and local competitive ability, highlights the complexity of species behavoiur and the need to consider multiple factors when studying the rarity of plant species. Even though there is no reason to believe in a unique mechanism that would explain rarity, recognizing common patterns might aid in identifying effective conservation strategies.

In overall, rare species are typically constrained by a combination of low seed output, high proportional seed predation (~ 65%), and limited dispersal capacity (exclusive reliance on abiotic vectors)45, 53. These limitations are compounded by poor seedling competitiveness (low Vigor Index II and biomass), creating demographic bottlenecks. Although common species suffer proportionally greater pre-dispersal seed predation (75% vs. 65% in rare species), their high absolute seed production and compensatory traits (biotic dispersal, rapid germination) buffer population impacts. Rare species lack such buffers, making them vulnerable despite lower predation proportions. Hence, rarity emerges from the interplay of multiple limitations, not from a single factor. Crucially, phylogenetic constraints (Pagel’s λ ≥ 0.7 for traits like seedling biomass) further entrench rarity in certain lineages, while labile traits (e.g., germination rate, Pagel’s λ = 0.42) allow ecological adaptation in others50, 52. Thus, rarity may emerge from synergistic trait deficiencies, whereas abundance reflects compensatory trait combinations. As a result, rarity is likely to determine by a distinctive combination and interplay of different traits that affect population size, establishment, and persistence within a changing environment. Even when several key traits are presumed to be responsible for the primary factors contributing to rarity, the answer is likely to emerge only by incorporating them into a unique combination specific to each species. Therefore, searching for a unique trait to explain the rarity should be discarded and the researches should fundamentally seek to find a unique combination of traits for each specific species. This may suggest that the conservation of rare species requires use of a unique scenario for each habitat. This complicates the comprehensive management tools at ecosystem level where multiple aspects of ecosystems need to take into account for interdisciplinary conservation efforts and restoration activities.
