Ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs: Addressing the challenging journey toward health and safety
Authors: Jose M Martín‐Miguélez, Irene Martín, Alberto González‐Mohíno, Lary Souza Olegario, Belén Peromingo, Josué Delgado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, plant‐based analogs are presented as healthier alternatives to the products they are intended to replace. However, the processing to which ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs are subjected to acquire the characteristics of animal‐derived products might result in the opposite effect, producing unhealthy ultra‐processed foods. In the present review, a list of strategies widely known and already employed in animal‐derived products is suggested to achieve healthier, safer, and tastier ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs: fermentation, employment of probiotics and postbiotics, NaCl replacement or substitution, addition of antioxidants, and fatty profile enhancement. In general, these strategies are not yet applied to the plant‐based products available on the market; thus, this research paper might induce new investigation pathways for researchers and producers to develop actually healthier alternatives.Keywords: lipid profile improvement, NaCl, probiotic, protein oxidation, starter cultures

INTRODUCTION

Food has drastically changed in the last decades in the persecution of convenient, palatable, and profitable products with longer shelf life, traditionally employing an elevated content of sugar, fat, and salt (Kearney, 2010; Marino et al., 2021). The processing trend has led to ultra‐processed foods (UPFs), which have been described as hyperpalatable industrial formulations with five or more ingredients including additives or substances directly extracted from foods, and their consumption is associated with overweight, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer (Askari et al., 2020; Monteiro et al., 2016). In the last decades, the consumption of UPFs has increased in every occidental sociodemographic group and parallels the percentage of people who have developed some of the previously described diseases (Juul et al., 2022; Rauber et al., 2020). As a result, some countries have set special taxes on UPF such as soft drinks or snacks, and they have developed impactful warning labels or school food policies. The hazard not only lies in sugar‐sweetened foods but also in the ingestion of high sodium levels, unhealthy fats or protein, and insufficient fiber and micronutrients (Popkin et al., 2021).

In recent years, there has been a noticeable shift in food consumption trends‐particularly in high‐income countries and among individuals with higher annual family incomes, leaning these consumers toward purchasing organic foods and reducing their intake of meat products and foods containing additives (Feil et al., 2020; Neville et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2018). Health concerns are one of the reasons for the actual consumption reduction of red meat and processed meat that is taking place in high‐income countries (Parlasca & Qaim, 2022). Several pathological conditions such as obesity, type II diabetes mellitus, colorectal cancer, and cardiovascular diseases have been linked to their consumption (Rohrmann & Linseisen, 2016; Wolk, 2017). Carnitine as a proatherogenic precursor, the use of additives, such as NaCl, and the oxidation of lipids are suggested to be the unhealthy components present in red meat and meat products (Delgado et al., 2021).

However, ultra‐processed meat analogs share the following features: preservatives as ingredients, high content of NaCl, and oxidation of proteins and lipids (Bohrer, 2019). The use of preservatives in plant‐based analogs is mandatory to avoid the development of spoilage and pathogenic bacteria, which can grow in these products as well as in the meat products they try to imitate (Akramzadeh et al., 2018; Wild et al., 2014). The content of NaCl in ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs is usually higher than in meat‐based products (Bohrer, 2019). Ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs undergo processing conditions that generate higher protein oxidation than the raw unprocessed meat they imitate (Estrada et al., 2018). Lipid oxidation is not only produced from the unsaturated fatty acid profile of plant‐based products, but it is also designed to imitate meat‐like flavor (Chen et al., 2022). Thus, they are not healthier than meat products, and in the case of the ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs that imitate raw unprocessed meat, the comparison is even worse for the plant‐based alternative. Therefore, “plant based” does not mean healthy per se.

Thus, it is required to develop tools to increase the quality of plant‐based analogs in terms of their impact on health, improving sensory characteristics, and being safe for consumers. Moreover, it is also important to develop clean‐label solutions that avoid the use of additives that negatively affect the willingness to buy from consumers (Filho et al., 2005; Szűcs et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2018).

The present review aims to gather those drawbacks related to health and safety from plant‐based analogs, explore improvement strategies applied, and consider potential tools that have not yet been used in this kind of product.

STRATEGIES TO IMPLEMENT

Microorganisms employment

The use of microorganisms in the development of meat products is a widely employed effective strategy that increases batch homogenization and prevents the hazard posed by pathogenic microorganisms, as it is easy to apply and has a low environmental impact (Delgado et al., 2023). Moreover, the use of microorganisms is an effective tool to develop flavor in safe clean‐label products with optimal sensory characteristics in other meat‐derived products such as cheese (Mayo et al., 2021) and in a wide variety of fermented fruits or vegetables (Swain et al., 2014).

The development of microorganisms during the shelf life of ultra‐processed plant‐based meat analogs is unavoidable as it would be in meat products; therefore, it could be a future strategy to introduce probiotics and postbiotics into the daily diet (Filho et al., 2005; Yadav et al., 2015). Moreover, meat analog industries are taking advantage of fermentation to develop new meat‐like proteins, pigments, or fibrous structures (Kim et al., 2011; Nisov et al., 2024; Ou et al., 2023). Fermentation is a studied strategy that upgrades the rheology of texturized vegetable protein, improves digestibility, reduces allergenicity, and increases protein concentration (Elhalis et al., 2023; Maung et al., 2020). However, fermentation in plant‐based meat alternatives can become a strategy to replace chemical preservatives, increase shelf life, and guarantee food safety (Dai et al., 2022). Thus, products aligned with the clean‐label trend and the new consumer would be developed (Valtonen et al., 2023).

The meat analog industry is even taking advantage of fermentation to avoid some of the problems of UPFs, such as protein oxidation and lipidic degradation, due to the metabolic effect of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeasts and their production of antioxidants (Miri et al., 2019; Razavizadeh et al., 2022; Tao et al., 2022). However, there are still some investigations that need to be performed, as in other industries in which fermentation is being analyzed as a technology with the aim to reduce NaCl content, which in turn is reducing lipid oxidation due to the prooxidant nature of this salt (da Silva Amorim Gomes et al., 2021).

Although fermentation is a strategy that can only be applied to products where the processing allows for it, this technology can be executed in previous steps and introduced into one of the ingredients of ultra‐processed meat analogs. These products can take advantage of the bioactivity of some of the molecules produced by fermentation, such as the introduction of antioxidant compounds or natural preservatives (Leonard et al., 2021). Moreover, the fermentation of byproducts can be employed as a sustainable strategy to increase the value of the developed product (Martí‐Quijal et al., 2021). The research that is being performed in plant‐based analogs is showing an innovative path in which the sensory characteristics, health, safety, and sustainability are being studied. Though the main retail platforms are not providing plant‐based analogs with these considerations, it is worth performing a review to remark on the innovations that are taking place in plant‐based analogs or meat‐based products, which could guide industries or researchers to develop better UPFs.

Flavor development

Plant‐based meat analogs are currently a market niche product, as they are improving their sensory characteristics while reducing costs (Boukid, 2020). Consumers have confirmed that meat alternatives should be similar to meat in texture, flavor, and convenience (Michel et al., 2021). As will be discussed later in Section 2.3, the texture is at this time achieved through the physicochemical formation of meat‐like fibrils as thermo‐extrusion, high‐temperature conical shear cells, or 3D printing (Sha & Xiong, 2020). Extrusion is the most employed method to achieve meat‐like fibrils, and it has been proven to destroy antinutrients and increase the availability of amino acids (Nikbakht Nasrabadi et al., 2021). However, the flavor of the extruded matrix changes during the process due to the Maillard reaction, lipid degradation, and deamidation, though the extrusion parameters can be optimized to achieve the less amount of off‐flavor possible (Wang et al., 2022). Thus, strategies to minimize these drawbacks linked to plant‐based UPFs are required.

LAB and yeast fermentation is a viable strategy to decrease the green and earthy off‐flavors found in the vegetable protein isolates that are employed to develop extruded protein (García Arteaga et al., 2021; Youssef et al., 2020). This strategy is currently employed by the industry, though the fermentation substrate, strains employed, and fermentation conditions must be optimized to avoid the formation of other unpleasant flavors (Jeon et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). On the other hand, fermentation can also be employed to produce appreciated flavor and taste by the consumers (Flores & Piornos, 2021).

Molds and bacteria are responsible for the development of kokumi and umami taste in several traditional fermented Asian foods, such as tofu, miso, natto, or tempeh (Canoy et al., 2023; He et al., 2020; Kaczmarska et al., 2021; Rodríguez Valerón et al., 2023). This technology has already been applied in the development of new products adapted to European tastes and could be introduced into plant‐based meat products (Cantabrana et al., 2015; Guixer, 2019). LAB are not only employed in the fermentation of the raw protein that will be extruded but also in the final product, allowing a reduction in the legume flavor and producing an umami taste in the fermented samples (Flores & Piornos, 2021; Valtonen et al., 2023). Also, LAB fermentation can affect the composition of phenolic substances and the antioxidant capacity of plant‐based products, so it will reduce the formation of off‐flavors induced by lipid oxidation (Chen et al., 2023). Yeasts have been mentioned to be employed during fermentation to eliminate off‐flavor while producing alcohols and esters (Tao et al., 2022). However, they are also rich in nutrients and single‐cell proteins that can be employed for the industry (Xia, Shen, et al., 2023; Xia, Song, et al., 2023). Microbial fermentation to achieve flavor has been performed for centuries, but currently, it has been evaluated as a strategy to produce healthier and more realistic plant‐based analogs. The described advances can be applied to develop better ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs.

Probiotics

According to the definition by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, probiotics are “live microorganisms that, when dosed in an adequate amount, provide a health benefit to the host” (Hill et al., 2014; Salminen et al., 2021). Therefore, a probiotic microorganism must not be pathogenic or toxic, nor should it cause allergic reactions, mutagenesis, and/or carcinogenesis even in immunocompromised persons (Bogdanović et al., 2024; Collins et al., 1998).

Some of the most common probiotic genera are Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Propionibacterium, Peptostreptococcus, Pediococcus, Leuconostoc, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, and Saccharomyces (Bogdanović et al., 2024; Ranjha et al., 2021).

The probiotic strain growth could be limited by the conditions applied in manufacture (fermentation temperature, pH, salt, and additives), and the strain viability could be influenced by many factors including processing, microbiological, and matrix‐specific parameters. Therefore, ensuring a suitable survival rate of probiotics during production and the product shelf life is essential in every processing in which probiotics are employed (Sirini et al., 2021).

Probiotics have been widely used in animal‐origin products (Jang et al., 2024; Munekata et al., 2020, 2022), but there are almost no studies on the application of probiotics in plant‐based meat analogs (Table 1). Interestingly, plant proteins are effective as probiotic carriers (Valero‐Cases et al., 2020), which makes them a suitable matrix to use these microorganisms as beneficial strategies for consumer health.

It is required to consume regularly probiotic microorganisms, so their application to ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs may be exploited to ensure the achievement of the desired beneficial effects, in particular for those who do not consume fermented foods or any source of probiotic microorganisms.

Postbiotics

“Postbiotic” refers to a preparation of nonliving microorganisms and/or their components that provide health benefits to the host (Salminen et al., 2021). The main postbiotics of interest are short‐chain fatty acids, vitamins, peptides like bacteriocins, teichoic acids, plasmalogens, enzymes, and organic acids made by live cells through the fermentation process (Gezginç et al., 2022; Hernández‐Granados & Franco‐Robles, 2020). Postbiotics have several therapeutic properties, including immunomodulatory, hypocholesterolemic, antiinflammatory, antiadhesion, antibiofilm, antiviral, antihypertensive, antiproliferative, antioxidant, gastro‐protective activity, and so forth (Abbasi et al., 2022; Gezginç et al., 2022). Postbiotics stand out for superior stability and security compared to probiotics, making them promising candidates in the areas of food safety and preservation along the food chain (Hernández‐Granados & Franco‐Robles, 2020; Mishra et al., 2024).

The presence of proteins, peptides, and microbial enzymes created during fermentation can transform plant‐based meat analogs into an excellent delivery system for improving their nutritional quality through postbiotics. It is recommended to use microorganisms and fermentation techniques to improve the safety of plant‐based meat alternatives by increasing digestibility, providing health benefits, and including high‐value nutrients such as polyphenols, vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids, and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) (Elhalis et al., 2023). These metabolites might be interesting in the development of ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs, and further research must be performed in this area.

Safety

Foods are suitable environments for the growth and survival of spoilage and pathogenic bacteria. Pseudomonas spp., Enterobacteriaceae, LAB, Flavobacterium, and Brochothrix thermosphacta are some of the microorganisms often responsible for the spoilage of animal‐derived foods, depending on the storage temperature and packaging conditions (Bahlinger et al., 2021; Doulgeraki et al., 2012; Saha et al., 2024), whereas the most common foodborne pathogenic microorganisms are Salmonella spp., Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium spp., Bacillus cereus, and Escherichia coli (Delgado et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2021). However, the information about whether these microorganisms can grow and become a threat to the quality and safety of plant‐based analogs remains limited (Luchansky et al., 2020). And even more important, there is a lack of information about the new microbial hazards that we are facing without a well‐established safety evaluation program.

The upward presence of analogs in the market is accompanied by increasing concerns about their safety and microbiological risks, both between consumers and manufacturers (Liu et al., 2023; Tóth et al., 2021). Analogs have usually high moisture and a neutral pH, a compendium of characteristics that favor the growth of spoilage microorganisms and foodborne pathogens (Hadi & Brightwell, 2021; Liu et al., 2023; Luchansky et al., 2020). However, the current knowledge about the food safety aspects of plant‐based analogs is reduced, since the studies focused on this topic are scarce. The few available studies reveal that during the storage of refrigerated and nonrefrigerated meat analogs, significant quantities of Enterobacteriaceae and yeast species can be found (Tóth et al., 2021). Similarly, Liu et al. (2023) suggested that both pea‐based (PBM) and soy‐based meat analogs (SBM) could support the survival and even growth of spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms. Nevertheless, almost no studies related to the control of these spoilage and pathogenic bacteria are available in plant‐based products. Thus, strategies applied to products of animal origin deserve to be gathered and analyzed to be applied to plant‐based products (Table 2). Different methods, including thermal or high‐pressure processing (HPP), food irradiation, and the inclusion of additives, are used to preserve food (Fu et al., 2016). However, they can all produce changes in chemical and organoleptic properties (Majhenič et al., 2014) or are not well accepted by consumers. Therefore, a possible alternative that has been tested in recent years is the use of biocontrol agents to guarantee food safety. Biocontrol agents usually come from epiphytic or controlled microbiota, or their metabolites, to prevent the growth of pathogenic and/or spoilage microorganisms, extend shelf life, and improve food safety without modifying the technological and sensory properties of the food (Gálvez et al., 2014).

NaCl replacement/reduction

It is widely known that there is a relationship between high salt consumption and the development of some noncommunicable diseases such as hypertension, which contributes to cardiovascular disorders (Nissaisorakarn et al., 2023). Market analysis and different studies have demonstrated that several plant‐based analogs contain more sodium compared to their animal‐based counterparts such as in meat (Crimarco et al., 2020; Harnack et al., 2021) or cheese products (Pointke & Pawelzik, 2022). In this context, alternatives to salt content in food products have been already explored, applying different strategies in the food industry, mainly in meat and meat products (Nie et al., 2024).

Since the development of plant‐based products that imitate animal ones is relatively recent, there is a lack of studies exploring NaCl replacement. Peng et al. (2021) studied the influence of NaCl in meat analogs based on wheat gluten, particularly on their physicochemical and structural properties, with the aim, among others, of providing foundational knowledge for the development of effective NaCl substitutes and valid strategies for salt reduction. This contribution concluded that the optimal NaCl level for these products was 3%. Cornet et al. (2021) showed that by carefully choosing marinade pH and ionic strength, the water‐holding capacity of meat analogs can be regulated, which could allow for the use of less NaCl in the marinating process and improved juiciness of this product.

On the other hand, in the context of animal‐origin source products, several NaCl replacement strategies could be applied to their respective analogs. The main strategies of this replacement are the application of other metal salts and using flavor enhancers. The most common sodium salt substitute commercially used is potassium chloride (KCl) (Tan et al., 2022); nevertheless, there are different metal salt alternatives, such as magnesium chloride, magnesium sulfate, and calcium chloride, among others (Shen et al., 2022). KCl was widely applied to muscle foods (da Silva Amorim Gomes et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2021) and dairy products (Machado et al., 2021) as an efficient substitute for sodium, with the challenges of keeping the quality profile of the products in comparison with the NaCl ones, mainly focusing on the appearance of off‐flavors (related to the use of KCl) (Nie et al., 2024). Due to this fact, the major applications of KCl in the food industry are not replacing but reducing the amount of NaCl. In this context, it is also common to apply non‐sodium metal salts, or even NaCl, with flavor enhancers. This strategy improves the organoleptic profile of food products, particularly processed products (Shen et al., 2022). Common flavor enhancers encompass a variety of compounds, including amino acids like monosodium glutamate and L‐alanine, nucleotides such as disodium inosinate and disodium guanylate, or plant‐derived compounds such as allicin and gingerol (Nie et al., 2024). These enhancers could be a great strategy for plant‐based analogs since most of these products are obtained from plants.

Another strategy studied in burgers meats is the addition of fish oil microcapsules, which increases the sensory perception of salty taste and allows for a reduction in NaCl content in their formulation (Solomando et al., 2023). The use of microcapsules allows a smaller amount of salt since, due to the structure of the microcapsule, the perception of NaCl is greater. This methodology has been widely applied to animal‐based foods (Pérez‐Palacios et al., 2019) and has enormous potential in plant‐based UFPs.

Finally, the denominated “emerging food processing techniques” can also be applied to processed plant‐based products as a methodology for NaCl replacement. Strategies focused on HPP, which applies pressures to food products from 300 to 600 MPa, and power ultrasound, which is based on high‐frequency waves, have been applied as an alternative to NaCl reduction compared to traditional methods in processed products (Inguglia et al., 2017). Both technologies enhance salt uptake, which limits the amount needed in these products and, therefore, the possibility of reducing the amount of NaCl or its replacers.

Since the novelty of plant‐based analogs, there are scarcely studies and policies on these kinds of products. Several strategies are indeed potentially applicable in plant‐based products based on the methodologies used in animal‐origin processed foods (Table 3). Further works must explore these strategies to evaluate their viability in different plant‐based analogs, ensuring safe products, enhancing the nutritional profile, and meeting the sensory expectations of the consumers.

Oxidation of proteins avoidance

Proteins play a key role in foods from a nutritional, sensory, and technological point of view. These are susceptible to oxidative processes that occur spontaneously, without provocation (in situ autooxidation), or are caused, desirably or not, by processing and storing treatments. It can be influenced by factors intrinsic or extrinsic to the food (characteristics and processing conditions) (Domínguez et al., 2022). When protein oxidation reactions are triggered, carbonylation, fragmentation, or crosslinking reactions occur, modifying the side chains of the amino acids and the protein skeleton (Estévez, 2017; Hellwig, 2019). The resulting products (e.g. alkyl radicals, peroxides, hydroperoxides, and carbonyl compounds) accumulate in foods during processing and storage, providing functional and nutritional changes, as well as harmful effects in vivo (Díaz‐Velasco et al., 2022).

All categories of proteins are susceptible to these oxidative processes, regardless they are products of plant or animal origin. The main sources of plant‐based proteins are soy, wheat, peas and other beans, legumes, nuts, seeds, and cereals. These vegetables are subjected to different technological processes to be used as ingredients in the formulation of ultra‐processed plant‐based analogs. Extrusion is the main technological strategy to obtain textured vegetable proteins with a texture profile that resembles meat fibers (Baune et al., 2022). During extrusion, protein ingredients are transformed into fibers, shreds, chunks, bits, granules, or slices to enhance the textural properties of the final product (Riaz, 2011). Soy, wheat (gluten), chickpeas, and peas, for example, are usually used as ingredients in their textured form. The main ones are isolated soy protein, isolated mung bean protein, isolated peanut protein, isolated pea protein, and wheat gluten (Samard & Ryu, 2019).

There is limited knowledge of how the processes applied to obtain flours and derived protein ingredients affect protein oxidation. The process of obtaining soy protein ingredients and/or subsequent storage conditions may promote protein oxidation, especially in hydrated form, given the known variation in protein‐bound carbonyl levels (Duque‐Estrada et al., 2020). Zhang et al. (2017) have identified a concentration of protein carbonyls in soy protein isolate heated for 8 h at 100°C to be only 10 nmol/mg protein. In the chickpea extrusion process, the oxidative changes identified in the protein vary depending on the moisture content and screw speed, in a range from 0.259 to 0.779 nmol/mg of protein (Petrova et al., 2023). Likewise, previous studies indicate that the application of lower temperature and moisture can reduce protein oxidation reactions (Webb et al., 2023), though not reaching the desirable technological characteristics is likely.

Currently, some processing, packaging, and antioxidant ingredient strategies are known to manipulate and/or control oxidative processes in food systems (Armenteros et al., 2016; Domínguez et al., 2022; Estévez & Xiong, 2021). Nevertheless, despite the relevance of food quality, its application in plant‐based UPFs is scarcely studied. Knowledge in the field should focus on studying the onset of protein oxidation during the manufacturing of ingredients and plant‐based products and, thus, on establishing efficient antioxidant strategies or processes that prevent oxidation but obtain the desired sensory characteristics.

Some of the efficient strategies currently applied for processed animal‐origin products can be highlighted for application in plant‐based UPFs. In the context of processing technologies, emerging strategies of nonthermal technologies (pulsed light, high hydrostatic pressure, etc.) can be an alternative to obtain a lower oxidative impact when sanitation treatments are applied. In the preliminary study by Fernández et al. (2014), pulsed light treatment of up to 4.2 J/cm2 on processed cheese slices did not increase protein oxidation, while higher treatments significantly promoted it. In plant proteins, nonthermal technologies result in morphological changes in the protein structure, which helps to modify the functional properties without losing the organoleptic properties and bioactivity of the protein (Dash et al., 2024). It is also important to consider different packaging strategies, which may involve coating packaging materials with plant extracts to improve the oxidative stability of the products. Among the different novel approaches, active packaging and edible coatings/films take advantage of the beneficial effects while avoiding commercially undesirable effects on the food (Fang et al., 2017; Manessis et al., 2020). On the other hand, a different strategy that could be applied in plant‐based UPFs is the application of a modified atmosphere packaging without oxygen, which has demonstrated its efficiency for the control of protein oxidation (Tørngren et al., 2018). Furthermore, there are technological strategies that involve applying antioxidants directly to foods. Natural plant extracts are effective antioxidants reducing the oxidation of meat and meat products, including heat‐treated products, similar to those provoking protein oxidation in plant‐based products. The addition of rosemary essential oil successfully enhanced the oxidation stability of proteins in processed meat products, preventing oxidation in frankfurters and liver pâtés (Estévez et al., 2005, 2006). Phytochemicals such as lutein, ellagic acid, and olive leaf extract present a protective role for proteins against oxidation in pork sausages, in addition to conferring functional properties (Hayes et al., 2011). Likewise, the addition of oregano extract in the preparation of cooked sausages showed good oxidative stability in a storage period of up to 135 days (Fernandes et al., 2018). The addition of pitanga leaf extract has shown a positive effect in inhibiting protein oxidation throughout the storage of pork burgers (Lorenzo et al., 2018) and in employing yellow mombin extract in ready‐to‐eat chicken patties (de Santana Neto et al., 2021). Finally, hydrolyzed proteins from the same vegetable protein sources used in plant‐based UPFs, such as soy, rice, pea, and wheat, show antioxidant activity (Etemadian et al., 2021).

Therefore, the most effective strategies to prevent protein oxidation seem to be the research on milder but efficient heat/pressure treatments as well as the use of antioxidant compounds derived from nonanimal sources.

Lipid profile improvements

The lipid profile in processed plant‐based products is another challenge that this segment of the food industry must confront. It is worth remembering that the consumption of UPFs is positively correlated with an increase in the intake of total and saturated fats, which leads to a nutritionally unbalanced diet when consumed regularly and in large quantities (Martini et al., 2021). Generally, the plant‐based analogs of animal‐origin products have been traditionally low in lipid content, particularly for meat analogs; however, modern meat analog products contain higher lipid content in comparison with the traditional ones (Bohrer, 2019). The plant‐based dairy products are also commonly known as low‐fat foods, but this generalization is not valid for all analogs (Chalupa‐Krebzdak et al., 2018; Jemaa et al., 2023). Regardless of the amount of fat, it is also important to focus on the improvement of the lipid profile of the plant‐based analogs by increasing the content of PUFAs. Unsaturated fatty acids are recognized for their beneficial effects on the diet, and it is necessary to establish strategies in this direction (Jimenez‐Colmenero et al., 2015).

As aforementioned, owing to the novelty of manufacturing products of plant origin, the information in the scientific literature regarding strategies for modifying the lipid profile is scarce. Likewise, methodologies applied to processed animal‐origin products have a great potential for application in their respective analogs. Among the different strategies to improve the lipid profile, processed products are reformulated through the addition and/or substitution of functional ingredients. This methodology aims to modify the product's properties, reducing the saturated fatty oil content and replacing it with PUFA‐rich components. The strategies for the inclusion of PUFA can be summarized in the explicit addition, through emulsification or using microcapsules (Pérez‐Palacios et al., 2019). In this context, it is known that fish oil is recognized as an efficient component for the incorporation of long‐chain fatty acids in the lipid profile of foods since fish and seafood are food sources with a high content of ω‐3 PUFAs, in particular, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (Perez‐Palacios et al., 2022). Nuggets of animal origin are an example of the incorporation of fish oil in an ultra‐processed product, where the viability of this incorporation has been proven through the enrichment of these products with bulk fish oil or using microcapsules (Pérez‐Palacios et al., 2018). Thus, following these strategies, but using plant‐based oils, could be a suitable alternative for lipid profile enhancement. In this sense, microalgae are another component that can be added to improve the lipid profile in food products, compatible with plant‐based products. Microalgae have become an economically and technologically viable solution to produce a great diversity of high‐value bioactive compounds, including PUFA (Ren et al., 2022). This product has even been included in meat analogs, improving their nutritional profile (Fu et al., 2021). However, economic constraints and technological challenges, such as developing effective texturing techniques and pretreating raw ingredients to remove undesirable odors and colors, need addressing.

Another methodology used to improve the lipid profile is the use of gels. Depending on the polarity of the liquid phase, gels can be classified as oleogels, emulsion gels, and hydrogels (Martins et al., 2018). One of the most promising gel strategies is oleogelation, which is based on oleogels made from crystalline gelators, such as waxes or monoglycerides (Dominguez et al., 2024). Oleogels and emulsion gels have gained popularity as substitutes for animal fats due to their solid fat textures, rich unsaturated fatty acids, and health benefits. Nevertheless, the preparation of oleogels requires heating, which makes them prone to lipid oxidation. Emulsion gels, on the other hand, are attracting increasing interest for their potential as fat substitutes and as innovative components in plant‐based product design, but their mechanical and textural properties still require significant improvements in structural design (Guo et al., 2023). On the other hand, hydrogel has been widely applied in processed meat, as in pork burgers substituting backfat fat, and postulated as a promising methodology to obtain healthier products (Foggiaro et al., 2022).

Regarding dairy products, the strategies to modify the lipid content are the same or similar to those described for processed meat and meat analogs, such as enhancing the lipid profile with vegetable oils, fish and seafood oils, or microalgae (Gebreyowhans et al., 2019), including the supplementation of the animals (Nguyen et al., 2019). Finally, it is important to note that unsaturated fatty acids are more unstable to oxidation, and a combination of the strategies mentioned in the previous sections must be performed. Therefore, although it is not a nutritional improvement itself, it is important to consider the antioxidants and components, such as natural extracts, that can be added with the methodologies described in this section (Kumar et al., 2015; Perez‐Palacios et al., 2022).

CONCLUSION

Given that plant‐based UPF alternatives are not healthier than their counterparts of animal origin, strategies to become healthier are required. Among them, those easy and ready to apply would be the NaCl replacement/reduction, addition of antioxidants, and improvement of their lipid profile, as the changes already applied in animal‐origin products are well known and compatible. The main challenge remains for microorganisms that should be tested on plant‐based products to confirm the ability to enhance their safety and their probiotic and postbiotic activities. Regarding protein oxidation, applying antioxidants, using microorganisms, and employing milder heat and pressure treatments could be achievable strategies to prevent this unwanted phenomenon. The strategies collected in the present review establish a guideline for the plant‐based global industries to make their ultra‐processed plant‐based products safer and healthier. Additionally, these proposed alternatives may contribute to reaching one of the current demands, producing cheaper plant‐based analogs, as they achieve a life‐time extension through the addition of antioxidants or starters.
