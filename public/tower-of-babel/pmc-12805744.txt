Cognitive training shapes brain plasticity and schematic representations
Authors: Wenqi Chen, Yan Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive functions are critical to everyday life, and enhancing cognitive abilities has significant implications for both individual development and societal advancement. However, there remains no consensus on whether cognitive capacities can be systematically improved through behavioral interventions, commonly known as cognitive training. Recent advancements in large-scale neural recordings offer unprecedented insights into the brain’s cognitive mechanisms, presenting new opportunities to rigorously assess the effectiveness of cognitive training. In this review, we examine the core neural substrates underlying cognitive processes and explore generalized mechanisms of neuroplasticity associated with cognitive training. Integrating findings from animal models and human research, we emphasize the role of emerging schematic neural representations as potential mediators of cognitive transfer. Finally, we discuss future directions that could shed light on the mechanistic foundations of transfer after training, aiming to bridge the gap between experimental findings and real-world cognitive enhancement.

Introduction

For decades, there has been a growing desire to train the brain much like we train the body [1]. Intense competition in education and the workplace has fueled hopes that repeated exercises in “brain gyms” could enhance cognitive abilities such as attention, memory, and problem-solving skills—in essence, helping individuals become smarter. This demand has sparked a massive commercial wave, with the global cognitive assessment and training market estimated at $4.2 billion in 2021, projected to grow to $43 billion by 2030 [2]. While the market thrives on optimism, researchers in psychology and neuroscience maintain a cautious stance. One of the most debated questions is whether and how training on specific cognitive tasks transfers to broader cognitive domains—i.e., does task-specific training lead to general cognitive improvement [3–6]?

Some studies report that cognitive training can improve performance on both closely related tasks (near transfer) and, to a lesser extent, unrelated tasks (far transfer) [7, 8]. However, a significant body of research suggests that cognitive training may fail to enhance real-world problem-solving abilities [9, 10]. Just as running on land can improve swimming performance, it is challenging to assess how training on digit span tasks [11] might enhance general cognitive functioning. Even if one improves short-term memory for numbers, does this translate into better memory for other stimuli, such as images, or improved business decisions? Undoubtedly, the first step in answering the question of whether cognitive training is effective lies in thoroughly examining the associated neural changes. While it is generally hypothesized that cognitive training induces brain neuroplasticity [12], the specific forms of plasticity involved—whether synaptic plasticity, neural resource reallocation, or task-specific representations—remain poorly understood. A deeper understanding of these neural mechanisms is crucial, especially given the inconsistent findings regarding transfer effects in human studies. Such research could reveal the reasons for transfer failures—for instance, by demonstrating that training reinforces highly specific neural pathways that are not applicable to other tasks. Conversely, it could also generate new hypotheses about the conditions under which transfer might be possible, such as through training paradigms specifically designed to promote the broad strengthening of neural pathways.

Reliable animal models are crucial for studying the underlying mechanisms of cognition. Rodents, in particular, offer significant advantages due to their suitability for genetic manipulation and ease of breeding. Additionally, evidence suggests that rodents share similar cognitive processing mechanisms with humans [13–15], enhancing the feasibility of incorporating rodent models into cognitive science research. Recent advances in electrophysiology, two-photo calcium imaging, and genetic manipulation have brought us closer to uncovering the neurobiological mechanisms underlying cognitive training [16, 17]. These technologies allow for targeted measurement and manipulation of specific neural cell types or brain regions, particularly when combined with animal models, enabling us to draw causal conclusions about the mechanisms at play. Large-scale recordings of single neurons have revealed that cognitive training leads to the formation of abstract representations of tasks (schema) in the brain [18]—representations that may be critical for the generalization of learning to novel situations.

This review will synthesize current knowledge on the neural substrates of cognition and explore the potential plasticity mechanisms underlying cognitive training, with a particular focus on the neural features of schema formation during the training process.

Core components of cognitive functions

At the heart of cognition lies executive function (EF), which is generally divided into three core components: inhibition control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [19]. Inhibition control involves the control of behavior and attention, allowing for the suppression of inappropriate responses and interference from irrelevant information to meet the demands of tasks and goals. Working memory, on the other hand, is responsible for maintaining and updating information, integrating sensory and behavioral inputs to sustain focus on task-relevant data. Cognitive flexibility refers to the ability to switch between tasks or mental operations, redirecting attention as needed. Importantly, these components do not function in isolation [20]. Inhibition often relies on working memory to maintain task goals, while selective attention during working memory tasks depends on inhibition. Cognitive flexibility, in turn, requires both inhibition and working memory to disengage from irrelevant tasks and focus on relevant ones. In real-world problem-solving, these components frequently overlap and interact to support higher-order cognitive functions such as planning and reasoning [21]. Additionally, the effective functioning of EF relies on the coordination of the emotional system, the integration of long-term memory, and the normal processing of sensorimotor information. These processes provide the foundation for an individual’s ability to engage with and experience the environment (Fig. 1A). The interdependence of these cognitive components underpins the theoretical basis for behavioral interventions aimed at enhancing cognitive abilities. Psychologists have developed a range of tasks designed to measure different aspects of EF, often using response times and accuracy as indicators of cognitive ability [22] (Fig. 1B). Cognitive training typically builds upon these tasks, requiring participants to repeatedly perform them in order to enhance their cognitive capacities. Notably, non-human primates and rodents have been trained to successfully complete a number of classic cognitive tasks, providing a foundation for studying the mechanisms underlying cognitive training in animal models [23, 24].

The ultimate goal of cognitive training is to seek evidence of enhanced cognitive abilities in real-world contexts (e.g., improved learning or work performance), thereby defining near and far transfer effects (Fig. 1C). While some studies report near and/or far from working memory or inhibition training [25], a significant number find no transfer effects at all [26, 27].

The transfer problem and the role of animal models

Primates and rodents can be trained to perform well on classic cognitive tasks such as go/no-go, delayed matching tasks, and reversal learning, which forms the basis for studying cognitive training mechanisms through animal experiments. In contrast to the widespread debate in human studies, cognitive training in animals is considered to exhibit transfer effects. One classic piece of evidence is that after rodents undergo parallel memory load training in dual eight-arm mazes, their overall performance on a subsequent set of heterogeneous tasks and selective attention indicators improve, interpreted as domain-general control enhancement rather than single-task proficiency increase (equivalent to “far transfer”, Fig. 1D) [28]. In another study, mice classified a set of gratings, easily generalizing the classification rules to new stimuli after learning (near transfer) [29]. Moreover, mice learned reversal tasks in the go/no-go paradigm at a faster rate than during initial learning (near transfer) [30], demonstrating the transfer of initial cognitive training results.

The two types of studies have structural differences in assessment protocols and comparability. Human studies are typically oriented toward ‘real-world relevant’ outcomes, emphasizing improvements across tests, domains, or even learning and work output [31]. Animal studies more commonly use paradigm variations with similar task structures but different rules to test transfer, viewing ‘faster dimensional switching’ and ‘better performance on new rule tasks’ as markers of successful generalization [28]. From strict human cognitive psychology standards, most transfer effects in animals can only be classified as “near transfer” [32, 33]. However, we believe that from a neural mechanism perspective, even minimal transfer is quite important because it also reflects the extrapolation of prior experience to new situations. They provide repeatable, manipulable, and observable windows that can directly interface with large-scale electrophysiology, calcium imaging, and optogenetic manipulation. Researchers can track population and single-neuron dimensional representations, encoding decomposition, and mapping reorganization throughout the learning-switching cycle in the same individuals, and further analyze the causal role of relevant brain regions or circuits in transfer effects—this mechanistic-level evidence is currently difficult to obtain in human research.

To facilitate discussion, it is necessary to first define different types of cognitive transfer and compare them with findings in animal studies.

Transfer: Refers to the ability of skills or knowledge acquired by an individual after completing a specific task or learning experience to be applied in new tasks or contexts, emphasizing the impact of learned content on subsequent tasks.

Near transfer: Involves enhanced performance in tasks that are structurally similar to the trained task, sharing cognitive processes or stimuli (e.g., mice trained on auditory Go/No-go paradigms learn visual Go/No-go tasks more quickly).

Far transfer: As the most challenging and elusive goal, refers to improvements in tasks that differ in both structure and appearance from the training task, theoretically reflecting enhancement of broad cognitive abilities (e.g., working memory training has been shown to enhance rodent performance across various tasks and promote attention control).While near and far transfer can be readily distinguished in most studies, it should be recognized that this distinction is often continuous rather than categorical.

Generalization: Refers to an individual's ability to extract rules or patterns from specific experiences and apply them to tasks, contexts, or stimuli not directly encountered. It emphasizes rule abstraction and knowledge structure formation. (e.g., solving complex math problems requires problem decomposition, logical reasoning, and updating intermediate results, which fosters a schema for structured thinking. When later applied to programming—where tasks must also be broken into submodules and organized by logical rules—this schema enables rapid skill acquisition, illustrating transfer across domains and reflecting higher cognitive ability).

Neural substrates of cognitive functions

A substantial body of evidence suggests that different components of EF do not possess specific anatomical representations in the brain [34]. The prefrontal cortex (PFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and posterior parietal cortex (PPC) integrate various higher-order cognitive functions, including attention control, working memory, and decision-making [35–37] (Fig. 2A). These core brain regions organized into several large-scale functional networks. Among them, three are considered particularly crucial for executive functioning and cognitive control: the Central Executive Network (CEN), the Default Mode Network (DMN), and the Salience Network (SN) [38–40]. The CEN primarily involves the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), ACC, PPC, supplementary motor area (SMA), and cerebellum(C), and is essential for integrating sensory and motor information, top-down control, and working memory [41]. The DMN includes the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), PCC, angular gyrus (AG), and medial and lateral temporal lobes, supporting self-referential and internally focused cognition, typically exhibiting decreased activity during cognitive demands [42]. The SN encompasses the anterior insula (AI), ACC, amygdala, hypothalamus, and striatum, responsible for detecting and filtering salient stimuli, allocating attention, and recruiting relevant functional networks, thereby gatekeeping sensory information processing [43]. The dynamic coupling among CEN, SN, and DMN mediates cognitive flexibility. During cognitive tasks, the SN engages with the CEN and disengages from the DMN [44]. As task complexity increases, the integration between CEN and SN also rises [45]. Furthermore, during self-referential information processing, the CEN and DMN exhibit functional synergy [46]. In summary, cognitive processes rely on the dynamic coupling between brain networks rather than on specific circuits (Fig. 2B).

Recent advancements in optogenetics, pharmacogenetics, and innovative imaging techniques have enabled the visualization and functional analysis of circuits in the brains of behaving animals. Applying these modern neuroscience technologies has significantly enhanced our understanding of the brain regions and neural circuits involved in cognition, providing valuable insights complementary to human imaging data. Experimental studies on rodents have demonstrated the multiplexing of various cognitive-related brain areas and neural circuits during cognitive tasks (Fig. 2C) [47]. Early work by Harvey et al. highlighted the critical role of the PPC in maintaining working memory [48]. In a virtual T-maze navigation task, visual cues at the maze’s initial section indicated whether to turn left or right at a T-intersection to obtain a reward. Between the cue section and the T-intersection, mice traversed a delayed maze portion, requiring them to maintain short-term memory of the prior cues. Imaging results showed that neural activity sequences in the PPC were associated with impending choices, and inhibiting the PPC severely impaired task performance. Zhong et al. demonstrated the causal role of the PPC in cognitive flexibility, where silencing PPC activity impaired the animals’ ability to reassign previously learned stimuli to new learned categories [49]. In a recent study, initially trained on a tactile Go/No-go discrimination task and subsequently tested on a visual Go/No-go version of the paradigm, with two-photon calcium imaging recording single neurons in the PPC [50]. After switching stimulus modalities, mice still showed excellent accuracy, and some PPC neurons exhibited common responses to tactile and visual stimuli, despite individual neurons having different upstream projection circuits. This suggests that in cross-modal sensorimotor association tasks (equivalent to “near transfer”), PPC neuronal activity reflects relatively abstract rules, and these changes are not limited to specific neural circuits.

Furthermore, Wilhelm et al. showed that maintaining working memory in a T-maze spatial memory task required activation of the mPFC to dorsomedial striatum (DMS) pathway [51]. Animals were guided to one arm of the T-maze for a water reward during the initial choice. During the subsequent delay period (at least 5 s), mice had to maintain information regarding the reward location in working memory and correctly switch to a previously unvisited T-maze arm for the reward. Inhibiting this pathway during the delay period severely impaired performance. Another study indicated that projections from the mPFC γ DMS represent a “stop” signal [52]. Rats performed the 5-choice serial reaction time task (5-CSRTT), responding to visual cues appearing randomly at one of five holes to receive food rewards. Inhibiting pathway activity led to premature responses before the cue appeared, indicating the involvement of the mPFCγ DMS in inhibitory control.

Consistent findings across animal and human studies suggest that few brain regions or neural circuits independently serve a single cognitive task. It is difficult to envision how the brain utilizes limited neural resources to accomplish complex, dynamic daily tasks if there were a one-to-one mapping between behavioral tasks and brain structures. The broad responses of regions such as PFC and PPC to cognitive tasks suggest one possibility for behavioral interventions to broadly enhance cognitive abilities. If cognitive task training causes structural or functional changes in the brain, such changes are likely to benefit other cognitive tasks as well. However, whether these structural and functional changes are specific to individual tasks or can produce broad benefits still requires more rigorous research, such as recording the activity of the same neurons during completely different task training to observe whether neuroplasticity changes induced by prior training can actually function in new tasks—this would provide the most direct evidence for transfer of training effects.

Neuroplasticity during cognitive training

Structural plasticity

Structural plasticity in the brain can be observed following motor training or prolonged sensory stimulation, leading to increases in dendritic spine and synaptic density, which depend on long-term potentiation (LTP) effects [53]. It has long been hypothesized that cognitive training also induces similar synaptic plasticity changes. Neuroimaging studies have shown that training on Go/No-Go tasks and working memory tasks results in increases in gray matter volume (GMV) in the prefrontal cortex [54, 55], providing a macro-level indicator of such changes, though the precise microstructural alterations, time scales, and contributing processes remain unclear.

A series of animal experiments support the notion that cognitive training enhances synaptic connections between neurons and increases activity-dependent myelination of axons (Fig. 3, left). In a cognitive flexibility task, mice were first trained to respond in two different nose-poke holes to obtain food. For one hole, the association between poking and food delivery was maintained (reinforced), while for the other, food was delivered at a fixed ratio, unrelated to poking. Typical mice preferentially used the reinforced hole, demonstrating a sustained and flexible response bias that relied on the BLA→OFC→DMS circuit. Compared to a control group that passively received food pellets without performing the task, trained mice showed an increase in the proportion of mushroom-shaped spines in OFC, a decrease in thin spines, and an enlargement of spines head diameter. When brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)—a known facilitator of dendritic spine plasticity—was knocked down in the OFC, the animals exhibited inflexible choice behaviors, indicating that dendritic plasticity is crucial for behavioral flexibility [56, 57].

In another study, mice first learned to perform a specific action (i.e., pressing a lever) to obtain food pellets and then had the relationship between lever presses and rewards manipulated by increasing the number of presses required. The mice displayed behavioral flexibility—as the number of presses required for a reward increased, they increased their effort, but if the task became too demanding, they abandoned it, reducing their lever-pressing rate. In comparison to untrained controls, trained mice exhibited an increase in positive excitatory synapses of ACC. The deletion of the key synaptic protein α2δ-1 abolished the training-induced increase in ACC excitatory synapses, impairing the mouse ability to assess the value of outcomes and effectively manage effort expenditure in challenging tasks [58].

From strict human cog optimized the information processing within neural circuits. Mice were trained to conditionally avoid specific locations in a rotating arena, requiring them to learn the shock’s fixed position relative to the room. Compared to both an untrained control group and a group with lower cognitive control demands, the cognitive training group demonstrated faster learning in new environments and improved reversal learning in a T-maze task. Although both tasks test spatial memory, their structures are distinct, suggesting a generalization of learning abilities (beyond “near transfer”). The effects of cognitive training rely on the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG), which modulates the inhibitory and disinhibitory circuits in the entorhinal-dentate pathway. This modulation mediates the coordination of excitation and inhibition of inputs, and the functional changes induced by cognitive training persist for at least 60 days [59]. Similar circuit plasticity is also evident in the mPFC, where short-term facilitation and inhibition of synaptic strength can be observed in rats during an odor-location matching working memory task [60].

In addition to synaptic changes, there is also evidence of cognitive training-induced alterations in axonal myelination. Using an eight-arm radial maze task taxing spatial working memory, mice were required to remember which arms they had previously visited and select unvisited ones during a free choice phase to obtain food. Maze training stimulated the differentiation of oligodendrocytes (OLs) in the prefrontal cortex and induced additional myelination in the corpus callosum (CC). The myelin-regulating factor Myrf, a transcription factor necessary for OL differentiation, was conditionally knocked out in mice, severely impairing the training-induced improvement in maze performance due to genetic disruption of OL differentiation and new myelin formation [61]. Changes in spine dynamics, such as elimination, enlargement, and formation, may facilitate the reorganization of brain networks, providing flexibility in determining which presynaptic neurons provide input to each postsynaptic cell [62]. These modifications in myelination could fine-tune information transmission through relevant pathways, allowing for more precise timing of inputs and outputs from distant regions [63]. Such mechanistic changes in the brain induced by cognitive training hold the potential to enhance general cognitive processes.

Functional plasticity

Large-scale neurophysiological recordings indicate that complex visual discrimination training recruit neurons across multiple brain regions, including the mPFC, PPC, RSP, hippocampus, cerebellum, and striatum [64, 65]. In working memory task training, there is not only an increase in neurons activated during stimulus presentation, but also a gradual rise in persistently active neurons in areas like the PFC, M2, and PPC during the delay period of the task [66, 67]. This recruitment of neurons typically encode multiple task-related aspects, providing a computational advantage for complex cognitive tasks [47, 68–71]. When task rules are reversed, the responses of many neurons are systematically remapped to new reward-related stimuli [30]. Consistent findings suggest that the recruitment and reorganization of neurons resulting from cognitive training are highly dynamic. Cognitive training also leads to alterations in functional brain networks. During the task delay period, global functional connectivity in cortical regions decreases, while connectivity within behaviorally relevant subnetworks is selectively enhanced [67]. Research by Cho et al. shows that when mice learn a “rule-switching” task from odor to texture, the inter-neuronal gamma frequency (~ 40 Hz) synchronization between parvalbumin (PV) interneurons in the prefrontal cortex significantly increases, establishing a causal link to the learning of the new rule (near transfer) [72]. Moreover, during rule learning in the Y-maze, rats exhibit a significant increase in theta oscillations (5–10 Hz) between the hippocampus and mPFC [73], a synchrony that is likewise enhanced in humans performing sequential memory tasks [74]. The heightened neuronal responsiveness and enhanced connectivity (Fig. 3, right) resulting from cognitive training are associated with general cognitive functions such as attention and decision optimization in humans [75]. This observation provides a potential neural basis for the transfer of cognitive improvements to untrained tasks. However, whether such changes serve broad cognitive processes remains unknown. Future research must directly link these network-level changes to behavioral performance on transfer tasks.

Emergent schematic representations

Schemas, conceptualized as templates built upon prior experiences and knowledge, are crucial for learning generalization [76] (Fig. 4A). For instance, mathematicians often find solving complex problems easier than the average person due to their developed problem-solving strategies, enabling them to apply these strategies to new challenges. Over time, humans have constructed knowledge structures that facilitate new learning [77, 78]. Recent neural geometric analyses combined with large-scale single-neuron recordings reveal the neural representation of schemas. These analyses project neural activity into a task-relevant variable space, where a representational manifold captures how neural populations integrate diverse stimulus features, aligning with the concept of schemas (Fig. 4B). Higher cognitive functions such as planning, strategy, and reasoning are achieved through transformations within or across neural spaces [79, 80]. Recently, Courellis et al. documented single-neuron activity in different brain regions of epilepsy patients during reasoning tasks, where individuals infer reversal rules based on learned stimulus-action-outcome associations. In the neural state space, hippocampal neurons orthogonally represent specific combinations of context and stimuli, suggesting that the human hippocampus supports inference by encoding abstract representations geometrically [81] (Fig. 4C). Another study [82] revealed a universal representation of “conflict monitoring” in human mPFC, where the geometric structure of collective activity adapts to task demands, generalizing across various types of conflicts (Fig. 4D). This suggests that the brain’s representation of schemas may underpin learning generalization.

As humans mature, they continually extract schemas from experiences [83]. However, most non-human animals lack such prior experiences, making the emergence of schemas from cognitive training particularly intriguing. In an elegant study, Zhou et al. demonstrated the neural characteristics of schema formation [84] (Fig. 5). Rats were trained on a complex odor sequence task in which 16 odors were organized into two sequences. Since the meanings of the same odors varied between the two subsequences, the rats had to not only remember the meanings of the odors but also maintain the sequence information across multiple trials to perform correctly (near transfer). Subsequently, the rats were presented with five new odor sequence problems, each containing novel odor features but maintaining the same sequence structure. The rats had to learn how the new odors fit into the schema template acquired in the earlier trials. During this learning, the neural activity space in the OFC compressed significantly, and the identified neural activity patterns converged, achieving high decoding accuracy across problems, indicating the development of a generalized neural code in the OFC. Notably, high decoding accuracy across individuals suggests that information is organized in a similar format, leading the OFC network to converge on a common neural representation, a generalized solution for complex cognitive operations. The recent follow-up study further underscored the critical role of schemas in supporting generalization [85]. Specifically, optogenetic inhibition of hippocampal inputs to OFC) during a novel problem-solving task facilitated the emergence of OFC schema cells and improved task performance. These findings suggest that while the hippocampus encodes task-specific details, it may also compete with the OFC’s capacity to apply generalized schemas.

Moreover, Samborska et al. trained mice on a series of reversal learning tasks, where animals selected between two ports for probabilistic rewards. Once a high-reward port was consistently chosen, the reward probabilities reversed, necessitating the development of different motor actions while keeping the task structure fixed. Mice gradually improved their learning efficiency and adapted more quickly to reversals, demonstrating generalization (near transfer). During this process, a general neural representation of behavioral initiation, selection, and outcomes emerged in the mPFC, reflecting the temporal structure of the problem independent of action details [86]. This abstract representation in the mPFC may allow for the extrapolation of actions from similar prior experiences. Furthermore, consistent with human data, monkeys trained on reasoning tasks exhibited comparable neural geometric representations in the hippocampus and prefrontal regions, highlighting the cross-species universality of schemas [87]. These findings indicate that animals can abstract commonalities from previous experiences through training, enabling rapid adaptation to new environments without starting from scratch.

It should be particularly noted that these pioneering studies have not yet provided direct evidence for completely unrelated far transfer between cognitive domains. However, they propose a highly compelling mechanistic hypothesis: the ability to extract abstract patterns may be a prerequisite for any form of transfer. From a behavioral perspective, evidence that induction depends on pattern formation comes from observations that both animals and humans learn subsequent problems with similar structures significantly faster, suggesting they have mastered a task ‘template’ rather than simple stimulus-response associations [88].

The formation of schemas is intrinsically linked to executive functions. The process of abstracting rules and applying them to new situations is itself an act of cognitive flexibility [89]. Once an abstract task schema is established, its application can become partially automatic, reducing the load on working memory and attentional control, thereby enhancing performance. The attentional schema theory posits that the brain builds an internal model of its own attentional processes to monitor and regulate them, which is key to inhibiting distractions and switching between tasks [90]. Conversely, strong executive functions are required to override an existing schema when it conflicts with a current task, necessitating robust inhibitory control and flexibility [91].

Conclusions and future directions

While the debate on cognitive training in humans continues, a series of animal studies provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how such training promotes neuroplasticity. These studies demonstrate that training mediates changes in dendritic spine morphology, inhibitory circuitry, and myelination, enhancing responsiveness and connectivity across multiple brain regions. Most importantly, it promotes the formation of abstract neural schemas, which form the foundation for “learning-to-learn” and facilitate learning generalization. Synaptic and myelin plasticity mediate the rewiring of neural circuits, enabling functional network changes, while schema formation depends on the systematic assembly of neurons. These findings suggest potential mechanisms for generalization through cognitive training. However, current studies have yet to rigorously examine the duration of these plastic changes or how they might facilitate new learning over time. Thus, caution is needed in drawing definitive conclusions.

It is also important to note that although animal studies demonstrate cognitive transfer, the scope of this transfer remains narrow. While training-induced neuroplasticity and schema formation theoretically support broader generalization, it remains to be proven whether this can be achieved in humans. If neuroplastic changes are the foundation for generalization, other factors related to neuroplasticity—such as environmental enrichment [92], physical exercise [93], and social engagement [94]—may also contribute to cognitive enhancement. Indeed, evidence suggests that these factors do play a role [95, 96]. Whether cognitive training has a unique advantage over these other interventions remains to be seen. Furthermore, combining cognitive training with social or physical interventions may prove a promising strategy for enhancing generalization.

Another key question is why transfer effects appear more pronounced in animal models than in humans. Multiple factors may contribute to this discrepancy. First, many animal studies use 8-week-old mice (or 3-month-old rats), a stage analogous to human adolescence (approximately 12–18 years), when neuronal plasticity is extremely high. In contrast, human studies often involve adults with lower brain plasticity. When participants are younger, more reliable evidence can be obtained at least for near transfer, likely due to enhanced brain plasticity during childhood [97]. Second, there are differences in the nature of training tasks. Animal tasks are often existentially meaningful, linked to primary rewards like food, which may drive more profound and lasting neural changes compared to the weak incentives common in human studies. Third, there are differences in cognitive baselines. Animals have fewer pre-existing cognitive schemas, making the formation of new generalizable strategies a more prominent and observable learning event. In contrast, humans may default to highly practiced specific strategies, which impedes the development of more abstract cognitive schemas. If generalization is based on schema formation, many current cognitive training tasks that can be easily completed using pre-existing human schemas may fail to achieve training effects (similar to a ceiling effect). The impoverished cognitive abilities of animals themselves may be the reason for the significant transfer effects of cognitive training. Similarly, the therapeutic effects of cognitive training for patients with cognitive impairments such as ADHD, dementia, and substance abuse are less controversial [98–100]. This suggests that we may need to design plans characterized by complexity and diversity to challenge old schematic representations, and that interventions during early life stages when schemas are lacking (e.g., ages 3–4) may provide broader benefits.

In conclusion, further research into the neuroplasticity and schema formation underlying cognitive training in both humans and animals is essential. Future studies should focus on how training-induced plasticity and schemas are applied in novel situations and even to entirely unrelated tasks, which would provide direct evidence for or against transfer. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms will help optimize training protocols and guide more effective cognitive interventions.
