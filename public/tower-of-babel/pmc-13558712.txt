Mathematical Model Analysis for the Transmission Dynamics of Wheat Yellow Rust Disease: In Case of Melga Woreda, Sidama Regional State, Ethiopia
Authors: Abayneh Kebede Fantaye, Mohammed Yiha Dawed, Zerihun Kinfe Birhanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we present a mathematical model that uses real data to characterize the transmission dynamics of wheat yellow rust (WYR) disease. The model’s well-posedness, such as the existence and uniqueness, the positivity, and boundedness of the solutions of the model, is examined. Additionally, the study looks at qualitative evaluations such as the existence and stability of equilibrium points and uses the next-generation matrix to derive the basic reproduction number. The Routh-Hurwitz criteria were used to assert and demonstrate the local stability of the model’s WYR free and endemic equilibrium points. Lyapunov’s method is also used to establish the global stability of the equilibrium point. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis of the model is carried out. Using the least squares approach, the parameters of the model are fitted with the real data obtained from Melga Woreda of Sidama Regional State, Ethiopia. Using the estimated parameters of the model, the analytical results are illustrated via numerical simulations using the MATLAB solver ode45. The key finding of our study shows that proper use of both preventive and anti-sporulant fungicides eliminates the WYR disease from wheat fields after 15 weeks.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) ranks as the world’s second most important cereal crop, following rice, and is commonly traded on a global scale1. In Ethiopia, it serves as a staple crop, offering a substantial supply of calories and protein to residents in the highlands. Additionally, Ethiopia holds the title of the second-largest wheat producer in Sub-Saharan Africa. Wheat grain is an essential cereal crop used in both traditional and modern processed foods, such as injera, macaroni, and pasta. Additionally, the straw serves as animal fodder and a roofing material, highlighting its significance in production and marketing. Wheat yellow rust (WYR), also known as wheat stripe rust, is a serious wheat infection caused by the biotrophic fungal pathogen2. The pathogens can occur at any stage, from one leaf to plant maturity, as long as the plants are green3. The main mode of dispersal for WYR is wind, and it can travel widely4. Once in the air, the spores spread randomly. The majority will stay in the air until they are killed by sunlight in a few days, while some will settle on soil or other plants. Although the disease primarily affects leaves, when it is severe, symptoms can also appear in the head tissue. Yellow rust appears as a swarm of yellow to orange urediniospores that emerge from pustules arranged in long, narrow stripes on leaves, leaf sheaths, glumes, and awns on vulnerable crops5. Stripe rust is a long-standing problem that still causes significant harm to vulnerable wheat cultivars worldwide6.

In the meantime, WYR outbreaks are present in more than 60 countries globally6, particularly in North Africa and central and western Asia. Due to cultivar vulnerability, the period of first infection, and the environmental conditions during epidemic growth, WYR has caused crop production losses in Ethiopia that have varied from 40% to 100%7. The 1970 s, 1980 s, 1988, 2000 s, and 2010 s had significant WYR epidemics in Ethiopia8. In 2010, almost 400,000 hectares of wheat were affected, leading to substantial productivity losses that are hard to quantify. Additionally, the devastating WYR disease has impacted Ethiopia’s wheat-growing regions, causing significant output losses and unfavorable economic outcomes for farmers.

To forecast, and control the dynamics of disease transmission, mathematical modeling is crucial. A mathematical representation for spore dissemination, namely a non-local diffusion equation in terms of the dispersal kernel, was proposed by9. The model used Banach’s fixed point theorem to determine the problems’ existence and uniqueness before applying the finite difference approach to solve them. The effective utilization of climate data in the disease’s development led to a widespread pandemic of rice blast illness in the research locations. In10, the authors studied how to apply of botanical fungicide as a preventive measure and developed a model based on the course of the leaf brown spot disease. They develop a model with suitable optimal control strategies as well. The number of diseased plants can be reduced by the optimal control that was achieved when compared to a condition without control. Additionally, the analytical conclusions were confirmed by numerical simulations of how the theoretical results appeared.

A comprehensive model-based forecasting approach for WYR disease risk at the regional level was developed and evaluated by11, utilizing satellite-based data, meteorological data, and airborne inoculum. In Luxembourg12, developed a threshold-based weather model that is now being operationalized in a warning system for prompt and ideal fungicide treatments for WYR. These techniques have been put forth to minimize financial costs, environmental effects, and productivity loss while improving the timeliness and efficacy of fungicide application. Hyperspectral remote sensing was presented by Zhang et al.13 as a unique nondestructive wheat stripe rust monitoring technique. The study demonstrated that the use of Gaussian process regression in conjunction with fractional order differential (FOD) equations based on the Grunwald-Letnikov (G-L) technique improved disease identification accuracy and reduced spectral data background noise. This development enables more accurate field management tactics.

Fantaye et al.14 proposed a mathematical model for the dynamics of wheat yellow rust disease. They describe the well-posedness of the model and, using the next-generation matrix approach, the basic reproduction number is determined. Also, the two equilibrium points, the endemic and disease-free equilibrium points, are determined. The Routh-Hurwitz stability criteria and center manifold theory are used to investigate the local stability of the equilibrium points and the presence of forward bifurcation, respectively. To determine the global stability of the equilibrium points, Lyapunov’s approach is also employed. Furthermore, a model sensitivity analysis is carried out. The study’s conclusions indicate that effective fungicide use can significantly reduce the spread of WYR disease.

However, none of the previous works incorporated a compartmental mathematical model with real data for the WYR transmission dynamics. To address this gap, it is essential to utilize compartmental mathematical modeling to gain insights into the spread of wheat yellow rust (WYR) disease. The proposed model categorizes the total wheat leaf population into four distinct subpopulations: susceptible, protected, infected, and treated. This structured approach enhances the understanding of WYR transmission patterns and their impact on wheat yields, leading to better management of agricultural threats.

Conclusion

In the present research, we established and studied a deterministic compartmental mathematical model for the dynamics of wheat yellow rust disease transmission using real data. Certain fundamental characteristics of the models were computed, including existence and uniqueness, positivity and boundedness of the model’s solution, disease-free , and endemic equilibrium points . Applying the next-generation matrix, the basic reproduction number was computed. When , the system only possesses the disease-free equilibrium , which is asymptotically stable both locally and globally, but unstable when . The system has a unique endemic equilibrium that is locally stable. Our data indicate that the WYR disease is endemic if and subsides once . The normalized forward sensitivity index was used to conduct sensitivity analysis. The value of rises as the transmission rates α , K, m rise, while declining as rise. As the protection rate ω anti-sporulant fungicide η increases, the infectious class decreases. The analytical results align with the numerical simulation, which was carried out using MATLAB ode45.

It is important to recognize that this study has limitations. Our findings may not be as applicable to different agro-ecological zones or seasonal circumstances because the field data utilized for parameter estimates were gathered from a specific location (Melga Woreda) during a brief period of time. Additionally, our model makes simplifying assumptions about fungicide efficacy and environmental factors, treating important parameters like transmission and protection loss rates as constants. In reality, these processes are impacted by temperature, humidity, rainfall, and fungicide degradation dynamics that change over time and space. To overcome such limitations, future studies ought to improve the model to incorporate stochastic effects to account for environmental and demographic randomness, incorporate spatial structure using partial differential equations or agent-based methods to account for field heterogeneity and pathogen dispersal, and incorporate environmental changes to more accurately depict seasonal forcing and its effects on disease transmission and intervention efficacy. The model’s prediction ability and usefulness for directing evidence-based disease control techniques in Ethiopian wheat production systems would both be improved by these modifications.
