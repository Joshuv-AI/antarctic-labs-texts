Further Insight into the Association of Long-Term Particulate Matter Exposure and Mortality in Hospitalized Patients with COVID-19
Authors: Kai Zhang, Yu Shi, Mao Xun Huang
Published: 2024

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the study by Oh and colleagues that was published recently in the Journal (1), which investigated the association between long-term particulate matter exposure and mortality in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 in a representative sample of the South Korea population. The key discovery was that adjusting for covariates showed that a 1-μg/m3 increase in exposure to particulate matter ≤10 μm and ≤2.5 μm (PM10 and PM2.5, respectively) corresponded to a 4% rise (odds ratio, 1.04; 95% confidence interval = 1.03–1.05; P < 0.001) and a 6% rise (odds ratio, 1.06; 95% confidence interval = 1.04–1.07; P < 0.001) in the risk of in-hospital mortality, respectively. Meanwhile, we have some further thoughts and considerations about this article.

First, the results of this study were adjusted for various potential confounding factors such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, national household income level, and employment status. It is important to note that additional confounding factors may still need to be taken into consideration. For instance, researchers in the United Kingdom have also accounted for body mass index and smoking as important confounding factors when examining the relationship between air pollution and coronavirus disease (COVID-19) (2).

Second, to ensure the stability of the results, this study utilized PM2.5 and PM10 as continuous variables in logistic regression analysis. Additionally, to further verify the findings, the authors also considered PM2.5 and PM10 as categorical variables at the third or fourth percentile.

Third, understanding the evolving patterns of particulate matter exposure is essential for informing clinical decision-making and identifying critical junctures. In clinical settings, individuals are consistently exposed to varying levels of particulate matter, indicating that relying on a singular measure may not provide a comprehensive understanding of the intricate dynamics at play. Recent research has indicated a nonlinear relationship between air pollutants and the risk of acute respiratory distress syndrome (3). Although the study populations may differ, the findings also highlight potential variations in the impact of different particulate matter concentrations on outcomes. This underscores the importance of conducting dose–response studies.

Fourth, the results of subgroup analysis in the article revealed that the odds ratios for exposure to PM10 or PM2.5 in relation to in-hospital mortality were higher among participants who were ≤60 years old compared with participants who were >60 years old. This finding aligns with previous research conducted by the Harvard team, which also highlighted a correlation between fine particulate matter and the risk of hospitalization for cardiovascular disease (4). Furthermore, the subgroup analyses indicated that older individuals were consistently less vulnerable to the effects of PM2.5 across all outcomes. This could be attributed to age-related differences in immune status, as COVID-19 primarily induces systemic immune pathogenesis through mechanisms such as systemic cytokine storm and microcirculation dysfunction (5).

Finally, further subgroup analyses can be conducted to explore the impact of family income level and the Charlson Comorbidities Index on patient prognosis. It is important to consider more detailed subgroup analysis in future research. Additionally, investigating the effects of particulate matter exposure on various special populations and social backgrounds is crucial for guiding the development of future policies.

Although COVID-19 has demonstrated a pattern of high prevalence and low mortality rates, it is crucial to consider the possibility of future outbreaks of other infectious diseases. Consequently, investigating the impact of particulate matter exposure and synthetic effects on human health is a significant area of research. It is anticipated that future clinical studies will involve larger sample sizes, randomized control groups, and multiple research centers.
