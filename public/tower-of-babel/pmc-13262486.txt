Within-host mechanisms of resistance evolution in polymyxin B-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae without prior exposure
Authors: Jun Li, Yuyao Wang, Haolan Wang, Zhaojun Liu, Yubing Xia, Yongmei Hu, Haichen Wang, Fengjun Xia, Mingxiang Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolymyxin B-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (PBR-KP) has been emerging as a significant concern. This study investigated the within-host mechanisms of resistance evolution in PBR-KP without prior PB exposure.MethodsSix groups were enrolled [PB-sensitive K. pneumoniae (PBS-KP) evolved into PBR-KP from the same patient], and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the microdilution broth method. Whole-genome sequencing was performed to analyze molecular characteristics, and to investigate the resistance mechanisms, gene expression analysis of pmrAB, phoPQ, crrAB, and eptA was performed using RT-qPCR. Fitness costs were assessed through biofilm formation, in vitro growth rate, and virulence analysis.ResultsAll 12 strains belonged to ST11-KL64, except for PB9 and PB10, which were classified as ST11-KL47. Each resistant strain was highly related to its corresponding sensitive strain. The relative expression levels of phoPQ, eptA, and pmrAB were significantly upregulated in most PBR-KP strains. PBR-KP strains exhibited no fitness cost regarding in vitro growth rate and biofilm formation. Notably, the eptA gene was highly expressed in the four PBR-KP strains with enhanced virulence, indicating its association with virulence. Furthermore, PBR-KP strains with enhanced virulence were highly associated with KL64, whereas those without enhanced virulence were linked to KL47, except for PB12, indicating a potential relationship between virulence and serotype.ConclusionsPB resistance can develop in KP strains without prior exposure to the drug, possibly driven by upregulation of phoPQ, pmrAB, and eptA, as supported by validated transcriptomics data. These resistant strains did not exhibit fitness costs related to biofilm formation or growth rate. Enhanced virulence, found in most PBR-KP strains, may be closely associated with eptA overexpression and specific serotypes.Keywords: Klebsiella pneumonia, Polymyxin B resistant, Resistance mechanism, Fitness cost, Virulence

Background

In recent years, the global prevalence of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has been rising, resulting in significant challenges in the treatment of CRKP infections [1–3]. Thus, CRKP was classified as part of the critical group in the most recent priority list of bacterial pathogens released by the World Health Organization (WHO) on May 17, 2024 [4].

Polymyxin B (PB), an antibiotic in the polymyxin class, interacts with phosphatidylinositol on the bacterial cell membrane, disrupting membrane integrity and causing bacterial death along with the leakage of intracellular contents. PB is particularly effective against Gram-negative bacteria, and it has been widely used to treat serious bacterial infections, such as Acinetobacter baumannii, K. pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [5]. Although its clinical utilization was initially discontinued due to nephrotoxicity, the rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections, particularly carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, including CRKP, has led to its resurgence in clinical practice, marking its return in the “post-antibiotic era.” However, in recent years, PBR-KP have emerged in the clinic [6, 7], posing great challenges to anti-infection treatments.

Previous studies have demonstrated that PB resistance is primarily acquired through mutations in chromosomal genes or the acquisition of resistance genes via plasmids [8, 9]. One mechanism of resistance involves the modification of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), reducing its negative charge and, consequently, its affinity for PB, ultimately leading to PB resistance [10]. LPS modifications can occur through covalent changes to its lipid A component, such as hydroxylation, deacylation, or the addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L-Ara4N) or phosphatidylethanolamine (PEtN) [11]. These modifications are typically mediated by phoPQ and pmrAB two-component systems (TCSs) in bacteria [12]. Conversely, polymyxin resistance mediated by mobile genetic elements is primarily driven by the mcr gene, which encodes phosphoethanolamine transferase (MCR). This enzyme catalyzes the addition of phosphoethanolamine (pEtN) to the phosphate group in lipid A, altering the polymyxin-binding site and thereby conferring resistance [13]. While polymyxin resistance is not typically associated with lipid A modification by pEtN, it is mainly attributed to the chromosomal gene eptA (pmrC) [14]. The mcr gene, carried by plasmids, can be transferred between bacterial strains, facilitating the spread of resistance [15].

Prolonged PB exposure may be a notable factor, influencing KP resistance [16–18]. Previous studies have found that bacteria may be resistant to an antibiotic even without prior exposure to it. For example, they may be resistant to tigecycline or ceftazidime–avibatam despite no prior exposure to these antibiotics [19, 20]. In this study, we found that CRKP with no prior PB exposure may also transition from PB sensitivity to PB resistance. In addition, molecular characteristics of PBR-KP and PBS-KP were analyzed using whole-genome sequencing, resistance mechanisms were investigated by quantifying the expression levels of pmrAB, phoPQ, crrAB, and eptA via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and the fitness cost was also assessed by measuring biofilm formation, in vitro growth rates, and virulence.

Methods

Bacterial strains and data collection

PBS-KP and PBR-KP were collected from lower respiratory tract specimens or drainage fluid specimens of the same patient between April 2020 to August 2023. This patient had no prior history of exposure to PB during this period, which was confirmed by referring to the patient’s history of antibiotic use during hospitalization. The isolates were cultured overnight at 37℃ and re-identified using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (Zhongyuan Huiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), and E. coli ATCC 25922 served as the quality-control strain. Clinical data were collected, including patient department, demographic characteristics, hospitalization history, clinical diagnoses, and prior exposure to antimicrobial drugs before isolation. The strains were stored at -80℃.

The quality-control strains (P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603, E. coli ATCC 25922) were obtained from the National Center for Clinical Laboratories (Beijing, China). K. pneumoniae NTUH-K2044 was obtained from National Taiwan University Hospital.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The broth microdilution method was employed for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, including cefepime (CFE), tigecycline (TGC), PB, cefoperazone-sulbactam (SCF), ceftriaxone (CRO), ceftazidime (CAZ), aztreonam (ATM), piperacillin-tazobactam (TZP), meropenem (MEM), ampicillin-sulbactam (SAM), sulfamethoxazole (SXT), imipenem (IPM), levofloxacin (LEV), and amikacin (AK), which were all purchased from Wenzhou Kont Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wenzhou, China). In brief, the original solution of antibacterial drugs was diluted to a different concentration and dispensed at a concentration of 0.05 mL/well into a 96-well microplate. The strains cultured overnight were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity units and then 1:100 diluted with cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth to achieve a bacterial concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Next, 0.05 mL of the diluted bacterial suspension was pipetted into each well of a 96-well microplate. The inoculated 96-well microplate was placed in a 37℃ incubator for 18 h. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is defined as the lowest concentration that inhibits the visible growth of bacteria. The quality-control strains used were P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and E. coli ATCC 25922. The drug sensitivity results of CRO, MEM, SAM, SCF, LEV, ATM, TZP, SXT, CAZ, CFE, IPM, and AK were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100-34 criteria [21]. The results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing for TGC were evaluated using breakpoints established by the United States Food and Drug Administration, and the results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing for PB followed the latest breakpoints provided by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) [22].

Whole-genome sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using the Ezup Column Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; cat: B518255). Briefly, 1 mL of overnight bacterial culture was centrifuged at 8,000 g for 1 min to pellet the cells. The pellet was resuspended in 180 µL of Buffer Digestion and 20 µL of proteinase K, followed by incubation at 56°C for 1 h for lysis. Then, 200 µL of Buffer BD was added, mixed, and incubated at 70°C for 10 min. Next, after adding 200 µL of absolute ethanol and mixing, the mixture was transferred to an adsorption column and allowed to stand for 2 min. Thereafter, the column was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 1 min, and the flow-through was discarded. Subsequently, the column was washed with 500 µL of PW solution (centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 s) and 500 µL of wash solution (centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 s), followed by final centrifugation at 12,000 g for 2 min to dry the column. DNA was eluted by adding 100 µL of CE buffer, incubating for 3 min, and centrifuging at 12,000 g for 2 min. The extracted DNA was sequenced on the double-ended 150-bp read length DNBSEQ-T7 platform by Wuhan Baiyi Huineng Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Bioinformatics processing encompassed variant calling, annotation, and interpretation. Raw sequencing data were processed using fastp online tool (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) for filtering and subjected to quality control via FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Final bacterial genome sequences were obtained by assembling and correcting the sequencing data using Unicycler software (version 3, https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler). Core single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling and phylogenetic tree construction based on the maximum-likelihood method were conducted using Snippy (version 2, https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and virulence genes were identified through ResFinder (versions 4.1 and 4.6, https://genepi.food.dtu.dk/resfinder) and VirulenceFinder (version 2.0, https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder/), respectively. The sequence type (ST) of the isolates was determined via the Center for Genomic Epidemiology web server (version 2.0.9, https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/). Capsular serotypes of the isolates were identified using Kaptive software (version 3.1.0, https://kaptive-web.erc.monash.edu/). Phylogenetic relationships, sequence types, serotypes, resistance genes, and virulence genes for all strains were visualized using ChiPlot software (version 2.6.1, https://www.chiplot.online/). The sequence was deposited in the NCBI database with ID PRJNA1207320.

Resistant mechanism

The expression levels of pmrB, phoQ, pmrA, crrB, eptA, crrA, and phoP were quantified using the RT-qPCR method, and the primers are listed in the supplementary materials (S1). In brief, reverse transcription of extracted bacterial RNA was carried out using Prime Script™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) was employed for RT-qPCR analysis, in which PBS-KP served as the control and 16S rRNA as the internal reference gene. The expression levels of eptA, crrA, crrB, pmrA, pmrB, phoP, and phoQ of the control strains were set as the standard (value = 1). Relative expression levels in PBR strains were calculated using the following formula: X = 2−ΔΔCt. Data were statistically analyzed using the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to evaluate differences in gene expression levels between strains.

Fitness cost

The methodology for generating growth curves was adapted from a previous study [23]. A single bacterial colony was standardized as per the 0.5 McFarland standard, which was followed by 1:100 dilution in LB broth at 37°C with medium shaking. Then, 200 µL bacterial solutions were taken to measure the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) every 1 h for the first 12 h. Subsequently, the measurement interval was extended to 12 h until the end of the 36 h experiment.

According to the previously described crystal violet staining method, the biofilm formation assay was performed in all KP isolates [24]. The KP isolates cultured overnight were adjusted to a 0.5 McF suspension, which was diluted with LB at 1:100 to obtain the final suspension concentration of 1.5 × 106 CFU/mL. Then, 100 µL of diluted bacterial suspension was added to a 96-well cell culture plate, and 100 µL of LB was added to each well in the control group, with four parallel wells in each group. The plate was sealed and incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. The liquid in the hole was discarded and then dried. Next, 100 µL of 0.1% crystal violet staining solution was used to dry at room temperature for 15 min in dark. Thereafter, 100 µL of 95% ethanol was added into each well and left for 20 min. K. pneumoniae NTUH-K2044 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 served as the positive and negative controls, respectively. Absorbance at 570 nm was measured, and results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation from triplicate assays. Absorbance values for the control and experimental groups were recorded as Ac and A, respectively. If A ≤ Ac, the strain had no ability to form a biofilm; if Ac < A ≤ 2Ac, the strain had a weak ability to form a biofilm; if 2Ac < A ≤ 4Ac, the strain had a medium ability to form a biofilm; and if A > 4Ac, the strain had a strong ability to form a biofilm.

The markers of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKP) were detected using the PCR method, including rmpA, rmpA2, iroB, peg344, and iucA [25]. Then, the Galleria mellonella infection model (Tianjin Huiyude Biotech Company, Tianjin, China) was used for virulence evaluations of collected clinical isolates [25]. Briefly, the experiment was divided into four groups: the K. pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 group (low-virulence control), K. pneumoniae NTUH-K2044 group (high-virulence control), experimental group, and PBS group (blank control), with 10 G. mellonella larvae in each group. All KP isolates cultured overnight were adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL with saline, and then, 10 µL of this solution was injected into G. mellonella larvae. The G. mellonella larvae were incubated in the dark at 37°C for 5 days, and the color change of G. mellonella larvae was observed. A change of color to black indicated larval death. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed by Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance, using GraphPad Prism 9 software. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (ns: P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Clinical characteristics of strains

A total of six PBS-KP and six PBR-KP were collected from six patients. These strains primarily originated from lower respiratory tract specimens, with only two strains sourced from drainage fluid. The strains, which were initially PBS-KP and evolved to develop PBR-KP, were isolated from five patients and obtained during the same hospitalization period, except for one patient, whose strains were isolated twice during different hospitalizations. These patients primarily hailed from the transplant center, the intensive care unit (ICU), or the combined traditional Chinese and Western medicine department. Notably, PB was not used for these patients between the isolation of PBS-KP and PBR-KP, and the time taken between the isolation of PBS-KP and PBR-KP was 7–56 days in these patients (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing results revealed that all 12 strains were resistant to the antimicrobial drugs tested except for TGC (all sensitive), PB (6 sensitive), and SXT (4 sensitive).

The resistance genes, virulence genes, serotypes, MLST, and phylogenetic relationship

Genome sequencing results indicated that the experimental strains carried multiple drug-resistance genes. The detection rates were presented as follows: phosphomycin resistance gene fosA: 100% (12/12); the carbapenemase genes blaKPC−2 and blaKPC−34: 83.3% (10/12) and 16.7% (2/12), respectively; the β-lactamase genes blaSHV−182, blaCTX−M−65, blaTEM−1B, and blaLAP−2: 8.3% (1/12), 41.7% (5/12), 41.7% (5/12), and 41.7% (5/12), respectively; the quinolone resistance gene qnrS1: 66.7% (8/12); the TGC resistance gene tet(A): 66.7% (8/12); the chloramphenicol resistance gene cmlA1: 16.7% (2/12); the aminoglycoside resistance genes aadA2, rmtB, and ant(2”)-la: 50% (6/12), 83.3% (10/12), and 16.7% (2/12), respectively; the phenol resistance gene catA2: 50% (6/12); the methotrexate resistance gene dfrA14: 66.7% (8/12); and the sulfonamide resistance gene sul2: 66.7% (8/12). None of the strains carried the plasmid-mediated PB resistance gene mcr (Fig. 2).

The detection results of the hvKP marker genes rmpA, rmpA2, iroB, peg344, and iucA indicated that although PB9 and PB10 did not carry these genes, other strains carried at least three or more genes, suggesting that they may be hvKP strains. Furthermore, the results of capsular polysaccharide serotype detection revealed that the majority of the strains belonged to KL64, with the exception of PB9 and PB10, which were identified as KL47 (Fig. 2).

The results of MLST demonstrated that the 12 strains all belonged to the sequence type 11 (ST11). The number of SNPs between the PBS-KP and PBR-KP isolated from each patient ranged from 4 to 36, suggesting that these strains belonged to the same clone (Fig. 2).

The resistance evolution mechanisms of PBR-KP

To determine whether there were changes in the relative expression levels of the two-component systems in the six groups of PBS-KP and six groups of PBR-KP, RT-qPCR was employed to quantify the expression levels of crrAB, phoPQ, pmrAB, and eptA. The results revealed that the relative expression levels were significantly upregulated as follows: eptA, phoPQ, and pmrA in PB2/PB1, pmrAB, phoQ, and eptA in PB4/PB3, phoPQ, pmrAB, and eptA in PB6/PB5, phoQ and eptA in PB8/PB7, phoPQ, pmrA, and eptA in PB10/PB9, and eptA in PB12/PB11. The relative expression level of crrAB was downregulated in PB6/PB5. The abovementioned findings suggested that the mechanisms underlying the evolution of strains to develop resistance to PB without previous PB exposure may involve multiple regulatory systems, particularly phoPQ, pmrAB, and eptA (Fig. 3).

Discussion

K. pneumonia, as a notable conditional pathogen in nosocomial infections, has become increasingly resistant in recent years, including resistance to PB. During strain collection, we discovered clonal transmission of K. pneumoniae carrying blaIMP−4 in our hospital [26]. Previous studies demonstrated that long-term PB exposure may be an important cause of KP resistance [16–18]. However, in the present study, PBR-KP strains without prior PB exposure were collected. Thus, the mechanisms and fitness cost of within-host resistance evolution were investigated in the absence of PB exposure.

A total of 12 PBS-KP and PBR-KP were collected for this study. Analysis revealed that PBS-KP evolved into PBR-KP within 7 to 56 days. These strains originated from six patients, primarily in the ICU, reflecting the severity of their conditions. Genome sequencing demonstrated a high degree of genetic similarity between the PBR-KP and their corresponding PBS-KP, confirming they belonged to the same lineage. While prior studies have suggested that PB usage is essential for the development of resistance, none of the six patients in this study had a history of PB usage before the detection of PBR strains [16–18]. This finding demonstrates that mechanisms other than prior PB exposure may drive the evolution of PB resistance. Further research, involving the collection of additional strains, is necessary to elucidate these underlying factors.

In K. pneumoniae, colistin resistance is mediated by LPS modification after the addition of PEtN or L-Ara4N to lipid A, reducing the affinity of colistin to LPS [11]. L-Ara4N and PEtN are positively regulated by TCSs, involving pmrAB and phoPQ, respectively, and these systems are triggered by environmental stimuli and specific mutations [27, 28]. In addition, a TCS crrAB serves as another mechanism of colistin resistance in K. pneumoniae, mediated by the regulation of pmrAB through crrC [29, 30]. In this study, the relative expression levels of pmrAB, phoPQ, crrAB, and the pmrC homolog eptA (modifies lipid A by PEtN addition) in these strains were detected using RT-qPCR. The results revealed that the phoPQ, pmrAB, and eptA were significantly upregulated in the 4, 5, and 6 groups of strains, suggesting that multiple TCSs are involved in the resistance mechanisms of strains developing PB resistance without previous PB exposure.

Biofilm-producing bacteria are mainly associated with bacterial drug resistance [31–33]. Compared with their resistance in the planktonic state, the drug resistance of biofilm-forming strains can increase more than 1000 times [34]. In this study, the film-forming ability of the six groups was weak and did not significantly change before and after PB resistance, suggesting that the development of PB resistance in previously sensitive strains cannot be attributed to the biofilm in these unexposed PB strains.

Colistin resistance is mainly associated with a loss of fitness both through the mutation of existing genes [35] and plasmid carriage of mcr genes [36]. However, this may be plasmid- and strain-dependent [37]. Choi et al. [35] found that ST23 KP underwent adaptive changes characterized by a decrease in mucosity during the transition from PB sensitivity to PB resistance. In the present study, although biofilm formation and growth curve did not significantly differ, the virulence of PB2, PB4, PB6, and PB8 was stronger than that of their PBS-KP. The bacterial virulence is closely related to several factors, such as increasing siderophore systems for iron acquisition, the colibactin toxin, and increased capsule production [38, 39]. In addition, high expression level of eptA has also been recently reported to be closely associated with the bacterial virulence. The eptA is a membrane protein responsible for catalyzing the transfer of PEA to the 1-phosphate or 4′-phosphate[1(4´)-phosphate] position of the lipid A glucosamine (GLcN) moiety [40]. Moreover, previous studies have discussed lipid A and LPS as potential virulence factors in A. baumannii, and notably, A. baumannii-derived LPS could reportedly stimulate innate immune responses through toll-like receptor 4 signaling pathway in humans [41, 42]. Therefore, the association of lipid A modifications with changes in the virulence of A. baumannii is plausible. The increased virulence of colistin-resistant strains can also be partially attributed to cross-resistance to antimicrobial peptides, in which G. mellonella larvae can be naturally produced as a part of their innate immunity [43]. Developing resistance to these peptides can compromise the animal’s immune defenses, potentially leading to increased virulence of A. baumannii isolates. To our knowledge, there is currently no research on the virulence-related role of eptA in PBR-KP. In this study, the expression level of eptA was significantly higher in PB2, PB4, PB6, and PB8 strains than in PBS-KP. It is hypothesized that eptA, a crucial component of lipid A previously implicated in virulence, may play a significant role in the emergence of highly virulent K. pneumoniae strains, even in the absence of prior PB exposure; however, the specific mechanism requires further investigation. Notably, these PBR-KP with enhanced virulence in this study belong to KL64, whereas PBR-KP without enhanced virulence belong to KL47, suggesting the presence of a certain relationship with their serotypes.

This study has some limitations, including the relatively small sample size, its single-center design, and lack of functional assays. Additionally, the in vivo environment presented numerous variables, and the transition from PBS-KP to PBR-KP might involve multiple factors [44]. However, this study primarily concentrated on potential drug resistance mechanisms from the perspective of the strain’s drug resistance profile, making it challenging to fully elucidate the underlying causes of resistance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study assessed the resistance mechanisms and fitness cost of PBR-KP that evolved from PBS-KP without prior exposure. The drug resistance mechanisms of PBR-KP were likely linked to phoPQ, pmrAB, and eptA genes. Furthermore, no fitness cost was found in terms of in vitro growth rate or biofilm formation for colistin-resistant isolates. Notably, most PBR-KP strains exhibited enhanced virulence, potentially associated with the upregulated expression level of eptA and specific serotypes.
