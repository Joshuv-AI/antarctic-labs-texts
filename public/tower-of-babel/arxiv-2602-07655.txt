Discrete Breathers in a Honeycomb Lattice Near a Semi-Dirac Point
Andrew Hofstrand

Abstract
We study the dynamics of discrete breathers—spatially localized and time-periodic solutions—inside the bandgap of a nonlinear honeycomb lattice where the dispersion landscape approaches a so-called semi-Dirac point in which the bands cross linearly in one direction and quadratically in the orthogonal direction. By studying breather dynamics in two opposing asymptotic regimes, near the continuum and anti-continuum limits, we capture the features of hybrid coherent structures on the lattice that are highly discrete at the breather’s central peak and have tails well approximated by exact separable solutions to an effective long-wave PDE theory at spatial infinity. We find that breathers are dynamically stable over a wide range of parameters and locate an instability transition. Finally, we analyze the Floquet stability of spatially extended nonlinear plane waves bifurcating from the zero solution at the edges of the gap and how they shape breather profiles inside the gap.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation, background, and outline

With the advent of realizable 2D materials such as graphene
[
30
]
—a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in an approximate honeycomb array—there has been an outpouring of theoretical and experimental studies on wave transport and localization properties in two-dimensional lattices. The bipartite honeycomb geometry frequently appears in applications in condensed matter systems
[
21
]
, ultra-cold atoms in optical lattices
[
20
,
7
]
, coupled waveguide arrays
[
26
]
, acoustic structures
[
35
]
, and mechanical metamaterials
[
3
]
. Many of these engineered platforms exhibit malleable nonlinear responses at sufficiently high excitation levels. Understanding the dynamics of energy localization in discrete nonlinear systems is of fundamental importance in their design. Here, we consider a well-known class of spatially localized and time-periodic solutions called
discrete breathers

[
15
]
in the honeycomb lattice. The majority of literature on discrete breathers is on one-dimensional lattices or rectangular lattices in higher dimensions. However, several studies on the properties of discrete breathers in hexagonal and honeycomb lattices are given in
[
24
,
23
,
8
,
39
,
31
]
.

Recently, there has been a renewed interest from the condensed matter community in a class of quasi-particle known as the
semi-Dirac fermion
. The semi-Dirac fermion has the peculiar property that its energy dispersion relation is
linear
in one direction and
quadratic
in the orthogonal direction. This implies that the quasi-particle is
massive
moving in one direction,
E(\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},0))=\hbar^{2}k_{x}^{2}/2m
, and
massless
moving in the perpendicular direction,
E(\mathbf{k}=(0,k_{y}))=\hbar vk_{y}
, where
E
is energy,
\mathbf{k}
is quasi-momentum,
m
is mass,
\hbar
is Planck’s constant, and
v
is the quasi-particle’s effective velocity. The semi-Dirac fermion was theoretically predicted to exist in a graphene-like structure in 2008
[
10
]
. In 2024, it was experimentally observed for the first time in the topological metal ZrSiS using spectroscopy
[
34
]
.

In the current work, we study the properties of discrete breathers in a conservative mass-spring system arranged in a honeycomb lattice with an in-cell cubic type nonlinearity. The dimerized linear couplings of the sites in the lattice we consider are analogous to those of the one-dimensional Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) model for polyacetylene
[
36
]
. Nonlinear extensions of the SSH model are analyzed in
[
18
,
25
]
. Here, we focus on discrete breathers that have a frequency located inside the honeycomb lattice’s phonon bandgap where the dispersion bands approach the energy landscape of a semi-Dirac fermion. Our model is described in the following subsection.

In Section
2
, we derive an effective PDE description for the envelopes of weakly nonlinear wavepackets with wavevectors concentrated around a critical point of the lattice’s band structure where the bands nearly meet in a semi-Dirac crossing. We then obtain exact expressions for line solitons to the PDE model and use them to derive asymptotic solutions that are separable and spatially localized in two dimensions in the limit in which one spatial variable goes to infinity. This separable solution can be formally expressed as the product of two exact one-dimensional solitons.

Section
3
specifies conditions for the existence of discrete breathers in the lattice and provides an iterative scheme to compute their spatiotemporal profiles along with their dynamic stability from exact seed solutions. We find that the asymptotic envelopes derived in Section
2
accurately represent the extended spatial tails of the computed breathers near the semi-Dirac crossing. Importantly, our combination of analytic and numerical methods near opposing limits (the continuum and anti-continuum limits) allows us to fully describe breather characteristics not captured by either asymptotic theory alone.

Finally, in Section
4
we study de-localized nonlinear plane waves that bifurcate from the trivial solution at the edges of the phonon gap and analyze their Floquet stability both perturbatively and numerically. Section
5
gives some concluding remarks.

1.2
Lattice model and band structure

We consider a two-dimensional Hamiltonian mass-spring system arranged in a regular honeycomb pattern (see Figure
1
(a)) with linear nearest-neighbor couplings. The unit cell of the lattice consists of two sub-sites, labeled
A
and
B
, with identical masses set to one. Here, we fix the
intra-cell
coupling strength to unity and allow the
inter-cell
coupling strength, denoted by
\lambda
, to vary in the interval
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
.

(a) schematic of honeycomb lattice with A (red) and B (blue) sites and staggered coupling strengths 1 (bold) and

\omega_{\pm}^{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda),

The family of dynamical equations on the lattice is given by

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n,m}^{A}=-\partial_{z_{1}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})+\lambda\left[x_{n,m-1}^{B}+x_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n,m}^{B}=-\partial_{z_{2}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})+\lambda\left[x_{n,m+1}^{A}+x_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right],

where
(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
x_{n,m}(t)
is the displacement of the mass of the
(n,m)
point in the direction perpendicular to the lattice, and
V(z_{1},z_{2})\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is an in-cell potential. We assume that
V
is a convex function near
(0,0)
.

Specifically, we consider the potential form,

V(z_{1},z_{2})=\dfrac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{2}\left(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}\right)-z_{1}z_{2}+\dfrac{1}{4}g\left(z_{1}^{4}+2z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}+z_{2}^{4}\right),

(2)

where
\omega_{0}^{2}>3
is the on-site harmonic oscillator frequency and
g=\pm 1
represents a
hardening
or
softening

We fix the spatial scale in the plane of the lattice so that the nearest-neighbor distance is unity. The infinite lattice is then generated by all integer linear combinations of the vectors

\mathbf{a}_{1}=\sqrt{3}(1,0)\text{ and }\mathbf{a}_{2}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(1,\sqrt{3}).

Similarly, the reciprocal lattice, given in terms of the wavevector
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is spanned by integer multiples of the vectors

\mathbf{b}_{1}=2\pi\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}},-\dfrac{1}{3}\right)\text{ and }\mathbf{b}_{2}=2\pi\left(0,\dfrac{2}{3}\right).

The hexagonal primitive cell or the first Brillouin zone,
\mathcal{B}
, of the reciprocal lattice is shown in Figure
1
(b).
\mathcal{B}
contains the labeled critical wavevectors
\mathbf{\Gamma}=(0,0)
,
\mathbf{M}=(0,2\pi/3)
,
\mathbf{K}=(4\pi/(3\sqrt{3}),0)
, and
\mathbf{K^{\prime}}=-\mathbf{K}
.

Neglecting the nonlinear terms and inserting the plane-wave ansatz,

\begin{pmatrix}x_{n,m}^{A}\\
x_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}e^{i(n\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{k}+m\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{k}-\omega t)}+c.c.,

in (
1
), where
(a,b)^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash\{0\}
and
c.c.
denotes complex conjugation, leads to a
2\times 2
self-adjoint eigenvalue problem that is continuously indexed by the wavevector
\mathbf{k}
. The resulting real-valued dispersion relation or band structure of the linearization of (
1
) is given by

\omega_{\pm}^{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)=\omega_{0}^{2}\pm\sqrt{1+2\lambda^{2}+2\lambda^{2}\cos{\left(\sqrt{3}k_{x}\right)}+4\lambda\cos{\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{x}\right)}\cos{\left(\dfrac{3}{2}k_{y}\right)}}.

(3)

We refer to the upper (lower) dispersion band as the optical (acoustic) band. When the intra-cell and inter-cell coupling strengths are equal (i.e.
\lambda=1
), the eigenvalue problem becomes degenerate and the two bands touch at
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{K^{\prime}}
due to the increased discrete rotational symmetry of the lattice’s point group. In a neighborhood of these high-symmetry points, the dispersion relation is approximately linear (see Figure
1
(c)) and forms Dirac cones as in the electronic model of graphene
[
13
]
. When the parameter
\lambda
is tuned away from 1, a bandgap opens at both
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{K^{\prime}}
.

Here, we are interested in the formation of a
semi-Dirac point
in the dispersion relation. Inspecting (
3
), we find that the two bands also cross when
\lambda=\lambda_{*}:=1/2
at the point
\mathbf{M}
in the Brillouin zone. Furthermore, at
\lambda_{*}
and in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{M}
, we calculate along the
k_{y}
-axis

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}^{2}\left(k_{x}=0,k_{y};\lambda_{*}\right)-\omega_{0}^{2}=\pm\dfrac{3}{2}\left|k_{y}-\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right|+o\left(\left|k_{y}-\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right|\right)\quad\text{as $k_{y}\to 2\pi/3$.}

In the perpendicular direction, we calculate

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}^{2}\left(k_{x},k_{y}=\dfrac{2\pi}{3};\lambda_{*}\right)-\omega_{0}^{2}=\pm\dfrac{3}{4}k_{x}^{2}+o\left(k_{x}^{2}\right)\quad\text{as $k_{x}\to 0$}.

The behavior of the band structure at the semi-Dirac point is illustrated in Figure
2
. For
\lambda<\lambda_{*}
, a direct bandgap opens at
\mathbf{M}
with width
\omega_{+}^{2}(\mathbf{M};\lambda)-\omega_{-}^{2}(\mathbf{M};\lambda)=2(1-2\lambda)
(see the bottom panels in Figure
2
). For
\lambda_{*}<\lambda\leq 1
, the bandgap remains closed (not shown).

2
Continuum regime near a semi-Dirac point

2.1
PDE approximation

To study the behavior of weakly nonlinear wave packets in (
1
), centered on the wavevector
\mathbf{M}
and having a frequency located inside the phonon bandgap (see Figure
2
), we define the parameter
\epsilon:=1-2\lambda
. We consider the regime
0<\epsilon\ll 1
, which corresponds to
\lambda
taking a value just below
\lambda_{*}
.

We begin by rewriting system (
1
) in terms of
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n,m}^{A}=-\partial_{z_{1}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[x_{n,m-1}^{B}+x_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]-\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\left[x_{n,m-1}^{B}+x_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n,m}^{B}=-\partial_{z_{2}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[x_{n,m+1}^{A}+x_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right]-\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\left[x_{n,m+1}^{A}+x_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right].

We introduce the following scaled variables to center the analysis at
\mathbf{M}
and balance the cubic nonlinearity in (
1
),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{n,m}^{A}\\
x_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\exp\left(i\left(n\mathbf{a}_{1}+m\mathbf{a}_{2}\right)\cdot\mathbf{M}\right)\begin{pmatrix}Y_{n,m}^{A}\\
Y_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\exp\left(i\pi m\right)\begin{pmatrix}Y_{n,m}^{A}\\
Y_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Upon making these substitutions, the system (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle\ddot{Y}_{n,m}^{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}Y_{n,m}^{A}+

\displaystyle\left[Y_{n,m}^{B}-\dfrac{1}{2}Y_{n,m-1}^{B}-\dfrac{1}{2}Y_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]

(4)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\left[Y_{n,m-1}^{B}+Y_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]-\epsilon g\left[\left(Y_{n,m}^{A}\right)^{2}+\left(Y_{n,m}^{B}\right)^{2}\right]Y_{n,m}^{A}

\displaystyle\ddot{Y}_{n,m}^{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}Y_{n,m}^{B}+

\displaystyle\left[Y_{n,m}^{A}-\dfrac{1}{2}Y_{n,m+1}^{A}-\dfrac{1}{2}Y_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right]

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\left[Y_{n,m+1}^{A}+Y_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right]-\epsilon g\left[\left(Y_{n,m}^{A}\right)^{2}+\left(Y_{n,m}^{B}\right)^{2}\right]Y_{n,m}^{B}.

We define the Fourier representation on the lattice,

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{Y}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\\
\hat{Y}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{n,m}^{A}\\
Y_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}e^{-i(n\mathbf{a}_{1}+m\mathbf{a}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{k}},

and its inverse,

\begin{pmatrix}Y_{n,m}^{A}\\
Y_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{B}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{Y}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\\
\hat{Y}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}\end{pmatrix}e^{i(n\mathbf{a}_{1}+m\mathbf{a}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{k}}d\mathbf{k},

where
\mathcal{A}
is the area of the Brillouin zone.

Since we are interested in wavepackets concentrated at
\mathbf{M}
with a spectral width of the order
\epsilon
, we seek solutions of the form

\hat{Y}_{\mathbf{k},\epsilon}^{A,B}:=\dfrac{1}{\epsilon^{3/2}}\chi^{A,B}\left(\dfrac{k_{x}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}},\dfrac{k_{y}}{\epsilon}\right),

where
\chi^{A,B}(\mathbf{k})
are smooth functions that rapidly decay away from the origin. The asymmetric dependence on
\epsilon
in
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
is chosen to match the linear-parabolic curvature of the lattice’s band structure (
3
) near
\mathbf{M}.

Substituting the above expressions into (
4
), performing a Taylor series expansion in
\epsilon
about the origin, and keeping only the leading-order terms gives

\displaystyle Y_{n,m-1}^{B}+Y_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\approx\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\int\limits_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left[2-3ik_{y}\epsilon-\dfrac{3}{4}k_{x}^{2}\epsilon\right]e^{i\left(\sqrt{3}(2n+m)\sqrt{\epsilon}k_{x}+3m\epsilon k_{y}\right)/2}\chi^{B}(\mathbf{k})d\mathbf{k}

(5)

\displaystyle Y_{n,m+1}^{A}+Y_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\approx\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\int\limits_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left[2+3ik_{y}\epsilon-\dfrac{3}{4}k_{x}^{2}\epsilon\right]e^{i\left(\sqrt{3}(2n+m)\sqrt{\epsilon}k_{x}+3m\epsilon k_{y}\right)/2}\chi^{A}(\mathbf{k})d\mathbf{k}.

Using (
5
), we introduce the continuum approximation

\displaystyle Y_{n,m-1}^{B}+Y_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\approx 2u_{B}(t,z,\eta)-2\partial_{z}u_{B}(t,z,\eta)+2\partial_{\eta}^{2}u_{B}(t,z,\eta)

(6)

\displaystyle Y_{n,m+1}^{A}+Y_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\approx 2u_{A}(t,z,\eta)+2\partial_{z}u_{A}(t,z,\eta)+2\partial_{\eta}^{2}u_{A}(t,z,\eta)

evaluated at
z=m
and
\eta=\sqrt{2}(2n+m).
The preceding discussion also motivates the long spatial scalings
Z=\epsilon z
and
H=\sqrt{\epsilon}\eta.

Plugging (
6
) into (
4
) leads to the PDE approximation of the lattice dynamics, centered on the semi-Dirac point, near the continuum limit

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u_{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}u_{A}+\partial_{z}u_{B}-\partial_{\eta}^{2}u_{B}+\epsilon\left(u_{B}-\partial_{z}u_{B}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}u_{B}\right)-\epsilon g\left(u_{A}^{2}+u_{B}^{2}\right)u_{A}

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u_{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}u_{B}-\partial_{z}u_{A}-\partial_{\eta}^{2}u_{A}+\epsilon\left(u_{A}+\partial_{z}u_{A}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}u_{A}\right)-\epsilon g\left(u_{A}^{2}+u_{B}^{2}\right)u_{B}.

2.2
Multiple-scale analysis

We define the functions of the multiple-scale independent variables

u_{A,B}(t,z,\eta,T,Z,H),\quad\text{where}\quad T=\epsilon t,Z=\epsilon z,H=\sqrt{\epsilon}\eta.

Furthermore, we consider the following expansion in powers of
\epsilon

u_{A,B}(t,z,\eta,T,Z,H)=u_{A,B}^{(0)}(t,z,\eta,T,Z,H)+\epsilon u_{A,B}^{(1)}(t,z,\eta,T,Z,H)+\cdots

Substituting the above expressions into (
7
) and collecting like-terms in powers of
\epsilon
gives at zeroth-order:

\mathcal{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}u_{A}^{(0)}\\
u_{B}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}=0,

where

\mathcal{L}_{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}&\partial_{z}-\partial_{\eta}^{2}\\
-\partial_{z}-\partial_{\eta}^{2}&\partial_{t}^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

At first-order, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}u_{A}^{(1)}\\
u_{B}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\partial_{t}\partial_{T}u_{A}^{(0)}+u_{B}^{(0)}+\partial_{Z}u_{B}^{(0)}-\partial_{H}^{2}u_{B}^{(0)}-g\left[\left(u_{A}^{(0)}\right)^{2}+\left(u_{B}^{(0)}\right)^{2}\right]u_{A}^{(0)}\\
-2\partial_{t}\partial_{T}u_{B}^{(0)}+u_{A}^{(0)}-\partial_{Z}u_{A}^{(0)}-\partial_{H}^{2}u_{A}^{(0)}-g\left[\left(u_{A}^{(0)}\right)^{2}+\left(u_{B}^{(0)}\right)^{2}\right]u_{B}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}.

At zeroth order, we search for time-harmonic solutions of the form

\begin{pmatrix}u_{A}^{(0)}\\
u_{B}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}U(T,Z,H)\\
V(T,Z,H)\end{pmatrix}e^{i\omega_{0}t}+c.c.

(8)

Note, since (
8
) does not depend on
\eta
, the equation at order
\sqrt{\epsilon}
in the expansion,

-2\partial_{H}\partial_{\eta}u_{A,B}^{(0)}=0

is automatically satisfied.

Since we seek bounded solutions, plugging (
8
) into the right-hand side of the first-order system, we remove the
secular terms
on the right-hand side by setting the terms with a time dependence given by
\exp(i\omega_{0}t)
to zero. This leads to the following PDE system

\displaystyle-2i\omega_{0}\partial_{T}U+V+\partial_{Z}V-\partial_{H}^{2}V-2g\left(|U|^{2}+|V|^{2}\right)U=0

(9)

\displaystyle-2i\omega_{0}\partial_{T}V+U-\partial_{Z}U-\partial_{H}^{2}U-2g\left(|U|^{2}+|V|^{2}\right)V=0.

We refer to (
9
) as the semi-Dirac equation. We note that a similar equation was derived in
[
11
]
to model the propagation of optical solitons through fibers with periodic gratings.

2.3
Stationary solutions

The stationary form of the semi-Dirac equation is found by plugging in the ansatz,

\left[U,V\right]^{\top}=\left[U(Z,H;\nu),V(Z,H;\nu)\right]^{\top}\exp\left(i\nu T/(2\omega_{0})\right)=:\mathbf{\Psi}\exp\left(i\nu T/(2\omega_{0})\right),

into (
9
).

Neglecting the nonlinear terms in the resulting equation, we obtain the self-adjoint spectral problem in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
,

\mathcal{L}_{SD}\mathbf{\Psi}:=\left[i\sigma_{2}\partial_{Z}+\sigma_{1}\left(1-\partial_{H}^{2}\right)\right]\mathbf{\Psi}=-\nu\mathbf{\Psi},

where
\sigma_{j}

(j=1,2,3)
are Pauli matrices and
\sigma_{0}
is the identity matrix. The domain of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{SD}
is the anisotropic Sobolev space is
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{SD}):=\text{H}_{Z}^{1}\text{H}_{H}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)
. The spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{SD}
is gapped and purely essential, given by

\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{SD})=(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty),

which can be shown by constructing a Weyl singular sequence of approximate eigenfunctions in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{SD})

[
17
]
. Note also that since
\mathcal{L}_{SD}
anticommutes with
\sigma_{3}
(it depends only on
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
), the spectrum is symmetric about zero.

Remark
: Due to the form of nonlinearity, if
\mathbf{\Psi}
is a stationary solution to (
9
) for
g=1
at
\nu
, then
\sigma_{3}\mathbf{\Psi}
is a stationary solution to (
9
) for
g=-1
at
-\nu
.

Localized solutions to the stationary semi-Dirac equation correspond to the critical points of the functional

I_{\nu}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\big\langle\mathcal{L}_{SD}\mathbf{\Psi},\mathbf{\Psi}\big\rangle+\nu|\mathbf{\Psi}|^{2}-g|\mathbf{\Psi}|^{4}\right)d\mathbf{x}

(10)

for
\mathbf{\Psi}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{SD}).

Like the “energy” functional for the nonlinear
Dirac equation
, where
\mathcal{L}_{SD}
in (
10
) is replaced by the operator

\mathcal{L}_{D}\mathbf{\Psi}:=\left[-i\sigma_{1}\partial_{Z}-i\sigma_{2}\partial_{H}+\sigma_{3}\right]\mathbf{\Psi},

(11)

I_{\nu}
is strongly indefinite, making variational arguments about the existence of critical points more difficult than those for nonlinear Schödinger (NLS) equations, where the spectrum is bounded from below
[
12
,
19
]
.

For the
1D Dirac equation
, explicit localized stationary solutions (gap solitons) have been obtained in the literature for various nonlinearities
[
33
,
28
]
. For instance, the massive Thirring
[
37
]
model is used to describe self-interacting fermions in one spatial dimension with a Lorentz-invariant form of nonlinearity and it is known to be completely integrable. Another example is the explicit gap solitons found in
[
1
]
to a 1D Dirac equation with a cubic Kerr nonlinearity used to model laser propagation through optical fibers with periodic gratings.

In the following theorem, we find explicit line-soliton solutions to (
9
) using known solutions to a related equation and exploiting the unitary symmetry of both systems. Within the bandgap, the stationary solutions obtained are smooth and exponentially localized in the
Z
direction, taking a particularly simple form at the center of the bandgap (
\nu=0
). The decay rate of the solution in the
Z
direction becomes algebraic in the limit approaching one edge of the gap and the solution is identically zero in the limit at the other edge. In Section
3
we compare the derived solitons with numerically computed discrete breathers near the semi-Dirac point.

Theorem 1
.

The semi-Dirac equation, (
9
), has a family of explicit stationary solutions,
\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)=\left[\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\right]^{\top}
, which are localized in the
Z
direction and constant in the
H
direction (i.e., line-solitons) for each
\nu\in(-1,1)
. For
g=1
, we have

1.

\lim_{\nu\to+1}\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)=\dfrac{1+i}{\sqrt{2}(1+4Z^{2})}\left[1-2Z,1+2Z\right]^{\top}

2.

\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)=\dfrac{1+i}{2}\textnormal{sech}\left(2Z\right)\left[e^{-Z},e^{Z}\right]^{\top}

3.

\lim_{\nu\to-1}\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)=[0,0]^{\top}.

Remark
: By the preceding remark, the above theorem holds when
g=-1
by replacing
\nu
with
-\nu.

Proof.

We introduce the related 1D system,

\left(\sigma_{1}+i\sigma_{3}\partial_{Z}+\nu\sigma_{0}\right)\mathbf{\Psi}=2g|\mathbf{\Psi}|^{2}\mathbf{\Psi},

(12)

like the one considered in
[
32
]
, for
\mathbf{\Psi}=\left[U(Z),V(Z)\right]^{\top}.
Making the ansatz,
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\nu}=\left[U_{\nu}(Z),U_{\nu}^{*}(Z)\right]^{\top},
(
12
) becomes the scalar equation

i\dfrac{dU_{\nu}}{dZ}+\nu U_{\nu}+U_{\nu}^{*}-4g|U_{\nu}|^{2}U_{\nu}=0,

which has the explicit smooth and exponentially localized solution family

U_{\nu}(Z)=\dfrac{\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}\left[\sqrt{1-\nu}\text{cosh}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}Z\right)-i\sqrt{1+\nu}\text{sinh}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}Z\right)\right]}{\sqrt{2}\left[\text{cosh}\left(2\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}Z\right)-\nu\right]}

(13)

for
\nu\in(-1,1)

[
32
]
.

If
\mathbf{\Psi}
is a solution to (
12
), then
\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}:=\mathcal{U}\mathbf{\Psi},
for
\mathcal{U}\in\text{SU}(2),
is a solution to

\mathcal{U}\left(\sigma_{1}+i\sigma_{3}\partial_{Z}+\nu\sigma_{0}\right)\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}=2g|\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}|^{2}\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}.

Letting
\mathcal{U}=\left(\sigma_{0}+i\sigma_{1}\right)/\sqrt{2},
we obtain

\left(\mathcal{L}_{SD}+\nu\right)\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}=2g|\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}|^{2}\mathbf{\tilde{\Psi}}.

Using (
13
), we get the solution to the semi-Dirac equation

\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)=\dfrac{1+i}{2}\left[\text{Re}\left(U_{\nu}\right)+\text{Im}\left(U_{\nu}\right),\text{Re}\left(U_{\nu}\right)-\text{Im}\left(U_{\nu}\right)\right]^{\top}.

(14)

Taking limits gives the result.

Remark
: The line-solitons above are infinite energy states in the bandgap since they are de-localized in the
H
direction. A formal proof for the existence or non-existence of fully
localized solutions in 2D
to the semi-Dirac equation, (

[

do not apply for the nonlinearity in (
9
). It was shown in
[

that the 2D

, with the linear terms in (

[

, to solve the compactness issue of a critical Sobolev embedding in

, which is not useful for the semi-Dirac equation due to the anisotropic appearance of spatial derivatives in
\mathcal{L}_{SD}.

From the 1D stationary solution (
14
) in Theorem (

) it can be shown that

\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)=\dfrac{\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}}{4}(1+i)\text{sech}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}Z\right)\left[1,1\right]^{\top}\text{ as }\nu\to-1.

Indeed, near the edges of the essential spectrum, the NLS equation is the leading-order approximation to the semi-Dirac equation. Heuristically, near
\nu=-1
, if we ignore the other branch of the spectrum in the semi-Dirac equation, after a simple re-scaling we obtain the following
focusing
NLS equation

\left(\mathcal{L}_{SD}+\nu-\left|\mathbf{\Psi}\right|^{2}\right)\mathbf{\Psi}\approx\left(1+\nu-\Delta-\left|\mathbf{\Psi}\right|^{2}\right)\mathbf{\Psi}.

Making the same approximation near
\nu=1
gives the

NLS equation which is known to not admit localized solutions
[
14
]
. This makes it clear that both branches of the essential spectrum shape the gap solitons, particularly for
\nu
away from
-1

Looking for localized solutions in both
Z
and
H

\begin{pmatrix}U(Z,H;\nu)\\
V(Z,H;\nu)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{1}(Z;\nu)G_{1}(H;\nu)\\
\Phi_{2}(Z;\nu)G_{2}(H;\nu)\end{pmatrix},

(15)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)
is the localized 1D solution from Theorem
1

Plugging into the stationary form of (
9
) gives

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mathbf{\Phi}):=\dfrac{2g\left(|\Phi_{1}|^{2}+|\Phi_{2}|^{2}\right)\Phi_{1}}{\Phi_{2}}=\dfrac{G_{1}^{\prime\prime}}{\left(1-|G_{1}|^{2}\right)G_{1}}

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathbf{\Phi}):=\dfrac{2g\left(|\Phi_{1}|^{2}+|\Phi_{2}|^{2}\right)\Phi_{2}}{\Phi_{1}}=\dfrac{G_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{\left(1-|G_{2}|^{2}\right)G_{2}}.

We note that
F_{1}
and

are real-valued functions. Using the expression for the gap soliton in item
2.
of Theorem

at the middle of the gap (

F_{1}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)\right)=1-\tanh(2Z)\quad\text{ and }\quad F_{2}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)\right)=1+\tanh(2Z).

We then have the limiting behaviors as
Z\to\pm\infty

\displaystyle F_{1,-}:=\lim_{Z\to-\infty}F_{1}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)\right)=2\implies-2G_{1,-}+G^{\prime\prime}_{1,-}+2|G_{1,-}|^{2}G_{1,-}=0

\displaystyle F_{2,-}:=\lim_{Z\to-\infty}F_{2}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)\right)=0\implies G^{\prime\prime}_{2,-}=0

and

\displaystyle F_{1,+}:=\lim_{Z\to+\infty}F_{1}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)\right)=0\implies G^{\prime\prime}_{1,+}=0

\displaystyle F_{2,+}:=\lim_{Z\to+\infty}F_{2}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;0)\right)=2\implies-2G_{2,+}+G^{\prime\prime}_{2,+}+2|G_{2,+}|^{2}G_{2,+}=0,

where the

It is clear that

|F_{j,\pm}-F_{j}|=\text{O}\left(\exp(\mp 4Z)\right)
for

The nontrivial limiting equation for

Thus, although we do not have a global localized solution to the stationary form of (

given, respectively, by

\mathbf{\Psi}_{-}:=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{1}(Z;0)G_{1,-}(H;0)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{\Psi}_{+}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\Phi_{2}(Z;0)G_{2,+}(H;0)\end{pmatrix},

(16)

where

G_{1,-}(H;0)=G_{2,+}(H;0)=\sqrt{2}\text{sech}\left(\sqrt{2}H\right).

We note that the separation in (

F_{1,2}\left(\mathbf{\Phi}(Z;\nu)\right)\to 0
as

In what follows, we numerically compute mid-gap discrete breathers on the lattice near the continuum limit at which the effective semi-Dirac equation approximation is valid. We find that the spatial tails of these breathers are accurately captured by these exact separable profiles for sufficiently large

3
Anti-continuum regime and discrete breathers

3.1
Existence of discrete breathers

We rewrite equation (
1
) in the form

F(X^{\lambda}(t);\lambda):=\begin{pmatrix}\ddot{x}_{n,m}^{A}+\partial_{z_{1}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})-\lambda\left[x_{n,m-1}^{B}+x_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]\\
\ddot{x}_{n,m}^{B}+\partial_{z_{2}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})-\lambda\left[x_{n,m+1}^{A}+x_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right]\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(17)

We restrict the domain of the mapping
F(X^{\lambda};\lambda)
to square-summable sequences, that is
X^{\lambda}(t)\in\ell^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\cong\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
for all
t\geq 0
. Additionally, we restrict the domain so that
X^{\lambda}(t)
is
T_{b}
–periodic and even in time. Finally, we restrict the domain so that
X^{\lambda}(t)
has the required regularity, i.e. each of its elements is in the Sobolev space
\text{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}).
We denote the resulting domain of
F(X^{\lambda};\lambda)
by
\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}.

We then seek a nontrivial solution to (
17
) in the simplified
anti-continuum limit
(
\lambda=0
),
X^{0}\in\mathcal{D},
for instance, of the form

X_{*}(t):=X^{0}(t)=\left\{\cdots,0,0,\begin{pmatrix}x_{*}^{A}(t)\\
x_{*}^{B}(t)\end{pmatrix},0,0,\cdots\right\},

(18)

with fixed frequency
\omega_{b}=2\pi/T_{b}.

Using the implicit function theorem
[
29
,
18
]
, it can be shown that
X^{0}
smoothly continues in
0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{b}
, for some nonzero value
\lambda_{b}
, to
X^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}
if two conditions are satisfied: 1.) (non-resonance) for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
we have that
\left(n\omega_{b}\right)^{2}\neq\Omega_{\pm}^{2}:=\omega_{0}^{2}\pm 1;
and 2.) (non-degeneracy) the null-space of the operator

L_{*}:=\sigma_{0}\dfrac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+\mathcal{H}_{V_{*}},

where
\mathcal{H}_{V_{*}}
is the Hessian matrix of the potential
V(z_{1},z_{2})
evaluated at
\left(x_{*}^{A},x_{*}^{B}\right)^{\top}
, is empty in
\mathcal{D}.

In the anti-continuum limit (
18
), there is an
in-phase
and
out-of-phase
one-parameter family of explicit solutions in the respective cases
g=\pm 1
given by

\begin{pmatrix}x_{*}^{A}(t)\\
x_{*}^{B}(t)\end{pmatrix}^{\pm}=a_{*}\text{cn}\left(\sqrt{(\Omega_{\mp}^{2}\pm 2a_{*}^{2})}t,\dfrac{\pm a_{*}^{2}}{(\Omega_{\mp}^{2}\pm 2a_{*}^{2})}\right)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\pm 1\end{pmatrix},

(19)

where
cn
is the even Jacobi elliptic function.

The period of (
19
) is

T_{b}^{\pm}(a_{*}^{2})=\dfrac{4}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mp}^{2}\pm 2a_{*}^{2}}}\mathcal{K}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{\pm a_{*}^{2}}{(\Omega_{\mp}^{2}\pm 2a_{*}^{2})}}\right),

(20)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. Note that periodic solutions exist in the anti-continuum limit, (
19
), with any frequency
\omega_{b}>\Omega_{-}
in the hardening case and
0<\omega_{b}<\Omega_{+}
in the softening case. As the solution amplitude,
a_{*}
, approaches zero, the time-dependent term in (
19
) approaches
a_{*}\cos(\Omega_{\mp}t)
and
T_{b}^{\pm}
in (
20
) approaches
2\pi/\Omega_{\mp}.
Both the non-resonance and non-degeneracy conditions fail in this limit. For almost every possible nonzero
a_{*}
, both conditions are satisfied and imply that discrete breathers with almost any
\omega_{b}
in the ranges above exist on the globally coupled lattice in (
1
), at least up to some coupling strength
\lambda_{b}>0
.

We note that the continued breathers are
exponentially localized
on the lattice. This is true for the nearest-neighbor couplings considered here, but also more generally for an infinite number of couplings so long as at each site there exists a positive number
R
such that the coupling strengths decay exponentially outside a circle of radius
R
centered on that site
[
18
]
.

3.2
Numerical continuation of discrete breathers

Discrete breathers to (
17
) (
\lambda\neq 0
) can be found using a numerical continuation scheme, seeded from the explicit anti-continuum states in (
19
). Since breathers are
T_{b}
-periodic, we represent system (
17
) in the Fourier domain using the discrete Fourier transform,
\mathcal{F},
in time and its inverse,
\mathcal{F}^{-1},
given by

\hat{x}[p]:=\mathcal{F}\{x\}[p]=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x[j]e^{-2\pi ijp/N},\quad x[j]=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\hat{x}\}[j]=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{p=0}^{N-1}\hat{x}[p]e^{2\pi ijp/N},

where
N
is the number of equally-spaced sampled points of
x(t)
in the interval
[0,T_{b}].
Furthermore, since we seek even-in-time solutions, only cosine terms are needed and the system size is reduced by half.

The left-hand side of equation (
17
) then becomes

\mathcal{F}\left\{F(X^{\lambda};\lambda)\right\}=\begin{pmatrix}-p^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{A}+\mathcal{F}\left\{\partial_{z_{1}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})\right\}-\lambda\left[\hat{x}_{n,m-1}^{B}+\hat{x}_{n+1,m-1}^{B}\right]\\
-p^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{B}+\mathcal{F}\left\{\partial_{z_{2}}V(x_{n,m}^{A},x_{n,m}^{B})\right\}-\lambda\left[\hat{x}_{n,m+1}^{A}+\hat{x}_{n-1,m+1}^{A}\right]\end{pmatrix}

(21)

for
p=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

In the Fourier domain and for
\lambda=0
, the linearized mapping of (
17
) about
X_{*}
is represented by the block diagonal matrix

\mathcal{J}(X_{*};0):=\begin{bmatrix}\ddots&&&\\
&D_{\omega_{b}}&\text{-}I&\\
&\text{-}I&D_{\omega_{b}}&\\
&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\ddots&&&&&\\
&0&&&&\\
&&J_{11}^{*}&J_{12}^{*}&&\\
&&J_{21}^{*}&J_{22}^{*}&&\\
&&&&0&\\
&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

Above,
I
is the
N\times N
identity matrix,
D_{\omega_{b}}
is the
N\times N
diagonal matrix,

D_{\omega_{b}}:=\text{diag}\left(\omega_{0}^{2}-\left(0\omega_{b}\right)^{2},\omega_{0}^{2}-\left(1\omega_{b}\right)^{2},\cdots,\omega_{0}^{2}-\left((N-1)\omega_{b}\right)^{2}\right),

and
J_{\alpha\beta}^{*}
(
\alpha,\beta=1,2
) are
N\times N

circulent matrices
generated from the vectors

\mathbf{v}_{\alpha\beta}^{*}=N^{-1}\mathcal{F}\left\{\partial_{z_{\alpha}}\partial_{z_{\beta}}V(x_{*}^{A},x_{*}^{B})-\partial_{z_{\alpha}}\partial_{z_{\beta}}V(0,0)\right\}[p],\quad p=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

The circulent matrices arise due to convolution in the Fourier domain. Note that when the non-resonance condition is violated, an infinite number of rows in (
22
) are scalar multiples of one another, and
\mathcal{J}
is singular.

For a
finite
honeycomb lattice consisting of
M
unit-cells, the matrix
\mathcal{J}
is of size
2MN\times 2MN.
See Figure
3
(a) for a schematic of the global hexagonal lattice used. When
\lambda\neq 0,

\mathcal{J}(X_{*};0)
becomes the symmetric block matrix
\mathcal{J}(X_{*},\lambda)
and, for nearest-neighbor couplings, each row in the first matrix has

additional nonzero blocks,
\lambda I,
with the exception of those rows corresponding to cells on the boundary of the lattice where we enforce the Dirichlet condition. We now implement a Newton-Raphson scheme in the temporal Fourier domain to solve (
17
) for some
\lambda\neq 0
with the initial guess

where at each Newton iteration we use the generalized minimal residual method (GMRES) to solve the resulting system within a set tolerance. We note that since the seeded solutions in (

3.3
Dynamical stability of discrete breathers

The spectral stability of discrete breathers is determined by the linearized system about
X^{\lambda}(t).
This entails solving a system of
4M
coupled
T_{b}
-periodic linear differential equations.

From Floquet theory

Since (

4
Nonlinear plane waves and their stability

To further elucidate breather properties in the bandgap, we derive exact
de-localized
nonlinear plane wave solutions to (
1
) and analyze their spectral stability. Specifically, we are interested in nonlinear plane waves bifurcating from the trivial solution at the band-edges of the phonon spectrum and into the gap as shown in Figure
2
(here we do not assume that the gap is necessarily small as in section
2
). We first analyze the stability of these states by using perturbation theory in the weakly nonlinear regime and then numerically compute their Floquet spectra in both the perturbative and non-perturbative regimes.

We find that for a hardening (softening) nonlinearity in the infinite lattice, the in-phase (out-of-phase) nonlinear plane wave bifurcating from the top (bottom) edge of the acoustic (optical) band into the gap becomes dynamically
unstable
to perturbative waves with wavevectors concentrated around
\mathbf{M}
. It has been hypothesized in
[
22
,
16
]
that the instability of band-edge plane waves in this regime gives rise to discrete breathers with arbitrarily small nonzero amplitudes, provided they exist. The energy thresholds for the formation of discrete breathers depend on both the dimension of the lattice and the highest exponent in the power-law potential function
[
40
]
. Numerical studies suggest that both the localization strength and amplitude of these breathers approach zero as their frequency approaches the edge of the band
[
18
]
. We expect that the continuum theory accurately describes breather dynamics in this regime (see section
2
).

However, not all breathers behave this way near the phonon spectrum. A generic subclass of breathers is known to maintain nonzero amplitudes even when their frequency approaches a band-edge. It has been hypothesized that this phenomenon is directly related to the lack of a bifurcating band-edge plane wave or, if one exists, its dynamic stability
[
22
]
. For the lattice model considered here, this argument suggests that small-amplitude discrete breathers do not exist near one edge of the gap. Indeed, this is consistent with the asymmetric limiting behaviors of gap solitons stated in Theorem
1
near the two band-edges.

4.1
Nonlinear plane waves

In this section, we first consider a lattice with a finite and even number of unit cells,
n,m=0,1,\cdots S-1
, like the one shown in Figure
3
(a) and with periodic boundary conditions. We then consider the lattice in the thermodynamic limit as
S\to\infty.

We define the discrete Fourier transform on the lattice

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\\
\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{1}{S^{2}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{S-1}\begin{pmatrix}x_{n,m}^{A}\\
x_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}e^{i\left(n\mathbf{a}_{1}+m\mathbf{a}_{2}\right)\cdot\mathbf{k}}

(23)

for discrete
\mathbf{k}=S^{-1}\left(l\mathbf{b_{1}}+p\mathbf{b}_{2}\right)
modulo
\mathcal{B},
where
\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2}
are the reciprocal vectors of the honeycomb lattice and

are integers in
\{-S/2+1,\cdots,S/2\}.
We denote this equally spaced partition of the Brillouin zone by the
S^{2}

The inverse transform is then given by

\begin{pmatrix}x_{n,m}^{A}\\
x_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\\
\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}\end{pmatrix}e^{-i\left(n\mathbf{a}_{1}+m\mathbf{a}_{2}\right)\cdot\mathbf{k}}.

(24)

Substituting (

1

\displaystyle\ddot{\hat{x}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}=\lambda e^{i\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}\left(1+e^{-i\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}

(25)

\displaystyle\text{-}\dfrac{1}{S^{2}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{S-1}\partial_{z_{1}}V\left(\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}\hat{x}^{A}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}e^{-i(\mathbf{a}_{1}n+\mathbf{a}_{2}m)\cdot{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}},\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}\hat{x}^{B}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}}e^{-i(\mathbf{a}_{1}n+\mathbf{a}_{2}m)\cdot{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}}\right)e^{i(\mathbf{a}_{1}n+\mathbf{a}_{2}m)\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle\ddot{\hat{x}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}=\lambda e^{-i\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}\left(1+e^{i\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}

\displaystyle\text{-}\dfrac{1}{S^{2}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{S-1}\partial_{z_{2}}V\left(\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}\hat{x}^{A}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}e^{-i(\mathbf{a}_{1}n+\mathbf{a}_{2}m)\cdot{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}},\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}\hat{x}^{B}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}}e^{-i(\mathbf{a}_{1}n+\mathbf{a}_{2}m)\cdot{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime}}\right)e^{i(\mathbf{a}_{1}n+\mathbf{a}_{2}m)\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}.

This equation is greatly simplified at the edges of the phonon spectrum
(
3
) at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{\Gamma}
and
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{M}
. Here, we are interested in the latter case and take
\hat{x}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A,B}=\hat{x}_{M}^{A,B}\delta_{\mathbf{M}},
where
\delta_{\mathbf{M}}
is the delta function centered on the wavevector
\mathbf{M}.

For the potential function in (
2
), equation (
25
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\ddot{\hat{x}}_{M}^{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}\hat{x}_{M}^{A}+\left(1-2\lambda\right)\hat{x}_{M}^{B}-g\left[\left(\hat{x}_{M}^{A}\right)^{2}+\left(\hat{x}_{M}^{B}\right)^{2}\right]\hat{x}_{M}^{A}

(26)

\displaystyle\ddot{\hat{x}}_{M}^{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}\hat{x}_{M}^{B}+\left(1-2\lambda\right)\hat{x}_{M}^{A}-g\left[\left(\hat{x}_{M}^{A}\right)^{2}+\left(\hat{x}_{M}^{B}\right)^{2}\right]\hat{x}_{M}^{B}.

We remark that the linear part of system (
26
) decouples at the semi-Dirac point when
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
and has a degenerate eigenvalue of
\omega_{0}^{2}.
Like the anti-continuum problem in section
3
, for
g=\pm 1
, there are in-phase and out-of-phase even-in-time periodic solutions for initial data
\hat{x}_{M}^{A}(0)=\pm\hat{x}_{M}^{B}(0)=a
and
\dot{\hat{x}}_{M}^{A}(0)=\dot{\hat{x}}_{M}^{B}(0)=0
, respectively.

For
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{*}),
the phonon spectrum has a bandgap of width
2(1-2\lambda)
(see Figure
2
). When
g=1,
the in-phase nonlinear plane-wave bifurcates from the zero solution at the top edge of the acoustic band where
\omega_{-}^{2}(\lambda):=\omega_{-}^{2}(\mathbf{M};\lambda)=\omega_{0}^{2}-1+2\lambda
into the gap. Similarly, when
g=-1,
the out-of-phase nonlinear plane-wave bifurcates from the zero solution at the bottom edge of the optical band where
\omega_{+}^{2}(\lambda):=\omega_{+}^{2}(\mathbf{M};\lambda)=\omega_{0}^{2}+1-2\lambda
into the gap.

These nonlinear plane-waves are given explicitly by

\begin{pmatrix}x_{n,m}^{A}\\
x_{n,m}^{B}\end{pmatrix}^{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}_{M}^{A}\\
\hat{x}_{M}^{B}\end{pmatrix}^{\pm}e^{-i\pi m}=P_{\pm}(t;\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\pm 1\end{pmatrix}e^{-i\pi m},

(27)

where
P_{\pm}(t;\lambda)
is the cnoidal function in (
19
) with
\Omega_{\pm}
replaced by
\omega_{\pm}
. We note that when
\lambda=0
,
\omega_{\pm}=\Omega_{\pm}
and
P_{\pm}
is identical to the anti-continuum limit solution in section
3

27
) is nonzero over the entire lattice.

To investigate the dynamic stability of the nonlinear plane waves in (
27
), we linearize system (
25
) about their orbit. Due to the fact that the potential (
2
) is even symmetric, we obtain

\begin{pmatrix}\ddot{y}_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\\
\ddot{y}_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\left[-\mathcal{H}_{V}[\hat{X}^{\pm}(t)]+\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{2}}(1+e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{1}})\\
\lambda e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{2}}(1+e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{1}})&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\begin{pmatrix}y_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\\
y_{\mathbf{k}}^{B}\end{pmatrix},

(28)

where
\mathcal{H}_{V}[\hat{X}^{\pm}(t)]
is the Hessian of
V
evaluated at
\hat{X}^{\pm}:=\left[\hat{x}_{M}^{A},\hat{x}_{M}^{B}\right]^{\pm}
in (

second-order differential equations with periodic coefficients
[
27
]
.

4.2
Perturbative stability analysis of band edge plane-waves

To study the stability of the nonlinear plane-waves near the edges of the bandgap, we first derive the leading-order expression to approximate
P_{\pm}(t;\lambda)
for small amplitude waves,
|a|\ll 1
, by using the Poincaré-Lindstedt method
[
38
]
. The result is

P_{\pm}(t;\lambda)=a\cos\left[\left(\omega_{\mp}\pm\dfrac{3a^{2}}{4\omega_{\mp}}+O\left(a^{4}\right)\right)t\right]+O\left(a^{3}\right).

(29)

In Floquet theory
[
27
]
, it is well known that a transition from linear stability to instability of the periodic solution in (
27
) occurs when it is perturbed by a periodic orbit of the same period. At this point a
tangent bifurcation
occurs when two Floquet multipliers collide at
+1
and leave the unit circle (i.e., see Figure
4
). Following the arguments in
[
16
,
22
]
, we locate this transition to leading order in
a
by using the Poincaré-Lindstedt method again on the linearized system (
28
) about (
29
). After obtaining its frequency expansion,
\omega(a)
, we equate it with the frequency expansion in (
20
) and solve for the critical value,
a_{c}.
We are interested in values of
a_{c}
in the thermodynamic limit.

Introducing the perturbation expansions in powers of
a^{2}
:

y_{\mathbf{k}}^{A,B}=y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{A,B}+y_{\mathbf{k},(1)}^{A,B}a^{2}+\cdots\quad\text{ and }\quad\omega^{2}(a)=\omega_{(0)}^{2}+\omega_{(1)}^{2}a^{2}+\cdots,

we define the scaled time variable
\tau=\omega(a)t.
Plugging the expansions into (
28
) gives at zeroth-order:

L_{0}\mathbf{Y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{(0)}^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{2}&-1-\lambda e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{2}}(1+e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{1}})\\
-1-\lambda e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{2}}(1+e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{1}})&\omega_{(0)}^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{A}\\
y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=0.

The general solution to the zeroth-order equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{A}\\
y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\left[\alpha\exp\left(\dfrac{i\omega_{-}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\tau}{\omega_{(0)}}\right)+\beta\exp\left(-\dfrac{i\omega_{-}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\tau}{\omega_{(0)}}\right)\right]\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\\
v_{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\left[\gamma\exp\left(\dfrac{i\omega_{+}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\tau}{\omega_{(0)}}\right)+\zeta\exp\left(-\dfrac{i\omega_{+}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\tau}{\omega_{(0)}}\right)\right]\begin{pmatrix}-v_{1}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\\
v_{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\end{pmatrix},

where
\omega^{2}_{\mp}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)
are the acoustic/optical bands in (
3
),
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\zeta
are complex constants, and
\left[\pm v_{1}(\mathbf{k};\lambda),v_{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\right]^{\top}
are the corresponding eigenvectors. The components
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are nonvanishing for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}
and
v_{1}=v_{2}=1
at both
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{\Gamma}
for all
0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{*}
.

At first-order, we obtain

L_{0}\begin{pmatrix}y_{\mathbf{k},(1)}^{A}\\
y_{\mathbf{k},(1)}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{(1)}^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}+4g\cos^{2}(\tau)&2\cos^{2}(\tau)\\
2\cos^{2}(\tau)&\omega_{(1)}^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}+4g\cos^{2}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{A}\\
y_{\mathbf{k},(0)}^{B}\end{pmatrix}.

The secular terms in the first-order equation can be removed only when the components
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are equal, which occurs only at the edges of the bands. Choosing
\omega_{(0)}^{2}=\omega_{\mp}^{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)
and
\omega_{(1)}^{2}=\pm 9/2
for
g=\pm 1,

respectively, removes the secular terms and gives a leading-order solution that is bounded for all time. The Floquet multiplier corresponding to these periodic orbits remains fixed at
+1
and does not lead to a tangent bifurcation
[
16
]
.

However, in the thermodynamic limit, equating the leading order frequency expansion,
\omega^{2}(a)
, with the nonlinear plane wave in (
29
), gives

\displaystyle a_{c,M}^{2}:=\dfrac{1}{3}\left(\omega^{2}_{\mp}(\lambda)-\omega_{\mp}^{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\right)\to 0\quad\text{ as }\mathbf{k}\to\mathbf{M},

(30)

\displaystyle a_{c,\Gamma}^{2}:=\dfrac{1}{3}\left(\omega^{2}_{\mp}(\lambda)-\omega_{\mp}^{2}(\mathbf{k};\lambda)\right)\to\dfrac{4\lambda}{3}\quad\text{ as }\mathbf{k}\to\mathbf{\Gamma}.

This is due to the fact that the phonon bands and the eigenvectors are continuous functions of the wavevector
\mathbf{k},
which densely fills the Brillouin zone as
S\to\infty.

The first limit in (
30
) is valid for all
0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{*}
. This suggests that (
20
) undergoes a first transition to instability near the wavevector
\mathbf{M}
as it bifurcates from the zero solution and into the bandgap. The second limit in (
30
) is valid as long as
\lambda
is small enough to safely neglect higher-order terms in the perturbation expansion. In this case, the nonlinear plane wave undergoes another tangent bifurcation near
\mathbf{\Gamma}
at the nonzero amplitude
2\sqrt{\lambda/3}.
We find excellent agreement between these perturbative results and the numerics presented below. We remark that when
\lambda=0
the unit cells of the lattice are uncoupled and the phonon bands are perfectly flat over the entire Brillouin zone. In this case, (
27
) is dynamically stable and nearly all of the system’s Floquet multipliers are degenerate and located exactly at
+1
.

5
Concluding remarks

We have conducted a thorough analysis of discrete breathers on a honeycomb lattice near a unique point in the dispersion relation known as semi-Dirac crossing. Our analysis connects breather dynamics in the highly localized and strongly nonlinear regime with breather dynamics in the long-wave and weakly nonlinear regime. Such an approach gives a complete description of hybrid localized structures with peaked discrete behavior at the breather’s central core and spatially extended tails described by exact separable solutions to an asymptotic effective PDE theory. Even though an existence proof for global localized solutions to the 2D semi-Dirac equation is lacking, we do not require them here to accurately describe a large family of discrete breathers on the lattice.

Finally, we studied the Floquet stability of breathers and of extended nonlinear plane waves bifurcating from the edges of the gap to provide insight into how breathers behave near the gap’s edges. Our work provides a framework for describing coherent structures in more complicated lattice models of current interest in condensed matter systems and metamaterial design, including 2D layered and twisted lattices
[
4
,
9
]
.
