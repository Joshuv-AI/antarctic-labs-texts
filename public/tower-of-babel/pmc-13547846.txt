Environmental Release of Genetically Intervened Microorganisms: Towards a New Narrative
Authors: Victor de Lorenzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe deliberate release of genetically engineered microorganisms for environmental applications has remained largely blocked since the early days of recombinant DNA technology, when limited ecological knowledge, lack of success stories and public apprehension shaped a culture of caution and restrictive regulation. Despite profound advances in microbial ecology, synthetic biology and genetic design, current frameworks still rely on outdated assumptions and legacy regulations that equate engineered microbes with inherent danger and demand unrealistic forms of absolute containment. This review examines how laboratory‐trained microorganisms exist on a continuum with naturally evolved life, and that their risks are neither categorically different nor greater. Rather than pursuing unachievable containment, governance should shift towards traceability, stewardship and long‐term monitoring through genomic barcodes, digital twins and transparent oversight. The vision moves from domination and control to care and partnership recognizing engineered microbes as live amendments capable of restoring degraded ecosystems. Achieving this transformation requires new terminology, phased field‐trial frameworks, improved scaling methods, and the integration of epistemological perspectives that emphasize reciprocity and coexistence with nature. Reframing biotechnology in this way could finally unlock the capacity of engineered microorganisms to contribute responsibly and effectively to planetary repair in an era of escalating environmental crises.

Introduction

Interest in using recombinant DNA technologies to address major environmental challenges has been visible almost since their inception, not only for their role in enabling cleaner production systems but also for their promise in direct environmental interventions. Scientists of the time envisioned the possibility of releasing laboratory‐programmed biological agents capable of performing complex ecological functions—such as nitrogen fixation, phosphorus mobilization or in situ biodegradation of persistent pollutants (Lindow et al. 1989). Yet, also since the very beginnings of this field, the issue has been surrounded by intense controversy. During the early years of genetic engineering, a combination of factors—most notably the lack of clear success stories, very limited knowledge of microbial ecology, little economic incentives, and public apprehension fueled by poor communication—created an atmosphere of caution and mistrust (Kocher et al. 2006). To this day, there remains a shortage of reliable data to predict the ecological consequences—positive or negative—of such interventions (George et al. 2025). Available studies repeatedly show that genetically modified strains have never managed to dominate or displace native microbial communities nor have they exhibited any explosive propagation comparable to what could be termed a Black Swan event (de Lorenzo 2009).

Bioremediation With Lab‐Trained Microorganisms: Then and Now

Most European countries have consistently made it extremely difficult—if not outright impossible—not only to release genetically improved microorganisms designed for environmental benefit, but even to conduct small‐scale field trials (Sundaram et al. 2023). This regulatory stagnation (Valverde and de Lorenzo 2026) has also discouraged investment (Sundaram et al. 2023; Wesseler et al. 2023): venture capital and industry alike tend to reject any initiative that involves the deliberate, uncontained use of microorganisms, regardless of potential benefits (Rodríguez‐Martínez et al. 2022). Since no data can be generated without trials, the situation has become self‐perpetuating: the lack of evidence prevents experimentation, while the lack of experimentation prevents evidence (de Lorenzo 2026). Funding for these studies is minimal, and legislative frameworks remain fossilized in the regulatory paradigms established at the dawn of genetic engineering. Consequently, across much of Europe, the possibility of deploying laboratory‐engineered microorganisms as environmental or industrial agents has been largely abandoned. Not a single field release of GM bacteria for bioremediation has been approved in Europe since the late 1990s. And commercialization of any bioremediation technology involving GMOs is simply non‐existent, surely due (among others) to the very complicated regulatory roadmap of the EU to that end (see below).

Meanwhile, the world has witnessed profound transformations. The climate crisis has intensified, and new forms of environmental degradation have emerged, including the loss of arable land through war, desertification, and fire, as well as the relentless rise in greenhouse gas concentrations and degradation of marine ecosystems with plastics (Richardson et al. 2023). Paradoxically, during this same period, our understanding of microbial ecology has advanced dramatically—revealing the complex networks of interaction that sustain ecosystem stability. Meanwhile, synthetic biology has matured into a discipline that can programme biological systems—including environmental microorganisms—with the precision and reliability of an engineer designing complex artefacts. Today's genetic tools enable the design of bacterial systems (Martínez‐García and de Lorenzo 2017; Martínez‐García et al. 2020; de Lorenzo and Martínez‐García 2025) with unprecedented precision, control and built‐in safety mechanisms that hold a great promise as bioremediation agents (Figure 1). However, regulatory and political attitudes have not evolved accordingly. Humanity is facing interconnected global environmental crises—including climate change, freshwater scarcity, pollution, land and soil degradation, biodiversity loss, and ecosystem disruption driven by deforestation, wildfires, and conflicts—which jointly threaten ecological stability and human well‐being (Pörtner et al. 2022). Added to these are persistent challenges such as contamination by plastics, microplastics and other chemical pollutants. These converging pressures have brought the topic back to the forefront. It is increasingly clear that genetics and synthetic biology offer unique opportunities to tackle environmental problems that cannot be solved by conventional means. Examples include biodegradation of plastics in soil and marine scenarios, elimination of poly‐fluorinated chemicals and capture of CO2 and other greenhouse gases (de Lorenzo 2017; de Lorenzo et al. 2018).

Evolution of genetic technologies alongside the environmental crisis. This figure illustrates the parallel trajectories of two major developments of the last few decades: (i) the rapid expansion of our understanding of microbial ecosystems and Earth‐scale processes, together with increasingly sophisticated genetic tools to programme microorganisms and other biological systems; and (ii) the intensification of global environmental crises, including climate change, widespread pollution, and, more recently, contamination from armed conflicts that release residues released from explosives into soils, limiting their safe agricultural reuse. Despite their clear complementarity and potential for convergence, these trajectories have remained largely disconnected for decades, primarily due to persistent concerns and negative perceptions surrounding the environmental release of genetically modified microorganisms.

In this renewed context (Brewer and George 2025; Brewer et al. 2026) it is hardly surprising that the scientific community has once again turned to the microbial gene pool as a reservoir of activities, tools and materials for tackling the persistent environmental challenges (Rylott and Bruce 2020; Rodríguez‐Martínez et al. 2022; Hassard et al. 2024; Jones et al. 2024; Rafique et al. 2024; Chemla et al. 2025; Lea‐Smith et al. 2025). It is important to note that addressing such challenges is not just about adopting substitutive technologies that allow business‐as‐usual with less, acceptable damage. The issue is complex and involves predominantly political and policy decisions, but bioengineering can become a phenomenal instrument to solve parts of the problem. What was a distant aspiration in the late 1980s, has now become a technically feasible—and increasingly urgent—agenda for planetary repair. However, realizing this potential requires addressing the longstanding challenges of deliberately releasing laboratory‐nurtured microorganisms. Key hurdles include technical feasibility and scale‐up (de Lorenzo 2022), regulatory frameworks, efficacy metrics and long‐term consequences (Chemla et al. 2025). The debate over deliberate release recombinant microorganisms into the environment has in fact returned, spurred to a large extent by their potential to rehabilitate soil and water degraded by human action—from small afflicted sites to planetary‐scale terraforming visions (de Lorenzo et al. 2016; Conde‐Pueyo et al. 2020). But where should we take the conversation from?

Arsonist Firefighters?

We can begin by recalling the early days of recombinant DNA technology, when both the fundamental concepts and much of the terminology were established. At its core, this innovation involved inserting a fragment of genetic material from one biological system into a vector derived from another, producing what became known as recombinant sequences (Cohen et al. 1992). From this foundational experiment grew the entire conceptual and technical framework of modern genetic engineering—an enterprise driven by the ambition to rationalize, control and programme the genetic content of living systems. The very use of the term engineering reflected a profound shift in mindset: it implied mastery over biological matter and the capacity to redirect natural processes according to human design. This notion captured the public imagination, generating both fascination and fear. Even before these techniques were fully developed, concern about their potential dangers had already been flagged. As an archetypal example, the same day in 1969 that Jon Beckwith published a paper in Nature reporting the physical isolation of a gene, he called a press conference to alert the public of all the dangers involved in such research (Beckwith 2002). This is a classic example of the arsonist‐firefighter phenomenon. It occurs when the same persons who ignite a problem later position themselves as the only ones capable of solving it, thereby gaining attention for the corresponding research topic. If this is so, one wonders from the outset why this work was started at all! We have seen and will surely see many more examples of this in the coming years. While the scientists themselves warned of these dangers, the ensuing alarm and controversy also gave the field unprecedented visibility.

The After Effects of Asilomar 1975

The echoes of both scientific ambition and ethical anxiety culminated in the famous Asilomar Conference of 1975, where the pioneers of recombinant DNA research gathered to establish guidelines for safety and self‐regulation (Watson et al. 1982). At that time, the emphasis was on the absolute distinction between recombinant and natural organisms and containment of modified strains and/or DNA thereof. This differentiation seemed justified given how little was known about horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and the handling of the resulting laboratory‐generated biologics; this was unknown territory. Today, however, we understand that certainty of full containment is not feasible (see below) and that the exchange of genetic material happens not only across microorganisms but also among distant biological actors (Soucy et al. 2015). From this perspective, what was once viewed as a wholly artificial technology can now be seen as a re‐enactment—or even a controlled extension—of a phenomenon that has always existed in nature: the movement of DNA between living, often apart, live hosts.

One of the unintended consequences of the Asilomar meeting was, ironically, the heightened emphasis it placed on the potential dangers of genetic engineering. From that concern emerged the notion that it was necessary to design what later became known as containment systems—mechanisms intended to prevent the spread of recombinant bacteria or the unintended transmission of engineered genetic material (Ramos et al. 1995; Pei et al. 2022). The goal was to ensure that these modified organisms, or the DNA they carried, would remain confined to controlled environments. Yet, even the very terms containment and confinement carried problematic implications from the start: they suggested that the technology itself was inherently hazardous and that the products of biotechnology were entities to be feared rather than understood and managed.

Some have argued that the 1975 Asilomar event was reminiscent of a perfect crime story in which someone, in trying to save a pedestrian from being hit by a car, warns him so abruptly that the person panics, steps the wrong way, and ends up being run over—when, without that warning, he would have escaped unharmed (Danchin 2010). In other words, Asilomar could have been a warning that backfired (i.e., the intended helper caused greater harm) in which intervening can cause worse outcomes than not ringing the bells. The terminology that later emerged to describe these safety strategies did little to ease public concern—if anything, it amplified it. Expressions such as conditional suicide systems (Paul et al. 2005), kill switches (Chan et al. 2016), genetic firewalls, etc. (Ramos et al. 1995) reinforced the association of laboratory‐engineered microorganisms with danger and need of control as if they were hostile entities to be neutralized rather than instruments of good applications. This choice of language inadvertently framed biotechnology within a vocabulary of threat and defence, evoking imagery of enemies at the gates. By contrast, the terminology used in other areas of microbiology reveals the opposite effect. Consider, for instance, the word probiotic, coined to describe microorganisms intended to promote health and restore balance within a microbial community. The term itself conveys a constructive intention—to act for life. This comparison illustrates how profoundly language shapes public perception: it can either inspire confidence and curiosity or reinforce anxiety and resistance towards new technologies. To be fair, Asilomar 1975 had also an enabling role, as the conference helped establish a social licence for recombinant DNA research, provided a framework for continuing experimentation, and ultimately facilitated the development of modern biotechnology. But in any case, the foundational narrative that should perhaps have served as the prelude to the entire discussion—the one emphasizing the advantages and opportunities offered by these emerging technologies to address major social, industrial and environmental challenges—was replaced almost from the outset by an emphasis on their potential dangers. Although such risks undeniably exist, they were largely overstated from the perspective of public perception. This distortion was further reinforced by the adoption of engineering jargon to describe biological systems and by their presentation as manipulable objects, akin to any other human‐designed artefact.

It is therefore unsurprising that much of the public felt uneasy with this discourse—particularly when scientists themselves placed greater emphasis on potential hazards, leaving the discussion of opportunities and benefits to companies primarily driven by economic profit. Recent studies have highlighted the negative impact of applying the conceptual and semantic framework of engineering to describe rational interventions in the genomes of living organisms (Schyfter 2020). Adopting this engineering vocabulary and stressing from the beginning the aspects of control and domination of nature to describe the new possibilities opened by deliberate DNA editing can be seen in hindsight as a strategic error.

If scientists themselves appeared alarmed by what they were creating, how could society not share their concern? The situation was further exacerbated by the clumsy communication strategies and arrogance of some major biotechnology companies, which dismissed public scepticism towards recombinant DNA as a mere product of ignorance—rather than as an expression of legitimate concern about the misuse of scientific advances, particularly when financial gain is prioritized over safety and equitable access to the benefits of research (Marris 2001). The patent wars over genetic engineering advances, which have persisted ever since, have only reinforced this perception (Motta 2014).

As a result, broad sectors of society continue to harbour a general, and at times vehement, rejection of any large‐scale application involving microorganisms whose genomes have been deliberately edited, even if changes were minimal. This rejection has translated into a complex network of local and regional regulations that, in practice, almost completely eliminate the possibility of releasing laboratory‐trained and potentially beneficial bacteria into the environment (Figure 2). These regulations in Europe are highly granular (Valverde and de Lorenzo 2026) and display remarkable heterogeneity depending on the biological system in question, its intended field of application, and its intrinsic properties (European Commission SCENIHR 2015; Sundaram et al. 2023). Drawing on experience from the United States, it seems logical that the use of EMERs (Engineered Microbes for Environmental Release) should become easier when their deployment yields demonstrable, localized environmental and economic benefits. A prime example is the use of bacteria engineered to enhance nitrogen fixation as a substitute for chemical fertilizers (Martinez‐Feria et al. 2024), such as the commercial products developed by Pivot Bio (https://www.pivotbio.com).

The regulatory roadmap required in the European Union for the commercialization of recombinant microorganisms intended for environmental release. The process begins with a request for authorization to conduct field trials, and this authority rests exclusively with the national regulatory body of each member state. However, if the aim is to go further and develop the microorganisms or the process into a commercial product, the proposal follows a highly complex procedure. Each member state must participate in multiple scientific consultations, votes, and appeals. At the end of this considerably lengthy process, the European Commission grants or denies authorization for commercial use throughout the territory. Needless to say, this system is extremely discouraging, to the point that there have been virtually no applications of this type in the European Union in the last 30 years (redrawn from Spanish Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food). EU regulations that affect engineered microbes for bioremediation include: Water Framework Directive (WFD) Directive 2000/60/EC; Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006; Contained Use Directive (2009/41/EC); Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) Directive 2010/75/EU and Site Permits.

New Terminology, New Narratives

One of the steps towards overcoming the current impasse surrounding the governance of genetically altered microorganisms involves revising and rebranding the terminology we use to describe biological materials that have undergone laboratory handling, which is more scientifically sound and updated to our current level of knowledge. Not all of these entities are equally distant from their natural origin, yet our vocabulary often fails to capture these nuances. Note that such relabelling cannot be just a strategy to push an agenda, but a genuine calling for an accompanying change in practice, as language and practice are deeply connected. Under this perspective, microbial agents for bioremediation could be grouped under the general category of laboratory‐trained strains—a term that differentiates them from unmodified natural isolates while acknowledging the continuum that connects natural and engineered forms of life.

The choice of the term trained as opposed to the traditional modified or manipulated deserves some rationale. Modified is technically correct but conceptually misleading. Every organism is modified relative to its ancestors. Mutation, recombination, horizontal gene transfer, transposition, genome reduction and duplication continuously modify genomes naturally. Yet we have reserved modified organism almost exclusively for deliberate changes entered by humans. The terminology therefore already embeds the very nature/culture boundary that we are trying to overcome. Calling one bacterium modified and another natural does not simply describe their status: it describes the history and agency we associate with their production. In contrast, the term trained shifts the emphasis from the process to the phenotype. A trained microorganism is one whose capabilities have been deliberately developed towards a human‐defined objective. The means of training thus forms a continuum: selection, adaptive laboratory evolution, mutagenesis, genome editing, pathway introduction, regulatory rewiring or combinations of these. Trained then becomes the higher‐order category, while the method remains in a second plane. In this context, a Laboratory‐trained microorganism is one whose biological capabilities have been deliberately developed towards a defined function under specific conditions, whether through selection, evolution, genetic intervention or combinations thereof. Finally, note that trained is not an euphemism intended to make genetic engineering sound softer or more natural. Its purpose is to avoid human‐mediated genetic change sounding ontologically different from every other source of biological change. Trained does not replace information about how the organism was produced, but it emphasizes function and performance instead of the roadmap for its occurrence. As a result, risk should be assessed from the actual properties of the organism, the introduced changes and the environmental context, rather than inferred primarily from whether those changes arose spontaneously or through deliberate intervention. Engineering can certainly alter the probability, speed or direction with which particular genetic configurations arise, but their consequences must therefore remain an empirical question, not an ontological issue.

Levels of Laboratory Training

On the basis of the above, laboratory‐trained strains could be classified into four interconnected levels with no rigid conceptual or technical boundaries separating one from another. In fact, there is little basis for such separation (Figure 3).

The gap between familiar and non‐familiar biology. The figure graphically represents how genetic engineering has been mistakenly presented as an intervention alien to nature, which has resulted in highly restrictive perceptions and regulations that have prevented the development of the field's full potential for bioremediation applications. In reality, as long as we operate within the realm of familiar biology, we are on a playing field with the same rules and using the tools that nature itself provides, regardless of the intensity of the genetic intervention. The proposal, therefore, is to group all microorganisms that follow these rules and that have been adopted for a biotechnological application under the designation of laboratory‐trained microorganisms, whose risk must be assessed, regardless of the degree of genetic intervention. But if we move beyond these rules of the biological game, we enter the realm of biological systems novel in nature, which certainly must be subject to specific regulations, and certainly more stringent ones than those applied to other types of biotechnological agents. This article thus advocates a replacement of the binary natural versus GMO versus. a continuum of laboratory‐trained organisms, as sketched in the figure and explained in the text. Note that Level 4 includes fully engineered strains, but still based on familial biochemistry and standard central dogma flow of information DNA → RNA → proteins and the standard genetic code. Only unfamiliar biochemistry (XNA, mirror life, alternative codes etc.) constitute a non‐natural discontinuity that may require special oversight.

At the zero level lay natural isolates that are brought into the laboratory for characterization and analysis, but that can, in principle, be returned to their original environment without further modification. The first level includes natural isolates subjected to spontaneous or stress‐induced variation to improve their performance or resilience. Such changes may occur naturally or be stimulated by exposure to mutagenic conditions, such as ultraviolet radiation, that increase genetic diversity. Interestingly, such variants have never been considered problematic from a regulatory viewpoint, as they simply represent the natural plasticity and variability of biological systems under stress and selective pressure. The second level comprises strains that, while initially natural, have been subjected to deliberate interventions designed to increase their basal mutation rate. Examples include the temporary relaxation of mismatch‐repair systems (Fernández‐Cabezón et al. 2021) or the conditional expression of base‐editing enzymes that randomly alter DNA bases. These methods promote genetic diversification without directing it towards a predetermined outcome; emergence of a desired trait occurs upon selection only afterward. When the genetic devices responsible for transient hypermutability are subsequently removed, the process constitutes a form of hit‐and‐run mutagenesis—producing diversity and then eliminating the inducing device. The resulting microorganisms may display improved traits after selection, but are indistinguishable from variants that could have arisen through spontaneous or stress‐induced mutation. From a regulatory or ethical standpoint, there should be no sound criterion by which they can be separated from natural isolates. The third level encompasses the cases traditionally described as recombinant bacteria or genetically modified organisms (GMOs)—strains whose genomes have been deliberately edited by selecting, modifying, or inserting genes, whether native or foreign, to achieve a defined biotechnological purpose. Although these changes result from intentional alterations, they essentially reproduce horizontal gene transfer processes that occur spontaneously in nature among different organisms and even kingdoms (Soucy et al. 2015). Even if a specific recombinant configuration has not yet appeared in nature, there is no inherent reason it could not arise through equivalent natural mechanisms. Moreover, once established, such constructs can evolve in natural environments and become assimilated into local gene pools (Peters et al. 1997). That the genetic modifications introduced are often unstable may be leveraged as starting points for synthetically primed evolution (Rodríguez‐Espeso et al. 2026), in which successive adaptive cycles enhance the expression, robustness, and stability of the engineered traits. Finally, the 4th level includes rationally all‐designed organisms whose genomes and enzymes are totally or partially built de novo but still remain grounded in what might be termed familiar biochemistry and genetics—systems that operate under the central dogma of molecular biology and the standard genetic code, where information flows from DNA to metabolism through proteins and their modifications. While these constructs may have no direct counterpart in nature, they are still governed by the same biochemical and enzymatic logic and rules that define all known life forms.

Although the boundary between natural/artificial is subject to social and cultural perceptions that change in space and time, there is little conceptual basis for considering such constructs of levels 2–4 radically unnatural. And from that follows that none of these categories of genetically intervened organisms pose inherently greater risks than those presented by naturally evolved life forms that have never been handled in the laboratory. This does not mean that they are risk‐free—it means that their risks are not different from naturally‐emerging counterparts, and therefore that they may not deserve separate regulatory provisions. Class 2–4 microorganisms can be invasive, persist in the environment and their modified DNA be transferred to the indigenous microbiota—exactly as Class 0–1 do. In reality, the dearth of field releases mentioned above has much prevented having good data on this matter. But the bottom line is that at least in the few cases when such releases have happened, no major detrimental effects have been reported (Sayler and Ripp 2000; de Lorenzo 2010).

In our opinion, real transit from natural to artificial arises only when we move beyond familiar biochemistry into the realm of life forms based on alternative molecular architectures—systems that no longer rely on the canonical chemistry of nucleic acids, amino acids and proteins, for example alternative genetic codes, xeno‐nucleic acids, mirror life (Figure 3). Only in such cases does a careful, empirical evaluation of potential hazards become necessary, though without succumbing to exaggerated alarmism or the arsonist firefighter syndrome mentioned above that has too often characterized discussions of biotechnology. But can we draw a clear boundary?

Innovation Versus Novelty in Biological Design

In this regard, it is worth recalling the distinction between innovation and novelty in the context of evolutionary biology (Payne and Wagner 2019; Erwin 2020). Innovation arises when existing components are combined in new ways, giving rise to emergent functions that were not previously observed but that still depend on pre‐existing molecular assets. Novelty, by contrast, refers to the appearance of a truly new property—something that is not a recombination or reconfiguration of what already exists, but rather the first emergence of a qualitatively different feature in nature. Viewed through this lens, traditional rDNA technology—despite its extraordinary technological achievements—should still be regarded as a domain of innovation rather than novelty (Figure 4). The methods developed under the framework of the Central Dogma and familiar biochemistry have enabled unprecedented manipulation of biological systems, yet they fundamentally operate by capturing and recombining pre‐existing biological activities. What we often celebrate as revolutionary breakthroughs are, in essence, new arrangements of processes that evolution itself has long mastered.

Innovation and novelty in environmental biotechnology. As argued in this review, the vast majority of modern biotechnology operations fall within the realm of innovation—that is, new combinations of existing components, using genetic tools provided by existing biology itself. Although the perception of what is natural or not has a strong cultural component, all these products resulting from innovation can still be considered part of the natural world. Conversely, novelty is that which has never existed before nor is it a consequence of anything that came before, and can therefore be labelled as artificial or genuinely synthetic. In the field of contemporary synthetic biology, both categories of products are often lumped together, generating some confusion and regulatory challenges.

Indeed, one could argue that genetic engineering represents not so much a wholly new invention as a recreation—perhaps even a rudimentary imitation—of nature's own powerful mechanisms for DNA transfer, recombination, and adaptation, processes that collectively underpin horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and evolutionary innovation. What humans have done is to identify, standardize and domesticate these natural mechanisms under controlled laboratory conditions, guided not by evolutionary fitness but by operator‐defined selected goals (Figure 4). In this sense, genetic engineering is best understood not as an invention, but as the technological application of a discovery—a human extension of principles that nature has been exploiting for billions of years, and whose depth and scope we continue to uncover.

In summary, all organisms that fall within the spectrum of laboratory‐trained life forms should not, in principle, be treated as categorically distinct from those that arise through natural evolution. A proposal emerging from such reasoning is that what ultimately distinguishes natural from artificial is not genetics, but biochemistry. Our frameworks for classification and risk assessment should reflect this fundamental distinction, moving beyond the problematic dichotomy of natural versus recombinant towards a more coherent and scientifically grounded view of life's continuum.

The Containment Paradox

In this context, the very notion of containment, long demanded by regulatory authorities as a prerequisite for the large‐scale use of recombinant microorganisms, becomes deeply problematic. Research on containment represents a case in the history of science in which a major research programme has arisen not from a biological question but from the need to satisfy regulatory expectations rooted in the assumption that recombinant organisms are inherently dangerous. As a result, studies on containment have generated technically impressive results at laboratory scale (Chan et al. 2016; Pei et al. 2022; Forti et al. 2025). But given that only a few of them have been tested outside the laboratory (Hartig et al. 2024, 2026), it is uncertain whether they will have practical relevance (Davison 2005; Figure 5).

The containment paradox: Why absolute control is impossible and undesirable. We argue that the 40‐year focus on containment and the pursuit of environmental orthogonality is conceptually flawed and technically unattainable. Even the best systems achieve escape frequencies of ~10−11 (in the laboratory) and cannot satisfy regulators asking for absolutes. Waves indicate that introduced bacteria rapidly integrate or else disappear, and their recombinant DNA can be transferred to others. Environmental stability and competition, not engineered kill switches, determine persistence. Containment is a regulatory artefact. Engineering resilience and ecological scaffolding is the real issue.

At the heart of the containment debate lies a deceptively simple question: how can we ensure that laboratory‐engineered bacteria remain confined within the spatial and temporal boundaries of their intended activity? Over the years, this challenge has inspired an extensive repertoire of ingenious proposals—ranging from simple nutritional dependencies (auxotrophies) to highly sophisticated genetic kill‐switch circuits, synthetic addictions and many others—all designed to prevent microorganisms from proliferating outside predefined conditions. Many of these designs are indeed conceptually brilliant, embodying remarkable creativity in genetic and systems engineering. Yet none of them can provide the absolute certainty that regulators desire. As popular culture has aptly reminded us—most memorably in Jurassic Park—Nature always finds a way. Despite continual refinements, all containment systems are ultimately vulnerable to mutation, recombination, or ecological compensation mechanisms that restore viability under unanticipated circumstances. A comprehensive overview of such containment strategies is provided in publication (Pei et al. 2022), which traces the evolution of concepts and tools over several decades. The most effective laboratory‐tested containment system to date achieved an escape frequency of approximately 10−11, but it has not been verified yet in large field trials—an extraordinary level of control, yet still far from absolute and insufficient to guarantee complete environmental confinement.

The only truly absolute containment conceivable would involve the use of unfamiliar biochemistries, that is organisms built upon molecular architectures incompatible with naturally occurring, extant life. However, such an approach introduces a paradox: while it could, in theory, prevent gene flow or ecological persistence, it simultaneously raises serious questions about the safety, predictability and controllability of the alternative biochemistries themselves.

From Containment to Traceability

What, then, should be done to address the legitimate concerns surrounding the safety of laboratory‐trained microorganisms? Fortunately, current models describing the release of both bacteria and recombinant DNA into environmental microbiomes suggest that the intrinsic homeostasis of microbial communities and niche‐addiction are themselves the most effective barrier against the uncontrolled spread of foreign organisms or genetic material (Maull and Solé 2024). These ecosystems possess powerful self‐regulatory mechanisms that limit the persistence and expansion of newcomers, maintaining the stability of the community's genetic and functional structure. Knowledge on this can in fact become a phenomenal tool for ecosystem design that incorporates both naturally‐occurring and lab‐trained actors (Maull et al. 2026).

A more realistic and effective approach to managing the presence of laboratory‐derived microbes and DNA in the environment would therefore be to focus on traceability rather than strict physical or genetic containment. This could be achieved through genomic barcoding (Tellechea‐Luzardo et al. 2022) and unique molecular identifiers (Gallegos et al. 2020; Berezin et al. 2024) that enable each engineered strain to be tracked and associated with a corresponding digital twin—a virtual representation containing comprehensive information about its genetic makeup, expected behaviour, and procedures for environmental remediation or removal if necessary. In other words, the emphasis of the discussion on containment and biosecurity should evolve from containment to traceability. Not only because of its technical feasibility, but also because it eases accountability and governance mechanisms to ensure management and equitable distribution of risks/benefits (Figure 6).

From control and containment to care and stewardship. This article proposes that, in order for society and the planet to benefit from the great advances of modern biotechnology, and especially applications of synthetic biology to environmental problems, it is necessary to shift the traditional emphasis from containment and control, a legacy of Asilomar 1975, towards a logic of care, responsibility, and traceability, for which specific molecular tools of the type discussed in the text are required.

Technically, traceability can be implemented not only through genomic barcodes (Tellechea‐Luzardo et al. 2022) but also by incorporating genetic watermarks—discrete, recognizable DNA sequences inserted into laboratory‐created strains that allow them to be distinguished from all‐naturally occurring microorganisms. Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that over time, bacteria released into the environment are likely to lose their artificial markers, as natural selection and genetic drift erode engineered sequences (Peters et al. 1997; Chemla et al. 2022, 2025). Eventually, these DNA fragments may become integrated into the broader metagenomic background through the same mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer that continuously reshape natural microbial communities. This process, far from exceptional, reflects the inherent fluidity of the genetic landscape of the biosphere that we need to navigate rather than combatting (Maull et al. 2025).

Taken together, these considerations suggest the need for a profound reframing of how we think about engineered life. Laboratory‐trained microorganisms do not constitute a categorical rupture with Nature but rather extend its evolutionary and biochemical continuum into new, rationally guided territories. Attempts to control them through rigid containment frameworks may prove both conceptually and practically inadequate since they rest on an arbitrary distinction between the natural and the artificial. Instead, a more scientifically coherent and operationally effective paradigm would place emphasis on traceability, transparency, and stewardship—ensuring that any organism derived from laboratory work can be identified and monitored.

Note, however, that once a self‐replicating organism has become established and dispersed in an open environment, full recall cannot generally be guaranteed. In some cases, retrieval may be possible in spatially contained applications and elimination or suppression could be feasible through biological or ecological countermeasures. But in general, these may not apply to all cases. Acknowledgement that environmental release is potentially irreversible makes identification, monitoring, and response capacity central to stewardship. Note also that irreversibility is not unique to engineered organisms: Agriculture, biological control, probiotics, introduced microorganisms, invasive species and many other human interventions involve living entities that cannot simply be retrieved once established. The question can we recall it? is probably the wrong one. The better questions are: Can we identify it? Can we follow it? Can we detect unexpected effects? And do we have measures available to counteract, or otherwise manage those effects if they occur?

Such an approach would merge biological innovation with ecological realism. It acknowledges that microorganisms, once introduced into complex ecosystems, inevitably engage in genetic and chemical dialogue with their surroundings (Figure 6). Rather than trying to suppress this interaction through impossible barriers, our responsibility should be to understand, leverage, and accompany these processes using the tools of genomic watermarking, digital twins, and systems‐level environmental surveillance. The goal, ultimately, is not absolute control, but responsible coexistence—a mature recognition that human‐designed biology is now part of the broader metabolic fabric of the planet. Or, as Emma Frow would put it, moving from control to care (Szymanski et al. 2024).

From Domination to Negotiation

This necessary transition must also be accompanied by a corresponding shift in terminology—not merely for rhetorical or marketing purposes, but as a genuine reflection of a revised conceptual stance towards our relationship with nature. Language shapes thought, and the terms we use to describe engineered life forms reveal much about our underlying intentions. Perhaps, instead of speaking of genetically modified organisms for bioremediation, we should speak of environmental probiotics or environmental amendments—agents designed to restore ecological balance rather than to dominate it. Likewise, instead of referring to kill switches and containment systems, we might speak of spatiotemporal addiction circuits or ecological tropisms—mechanisms that anchor microorganisms to specific niches or sites of action by creating incentives for remaining within their designated habitat rather than punishments for escaping it.

Such a linguistic and conceptual reorientation could also help reshape experimental strategies. The optimization of laboratory‐trained bacteria might benefit from hybrid approaches that combine top‐down rational design with evolutionary exploration, allowing biological systems to navigate a broader solution space than what rational top‐down design can envision. As discussed earlier, since genetic engineering ultimately represents a formalized subset of natural horizontal gene transfer, it is only logical to learn from how nature itself resolves complexity—by balancing multiple objectives through evolutionary negotiation and adaptation. The corresponding technological shift involves focusing on microbial ecosystems, rather than individual strains, as the unit of design. Coupling advanced genetic technologies with ecological and evolutionary principles, it will be possible to develop new tools and strategies to steer gene flow, metabolic interactions and community dynamics in situ. This will enable a shift from containment to stewardship, and from organism‐centric engineering to ecosystem programming (Figure 7). If traditional genetic engineering embodies control, then evolution embodies conversation—a continuous dialogue between the designed system and the receiving environment that seeks equilibrium rather than domination. Embracing this model of negotiation over command may well lead to more resilient and enduring solutions than those derived from a control mentality rooted in penalizing malfunction rather than nurturing compatibility.

From designing microorganisms to designing microbial ecosystems. The prevailing view on the potential use of laboratory‐trained bacteria for environmental bioremediation has focused on engineering new metabolic capabilities into potential environmental agents intended for their release. Regardless of regulatory limitations, the available evidence (de Lorenzo 2009) suggests that this approach has not been successful unless there is massive selective pressure in the ecosystem in question to select for that function. In contrast, growing data on environmental microbiomes, as well as new concepts stemming from indigenous epistemologies, call that functions do not reside or emerge exclusively in individuals, but rather in interactions between individuals. Following this logic, future environmental interventions will likely focus not on implanting new active members into a pre‐existing microbiome, but rather on integrating new ecological properties—such as degradative functions—and scaffolding them within the standing community.

Reframing environmental biotechnology through the lens of stewardship rather than control also brings the notion of responsibility to the forefront. Care implies continuity—it does not end with the act of design or release but extends into monitoring, understanding, and accompanying biologics we create as they interact with the world (Figure 7). In this sense, the logic of traceability—through genomic barcodes, watermarks, or digital twins—is not so much a tool of surveillance, but a step towards stewardship allowing us to remain accountable for the organisms we introduce into the biosphere. What emerges, then, is a vision of responsible coexistence between natural and laboratory‐trained forms of life—one guided not by fear or domination, but by dialogue, transparency and mutual adaptation.

Research Gaps

Logically, the release of laboratory‐trained bacteria into the environment is not only a regulatory or conceptual challenge—it is also a profoundly technical one. The methodological path from laboratory design to large‐scale environmental deployment remains riddled with unresolved issues, particularly concerning how to scale up experimental systems to real‐world conditions. One major obstacle is the inoculation of target sites with the biological agents of interest (de Lorenzo 2022). These agents may consist of a single engineered strain, a defined microbial consortium, or even a synthetic community composed of multiple species programmed to act cooperatively within a designed ecological framework. Determining how best to introduce such systems into complex and dynamic environments raises difficult questions about delivery formats and dispersal methods that should be the subject of research.

At smaller scales, techniques borrowed from agriculture—such as irrigation‐based delivery or aerial spraying—may suffice. For larger or less accessible areas, one might envision exploiting natural vectors of dispersal, such as insects, dust particles or meteorological currents (de Lorenzo 2022). More conceptually, one could even consider leveraging horizontal gene transfer itself as a vector for fortifying existing environmental microbiomes not by adding new organisms, but by transferring genetic information that enables native communities to acquire desired metabolic functions (Aparicio et al. 2022; Maull et al. 2025). Yet, as in all biological systems, scaling introduces emergence: with every increase in scale, new interactions and unforeseen behaviours appear. This makes it clear that one cannot simply move from the laboratory bench to environmental deployment in a single step. Instead, each level of scale requires careful evaluation and recalibration. In this sense, it has been proposed that planning and approval of environmental interventions involving laboratory‐trained microorganisms should adopt an approach analogous to that used in clinical trials for new therapeutics—progressing through phased field evaluations that assess both efficacy and risk at increasing levels of complexity and exposure. Implementing such a framework would also require the development of specific environmental simulation techniques and quantitative metrics for measuring impact and functional success (de Lorenzo 2022). All of this, however, can only become possible if we go beyond the mindset of control and embrace an ethos of care—a perspective that treats laboratory‐trained organisms not as external instruments to be restrained, but as cooperative agents whose behaviour and integration into natural ecosystems must be guided, supported and continuously monitored.

The Business of Bioremediation

Beyond the technical issues of risk assessment, public perception and conceptual framing discussed above, there is another dimension of the debate on releasing microorganisms for environmental bioremediation that has received remarkably little attention. This concerns the position of environmental biotechnology within the broader landscape of contemporary biotechnology—particularly biotechnology as an economic activity, as a business model, and as a product destined for the marketplace. In practice, no conceptual breakthrough or technological advance in biotechnology reaches society unless, somewhere along the path from laboratory to application, a viable market exists. Innovation must be coupled to demand, and demand must generate sufficient economic return to sustain the companies that develop and deploy the technology. Without this material engine, even the most elegant solutions remain unrealized. Environmental biotechnology—and especially large‐scale bioremediation—presents a striking paradox in this respect. On the one hand, its potential benefits for ecosystems, public health and planetary sustainability are undeniable. On the other hand, as a marketable product, it is comparatively unattractive to investors and entrepreneurs. Unlike therapeutic biotechnology, which addresses acute medical needs and therefore generates immediate and high‐value demand, bioremediation technologies rarely create spontaneous markets. Instead, they are almost entirely regulation‐driven.

A new drug for a serious disease has an intrinsic and urgent demand: patients, healthcare systems, and insurers actively seek solutions, and the resulting value generation is evident. The pharmaceutical sector clearly illustrates how medical necessity translates into robust business models. By contrast, a novel microorganism engineered to remove contaminants from soil or water typically attracts interest only when legislation compels remediation, or when regulatory frameworks impose penalties for inaction. In other words, the market for bioremediation is largely constructed through policy and the demand itself emerges only once regulations define liabilities and cleanup obligations (Ashford et al. 2002). This structural difference has profound consequences. Business models for large‐scale bioremediation have historically been comparatively fragile and difficult to scale. Considerable effort is required to integrate such technologies into the mainstream of modern biotechnology. Absent that integration, environmental biotechnology risks falling short of its transformative potential—not because of scientific limitations, but because the economic incentives are not there. The primary beneficiaries are diffuse: communities, ecosystems, and future generations, meaning that financial returns to individual companies are less direct and less immediate.

From this perspective, one might reasonably suspect that many of the perceived risks and regulatory burdens associated with releasing engineered microorganisms would rapidly diminish if a clear and compelling profit opportunity were widely recognized. Economic incentives most often reshape regulatory and social landscapes. The case of Pivot Bio (https://www.pivotbio.com/) is illustrative. Its nitrogen‐fixing bacteria—engineered yet deployed as biological alternatives to synthetic fertilizers—have been adopted extensively across the US and parts of Africa. The value proposition is clear: improved yields, reduced fertilizer inputs, and economic return for farmers. In this context, despite their genetically edited nature, these microorganisms have not triggered much resistance simply because they are engineered. The business model works; the benefits are tangible; adoption follows.

Therefore, the remaining challenges in deploying microorganisms for environmental bioremediation are not confined to technical feasibility, public perception or regulatory criteria. Equally critical is the question of economic viability and market attractiveness. Until robust and scalable business models are developed, environmental biotechnology may remain constrained—not by science, nor even by regulation, but by the absence of durable material incentives.

Learning From Indigenous Epistemologies?

Unfortunately, the dominant biotechnological culture in the West remains deeply rooted in a logic of domination, control, emphasis on individuality and money‐making which makes it difficult to articulate a genuinely new narrative about our relationship with the living world. The prevailing paradigm still assumes that to leverage live systems to our own benefit, we must first master them – an attitude that obscures alternative ways of conceiving human participation in the biosphere.

It is increasingly evident, however, that a more appropriate framework for redefining this relationship—not in terms of domination, but of belonging, partnership, and negotiation—may be found in indigenous worldviews and ancestral traditions. These cosmologies do not place human beings at the top of a hierarchy of life, endowed with the right to manipulate everything below, but rather within a continuum of mutual interdependence, where respect, reciprocity, and care govern the interactions between all living entities (Kimmerer 2013; Villanueva‐Flores and Garcia‐Atutxa 2025). Within such a perspective, human interventions in nature, including those directed towards our own sake—from simple domestication of animals and plants to deep genetic engineering—are conceived as outcomes of a win–win negotiation, not as acts of imposition or conquest. This is not a mere blurb to make recombinant DNA technology gullible to the public: it has deep practical consequences also in the way we may conceive environmental interventions.

For more than a century, microbiology has been built on a simple idea: that microbes are discrete, autonomous entities whose functions reside within their genomes and can be isolated, modified and controlled. But what we have observed in the last decade tells a different story. Microbial activities unfold through dense webs of interaction—metabolic exchanges, horizontal gene transfer and ecological feedbacks that blur the boundaries between individual organisms and their biological and abiotic surroundings (Maull and Solé 2024; Maull et al. 2025). Interestingly, this relational view of life echoes indigenous epistemologies which have long emphasized that knowledge and phenomena emerge through interactions and that living actors are understood as part of networks of reciprocity, embedded in place and sustained through relationships (Gugulethu 2025). Synthetic biology now gives us the means not only to observe these interactions, but to engage with them deliberately. This invites a shift in ambition: from engineering individual organisms to designing and stewarding microbial ecosystems—moving, in essence, from control to orchestration (Figure 7).

It is no coincidence that there has been a recent emergence of what some have called indigenous biotechnology—innovative approaches that integrate traditional ecological knowledge with contemporary biotechnological insight (Astolfi et al. 2025). These developments offer promising avenues for bridging the conceptual and cultural gaps that currently separate modern genetic engineering from the broader ecological dialogue of which it should form a part. Embracing such perspectives could help transform biotechnology itself—from a technology of control into an instrument of relationship, capable of harmonizing human creativity with the living systems that sustain it. These ideas align well with the propositions emerging from the Asilomar II conference that took place in February 2025 to celebrate the 50th anniversary of the original meeting of the genetic engineering pioneers (Chemla and Voigt 2026).

Conclusion

The debate on the deliberate release of genetically engineered microorganisms is no longer a theoretical or ethical curiosity—it is a practical necessity for addressing the escalating environmental crises of our time. After decades of paralysis driven by outdated regulatory frameworks and a culture of fear, the convergence of synthetic biology, microbial ecology and systems‐level environmental science invites a new narrative and a new practice—one that replaces control with care, and containment with traceability and responsibility. Laboratory‐trained organisms are not foreign entities plummeted into nature but extensions of its own evolutionary creativity, capable of partnering with ecosystems to restore balance and function. The task before us is to build governance systems grounded in scientific realism and moral maturity, integrating transparency, stewardship and cross‐cultural perspectives that see humanity not as the master of life, but as one of its caretakers. Only by reframing our relationship with living systems in this way can biotechnology evolve not only into a genuine instrument of planetary repair but also a source of wealth and prosperity. Finally, this review calls for a broader transformation in our collective narrative about nature. We must move beyond viewing the natural world as a passive resource to be exploited and instead recognize it as a partner in achieving a sustainable, habitable planet. This shift requires integrating perspectives from other cultures and traditional knowledge systems that see humans and nature as co‐participants in a shared biosphere. Only through such a change in vision—from domination to collaboration—can environmental biotechnology unleash its potential and fulfil its promise as a force for planetary renewal.
