Peak Fiction: XRPD and the Illusion of Novelty
Authors: Jeremy Rolquin, Shahin Shams, Mike Faley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This viewpoint addresses the growing use of X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) data to support patent claims on crystalline forms of pharmaceutical compounds. XRPD patterns are inherent material properties, not inventions. We demonstrate that different sample preparations and holders impact the XRPD peaks returned for the same commercially available sample. Thus, treating XRPD peaks as stand-alone proof of novelty weakens patentability standards across the field of medicinal chemistry and pharmaceutical intellectual property.

A Tool Miscast as a Source of Novelty

Polymorphism is the ability of a molecule to crystallize into different forms, which can directly affect a drug’s solubility, dissolution rate, and bioavailability. The FDA encourages comprehensive polymorph screening during the preclinical and early formulation phases to fully characterize molecules for therapeutic use. The most common analytical method for this purpose, X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD), is one of the most reliable and accessible.

XRPD identifies polymorphic forms of compounds by detecting how they diffract X-rays, a process derived from Bragg’s Law, which assigns diffraction angles (2θ) to atomic spacing in the crystal. Each crystalline structure has a distinct, reproducible pattern of peaks. These patterns are considered the “fingerprints” of crystalline forms, and are used for quality control, regulatory filings, and publication. XRPD has been an essential analytical method since the 20th century, but its role is now extending beyond structure confirmation, and into legal territory.

Over the past decade, a growing number of pharmaceutical patent filings have defined claimed polymorphic forms by a series of XRPD peak positions. Patent applications claiming crystalline polymorphs through XRPD peaks are often submitted years after the initial compound was disclosed. In many cases, the filings include no evidence of improved functional performance, or new data on efficacy, stability, or manufacturability. The issue with this trend is that measurement alone, instead of new utility, can qualify as an invention. This practice crosses the line between characterization and innovation, and rewards analysis rather than scientific invention.

The Illusion of Distinction

In U.S. patent law, an invention must be new, nonobvious, and useful. The doctrine of inherent disclosure says that prior art (information publicly disclosed prior to the effective filing date of a patent application) can be used for not only what it teaches, but also for properties inherent to what the prior art discloses. For pharmaceuticals, this can include chemical characteristics of a drug, even if those characteristics are not explicitly disclosed in the prior art. Courts have ruled repeatedly that if a property results from a known inherent composition or process, it cannot form the basis for a new patent.

X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) simply verifies that the atoms within a crystalline solid adopt an ordered arrangement consistent with a specific lattice. However, small differences in XRPD peaks have been used as a basis for novelty. Critically, we are not challenging the viability of patenting a crystalline drug form using XRPD peaks if that form has demonstrated a substantive functional pharmacological characteristic difference. Rather, we are contesting the increased use of XRPD peaks alone in forming a basis of patentability without any demonstration that the claimed crystal form has a different pharmacological profile than forms that exist in prior art. , The use of XRPD peaks alone is problematic because small differences can be accounted for by experimental variation in generating and analyzing the XRPD data. This variation provides room for intentional manipulation or unintentional selection of conditions that suggest novelty when none exists. This allows patent seekers the chance to legally redefine existing chemical compounds through selective peak set claiming.

To demonstrate that variations in XRPD data do not necessarily indicate the presence of a new solid form, a control experiment was performed using a commercially available caffeine sample. The resulting diffraction patterns reveal that small differences in sample preparation and mounting, introduced without altering the chemical composition, lead to measurable changes in peak intensity, position, and profile. These observations highlight the extent of analytical variability that may be misinterpreted as structural novelty when XRPD patterns are evaluated in isolation (Figure ).

Methods

Commercially available caffeine was purchased (MilliporeSigma PN:C0750 Lot: BCCM3313) and analyzed by XRPD using two different sample holders and two different preparations with each holder. The instrument is a Bruker D2 Phaser XRPD System with a Goniometer Radius of 141.0 mm fitted with a LYNXEYE_2 detector. Diffraction data is acquired by exposing powder samples to Cu–Kα X-ray radiation, which has a characteristic wavelength (λ) of 1.5406 Å. X-rays were generated from a Cu anode supplied with 30 kV and a current of 10 mA. The data were collected over a range of 3–40 °2θ with a step size of 0.012 °2θ and nominal time per step of 0.80 s. The sample rotation was set at 15 rpm. Fixed divergence and antiscatter slits of 1.0 and 10.5 mm respectively were used together with a beam mask of 10 mm and all scans were carried out in “continuous” mode. Generated data was exported and overlaid using ACD/Laboratories Spectrus Processor.

Results and Discussion

Compound preparation techniques are usually not included in patent documents or lack sufficient detail. , In this manuscript, we prepared the anhydrous β form of crystalline polymorph caffeine four ways; Preparation A1 (Silicon Zero-Background Sample Holder with well 20 × 0.5 mm, cavity fully packed), Preparation A2 (Silicon Zero-Background Sample Holder with well 20 × 0.5 mm, cavity 60% packed), Preparation B1 (Silicon Zero-Background Sample Holder, Powder on surface) and Preparation B2 (Silicon Zero-Background Sample Holder, Powder thin layer of vacuum grease). These are all accepted preparation methods for compounds analyzed using XRPD.

The variations for all five peak positions exceeded ± 0.1, and in some cases also exceeded the broader ± 0.2 tolerance allowed under USP <941>. This range in peak positions is significant, due to the increasingly tight tolerances claimed in patents for polymorphs of different compounds. Therefore, claiming novelty based on tight tolerances in peak positions is insufficient without a full disclosure of the experimental conditions used to obtain the diffractogram.

The experimental data demonstrates that narrow tolerances fall within the variability of commonly used preparation techniques, and do not necessarily distinguish between different polymorphs of the same compound. Using this data, one could claim multiple different unique and novel polymorphs of caffeine based on the same crystalline form using tight tolerances to separate the 2θ values from one another. This overlaps with the recent patent trend of using small differences in 2θ values to claim crystalline forms of compounds that may have already entered the public domain. Sometimes these small differences are within the margin of error of previously described polymorphic forms. This should raise concerns about the validity of this patent trend.

These results reinforce the argument that evidence of novelty based on narrowly defined XRPD data alone is insufficient, and should not alone be used to claim crystalline forms of a compound. Exploitation of small differences in peak positions, which we have demonstrated to be highly dependent on experimental conditions, risks damaging the integrity of the chemical patent process. This example emphasizes the risks of interpretation of XRPD data on its own, without further supporting evidence of improved functional properties over other polymorphic forms.

Implications for Patent Policy and Scientific Integrity

As XRPD-based claims become more prevalent, the consequences for the pharmaceutical landscape could be extensive. These types of claims facilitate a form of evergreening, i.e., strategic filings which extend exclusivity of brand name versions of drugs. When evergreening patents are granted, they delay entry of generic drug compositions into the market, especially if they are accompanied by litigation threats.

This practice reduces the value of analytical chemistry techniques in the eyes of regulators and courts. If XRPD is increasingly used as a proxy to suggest a novel property, its credibility as a scientific method could be decreased. XRPD is one of the best tools scientists have for characterizing compounds, but we must recognize its limitations.

This is not a call to ban solid-state patents. Crystalline forms that demonstrate meaningful advantages such as improved stability, reduced hygroscopicity, or increased efficacy for certain conditions deserve protection. Those advantages must be shown, not assumed. XRPD data is supporting evidence, but it should not comprise the entire basis for the novelty of a claim. Under the logic of current claiming practices using XRPD data, even well-known polymorphs of caffeine might be found to satisfy novelty thresholds. Precedent has shown that the existing patent examination systems currently in place may not be sufficient to prevent overly broad patents from being granted.

A More Rigorous and Ethical Standard

To prevent further issues with novelty standards in pharmaceutical patenting, the field must be aware of the differences between observational techniques and invention. The doctrine of inherency should be applied consistently when evaluating XRPD-only claims. If XRPD patterns are considered inherent, novelty and utility should not be presumed.

Patent applicants should provide functional data to accompany XRPD-based claims. This includes physical stability, dissolution rate, solubility, efficacy, or enhanced processability. Requiring these measurements for patenting a therapeutic compound would improve the scientific standard, while also deterring applicants from patenting on the basis of inherent properties. Claiming a diffraction pattern difference without practical benefits should be insufficient. Likewise, patent applicants have sought broader control through defining claimed compounds by a limited number of peaks that encompass multiple different polymorph forms. In such an instance, it is important that evidence is presented demonstrating that all of the different claimed structures are novel.

Conclusion

XRPD is one of the most powerful tools in the chemist’s arsenal. But XRPD fingerprints are not created by analysis; they are created by nature. When diffraction patterns alone, without demonstrated differences in functional properties, are used to claim invention, we risk mistaking measurements for innovation. The reporting of peaks may be new, but they are not new to the material. If we allow XRPD to be a sole basis for novelty, we open the door to analytical evergreening, to patents which narrow the acceptable limits of the technique to distinguish their peaks from others, and to the erosion of pharmaceutical progress and innovation. To preserve the integrity of patent law and pharmaceutical science, rigorous standards must be implemented and enforced by both inventors and patent professionals. Stakeholders in the pharmaceutical patenting process, including researchers, patent professionals, and regulators, have a shared interest in rewarding innovation. While analytical measurements are reliable, interpretable, and replicable, solid-state characterization and intellectual property standards should align to ultimately benefit the pharmaceutical industry at large.

Future Outlook

As the occurrence of XRPD-based polymorph claims in patents increases, patent offices and regulators will face increased pressure to establish clearer standards and enforce more rigorous disclosure requirements for solid-state characterization claims. The field has an opportunity to adopt stricter sample preparation protocols through organizations like USP, which would encourage pharmaceutical development entities to raise their experimental standards, while also making their portfolios more defensible and their innovations more credible. Ultimately, enforcing these higher standards protects the integrity of the pharmaceutical patent landscape and the scientific credibility of these extremely useful analytical chemistry techniques.
