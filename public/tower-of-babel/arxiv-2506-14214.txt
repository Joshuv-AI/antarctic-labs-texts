Breakdown of the Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem in Kinetic Ising Models
Parbati Saha, Sanjay Puri, Varsha Banerjee

Abstract
Out-of-equilibrium dynamics, even in the simplest spin models, is still not well understood. The celebrated fluctuation dissipation theorem (FDT) does not hold for non-equilibrium systems. In this context, Cugliandolo and Kurchan introduced a generalized FDT which elucidates the non-equilibrium evolution as a composition of time sectors corresponding to different effective temperatures cugliandoloKurchan1997efft ; cugliandoloKurchan1997FDR . We address these evaluations in the d = 2 d=2 long-range Ising model (LRIM), which surprisingly still has many lessons to teach. In particular, we investigate how interactions and conservation laws affect the non-equilibrium dynamics that is initiated by coarsening experiments. Quantifying the deviations from FDT in terms of T e ​ f ​ f T_{eff} , we find different aging scenarios for short- and long-range IM with non-conserved (Glauber) dynamics and conserved (Kawasaki) dynamics.



I
Introduction

An important non-equilibrium phenomena is the kinetics of phase transition, when a system is quenched from a high temperature disordered state to a low temperature ordered state. Such a system does not order instantaneously. Instead, there is formation of domains of degenerate ground states which are separated by interfaces or defects. In many simple systems, the dynamics after a quench or
coarsening
is understood in terms of a domain growth law:
\ell(t)\sim t^{z}
, where
\ell(t)
is the typical length scale in the system
Bray1994
. The determination of the domain growth laws is an important aspect in coarsening experiments, as it reveals details of the free-energy landscape and relaxation time scales in the system. For example, pure isotropic systems with single-spin-flip Glauber dynamics (GD) obey the Lifshitz-Allen-Cahn law:
\ell(t)\sim t^{1/2}
. Systems with spin-exchange Kawasaki dynamics (KD) follow the Lifshitz-Slyozov law:
\ell(t)\sim t^{1/3}
. These growth laws are characteristic of systems without energy barriers to coarsening and a unique relaxation timescale. Disordered systems, on the other hand, have logarithmic corrections at late times. They are a consequence of slow relaxation that arises from a complex free-energy landscape with deep local minima separated by high-energy barriers, leading to a plethora of relaxation times. Understanding the non-equilibrium process of coarsening is key for predicting structure formation on varied scales and contexts. Some examples include the growth of embryos on a microscopic scale
microscopic1
;
microscopic2
;
microscopic3
to condensed matter on a mesoscopic scale
mesoscopic1
;
mesoscopic2
;
mesoscopic3
;
mesoscopic4
;
mesoscopic5
, from quantum physics at nanoscale
nanoscale1
;
nanoscale2
;
nanoscale3
to astrophysics on a cosmic scale
cosmicscale1
;
cosmicscale2
;
cosmicscale3
.

Concomitant with slow relaxation is the phenomenon of
aging

Henkel2008
. As the system ages, it keeps getting trapped in the multitude of free-energy minima and faces larger barriers because it has already crossed the smaller barriers. (Barriers are limited only by the size of the system.) An older system therefore experiences a more rugged landscape with deeper local minima not because it has changed with time, but now the system has more to explore. Consequently, the process of aging never stops and complex systems continue to remain out-of-equilibrium. In the generic picture, aging is studied using two-time quantities such as the autocorrelation function that relates a local observable
s
at different points of time, and is given by:

C(t,t_{w})=\langle s(t)s(t_{w})\rangle-\langle s(t)\rangle\langle s(t_{w})\rangle.

(1)

Both
t
and
t_{w}
(
t>t_{w}
) are measured after quench, and
t_{w}
is the so-called waiting or annealing time. Unlike systems in equilibrium,
C(t,t_{w})
does not exhibit time-translational invariance. The time scale for correlation decay increases with
t_{w}
and far exceeds the experimental time scale. This decay is described by a universal scaling function
f(t/t_{w})
, implying that time evolution can be described in terms of a single time-dependent length scale
\ell(t)
.

Dynamic sluggishness makes aging systems highly susceptible to even the smallest perturbations. The standard probe that describes the effect of an external field
h
applied at time
t_{w}
(say) on the local observable
s
is the linear response function, the form of which is given by:

R(t,t_{w})=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\frac{\partial\langle s(t)\rangle}{\partial h(t_{w})}\bigg|_{h=0}.

(2)

In thermodynamic equilibrium, the two-time quantities in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) satisfy the translational invariance of time, i.e.
C(t,t_{w})=C(t-t_{w})
and
R(t,t_{w})=R(t-t_{w})
. The fluctuation dissipation theorem (FDT) relating correlation and response also holds at equilibrium, and is given by:

R(t-t_{w})=\frac{1}{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{w}}C(t-t_{w}),

(3)

where the
T
is the equilibrium (bath) temperature defined through the Boltzmann distribution. The FDT thus controls the relationship between fluctuations (random motion of the constituents due to thermal noise) and response (their reaction to external perturbations) and is directly related to
T
.

Aging systems lose translational symmetry in time and do not follow the FDT. In this context, Cugliandolo and Kurchan proposed the notion of fluctuation dissipation ratio (FDR), as an indicator of deviation from equilibrium, defined as follows:

X(t,t_{w})=TR(t,t_{w}){\partial_{t_{w}}C(t,t_{w})}.

(4)

Systems in equilibrium are characterized by
X(t,t_{w})=1
, whereas non-equilibrium behavior results when
X(t,t_{w})\neq 1
. At high temperatures, the system experiences larger thermal fluctuations, leading to
X>1
. At low temperatures, the system slowly equilibrates due to trapping in local minima, resulting in
X<1
. Cugliandolo and Kurchan also introduced the concept of effective temperature
T_{eff}
by rewriting Eq. (
4
) to yield a
generalized
FDT:

R(t,t_{w})=\frac{X(t,t_{w})}{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{w}}C(t,t_{w})=\frac{1}{T_{eff}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{w}}C(t,t_{w}),

(5)

where
T_{eff}=T/X(t,t_{w})
is the effective temperature. Needless to say that, for systems in equilibrium,
T_{eff}=T
. As emphasized earlier, in slowly relaxing systems, different degrees of freedom have different time scales. The non-equilibrium evolution can thus be elucidated as a composition of time sectors corresponding to distinct effective temperatures. So, physically,
T_{eff}
plays a role similar to temperature in equilibrium thermodynamics, but applies to the different time scales in an aging system.

In experiments, the applied field is not an impulse but is generally over an interval of time. Consequently, rather than evaluating the response to an instantaneous stimulus applied at
t
, the experimental observations are better represented by the so-called integrated response function (IRF). It is the cumulative response of the system to a field applied over a time window
[t_{w},t-\epsilon]
with
t_{w}\leq t-\epsilon
. The evaluation of IRF is provided by
Lippiello2005
:

\chi(t,[t_{w},t-\epsilon])=\int_{t_{w}}^{t-\epsilon}dt^{\prime}R(t,t^{\prime})=\int_{t_{w}}^{t-\epsilon}dt^{\prime}\frac{X(t,t^{\prime})}{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}C(t,t^{\prime}).

(6)

Since
\epsilon
is generally taken to be the smallest time interval used in the calculation, it is reasonable to substitute
\chi(t,[t_{w},t-\epsilon])\equiv\chi(t,t_{w})
. In addition, for systems where the dynamics is slow, it is reasonable to assume that
X(t,t^{\prime})
varies slowly in the integration window such that
X(t,t^{\prime})\simeq X(t,t_{w})
, for all
t^{\prime}\in[t_{w},t]
to obtain the following simplified form for the IRF:

\chi(t,t_{w})\simeq\frac{X(t,t_{w})}{T}\int_{t_{w}}^{t-\epsilon}dt^{\prime}\,\frac{\partial C(t,t^{\prime})}{\partial t^{\prime}}=\frac{X(t,t_{w})}{T}[C(t,t-\epsilon)-C(t,t_{w})].

(7)

Now, because
\epsilon
is infinitesimal, we take

C(t,t-\epsilon)\simeq C(t,t)=1
. Eq. (
7
) further simplifies to:

\chi(t,t_{w})\simeq\frac{X(t,t_{w})}{T}[1-C(t,t_{w})]=\frac{1-C(t,t_{w})}{T_{eff}}.

(8)

In equilibrium,
X(t,t_{w})=1
and the above form reduces to a linear relation usually referred to as the FDT line:

T\chi(t,t_{w})=1-C(t,t_{w}).

(9)

Deviations from this straight-line behavior (slope-1) indicate a violation of the FDT. In slowly relaxing systems, the plot of
T\chi(t,t_{w})
vs.
C(t,t_{w})
is used most often to identify the FDR, as well as the time sectors that indicate distinct values of
T_{eff}
.

The preceding discussion may suggest that aging is prototypical of systems with complex free-energy landscapes arising from disorder and frustration, the traditional example being a spin glass. However, the Ising model (IM) with no disorder and absence of frustration also exhibits aging, making it the simplest system to study the paradigms of non-equilibrium physics. This is very fortunate because the dynamics of simple magnets should be much easier to understand than that of glasses. There have been several insightful studies on coarsening in the short-range or nearest-neighbor Ising model (NNIM), and a few even with disorder
lippiello2010
;
corberi2011
;
corberi2012
. These have provided accurate estimates of growth exponents and also a good understanding of the aging scenario. However, spin-spin interactions in several magnetic materials often extend beyond the NNs to large distances. A realistic picture therefore requires the use of models that incorporate long-range interactions. While their need has been evident for a long time, the analytical and computational complexities to deal with them have been a major impediment. As a result, the LRIM, though of immense experimental relevance, continues to be less explored.

In recent years, some numerical works have conducted non-equilibrium studies of the LRIM using efficient Monte Carlo (MC) procedures that allowed exploration of large systems over longer time scales. These have validated growth laws and aging exponents employing GD and KD
Amar1988
;
Barrat1998
;
Newman1999
;
Krzakala2005
;
christiansen2019
;
christiansen2020
;
Fabio2022
;
Fabio2024
;
Subir2024
. In the present work, we go beyond these investigations to examine the role of interactions, NN and LR, on the violation of FDT. By evaluating the IRF and
T_{eff}
, we make some novel observations: (i) FDT violations are prevalent in the IM at large times (
t\gg t_{w}
) in the NN and LR IMs with non-conserved as well as conserved order parameters. (ii) In the IM with single spin-flip GD,
T_{eff}
grows
exponentially
with time for LR interactions. On the other hand,
T_{eff}
saturates in the case of NN interactions. (iii) With KD,
T_{eff}
grows
linearly
with time, and the growth is faster for LR interactions.
Mean-field calculations have predicted
T_{eff}\rightarrow\infty
in coarsening systems. Our extensive numerical simulations reveal that the approach to infinity is distinct for GD and KD. Furthermore, we observe that for NNIM with GD, there are time sectors where
T_{eff}
is a constant. We expect these observations to provide fresh insights into the relaxation modes that exist in the simplest and cleanest IMs.

The remainder of the paper is devoted to understanding the different scenarios dictated by the interactions and the number of components (one or two) in the IM. Section

II
Theoretical Framework

The Hamiltonian of
d=2
LRIM is given by

\mathcal{H}=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_{i}s_{j},\quad J_{ij}=r_{ij}^{-(2+\sigma)},

(10)

where
s_{i}=\pm 1
and
r_{ij}
is the distance between the spins at sites
i
and
j
that interact with a potential of strength
J_{ij}
. The exponent
\sigma
, which regulates power-law decay, smoothly transitions from short-range NN interactions (
\sigma\gg 1
) through intermediate ranges (
\sigma\sim 1
) to long-range interactions(
\sigma<1
)
Sak1973
. The model system exhibits a phase transition from a paramagnetic state to a ferromagnetic state at critical temperature
T_{c}(\sigma)

Horita2017
.

As Ising spins do not have intrinsic dynamics, the system is placed in contact with a heat bath at temperature
T
that generates stochastic spin-flips. The probability of spin flip (
s_{i}\rightarrow-s_{i}
) is based on standard Metropolis criteria:
P_{i}=\mbox{min}[1,\exp(-\beta\Delta E_{i})]
, where
\beta=1/k_{B}T
and
\Delta E_{i}=E_{i}^{new}-E_{i}^{old}
is a change of energy between the new spin configuration and the old spin state
metropolis1953
. Since interactions are LR, a MC step involves computing
N
sums to evaluate
\Delta E_{i}
, making these evaluations computationally intensive. For efficiency, it is useful to store the effective field
h_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}s_{j}
for each spin
i
at the beginning of the simulation. A recalculation is then required only if a spin-flip is accepted. In the GD, where a randomly chosen (single) spin
s_{m}
is flipped with Metropolis probability,
\Delta E_{m}=2s_{m}h_{m}
. Similarly, in the conserved model with KD, let
s_{m}
and
s_{n}
be a pair of NN spins selected for exchange
s_{n}\leftrightarrow s_{m}
. In this case, a simple derivation along the lines of GD yields
\Delta E_{nm}=2h_{m}s_{m}+2h_{n}s_{n}-4J_{nm}s_{m}s_{n}
. The effective field approach significantly speeds up the computation at low temperature. This method accelerates the simulation by approximately a factor of
10^{3}
compared to the standard Metropolis algorithm.

Simulations involving LR interactions exhibit strong finite-size effects. Much larger lattices are required as compared to those with short-range interactions. They can be created by employing periodic boundary conditions through the minimum-image convention. The interaction strength between spins in a square lattice gradually diminishes until it reaches a specific cutoff distance. The effective interaction strength can be written as an infinite sum over all images

J(s_{i},s_{j})=\sum_{n_{x}}\sum_{n_{y}}\frac{1}{|\vec{n}_{i}+\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}|^{d+\sigma}}

(11)

where
\vec{n_{i}}
= (
n_{x},n_{y}
) is the displacement vector with
n_{x},n_{y}=0,\pm L,\pm 2L,\cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot
denotes the co-ordinates of imaginary copies. The infinite sums are computed using the Ewald summation technique that is well established for dealing with LR interactions
Frenkel1996
.

Let us now consider a quench from a high-temperature paramagnetic state (
T_{i}>T_{c}(\sigma)
) to the ferromagnetic phase (
T_{q}<T_{c}(\sigma)
). The system develops correlated regions of up-and-down spins or domains that coarsen with time. The usual probe to quantify the morphologies is the two-point spatial correlation function defined by:

C(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\langle\psi(\vec{r}_{i},t)\psi(\vec{r}_{i}+\vec{r},t)\rangle-\langle\psi(\vec{r_{i}},t)\rangle\langle\psi(\vec{r}_{i}+\vec{r},t)\rangle]

(12)

where the angular brackets represent an average taken over independent initial conditions and
\psi(\vec{r}_{i},t)
=
s_{i}
is the order parameter.
A characteristic length scale
\ell(t)
is usually defined as the distance at which
C(\vec{r},t)
decays to some fraction of its maximum value. If domain growth is characterized by a unique length scale
\ell(t)
, the morphology is not affected as time evolves, except for a scale factor. In this case, the correlation function demonstrates a dynamic scaling property
C(\vec{r})=g(r/\ell)
where
g(x)
is the scaling function
Puri2009
;
Bray1994
.

In a classic paper, Bray and Rutenberg predicted growth laws in the LRIM by using an “energy scaling” argument. For the GD (non-conserved order parameter), they obtained:

\ell(t)\propto t^{z}=\begin{cases}t^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}&\sigma<1,\\
(t\ln t)^{\frac{1}{2}}&\sigma=1,\\
t^{\frac{1}{2}}&\sigma>1.\end{cases}

(13)

For systems with KD (conserved order parameter) on the other hand, they found that:

\ell(t)\propto t^{z}=\begin{cases}t^{\frac{1}{2+\sigma}}&\sigma<1,\\
(t\ln t)^{\frac{1}{3}}&\sigma=1,\\
t^{\frac{1}{3}}&\sigma>1.\end{cases}

(14)

Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) indicate that the growth exponents depend on
\sigma
in the regime where the effects of LR dominate (
\sigma<1
).

Traditionally, non-equilibrium processes are understood by evaluating multiple-time quantities that provide information about the change in the properties of the system as it grows in age. In context, the most frequently evaluated is the two-time autocorrelation function defined by Eq. (
1
), and the numerical evaluation is simply:

C(t,t_{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle s_{i}(t)s_{i}(t_{w})\rangle-\langle s_{i}(t)\rangle\langle s_{i}(t_{w})\rangle,

(15)

where
N
is the number of spins in the system. The autocorrelation function comprises the equilibrium and the aging parts:

C(t,t_{w})=C_{eq}(t-t_{w})+C_{ag}(t,t_{w}).

(16)

The contribution
C_{eq}(t-t_{w})
originates from the interior of the domains that evolve rapidly towards equilibrium. It exhibits time-translational invariance, contributing significantly when the difference
(t-t_{w})\ll t_{w}
is considerably smaller. The aging contribution arises from the interfaces and is triggered when
(t-t_{w})\gg t_{w}
. It has a slow evolution and lacks the time-translational invariance, a characteristic feature of aging in the system. The decay of the auto-correlation function is determined by the auto-correlation exponent (
\lambda
). This dimension-dependent critical exponent does not correlate with the growth exponent
z
. In the asymptotic limit of
t\rightarrow\infty
:

C(t,t_{w})\sim\bigg(\frac{\ell(t)}{\ell(t_{w})}\bigg)^{-\lambda}\sim\bigg(\frac{t}{t_{w}}\bigg)^{-\gamma},

(17)

with
\lambda=z\gamma
. For systems with non-conserved order parameters,
\lambda\geq d/2
. For systems with conserved order parameter,
\lambda\geq(2d+1)/2
. As a thumb rule, faster dynamics leads to smaller values of
\lambda
. For example, ferromagnetic systems near
T_{c}
, non-glassy systems, and LR models exhibit fast relaxation that is captured by a smaller aging exponent. However, disordered magnetic systems, spin glasses, and systems with short-range interactions exhibit larger aging exponents because of their slow, out-of-equilibrium dynamics. An elaborate study that produces accurate aging exponents with GD and KD can be found in
Yeung1996
.

Let us move on to the main focus of our work, that is, the evaluation of the IRF for the LRIM. From an off-equilibrium generalization of the FDT, Lippiello et al. constructed an efficient numerical algorithm for the computation of the LR after an application of the perturbing field. The linear response at site
i
due to an application of an external field
h
at site
j
for just an instant at
t_{w}
can be numerically computed from Eq. (
2

R_{i,j}(t,t_{w})=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\frac{\partial\langle s_{i}(t)\rangle}{\partial h_{j}(t_{w})}\bigg|_{h=0}.

(18)

Consider now a continued application of the field
h_{j}
in the time interval
[t_{w},\Delta_{t}]
such that
t_{w}\leq\Delta_{t}\leq t-\epsilon
and
\epsilon
is arbitrarily small. The corresponding contribution to the IRF can be numerically evaluated by:

\chi_{i,j}(t,[t_{w},\Delta_{t}])=\epsilon\sum^{\Delta_{t}}_{t^{\prime}=t_{w}}R_{i,j}(t,t^{\prime}).\\

(19)

The value of
\epsilon=1/N
, which corresponds to a single spin update in an MC step. The summation is performed over discrete times in the interval
[t_{w},\Delta_{t}]
.
Using Eq.(
6
), the above evaluation takes the explicit form

;
Corberi2006

\chi_{i,j}(t,[t_{w},\Delta_{t}])=\frac{1}{T}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left[C_{i,j}(t,\Delta_{t})-C_{i,j}(t,t_{w})\right]-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\sum^{\Delta_{t}}_{t^{\prime}=t_{w}}\langle s_{i}(t-\epsilon)B_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle\right\},

(20)

where
T
is the temperature of the thermal bath and
B_{j}=-\sum_{\{s^{\prime}\}}(s_{j}-s^{\prime}_{j})w(\{s\}\rightarrow\{s^{\prime}\})
with

being the transition probability for
\{s\}\rightarrow\{s^{\prime}\}
that follows the detailed balance condition
Puri2009
. The angular brackets indicate an ensemble average. Assuming that the perturbation is applied at site

Lippiello2005

\chi(t,t_{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\chi_{i,i}(t,[t_{w},\Delta_{t}].

(21)

Lippiello et al. obtained Eq. (
20

The FDR,

IV
Discussion and Conclusion

So, what are the key observations that we have unraveled regarding FDT violation in the simplest and cleanest IM? In this context, let us reminisce about the physical implications of the effective temperature
T_{e}ff

cugliandolo1999effective
;
cugliandolo2011effective
. It has been introduced to describe the deviation from equilibrium in a way that still retains a thermodynamic interpretation. For fast fluctuations [small
C(t,t_{w})
] at an early time
t\gtrsim t_{w}
, the system behaves as if it were in local equilibrium with the heat bath, that is,
T_{eff}=T
. In the late-time sluggish aging regime when
t\gg t_{w}
, slow rearrangements or modes behave as if they are at a higher temperature
T_{eff}>T
. In addition, saturation of the effective temperature indicates that aging stops in certain degrees of freedom in that time sector, and the system behaves as if it has reached an equilibrium regime where fluctuations are governed by the thermal bath at
T_{eff}
. A finite effective temperature allows for a quasi-thermodynamic description of the out-of-equilibrium system
marco2001
;
marco2007
. It allows us to define entropy, free energy, or even work relations for those slow degrees of freedom that have stopped aging. In
coarsening systems
, as discussed in Eq. (
16
, there is a two-time-scale separation: a fast equilibrated regime arising from thermal excitations of the spins in the bulk domain and a slow aging regime, where correlations decay very slowly. Physically, this means that the system has very large effective fluctuations, much larger than those at equilibrium. These large fluctuations arise from the movement of domain walls rather than from thermal excitations. As the process of domain growth (through annihilation of interfacial defects) continues indefinitely, no finite temperature can describe the aging dynamics. Consequently, for coarsening systems,
T_{eff}=\infty
in the asymptotic limit of
t\rightarrow\infty
with
t\gg t_{w}

Claudio2010
;
Ludovic2022
.

In light of the above discussion, we arrive at the following conclusion. (i) There are strong violations of the FDT in IM. They depend on the range of spin-spin interactions and conservation laws.
(ii) In the non-conserved GD model with LR interactions (
\sigma=0.6
),
T_{eff}\rightarrow\infty
with age, and the approach to infinity is
exponential
. The monotonic evolution suggests that the system continuously explores the free-energy landscape with (local) rearrangement of the spins. This process becomes harder and harder as the system ages, due to the annihilation of interfacial defects that is characteristic of coarsening. These slow relaxation modes are interpreted as a consequence of the high effective temperature (
T_{eff}
) that the system acquires as a result of contact with a heat bath at
T_{eff}>T
.
(iii) For NN interactions, where the neighbors are only four, the system acquires a non-equilibrium steady state that is reflected by the saturation to
T_{eff}>T
in our simulations. The emergence of time sectors is unusual in the context of coarsening. A naive explanation could be that the NN interactions cannot provide sufficient energy to overcome the local minima forcing the system to settle in a
hot
steady state, manifested as saturation of the effective temperature to
T_{eff}>T
. (We have checked that this observation is not a finite-size effect, and larger system sizes and longer runs will be required to confirm the length of the time sectors.)
(iv) In the conserved model with KD, the approach of
T_{eff}\rightarrow\infty
is
linear
. Although there is not much influence of the interaction range, the approach depends on
t_{w}
and is slower for older systems. This suggests that the system explores deeper and deeper valleys in the free-energy landscape with aging. However, large-scale rearrangements due to the motion of interfaces occur much more slowly in spin-exchange KD, and may be the reason for (slower) linear approach to infinity vis-a-vis the exponential form in GD that arises due to fast single-spin flip moves.

To conclude, we investigated the phase ordering kinetics in a
d=2
LRIM through MC simulations employing efficient techniques using non-conserved and conserved dynamics. We proposed storing the effective field for each spin rather than calculating the total energy of spins at each step, which helps to reduce computational cost. In spite of its simplicity, the IM provided some important lessons in understanding the out-of-equilibrium dynamics by evaluating deviations from the FDT that is obeyed in equilibrium. Although these methodologies have existed in the literature for a long time, our comprehensive study has contributed to novel findings and new questions. For example, the transition from
T_{eff}=\infty
to a finite value as the strength of the interaction range is varied needs to be carefully investigated. The role of conservation laws on
T_{eff}\rightarrow\infty
requires further understanding. Obtaining conventional thermodynamics and work-energy theorems in identified time sectors is an unaddressed curiosity.
Although many aspects of the IM still remain unexplored, we hope that our investigations provide a comprehensive framework for addressing related models, with dilution and disorder for instance, that are closer to experimental observations.
