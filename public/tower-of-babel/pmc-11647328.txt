Performance of automated machine learning in detecting fundus diseases based on ophthalmologic B-scan ultrasound images
Authors: Qiaoling Wei, Qian Chen, Chen Zhao, Rui Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbtractAimTo evaluate the efficacy of automated machine learning (AutoML) models in detecting fundus diseases using ocular B-scan ultrasound images.MethodsOphthalmologists annotated two B-scan ultrasound image datasets to develop three AutoML models—single-label, multi-class single-label and multi-label—on the Vertex artificial intelligence (AI) platform. Performance of these models was compared among themselves and against existing bespoke models for binary classification tasks.ResultsThe training set involved 3938 images from 1378 patients, while batch predictions used an additional set of 336 images from 180 patients. The single-label AutoML model, trained on normal and abnormal fundus images, achieved an area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.9943. The multi-class single-label model, focused on single-pathology images, recorded an AUPRC of 0.9617, with performance metrics of these two single-label models proving comparable to those of previously published models. The multi-label model, designed to detect both single and multiple pathologies, posted an AUPRC of 0.9650. Pathology classification AUPRCs for the multi-class single-label model ranged from 0.9277 to 1.0000 and from 0.8780 to 0.9980 for the multi-label model. Batch prediction accuracies ranged from 86.57% to 97.65% for various fundus conditions in the multi-label AutoML model. Statistical analysis demonstrated that the single-label model significantly outperformed the other two models in all evaluated metrics (p<0.05).ConclusionAutoML models, developed by clinicians, effectively detected multiple fundus lesions with performance on par with that of deep-learning models crafted by AI specialists. This underscores AutoML’s potential to revolutionise ophthalmologic diagnostics, facilitating broader accessibility and application of sophisticated diagnostic technologies.Keywords: Imaging, Pathology, Retina

This study is the first to introduce and validate the use of AutoML for multi-label classification of fundus diseases from B-scan ultrasound images, reflecting more accurately the multifaceted nature of real-world clinical scenarios. Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly revolutionised the field of ophthalmology, enhancing diagnostic precision and treatment efficacy across various applications.1,3 While the integration of AI with fundus photography4 5 and optical coherence tomography6,8 has been extensively documented, its use in ophthalmologic ultrasound, particularly B-scan ultrasound, remains comparatively underexplored.9,18 B-scan ultrasound is crucial in clinical settings for evaluating posterior segment diseases such as retinal detachment (RD), vitreous haemorrhage, tumours (T) and traumatic ocular injuries.19,21 Its ability to penetrate ocular opacities allows for essential cross-sectional views of the vitreous body and the rear of the eye. This makes B-scan ultrasound an invaluable diagnostic tool, either complementing other optical imaging methods or acting as the sole diagnostic resource when opacities preclude the use of standard imaging techniques. Furthermore, the scarcity of specialists skilled in interpreting these B-scan images for fundus disease diagnosis, especially in rural areas, can impede timely diagnosis of severe conditions, highlighting the potential of AI to bridge these gaps and extend expert-level diagnostics to underserved regions. Recent advancements in deep learning have substantially improved the screening and classification of fundus diseases via B-scan ultrasound imaging. Notable efforts include Chen et al, who applied a deep convolutional neural network to detect multiple abnormalities in ophthalmic ultrasonography, achieving an accuracy of 0.73 for classifying conditions such as RD, vitreous detachment (VD) and vitreous hemorrhage.12 In 2022, Adithya et al used the Inception-ResNet-V2 architecture, achieving a robust AUROC value of 0.9399 in identifying vitreoretinal abnormalities.9 Subsequently, Wang et al introduced DPLA-Net, a multi-branch Transformer network, achieving a mean accuracy of 0.943 on a testing dataset.10 Additionally, Li et al employed an InceptionV3-Xception fusion model, attaining an accuracy of 0.9673 in classifying ophthalmic ultrasound images.11 Furthermore, Tan et al developed a Siamese convolutional neural network with multi-attention (SVK_MA) for the automatic classification of vitreous opacities and posterior vitreous detachment, achieving an accuracy of 0.94014. Li et al also introduced DCT-Net, a hybrid deep-learning model that demonstrated remarkable accuracy in detecting retinal holes (RH).15 These studies showcase a spectrum of architectural approaches, from traditional networks like LeNet5, AlexNet, and VGG16 to modern innovations such as GoogLeNet Inception V1, which Feng et al found superior to several ResNet models.18

However, developing these sophisticated models typically requires experts with extensive coding skills and deep knowledge of machine learning algorithms, as well as considerable time investment for tasks like feature and algorithm selection, and hyperparameter tuning. In response, Automated machine learning (AutoML) platforms offer a promising solution to these complexities, especially in areas traditionally demanding significant specialist knowledge.22,24 AutoML enables clinicians, even those without prior coding or machine learning expertise, to develop robust diagnostic models. These systems simplify the model-building process by automating algorithm selection and tuning, significantly reducing the barrier to entry for deploying AI in medical diagnostics. In this context, our study employs the Vertex AI platform to develop and evaluate AutoML models for detecting fundus diseases from ophthalmologic ultrasound images. Through the comparative analysis of single-label, multi-class single-label and multi-label AutoML models, we aim to not only demonstrate the versatility and efficacy of AutoML in handling complex diagnostic tasks but also to underscore its potential to expand the use of AI in ophthalmology. The ultimate goal is to equip ophthalmologists with intuitive AI tools that enhance diagnostic outcomes, particularly in settings where conventional imaging is limited.

Method

Ocular ultrasound image dataset

The ocular B-mode ultrasound image dataset was compiled from images collected at the eye and ENT (EENT) Hospital of Fudan University, Shanghai, China. These images were selectively captured from patients who visited the EENT Hospital for routine eye examinations or disease treatment between 1 January 2023 and 31 March 2024. This research received approval from the ethics committee of the EENT Hospital of Fudan University (approval no. 2023427) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All ocular B-mode ultrasound scans were performed using the Aviso Ultrasound Platform A/B, equipped with a 10 MHz linear transducer (Quantel Medical, Clermont-Ferrand, France). The dataset includes ultrasound scans from patients instructed to look in primary, upward, downward, nasal and temporal directions. The dataset was curated to include normal fundus images alongside images exhibiting six types of fundus pathologies, each annotated based on specific lesion characteristics. These pathologies included normal (N), chorioretinal detachment (CD), posterior Staphyloma syndrome (PSS), RD, RH, VD, T and vitreous opacities (VO). The 'T' category comprised choroidal osteoma, choroidal hemangioma, choroidal metastasis, uveal melanoma and retinoblastoma, while the 'VO' category included physiological vitreous opacity, vitreous haemorrhage and silicone oil residue post-silicone removal surgery. This diverse collection aimed to cover a wide range of clinical presentation, including RD with different heights, various degrees of VO and PSS and Ts of different shapes and sizes. All images were stored in PNG format with dimensions of 640×419 pixels. Demographic data, such as age and gender, were extracted from medical records to provide comprehensive metadata for each image. Annotations were conducted by an ophthalmologist (QLW) using each patient’s medical history, preliminary ultrasound reports and complementary diagnostic findings from fundus photography, CT scans and MRI. Discrepancies between the annotations and preliminary reports or medical histories were resolved by senior specialists (QC and RJ). Measures were taken to ensure patient confidentiality through strict anonymisation protocols. The dataset assembly and annotation process are depicted in figure 1A. Model Development on the Google Vertex AI Platform The Google Vertex AI platform offers a user-friendly interface that significantly simplifies the model development process. Starting with the selection of a data type (image, tabular, text, video), users can easily navigate to choose an objective—such as single-label classification, multi-label classification, object detection or segmentation. Once the objective is set, images can be uploaded from a local computer. Following the upload, the training process begins automatically, allowing for a seamless transition from data input to model development.

To enable a rigorous comparison with bespoke deep-learning models, we developed three distinct AutoML models on the Google Vertex AI platform, each undergoing three iterations of training. These models were designed to address different aspects of disease classification:

Single-Label AutoML: This model targets the differentiation between 'Normal' and 'Abnormality' categories in our dataset. The latter includes images showing one or multiple pathologies, which are critical for efficiently identifying abnormal findings from normal cases. After selecting the ‘single-label classification’ option on the platform and uploading the labelled images, the tool automatically selects the optimal model architecture based on the dataset characteristics.

The models were constructed by two ophthalmologists, QLW and JR, who had no prior programming experience. Images were uploaded to the platform in an unlabeled state and systematically categorised into predefined label groups based on existing annotations. The dataset was divided into three subsets—80% for training, 10% for validation and 10% for testing—adhering to the platform’s default recommendations. Each training iteration was run for eight node hours, a duration recommended by the platform to optimise learning outcomes for our specific dataset size. To prevent overfitting, early stopping protocols were implemented automatically during each training phase. The inherently random division of the dataset necessitated that each experiment be conducted three times to ensure the reliability of the results, with the average of the performance metrics used to assess the efficacy of each model (detailed in figure 1B).

The Google Cloud Vertex AI platform assesses model performance by computing the AUPRC, ranging from 0.5 to 1.0, where higher values indicate greater model precision. This measure is complemented by precision and recall metrics, which are crucial in medical image classification due to the severe implications of false negatives. Additionally, the platform generates a confusion matrix that provides detailed insights into the model’s classification accuracy across different labels, thus enabling comprehensive performance evaluation. However, it is noted that the platform does not support the generation of measures of variability, such as CIs, which are essential for confirming the statistical robustness of the findings.

Validation of the established multi-label AutoML model

Given the multi-label AutoML model’s design to reflect real-world clinical scenarios, where patients frequently present with complex and overlapping pathologies, we prioritised its validation. We initially sought publicly available ocular ultrasonic images for external validation using platforms such as Google, Kaggle (https://www.kaggle.com/) and Grand Challenge (https://grand-challenge.org/). However, no suitable datasets were found, which aligns with recent systematic reviews noting a scarcity of appropriate ophthalmological imaging datasets.25 26 Consequently, we curated a separate dataset to evaluate the model’s functional performance. This internal validation employed batch prediction, where image data were processed in bulk to examine the model’s efficiency and accuracy. The images were organised into a JSONL (JSON Lines) file format, allowing structured and efficient data handling. Each line represented a single image’s data in JSON format, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the model’s predictive capabilities and the identification of any potential discrepancies or biases when applied to new datasets. This approach ensured rigorous validation of the model’s real-world applicability.

Literature review

A targeted literature review was conducted using MEDLINE via PubMed and Google Scholar, covering all entries up to 30 April 2024. The search employed keywords such as ‘ocular ultrasonic images’, ‘ophthalmic ultrasonic images’, ‘AI, and ‘deep learning’ to identify peer-reviewed articles. Only articles written in English and available in full text were included. These articles discussed bespoke deep-learning models developed by AI specialists for ophthalmic ultrasound imaging. This search yielded ten publications that detailed the creation and validation of deep-learning models for the detection of retinal diseases using ultrasonic images with English written.9,18 These studies universally employed convolutional neural networks designed by AI experts to distinguish diseased from normal fundi. The outcomes reported in these studies included metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROC), sensitivity and specificity, providing a quantitative measure of each model’s performance.

Statistical analysis

Performance comparisons between the Single-Label AutoML, Multi-Class Single-Label AutoML and Multi-Label AutoML models were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance determined at p<0.05. Metrics used included average precision, precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity and F1 score. Average precision, precision and recall were automatically generated by the Google Vertex AI platform, while specificity and F1 score were calculated from confusion matrices, the former being defined as the ratio of true negatives to the combined total of true negatives and false positives. The average precision metric, representing the AUPRC, ranges from zero to one, with higher values indicating superior performance. Distinct from the AUROC commonly employed in deep learning studies, the AUPRC is particularly beneficial for evaluating models within imbalanced datasets as it emphasises the model’s precision in correctly identifying positive instances. This specificity makes the AUPRC a valuable measure for assessing the practical utility of predictive models in clinical settings.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design and conduct of this study.

Results

Characteristics of the Datasets

Two distinct datasets were curated for this study. Dataset A comprised 3938 images from 1378 patients, consisting of 517 images labelled as 'N' (normal) and 3421 images labelled as abnormalities. These underwent three iterations of training. Specifically, 1861 images from Dataset A with singular labels—'N' (517), 'CD' (177), 'PSS' (255), 'RD' (382), 'T' (158), 'VD' (103) and 'VO' (269)—were used for the Multi-Class Single-Label AutoML training. The entire set of 3938 images, which included both the 1861 single-label images and 2077 images with multiple labels, was employed to train the Multi-Label AutoML model. Dataset B, in contrast, included 336 images from 180 patients, segmented into 147 single-label images and 189 multi-label images. Details of the image datasets are provided in online supplemental table 1).

Discussion

When designed by clinicians, AI systems benefit from medical expertise that significantly enhances their development and implementation within healthcare settings. Clinicians' deep understanding of medical and diagnostic complexities ensures these systems are closely aligned with real-world clinical demands. AutoML platforms enable clinicians without deep-learning expertise to efficiently prototype and develop clinical AI applications, accelerating the model development process. This approach has produced promising results in a range of ophthalmological tasks, such as diabetic retinopathy screening,27 28 multi-retinal disease classification,29 30 surgical video analysis,31 32 oculomics research33 34 and resource management,35 achieving performance comparable to bespoke deep-learning models and offering significant benefits in terms of accessibility and operational efficiency. However, to date, AutoML has not been applied to multi-label fundus disease classification using ocular B-scan images, a scenario that closely mirrors real-world clinical settings.

Our methodological design effectively employs AutoML to diagnose fundus diseases using ocular ultrasound images, deploying three distinct models: a Single-Label AutoML to differentiate normal from abnormal findings across 3938 images, a Multi-Class Single-Label AutoML for classifying six specific pathologies using 1861 images and a Multi-Label AutoML trained on the same 3938 images to identify multiple pathologies simultaneously. Each model underwent three training iterations to ensure consistency and mitigate random variabilities, aligning with best practices that recommend multiple initialisations to confirm result stability. This approach not only enhances reliability but also allows a controlled comparison across models and with existing bespoke deep-learning models, thereby providing a robust framework for evaluating the effectiveness of single-label versus multi-label strategies in medical imaging diagnostics. The performance metrics indicated that all three models achieved commendable precision, with the Single-Label AutoML model showing superior precision at 0.9943. For single pathology detection, precision values for the Multi-Class Single-Label model ranged from 0.9277 to 1.0000, peaking for T and lowest for CD. The Multi-Label model’s precision varied from 0.8780 to 0.9980, highest for T (0.9980) and lowest for RH (0.9277). These findings underscore AutoML’s potential to enable clinicians without computational expertise to develop highly accurate deep-learning disease classification systems.

In the realm of medical image classification, particularly for ocular B-scan analysis, the performance of various deep-learning models is significantly influenced by factors such as the choice of architecture, dataset size and specificity of the task challenges. For instance, Feng et al.’s comparative study evaluated the classification accuracy of seven deep learning models—including various versions of ResNet and GoogLeNet Inception—for diagnosing vitreous opacity in ophthalmic ultrasound images.18 They found that GoogLeNet Inception V1 achieved the highest accuracy at 0.9550. In contrast, our study used the Google Vertex AI platform to evaluate a dataset formatted in PNG (640×419 pixels) from an Aviso Ultrasound Platform A/B equipped with a 10 MHz linear transducer, achieving an average precision ranging from 0.968 to 0.975 for VO. Despite differences in dataset characteristics and calculation methods, the high precision achieved in our study demonstrates the comparable efficacy of the Vertex AI platform in classifying multi-label grayscale image datasets. Furthermore, when employing the Vertex AI platform for binary classification of 'Normal' versus 'Abnormalities' in ocular B-scans, our results demonstrated competitive performance compared with bespoke models developed by Adithya et al.,9 Wang et al,13 and Li et al,15 each employing distinct architectures and dataset scopes. Notably, our model exhibited particularly strong specificity, effectively distinguishing true negative cases (as detailed in table 2.).

However, challenges arise when our models are subjected to Multi-Class classification tasks. Our Google Vertex AI’s Multi-Class Single-Label Model did not reach the high precision and sensitivity of bespoke models like those by Chen et al12 and Li et al,11 who used advanced architectures and larger datasets. Specifically, Chen et al.’s use of a ResNet-50 architecture on a larger dataset yielded a precision of 0.9900 and sensitivity of 0.940012, while Li et al achieved a precision of 0.9521 and sensitivity of 0.9528 using a fusion model on 16 937 images.11 The superior performance of these models, compared with ours with precision and sensitivity of 0.9233 and 0.9083 respectively, likely stems from their larger datasets, which enhance robustness and generalisation capabilities—unlike our smaller dataset that might restrict learning capacity for rare or nuanced ocular conditions. Moreover, the architectural sophistication of Chen’s and Li’s models, optimised for feature extraction and image classification, starkly contrasts with our more accessible but less customised Google Vertex AI’s AutoML platform. Furthermore, the complexity of the tasks our model addressed—with a broader range of classification labels (N, CD, PSS, RD, T, VD, VO) than those used by Chen et al—likely increased the classification challenges. It is noteworthy, however, that our model achieved the highest specificity (0.9750) among the compared studies, suggesting it is particularly effective at correctly identifying true negatives, a crucial metric in medical diagnostics where false negatives can have serious implications. Moreover, in a study by Wang Y et al, a bespoke single-label model, employing CNN and Transformer architectures, achieved commendable sensitivities in classifying various pathologies—RD, VO, PSS, normal and T—yielding values of 0.9127, 0.9200, 0.9397, 0.7992 and 0.8596, respectively.10 In contrast, our multi-class single-label AutoML model demonstrated superior sensitivity across these conditions, with values of 0.9850, 0.9530, 0.9717, 0.9483 and 1.000. Additionally, our multi-label AutoML model reported robust sensitivity scores: 0.9513 for RD, 0.8827 for VO, 0.9120 for PSS, 0.8910 for normal and 0.9353 for T. These results underscore AutoML’s potential to surpass traditional bespoke models in diagnostic accuracy. The multi-label AutoML model, though slightly less sensitive than its multi-class counterpart, still delivers strong performance, illustrating its effectiveness in handling complex clinical scenarios with multiple concurrent pathologies. This capacity to classify multiple conditions efficiently in one session enhances clinical workflows, reduces the necessity for multiple tests and accelerates comprehensive patient care. It is noteworthy that the precision for RH was lower compared with other conditions in our study, likely due to the characteristics of our dataset where all RH-labelled images were also marked with other signs. This resulted in an average precision of 0.878, a precision of 0.930 and a recall of 0.834 for RH in the Multi-Label AutoML. In contrast, Li, K. et al used a Vision Transformer (ViT) model on a balanced dataset of 1831 samples (910 with RH and 921 without RH) achieving superior performance metrics, including a 0.9778 accuracy, a 0.9734 precision, a 0.9713 recall rate and an F1 score of 0.968215. These findings illustrate how differences in model architecture—Google Vertex AI vs Vision Transformer—and dataset specifics must be considered. Enhancing our dataset, particularly by increasing the number of isolated RH samples, could potentially improve our model’s performance, although results will inherently reflect the distinct capabilities of the AI platforms employed.

Our study is subject to several limitations that must be considered when evaluating the results. First, the lack of bespoke models developed by AI experts using identical datasets limited our ability to fully explore the potential of AutoML for disease classification from B-scan images. Additionally, our exclusive reliance on an internally developed dataset—while necessary due to the lack of suitable external datasets—might limit the generalisability of our results. While published datasets often come with annotations that do not align with our system requirements, our curated dataset ensured no image overlaps and demonstrated the AutoML’s effectiveness, as evidenced by batch predictions from our multi-label model, which achieved accuracies ranging from 0.8657 to 0.9756 for single pathologies. However, the absence of external validation calls for further studies to affirm the robustness of these findings across broader populations. Furthermore, the inclusion of explainable AI components was not feasible in our model development process, as these elements were not featured during the training or prediction tasks of the three AutoML models we developed. This omission limits the transparency of the predictive decision-making, crucial for clinical applicability and trust in automated systems.

Despite these inherent limitations, our study provides insights into the application of AutoML for multi-label classification of diseases from B-scan images, which more accurately mirror the complexities encountered in clinical practice. We made our training set and internally curated dataset publicly available to facilitate broader research and replication efforts. Multi-label classification allows for the detection of multiple pathologies within a single image, a scenario common in clinical environments where patients may present with co-existing conditions. This approach enhances the diagnostic process but also adds to the complexity of model training and performance evaluation, requiring advanced algorithms that can effectively differentiate and label overlapping disease states. The performance metrics of our models are encouraging, highlighting their potential in clinical environments. However, these results are initial, and extensive further research is required to confirm the effectiveness of AutoML for such complex applications.

In conclusion, this study leverages AutoML to classify diseases from B-scan images, marking a novel advancement in ophthalmological diagnostics. Our approach involved curating a comprehensive dataset, which will be made publicly available, and implementing multi-label classification to mirror the complexity of real-world clinical scenarios more accurately. However, the absence of comparable bespoke models developed by AI experts on the same datasets has restricted our full assessment of AutoML’s capabilities in this specific context. Despite these limitations, the encouraging performance metrics of our models suggest that AutoML could significantly enhance diagnostic processes in clinical settings. To build on these preliminary findings, further research involving the parallel development of bespoke and AutoML models on uniform datasets is essential. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of AutoML’s effectiveness and ensure its practical applicability in enhancing patient care through advanced diagnostic technologies.
