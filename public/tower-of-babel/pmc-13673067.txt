Improved Deformability of Co‐Free Medium Entropy Oxides by Controlled Entropy Destabilization
Authors: Chang Liu, Ke Xu, Zhongxia Shang, Yang Chen, Debargha Paul, Yinghang Liu, Jeremy Gan, Shiyu Zhou, R Edwin García, Xinghang Zhang, Haiyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐entropy oxides (HEOs), such as (CoCuMgNiZn)O, are attractive structural ceramics due to their compositional complexity and single‐phase stability. However, their intrinsic brittleness remains a major limitation. Here, we introduce a strategy termed as “controlled entropy destabilization” to activate defect‐mediated plasticity in a Co‐free medium‐entropy oxide (MEO) (CuMgNiZn)O. Spark plasma sintering produces a Rocksalt structure containing pre‐existing Cu‐rich secondary phases that act as defect sources during deformation. In situ SEM micropillar compression studies from room temperature to 600°C, combined with transmission electron microscopy and crystallographic orientation mapping, reveal a clear temperature‐dependent transition in deformation mechanisms. At room temperature, deformation is dominated by crack initiation with limited dislocation activity. At 400°C, thermally activated dislocation motion leads to delayed cracking and increased plastic strain accommodation. At 600°C, extensive dislocation glide and stacking‐fault formation produce pronounced plasticity and strain hardening. The fracture strain increases from 2.0% ± 0.3% at room temperature to 15.9% ± 0.1% at 600°C. These findings demonstrate that the controlled entropy destabilization approach can activate dislocation nucleation, migration, and storage, enabling intrinsic plasticity in multielement oxide ceramics at moderate temperatures.Keywords: coherent Cu‐rich secondary phases, controlled entropy destabilization (CED), in situ micropillar compression, medium‐entropy oxide

Introduction

Achieving plastic deformability in ceramics at room or moderate temperatures remains a long‐standing challenge because of their strong covalent bonding and the lack of effective mechanisms for strain accommodation [1]. Conventional toughening strategies such as grain refinement [2, 3], second‐phase reinforcement [4], phase transformation [5, 6], primarily rely on extrinsic crack shielding mechanisms rather than intrinsic plastic flow. Recent studies suggest that plasticity can be activated through defect engineering. Techniques such as spark plasma sintering (SPS) and flash sintering have been shown to introduce high densities of defects, including dislocations, stacking faults, and vacancies [7, 8, 9, 10], while preloading can further stimulate defect activity and dislocation motion [11]. These pre‐existing defects can enable plasticity in ceramics at moderate temperatures or even at room temperature.

High‐entropy oxides (HEOs) offer a different framework for tailoring plasticity in ceramics. By incorporating multiple cations on a single sublattice, configurational entropy lowers the Gibbs free energy of a homogeneous solid solution, giving rise to lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion, and solid‐solution strengthening, which have been associated with disrupt strain localization, delay crack propagation [12, 13, 14]. Recent studies have revealed that such compositional complexity can give rise to unexpected deformation behaviors. In Rocksalt (CoCuMgNiZn)O, room‐temperature plasticity has been directly observed in micron‐sized grain samples through dislocation‐mediated slip, with slip bands and activation of 1211011¯0 slip systems [15]. Complementary micropillar compression studies further reveal that deformation is highly orientation dependent using Schmid factor analysis. Specifically, pillars along (001) and (101) activate dislocation slip, whereas (111) oriented pillars experience predominantly brittle fracture [16]. At elevated temperatures, the deformability becomes even more pronounced. Recent studies show that (CoCuMgNiZn)O undergoes superplastic deformation at 600°C–850°C with low creep stress exponents (n ≈ 0.2‐2.5), indicating grain boundary sliding dominated behavior. Shear‐thickening transitions occur at stresses above ∼9 MPa, associated with a shift toward solution‐precipitation mechanisms facilitated by Cu‐rich secondary phases [17]. Notably, superplastic forming can be achieved at relatively low pressures (∼70 kPa) and temperatures (∼800°C) [18]. More recently, in situ micropillar compression studies have provided direct evidence of temperature‐dependent plasticity in (CoCuMgNiZn)O, revealing the transition from brittle fracture to dislocation‐mediated plastic flow, accompanied by slip band formation, grain fragmentation, and the emergence of Cu‐rich phases that actively participate in strain accommodation [19]. However, excessive entropy driven stabilization could over homogenize the lattice and suppress local chemical and structural fluctuations, which could otherwise serve as alternative mechanisms for strain accommodation. This analysis raises an important question: could deliberately reducing configurational entropy to activate defect mediated mechanisms enable plastic deformation in ceramics?

Here we introduce a design strategy of “controlled entropy destabilization” for defect activation and plastic deformation in ceramics. Rather than maximizing entropy to enforce phase stability via dopant additions (e.g., Li, Na Fe) [20, 21, 22, 23, 24], we intentionally reduce the number of constituent elements while preserving the parent Rocksalt structure. This design is motivated by the distinct roles of individual cations in multicomponent Rocksalt oxides. Particularly, Co acts as a strong lattice stabilizer due to its intermediate ionic size, robust Co─O bonding, and favorable mixing thermodynamics that promote a uniform cation distribution and suppress local chemical segregation [25, 26]. Furthermore, Co is relatively costly and toxic for synthesis of oxides [27]. Exposure to cobalt powders and dust can cause respiratory issues, while the release of waste disposal may contaminate soil and water. By contrast, Cu exhibits a strong tendency for local enrichment and planar defect formation due to its pronounced Jahn‐Teller instability, which destabilizes ideal octahedral coordination and induces local lattice distortion [28]. Moreover, Cu valence fluctuations between Cu2+ and Cu+ enable local charge compensation and facilitating solute redistribution [29, 30]. By removing Co from the parent (CoCuMgNiZn)O high‐entropy oxide while retaining Cu, we intentionally reduce excessive lattice stabilization and activate Cu‐rich second phases.

Based on this design, we investigate the temperature‐dependent deformation behavior of (CuMgNiZn)O using in situ SEM micropillar compression combined with post‐mortem microscopy and precession electron diffraction (PED). By tuning configurational entropy, second phase could be used to promote dislocation nucleation, transmission, and work hardening, thereby opening a pathway toward more damage‐tolerant deformable ceramic materials. Although demonstrated in a specific oxide composition, this strategy can be applicable to other multicomponent oxide ceramics.

Experimental Methods

Processing and Synthesis

CuO (with 25–55 nm reported particle size, 99.95%), MgO (50 nm, 99.95%), NiO (18 nm, 99.98%), and ZnO (18 nm, 99.95%) nanosized powders were blended in equimolar amounts. The raw powders were mixed via high‐energy ball milling (HEBM) utilizing a SPEX 8000D mill for 30 hrs in an alumina jar with alumina media. The HEBM was done in 30 min intervals, interrupted by 10‐min pauses to avoid overheating and powder agglomerating. These powders were used to consolidate coarse‐grained samples using conventional sintering furnace at 900°C for 20 min. Then the powders were sintered by the spark plasma sintering (SPS) technique using SPS‐10, Thermal Technologies LLC. The powder was placed in a cylindrical graphite die with an inner diameter of 10 mm. A graphite foil was inserted into the inner wall to prevent the powder from adhering to the graphite tooling. The SPS system was powered on when the vacuum in the chamber reached 2 × 10−2 torr. The sintering temperature ramped to 800°C for 5 min at a heating rate of 200°C/min along with a pressure of 35 MPa. The relative density of the sintered specimen was measured to be 97% by the Archimedes method.

Microstructure Characterizations

The structure of SPS MEO was characterized by the x‐ray diffraction (XRD) technique by using a PANalytical Empyrean X'pert PRO MRD diffractometer with a 2 × Ge (220) hybrid monochromator to select Cu Kα1 in the 2θ—ω geometrical configuration. A Thermo Fisher Quanta 650 FEG scanning electron microscope and an FEI Talos 200X transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV were utilized to analyze the microstructure of specimens. Grain sizes were estimated by the line intercept method over 200 grains based on transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images. TEM samples were manually prepared by grinding, polishing, dimpling and final polishing in an Ar ion milling system (PIPS ii, Gatan) at 2 kV. The crystallographic information embedded in the Inverse pole figure (IPF) and Kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps was generated by Precession electron diffraction (PED) equipment (ASTAR, NanoMEGAS, Belgium) installed in the FEI Talos 200X TEM microscope.

in situ Micropillar Compression Testing

The SPS MEO was manually polished on diamond paper (15 to 1 µm) and colloidal silica (20 nm) to minimize the surface roughness. The polished samples were mounted on a specimen stub to coat a thin layer of platinum at 40 mA for 60 s to enhance the electric conductivity in SEM. Micropillars with ∼3.5 µm in diameter and 7 µm in height (an aspect ratio of diameter to height of ∼ 1:2) were fabricated using a focused ion beam (FIB) in a Thermo Fisher Quanta 3D FEG scanning electron microscope. A series of decelerated currents (from 30 to 0.3 nA) was used to fabricate the pillars in the center of the initial SPS‐sintered sample, with a smooth surface and minimized tapering angle (average taper angle of 2°–3°). 5 pillars were tested at each temperature, including room temperature, 400°C, and 600°C. A Hysitron PI 88 × R PicoIndenter equipped with a piezoelectric actuator on the capacitive transducer was installed in Quanta 3D FEG microscope to perform in situ SEM micropillar compression tests. A 20‐µm diameter diamond flat punch was mounted on the transducer to collect force‐displacement data during compression. The specimen was tightly fixed by a V‐shape Mo clamp on a ceramic heating stage to maximize the heat transfer and minimize machine compliance. The temperature on both the heating stage and probe heater was ramped up simultaneously at a rate of 10°C/min and isothermally stabilized for 30 min to eliminate thermal drift. An average drift rate of less than 0.7 nm/s was monitored in the preloading process at each testing temperature. Each mechanical test was performed at a strain rate of 5 × 10−3 s−1 under displacement control mode. All compression tests were conducted under displacement‐controlled loading with a maximum displacement of 1400 nm, corresponding to approximately 20% strain. Tests were terminated upon catastrophic failure if failure occurred before reaching this displacement limit. Each stress‐strain curve shown in Figure 5 corresponds to an individual micropillar tested under identical conditions (same temperature, same strain rate, and comparable pillar geometry). For each temperature, 3–4 representative pillars are shown to illustrate the reproducibility and scatter inherent to small‐scale mechanical testing.

Results

Figure 1 summarizes the synthesis route and phase evolution of the (CuMgNiZn)O medium‐entropy oxide (MEO). The starting powders consist of multiple crystal structures including Rocksalt MgO and NiO, Tenorite CuO, and Wurtzite ZnO (Figure 1a). After HEBM for 30 hrs, the mixed oxide powders exhibit broadened diffraction peaks (Figure 1f), indicating severe lattice distortion and primary defect accumulation. After heat treatment at 900°C for 20 min, the Rocksalt phase remains dominant, while additional diffraction peaks indicate the presence of secondary phases. This phase separation is attributed to increased atomic mobility at high temperature and the reduced solid‐solution stability of the MEO system. The heat‐treated powders were subsequently consolidated by SPS. After SPS, the secondary‐phase peaks disappear and a single‐phase Rocksalt structure is obtained (Figure 1f), indicating the reincorporation of the secondary phases into the Rocksalt structure. The broader XRD peaks after SPS are attributed to increased lattice strain and defect accumulation, consistent with the TEM observations. Figure 1e illustrates the distribution of Mg, Ni, Cu, and Zn cations within the Rocksalt lattice. The corresponding crystallographic representation (Figure 1g) further confirms the Rocksalt structure for the 4‐element MEOs.

Figure 4 shows atomic‐scale TEM and STEM characterization of Cu‐rich secondary phases before deformation. A cross‐over shaped Cu‐rich secondary phases in Figure 4a exhibits lattice modulation around 0.9 nm. Geometric phase analysis (GPA) strain mapping in Figure 4b reveals compressive shear strain (εxy) localized within the cross‐over Cu‐rich secondary phases. An enlarged HRTEM image from the defect region in Figure 4c shows locally distorted lattice fringes with diffuse FFT streaking, consistent with lattice disorder and strain. Regions outside the X‐shaped defect in Figure 4d exhibit a highly‐ordered lattice with a 2H‐like ABAB stacking sequence, as confirmed by sharp FFT reflections. Cu‐rich band‐shaped secondary phases along the zone axis [001] in Figure 4e shows a coherent interface between precipitate and matrix with an angular relationship of approximately 84.9°. The corresponding GPA map in Figure 4f reveals a tensile strain band (εxx) in the Cu‐rich secondary phases. Within the Cu‐rich secondary phases in Figure 4g, a lattice expansion with d(200) = 0.236 nm is measured, whereas regions outside Cu‐rich secondary phases in Figure 4h exhibit d(200) = 0.223 nm. Notably, there are no obvious pre‐existing dislocations. Based on these structural observations together with the compositional analysis, these nanoscale, compositionally distinct Cu‐rich regions that retain the Rocksalt structure are referred to as “Cu‐rich secondary phases” throughout this work, consistent with the terminology commonly used in high‐entropy/entropy‐stabilized oxide system. The different compositions and structures of these two Cu‐rich secondary phases may reflect different stages of Cu segregation. The cross‐over shaped region shows moderate Cu enrichment with pronounced lattice distortion, whereas the band‐shaped region exhibits stronger Cu enrichment and a more ordered coherent structure. Therefore, the cross‐over shaped region may represent a precursor state that evolves into the more Cu‐rich band‐shaped region through further Cu segregation and structural reorganization. However, this precursor relationship should be further confirmed using in situ TEM experiment.

Discussion

Single Phase Formation in Medium Entropy Oxide

Whether removal of Co destabilizes the single phase represents a central question of the present entropy‐destabilization strategy. XRD results (Figure 1f) show that SPS processed (CuMgNiZn)O maintains a single‐phase Rocksalt structure without detectable secondary phases. For equimolar compositions, reducing the oxide system from five to four cations decreases configurational entropy from Rln5 to Rln4. Previous studies have shown that removal of individual cations from the prototypical Rocksalt high‐entropy oxide can still yield single‐phase four‐component solid solutions [33]. Moreover, stable Rocksalt oxides have been reported at entropy levels as low as ∼0.95R [34], indicating that high entropy is not a strict prerequisite for phase stabilization.

Importantly, phase stability in Rocksalt multicomponent oxides dependents not solely on entropy magnitude but also on the specific roles and structural compatibility of constituent cations. Recent studies indicate that the stability of Rocksalt structure is mainly controlled by cations favoring octahedral coordination, such as Mg2 + and Ni2 +, which provide a stable structural framework. Zn2 + can adopt Rocksalt coordination under multicomponent mixing conditions, contributing to solid‐solution formation. However, Co is not essential for maintaining the Rocksalt structure but mainly assists chemical homogenization during synthesis [33]. Therefore, although removal of Co may slightly reduce lattice stability, the remaining cation network remains sufficient to form a homogeneous Rocksalt phase.

Nature of the Cu‐Rich Secondary Phases Regions

Although XRD confirms formation of a single‐phase Rocksalt structure after SPS, TEM reveals Cu‐rich secondary phases embedded within the matrix (Figure 2, 3, 4). Similar Cu‐rich secondary phases have been widely reported in Rocksalt entropy‐stabilized (CoCuMgNiZn)O [35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. However, a quantitative description that links the Cu‐rich secondary phases stress field to solute redistribution has remained largely unexplored. Here we address this gap using the classical Cottrell‐Eshelby elastic interaction framework.

KAM and GND analyses (Figure 2f‐h) reveal pronounced misorientation and localized strain gradient associated with cross‐over and band‐like Cu‐rich secondary phases. GPA strain mapping (Figure 4) further reveals tensile and compressive strain fields surrounding these planar Cu‐rich secondary phases, indicating prominent localized stress within the lattice. where σ ij represents the local stress associated with Cu‐rich secondary phases, εij∗ denotes the eigenstrain introduced by solute substitution, and V is the effective interaction volume associated with the substitutional solute atom. Segregation becomes energetically favorable when the product σijεij∗ is positive (ΔE < 0), resulting in a reduction of elastic strain energy. Consequently, Cu‐rich secondary phases act as sinks for solutes to accommodate lattice strain. Among the constituent cations, Cu2 + produces the largest lattice distortion due to its Jahn–Teller active octahedral coordination [33, 43, 44], leading to a larger eigenstrain (εij∗) compared with Mg2 +, Ni2 +, and Zn2 +. Consequently, segregation of Cu to planar Cu‐rich secondary phases most effectively reduces local elastic energy. In contrast, Mg2+ exhibits a nearly symmetric ionic configuration and moderate ionic size mismatch, generating limited lattice distortion and thus a weak elastic driving force for segregation [45]. As Cu preferentially occupies Cu‐rich secondary phases, Ni‐ and Zn‐rich species are correspondingly redistributed into the surrounding matrix to maintain local structural and compositional equilibrium. In addition to the thermodynamic driving force, kinetic factors may further promote segregation. Cu2 + is known to exhibit relatively greater cation mobility than Mg2+, Ni2+, Zn2+ in Rocksalt oxides due to its Jahn‐Teller induced lattice distortion and weaker bonding environment [46]. The relatively higher diffusivity enables rapid redistribution of Cu toward defect strain fields within the short SPS sintering duration.

With increasing Cu enrichment, the initially distorted region evolves into a continuous Cu‐rich secondary phases (Figure 4e,f). GPA strain mapping shows that the strain is greater inside the band core (εx x ≈ 0.04) and gradually decreases toward the band boundary (εx x ≈ ∼0.01), indicating strain redistribution. The absence of periodic misfit dislocations across the band/matrix boundary indicates that the interface remains largely coherent. Such a coherent strain region can redistribute local deformation, reduce interfacial stress concentration, and delay strain localization, thereby promoting more stable plastic flow compared with shear‐dominated cross‐over shaped defects. However, few dislocations are observed in Figure 4e–h, which indicates that most of the lattice mismatch is accommodated internally within the Cu‐rich band prior to interface stabilization.

Deformation Mechanisms

Brittle Feature at RT

Despite the presence of Cu‐rich secondary phases and pre‐existing defects that may act as potential dislocation nucleation sites, the overall plastic strain at RT remains low. TEM observations reveal microcracks with sizes (a) of approximately 350 nm. The critical condition for crack propagation can be estimated using linear elastic fracture mechanics [61, 62]:

Using the estimated indentation‐derived fracture toughness KIC≈1.9MPam, ​we estimate that σ c ≈ 1.8GPa. This critical stress is lower than the stress at which the first surface crack is observed during in‐situ SEM compression (∼2.7 GPa), suggesting that crack initiation likely occurred within the pillar interior before being detected at the surface. Consequently, deformation is governed by internal crack nucleation and subsequent crack propagation rather than sustained dislocation plasticity.

Deformation Transition Regime at 400°C

Figure 10 summarizes the temperature dependence of the yield stress measured in the micropillar compression tests. The yield stress was determined from the stress‐strain curves as the point where the loading curve first deviates from the elastic behavior [63]. The average yield stress (Table S1) decreases from 1.8 GPa at RT to 1.4 GPa at 400°C, followed by a moderate increase to 1.6 GPa at 600°C. The moderate recovery in yield stress at 600°C may be associated with the increased contribution of Cu‐rich secondary phases strengthening. At elevated temperature, enhanced dislocation activity promotes interactions between mobile dislocations and the Cu‐rich secondary phase regions, which can act as obstacles to dislocation motion. Together with the grain refinement observed in Figure 9i, these defect‐dislocation interactions may partially offset thermal softening. High‐Temperature Plastic Regime at 600°C

At 600°C, the stress‐strain curves exhibit pronounced strain hardening accompanied by multiple slip traces (Figure 5i,l). TEM observations further reveal the formation of stacking faults (Figure 8e). These observations indicate that plastic deformation at this temperature is dominated by extensive dislocation activity and defect interactions. In Rocksalt oxides, dislocation glide on {111} planes can locally produce stacking faults through partial dislocation activity. As deformation proceeds, interactions between mobile dislocations, stacking faults, and nanoscale Cu‐rich secondary phases create strong obstacles to dislocation motion. These interactions promote dislocation accumulation and enhance strain hardening.

Comparison With (CoCuMgNiZn)O and a Classical Rocksalt Structured MgO

It is noted that the micropillar compression tests for (CuMgNiZn)O and (CoCuMgNiZn)O were conducted under the same experimental conditions, including identical FIB fabrication procedures, pillar dimensions, strain rate, and heating stage. Therefore, the mechanical responses of these two material systems can be directly compared.

A direct comparison with the five‐component (CoCuMgNiZn)O system in our previous work [19] reveals that removing Co enhances plastic deformability without compromising the mechanical strength. At room temperature, the fracture strain increases from 1.5% ± 0.3% to 2.0% ± 0.3% (∼1.3 ×) while maintaining comparable strength (∼3.2 to 3.0 GPa). The advantage becomes more pronounced at elevated temperatures. At 400°C, strain to failure increases from 4.5% ± 0.5% to 6.1% ± 0.1% (∼1.4×) with a moderate strength reduction (2.7 to 2.1 GPa), and at 600°C, failure strain increases from 9.9% ± 0.9% to beyond 15.9% ± 0.1% (∼1.6×) with enhanced flow strength (2.1 to 2.4 GPa). Correspondingly, the stress‐strain plots for the four‐component system exhibit pronounced serrated flow at 400°C (Figure 5e) and stronger work hardening at 600°C (Figure 5i), indicating improved defect‐mediated plasticity. These results demonstrate that reducing configurational complexity can promote strain accommodation, making MEOs promising candidates for achieving enhanced plasticity (∼60% increase in strain) without sacrificing strength.

Using the Vickers indentation method established in our previous work [19], the HEO exhibited a hardness of 7.44 ± 0.27 GPa and a fracture toughness of 1.42 ± 0.02 MPa·m. In comparison, the MEO exhibited a higher hardness of 8.05 ± 0.21 GPa and a substantially improved fracture toughness of 1.92 ± 0.06 MPa·m, corresponding to increases of approximately 8.2% and 35.2%, respectively. As shown in Figure S2, cracks in the HEO propagates along relatively straight paths, whereas crack deflection is observed in the MEO.

MgO, a classical Rocksalt structured oxide, provides a useful reference for evaluating mechanical behavior of the MEO in this work. The deformation response of MgO is strongly dependent on crystal orientation, microstructure, temperature, and specimen size. For example, [100]‐oriented MgO single crystals can undergo approximately 6% plastic deformation at room temperature before fracture [77], whereas bulk polycrystalline MgO exhibits only approximately 0.6% total strain at fracture at room temperature and approximately 2% above 600°C [78]. Increasing temperature promotes dislocation mobility and activates additional slip systems, leading to improved plastic deformation [79, 80]. In comparison, the present MEO exhibits enhanced deformability, reaching ∼15.9% strain at 600°C with strain hardening. Unlike single phase MgO, the MEO contains Cu‐rich secondary phases that can act as both dislocation sources and barriers, facilitating dislocation activity and accumulation during deformation.

Conclusion

In this work, enhanced plastic deformability in the Co‐free medium‐entropy oxide (CuMgNiZn)O is achieved through controlled entropy destabilization. The formation of nanoscale Cu‐rich secondary phases introduces local lattice distortion and internal stress fields that strongly influence the deformation behavior. With increasing temperature, the deformation mechanism evolves from the crack‐dominated brittle fracture at room temperature to the thermally activated dislocation glide at 400°C, and further to stacking‐fault mediated plasticity and dislocation network induced hardening at 600°C. These defect interactions promote dislocation nucleation, transport, and storage, enabling significant strain hardening and large plastic strain at elevated temperatures. The results demonstrate that entropy destabilization can activate defect‐assisted plasticity in multicomponent oxides, providing an effective strategy to overcome the intrinsic brittleness of oxide ceramics.
