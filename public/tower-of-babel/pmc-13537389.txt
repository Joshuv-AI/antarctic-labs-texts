The Potential Physiologic Mechanisms by Which Early-Life Nutrition May Influence Type 2 Diabetes Pathophysiology in Humans
Authors: Elaine A. Yu, Melissa A. Chapnick, Aryeh D. Stein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe nutrient deficiency in utero has been associated with elevated type 2 diabetes (T2D) odds across the life span. Optimal early-life nutrition interventions that mitigate T2D in adulthood remain unclear, largely due to limited understanding of the underlying biologic pathways. In this review, we summarize recent evidence of physiologic mechanisms by which early-life nutrition could influence T2D pathophysiology in adulthood. We focus on studies evaluating genes as effect modifiers as well as epigenetic reprogramming of gene expression, structural development of key tissues and organs, and accelerated biological aging as causal mediators. We present evidence regarding biologic alterations that are nutrition sensitive during the first 1,000 days of life and could persistently impact glucose-insulin homeostasis across decades. This review emphasizes studies published from 2015 to 2025, with large-scale biological data generated from high-throughput technologies.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) prevalence has risen substantially over the past three decades (100). Globally, the number of people living with diabetes is projected to increase by 45% from 589 million in 2024 to 853 million in 2050 (71). Among people with diabetes, more than 90% have T2D (71). Preventing T2D is challenging because its multifactorial etiology arises from the complex interplay of genetics (126), the exposome—including lifestyle factors such as diet (80), physical activity, and environmental exposures—and gene–exposome interactions across the life span (130). Although heritability estimates are substantial, ranging from 31% to 72% in twin and family studies (4, 10, 36, 150), shifts in population genetics alone cannot explain this rapid increase. This trend emphasizes the need to identify modifiable environmental risk factors to mitigate the growing global burden of T2D.

Nutrition, especially during critical periods of in utero and early postnatal development, is an important environmental factor influencing T2D risk (13, 16). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that early-life exposure to severe undernutrition is linked to a higher risk of developing T2D in adulthood (58, 60, 85) (Figure 1). This association has been observed consistently across populations born in China, Ukraine, Israel, the Netherlands, Germany, and other European countries (60, 85). A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials suggests that early-life nutrition interventions have protective effects on fasting blood glucose concentrations (58). Murine models support the significance of the in utero environment, particularly the intergenerational consequences of maternal obesogenic diet on impaired glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity among offspring (78). Efforts to translate these findings into prevention strategies are constrained by an incomplete understanding of the biological pathways involved. This challenge is compounded by the long latency period between early-life exposures and adult diabetes development in humans.

This review aims to summarize recent evidence of potential physiologic mechanisms by which early-life nutrition may influence T2D pathophysiology in humans. We provide an updated overview of recent evidence regarding putative mechanisms, including gene-environment interactions (Section 2), epigenetic reprogramming of gene expression (Section 3), structural development of key tissues and organs (Section 4), and accelerated physiological aging (Section 5). We focus on literature published in the past decade (2015–2025) regarding biological pathways that could be influenced during the first 1,000 days of life, the period between conception and the second birthday, and underly lasting metabolic impacts in adulthood (Supplemental Figure 1, Supplemental Tables 1–4).Our review emphasizes studies that incorporate large-scale biological data generated from high-throughput technologies.

GENETICS AS AN EFFECT MODIFIER

Genetic Influences on Type 2 Diabetes Risk Across the Human Life Span

The adult human body contains 1013–1014 human cells (57), which are predominantly somatic. Because somatic cells replicate via mitosis to produce genetically identical diploid cells, genetic influences on T2D risk are generally considered nonmodifiable. We therefore consider evidence evaluating genetic predisposition as a potential effect modifier of the relationship between early-life nutrition and adult T2D risk (Supplemental Figure 2).

Since the sequencing of the human genome in 2003, research consortia have used genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and genetic loci associated with human disease (25). Recent studies estimate ~243–611 genetic loci associated with T2D (25, 91, 126). Common and rare genetic variants have been associated with both early- and adult-onset T2D, though genes had a greater relative importance (explained variance) on early-onset T2D (94). Relatedly, a T2D polygenic risk score was associated with higher T2D probability among youth relative to adults (94, 122). The genetic predisposition for T2D is distinct from type 1 diabetes, although it shares allelic variants (polymorphisms, mutations) associated with maturity-onset diabetes of the young, a rare inherited form of diabetes that typically develops in young adults (48).

Moving beyond T2D diagnosis and status, studies have evaluated the extent to which genetic predisposition may influence disease progression and severity. Diabetes-induced microvascular and macrovascular complications differ by family history and by certain candidate genes (25). Other studies, however, have found no evidence of genetic contributions to T2D-associated microvascular complications, including nephropathy (89). Larger studies to address the complex challenges of defining T2D complications are needed (25, 48). More specifically, as many early genetic studies focused on populations of European ancestry, more GWAS of T2D should include multiethnic or admixed populations (53, 112).

Evaluating the Fetal Insulin Hypothesis: Genetic Contributions to the Low Birth Weight–Type 2 Diabetes Relationship

The developmental origins of the health and disease paradigm posits that discordance between early- and later-life environments, particularly nutrient availability and infection, increases the risk of noncommunicable diseases such as T2D (7, 123). In an early formulation, Hattersley & Tooke (56) proposed the fetal insulin hypothesis, which suggests that an insulin-resistant genotype simultaneously underlies low birth weight, insulin resistance across the life span, glucose intolerance, and diabetes.

Evidence from recent genome-wide association studies.

GWAS have linked genetic predisposition to impaired pancreatic β-cell function, reduced insulin secretion, higher likelihood of low birth weight, and T2D in adulthood (68, 112). A meta-analysis of multiancestry GWAS with 153,781 participants identified 60 fetal genetic loci associated with birth weight (64). Based on pathway enrichment analysis, protein products of these loci were over-represented in insulin signaling and glucose homeostasis pathways (64). An ancestry-specific GWAS found that 44 T2D-associated loci had differing effect sizes in African American participants (N = 56,092) compared to participants of European ancestry (N = 1,114,458) (138). These findings underscore the need for further evaluation of the genetic contributions of both common and rare variants to low birth weight and T2D in ancestry-specific populations (112). Among Helsinki Birth Cohort Study participants (N = 2,003), allelic variants (HHEX, CDKN2A/2B, JAZF1) interacted with birth weight to affect T2D risk (34, 104). Specifically, participants with lower birth weight and higher-risk variants, relative to those without, had elevated T2D risk (34, 104). Some recent GWAS and candidate gene studies have shown evidence of specific genetic loci associated with low birth weight and increased adult T2D risk (56).

Evidence from twin studies.

Two studies (N = 18,230 and 11,162) from a Swedish population-based cohort of same-sex twins with known zygosity, the Screening Across the Lifespan Twin Study, evaluated the relationship between birth weight and T2D (70, 74). Both studies found consistent directionality of associations between lower birth weight and elevated T2D odds during adulthood after adjusting for twin pair clustering (70, 74). In a case-control analysis of discordant twin pairs (i.e., T2D in only one twin), lower birth weight was associated with higher odds of T2D among dizygotic twins [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 1.38; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.02, 1.85] but not monozygotic twins (aOR 1.02; 95% CI 0.63, 1.64) (74). No differences in associations within or between twin pairs were reported in an earlier analysis (70), and authors suggest this discrepancy could be related to a smaller sample size and fewer available covariates since the Swedish Twin Registry was subsequently updated (74).

Genetic Differences in Association Between In Utero Famine Exposure and Adulthood Type 2 Diabetes Risk

In a historical cohort among people in China, prenatal famine exposure was associated with diabetes likelihood (aOR 1.47; 95% CI 1.13, 1.93), relative to people without famine exposure (140). Participants with fetal (aOR 2.60; 95% CI 1.71, 3.93) or childhood (aOR 1.95; 95% CI 1.24, 3.05) famine exposure and higher genetic risk (top tertile of genetic risk score) had higher odds of diabetes, relative to participants without famine exposure and lower genetic risk (lowest tertile) (140). The study team suggested that famine exposure and T2D genetic susceptibility could have additive impacts on T2D risk (140).

Indirect Pathways: Genetic Predisposition of Other Nutrition-Sensitive Factors

WAS and Mendelian randomization studies have identified indirect associations suggesting that genetic predisposition to key risk factors may also contribute to T2D etiology. For example, there are allelic variants associated with intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) (26), birth weight (64, 131, 145), obesity (e.g., FTO locus), and cardiovascular disease (43), which are risk factors for T2D and insulin resistance. Additionally, allelic variants associated with pancreatic β-cell survival and function (e.g., GLIS3) (92, 149), as well as insulin secretion, have been linked to low birth weight and increased T2D risk (68). From a GWAS from the UK Biobank and Early Growth Genetics Consortium, 209 lead SNPs were associated with maternal (N = 321,223) and offspring (N = 230,069) birth weight (145). In the same study, authors subsequently conducted two-sample Mendelian randomization with offspring birth weight as the outcome and fasting glucose as an exposure (145). Based on 33 fasting glucose–associated SNPs, infant birth weight and maternal glucose were positively correlated, independent of direct fetal effects based on the offspring’s fasting glucose genetic score (145). Separately, Tian et al. (131) also proposed that low birth weight, regardless of whether it results from genetics or fetal undernutrition, is a proxy indicator of early-life insulin resistance, which is a view more consistent with the hypothesis that undernutrition in utero results in altered body structure, function, metabolism, and programming, which are associated with T2D risk in adulthood (54). Hales & Barker (54) further refined the thrifty phenotype hypothesis as stemming from an environmental nutrient availability mismatch during prenatal and postnatal periods.

EPIGENETIC PROGRAMMING AS A MEDIATOR

Epigenetic Mechanisms Connecting Early-Life Nutrition and Type 2 Diabetes

Epigenetic modifications—chemical changes to RNA, DNA, and chromatin—play a central role in developmental programming by regulating gene expression and guiding cellular differentiation and development (127, 135). While the DNA sequence itself remains stable, the epigenome is extensively reprogrammed during fetal development, influencing gene expression and impacting cells, tissues, and the organism (127). Some changes are stable through cell division and can persist across the life span, while certain epigenetic patterns can even be transmitted to offspring, linking the experiences of one generation—such as diet, stress, or toxin exposure—to gene regulation in the next (127). Such environmentally influenced patterns may help explain the heritability gap, where genetic variation alone accounts for only part of T2D risk (127).

Stöger (124) proposed the thrifty epigenotype hypothesis, suggesting that under normal conditions, metabolism develops along a healthy trajectory, but in the context of exposure to malnutrition in utero, epigenetic alterations occur in genes related to energy balance and metabolism (135). These changes are hypothesized to affect pathways critical to metabolic health, including insulin production in pancreatic β-cells, growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling, adipose tissue storage, and satiety regulation (135). While adaptive in contexts of scarcity, these changes may increase cardiometabolic disease risk in environments of abundance (135). Epigenetic regulation occurs through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including DNA methylation at cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites, noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), and histone modifications (135). Together, these mechanisms form an integrated epigenetic landscape through which early-life exposures can leave lasting marks on metabolic and overall health (Figure 2).

DNA Methylation at Specific Cytosine-Phosphate-Guanine Sites

DNA methylation is one of the most well-characterized epigenetic mechanisms and plays a key role in regulating gene expression. It involves the covalent addition of a methyl group (CH3) to cytosine residues next to guanine nucleotides in the DNA sequence—known as CpG sites. These CpG dinucleotides are unevenly distributed throughout the genome; regions rich in CpG sites, called CpG islands, are often located in gene promoter regions, where methylation typically suppresses transcriptional activity by altering chromatin structure and reducing transcription factor binding (127). CpG sites also occur within gene bodies and intergenic regions, where methylation can influence transcription elongation, alternative promoter usage, or splicing, depending on their genomic context (75).

The methylation process is catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), a family of enzymes responsible for both establishing and maintaining methylation patterns. De novo DNMTs (DNMT3A and DNMT3B) generate new methylation marks across the genome, setting up cell type–specific expression programs (127).Maintenance methyltransferases, such as DNMT1, copy existing methylation patterns to daughter DNA strands during replication, ensuring that the epigenetic landscape is stably inherited through cell division (88). This stability allows DNA methylation patterns established during early life to persist across tissues and time, providing a potential mechanism through which early-life environmental exposures, such as nutrition, can have long-term biological effects.

Early development is therefore a critical window for epigenetic programming. During this period, rapid cell division, differentiation, and organogenesis coincide with high DNMT activity, making the methylome especially responsive to environmental inputs (127). Nutritional factors play central roles in this process, as one-carbon metabolism provides the methyl groups required for DNA methylation (Figure 3). Nutrients such as folate, choline, methionine, and vitamins B2, B6, and B12 act as methyl donors or cofactors in the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) pathway, the universal methyl group donor for methylation reactions (14). Disruptions in maternal or early-life nutrient supply can therefore influence SAM availability and alter methylation at key regulatory sites, with potential downstream effects on growth, metabolism, and disease risk later in life.

Differences in micronutrient availability, particularly of methyl-donor nutrients, have been shown to directly affect the establishment of DNA methylation patterns. In a mouse model, pregnant agouti mice fed diets with varying levels of folate, choline, betaine, and vitamin B12 produced genetically identical offspring that differed markedly in coat color, appetite, body fat, and glucose tolerance (146). These phenotypic differences were associated with altered methylation at an intracisternal A particle near the agouti gene, demonstrating how maternal nutrition can durably reprogram gene expression via DNA methylation (146). Similarly, research from rural Gambia leveraged seasonal variation in diet quality as a natural experiment in early-life epigenetic programming (95). Women who conceived during the rainy season, when diets are richer in micronutrient-dense leafy vegetables, had higher plasma concentrations of folate, betaine, and methionine, as well as higher SAM to S-adenosylhomocysteine ratios, reflecting greater methylation potential (32). Their offspring exhibited higher methylation at multiple metastable epialleles compared with offspring conceived in the dry season (147). Metastable epialleles are regions of the genome where DNA methylation is established very early in development and maintained across all tissues, yet varies between individuals (147). These findings suggest that maternal methyl-donor nutrition during conception and early pregnancy can influence the offspring epigenome.

The literature linking early-life nutrition to later metabolic outcomes is extensive and heterogeneous, encompassing a wide range of exposures and epigenetic marks across the genome. Despite this diversity, findings appear to converge on a set of core biological pathways, particularly those related to pancreatic β-cell and insulin signaling, growth hormone and endocrine function, and appetite regulation.

Pancreatic β-cell and insulin signaling pathways.

Evidence from animal models shows that the intrauterine environment can epigenetically reprogram genes involved in pancreatic development and insulin secretion. In murine models, maternal dietary restriction (50% calories) leads to hypermethylation and reduced expression of pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 (PDX1), a transcription factor essential for β-cell differentiation and insulin secretion (136). These changes persist postnatally, restricting β-cell proliferation and increasing susceptibility to glucose intolerance in later life (136). IUGR models similarly demonstrate reduced PDX1 expression alongside altered methylation of key transcription factors such as FoxO1 and MafA, both essential for β-cell maturation (136). Uterine artery ligation experiments, which induce fetal hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, further show promoter hypermethylation and histone deacetylation at PDX1, impairing β-cell maturation and resulting in altered methylation at more than 1,000 loci in the fetal pancreas (127).

Human evidence supports the idea that early-life undernutrition can epigenetically modify genes regulating insulin signaling and pancreatic β-cell function. In the Dutch Hunger Winter, researchers examined genome-wide DNA methylation in 60 adults (mean age 58.3) prenatally exposed to famine and their unexposed same-sex siblings, identifying six persistent differentially methylated regions (P-DMRs), including hypermethylation of INSR, the insulin receptor gene central to glucose uptake and insulin signaling, and SMAD7, involved in pancreatic β-cell function (132). DNA methylation at the INSR P-DMR was positively associated with birth weight among famine-exposed individuals, and this association remained significant after adjusting for gestational age, adult body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status, diet, smoking, and multiple testing, suggesting that INSR methylation may mediate part of the link between early gestational famine exposure and prenatal growth (132). Similarly, a study of the Chinese famine (235 adults, ~55 years of age) found higher INSR methylation in prenatally exposed individuals, which was positively associated with triglycerides and negatively with high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (144). Together, these studies show consistent INSR hypermethylation following prenatal famine exposure, indicating a persistent epigenetic imprint that may reduce transcription and contribute to later-life metabolic dysfunction. These human findings align with animal studies demonstrating altered methylation of β-cell transcription factors, highlighting the role of early-life epigenetic programming in glucose homeostasis.

Growth hormone and endocrine pathways.

Murine models of maternal protein restriction during gestation altered DNA methylation and expression of the IGF2/H19 locus in offspring livers, an effect that could be prevented with folic acid supplementation (137). Similarly, murine models of uterine artery ligation-induced IUGR in grandmother rats produced epigenetic changes at the same locus that persisted into granddaughters, highlighting potential transgenerational effects of developmental programming (137). In sheep, maternal calorie restriction induces DNA hypomethylation at IGF2/H19, increasing IGF2 expression and augmenting offspring cortisol responses, indicating cross talk between growth and stress-response pathways (127).

Evidence from humans also supports a role for IGF2 in developmental epigenetic programming of T2D. In the Dutch Hunger Winter cohort, individuals exposed to severe famine in utero showed persistently lower IGF2 methylation six decades later compared to unexposed controls (59). Similarly, Tobi et al. (133) found that periconceptional famine exposure was associated with small reductions in IGF2 DMR methylation, but not at H19. They also showed that genetic differences at IGF2 affected methylation, and that the effects of famine and genetics were separate but cumulative, meaning that having both prenatal famine exposure and certain IGF2 genetic variants led to slightly larger differences in methylation (133). This suggests that IGF2 methylation is influenced by both early-life environment and genetic variation, whereas H19 methylation was unaffected (133). In contrast, evidence from the Chinese Great Famine (1959–1961) indicates that severe early-life undernutrition was associated with increased IGF2 methylation at specific CpG sites (118). Together, results suggest that IGF2 methylation is sensitive to nutritional and hormonal milieu of early development, although inconsistent directionality between studies emphasizes the need to elucidate key drivers of these differences.

Appetite regulation.

Proopiomelanocortin (POMC) is a polypeptide precursor that produces peptides with key roles in regulating satiety. POMC gene expression can be modified by epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modifications, linking early-life nutrition to long-term metabolic outcomes, as demonstrated by animal models (15). Maternal overnutrition, modeled by high-fat or high-energy diets during pregnancy, generally leads to hypermethylation of the POMC promoter, higher birth weight, increased postnatal food intake, and elevated adult body weight (15). These effects are most pronounced when postnatal diet is normal, suggesting prenatal exposure has a dominant influence, though postnatal high-energy feeding can further modulate methylation (15). Conversely, maternal undernutrition during the periconceptional or early gestational period, modeled as caloric restriction or reduced protein intake, typically results in POMC hypomethylation, increased histone acetylation, and, in some studies, sex-specific effects on offspring weight and fat mass (15).

Human studies suggest that periconceptional nutrition can influence offspring POMC methylation, consistent with animal models. Evidence comes from Gambian cohorts, where maternal levels of one-carbon metabolites (folate, betaine, choline, B12) and season of conception were associated with methylation at the POMC gene, independent of maternal body weight (15). These methylation patterns are established early in embryonic development, stable across tissues, and persistent from birth into adolescence (15). In human studies, participants with higher methylation at certain regions of the POMC gene tend to have higher BMI, higher fasting triglycerides, lower HDL cholesterol, and higher insulin levels, even when controlling for differences in body fat or weight (15). In conjunction with animal models, these results indicate that specific maternal nutritional exposures around conception could epigenetically program POMC gene expression, influencing appetite regulation, energy balance, and metabolic health.

Noncoding RNA

ncRNAs are a key component of epigenetic regulation influenced by early-life nutrition. These RNAs are transcribed from DNA but are not translated into proteins; instead, they regulate gene expression by degrading messenger RNA (mRNA), silencing DNA sequences, or inhibiting translation (14, 152). Although most of the genome is transcribed, only 1–2% encodes proteins, with the remainder producing ncRNAs that have important regulatory roles (14). Once disregarded as junk, ncRNAs are now recognized as critical regulators of cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, development, and apoptosis (152). Major classes of ncRNAs include microRNAs (miRNAs), long ncRNAs, circular RNAs, and transfer RNAs, each using distinct mechanisms to control gene expression (14, 152).

Maternal nutrition can shape offspring metabolism through ncRNAs, particularly miRNAs, which record prenatal exposures, influence fetal development, and help predict long-term risks for metabolic diseases such as T2D and cardiovascular disease (14, 152). miRNAs are short molecules (~18–25 nucleotides) that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by promoting mRNA degradation or blocking translation (14). They can also bind gene promoters to induce DNA methylation and repressive histone modifications, and they often act in concert with other epigenetic mechanisms to control gene expression (14).

The let-7 family of miRNAs include 13 members encoding 9 mature miRNAs in the human genome (22). Let-7 family miRNAs have key functional roles in metabolic regulation, influencing glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and autophagy under nutrient stress. Early-life nutritional exposures, including maternal high-fat or low-protein diets, can alter let-7 expression in offspring liver and skeletal muscle, contributing to metabolic programming (14). For example, in offspring of high fat diet–fed, obesity-prone dams, hepatic let-7a is upregulated, leading to reductions in AMPKα2 and LIN28A, which disrupt fatty acid, sterol, and glycogen metabolism, elevate triglycerides, glucose, and cholesterol, and increase susceptibility to nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (152). Let-7c expression in sperm of fathers fed high-fat or low-protein diets—and in sperm of their offspring—is altered, suggesting a mechanism for transgenerational metabolic effects (14). Notably, global inhibition of the let-7 family using anti-miRNAs can prevent fat accumulation, highlighting let-7 as a potential therapeutic target for metabolic disease (42, 152).

miR-126 is highly expressed in endothelial cells, where it regulates vascular integrity and angiogenesis, and its levels are reduced in adults with T2D, correlating with diabetic complications (152). Early-life overnutrition, such as in cases of macrosomia, is associated with elevated miR-126, which may program increased risk of obesity and diabetes later in life through the FOXO and PI3K/AKT pathways (152). Beyond its vascular roles, miR-126 also regulates critical metabolic proteins in adipose tissue, including IRS-1, a key substrate of insulin signaling, and LNPK, which stabilizes endoplasmic reticulum structure (152). Maternal diet-induced obesity upregulates miR-126 in offspring adipose tissue, reducing IRS-1 and LNPK levels, impairing glucose tolerance, and promoting insulin resistance even before obesity develops (152). Postnatal exposure to obesogenic diets can further exacerbate IRS-1 degradation, highlighting miR-126 as a mediator linking early-life overnutrition to later risk of T2D (152).

Several other miRNAs have been implicated in linking early-life nutrition to later metabolic outcomes (152). Maternal low-protein diets upregulate miR-15b, miR-143, miR-199a-3p, and miR-342 in offspring, impairing pancreatic β-cell function, insulin signaling, and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathways, which contribute to glucose intolerance and insulin resistance (152). Other miRNAs, such as miR-29a and miR-130a, influence glucolipid metabolism in liver, muscle, and adipose tissue, and their early-life expression may serve as predictive biomarkers or therapeutic targets for future metabolic disease risk (152).

Histone Posttranslational Modifications

Histone modifications are key epigenetic mechanisms that regulate gene activity without altering the DNA sequence itself (14). In the nucleus, DNA is wrapped around histone proteins to form nucleosomes, which help compact the genome into chromatin (14). Beyond packaging, histones play an active role in controlling gene expression through chemical modifications—such as acetylation and methylation—on their tail regions (14). These modifications act as molecular switches, influencing how tightly DNA is wound and whether genes in that region are accessible for transcription (14). Loosely packed chromatin (euchromatin) allows genes to be expressed, whereas tightly packed chromatin (heterochromatin) leads to gene silencing (14). Histone modifications are dynamic and reversible, enabling cells to rapidly respond to developmental cues or environmental factors including dietary components such as folate and other methyl donor nutrients (127). These processes provide one mechanism through which early-life nutrition can shape long-term patterns of gene regulation and influence susceptibility to metabolic disease in adulthood (14).

IUGR, a condition in which the fetus receives inadequate nutrients or oxygen, has been widely used to study developmental programming of metabolic disease (14). In rat models, uterine artery ligation induces fetal hypoxia and undernutrition, leading to long-term epigenetic changes in genes essential for growth and metabolism (127). A key target is PDX1, which is crucial for pancreatic β-cell development and insulin secretion (14). In IUGR fetuses, histone acetylation—an activating mark—at the PDX1 promoter is reduced, decreasing transcriptional accessibility (14). After birth, repressive histone methylation (H3K9me2) accumulates at the same site, and by adulthood, DNA methylation is added, permanently silencing the gene (14). These sequential epigenetic changes result in impaired β-cell maturation and reduced insulin secretion, which are associated with increased susceptibility to T2D in adulthood (127). Similar effects are seen for IGF1, where altered DNA methylation patterns suppress expression and disrupt fetal growth signaling (127). Together, these studies demonstrate how intrauterine stressors linked to IUGR can trigger a cascade of histone and DNA modifications that durably alter metabolic gene expression and disease risk later in life (129).

IUGR can reduce expression of GLUT4 in offspring skeletal muscle through histone H3K14 deacetylation (107, 127). Similarly, in protein-restricted pregnancies, decreased hepatic HNF4α expression is linked to reduced activating histone marks (H3K4 methylation) and increased repressive marks (H3K9 and H3K27 methylation), even in the absence of major DNA methylation changes (14). Low-protein diets during gestation also decrease histone acetylation in the fetal liver, lowering Cyp7a1, which encodes for a protein essential for bile acid metabolism and cholesterol excretion, expression, and raising cholesterol levels (127). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that early-life nutrient restriction—whether through reduced oxygen, total calories, or protein—can reprogram metabolic pathways in key tissues via histone modifications, setting the stage for cardiometabolic disease risk later in life (129).

Maternal overnutrition and high-fat diets induce distinct epigenetic changes that can disrupt offspring metabolism. In primate models, maternal high-fat diets (35–60% of total calories from fat) lead to hepatic histone hyperacetylation at H3K9, H3K14, and H3K18, elevated triglyceride accumulation, increased DNMT1 expression, and reduced HDAC1 activity in offspring (127). These chromatin alterations suggest an overall shift toward transcriptional activation of lipid metabolic pathways. Consistent with this, maternal obesity in primates produces widespread histone hyperacetylation, reflecting globally increased chromatin accessibility and altered metabolic gene expression (129). Conversely, physical activity can normalize histone deacetylase function and enhance GLUT4 synthesis in adipose tissue, demonstrating that postnatal environmental factors may partially reverse diet-induced epigenetic dysregulation (127).

EMBRYOLOGIC ADAPTATION OF KEY METABOLIC TISSUES, ORGANS, AND SYSTEMS AS MEDIATORS

Early-Life Nutrient Availability and the Development of Metabolic Organs

In a growing embryo, the number and diversity of cells rapidly increase (113). By week six after conception, embryonic tissues have begun differentiating into more than 200 cell types, which are the building blocks of nearly all major organs (113). During the first year after birth, infants accumulate cellular mass at a substantially higher rate than in adulthood (116). Average cellular turnover is estimated as 330 billion cells/day, the equivalent of 4 million cells/second, among adults (116), but this rate varies dramatically across tissue types. Neurons, glial cells, cardiomyocytes, pancreatic islet β-cells (120), and myocytes have mean cellular life spans of years and decades, considerably longer than many other cell types (116) such as monocytes (115) and neutrophils (108).

The development of metabolic organs such as the pancreas and cardiovascular system (73, 76) is highly sensitive to nutrient availability during the periconceptional (e.g., maternal dietary intake and nutritional status, placental development and function) (5) and early postnatal environments (35). Reduced fetal growth is linked to lower insulin secretion and higher lifelong risk of T2D in offspring (7, 9, 54).

Adipose Tissues

Fetal adipocyte precursor cells begin developing during the second and third trimesters (31). Fat lobule precursor cells differentiate into mature adipocyte cells, which increase in number (hyperplasia) and size (hypertrophy) in adipose tissue depots (31). Interscapular brown adipose tissue depots are larger in infancy and diminish with age (24). Adipogenesis is jointly regulated by nutrients, hormones, and epigenetic modifications (31).

Adipose tissues and body composition.

Infants with low birth weight show more visceral and intra-abdominal adipose and lower subcutaneous adipose tissue (99). One hypothesis is that individuals with low birth weight have fewer adipocytes and more limited capacity for adipose tissue expansion (99). When expansion peaks, adipocytes can become overloaded and exhausted, increasing the risk for insulin resistance and T2D (99).

Children born to mothers with diabetes or insulin resistance have increased likelihood for fetal adiposity and impaired glucose tolerance in adulthood (69, 82). Maternal diabetes is correlated with fetal hyperinsulinemia, a key risk factor for excessive fetal growth (69) and macrosomia (134). Adiposity indicators, such as body fat percentage, fat mass, and BMI, at each life stage are positively associated with adiposity in the subsequent stage (e.g., fetal and neonatal, neonatal and childhood, childhood and adolescence) (45, 69). Higher BMI, a proxy measure of excess adiposity, is a risk factor for T2D (82).

Adipokines and the neuroendocrine system.

Adiponectin, leptin, and other cytokines are produced and secreted by adipocytes. Adipokines may affect long-term T2D risk due to their roles as signaling mediators and hormones that regulate fetal growth and energy balance (33). Insulin-sensitive tissues have adiponectin receptors (33). At the maternal-fetal interface, adiponectin regulates nutrient availability for the fetus (33). Maternal blood concentrations of adiponectin are negatively correlated with infant birth weight (33). In contrast, higher cord blood concentrations of adiponectin are positively correlated with infant birth weight (33, 101).

Higher maternal adiposity is positively correlated with higher leptin concentrations in milk; however, studies of leptin exposure and infant birthweight have shown inconsistent findings (33). Leptin receptors are expressed in hypothalamic neurons (11). Early-life exposure to leptin is therefore hypothesized to program the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (109), which subsequently could affect energy balance and subsequent T2D risk (33). As summarized by Donato (33), other putative mechanisms by which leptin could affect T2D include alterations in the development of energy homeostasis–related neurons, leptin resistance or hyperleptinemia, dietary behaviors, energy expenditure, and epigenetics. Further research is needed to delineate specific contributions of endogenous (fetal) versus exogenous (maternal, placental) sources of adipokines to infant nutritional status and glucose-insulin metabolism.

Pancreas and Pancreatic β-Cell Development in Early Life

Pancreatic β-cells in the islets of Langerhans are the primary endogenous source of insulin production, which regulates glucose homeostasis by promoting glucose uptake in peripheral muscle and adipose tissues (121). Pancreatic β-cells are highly responsive to environmental fluctuations in availability of nutrients, hormones, and growth factors (1, 61), including via binding of amino acids and fatty acids to specific G-protein coupled receptors on β-cells (96). Pancreatic β-cell plasticity is affected by several key cellular factors, including insulin secretion (61). β-cell expansion and insulin secretion progressively decline in some people with T2D (2, 73); however, the relative importance of lower β-cell mass in T2D etiology remains an active area of investigation (63, 148). The hypothesized influence of early-life nutrient availability on β-cell function is due to the inverse correlation of age and β-cell mass replication (73), which has age-dependent reductions in cell cycle entry (79).

Historic famine studies in China (143) and the Netherlands (27) have demonstrated that severe undernutrition exposure in utero and during early childhood was associated with lower disposition index, a measure of β-cell function, in adulthood. In the Dutch Famine Birth Cohort, the association was significant for famine exposure during mid-gestation but not for exposure in early gestation (27). In starvation, autophagy initially preserves pancreatic β-cell function by selectively degrading specific organelles and proteins to provide energy (3). Simultaneously, excessive autophagy can lead to β-cell apoptosis (3), which could potentially explain the observations in famine studies. Mice and rat offspring exposed to low-protein maternal diets had increased pancreatic islet cell apoptosis, as well as reduced β-cell replication, mass, islet size, and vascularization (129). One study demonstrated that increased expression of miR-15b downregulated β-cell proliferation via modulation of key cell cycle regulatory proteins (cyclin D1/D2) (125).

Early-life overnutrition has also been hypothesized to increase the risk for impaired β-cell function. Maternal high-fat diets are linked with chronic hyperglycemia, which results in β-cell dysfunction, exhaustion (hyperplasia), and β-cell death among offspring (16). One hypothesis is that acute hyperglycemia triggers compensatory β-cell mechanisms (e.g., hyperplasia, hypertrophy, higher proliferation, hyperinsulinemia); these compensatory activities and chronic hyperglycemia jointly contribute to β-cell exhaustion, impaired function, and apoptosis (16). To our best knowledge, there is scant human evidence with simultaneous evaluation of these hypothesized overnutrition mechanisms in early life and T2D risk in adulthood (13).

Suboptimal maternal diet alters placental nutrient availability and the risk for IUGR, which can impair pancreatic β-cell development (73). Severe IUGR has adverse effects on pancreatic β-cell development, as proxied by fewer β-cells, smaller islets, and reduced insulin secretion (9). In sheep, fetuses with IUGR had lower pancreatic weight and a smaller proportion of pancreatic β-cells undergoing mitosis, compared to unaffected fetuses (84), suggesting that IUGR exposure resulted in fetal pancreatic β-cells with longer interphase stages of the cell cycle (G1, S, S2) that undergo mitosis less frequently when approaching full term (84). Reduced nutrient availability in an IUGR fetus could disrupt the intraislet hepatocyte growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor A (HGF-VEGFA) signaling pathway, altering islet development (111). Overall, preliminary evidence suggests that autophagy, miRNAs, as well as HGF-VEGFA and mTOR signaling pathways could underpin previously observed associations between early-life undernutrition and pancreatic β-cell function; however, further evidence in T2D and elucidation of any implications are needed (3, 73, 111, 125).

Microvasculature and Cardiovascular System

Most organ tissues contain and rely on the microvasculature, the system of blood vessels linking blood from the circulatory system and tissues (65). Crucial microvascular functions include the provision of nutrients, protein, and oxygen; removal of waste carbon dioxide; and exchanging hormones as well as drugs (65). In a healthy embryo, the cardiovascular system develops earlier than many other organs to facilitate this transfer (23).

Maternal dietary intake restriction and composition affected microvascular angiogenesis, blood vessel volume density and length, and barrier integrity in the offspring vascular system (23). Maternal diabetes and obesity are linked with lower uterine spiral artery remodeling and placental oxygen transport, which increase the risk of fetal hypoxia (30). In zebrafish models, dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and redox state in the early-life milieu affect the development of the vascular system (23). Important caveats are that lifestyle intervention findings were inconsistent across studies, specific mechanisms remain unclear, and comparability across a multitude of microvascular measurements is still needed (23).

BIOLOGICAL AGING PATHWAYS AS MEDIATORS

Biological Aging Processes Linking Early-Life Nutrition and Type 2 Diabetes

The incidence of T2D rises in adulthood, relative to childhood, and is highest among adults aged 45–64 years (139). Although the average age of T2D diagnosis varies across populations (135, 139), there is increasing incidence and prevalence of early-onset T2D (102). T2D shares phenotypic features with aging, such as chronic inflammation and metabolic decline (135). Emerging evidence links early-life nutritional exposures to accelerated biological aging (18, 21, 90). Early-life nutrition has also been associated with an increased risk of diabetes (37, 38, 87, 106, 142), a condition that itself is independently related to accelerated aging (40, 47, 83, 93). Although these interrelationships are increasingly documented, their causal directions remain unclear. Studies of aging-related processes, including telomere attrition, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and age-related epigenetic modifications, provide insight into biological pathways linking early-life exposures, accelerated aging, and diabetes. Understanding these aging-related processes may also provide insight into the growing number of people with early-onset T2D (102).

Telomere Attrition and Cellular Senescence

Telomeres are repetitive DNA–protein complexes that cap chromosome ends and are essential for maintaining genomic stability during cell replication. Telomeres shorten with each cell division. Once they become critically short, cells can no longer divide safely and enter either senescence (stop dividing but remain metabolically active) or undergo apoptosis (programmed cell death) (129). By limiting the regenerative capacity of cells, telomere shortening affects the maintenance of healthy tissues and organs and has long been proposed as a biomarker of aging. Notably, individuals with T2D often have shorter telomeres (141). Lifestyle factors including dietary patterns and physical activity have been evaluated as potential modifiable influences on telomere length (44, 52). However, debate remains over whether telomere length reliably reflects biological aging, life span, or lifestyle factors, as some studies show strong associations, whereas others find weak or inconsistent links (129).

In a murine model, offspring of mothers fed a low-protein diet during pregnancy who then experienced rapid postnatal growth had shorter telomeres in the aorta, heart, female reproductive tract, and pancreatic islets, tissues central to vascular and metabolic aging (129). In contrast, pups that continued on a low-protein diet during lactation (via maternal protein restriction) grew more slowly, had less DNA damage, and had fewer critically short telomeres (129). In an intergenerational murine model, grandmothers fed a low-protein diet during pregnancy had granddaughters with reduced ovarian reserve (fewer eggs) and shorter ovarian cell telomeres, even when their own daughters consumed a nutritionally adequate diet (129). Together, these findings indicate both maternal nutritional status and rate of postnatal growth can have lasting effects on biomarkers of aging, with consequences that may extend across multiple generations (129).

In humans, telomere length varies between individuals of the same age, even at birth, reflecting the influence of pregnancy-related factors (20). In newborns, longer maternal telomeres and older paternal age were associated with longer newborn telomeres, whereas maternal anxiety, high fasting glucose, low IGF-binding protein 3, low vitamin B12, and smoking were linked to shorter telomeres (20). Sex-specific patterns were also observed. Female newborn telomere length was primarily influenced by maternal telomere length, whereas male telomere length was more influenced by paternal age, maternal education, and maternal metabolic health (20). These early influences may have lasting implications. For example, adolescent girls born to mothers with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) had significantly shorter telomeres than controls, and this shorter telomere length was associated with higher fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, indicating early insulin resistance in these adolescents (62). In young adult men, low birth weight was associated with higher insulin resistance, but not with leukocyte telomere length (49). In both men and women, shorter telomeres were linked to higher fasting glucose and HbA1c, independent of birth weight (49). Taken together, these findings suggest that telomere length reflects early-life environmental factors and may have lasting, sex-specific consequences for metabolic health and cellular aging.

Some famine studies suggested that early-life undernutrition is associated with reduced telomere length, and other studies found no differences. Survivors of the Siege of Leningrad had shorter telomeres than controls, particularly among those exposed in utero or during late childhood (110). Men born after the 1922–1923 Ukrainian famine had shorter telomeres at midlife compared with men born before the famine, and this pattern persisted in their male descendants, but no similar effects were observed in women (77). By contrast, in the Netherlands, individuals exposed to famine in early gestation did not show differences in leukocyte telomere length or the proportion of short telomeres at average age 68 years compared with unexposed individuals (28). Instead, shorter telomeres were associated with lower socioeconomic status at birth, alcohol consumption, cancer history, and poorer self-reported health, and current employment was linked to fewer short telomeres (28). Overall, these findings suggest that early-life famine could have lasting, sex-specific and intergenerational effects on biological aging measured by leukocyte telomere length and confirm that telomere length is additionally associated with postnatal environment, lifestyle, and health. It remains unclear whether telomere shortening contributes to, or merely reflects, the increased cardiometabolic disease risk also observed among famine-exposed individuals (85).

Oxidative Stress and Mitochondrial Dysfunction

The free radical theory of aging proposes that reactive oxygen species (ROS), molecules containing unpaired electrons, accumulate in cells over time and contribute to accelerated aging (129). Oxidative stress occurs when ROS production exceeds the body’s antioxidant defenses, limiting their ability to neutralize these highly reactive molecules and prevent cellular damage. Mitochondria are a primary source of ROS and are particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress. Excess ROS can damage mitochondrial DNA, disrupt oxidative phosphorylation, and impair adenosine triphosphate production, creating a feedback loop that further overwhelms cellular defenses (129). ROS also damage DNA, lipids, and proteins and can accelerate telomere shortening, ultimately leading to impaired metabolic tissue functioning and promoting pathways that increase T2D risk (129). Importantly, ROS-induced cellular senescence amplifies this cycle by promoting chronic low-grade inflammation, which in turn enhances oxidative stress and tissue damage, creating a self-perpetuating loop of oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction (19).

Murine models demonstrate that adverse early-life nutritional environments increase oxidative stress in metabolically relevant tissues. Gestational undernutrition, including maternal calorie restriction, protein-restricted diets, and placental insufficiency, increases offspring ROS production and mitochondrial dysfunction (128, 129). Maternal calorie restriction during pregnancy, as well as low-protein diets followed by rapid postnatal growth, lead to increased oxidative stress in offspring, with elevated ROS observed in the heart, aorta, and ovarian tissue (129). Similarly, placental insufficiency, when the placenta cannot deliver sufficient oxygen and nutrients to support normal fetal growth, results in impaired oxidative phosphorylation in hepatic, pancreatic, and skeletal muscle tissue, accompanied by mitochondrial DNA mutations, and ROS accumulation in pancreatic islets (129). Conversely, in a context of overnutrition, offspring of dams fed high-fat or junk food diets during pregnancy show comparable increases in oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, including defects in components of the electron transport chain (129). Consistent with these findings, evidence from murine and human models indicates that maternal high-fat diets and maternal obesity, respectively, activate the p53/p21 stress response pathway (19). This activation induces premature cellular senescence in fetal tissues, leading to impaired glucose metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction (19). Collectively, these findings suggest that multiple forms of adverse early-life nutritional environments result in mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, processes that contribute to accelerated cellular aging, potentially leading to increased T2D risk.

Epigenetic Age Measured by DNA Methylation Clocks

Genome-wide DNA methylation levels change in predictable ways as chronological age increases. Epigenetic clocks leverage these patterns to estimate how biologically old the body’s cells appear. These models are typically built by regressing chronological age on a set of CpGs using penalized regression, a machine learning method that selects the most informative CpG sites for age prediction (67). The resulting collection of CpGs forms a DNA methylation clock. Biological age can then be estimated in a biological sample (typically obtained from serum buffy coat or saliva) by measuring methylation at the clock-specific CpG sites and applying the machine-learning algorithm to calculate a weighted average, yielding a DNA methylation–based estimate of biological age (67). Clock CpGs are typically located in regulatory regions of the genome that control processes such as cell differentiation and the maintenance of cellular identity (67). Epigenetic clocks are not mechanisms of aging; rather, they provide a composite measure of age-related changes across many CpG sites. Although most individual CpGs weakly correlate with chronological age, together they provide a highly predictive measure (67). In this way, epigenetic clocks serve as biomarkers of the aging process, capturing the underlying biological changes that accumulate over time.

Thus far, these clocks have been developed over three generations. Hannum et al. (55) built the first DNA methylation clock in 2013. It consists of 71 CpG sites identified in whole blood tissue of adults (55). This initial clock was highly predictive of chronological age. The Horvath (66) clock, developed the same year, was the first multi-tissue DNA methylation estimation clock. It was trained on 30 different tissue and cell types from both adults and children and addressed some of the limitations of the Hannum clock (66).

First-generation clocks were highly correlated with chronological age but weakly associated with clinical biomarkers, such as blood pressure or glucose levels (67, 105). Physiological health is often better reflected by clinical biomarkers than by chronological age alone. Second-generation clocks, such as PhenoAge (81) and GrimAge (86), were designed to incorporate these biomarkers of health status to better predict morbidity and mortality risk among individuals of the same age. For PhenoAge, researchers first generated a phenotypic age by calculating a weighted average of 10 clinical characteristics: chronological age; albumin, creatinine, glucose, and C-reactive protein levels; lymphocyte percentage; mean cell volume; red blood cell distribution width; alkaline phosphatase; and white blood cell count (81). This composite phenotypic age was then regressed on DNA methylation levels using a penalized regression model, resulting in a DNA methylation clock consisting of 513 CpGs that estimates phenotypic age (81). GrimAge is based on 1,030 sites and incorporates DNA methylation surrogates of plasma proteins and smoking history to predict time-to-death and disease risk more directly, providing an estimate of biological age that is strongly associated with life span and health span (86).

The third-generation epigenetic clocks, such as DunedinPACE, quantify the rate of aging, or pace at which an individual experiences systemic physiological decline (8). DunedinPACE was developed using longitudinal data capturing changes across multiple physiological systems. Specifically, the clock was trained on repeated measures of 19 biomarkers reflecting cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, hepatic, immune, and pulmonary function in the Dunedin Study cohort, spanning ages 26 to 45 (8, 103). These longitudinal physiological measures were then regressed onto DNA methylation profiles to identify CpG sites predictive of the pace of aging (8), resulting in a clock that provides a dynamic measure of how fast an individual is aging biologically, rather than a static estimate of biological age.

Recent studies have examined the impact of early-life nutritional exposures on epigenetic aging in midlife. In the Dutch Hunger Winter Families Study, individuals exposed to famine in utero during the winter of 1944 to 1945 exhibited faster DunedinPACE at an average age 58 years, indicating accelerated biological aging compared with unexposed sibling controls; effects were strongest in women. GrimAge showed a similar but smaller trend, and PhenoAge did not differ across categories of exposure to famine in utero (21). These differences were not explained by blood cell composition and were observed regardless of the timing of gestational exposure to famine (21). Complementing these findings, the INCAP Nutrition Supplementation Trial in Guatemala evaluated the long-term effects of a protein-energy supplement (atole) provided during the first 1,000 days of life compared with a lower-energy protein free drink (fresco) (18). In mid-adulthood (mean age 45 years), participants exposed to atole had lower DunedinPACE acceleration, PhenoAge, and GrimAge, suggesting slower biological aging, although adjustment for blood cell composition attenuated statistical significance (18). Together, these studies suggest that early-life nutritional deprivation accelerates, whereas nutritional supplementation modestly slows, epigenetic aging, highlighting the lasting influence of early-life nutrition on biological aging trajectories.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Overall, genetic predisposition affects the association between low birth weight and T2D. Recent evidence supports multiple mediating pathways, particularly epigenetic mechanisms, adipose tissue and pancreatic β-cell differences, that could explain prior associations between early-life nutrition and T2D risk in adulthood. We identified four categories of early-life nutrition exposure: (a) maternal and infant dietary intake or supplementation, (b) maternal and infant anthropometry, (c) GDM as a proxy indicator of dysregulated metabolism during pregnancy, and (d) uteroplacental insufficiency (Figure 1a).

To summarize key findings from Section 2, GWAS (64) and candidate gene analyses (34, 104) provide support for the fetal insulin hypothesis, insofar as low birth weight and T2D phenotypes shared genetic profiles. The association between fetal or childhood famine exposure and higher odds of diabetes differed by tertiles of a 21-variant T2D genetic risk score (140). Twin studies have produced inconsistent evidence regarding whether genetic predisposition modifies the associations between low birth weight and T2D in adulthood (70, 74). From Section 3, evidence from animal studies and natural experiments suggests that epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and ncRNA may mediate these effects. A few specific sites (PDX1, INSR, IGF2) have been consistently identified as sensitive to early-life nutritional exposures. In Section 4, maternal diabetes and insulin resistance were associated with higher fetal adiposity and impaired glucose tolerance in offspring as adults. Murine models (129) and historic famine studies (27, 143) among humans show that undernutrition (low protein, severe caloric deficiency) in utero is associated with alterations in pancreatic islet size as well as β-cell replication and function. Other studies report limited, and therefore preliminary, evidence of the mediating roles of microvasculature. Finally, evidence presented in Section 5 suggests several biological processes and aging biomarkers (telomere length, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic age) that could either mediate or reflect underlying mechanisms linking early-life exposures to later disease. As an important caveat, prior studies had shortcomings that should be accounted for while interpreting findings (Table 1). Simultaneously, the current state of evidence highlights the importance and substantial potential of future studies to further extend insights (Table 1), particularly systems biology and multi-omics studies that leverage novel methodologic and technologic developments underway.

This review had several limitations, including the narrative review approach. There is limited ancestral diversity represented by prior human study populations and birth cohorts. Other plausible intermediate pathways are beyond the scope of this review and not discussed in depth. Key examples include socioeconomic, educational, and environmental exposures such as endocrine disrupting chemicals and secular trends including increasing overweight and obesity prevalence, diabetes subtypes, and T2D comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease. Finally, some biological mechanisms are implicated in diabetes pathogenesis but are not well elucidated in the context of early-life nutritional exposures. For example, mTOR signaling is a nutrient-sensitive and central regulator of cellular homeostasis, including protein synthesis and autophagy (46, 85). Dysregulated mTOR signaling is associated with T2D (46, 81); however, a key research gap is evaluating mTOR pathway impacts in specific organ tissues as potential mediators of the association between early-life nutrition exposure and T2D risk. Interorgan cross talk (151), such as neuroendocrine (119) and immunometabolic (117) axes, has been demonstrated; however, the impacts on early-life nutrition–T2D associations remain important research gaps and opportunities for future studies.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has shown multiple biological pathways linking early-life nutrition and T2D in adulthood. These pathways include shared genes between birth weight and adult diabetes status, DNA methylation of glucose-insulin homeostasis and energy metabolism-related genes, and adipose tissue and pancreatic β-cell function. Most prior studies have evaluated differing combinations of early-life nutrition exposure, an intermediate effect modification or causal mediation pathway, and T2D assessment, which precludes direct comparisons among studies and simultaneously evaluating the relative contributions of multiple pathways. Our findings emphasize that future studies are needed to elucidate the extent to which recent key findings can be translated into concrete public health recommendations, such as nutrition interventions during the first 1,000 days of life.
