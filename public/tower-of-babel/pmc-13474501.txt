Protaetia brevitarsis larvae frass affects substrate microecological systems via two suggestive pathways to enhance cherry tomato growth
Authors: Kui Wang, Qi Peng, Lili Geng, Fang Zhang, Ruoyun Liu, Xingyu Liu, Jie Zhang, Changlong Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe identification and development of high-quality humus sources to enhance the productivity and performance of substrate-based vegetable cultivation systems remains a significant challenge in sustainable agriculture. Protaetia brevitarsis larvae (PBL) exhibit exceptional efficiency in decomposing decaying crop straw and produce nutrient-rich frass with high humic acid content and a complex microbial community. However, its impacts on substrate microecological systems and the underlying functional mechanisms remain unclear, limiting its rational application in substrate cultivation. This study aimed to investigate the effects of PBL frass on substrate microecology and elucidate the associated mechanisms using cherry tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill. var. cerasiforme Alef) pot experiments.ResultsIncorporation of 2% or 4% (w/w) PBL frass into cherry tomato cultivation substrates significantly promoted plant growth, characterized by reduced plant height (indicating more robust, dwarf-type growth) and increased aboveground (stem) and belowground (root) biomass. Furthermore, PBL frass application enhanced substrate microbial diversity through two distinct, complementary pathways: (ⅰ) Frass-derived microbes, which possess specific colonization capabilities, directly augmented microbial communities in both the rhizoplane and bulk substrate; and (ⅱ) Organic compounds in PBL frass may have activated a broad range of microbes, enriching the rhizosphere microbiome. This enhanced microbial diversity was associated with an increased abundance of plant-beneficial taxa, which likely contributed to growth promotion and substrate health maintenance.ConclusionsThis study uncovers the multifaceted contributions of PBL frass to substrate microbial ecology and reveals its two suggestive regulatory pathways. These results provide a theoretical basis for the sustainable utilization of PBL frass and advance the development of eco-friendly amendments for modern vegetable production.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00915-6.

Introduction

As one of the dominant forms of soilless cultivation, substrate cultivation employs solid substrates as physical supports for anchoring vegetable roots, while also regulating root zone conditions—including water availability, nutrient supply, and aeration—through the inherent physicochemical properties of these materials [1, 2]. Mineral-based substrates such as vermiculite provide dual advantages: they serve as stable rooting media and supply essential mineral nutrients, such as magnesium and potassium, to plants. However, a significant limitation of these substrates is their absence of humus—a crucial component of natural soils that plays a key role in nutrient retention, facilitates beneficial microbial activity, and influences root development [3]. The lack of humus can significantly compromise both the yield and quality of vegetables cultivated in such systems. Therefore, the exploration and development of high-quality humus sources have become a critical research priority for improving the efficiency and performance of substrate-based vegetable production systems.

Soil fauna consumes substantial amounts of plant litter, and in some ecosystems, they can even decompose the entire annual litterfall [4, 5]. During this process, soil fauna transform litter into frass that contains humus-like substances; this frass is then deposited into the soil, contributing to the formation of soil humus [6]. Protaetia brevitarsis, commonly known as the white-spotted flower chafer, is an important Scarabaeidae insect that is distributed in most Asian countries. P. brevitarsis larvae (PBL), a promising insect species, possess the ability to convert crop straw into edible proteins and valuable insect frass. Notably, the frass produced by PBL is non-phytotoxic and exhibits plant growth-promoting activity. Our previous study demonstrated that PBL frass is rich in humic acid (HA), a biostimulant that plays a pivotal role in regulating plant growth [7, 8]. Furthermore, our recent findings revealed that HA precursors or their decomposition products may coexist in the PBL gut, and six HA-related aromatic compounds isolated from the PBL digestive tract have been shown to enhance seed germination and stimulate root growth [9]. Beyond its organic matter content, PBL frass also harbors a variety of agriculturally beneficial microorganisms, such as Bacillus species [10]. Additionally, the total content of key nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) in PBL frass exceeds the 5% threshold specified in the Agricultural Industry Standard of the People’s Republic of China (NY 525–2012) [8]. Collectively, these lines of evidence indicate that PBL frass represents a potential high-quality humus source and exhibits promising application value for improving the performance of substrate-based vegetable cultivation systems. However, the functional mechanisms by which frass-derived organic matter and associated microorganisms regulate substrate microecological systems remain poorly understood. This critical knowledge gap has become a major bottleneck hindering the further development, optimization, and large-scale application of frass-based products in agricultural systems.

To address this limitation, we conducted pot experiments using sterile vermiculite as the basal substrate to eliminate confounding effects from native organic matter and microbiota, thereby allowing a clear assessment of PBL frass as an independent humus source. Cherry tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill. var. cerasiforme Alef) was used as the model plant under nutritionally uniform conditions. The specific objectives of this study were to: (ⅰ) evaluate the effects of PBL frass on three distinct substrate microenvironments, namely the rhizoplane, rhizosphere, and bulk substrate; and (ⅱ) elucidate the underlying functional mechanisms governing these observed effects. This research not only provides novel insights into how PBL frass modulates plant-associated microbial communities and the corresponding regulatory mechanisms but also lays a theoretical and empirical foundation for the future rational utilization and value-added application of PBL-derived crop straw conversion products in sustainable agriculture.

Materials and methods

Sampling

The PBL used in this study were reared as previously described [10]. To collect frass, well-grown larvae were removed from the rearing substrate, cleaned with sterile water, and then placed in an empty container at 25℃ for 2 days. The freshly defecated frass was collected, air-dried at room temperature for 48 h before use. The detailed chemical compositions and related reference standard methods of the PBL frass are provided in Table S1. The vermiculite used as the basal substrate was commercially sourced and pre-sterilized via high-temperature drying during manufacturing. Sterility was independently verified prior to use by plating a sterile distilled water rinse onto LB agar plates and incubating them aerobically at 30℃ for 48 h; no microbial growth was observed. The processed PBL frass was then homogeneously mixed with the sterilized vermiculite prior to seedling transplantation to achieve final concentrations of 1% (T1), 2% (T2), 4% (T3), and 8% (T4, w/w). No additional frass was supplied during the whole growth period. As a positive control, sterile vermiculite without any addition of frass was employed (CK).

The vigorous cherry tomato seedlings were bought from a local retail market in Beijing, China on January 14, 2023, and subsequently transplanted on January 17, 2023 into individual pots filled with the above mixtures at the Langfang Scientific Research Trial Station of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Langfang, Hebei, China). The plants were cultivated under natural light conditions in a glasshouse without the use of supplementary lamps. Prior to flowering, the plants received regular watering using a low concentration Hoagland Basal Salt culture medium prepared by dissolving 0.52 g Hoagland Basal Salt and 1 mL of 500× Calcium Salt Solution in 1 L ddH20. Following flowering, the plants were watered with a high concentration Hoagland Basal Salt solution prepared by dissolving 1.04 g Hoagland Basal Salt and adding 2 mL of 500× Calcium Salt Solution in each liter of ddH20. The detailed chemical compositions of the nutrient solutions are provided in Table S2. The formulation is based on Hoagland and Arnon (1950) [11].

The plant height was recorded as the absolute length from the base of the stem to the point of growth when more than 90% of plants exhibited over five functional leaves. After more than 90% of plants reached flowering stage, the number and leaf area of all functional leaves, as well as number of lateral branches in each sample were recorded. Additionally, above-ground and below-ground biomass were determined by measuring the weights (both fresh and dry) of stems and roots separately. The fresh weight was measured after plant harvesting and root cleaning, while the dry weight was obtained by drying the plant material at 70℃ for 96 h. To assess the impact of PBL frass on substrate microbial communities associated with plants, the bacterial composition of bulk and rhizo- substrate samples was determined by analyzing the 16 S rRNA gene sequences. Samples were collected from CK, T1, T2 and T3 treatments as adding 8% frass (T4) to the substrate is not suitable for plant growth. The original nutrient substrate at the roots of cherry tomato seedlings and the PBL frass used during cultivation were retained and labeled as sample SS and SF, respectively. The SS samples were collected on January 17, 2023 (the day of transplantation), and the SF samples were collected from the frass batch used for mixing the substrates. The rhizoplane (BW), rhizosphere (GS), and bulk substrate (DS) samples were collected on March 7, 2023. Each treatment group consisted of four replicates, resulting in a total of 56 samples being collected. The DS samples were obtained from the substrate away from the plant roots. Then the tomato plants with roots were removed from the substrate and the excess substrate was manually shaken from the roots leaving approximately 1 mm of substrate attached to the roots. These root samples were then placed in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (50 mL) and vortexed for 15 s to ensure thorough mixing. The resulting solution was centrifuged to obtain pellets representing GS samples. The roots for rhizosphere collection were cleaned and placed in PBS solution, and sonicated for 30 s twice to strip the rhizoplane microbes from the root surface. The BW samples were collected through multiple centrifugations. Prior to DNA extraction, all samples were stored at −80℃.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

All samples (SS, SF, BW, GS, DS) were subjected to 16 S rRNA gene sequencing on March 15, 2023. The DNA from each sample was extracted using the PowerSoil DNA isolation kit (MoBio Laboratories, USA). The V3–V4 region of microbial 16 S rRNA genes in a total of 56 samples (including 16 BW samples, 16 GS samples, 16 DS samples, 4 SS samples and 4 SF samples) were amplified using the barcoded primers 341 F and 806R [12]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out as follows: initial denaturation at 94℃ for 3 min, 30 cycles at 94℃ for 30 s, 55℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 90 s, and final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. Then the PCR products were purified using a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The 16 S rRNA gene amplicon library construction was performed as previous described [12]. Finally, the resulting libraries were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer (ILLUMINA, USA) on March 15, 2023, generating paired-end reads with lengths reaching up to 250 bp.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw Data were trimmed using Trimmomatic (version 0.38) [13] with default parameters. Subsequently, the clean paired-end reads were assembled into raw tags using Usearch (version 10.0.240) [14]. Primer sequences within the raw tags were removed to obtain high-quality clean tags. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% identity cutoff using the Usearch UPARSE algorithm. Chimera sequences were filtered out by aligning against the silva database (https://www.arb-silva.de/). OTU annotation was performed using the Usearch SINTAX algorithm based on the RDP database (version 16) [15], followed by exclusion of chloroplast or mitochondria-associated OTUs. For diversity analysis, all samples’ OTU tables were standardized to a minimum data size using the vegan R package (https://www.r-project.org/). Alpha and Beta diversity metrics were calculated employing Usearch (version 10.0.240) [14]. The Principal Co-ordinates Analysis (PCoA) was conducted using the R package (https://www.r-project.org/) to visualize complex relationships between samples. The SourceTracker [16] model was employed to trace the microbial sources for both bulk and rhizo-substrate samples. This model could predict the composition ratio of the target samples from each source sample based on the distribution of microbial community structure [17]. The MEGA (version 11.0.11) [18] was used for phylogenetic analysis and iTOL (Interactive Tree of Life, https://itol.embl.de/) [19] was used for tree annotation. The analysis included bootstrapping values with 500 replications.

Statistical analysis

All statistical comparisons in this study were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc test, after confirming normality and homoscedasticity of the data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homoscedasticity using Levene’s test. The only fixed factor in the model was frass concentration, and no covariates were included. The number of biological replicates was four for microbial samples and five for plant growth traits. Statistical calculations and data analysis were performed using OriginPro (version 2021, OriginLab Corporation, USA) and TBtools [20].

Results

The effect of PBL frass on plant growth

Compared to the untreated substrate (CK), application of PBL frass exhibited significant effects on plant growth. The height of cherry tomato plants in the T1-T4 groups treated with PBL frass was significantly reduced, showing an average decrease of 21% (Fig. 1a). Meanwhile, the addition of 2% or 4% frass increased the fresh (Fig. 1b) and dry (Fig. 1c) weights of stems in cherry tomatoes. Notably, the inclusion of 8% frass resulted in a significant reduction in plant height and above-ground biomass, suggesting that excessive concentrations might impede plant growth. In addition, it was observed that adding frass at concentrations ranging from 2% to 8% enhanced both fresh (Fig. 1d) and dry (Fig. 1e) weights of roots. Notably, incorporating 4% frass led to a significant increase in below-ground (roots) biomass, further demonstrating the potential application of PBL frass for promoting root growth. The root mass fraction which quantifies the proportion of total plant biomass allocated to roots, is indicative of a plant’s investment in below-ground organs for nutrient acquisition. Consequently, different volume ratios of PBL frass were investigated for their impact on root mass fraction (Fig. 1f). Application of frass also increased root mass fraction in cherry tomatoes. Adding 8% frass showed a significant increase compared to the untreated group, with an increase of up to 47.0%. However, this was primarily attributed to a substantial decrease in stem dry weight rather than an absolute increase in root investment. Moreover, the statistical analysis of lateral branches and leaves revealed that the application of PBL frass resulted in reduction in the number of lateral branches along the main stem of cherry tomatoes, while concurrently promoting an increase in leaf count and leaf area with appropriate addition of frass (Fig. S1).

Microbial community responses to PBL frass

Following the removal of short reads and trimming low-quality regions, a total of 6,041,709 clean reads were generated from 56 samples. This included 1,450,250 clean reads from 16 rhizoplane (BW) samples, 1,556,136 clean reads from 16 rhizosphere (GS) samples, 2,201,651 clean reads from 16 bulk substrate (DS) samples, as well as 397,820 clean reads from four seedling frass (SF) samples and 435,852 clean reads from four seedling substrate (SS) samples. Finally, a total of 4,157,994 effective sequences were grouped at 97% DNA sequence similarity, and an average of 991, 949, 773, 959 and 971 OTUs were obtained from BW, GS, DS, SF and SS samples, respectively (Table S3). Analysis of rarefaction curves based on the Richness index demonstrated that all samples reached a plateau, suggesting that our sampling effort was sufficient to obtain a full estimate of OTU richness (Fig. 2a).

The multivariate Principal co-ordinate analysis (PCoA) analysis based on the Bray Curtis distance matrix was employed for analyzing community structures among different samples. The results revealed that the microbial community composition in DS samples exhibited closer resemblance to that in SF samples, indicating a greater influence of frass-derived microbes on bulk substrate microbial community composition. Conversely, BW and GS samples displayed similar compositions and were more akin to those observed in SS samples, suggesting that the presence of plant roots could influence the community composition of rhizo- substrate (Fig. 2b).

Although the community structures varied across different samples, the Source Tracker model analysis revealed unrestricted dispersion of microbes from seedling frass and seedling substrate throughout the entire cultivation substrate (Fig. 3a, b and Table S4). The Source Tracker model considered each individual community as a mixed community deposited from known or unknown environmental sources. The most of the rhizoplane and rhizosphere microorganisms were from the SS samples, which accounted for over 60% of the total microbial communities. This further highlight those microbes from the planting substrate contribute significantly to flora formation in the planting system. Since the original SS samples consisted of substrates tightly attached to tomato seedlings, transplantation would not intentionally introduce these microbes into the bulk cultivation substrates. Nevertheless, SS‑derived microbes were detected in bulk substrate samples (22.2–45.4%). Several non‑mutually exclusive explanations may account for this observation: (i) minor physical carryover of SS material during transplantation despite careful handling; (ii) subsequent dispersal via watering practices; or (iii) gradual spread of microbes from the developing rhizosphere into the surrounding substrate over the growth period. Regardless of the precise mechanism, the widespread presence of SS‑derived microbes across all substrate compartments formed the foundation for investigating the impact of PBL frass on substrate microbial communities.

The alpha diversity analysis revealed that the addition of PBL frass significantly enhanced the richness of substrate microbial communities (Fig. 3 c, d). Regarding bulk substrate, microbial diversity was lower than in the rhizoplane and rhizosphere. Frass addition significantly increased alpha diversity in bulk substrate samples. Similarly, frass addition increased alpha diversity in the rhizoplane, with significant effects observed at higher addition rates. In the rhizosphere, even a low rate of frass addition significantly enhanced richness, suggesting that the high diversity in this microenvironment is supported by both plant root function and frass.

The PBL frass affects substrate microecological systems through two suggestive pathways: direct colonization by frass-derived microbes and indirect activation of numerous unknown sourced microbes. Firstly, the frass-derived microbes could directly influence rhizo- and bulk substrate samples (Fig. 3a, b and Table S4). The contributions of SF microbes ranged from 5.5% (DS0) to 19.8% (DS4) for bulk substrate and from 2.8% (BW1) to 6.2% (BW4) for rhizoplane. Although 34 OTUs were found in DS0 samples, this could be attributed to a similarity between the community in the substrate and that in PBL frass. Regarding rhizosphere, no frass-derived microbes were detected in the GS1, while GS2 and GS4 showed contributions of 2.5% and 2.4%, respectively. Overall, following the incorporation of PBL frass, a substantial increase in the abundance of frass-derived microorganisms was observed in all three samples, with the impact ranked in descending order as bulk substrate, rhizoplane, and rhizosphere (Fig. 3b). Secondly, the application of PBL frass activated enormous unknown sourced microbes. The bulk substrate was most affected by microorganisms of unknown sources, followed by rhizosphere. Regarding the seedling substrate, the SS microbe numbers exhibited inconsistent change patterns across different samples; however, it was observed that the introduction of PBL frass did not lead to a reduction in the overall microbial communities present within the original seedling substrate.

Differentially featured taxa from frass and unknown sources

To further elucidate the impact pathways of PBL frass on different micro-ecosystems, the community composition of frass-derived microbes and unknown sourced microbes present in rhizo-and bulk substrate samples were analyzed separately, revealing selective colonization of these microbes within different microenvironments. Regarding frass-derived microbes, a total of 68, 51 and 110 frass-derived OTUs contributed to BW, GS, and DS samples, respectively (Fig. 4a). Among these OTUs, 57 were specific in the DS samples, while 28 were specifically colonized in the BW, a higher number compared to those found (17 OTUs) in the GS. This suggested that microbes from PBL frass could selectively colonize plant roots, thereby influencing plant growth. The community structure analysis of all frass-derived OTUs revealed that the microbial composition derived from frass consisted of ten phyla, with Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria being the predominant taxa (Fig. 4b). The phylum Proteobacteria constituted an average of 56.0% of the BW samples, 56.5% of the GS samples, and 49.2% of the DS samples, respectively. The phylum Bacteroidetes constituted an average of 21.4% of the BW samples, 17.5% of the GS samples, and 23.2% of the DS samples, respectively. The phylum Actinobacteria constituted an average of 13.4% of the BW samples, 11.4% of the GS samples, and 22.1% of the DS samples, respectively. Moreover, with the increase of PBL frass, Firmicutes was abundant in BW4 samples.

Therefore, we further specifically focused on the presence of several known beneficial species within these phyla, including Bacillus (Firmicutes), Pseudomonas (Proteobacteria) and Streptomyces (Actinobacteria). The results indicated that these three species selectively colonize plant-associated environments (Fig. 4c). Bacillus and Pseudomonas could be introduced into bulk substrate, and with the addition of PBL frass, all three species were able to colonize the rhizoplane. However, none of these species were found to contribute to the rhizosphere. In addition to these three species, the investigation also encompassed the abundance of other frass-derived species at the genus level (Table S5). The findings revealed that certain frass-derived species with substrate or plant benefits exhibited a specific tendency to colonize in the rhizoplane as opposed to the rhizosphere. At the genus level, Flavobacterium and Taibaiella in the phylum Bacteroidetes, as well as Methylotenera, Acinetobacter and Cellvibrio in the phylum Proteobacteria, were dominant in rhizoplane with an average representation of > 3% within the bacterial community. Regarding rhizosphere, Hydrogenophaga and Paracoccus in the phylum Proteobacteria, as well as Flavobacterium in the phylum Bacteroidetes were abundant. Flavobacterium was also dominant in bulk substrate samples, constituting 5.9% of the bacterial community. Additionally, Sphingobacterium and Parapedobacter in the phylum Bacteroidetes, as well as Pseudoxanthomonas in the phylum Proteobacteria also exhibited higher abundance in DS samples (> 3%). Furthermore, analysis of the total contributions of 157 frass-derived OTUs in rhizo- and bulk substrate samples were performed (Fig. S2 and Table S6). The results also revealed that the frass-derived OTUs were mainly contributed to bulk substrate, followed by rhizoplane and rhizosphere.

The application of PBL frass also selectively activated certain beneficial species to different microenvironments. A total of 439, 475 and 687 unknown-sourced OTUs contributed to BW, GS, and DS samples, respectively (Fig. 5a). Among these OTUs, 167 OTUs were shared collectively, while a greater number of OTUs were found to be specific to each sample: 371 in DS, 131 in GS, and 112 in BW. The community structure analysis of these OTUs revealed that Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes were also the dominant phyla, comprising 44.6% and 29.0% of the bacterial community in BW samples, 48.3% and 25.7% of the bacterial community in GS samples, while 49.7% and 18.4% of the bacterial community in DS samples (Fig. 5b). Moreover, Verrucomicrobia exhibited high abundance in both BW and GS samples, accounting for 8.6% and 9.2% of the bacterial community respectively; while Actinobacteria and Firmicutes were predominant in DS, representing 12.1% and 5.2% of the bacterial community respectively.

The prevalence analysis of three selected beneficial species revealed that both Bacillus and Pseudomonas could be activated to rhizoplane, rhizosphere and bulk substrate, whereas Streptomyces demonstrated preference for the rhizoplane as well as bulk substrate (Fig. 5c). Regarding other abundant genus from unknown sources, the plant beneficial species Flavobacterium were dominant in BW, GS and DS samples with an average composition of 6.9%, 6.3% and 2.2% respectively within the bacterial community (Table S7). Moreover, an abundance of Fluviicola from Bacteroidetes, Opitutus and Subdivision3_genera_incertae_sedis from Verrucomicrobia were also enriched both in BW and GS samples (average abundance > 2%). Regarding the total contributions of 1024 unknown sourced OTUs in rhizo- and bulk substrate samples (Fig. S3 and Table S8), the results revealed that the unknown sourced OTUs were also mainly contributed to bulk substrate, followed by rhizosphere and rhizoplane.

Discussion

In natural ecosystems, plant litter transformed by soil fauna enhances soil organic matter enrichment, fosters a bacterial-driven food web and mull-type humus with high nutrient availability, thus better supporting plant growth; in contrast, nutrient-poor soils fail to attract soil fauna, instead sustaining conservative-growth plants that produce poorly decomposable litter, which favors a fungal-dominated food web and results in low nutrient availability [6, 21]. In this study, we aimed to mimic natural ecological processes by incorporating PBL frass—a key byproduct of straw conversion—as an additive into substrate‑based soilless cultivation substrates. The substrate used in our study (vermiculite) is inert, with neutral pH, high water holding, and negligible microbial load after autoclaving; its main function is to provide physical support and aeration, while all essential nutrients were supplied via Hoagland solution [22]. The results demonstrate that the addition of PBL frass significantly promotes plant growth and enhances substrate microbial properties, indicating their potential as a promising additive for substrate‑based soilless cultivation systems (e.g., using vermiculite, perlite, or coconut coir). This does not extend to true hydroponic systems, where particulate organic matter would be incompatible.

Studies have demonstrated that partial suppression of shoot elongation is associated with enhanced cereal production, likely due to the reallocation of resources toward reproductive organs or nutrient storage [23]. Our study showed that application rates of 2% and 4% (w/w) reduced plant height while increasing both above-ground (stem) and below-ground (root) biomass, resulting in a “stunted but robust” phenotype. The simultaneous increase in root biomass and root mass fraction further suggests an improved capacity for nutrient uptake, which can be attributed to two key properties of PBL frass identified in previous research: its high nutrient content and elevated levels of humic acids [7, 8]. However, excessive application (8%) suppressed plant growth, which might be partly due to a disruption in the substrate’s carbon‑to‑nitrogen (C: N) balance, although direct measurements of substrate C and N were not performed in this study. The C: N ratio serves as a critical regulator of plant development and microbial activity [24]; an overabundance of frass may shift this ratio beyond optimal thresholds, thereby inhibiting nutrient mineralization or triggering nutrient imbalances, such as nitrogen immobilization. Therefore, the findings indicate that PBL frass is a promising additive for substrate-based soilless cultivation systems, but its application rates must be precisely controlled to avoid adverse effects.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that the addition of PBL frass can influence the substrate microbial community. Our analyses revealed that PBL frass contains a rich and diverse microbial assemblage, which are dominated by Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria. Among these frass-derived microbes, only a small proportion successfully colonize the substrates and plant roots, with the strongest influence observed in the bulk substrate, followed by the rhizoplane, and rhizosphere. This reflects “microbial source specificity”: bulk substrate, less influenced by root exudates, is more readily colonized by frass-derived microbes, while rhizoplane and rhizosphere are primarily shaped by seedling substrate microbes. Notably, frass-derived microbes exhibit selective colonization of the rhizoplane (more unique OTUs than the rhizosphere), consistent with the rhizoplane’s role as a “selective barrier” for root-associated microbes [25].

Beyond direct colonization, PBL frass activates a large pool of unknown-sourced microbes, whose contribution to community structure exceeds that of frass-derived microbes. These unknown-sourced microbes also dominate in Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, with microenvironment-specific enrichment of functional taxa (e.g., Verrucomicrobia in rhizoplane/rhizosphere, Firmicutes in bulk substrate). This activation is likely fueled by frass organic matter, which acts as a “hot spot and hot moment” for microbial activity—alleviating microbial dependence on carbon/nitrogen and dispersal limitations, thus stimulating dormant microbial populations [26]. This is supported by alpha diversity results: 2% or 4% frass significantly enhanced microbial richness across all microenvironments, and higher diversity not only boosts soil functional redundancy but also supports key processes like nutrient cycling and pathogen suppression [27]. We note that the ‘unknown source’ category in our SourceTracker model may also include plant‑endophytic microorganisms derived from the tomato seedlings themselves, as we did not independently sequence the plant tissue microbiome.

Taxonomically, both frass-derived and unclassified microbial communities were predominantly composed of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes—taxonomic groups previously associated with nutrient cycling and plant health [28–31]. Studies have shown that reductions in protective microbiota, such as Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, can increase the incidence of bacterial diseases [32]. Among these phyla, we focused specifically on three genera widely recognized for their beneficial effects on plant and soil health: Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Streptomyces [33–37]. Our analysis revealed that all three genera successfully colonized the rhizoplane following the application of PBL frass. Recent research has indicated that synergistic interactions between Bacillus and Pseudomonas can enhance plant health [38]. Furthermore, other beneficial genera were found to proliferate after PBL frass application. For instance, Flavobacterium, which was enriched across all three microenvironments, has been shown positive contribution to plant health and development, including growth promotion, disease control, and tolerance to abiotic stress [39, 40]. Acinetobacter, which was dominant in the rhizoplane, are known for their plant growth-promoting properties and pathogen-inhibiting capabilities [41–43].

Notably, to obtain more stable and reliable data on microbial community dynamics, we conducted controlled indoor pot experiments. However, this experimental design was constrained by the limited space of indoor growth chambers, which prevented the measurement of crop yield. Therefore, further research is needed under field conditions, with a specific focus on investigating the impact of PBL frass on crop yield. This will help validate its effectiveness in actual agricultural production and provide robust evidence to support its broader application in substrate-based soilless cultivation systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates the feasibility of employing PBL frass as valuable amendment in substrate cultivation to promote plant growth and modulate plant-associated substrate microbial communities. The PBL frass affects substrate microecological systems through two suggestive pathways on the basis of not influencing the microbial community in the original seedling substrate: direct contribution by frass-derived microbes and indirect activation of numerous unknown sourced microbes to the substrate. And the increased microbial diversity resulting from PBL frass benefits both substrate function and plant growth. This work holds significant importance for agricultural sustainable development.
