Comparison of catheter outcomes according to dialysis modality and clinical factors in patients with femoral dialysis catheters
Authors: A-Young Kim, Kyu Hyang Cho, Jong Won Park, Jun Young Do, Seok Hui Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough femoral vein catheters are less preferred than internal jugular vein catheters, they are still actively used as an alternative. However, limited studies have assessed whether the patency of femoral vein catheters differs according to the initial dialysis modality. We compared and analyzed femoral vein catheter outcomes according to the initial dialysis modality and clinical factors.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed medical records of 279 patients (a total of 318 cases) who underwent femoral vein catheterization for hemodialysis from January 2019 to August 2023. We divided the patients into two groups according to the initial dialysis modality (hemodialysis group [n = 182] and continuous renal replacement therapy group [n = 121]) and analyzed catheter survival.ResultsWe eventually included 303 cases of femoral vein catheterization for hemodialysis from 264 patients. Catheter survival did not differ significantly between the hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapy groups. Body mass index was associated with overall catheter survival. In the hemodialysis group, no significant factor was associated with catheter survival. However, in the continuous renal replacement therapy group, increased body mass index, age, and platelet counts were associated with low catheter survival.ConclusionFemoral dialysis catheter survival was not associated with the initial dialysis modality, and a high body mass index was associated with low survival rates of femoral dialysis catheters in patients who underwent continuous renal replacement therapy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Hemodialysis (HD) catheters are used for vascular access not only in acute HD patients but also in maintenance HD patients who cannot use arteriovenous fistulas or grafts [1]. HD catheters are usually placed in central veins, such as the internal jugular vein, subclavian vein, or femoral vein. Femoral vein catheters have lower survival rates than jugular vein catheters because they are located around the groin and have a higher risk of infection or kinking [2, 3]. However, femoral vein catheterization has the advantages of a low risk of bleeding, a simple procedure, and the non-requirement of imaging confirmation before dialysis [4]. Additionally, internal jugular vein catheters cannot be inserted in patients with swelling, anatomical distortion, or skin infection around the neck due to trauma or surgery [5]. Femoral vein catheterization is still actively used as an alternative when internal jugular vein catheterization cannot be performed. The proportion of femoral vein catheters among central venous catheters differs in different studies. According to one study, the femoral vein accounts for approximately 7.4%–38.3% of dialysis catheter insertion sites [6]. This proportion has been reported to be up to 46.6–48.4% in another study [7]. According to a report from the Korean Society of Nephrology, the femoral vein accounts for 0.5% of central venous catheter sites in Korea [8].

Studies have assessed the factors that affect the survival rate of femoral vein catheters. The patency of femoral vein catheters was found to decrease with an increase in age and with the presence of an underlying disease, such as diabetes or hypertension [9]. Moreover, the patency decreased when the catheter was placed in the left femoral vein rather than in the right one [9, 10]. The rate of catheter thrombosis was higher for femoral vein catheters than for internal jugular vein catheters [11]. However, limited studies have assessed whether the patency of femoral vein catheters differs according to the initial dialysis modality. In this study, we compared and analyzed femoral vein catheter outcomes according to the initial dialysis modality and clinical factors.

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed medical records of 279 patients (a total of 318 cases) who underwent femoral vein catheterization for HD from January 2019 to August 2023. We excluded patients who died within 24 h after starting dialysis. Ultimately, we included 264 patients (303 cases). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Yeungnam University Hospital (2024-08-012). The requirement of informed consent was waived, as patients’ records and information were anonymized and de-identified before analysis. The study was conducted ethically in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number; not applicable.

Clinical characteristics

Data on patient’s sex; age; underlying disease; catheter insertion side; body mass index (BMI); antiplatelet or anticoagulation drug use; blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, serum albumin, and hemoglobin levels; platelet counts; and international normalized ratio (INR) were obtained. The patients were divided into two groups, namely HD and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) groups, according to the initial dialysis modality. The initial dialysis modality was based on the dialysis method when the femoral vein catheter was inserted. CRRT was performed if a patient was hemodynamically unstable and required vasopressor administration during dialysis. The catheter insertion side was classified based on whether the catheter was inserted into the right femoral vein or the left femoral vein. Catheter insertion was performed aseptically under ultrasound guidance. Catheter insertion was performed by a nephrologist or an internal medicine resident with at least one year of experience. An 11.5-Fr × 19.5-cm dual-lumen catheter was used in this study. Information about the location of the catheter tip could not be confirmed. BMI was calculated based on the patients’ height and weight measured at the time of admission using the following equation: BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m2) [11, 12]. BMI was classified according to previous guidelines as follows: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5 ≤ BMI < 23 kg/m2), overweight (23 ≤ BMI < 25 kg/m2), obesity class I (25 ≤ BMI < 29.9 kg/m2), and obesity class II/III (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) [13]. Antiplatelet or anticoagulant drug use was defined as the consumption of antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs, such as aspirin, clopidogrel, or warfarin, at the time of catheter insertion.

Catheter survival was defined as the period from catheter insertion to removal. Causes of catheter removal were classified into six categories: dialysis termination, patient death, change to long-term catheterization, catheter malfunction, self-removal of the catheter, or infection. Dialysis termination was defined as the removal of the catheter because the patient no longer needed dialysis treatment. The change to long-term catheterization was defined as the transition to a permanent catheter when the patient needed continuous HD. Cases of dialysis termination, patient death, change to long-term catheterization, or self-removal of the catheter by the patient were considered censored data. Catheter malfunction was defined as poor blood flow into or out of the conduit during dialysis. Catheter-related infection was defined as the presence of bacteria in the blood or the presence of symptoms of an infection, such as redness or tenderness, around the catheter insertion site.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentages), while continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations. The t-test was used to compare continuous variables between the two groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to plot the survival in the two groups, and the log-rank test was performed to determine statistical significance. Hazard ratios (HRs) and confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox regression analysis. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were adjusted for age; sex; BMI; catheter insertion side; underlying diabetes or chronic kidney disease (CKD); antiplatelet drug use; BUN, serum creatinine, serum albumin, and hemoglobin levels; platelet counts; INR; and initial dialysis modality. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed in the backward mode. The proportional hazard assumption was satisfied for all the variables. Moreover, competing risk analyses were performed to decrease the effects of censored data. Censored cases, including patient death, change to long-term catheterization, and self-removal of the catheter, were defined as competing risks for competing risk analyses, and analyses were performed using the Fine and Gray competing risk model. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of participants

The HD and CRRT groups consisted of 182 and 121 cases, respectively (Table 1). The HD group had a greater prevalence of underlying CKD and higher BMI, BUN and serum creatinine levels, and platelet counts than the CRRT group. On the other hand, the CRRT group had a greater prevalence of diabetes and a higher hemoglobin level and INR than the HD group. No significant differences were noted in age, sex, catheter insertion side, antiplatelet drug use, and serum albumin levels. Age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index scores were analyzed to assess the severity of the patient’s comorbidities. In the hemodialysis group, the average was 5.4 ± 2.7, and in the CRRT group, the average was 4.5 ± 2.4 (P = 0.04).

Catheter outcomes according to initial dialysis modality

Dialysis termination, patient death, and change to long-term catheterization were noted in 56 (30.8%), 25 (13.7%), and 61 (33.5%) cases, respectively, in the HD group and 35 (28.9%), 56 (46.3%), and 11 (9.1%) cases, respectively, in the CRRT group. Catheter removal due to malfunction, self-removal of the catheter, and infection were noted in 31 (17%), six (3.3%), and three (1.6%) cases, respectively, in the HD group and 16 (13.2%), three (2.5%), and zero cases, respectively, in the CRRT group. The average duration of catheterization was 8.86 ± 6.2 days in the HD group and 9.01 ± 7.7 days in the CRRT group. No significant correlation was noted between the duration of catheterization and dialysis modality (P = 0.86). Additionally, the patients were classified into four groups based on their dialysis modality and duration of catheterization: short-term conventional HD (n = 106), long-term conventional HD (n = 74), short-term CRRT (n = 77), and long-term CRRT (n = 46). The aforementioned short-term and long-term groups were formed based on the median duration of catheterization. No significant correlation was noted between each group and catheter survival (P = 0.76).

Patient mortality was higher in the CRRT group (64.3%). The correlation between patient death and catheter survival was analyzed using a Cox proportional hazards model. No significant correlation was noted between catheter survival and patient death (P = 0.94). Moreover, no significant correlation was noted between patient death and catheter infection (P = 0.17) or catheter malfunction (P = 0.96).

The Kaplan–Meier curves are presented in Fig. 1. Both overall catheter survival and competing risk model results were similar between the HD and CRRT groups. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed a greater HR for catheter survival with an increase in age or BMI; however, the initial dialysis modality was not associated with catheter survival. In the competing risk model, none of the factors exhibited a statistically significant association with catheter survival (Table 2).

Catheter outcomes according to initial dialysis modality and BMI

The number of patients classified as underweight, normal, overweight, obesity class I, and obesity class II/III was 7 (3.8%), 65 (35.7%), 41 (22.5%), 54 (29.7%), and 15 (8.2%) in the HD group and 14 (11.6%), 51 (42.1%), 25 (20.7%), 25 (20.7%), and 6 (5.0%) in the CRRT group, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analyses were performed according to BMI categories. The 10-day catheter survival rates in the HD group were 83.3% (underweight), 79.6% (normal), 76.4% (overweight), 80.5% (obesity I), and 83.0% (obesity II/III), respectively, and 100%, 95.2%, 66.3%, 75.9%, and 41.7%, respectively, in the CRRT group (Fig. 2A and C). In the competing risk model, the 10-day catheter survival rates in the HD group were 100% (underweight), 56.1% (normal), 66.1% (overweight), 78.8% (obesity class I), and 72.0% (obesity class II/III), respectively, and 100%, 94.1%, 59.1%, 65.3%, and 50.0%, respectively, in the CRRT group (Fig. 2B and D). No significant differences in catheter survival were observed between BMI groups in the HD group. In the CRRT group, high BMI was significantly associated with poor catheter survival, although statistical significance was attenuated in the competing risk model.

In both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, catheter survival in the HD group showed no association with any covariate, including BMI. In contrast, multivariate analysis of the total cohort identified age and platelet count as significant predictors: each one-year increase in age was associated with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.05 (95% CI: 1.00–1.11; P = 0.042), and each 1 K/µL increase in platelet count corresponded to an HR of 1.01 (95% CI: 1.00–1.01; P = 0.032). Obese BMI categories exhibited higher HRs compared to normal BMI. Multivariate analysis incorporating a competing risk model revealed similar trends (Table 3). No significant differences were noted in catheter survival based on changes in initial dialysis modality (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we enrolled 303 cases of femoral vein catheterization for HD from 264 patients. We evaluated catheter survival based on the initial dialysis modality. There was no significant difference in catheter survival between HD and CRRT groups. BMI was associated with overall catheter survival. Therefore, we performed survival analyses using subgroups based on the initial dialysis modality. In the HD group, no significant factor was associated with catheter survival. However, in the CRRT group, obesity, age, and platelet count were associated with reduced catheter survival rates. A similar trend was observed for BMI, although its statistical significance diminished in the competing risk model.

Both HD and CRRT can be used to filter fluids and solutes. CRRT has a slower clearance rate than HD and is mainly used in severely hemodynamically unstable patients [14]. CRRT is associated with a 30% lower mortality rate than is HD [15]. However, a recent study has reported that CRRT offers no benefits in terms of survival or renal recovery [16]. In our center, the blood flow rate wais adjusted to 150–200 mL/min for HD and 100–200 mL/min for CRRT depending on each patient’s condition. Central venous catheter dysfunction has been reported to increase significantly when the mean blood flow is less than 325 mL/min [17]. However, the blood flow rate varies by region or faculty [18]. Studies have revealed that CRRT circuit life and high blood flow rate are not correlated [19, 20]. Although this finding wais not directly related to catheter survival, further research is warranted to assess the correlation between catheter survival and blood flow rate.

Previous studies have assessed the difference in catheter survival based on catheter insertion sites [21–25]. In these studies, internal jugular vein catheters have shown better or similar outcomes to femoral vein catheters. Bellmo et al. found that the prevalence of femoral vein catheters was higher than that of jugular vein catheters in patients with a high BMI or severe conditions [23]. However, few studies have assessed the difference in outcomes of non-cuffed/non-tunneled femoral vein catheters based on the initial dialysis modality as a primary outcome. In our study, no significant difference existed in catheter survival between the HD and CRRT groups. The competing risk model also showed similar trends in all cases. Despite the weak statistical significance, the results of Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses revealed lower catheter survival in the HD group than in the CRRT group. Although we could not identify a clear mechanism for this phenomenon, patients in the HD group generally had a better overall condition and were more ambulatory than those in the CRRT group. These factors may have contributed to catheter malfunction and infection rates, in turn resulting in the statistically non-significant poor catheter survival observed in the HD group. In our study, antiplatelet or anticoagulant drug use was not significantly associated with catheter survival. In the case of circuit anticoagulation, the analysis was limited because of its flexible use for each dialysis session depending on each patient’s condition. The use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs was not significantly associated with catheter dysfunction. Instead, it increased the risk of bleeding [26, 27]. Moreover, no significant correlation was noted between circuit anticoagulation, such as the use of low-molecular-weight heparin or nafamostat, and catheter survival [28].

We also evaluated the factors associated with catheter survival within subgroups based on the initial dialysis modality. A high BMI was associated with poor catheter survival in the CRRT group. This association has also been identified in previous studies. In a post hoc study based on the RENAL trial, patients with femoral vein catheters were thinner and more ill than those with jugular vein catheters [23]. Moreover, a randomized study demonstrated that patients with a high BMI were more prone to catheter-related infections [21]. Chua et al. compared the prevalence of catheter-related infections between femoral and non-femoral catheter groups [22]. The difference in infection rates between the two groups increased with an increase in BMI. Moreover, the infection risk associated with femoral vein catheters was significantly higher in the group with a higher BMI. A high risk of infection in patients with femoral vein catheters may be associated with some challenges. Femoral catheterization would be technically challenging in patients with a high BMI. The increased risk of flesh folding or bacterial colonization in these patients could be a contributing factor. Moreover, in patients with a higher BMI, the subcutaneous tissue is thicker. Thus, even with the same catheter length, the intravascular component is relatively shorter. Consequently, the vein diameter at the final position of the catheter tip is smaller, which may be associated with lower catheter survival.

Unlike the CRRT group, BMI was not associated with catheter survival in the HD group. Several factors may have contributed to this discrepancy. First, the HD group had a relatively high rate of change to long-term catheterization. Catheter duration is a well-known high-risk factor for catheter-related infections [29, 30]. The timely transition to long-term catheterization in the HD group may reduce the duration of short-term catheter use, thereby lowering complications in patients with a high BMI. Second, at our center, catheter locking is exclusively performed using heparin. For HD patients, catheter locking using heparin is intermittently performed during the interdialytic period. Although the antimicrobial effect of heparin is not as definite as that of antibiotics or sodium citrate, heparin does possess some immunomodulatory properties, which are associated with favorable outcomes in patients with sepsis [31]. This effect of heparin may reduce the risk of infection in high BMI patients in the HD group.

Catheter design and diameter are significantly correlated with the survival rate of temporary hemodialysis catheters [32]. The incidence of thrombosis was higher with straight catheters than with precurved ones. The authors speculated that damage due to endothelial contact with the catheter may have influenced these results [32]. Although this study targeted internal jugular vein catheters, additional research is needed to determine how catheter design and diameter affect the catheter function in femoral vein catheters.

The study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study with a limited sample size. Statistical significance may be not achieved because of the limited sample size. This finding limits robust conclusions and mandates caution during interpretation. Extended studies involving multicenter or large data sets are warranted. Second, significant differences in patient characteristics or causes of renal replacement therapy existed between the HD and CRRT groups. Because CRRT is generally performed in hemodynamically unstable patients, more critically ill patients were included in the CRRT group. There was no significant difference in catheter function according to the modality; however, the heterogeneity between the two groups was a limitation of this study. We tried to attenuate this difference by performing multivariate analysis; however, these confounding factors may not have been completely controlled. Third, high rates of competing risks and different distributions of causes of censoring can be a limitation when comparing catheter survival. Fourth, catheter survival may be significantly influenced by the experience and training level of the personnel performing the insertion. Catheter insertion was not performed by the same person, which may be a limitation of this study. Fifth, patients who died within 24 h were excluded, introducing a potential bias related to illness severity, a recognized limitation of this study. However, their inclusion may have influenced the findings, as the follow-up period would have been insufficient to assess catheter-related outcomes such as infection or malfunction.

Catheter survival is affected by various confounding factors; therefore, it is very difficult to confirm the independent effect of each factor. Moreover, as noted in our study, it is widely accepted in general medical practice that a patient’s condition or clinical judgment takes precedence over modality selection to improve catheter survival. Nevertheless, we believe that our findings can help in the initial selection of the catheter insertion site by comparing femoral catheter survival across modalities. In patients requiring catheter insertion, CRRT may result in lower survival of femoral vein catheters than HD; therefore, internal jugular vein catheterization should be preferred for more stable catheter maintenance, unless femoral vein catheterization is necessary. On the other hand, if patients who require HD prioritize the cosmetic effect and can consider early discontinuation, femoral vein catheterization should be preferred.

Conclusion

In conclusion, femoral dialysis catheter survival was not influenced by the initial dialysis modality. However, among patients undergoing CRRT, obesity reflected by a high BMI was associated with reduced catheter survival rates. Therefore, the initial dialysis modality appears to have minimal impact on femoral vein catheter outcomes. However, in obese patients with elevated BMI undergoing CRRT, opting for non-femoral vascular access may significantly enhance catheter survival.
