Tensor Stochastic Regression for High-dimensional Time Series via CP Decomposition
Shibo Li, Yao Zheng

Abstract
As tensor-valued data become increasingly common in time series analysis, there is a growing need for flexible and interpretable models that can handle high-dimensional predictors and responses across multiple modes. We propose a unified framework for high-dimensional tensor stochastic regression based on CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition, which encompasses vector, matrix, and tensor responses and predictors as special cases. Tensor autoregression naturally arises as a special case within this framework. By leveraging CP decomposition, the proposed models interpret the interactive roles of any two distinct tensor modes, enabling dynamic modeling of input-output mechanisms. We develop both CP low-rank and sparse CP low-rank estimators, establish their non-asymptotic error bounds, and propose an efficient alternating minimization algorithm for estimation. Simulation studies confirm the theoretical properties and demonstrate the computational advantage. Applications to mixed-frequency macroeconomic data and spatio-temporal air pollution data reveal interpretable low-dimensional structures and meaningful dynamic dependencies.



Keywords
: CANDECOMP/PARAFAC decomposition; High-dimensional time series; Low-rank estimation; Tensor autoregression; Sparse estimation;

: Primary 62M10; secondary 62H12, 60G10

Introduction

Driven by the need for analyzing complex dynamic systems and advancements in data collection capability, tensor-valued time series data have become increasingly common in many areas, such as economics
(
Chen et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
, finance
(
Rogers et al., 2013
, e.g.)
, transportation engineering
(
Han et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
, medical imaging analyses
(
Bi et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
. Tensor-valued time series
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\dots\times p_{m}}
are typically formed when more granular data are available, where
t
denotes time. This granularity may arise from the cross-sectional dimension, i.e., when
p_{d}
outcome variables are observed at disaggregated levels, such as by industry or geographic region, or across different categories. It may also result from the temporal dimension, which could be due to the incorporation of multiple time lags or the availability of higher-frequency observations. In particular, when modeling mixed-frequency time series data (e.g., yearly and monthly), it is natural to treat the additional temporal granularity (e.g,
p_{d}=12
months per year) as another dimension. This allows higher-frequency information to be easily leveraged within a modeling framework indexed by a lower-frequency time index
t
; e.g., higher-frequency variables can be used to predict lower-frequency variables, or vice versa.

In this paper, we consider the tensor stochastic regression for time series data, where the response
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
and the predictor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
can be vectors, matrices or high-order tensors of different dimensions. The tensor forms of the response or predictor can result from more detailed data in variables and temporal dimensions. It is noteworthy that our framework includes vector, matrix and tensor autoregressions
(
Chen et al., 2021a
;
Wang et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
as special cases, when
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
consists of lagged values of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
. However, it also allows for mixed-frequency tensor regression, which generalizes the mixed-data sampling (MIDAS) regression
(
Ghysels et al., 2006
)
to tensor time series. As emphasized in the rapidly growing literature on matrix- and tensor-valued time series, preserving the tensor form of the data—rather than vectorizing them and applying conventional multivariate modeling—is essential for maintaining multi-dimensional structural information. This preservation not only enables the extraction of distinct insights across each dimension of the data but also improves estimation efficiency, as dimension reduction can be achieved simultaneously across all directions.

There is an extensive body of literature on tensor regression for
i.i.d.
data, where low-rank assumptions are imposed on coefficient tensors typically via CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) or Tucker decomposition; see
Liu et al., 2021
and
Bi et al., 2021
for recent reviews. Our paper is most closely related to the literature on tensor regression via CP decomposition. For example,
Zhou et al., 2013
;
Wang et al., 2017
;
Feng et al., 2021
focus on models with scalar responses, whereas
Lock, 2018
and
Raskutti et al., 2019
, among others, study tensor-on-tensor regression, which includes scalar-on-tensor, vector-on-vector, vector-on-tensor regression, etc., as special cases. Our setup aligns with the latter, as we similarly aim for generality. For tensor-on-tensor regression via Tucker decomposition, see the recent work by
Luo and Zhang, 2024
and the references therein. Our paper differs considerably from the above works as we focus on time series rather than independent data. From a theoretical perspective, our analysis is complicated by the temporal dependencies in the response and predictor series. From an empirical perspective, the temporal dimensions, whether arising from time lags or mixed frequencies, carry distinct interpretations for tensor time series compared to models for
i.i.d.
data.

Recently, tensor decomposition methods have also been introduced to the modeling of vector-, matrix- and tensor-valued time series. For example, Tucker decomposition has been applied to high-dimensional vector
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
and tensor
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
autoregressions. Moreover, various matrix autoregressive (MAR) and tensor autoregressive (TAR) models with low-rank assumptions are intrinsically connected to the Tucker decomposition
(
Chen et al., 2021a
;
Samadi and Billard, 2024
, e.g.,)
; see also
Tsay, 2024
for a recent survey.
Chang et al., 2023
proposes regression with tensor response and vector predictors via CP decomposition for time series data. In contrast, CP decomposition has been rarely explored in autoregression and tensor-on-tensor regression for time series; an exception is the recent work by
Chan and Qi, 2024
where CP decomposition is applied to Bayesian vector autoregression (VAR). The popularity of Tucker decomposition may be partly due to its more natural connection with the classical reduced-rank VAR
(
Velu et al., 1986
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
. Nevertheless, CP decomposition has unique advantages over Tucker decomposition. Indeed, beyond the regression setting, there has been a recent surge of interest in applying CP decomposition to factor models. Compared to factor models based on Tucker decomposition
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Barigozzi et al., 2022
;
Chen and Lam, 2024
, e.g.,)
, those based on CP decomposition are attractive
(
Babii et al., 2024
;
Han et al., 2024
;
Chen et al., 2024
, e.g.)
as their identification does not rely on the orthogonality constraints typically required for the identifiability of Tucker decomposition based models. As discussed in these latter works, such constraints can be difficult to interpret and may limit the model’s flexibility. This drawback motivates the growing interest in incorporating CP decomposition rather than Tucker decomposition in the construction of factor models.
However, tensor factor models cannot be directly used for forecasting as they are fundamentally unsupervised dimension reduction tools. To fill this gap, we consider predictive modeling for tensor-valued time series via CP decomposition, which simultaneously accomplishes dimension reduction and forecasting.

Furthermore, an important insight revealed in this paper is that, in the context of (auto)regression, CP decomposition can provide meaningful interpretations of
interactive
patterns across various dimensions, as well as input-output dynamic mechanisms. In contrast, methods based on Tucker decomposition, such as the TAR model in
Wang et al., 2024
, can only extract patterns separately for each dimension of the tensor-variate data. This can be a significant limitation in empirical analysis. For instance, applying our method to mixed-frequency macroeconomic data in Section
5.1
reveals the dynamic mechanism between quarterly responses and monthly predictors.
For the air pollution data in Section
5.2
, we are able to not only discover patterns within individual dimensions such as pollutants, regions, or months, but also explore interactions among these dimensions: e.g., do different pollutants concentrate in specific regions or during certain months? Additionally, we can examine input-output dynamics: how do past levels of different pollutants influence current concentrations, and how do past pollution in various regions affect the present distribution of pollutants across these regions?
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

For the general tensor stochastic regression, we propose dimension reduction via the CP low-rank assumption on the coefficient tensor. This framework encompasses vector, matrix and tensor autoregressions (TAR) via CP decomposition as special cases, which have not been studied in the literature.

•

To improve the estimation efficiency and interpretability when the tensor dimensions are large, we further impose entrywise sparsity on the component vectors. This leads to the sparse CP low-rank stochastic tensor regression and TAR.
For both the non-sparse and sparse models, we establish non-asymptotic statistical error bounds for the corresponding estimators. An efficient algorithm is also developed, and its computational advantage is verified through simulation studies.

•

As demonstrated by our empirical analysis, unlike models based on Tucker low-rank structures, our approach provides meaningful insights into interactions across different dimensions, rather than only extracting patterns from each individual dimension.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the proposed models. Section
3
presents non-asymptotic properties for the proposed non-sparse and sparse CP low-rank estimators. Simulation and empirical studies are presented in Sections
4
and
5
, respectively. Section
6
concludes with a brief discussion. A detailed discussion of the connections between the proposed model and Tucker low-rank models, the proposed algorithm, additional simulation and empirical results, as well as all technical proofs, is provided in a separate supplementary file.

2
Tensor stochastic regression

2.1
Notation and tensor algebra

Tensors, a.k.a. multi-dimensional arrays, are higher-order extensions of matrices. A multidimensional array
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
is called an
m
th order tensor
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
)
.
In this paper, we use bold lowercase letters to represent vectors, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\boldsymbol{y}
; bold capital letters to represent matrices, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{X}
,
\boldsymbol{Y}
; and bold Euler capital letters to represent tensors, e.g.,
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathscr{B}
. For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, denote its
\ell_{q}
-norm by
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}=(\sum_{j=1}^{p}|x_{j}|^{q})^{1/q}
for
q>0
, its
\ell_{0}
-norm by
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{0}=|\textrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{x})|=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{I}_{\{x_{j}\neq 0\}}
, and its normalization by
\textrm{Norm}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}
. For a positive integer
s
, we let
\textrm{Truncate}(\boldsymbol{x},s)
denote the operation that sets all but the
s
largest (in absolute value) entries of
\boldsymbol{x}
to zero.
For any
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
, let
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{X})
(or
\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{X})
) denote its transpose and largest (or smallest) eigenvalue, respectively.
For any sequences
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
, denote
x_{n}\lesssim y_{n}
(or
x_{n}\gtrsim y_{n}
) if there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that
x_{n}\leq Cy_{n}
(or
x_{n}\geq Cy_{n}
).

The outer product of a set of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{d}\}_{k=1}^{K}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{k}}
, denoted by
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{z}_{K}
, equals
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{K}}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{z}_{k,i_{k}}
. The tensor-matrix multiplication of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{K}}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{k}\times p_{k}}
on the
k
th mode with
1\leq k\leq K
is denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\times_{k}\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{k-1}\times q_{k}\times p_{k+1}\cdots\times p_{K}}
, where
(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\times_{k}\boldsymbol{Y})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}ji_{k+1}\cdots i_{K}}=\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{p_{k}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}\boldsymbol{Y}_{ji_{k}}
. For
m,n\geq 1
, the generalized inner product of two tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
on the last
n
modes is denoted by
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
, where
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\rangle_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}
. The outer product between two tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
is denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
, where
(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}
. The Frobenius norm of a tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{K}}
is
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}^{2}}
. The vectorization of a tensor,
\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$})
, is defined analogously to that of a matrix.

For a tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{N}}
, the CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition
factorizes it into a sum of component rank-one
tensors as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=[\![\boldsymbol{\omega};\boldsymbol{B}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{B}^{(2)},\dots,\boldsymbol{B}^{(N)}]\!]=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N},

(2.1)

where
\omega_{r}\in(0,\infty)
and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}=1
, for
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
, with
R
being a positive integer.
The rank of a tensor
\mathscr{B}
is defined as the
smallest number of rank-one tensors that generate
\mathscr{B}
as their sum. In other words, this is the smallest
R
such that the CP decomposition in (
2.1
) holds for
\mathscr{B}
. A sufficient condition for the uniqueness of the CP decomposition for a general
n
th-order tensor
\mathscr{B}
, up to a permutation, is

\sum_{d=1}^{N}k_{\boldsymbol{B}^{(d)}}\geq 2R+(N-1)
, where
k_{\boldsymbol{B}^{(d)}}
denotes
the Kruskal rank of the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}^{(d)}=(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1,d},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{R,d})
; see
Sidiropoulos and Bro, 2000
. Here the Kruskal rank of a matrix is defined as the maximum value
k
such that any
k
columns of the matrix are linearly independent
(
Kruskal, 1976
)
.

Remark 1
.

The sufficient condition for the uniqueness of the CP decomposition, up to a permutation, is a typically mild requirement in practice
(
Ten Berge and Sidiropoulos, 2002
;
Sun and Li, 2017
;
Zeng and Ng, 2021
)
: when
R
is fixed, it implies that the identifiability is guaranteed when the components
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}
are not highly dependent across
r
, and it is reasonable to assume that this condition is fulfilled in most empirical applications.

2.2
Model formulation

We consider the regression of an
m
th-order tensor response series
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
on an
n
th-order tensor predictor series
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t},\quad 1\leq t\leq T,

(2.2)

where
m,n\geq 1
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
is the innovation at time
t
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
is the
N
th-order coefficient tensor, with
N=m+n
. Here, without loss of generality, by centering both series
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\}
and
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\}
, we set the intercept to zero.
Let
p_{y}=\prod_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}
and
q_{x}=\prod_{d=1}^{n}q_{d}
.
Note that even when each individual dimension
p_{d}
or
q_{d}
is relatively small, the ambient dimension of
\mathscr{B}
, i.e.,
p_{y}q_{x}
, can be very large due to a moderately large
N
. For example,
\mathscr{B}
is a sixth-order tensor with more than
10^{6}
entries for the air pollution data in Section
5.2
.
Thus, efficient dimension reduction is required for model (
2.2
).

To reduce the dimensionality of
\mathscr{B}
while achieving the interpretability of the tensor dynamic structure, we assume that
\mathscr{B}
admits the rank-
R
CP decomposition in (
2.1
) with
N=m+n
. This assumption reduces the number of parameters from
p_{y}q_{x}
to
R(1+\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})
.
Moreover, for an even more substantial dimension reduction, we can further impose entrywise sparsity assumptions
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{0}\leq s_{d}
, where
s_{d}
represents the sparsity level for the
d
th mode, for
1\leq d\leq N
.

Combining (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), the model can be written as

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\big(\underbrace{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}}_{f_{r,t}}\big)+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t},

(2.3)

where
f_{r,t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}
for
1\leq r\leq R
are univariate factor processes summarizing the information in the predictor series. Model (
2.3
) shows that we can separate
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\}_{d=1}^{N}
into two parts,
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\}_{d=1}^{m}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}\}_{d=1}^{n}
, which we call the
response
and
predictor
loading vectors, respectively. The latter encodes the high-dimensional predictor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
into univariate factors
f_{r,t}
for
1\leq r\leq R
. The
predictor
loading vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}
, which consists of relative weights across variables along the
d
th mode of the predictor, can vary across
r
. Then the information in each factor
f_{r,t}
is disseminated across
m
modes of the response
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
, where the relative weights across variables in the
d
th mode of the response are given by the
response
loading vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}
for
1\leq d\leq m
. In addition,
\omega_{r}
controls the relative importance of different factors.

As discussed above, for every
r
, the loading vectors
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}
provide insights into each mode of the response and predictor tensors individually. More interestingly, CP decomposition enables us to interpret the interactive roles of any two distinct modes
1\leq d_{1}\neq d_{2}\leq N
via the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}}
. There are three types of interactive relationships implied by (
2.3
):

(i)

response-response (
1\leq d_{1}\neq d_{2}\leq m
): Since the outer product is permutation-invariant, the full tensor
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}
can be viewed as an extension of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}}
along the remaining
m-2
modes.

(ii)

predictor-predictor (
d_{1}=m+d^{\prime}
and
d_{2}=m+d^{\prime\prime}
, with
1\leq d^{\prime}\neq d^{\prime\prime}\leq n
): We can separate out
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}}
by rewriting
f_{r,t}=\langle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1,d\notin\{d_{1}-m,d_{2}-m\}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}\rangle
.

(iii)

response-predictor (
1\leq d_{1}\leq m
and
d_{2}=m+d^{\prime\prime}
, with
1\leq d^{\prime\prime}\leq n
): It can be shown that, up to a permutation of modes,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}f_{r,t}
is equivalent to

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}+1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\circ\langle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1,d\neq d_{2}-m}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}\rangle.

(2.4)

Thus, in all three cases, the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}}
fully encapsulates the effects associated with two specific modes, which may belong to the response or predictor tensor.

Interestingly, model (
2.3
) offers a simple data-driven framework to accommodate high-dimensional response and predictor series with mismatched sampling frequencies.
In Section
5.1
, we demonstrate a macroeconomic analysis based on the model
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$},\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\rangle+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{179}
is a low-frequency response, containing 179 economic variables observed each quarter
t
. The predictor
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{112\times 3}
is high-frequency, containing 112 variables, with three monthly observations available in each quarter
t
. Based on the decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,3}
, group patterns within quarterly and monthly variables can be identified from
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\in\mathbb{R}^{179}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2}\in\mathbb{R}^{112}
, respectively. More interestingly, we can understand the response-predictor (i.e., output-input) relationship by visualizing the heatmap of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2}
, despite the different sampling frequencies of predictors and responses. Furthermore,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
captures the temporal aggregation pattern, i.e., how the high-frequency signals are summarized into the low-frequency scale. Importantly, since
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,3}
is an unknown parameter, it allows the data to determine the aggregation weights rather than relying on predetermined ones; see Remark
3
in section for more discussions. In short, as a useful by-product, our CP decomposition-based approach offers a simple, data-driven tool for disentangling variables and sampling frequencies in mixed-frequency time series modeling.

In fact, it is straightforward to further incorporate lags of predictor variables. For instance, to include data from the
L>1
most recent quarters up to quarter
t
, we can simply form a tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{112\times 3\times L}
. Moreover, it is noteworthy that our approach is equally applicable in settings with high-frequency responses and low-frequency predictors. In such cases, the frequency difference will be treated as a mode of the response tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
, while the corresponding loading vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}
indicates how the low-frequency information is exploited at the high-frequency scale.

Remark 2

(Supervised factor modeling)
.

While we refer to
\{f_{r,t}\}
in (
2.3
) as factor processes, they are different from conventional factors in unsupervised settings
(
Bai and Wang, 2016
;
Wang et al., 2019
;
Han et al., 2024
;
Babii et al., 2024
)
, where the goal is to identify latent drivers of co-movement in high-dimensional time series. In contrast, our factors are extracted from the observed predictors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
and are supervised, i.e., chosen to be most predictive of the response
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
. This supervised nature arises directly from the regression framework, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
serves as input and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
as output. Therefore, although (
2.3
) resembles tensor factor models in
Han et al., 2024
;
Babii et al., 2024
in form, the meaning of
\{f_{r,t}\}
differs. For instance,
Han et al., 2024
assume uncorrelated factors, and
Babii et al., 2024
impose orthogonality assumptions. These assumptions are not applicable to our model due to the different nature of those factors.

Remark 3

(Connection with MIDAS)
.

There is extensive literature on mixed-frequency regression, particularly Mixed Data Sampling (MIDAS) models
(
Ghysels et al., 2006
;
Foroni and Marcellino, 2013
;
Babii et al., 2022
)
, which typically forecast a univariate low-frequency variable using high-frequency predictors.
However, the case involving high-dimensional responses and predictors remains largely underexplored. While a naive extension is to apply univariate MIDAS entrywise, it would inevitably ignore potential low-dimensional structures in the response space. Traditional MIDAS methods often impose parametric lag structures, which can be restrictive when applied uniformly across responses. While U-MIDAS variants
(
Foroni et al., 2015
)
avoid these structures, it becomes overparameterized in high dimensions.
Our method can be viewed as a structured U-MIDAS approach using CP decomposition: it tensorizes the coefficient array across frequency, predictors, and responses, enabling flexible modeling of lag effects and response interactions. The CP low-rank structure ensures both scalability and interpretability in high-dimensional mixed-frequency settings. Moreover, our approach applies to high-frequency responses and low-frequency predictors.

2.3
Tensor autoregression

This section introduces a new tensor autoregressive (TAR) model as an important special case of model (
2.2
).

In the simple case where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1}
, the TAR(1) model is formed, where the coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
has the CP decomposition in (
2.1
), with
N=2m=2n
, and
(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
. The interpretations regarding (
2.3
), particularly those regarding individual predictor or response loading vectors and various loading matrices
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}}
, apply immediately.
Consider the Taiwan air pollution time series data in Section
5.2
as a concrete example. For each year
t
, the observations can be arranged into a third-order tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{12\times 7\times 12}
, for 12 monitoring stations, 7 air pollutants, and 12 months. Then
\mathscr{B}
is a tensor with
N=6
modes satisfying (
2.1
). In this case, for each
1\leq r\leq R
, the
predictor
loading vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\}_{d=4}^{6}
capture important signals from the previous year across various monitoring stations, air pollutants, and months, which are predictive of the following year. Analogously, the
response
loading vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\}_{d=1}^{3}
summarize how the past influence manifests in the following year across stations, pollutants, and months. Moreover, we can interpret interactions between any two modes. For example,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,4}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,5}
reveals the station-pollutant interaction in signals from the past year, while
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2}
captures this interaction in terms of the response that manifests in the following year.
In addition, as demonstrated in (
2.4
),
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,4}
summarizes dynamic interactions across stations from one year to the next; see Section
5.2
for details.

More generally, for an
m
th-order tensor time series
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
, we can take
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times L}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{\cdots\ell}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-\ell}
for
1\leq\ell\leq L
, given that
L>1
; i.e.,
n=m+1
and
(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m},L)
. Thus, if
L>1
, we can define the TAR(
L
) model,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{\ell},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-\ell}\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t},

(2.5)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times L}
satisfies (
2.1
) with
N=2m+1
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{\ell}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{\cdots\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
.
The interpretation of (
2.3
) can be generalized to model (
2.5
), where the factor process
f_{r,t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1}^{m+1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}
in (
2.3
)
captures the
r
th input signals of the dynamic system across not only
m
different cross-sectional modes, but also the lag mode. That is, the entries of the loading vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
are relative weights across
L
lags. They control how the strength of the
r
th cross-sectional dynamic dependence pattern, as characterized by
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}
for
1\leq d\leq 2m
, varies over time lags.

Remark 4

(Stationarity condition)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{y}}
be the vectorization of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
, and similarly
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})
.
For
1\leq\ell\leq L
, let
\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell}
denote the
p_{y}\times p_{y}
matricization of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{\ell}
obtained by combining its first
m
modes to rows and the other
m
modes to columns. Then, (
2.5
) can be written in the vector form as

\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\boldsymbol{B}_{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{y}_{t-\ell}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
. By (
2.1
) it can be shown that
\boldsymbol{B}_{(\ell)}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\beta_{r,2m+1}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+1})^{\top}
, where
\beta_{r,2m+1}^{(\ell)}
is the
\ell
th entry of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
for
1\leq\ell\leq L
, given that
L>1
. Thus, by the stationarity condition of the vector autoregressive model
(
Lütkepohl, 2005
)
, the TAR(
L
) model in (
2.5
) is strictly stationarity if and only if
\rho(\boldsymbol{\underline{B}})<1
, where
\rho(\boldsymbol{\underline{B}})
is the spectral radius of

\boldsymbol{\underline{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+1})^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{(L-1)p_{y}}\quad\boldsymbol{0}_{(L-1)p_{y}\times p_{y}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Lp_{y}\times Lp_{y}},

with
\boldsymbol{I}_{(L-1)p_{y}}
being the identity matrix of size
(L-1)p_{y}
. Note that the top block of
\boldsymbol{\underline{B}}
is
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+1})^{\top}=(\boldsymbol{B}_{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{B}_{(L)})
. In the special case where
L=1
, the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2m+1}
is simply omitted as the
(2m+1)
-th mode of
\mathscr{B}
is collapsed.

Remark 5

(Special case with
m=1
)
.

When
m=1
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{y}}
is a vector. In this case, (
2.5
) reduces to a vector-valued time series model,
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{y}_{t-\ell}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, with coefficient matrices
\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,2}^{\top}\beta_{r,3}^{(\ell)}
; i.e., in (
2.5
),
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}=\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{t-L})
is a
p_{y}\times L
matrix, and
\mathscr{B}
is a
p_{y}\times p_{y}\times L
tensor that satisfies (
2.1
) with
N=3
and is formed by stacking
\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell}
for
1\leq\ell\leq L
. Thus, a by-product of the proposed TAR model in the case of
m=1
is a new high-dimensional vector autoregressive (VAR) model based on the CP decomposition. Note that this is different from the vector form of the TAR model for a general
m
-th order tensor time series
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
for
m>1
; see Remark
4
.

3
High-dimensional estimation

3.1
CP low-rank estimator

Suppose that an observed sequence
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
of length
T
follows the tensor stochastic regression in (
2.3
).

While the ambient dimension of
\mathscr{B}
is
p_{y}q_{x}
, where
p_{y}=\prod_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}
and
q_{x}=\prod_{d=1}^{n}q_{d}
, by exploiting its CP low-rank structure, the required sample size for its consistent estimation can be reduced to
T\gg\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d}
. Specifically, we propose the CP low-rank estimator for
\mathscr{B}
as follows:

\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}=[\![\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{(1)},\dots,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{(N)}]\!]=\underset{\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{B}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{B}^{(N)}}{\argmin}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}\big)\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

subject to
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}=1
for
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
, where
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{r})_{1\leq r\leq R}
, and
\boldsymbol{B}^{(d)}=(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1,d},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{R,d})
.

Denote by
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
the true value of
\mathscr{B}
. Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{y}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{x}}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\boldsymbol{0}
, i.e., the data are centered. We also make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Identifiability)
.

The coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
has a unique CP decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}^{*}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}^{*}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}^{*}
, up to a permutation, where
\omega_{r}^{*}\in(0,\infty)
and
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}^{*}\|_{2}=1
for
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
.

Assumption 2

(Innovation process)
.

(i) Let
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}
is a positive definite covariance matrix,
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}
is a sequence of
i.i.d.
random vectors with zero mean and
\var(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})=\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{y}}
, and the coordinates
\xi_{it}
are mutually independent and
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian. (ii) Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
is independent of the
\sigma
-field
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{F}$}_{t}:=\sigma\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-2},\dots\}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Assumption 3

(Response and predictor processes)
.

(i) The process
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}),t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is stationary. (ii) The process
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\}
has an infinite-order moving average form,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}^{x}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-j}
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{\epsilon}}
,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}^{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{x}\times q_{\epsilon}}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}^{x}\|_{\mathrm{F}}<\infty
, and
q_{\epsilon}\leq q_{x}
. Moreover,
\{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\}
is a sequence of
i.i.d.
random vectors with zero mean and
\var(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t})=\boldsymbol{I}_{q_{\epsilon}}
, and the coordinates
\epsilon_{it}
are mutually independent and sub-Gaussian.

Assumption
1
requires the uniqueness of the CP decomposition; see a sufficient condition given in Section
2.1
and a discussion on its mildness in Remark
1
. The sub-Gaussian condition in Assumption
2
(i) is standard in the literature
(
Wang et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2024
;
Zheng, 2025
)
. Assumption
2
(ii) implies that the innovation
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}
is independent of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{F}$}_{t}
, i.e., information up to time
t
. This condition enables us to establish martingale concentration results under the general random design
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
. The stationarity of the time series, i.e., Assumption
3
(i), is standard. The infinite-order moving average form in Assumption
3
(ii) allows
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\}
to be a general linear process. The latter is used to establish the restricted strong convexity of the loss function; see the proofs of main theorems in the supplementary file.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}^{x}z^{j}
, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
Denote

\kappa_{1}=\min_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}}}(z)\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}(z))
and
\kappa_{2}=\max_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}}}(z)\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}(z))
,
where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}}}(z)
is the conjugate transpose of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{x}(z)
. In particular, Assumption
3
(ii) implies
\kappa_{1}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x})\leq\kappa_{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x}=\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top})
is the coveriance matrix of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
; see more details in Lemma
S.1
in the supplementary file. Note that
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are allowed to vary with the dimension.

The following theorem presents the estimation and prediction error bounds for the CP low-rank estimator.

Theorem 1

(CP low-rank estimator)
.

Suppose that the observed response and predictor series
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
follow model (
2.3
). Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, if
\kappa_{1}>0
and
T\gtrsim(\kappa_{2}/\kappa_{1})^{2}R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})\log N
, then

\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})\log N}{\kappa_{1}^{2}T}},\quad\text{and}

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\langle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})\log N}{\kappa_{1}T}

with probability at least
1-5e^{-2R(\sum_{d=1}^{N}p_{d}+1)\log\{6(N+1)\}}
.

By Theorem
1
, when the rank
R
and the constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})
are fixed, the estimation error rate is
\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})\log N/T})
, and the prediction error rate is
T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\langle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=O_{p}((\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})\log N/T)
. The required sample size
T
is at least
O(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d})
, a substantial reduction from
O(p_{y}q_{x})
.

3.2
Sparse CP low-rank estimator

In the ultra-high-dimensional setup where the ambient dimension of
\mathscr{B}
can grow at an exponential rate of the sample size, i.e.,
T\gtrsim\log(p_{y}q_{x})
, we propose the sparse CP low-rank estimator as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}=[\![\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}};\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{(N)}]\!]=\underset{\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{B}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{B}^{(N)}}{\argmin}

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}\big)\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

subject to
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{0}\leq s_{d}
for
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
, where
s_{d}
is a predetermined positive integer that bounds the sparsity level of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}
for each mode
d
. The
\ell_{0}
-constraints correspond to the hard-thresholding method for enforcing sparsity. It is favorable than the Lasso-type penalized approach as the latter always leads to a biased estimator although it is sparse. In fact, the Lasso or soft-thresholding method is typically preferred for ensuring the convexity of the loss function. However, since the above loss function is nonconvex due to the low-rankness, it is not necessary to insist on
using the Lasso-type penalty. In addition, the hard-thresholding method often provides greater numerical stability, as results tend to be more sensitive to the Lasso regularization parameter than to the sparsity upper bounds; see
Sun and Li, 2017
;
Huang et al., 2025
and the references therein.

Assumption 4

(Sparsity)
.

For
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
,
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}^{*}\|_{0}\leq s_{d}
, where the sparsity levels
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
are less than
p_{1},\dots,p_{m},q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
, respectively.

If
(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})\geq(p_{1},\dots,p_{m},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
holds elementwise, the sparsity constraints will become inactive, and
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
will reduce to
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
in Section
3.1
. To avoid uninformative results, we impose the upper bounds on
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
in Assumption
4
.

Similar to Theorem
1
, we provide nonasymptotic results for the sparse CP low-rank estimator as follows.

Theorem 2

(Sparse CP low-rank estimator)
.

Suppose that the observed response and predictor series
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
follow model (
2.3
).
Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, if
\kappa_{1}>0
and
T\gtrsim(\kappa_{2}/\kappa_{1})^{2}Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[21ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}
, then

\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}T}},\quad\text{and}

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\langle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}}{\kappa_{1}T}

with probability at least
1-5e^{-2Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}}
, where
s=\prod_{d=1}^{N}s_{d}
.

When
R,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
, and
\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})
are fixed, Theorem
2
implies the estimation and prediction error rates
\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{s\log(p_{y}q_{x})/T})
and
T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\langle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=O_{p}(s\log(p_{y}q_{x})/T)
. They are much sharper than the results for the non-sparse estimator in Theorem
1
, as
\log(p_{y}q_{x})=\sum_{d=1}^{n}\log(q_{d})+\sum_{d=1}^{m}\log(p_{d})
is much smaller than
\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d}
.

Remark 6

(Sparse estimation without low-rank structures)
.

Due to the tensor structure of the data, the primary goal of our model and estimators is to capture and interpret the multi-dimensional structures across various modes of the response and predictors. This requires leveraging the tensor factorization and hence low-rank structures. An alternative dimension reduction approach is to simply vectorize the response and predictor tensors, and perform the sparse regression
\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{B}\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})+\vect(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})
, where
\boldsymbol{B}
is the
p_{y}\times q_{x}
matricization of the tensor
\mathscr{B}
from (
2.2
), obtained by unfolding its first
m
modes into rows and the last
n
modes into columns. A Lasso estimator can then be applied to estimate
\boldsymbol{B}
, yielding the estimation error rate
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\textrm{Lasso}}-\boldsymbol{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{S\log(p_{y}q_{x})/T})
, assuming exact entrywise sparsity, where
S=\|\vect(\boldsymbol{B}^{*})\|_{0}
. This rate is comparable to that in Theorem
2
, since
Rs\asymp S
.
However, this approach does not preserve any low-rank structure in the estimated tensor
\mathscr{B}
, and consequently, the inherent multi-dimensional information in the data will be lost.

3.3
Application to tensor autoregression

In this section, we consider the TAR(
L
) model, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
comprises
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-L}
, and analyze the proposed non-sparse and sparse CP low-rank estimators.

Some notations and assumptions can be simplified now that
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}^{\top},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{t-L}^{\top})^{\top}
. Specifically, we have
q_{d}=p_{d}
for
1\leq d\leq m
. If
L>1
, then
n=m+1
,
N=2m+1
, and
q_{n}=L
; otherwise,
n=m
and
N=2m
. In both cases,
q_{x}=p_{y}L
. Assumption
2
(ii) is no longer needed, since it directly follows from the independence of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
required in Assumption
2
(i), and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{F}$}_{t}=\sigma\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-2},\dots\}
.

Moreover, Assumption
3
(i) can be replaced by the stationarity condition provided in Remark
4
. Furthermore, the stationarity implies that
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\}
has the infinite-order moving average form,
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-j}
, where the coefficient matrices
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{y}\times p_{y}}
depend on
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
. Consequently, under Assumption
2
(i), we can verify that Assumption
3
(ii) is automatically fulfilled, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
. Therefore, the corollaries below are stated without Assumption
3
.

In addition, due to the specific definition of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
for the TAR(
L
) case and the removal of Assumption
3
, we can replace
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
with specific quantities related to
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^{*}
’s. Denote
\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{AR}}=\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})\mu_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*})
and
\kappa_{2}^{\textrm{AR}}=\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})\mu_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*})
. Here
\mu_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*})=\min_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}(z)\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}}}(z))
and
\mu_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*})=\max_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}(z)\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}}}(z))
, where

\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}(z)=\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{y}}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^{*}z^{j}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}}}(z)
is the conjugate transpose of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{*}(z)
, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
. By techniques similar to those in
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
, we can verify that
\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{AR}}>0
.

Corollaries
1
and
2
provide results for the estimation of TAR(
L
) models analogous to Theorems
1
and
2
, respectively.

Corollary 1

(CP low-rank TAR estimator)
.

Suppose that the observed time series
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\}
follow a stationary TAR(
L
) model in the form of (
2.5
). Under Assumptions
1
and
2
(i), if
T\gtrsim(\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{AR}}L/\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{AR}})^{2}R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)\log N
, then

\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{AR}}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)\log N}{(\kappa_{1}^{{\mathrm{AR}}})^{2}T}},\quad\text{and}

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\langle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{AR}}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)\log N}{\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{AR}}T}

with probability at least
1-5e^{-2R(2\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)\log\{6(N+1)\}}
.

Corollary 2

(Sparse CP low-rank TAR estimator)
.

Suppose that the observed time series
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\}
follow a stationary TAR(
L
) model in the form of (
2.5
). Under Assumptions
1
,
2
(i), and
4
, if
T\gtrsim(\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{AR}}L/\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{AR}})^{2}Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}^{2}L),\log[21ep_{y}^{2}L/(2Rs)]\}
, then

\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{AR}}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}^{2}L),\log[ep_{y}^{2}L/(2Rs)]\}}{(\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{AR}})^{2}T}},\quad\text{and}

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\langle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{AR}}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}^{2}L),\log[ep_{y}^{2}L/(2Rs)]\}}{\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{AR}}T}

with probability at least
1-5e^{-2Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}^{2}L),\log[ep_{y}^{2}L/(2Rs)]\}}
, where
s=\prod_{d=1}^{N}s_{d}
.

We can compare the estimation error rate in Corollary
1
with that of the non-convex estimator, denoted
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}_{\textrm{Tucker}}
, for the Tucker low-rank TAR model in
Wang et al., 2024
, where the coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
is assumed to have Tucker ranks
r_{1},\dots,r_{N}
. The latter has the estimation error rate
\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}_{\textrm{Tucker}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{(\sum_{d=1}^{m}r_{d}p_{d}+r_{N}L+\prod_{d=1}^{N}r_{d})/T})
. By Remark
S1
in the supplementary file,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
can be rewritten in a CP low-rank form with CP-rank
\prod_{d=1}^{N}r_{d}
. In this case, Corollary
1
yields
\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\prod_{d=1}^{N}r_{d}(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)\log N/T})
, which is less sharp due to the multiplicative factor
\prod_{d=1}^{N}r_{d}
. Thus, when
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
is Tucker low-rank, the proposed CP low-rank estimator
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
remains consistent, albeit less efficient than the Tucker low-rank estimator. By contrast, suppose that
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
is CP low-rank, as assumed in Corollary
1
. As discussed in Remark
S1
in the supplementary file, it has the Tucker decomposition form with Tucker ranks
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{N}=R
. In this case,
\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}_{\textrm{Tucker}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{[R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)+R^{N}]/T})
, whereas

\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{R(\sum_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}+L)\log N/T})
, which can be much sharper than the former as it does not involve
R^{N}
. Additionally, it is worth noting that the TAR model in
Wang et al., 2024
does not allow sparse factor matrices, while
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
in Corollary
2
accommodates such cases. Consequently, even if
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
is Tucker low-rank, as long as its factor matrices are sparse,
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
generally outperforms
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}_{\textrm{Tucker}}
.

Remark 7

(Sparse estimation in the special case with
m=1
)
.

Consider the special case
m=1
discussed in Remark
5
. Here, the proposed TAR model reduces to a CP low-rank counterpart of the high-dimensional VAR model for
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{y}}
in
Wang et al., 2022
, which assumes the coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{y}\times p_{y}\times L}
admits a sparse Tucker decomposition with ranks
(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})
and
\ell_{1}
-penalized SHORR estimator achieving error rate
O_{p}(r\sqrt{s\log(p_{y}^{2}L)/T})
, where
r=r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}
and
s=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}
. By Remark
S1
in the supplement, such
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
also satisfies our sparse CP low-rank assumption with rank
r
, yielding a sharper rate via Corollary
2
:

\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{rs\min\{\log(p_{y}^{2}L),\log[21ep_{y}^{2}L/(2rs)]\}/T}\right).

Conversely, if the CP assumption holds with rank
R
, then SHORR has rate
O_{p}(R^{3}\sqrt{s\log(p_{y}^{2}L)/T})
, while our estimator achieves a sharper rate with only
\sqrt{R}
dependence. The improved efficiency of our method arises from the interplay between CP and Tucker structures and the use of
\ell_{0}
-constraints rather than
\ell_{1}
-penalties.

Simulation experiments

This section presents two simulation experiments, which respectively verify the consistency of the estimators in Section

The following two data generating processes (DGPs) are considered:

•

DGP 1 (Tensor stochastic regression):

•

5.1
Mixed-frequency macroeconomic data

We apply the proposed CP low-rank tensor stochastic regression to a mixed-frequency dataset consisting of all macroeconomic indicators available since 1967 from the FRED-QD and FRED-MD databases at

6
Conclusion and discussion

This paper proposes high-dimensional tensor stochastic regression and TAR models via CP decomposition, which include vector, matrix, or tensor responses and predictors as special cases. It further leads to new vector, matrix, and tensor autoregressive (AR) models of general lag orders via CP decomposition.
Unlike existing AR models based on Tucker decomposition, CP decomposition enables the exploration of interactive relationship across various dimensions, as well as input-output dynamic mechanisms.
Additionally, this general framework provides an efficient and interpretable approach to mixed-frequency regression with high-dimensional responses.
For the proposed models, CP low-rank and sparse CP low-rank estimators are introduced, for which non-asymptotic estimation error bounds are established, and an efficient alternating minimization algorithm is developed. Simulation studies and empirical analyses of mixed-frequency macroeconomic data and spatio-temporal air pollution data demonstrate the advantages of the proposed methods in terms of both efficiency and interpretability.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to exploit CP decomposition in general tensor stochastic regression. There are several important questions that require future research. First, we use time series cross-validation to select the rank and sparsity parameters due to its practical simplicity and favorable performance in general model settings. It is worth exploring consistent estimation methods for the tuning parameters, such as the high-dimensional information criteria
(
Zheng, 2025
, e.g.,)
or a ridge-type ratio estimator
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
. Second, while we study the estimation error bounds, the convergence analysis of the algorithm, similar to that in
Sun and Li, 2017
, has not been established. The main challenge arises from the stochastic nature of the design matrix, whereas the former assumes a fixed design matrix. We leave this challenging problem for future investigation.

S1
Comparison with Tucker low-rank models

The proposed TAR model and its VAR special case (see Remark
5
in Section
2.2
of the main paper) are reminiscent of the high-dimensional TAR and VAR models introduced in
Wang et al., 2022
and
Wang et al., 2024
, respectively. However, the latter are based on the Tucker decomposition of the coefficient tensor
\mathscr{B}
, rather than the CP decomposition.

If an
N
th-order tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{N}}
has Tucker ranks
(r_{1},\dots,r_{N})
, then it admits the Tucker decomposition,

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{d=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{d},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{N}}
is called the core tensor, and
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{d}\times r_{d}}
for
1\leq d\leq N
are called factor matrices
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
)
.

Our motivation for considering the CP decomposition instead of the above Tucker decomposition is twofold:

(i)

The identifiability of the Tucker decomposition requires much more complex constraints than the CP decomposition
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
;
Han et al., 2024
)
.

(ii)

The CP decomposition provides valuable insights into interactions across different cross-sectional or temporal modes, a capability that the Tucker decomposition lacks.

First, unlike the CP decomposition, which easily achieves uniqueness in practice (see Remark
1
in Section
2.1
of the main paper), the Tucker decomposition suffers from rotational indeterminacy. Namely, for any nonsingular matrices
\boldsymbol{O}_{d}
, it holds
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{d=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{d}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{d=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{O}_{d})\times_{i=d}^{N}(\boldsymbol{O}_{d}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{d})
. A common approach to addressing this problem is to consider the higher-order singular value decomposition (HOSVD)
(
De Lathauwer et al., 2000
)
. This requires orthogonality constraints on
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
’s and
\mathscr{G}
; in particular,
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{d}=\boldsymbol{I}_{r_{d}}
. However, these constraints lead to difficulties in not only algorithmic development but also the interpretation of factor loadings, especially when the small sample size necessitates further sparsity assumptions on
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
.
On the one hand, it is challenging to simultaneously ensure both orthogonality and sparsity of
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
’s in the optimization algorithm
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
. On the other hand, it may not be reasonable to believe that the orthonormal matrices
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
’s are truly sparse, even though they could be sparse up to a rotation. Thus, the estimates of
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
’s may not be interpretable.

Second, in time series modeling, the use of Tucker decomposition is especially motivated from the factor modeling perspective, as
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
’s naturally generalize the loading matrix in vector-valued factor models to the tensor-valued setting; see
Wang et al., 2022
and
Wang et al., 2024
for detailed discussions on the connection with factor models
(
Bai and Wang, 2016
)
. Thus, based on each loading matrix
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
in the Tucker decomposition, the factor structure for each mode can be interpreted.
Nonetheless, individually interpreting the loading matrices
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
’s cannot offer insights into interactions across different cross-sectional or temporal modes. In contrast, as discussed in Section
2.2
of the main paper, the CP decomposition enables us to conveniently explore interactive patterns for any pair of modes
(d_{1},d_{2})
by visualizing the matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d_{2}}
.

Remark S1

(Connection between CP and Tucker decompositions)
.

Suppose
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{N}}
has the Tucker decomposition,

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{d=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{U}_{d}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}_{d}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1,d},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{r_{d},d})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{d}\times r_{d}}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{N}}
. Then it can be written in the form of the CP decomposition,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{N}=1}^{r_{N}}g_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\boldsymbol{u}_{i_{1},1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{u}_{i_{N},N}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{i}},1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{i}},N}
, which is the sum of
\prod_{d=1}^{N}r_{d}
rank-one tensors, where
\boldsymbol{i}:=(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})
,
\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=g_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}
is the
(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})
-th entry of
\mathscr{G}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{i},d}:=\boldsymbol{u}_{i_{d},d}
, for
1\leq i_{d}\leq r_{d}
and
1\leq d\leq N
. Nonetheless, note that the actual rank
R
of
\mathscr{B}
is usually smaller than
\prod_{d=1}^{N}r_{d}
, i.e., a more parsimonious CP decomposition may exist. Conversely, if
\mathscr{B}
has the CP decomposition in (
2.1
) with rank
R
(see Section
2.1
of the main paper), then it can be written in the Tucker decomposition form where the core tensor
\mathscr{G}
is superdiagonal (i.e.,
g_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}=0
unless
i_{1}=\cdots=i_{N}
), and the Tucker ranks,
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{N}=R
, cannot be further reduced.

S2
Alternating minimization algorithm

In this section, we develop an efficient alternating minimization algorithm for the proposed CP low-rank estimators in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
of the main paper by adapting the alternating update algorithm in
Sun and Li, 2017
.
At each iteration, our algorithm consists of two main steps. In Step 1, we fix the parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}:1\leq d\leq n,1\leq r\leq R\}
and update the parameters
\{\omega_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}:1\leq d\leq m,1\leq r\leq R\}
. Note that the first group of parameters corresponds to loading vectors associated with predictor tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
, while the second group includes those associated with the response tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
. Then in Step 2, we fix the latter and update the former.

Sun and Li, 2017
focused on the sparse CP low-rank regression for
i.i.d.
data with a tensor-valued response and a vector-valued predictor, i.e.,
n=1
. In addition, the entrywise sparsity was imposed only on the loading vectors corresponding to the response, i.e.,
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}:1\leq d\leq m,1\leq r\leq R\}
. We extend their approach in two directions: (i) We allow the predictor to be an
n
-th order tensor for
n\geq 1
; and (ii) the loading vectors corresponding to the predictor, i.e.,
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}:1\leq d\leq n,1\leq r\leq R\}
, can also be sparse.

Denote
f_{r,t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}-\sum_{r^{\prime}\neq r}\omega_{r^{\prime}}f_{r^{\prime},t}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r^{\prime},1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r^{\prime},m}
. Since the parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}:1\leq d\leq n,1\leq r\leq R\}
are fixed in Step 1,
f_{r,t}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t}
for
1\leq r\leq R
are also fixed. The minimization of the loss function with respect to
\{\omega_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}:1\leq d\leq m,1\leq r\leq R\}
is equivalent to the following minimization that is conducted separately for each
1\leq r\leq R
:

\underset{\omega_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}}{\min}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{r,t}^{2}\Big\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{S}$}_{r,t}-\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

(S1)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{S}$}_{r,t}=f_{r,t}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t}
.
The constraints for (
S1
) are
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}=1
for the CP low-rank estimation in Section
3.1
, or
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{0}\leq s_{d}
for the sparse CP low-rank estimation in Section
3.2
, where
1\leq d\leq m
. Note that (
S1
) is equivalent to the rank-one tensor decomposition,

\underset{\omega_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}}{\min}\left\lVert\frac{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{r,t}^{2}}-\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

where
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{r,t}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t}
. Using alternating minimization with respect to each
1\leq d\leq m
, under the constraint
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}=1
, the solution is given by
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}=\textrm{Norm}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r}\times_{d^{\prime}=1,d^{\prime}\neq d}^{m}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d^{\prime}})
, and then
\widetilde{\omega}_{r}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r}\times_{d=1}^{m}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}
. If the constraints
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\rVert_{0}\leq s_{d}
are further imposed, the sparse rank-one tensor decomposition can be solved by a truncation-based procedure
(
Sun et al., 2017
;
Sun and Li, 2017
)
: the solution to the alternating minimization is
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}=\textrm{Norm}(\textrm{Truncate}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d},s_{d}))=\textrm{Norm}(\textrm{Truncate}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r}\times_{d^{\prime}=1,d^{\prime}\neq d}^{m}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d^{\prime}}))
, for
1\leq d\leq m
, and then
\widehat{\omega}_{r}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r}\times_{d=1}^{m}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}
.

Input:
Rank
R
, regularization parameters
s_{d}
, initialization
\omega_{r}^{(0)}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}^{(0)}
, for
1\leq d\leq N
,
1\leq r\leq R
.

repeat

i=0,1,2,\dots

Step 1:
for

r\in\{1,\dots,R\}

do

for

d\in\{1,\ldots,m\}

do

\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}^{(i+1)}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r,t}^{(i)}\times_{d^{\prime}=1,d^{\prime}\neq d}^{m}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d^{\prime}}^{(i)}

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}^{(i+1)}=\textrm{Norm}(\textrm{Truncate}(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}^{(i+1)},s_{d}))

end for

\omega_{r}^{(i+1)}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r,t}^{(i)}\times_{d=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}^{(i+1)}

end for

Step 2:
for

r\in\{1,\dots,R\}

do

for

d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

do

\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,m+d}^{(i+1)}=\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}^{(i)\top}\big)^{-1}\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}^{(i)}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t}^{(i)},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}^{(i)}\rangle\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}^{(i)}

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}^{(i+1)}=\text{Norm}(\textrm{Truncate}(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,m+d}^{(i+1)},s_{d}))

end for

end for

until convergence

Algorithm 1

Sparse CP low-rank tensor stochastic regression

In Step 2, as the parameters
\{\omega_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}:1\leq d\leq m,1\leq r\leq R\}
are fixed, due to the bi-convex structure of the loss function, the alternating update of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}
for
1\leq d\leq n
reduces to an ordinary least squares regression, which has a closed-form solution as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}\rangle\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}}{\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}=\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{d^{\prime}=1,d^{\prime}\neq d}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d^{\prime}}
; see also
Sun and Li, 2017
. Then, for
1\leq d\leq n
, we let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,m+d}=\textrm{Norm}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,m+d})
for the CP low-rank estimation in Section
3.1
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,m+d}=\textrm{Norm}(\textrm{Truncate}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,m+d},s_{d}))
for the sparse CP low-rank estimation in Section
3.2
.
Note that the equivalent response in Step 2 is a scalar,
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}_{r,t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}\rangle
for
t=1,\ldots,T
. This significantly reduces the computational burden compared to a tensor-valued response problem.
We present the algorithm for sparse CP low-rank estimation in Algorithm
1
. Here
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{R}$}}_{r,t}^{(i)}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{r,d,t}^{(i)}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{r}^{(i)}
are all computed using parameters obtained from the
i
th iteration. For the non-sparse case, the same algorithm can be used by simply omitting the truncation operations in lines 6 and 13 of Algorithm
1
.

Remark S2

(Computational complexities)
.

Algorithm
1
has a per-iteration computational complexity of
O(R(mTp_{y}+nTq_{x}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}q_{d}^{3}))\asymp O(R[T(p_{y}+q_{x})+q_{\max}^{3})])
, if
m
and
n
are fixed, where
q_{\max}=\max_{1\leq d\leq n}q_{d}
.
As a comparison, consider the Lasso estimator for sparse regression with vectorized response and predictor, which uses the
\ell_{1}
-regularization and does not assume any low-rank structure; see Remark
6
in Section
3.2
of the main paper. The commonly used iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (ISTA)
(
Beck and Teboulle, 2009
)
for the Lasso has a per-iteration computational complexity of
O(Tp_{y}q_{x})
, which is generally much higher than Algorithm
1
, since
p_{y}=\prod_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}
and
q_{x}=\prod_{d=1}^{n}q_{d}
can rapidly increase when
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
are third- or higher-order tensors, where
R,m
and
n
are much smaller. The advantage of Algorithm
1
is two-fold: In Step 1, the rank-one tensor decomposition is highly efficient, and in Step 2, rather than having
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
interact with all dimensions of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
, we only need to solve a regression problem with a scalar response. On the other hand, if we consider the TAR(
L
) model, the non-convex Tucker low-rank estimator introduced in
Wang et al., 2024
serves as a baseline. For TAR(
L
) models, the per-iteration computational complexity of our algorithm can be simplified to

O(R(Tp_{y}L+p_{\max}^{3}))
, whereas that for the gradient descent algorithm in
Wang et al., 2024
is
O((T+r_{\max})p_{y}^{2}L+r_{\max}^{3})
, if
m
is fixed, where
p_{\max}=\max_{1\leq d\leq m}p_{d}
,
r_{\max}=\max_{1\leq d\leq 2m+1}r_{d}
, and
r_{d}
for
1\leq d\leq 2m+1
are the Tucker ranks. Since the latter rate scales quadratically rather than linearly with
p_{y}
, the algorithm for the Tucker low-rank estimator in
Wang et al., 2024
is generally much slower than our method for TAR models; this is also confirmed by our simulation study in Section
S3
.

Remark S3

(Initialization)
.

Algorithm
1
requires predetermined values of
R
and
s_{d}
’s. Note that the estimation theory in Section
3
of the main paper does not require these quantities to be minimal; rather, it suffices that the true rank and sparsity levels are bounded by them. In practice, we may consider a range of
R
and
s_{d}
’s, and select them via time-series cross-validation; i.e., we partition the sample into training and validation sets while preserving the temporal order, and select these parameters based on the out-of-sample forecast accuracy on the validation set; see, e.g.,
Wilms et al., 2023
. In addition, for the initial values
\omega_{r}^{(0)}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}^{(0)}
’s, we can first obtain an initial estimate of
\mathscr{B}
via a convex optimization, and then conduct a CP decomposition for
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(0)}
for any given

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(0)}
.

Lemma S.5

(Covering number and discretization for CP low-rank tensors)
.

For a given rank
R
, let
\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{N}}:\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=1,\mathrm{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$})\leq R\}
. For any
0<\epsilon<1
, let
\mathscr{N}(R,\epsilon)
be a minimal
\epsilon
-net of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
in the Frobenius norm.

(i)

The cardinality of
\mathscr{N}(R,\epsilon)
satisfies
\log|\mathscr{N}(R,\epsilon)|\leq R(\sum_{d=1}^{N}p_{d}+1)\log\{3(N+1)/\epsilon\}
.

(ii)

For any
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{N}}
, let
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{[m]}
be the
p_{x}
-by-
q_{y}
multi-mode matricization obtained by collapsing the first
m
modes of
\mathscr{T}
into rows and the last
n
modes into columns, where
N=m+n
,
p_{x}=\prod_{d=1}^{m}p_{d}
, and
q_{y}=\prod_{d=m+1}^{N}p_{d}
. For any
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{y}\times T}
, it holds

\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath\scriptsize$\mathscr{T}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)}\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\rangle\leq(1-\sqrt{2}\epsilon)^{-1}\max_{\mbox{\boldmath\scriptsize$\mathscr{T}$}\in\mathscr{N}(R,\epsilon)}\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Z}$}\rangle

and

\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath\scriptsize$\mathscr{T}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)}\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{X}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leq(1-\sqrt{2}\epsilon)^{-1}\max_{\mbox{\boldmath\scriptsize$\mathscr{T}$}\in\mathscr{N}(R,\epsilon)}\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{X}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}.

Proof of Lemma
S.5
.

Claim (i) can be proved by a method similar to that for Lemma 6 in
Raskutti et al., 2011
. Specifically, any
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
has the CP decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}
, where
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{R})^{\top}\in\pazocal{S}^{R-1}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}\in\pazocal{S}^{p_{d}-1}
for
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
. Thus, the problem of covering
\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
can be converted to those of covering
\pazocal{S}^{R-1}
and
\pazocal{S}^{p_{d}-1}
’s. Denote by
\bar{\pazocal{S}}^{p-1}(\epsilon_{1})
a minimal
\epsilon_{1}
-net of
\pazocal{S}^{p-1}
in the Euclidean norm for any positive integer
p
and
\epsilon_{1}>0
. Note that
|\bar{\pazocal{S}}^{p-1}(\epsilon_{1})|\leq(3/\epsilon_{1})^{p}
. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(R,\epsilon_{1})=\{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bar{\omega}_{r}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,N}:\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\in\bar{\pazocal{S}}^{R-1}(\epsilon_{1}),\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}\in\bar{\pazocal{S}}^{p_{d}-1}(\epsilon_{1}),1\leq r\leq R,1\leq d\leq N\}
. As a result,
|\bar{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(R,\epsilon_{1})|\leq(3/\epsilon_{1})^{R(\sum_{d=1}^{N}p_{d}+1)}
.

Moreover, for any
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
, there exists
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}\in\bar{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(R,\epsilon)
such that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\omega}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}
, and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,d}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,d}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}
for
1\leq r\leq R
and
1\leq d\leq N
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lVert\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}-\bar{\omega}_{r}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,N}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Bigg\{\Big\lVert\lvert\omega_{r}-\bar{\omega}_{r}\rvert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\Big\lVert\bar{\omega}_{r}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,1}\rVert_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big\lVert\bar{\omega}_{r}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,N}\rVert_{2}\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}+N\epsilon_{1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bar{\omega}_{r}^{2}=\epsilon,

by setting
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon/(N+1)
. This shows that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(R,\epsilon_{1})
is an
\epsilon
-net of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
, and thus
\log|\mathscr{N}(R,\epsilon)|\leq\log|\bar{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(R,\epsilon_{1})|\leq R(\sum_{d=1}^{N}p_{d}+1)\log\{3(N+1)/\epsilon\}
.

Next we prove claim (ii). For any
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}=\omega_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,N}\in\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
, there exists
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}=\bar{\omega}_{r}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,1}\circ\cdots\circ\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r,N}\in\bar{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(R,\epsilon)
such that
\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\epsilon
. Moreover, we can partition
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{2}
such that
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{i}/\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in\boldsymbol{\Pi}(R)
for
i=1,2
. As a result,
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. Then, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{T}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mbox{\boldmath\scriptsize$\mathscr{K}$}(2Rs)}\big\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}},\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{T}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\pazocal{N}(2Rs,1/2)}\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta},\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle,

(S22)

where

is the minimal
1/2
-net of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{K}$}(2Rs)
in the Frobenius norm.

Moreover, by Lemma
S.6
(i) and the fact that
{p\choose K}\leq\min\{p^{K},(ep/K)^{K}\}
, we have

\log|\pazocal{N}(2Rs,1/2)|\leq 2Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}+4Rs;

see also the proof of Lemma F.2 in
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
.
Note that

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta},\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{[m]},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\rangle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\rangle.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\rangle\geq a\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\rangle\geq a,\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 3\kappa_{2}T/2\right\}+\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{[m]}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq 3\kappa_{2}T/2\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{a^{2}}{3\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})T}\right\}+2e^{-c_{1}T}.

As a result, by setting
a=6\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})TRs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\pazocal{N}(2Rs,1/2)}\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta},\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\geq a\right\}

\displaystyle\leq|\pazocal{N}(2Rs,1/2)|\max_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\pazocal{N}(2Rs,1/2)}\mathbb{P}\left\{\langle\boldsymbol{\Delta},\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\rangle\geq a\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-6Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}},

given that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{E}_{2}^{\complement})\leq 3e^{-6Rs\min\{\log(p_{y}q_{x}),\log[ep_{y}q_{x}/(2Rs)]\}},
