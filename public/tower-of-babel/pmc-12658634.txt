A Tetrapodal Tryptophan Derivative with Multiple Exposed Free Carboxylic Acids Blocks Host Cell Entry of Omicron SARS-Cov-2 and Respiratory Syncytial Virus
Authors: Olaia Martí-Marí, Marta Moreno-Simoni, Ana Isabel Avilés-Alía, Luciana Rusu, Alicia Forcada-Nadal, Anmol Adhav, Maria Luisa López-Redondo, Ana-Belén Blázquez, Ana Esteban, Nereida Jiménez de Oya, Alberto Marina, Vicente Rubio, José Luis Llácer, Federico Gago, Miguel A. Martín-Acebes, María-Jesús Pérez-Pérez, Ron Geller, Ana San-Félix
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Omicron sublineages of SARS-CoV-2 have accelerated the spread of the virus and facilitated immune escape. In this work, we demonstrate that compound 2, a potent HIV and enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) entry inhibitor previously discovered in our research group, also displays potent in vitro activity against different SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variants while showing no activity against the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan strain. Moreover, its sodium salt (2-Na salt) exhibited in vivo antiviral activity in a murine model of Omicron BA.1. infection. Indeed, biophysical and cryo-EM studies revealed binding of 2-Na salt to the Omicron BA.1 spike (S) protein stabilizing a “closed” form in which ∼75% of the S particles have all RBDs down, unlike the usual “open” form with one RBD up. Such “closed” form decreases S avidity for the cellular receptor ACE2, thus inhibiting viral entry. Computer-assisted modeling studies strongly suggest that 2 can interact with the intersubunit cavity of the S trimer of the Omicron BA.1 subvariant, making use of the multivalency principle. In this context, compound 2 can be considered a pharmacological tool for studying and validating new antiviral strategies against Omicron variants. We also identified 2 as a potent inhibitor of RSV and the Ebola virus. The effectiveness of 2 against a diverse set of viruses of different families supports its use as a promising lead for the development of entry inhibitors against current and future viral infections, representing a meaningful advance in the field.

Introduction

Respiratory viral infections are among the most common human diseases, likely due to their ease of spread. , While multiple viruses can cause respiratory infections, influenza virus and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections were traditionally the most relevant. However, the emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) in 2019 and its subsequent spread throughout the human population now make coronavirus disease 19 (COVID-19) one of the top respiratory infections afflicting humans. Since SARS-CoV-2 emergence, multiple variants of this virus have appeared and spread, − in many cases exhibiting enhanced transmission or immune escape compared with the original Wuhan-Hu-1 strain. In particular, since its emergence in late 2021, the Omicron (B.1.1.529) variant of concern (VOC) and its subvariants (BA.1 to BA.5) have become predominant worldwide. More recently, different sublineages of Omicron have also been identified, including BQ.1, BQ.1.1, XBB, XBB.1.5, EG.5.1, and BA.2.86, among others. − The Omicron strains have accumulated more than 50 changes compared to the ancestral Wuhan-Hu-1 strain. , A significant number of these changes are located in the spike (S) protein, at or near the receptor-binding domain (RBD; residues 319–541 of the S protein according to the Wuhan-Hu-1 strain sequence, GenBank NC_045512.2), with three mutations occurring in the S furin cleavage site, which enhances viral transmissibility.

Few effective antivirals are available to treat respiratory viral infections despite their impact on global morbidity and mortality. For RSV, therapeutic monoclonal antibodies have been utilized with success, but they remain prohibitive for widespread use due to their high costs. − For SARS-CoV-2, several monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and small molecules have already been approved or are in clinical trials. However, the new Omicron subvariants exhibit resistances to nearly all of them, making it imperative to develop novel antivirals. These compounds can be used alone or in combination with existing antivirals to minimize the risk of resistance.

We have previously reported the synthesis of a new class of C2-thiophenyl tryptophan (Trp) tripodal derivatives that show antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2. The prototype compound of this family 1 (ASF-071; Figure ) contains three Trp units bearing a 4-NO2-thiophenyl moiety attached to the C2 position of each indole ring and a glycine (Gly) residue linked to the NH2 at the central quaternary carbon (the focal point). This compound inhibits cellular entry of SARS-CoV-2 through interaction with the viral S protein. Compound 1 (ASF-071) was effective against the ancestral Wuhan-Hu-1 SARS-CoV-2 strain but had reduced antiviral potency against the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron subvariants, limiting its efficacy.

Structures of tripodal 1 (ASF-071) and tetrapodal 2 (ASF-006).

The S proteins of the Omicron subvariants are characterized by an increased number of positively charged amino acids relative to the Wuhan-Hu-1 strain. − Compounds resembling 1 (ASF-071), but exhibiting increased exposed negative charges, might therefore have enhanced potency against the Omicron subvariants. We previously synthesized multivalent Trp derivatives functionalized with different numbers of exposed free carboxylic acids and showed that some of them have antiviral activity against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and enterovirus A71 (EV-71). − Thus, we hypothesized that some of these negatively charged compounds could prove valuable against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron subvariants. To test such a hypothesis, we evaluated the efficacy of representative members of this Trp family against Omicron SARS-CoV-2 subvariants and found that compound 2 (ASF-006) (Figure ) is endowed with significant efficacy.

Compound 2, a tetrapodal Trp derivative with indolic side chains bearing an isophthalic acid moiety at the C2 and C7 positions (i.e., a phenyl ring with carboxylic acid groups at positions 3 and 5; Figures and ), was previously reported to be active against HIV and EV-71. In the present work, we extend its evaluation to Omicron SARS-CoV-2 and other enveloped viruses, including SARS-CoV-1, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), Ebola, Nipah, and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), observing clear activity against RSV and Ebola virus as well as Omicron variants of SARS-CoV-2. The promising activity against two widespread respiratory pathogens, SARS-CoV-2 and RSV, motivated further mechanistic studies by biophysical methods and cryo-EM as well as evaluation in a mouse model of SARS-CoV-2 infection. These findings identify compound 2 as a promising viral entry inhibitor with in vivo efficacy.

Results and Discussion

Identification of Trp Derivatives with Antiviral Activity against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron Subvariants

Using a high-throughput screening (HTS) assay based on pseudotyped particles of vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) expressing the S protein of the original SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-Hu-1 strain (VSV-S), we previously found that tripodal 1 (ASF-071) showed antiviral activity via inhibition of viral entry. However, this compound showed poor activity against newer Omicron subvariants of SARS-CoV-2.

To assess whether similar compounds could act against SARS-CoV-2 variants, our in-house collection of multivalent Trp derivatives was screened against two Omicron subvariants, BA.2 and BA.4/5. The following compounds were tested (Figure ): tetrapodal compounds including 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 and tripodal compounds, such as 8 , and 9, as representative examples of our in-house collection. For comparative purposes, we also screened the newly synthesized (see below) 10-Na salt (ASF-115), a diarylated tripodal derivative with a Gly residue linked to the NH2 of the focal point as in the previous prototype, 1 (ASF-071). Specifically, VSV encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP) and pseudotyped with the S proteins of the Omicron BA.2 or BA.4/5 subvariants was preincubated with the different compounds at a single 20 μM concentration. , Subsequently, this mixture was used to infect human lung A549 cells expressing both angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) and the TMPRSS2 entry factor (A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2). Finally, viral infection was quantified following 16 h by assessment of GFP fluorescence, while drug toxicity was estimated based on cell confluency.

As shown in Figure , the doubly arylated tetrapodal compound 2 (ASF-006) showed strong inhibition of infection by VSV displaying BA.2 or BA.4/5 S proteins on its surface in the absence of significant toxicity to cells. In contrast, its C7 or C2 monoarylated counterparts (compounds 3 and 4, respectively) showed reduced inhibition while compound 5, a monoarylated tetrapodal derivative with a p-COOH benzyl moiety at N-1 of the indole ring, was only marginally inhibitory; compounds 6 or 7, with 12 or 8 Trp residues, respectively (Figures and ), showed modest antiviral activity. Similarly, tripodal compounds 8 and 9, bearing p-COOH phenyl rings at N1 or C2, respectively, had no or little inhibitory activity, as well as the newly synthesized 10-Na salt, a C2/C7 double arylated Trp tripodal derivative (Figures and ). In stark contrast, tetrapodal compound 2 was poorly active against the original Wuhan-Hu-1 strain (Figure S11).

Compound 2 Is Active against Different Omicron Subvariants

We next determined the concentration of 2 that reduced infection by 50% (IC50) for VSV pseudotyped with BA.2 and BA.1 Omicron variant S proteins in A549-ACE2-TMPRSS2 cells. The IC50 values for all variants were below 1 μM (Table and Figure S12), showing potent antiviral activity. Importantly, no significant toxicity was observed even at the highest tested concentrations (Table and Figure S12), with the concentration reducing confluency by 50% (CC50) being >50 μM. To ensure that the results were not cell-specific, we also evaluated the antiviral and toxicity profile of VSV pseudotyped with BA.1 in VeroE6 cells encoding the TMPRSS2 entry cofactor (VeroE6-TMPRSS2). Again, the IC50 was <1 μM in these cells in the absence of toxicity (Table and Figures S12 and S13). Finally, we evaluated the ability of 2 to inhibit infection of VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells by VSV pseudotyped with a more recent Omicron subvariant, XBB1.5, as well as a more distant coronavirus, SARS-CoV. As with the other Omicron variants, IC50 values <1 μM were observed for XBB1.5, while no significant reduction in the entry of SARS-CoV was observed (Table and Figure S11). Overall, these results not only reveal the promising antiviral activity of compound 2 against different SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variants but also highlight its limited activity against more ancestral strains (Wuhan-Hu-1; Figure S10) or distant coronaviruses (SARS-CoV).

Evaluation of Antiviral Activity against Different Enveloped Viruses

We have previously reported that 2 inhibits cell entry of both HIV and enterovirus A71. To better define the spectrum of its antiviral activity, we evaluated the ability of 2 to inhibit a panel of additional viruses belonging to five distinct viral families, RSV-A (Pneumoviridae), Ebola virus (Filoviridae), VSV (Rhabdoviridae), Nipah virus (Paramyxoviridae), and Sindbis virus (Togaviridae). Compound 2 showed strong antiviral activity against RSV-A, with IC50 values ranging between 0.05 and 0.2 μM when infecting A549, HEp-2, or Vero cells (Table and Figure S11). Similarly, Ebola virus was inhibited with an IC50 of 0.2 μM. In contrast, no antiviral activity was detected against VSV, Nipah virus, or Sindbis virus (Table and Figure S11). In combination with our prior findings, compound 2 shows antiviral activity against the members of five distinct viral families.

Compound 2 Interacts with the S Protein, Interfering with Binding to Its Cellular Receptor ACE2

To gain insights into the mechanism of action of 2 against SARS-CoV-2, we first sought to determine if 2 interacts with the S protein of Omicron. To increase its water solubility and facilitate these studies, we synthesized the corresponding Na salt (2-Na salt; synthesis details are included in the chemistry section). To ensure that no changes in antiviral activity occurred due to this modification, we tested the IC50 and CC50 of the 2-Na salt for VSV pseudotyped with the BA.1 and BA.2 S protein as well as for RSV-A. As expected, the antiviral activity and toxicity profiles of both compounds were similar (Table and Figures S11 and S12). Moreover, effects on cell viability for both 2 and 2-Na salt were validated using the resazurin reduction assay, which confirmed the results of the confluency analysis (Figure S12).

We first obtained qualitative molecular evidence for the interaction of compound 2-Na salt with the S protein using thermofluor assays. These assays revealed that submillimolar concentrations of 2-Na salt significantly decreased the thermal stability of the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the BA.1 S protein (RBDBA.1) (Figure A). This destabilization effect was similar to those found upon binding of compound 1 with RBDWuhan.

We then obtained a quantitative measure of binding affinity using microscale thermophoresis (MST). , For the binding of 2-Na salt to RBDBA.1, an estimated K D value of ∼60 μM was observed (Figure B). Interestingly, the K D value for the SBA.1 protein was considerably lower than for the RBDBA.1 domain (∼11-fold lower: K D ≈ 5 μM; Figure B). This difference suggests that a part of 2-Na salt can establish interaction with the RBD domain, while the rest of the molecule may interact with nearby regions of S outside this domain. We previously observed a similar effect although of lower magnitude (an ∼4-fold decrease in the K D value) for the binding to the Wuhan-Hu-1 S protein and RBD domain of a dimer containing two units of 1 (ASF-071) linked through a polyethylene glycol spacer. Both findings support the hypothesis that partial binding to the isolated RBD domain accounts for higher K D values than that for the full-length S protein.

We next investigated whether the binding of 2-Na salt to the SBA.1 protein interferes with the binding of this protein to the ACE2 cellular receptor. To this end, recombinantly produced and purified RBDBA.1 and SBA.1 proteins were used, together with a monomeric catalytic domain of ACE2 (see Experimental Section). Indeed, monomeric ACE2 was able to bind to RBDBA.1 and SBA.1 with K D values in the range 10–100 nM (Figure C,D). As expected for a viral entry inhibitor, the K D values for binding of ACE2 to the RBDBA.1 and SBA.1 protein increased in the presence of 2-Na salt (∼4-fold and ∼10-fold for RBDBA.1 and SBA.1, respectively; Figure C,D). Together, these findings support a mechanism in which 2-Na salt prevents viral entry by competitively inhibiting binding to the receptor.

Structural Analysis of Compound 2-Na salt Binding to SBA.1

In a previous study, we performed cryo-EM analysis of the SD614G variant in complex with 1 (ASF-071). Despite obtaining a resolution of ∼4.2 Å, we could not trace in detail the bound compound. However, a small superficial patch of extra mass could be observed, mainly in the RBD domain of the S protein, which had the three RBDs buried inside the trimer (“down” conformation). This suggested that 1 (ASF-071) prevented the adoption by trimeric S of a conformation that enables efficient interaction with the host receptor, ACE2.

To examine whether the mechanism of action for 2-Na salt is similar to that observed for compound 1, recombinantly produced trimeric viral SBA.1 bound to the compound was subjected to cryo-EM (Figure ) using conditions similar to those employed in the prior study (see Experimental Section for details). The average resolution of the cryo-EM density map was 6.25 Å (see the legend to Figure ), which precluded delineating the precise binding of 2-Na salt. However, clear differentiation between the “up” and “down” positions of the RBD in each subunit of the SBA.1 protein trimer was observed. Thus, it was revealed that ∼75% of the spike particles had all three RBDs in the down orientation in the presence of the compound (Figure D). This is in contrast with multiple prior reports for the SBA.1 protein showing the predominance of the conformation having one RBD up , with the same stabilizing proline mutations at residues 986 and 987 that are used in our construct (see Experimental Section). These findings suggest that compound 2-Na salt could interfere with ACE2 receptor binding via an allosteric mechanism in which a reduced exposure of the RBD of the S protein may play a key role, as proposed previously for compound 1 for pre-Omicron variants of the spike protein.

Overall, it can be assumed that the binding of 2-Na salt to SBA.1 induces a conformation in this viral protein unfavorable for viral entry as the RBDs are not available to bind to the ACE2 cellular receptor. −

Putative Binding Site of 2 Determined through Computer-Assisted Modeling Studies

In the search for putative binding regions for 2 on the SARS-CoV-2 spike trimer of the Omicron BA.1 variant, we employed the FTMap server, which computationally identifies binding hotspots on a protein using 16 small organic molecules as probes. For comparative purposes, the spike trimer of the Delta variant (PDB ID: 8P9Y) was also used. Interestingly, the FTMap program identified three vicinal clusters of multiple probe types in the vicinity of the RBD only in the case of the BA.1 variant. Automated docking of the N-capped isophthalyl-decorated l-Trp unit present in 2 provided five quasi-identical top-ranking poses that were fully compatible with this binding hotspot (Figure ). We take these results as indicative that one of the “legs” of 2 can fit snugly into the proposed binding site (intersubunit cavity of the S trimer), whereas the other three, which cannot bind in a similar fashion simultaneously, establish less specific van der Waals and electrostatic interactions with other protein residues so as to stabilize the three-down conformation. Since the four legs can exchange over time, 2 would be exploiting multivalency to increase the residence time, which likely explains its high antiviral potency (IC5 0 ∼20 nM) despite showing weak affinity for the isolated RBD (K D of ∼60 μM).

Schematic view of the Omicron BA.1 spike trimer with subunits colored in green, cyan, and magenta showing the proposed binding mode for one of the decorated l-Trp units present in 2 (C atoms in gray) at the intersubunit cavity. For reference, the side chains of Lys375 and Arg405 are also displayed as sticks.

It can be assumed that the particular structural features of 2, such as the flat surfaces provided by the indole and phenyl rings, and the hydrogen bond acceptors from its carboxylic acids, may contribute to establishing CH···π interactions, as well as hydrogen-bonds and/or ionic interactions with the amino acids present in the binding site. These multiple interactions might stabilize the three-down conformation of the spike S protein, hindering its interaction with the ACE2 receptor on human cells. This would be consistent with our previous results, according to which compound 2 binds through multiple interactions to the surface of EV-71 and HIV. Thus, the success of 2 as a viral entry inhibitor seems to reside in its multivalent nature, which allows blocking the cellular entry of different viruses that share common structural features on their external surfaces.

Compound 2-Na salt Has In Vivo Antiviral Activity in a Murine Model of Omicron BA.1. Infection

As a final preclinical test of efficacy, we assessed the in vivo antiviral activity of compound 2-Na salt against the Omicron BA.1 variant in a murine model of SARS-CoV-2 infection. For this, we used six-week-old K18-hACE2 mice from our own colony that are genetically modified to express human ACE2 under the control of the human promoter for cytokeratin 18 (K18). Mice were split into two groups (n = 8 per group) and infected with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 via intranasal administration. Treatment was administered intranasally with either saline (vehicle) or 2-Na salt (100 mg/kg in saline) once daily for three consecutive days, starting 2 h prior to infection. Finally, the animals were sacrificed on day 3 postinfection and the lungs were obtained for quantification of viral load. Importantly, mice receiving compound 2 did not exhibit signs of drug toxicity throughout the course of the experiment.

To determine whether 2-Na salt was capable of reducing viral burden in vivo, SARS-CoV-2 genome copy numbers were obtained in the lungs of both vehicle and drug-treated mice by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-PCR). A significant >3.5-fold reduction in viral load was observed upon drug treatment (7870 vs 2185 genome equivalents/gram in the vehicle and 2-Na salt treated groups, respectively; p < 0.05 by Mann–Whitney test; Figure ). Hence, 2-Na salt is effective at reducing the viral load in vivo, highlighting its promise for further development.

Mechanism of Action against RSV

To gain insight into whether compound 2 was also acting as an entry inhibitor for RSV, we performed a time-of-addition experiment (Figure ). Three conditions were tested: preincubation, where RSV was pretreated with 2 before adding the mixture to the cells in the infection stage, after which 2 was removed by replacing the culture medium by new medium; pre- + post-incubation, where RSV was pretreated with 2 and the compound was present during infection and maintained afterward in the culture medium; and postinfection, when 2 was added after the infection stage was completed. Only when 2 was present at the beginning (preincubation or pre- + post-incubation) an antiviral effect was observed. This result suggests RSV entry as the step targeted by compound 2.

Optimized Synthetic Procedure for Obtaining 2 and 2-Na salt

Although compound 2 was synthesized previously in our laboratory, we developed an improved synthetic route for more efficient production.

For the synthesis of 2, a convergent synthetic strategy was followed (Scheme ). In this approach, a building block (13) was constructed first and then attached to a central scaffold (15) in the final step of the synthesis. Initially, the building block 13 was prepared in two steps following the protocol established by our group in which conventional heating was applied. This reaction renders the desired diarylated Trp intermediate 13, although in a low yield (20%). With the aim to increase the yield, we now opted by an alternative process that employs microwave conditions. The first step consisted of the diboronation of the commercially available N-Boc-Trp methyl ester 11 with bis­(pinacolato)­diboron (B2pin). This reaction was now performed under iridium catalyzed conditions ([Ir­(cod)­OMe]2) in the presence of 3,4,7,8-tetramethyl-1.10-phenantroline (Me4Phen), instead of the 4,4′-di-tert-butyl-2,2′-bipyridine (dtbpy), used in our previous work. Moreover, the reaction mixture was microwave (MW)-irradiated at 100 °C for 1.5 h using anhydrous THF as a solvent to afford the C2/C7 diboronated intermediate 12 that was filtered. Isolation of 12, based on filtering, proven advantageous because the next step proceed more cleanly than before. The second step involved a Suzuki–Miyaura reaction , between the crude organoboron Trp intermediate 12 and isophthaloyl bromide. In this second reaction, Pd2(dba)3 was used as a catalyst, SPhos as a ligand, and potassium phosphate (K3PO4) as a base. The reaction mixture, in anhydrous DMF, was heated in a MW reactor at 100 °C for 1.5 h to afford the desired diarylated Trp intermediate 13 with 65% yield. Again, microwave conditions reduced the reaction time (1.5 h at 100 °C vs 24 h at 80 °C) and increased the yields (65% vs 20%) proving superior than the conventional heating conditions used previously.

Synthesis of the Tetrapodal 2 (ASF-006) and Tripodal 10 Derivatives and Its Corresponding Sodium Salts 2-Na Salt (ASF-112) and 10-Na Salt (ASF-115)

Having intermediate 13 in hand, the next steps were performed as already described. Thus, NHBoc deprotection in an acidic medium (TFA) afforded intermediate 14, which has a free amino group, in quantitative yield. Coupling of this amino intermediate with the commercially available central scaffold 15 , afforded the tetrapodal derivative 16 in 54% yield. Finally, saponification of the methyl ester groups (LiOH/H2O), followed by acidification (1 M HCl), afforded the desired final tetrapodal compound 2 (ASF-006) in high yield (95%). Finally, with the aim to increase the solubility in water, the sodium salt of compound 2 (2-Na salt; ASF-112) was obtained in quantitative yield by reaction of 2 with hydrogen sodium carbonate in an acetone/water mixture.

As mentioned above, we also synthesized diarylated tripodal 10, which possesses in its structure a Gly residue linked to the NH2 of the focal point as in the previous prototype 1. For the synthesis of this compound, which was used for comparative purposes, the diarylated amino intermediate 14 was reacted with the triacid 17 in the presence of HATU, as the coupling reagent, and N,N′-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as the base, to afford 18 (72%) (Scheme ). Subsequent saponification of the protecting ester moieties, using LiOH/H2O, accompanied by the simultaneous removal of the Fmoc group, afforded the desired tripodal 10 in 90% yield. The preparation of the 10-Na salt was accomplished by treatment of 10 with hydrogen sodium carbonate in an acetone/water mixture, yielding the salt in a quantitative yield.

Conclusions

In this work, we demonstrate that 2, a tetrapodal compound with multiple exposed free carboxylic acids that belongs to our in-house library of tryptophan derivatives, shows potent antiviral activity against different SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variants but not against the ancestral SARS-CoV.2 strain (Wuhan-Hu-1). This finding makes compound 2 unique with respect to other existing anti-SARS-CoV-2 drugs, the main problem of which is their decaying efficacy for new SARS-CoV-2 variants. Biophysical studies confirmed that 2-Na salt interacts with both the RBD and full-length Omicron BA.1 S protein, competitively inhibiting RBD-ACE2 binding. Cryo-EM studies revealed a predominance of a closed “three-down” RBD conformation, consistent with reduced viral entry. − Computer-assisted modeling supports these findings, identifying a binding site in the intersubunit cavity of the S trimer. Notably, 2-Na salt reduced viral load in mice infected with Omicron BA.1 after nasal administration, providing the first in vivo proof of efficacy for this multivalent Trp-based compound.

Beyond SARS-CoV-2, compound 2 also inhibited RSV and Ebola virus infection, and previous studies showed activity against HIV and enterovirus A71. This antiviral effect against a diverse set of viruses with little evolutionary relationship between them likely arises from multivalent binding to shared structural features on viral surfaces. Together with the improved synthetic route, these results highlight compound 2 as a promising lead for the development of entry inhibitors against current and future viral threats.

Synthesis

General Chemistry Procedures

The solvents used in some reactions were dried prior to use, except DMF, which was acquired in a dry state from Aldrich. Other commercial reagents were used as received from the suppliers without further purification unless otherwise stated. Analytical thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on aluminum plates precoated with silica gel 60 (F254, 0.20 mm). Products were visualized using an ultraviolet lamp (254 and 365 nm) or by heating after treatment with a 5% solution of phosphomolybdic acid (PMA) or vanillin in ethanol.

Antiviral Assays

Antiviral assays were performed as previously reported. Briefly, for the primary screening, pseudotyped viruses were pretreated with the indicated compounds for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by the infection of cells that were previously seeded in a 96-well plate. After 16 h, viral infection was quantified by measuring the number of cells expressing the virus-encoded GFP reporter using a live-cell microscope (Incucyte SX5, Sartorius) or by measuring virus-expressed luciferase activity using the Thermo Scientific Pierce Firefly Luciferase Glow Assay Kit on a Tecan Spark plate reader. Assays were performed with sufficient virus to result in 500–1000 green cells per well. Cell toxicity was monitored in the same well by quantification of cell confluence (Incucyte SX5, Sartorius) or by resazurin reduction assays as previously detailed. For dose–response curves, infections were performed in a similar fashion, except that different drug concentrations were utilized. In addition, for RSV-A encoding mKate2 and Sindbis virus encoding eGFP viral infection was quantified at 24 h. The calculations of the dose resulting in 50% reduction in infection and the standard error were then obtained using the 3-parameter log-logistic function (LL.3) of the DRC package in R (version 4.4.2) as previously described. When the reduction of viral infection or cell confluency (confluency or resazurin reduction assay) did not reach 50% of mock-treated, the dose resulting in 50% reduction (IC50 or CC50) could not be determined, and the value of the highest drug concentration is reported.

Protein Production

The RBD of the S protein of B.1.1.529.1 (Omicron BA.1, as encoded in Genescript MC_0101274 plasmid) was produced in a baculovirus/insect cell system, and it was purified as previously reported for the Wuhan variant, from a pFastBac-Dual plasmid hosting the corresponding coding sequence (generated by Proteogenix after codon optimization for insect cell expression). This system excretes the protein in the medium and endows it with a C-terminal His6-tag.

The SARS-CoV-2 spike protein ectodomain of the omicron BA.1 variant was produced from Plasmid #180593 from Addgene in Expi293 human cells as previously reported for expression of the S protein for the Wuhan variant for MST experiments. The protein hosts the stabilizing mutations K986P and V987P and a C-terminal fibritin trimerization motif followed by His8 and TwinStrep tags and lacks the furin cleavage site. Expression and purification were carried out as reported. The N-terminal peptidase domain of human ACE2 (residues Ser19–Asp615) was produced and purified as reported.

Thermal Shift Assays

Thermofluor assays with the RBDBA.1 were performed as previously described for the RBDWuhan using 25 μg RBD/mL. Compound 2-Na salt was used at the indicated concentration; since it is soluble in water, the solution contained no DMSO. Each assay with three replicate wells for each point was repeated at least two times on different days. Plots, curve fittings, and numerical calculations were performed with Graphpad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Microscale Thermophoresis (MST)

MST assays of 2-Na salt binding to RBDBA.1 or S BA.1 protein were carried out as previously reported. Briefly, 50 nM (as polypeptide chains) polyHis-tagged protein, 25 nM dye, and the indicated concentration of 2-Na salt were incubated 15 min at 21 °C and then placed in capillaries (catalog number MO-K022) for running the assay in a Monolith NT.115 apparatus (NanoTemper Technologies), registering the fluorescence profile before and 21 s following infrared laser excitation and 4 additional s after the laser was turned off. Data analysis was performed using MO control software (NanoTemper Technologies, München, Germany).

The binding of the catalytic domain of ACE2 to the labeled RBD and S proteins was tested in the same way, except for the use of decreasing concentrations of ACE2 (tagless), in 2-fold dilution steps, from an initial concentration of 5 μM (as ACE2 chains). The titrations were done in the absence or in the presence of 0.5 mM 2-Na salt.

Cryo-Electron Microscopy Sample Preparation (Cryo-EM)

Data Acquisition

The complex of the SBA.1 variant protein (see the section above on Protein Production) with compound 2-Na salt was prepared by incubating 30 min at 25 °C 0.5 mg/mL SBA.1 and 10 μM of the compound in 10 mM Hepes pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl. Prior to the addition of the compound, the spike protein was kept at 22 °C for 30 min, then the compound was added and the incubation with the compound was continued for 30 min before 3 μL aliquots were placed on cryo-EM grids (QUANTIFOIL R 1.2/1.3 Au:300-mesh grids) that had been made hydrophilic by glow discharge (30 s using a Leica EM ACE600 device). The grids were then placed in the blotting chamber of a Leica EM GP2 at 18 °C and 95% ambient humidity and immediately vitrified in liquid-N2-cooled liquid ethane. Transmission cryo-EM images were collected automatically (EPU Automated Data Acquisition Software for Single Particle Analysis; Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a 200 kV Glacios electron microscope operated at 200 kV under low-dose conditions, and images were recorded on a Thermo Scientific Falcon IV detector operating in electron counting mode, at −1 to −2.5 μm defocus. A total of 5972 movies were recorded at a calibrated magnification of 150,000×, yielding a pixel size of 0.93 Å on the specimen. Each movie, with a total exposure time of 5 s, comprises 1204 frames, and an accumulated exposure of 36 e–/Å2.

Image Processing and Model Fitting

All image processing steps were performed using programs within Scipion. Movies were motion-corrected and dose weighted with RELION-Motion Correction. Aligned, nondose weighted micrographs were then used to estimate the contrast transfer function (CTF) with GCTF. A number of 451,475 particles were then automatically picked using Gautomatch (https://www2.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/download/gautomatch-056/). All other steps were done with the cryoSPARC software within Scipion, including 2D classification, which led to the selection of 135,589 particles. The initial model protocol was then used with a subset of the total particles to classify them into 2 classes. The best generated class corresponding to the spike trimer (around 80% of the particles) was uniformly refined with cryoSPARC applying 3-fold-symmetry and then used as an initial model for 3D Heterogeneous Refinement in cryoSPARC, without applying symmetry, to isolate two distinct particle classes, one (37,895 particles) for the spike trimer with 1 RBD domain up and 2 RBDs down and another one (97,694 particles) with all three RBD domains down. We subsequently homogeneously refined the map obtained with all three RBD domains down to 6.25A resolution, based on the gold-standard criterion (FSC = 0.143).

To assess whether the compound was bound to the spike protein, we used Chimera to fit our previous model of the D614G Spike in 3-down conformation with compound 1 bound (PDB ID: 8P9Y) into the all three RBD domains down map. Despite the quite good fitting of the protein trimer in the map, at this modest resolution, no direct visualization of the compound was observed in any of the three subunits.

Computational Studies

The FTMap server, which computationally identifies binding hotspots on a protein using 16 small organic molecules as probes, was employed in the search for putative binding regions for 2 on the SARS-CoV-2 spike trimer of both the Delta (B.1.617.2, PDB ID: 8P9Y) and the Omicron BA.1 (B.1.1.529.1) variants. The resulting highest populated cluster region in the Omicron BA.1 variant was then used to define the center of a three-dimensional cubic box for program AutoDock Vina, as implemented in the SwissDock web server, to dock the common isophthaloyl-decorated tryptophan residue that is attached to each of the four “legs” of tetrapodal 2 essentially as described earlier. ,

Murine Experimental Infection and In Vivo Treatment Assays

Experiments were carried out at the Biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) animal facilities at the Centro de Investigación en Sanidad Animal (CISA, INIA-CSIC). Procedures were reviewed and approved by the INIA’s Ethical Committee of Animal Experimentation and by the Division of Animal Protection of the Comunidad de Madrid (PROEX 115.5-21).

Sixteen six-week-old K18-hACE2 mice were used. Eight mice were administered intranasally 100 mg/kg of 2-Na salt dissolved in 50 μL of 0.9% saline, while another eight animals were administered the corresponding volume of vehicle lacking the drug. Two hours later, all the animals were infected intranasally with 5 × 103 TCID50 of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 (hCoV-19/Spain/MD-H12O_1620/2021; Gisaid: EPI_ISL_7781198) kindly provided by Prof. Rafael Delgado (Hospital 12 de Octubre, Madrid, Spain) in 50 μL of EMEM. The animals were offered food and water ad libitum and were kept in isolated ventilated cages (four animals/cage), under 12 h light/darkness cycles. Administration of the drug or the vehicle was repeated as in the first round, at 24 and 48 h after infection under isoflurane anesthesia. The animals were anesthesized under isoflurane and humanly sacrificed by cervical dislocation 24 h after the last round of administration of drug or vehicle, and the entire right lung lobe was harvested, weighed, and homogenized; viral RNA was extracted. The viral load therein was determined by quantitative RT-PCR as described.
