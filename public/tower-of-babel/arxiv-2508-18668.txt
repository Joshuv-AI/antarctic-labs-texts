Coagulation-Fragmentation Duality of Infinitely Exchangeable Partitions from Coupled Mixed Poisson Species Sampling Models
Lancelot F. James

Abstract
Jim Pitman’s [ 34 ] celebrated coagulation-fragmentation duality for the PD( α \alpha , θ \theta ) family of laws of Pitman and Marc Yor [ 39 ] has resisted generalization beyond its canonical setting. We resolve this by introducing a novel, four-part coupled process built upon the Poisson Hierarchical Indian Buffet Process (PHIBP), a framework developed for modeling microbiome species sampling across multiple ( J ≥ 1 J\geq 1 ) groups in [ 22 ] . This provides a tractable generalization of the duality to processes driven by arbitrary subordinators, yielding explicit laws for both single-group and the previously uncharacterised multi-group structured partitions. The static, fixed-time partitions are revealed to be a single projection of an inherently dynamic, four-component coupled system. This new construction simultaneously defines: (i) the fine-grained partition, (ii) its coagulation operator, (iii) a forward-in-time system of coupled, time-homogeneous fragmentation processes in the sense of Bertoin [ 5 ] , and (iv) a dual, backward-in-time structured coalescent. All four components are governed by the same underlying compositional structure, which yields their exact compound Poisson representations and, for the coalescent, drives simultaneous, across-group merger events. The chief result is a general coagulation-fragmentation duality that generalizes Pitman’s duality in two fundamental directions. First, it holds for arbitrary driving subordinators, defining a vast new family of single-group dualities. Second, it operates in the previously uncharacterised multi-group ( J J -group) setting. This work establishes a unified and tractable framework for a rich class of partition-valued dynamics, revealing structural connections between the theories of fragmentation and coalescence.

Introduction

The study of exchangeable random partitions, which provide a mathematical framework for modeling the clustering of data, has deep roots in population genetics, Bayesian statistics, and the theory of stochastic processes. A cornerstone of this field is the two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet family as developed and discussed by Jim Pitman and Marc Yor
[
39
]
and its remarkable coagulation-fragmentation duality, discovered by Pitman
[
34
]
. Extending this elegant duality to processes driven by more general Lévy subordinators has, however, remained a significant challenge. This paper resolves this challenge by introducing a novel, four-part coupled dynamic system, built upon a hierarchical mixed Poisson perspective, that provides a tractable generalization of this duality.

To lay the groundwork for this result, this introduction will proceed as follows: first, we review the foundational concepts of exchangeable partitions and the specific generative mechanisms that motivate our approach (Section
1.1
); second, we detail the classical duality that serves as our point of departure (Section
1.2
). With this context in place, we then introduce the engine for our generalization, the four-part coupled process built upon the Poisson Hierarchical Indian Buffet Process (PHIBP) framework, and outline its main contributions (Section
1.3
). We conclude with a discussion of the broader implications for time-dynamic processes and an outline of the paper (Section
1.4
).

1.2
Duality for the Two-Parameter Poisson-Dirichlet Distribution

The most distinguished class of laws on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}
are the two parameter Poisson Dirichlet distribution with parameters
0\leq\beta<1,\theta>-\beta
denoted as
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
. The origins of these distributions are in excursion theory, based primarily on a series of developments by Jim Pitman and Marc Yor, as summarized in
[
2
,
38
,
39
]
, with a notable contribution of Perman, Pitman and Yor
[
31
]
resulting in the more tractable
\mathrm{GEM}(\beta,\theta)
distributions arising from size-biased sampling. It is important to note that this class, due to their many remarkable attributes, has gained considerable importance and utility across a wide variety of fields as summarized in the references mentioned above as well as in
[
9
]
. Notably, and with a nod to a similar construction in Kingman
[
26
]
, the suite of entities of the
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
family was termed the “Pitman-Yor process” by Ishwaran and James
[
16
,
17
]
. This framework subsequently saw prolific usage in intricate applications in Bayesian Statistics and Machine Learning. In that setting, one often writes
G_{\beta,\theta}:=G
for the corresponding random bridge, or simply Pitman-Yor process.
The special case
G_{0,\theta}
corresponds to a Dirichlet process
[
11
]
, where the underlying mass partition
\mathbf{V}\sim\mathrm{PD}(0,\theta)
, which precedes but arises as a continuous limit of the important two-parameter family is central to population genetics and a host of other fields
[
43
]
. A clear articulation from these perspectives is given in
[
32
]
.

In particular the EPPF arising from sampling
G_{\beta,0}
corresponding to the random partition
(N_{1},\ldots,N_{K^{[\beta]}_{n}}),
of

with

p_{\beta}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}):=\frac{\beta^{r-1}\Gamma(r)}{\Gamma(n)}\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-\beta)_{n_{j}-1},

(1.4)

where, for any non-negative integer

(x)_{n}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)={\Gamma(x+n)}/{\Gamma(x)}
denotes the Pochhammer symbol. Furthermore,
\mathbb{P}_{\beta,0}(K^{[\beta]}_{n}=k)=\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{\beta,0}(k),
is the corresponding distribution of the number of blocks
K^{[\beta]}_{n},
where
\mathbb{P}_{\beta,0}^{(n)}(k)=\frac{\beta^{k-1}\Gamma(k)}{\Gamma(n)}S_{\beta}(n,k)

denoting the generalized Stirling number, as described in

With this foundational understanding of the
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
family in place, we now turn to the specific result central to this section: the classical coagulation-fragmentation duality established by Pitman
[
34
]
. This remarkable theorem reveals a deep structural relationship between the evolution of partitions governed by two distinct Poisson-Dirichlet laws. Specifically, it connects a fragmentation process derived from a
\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,\theta)
law to a coagulation process associated with a
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
law, providing remarkable analytical tool that forms the basis for the generalizations we develop subsequently.

In the setting of
[
34
]
, these actions are based on the two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet distribution, denoted
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
for
0\leq\beta<1
and
\theta>-\beta
. To be more precise, the duality involves a nested family
(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{P})
with marginal laws
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
and
\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,\theta)
, and with corresponding bridges
(G_{\beta,\theta},F_{\alpha,\theta}),
for
0\leq\beta<\alpha<1
. As a sketch: given an input
\mathbf{V}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
, one shatters each of its masses with an independent collection of i.i.d. mass partitions,
(\mathbf{Q}^{(l)})_{l\geq 1}
, where
\mathbf{Q}^{(l)}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,-\beta)
. This fragmentation operator, say
\mathrm{Frag}_{\alpha,-\beta}
, results in a new partition
\mathbf{P}=\mathrm{Frag}_{\alpha,-\beta}(\mathbf{V})
whose law is
\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,\theta)
. Conversely, given
\mathbf{P}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,\theta)
as input, one merges its masses according to an independent mass partition
\mathbf{Q}_{0}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\frac{\beta}{\alpha},\frac{\theta}{\alpha})
. This coagulation operator,
\mathrm{Coag}_{\beta/\alpha,\theta/\alpha}
, results in a partition
\mathbf{V}=\mathrm{Coag}_{\beta/\alpha,\theta/\alpha}(\mathbf{P})
with law
\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta)
.

In general, and even in this remarkable case, direct descriptions and manipulations of laws on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}
, while certainly informative, can become unwieldy. Here is where Kingman’s correspondence allows one to move from an abstract truth to an equivalent, but in many respects more tangible, description via their joint EPPFs in terms of their relevant conditional and marginal EPPFs, establishing a coagulation/fragmentation on the space of integer partitions of
[n]
. We now describe the actions of coagulation and fragmentation on those spaces, and in doing so will set the notation and constructions for the general case we will consider in this work, based on more complex laws which we will first need to identify. We now describe the action of these operations on integer partitions.
For concreteness, consider:

1.3
A General Blueprint for Duality: The PHIBP Framework

Our focus in this work is primarily within the context of the works of Bertoin
[
5
]
and Pitman
[
36
]
concerning what are now referred to as combinatorial stochastic processes. We concentrate on processes subject to the actions of coagulation (merging of partitions) and fragmentation (splitting of partitions), a primary focus of
[
5
]
, with a special emphasis on obtaining distributional duals.
In fact, as the centerpiece, we discuss significant extensions of a remarkable, but quite difficult to extend, coagulation/fragmentation duality of Pitman
[
34
]
for coupled mass partitions say
(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{P})
following respectively marginal two parameter Poisson Dirichlet laws denoted as
(\mathrm{PD}(\beta,\theta),\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,\theta)).

These extensions apply to variants of the class of laws on
\mathcal{P}_{\infty}
, and corresponding EPPFs, known as Poisson-Kingman distributions
[
5
,
23
,
26
,
31
,
35
,
36
]
, which are generated by the normalization of jumps of a general subordinator with non-negative jumps whose total mass possesses a proper probability density function (pdf). However our results apply much more generally as discussed in Remark
1.1
.

Our point of departure is the Poisson Hierarchical Indian Buffet Process (PHIBP), a framework we developed in
[
22
]
. That work was dedicated to establishing the PHIBP as a tractable and flexible species sampling model applied to a complex microbiome setting. Its primary contributions were the development of a complete generative process for hierarchical count data and the rigorous characterization of its marginal, conditional, and predictive laws. These results provided a complete probabilistic description of the model’s structure, including a formal solution to the unseen species problem. The generative viewpoint of the PHIBP was heavily influenced by the insights in an unpublished manuscript of Pitman
[
37
]
, a connection that will be detailed starting in Section 3.

The central innovation of the present work is to reveal and formalize a completely distinct, dynamic perspective that is latent within the PHIBP’s structure. While the framework in
[
22
]
was developed for its species sampling properties, we show here that its underlying random measures implicitly define a fully coupled dynamic system. The linchpin for this generalization is the explicit characterization of the Allocation process, which provides the correct conditioning variable to render the coagulation operator analytically tractable. Our contribution is to formally unearth this structure, elevating the PHIBP from a law on random partitions to a four-part system by explicitly constructing its corresponding coagulation operator, a forward-in-time fragmentation process, and a dual, backward-in-time structured coalescent, all driven by a single, unified mechanism. This is embodied in our static main result in Theorem
3.1

Pitman
[
36
, Chapter 5.5, pp. 116–120]
offers a wonderful schematic of the difficulties of the general problem of finding dualities and a further illustration of his own. Here, we leverage the four-part system built from the PHIBP to establish a general coagulation-fragmentation duality, deriving the explicit joint and conditional laws for the resulting infinitely exchangeable partitions. This provides a complete blueprint for the
J=1
case—no guesswork required—generating general analogues of the PD(
\alpha,\theta
) duality, while also serving as a unifying framework for the full multi-group (
J\geq 1
) setting of structured hierarchical processes. In this respect, the PHIBP framework acts as a probabilistic Rosetta Stone. It provides the key to translating between the previously disconnected languages of
Fine-to-Coarse
generative mechanisms (the step-by-step construction of features and counts from underlying Poisson processes) and their
Coarse-to-Fine
analytical duals (the composed Lévy processes and their resulting EPPFs).

The PHIBP was introduced in
[
22
]
as a general mathematical framework for the hierarchical modeling of sparse count data, and its theoretical properties place it among the general constructs developed in
[
20
, Section 5]
. The framework proved particularly effective for the challenges of microbiome analysis, where a principled probabilistic architecture is required to model properties like severe overdispersion and high volumes of zero counts. Applying the model to demanding datasets, such as the sparse counts of the Dorado Outcrop, was critical. This application was instrumental in revealing the model’s deeper mathematical properties, illuminating the general blueprint for coagulation-fragmentation duality that is the subject of this paper. This progression from a general construct to its application in a challenging scientific domain exemplifies Poincaré’s insight:

C’est avec la logique que nous prouvons, c’est avec l’intuition que nous trouvons.

— Henri Poincaré

This complete blueprint has immediate consequences. For
J=1
, we obtain explicit analogues of Pitman’s celebrated
\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,\theta)
duality for arbitrary subordinators, solving a problem that has remained open since Pitman’s original work. For
J>1
, we enter entirely new territory—there exists no precedent, even for the PD case, of such multi-group dualities. The PHIBP framework reveals these as special cases of a general principle: the duality is governed by conditionally independent operators within the Fine-to-Coarse generative framework (the "Poissonized world"), from which the complex marginal dependencies of the Coarse-to-Fine laws emerge naturally through integration.

Remark 1.3
.

We stress that our reliance on a "Poissonized world" is
not merely a mathematical device for convenience
; it reflects the fundamental reality of complex count models, a fact definitively exhibited by the framework’s success in the complex microbiome species sampling scenario in
[
22
]
. This perspective therefore serves as our guiding premise throughout the paper. We depart from it only when recovering the
J=1
analogues of Pitman’s duality, where a direct comparison requires bridging our generative model back to the classical, non-Poissonized setting. Yet, as we shall show, even this classical view is a logical consequence of the Poissonized world—an insight that follows the work of Pitman
[
37
]
.

1.4
Broader Implications and Outline of the Paper

What emerges from our framework is far richer than we initially imagined. The static duality laws, already a complete solution to a challenge implicit in Pitman’s
[
34
]
work, are revealed to be the time
t=1
marginals of an entirely new class of continuous-time processes. The four components of our coupled system themselves extend into dynamic processes that evolve as systems of coupled, time-homogeneous fragmentations in the sense of Bertoin
[
5
]
, whose states at every instant preserve the coagulation-fragmentation duality. Dually, the framework generates a novel class of continuous-time structured coalescents. These processes exhibit simultaneous, across-group merger events at each time point.

The implications for population genetics are direct. The framework not only recovers foundational
\Lambda
-coalescents
[
24
,
34
]
and models for subdivided populations with asynchronous events
[
30
,
44
]
, but extends to more complex scenarios. It offers a general framework for understanding the simultaneous, correlated merger and migration events that arise from punctuated demographic histories, such as those explored in recent discoveries
[
10
]
. For the microbiome, where our extensive developments in
[
22
]
already establish the PHIBP as a bonafide model for complex problems of practical interest, our work provides the temporal dynamics of species assembly and disassembly across multiple coupled communities.

Taking this as our starting point, the remainder of this paper is structured to develop these results by surmounting several challenges. To achieve duals comparable in tractability to
[
34
]
, one must first identify the four major components that logically fit together to guarantee a distributional duality. Even if such components are identified, their complex dependence structures may preclude relevant informative use, leading to what we term a "soft" duality. The desired "real" duality therefore must provide explicit descriptions of both the marginal distributions of the inputs and the conditional distributions of the coagulation and fragmentation operators. As we shall show, the independence seen in the classical case is a hopeless desire in the general setting.

Remark 1.4

The primary intellectual effort lies in establishing explicit, self-consistent constructions. Once in place, these frameworks are designed to let the desired results reveal themselves with minimal additional machinery. We hope the reader will find that this constructive approach not only leads to brevity but also provides a deeper intuition for the results themselves.

2.2
A Note on the Duality Strategy

The results above highlight a subtle but important distinction between fragmentation processes that are structurally dependent and those driven by external, independent operators. This distinction is nicely explored in the work of Bertoin and Pitman
[
7
]
, and their framework is essential for contextualizing our contribution. They present a dichotomy between two methods of generating nested stable sets:

1.

The Subordination Scheme: This method creates a structural relationship where the fragmentation process is intrinsically linked to the object being fragmented. The laws governing the refinement are inherent to the model’s compositional structure.

2.

The Intersection Scheme: This corresponds to an external fragmentation, where an independent Poissonian mechanism refines the existing set, analogous to applying an operator whose law is unaffected by conditioning.

Our model presents a novel hybrid of these two approaches. We use the Subordination Scheme to construct the underlying superstructure of our process, thereby inheriting its rich, structurally-dependent properties. However, for the fragmentation step itself, we deliberately employ an external
\mathrm{Frag}_{\alpha,-\beta}
operator, which is independent of its input.

This strategic separation of the superstructure from the fragmentation mechanism is the key to our framework’s analytical tractability. A purely structural model, built entirely on the Subordination Scheme, introduces formidable challenges. Under the conditioning and changes of measure required for duality arguments, the fragmentation law itself would become entangled with the state of the process, leading to a cascade of complex, state-dependent calculations. The mechanism that allows us to sidestep these dependencies hinges on preserving conditional independence. Its essence can be captured by a simple illustration which, in its reliance on the elegant properties of the
stable-beta-gamma
algebra, is presented in a spirit reminiscent of the many insightful calculations of Marc Yor.

Consider two scenarios. In the first, we have a pair of independent random variables
(X,Y)
. In the second, we have a pair
(X,W)
, where
W
is also independent of
X
and has the same marginal distribution as
Y
. Now, let
E
be a conditioning event.

\bullet

In the general case, conditioning
(X,Y)
on an event
E
that depends on both variables will typically destroy their independence. The conditional law of
(X,Y)|E
is often complex and entangled.

\bullet

Our approach, however, mirrors the second scenario. We construct the framework such that the key operator—represented here by
W
—is independent of the conditioning event
E
. Consequently, upon conditioning
(X,W)
on
E
, the distribution of
X
is altered, but
W
remains independent of the newly transformed
X
and, crucially, retains its original marginal distribution.

This preservation of independence under conditioning is precisely the property that allows us to avoid the "hairy" and often intractable calculations of a fully coupled system. By importing tools, such as the techniques from
[
15
]
, into a subordination-based setting where they would otherwise be inapplicable, this synthesis of the structural and external viewpoints enables a considerable expansion of the known duality relationships, bridging the analytical gap between the two canonical schemes.

In this section, we introduce the precise stochastic objects that form the basis of our framework. These components are constructed from fundamental concepts including subordinators, Poisson-Kingman distributions, and their extensions via mixed Poisson processes, which also connect to finite Gibbs EPPFs. While we define our specific versions here, we refer the reader to the foundational
works of
[
5
,
33
,

,

]
for broader context, and to
[
21

,
37
]
for details on related distributions. The elements defined herein will play a key and recurring role throughout our analysis.

The building blocks for our construction are
J+1
independent subordinators. We define a set of subordinators,
(\sigma_{j}:j\in\{1,\dots,J\})
, to model group-specific effects, and a single subordinator,
\sigma_{0}
, which serves as a common tethering mechanism. Associated with these are corresponding families of independent Poisson processes.

Formally, for each
j\in\{0,1,\dots,J\}
, we define the subordinator
\sigma_{j}=(\sigma_{j}(t):t\geq 0)
as a Lévy process with no drift and no Gaussian component. Its law is characterized by the points of a Poisson random measure,
(s_{j,k},U_{j,k})_{k\geq 1}
, on
(0,\infty)\times[0,1]
with mean measure
\tau_{j}(s)ds\,\mathbb{U}(du)
, where
\tau_{j}
is its Lévy measure and
\mathbb{U}
is the uniform measure. The points
(s_{j,k})_{k\geq 1}
represent the ranked jumps of the subordinator. As a Lévy process, it has stationary and independent increments: for any
t,s\geq 0
, the random variable
\sigma_{j}(t+s)-\sigma_{j}(s)
is equal in distribution to
\sigma_{j}(t)
and is independent of the process history
(\sigma_{j}(u):u\leq s)
. The value of the subordinator at time
t=1
is the sum of its jumps,
\sigma_{j}(1)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{j,k}
, and its law is determined by its Laplace transform,

\mathbb{E}[e^{-\gamma_{j}\sigma_{j}(t)}]=e^{-t\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})},

where the Laplace exponent
\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})
is given by the Lévy-Khintchine formula,

\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\gamma_{j}s})\tau_{j}(s)ds.

The Lévy measure
\tau_{j}
must satisfy the integrability condition
\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(s,1)\tau_{j}(s)ds<\infty
. Furthermore, we will have need of the "exponential cumulants," which for an integer

\nu(d\mathbf{t},d\lambda)=\underbrace{e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}t_{j}\gamma_{j}}\nu(d\mathbf{t},d\lambda)}_{\text{part yielding no observations}}+\underbrace{\left(1-e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}t_{j}\gamma_{j}}\right)\nu(d\mathbf{t},d\lambda)}_{\text{part yielding at least one observation}}.

The total mass of the second term, which corresponds to the rate of observing new species, is given by the identity:

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{J+1}}\left(1-e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}t_{j}\gamma_{j}}\right)\nu(d\mathbf{t},d\lambda)=\Psi_{0}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})\right).

(3.21)

This implies that
\varphi
, the total number of distinct species observed, follows a
\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\Psi_{0}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})\right)\right)
distribution.

This decomposition of
\nu
corresponds to a thinning of the underlying Poisson point process that generates the jumps of the parent subordinator
\sigma_{0}
. We can thus partition the set of all species into two disjoint collections:

\bullet

The set of
\varphi

observed
species, associated with parent jump sizes
(H_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{\varphi}
and locations
(\tilde{Y}_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{\varphi}
.

\bullet

The infinite set of
unobserved
species, associated with parent jump sizes
(\lambda^{\prime}_{l})_{l\geq 1}
and locations
(Y^{\prime}_{l})_{l\geq 1}
.

This allows us to represent the compound subordinator
\sigma_{j}\circ\sigma_{0}
as a sum of contributions from these two distinct sets of jumps. Let the jump sizes of the child process
\sigma_{j}
corresponding to observed and unobserved parent jumps be denoted by
\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}(H_{\ell})
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{j,l}(\lambda^{\prime}_{l})
, respectively. The process can then be written as:

(\sigma_{j}\circ\sigma_{0})(y)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sigma^{\prime}_{j,l}(\lambda^{\prime}_{l})\mathbb{I}_{\{Y^{\prime}_{l}\leq y\}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\varphi}\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}(H_{\ell})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tilde{Y}_{\ell}\leq y\}}.

(3.22)

furthermore

\sigma_{0}(y)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\prime}_{l}\mathbb{I}_{\{Y^{\prime}_{l}\leq y\}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\varphi}H_{\ell}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tilde{Y}_{\ell}\leq y\}}.

(3.23)

And importantly, for each observed species
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,\varphi\}
, we specify the law governing its properties. This process begins with the parent jump size
H_{\ell}
and the corresponding vector of child jump sizes,
(\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}(H_{\ell}))_{j\in[J]}
.
A crucial feature of the model is that a jump from the underlying Lévy process is only
observed
if it generates at least one individual in at least one of the
J
features. Given a vector of child jump sizes
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{J})
, the total rate of generating individuals is
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\gamma_{j}t_{j}
. The probability of observing at least one individual is therefore
\left(1-e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}t_{j}\gamma_{j}}\right)
.

We define the joint law of an observed parent jump
H_{\ell}
and its child jumps
(\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}(H_{\ell}))
by conditioning the base Lévy measure
\nu(\text{d}\mathbf{t},\text{d}\lambda)
on this observation event. This yields a new probability measure, where the denominator is the total mass of all such observable jumps, ensuring it integrates to one:

\mathbb{P}\left(H_{\ell}\in\text{d}\lambda,(\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}(H_{\ell}))_{j\in[J]}\in\text{d}\mathbf{t}\right)=\frac{\left(1-e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}t_{j}\gamma_{j}}\right)\nu(\text{d}\mathbf{t},\text{d}\lambda)}{\Psi_{0}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})\right)}.

To simplify, we can derive the marginal distribution for the parent jump size
H_{\ell}
alone. This is achieved by integrating the joint law above over all possible child jump size vectors
\mathbf{t}
, which gives:

\mathbb{P}(H_{\ell}\in\text{d}\lambda)=\frac{\left(1-e^{-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})}\right)\tau_{0}(\lambda)\text{d}\lambda}{\Psi_{0}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})\right)}.

Now, we can assemble the complete picture. For each observed species
\ell
, we describe the full joint distribution for:

1.

The specific count vector that was observed,
(\mathscr{F}^{(H_{\ell})}_{j,\ell}(\gamma_{j},1)=n_{j,\ell},j\in[J])
.

2.

The underlying child jump sizes that generated those counts,
(\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}(H_{\ell})=t_{j},j\in[J])
.

3.

The parent jump size,
H_{\ell}
.

This full distribution can be understood as a product of conditional probabilities that reflect the generative story. The final expression, which we present below, combines the probability of the counts given the jumps, the probability of the child jumps given the parent jump, and the probability of the parent jump.

For each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,\varphi\}
, the joint distribution is given by:

\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{J}(\gamma_{j})^{n_{j,\ell}}t_{j}^{n_{j,\ell}}e^{-t_{j}\gamma_{j}}}{\left(1-e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\gamma_{j}t_{j}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{J}n_{j,\ell}!}\cdot\frac{\left(1-e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\gamma_{j}t_{j}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{J}\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{j}(\lambda)\in\text{d}t_{j})}{\left(1-e^{-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{j}(\gamma_{j})}\right)}\mathbb{P}(H_{\ell}\in\text{d}\lambda).

(3.24)

Here, the first term is the probability mass function of a zero-truncated multivariate Poisson distribution. It gives the probability of observing the specific count vector
(n_{j,\ell})
under the explicit condition that the total count is at least one, i.e.,
\sum_{j=1}^{J}n_{j,\ell}\geq 1
. These points, which can be read from
[
22
]
, leads directly to a structural decomposition of the subordinators
(\sigma_{j}\circ\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0})
themselves, which we formalize in the following proposition. This result is a direct application of the characterization in
[
22
, Theorem 4.1, eq (4.4)]
.

Remark 3.6
.

Proposition
3.1
formalizes a critical insight: conditioning on the data imposes a specific structure on the law of the underlying process. The simple independent subordinators of the original construction are, under this conditioning, described by a more complex law. This conditional law, characterized by a thinned unobserved part and a size-biased observed part, dictates the behavior of the fragmentation and coagulation operators. This explains why they become dependent on the structure of the observed partition. The subsequent results will leverage this decomposition to derive explicit forms of these state-dependent operators. As mentioned, these results are strictly extracted from the results in
[
22
]
, tailored to the present setting. We refer the reader to that work for further details and interpretations.

The equivalence established in the corollary illustrates a central theme in our current work: the interplay between a tractable generative construction and its corresponding analytical representation. Our approach is to first define a process via a simple, step-wise generative algorithm built from fundamental discrete distributions. We then derive the compact analytical forms for the resulting probability laws, which are often expressed in terms of composed Laplace exponents and their cumulants.

This duality is developed explicitly in our work on the PHIBP

For instance, Proposition 4.3 therein provides two equivalent expressions for a key marginal distribution: one as a sum over the latent histories of the generative path, and the other as a single expression involving the system’s overall Laplace exponent. The practical consequence of this theme is that computational implementation can proceed via the simpler generative path, while the analytical forms provide a concise, neat, and structurally appealing framework for developing proofs and clarifying the underlying mathematical structure.

4
Poisson-Kingman Distributions Derived from the Couplings

The constructed framework provides a generative model for coupled partition structures. By taking a snapshot of the system at a fixed time, for instance
t=1
, we can analyze the resulting random mass partitions. We assume that the Lévy measures of the subordinators
\sigma_{j}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,J
are such that the processes admit proper probability density functions. Under this condition, we can describe the corresponding Poisson-Kingman (PK) distributions
[
35
]
, see also
[
5
, pp. 108-109]
.

Although the previous section derived the conditional EPPFs for these cases and established various equivalent representations of their dualities, this section has a twofold objective. First, we demonstrate how to obtain these conditional EPPFs from basic methods typically used for PK models. Second, and more importantly, we explicitly name the laws of the relevant conditional mass partitions. These are mixtures of laws, denoted by
\mathbb{P}^{[n]}
for various integers
n
. See
[
21
]
which we will utilize later in this work.

4.1
General
\mathbb{P}^{[n]}
laws

Generically, let
\sigma
be a subordinator specified by a Lévy density
\tau
and Laplace exponent
\Psi.
We denote the probability density function of its total mass at time
\lambda
,
\sigma(\lambda)
, as

f(t|\lambda\tau):=\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\lambda)\in dt)/dt.

The jump points
(s_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfy
\sigma(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}
, the normalized mass partition
(\frac{s_{k}}{\sigma(\lambda)})_{k\geq 1}
follows a Poisson-Kingman distribution, denoted
\mathrm{PK}(\lambda\tau)
, with Lévy density
\lambda\tau(s)
.

Now, consider a variable such as
\Gamma_{n}/\sigma(\lambda)
, which may be interpreted as the
n
-th arrival time in a trapping model, among other possibilities. The conditional density of
\sigma(\lambda)
given
\Gamma_{n}/\sigma(\lambda)=n
is :

f^{[n]}(t|\lambda\tau,\gamma)=\frac{t^{n}e^{-\gamma t}f(t|\lambda\tau)}{\mathbb{E}[(\sigma(\lambda))^{n}e^{-\gamma\sigma(\lambda)}]}=\frac{t^{n}f^{[0]}(t|\lambda\tau,\gamma)}{\Xi^{[n]}(\lambda\tau,\gamma)}:=h^{[n]}(t|\lambda\tau,\gamma)\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\lambda)\in dt)/dt

(4.1)

or equivalently

f^{[n]}(t|\lambda\tau,\gamma)=\frac{t^{n}e^{-\gamma t}f(t|\lambda\tau)}{\mathbb{E}[(\sigma(\lambda))^{n}e^{-\gamma\sigma(\lambda)}]}=\frac{t^{n}e^{-\gamma t}e^{\lambda\Psi(\gamma)}\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\lambda)\in dt)/dt}{\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda^{r}\Xi^{[n]}_{r}(\tau,\gamma)}

(4.2)

This in turn defines a family of mixed laws:

\mathbb{P}^{[n]}(\lambda\tau,\gamma)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{PK}(\lambda\tau|t)f^{[n]}(t|\lambda\tau,\gamma)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{PK}(\lambda\tau|t)h^{[n]}(t|\lambda\tau,\gamma)\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\lambda)\in dt)

(4.3)

The laws are special cases of mixed Poisson-Kingman distributions as described in

5
The Conditioned Pitman-Yor Process, an Explicit Blueprint for Duality

In this section, we present a detailed exposition that serves a dual purpose. First, it acts as a concrete demonstration of our 4 point coupled PHIBP framework in the setting of stable subordinators, for
J=1,
showing how it recovers and re-contextualizes Pitman’s classical duality results
[
37
]
. Second, and more importantly, it serves as a digestible blueprint for the general subordinator case. There are however, specialized results for the stable based case developed upon conditioning on the species sampling size in the form of unique and explicit stick-breaking representations for key components in the duality. This is obtained by using the unpublished work of
[
21
]
.

Specifically, the framework of
[
21
]
has two major components. 1. General projective distributional maps which work for general processes through Proposition 1.1 of that work and specialize to the stable setting in
[
21
, Corollary 2.1, Lemma 2.1]
. We demonstrate how to use that to obtain a generalization of Pitman’s duality within the stable framework.
2.
[
21
]
provides a very explicit stick-breaking calculus for Pitman-Yor processes conditioned on their mixed Poisson counts. This directly relates to stick-breaking representations for laws of the form
\mathbb{P}^{[n]}
and allows for previously unseen characterizations of the various components of the coag-frag duality at the level of mass partitions placed in size-biased order.

5.1
A Tale of Two Views: The Coupled Process Construction

We begin by constructing the coupled processes that form the heart of the framework, presenting them from two complementary perspectives.

5.1.1
Fine-to-Coarse View

Let
(s_{k},U_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be the points of a Poisson random measure (PRM) with mean measure
\rho_{\alpha}(s)ds\,du
, where
(s_{k})
are the ranked jumps of a stable subordinator
\sigma_{\alpha}
. Independently, let
(\lambda_{l},Y_{l})_{l\geq 1}
be the points of a PRM with mean measure
\rho_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}(\lambda)d\lambda\,dy
, where
(\lambda_{l})
are the ranked jumps of a stable subordinator
\sigma_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}
. The corresponding unconditioned mass partitions are

\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}:=\left(\frac{s_{k}}{\sigma_{\alpha}(1)}\right)_{k\geq 1}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\alpha,0),\quad\mathbf{Q}_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}:=\left(\frac{\lambda_{l}}{\sigma_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}(1)}\right)_{l\geq 1}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\frac{\beta}{\alpha},0).

The composition
\sigma_{\beta}=\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\sigma_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}
defines the coarse-grained process. The normalized jumps of this composed subordinator,
(z_{l})_{l\geq 1}
, form the coarse-grained mass partition which is the ultimate target of this view:

\mathbf{P}_{\beta}:=\left(\frac{z_{l}}{\sigma_{\beta}(1)}\right)_{l\geq 1}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\beta,0).

We build the coupled processes
(I,Z)
for
y\in[0,1]
and a time/intensity parameter
\zeta>0
:

(I(\zeta^{1/\beta},y),Z(\zeta^{1/\beta},y)):=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{P}_{1,k}(s_{k}\zeta^{1/\beta})\mathbb{I}_{\{U_{k}\leq y\}},\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{P}_{1,k}(s_{k}\zeta^{1/\beta})\mathbb{I}_{\{w_{k}\leq y\}}\right),

(5.1)

where
(\mathscr{P}_{1,k})
are independent standard Poisson processes and
w_{k}=G^{-1}_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha},0}(U_{k})
maps the uniform labels of the fine partition to the labels of the coarse partition.

5.1.2
Coarse-to-Fine View

We now view the same system through the lens of composition. The composed
\beta
-stable subordinator
\sigma_{\beta}=\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\sigma_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}
and its jumps
(z_{l})_{l\geq 1}
are the starting point. Each coarse jump
z_{l}
can be represented as
z_{l}\overset{d}{=}\sigma_{\alpha}(\lambda_{l})
, where
(\lambda_{l})
are the jumps of the directing subordinator
\sigma_{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}
. More formally, for each
l
, the jump
z_{l}
is the sum of points
(s_{k,l})_{k\geq 1}
from a Poisson process with Lévy density
\lambda_{l}\rho_{\alpha}(s)ds
. This gives the coarse-to-fine construction:

(I(\zeta^{1/\beta},y),Z(\zeta^{1/\beta},y)):=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{P}_{1,k,l}(s_{k,l}\zeta^{1/\beta})\mathbb{I}_{\{U_{k,l}\leq y\}},\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{P}_{1,k,l}(s_{k,l}\zeta^{1/\beta})\right]\mathbb{I}_{\{Y_{l}\leq y\}}\right).

(5.2)

From this view, we can construct the fragmenting mass partitions for each coarse block
l
, whose law is the central object of interest:

\left(Q_{k,l}=\frac{s_{k,l}}{\sigma_{\alpha,l}(\lambda_{l})}\right)_{k\geq 1}\quad\text{with corresponding bridge}\quad F_{1,l}(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}Q_{k,l}\mathbb{I}_{\{U_{k,l}\leq y\}}.

Remark 5.1
(The Law of the Fragments).

In this stable setting, one can formally show that the fragmenting bridges
(F_{1,l})_{l\geq 1}

6
The Dynamic Framework: An Extension of the Duality

The coupled duality established in Theorem
3.1
, and its demonstrated consequences, is the central result of this work, providing a complete characterization of the model’s partition structures. The robustness of this result stems from its foundation in the theory of Lévy processes. As a direct, albeit powerful, consequence of this foundation, the static framework can be naturally embedded within a continuous-time dynamic process. We develop this extension in this section to demonstrate the full generative potential inherent in the duality’s underlying mathematical structure. To do so, we follow closely the Homogeneous Fragmentation Framework in Bertoin
[
5
]
.

The conceptual foundation for this dynamic view is the recognition that our hierarchical model is the discrete-time analogue of a family of subordinated Lévy processes. The system’s evolution is not governed by a single, absolute time
t
. Instead, its temporal structure is two-layered:

\bullet

A single global subordinator,
\sigma_{0}(t)
, serves as a system-wide “master clock.” Its jumps correspond to major events that affect all populations simultaneously, representing a shared evolutionary or demographic history.

\bullet

A family of independent local subordinators,
\sigma_{j}(t)
, act as “local clocks.” Each is specific to a population
j
and marks events relevant only to that population’s unique trajectory.

The critical mechanism is subordination: the local clocks do not advance according to absolute time
t
, but according to the time measured by the master clock,
\sigma_{0}(t)
. Therefore, the complete description of the system’s state over time is not merely the collection of local processes, but the joint evolution of the subordinated local clocks and the master clock itself. This is captured by the state vector:

\left(\sigma_{j}(\sigma_{0}(t)),\quad j\in\{1,\dots,J\},\quad\sigma_{0}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}.

(6.1)

Including
\sigma_{0}(t)
explicitly is essential, as its process carries the information about the timing and magnitude of shared, system-wide events.

The formal bridge from this conceptual structure to a well-defined, continuous-time stochastic process is forged by a crucial extension of the model’s foundational element: the Poisson Random Measure (PRM). To introduce dynamics, we elevate the mean measure from a static space of potential jumps to a dynamic space of time and jumps. This gives rise to the central mathematical construction of our dynamic framework:

dt\otimes\nu(d\mathbf{t},d\lambda).

(6.2)

This object is the engine of our entire dynamic theory, and its structure warrants careful decomposition.

\bullet

The term
dt
represents the infinitesimal, inexorable flow of objective, external time. It provides the canvas upon which the dynamics unfold.

\bullet

The Lévy measure
\nu(d\mathbf{t},d\lambda)
resides on a product space. The space of
\mathbf{t}
represents the space of all possible event "types" or "features" (e.g., which sub-population is affected), while the space of
\lambda
represents the "magnitudes" of those events (e.g., the size of a fragmentation or coalescence). This measure encodes the full universe of potential changes the system can undergo.

\bullet

The tensor product,
\otimes
, is the key operation. It constructs a new measure on the product space of time and events. Its presence signifies that for any infinitesimal interval of time
dt
, the process has access to the entire set of possible events specified by
\nu
.

This construction is not an ad-hoc choice. It is rigorously justified by the Lévy-Itô decomposition, a cornerstone of the theory of stochastic processes. This theorem guarantees that a process driven by a PRM with a mean measure of the form (
6.2
) is a Lévy process. Consequently, our entire construction inherits the properties of stationary and independent increments. This ensures the resulting dynamics are time-homogeneous, providing a principled and robust foundation for modeling steady-state evolutionary dynamics, directly in the spirit of Bertoin’s framework for homogeneous fragmentations.

This principle of subordinated time, made concrete by the measure in (
6.2
), brings to life four distinct but intimately coupled stochastic flows.

6.1
The Four-Component Dynamic Flow

1.

Z(t): The Structured Coalescent Process (The Backward View of Ancestry)

Viewed backward in time, the framework defines a structured coalescent process,
Z(t)=(Z_{1}(t),\dots,Z_{J}(t))
, on the lineages sampled from the
J
groups. This process defines a novel class of structured
\Lambda
-coalescents. The subordination principle manifests with mathematical precision in its coagulation law. A fundamental result in the theory of Lévy processes states that the Laplace exponent of a subordinated process is the composition of the individual exponents. Accordingly, the exponent governing mergers in group
j
,
\Psi_{\text{coag}}(\gamma)
, is given by
\Psi_{0}(\psi_{j}(\gamma))
. This is the direct spectral consequence of the time-domain subordination in (
6.1
). This structure provides the model’s key explanatory power: jumps in the local clock
\sigma_{j}(t)
drive local, within-group mergers, whereas jumps in the master clock
\sigma_{0}(t)
drive synchronous, system-wide merger events, providing a mechanism for correlated demographic histories.

2.

I(t) & A(t): The Diversification and Allocation Engine (The Forward View of Ecology)

Viewed forward in time, the very same underlying PRM generates a dual flow modeling the emergence of diversity.

\bullet

The
Fragmentation Flow I(t)
acts as an engine of novelty, describing the continuous birth of new fine-grained features (e.g., alleles, traits). Its rate is governed by the ticking of the subordinated clocks.

\bullet

The
Dynamic Allocation Flow A(t)
evolves as a time-continuous Allocation Process, modeling how these new features are partitioned among populations.

Together, they provide a mechanistic model for ecological diversification and niche construction. The shared master clock,
\sigma_{0}(t)
, ensures that major bursts of innovation and reallocation can be coupled across otherwise distinct populations.

3.

F(t): The Conditional Lineage-Specific Flow and Post-Colonization Adaptation

The framework provides a granular, conditional view of evolution through the process
F^{(H_{\ell})}_{j,\ell}(t)
, which we interpret as a formal model for post-colonization adaptation. This dynamic unfolds in two stages. First, a jump in the master subordinator,
\sigma_{0}(t)
, establishes a new lineage
\ell
across all environments. This system-wide event is analogous to a founder event
[
29
]
, which creates the conditions for subsequent divergent evolution.

Following this founding, the process
F^{(H_{\ell})}_{j,\ell}(t)
describes the subsequent, private evolutionary trajectory of that lineage as it undergoes local adaptation to the specific selective pressures of environment
j

[
25
]
. Taken together, the overall system provides a generative mathematical process for the patterns of adaptive radiation
[
42
]
, whereby a single ancestral lineage diversifies to fill distinct ecological niches.

6.2
Broader Implications and Validation

This framework thus reveals a fundamental symmetry born from the principle of subordination: the backward-in-time flow of lineage coalescence, Z(t), is the precise mathematical dual to the forward-in-time flow of ecological diversification,
(\texttt{I(t)},\texttt{A(t)})
. This structure can be interpreted as a generative model for a multi-level random cascade, in the spirit of Ruelle. The master process
\sigma_{0}(t)
drives the primary cascade (e.g., metacommunity to species), while the local processes
\sigma_{j}(\sigma_{0}(t))
drive the secondary cascades (e.g., species to strains).

6.3
A Suggestive Application: Dynamically Tethering U-Coalescents

To illustrate the generative power of our framework, we sketch how it can provide a mechanism to dynamically couple and decouple well-understood, independent stochastic processes. We use the canonical U-coalescent of Pitman
[
34
]
as a substrate to demonstrate how our subordination principle can compose them into a new, tethered system with predictable marginal laws. This serves not as a complete derivation, but as an illustration of the broad applicability of our results.

6.3.1
Foundational Components

We begin with
J+1
independent U-coalescent processes,
(\mathcal{X}_{j}(z))_{z\geq 0}
for
j\in\{0,1,\dots,J\}
, where
z
is the intrinsic time parameter. For each
j
,
\mathcal{X}_{j}(z)
is a random partition of
\mathbb{N}
whose law is that of a Poisson-Dirichlet partition with parameters
\mathrm{PD}(e^{-\lambda_{j}z},0)

[
34
]
.

A key insight is that the mass partitions of these processes can be constructed from the jumps of stable subordinators. Let
(S_{\alpha}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a stable subordinator of index
\alpha
. The ranked and normalized jumps of
S_{e^{-\lambda_{j}z}}(t)
(for any
t>0
) yield a random variable with distribution
\mathrm{PD}(e^{-\lambda_{j}z},0)
, a construction we may view (in z) in terms of flows of bridges
[
5
,
6
]
.

6.3.2
A Proposed Construction for a Composed Process

Our framework suggests a natural way to construct a new family of
composed processes
by subordinating these underlying stable laws. For a fixed internal time
z
, we can define a composite temporal structure using our master-clock principle:

\left(S_{e^{-\lambda_{j}z}}\circ S_{e^{-\lambda_{0}z}}(t),\quad j\in\{1,\dots,J\},\quad S_{e^{-\lambda_{0}z}}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}.

(6.3)

Here,
S_{e^{-\lambda_{0}z}}(t)
acts as the global clock. As the composition of a stable subordinator of index
\alpha_{1}
by an independent one of index
\alpha_{2}
yields a new stable subordinator of index
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
, the resulting composed process for group
j
is driven by a stable subordinator with index
e^{-z(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{j})}
.

This construction immediately implies that for any fixed
z>0
and
t>0
, the marginal law of the resulting tethered coalescent, which we may denote
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{0,j}(z,t)
, is given by:

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{0,j}(z,t)\sim\mathrm{PD}(e^{-z(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{j})},0).

(6.4)

This demonstrates that our framework can produce tethered processes with predictable, non-trivial marginal distributions.

6.3.3
An Operational Sketch

This construction points towards a highly flexible playbook for analyzing coupled systems:

1.

Internal Time Evolution (The ‘z‘-axis):
The internal dynamics of the U-coalescent are governed by a well-defined fragmentation operator (see, e.g.,
[
34
]
, Cor. 15). Our construction suggests that a corresponding, albeit more complex, fragmentation structure should govern the evolution of the tethered process from an internal time
s
to
z>s
. A full derivation of this operator is a promising avenue for future work.

2.

Coupling and Decoupling (The ‘t‘-axis):
For any fixed internal time
z
, our framework provides the formal mechanism to move between the coupled representation (for
t>0
) and a decoupled state of
J+1
independent U-coalescents (as
t\to 0
).

3.

Consistency:
The well-defined nature of the underlying Lévy processes provides confidence that these operations can be performed consistently.

In summary, this sketch shows how our framework can be viewed not just as a specific model, but as a general mathematical engine for composing existing dynamic processes. It enriches the U-coalescent universe by providing a rigorous method for introducing cross-population correlations, pointing the way toward a new class of analyzable, composite stochastic systems. A full, formal development of these ideas is beyond the scope of the present work, but we hope this sketch might encourage others to explore them in greater detail.

Concluding Remarks

We conclude by highlighting two key aspects of the framework developed herein.

Remark 6.1
(Analogy to Multi-Deme Population Genetics).

The framework serves as a direct analogue to multi-deme models in population genetics. The groups
[J]
can be interpreted as distinct demes or subpopulations, while the species
(Y_{l})_{l\geq 1}
are analogous to alleles in a gene pool. The global process
\sigma_{0}
models the ancestral population, and the local subordinators
(\sigma_{j})_{j\in[J]}
model the effects of genetic drift and mutation within each deme. The sharing of species across groups implicitly models migration or shared ancestry, providing a structure related to multi-type or structured coalescent processes
[
3
,
24
,
30
,
44
]
, including recent models emerging from individual-based dynamics with demographic bottlenecks
[
10
]
. A notable feature of this construction is its scalability, which allows for the addition of groups after initial observations. The practical application of this multi-group structure has been demonstrated in the context of complex microbiome data
[
22
]
.

Remark 6.2
(A Fundamental and Extensible Architecture).

More fundamentally, the model’s architecture is built from Lévy and Poisson processes. This facilitates its integration with other systems that admit a Poisson random measure representation, in the spirit of Pitman’s
big Poisson
formulations. This allows for established transformations from the theory of stochastic processes, such as Bertoin’s method for inducing self-similarity in homogeneous fragmentations
[
5
, Sec. 3.3]
, to be adapted. Otherwise, it is up to the imagination and utility of the user.
