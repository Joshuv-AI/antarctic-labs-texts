Botulinum toxin type A attenuates trigeminal neuralgia-like pain by suppressing CGRP release and modulating NaV1.7-associated signaling
Authors: Min-Hua Yao, Hai-Chao Chen, Xiao-Yu Zhang, Si-Wei Zhou, Chuan-Xiong Li, Yan-Yu Lu, Zi-Rui Liao, Lei Wang, Ruo-Hui Zhang, Yu-Hong Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrigeminal neuralgia (TN) is a disabling facial pain disorder, and current treatments remain insufficient for a substantial proportion of patients. Botulinum toxin type A (BoNT/A) has shown therapeutic benefit in TN, but its peripheral analgesic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. We aimed to determine whether BoNT/A alleviates TN-like pain by modulating the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)–extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)–NaV1.7 pathway in trigeminal ganglion neurons.MethodsTN-like pain was induced in mice by chronic constriction injury of the infraorbital nerve (CCI-ION). On postoperative day 14, BoNT/A (10 U) or saline was injected intradermally into the whisker pad. Evoked and spontaneous pain-like behaviors were assessed using von Frey testing, the Ugo Basile facial pain test, and spontaneous scratching recordings; anxiety-like behavior was evaluated using the elevated plus maze. Myelin pathology was examined by Luxol fast blue staining and transmission electron microscopy. CGRP, NaV1.7/NaV1.8, and ERK/MAPK signaling were examined in trigeminal ganglion neurons using immunofluorescence, Western blotting, RT-qPCR, ELISA, calcium-influx assays, and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Olcegepant and PD98059 were used to interrogate CGRP receptor- and ERK-dependent mechanisms.ResultsBoNT/A significantly attenuated CCI-ION-induced mechanical allodynia, increasing the facial withdrawal threshold from 0.11 to 1.29 g, and reducing spontaneous pain-like behaviors. BoNT/A also improved anxiety-like behavioral measures in CCI-ION mice. Histological and ultrastructural analyses shown partial amelioration of myelin pathology, accompanied by increased myelin basic protein expression and a lower g-ratio. Mechanistically, BoNT/A reduced CGRP-associated activity in TRPV1-positive trigeminal ganglion neurons, accompanied by lower serum CGRP levels and attenuated capsaicin-evoked calcium influx. These changes were associated with a preferential reduction in NaV1.7 membrane localization, without a comparable reduction in NaV1.8, and with a marked decrease in sodium current density, with peak sodium current reduced by 78.6%. Pharmacological inhibition of CGRP receptors or ERK phosphorylation reproduced the effects of BoNT/A on NaV1.7 membrane localization.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that BoNT/A alleviates CCI-ION-induced TN-like pain, at least in part, by suppressing CGRP-dependent ERK activation and subsequent NaV1.7 membrane recruitment in trigeminal ganglion neurons. The CGRP–ERK–NaV1.7 pathway may represent a peripheral mechanism of BoNT/A-mediated analgesia and a potential target for neuropathic facial pain.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02454-4.Keywords: BoNT/A, Trigeminal neuralgia, NaV1.7, CGRP, MAPK/ERK signaling pathway

Introduction

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is one of the most severe neuropathic pain disorders, characterized by recurrent, electric-shock-like unilateral facial pain [1–3]. Current pharmacotherapies, primarily sodium channel blockers like carbamazepine, fail to adequately control pain in a substantial proportion of patients due to ineffectiveness or intolerable adverse effects [4]. This therapeutic gap highlights the need to identify disease-relevant mechanisms and develop mechanism-based treatments for TN.

Neurovascular compression is the causes focal demyelination of the trigeminal root and leads to a classical TN [4–7]. The resulting structural damage causes the abnormal accumulation and redistribution of voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs), such as NaV1.3 (Voltage-gated Na+ Channels 1.3), NaV1.7, and NaV1.8, along demyelinated axonal segments. The redistribution lowers threshold of action potential (AP) and causes neuronal hyperexcitability, the electrophysiological substrate of TN paroxysms [8–11]. Among VGSC subtypes, NaV1.8 has been implicated in peripheral nociceptive signaling, partly due to its ability to support repetitive firing in sensory neurons. This has encouraged the development of selective NaV1.8 inhibitors, such as VX-548 [12, 13]. Similarly, human genetics provides strong evidence for NaV1.7, as loss-of-function mutations in SCN9A cause congenital insensitivity to pain and gain-of-function mutations cause severe inherited pain syndromes [14–16]. NaV1.7 was targeted pharmacologically, which achieved poor clinical outcomes. This suggests that the activity of this sodium channel is likely to be regulated by complex networks [17–20].

In addition to ion channels, neuropeptides such as CGRP have emerged as important mediators of neurogenic inflammation and pain sensitization [21–23]. Significantly, a two-way communication between CGRP and VGSCs, as although the NaV1.7 activity increases CGRP secretion [15–17], CGRP can also alter the expression and membrane trafficking of sodium channels via downstream pathways like MAPK/ERK [24–26]. This cycle, it is hypothesized to amplify and sustain the pain signaling; however, its exact role it plays in TN pathology is quite poorly defined.

BoNT/A has emerged as a beneficial treatment for refractory TN. Clinical trials have shown a remarkable reduction in pain and the need for systemic medications [27–30]. However, the precise mechanism by which BoNT/A ameliorates TN remains incompletely understood. Given that CGRP, a peptide neurotransmitter, is released in a vesicle-dependent manner and plays a well-established role in both TN and migraine pathophysiology [31], it is plausible that BoNT/A exerts a regulatory effect on CGRP release [32].Moreover, CGRP can modulate nociceptor responses to painful stimuli through autocrine and paracrine signaling [33].To explore this hypothesis, we established a TN mouse model. In line with this rationale, recent experimental evidence has demonstrated that BoNT/A differentially modulates baseline and evoked CGRP release under inflammatory conditions [34]. We combined behavioral assays, myelin pathology analysis, CGRP release assays, pharmacological blockade, and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in a mouse CCI-ION model and cultured trigeminal ganglion neurons. The alleviating effect of BoNT/A on TN was observed, and then it was investigated whether this effect was exerted by regulating CGRP release and influencing pain-related sodium ion channels.

Methods

Animal

Two‑month‑old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Lanzhou University, China (Laboratory Animal Production License No. SYXK [Gan] 2018‑0002). Under specific pathogen‑free (SPF) conditions in a temperature‑controlled room (20–25 °C) at 45–55% humidity and in a 12 h light/dark cycle. Food and water were supplied as required. The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the School of Basic Medical Sciences in Lanzhou University has approved all the experimental procedures (Approval No. Lzujcyxy20251004). Healthy male mice weighing 22 ± 3 g were used in the study. The animals were randomly assigned to three groups: a normal control (Control), trigeminal neuralgia model (TN), and treatment group (TN + BoNT/A).

Establishment of TN model

Mice received a dose of 0.6 mL of 0.3% pentobarbital sodium via subcutaneous injection. A curved incision was made along the gingival margin next to the second permanent molar for exposure of the infraorbital nerve (ION) main trunk. Using a surgical microscope, the ION was carefully dissected and loosely tied with a one 4–0 chromic catgut suture. The ligation was modified to limit nerve diameter to about one‑third while not totally blocking neural conduction or perineurial blood flow. The ION was then traced back to reveal the infraorbital foramen. A sterile cotton ball weighing 0.1 mg was inserted into the foramen. A distance of 2–3 mm was maintained between the ligation site and the compression site at the foramen. The ION was exposed with the same surgical but was neither ligated nor compressed. Ultimately, the incision was closed with 4 − 0 surgical sutures.

BoNT/A administration

The facial target areas of the mice were shaved and were disinfected with 75% alcohol. A microsyringe device was used to draw out BoNT/A working solution (Hengli, Sinopharm Lanzhou Biological Products Co., Ltd., China) was accurately drawn. A single-point intradermal injection of 6 µL (equivalent to 10 units [35]) was administered into the unilateral whisker pad. The injection was slowly administered under the skin subcutaneously so that it would be retained locally in a place of injection of 16 mm2 area. Control mice were administered the same volume of 0.9% sterile normal saline at the same site and in the same way. Upon completion of the injection, the mice were placed on a warming pad until full recovery and mobility were achieved. The general behaviour of injection sites and other components was watched. All injections were performed by the same trained personnel to ensure consistency throughout the study.

Behavioral analysis

Facial pain threshold testing

The subjects entered the modified circular EP‑tube restraint device with their heads exposed. They were allowed to habituate the device for 30 min prior to the test. To evaluate the mechanical sensitivity, the calibration of von‑Frey was administered perpendicularly on the skin of the infraorbital skin zone. Each filament was applied for 1–2 s, and a positive response was defined as an avoidance behavior, including head withdrawal, escape attempts, vocalization, or vigorous facial grooming activity. The method used for testing was an up‑down method. In the absence of a response, a filament with, higher force was applied, whereas a filament with lower force was used following a withdrawal response. This procedure was repeated until the withdrawal threshold was determined, defined as the lowest filament force that elicited a consistent reflexive withdrawal. Threshold values were recorded and subsequently converted into pain threshold estimates using the UP‑Down analysis software.

Facial pain detection

The Ugo Basile facial pain test system (Model 31300, Ugo Basile, Italy) was used to assess changes in pain threshold in mice. The system consists of a standardized testing chamber, an infrared detection unit with a water access window, and a mechanical stimulation module. During the training phase, a plastic bottle containing 2% sucrose solution was placed at the water window without any stimulation device. At the start of training, each mouse was placed in the testing chamber for a 10-min habituation. The box was then removed, and the water window was opened to allow the mouse to drink the sucrose solution freely for 10 min. During the testing phase, a mechanical stimulation device was installed at the water window. The device included three interchangeable modules with different numbers of NiTi wires on each side: 6 + 6, 9 + 9, and 13 + 13 wires. The module with 9 + 9 wires was used in this experiment. Mice were deprived of food and water for 12 h prior to testing. During testing, when the animal extended its head through the window to drink the sucrose solution, it received mechanical stimulation from the NiTi wires. An infrared sensor automatically recorded the number of head entries into the window and the total duration of drinking behavior. These measurements were used to evaluate the pain threshold of the mice.

Facial scratching detection

After a 20-min habituation period in the test cage, each animal was video-recorded for 5 min for subsequent analysis. A scratching episode was defined as the animal lifting one hind paw and making repeated scratching movements directed toward the facial region. The episode was considered to end when the hind paw was returned to the floor or when the animal engaged in other behaviors. The number of scratching episodes was counted for each animal.

Open field test

The OFT-100 system (Chengdu Taimeng Software Co., Ltd.) was used to conduct the open field test. The test arena measured 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm and the central zone was a square measuring 16 cm × 16 cm. After 30 min of getting used to the behavioral testing room, the mouse was placed at the center of the arena and filmed for 10 min. The video tracking system (TM‑Vision, Chengdu Taimeng Software Co., Ltd.) was used for analyzing total movement time, total distance travelled resting time as well as movement time and distance spent in each zone of the arena. After each trial, the arena was thoroughly wiped with 75% ethanol to eliminate odor and residue before proceeding with the next test.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze (EPM) test is based on the natural aversion of rodents to open, elevated spaces and their tendency to explore novel environments, and is used to assess anxiety-like behavior. Animals displaying higher levels of anxiety typically spend more time in the enclosed arms compared to those with lower anxiety. The maze consists of two open arms (35 cm × 5 cm × 0.5 cm) and two enclosed arms (35 cm× 5 cm × 20 cm), arranged in a cross shape with a central platform (5 cm × 5 cm). The equipment was positioned 50 cm above the ground. On the day of the testing, all the animals were transferred, from the housing room to the behavioral testing room and allowed to acclimate for 30 min, before the experiment. Each time the mouse was placed on a central platform facing an open arm, and the behavior was video-recorded for 10 min. The parameters of the experiment were analyzed using video tracking software (PTM-100 EPM System, Chengdu Taimeng Software Co., Ltd.) that included time spent in open and closed arms, number of entries into each arm, and total distance moved. After every trial, the maze was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to remove olfactory cues and residual tampering before testing the next animal.

Nerve fiber conduction velocity detection

The infraorbital branch of the trigeminal nerve was isolated in the mouse under anesthesia. The BL420A/F biological signal gathering and analysis system (Chengdu Taimeng Co., Ltd.) was utilzed to measure nerve conduction time. The isolated nerve fiber was fixed at both ends with electrodes and a proximal stimulus was given. The time required for signal propagation from the stimulating electrode to the recording electrode, was recorded.

Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining

The paraffin sections had their paraffin removed and were re-rehydrated. A myelin staining solution A (Servicebio, G1030, China) was placed in a 65 °C oven for 30 min. The rehydrated sections were incubated in solution, pre-heated to 65 °C for 4 h. After staining, sections were cooled to 22 ± 3 °C and rinsed with deionized water, before being differentiated by sequentially dipping in myelin staining solution B (Servicebio, G1030, China) for 5 s and solution C for 10 s. This differentiation step was repeated as necessary until myelin sheaths turned blue under the microscope.The sections were subsequently dehydrated through three absolute changes (3 min each), cleared in one xylene change for 5 min, and mounted in neutral resin.

Immunofluorescence histochemical staining

Frozen sections of the medulla oblongata (20 μm thick) were prepared in horizontal or coronal planes. For each animal, one in every five serial sections was selected for staining, resulting in three stained sections per subject. After fixation and permeabilization, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies, respectively: rabbit anti-CGRP (Santa Cruz, sc‑57053, 1:300), rabbit anti‑NF200 (Servicebio, GB12144, 1:200), rabbit anti‑MBP (Servicebio, GB12226, 1:200), mouse anti‑NaV1.7 (Abinscience, HS929033, 1:200), and rabbit anti‑NaV1.8 (Abinscience, HA286013, 1:200). The following day, sections were equilibrated to 22 ± 3 °C for 1 h, washed with 0.01 M PBS, and incubated for 2 h at 22 ± 3 °C with appropriate secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (Zenbio, 55037, 1:500), Alexa Fluor 594‑conjugated donkey anti‑mouse IgG (ImmunoWay, YM2062, 1:500), Alexa Fluor 488‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (Zenbio, 550036, 1:500), and Alexa Fluor 594‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (Zenbio, 550043, 1:500). After washing with 0.01 M PBS, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Sections were then washed again, mounted, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Western blotting

Following behavioral testing, animals from all three groups were euthanized for tissue collection. Under anesthesia induced by 0.3% pentobarbital sodium, mice were decapitated and the whole brain was rapidly removed and placed on ice. The spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (SP5C) and TG were then carefully dissected under a microscope, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. For protein extraction, SP5C or TG tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer according to the standard protocol. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‑PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at 22 ± 3 °C and then incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h at 22 ± 3 °C, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. Primary antibodies included: rabbit anti‑CGRP (Santa Cruz, sc‑57053, 1:500), rabbit anti‑NF200 (Servicebio, GB12144, 1:500), rabbit anti‑MBP (Abcam, ab18011, 1:500), mouse anti‑NaV1.7 (Abinscience, HS929033, 1:750), and rabbit anti‑NaV1.8 (Abinscience, HA286013, 1:500). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‑conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat Anti‑Rabbit IgG, Zenbio, 511203, 1:1000; Goat Anti‑Mouse IgG, Zenbio, 511103, 1:1000) for 1 h at 22 ± 3 °C and washed again. For detection, an ECL working solution was prepared by mixing luminol solution A and B in equal volumes. Membranes were incubated with the working solution (0.01–0.1 mL per cm²) for 5 min, then placed protein‑side‑up in a chemiluminescence imaging system (VILBER FUSION SOLO6S EDGE, France) for exposure. Acquired images were saved and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

Upon completion of behavioral testing, tissue samples were freshly collected from animals in all three groups. Mice were anesthetized with 0.3% pentobarbital sodium and decapitated. The whole brain was rapidly removed, placed on ice, and the SP5C and TG were carefully dissected under a microscope. The isolated tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until urther analysis. For RNA extraction, the SP5C or TG tissues were homogenized in RNAiso Plus (100 mg tissue/mL) on ice according to the standard protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (TaKaRa, RR820A, Japan) to measure the relative expression levels of target and reference genes. Acute isolation of TG neurons

Mice were anesthetized, and the TG were rapidly dissected after opening the skull. The isolated ganglia were placed in ice-cold D-Hanks solution. After washing with D-Hanks solution, the ganglia were minced and digested in oxygenated D-Hanks solution containing 1.25% trypsin (Solarbio, D6430, China) and 1 mg/mL collagenase (Solarbio, C8160, China) at 37 °C for 35 min, with gentle agitation every 10 min. The digestion was terminated by discarding the enzyme solution. The tissue was then washed and centrifuged (500 rpm, 5 min) in 1 mL of culture medium (DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% glutamine, and 0.5% penicillin). After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was gently suspended in 2 mL of fresh culture medium. The cells were inoculated onto cell slides and treated with BoNT/A (0.3–0.5 units/ml) for 6 h in a humidified incubator at 37 °C.

Whole‑cell patch‑clamp recording

Freshly isolated TG neurons were allowed to adhere to poly‑L‑lysine‑coated coverslips. For sodium current recordings, the patch pipette was filled with an intracellular solution containing: KCl (120 mM), CaCl₂ (5.37 mM), MgCl₂ (1.75 mM), EGTA (10 mM), HEPES (10 mM), Na2‑ATP (4 mM) (pH adjusted to 7.2 with NaOH). Cells were perfused with an extracellular solution containing: NaCl (140 mM), KCl (5 mM), CaCl₂ (2 mM), MgCl₂ (1 mM), HEPES (10 mM), glucose (10 mM), TEA‑Cl (10 mM) (pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH). Prior to recording, cells were incubated for 6 h in medium containing BoNT/A (0.25 U/mL). The cells were cultured in the medium containing 1 µM OL (Sigma, ST9H9BC1D2C1, USA) or 10 µM PD98059 (Sigma, P215, USA) for 1 h, and then 100 nM ProTxII (MCE, HY-P1221, USA) or 1 µM VX-548 (MCE, HY-148800, USA) was added for another 1 h of culture. Neurons were voltage clamped at − 70 mV, and sodium currents were elicited by step depolarizations from − 80 mV to + 50 mV (140 ms duration). The identity of the sodium current was confirmed by its near‑complete inhibition after bath application of 1 µM tetrodotoxin (TTX), a selective sodium channel blocker. For AP recordings, the extracellular solution contained: NaCl (140 mM), KCl (5 mM), CaCl₂ (2 mM), MgCl₂ (1 mM), HEPES (10 mM), glucose (10 mM) (pH 7.3 with NaOH). The pipette solution contained: K‑gluconate (136 mM), NaCl (10 mM), MgCl₂ (1 mM), EGTA (10 mM), HEPES (10 mM), Mg‑ATP (2 mM) (pH 7.3 with KOH). Current clamp recordings were performed to examine neuronal excitability under the same pharmacological treatment conditions. All the reagents mentioned above are from Sigma.

Transmission electron microscopy

Mice were anesthetized and perfused transcardially. The thoracic cavity was opened, and the heart was exposed. Perfusion was initiated with 3 mL of 0.9% saline delivered into the left ventricle at a constant flow for 1 min to clear blood, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde plus 0.25% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) for 3 min. After perfusion, the infraorbital nerve at the compression site was rapidly dissected and immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C in the dark for 48 h. The tissue was then washed three times in 0.1 M PB (10 min each), post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, and dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol and acetone (10 min each). After embedding in epoxy resin, ultrathin sections (50 nm) were cut, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined under a transmission electron microscope. Images were acquired systematically, and myelin thickness was assessed by calculating the g‑ratio, defined as the ratio of the inner axonal diameter to the total outer diameter (including myelin). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Primary culture of TG neurons

Primary trigeminal ganglion (TG) neuronal cultures were prepared from 10 postnatal day 3 (P3) male C57BL/6 mice under aseptic conditions. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and euthanized according to the approved protocol, and the skin was disinfected with 70% ethanol. Bilateral TGs were rapidly dissected under a stereomicroscope and transferred into ice-cold Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS). The tissues were sequentially digested with 1.25 mg/mL collagenase type I (Solarbio, C8160, China) at 37 °C for 90 min with gentle agitation, followed by digestion with 2.5 mg/mL trypsin (Solarbio, D6430, China) at 37 °C for 40 min. After enzymatic digestion, the resulting cell suspension was passed through a 200-mesh cell strainer and centrifuged. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM and gently triturated with a sterile pipette to obtain a single-cell suspension.

Cells were seeded onto sterile cell culture slides and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. After 8 h, the medium was completely replaced with Neurobasal-A medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21103049, USA) supplemented with B-27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17504044, USA). The culture medium was replaced every 3 days thereafter.

For pharmacological treatments, primary TG cultures were incubated in fresh medium containing 1 µM olcegepant (OL; Sigma, ST9H9BC1D2C1, USA), 10 µM PD98059 (Sigma, P215, USA), or 1 µM bafilomycin A1 (MCE, HY-100558, USA) for 1 h before subsequent experiments. In separate experiments, cultures were stimulated with 0.4 µg/mL α-CGRP (MCE, HY-P0203, USA) for 3 h before analysis.

ELISA

Plasma samples stored at − 80 °C were taken out and dissolved on ice for later use. The ELISA test kit (Jonin, JL1651, China) was retrieved from a 4 °C refrigerator and allowed to equilibrate at 22 ± 3 °C for 30 min. The OT/GC standard was serially diluted as per the kit instructions: the OT/GC matrix solution was placed in a water bath at 37 °C for 5 min. The blank well was zeroed, and the absorbance (OD value) of each sample was measured at 450 nm using a full-function microplate reader. A standard curve was plotted with the standard concentration on the x-axis and the OD value on the y-axis. The sample concentration was calculated and multiplied by the dilution factor to obtain the actual concentration of the sample.

Data analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. For behavioral experiments, 𝑛 represents the number of animals, with 𝑛 = 10 mice per group. For molecular and histological analyses, including Western blotting, qPCR, and immunofluorescence staining, 𝑛 = 3 per group represents three independent biological replicates obtained from three different animals. Owing to the limited amount of trigeminal ganglion tissue available from each mouse and the need to allocate tissues for multiple downstream assays, samples for different analyses were collected and processed independently. Technical replicates or multiple images/fields obtained from the same animal were averaged and were not treated as independent biological replicates.

For immunofluorescence analysis, protein expression levels were quantified based on fluorescence intensity. Band intensities in Western blotting experiments were measured using ImageJ software and normalized to the corresponding loading controls. All statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1.

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t‑test was used when the data followed a normal distribution; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when the data met the normality assumption; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used where appropriate. Exact sample sizes, individual data points, p values, and effect sizes are provided in the corresponding figures and figure legends. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

BoNT/A ameliorates trigeminal neuropathic pain and pain associated anxiety in mice

A mouse model of TN was established in C57BL/6J mice via CCI-ION. On day 14 after surgery, a single-point injection of BoNT/A solution was administered into the region innervated by the infraorbital nerve. Mechanical pain thresholds were assessed using the von Frey test 7 days after BoNT/A injection. The results showed that BoNT/A significantly attenuated CCI-ION-induced mechanical hypersensitivity (Fig. 1a-c). We also observed pain-related grooming behavior on the affected side over a 10-min period, which revealed that BoNT/A markedly reduced TN-induced facial grooming (Fig. 1d).

Considering that the von Frey test is based on passive avoidance of noxious stimuli and to avoid potential false-positive outcomes due to direct facial stimulation, we further evaluated pain thresholds using the Ugo Basile device. During the training phase, no significant differences were observed among groups in the number of drinking episodes or total drinking duration (Fig. 1e, g and h). In the testing phase, mice could access water through a gap between two metal filaments, each delivering nine moderate-intensity mechanical stimuli; thus, drinking required contact of the facial region with the filaments. The tolerance to filament stimulation reflected the pain threshold. Compared with the control group, TN mice showed a significant decrease in both the number of drinking episodes and total drinking duration. Conversely, BoNT/A treatment significantly increased these parameters compared with the TN group (Fig. 1f, i and j). These data indicate that, even under an active avoidance paradigm, BoNT/A significantly improved the reduced facial pain threshold induced by TN.

Chronic pain often leads to emotional alterations. We therefore examined anxiety-like behaviors using the EPM (Fig. 1k) and OFT (Fig. 1o). Compared with controls, TN mice entered the open arms less frequently, spent less time in the open arms, and exhibited reduced total movement duration in the maze. BoNT/A treatment significantly increased the frequency (Fig. 1l) and duration (Fig. 1m) of open-arm entries, as well as the total movement time in the EPM (Fig. 1n). To further clarify the effect of BoNT/A on TN-related emotional behavior, we performed the open field test. The results showed that, compared with the control group, TN mice displayed no difference in peripheral locomotion but entered the central zone less frequently. BoNT/A treatment significantly increased the number of central zone entries (Fig. 1p), time (Fig. 1q) and the distance moved (Fig. 1r) compared with the TN group.

BoNT/A treatment attenuates myelin damage in the infraorbital nerve of TN mice

The main pathological change of primary TN is demyelination of nerve fibers. To further clarify whether BoNT/A exerts its effect by influencing the pathology of myelin, we first observed the expression levels of myelin basic protein (MBP) and neurofilament protein heavy chain (NF200) in the V2 branch of the trigeminal nerve by immunofluorescence. The results showed that the area of MBP-positive regions in the TN group significantly, whereas the area increased significantly after BoNT/A treatment; however, there was no difference in the area of NF200-positive regions among the three groups (Fig. 2a-c). Based on the WB detection results, there were no significant changes in MBP and NF200 in the TN group compared with the control group. However, after BoNT/A treatment, MBP significantly increased (Fig. 2d-f). Meanwhile, both MBP and NF200 showed no significant changes in the SP5C (Figure S1). The above results indicate that in the mouse ION-CCI TN model, the pathological changes are mainly characterized by demyelination.

To further verify the demyelination pathology, we assessed myelin integrity using LFB staining. The results showed that compared with the control group, the score of myelin loss in the TN group was significantly increased, suggesting myelin structure damage; the score significantly decreased after BoNT/A treatment (Fig. 2g-h), indicating that myelin loss was effectively improved. Given that the integrity of the myelin sheath is the basis for the jump-like conduction of nerve impulses, we further detected the nerve conduction velocity. The results showed that the conduction velocity of the V2 branch of the infraorbital nerve on the compressed side in the TN group was significantly slower than that in the control group, and it significantly recovered after BoNT/A intervention (Fig. 2i).

To obtain direct morphological evidence of myelin thickness and ultrastructural changes, we observed the infraorbital nerve using transmission electron microscopy and calculated the g-ratio value of the myelin sheath. This ratio usually reflects the thinning or loose structure of the myelin sheath. The results showed that the g-ratio value in the TN group was significantly higher than that in the control group, and it significantly decreased after BoNT/A treatment (Fig. 2j-k), indicating that BoNT/A can improve the integrity of the myelin sheath. The above results indicate that the TN induced by CCI-ION is accompanied by significant myelin structure damage and functional abnormalities, and BoNT/A treatment can effectively improve this pathological process.

Whether BoNT/A inhibits the excitability of CAP-induced TG neurons is related to its classical neurotransmitter release mechanism. High concentrations of K+ can activate VGCC on the cell membrane, promoting calcium influx and triggering the release of CGRP from synaptic vesicles. To verify the blocking effect of BoNT/A on CGRP release, we further detected the content of CGRP in the cell supernatant of TG primary cells induced by high potassium using ELISA. Compared with the control group, the CGRP content in the BoNT/A single treatment group showed no significant difference (Fig. 4e). However, after 30 min of 50 mM KCl stimulation, the CGRP content in the cell supernatant was significantly higher than that of the control group. In contrast, the CGRP levels in the KCl + BoNT/A co-treatment group were significantly lower than those of the single KCl treatment group (Fig. 4f). Additionally, after 30 min of 3 µM CAP stimulation, the CGRP content in the CAP + BoNT/A group was also significantly lower than that in the CAP alone group (Fig. 4g), indicating that BoNT/A can inhibit the release of CGRP induced by depolarization. Meanwhile, neurotransmitter release is usually coupled with calcium signals. We loaded calcium fluorescent probes Fluro-4 into TG primary cells and incubation with 0.1 IU BoNT/A, 50 mM KCl or 1 µM CAP respectively, and analyzed the changes in intracellular calcium signals using calcium imaging technology. The results showed that compared with the KCl stimulation group alone, the calcium signal in the KCl + BoNT/A co-treatment group was significantly reduced (Fig. 4h); in agreement with this, the calcium signal in the CAP + BoNT/A co-treatment group was also significantly lower than that in the CAP stimulation group alone (Fig. 4i), suggesting that BoNT/A can inhibit the calcium signal induced by depolarization.

To elucidate the potential mechanism by which BoNT/A regulates CGRP release, we used whole-cell patch clamp technology to detect the changes in calcium current in TG primary cells. The results showed that the peak calcium current in the BoNT/A pre-treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control group; after CAP stimulation, although the peak calcium current slightly recovered, it did not return to the level of the control group, and the peak calcium current in the BoNT/A treatment group was still significantly lower than that of the control group (Fig. 4j-l), suggesting that BoNT/A can reduce CGRP release by inhibiting calcium influx.

To determine whether BoNT/A participates in the pathological process of TN by regulating VGSC subtypes, this study used whole-cell patch-clamp technology to analyze the changes in sodium currents in acutely isolated TG cells. After adding BoNT/A, a typical slow sodium current trajectory was observed (Fig. 5a-b). The results showed that compared with the control group, the peak sodium current in the BoNT/A treatment group was significantly reduced (Fig. 5c and d), and the activation curve shifted to the right (Fig. 5e), indicating that BoNT/A could inhibit the sodium current mediated by VGSCs and convert it into a slow sodium current. Among them, NaV1.3, NaV1.7, NaV1.8, and NaV1.9 are highly expressed in nociceptive sensory neurons, and their functional abnormalities are one of the pathogenic mechanisms of neuropathic pain. This study used RT-qPCR, WB, and immunofluorescence staining techniques to detect the expression changes of NaV1.3, NaV1.7, NaV1.8, and NaV1.9 in the TG and medulla of TN model mice. The results showed that compared with the control group (Control), the mRNA expression level of NaV1.7 in the TG of TN model mice was significantly increased, whereas the mRNA expression level of NaV1.8 was significantly decreased (Figure S3d), and the mRNA expression levels of NaV1.7 and NaV1.8 in the medulla tissue showed an upward trend (Figure S4a); immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that the number of NaV1.7 positive cells in the TG of the TN group was significantly higher than that of the control group, indicating that NaV1.7 was in a high expression state in nociceptive sensory neurons under TN conditions (Figure S3a-c). To further clarify the specific regulatory effect of BoNT/A on the functional activities of NaV1.7 and NaV1.8 channels, we employed whole-cell patch clamp recording technology combined with subtype-specific channel inhibitors to analyze the changes in sodium current in the acutely isolated TG neurons. Discussion

The present study provides integrated behavioral, ultrastructural, biochemical, and electrophysiological evidence that peripheral BoNT/A attenuates CCI-ION-induced TN-like pain. A key advance of this study is the identification of a previously underexplored neuropeptide–ion channel link, whereby BoNT/A-mediated suppression of activity-dependent CGRP release is associated with reduced ERK activation and attenuation of NaV1.7-associated sodium currents in trigeminal neurons. BoNT/A also reduced anxiety-like behaviors and improved indices of myelin integrity, suggesting that its effects may extend beyond sensory hypersensitivity to broader pain-related pathological changes. Together, these findings expand the conventional view of BoNT/A as a SNAP-25-dependent inhibitor of vesicle exocytosis and suggest that reduced CGRP signaling is associated with decreased ERK activation and diminished NaV1.7-associated neuronal excitability. Nevertheless, given that the current evidence is derived mainly from pharmacological inhibition, expression analyses, colocalization imaging, and whole-cell current recordings, the proposed CGRP–ERK–NaV1.7 pathway should be viewed as a plausible mechanistic framework that warrants further validation in future studies.

The classical clinical manifestation of TN is a brief, electric shock-like pain attack, suggesting that abnormal excitability of trigeminal afferent fibers is a key pathophysiological feature [36–38]. In 2002, Devor et al. proposed the ignition hypothesis based on ultrastructural observations of trigeminal nerve roots from patients with TN undergoing microvascular decompression [39]. In demyelinated regions, adjacent axons may become closely apposed with little intervening glial insulation, creating a structural substrate for ectopic discharge and ephaptic cross-excitation. Following myelin disruption, voltage-gated sodium channels normally enriched at nodes of Ranvier may redistribute along exposed axonal membranes. Injury-associated inflammatory signals can also promote re-expression of embryonic sodium channels, such as NaV1.3, and increase the expression or membrane availability of other sodium channel subtypes [40]. These changes lower the threshold for action potential initiation and facilitate abnormal impulse generation. Once one fiber is activated, excitation may spread to neighboring demyelinated fibers, producing after discharge and transforming a brief trigger into an explosive pain attack [38, 41]. The clinical efficacy of sodium channel blockers such as carbamazepine further supports the importance of abnormal sodium channel-dependent firing in TN [4, 41, 42].

In the present CCI-ION mouse model, we observed marked structural myelin injury. LFB staining showed increased myelin loss scores, and TEM analysis revealed an increased g-ratio in the TN group, consistent with demyelination or myelin thinning. BoNT/A treatment partially normalized these changes, suggesting improved myelin integrity. This effect should be interpreted cautiously. BoNT/A is unlikely to directly “repair” myelin in a simple manner; rather, it may reduce ongoing ectopic firing, neurogenic inflammation, and peripheral nociceptive input, thereby creating a more permissive environment for myelin stabilization or recovery. Longitudinal ultrastructural studies and direct assessment of Schwann cell responses will be needed to determine whether BoNT/A actively promotes remyelination or indirectly limits further myelin damage. The analgesic effect of BoNT/A in neuropathic pain has been well documented clinically, but its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined. The best-established mechanism is cleavage of SNAP-25 within the SNARE complex, thereby inhibiting vesicle fusion and reducing the release of neurotransmitters and neuropeptides from nociceptive sensory terminals [43, 44]. In our study, BoNT/A reduced CGRP expression in both the TG and Sp5C and decreased serum CGRP levels. These findings are consistent with the known ability of BoNT/A to suppress CGRP release, although the exact contribution of local peripheral actions versus secondary central effects remains unresolved.

Our in vitro experiments further suggest that BoNT/A-mediated inhibition of CGRP release may involve reduced calcium-dependent excitability. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings showed that BoNT/A pretreatment decreased capsaicin-induced high-frequency firing and action potential amplitude in TG neurons. Calcium imaging also showed that BoNT/A suppressed intracellular calcium responses induced by KCl or capsaicin. In addition, TRPV1-dependent inward currents were reduced after BoNT/A pretreatment. These findings suggest that BoNT/A may inhibit CGRP release not only by preventing vesicle fusion through SNAP-25 cleavage, but also by reducing the ability of nociceptive neurons to respond to excitatory stimuli with robust calcium influx. This calcium-related effect may occur in parallel with, or downstream of, SNARE disruption. In this context, impaired vesicle release and reduced calcium signal transduction may cooperate to limit activity-dependent CGRP secretion [32, 45–48].

An apparent discrepancy was observed between Calca mRNA and CGRP protein levels after BoNT/A treatment. BoNT/A markedly reduced Calca transcription, whereas CGRP protein in tissue and circulating CGRP levels were reduced toward, but not below, control values. This difference may reflect distinct turnover kinetics of mRNA and peptide pools. qPCR captures the current transcriptional state, whereas Western blotting and ELISA detect pre-existing CGRP peptide stored in dense-core vesicles, axonal terminals, extracellular compartments, or circulation. In addition, circulating CGRP should be interpreted cautiously because serum CGRP measurements can be influenced by peptide isoform, assay platform, sample processing, storage time, sex, and age [49]. Because BoNT/A primarily blocks activity-dependent exocytosis, suppression of neuronal activity and transcription may precede complete depletion of stored peptide protein. Consistent with this interpretation, recent experimental evidence indicates that BoNT/A can differentially modulate baseline and evoked CGRP release under inflammatory conditions, suggesting that CGRP release and peptide storage may not change in parallel after BoNT/A treatment [34]. In addition, serum CGRP may originate from multiple peripheral sources and may not precisely mirror TG transcription. Time-course experiments will therefore be required to determine whether CGRP protein levels normalize at later stages after BoNT/A treatment.

Chronic orofacial pain is frequently accompanied by affective disturbances, including anxiety-like and depressive-like symptoms; a recent systematic review reported a positive association between orofacial pain and anxiety, particularly in chronic orofacial pain populations [50] Persistent nociceptive input from trigeminal afferents may engage not only the Sp5C and thalamocortical sensory pathways, but also supraspinal cortical and limbic regions involved in affective pain processing, such as the anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus. In a recent mouse model of trigeminal neuralgia, trigeminal nerve root compression induced demyelination, persistent pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors, and widespread activation of the S1BF, ACC, amygdala, and hippocampus; importantly, chemogenetic inhibition of ACC excitatory neurons alleviated both pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors [51]. Conversely, anxiety and negative emotional states can amplify pain perception and avoidance behavior, forming a reciprocal pain–affect interaction. In our experiment, the increased open-arm exploration in the EPM and increased central-zone activity in the OFT after BoNT/A treatment may reflect attenuation of pain-induced anxiety-like behavior. However, these behavioral effects should not be interpreted as direct anxiolytic actions of BoNT/A without additional circuit-level evidence.

Whether BoNT/A directly modulates sodium channels remains controversial. Yang et al. reported that BoNT/A inhibited CCI-ION-induced NaV1.7 upregulation in the TG and reduced NaV1.8 expression and current density in DRG neurons, suggesting that its analgesic effect may involve regulation of specific sodium channel subtypes [52]. In contrast, Kesdoğan et al. used heterologous expression systems, neuronal cell lines, and human iPSC-derived sensory neurons and concluded that BoNT/A does not directly block sodium channels [53]. Our data are broadly compatible with the latter view in that BoNT/A did not behave simply as an acute sodium channel blocker. Instead, the electrophysiological profile suggests a remodeling of sodium current composition or channel availability in TG neurons.

BoNT/A treatment significantly reduced total sodium current density in TG neurons, with an inhibition rate of approximately 78.6%. Interestingly, the residual current displayed a rightward shift in the activation curve and slower deactivation kinetics, consistent with a shift from a fast sodium current-dominant profile toward a slower residual sodium current phenotype. Functionally, fast sodium channels such as NaV1.7 contribute to rapid action potential initiation and high-frequency firing, whereas NaV1.8 and other slower sodium channel components can support sustained depolarizing drive under pathological conditions [54–56]. Thus, the net effect of BoNT/A may be a reduction in overall neuronal excitability rather than a simple blockade of one channel subtype. Pharmacological analysis provided additional support for this interpretation. In control TG neurons, the ProTxII-sensitive component accounted for a large proportion of the total sodium current, whereas after BoNT/A pretreatment, the additional inhibitory effect of ProTxII was markedly reduced. In contrast, VX-548-sensitive residual currents remained detectable after BoNT/A treatment. This pattern is consistent with a preferential effect of BoNT/A on NaV1.7-associated sodium currents, with relative preservation of NaV1.8-associated and other residual currents. However, because these conclusions rely on pharmacological inhibitors and whole-cell sodium current recordings, we cannot exclude contributions from other sodium channel subtypes, altered channel gating, changes in channel trafficking, or indirect effects secondary to reduced CGRP signaling. Therefore, the effect of BoNT/A on NaV1.7 should be described as preferential rather than exclusive.

Although the present study focused on NaV1.7 and NaV1.8 because of their established roles in peripheral nociception and the availability of pharmacological tools, changes in NaV1.3 and NaV1.9 should not be ignored. NaV1.3 is often re-expressed after nerve injury and may contribute to ectopic firing and neuronal hyperexcitability; consistent with this, a recent ION-CCI study showed increased NaV1.3 expression in the trigeminal ganglion in association with mechanical hypersensitivity [57]. NaV1.9 contributes to low-threshold persistent sodium currents that regulate resting membrane potential and sensory neuronal excitability [58]. Therefore, BoNT/A-induced changes in NaV1.3 and NaV1.9 may either contribute to the overall reduction in trigeminal neuronal excitability or represent compensatory remodeling after suppression of CGRP and NaV1.7-associated signaling. Because we did not directly isolate NaV1.3- or NaV1.9-mediated currents, their functional contribution remains to be determined.

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, including ERK, p38, and JNK, plays an important role in peripheral and central sensitization after tissue injury or nerve damage [59–63]. MAPK activation is temporally and spatially dynamic and may occur in both neurons and glial cells [59, 64]. In the early phase of injury, nociceptive input can rapidly activate ERK in spinal dorsal horn or trigeminal sensory neurons through neurotransmitter receptors and second messenger pathways. This process may alter neuronal excitability through post-translational regulation of ion channels and receptors without requiring new gene transcription [65–67]. In later stages, MAPK activation can extend to glial cells. For example, p38 activation in microglia and ERK or JNK activation in astrocytes may promote the release of IL-1β, TNF-α, BDNF, and other mediators, thereby sustaining neuron–glia positive feedback and prolonging pain sensitization [61, 68–72].

In our study, p-ERK levels were increased in the TN group and reduced after BoNT/A treatment. In addition, α-CGRP stimulation increased the p-ERK1/2/total ERK1/2 ratio in primary TG cultures, suggesting that CGRP can activate ERK signaling in trigeminal sensory neurons. This finding is consistent with previous evidence that MAPK signaling contributes to TN-like pain. For example, Li et al. reported that the scorpion-derived analgesic peptide N58A reduced the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, p38, JNK, and ERK5/CREB pathways, as well as NaV1.8 and NaV1.9 expression, in a CCI-ION rat model [73]. These findings support the broader concept that MAPK signaling participates in the maintenance of trigeminal neuropathic pain. However, in the present study, MAPK analysis was centered mainly on ERK1/2 phosphorylation. Because MAPK pathways are regulated primarily through protein phosphorylation rather than transcription, future studies should systematically examine total and phosphorylated ERK1/2, p38, and JNK proteins to define their relative contributions.

MAPK signaling may regulate sodium channel function through both transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms [25, 74, 75]. Previous studies have shown that inflammatory mediators can rapidly increase nociceptor excitability through p38-dependent modulation of TTX-resistant sodium currents [25, 76–79]. For example, IL-1β can increase neuronal excitability through p38-mediated relief of slow inactivation and enhancement of persistent sodium currents [76]. TNF-α has also been reported to increase NaV1.7 membrane insertion through p38-dependent phosphorylation of the NaV1.7 N-terminal Ser110 site, thereby increasing NaV1.7 current density and neuronal excitability [80]. In the present study, p-ERK levels were positively associated with NaV1.7 expression. Treatment of TG neurons with the MEK/ERK pathway inhibitor PD98059 reduced NaV1.7 expression and sodium current density, and combined treatment with PD98059 and ProTx-II did not produce further marked suppression of sodium currents. Together with the α-CGRP-induced increase in ERK1/2 phosphorylation, these observations support a functional relationship between CGRP-related ERK activation and NaV1.7-associated sodium currents. Nevertheless, they do not prove that CGRP regulates NaV1.7 exclusively through ERK. Direct CGRP stimulation combined with CGRP receptor antagonism or ERK inhibition, followed by NaV1.7 trafficking analysis and sodium-current recordings in the same cellular system, will be needed to establish the sequence and causality of this pathway.

We also observed increased colocalization of NaV1.7 with the lysosomal marker LAMP1 after BoNT/A treatment. This finding suggests enhanced lysosomal targeting or intracellular redistribution of NaV1.7. However, colocalization alone does not prove lysosomal degradation. We conducted a lysosome inhibition rescue experiment using bacitracin A1. The immunofluorescence results showed that the LAMP1 signal was weakened while the NaV1.7 signal was enhanced. This preliminary observation may suggest that the effect of BoNT/A is more likely to influence the stability of NaV1.7 through the lysosome pathway.

In addition to peripheral changes in the TG, we observed alterations in CGRP-related and sodium channel-related signals in the Sp5C. These central changes may reflect reduced nociceptive input from peripheral trigeminal afferents after BoNT/A treatment, leading to attenuation of central sensitization. Alternatively, they may involve retrograde axonal transport of BoNT/A or BoNT/A-cleaved signaling components from peripheral terminals to central trigeminal projections. The present study cannot distinguish between these possibilities. Future experiments using fluorescently labeled BoNT/A, detection of cleaved SNAP-25 in the Sp5C, selective blockade of axonal transport, or region-specific delivery of BoNT/A will be necessary to determine whether the Sp5C effects are direct central actions or secondary consequences of reduced peripheral input.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our pharmacological and electrophysiological data support an association among CGRP signaling, ERK activation, and NaV1.7 regulation, direct CGRP stimulation and rescue experiments were not performed. Second, the conclusion that BoNT/A preferentially affects NaV1.7-associated currents is based on pharmacological inhibitors and whole-cell current recordings; therefore, contributions from other sodium channel subtypes cannot be excluded. Third, increased NaV1.7 colocalization with LAMP1 suggests lysosomal targeting but does not directly demonstrate lysosomal degradation. Fourth, some morphological and biochemical analyses were based on relatively small sample sizes and should be regarded as exploratory until further validated in larger cohorts. Fifth, the Sp5C changes may reflect either retrograde effects of BoNT/A or secondary reduction of peripheral nociceptive input. Finally, only male mice were used, and potential sex-dependent mechanisms remain to be examined.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that BoNT/A attenuates TN-like pain at least in part by suppressing activity-dependent CGRP release and reducing NaV1.7-associated sodium channel activity in trigeminal neurons. The data further support a potential link among CGRP signaling, ERK phosphorylation, and NaV1.7 trafficking or function. However, direct causality within the CGRP–ERK–NaV1.7 pathway remains to be established. These findings provide a useful framework for understanding BoNT/A-mediated analgesia and suggest that neuropeptide–ion channel interactions may represent promising targets for neuropathic orofacial pain treatment.
