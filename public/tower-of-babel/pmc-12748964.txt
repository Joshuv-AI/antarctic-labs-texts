Effect of hybrid fibre reinforcement and rubber aggregates on the flexural performance of reinforced concrete beams
Authors: R Karthikeyan, B Anuradha, K Deepa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research examines the effect of incorporating hybrid fibres and rubber aggregates on the flexural behaviour of reinforced concrete beams. Rubber shreds, prepared from waste conveyor belts and coated with sand to enhance bonding, were used as partial replacements for coarse aggregates at levels of 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5%. Additionally, glass fibre and polypropylene fibres were introduced into the concrete mix at dosages of 0.3% at different combinations, including PP 70% + Glass 30%, PP 60% + Glass 40%, PP 50% + Glass 50%, PP 40% + Glass 60%, and PP 30% + Glass 70%. A total of eighteen reinforced concrete beams were cast for this investigation, each with dimensions of 250 mm × 150 mm × 3000 mm. The beams were subjected to four-point bending tests over a clear span of 2800 mm to evaluate their flexural response. Values such as first crack load, deflection at first crack, yield load, deflection at yield, ultimate load, and deflection at ultimate were recorded. Load–deflection curves were plotted for all beam types. The results revealed that beams incorporating both sand-coated rubber shreds and hybrid fibres generally exhibited improved flexural performance compared to conventional concrete beams and those containing only rubber aggregates. Specifically, the beam containing 7.5% sand coated rubber and hybrid fibres (40% polypropylene + 60%glass fibres) showed notable improvement, with the first crack load increasing by 29.77%, deflection at first crack load by 21.47%, yield load by 52.86%, deflection at yield load by 28.73%, ultimate load by 14.32% and deflection at load by 53.84 relative to the control specimens.Keywords: Hybrid fibre reinforcement, Polypropylene fibre, Glass fibre, Sand coated rubber shreds, Load-deflection curveSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete remains the most widely used construction material globally due to its versatility and strength. However, the rising cost of raw materials and environmental concerns associated with natural aggregate extraction have stimulated interest in sustainable alternatives, such as rubberized concrete, which replaces coarse aggregates with waste rubber particles1,2. Utilising waste rubber from sources such as used tires or conveyor belts not only reduces environmental pollution but also improves certain mechanical properties like toughness and energy absorption3,4. Despite these benefits, rubberized concrete generally suffers from reduced compressive strength and increased, primarily due to the poor interfacial bond between rubber particles and the cement matrix5,6. This weakness can limit its practical application in structural components that require high strength and durability7. To overcome these limitations, hybrid fibre reinforcement—combining polypropylene (PP) fibres and glass fibres has been widely studied. Hybrid fibres enhance concrete’s crack resistance, tensile strength, and overall toughness by bridging cracks and restricting crack propagation8–10. Several studies have reported significant improvements in mechanical properties when rubberized concrete is reinforced with hybrid fibres11–13. For instance, the combination of PP and glass fibres at optimized dosages led to enhanced flexural strength and ductility compared to single-fibre reinforced mixes14,15. Moreover, the addition of hybrid fibres improves the durability of rubberized concrete by reducing water absorption, porosity, and chloride ion penetration, which are critical factors for long-term service life in aggressive environments16–18. Recent research indicates that fibre-reinforced rubberized concrete shows better resistance to acid attack and carbonation, contributing to improved durability performance19,20. Several investigations have focused on optimizing fibre content to balance workability and mechanical performance. A total fibre volume fraction of around 0.3% (combining PP and glass fibres) has been found optimal in various studies, producing significant strength and durability gains without compromising concrete mix workability21–23. Different hybrid ratios such as PP 70% + Glass 30%, PP 50% + Glass 50%, and PP 30% + Glass 70% have been explored to determine the best fibre synergy24–28.This study aims to evaluate the combined effects of waste rubber aggregate replacement and hybrid fibre reinforcement on both flexural strength and ductility properties of concrete. Various levels of rubber substitution (2.5%, 5%, and 7.5%) and hybrid fibre combinations at a fixed total fibre content of 0.3% by volume are investigated. The findings will help to advance sustainable concrete technology with enhanced performance for structural applications. This study presents a novel approach by combining sand-coated rubber shreds derived from waste conveyor belts with optimally dosed hybrid fibre reinforcement (glass and polypropylene fibres) to enhance the flexural performance of reinforced concrete beams.

Mix design

In this study, M20 grade concrete mix was designed based on IS 10,262: 2009, which provides guidelines for proportioning concrete. The mix was made with a water-to-cement ratio of 0.49, which is typical for the above mentioned grade of concrete, ensuring a balance between workability and strength. The mix design incorporated various materials, including Portland cement (53-grade), fine aggregate, cement, and coarse aggregate as partial replacement of rubber shreds. The rubber shreds were added in different ratios: 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5% along with Polypropylene Fibre and Glass Fibre at 0.3% dosage for different combinations, which were tested to assess their impacts on the mechanical properties and durability of the concrete. The mix proportions for M20 grade concrete were calculated based on the volume of materials required for 1 m³ of concrete. The cement, water, fine aggregate, and coarse aggregate content were initially determined for the control mix, and then adjusted for rubber shreds replacements at 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5%. The control mix used 11,300 kg per cubic meter, and the replacement dosages for rubber shreds were calculated as follows: SRBC1 (2.5% replacement) used 29 kg of rubber shreds, SRBC2 (5% replacement) used 58 kg of rubber shreds, andS RBC3 (7.5% replacement) used 87 kg. Water to cement ratio of 0.49, only fine aggregate kept constant, ensuring consistency in terms of aggregate content. To enhance the performance of rubberized concrete, a hybrid fibre system was introduced with a constant total fibre content of 0.3%, while varying the proportions of polypropylene (PP) and glass fibres (GF) in different ratios ranging from 70:30 to 30:70. The final proportions were adjusted for workability and consistency to achieve the desired slump and strength. Table 4 displays the mix proportions for various concrete mixes.

Stages of beam specimens casting

Fresh concrete was prepared using a rotating drum mixer. The moist drum was filled with cement, sand, coarse aggregate and sand layered rubber shreds, which were subsequently dry mixed. 80% of the water was gradually added to this dry mixture and mixed well. To stop the development of fibre balls and clustering, fibres were introduced gradually and uniformly along the drum’s wall. Slowly, the remaining water was added, and everything was well combined. Utilising wooden moulds, the beam specimens were made. Care was taken to ensure that the concrete was evenly compacted using a needle vibrator and positioned correctly in the moulds. After casting for 24 h, the beam specimens were demolded and allowed to cure according to traditional methods. The test beams and control specimens were cast at the same time. Experimental setup

Nineteen reinforced concrete beams were subjected to four-point bending using a 500 kN capacity hydraulic loading frame. The beams were simply supported, with one roller support and one hinge support at opposite ends. To facilitate effective load transfer and prevent stress concentrations at supports, 100 mm bearing plates were used at both ends, leading to a clear test span of 2800 mm as shown in Fig. 7.

Details of steel reinforcement.

The load was applied with a spreader beam that split the load into two point loads symmetrically along the span of the beam. This loading system produced a region of constant bending moment between the two applied loads with very little shear within this area a characteristic of four-point bending tests. This setup was chosen in order to test directly the beam’s flexural response to pure bending without shear interference. To capture deformation behaviour, mechanical dial gauges with a resolution of 0.01 mm were placed at mid-span (where the maximum deflection takes place) and directly below each load point in order to monitor local deformations under the acting loads. The gauges offered continuous displacement measurement during the test in order to allow high-precision monitoring of flexural deformation with rising load levels. Crack development was continuously monitored in the test via a crack detection microscope of ± 0.02 mm accuracy so that precise crack widths could be measured as cracks developed and progressed. Crack mapping was also made at routine intervals of load from the initial load at which crack formation was noticed to the failure point. Through this, crack sequence, spacing, and progressive development of crack widths were unambiguously identified. The loading was performed in displacement control to enable steady and progressive development of the cracks.

During the test, both load-deflection and load-crack width traces were recorded to furnish the key information necessary for evaluation of the beam’s performance according to serviceability requirements (deflection capacity and crack restraint) and ultimate strength (peak load-carrying capacity and failure mode).Instrumentation and loading configurations are indicated in Fig. 8, specifying the location of supports, the spreader beam, load points, and the location of gauges and crack monitoring devices. This detailed instrumentation allowed for the measurement of global response characteristics (total beam deflection and strength) and local response behavior (crack initiation, crack propagation, and localized deformations).

Result and discussion

Load - deflection response of the beam

Figures 9, 10 and 11 show the load-central deflection responses measured for all the tested beam specimens. In each instance, the load-deflection relationship initially followed a linear trend with a relatively steep slope, reflecting the elastic behavior of the uncracked concrete section. In this phase, the applied load was resisted mainly by the uncracked concrete with little contribution from the embedded reinforcement. The initial visible crack indicated a definitive change in behavior. Following the appearance of the first crack, the stiffness of the beams deteriorated progressively, as indicated by a gradual reduction in the gradient of the load-deflection curves. This reduction in stiffness corresponds to the reallocation of the internal forces, with an increasing proportion of tensile force relocated from the cracked concrete to the longitudinal reinforcement. The number and the crack width grew uniformly as the load was increased, particularly in the region of constant moment between the load applied. At elevated levels of load, the longitudinal steel reinforcement started to yield, marking the onset of the post-yielding stage of the response. This phase was defined by a dramatic decrease in stiffness, as evident from a high degree of flattening of the load-deflection curves. From this point on, comparatively large deflections were noted even for small increases in applied load, reflecting the plastic deformation and limited ability to recover stiffness. The beams sustained further load up to their ultimate capacity, after which one or more modes of failure mechanisms such as gross flexural cracking, crushing of concrete in the compressive zone, or tensile reinforcement rupture controlled ultimate failure. Effect of first crack load, yield load and ultimate load for rubberized concrete beam with/without hybrid fibre

The initial Crack load was obtained in rubberized concrete with and without hybrid fibres (Polypropylene fibre and glass fibre), as shown in Table 5. The reference specimens included the control concrete (CC) and the rubberized concrete mixes without fibre such as SRBC1, SRBC2, and SRBC3. Specimens SRBC1, SRBC2, and SRBC3 exhibited a gradual decrease in first crack load by 15.01%, 16.03%, and 18.58%, respectively, compared to CC since rubber aggregates are softer and more flexible than natural coarse aggregates. They have lower stiffness and do not bond well with the cement paste. As a result, the concrete becomes less resistant to tensile stresses, causing cracks to form earlier under loading. Therefore, the use of rubber without fibres reduces the material’s ability to resist the first crack. However, a significant increase in first crack was observed in the hybrid fibre-reinforced rubberized concrete mixes, samples SRHFB11, SRHB12, SRHB13, SRHFB14 and SRHFB15, which showed improvements of 4.33%, 9.41%, 13.23%, 17.05% and 20.87%, respectively, compared to both CC and RBC1. Similarly, mixes SRHB21, SRHFB22, SRHFB23, SRHFB24 and SRHFB25, achieved gain of 5.6%, 9.41% 13.23%, 17.05% and 20.87%, respectively, compared to both CC and RBC2. Furthermore, the SRHFB31, SRHFB32, SRHB33, SRHFB34 and SRHBC35, recorded improvements of 17.05%, 20.87%, 24.68%, 27.23% and 29.77%, respectively, compared to both CC and RBC3. Particularly, SRHFB15, SRHFB25 and SRHFB35, which contained 7.5% of rubber shreds with 40% polypropylene fibre and 60% glass fibre at an optimum total dosage of 0.3%, achieved the highest first crack load among all tested mixes. The remaining mixes in each series showed slightly reduced strengths, as shown in Fig. 12. The improved performance of the hybrid fibre-reinforced mixes can be attributed to the synergistic effect of polypropylene and glass fibres. Polypropylene fibres, being flexible, help control micro-cracks and enhance ductility, while glass fibres, with their high tensile strength, provide stiffness and bridge larger cracks. Together, these fibres enhance the tensile resistance of the matrix, delay crack initiation and propagation, and improve stress distribution around the rubber particles.

The yield load was obtained for rubberised concrete with and without hybrid fibres (polypropylene and glass fibres), as shown in Table 5. The reference specimens included the control concrete (CC) and the rubberized concrete mixes without fibre such as SRBC1, SRBC2, and SRBC3. Among these, the specimens SRBC1, SRBC2, and SRBC3 exhibited a gradual decrease in first crack load by 27.34%, 29.35%, and 32.56%, respectively, compared to CC, indicating a lower ability to sustain elastic behavior under increasing load. This reduction can be attributed to the inclusion of rubber aggregates, which weaken the internal structure of the concrete due to their low stiffness, compressibility, and poor interfacial bonding with the cement matrix. As a result, the transition from elastic to plastic behavior occurs earlier, reducing the overall load-carrying capacity before yielding. However, a significant increase in yield load was observed in the hybrid fibre-reinforced rubberized concrete mixes, samples SRHFB11, SRHB12, SRHB13, SRHFB14 and SRHFB15, which showed improvements of 14.5%, 20.92%, 26.92%, 39.50% and 42.26%, respectively, compared to both CC and RBC1. Furthermore, the SRHFB31, SRHFB32, SRHB33, SRHFB34 and SRHBC35, recorded improvements of 30.04%, 3.52%, 42.4%, 49.65% and 52.86%, respectively, compared to both CC and RBC3, as shown in Fig. 13. On the other hand, a significant achievement in yield load was observed in the hybrid fibre-reinforced rubberized concrete mixes. Specimens such as SRHFB15, SRHFB25, and SRHFB35 which containing 7.5% rubber shreds and a 0.3% hybrid fibre dosage (40% polypropylene and 60% glass fibres), recorded the highest yield loads among all tested samples29,30. This enhancement in yield performance is primarily due to the synergistic action of the fibres. The polypropylene fibres help delay the onset of micro-cracking and enhance post-crack ductility31–33, while the glass fibres provide tensile reinforcement and stiffness. Together, these fibres improve the overall stress transfer mechanism within the concrete, increase the tensile resistance of the matrix, and delay the formation of critical cracks, thereby elevating the yield point. As a result, the hybrid fibre-reinforced concrete maintains its elastic behavior for a longer range under applied loads, demonstrating superior structural performance.

Cost analysis of hybrid fibre reinforced rubberized concrete beam

The cost calculation was performed for the control beam and hybrid fibre reinforced rubberized concrete beam of size 150 × 250 × 3000 mm, with concrete volume of 0.1125 m³, reinforced with 4 Ø10 mm bars and Ø8 mm stirrups at 125 mm c/c. The analysis indicated that substituting coarse aggregate with 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5% with waste rubber slightly reduced the coarse aggregate while maintained the overall cost equivalent. For control concrete, coarse aggregate volume was 0.07946 m³ (127.13 kg), cement 42.75 kg, sand 80.44 kg, steel 16.60 kg with 2% wastage, and bar bending and concrete casting labour were added, and the total cost was around ₹1,894. With the inclusion of hybrid fibres (Polypropylene + Glass), the cost of the beams went up marginally to ₹2,131, ₹2,109, and ₹2,109 for rubber replacement levels of 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5%, respectively. While the price was higher because of the fibres, adding waste rubber and fibres had several advantages: recycled non-biodegradable waste, enhanced flexural strength, control of cracking, and post-cracking ductility, and encouraged sustainability through minimizing natural aggregate consumption, making the concrete more durable, ductile, and environmentally friendly at the expense of not much additional cost.

Conclusion

This study investigated the flexural behavior of sand-coated rubberized concrete beams with and without hybrid fibres (glass and polypropylene fibre), aiming to enhance both sustainability and structural performance. The experimental results showed that the inclusion of hybrid fibres significantly improved key flexural parameters such as first crack load, yield load, ultimate load, deflection at first crack load, deflection at yield load, deflection at ultimate load, deflection ductility, and energy ductility. The greatest improvements were observed in beams containing 2.5% rubber with 40% polypropylene and 60% of glass fibre in the 0.3% dosage when compared to both control beam and beams without hybrid fibre. Similar enhancements were noted for beams with 5% rubber with 40% polypropylene and 60% of glass fibre in the 0.3% dosage when compared to control beam and beams without hybrid fibre and for beams with 7.5% rubber and 40% polypropylene and 60% of glass fibre in the 0.3% dosage compared to control beam and beam without hybrid fibres. These findings highlight the practical viability of combining treated rubber waste and hybrid fibres in concrete to produce beams with superior flexural strength, ductility and energy absorption capacity, offering an environmentally responsible method for rubber waste utilization. The results support the adoption of this composite system in structural applications, especially where improved flexural performance is essential. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating advanced surface treatment methods for rubber shreds to enhance their bonding characteristics with the cement matrix. It should also aim to improve the flexural strength and overall performance of rubberized concrete beams. Moreover, extending the investigation to include the use of different mineral admixtures such as silica fume, zeolite, and metakaolin could offer further improvements in strength, durability, and workability. These efforts would help expand the practical applications of rubberized concrete as a sustainable and high-performance construction material in modern structural engineering. For future applications, the optimal fibre ratios identified in this study can be utilized in structural and non-structural concrete elements where enhanced ductility, crack control, and durability are required. Examples include pavements, slabs, beams, bridge decks, and precast elements exposed to dynamic loads or aggressive environments. The hybrid fibre system can also be applied in sustainable construction projects using recycled rubber and waste PET fibres, contributing to environmentally friendly, resilient concrete with improved post-cracking behaviour. Additionally, these mixes can be tailored for seismic-resistant structures or industrial flooring where high impact resistance and energy absorption are critical.
