Diminished lung function mediating the associations between blood levels of volatile organic compounds and respiratory morbidity and mortality
Authors: Quanhong Chen, Ziqi Zeng, Shaohui Liu, Anan Zhou, Hao Chen, Yunfeng Zou, Yunan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to individual volatile organic compound (VOC) is associated with reduced lung function and increased respiratory disease (RD) morbidity and mortality. However, whether a mediating pathway among exposure to VOC mixtures, diminished lung function, and increased RD risk exists statistically remains undisclosed. This study analyzed 6156 adults (aged ≥ 18) from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2012. We applied Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) models to analyze the association between VOCs and lung function, and then used mediation models to assess lung function’s role in RD morbidity and mortality. Changes of forced vital capacity (FVC) accounted for 12.22%, 29.76%, and 17.34% of the association of nitromethane with asthma [0.004 (0.002, 0.005), P < 0.001], emphysema [0.001 (0.001, 0.002), P < 0.001], and chronic bronchitis [0.003 (0.002, 0.005), P < 0.001], respectively. We also observed significant indirect effect of nitromethane with all-cause mortality through the mediation of FVC [0.042 (0.018, 0.066), P < 0.001, proportion = 23.13%] and forced expiratory volume at 1 s (FEV1) [0.054 (0.029, 0.079), P < 0.001, proportion = 27.89%]. Results indicate that VOC exposure reduces lung function, increasing RD morbidity and mortality risks. Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) report, air pollution ranks among the top five risk factors contributing to mortality globally, inducing heavy health and economic burden1,2. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are a class of highly reactive compounds containing carbon-hydrogen covalent bonds and widely present in the atmosphere as a kind of air pollutants3,4. VOCs originating from construction materials, household biomass combustion, and furniture are major contributors of indoor air pollution. Sources of industrial emissions, traffic-related emissions, and wildfires contribute to outdoor pollutant levels of VOCs5–13. The situation of VOCs pollution in the United States is also concerning. Research indicates that chloroform is the most frequently detected anthropogenic VOC in U.S. groundwater, followed by MTBE (methyl tert-butyl ether)14. In terms of air quality, VOCs like toluene often show elevated concentrations, particularly in urban areas such as Los Angeles15. Biomonitoring studies further highlight that VOC exposure is widespread, with 15 out of 16 measured urinary VOC metabolites detected above the limit of detection in over 90% of individuals tested16. VOCs may enter the body and be metabolized into smaller molecules or bind with other substances in the body. For example, toluene is metabolized into benzyl alcohol through the mixed-function oxidase system, and toluene can also bind with lipids, affecting the integrity of cell membranes. These metabolic products and binding interactions may induce stronger toxicity17. Human exposure to VOCs has been shown to induce adverse health impacts on the respiratory, cardiovascular, and neurological systems8,18–22. However, specific biological pathways of VOC induced respiratory morbidity and mortality remain unclear.

Respiratory system is the main target of exposure to ambient air pollutants including VOCs. Due to their hydrophobicity properties, VOCs are challenging to be cleared in the upper respiratory tract, leading to their entry into the alveoli during ventilation23,24. Exposure to VOCs was associated with pulmonary dysfunctions and various lung diseases25. For example, a significant decline in lung function parameters [e.g., forced expiratory volume at 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC)] was observed in association with nine VOCs (e.g., decanal and hexanal) in individuals aged 17 and younger. Similarly, in the population aged over 65 years old, only three VOCs (hexanal, α-pinene and 2-methyl-1,3-butadiene) were observed to have a noticeable impact on lung function26. These previous studies only addressed lung function effects of individual VOCs while a mixture effects of exposure to multiple VOC was not fully investigated. However, exposure to mixture VOCs was more environmentally relevant considering the co-presence of many VOCs in the environment.

Exposure to VOCs may be associated with elevated respiratory morbidity and mortality. The effects of such exposure can range from mild upper respiratory tract irritation to more severe conditions like respiratory infections and chronic bronchitis27. As impaired lung function may show a direct impact of VOC exposure, it may also chronically lead to elevated risk of respiratory morbidity and mortality. In the United States, the morbidity of lower respiratory tract diseases is estimated at approximately 1360 to 1660 cases per 100,000 individuals. Additionally, the mortality rate from respiratory diseases ranges between 14.5 and 18.2 per 100,000 individuals28. But how much does impaired respiratory health contribute to the association? From a pathophysiological perspective, impaired lung function is an intermediate phenotype between health and disease29. Indoor exposure at early life stage to biomass smoke, containing various kinds of VOCs, was associated with reduced lung function parameters including a 225mL reduction in mean FEV130, which may lead to a higher risk of respiratory symptoms, exacerbations of respiratory diseases, and higher all-cause mortality later in life. However, the contribution of impaired lung function on the association between VOC exposure and pulmonary morbidity and mortality was rarely quantified.

Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to fill the knowledge gap by employing the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from 2007 to 2012 cycles. We hypothesize that VOCs mixture as well as individual VOCs are associated with alterations in lung function parameters and there is a potential mediating role of altered lung function on the association between VOCs exposure and respiratory morbidity and mortality risk.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES study is an ongoing cross-sectional nationwide survey conducted by the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The study utilizes a multistage probability sampling method to select individuals from the noninstitutionalized U.S. population. The cross-sectional dataset cover variables including nutrition, general health, disease history, and health behaviors on a population-wide scale. Data from the three NHANES survey cycles (i.e., 2007–2008, 2009–2010, and 2011–2012) were employed for the analysis because the lung function parameters were assessed during these three cycles. As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 30,442 participants from the three survey cycles were included. We excluded individuals who were younger than 18 years old (n = 11,823), had missing data regarding blood VOCs (n = 9618)31, lacked lung function data (n = 1878)32, or had forced expiratory volume at 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) quality codes below B (n = 967). After excluding 24,286 participants (Table S1), the final number of participants included in the analysis was 6,156.

Measurement of blood VOCs

Blood samples were collected from a sub-sample of NHANES participants, and blood levels of VOCs were determined by gas chromatography (GC) and mass spectrometry (MS) with selected-ion monitoring (SIM) detection and isotope-dilution33. In the NHANES database, we used the 2007–2008 cycle as a baseline that included 39 VOCs measured in the blood samples. Then we excluded three VOCs from the 39 when they were not assessed in all three cycles (e.g., 1,2-dibromo-3-chloropropane, styrene, and toluene). To balance data completeness with quality, we further excluded VOCs only when their median levels were below the LLOD in both sexes. This criterion eliminated twenty-nine VOCs (e.g., 2,5-Dimethylfuran, 1,1-Dichloroethane), ensuring analyses focused on reliably quantifiable compounds (Table S2)34. In the NHANES data, values below the LLOD are reported as the LLOD divided by the square root of 2 (LLOD/√2). Finally, seven VOCs were included in the analysis (e.g., 1,4-dichlorobenzene, o-xylene, m-/p-xylene, bromodichloromethane, chloroform, dibromochloromethane, nitromethane).

Assessment of lung function, pulmonary diseases, and mortality outcomes

Lung function data were obtained through spirometry measurements recommended by the American Thoracic Society, with the National Institute of Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) Division of Respiratory Disease Studies serving as the quality control advisor. According to NHANES, technicians received formal training and successfully completed the NIOSH-approved spirometry course35. The pulmonary diseases primarily involved in this study are asthma, emphysema and chronic bronchitis. These disease diagnoses were reported at baseline without subsequent follow-up information, the pulmonary disease outcomes in our analysis represent cross-sectional conditions rather than incident events. Diagnosis was based on whether doctors or other healthcare professionals had informed individuals about having these diseases, as indicated in the questionnaire. Vital status information of participants was integrated with mortality certificates obtained from the National Death Index (NDI) within the NHANES data36. The mortality data employed in this study is current as of December 31, 2019.

Covariates

Gender (female/male) and age were included in the demographic covariates. Race was classified as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other race. Education level was dichotomized as below high school and high school or above (the minimum milestone for stable employment, welfare eligibility, and health insurance). Annual household income was classified as less than $20,000 and greater than or equal to $20,000 (approximating the U.S. federal poverty line and associated with limited healthcare access). Covariates regarding lifestyle included smoking status (yes/no), alcohol use in the past year (yes/no), and body mass index (kg/m2, < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, ≥ 30).

Statistical analysis

In the description of demographic characteristics for all participants, continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are reported as counts and weighted percentages. Weighted percentages were calculated using the NHANES interview sampling weight (WTINT2YR) to account for the complex, multistage probability sampling design.

Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) was employed as the statistical method to characterize the association between VOCs and lung function parameters, specifically FEV1 and FVC. Prior to modeling, the levels of VOCs underwent a standardization process to ensure uniformity and facilitate meaningful comparisons. BKMR, a Bayesian statistical technique, allows for a flexible and data-driven exploration of potential non-linear relationships between VOC exposures and lung function outcomes37,38. This approach takes into account the complex interplay of multiple VOCs and their collective impact on respiratory health, providing a comprehensive understanding of the associations in the study population.

To validate the association between VOCs and lung function, we employed the quantile g-computation (qgcomp) model to estimate the joint effects of incremental increases in individual quantiles of VOCs. Additionally, the qgcomp model was employed to calculate the weights of each VOC in the mixture. The aforementioned models are all subjected to additional adjustments for covariates, encompassing demographic and socioeconomic factors, as well as lifestyle variables.

Next, we established a theoretical mediation pathway, VOCs - lung function - pulmonary diseases. We utilized data to assess the established pathway, aiming to validate whether the mediation effect of lung function in the association between VOC levels and lung function parameters holds statistical significance. This approach decomposes the total estimated effects of VOCs on the pulmonary diseases into an estimate of the direct effect and an estimate of the indirect effect of VOCs on the pulmonary disease mediated by lung function. To allow for a comparable analysis, we standardized the data of exposure (VOCs), mediator (lung function), and outcomes (pulmonary diseases) by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. Two models have been fitted using the generalized linear model as described above. The generalized linear model was employed with adjustment for covariates including age, sex/gender, race, educational level, income, body mass index, cigarette smoking, alcohol use. The first model (X → M) is to assess the association between exposure to VOCs (X) and lung function (M). The second model (X + M → Y) is to assess the link between lung function and pulmonary diseases (Y) while also considering VOCs (X). We conducted mediation analyses using functions implemented in the R package ‘mediation’, employing Bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations to estimate indirect effects. This resampling technique allows for robust estimation of the distribution of the mediated effects and enhances the reliability of our statistical inferences.

Subsequently, a Cox Proportional-Hazards Regression model was employed to estimate the Hazard Ratio (HR) in relationship between VOCs and survival outcomes. We additionally conducted a mediation analysis examining the role of lung function parameters between VOCs exposure and mortality. To allow for a comparable analysis, log transformation was applied to the VOCs data showing skewed distribution. Due to the disparate frameworks between linear models and survival analysis models, a Sobel test was utilized for the mediation analysis in this context39.

Statistical analysis was conducted in R version 4.3.0, utilizing functions from the survey package to accommodate the intricate design employed in the construction of the NHANES survey. P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

From the results of BKMR, taking the 50th percentile as a reference for multiple exposures, increasing concentration of mixture VOCs was associated with reduced FVC and FEV1 levels in NHANES participants. A stronger negative correlation was observed in participants with VOC concentration exceeding the median value (Fig. 2). We further investigated the contribution of key VOCs to the combined effects on lung function. The concentrations of these individual VOC were also fixed at the 75th, 50th, and 25th percentiles to observe dose-dependent associations between VOC levels and lung function changes (Fig. 3). We found that, among the seven VOCs, only nitromethane and 1,4-dichlorobenzene were significantly associated with decreased lung function measurements. For instance, compared with the 0th percentile of nitromethane concentration, FEV1 was estimated to decrease 0.105 L (95% CI − 0.134, − 0.075), 0.110 L (95% CI − 0.137, − 0.083), 0.114 L (95% CI − 0.141, − 0.087) at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. Similarly, FVC was estimated to decrease 0.121 L (95% CI − 0.152, − 0.091), 0.126 L (95% CI − 0.155, − 0.098), 0.122 L (95% CI − 0.150, − 0.093) at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively.

According to the qgcomp model, an interquartile range (IQR) increase in the VOC mixture is associated with a discernible decline in lung function (Fig. S1). Specifically, exposure to a mixture of VOCs was associated with declines in both FVC (β = − 0.177, P < 0.001) and FEV1 (β = − 0.269, P < 0.001). Among the VOCs, nitromethane contributes most negative weights to the association between VOC exposure and lung function parameters (Fig. S2).

Given that prolonged exposure to VOCs was potentially linked to increased risk of chronic respiratory diseases, we assessed the associations between blood levels of mixture VOCs and respiratory morbidity and mortality risk. We observed that exposure to mixture VOCs was associated with elevated risk of asthma, chronic bronchitis, and death due to chronic lower respiratory diseases (Tables S3 and S4). We further investigated whether VOCs contribute to the occurrence of chronic respiratory diseases through impairing lung function. Our results suggest that a portion of the association of nitromethane and 1,4-dichlorobenzene exposure with chronic respiratory diseases can be explained by the impaired lung function (Table 2). For instance, changes in FVC accounted for the indirect effect of nitromethane on asthma [adjusted hazards ratio, 95% confidence interval: 0.004 (0.002, 0.005), P < 0.001, proportion = 12.22%], emphysema [0.001 (0.001, 0.002), P < 0.001, proportion = 29.76%], and chronic bronchitis [0.003 (0.002, 0.005), P < 0.001, proportion = 17.34%]. Changes in FEV1 accounted for the indirect effect of nitromethane on asthma [0.009 (0.006, 0.013), P < 0.001, proportion = 32.34%], emphysema [0.002 (0.001, 0.003), P < 0.001, proportion = 73.29%], and chronic bronchitis [0.005 (0.003, 0.007), P < 0.001, proportion = 25.21%]. Changes in FVC accounted for the indirect effect of 1,4-dichlorobenzene on asthma [0.0004 (0.0001, 0.0007), P = 0.008, proportion = 43.41%], emphysema [0.0001 (0.000, 0.0002), P = 0.004, proportion = 20.60%], and chronic bronchitis [0.0003 (0.0001, 0.0006), P < 0.001, proportion = 20.25%]. Changes in FEV1 accounted for the indirect effect of 1,4-dichlorobenzene on asthma [0.0008 (0.0002, 0.0014), P = 0.004, proportion = 96.10%], emphysema [0.0002 (0.000, 0.0003), P = 0.004, proportion = 21.88%], and chronic bronchitis [0.0004 (0.0001, 0.0007), P = 0.012, proportion = 23.43%].

We further investigated the association between concentrations of nitromethane and 1,4-dichlorobenzene exposure and mortality risk (Table S5), particularly focusing on their potential impact on all-cause mortality and chronic lower respiratory disease mortality through mediating effects on lung function. Among the eight “statistically significant” mediation effects, six results showed inconsistent directions between direct and indirect effects, constituting possible suppression40. Here, we only reported the coefficients of the indirect effects [estimates (95% CI)] with significant mediation statistics (P < 0.05) that showed the same directional changes as direct effects. As shown in Table 3, during a median follow-up of 9.83 years [IQR 8.25, 11.17], we observed the indirect effect of nitromethane on all-cause mortality via FVC [0.042 (0.018, 0.066), P < 0.001, proportion = 23.13%] or FEV1 [0.054 (0.029, 0.079), P < 0.001, proportion = 27.89%].

Discussion

In this study, we observed significant associations between blood levels of VOC mixture and reduced pulmonary function parameters as well as increased risk of respiratory morbidity and mortality. Moreover, the impaired lung function may play a mediating role in the correlation between VOCs exposure and the incidence of chronic respiratory diseases and mortality in the NHANES participants.

Our research revealed that a dose-dependent association between exposure to a VOC mixture and lower lung function parameters, showing possible respiratory health impacts of elevated levels of VOC exposure. A decade-long cohort study involving 159 adults in the Nordic region also found that VOCs may be associated with increased asthma risk and decreased lung function41. Additionally, among the seven VOCs, individual analysis of each VOC showed that only blood levels of nitromethane and 1,4-dichlorebenzene were significantly associated with decreased lung function parameters, indicating that these two VOCs may be the main drivers of the observed association. Previous studies also indicated these two chemicals may possess significant pulmonary toxicity. For instance, using NHANES data collected between 1988 and 1994, an exploratory investigation found that exposure to 1,4-dichlorobenzene may be associated with decreased lung function parameters (e.g., FEV1, maximum mid-expiratory flow rate)34. Specifically focusing on nitromethane, our study found a significant association between its blood levels and reduced lung function. Critically, lung function alterations were found to significantly mediate the associations between nitromethane and respiratory disease outcomes, highlighting its potential role in disease pathways. Nitromethane is a colorless and flammable polar liquid that may be released into the environment during its production or through its use as a fuel additive42. This release can occur into the atmosphere, and human exposure may occur through inhalation, skin contact or accidental ingestion. On the other hand, halonitromethanes can form as byproducts during water disinfection processes. These compounds have demonstrated potent toxicity to mammals43,44. In experiments involving nitromethane inhalation exposure in mice, the incidence of cellular infiltration in lung tissue was significantly higher compared to the control group. Moreover, the occurrence of alveolar/bronchiolar lesions in the exposed mice was markedly elevated compared to the controls45. A longitudinal study based on 154 individuals aged 60 years and older in South Korea found that toluene and xylene exacerbated oxidative stress, thereby exerting harmful effects on lung function46. Notably, VOC levels in our participants were comparable to those reported in a Canadian cohort, where the median blood concentration of m-/p-Xylene was 0.064 ng/mL versus 0.071 ng/mL in our study, suggesting consistent exposure levels across regions22.

The impairment of lung function by VOCs leading to the occurrence of respiratory diseases and even death is also a widely recognized concern. Chronic respiratory diseases, including chronic bronchitis, emphysema, and asthma are categorized into non-communicable diseases characterized by a continuous increase in both incidence and mortality rates, imposing a significant global disease burden20,47. Environmental and occupational inhalation of airborne toxic substances was linked to the occurrence and prognosis of respiratory disorders48–50. Lung function reductions are the immediate symptom phenotypes in response to VOC exposures mediated by vagal sensory nerves and may be indicative of future occurrence and prognosis of respiratory diseases. It is possible that the activated vagal sensory nerves by exposure to VOCs may also lead to respiratory inflammation, ultimately causing respiratory diseases, exacerbating existing pulmonary conditions, and increasing respiratory mortality risk27,51,52. In an experiment involving the exposure of two rat strains to VOCs, pulmonary septal cell apoptosis and emphysema accompanied by substantial microvascular endothelial damage were observed53. Similar to our findings, several research reports have indicated an association between VOCs and chronic respiratory diseases. These studies have uncovered varying degrees of associations between VOCs and significant correlations with asthma inflammation20,54,55. Therefore, the impairment of lung function is not only a consequence of VOCs exposure but also a potential risk factor for the development of chronic respiratory diseases and mortality.

This study possesses several strengths. It encompasses a large and representative sample of the U.S. population, utilizing objective and high-quality data collected through the NHANES survey. Additionally, we implemented rigorous control measures for various covariates, including demographic, socio-economic, health status, and lifestyle factors, aiming to mitigate potential confounding effects. However, this study is subject to certain limitations. Due to the cross-sectional nature of NHANES data, it precludes the assessment of temporal relationships and the establishment of causal connections. While data on blood VOC metabolites for analysis are available throughout NHANES cycles, pulmonary function measurements were only conducted from 2007 to 2012. Despite adjusting for several confounding factors, the potential influence of other unaccounted confounders cannot be ruled out. Additionally, given the observed associations between VOC exposure and respiratory outcomes were made in the U.S. population, these findings may not be generalizable other populations.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that mixture concentrations of blood VOCs were associated with reductions in lung function parameters, including FVC and FEV1 as well as elevated risk of respiratory morbidity and mortality. Among the various VOCs, nitromethane was related to higher risk of chronic respiratory diseases and mortality through impaired lung function. The results of this study indicated that potential respiratory health effects of VOC exposure and future research attention should be directed to mitigation.
