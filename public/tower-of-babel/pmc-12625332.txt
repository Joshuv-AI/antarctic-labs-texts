Academic procrastination among Sudanese medical students through the lens of gender: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Khadija A Khalil, Weam Mohamed Meargni Ahmed, Ahmed Balla M Ahmed, Nashwa Awad Eltom, Mohamed H Elbadawi, Mohaned Ali Bala Babiker, Abozaid Abdalbasit A Alshafee, Mashair Mohamed Gismelseed, Sojood Hassan Abdalla Mohamed, Alaa T Omer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic procrastination (AP) refers to the intentional delay of academic tasks despite knowing its negative impact. It is considered a prevailing phenomenon among medical students. Numerous discussions have explored the potential influence of gender on this behavior. This study aimed to assess the effect of gender differences on academic procrastination among Sudanese medical students.MethodsThis analytical, institutional based cross-sectional study was conducted in five major public Sudanese universities. Convenience sampling was employed to recruit participants. The Academic Procrastination Scale (APS) was used to assess academic procrastination after obtaining informed consent from participants. The questionnaire was distributed online via Google Forms across various social media platforms. A histogram illustrated gender differences in APS scores, while Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis H test examined variations across socio-demographic groups. Hierarchical linear regression analyzed gender’s effect on AP, controlling for other socio-demographic factors. A significance level of 0.05 was applied. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.ResultsThis study included 386 participants, with 68.9% from the University of Khartoum. The median academic procrastination score was 71.5 (22.3). Gender was not a significant predictor of academic procrastination (p-value = 0.404), even after adjusting for other socio-demographic factors (p-value = 0.162). Married students showed significantly higher procrastination levels compared to single or engaged students (p-value = 0.024). Employment status and income were also significant predictors in the linear regression analysis (p-value = 0.039 and p-value = 0.012, respectively).ConclusionThis study found that gender was not a significant factor in academic procrastination among Sudanese medical students. Socio-economic factors, particularly employment status and income, had a greater influence. Interventions should focus on addressing these broader issues rather than gender-specific strategies.Keywords: Academic procrastination, Gender, Medical students, Sudan

Background

Academic procrastination (AP) refers to the voluntary delay of study-related activities despite knowing its negative consequences [1]. Studies have shown that procrastination in academic contexts can lead to several adverse outcomes, such as lower academic grades, reduced self-efficacy, decreased self-esteem, and negative physical health effects [2]. Furthermore, procrastination is often associated with emotional challenges, including shame, sorrow, anxiety, and depression [3, 4]. It is a pervasive issue, observed in many educational settings, workplace and online learning environments [5, 6], and is commonly experienced in everyday life.

While some individuals, known as chronic procrastinators, consistently delay tasks across various aspects of their lives, others may only procrastinate in specific contexts [7]. Previous studies have reported that chronic procrastination affects 80–95% of college students and 15–25% of adults [8]. AP can manifest in two distinct forms: active procrastination, where students intentionally delay tasks to work more effectively under time pressure, and passive procrastination, where students fail to complete their assignments due to inactivity [9].

AP is often linked to factors such as laziness, poor time management skills [10], emotional distress [11], and anxiety during academic tasks and exam preparation [12, 13]. It is also associated with external factors, including problematic internet usage [14]. Given its negative impact, AP is generally considered as a dysfunctional academic behavior. Fear of failure is a central contributing factor for AP, it has been reported to account for nearly 50% of procrastination cases. This fear is often driven by high performance expectations, overestimation of task difficulty, low self-confidence, and pessimistic outlooks on success [15, 16].

Although various interventions have explored the AP, there are restricted studies focusing on the effectiveness of such interventions [17]. Certain teaching strategies used by college instructors have been found to target and reduce procrastination among students, suggesting that procrastination can be understood from both situational and developmental perspectives [18, 19]. Given that AP impacts both individuals and society [20], it is crucial for both institutions and procrastinators themselves to address the issue.

However predicting the correlation between gender and academic procrastination remains a debated topic [21]. Some studies have suggested that male university students procrastinate more than their female counterparts [22–24], while others have found that females tend to procrastinate more [25, 26]. Other research has indicated no significant gender-based differences in academic procrastination [27, 28].

Despite the widespread nature of procrastination, there were limited studies focusing on addressing academic procrastination among medical students in Sudan. Therefore, this study aims to explore the impact of gender on academic procrastination, as well as examine how other socio-demographic factors influence procrastination levels among Sudanese medical students.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted between January 12, 2024 and March 25, 2024, in five major Sudanese medical faculties affiliated with Khartoum, Al Neelain, Sudan University of Science and Technology, Alzaiem Alazhari, and Omdurman Islamic Universities. These faculties, located in Khartoum State, are among the largest in Sudan. The study’s methodology and findings are thoroughly presented in the manuscript, adhering to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [29].

Study population and sample

We targeted medical students enrolled in the medical colleges of the five Sudanese universities.

Inclusion criteria

We included medical students aged 18 years or older at the targeted universities and willingness to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals who were unwilling to participate or did not provide informed consent were excluded.

The final sample exceeded the minimum required sample (n = 386 vs. 384). This high response rate was facilitated by the use of online distribution, ensuring adequate participation.

Given the lack of previous data on the specific prevalence of procrastination among Sudanese medical students, a conservative estimate of 50% is used to ensure an adequate sample size for capturing potential trends and variations. Convenience sampling technique was employed due to its accessibility, feasibility, and easier to conduct during the current armed conflict in Sudan. The Sudan conflict began in April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces and Rapid Support Forces. It resulted in over 12.5 million displaced people and caused widespread internet and power outages [31].

Data collection tools and procedures

We used the Academic Procrastination Scale (APS) to assess the academic procrastination level among Sudanese medical students [32]. This scale is widely recognized and provides a comprehensive framework for understanding procrastination in academic settings. It has been used in similar contexts, including a study among university students in Brazil to evaluate procrastination levels among undergraduates [33]. The scale contains 25 items covering six conceptual areas of academic procrastination: laziness, distraction, beliefs about abilities, time management, social factors, and individual initiative, answering on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree). After consideration of the reverse coding of some items (items 1, 8, 12, 14 and 25), the total APS scale was calculated by summation of individual scores. Higher scale indicated higher level of procrastination. The scale was used as a continuous measure without categorizing levels of procrastination, as the scale does not provide predefined cut-off scores. Therefore, the total scores were described using descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range) to represent the overall level of procrastination in the sample. It has been piloted and validated before with a Cronbach alpha value of 0.95, indicating good reliability [32]. To identify potential factors linked to participants’ procrastination level, data on demographic characteristics such as gender, age, marital status, residence, living accommodation, employment status, and monthly income was gathered.

Because of the ongoing conflict, we were unable to employ field data collectors and instead distributed self-administered anonymous questionnaires online through Google Forms and shared them via various social media platforms such as: Telegram, WhatsApp, Facebook, and Linkedin.

Data analysis plan

After data collection, data were entered into excel sheets, then cleaned and organized by SPSS version 26. Normality was assessed for age and academic procrastination scale which yielded non normal distribution (p-value<0.05).

Frequency tables and descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range) were used to represent the data. Histogram was used to present the difference between males and females regarding academic procrastination scale. Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis H test, and Spearman’s rank correlation test were used to identify the differences across socio-demographic groups regarding academic procrastination scale. Hierarchical multiple linear regression was used to study the effect of gender in academic procrastination when other socio-demographic characteristics have been controlled. Variables with p-value less than 0.25 were used to enter the model to avoid dropping important adjustment variables [34]. In all tests the level of significance was 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic data

This study included 386 Sudanese medical students. The median age was 22 [3]. The majority of the participants (63.7%) were females, with the University of Khartoum being the most affiliated university among the sample (66.1%). Almost 95% of the students were single. The highest percentage of students (32.9%) were from the third academic year. Most of the students were not employed (93.3%), while 135 students (35.0%) had family income below 50,000 SDGs (almost 20 US $). More than four fifths of the students (83.7%) were living with their families (Table 1).

Married students tend to be significantly more procrastinators than singles and engaged (median = 80.0, p-value = 0.024). There was no association with age, academic year, income, employment status, or university (Table 3).

Discussion

This study explored the effect of medical students’ gender differences in academic Procrastination. Based on our findings, gender was not a significant contributor to academic procrastination, even though males showed slightly higher scores. The absence of significant gender differences in academic procrastination aligns with some previous studies but contrasts with others. For instance, some researches have reported higher procrastination tendencies among males [20, 22, 25], often attributing this to differences in motivation and time management between genders. However, other studies, similar to our study, have found no significant gender differences [21, 27, 35], suggesting that procrastination may be more influenced by individual traits or external factors rather than gender alone. This inconsistency in findings across different studies indicates that the relationship between gender and procrastination may be context-dependent, possibly varying based on cultural, academic, or environmental factors. In the Sudanese context, shifting gender roles and increased female participation in higher education [36] may contribute to minimizing traditional differences in academic behaviors.

The Academic Procrastination Scale (APS) used in this study captured a range of procrastination behaviors among Sudanese medical students, indicating a moderate overall tendency to procrastinate. Although we did not find a specific cutoff point in the literature to define high or low levels of procrastination, the scale nonetheless highlighted key patterns in student behavior. For instance, some students manage time effectively, while others face challenges with distractions and time management. These findings suggest a mixed level of academic engagement, which could inform interventions to reduce procrastination.

Interestingly, marital status emerged as a significant factor associated with procrastination, with married students showing higher levels of procrastination compared to their single or engaged peers. This may be explained by prior findings indicating that married students often face both emotional and practical challenges while balancing family responsibilities with academic demands. Tasks such as childcare, household management, and emotional support can reduce academic focus and increase stress [37], potentially leading to greater procrastination. However, this finding contrasts with another study that reported higher levels of procrastination among single males [38], suggesting that the relationship between marital status and procrastination may vary depending on context and population. The hierarchical linear regression further confirmed that gender was not significant after controlling for socio-demographic factors, reinforcing that gender alone does not influence procrastination levels.

Employment status was associated with lower academic procrastination, which may reflect that students who work develop greater time-management skills and structured routines that mitigate procrastination. A study among students in Nigeria similarly found that employed students exhibited both the lowest and highest levels of procrastination compared to unemployed and self-employed students, reflecting the diverse challenges they face [39]. Conversely, higher monthly income was linked to increased procrastination, possibly because students with greater financial resources experience less immediate pressure to complete academic tasks or may allocate time to non-academic activities. These findings highlight that both work commitments and economic resources play nuanced roles in procrastination behaviors, emphasizing the importance of considering contextual and socio-economic factors in interventions targeting academic procrastination.

Based on our results, interventions to reduce academic procrastination among Sudanese medical students should consider socio-economic factors, particularly employment and income. For instance, supporting employed students in balancing work and study demands, and providing guidance for students with higher income who may be more prone to procrastination, could help mitigate delays in academic tasks. Additionally, tailored support for married students, who may face unique time and family pressures, may further enhance these efforts.

The strength of this study lies in its inclusion of students from five major public medical universities, which enhances the representativeness of the sample and provides broader insight into academic procrastination among Sudanese medical students.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it lacks a clear cutoff point for categorizing levels of academic procrastination, which would have provided deeper insights into variations in procrastination behaviors across the sample. Although the Academic Procrastination Scale is widely used and offers a comprehensive framework for understanding procrastination in academic contexts [33], it requires further refinement to establish precise thresholds for classifying students as low, moderate, or high procrastinators. Additionally, the study was limited to a specific sample of Sudanese medical students and relied on a non-probability convenience sampling method, restricting the generalizability of the findings to a broader student population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found no significant gender differences in academic procrastination among Sudanese medical students, highlighting the greater influence of socio-economic factors. Efforts to reduce procrastination should prioritize addressing these broader challenges rather than implementing gender-specific approaches. Universities should consider implementing structured time management training and procrastination reduction programs tailored to students’ socio-economic contexts. Future researches should focus on defining cutoff points to differentiate levels of procrastination and investigate their relationship with academic performance and other outcomes. Expanding studies to include a diverse range of students and a broad population beyond medical fields and employing more robust sampling techniques are essential. Moreover, future studies should explore intervention strategies to effectively address academic procrastination.
