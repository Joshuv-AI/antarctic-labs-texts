Refining Western Dementia-Risk Paradigms: Evidence From a Decade of the Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia
Authors: Ji Won Han, Dae Jong Oh, Tae Hui Kim, Kyung Phil Kwak, Bong Jo Kim, Shin Gyeom Kim, Jeong Lan Kim, Seok Woo Moon, Joon Hyuk Park, Seung-Ho Ryu, Jong Chul Youn, Dong Young Lee, Dong Woo Lee, Seok Bum Lee, Jung Jae Lee, Jin Hyeong Jhoo, Ki Woong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the most rapidly aging nation, Korea offers a unique lens into the future of dementia, one of the most pressing global health challenges of the 21st century. The Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia (KLOSCAD), launched amid the country’s rapid demographic transition, exemplifies how population-specific research can guide national policy and challenge universal assumptions in dementia science. More than a cohort study, KLOSCAD provides a methodological blueprint for identifying culturally relevant risk factors, testing the applicability of Western models, and shaping prevention strategies for local contexts. Its evolution from a national study to a globally recognized platform reflects the value of sustained governmental support, strategic design, and international collaboration. As the dementia epidemic’s center shifts to Asia, the KLOSCAD experience underscores that diversity is not a confounder but a scientific opportunity, critical for building both universal and context-sensitive approaches to understanding and preventing dementia worldwide.Keywords: Dementia, Cognitive Dysfunction, Longitudinal Studies, Risk Factors, Epidemiology, Korea

INTRODUCTION

South Korea exemplifies the future that awaits many nations; a society aging at unprecedented speed. The country’s transformation from an aging society (7% of population ≥ 65 years) to a super-aged society (20%) will span merely 25 years (2000–2025), compared to 50–100 years in Western nations.1 By 2050, dementia cases are projected to reach 3 million, quadrupling from 2017 levels.2 This compressed timeline offers both a warning and an opportunity—a natural experiment in how rapidly changing societies confront cognitive aging.

The urgency of this demographic shift coincided with a growing recognition that dementia research suffered from a critical blind spot. While landmark Western cohorts like the Framingham Heart Study,3 Rotterdam Study,4 and UK Biobank5 provided invaluable insights over decades, their findings were being applied globally without adequate consideration of population differences. Asian cohorts, when they existed, were often smaller, shorter in duration, or limited in scope.

Against this backdrop, the Korean government declared “War Against Dementia” in 2008, culminating in the 2011 Dementia Management Act.6 The Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia (KLOSCAD) emerged from this national commitment, launching in 2009 with support from the Korean Health Industry Development Institute.7 Initially conceived to address four pragmatic aims—estimating dementia epidemiology, exploring diagnostic markers, proposing prevention strategies, and informing policy—KLOSCAD evolved into something more profound: a lens through which to examine whether dementia science's fundamental assumptions hold across diverse populations.

Here, we synthesize a decade of research from KLOSCAD, demonstrating how its methodological innovations and paradigm-shifting discoveries provide a blueprint for dementia research in rapidly aging non-Western populations, fundamentally demonstrating that dementia risk arises from complex gene–environment–culture interactions that require population-specific models.

THE KLOSCAD STUDY: METHODOLOGICAL INNOVATIONS

Design and sampling strategy - addressing the urban-rural divide

Most distinctive methodological innovation of KLOSCAD lies in its differential urban-rural sampling strategy, employing 10% sampling in urban areas and 20% in rural areas. This design is unique among all reviewed international cohorts and directly addresses systematic biases observed across four predominant sampling paradigms in dementia research (Table 2). Western cohorts demonstrate heavy reliance on administrative infrastructure that, while enabling systematic recruitment, inherently limits geographic scope. Studies like the Rotterdam Study4 and the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging8 achieve systematic recruitment through population registers or medical records but remain geographically confined to single metropolitan areas. The UK Biobank exemplifies both the potential and limitations of this approach, achieving unprecedented scale (n = 502,617) through NHS registry postal invitations, yet suffering from only 5.5% response rate that creates severe healthy volunteer bias with mortality rates 46–55% lower than population norms.5

Japanese cohorts such as the Hisayama Study9 have adopted a total enumeration strategy, conducting complete community surveys that achieve participation rates exceeding 80%. However, these studies sacrifice national representativeness for local depth, remaining confined to single towns and limiting their generalizability to broader Japanese populations. Meanwhile, other Asian cohorts demonstrate an even more problematic pattern of urban-centric convenience sampling. The Chinese Longitudinal Aging Study (CLAS) samples only four cities despite China’s vast rural population where dementia burden may be highest due to lower education and limited healthcare access.10 Similarly, the Singapore Longitudinal Ageing Study (SLAS) restricts recruitment to public housing estates, systematically excluding both rural areas and private housing residents who may have different risk profiles.11

Specialized US cohorts prioritize specific research objectives over population representation. The National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) aggregates data from 36 Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers, creating a referral-based convenience sample of over 54,000 participants that maximizes clinical heterogeneity but sacrifices population-based sampling.12 The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) takes this specialization further, enrolling approximately 2,000 participants under strict inclusion criteria at over 60 sites to achieve unparalleled biomarker standardization at the cost of demographic diversity.13

Differential sampling strategy of KLOSCAD represents a methodological innovation specifically designed to address these systematic biases. By deliberately oversampling rural areas where traditional risk factors for dementia are concentrated, including lower educational attainment, limited healthcare access, and higher vascular risk burden, KLOSCAD captures the full spectrum of urbanization and its associated health disparities. This approach achieves a rare combination of both national representation and adequate rural coverage.

Multi-center infrastructure and retention strategies - balancing ambition with sustainability

Multi-center infrastructure of the KLOSCAD, comprising 13 research centers across South Korea, represents a significant organizational achievement in Asian dementia research. With 6,818 baseline participants recruited from 12,694 sampled individuals (53.7% participation rate), KLOSCAD occupies a strategic middle ground in the landscape of international dementia cohorts - larger than most Asian studies yet more manageable than Western mega-cohorts (Table 2). This scale and distributed model enable comprehensive geographic coverage while reducing selection bias, contrasting with single-center studies which, while methodologically rigorous, may miss regional variations in dementia risk and expression.

The multi-center approach presents both opportunities and challenges that KLOSCAD has addressed through several innovations. First, the establishment of a central coordinating center implemented extraordinary quality control procedures. All investigators and research staff underwent mandatory 8-hour protocol training and passed 10 standardized case examinations before participating in data collection, exceeding training requirements reported by most international cohorts. Second, KLOSCAD implemented real-time data monitoring systems allowing the coordinating center to identify protocol deviations promptly, tracking key quality indicators including interview duration, missing data patterns, and inter-rater reliability scores across all sites. This contrasts with traditional approaches where data quality issues might only be discovered during periodic audits.7

The biennial assessment schedule aligns with international standards offering optimal balance between capturing meaningful cognitive changes and minimizing participant burden. Despite Korea's rapid urbanization and high residential mobility, KLOSCAD maintained retention rates of 75.2% at first follow-up and 61.3% at second follow-up, with an overall annual dropout rate of 7.88%.7 This performance is comparable to that of many well-established longitudinal cohorts, though it remains higher than exceptional studies such as the Framingham Heart Study offspring cohort (4.3%).3 The systematic tracking of reasons for non-participation, categorized as refusal, institutionalization, illness, or relocation, combined with integration with national databases, enables sophisticated missing data analyses that maintain the validity of longitudinal findings (Fig. 2). Culturally sensitive retention strategies of KLOSCAD included flexible scheduling accommodating agricultural seasons in rural areas, provision of transportation for participants with mobility limitations, and engagement of local community leaders as study ambassadors. The study maintained contact through birthday cards and regular newsletters, practices shown to enhance retention in longitudinal studies.

Triangulated multi-domain assessment - maximizing diagnostic precision and translational impact

A key methodological innovation of the KLOSCAD was its triangulated assessment strategy, evaluating identical functional domains through three complementary perspectives: self-report, informant assessment, and objective clinical evaluation (Table 3). This multi-angle strategy captured discrepancies between perceived and actual deficits, reducing bias from single-source reports and enhancing diagnostic accuracy.

Another strength was the breadth of its multi-domain assessment. Cognitive testing spanned all major domains using 14 standardized neuropsychological measures. Psychiatric evaluation went beyond depression to include neuropsychiatric symptoms and executive dysfunction. Sleep was assessed with validated tools covering sleep quality, REM sleep behavior disorder, sleep apnea, and restless legs syndrome. Functional capacity was measured using culturally validated instruments addressing activities of daily living and dependency. Physical health domains included gait and balance, comorbidity burden, and anthropometry. Lifestyle factors—alcohol, nicotine, diet, physical activity, and social engagement—were evaluated with standardized tools. Notably, KLOSCAD introduced policy-relevant instruments like the Driving Risk Questionnaire.14 This comprehensive approach enabled sophisticated analyses of dementia's multifactorial nature and complex risk factor interactions.

Structured diagnostic tools further strengthened diagnostic reliability. Cognitive disorders were assessed using the Korean version of the CERAD Clinical Assessment Battery,15 while psychiatric disorders were evaluated with the Korean version of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Inventory.16 This structured, dual-domain approach helped differentiate cognitive impairment from psychiatric conditions—a key challenge in geriatric populations—and ensured high diagnostic validity, unlike many epidemiologic studies relying on brief or unstructured assessments.

KLOSCAD's development of Korean-specific brain MRI templates (KNE96 and KNE20017) demonstrates how population-tailored methods enhance research precision. Western templates like MNI or Talairach introduce bias when applied to Asian brains, which exhibit distinct morphologies including relatively larger frontal and smaller occipital regions. Reanalysis using Korean templates increased sensitivity for detecting subtle volumetric changes by 15–20%, especially in subcortical regions critical to dementia progression. Building on this, KLOSCAD adopted texture-based neuroimaging, which detects microstructural changes 2–3 years before volumetric atrophy.18,19 In 449 amyloid-negative individuals, early texture changes in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and orbitofrontal cortex preceded volume loss,19 offering sensitive markers for non-Alzheimer neurodegeneration. These innovations underscore that addressing population heterogeneity in dementia requires not only identifying unique risk factors but also developing population-appropriate tools—from customized brain templates to advanced image analytics.

Comprehensive biobanking and biomarker strategy - from universal collection to targeted innovation

The universal biospecimen collection in KLOSCAD achieved remarkable coverage, with serum and plasma samples obtained from over 80% of participants across all assessment waves, accumulating more than 18,800 paired samples by the fourth follow-up. The DNA repository, containing 9,632 samples, has enabled population-specific genetic discoveries including novel APOE ε4 interaction patterns unique to Korean populations. In addition, strategic subcohort studies demonstrate the innovative approach of KLOSCAD to resource-intensive assessments. The polysomnography subcohort (n = 343) revealed critical associations between sleep microarchitecture and cognitive decline.20 The neuroimaging subcohort, with 413 MRI scans and 249 amyloid PET scans, enabled development of Korean-specific brain templates17,21 and WMH probability maps.22 The gait analysis subcohort validated single-sensor wearable technology in 299 participants, demonstrating that simplified biomechanical assessments can predict cognitive decline with accuracy comparable to laboratory-based systems.23,24

This dual-approach biobanking strategy - comprehensive coverage for scalable markers, targeted depth for resource-intensive assessments - has proven highly productive, generating discoveries that inform both population health strategies and precision medicine approaches. The model demonstrates how cohort studies in resource-conscious settings can achieve scientific excellence through strategic design rather than unlimited resources.

International collaboration: enabling discoveries beyond single cohort capacity

KLOSCAD’s participation in international consortia such as the Cohort Studies of Memory in an International Consortium (COSMIC) and the Global Alzheimer’s Association Interactive Network (GAAIN) demonstrates how multi-cohort collaboration can overcome the statistical limitations of single studies, enabling robust analyses of gene-environment interactions and validation of population-specific risk thresholds. The differential effect of APOE ε4, showing 40% lower dementia risk in East Asians and education-related protection limited to non-carriers, exemplifies how COSMIC’s multi-ethnic collaboration enabled discoveries beyond the reach of single cohorts.25,26

The international collaboration through the Thyroid Studies Collaboration (TSC) provided complementary metabolic insights. The TSC revealed that subclinical thyroid dysfunction's cognitive impact varies significantly across populations. While subclinical hyperthyroidism increased dementia risk in Western populations, this association was attenuated in Asian cohorts including KLOSCAD.27 Furthermore, subclinical thyroid dysfunction increased diabetes risk differentially across age groups and ethnicities, with stronger associations in younger participants—findings that required the statistical power of multiple cohorts to detect.28

Beyond metabolic markers, KLOSCAD also contributed to global neuroimaging advances. In the Lifetime Brain Chart Consortium project,29 analyzing neuroimaging data from over 100,000 individuals across the lifespan, KLOSCAD provided high-quality brain MRI scans of the KNE templates,17,21 representing crucial data from an East Asian population in establishing normative brain aging trajectories across diverse populations - an achievement requiring unprecedented international coordination and standardized imaging protocols.

These biological findings gained further relevance through sociocultural context. COSMIC’s collaborative analyses showed that frequent social contact reduced dementia risk by 40% in individualistic cultures but had minimal impact in collectivistic Asian societies.30 Similarly, risk factor thresholds required population-specific calibration. Population-specific effects were also found for blood pressure management, where optimal ranges for preventing cognitive decline differed between Asian and Western populations.22,31 Educational protection against dementia showed cultural mediation, with stronger effects observed in societies with greater educational disparities.32 Even cognitive decline patterns differed culturally: memory decline progressed more slowly in Asian populations, while executive function deterioration was more rapid in Western populations.25,33

NOVEL RISK FACTORS AND POPULATION-SPECIFIC EFFECTS: INSIGHTS FROM KLOSCAD

The KLOSCAD has uncovered previously unrecognized dimensions of dementia risk while revealing how established risk factors operate differently across populations. These contributions can be organized into three main categories: 1) novel social mechanisms of dementia risk in cultural context, 2) novel endophenotypes and biological pathways of dementia, and 3) population-specific effects of established risk factors. Together, these findings challenge the assumption that dementia risk factors identified in Western populations can be universally applied, highlighting instead the complex interplay between genetic background, cultural practices, and environmental exposures in shaping dementia risk. This body of work underscores the necessity of moving beyond one-size-fits-all prevention strategies toward approaches that integrate universal principles with population-specific insights.

Novel social mechanisms of dementia risk in cultural context

Spousal concordance

One particularly innovative finding concerns the shared risk between spouses. Yang et al.34 discovered that having a cognitively impaired spouse was associated with a 74% increased risk of cognitive decline, with 75% of this shared risk attributed to modifiable factors including physical inactivity, depression, and history of head injury. This bidirectional influence extends beyond the caregiver stress effects documented in Western studies like the Cache County Study,35 suggesting that couples develop synchronized health trajectories through shared lifestyle patterns that begin long before cognitive symptoms appear. Han et al.36 and An et al.37 have extended these observations to show similar patterns for depression and chronic diseases within couples. These findings highlight the need to broaden dementia prevention strategies from an individual-centered model to a dyadic framework. Targeting couples, given the shared and modifiable nature of many risk factors, may enhance the effectiveness and scalability of prevention efforts at the population level.

Emotional social support

The KLOSCAD has also offered nuanced insights into how social support affects cognitive health in ways that diverge from Western findings. While Western studies emphasize the quantity of social connections, such as network size, contact frequency, and social isolation,38,39 Oh et al.40 found that in Korean elderly, the type and quality of support are more influential. Specifically, low emotional support (e.g., empathy, caring, trust) was linked to a 46% increased dementia risk, whereas tangible support (e.g., help with daily tasks or finances) showed no significant association. This distinction, confirmed across multiple Asian cohorts in the COSMIC collaboration, contrasts with Western approaches that often conceptualize social support as a single dimension.30 In collectivistic Asian cultures, where instrumental family support is typically expected, emotional quality appears to play a more critical role in cognitive protection—highlighting how cultural context shapes the mechanisms linking social relationships to brain health. These findings underscore the need for culturally informed approaches to social relationship–based dementia prevention strategies, recognizing that the protective dimensions of support may differ across populations.

Novel endophenotypes and biological pathways of dementia

Homocysteine

One of the most paradigm-shifting discoveries involves homocysteine levels, where KLOSCAD identified a U-shaped relationship with dementia risk. Bae et al.41 found that both low (≤ 8.9 μmol/L) and high (≥ 10.6 μmol/L) homocysteine levels were associated with 4–5 fold increased dementia risk compared to intermediate levels. This challenges decades of Western research, including the Framingham Heart Study, which focused exclusively on elevated homocysteine as a risk factor.42 Notably, 41.2% of participants with dangerously low homocysteine levels were taking vitamin supplements, suggesting potential risks of excessive supplementation and the importance of maintaining optimal methylation balance.41

Sleep architectures

Sleep represents another domain where KLOSCAD has provided novel insights by examining microarchitectural features rather than just duration or efficiency. Suh et al.20 found that shorter NREM/REM cycle duration was associated with increased cognitive decline risk, with average REM duration per cycle showing particularly strong associations. This finding was complemented by Park et al.,43 who identified that pineal gland volume below 70 mm3 predicted REM sleep behavior disorder with high sensitivity and specificity. These structure–function relationships provide new dimensions beyond the traditional sleep measures examined in large Western cohorts such as the Sleep Heart Health Study,44 and Whitehall II study45 which have primarily focused on identifying optimal sleep duration and its associations with dementia risk. While these studies have contributed to establishing population-level associations between sleep quantity and cognitive risk, their reliance on coarse measures like total sleep time and sleep fragmentation has limited their capacity to capture the finer microarchitectural dynamics that may more sensitively reflect early neurodegenerative changes.

Retinal thickness

A large body of epidemiological research, predominantly based on Western population-based cohorts, has established a robust association between vision loss and increased dementia risk,46 yet the biological mechanisms underlying this link have remained largely unclear. While previous studies have linked retinal changes to cognitive impairment mainly in clinical or cross-sectional contexts, KLOSCAD’s longitudinal research in community-dwelling elderly has demonstrated their predictive value years before symptom onset. Kim et al.47 showed that baseline macular retinal nerve fiber layer thickness predicted cognitive decline over 5.4 years. Those in the lowest quartile (< 231 μm) experienced greater cognitive decline, suggesting that subtle retinal neurodegeneration, detectable via routine OCT, may precede clinical symptoms. A follow-up study of cognitively healthy elderly revealed age-related thinning patterns in the ganglion cell–inner plexiform and outer nuclear layers at rates of –1.2 μm/year and –0.6 μm/year, respectively, at 1 mm from the fovea.48 Most recently, KLOSCAD’s genome-wide association study identified FAM124B as a novel susceptibility gene for subfoveal choroidal thickness; functional studies showed that FAM124B inhibition reduced vascular endothelial proliferation.49 Together, these findings suggest that retinal layer alterations reflect shared neurodegenerative processes between the retina and brain, highlighting their potential as biologically meaningful, quantifiable biomarkers for early detection of cognitive decline.

Masticatory function

The KLOSCAD has revealed distinct links between dental health and cognitive function, suggesting mechanisms beyond the inflammatory pathways emphasized in Western research. While Western studies have primarily focused on periodontal inflammation,50,51 KLOSCAD highlights masticatory function as a key factor. In a longitudinal study, Kim et al.52 found that poor posterior mastication (modified Eichner index 2, P = 0.04) predicted conversion to cognitive impairment. Participants who progressed to MCI or dementia had fewer teeth, more removable dentures, and reduced chewing ability. Similarly, Han et al.53 reported that loss of functional dentition increased cognitive impairment risk by 28%. Another analysis showed that older men with frequent chewing—a proxy for masticatory dysfunction—exhibited faster cognitive decline, higher dementia risk, and smaller brain volumes, particularly in regions controlling mastication.54 These findings suggest that beyond inflammation, the dental–cognitive relationship may involve diminished neural stimulation, nutritional compromise, and brain structural changes—mechanisms especially relevant in populations with traditional diets requiring vigorous mastication.

Behavioral changes: driving and gait

Pioneering work from KLOSCAD in behavioral biomarkers has shown that driving patterns and gait characteristics can signal cognitive decline years before clinical symptoms. In driving behavior, elderly men with mild cognitive impairment had a 2.7-fold increase in adverse driving events, occurring an average of 2.3 years before diagnosis.55 Byeon et al.56 further demonstrated that risky behaviors like lane deviation and erratic speed predicted decline even earlier. KLOSCAD also became one of the first cohorts to validate wearable devices for dementia risk assessment. A validation study found that a single tri-axial accelerometer costing under $50 could measure gait parameters typically requiring expensive lab equipment.57 Follow-up research showed that gait variability captured by such devices predicted cognitive decline with accuracy comparable to full neuropsychological testing, and machine learning algorithms enabled population-level screening.24,58 These behavioral markers represent a shift from costly, clinic-based assessments to affordable, real-world tools that can detect early changes in motor and executive function before memory loss emerges.

Dual sensory impairment (DSI)

Recent findings from KLOSCAD also identify DSI—the co-occurrence of hearing and vision loss—as a novel endophenotypic risk factor for cognitive decline. Byeon et al.59 found that DSI was significantly associated with both a twofold increase in dementia prevalence and incidence, while single sensory impairments showed no such associations. These findings suggest that dual sensory loss may disrupt cognitive networks through compounded deprivation, pointing to a non-Alzheimer pathway to dementia. Given global aging trends, the number of individuals living with DSI is projected to exceed 300 million by 2050.60 This projection underscores the urgent need for public health and policy interventions targeting this vulnerable population, particularly in older adults with limited access to sensory rehabilitation.

Population-specific effects of established risk factors

Coffee consumption

One of the most striking findings from KLOSCAD concerns coffee consumption, a factor typically linked to neuroprotection in Western populations. For example, the Hamburg City Health Study found no significant association between coffee intake and WMH volume but reported better white matter microstructural integrity among those consuming 3–4 cups daily.61 Similarly, a French longitudinal study of 641 elderly women found 35% lower WMH volume in those consuming over 3 caffeine units daily.62 In the UK Biobank, coffee intake was inversely associated with total brain and gray matter volume, though not with WMH.63 In contrast, KLOSCAD revealed different patterns among elderly Koreans. Park et al.64 showed that consuming more than 2 cups per day was associated with increased WMH volume especially in women. This divergence may stem from genetic differences in caffeine metabolism: Asian populations have over 25 times the frequency of slow CYP1A2 metabolizers compared to Europeans,65 leading to prolonged caffeine exposure and vascular effects.66 Although coffee’s effect on brain health generally follows a U-shaped curve across populations, KLOSCAD suggests the optimal intake threshold is lower in Asians compared to Western populations. These findings underscore the need for population-specific considerations when defining safe and optimal levels of coffee consumption in dementia prevention guidelines. Incorporating genetic and metabolic diversity into such frameworks may improve their global applicability and effectiveness.

Parity

Reproductive history is another domain where population-specific effects are evident. While Western studies have shown neutral or protective associations between multiparity and cognition, such patterns differ in Asian populations. In the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study, women with ≥ 5 births had a 15% reduced dementia risk compared to those with two births.67 Similarly, UK Biobank study found that higher parity was linked to younger-appearing brains, especially in reward-related regions.68 In contrast, KLOSCAD data found that grand multiparity was associated with a 68% increased AD risk, while incomplete pregnancies were protective.69 These patterns, confirmed across multiple Asian cohorts in the COSMIC collaboration,70 may reflect three key population differences. First, Asians have twice the prevalence of the Px estrogen receptor haplotype71 and significantly lower plasma estrogen levels than Caucasians.72 Second, Asian mothers often lack autonomy during postpartum confinement, facing pressures from extended families, unlike Western women who typically parent independently.73 Third, many Asian women experienced nutritional deprivation during reproductive years, including high anemia and underweight rates, in contrast to better-nourished Western counterparts.74 These findings illustrate how gene-environment-culture interactions can transform multiparity from a protective factor in the West to a risk factor in Asia. While South Korea now has one of the world’s lowest fertility rates, these findings remain relevant for regions with high parity, such as sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and South Asia. As these areas undergo rapid demographic transitions, culturally-sensitive reproductive health strategies must consider long-term cognitive outcomes alongside maternal and child health. These findings emphasize the importance of incorporating reproductive history into dementia prevention strategies in ways that reflect population-specific biological and sociocultural contexts.

General patterns of East–West differences in risk factor associations

Beyond specific risk factors, KLOSCAD studies within the COSMIC consortium have highlighted substantial East-West differences in dementia risk profiles. The association between APOE ε4 and cognitive decline appears consistently weaker in Asian populations, including Koreans, than in Western cohorts,75 while the protective role of education seems stronger in East Asian countries—particularly among women.26 Memory decline progresses more slowly in Asian populations, whereas executive function deterioration is more rapid in Western populations.25,33 Lifestyle factors also show population-specific associations. Plant-based diets are more strongly linked to cognitive protection in Asians, whereas physical activity shows greater benefits in Western groups.76 Social support differs culturally, with family-centered support being more critical in Asian societies and peer networks more influential in Western contexts.30 Vascular risk factors also vary in effect: hypertension is more strongly linked to WMH in Asians, while diabetes is more strongly associated with cognitive outcomes in Western populations.77 These findings underscore the importance of culturally tailored dementia prevention strategies that reflect genetic, environmental, and sociocultural variations across populations.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

While KLOSCAD has significantly contributed to global dementia research over its first decade, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort’s relative ethnic homogeneity allows for focused investigation of culturally specific risk factors but limits generalizability to multi-ethnic populations. This constraint is particularly relevant as global dementia research increasingly values diversity for uncovering mechanisms across genetic and cultural contexts. To address this, KLOSCAD engages in international consortia such as COSMIC, facilitating cross-ethnic comparisons that differentiate universal from population-specific pathways.

The inclusion of participants aged 60 and above, rather than the conventional 65 years, reflects Korea’s demographic shift and enables early identification of risk trajectories. However, it also lowers baseline dementia prevalence, reducing statistical power for some analyses. The eight-year cumulative attrition rate of 38.7%, while comparable to other long-term cohorts like Framingham Heart Study and Rotterdam Study, may introduce survival bias, potentially underestimating associations. Additionally, the lack of a brain donation program limits neuropathological validation of clinical diagnoses—a shared constraint among many Asian cohorts but nonetheless important for elucidating disease mechanisms.

The original KLOSCAD design, launched in 2009, did not fully anticipate later technological advances in epidemiology. While biennial assessments provided valuable longitudinal data, they missed short-term cognitive fluctuations and daily functional changes. Initial lack of linkage with Korea’s national health databases also limited analysis of healthcare use and medication effects. To address these gaps, we initiated KLOSCAD-GO (KLOSCAD-Going On), a 15–20 year extension designed to track long-term disease trajectories in an Asian population. Drawing from successful retention strategies employed in other long-term cohort studies, we have implemented enhanced engagement protocols including regular health feedback sessions, community events, and flexible assessment to minimize attrition. The inclusion of next-generation blood biomarkers, such as phosphorylated tau, neurofilament light chain, and glial fibrillary acidic protein, positions KLOSCAD within global efforts to advance minimally invasive dementia diagnostics.

Perhaps most critically, KLOSCAD-GO will establish Korea's first dementia-focused brain donation program, enabling neuropathological validation of our longitudinal clinical and biomarker data. This initiative, modeled after successful programs such as the Arizona Study of Aging and Neurodegenerative Disorders and the Sydney Brain Bank, will definitively address questions about the biological underpinnings of the unique risk factor profiles observed in our Korean population. Unlike the U.S. and U.K., where systematic brain banking has a long history, brain bank–based dementia research in Asia remains at a relatively early stage. Although recent reports from Japan,78 China,79 and Korea80 have signaled emerging efforts, cultural resistance continues to limit program scale and participation. The KLOSCAD-GO will be culturally adapted to address local attitudes toward brain donation while maintaining international standards for tissue collection and analysis. Through efforts to overcome these feasibility challenges, this initiative aims to build a foundation for more robust neuropathological research infrastructures in Asia.

The digital transformation of KLOSCAD represents another key strategic direction. We are implementing smartphone-based cognitive assessments that can be self-administered between formal evaluations, potentially capturing cognitive fluctuations invisible to biennial testing. This approach will be complemented by passive digital phenotyping through smartphone sensors and wearable devices. These technologies will enable continuous monitoring of gait patterns, sleep quality, social engagement, and daily activity levels, providing an unprecedented window into real-world functional changes. Machine learning algorithms will integrate these multi-modal data streams to develop AI-driven risk prediction models specifically calibrated for Korean older adults. Although multimodal digital phenotyping for dementia risk prediction in real-world settings is an emerging area globally, the development of culturally and linguistically tailored tools for Asian populations remains limited. Overcoming digital illiteracy among older adults will be essential, but this initiative can lay the foundation for population-specific digital assessments and intervention delivery models in Asia.

While KLOSCAD-GO offers unique strengths as a large-scale, population-based cohort, its insights can be further enriched through strategic integration with referral- or clinic-based cohort studies. In Korea, major initiatives such as the Korean Brain Aging Study for the Early Diagnosis and Prediction of Alzheimer's Disease (KBASE),81 Biobank Innovation for Chronic Cerebrovascular and Alzheimer's Disease Studies (BICWALZS),82 and Korea-Registries to Overcome Dementia and Accelerate Dementia Research (K-ROAD)83 provide rich clinical samples, biospecimens, and neuroimaging datasets that can complement KLOSCAD’s strengths in population representativeness and urban–rural coverage. Although referral-based cohorts may introduce sampling bias, their depth of phenotyping and biological characterization can help address KLOSCAD’s inherent limitations. Data harmonization across these cohorts will allow for more comprehensive characterization of dementia’s biological underpinnings specific to Korean and Asian populations.

Looking beyond the current cohort, we are planning KLOSCAD-II, designed to examine a new generation of Koreans born approximately 20 years after our original participants. This cohort will have experienced dramatically different educational opportunities, nutritional environments, and healthcare access throughout their lives, enabling examination of cohort effects on dementia risk. KLOSCAD-II will natively integrate digital assessment tools and comprehensive multi-omics profiling from baseline, positioning it to address emerging questions about gene-environment interactions in the context of rapid societal modernization. The design will also incorporate capacity for embedded intervention trials, facilitating the translation of observational findings into preventive strategies.

CONCLUSIONS

Over its first decade, KLOSCAD has progressed from a nationally focused initiative to a key contributor to global dementia research. Its findings highlight the limitations of applying Western-derived dementia-risk paradigms universally and underscore the need for frameworks that integrate universal principles with population-specific insights.

Beyond its scientific output, KLOSCAD demonstrates how culturally informed, population-based research can reshape the global understanding of dementia risk and prevention. Future priorities include strengthening cross-cohort harmonization with other Korean and international studies, expanding digital and neuropathological infrastructures, and deepening mechanistic investigations of population-specific risk pathways. As Asia emerges as the epicenter of the dementia epidemic, KLOSCAD has the potential to provide a critical foundation for globally relevant, tailored prevention strategies and to inform the refinement of international dementia guidelines to better reflect cultural and ethnic diversity. Sustained governmental and private support will be essential to realize this vision.
