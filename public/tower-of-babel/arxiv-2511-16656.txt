1.
Introduction

We write
G\to_{r}H
if every
r
-colouring of
G
contains a monochromatic copy of
H
. The
r
-
colour size Ramsey number
of a graph
H
, denoted
\widehat{R}_{r}(H)
, is the smallest number of edges in a graph
G
for which
G\to_{r}H
. That is,

\widehat{R}_{r}(H)=\min\{e(G):G\to_{r}H\}.

This is a natural relative of the classical
r
-colour Ramsey number of a graph
H
, denoted
R_{r}(H)
, which is the minimum number of
vertices
in a graph
G
for which
G\rightarrow_{r}H
.

The size Ramsey number was originally introduced by Erdős, Fraudee, Rousseau and Schelp
[
4
]
in the 1970s and has since become a central topic in Ramsey theory (see
[
2
]
). One of the most celebrated results in this line is the 1983 work of Beck
[
1
]
who showed, perhaps surprisingly, that the size Ramsey number of a path is as small as it could possibly be: linear in its number of vertices. That is, he showed that

\widehat{R}_{2}(P_{k})=\Theta(k),

thereby settling a question of Erdős.

While Beck’s work also implies that
\widehat{R}_{r}(P_{k})=\Theta_{r}(k)
, it leaves open the interesting question regarding the dependence of
\widehat{R}_{r}(P_{k})
on the number of colours
r
, a problem which has gained some traction in the community in recent years. To date, the best-known bounds are

\hskip 8.00003ptc_{1}r^{2}k\leqslant\widehat{R}_{r}(P_{k})\leqslant c_{2}r^{2}(\log r)k,

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
,
r
is fixed and
k
is sufficiently large. The upper bound is due to Krivelevich
[
8
]
and the lower bound is due to Dudek and Prałat
[
3
]
, though the implicit constant has been improved by Krivelevich
[
8
]
. In this paper, we determine
\widehat{R}_{r}(P_{k})
up to constant factors.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
r
and
k\geqslant 100\log r
, we have

\widehat{R}_{r}(P_{k})=\Theta\left((r^{2}\log r)\,k\right).

Our approach in this paper is based on a novel randomised edge-colouring strategy that avoids generating large (in a suitable sense) monochromatic connected components.

2.
Proof overview

In this section, we begin by sketching the main idea in the proof, and then go on to describe the setup and some high-level intuition behind our random colouring process.

2.1.
Sketch of main idea

Given a graph
G
with

e(G)\leqslant c_{0}(r^{2}\log r)\,k,

(1)

for some
c_{0}
to be determined and
r,k
sufficiently large, we iteratively find large
P_{k}
-free subgraphs of
G
, assign an unused colour for them and remove them from
G
.

We now motivate a first attempt where we aim to partition
G=H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup\cdots\cup H_{r}
so that for each colour class
i\in[r]
, there is a further partition

V(H_{i})=X_{1}^{(i)}\cup X_{2}^{(i)}\cup\ldots\cup X_{q}^{(i)},

where

q=\frac{3v(G)}{k},\qquad|X_{j}^{(i)}|\leqslant\frac{k}{2},\hskip 8.99994pt\text{and}\hskip 8.99994ptH_{i}=G[X_{1}^{(i)}]\cup\dots\cup G[X_{q}^{(i)}],

for each
i\in[r]
and
j\in[q]
. That is, the only edges in the
i
th colour class
H_{i}
are between vertices from the same component
X_{j}^{(i)}
. This is clearly a monochromatic
P_{k}
-free colouring, as each connected monochromatic component has size at most
k/2
. Here we construct the
i
th colour class
H_{i}
iteratively by considering

G=F_{1}\supset F_{2}\supset\dots\supset F_{r}\supset F_{r+1}=\emptyset,

where
F_{i+1}=F_{i}\setminus H_{i}
for
i\in[r+1]
. Here
V(F_{i})=V(G)
for
i\in[r+1]
, with the union of the
H_{i}
covering the whole graph.

A naïve first attempt might be to uniformly at random partition the vertices of
V(G)
into
q
parts. Each edge would then have probability around
1/q
of being contained in a given
H_{i}
, so
e(H_{i})\approx e(F_{i})/q
. In particular, we would have

e(F_{r+1})=\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)^{r}\simeq\exp\left(-\frac{r}{q}\right)e(G)\simeq e(G)e^{-1},

so even after
r
colours, we still have a constant fraction of the edges left uncoloured (which is not enough for this strategy).

The crucial observation behind this paper is that, in the context of this problem, one can in fact do significantly better. That is, we will be able to remove
P_{k}
-free subgraphs
H_{i}
with
e(H_{i})\gg e(F_{i})/q
. Interestingly, this process of extracting
P_{k}
-free subgraphs will “bootstrap”; in fact, we will be able to extract increasingly denser
H_{i}
as the process runs. We now turn to outline the colouring process where the colour classes are these
P_{k}
-free subgraphs with more edges than a random partition.

(2)

2.3.
Other colouring techniques

We now describe two other colouring techniques which we will use for the clean-up step at the end of each round and to colour the graph
G_{t}
in (
8
).

To apply Lemma
2.1
, we need control on
v(G)
and
\Delta(G)
. This next lemma helps us achieve the required bound on
v(G)
.

Lemma 2.2

(Vizing-type colouring)
.

Let
r
be sufficiently large and
G
be a graph satisfying
e(G)\leqslant(r^{2}\log r)k/56
. Then there exists
G_{0}\subset G
with
v(G_{0})\leqslant(r\log r)k/4
and an
r/3
colouring of
G\setminus G_{0}
containing no monochromatic
P_{4}
.

Proof.

Let
V^{\prime}
be the vertices of degree at most
r/7
. Note that by definition,
G[V^{\prime}]
has maximum degree at most
r/7
, so by Vizing’s theorem, the edges of
G[V^{\prime}]
can be coloured with at most
\lceil r/7\rceil+1<r/6
colours, without containing a monochromatic
P_{3}
.

Next, let
G^{\prime}
be the bipartite graph between
V^{\prime}
and
V(G)\setminus V^{\prime}
. For any
v\in V^{\prime}
, we have
\deg_{G^{\prime}}(v)\leqslant\deg_{G}(v)\leqslant r/7
. The edges of
G^{\prime}
can be coloured with an additional
\left\lceil r/7\right\rceil\leqslant r/6
colours such that for each
v\in V^{\prime}
, all edges incident to
v
have different colours. Each monochromatic connected component is a vertex-disjoint star and is therefore
P_{4}
-free.

Thus, we let
G_{0}=G[V\setminus V^{\prime}]
and it remains to compute the size of
v(G_{0})
. By counting the edges in
G_{0}
, we obtain the desired bound

v(G_{0})\leqslant\frac{2e(G)}{(r/7)}\leqslant\frac{(r\log r)\,k}{4}.\qed

The second colouring technique allows us to remove high-degree vertices from a graph.

Lemma 2.3

(Star-type colouring)
.

Let
k,s\in\mathbb{N}
and
G
be a graph. Then there exists
G_{0}\subset G
and an
s
-colouring of
G\setminus G_{0}
with no monochromatic
P_{k}
, such that
\Delta(G_{0})\leqslant 8e(G)/(ks)
.

Proof.

Let
V^{\prime}\subset V
be the vertices of degree at least
8e(G)/(ks)
and define the subgraph
G^{\prime}=\{e\in E(G):|e\cap V^{\prime}|\geqslant 1\}
to be the edges incident to at least one vertex of
V^{\prime}
. By double-counting the edges of
G^{\prime}
, we have

|V^{\prime}|(8e(G)/(ks))\leqslant 2e(G^{\prime})\leqslant 2e(G)

so that
|V^{\prime}|\leqslant sk/4
.

Now, we take any partition
V^{\prime}=V_{1}^{\prime}\cup\cdots\cup V_{s}^{\prime}
so that
|V_{i}^{\prime}|\leqslant\frac{k}{3}
for all
i\in[s]
. For each edge of
G^{\prime}
, we assign it a colour corresponding to the index of either one of its endpoints. Each colour class is then a vertex-disjoint union of at most
k/3
stars and does not contain a monochromatic
P_{k}
. Therefore, we may set
G_{0}=G[V\setminus V^{\prime}]
with the described colouring.
∎

G_{t}\setminus G_{t}^{\prime}
with at most
56r^{3/4}
colours and no monochromatic
P_{k}
. Now apply Vizing’s theorem to
G_{t}^{\prime}
to obtain a colouring with
\left\lceil r/7\right\rceil
colours that avoids monochromatic
P_{3}
. In the second case, when
\Delta(G_{t})\leqslant r/7
, simply apply Vizing’s theorem as above. In total, for large enough

r/2+r/3+\left\lceil r/7\right\rceil+56r^{3/4}<r

∎

Thus, we have proved our main result Theorem

.

4.
Proof of Lemma
2.1

We now describe the random process we use to find a
P_{k}
-free subgraph with many edges. Given a graph
G
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
2.1
, we first extract a bipartite subgraph with vertex set
A\cup B
such that
e(A,B)
contains a large fraction of the edges
e(G)
. For some
q
to be determined later, we then find partitions

A=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{q}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B=B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{q},

(10)

with the property that for each
i\in[q]
the bipartite induced subgraph
G[A_{i},B_{i}]
of
G
between
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
is
P_{k}
-free. We then define

H=G[A_{1},B_{1}]\cup\cdots\cup G[A_{q},B_{q}]

(11)

to be our desired
P_{k}
-free subgraph. In order to ensure that
e(H)
is large, we find the partitions in (
10
) as follows. We first partition
A=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{q}
by assigning each vertex of
A

to one of the
A_{i}
uniformly at random. Then we define for each
i\in[q]
,

B_{i}=\{x\in B:|N(x)\cap A_{i}|\text{ is maximal among all }i\in[q]\},

(12)

breaking ties arbitrarily. The key here is that, due to the heavy tails of the Poisson distribution,
H
will induce many more edges than if we were to have chosen both of the
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
uniformly at random.

To analyse the number of edges in
H
, we are led to the well-studied “balls-and-bins” problem
[
5
,
12
,
11
]
. Focusing on a single vertex
v\in B
with
\deg_{G^{\prime}}(v)=d
, we obtain the following problem: given
q
bins (the sets
B_{1},\dots,B_{q}
), and
d
balls (
N(v)\cap A
), we throw each ball into one of the
q
bins uniformly and independently at random. How many balls are there in the bin that has the most balls?

Alternatively, given a random function
f\colon[n]\to[q]
, we define

M_{q,n}=\max_{i\in[q]}|f^{-1}(i)|\qquad\text{and}\qquad W(q,n)=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}/n.

(13)

In order to get a feeling for the behaviour of
W(q,n)
, we consider the expected number of bins with at least
t
balls. Indeed, for any
t
, we have

\mathbb{E}\,\big|\big\{i\in[q]:|f^{-1}(i)|\geqslant t\big\}\big|\approx q\cdot\binom{n}{t}q^{-t}\approx q\cdot\left(\frac{n}{qt}\right)^{t}.

Around the value
t=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}
, we expect the above to be
\gg 1
, so
t(\log(tq/n))\approx\log q
. Solving for
t
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}\approx xn/q
, where
x
is the solution to
x\log x=(q\log q)/n
. By the second moment method, we can verify that up to constant factors, this first moment estimate is indeed the right behaviour for
W(q,n)
. This is made precise in the following lemma, the proof of which is deferred to Appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
q>1
and
n,q\in\mathbb{N}
and let
x>1
be the unique solution to
\frac{q\log q}{2n}=x\log x
. Then,
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}\geqslant\frac{xn}{10q}
.

It will be more convenient to apply the following consequence of Lemma
4.1
(see Appendix
A.2
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
q,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
q>1
and
q_{0},n_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 1}
with
q\leqslant q_{0}
and
n\leqslant n_{0}
. Then

W(q,n)\geqslant\frac{\log q_{0}}{120n_{0}\log\left(\frac{q_{0}\log q_{0}}{2n_{0}}+1\right)}.

Additionally, we need the following monotonicity properties of
W(q,n)
. Surprisingly, monotonicity in
q
seems to be non-trivial; we defer the proof to Appendix
A.1
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
q,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
W(q,n)
is monotonically decreasing in
q
and
n
.

We can now calculate the expected number of edges in
H
.

Observation 4.4
.

\mathbb{E}\,e(H)\geqslant e(A,B)\cdot W\left(q,\Delta(G)\right)
.

Proof.

Since a single vertex
v\in B
of degree
d(v)=\deg_{G}(v)
contributes on expectation
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,\hskip 0.81949ptd(v)}
edges to
H
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.99997pte(H)=\sum_{v}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,\hskip 0.81949ptd(v)}=\sum_{v}d(v)\cdot W\left(q,\,d(v)\right)\geqslant e(A,B)\cdot W\left(q,\Delta(G)\right),

where the final inequality follows from the monotonicity in

of

by Lemma

.
∎

To ensure the bipartite graphs

are

-free, we use the following simple observation

|A_{i}|<k/2-1\;\Longrightarrow\;G[A_{i},B_{i}]\not\supset P_{k},

and so we choose

We remark that this sketch differs slightly from what we sketched in Section

Lemma 4.5

(Subgraph finding lemma)

For

Appendix A
Deferred analysis of balls-and-bins

In this appendix, we study the
balls and bins problem
, and we refer the reader to the main body of this paper for a short list of references for this problem.

We begin by briefly recalling the set-up of the problem and our notation for the various random variables. Suppose that we sequentially throw
n
balls into
q
bins by placing each ball into a bin chosen independently and uniformly at random. Here we study the maximum number of balls in a bin, which we denote by
M_{q,n}
. In our application, we are mostly interested in
W(q,n)=\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}/n
. We first establish some monotonicity properties of
W(q,n)
in Section
A.1
and then prove an efficient bound for
\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}
in Section
A.2
, which is tight up to constants.

A.1.
Properties of
W(q,n)

See
4.3

We break the proof of this lemma into two parts.

Proof of monotonicity of
W(q,n)
in
n
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that in the
n
balls and
q
bins, the maximum bin is unique so that exactly one bin attains
M_{q,n}
. First, we claim that

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n-1}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathbb{E}(M_{q,n}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{E})).

To see this, we first fix a realisation of the
n
balls, and note that on the event
\mathcal{E}
, if we remove one of the balls chosen uniformly at random among the
n
balls and if the ball were to be from the maximum bin, then the new maximum drops from
M_{q,n}
to
M_{q,n}-1
; otherwise the maximum stays at
M_{q,n}
. Now, the probability that the ball we removed comes from the unique maximal bin is
\frac{M_{q,n}}{n}
by symmetry, and so conditional on the realisation of the throws, the change in the maximum value after the removal of a ball is
-M_{q,n}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{E})/n
. By averaging over all realisations, it follows that the expected change in maximum value is given by
\mathbb{E}(M_{q,n}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{E}))/n
. On the other hand, the distribution of the balls into the bins after removing a uniformly random ball is basically the same as throwing
n-1
balls into
q
bins. That is, the expected change is
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n-1}
, and equating the two expressions we obtained gives the desired conclusion.

Using the pointwise bound
M_{q,n}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{E})\leqslant M_{q,n}
, this implies that

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n-1}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathbb{E}(M_{q,n}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{E}))\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n},

which rearranges to
W(q,n)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\hskip 0.81949ptM_{q,n}}{n}\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\hskip 0.81949ptM_{q,n-1}}{n-1}=W(q,n-1)
, as desired.
∎

It is surprisingly much more difficult to prove the monotonicity of
W(q,n)
in
q
. As
W(q,n)=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}/n
, we instead prove monotonicity of
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}
in
q
. To do so, we consider the following generalisation of the balls and bins problem, where we throw each ball into bin
i
with probability
p_{i}
for some fixed distribution
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{q})
. Fix some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{q})\sim\mathrm{Mult}(n;\mathbf{p})
and define
M(\mathbf{p}):=\mathbb{E}[\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant q}X_{i}]
. In particular, we have

M\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{q+1},\ldots,\frac{1}{q+1}}_{q+1\text{ times}}\Bigg)=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q+1,n}\text{ and }M\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{1}{q}}_{q\text{ times}},0\Bigg)=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}.

Let
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{q})
,
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}=(p_{1}^{\prime},\dots,p_{q}^{\prime})
be two probability distributions and let
\mathbf{p}^{\downarrow}
and
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}^{\downarrow}
denote the vectors obtained by rearranging the components of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
in non-increasing order, i.e.

p_{1}^{\downarrow}\geqslant p_{2}^{\downarrow}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant p_{q}^{\downarrow},\quad p_{1}^{\prime\downarrow}\geqslant p_{2}^{\prime\downarrow}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant p_{q}^{\prime\downarrow}.

We say that
\mathbf{p}
is
majorised
by
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
, and write
\mathbf{p}\prec\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
, if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\downarrow}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\prime\downarrow}\quad\text{for all }k=1,\dots,q.

Note that the function
M
is symmetric in all entries, in particular
M(\mathbf{p})=M(\mathbf{p}^{\downarrow})
.

We essentially prove that
M
is a Schur-convex function, that is if
\mathbf{p}\prec\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
, then we have
M(\mathbf{p})\leqslant M(\mathbf{p^{\prime}})
. This implies that
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}
is non-increasing in
q
, as

\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{q+1},\ldots,\frac{1}{q+1}}_{q+1\text{ times}}\Bigg)\prec\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{1}{q}}_{q\text{ times}},0\Bigg).

Schur-convexity in a similar setting has been studied by Holst
[
6
]
. They considered the general birthday problem, defined as follows. Given a distribution
\mathbf{p}
, let
N(\mathbf{p})
be the least positive integer such that
\mathbb{P}(\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant q}\text{Mult}(N(\mathbf{p}),\mathbf{p})\geqslant 2)\geqslant 1/2
. They prove that
N(\mathbf{p})
is Schur-concave
[
6
]
; we essentially use the same steps to prove Schur-convexity of
M(\mathbf{p})
.

Next, we define
T-transformations

[
6
]
. We say that
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
is derived from
\mathbf{p}
by a T-transformation if for some
i,j
and
0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1
,

p_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda p_{i}+(1-\lambda)p_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{j}^{\prime}=\lambda p_{j}+(1-\lambda)p_{i}

with the other coordinates of the the vectors equal; that is
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
is obtained from
\mathbf{p}
by some (weighted) average of two coordinates. It is well known that
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}\prec\mathbf{p}
if and only if
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
can be derived from
\mathbf{p}
by a sequence of T-transformations and permutations (see the paper of Marshall and Olkin
[
9
]
).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{q})
be a probability distribution and let
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
be obtained from
\mathbf{p}
by a T-transformation. Then
M(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\leqslant M(\mathbf{p})
.

This lemma essentially follows from the two bins version of the problem.

Claim A.2
.

For any
0\leqslant p<q\leqslant 1/2
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X_{p}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
and
X_{q}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,q)
. Then
\mathbb{E}\max\{X_{p},n-X_{p}\}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\max\{X_{q},n-X_{q}\}
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbb{E}\max\{X_{p},n-X_{p}\}=n/2+\mathbb{E}|X_{p}-n/2|
, so we only need to prove that
\mathbb{E}|X_{p}-n/2|\geqslant\mathbb{E}|X_{q}-n/2|
.
We show that
f(x)=\mathbb{E}|\operatorname{Bin}(x,n)-n/2|
is non-increasing in the interval
[0,1/2]
by showing
f^{\prime}(x)\leqslant 0
. Let
g(k)=|k-n/2|
. Then

f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}g(k)\binom{n}{k}kx^{k-1}(1-x)^{n-k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}g(k)\binom{n}{k}x^{k}(n-k)(1-x)^{n-k-1}.

We now rearrange the terms slightly to get

f^{\prime}(x)=n\sum_{k=0}^{n}g(k)\binom{n-1}{k-1}x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n-k}-n\sum_{k=0}^{n}g(k)\binom{n-1}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{n-k-1}.

Next, by directly writing out the expectation of a binomial random variable, we obtain

f^{\prime}(x)=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptg(\operatorname{Bin}(n-1,x)+1)-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptg(\operatorname{Bin}(n-1,x)).

Now, we may expand and regroup the terms to get

f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{n-1-k}\left(\left|k-\frac{n}{2}+1\right|-\left|k-\frac{n}{2}\right|\right)\\
+\binom{n-1}{n-1-k}x^{n-1-k}(1-x)^{k}\left(\left|\frac{n}{2}-1-k+1\right|-\left|\frac{n}{2}-1-k\right|\right).

This can be simplified to give

f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{n-1-k}\left(\left|k-\frac{n}{2}+1\right|-\left|k-\frac{n}{2}\right|\right)\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)^{n-1-2k}\right).

Now, we note that for
k\leqslant\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor
we have
|k-n/2+1|-|k-n/2|\leqslant 0
and for
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1/2
we have
\frac{x}{1-x}\leqslant 1
, so as
n-1-2k\geqslant 0
we have
1-\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)^{n-1-2k}\geqslant 0
. In conclusion, each term of the above sum is non-positive, and
f^{\prime}(x)\leqslant 0
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

As
M
is a symmetric function, we may suppose without loss of generality that the T-transformation modifies the first two coordinates, and that
p_{2}\leqslant p_{2}^{\prime}\leqslant p_{1}^{\prime}\leqslant p_{1}
. Let

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{q})\sim\text{Mult}(n;\mathbf{p})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2},X_{3},X_{4},\dots,X_{q})\sim\text{Mult}(n;\mathbf{p^{\prime}}).

We need to prove
\mathbb{E}\max\{\mathbf{X}\}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\max\{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\}
.
To prove this, we first let
N=X_{1}+X_{2}=X_{1}^{\prime}+X_{2}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p_{1}+p_{2})
be the number of balls allocated to the first two bins in total. Then conditional on
N
, we have that

(X_{2},X_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Y,N-Y)\text{ where }Y\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,r)\text{ with }r=\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}+p_{2}},

and

(X_{2}^{\prime},X_{1}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Y^{\prime},N-Y^{\prime})\text{ where }Y^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,r^{\prime})\text{ with }r^{\prime}=\frac{p_{2}^{\prime}}{p_{2}^{\prime}+p_{1}^{\prime}}.

Note that
0\leqslant r\leqslant r^{\prime}\leqslant 1/2
. Let
M=\max_{\ell\neq 1,2}X_{\ell}
be the maximum number of balls in the
q-2
bins that are not
1
or
2
, so that

\max\{\mathbf{X}\}=\max\{M,\max\{Y,N-Y\}\},\quad\text{and}\quad\max\{\mathbf{X^{\prime}}\}=\max\{M,\max\{Y^{\prime},N-Y^{\prime}\}\}.

By Claim
A.2
, as
0\leqslant r\leqslant r^{\prime}\leqslant 1/2
we obtain

\mathbb{E}(\max\{Y,N-Y\}\,|N)\leqslant\mathbb{E}(\max\{Y^{\prime},N-Y^{\prime}\}\,|N),

so we also have

\mathbb{E}(\max\{M,\max\{Y,N-Y\}\}\,|\,M,N)\leqslant\mathbb{E}(\max\{M,\max\{Y^{\prime},N-Y^{\prime}\}\}\,|\,M,N).

Taking expectations over
(M,N)
then gives the desired conclusion that
M(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\leqslant M(\mathbf{p})
.
∎

Proof of monotonicity of
W(q,n)
in
q
.

Let

\mathbf{p^{\prime}}=\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{q+1},\ldots,\frac{1}{q+1}}_{q+1\text{ times}}\Bigg)\prec\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{1}{q}}_{q\text{ times}},0\Bigg)=\mathbf{p}.

As we mentioned before,
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\prec\mathbf{p}
implies that there is a sequence of distributions

\mathbf{p}_{0}=\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{p}_{1}\,,\mathbf{p}_{2}\,,\dots,\mathbf{p}_{t}=\mathbf{p^{\prime}},

so that each
\mathbf{p}_{i}
is obtained from
\mathbf{p}_{i-1}
via a T-transformation (see for example
[
9
]
). By Lemma
A.1
we obtain

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q,n}=M(\mathbf{p})=M(\mathbf{p}_{0})\geqslant M(\mathbf{p}_{1})\geqslant\dots\geqslant M(\mathbf{p}_{t})=M(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.00006ptM_{q+1,n}.

Therefore for any
q,n
we have
\mathbb{E}\,M_{q+1,n}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}
, so
\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}
, and
W(q,n)=\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}/n
are monotonically decreasing in
q
.
∎

A.2.
Bounds on
\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}

Fact A.3
.

For any
q,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the bound
W(q,n)\geqslant\max\big\{\tfrac{1}{q},\tfrac{1}{n}\big\}
.

Proof.

By the pigeonhole principle, no matter how the balls are distributed among the bins, there will be one with at least
n/q
balls in it. It is also clear that there will be at least one non-empty bin. Therefore,
M_{q,n}\geqslant n/q
and so
W(q,n)=\mathbb{E}M_{q,n}/n\geqslant\max\{1/q,1/n\}
.
∎

Next, we prove the driving force behind the key lemma in our paper; we restate Lemma
4.1
for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma A.4

(= Lemma
4.1
)
.

Let
q>1
and
n,q\in\mathbb{N}
and let
x>1
be the unique solution to
\frac{q\log q}{2n}=x\log x

\mathbb{E}\,M_{q,n}\geqslant\frac{xn}{10q}

The proof of Lemma
4.1
mainly follows from ideas in the survey of Raab and Steger
[
12
]
, and relies on a second moment argument. Suppose that we have
n
balls and
q
bins. For simplicity of notation, for each
i\in[q]
we let
Y_{i}
be the random variable denoting the number of balls in the
i
th bin, hence they have marginal distribution
Y_{i}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
for
p=\frac{1}{q}
. For
t>0
, let
I_{t}^{(i)}=\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{i}\geqslant t}
, and let
B_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}I_{t}^{(i)}
. Note that
B_{t}>0
if and only if
M_{q,n}\geqslant t
. To use second moment bounds on
B_{t}
, we need to have control on
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptI_{t}^{(1)}I_{t}^{(2)}
.

Claim A.5
.

If
q\geqslant 4
and
\frac{n}{q}\leqslant t\leqslant n
, then
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptI_{t}^{(1)}I_{t}^{(2)}\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{n}{q^{2}}+\frac{1}{q}\right)\big(\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptI_{t}^{(1)}\big)^{2}
.

Before proving the above claim, we use the following technical lemma from the paper of Raab and Steger
[
12
, Lemma 4]
that compares tails of binomial distributions. We include the proof for completeness.

Claim A.6
.

Let
p\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
, then for all
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\operatorname{Bin}\left(\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor,\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\geqslant t\bigg)\leqslant e^{p^{2}n+p}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Bin}\left(n,p\right)\geqslant t\big).

Proof.

It suffices to prove
\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Bin}\left(\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor,\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=t\big)\leqslant e^{p^{2}n+p}\hskip 1.49994pt\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Bin}\left(n,p\right)=t\big)
for any
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
, from which the statement follows by summing up the values of
t
. Since the bound is clear for
t>\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor
, we may assume that
t\leqslant\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor
. Then we note that
\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\frac{\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor-i}{n-i}\leqslant(1-p)^{t}
and consequently,

\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Bin}\left(\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor,\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=t\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)=t\big)}=\frac{\binom{\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor}{t}\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{t}\left(1-\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor-t}}{\binom{n}{t}\cdot p^{t}\cdot(1-p)^{n-t}}\leqslant\frac{(1-2p)^{\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor-t}}{(1-p)^{n+\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor-2t}}

Since
1-2p\leqslant(1-p)^{2}
, this is maximised when
t
is as large as possible, i.e. when
t=\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor
.
In this case, we note that the above simplifies to

(1-p)^{-(n-\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor)}\leqslant(1-p)^{-np-1}\leqslant\exp(p^{2}n+p).\qed

Proof of Claim
A.5
.

Let
p=\frac{1}{q}
. By conditioning on the number of balls in the first bin, we can obtain the following formula

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptI_{t}^{(1)}I_{t}^{(2)}=\sum_{k=t}^{n-t}\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\cdot\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathrm{Bin}\left(n-k,\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\geqslant t\bigg).

Note that as
k\geqslant t\geqslant pn
, we have
n(1-p)\geqslant n-k\in\mathbb{Z}
, so in particular

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathrm{Bin}\left(n-k,\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\geqslant t\bigg)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathrm{Bin}\left(\lfloor n(1-p)\rfloor,\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\geqslant t\bigg)\leqslant e^{p^{2}n+p}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Bin}\left(n,p\right)\geqslant t\big),

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
A.6
as
q\geqslant 4
and
p\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptI_{t}^{(1)}I_{t}^{(2)}\leqslant\sum_{k=t}^{n-t}\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}e^{p^{2}n+p}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Bin}\left(n,p\right)\geqslant t\right]=\exp\left(\frac{n}{q^{2}}+\frac{1}{q}\right)\big(\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.49994ptI_{t}^{(1)}\big)^{2}.\qed

We will also need the following inequalities to analyse the tails of certain binomial random variables. We first quote an estimate from
[
7
]
, which is a generalisation of Stirling’s approximation.

Theorem A.7
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\geqslant 0}
, we have

\sqrt{2\pi}x^{x+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-x+\frac{1}{12x+1}}<\Gamma(x+1)\leqslant\sqrt{2\pi}x^{x+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-x+\frac{1}{12x}}.

In particular, when
x\geqslant 1
, we have

\sqrt{2\pi x}e^{-x}x^{x}\leqslant\Gamma(x+1)\leqslant\sqrt{2\pi x}e^{-x}x^{x}e^{\frac{1}{12}}.

We shall also need Gautschi’s inequality
[
10
]
.

Theorem A.8
.

If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\geqslant 0}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
are such that
x>y>x-1
, then
x^{x-y}\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(y+1)}\leqslant(x+1)^{x-y}
.

.

Proof.
