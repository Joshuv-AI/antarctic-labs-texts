Modelling Realistic Multi-layer devices for superconducting quantum electronic circuits Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Giuseppe Colletta, Susan Johny, Jonathan A. Collins, Alessandro Casaburi, Martin Weides

Abstract
In this work, we present a numerical model specifically designed for 3D multilayer devices, with a focus on nanobridge junctions and coplanar waveguides. Unlike existing numerical models, ours does not approximate the physical layout or limit the number of constituent materials, providing a more accurate and flexible design tool. We calculate critical currents, current phase relationships, and the energy gap where relevant. We validate our model by comparing it with published data. Through our analysis, we found that using multilayer films significantly enhances control over these quantities. For nanobridge junctions in particular, multilayer structures improve qubit anharmonicity compared to monolayer junctions, offering a substantial advantage for qubit performance. For coated multilayer microwave circuits it allows for better studies of the proximity effect, including their effective kinetic inductance.

This area has attracted enormous interest, especially since recent studies have shown that surface encapsulation techniques, involving passivating the surface of superconducting materials like niobium to prevent the formation of lossy oxides, can significantly enhance qubits’ performance

II
Theoretical Background

A nanobridge Josephson junction consists of two superconducting electrodes connected by a small constriction, usually of order of a few tens of nanometers. These constrictions may consist of the same material or a different one, with the latter referred to as SS’S or SNS junctions, where S represents a superconductor and S’ or N represents the filling material that connects the two electrodes, which may be another superconductor (S’) or a normal metal (N).

IV
Nanobridge Junction

Nanobridge junctions are interesting for quantum computing because they offer a smaller footprint, potentially improving coherence due to the absence of dielectrics and hereby reducing noise. Their precise fabrication will allow for good control over qubit properties. Additionally, their lateral dimensions are compatible with modern semiconductor nanofabrication techniques, making them a promising option for building large-scale quantum processors. Superconducting quantum computing circuits require Josephson junctions with small critical currents, of the order of 10 to 100 nA, and CPRs as close as possible to a sinusoid in order to obtain non-linear energy levels with high anharmonicity
Liu and Black 2023
. For SFQs the constrain of low critical currents is lifted but the shape of the CPR still plays a crucial role in the generation of the pulses
Likharev and Semenov 1991
. Moreover, to stay within the Josephson effect, the dimensions of the bridge should be less than 3 times the coherence length
Golubov, Kupriyanov, and Il’ichev 2004
. For these reasons, different layouts have been numerically investigated as shown in Fig.
1
, also taking into account realistically rounded edges that can come for an eventual etching process
Collins, Rose, and Casaburi 2023
, the lateral dimension W of the nano-bridge was chosen to be
W=0.5\xi
and
L=2\xi
for all the simulated devices.
We calculated the current-phase relationship (CPR) for the following use-case scenarios:

•

Planar junctions with rectangular and rounded cross-sections

•

Variable Thickness Nanobridge SS’S Junctions (VTB) with rectangular and rounded cross-sections

These devices are illustrated in Fig.
1
. The simulation results are presented in Fig.
2
, where we demonstrate how the rounded edges reduce the critical current in the junction, even after normalizing for the reduced cross-section. Additionally, in the case of the planar junction, the rounded edges slightly shift the maximum of the CPR to the left. This occurs because a reduced cross-section weakens the connection between the electrodes while maintaining the
L/\xi
ratio, bringing the junction closer to the ideal case described by the one-dimensional Usadel equations, known as the KO-1 model
Kupriyanov, Likharev, and Lukichev 1981
, which is also shown in the figure.

Compared to the KO-1 model, the CPR exhibits lower values and increased skewness, which can be attributed to the reduction of the energy gap in the bridge area relative to the bulk electrodes, as illustrated in the inset. This reduction does not occur in the KO-1 model, where only the bridge is simulated with the assumption that the energy gap at its boundaries matches the bulk value. However, VTB junctions, for the same set of parameters, produce less skewed CPRs, which can improve qubit anharmonicity as discussed in Sec.
V
. This improvement is primarily due to the variable thickness design, where the electrodes act as a robust phase reservoir, leading to a more significant phase change across the bridge rather than within the electrodes, compared to planar junctions. This result aligns with the findings in Ref.
Vijay et al. 2009
. The use of different materials enables better control over the device’s critical current
I_{C}
by selecting a bottom material with the appropriate resistivity.
We further validated our model by comparing it with the experimental work in Ref.
Vijay et al. 2010
, where aluminium VTB and planar nanobridges of different lengths were measured, as shown in Fig.
2

(b). To do this, we first estimated the resistivity from the IV curve of a 75 nm long VTB nanobridge, and then plotted the simulated data obtained by using both the reported coherence length of 40 nm and an adjusted value of 110 nm, found in Ref.
Tarasov et al. 2022
, which aligned more closely to the experimental data. This adjustment is based on the assumption that the coherence length might be larger than the reported one, calculated by using the film’s resistivity that could have been made at room temperature, thus lowering the value of
\xi
. This comparison underscores the accuracy of our model and validates our design choices.

V
Transmon and Fluxonium Qubits with nanobridge junctions

We are now using our numerical model to optimize the calculation of energy levels in superconducting qubits. The Hamiltonian for the transmon qubit with a junction featuring a generic current-phase relation (CPR) is given by:

\hat{H}=4E_{C}(\hat{n}-n_{g})^{2}-E_{J}f(\hat{\varphi}),

(6)

while the Hamiltonian for the fluxonium qubit is

;
Zhu et al. 2013
;
Nguyen et al. 2019

\hat{H}=4E_{C}(\hat{n}-n_{g})^{2}-E_{J}f(\hat{\varphi})+\frac{E_{L}}{2}\varphi^{2}.

In the case of the fluxonium, the following constraints need to be imposed to differentiate it from other types of inductively shunted devices

From the CPR in Fig.

\int I_{J}(\varphi)\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{dt}dt=\int I_{J}(\varphi)\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}d\varphi

To solve the equation we incorporated in scqubits

and qiskit metal

The eigenenergies of the Hamiltonian are solved for different realistic ratios of
