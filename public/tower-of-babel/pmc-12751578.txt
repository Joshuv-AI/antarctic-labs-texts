Spatiotemporal trends in hospitalizations and mortality due to mental disorders in Ecuador (2014–2023): a national epidemiological study
Authors: Cristina Núñez-Vásquez, Karina Lalangui-Vivanco, Mónica Izurieta-Guevara, Yekaterina Altuna, Daniel Simancas-Racines, Emmanuelle Quentin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental disorders constitute a growing public health concern, accounting for a substantial share of global morbidity and mortality. In Ecuador, despite increasing policy attention, the availability of epidemiological evidence remains limited. This study examines hospitalizations and mortality due to mental disorders (ICD-10 F00–F99) from 2014 to 2023, aiming to generate robust evidence to support informed decision-making and strengthen mental health planning within the national health system.MethodsThis study analyzed data from national public-access registries. Descriptive statistics were calculated by sex, age, year, region, and province, and crude rates per 100,000 inhabitants were estimated. Spatiotemporal patterns were examined using k-means clustering and visualized through thematic maps.ResultsBetween 2014 and 2023, Ecuador recorded 93,680 hospitalizations and 2,281 deaths due to mental disorders, with the highest hospitalization burden observed among individuals aged 20–29 years and a notable increase among females aged 10–19. Substance use disorders predominated in men, whereas mood disorders were more frequent in women. Spatial clustering revealed distinct regional patterns, with the Sierra region, home to approximately 43% of the population, exhibiting the highest hospitalization and mortality rates, whereas the Galápagos province showed extreme temporal variability.ConclusionOver the past decade, Ecuador has experienced a steady increase in hospitalizations and deaths associated with mental disorders. The results underscore the urgent need to strengthen community-based mental health systems, update national care models, and develop evidence-based promotion and prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01793-z.

Background

Mental disorders constitute clinically significant alterations in cognition, emotional regulation, behavior, and interpersonal relationships. According to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), these disorders include anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, schizophrenia, disruptive behaviors, and substance use disorders, among others. The associated risk factors encompass biological, genetic, familial, social, and environmental dimensions [1].

As part of the protective factors, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends comprehensive mental health services and community social assistance connected to hospitals; early intervention; multisectoral support emphasizing various stages of life and services with general and focused attention to vulnerable groups and those exposed to extreme stress due to humanitarian crises [2].

Worldwide, by 2019, approximately 13% of the population suffered from some mental disorder; one in three women and one in five men will experience major depression in their lifetime [3]. In the Americas, prevalence is higher, reaching 15.6%. In children aged 5–9 years, it reached 8%, and in adolescents, 14%. Sex-based differences are evident, with 13.4% of men and 15.7% of women over 20 years affected [1]. These differences, along with variations by age, socioeconomic status, and exposure to stressors, underscore the importance of analyzing demographic and contextual variables to fully understand the distribution and determinants of mental health disorders. A comparative study in South America identified that the most frequent diagnoses in Psychiatric Units of General Hospitals were schizophrenia and other non-organic delusional disorders, affective disorders, organic-brain disorders, alcohol-related disorders, and self-harm attempts [4].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, mental disorders are estimated to rise from 25% to 27% [5]. According to the World Mental Health Report, mental well-being in the Americas remains at persistently low levels, with no significant recovery to pre-pandemic conditions. Prior to the pandemic, approximately 193 million people (2,471 cases per 100,000 population) were affected by major depressive disorders, and 298 million (3,825 cases per 100,000) experienced anxiety disorders. Adjusted post-pandemic estimates indicate a marked increase to 246 million individuals (3,153 per 100,000) with major depressive disorders and 374 million (4,802 per 100,000) with anxiety disorders. Mood disorders remain the most common among both men and women [6, 7].

The average Mental Health Quotient (MHQ) score for the region stands at 65 on a 300-point scale, with 27% of individuals classified as Distressed or Struggling and only 38% Succeeding or Thriving in terms of mental well-being [8].

Humanitarian crises, such as forced displacements and armed conflicts, are also associated with elevated prevalence, with 1 in 5 affected individuals developing a mental disorder. Despite this, 85% of people with mental disorders do not receive treatment [1]. Considering sex, age, and social determinants is therefore essential for designing effective prevention strategies, allocating resources appropriately, and implementing interventions that are sensitive to population subgroups.

Mental health has gained increasing visibility on public health agendas. Target 3.4 of the Sustainable Development Goals aims to reduce premature mortality from noncommunicable diseases by one-third through prevention, treatment, and the promotion of mental health and well-being by 2030 [9]. At the same time, the impact of mental health on productivity and socioeconomic development has been increasingly recognized, supported by scientific evidence highlighting the relationships between these variables. These insights may have contributed to the strengthening of health systems, beginning with the expansion of existing regulatory and policy frameworks [10–13].

In Ecuador, mental health has become increasingly prominent in public policy. The Organic Health Law emphasizes holistic physical, mental, and social well-being, while the 2023 Mental Health Law establishes a legal framework to guarantee mental health rights across all life stages under a comprehensive, community-based care model. Article 3 of the Law defines health as “the complete state of physical, mental, and social well-being and not only the absence of conditions or diseases” [14, 15].

The national legislation builds upon the Health Care Model (MAIS) of the Ministry of Public Health (MSP), which promotes coordinated care across primary to specialized levels. In this context, it is recognized that not all mental disorders are psychiatric pathologies; many are linked to social determinants and community contexts, requiring approaches based on promotion, prevention, and primary care to ensure comprehensive mental well-being and reduce stigma [16].

Furthermore, in 2014, the MSP officially implemented the National Strategic Plan for Mental Health 2014–2017 and the Mental Health Care Model through Ministerial Agreement No. 0004927 [17]. These initiatives also aimed to support the deinstitutionalization of patients in psychiatric hospitals by strengthening community-based services. By expanding access to local mental health programs, integrating care across primary, secondary, and specialized levels, and fostering family and community involvement, the strategy sought to reduce reliance on long-term institutionalization. This approach not only improves patient autonomy and quality of life but also promotes early intervention, continuity of care, and the integration of social and environmental determinants into mental health management. However, neither the Plan nor the Model has been updated since their implementation, particularly the National Strategic Plan, which was intended to guide resource allocation, service organization, health promotion, prevention, and research within a community mental health framework. This gap underscores the need for empirical data analysis to inform policy and program development.

Ecuador currently lacks an official national mental health study that systematically assesses the burden and distribution of mental disorders within the population. In this regard, understanding their temporal dynamics and spatial patterns becomes essential for evidence-based decision-making. This raises the question of to what extent the epidemiological analysis and characterization of mental disorders in Ecuador from 2014 to 2023 can contribute to establishing a national evidence base to support data-driven decision-making in mental health planning and policy. The present study offers an analysis of hospitalizations and mortality records related to mental disorders (F00–F99) from 2014 to 2023, integrating spatial and temporal perspectives. Its findings help bridge this knowledge gap by outlining a nationwide epidemiological profile of hospitalizations and deaths associated with mental disorders, providing valuable evidence to guide mental health policies, strategic planning, and public health interventions.

Materials and methodology

Study design and data processing

This study uses the Register of Hospitalizations [18] and the Statistical Register of General Deaths [19], both with public access through the website of the National Institute of Statistics and Census (INEC). These databases are published as independent records on an annual basis. For the analysis, all registers were compiled in a single dataset. Also, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (i) records outside the study period (2014–2023); (ii) records with province code “88” (corresponding to foreign nationals); and (iii) records with missing data in key variables (sex, age, or place of residence).

The analysis included hospitalizations and deaths with a primary diagnosis within ICD-10 codes F00–F99, covering mental disorders such as organic disorders (F01–F09), disorders due to psychoactive substance use (F10–F19), schizophrenia (F20–F29), mood disorders (F30–F39), neurotic, stress-related, and somatoform disorders (F40–F48), behavioral syndromes associated with physiological disturbances and physical factors (F50–F59), personality disorders in adults (F60–F69), intellectual disabilities (F70–F79), disorders of psychological development (F80–F89), emotional disorders commonly occurring in childhood and adolescence (F90–F98) and unspecified (F99). Other classifications related to mental health were not considered in this study. Therefore, future research could be complemented by including neurological diseases or poisoning-related conditions from other classifications to provide a more comprehensive perspective.

Statistical and spatiotemporal analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software (version 4.4.3) with complementary libraries such as tidyverse and gtsummary. Data were grouped by sex, 10-year age intervals, year of registration, region, and province of residence.

Crude hospitalization and death rates for mental disorders per 100,000 inhabitants were estimated by year and province using population estimates and projections published by INEC. Crude rates were preferred over age-adjusted rates, given the relatively homogeneous age and sex distribution across provinces, providing a more accurate reflection of the actual burden in each territory.

To complement the descriptive analysis and explore territorial patterns, an unsupervised k-means cluster analysis was applied to the average crude hospitalization and death rates by province between 2014 and 2023. The analysis was conducted in GeoDa, using default parameters: KMeans + + initialization, Z-standardization, Euclidean distance, 15,000 initialization restarts, and a maximum of 100,000 iterations. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method. Cluster results were subsequently visualized through thematic maps generated in QGIS 3.34.7, facilitating spatial interpretation of epidemiological patterns across provinces.

The year 2019 had the highest number of hospital admissions, with 11,280 (12%), followed by 2022, with a total of 11,245 (12%). The year 2020 showed a reduction in hospitalizations, with 7,323 (7.8%).

According to the analysis by age groups, the group aged 20–29 years recorded the highest number of hospital admissions overall, with 22,500 (24%), predominantely males (59%), followed by the 10–19 years group with 18,409 (20%), most of whom were women (23%), and then the 30–39 years group with 17,335 hospitalizations (19%). Regarding diagnostic categories, hospitalizations due to substance use disorders (F10–F19) were predominantly male (19,583 cases in men vs. 4,166 in women). Conversely, mood disorders (F30–F39) were more frequent in women (17,992 vs. 9,265 in men).

These trends are visually depicted in Figures A1 and A2. Notably, the 20–29 age group consistently showed the highest hospitalization rates throughout the study period, particularly among men, largely driven by substance use disorders (F10–F19). However, a shift was observed in 2023, with the 10–19 age group emerging as the one with the highest hospitalization burden, predominantly among females. This group had already exhibited a secondary peak in hospitalizations in previous years, which intensified from 2021 onward, mainly due to an increase in mood disorders (F30–F39) among young women.

Geographically, the Sierra had the highest number of hospitalizations with 59% (55,713), with a slightly higher predominance in women. Conversely, the Coast accounted for 36% (33,916) of cases, with a predominance of hospitalizations in men. The Amazon and Insular (Galapagos) regions had lower percentages: 3.8% and 0.5%, respectively.

By age group, the highest mortality occurred in older adults aged 80–89 years (25%) and 90–99 years (25%), with a similar distribution between sexes. The younger age groups of 0–9 and 10–19 years represented a much smaller percentage of mortality, with 0.3% and 1.3% of the total, respectively. Regarding ICD-10 categories, most deaths were due to mental disorders resulting from organic causes (F01–F09), accounting for 1,262 cases (55%), with 734 (78%) in females and 528 (40%) in males. The second most frequent cause of death was substance use disorders (F10–F19), which represented 696 deaths (31%) overall. This category exhibited a striking gender disparity, with 649 deaths in males (49%) and only 47 in females (5%).

The temporal distribution by age and sex (Figure B2) reveals a progressive increase in deaths among older adult women, particularly after 2020, consistent with the predominance of organic mental disorders (F00–F09) observed in the data. In contrast, male mortality due to substance use disorders (F10–F19) remained relatively stable throughout the study period, primarily concentrated in middle-aged groups (40–59 years), as shown in Figure B1.

Regionally, the Sierra region concentrated the majority of deaths (1,533; 67%), followed by the Coast (693; 30%), and the Amazon region (55 deaths; 2.4%) (Fig. 1).

Spatio-temporal analysis

The spatial-temporal analysis showed a persistent concentration of high rates in provinces of the central-southern Sierra, such as Azuay, Cañar, and Tungurahua. In contrast, provinces in the coastal region and the northern Amazon showed consistently lower rates. In 2020, a generalized reduction in rates is evident. Discussion

According to data from the INEC, overall hospital admissions for mental disorders figures and rates in Ecuador had not recovered to pre-pandemic levels [20]. This pattern is consistent with a persistent decline in male hospitalizations due to mental disorders, which peaked at 5,895 cases in 2019 and had since remained lower, with a post-2020 maximum of only 5,300 in 2022. In contrast, female hospitalizations had not only recovered but had surpassed pre-pandemic figures, rising from 5,385 cases in 2019 to a peak of 5,945 in 2022 and remaining elevated at 5,632 in 2023. Consequently, these divergent recovery paths have produced a significant and lasting shift in the gender distribution of patients, which had equalized in 2020 and culminated in 2023 with female admissions (5,632) exceeding male admissions (4,763) by 18%.

A multicenter study conducted in Italy similarly reported an overall decrease in psychiatric hospital admissions following the COVID-19 outbreak, with reductions evident across various diagnostic categories and some gender variation in admission rates, aligning with Ecuador’s documented decline in male admissions and the rise in female hospitalizations [21]. In Greece, research indicated a significant reduction in voluntary psychiatric admissions during the pandemic period, which reinforced the diminished hospital utilization observed in Ecuador at that time, though this study did not explicitly analyze gender differences [22]. Complementing these findings, a Canadian study found an initial drop in mental disorder hospitalizations at the pandemic’s start, followed by a recovery or increase in specific groups, notably with females showing a greater rise in admissions. This corresponds closely with Ecuador’s experience of female hospitalization rates surpassing those of males post-2020 [23].

The divergent post-pandemic recovery trajectories between genders can be explained by the primary diagnostic drivers of hospitalization. Historical data demonstrated that mood disorders (F30–F39) and substance use disorders (F10–F19) constituted the most prevalent categories, accounting for 29% and 25% of cases, respectively. This finding is consistent with established literature identifying depressive episodes and alcohol abuse as highly prevalent conditions (14, 15). Crucially, these dominant diagnostic categories exhibit well-documented gender disparities: mood disorders demonstrate a higher prevalence in females [6], while substance use disorders are more frequently diagnosed in males [24].

Consequently, the established gender disparities in the prevalence of mood disorders, which are higher in women, and substance use disorders, which are higher in men, provide a plausible epidemiological explanation for the post-2020 shift in hospitalization trends. This evolving caseload composition necessitates an adaptive response from mental health services, specifically the reallocation of resources to address the growing demand for mood disorder treatment. To inform such policy and clinical adjustments effectively, future research is critical to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and its associated social determinants of health, such as inequalities in the labor market and living conditions, in amplifying these pre-existing vulnerabilities, particularly among women, and thereby accelerating the observed demographic shift [25, 26].

An important trend is the increasing rate of hospitalizations among younger age groups, suggesting that significant psycho-emotional challenges are emerging at earlier stages of life. This is clearly evidenced by the data, which show that 44% of all cases are concentrated within the 10–29 age cohort, with significant proportions further observed among those aged 30–39 (19%) and 40–49 (13%). This clinical pattern was likely driven by a documented rise in risk factors such as anxiety, uncertainty, and loneliness among children and adolescents, a phenomenon exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. For instance, a rapid UNICEF survey conducted in the region in 2020 had reported that 27% of participants aged 13 to 29 experienced anxiety and 15% had reported depressive symptoms in the preceding week [6, 27].

In Canada, a study revealed a significant decrease in youth hospitalizations during the pandemic, which contrasts with Ecuador’s increase but may reflect regional differences in healthcare access and response [28]. Extensively reviewed evidence on the impact of social isolation stress globally corroborates the hypothesis that pandemic-induced anxiety, loneliness, and depression substantially affected children and adolescents, elevating psycho-emotional risks at earlier life stages [29]. Additionally, a multi-country analysis confirms rising acute mental health service use among younger people post-pandemic, reinforcing the need for community-based prevention and early intervention programs [30].

Previous findings together with international studies, support the conclusion that younger and working-age populations face escalating mental health burdens. Given the inability of health systems to provide adequate means for the prevention and treatment of mental health disorders, risk factors are likely to increase for these populations as already evidenced by the data. The escalation of these risks undermines productive capacity, contributes to social isolation, and increases long-term reliance on more intensive and costly health services [31]. Furthermore, mental health disorders exert a substantial impact on daily life, adversely affecting interpersonal relationships, educational attainment, and employment stability. Therefore, this epidemiological profile highlights the urgent need for public health policy to prioritize scalable, community-based prevention and early intervention programs, particularly targeted at younger and working-age populations, to mitigate long-term disability and reduce the future economic burden on the healthcare system.

The hospitalization rate per 100,000 inhabitants increased from 49.95 in 2014 to 64.99 in 2019. This rise may have also reflected the implementation of efforts to raise awareness of mental health’s importance and strengthen service provision. In fact, June 2014 saw the launch of the National Mental Health Model, the National Strategic Mental Health Plan 2014–2017, the Health Regulation for the Control and Surveillance of Health Facilities Providing Treatment Services to Individuals with Problematic Alcohol and Drug Use (ESTAD) [32–34].

Geographic analysis of hospital discharges reveals a pronounced concentration of mental health admissions in the Sierra region (59%), which significantly exceeds the proportion recorded in the more populous Coastal region (36%). This disparity is particularly notable given that the Coast is home around 54% of Ecuador’s population according to INECs projection. The observed pattern suggests two potential, non-exclusive explanations: either a genuinely higher prevalence or severity of mental health disorders in Sierra, or more plausibly, a combination of significantly better service availability and lower mental health stigma in that region compared to the Coast. Meanwhile, the Amazon and Galápagos regions face the most severe barriers, where geographic isolation, sparse population distribution, and elevated operational costs critically limit service provision and are likely compounded by profound stigma.

The provincial analysis of hospitalization rates identifies Pichincha, Tungurahua, Cañar, Azuay, and Galápagos as consistently having the highest rates across the study period. A more concerning pattern emerges when examining mortality rates, where Cañar and Azuay maintain the country’s highest figures and Pichincha shows a recent increase. Notably, the clustering of Cañar, Azuay, and Loja (three geographically and culturally proximate provinces) presents a critical area for intervention. While Loja has historically shown moderately high hospitalization rates, its post-pandemic surge in mortality suggests deteriorating outcomes, potentially indicating severe service inadequacies. This regional concentration of high hospitalization and mortality in the southern Sierra likely reflects shared cultural factors, risk profiles, and systemic gaps in mental health service provision, demanding an integrated, regional approach to resource allocation and policy development.

The stark provincial disparities, with Galápagos, Cañar, and Azuay exhibiting the most acute hospitalization and mortality rates, underscore the need for targeted interventions informed by localized evidence. We recommend mixed-methods research to quantitatively map service infrastructure and qualitatively explore cultural stigma and access barriers specific to each high-burden province. Findings must directly guide the strategic strengthening of mental health services, deploying tailored solutions such as telepsychiatry and mobile units to overcome geographic isolation in Galápagos, and expanding community-based and inpatient care capacity to manage the high caseloads in Cañar and Azuay.

The effects of COVID-19 were evident in the decline in hospitalization rates in 2020, driven by mobility restrictions, overburdened health systems, and widespread fear of contagion, all of which discouraged the population from seeking care. Nonetheless, a rebound was observed the following year, with the rate reaching 52.14 and rising to 58.28 in 2023. This upturn may reflect the reactivation of services and an increased demand for mental health care in the post-pandemic context.

These findings underscore the urgent need to strengthen national mental health strategies, particularly those aimed at younger populations, to prevent and mitigate the consequences associated with mental disorders. The increase in hospitalizations among younger age groups serves as a critical call to address mental health comprehensively, recognizing that broader structural and social transformations, such as digital hyperconnectivity, the fragmentation of family bonds, and the lingering effects of the pandemic, are having a significant impact on our societies. In a world marked by rapid and profound change, it is evident that these dynamics affect both individuals and their social environments. From a systemic perspective, the various units, individual, couple, family, community, interact through reciprocal influence processes, amplifying the effects of such transformations [35]. In this context, it is imperative to deepen interdisciplinary analyses of these phenomena and to expand the availability and transparency of information produced by the institutions responsible for designing and implementing public mental health policies.

Although mental health conditions are increasingly recognized, it remains essential to strengthen public and professional awareness to enable early symptom recognition, particularly among vulnerable populations. Evidence indicates that even healthcare personnel often fail to detect mental health problems [36], underscoring the critical need for targeted educational and training interventions to improve early identification and response.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its use of administrative records, including potential underreporting, inter-institutional variability in data quality, and diagnostic misclassification. The latter is of particular concern for mortality data, where the true burden is likely underestimated. For instance, deaths where conditions such as alcohol use disorder are underlying factors, such as those from accidents, injuries, or violence, may be misclassified under external causes rather than the associated mental disorder. Furthermore, trends in hospitalizations and mortality may have been influenced by health system factors, including policy reforms and service disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite these limitations, this analysis offers the first comprehensive national epidemiological profile of mental disorders in Ecuador.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a progressive increase in hospitalizations and deaths associated with mental disorders in Ecuador between 2014 and 2023, with distinct variations by sex, age and geographic distribution. These findings highlight the urgent need to strengthen mental health infrastructure and services through differentiated approaches tailored to age, sex and location, alongside the development of specific promotion, prevention and comprehensive care interventions to curb the rising incidence of disorders in younger populations. The normative advances since 2014, reflected in these results, may have contributed to greater visibility of mental health issues and increased demand for specialized services.

From a public policy perspective, we propose several key strategies to address these challenges:
