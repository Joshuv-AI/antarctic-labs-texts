Efficacy of Valsartan-HCTZ in hypertension: a cohort study exploring age as a key determinant
Authors: Abdur Rauf, Yasar Shah, Zaki Ullah, Fawad Inayat, Wahby M Babaresh, Aiman Begum, Salman Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is a major global public health challenge, and effective management of antihypertensive treatment response is crucial. This study aimed to analyze the demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors influencing treatment response in hypertensive patients receiving Valsartan monotherapy or Valsartan/Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) combination therapy.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 354 adult patients with hypertension who were on stable antihypertensive therapy for at least 6 months prior to enrollment and were initiated on or switched to Valsartan monotherapy (80 mg) or Valsartan/HCTZ combination therapy (80/12.5 mg or 160/12.5 mg) based on clinical indication. Baseline blood pressure inclusion criteria were 140–179 mmHg systolic and/or 90–109 mmHg diastolic. Demographic, clinical, and lifestyle data were collected. The primary outcome was achievement of target blood pressure (< 140/90 mmHg) after 3 weeks of treatment; the secondary outcome was reduction in systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressure. Statistical analysis included repeated measures ANOVA, multivariable logistic regression, and post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction.ResultsThe sample consisted of 58.0% females and 42.0% males. Baseline mean SBP was 157.50 ± 17.23 mmHg and DBP was 103.23 ± 10.17 mmHg. After three weeks of treatment, mean SBP decreased to 131.44 ± 20.40 mmHg and mean DBP to 85.08 ± 12.54 mmHg (p < 0.001). Target BP achievement at Week 3 was 82.6% for Valsartan 80 mg, 70.5% for Valsartan/HCTZ 80/12.5 mg, and 56.4% for Valsartan/HCTZ 160/12.5 mg. These unadjusted rates reflect baseline differences in hypertension severity and comorbidity burden rather than differential regimen efficacy. Younger age, shorter hypertension duration, and absence of diabetes were independently associated with better treatment response in multivariable analysis.ConclusionYounger age and shorter disease duration are key predictors of successful initial blood pressure control with Valsartan-based therapy in this short-term assessment. Longer-term studies are needed to confirm whether these early predictors translate to sustained blood pressure control and reduced cardiovascular events. Treatment should be individualized based on patient age, comorbidity profile, and duration of hypertension, with dose adjustment as needed to achieve optimal outcomes.Keywords: Hypertension, Antihypertensive agents, Valsartan, Hydrochlorothiazide, Treatment outcome, Blood pressure

Introduction

Hypertension, a major global public health challenge, is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and renal failure [1]. Despite the availability of effective antihypertensive therapies, achieving optimal blood pressure control remains a significant challenge worldwide [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that hypertension affects over 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years globally, with a disproportionately higher burden in low- and middle-income countries [3]. Effective management of hypertension is critical to reducing the associated morbidity and mortality. However, the response to antihypertensive treatment varies widely among individuals, influenced by a complex interplay of demographic, clinical, genetic, and lifestyle factors [4].

Previous studies have highlighted the role of demographic factors such as age, sex, and ethnicity in determining treatment outcomes [5, 6]. Younger patients often exhibit better responses to antihypertensive therapy compared to older individuals, potentially due to differences in vascular compliance and comorbidities [7]. Clinical factors, including the duration of hypertension, baseline blood pressure levels, and the presence of comorbid conditions such as diabetes and obesity, have also been shown to significantly impact treatment efficacy [8, 9]. Moreover, genetic predisposition, including polymorphisms in genes related to the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, has been implicated in interindividual variability in drug response [10, 11].

Lifestyle factors, such as dietary habits, physical activity, and adherence to medication, further complicate the management of hypertension [12]. High sodium intake and low potassium consumption are well-established contributors to poor blood pressure control, while regular physical activity has been associated with improved treatment outcomes [13]. Adherence to prescribed antihypertensive regimens is another critical determinant of therapeutic success, with nonadherence being a major barrier to achieving target blood pressure goals [14].

Despite the extensive body of literature on hypertension management, there remains a paucity of data from South Asian populations, where genetic, dietary, and healthcare system factors may differ substantially from Western cohorts. Furthermore, to our knowledge, few studies have prospectively examined how age and duration of hypertension modify response to Valsartan-based therapy in real-world clinical settings within South Asia. This study aims to bridge this gap by conducting a comprehensive analysis of demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors influencing antihypertensive treatment response in a Pakistani cohort. By identifying key determinants of treatment efficacy, this research seeks to inform personalized approaches to hypertension management in similar resource-limited settings.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

This study employed a prospective cohort design to evaluate the factors influencing antihypertensive treatment response in a real-world clinical setting. Treatment regimens were not randomized; they were prescribed by treating physicians based on clinical judgment, reflecting routine practice. The research was conducted at secondary and tertiary care hospitals in Peshawar between January 1, 2024 and November 30, 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and written informed consent was secured from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study population

The study included adult patients aged 30 years and older who were diagnosed with primary hypertension. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with a confirmed diagnosis of primary hypertension based on repeated office BP measurements and clinical assessment, with routine laboratory tests (renal function, electrolytes, urinalysis) performed to exclude common secondary causes; (2) baseline blood pressure at enrollment ranging from 140 to 179 mmHg systolic and/or 90–109 mmHg diastolic (Stage 1–2 hypertension per ACC/AHA guidelines); (3) patients on stable antihypertensive therapy for at least 6 months prior to enrollment, with either (a) stable on one antihypertensive drug (monotherapy) or (b) stable on two antihypertensive drugs (combination therapy) for at least 4 weeks before the study-specific regimen was initiated; (4) availability of complete medical records. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Having a mental disorder; (2) Presenting with a chronic infection, such as HCV, HBV; (3) Patients with suspected secondary hypertension (e.g., primary hyperaldosteronism, renal artery stenosis) based on clinical or laboratory findings were referred for specialized evaluation and excluded if confirmed; (4) Patients with uncontrolled major comorbidities (e.g., decompensated heart failure, severe renal impairment with eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²) that could independently affect BP or treatment response; and (5) individuals who declined to participate.

At enrollment, patients were either initiated on or switched to one of three study-specific regimens based on physician discretion: Valsartan 80 mg monotherapy, Valsartan/HCTZ 80/12.5 mg combination, or Valsartan/HCTZ 160/12.5 mg combination. The “start of therapy” in all analyses refers to the initiation of these study-specific regimens.

Prior Antihypertensive Therapy: Among enrolled patients, 42.4% (n = 150) were on monotherapy prior to study enrollment, most commonly amlodipine (n = 68, 19.2%), losartan (n = 42, 11.9%), or enalapril (n = 40, 11.3%). The remaining 57.6% (n = 204) were on combination therapy, predominantly amlodipine/losartan (n = 89, 25.1%) or amlodipine/enalapril (n = 71, 20.1%). Reasons for switching to study regimens included: inadequate blood pressure control on current therapy (68.4%, n = 242), physician preference for valsartan-based regimen (18.6%, n = 66), patient request or cost considerations (8.5%, n = 30), and adverse effects from prior medication (4.5%, n = 16). Patients switched due to inadequate control had significantly higher baseline blood pressure (161.3 ± 16.8 vs. 152.1 ± 15.2 mmHg, p < 0.001) and were more likely to receive combination therapy (p < 0.001). Prior treatment exposure was tested in the multivariable model but was not independently associated with target BP achievement (AOR for switch due to inadequate control: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.46–1.53, p = 0.568).

Blood pressure measurement

Blood pressure was measured using a validated automated oscillometric device (Omron HEM-7120, Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan) with an appropriate cuff size based on arm circumference. Measurements were performed according to standardized guidelines: patients were seated quietly for at least 5 min in a chair with feet on the floor and arm supported at heart level. Three consecutive readings were taken at 1-minute intervals from the right arm, and the average of the three readings was recorded as the clinic BP. At the initial visit, BP was measured in both arms; if a consistent difference > 10 mmHg was observed, the arm with the higher reading was used for all subsequent measurements. The difference between arms was recorded but not used as an exclusion criterion. Blood pressure measurements at follow-up visits were performed by trained research staff who were blinded to the patient’s treatment regimen to minimize measurement bias. However, complete blinding could not be guaranteed in all cases, as patients occasionally disclosed their medication regimen during visits.

The calculated minimum sample size required was 322 patients. To account for anticipated dropout and to ensure adequate representation across the three treatment groups (which were expected to be unequal based on local prescribing patterns), the target enrollment was set at 354 patients, which was achieved. Post-hoc power analysis for the Valsartan/HCTZ 160/12.5 mg subgroup (n = 43) indicated 68% power to detect a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for within-group BP reduction, and 42% power for between-group comparisons, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was achievement of target blood pressure, defined as BP < 140/90 mmHg at the end of the 3-week treatment period, in accordance with current hypertension management guidelines. The secondary outcome was reduction of systolic blood pressure by ≥ 20 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure by ≥ 10 mmHg from baseline. Additional secondary outcomes included identification of demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors independently associated with treatment response.

The 3-week follow-up period was selected based on the established pharmacodynamic profile of valsartan, which achieves maximal antihypertensive effect within 2–4 weeks of initiation [15]. Additionally, this timeframe aligns with local clinical practice patterns in our setting, where initial follow-up is typically scheduled at 2–4 weeks to assess early response and tolerability. However, we acknowledge that current hypertension guidelines recommend reassessment at 4–12 weeks before making definitive treatment decisions. Therefore, our findings reflect early treatment response and should be interpreted as preliminary evidence requiring confirmation with longer-term follow-up.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Govt: Naseerullah Khan Babar Memorial Hospital Peshawar having Approval Number: No. 293.NKBMH, dated 01.01.2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their data and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All continuous variables (age, SBP, DBP, duration of hypertension) were found to be normally distributed (p > 0.05); therefore, data are expressed as means and standard deviations (mean ± SD).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between treatment groups using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

For longitudinal BP measurements, repeated measures ANOVA was performed with Greenhouse-Geisser correction for violations of sphericity. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction to identify significant differences between specific time points (Baseline vs. Week 1, Baseline vs. Week 2, Baseline vs. Week 3, Week 1 vs. Week 2, Week 2 vs. Week 3). Effect sizes (partial η²) were calculated for repeated measures analyses.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of target BP achievement. Variables included in the model were: age, sex, baseline SBP, baseline DBP, BMI, duration of hypertension (years), diabetes (yes/no), chronic kidney disease (yes/no), medication adherence (≥ 80% vs. < 80%), and treatment group. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

For all analyses, a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. The extent and pattern of missing data are reported in the Results section, with sensitivity analysis performed using multiple imputation to assess the robustness of findings under missing at random assumptions.

Missing data and sensitivity analysis

Complete data were available for 348 of 354 patients (98.3%). Missing data were limited to: Week 3 BP measurements (n = 2, 0.6%, both lost to follow-up), adherence data (n = 4, 1.1%, incomplete pill counts), and physical activity questionnaire (n = 6, 1.7%, incomplete responses). The pattern of missing data was assessed using Little’s MCAR test, which was not significant (χ² = 18.34, df = 15, p = 0.246), consistent with data missing completely at random.

Sensitivity analyses were performed using multiple imputation (5 imputed datasets) under the missing at random assumption. Results from imputed analyses were consistent with complete-case findings: age remained the strongest predictor of target BP achievement (pooled AOR per 10-year decrease: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.39–2.35, p < 0.001), and treatment group remained non-significant. Given the minimal missing data and consistent findings, complete-case results are presented throughout.

Effect of treatment on SBP and DBP

Table 3 shows changes in SBP and DBP over the 3-week treatment period. There was a significant reduction in both SBP and DBP across all time points (repeated measures ANOVA, p < 0.001). Mean SBP decreased from 157.50 ± 17.23 mmHg at baseline to 131.44 ± 20.40 mmHg at Week 3 (mean reduction: 26.06 mmHg, 95% CI: 24.12-28.00). Mean DBP decreased from 103.23 ± 10.17 mmHg to 85.08 ± 12.54 mmHg (mean reduction: 18.15 mmHg, 95% CI: 16.89–19.41).

Achievement of target blood pressure by treatment group

Table 4 presents cumulative achievement of target BP (< 140/90 mmHg) by treatment group at each week. At Week 3, 82.6% of patients on Valsartan 80 mg achieved target BP, compared to 70.5% on Valsartan/HCTZ 80/12.5 mg and 56.4% on Valsartan/HCTZ 160/12.5 mg. These differences reflect channeling bias, as patients with more severe hypertension at baseline, longer disease duration, and greater comorbidity burden were preferentially prescribed combination therapy. Therefore, unadjusted comparisons between treatment groups should not be interpreted as evidence of differential regimen efficacy.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study provides real-world evidence on factors influencing initial antihypertensive treatment response in a Pakistani population. The key findings are that younger age, shorter duration of hypertension, absence of diabetes and chronic kidney disease, and lower baseline systolic blood pressure are independent predictors of achieving target blood pressure in the short-term with Valsartan-based therapy. Importantly, after adjusting for these confounders, the specific treatment regimen (monotherapy versus combination therapy) was not independently associated with outcomes, though this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the non-randomized design and potential for residual confounding. The results highlight that patient characteristics are stronger determinants of early response than the choice of initial regimen in this observational cohort.

Clinical gaps and context of this study

Despite extensive literature on hypertension management, several gaps remain that this study addresses. First, there is a notable paucity of South Asian data, as most clinical trials of antihypertensive agents have been conducted in Western populations with limited representation from South Asia, where genetic predisposition, dietary patterns including high salt intake, and healthcare delivery systems differ substantially Gupta, [16]. This study provides much-needed region-specific evidence from a Pakistani cohort. Second, while age is known to influence hypertension outcomes, few studies have prospectively examined how age modifies response to Valsartan-based therapy specifically in routine South Asian clinical settings. Although the association between younger age and better antihypertensive response is well-established in general populations, the magnitude of this effect in our cohort—where each 10-year decrease in age nearly doubles the odds of treatment success—provides region-specific quantification that has direct clinical implications for treatment selection in similar settings. Third, the observation that longer disease duration predicts poorer response independent of age and baseline blood pressure suggests that early intervention may be critical for optimizing outcomes, a relationship that has been hypothesized but rarely demonstrated in prospective cohorts from this region. Fourth, unlike randomized controlled trials with strict inclusion criteria, this study reflects actual clinical practice where patients have multiple comorbidities and treatment decisions are individualized, which may enhance generalizability to similar healthcare settings, though the single-center design remains a limitation.

The clinical relevance of these findings should be framed cautiously. Rather than a one-size-fits-all approach, these preliminary data suggest that treatment may be better tailored based on patient age and hypertension duration. Younger patients with recent-onset hypertension in this cohort achieved excellent control with monotherapy, while older patients with longer disease duration had lower response rates even with more intensive therapy, potentially indicating that such patients could require more aggressive combination therapy from the outset or longer titration periods. The strong association between shorter disease duration and better outcomes provides supportive evidence for the importance of early detection and treatment, which is particularly relevant given that 54.8% of our cohort was aged 30–45 years, highlighting the shifting demographics of hypertension toward younger adults in this population. Furthermore, the independent negative impact of diabetes and chronic kidney disease on treatment response underscores the need for integrated care models that address multiple cardiovascular risk factors simultaneously, rather than focusing on blood pressure alone. The 82.6% target achievement rate with monotherapy in appropriately selected patients suggests that excellent short-term outcomes are achievable in real-world settings when therapy is individualized, though long-term durability remains to be established.

Comparison with previous studies

The observed female predominance of 58.0% in our cohort is consistent with studies showing that women are more likely to seek healthcare and be diagnosed with hypertension in South Asian populations Anchala, [17]. The high proportion of younger patients, with 54.8% aged 30–45 years, aligns with global trends of increasing hypertension prevalence in younger adults. Chia et al. [18] and Gupta et al. [16] reported similar patterns in Asian cohorts, attributing this to lifestyle changes, urbanization, and rising obesity rates, which underscores the need for targeted screening and prevention programs in younger populations.

The obesity prevalence of 27.1% (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) in our cohort is concerning but consistent with regional data. Gupta et al. [16] documented strong associations between obesity and hypertension in South Asian populations, with metabolic consequences that may complicate treatment response. The strong family history of hypertension reported by 68.6% of patients supports a genetic component, consistent with Moseti et al. [19] and Reddy et al. [20], who identified family history as a critical risk factor. However, in our adjusted analysis, family history was not independently associated with treatment response, suggesting that while genetics influence hypertension development, they may be less important for short-term treatment response once disease is established.

The significant blood pressure reductions observed in our study (26 mmHg systolic, 18 mmHg diastolic) are comparable to clinical trial data. Parati et al. [15] reported similar reductions with Valsartan therapy in the ValTop study, though our real-world cohort had higher baseline blood pressure and more comorbidities. The sustained reduction over three weeks aligns with the pharmacodynamic profile of Valsartan, which achieves maximal effect within 2–4 weeks [15]. However, the 3-week timeframe represents early response and may not reflect sustained control at 12 weeks or beyond.

The observation that combination therapy groups had numerically lower response rates in unadjusted analysis should not be interpreted as evidence that monotherapy is superior. Rather, this pattern reflects confounding by indication—a well-recognized phenomenon in observational research where patients with more severe disease, longer hypertension duration, and greater comorbidity burden are preferentially prescribed more intensive therapy Burnier, [8] ; Whelton, [21]. These patients were inherently more difficult to control from the outset. Our multivariable analysis confirms that when baseline severity, age, and comorbidity burden are accounted for, the specific treatment regimen was not independently associated with treatment success. However, given the non-randomized design and substantial baseline imbalances—including the small sample size in the 160/12.5 mg group (n = 43) with limited statistical power for between-group comparisons—these comparative findings should be interpreted with considerable caution, as residual confounding may persist despite statistical adjustment.

Clinical implications

These preliminary findings suggest several potential considerations for hypertension management in similar settings, though confirmatory studies are needed before practice changes can be recommended. First, age-stratified treatment approaches may warrant further investigation, whereby clinicians consider age when selecting initial therapy. Younger patients under 45 years with recent-onset hypertension in this cohort responded well to monotherapy with close follow-up for dose adjustment, while older patients over 60 years with longer disease duration had lower response rates and might benefit from earlier combination therapy or more gradual titration. Second, given that each year of shorter disease duration increased the odds of early success by 15%, healthcare systems could consider prioritizing early detection through community screening, workplace health programs, and integration with non-communicable disease services. Third, the independent negative impact of diabetes and chronic kidney disease suggests that blood pressure control cannot be achieved in isolation; integrated care models addressing glycemic control, renal protection, and cardiovascular risk reduction are essential. Fourth, the significant reductions observed at Week 1 and continued improvement through Week 3 are consistent with current guidelines recommending initial follow-up within 2–4 weeks to assess early response and adjust therapy. However, these findings require validation in longer-term studies with assessment at 12 weeks and beyond before definitive recommendations can be made.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the non-randomized design means that treatment regimens were assigned based on clinical judgment, introducing channeling bias. Patients with more severe hypertension preferentially received combination therapy, limiting direct comparability between regimens. While multivariable adjustment partially addresses this, residual confounding may persist. Second, the single-center setting, conducted at hospitals in Peshawar, may limit generalizability to other regions or primary care settings where patient characteristics and treatment patterns differ. Third, the short follow-up period of three weeks captures initial pharmacodynamic response but cannot address long-term blood pressure control, medication adherence, tolerability, or cardiovascular outcomes. Current hypertension guidelines Whelton et al., [21] recommend reassessment at 4–12 weeks before making definitive treatment decisions. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of early treatment response rather than established long-term effectiveness. Additionally, medication side effects that could lead to discontinuation typically manifest within the first few weeks of therapy; our study did not systematically collect adverse event data, which may affect the generalizability of the 3-week response rates to longer-term clinical practice. Extended follow-up is needed to determine whether early response predicts sustained control and to assess the durability of the age-stratified patterns observed. Fourth, while patients were diagnosed with primary hypertension based on standard clinical evaluation, comprehensive screening for all forms of secondary hypertension such as primary hyperaldosteronism or renal artery stenosis was not routinely performed, which may be particularly relevant in younger patients where secondary causes are more common. Fifth, lifestyle factors including dietary salt intake and physical activity were assessed by questionnaire, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias; objective measures such as urinary sodium excretion were not feasible in this real-world setting. Sixth, while pill counts were performed to assess adherence, this method may overestimate adherence compared to electronic monitoring or drug levels in biological samples. Seventh, although the overall sample size was adequate for the primary analysis, the Valsartan/HCTZ 160/12.5 mg subgroup was small (n = 43), and post-hoc power analysis indicated only 42% power for between-group comparisons, limiting the reliability of subgroup-specific conclusions. Eighth, heterogeneity in prior antihypertensive therapy and reasons for switching may have influenced baseline blood pressure and treatment response. Although we adjusted for baseline severity and tested prior treatment variables in multivariable models, residual confounding from treatment history cannot be fully excluded.

Future directions

Future research should address these limitations through multi-center prospective studies with longer follow-up (minimum 12 weeks, ideally 6–12 months) to assess sustained blood pressure control and cardiovascular outcomes. Randomized controlled trials comparing age-stratified treatment algorithms in South Asian populations are needed to confirm these observational findings before such approaches can be recommended for clinical practice. Incorporation of ambulatory blood pressure monitoring would provide valuable data on 24-hour control and circadian patterns. Objective adherence monitoring using electronic pill bottles or drug assays would strengthen adherence assessment. Systematic collection of adverse event data would clarify tolerability patterns across regimens. Genetic studies to identify polymorphisms influencing treatment response in this population may further refine personalized treatment approaches. Finally, implementation research is needed to translate these findings into clinical practice guidelines and decision support tools for use in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

This prospective cohort study from Pakistan demonstrates that younger age, shorter duration of hypertension, absence of diabetes and CKD, and lower baseline systolic blood pressure are independent predictors of favorable early blood pressure response to Valsartan-based therapy. However, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. The observed associations between patient characteristics and short-term treatment response warrant confirmation in longer-term, multi-center studies with rigorous randomization. The 3-week follow-up period limits conclusions about sustained blood pressure control, and the non-randomized design precludes definitive comparisons between treatment regimens. An explicit call for confirmatory randomized evidence in South Asian populations would substantially strengthen the evidence base for personalized hypertension management in the region. Until such evidence is available, treatment should be individualized based on patient age, comorbidity profile, and duration of hypertension, with close follow-up and dose adjustment as needed to achieve optimal outcomes.
