Strongly Polynomial Time Complexity of Policy Iteration for L ∞ L_{\infty} Robust MDPs
Ali Asadi, Krishnendu Chatterjee, Ehsan Goharshady, Mehrdad Karrabi, Alipasha Montaseri, Carlo Pagano

Abstract
Markov decision processes (MDPs) are a fundamental model in sequential decision making. Robust MDPs (RMDPs) extend this framework by allowing uncertainty in transition probabilities and optimizing against the worst-case realization of that uncertainty. In particular, ( s , a ) (s,a) -rectangular RMDPs with L ∞ L_{\infty} uncertainty sets form a fundamental and expressive model: they subsume classical MDPs and turn-based stochastic games. We consider this model with discounted payoffs. The existence of polynomial and strongly–polynomial time algorithms is a fundamental problem for these optimization models. For MDPs, linear programming yields polynomial-time algorithms for any arbitrary discount factor, and the seminal work of Ye established strongly–polynomial time for a fixed discount factor. The generalization of such results to RMDPs has remained an important open problem. In this work, we show that a robust policy iteration algorithm runs in strongly-polynomial time for ( s , a ) (s,a) -rectangular L ∞ L_{\infty} RMDPs with a constant (fixed) discount factor, resolving an important algorithmic question.

Introduction

Robust Markov Decision Processes

Markov decision processes (MDPs) are a fundamental model in sequential decision making, where an agent interacts with a finite-state stochastic environment
(
14
)
. Solving MDPs focuses on minimizing the expected payoff with respect to some given cost function. In the context of MDPs, a common assumption is that the transition function is known. Although this assumption is plausible from a theoretical perspective, it is not always justified in practice since MDPs are constructed from data, and the transition functions are estimated with a level of uncertainty. This issue has motivated the study of
Robust Markov decision processes
(RMDPs)
(
13
;
11
)
. RMDPs weaken the assumption by only assuming knowledge of some uncertainty set containing the true transition function. The goal of solving RMDPs is to minimize the worst-case expected payoff with respect to all possible choices of transition functions belonging to the uncertainty set.

Discounted-sum payoff.

The agent aims to minimize a
payoff function
which formally captures the desired behavior of the model. Discounted-sum payoffs are the most fundamental payoffs, i.e., given a cost function and discount factor, the payoff of a run (infinite trajectory of states and actions) is the sum of discounted costs of the run.

Policies and values.

Policies are recipes that define the choice of actions of the agent and the environment. They are functions that, given a finite sequence of states and actions (also known as history), return a distribution over actions. Given an RMDP and a discount factor, the payoff minimization is achieved over all possible choices of agent policies. The value of the agent at a state is the minimal payoff that the agent can guarantee against all policies of the environment.

\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets.

A central modeling choice in RMDPs is how we describe the uncertainty of transition probabilities. The literature considers several classes of uncertainty, including
(s,a)
-rectangular,
s
-rectangular, and more general non-rectangular models. In
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs, the environment selects transition probabilities separately for each state-action pair. In this paper, we adopt this rectangularity assumption for two reasons: (a) it matches a common data-driven setting where each state-action
(s,a)
, transition probabilities are estimated from local samples, so uncertainty decomposes across state-action pairs; and (b) it preserves a dynamic-programming structure, yielding robust Bellman operators
(
13
;
11
)
. We further assume
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets, i.e., for every state-action pair
(s,a)
, the true transition vector lies in an
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
ball of radius
\delta_{s,a}
around a nominal estimate
\hat{P}_{s,a}
.

Motivation.

The study of
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs with
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets are motivated by three reasons: (a) they generalize several classical formalisms, including MDPs, and turn-based stochastic games, where two adversarial players interact over a finite-state graph (see reduction in Appendix
A
); (b) they provide a simple and interpretable bound on the
maximum coordinate-wise
estimation error for the transition probabilities
(
9
;
4
)
; and (c) they keep the robust Bellman update computationally efficient
(
2
)
. Hence,
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs with
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets are a fundamental model.

Previous computational results.

In the study of MDPs and related optimization problems, the study of efficient algorithms is a central problem. For example, MDPs with discounted-sum objectives can be solved in polynomial-time via linear-programming
6
;
5
.
It has been a long-standing problem to obtain a strongly polynomial-time algorithm where the number of arithmetic operations is polynomial independent of the bit length.
For the special case of fixed discount factor, the seminal work of
16
obtains the first strongly–polynomial time algorithm, which has also been extended to
stochastic games by
10
. Efficient (i.e., polynomial and strongly-polynomial time) algorithms for RMDPs with
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets is a fundamental algorithmic problem. The only claimed result in the literature is a polynomial-time bound from
2
(which claims without proof that this follows from
10
).
However, this claim was an oversight as the mentioned technique does not yield the desired result for two reasons. First,
10
studies turn-based stochastic games, and the only known reduction, presented in
3
, is from
(s,a)
-rectangular
RMDPs (not
s
-rectangular RMDPs) to turn-based stochastic games. Second, even for
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs, the reduction is not of polynomial size in general. For each uncertainty set, it introduces an action for each corner of the corresponding polytope, and for
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets, the number of actions can be exponential.

Main open problem.

Hence, the existence of polynomial and more importantly strongly-polynomial time algorithms for
RMDPs with
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets, and a fixed discount factor, are an important open question in the study of sequential decision making under uncertainty.

Our contributions.

In this work, we affirmatively answer the above open problems. In particular, we show that a robust policy iteration algorithm
terminates in strongly polynomial time, which is a generalization of the result of
10
.

In this work we focus on the exact value computation for RMDPs with
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets. There are many related works that consider a more general model, but for the value approximation problem.

-rectangular RMDPs with discounted-sum payoffs have been extensively studied in the literature (see

Preliminaries and Model Description

Markov Chains.

A Markov chain (MC) is a tuple
M=(\mathcal{S},c,P)
where
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{S}|\}
is the set of states,
c\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
specifies the cost of visiting each state, and
P\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
denotes the transition probabilities. The transition probability function
P
can be seen as an
n\times n
matrix where
P_{s,t}=P(s)[t]
. Similarly, the cost function
c
can be presented as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The semantics of MCs are defined over sequences
\pi=\langle\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\dots\rangle
of states. Using
\Pr[\pi_{i+1}|\pi_{i}]=P_{\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}}
, the cylinder construction of
1
, yields a probability distribution over the set of all infinite sequences of states. Given a discount factor
\gamma
, the value function
\boldsymbol{v}_{M}(s)
for each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is defined as the expected discounted total cost of sequences that start at
s
:

\boldsymbol{v}_{M}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[(1-\gamma)\sum_{i}\gamma^{i}c(\pi_{i})|\pi_{0}=s\big]=\big[(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P)^{-1}c\big](s)

(1)

Robust MCs.

Robust MCs (RMCs) are an extension of MCs by considering a particular uncertainty in the transition probabilities. Formally, an RMC is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},c,\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
where
\mathcal{S}
and
c
are the same as in MCs,
\textit{Succ}\colon\mathcal{S}\to 2^{\mathcal{S}}
indicates the set of possible successors of each state, and
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{S}\to 2^{\Delta(\mathcal{S})}
is the uncertain transition function satisfying
\mathcal{P}(s)\subseteq{\Delta(\textit{Succ}(s))}
. The semantics of RMCs are defined with respect to environment policies. A stationary environment policy is a function
\rho\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
mapping each state to an element of its uncertainty set. Formally,
\rho(s)\in\mathcal{P}(s)
for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
. By fixing an environment policy
\rho
, the uncertainty of the RMC is resolved, yielding an MC
\mathcal{M}^{\rho}=(\mathcal{S},c,\mathcal{P}^{\rho})
. The value of a policy
\rho
is then defined as the value of its induced MC, i.e.
\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{M}^{\rho}}(s)
. The goal of the environment is to choose a policy that maximizes the expected discounted cost. Formally, an optimal policy
\rho^{*}
is a policy that satisfies
\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{*}}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)=\max_{\rho}\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
. We drop the subscript
\mathcal{M}
whenever it is clear from the context and use
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
to denote
\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{*}}
.

Robust Markov Decision Processes.

It is possible to further extend RMCs into robust Markov Decision Processes (RMDPs) by considering a finite action space for the agent. Formally, an RMDP is a tuple
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{S},c,\mathcal{A},\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
where
\mathcal{S}
and
c
are defined similarly as before,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set of actions,
\textit{Succ}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to 2^{\mathcal{S}}
, and
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to 2^{\Delta(\mathcal{S})}
is the uncertain transition function where
\mathcal{P}(s,a)\subseteq{\Delta(\textit{Succ}(s,a))}
for each state-action pair
(s,a)
. The semantics of RMDPs are defined with respect to an agent policy
\sigma\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
that maps each state to a distribution over the action-set
\mathcal{A}
. By fixing such an agent policy, the agent’s choices are resolved, leaving an RMC
\mathcal{R}^{\sigma}=(\mathcal{S},c,\textit{Succ}^{\sigma},\mathcal{P}^{\sigma})
. The value of an agent policy is defined as the value of its induced RMC, i.e.
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{R}^{\sigma}}(s)
. The agent’s objective is to find a policy that minimizes the worst-case expected cost. The optimal agent policy is then denoted by
\sigma^{*}
and satisfies:

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\sigma^{*}}(s)=\min_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\sigma}}(s)=\min_{\sigma}\max_{\rho}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{R}^{\sigma,\rho}}(s)=\min_{\sigma}\max_{\rho}\big[(\mathbb{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P}^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}c\big](s)

(2)

When
\mathcal{R}
is clear from the context, we use
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}(s)
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma,\rho}(s)
to denote
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{R}^{\sigma}}(s)
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{R}^{\sigma,\rho}}(s)
, respectively, for agent policy
\sigma
and environment policy
\rho
. We use
\sigma^{*}
and
\rho^{*}
for the optimal agent policy and the best environment response, respectively, satisfying
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)=\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{*}}(s)=\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{*},\rho^{*}}(s)
.

Assumption 1. (Rectangularity)

The uncertainty considered in this paper is also known as
(s,a)
-rectangular uncertainty since the environment can observe the agent’s action before choosing a transition function from the uncertainty set. Other classes of uncertainty, such as
s
-rectangularity, have been studied in the literature (
15
) but are not the focus of our work.

Assumption 2. (
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
Uncertainty Sets)

In this work, we focus on RMCs and RMDPs whose uncertainty sets are defined as
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
balls around a nominal transition function. Formally, in an
L_{\infty}
RMC
\mathcal{M}
, for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists non-negative real number
\delta_{s}
and a nominal probabilistic transition function
\hat{P}_{s}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
such that
\mathcal{P}(s)=\{P\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})|\|P-\hat{P}_{s}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{s}\}
. Similarly, in an
L_{\infty}
RMDP, for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a non-negative real number
\delta_{s,a}
and nominal transition function
\hat{P}_{s,a}
such that
\mathcal{P}(s,a)=\{P\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})|\|P-\hat{P}_{s,a}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{s,a}\}
. For the rest of the paper, we assume all the uncertainty sets are of this type.

Policy Types.

The policies considered in the above definitions are positional (their value depends only on the current state of the model). In general, policies can also depend on the history of visited states and chosen actions. However, it is a classical result that in discounted-sum RMDPs, positional (memoryless) deterministic optimal policies exist (
11
;
13
). Hence, in the rest of the paper, all the policies are assumed to be positional and deterministic, i.e. an agent policy in an RMDP is a map
\sigma\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
and an environment policy is a map
\rho\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
where
\rho(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)
.

Strongly Polynomial Algorithms.

An algorithm is strongly polynomial if the following two conditions hold: (i) The number of arithmetic operations is bounded by a polynomial in the number of input variables and constraints, and (ii) the size of all numbers during the computation is bounded by a polynomial in the input size.

Recall that an algorithm runs in polynomial time if its runtime is polynomial in the input (bit-length) size. Hence, in polynomial time algorithms, the runtime of the algorithm might depend on the bit-size of the input coefficients. In contrast, in strongly polynomial algorithms, the number of arithmetic operations is independent of the numerical values of the coefficients, and any dependence on the input coefficients is limited to their unavoidable contribution to the input encoding length.

Problem Statement.

Given an RMC
\mathcal{M}
or an RMDP
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{S},c,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
that satisfies Assumptions 1 and 2, algorithms such as
value iteration
and
policy iteration
are designed for computing the value function
\boldsymbol{v}
together with the optimal agent policy
\sigma^{*}
and environment policy
\rho^{*}
that correspond to
\boldsymbol{v}
. Our goal in this work is to study the policy iteration method (explained in Section
3
) and to show that it is strongly polynomial.

4
Robust Markov Chains

In this section, we focus our attention on RMCs and their corresponding policy iteration algorithm
RMC-PI
presented in Algorithm
1
. Specifically, we assume
RMC-PI
is executed on an RMC
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},c,\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
with an
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty set,
|\mathcal{S}|=n
and a constant discount factor
\gamma
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the input RMC in an execution of
RMC-PI
. The Bellman operator for computing the optimal values in
\mathcal{M}
is defined as:

(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\max_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s)}\mathbf{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}.

(3)

We first note that
RMC-PI
converges monotonically to the unique fixed-point of
\mathcal{T}
at an exponential rate. This is a classical result in the analysis of Markov models (
14
;
11
), presented in Lemma
1
(proved in Appendix
B
) and Lemma
2
(proved in Appendix
C
).

Lemma 1
.

The following statements hold:

•

For every
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\,\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}.

•

For every environment policy
\rho
with value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}
,
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}
.

•

There exists a unique vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

Using the improvement properties established in Lemma
1
, we show that the sequence of values generated by
RMC-PI
is non-decreasing and converging at an exponential rate:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\dots
be the sequence of policies generated by
RMC-PI
(Algorithm
1
), and let
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
be the value of
\rho^{t}
. Then the following statements hold for each
t
:

•

\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}

•

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

Lemma
2
establishes that policy iteration converges exponentially fast to the optimal policy. Given the exponential upper-bound on the number of homotopic policies that Algorithm
2
can return, Lemma
2
immediately implies that
RMC-PI
terminates in finite time and computes the optimal environment policy
\rho^{*}
together with its value function
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

To show that
RMC-PI
is strongly polynomial, we must show two statements: (i) it terminates within a polynomial number of arithmetic operations, and (ii) the bit-size of all intermediate values remains polynomial in the input size. Both conditions are satisfied if the total number of iterations of
RMC-PI
is bounded by a polynomial in
|\mathcal{S}|
. Specifically, the former is implied by the fact that both policy evaluation and policy improvement are themselves polynomial. The latter holds because the bit size of the stored numbers increases by at most
\mathit{size}(\gamma)
in each iteration (due to the
\gamma
factor in policy evaluation). Hence, after
t
steps, the bit-size of each stored number is increased by at most
t\times\mathit{size}(\gamma)
. So, we focus on showing that the number of iterations of
RMC-PI
is polynomial in
|\mathcal{S}|
.

To this end, our goal is to bound the change in
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
compared to
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
and then use the bound to obtain bounds on the maximum number of iterations before termination. Given the structure of the policies returned by the homotopy algorithm (Algorithm
2
), we introduce a specific
potential function

f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
as an estimate on how much the value of
\rho
can be improved by donating some probability mass from
\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}
to
\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime}}
:

Definition 3

(
Potential Function
)
.

Given a policy
\rho
, for any
s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
define:

f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}):=\min\!\left(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime}},\;\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s^{\prime\prime}}\right),

Intuitively, the
\min
fragment in the definition of the potential function specifies the probability mass being donated to
\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime}}
from
\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}
, and the
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
fragment quantifies the change in values.

We now establish a lower bound on the optimality gap in terms of the potential function. This result (proved in Appendix
D
) connects the potential function to the actual difference in values.

Lemma 4
.

For every policy
\rho
, and states
s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
it holds that

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}_{s}\geq\gamma\,f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}).

Next, we derive an upper-bound on the distance between
\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
based on the potential of
\rho
. We view the difference between the optimal transition matrix and the current policy’s matrix as a set of probability mass transfers. By bounding the impact of these transfers using the potential function, we link the global error (distance to optimal
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
) back to the local slack (potential function).

Lemma 5
.

Let
\rho
be a policy. Let
s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
be the states that maximize
f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
Then:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{n^{2}\gamma}{1-\gamma}f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}).

Proof Sketch.

We first show that

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P}^{\rho})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|(\mathcal{P}^{*}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

and proceed by showing an upper-bound on each factor of the RHS. (i) We show:
\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma\mathcal{P}^{\rho})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
by using the geometric series sum. (ii) We then show that
\left\|(\mathcal{P}^{*}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq n^{2}f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
by decomposing
\mathcal{P}^{*}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho}
into a series of at most
n^{2}

mass transfer
operations each contributing at most
f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
to the LHS. The full proof is presented in Appendix
E
.

\blacksquare

As a byproduct of Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
, we can now obtain a bound between
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}
based on the potential functions of
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
:

Lemma 6
.

Assume
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are two policies. Let
s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
be the states that maximize
f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
.
Suppose further that
\rho^{\prime}
satisfies:

\min\left(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{\prime}}_{s,s^{\prime}},\;\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{\prime}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\left(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime}},\;\mathcal{P}^{\rho}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\right).

Then
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{2n^{2}}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We chain the upper bound of Lemma
5
(for
\rho
) and the lower bound of Lemma
4
(for
\rho^{\prime}
) to derive the result. First, we apply the upper bound to the policy
\rho
:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2}\gamma}{1-\gamma}f_{\rho}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})

(By Lemma
5
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2}\gamma}{1-\gamma}\cdot 2f_{\rho^{\prime}}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})

(By definition of
f_{\rho^{\prime}}
and assumption )

Finally, we link this to the value error of
\rho^{\prime}
using the lower bound:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2n^{2}}{1-\gamma}\left(\gamma f_{\rho^{\prime}}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\right)\leq\frac{2n^{2}}{1-\gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{\prime}}_{s}\right)\leq\frac{2n^{2}}{1-\gamma}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\\

Where the second inequality comes from Lemma
4
and the third one is due to the definition of
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
norm. Rearranging the terms gives the claimed bound:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{2n^{2}}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}.

∎

We now show that the algorithm cannot get “stuck” making small updates to the same constraints. Specifically, we prove that within a fixed window of
L
steps, the difference between the probability at
\mathcal{P}^{*}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\rho}
on the critical constraint must be at least halved. The intuition relies on a contradiction: if this difference remained large, Lemma
6
would imply that the value error also remains large. However, we know from Lemma
2
that the value error contracts exponentially. Therefore, this difference must decrease significantly to match this contraction.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\rho^{0},\ldots,\rho^{T}
be the sequence of policies generated by the policy iteration algorithm.
Define the step threshold
L:=\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{2n^{2}}\right)
.
For a specific policy
\rho^{t}
, let
(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})=\argmax_{s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}f_{\rho^{t}}(s,s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
be the triple with the maximum potential in
\rho^{t}
. Then, in any subsequent policy
\rho^{l}
with
l>t+L
, we have:

\min\!\left(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{l}}_{s,s^{\prime}},\;\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{l}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\!\left(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{t}}_{s,s^{\prime}},\;\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{t}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\right).

Lemma
7
(proved in Appendix
F
) shows that after every
L
iterations of
RMC-PI
, the amount of
\min(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{l}}_{s,s^{\prime}},\mathcal{P}^{\rho^{l}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}-\mathcal{P}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}})
reduces by at least half. Specifically, this means that the
most significant bit (MSB)
in its binary presentation changes by at least one index. Our goal is to show that there are polynomially many possible MSBs for this value, hence it can only change a polynomial amount of times. To this end, we introduce the following combinatorial lemma (proved in Appendix
G
):

Lemma 8
.

Let
X
be a finite set of nonnegative real numbers. Define the set of all unitary signed subset sums as:

A(X)=\left\{\left|\sum_{x\in X}g(x)\cdot x\right|\;\middle|\;g:X\to\{-1,0,1\}\right\}.

Define the
degree
of a set of positive real numbers
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
as the number of distinct MSBs in
Y
:

\mathrm{Deg}(Y)=\left|\left\{\lfloor\log_{2}y\rfloor\;\middle|\;y\in Y\right\}\right|.

Then for all
X
, it holds that

\mathrm{Deg}(A(X)\setminus\{0\})\in\mathcal{O}(|X|\log|X|).

Lemma
8
shows that for a set
X
of non-negative reals, the number of MSBs in the set
A(X)
is a polynomial bounded by a polynomial in the size of
X
. We derive the final complexity bound by combining the geometric contraction of the discrepancy with the finite number of possible discrepancy values.

Theorem 9
.

RMC-PI
terminates with an optimal policy in

\mathcal{O}\left(n^{4}\log n\cdot\frac{\log\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{n^{2}}\right)}{\log{\gamma}}\right)

iterations where
n=|\mathcal{S}|
.

Proof Sketch.

The proof is done in three steps:

•

Geometric Contraction:
Lemma
7
shows that for the maximizing triple, the discrepancy between the current and optimal transition function decreases by a factor of at least two every
L
steps.

•

Combinatorial Finiteness:
Lemma
8
restricts the possible values of the transition probabilities. These values are generated by a finite set of parameters (the nominal probabilities and uncertainty radii). Specifically, by invoking Lemma
8
on a set with
O(n)
elements, we show that there are
O(n\log n)
possible MSBs for the discrepancy.

•

There are at most
n^{3}
possibilities for the triple with the highest discrepancy. Hence, after every
O(n^{3}\cdot L)
iterations, the MSB of the highest discrepancy decreases by at least one. Given that there are
O(n\log n)
possible different MSBs, the algorithm must terminate within
O(n^{4}\log n\cdot L)
iterations.

Full proof is available in Appendix
H
.

\blacksquare

Since the number of iterations required by the
RMC-PI
algorithm (Algorithm
1
) is polynomial, we can conclude that
RMC-PI
is a strongly polynomial algorithm.

Corollary 10
.

RMC-PI
is strongly polynomial given an RMC with
n
states and constant discount factor
\gamma
.

5
Robust Markov Decision Processes

In this section, we analyze
RMDP-PI
(Algorithm
3
) and its convergence to the optimal policy. Let
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{S},c,\mathcal{A},\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
be the input RMDP where
|\mathcal{S}|=n
,
|\mathcal{A}|=m
and
\mathcal{P}
is an
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertaninty set. We follow a pipeline similar to our analysis of
RMC-PI
in Section
4
: We first recall the Bellman operator and its properties (Lemma
11
), which imply the exponential convergence rate of
RMDP-PI
(Lemma
11
). We then define a potential function (Definition
13
) and show a lower and an upper bound on the value error of policies based on their potentials (Lemma
14
and Lemma
14
). Finally, we use the obtained bounds to show that
RMDP-PI
terminates after a polynomial number of iterations (Theorem
17
).

The Bellman optimality operator
\mathcal{T}
for
\mathcal{R}
is defined as:

(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\;\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\;\max_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}{\mathbf{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}}.

We first establish that
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction mapping with a unique fixed point. This result is standard in the literature of robust dynamic programming (
11
). The full proof of Lemma
11
is available in Appendix
I
.

Lemma 11
.

The following statements hold:

•

For all
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\,\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}
.

•

For every agent policy
\sigma
with value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
,
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
.

•

There exists a unique
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

Next, we prove that the sequence of value functions generated by Policy Iteration is non-increasing and exponentially converging to the optimal value. This monotonicity ensures that the algorithm consistently improves the policy. The full proof of Lemma
12
is available in Appendix
J
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\sigma^{t}
and
\sigma^{t+1}
be sequence of policies generated by
RMDP-PI
(Algorithm
3
) and let
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
be the value of
\sigma^{t}
. Then the following statements hold for each
t
:

•

\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}

•

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

We proceed by defining the potential function. Intuitively, the potential of an action
a
in state
s
, estimates the amount of incurred cost if one would deviate from the optimal policy
\sigma^{*}
by choosing
a
at
s
instead of
\sigma^{*}(s)
.

Definition 13

(
Potential function
)
.

Given a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
the potential function
f(s,a)
is defined as follows:

f(s,a):=\max_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}{\mathbf{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\max_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathbf{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}

Next, similar to Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
, we establish a lower-bound and an upper-bound on the difference of a policy value vector and the optimal value in terms of the potential function. The full proof of Lemma
14
is available in Appendix
K
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\sigma
be an arbitrary policy. The following hold:

•

(Lower-bound)
If
\sigma(s)=a
for some state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}\geq\gamma f(s,a)
.

•

(Upper-bound)
Suppose
\hat{s}:=\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma(s))
. Then,
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}f(\hat{s},\sigma(\hat{s}))
.

We now combine the lower and upper bounds to derive a relationship between the value errors of two different policies. Specifically, we show that if the action chosen at the state with maximum potential remains unchanged between two policies, the value error cannot decrease by more than a factor of
1-\gamma
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
be two arbitrary policies such that
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}
. Let
\hat{s}=\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{f(s,\sigma(s))}
. Also assume that
\sigma^{\prime}(\hat{s})=\sigma(\hat{s})
. Then we have that:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\geq(1-\gamma)\cdot\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

Proof.

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\geq(\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\hat{s}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\hat{s}})\geq\gamma f(\hat{s},\sigma^{\prime}(\hat{s}))=\gamma f(\hat{s},\sigma(\hat{s}))\geq(1-\gamma)\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

where the second and the last inequalities are due to the bounds in Lemma
14
.
∎

We now utilize the exponential convergence rate established in Lemma
12
to derive that an action with maximum potential at some iteration cannot persist for more than
L
iterations. The full proof of Lemma
16
is available in Appendix
L
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\sigma^{0},\ldots,\sigma^{T}
be the sequence of agent policies generated by
RMDP-PI
. Let
L=\log_{\gamma}\left(1-\gamma\right)
and for each policy
\sigma^{l}
, let
\hat{s}=\argmax_{s}f(s,\sigma^{l}(s))
. For all
k>l+L
, it holds that
\sigma^{k}(\hat{s})\neq\sigma^{l}(\hat{s})
.

Finally, we aggregate the elimination steps to provide a polynomial upper bound on the total number of iterations. Since an action is eliminated from the set of potential maximizers every
L
steps, the algorithm must terminate within
O(n\cdot m\cdot L)
iterations for
n=|\mathcal{S}|
and
m=|\mathcal{A}|
. The full proof of Lemma
17
is available in Appendix
M
.

Theorem 17
.

RMDP-PI
terminates with an optimal policy in
\mathcal{O}\left(n\cdot m\cdot\frac{\log{(1-\gamma)}}{\log{\gamma}}\right)
iterations.

The policy evaluation step of
RMDP-PI
is strongly polynomial due to Corollary
10
. The policy improvement is also trivially strongly polynomial, implying the following corollary immediately:

Corollary 18
.

RMDP-PI
is strongly polynomial given an RMDP
\mathcal{R}
with
n
states,
m
actions and constant discount factor
\gamma
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied discounted
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs with
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty sets, a model that captures classical MDPs and turn-based stochastic games. Our main contribution is to resolve a fundamental algorithmic open problem for this setting. we showed that a robust policy iteration algorithm terminates in strongly polynomial time when the discount factor is fixed. Several directions remain open and relevant. While the present work establishes the theoretical
foundations, it is an interesting future direction to complement the worst-case analysis with empirical studies of robust policy iteration on data-driven uncertainty sets, and to explore whether the potential-based arguments suggest improved practical variants or stopping criteria. Another related major question is whether policy iteration for MDPs admits polynomial or strongly polynomial bounds.
7
showed that one variant can take exponentially many iterations for discounted-sum MDPs, leaving open whether other variants run in polynomial time.

Appendix A
Reduction from Turn Based Stochastic Games to
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
RMDPs

A turn based stochastic game
G
is defined as a tuple
(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{r},c,\textit{Succ},P)
, where

•

\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{r}
are disjoint sets.
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is a set of player-1 states,
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is a set of player-2 states, and
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is a set of randomized states. Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\uplus\mathcal{S}_{2}\uplus\mathcal{S}_{r}

•

c\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is a cost function

•

\textit{Succ}\colon\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}\to 2^{\mathcal{S}}
specifies the set of possible successors from each
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
state.

•

P\colon\mathcal{S}_{r}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the randomized transition function from
\mathcal{S}_{r}
states.

The semantics are defined by player strategies as follows: a randomized strategy
\sigma_{i}
of player-
i
is a function
\sigma_{i}\colon\mathcal{S}_{i}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
where
\textit{support}(\pi_{i}(s))\subseteq\textit{Succ}(s)
. A run of
G
is then an infinite sequence
\pi_{0},\pi_{1}\dots
where if
\pi_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
\Pr[\pi_{j+1}|\pi_{j}]=\sigma_{i}(\pi_{j})[\pi_{j+1}]
, and if
\pi_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
, then
\Pr[\pi_{j+1}|\pi_{j}]=P(\pi_{j})[\pi_{j+1}]
. The cylinder construction of
1
synthesizes the full probability distribution over infinite sequences of states in
G
. For a discount factor
\gamma
, the discounted sum of costs over a run
\pi
is then defined as

c(\pi)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}c(\pi_{j})

The goal of player-1 is to minimize the expected cost of the induced run while player-2 wants to maximize it. Hence the value of the game is defined as

\boldsymbol{v}_{G}(s)=\min_{\sigma_{1}}\max_{\sigma_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})}[c(\pi)]

We show that any game
G=(\mathcal{S}^{G}_{1},\mathcal{S}^{G}_{2},\mathcal{S}^{G}_{r},c^{G},\textit{Succ}^{G},P^{G})
is equivalent to an RMDP
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{R}},c^{\mathcal{R}},\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{R}},\textit{Succ}^{\mathcal{R}},\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{R}})
where
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{R}}
is an
\mathbf{L_{\infty}}
uncertainty set specified by the nominal transition probabilities
\hat{P}^{\mathcal{R}}(s,a)
and the uncertainty radii
\delta(s,a)
for each state-action pair
(s,a)
. The RMDP
\mathcal{R}
is constructed as follows:

•

\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{S}^{G}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}^{G}_{2}\cup\mathcal{S}^{G}_{r}

•

c^{\mathcal{R}}=c^{G}

•

For each
s\in\mathcal{S}^{G}_{1}
and
s^{\prime}\in\textit{Succ}^{G}(s)
, there is an action
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{R}}
where
\textit{Succ}^{\mathcal{R}}(s,a)=\{s^{\prime}\}
,
\hat{P}^{\mathcal{R}}(s,a)[s^{\prime}]=1
and
\delta(s,a)=0

•

For each
s\in\mathcal{S}^{G}_{r}
, there is an action
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{R}}
where
\textit{Succ}^{\mathcal{R}}(s,a)=\textit{support}(P^{G}(s))
,
\hat{P}^{\mathcal{R}}(s,a)[s^{\prime}]=P^{G}(s)[s^{\prime}]
and
\delta^{\mathcal{R}}(s,a)=0
.

•

For each
s\in\mathcal{S}^{G}_{2}
, there is an action

Let us formalize the notion of linear independence explained above.

Definition 23
.

Let
N
be a positive integer and
C_{1}
a positive real number. We say that a collection
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
of real numbers is
C_{1}
-linearly independent in case

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\cdot\underline{x}\neq 0,

for all non-zero vectors
\underline{x}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
such that

||\underline{x}||_{\infty}\leq C_{1}.

They are said
C_{1}
-linearly dependent if they are not
C_{1}
-linearly independent.

The following fact can be viewed as a criterion for “linear independence" of a set of real numbers over a set of coefficients with bounded size: it is the analogue of the observation that over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[T]
a set of non-zero polynomials of different degrees must be linearly independent.

Proposition 24
.

Let
C_{1}
be a positive real. Then there exists a positive integer
D
such that the following holds. Let
N\geq 2
be an integer. Let
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
y_{1}\neq 0
and for each
1\leq i<N
, we have that
\textup{deg}(y_{i+1})>D+\textup{deg}(y_{i})
.

Then we have that
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
is
C_{1}
-linear independent.

Proof.

Let
\underline{x}
be in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
such that

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\cdot\underline{x}=0.

It will suffice to show that
x_{N}=0
, and then proceed by induction on
N
, which will give that
x_{i}=0
for each
i\geq 2
. At that point it must follow that
x_{1}=0
, since
y_{1}\neq 0
by assumption.

To this end notice that, as soon as
D
is sufficiently large we have the preliminary bound

|y_{i+1}|>2\cdot|y_{i}|,

for all
1\leq i<N
. This implies in particular that

|y_{N-1}|>\sum_{1\leq i\leq N-2}|y_{i}|.

On the other hand, when
D
is sufficiently large, we also have the bound

|y_{N}|>2\cdot C\cdot|y_{N-1}|.

Thus we deduce that, if
x_{N}
is non-zero, since it is an integer, it must be that
|x_{N}\cdot y_{N}|\geq|y_{N}|
and therefore

|x_{N}||y_{N}|\geq|y_{N}|>2C\cdot|y_{N-1}|\geq C\cdot(|y_{N-1}|+\ldots+|y_{1}|)\geq|\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}x_{i}y_{i}|,

which directly contradicts

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\cdot\underline{x}=0.

This contradiction stemmed from the assumption that
x_{N}\neq 0
, and hence it must be that
x_{N}=0
. Now we can proceed inductively on the rest of the coefficients and find that they are all
0
, where in the base case we use that
y_{1}\neq 0
by assumption, thus yielding that
\underline{x}=0
.
∎

We need the following application of Siegel’s lemma.

Proposition 25
.

There exists an absolute constant
C_{0}>0
such that the following holds. For all positive integers
n
and any real number

at least
1
, and any matrix

with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
with

Siegel’s lemma tells us that there is a vector

of norm at most

We utilize Siegel’s Lemma to establish a Gap Principle.

Lemma 27

(Siegel’s Lemma)
.

Let
B
be an
M\times N
matrix with integer entries, with
N>M>0
. Let
H\geq 1
be an upper bound for the absolute values of the entries of
B
. Then there exists a non-zero integer vector
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
such that
Bc=0
and

\|c\|_{\infty}\leq(NH)^{M/(N-M)}.

Proof.

See, for example, M. Hindry and J. H. Silverman, Diophantine Geometry: An Introduction, Springer, 2000, Part D, Lemma 4.1.
∎

A sequence of positive real numbers
(z_{i})
is called
R
-separated if
z_{i+1}/z_{i}\geq R
for all
i
.

Lemma 28

(Gap Principle)
.

Let
k\geq 1,C\geq 1
. Let
R=2kC+1
. Any
R
-separated sequence of distinct positive elements in
A(X,C)
has length
m\leq 2k-1
.

Proof.

Let
C^{\prime}=2kC
. Note that
R=C^{\prime}+1
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists an
R
-separated sequence
0<z_{1}<z_{2}<\dots<z_{m}
in
A(X,C)
with
m=2k
.

For each
i=1,\dots,m
,
z_{i}\in A(X,C)
, so
z_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}x_{j}
with
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|a_{ij}|\leq C
. Let
v_{i}=(a_{i1},\dots,a_{ik})
.

Let
B
be the
k\times m
matrix whose columns are the vectors
v_{i}
. The entries of
B
are bounded by
H=C
. We apply Lemma
27
with
M=k
and
N=m=2k
. Since

, there exists a non-zero integer vector

such that

. The bound on the coefficients is

\|c\|_{\infty}\leq(NH)^{M/(N-M)}=(2kC)^{k/(2k-k)}=2kC=C^{\prime}.

The condition
Bc=0
implies a linear relation among the elements

:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}z_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}a_{ij}\right)=0.

Let
J
be the largest index such that
c_{J}\neq 0
. Then
c_{J}z_{J}=-\sum_{i=1}^{J-1}c_{i}z_{i}
. Taking absolute values (and noting

):

|c_{J}|z_{J}=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{J-1}c_{i}z_{i}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{J-1}|c_{i}|z_{i}\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{J-1}z_{i}.

Since the sequence

is

-separated and increasing,

\sum_{i=1}^{J-1}z_{i}\leq z_{J-1}\sum_{l=0}^{J-2}R^{-l}<z_{J-1}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}R^{-l}=z_{J-1}\frac{R}{R-1}.

Also, by the separation property,

. Combining these inequalities:

|c_{J}|Rz_{J-1}\leq|c_{J}|z_{J}<C^{\prime}z_{J-1}\frac{R}{R-1}.

Since
z_{J-1}>0
and
R>0
, we can divide by
Rz_{J-1}
:

|c_{J}|<\frac{C^{\prime}}{R-1}.

We chose
R=C^{\prime}+1
, so
R-1=C^{\prime}
.

|c_{J}|<\frac{C^{\prime}}{C^{\prime}}=1.

This contradicts the fact that

is a non-zero integer. Therefore, the length of the sequence must be
m\leq 2k-1
.

.

. Let

\rho

P^{*}
. We begin by expanding the value difference using the value definition from Equation

:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}\mathbf{c}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bigg((\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\bigg)\mathbf{c}\right\|_{\infty}

We apply the matrix identity
A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}
to the term inside the norm:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}\bigg((\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})\bigg)(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}(P^{\sigma,\rho}-P^{*})(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right\|_{\infty}

Substituting back the definition of the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}
and using the submultiplicativity of the

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}(P^{\sigma,\rho}-P^{*})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\,\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\,\left\|(P^{\sigma,\rho}-P^{*})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

We bound the first term using the Neumann series expansion. Since
P^{\sigma,\rho}
is a stochastic matrix,
\left\|(P^{\sigma,\rho})^{i}\right\|_{\infty}=1
, and the series converges as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\gamma P^{\sigma,\rho})^{i}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}\left\|(P^{\sigma,\rho})^{i}\right\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

Appendix L
Proof of Lemma
16

Proof.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that there exists
k>l+L
such that
\sigma^{k}(\hat{s})=\sigma^{l}(\hat{s})
. Since the value vectors are non-increasing,
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{k}}
. Applying Lemma
15
:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{k}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\geq(1-\gamma)\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

However, from the global convergence rate (Lemma
12
), we know:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{k}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k-l}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

We note that if
\|\mathbf{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\mathbf{v}^{*}\|_{\infty}=0
, then
\sigma^{l}
is optimal, and the algorithm terminates at iteration
l
. Since the sequence continues to
k>l
, we must have
\|\mathbf{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\mathbf{v}^{*}\|_{\infty}>0
. We can therefore divide both inequalities by this strictly positive term to obtain:

\log_{\gamma}(1-\gamma)\geq k-l\implies L\geq k-l.

This contradicts the assumption that
k>l+L
.
∎

Appendix M
Proof of Theorem
17

Proof.

There are exactly
n\cdot m
distinct state-action pairs. According to Lemma
16
, if a specific pair
(\hat{s},\hat{a})
is the critical maximizer of the potential function at iteration
l
, it cannot be reused at state
\hat{s}
after
L
subsequent iterations. So, every
L
steps, at least one state-action pair is removed from the set of possible critical maximizers. The total number of iterations
T
is bounded by:

T\leq n\cdot m\cdot L=n\cdot m\cdot\log_{\gamma}(1-\gamma)=n\cdot m\cdot\frac{\log{1-\gamma}}{\log{\gamma}}.

∎
