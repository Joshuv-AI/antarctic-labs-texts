Neural Identification of Feedback-Stabilized Nonlinear Systems
Mahrokh G. Boroujeni, Laura Meroi, Leonardo Massai, Clara L. Galimberti, and Giancarlo Ferrari-Trecate

Abstract
Neural networks have demonstrated remarkable success in modeling nonlinear dynamical systems. However, identifying these systems from closed-loop experimental data remains a challenge due to the correlations induced by the feedback loop. Traditional nonlinear closed-loop system identification methods struggle with reliance on precise noise models, robustness to data variations, or computational feasibility. Additionally, it is essential to ensure that the identified model is stabilized by the same controller used during data collection, ensuring alignment with the true system’s closed-loop behavior. The dual Youla parameterization provides a promising solution for linear systems, offering statistical guarantees and closed-loop stability. However, extending this approach to nonlinear systems presents additional complexities. In this work, we propose a computationally tractable framework for identifying complex, potentially unstable systems while ensuring closed-loop stability using a complete parameterization of systems stabilized by a given controller. We establish asymptotic consistency in the linear case and validate our method through numerical comparisons, demonstrating superior accuracy over direct identification baselines and compatibility with the true system in stability properties.



I
Introduction

Neural networks have shown strong performance in learning nonlinear dynamical systems from input-output data, due to their flexibility and expressive power
[
1
,
2
]
. However, in many practical situations, collecting data under open-loop conditions is impractical or unsafe, particularly for unstable systems. Even for stable systems, feedback control may be necessary for safety, efficiency, or operation near desired setpoints. For instance, drones are controlled to fly safely, robots are operated to achieve certain tasks, and biological systems are regulated by natural mechanisms. In many cases, disconnecting the controller to collect open-loop data is either infeasible or prohibitively costly.

In such scenarios, data is typically gathered while the system operates in closed-loop with a stabilizing controller
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. This motivates closed-loop system identification, which aims to learn models from such data. Compared to the open-loop case, this setting introduces challenges, most notably the correlation between input signals and measurement noise caused by feedback
[
3
,
4
]
.

Classical methods for closed-loop identification are grouped into direct, joint input-output, and indirect approaches
[
6
]
.
Direct methods estimate both the system and noise model independently of the feedback. They are consistent, i.e., can recover the true model with infinite data, if the noise model is accurate. However, real-world disturbances are often too complex to model precisely
[
3
,
4
]
.

Joint input-output methods first identify a system driven by white noise that generates the joint input-output signal, then simultaneously estimate the target system and controller
[
7
]
.
While consistent even with imperfect noise models, these methods often suffer from theoretically unbounded variance
[
8
]
and high computational cost, especially for nonlinear controllers
[
6
]
.
Indirect methods consider the closed-loop mapping and identify the system by exploiting knowledge of the controller
[
4
]
.
These methods parameterize the model to reformulate the problem, eliminating noise effects at the plant input and enabling the use of standard open-loop techniques
[
3
,
4
]
. This ensures consistency even when the noise model is misspecified, at least in the linear case.

One commonly used parameterization is the
dual Youla
parameterization, which describes all systems stabilized by a given controller
[
9
]
. This formulation is the dual of the Youla-Kučera parameterization, which characterizes all controllers that stabilize a given system
[
10
]
.
A key advantage of this approach is that it inherently ensures the identified model is stabilized by the controller. This aligns with the stability of the true system under the given closed-loop configuration
[
4
]
.
The dual Youla parameterization involves the coprime factorizations of both the controller and a nominal plant stabilized by it, along with Youla parameters that can be optimized to minimize the model’s discrepancy from the target system.
For a linear plant and controller, the Youla parameters correspond to a stable rational transfer function, making optimization straightforward.

Given dual Youla’s success in identifying linear systems, prior work has explored extending this parameterization to cases with nonlinear systems but linear controllers (see
[
11
]
for a review).
However, two key challenges arise:

•

Coprime factorizations exist only for a subclass of nonlinear systems and are typically difficult to obtain even when they do exist
[
11
]
.

•

The parameterization relies on stable operators, the nonlinear counterparts of the Youla parameter, and their inverses which are challenging to optimize over. A similar challenge has been noted in the context of learning stabilizing nonlinear controllers using the Youla parameterization
[
12
]
.

As a result, existing methods do not lend themselves to a computationally tractable framework.

More recently,
[
5
]
proposed a tractable framework for closed-loop identification of nonlinear systems. While effective in their experiments, this method has two key limitations:

(i)

the closed-loop system of the learned model and controller may become unstable, and

(ii)

it assumes a predefined parametric form for the system, limiting flexibility.

This leaves closed-loop identification of expressive, nonlinear models a critical yet not sufficiently explored challenge.

Contributions.

We propose a closed-loop system identification framework for learning complex, potentially unstable models using neural networks while ensuring closed-loop stability with a given controller. Our approach is based on a parameterization inspired by the dual Youla framework, adapted to settings where both the system and controller may be nonlinear.
To this end, we first derive a complete characterization of all systems that can be stabilized by a given controller in terms of an operator in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
. Motivated by
[
12
]
, we then focus on a parametric subset of these operators, resulting in a computationally tractable optimization framework.
In the linear case, we establish the asymptotic consistency of our approach, aligning with existing results on the consistency of the dual Youla parameterization
[
3
,
4
]
.
Finally, numerical comparisons with direct baselines demonstrate the superiority of our method in capturing instability and achieving improved identification accuracy.

II-A

Problem setup

We consider a discrete-time, time-varying dynamical system that may be nonlinear and unstable, described by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{t}=G_{t}(u_{t-1:0})+v_{t},\quad t\in\mathbb{N},\\
y_{0}=G_{0}(\varnothing)+v_{0},\end{cases}

(1)

with input
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
, nominal initial output
G_{0}(\varnothing)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}

y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
. The output is corrupted by independent noise
v_{t}
. The system is strictly causal, meaning its output at time
t
depends only on past inputs up to time
t-1

The model (
1
) is an input-output relationship, representing all strictly causal dynamical systems. The next example
shows how to derive it from a state-space model.

Example 1
(From state-space to input-output models)

Consider a noise-free, strictly causal, time-invariant system in the state-space form:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{t+1}=F(x_{t},u_{t})\,,\quad x_{0}=\bar{x},\\
y_{t}=H(x_{t}),\end{cases}

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is the state at time
t
and
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is the initial state.
By recursion, the functions
G_{t}
in (
1
) are defined as:

\displaystyle G_{0}(\varnothing)

\displaystyle=H\bigl(\bar{x}\bigr),\;G_{1}(u_{0})=H\bigl(F(\bar{x},u_{0})\bigr),\;\dots

\displaystyle G_{t}(u_{t-1:0})

\displaystyle=H\bigl(\underbrace{F(\dots(F}_{t\textit{ times}}(\bar{x},u_{0}),u_{1}),\dots,u_{t-1})\bigr)\,,\;\dots

These expressions show that
G_{t}
depends explicitly on the initial state
\bar{x}
for all
t
, which is coherent with the definition of input-output models in the literature
[
13
]
.

Moreover, linear operators with zero initial conditions represent
transfer functions, highlighting their universality in characterizing LTI systems
[
12
]
.

The system (
1
) can be expressed in operator form as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{v},

(2)

where
\mathbf{G}
is a strictly causal operator such that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{u})=[G_{0}(\varnothing),G_{1}(u_{0}),\dots]^{\top}
,
\mathbf{u}=[u_{0},u_{1},\dots]^{\top}
, etc.

\hat{\mathbf{G}}

\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{SC}
. The model input and output are denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}
, respectively.

We assume the system is controlled by a causal dynamic output feedback controller
\mathbf{K}
. In addition to the feedback loop, a persistently exciting (PE) signal
r_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is applied to the system.
The resulting closed-loop system, illustrated in the left panel of
Fig. 1
, is:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{v},\\
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{y}),\end{cases}

(3)

where
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{y})=[K_{0}(y_{0}),K_{1}(y_{1:0}),\dots]^{\top}
and

.
We assume the closed-loop system formed by
\mathbf{G}
and

is
\mathcal{L}_{p}
-stable, as defined below.

Definition 1
(Closed-loop
\mathcal{L}_{p}
-stability
[

]
)

The closed-loop of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{K}
, as shown in the left panel of
Fig. 1
, is
\mathcal{L}_{p}
-stable if for every
\mathbf{r}\in\ell_{p}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\ell_{p}

\mathbf{u}\in\ell_{p}
and

Equivalently, the mapping

Our goal is to learn a dynamical system
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
that approximates
\mathbf{G}
, given knowledge of the controller
\mathbf{K}
. Crucially, we allow
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
to be unstable, enabling the identification of an unstable
\mathbf{G}
, while ensuring that the closed-loop system formed by
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
and
\mathbf{K}
remains stable. This requirement maintains consistency with the fact that
\mathbf{K}
stabilizes
\mathbf{G}
.

II-B

Internal controller identification framework

Learning over the space of systems
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
stabilized by controller
\mathbf{K}
is challenging, as this space is generally nonconvex.
To address this, we parameterize all and only systems stabilized by
\mathbf{K}
using a dual formulation of the Internal Model Control (IMC) framework
[
15
]
, which we call Internal Controller Identification (ICI). Here,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
is structured as an interconnection of controller
\mathbf{K}
and a trainable strictly causal operator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{SC}
. This transforms the problem of learning
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
into optimizing
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
within
\mathcal{L}_{p}^{SC}
operators, enabling unconstrained optimization
[
12
,
14
]
.
The corresponding model is illustrated in the right panel of
Fig. 1
, where the noise-free output
\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
for an input
\hat{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is given by:

\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}=\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}}-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ})\bigr).

(4)

Remark 1
(Recursive implementation of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
)

Since
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
is strictly causal, the output
\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}
depends on past outputs only up to time
t-1
, i.e.,

\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}=\hat{S}_{t}\bigl(\hat{u}_{t-1:0}-K_{t-1:0}(\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t-1:0})\bigr).

This structure enables a recursive computation of
\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}
, eliminating the need to solve the implicit nonlinear equation (
4
) at each time step.
Additionally, if
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
and
\mathbf{K}
are input-output operators associated with dynamical systems (see
Example 1
), their state-space representations can be used to implement (
4
) as a Markovian dynamical system, thereby avoiding the need to store the full history of
\hat{u}_{t-1:0}
and
\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t-1:0}
.

The following theorem establishes that a given stable controller,
\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
, can stabilize a system if and only if the system can be represented within the ICI framework.

Theorem 1

Let the controller
\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{C}}
be an incrementally stable operator. Under the ICI framework, as shown in the right panel of
Fig. 1
and described by (
4
), the following holds:

T.1

Let
\hat{\mathbf{S}}:\ell_{p}^{d_{u}}\rightarrow\ell_{p}^{d_{y}}
be an
\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
operator and consider the ICI model
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
shown in the right plot of
Fig. 1
. Then,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
is stabilized by
\mathbf{K}
in the sense of
Definition 1
.

T.2

Let
\mathbf{G}
in
Fig. 1
be a strictly causal system such that the closed-loop system is stable according to
Definition 1
. Then, there exists
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
such that
\mathbf{G}=\hat{\mathbf{G}}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
is constructed as in the right panel of
Fig. 1
.

The proof can be found in the appendix of
[
16
]
.

Since
\mathbf{G}
is stabilized by
\mathbf{K}
, by point
T.2
of
Theorem 1
, it admits an ICI representation in terms of an operator
\mathbf{S}
, which we refer to as the
true ICI
parameterization.
The true operator
\mathbf{S}
serves as a benchmark for evaluating algorithms that learn
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
. Specifically, we assess a learning algorithm’s effectiveness by its ability to recover
\mathbf{S}
in
Sec. IV
.

III
Training procedure

In this section, we discuss learning
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
from data to approximate the unknown system
\mathbf{G}
. The dataset is generated by applying
N\in\mathbb{N}
excitation signals, each over a horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
, to the closed-loop system in the left plot of
Fig. 1
, and recording the resulting system inputs and noisy outputs, defined as:

\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{(r_{T-1:0}^{n},u_{T-1:0}^{n},y_{T-1:0}^{n})\bigr\}_{n=0}^{N-1},

where
r_{T-1:0}^{n}
represents the first
T
time steps of
r
in the experiment
n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
(similarly for
u
and
y
).

Using this dataset, we explore three identification strategies: two direct and one indirect. These strategies aim to minimize the discrepancy between the measured and predicted outputs,
\{y_{T-1:0}^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
and
\{\hat{y}_{T-1:0}^{\circ^{n}}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
, based on the mean square error (MSE) cost function:

\displaystyle J\bigl(\mathcal{D},\{\hat{y}_{T-1:0}^{\circ^{n}}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\bigr)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|y_{t}^{n}-\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}{{}^{n}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(5)

The key difference between the learning strategies lies in how the predicted output is obtained, as outlined below.

III-A

Direct identification

In direct identification, the measured inputs
\{u_{T-1:0}^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
are applied to the model
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
, without considering the controller
\mathbf{K}
. This effectively treats the data as if collected in an open-loop setting. We use this approach to learn both a general
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
(not necessarily in the ICI formulation) and
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
within the ICI framework, leading to the following strategies.

Strategy 1

Direct identification of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
, formulated as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\textit{dir-ID}}=\argmin_{\hat{\mathbf{G}}}\;

\displaystyle J\bigl(\mathcal{D},\{\hat{y}_{T-1:0}^{\circ^{n}}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\bigr),

s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}{{}^{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{u}^{n})\quad\forall n.

The resulting model
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\textit{dir-ID}}
is not necessarily stabilized by
\mathbf{K}
, neglecting the knowledge that the true system
\mathbf{G}
is stabilized by
\mathbf{K}
.
Nonetheless, we use this strategy as a baseline due to its widespread use. To ensure closed-loop stability, the next two strategies leverage the ICI framework.

Strategy 2

Direct identification of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
in the ICI framework, leading to:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\textit{dir-ICI}}=\argmin_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}}\;

\displaystyle J\bigl(\mathcal{D},\{\hat{y}_{T-1:0}^{\circ^{n}}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\bigr),

s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}{{}^{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigl(\mathbf{u}^{n}-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}{{}^{n}})\bigr)\quad\forall n,

where the constraint follows from substituting
\hat{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{u}
in (
4
). Recall that
\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}
can be computed recursively by
Remark 1
.
The corresponding model,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\textit{dir-ICI}}
, is then defined as the closed-loop system of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\textit{dir-ICI}}
and
\mathbf{K}
. Notably,
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\textit{dir-ICI}}
is stabilized by
\mathbf{K}
, as guaranteed by
Theorem 1
.

While direct methods lead to tractable optimization problems, they have two fundamental drawbacks.

D.1

Direct methods apply only when
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
is stable
[
4
]
since the cost function

is evaluated using open-loop predictions of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
, which may diverge if the model is unstable.

D.2

Direct methods can yield inconsistent estimates, meaning that the true
\mathbf{G}
is not recovered even with infinite data
[
6
,
3
,
17
]
. This inconsistency arises because the noise

affects both the trainable operator’s input,
\mathbf{u}
in
Strategy 1
and
\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ})
in
Strategy 2
, and the measured output
\mathbf{y}
, due to the feedback loop present during data collection, as illustrated in the left plot of

. We further discuss this issue in
Sec. IV

Indirect methods first identify the closed-loop system of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
and
\mathbf{K}
, using the excitation signal
\mathbf{r}
as input, as shown in the top-left plot of

\mathbf{K}
to extract a system model from the identified closed-loop system
[
6

.
Since learning is based on the closed-loop system of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
and
\mathbf{K}
, these methods avoid direct open-loop simulation of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
, thus addressing the problem

. Additionally, indirect methods assume a particular model parameterization in order to remove the noise contribution on the input of the trainable model, resolving issue

In the following, we develop an indirect strategy leveraging the ICI framework. The proposed scheme is inspired by indirect methods based on the dual Youla parameterization (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
).
As discussed in
Sec. I
, classical Youla-based methods are restricted to linear controllers, require intractable coprime factorizations for nonlinear systems, and involve operators that hinder computational optimization. By utilizing the ICI framework and recent techniques for parameterizing stable operators, we circumvent coprime factorizations and establish a computationally tractable framework.

We begin by computing the noisy output
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
resulting from applying
\mathbf{r}
to the closed-loop system of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
and
\mathbf{K}
, as illustrated in the top-left plot of

:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}}-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{v})\bigr)+\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigl(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{v})\bigr)+\mathbf{v}.

(6)

Next, following

]
, we move the noise outside the operand of

in (

around

and

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\approx\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigl(\mathbf{r}\bigr)+\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{L}\bigl(\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{v})\bigr)+\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\approx\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hskip-24.0pt\underbrace{\mathbf{r}}_{\textit{noise-independent signal}}\hskip-24.0pt)+\underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{L}\bigl(\mathbf{K}_{L}(\mathbf{v})\bigr)+\mathbf{v}}_{\textit{noise}},

Strategy 3

Indirect identification of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
in the ICI framework via output linearization, formulated as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\textit{indir-ICI}}=\argmin_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}}\;

\displaystyle J\bigl(\mathcal{D},\{\hat{y}_{T-1:0}^{\circ^{n}}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\bigr),

s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ}{{}^{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{r}^{n})\quad\forall n.

The corresponding model
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\textit{indir-ICI}}
is then defined as the closed-loop system formed by
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\textit{indir-ICI}}
and
\mathbf{K}
.

Remark 3
(Open-loop equivalence)

We observe from (
7
) that (i)
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
can be approximated as
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
applied to
\mathbf{r}
, which is independent of the noise, plus an additive noise term, and (ii)
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
is a stable operator. As a result,
Strategy 3
is a
standard open-loop
problem, eliminating the instability (
D.1
) and correlation (
D.2
) issues of direct methods. This equivalence is well-known for classic indirect approaches, such as the dual Youla parameterization
[
4
]
.

Following
Remark 3
, we can use any open-loop method, such as subspace identification or instrumental variables (see
[
6
]
for an overview), to obtain the system model. Here, we minimize the least squares cost in (
5
), aligning with the prediction error method
[
6
]
. Moreover,
Remark 3
allows us to directly leverage the consistency properties of open-loop identification, as discussed in
Sec. IV
.

III-C

Parameterizing the operator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}

To implement the ICI framework, we must parameterize the operator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
. This can be achieved using various families of parameterized
\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
operators, such as Recurrent Equilibrium Networks (RENs)
[
19
]
, certain classes of State-Space Models (SSMs)
[
20
]
, and neural network representations of Hamiltonian systems
[
21
]
. These operators all admit a state-space form, enabling recursive computation of the output of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
(see
Remark 1
). In our experiments, we follow
[
14
,
22
]
and employ RENs, which define a broad class of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
operators and embed arbitrarily deep neural networks
[
19
]
.

For any chosen operator family, we denote the parameterized operator and the associated model by
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\theta}
and
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\theta}
, with learnable parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
.
Importantly, these families are constructed so that
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
.
Consequently, optimizing over
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
in
Strategy 2
and
Strategy 3
reduces to an unconstrained optimization over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
, which can be efficiently solved using gradient descent methods.

IV
Statistical analysis in the linear case

The identification procedure is inherently stochastic, as it depends on the realizations of noise and excitation signals in the dataset. A central question is whether the learned models
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
asymptotically converge in probability to the true system
\mathbf{G}
or the true
\mathbf{S}
, defined in
Sec. II-B
, as the sample size grows. This notion of convergence is formalized through the concept of consistency:
2
2

2

In some classic system identification literature, the term unbiasedness is also used to describe consistency (e.g.,
[
4
]
). To align with machine learning terminology, we use consistency throughout.

Definition 2
(Consistency
[
3
]
)

An estimate
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
(or
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
) obtained based on a dataset of
N
sequences, each of horizon
T
, is consistent if it converges to the true value
\mathbf{G}
(or
\mathbf{S}
) with probability
1
as the sample size grows indefinitely, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\,T\to\infty}\hat{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{G},\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{N\,T\to\infty}\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}.

Since
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
can be uniquely determined from one another, the consistency of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
is equivalent to that of
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
.

We discuss the consistency of the proposed strategies when
\mathbf{K}
,
\mathbf{G}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
are linear.
Throughout this section, we assume that the excitation signal is quasi-stationary and sufficiently rich to identify the plant dynamics
[
23
,
4
]
.

The consistency of direct methods (
Strategy 1
and
Strategy 2
) can only be analyzed for a stable
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
(see
D.1
). Even in this case, it depends heavily on the noise model. Let the noise
\mathbf{v}
in (
2
) be expressed as
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{e})
, where
\mathbf{e}
is white noise and
\mathbf{H}
is a linear noise model.
Direct methods typically estimate both the target system
\mathbf{G}
and the operator
\mathbf{H}
. It is well-established that a consistent estimate of
\mathbf{G}
can only be obtained if
\mathbf{H}
is either known or exactly learned
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
24
]
. However, many real-world disturbances exhibit colored noise characteristics
[
25
]
, which cannot be captured by a finite-order noise model
\mathbf{H}
. As a result, direct identification methods are often inconsistent in practice
[
24
,
5
]
.

Next, we discuss the consistency of
Strategy 3
. As noted in
Remark 3
,
Strategy 3
is equivalent to identifying
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
in an open-loop setting.
Consequently, consistency results from open-loop identification apply directly, as stated below.

Proposition 1
(Corollary 6 in
[
4
]
)

Consider the stable closed-loop system given by (
3
), where the system
\mathbf{G}
and the controller
\mathbf{K}
are linear.
Suppose the dataset
\mathcal{D}
consists of
N
sequences, each of horizon
T
, collected under quasi-stationary persistent excitation
\mathbf{r}
.
Let
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\theta}
be the estimate obtained by
Strategy 3
using
\mathcal{D}
, optimized over a parametric family of
\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
operators. Then, both
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\theta}
and the corresponding
\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{\theta}
are consistent, provided that the true
\mathbf{S}
, as introduced in
Sec. II-B
, belongs to the chosen family.

The consistency of
Strategy 3
only requires the noise to be independent of the excitation signal, without imposing any additional assumptions on the noise.

Proposition 1
requires that the true
\mathbf{S}
belongs to the chosen parametric set. If this set covered the entire
\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
space, consistency would be guaranteed. However, since most parametric families only span a subset of it,
\mathbf{S}
may fall outside, leading to inconsistency. This is a common trait in the analysis of learning algorithm consistency
[
4
]
, which can be addressed by using a more flexible parameterization or increasing the dimensionality of the parameters,
{d_{\theta}}
.

VI
Conclusions and future work

This paper presents a novel computationally tractable framework for closed-loop identification of nonlinear dynamical systems using a complete parameterization of systems stabilized by a given controller. The proposed method addresses key challenges, including instability risks and computational complexity. We theoretically show asymptotic consistency in the linear setting, aligning with established results. Numerical experiments demonstrate that our method outperforms existing work by accurately capturing system instabilities and achieving superior prediction performance.

Future work includes conducting a non-asymptotic statistical analysis in the spirit of
[
27
]
, applying the identified model to adaptive control, and designing an iterative procedure that integrates system identification and control.

Proof of

We first state a useful corollary from
[
28
]
.

Corollary 1

Consider a causal operator
\bm{\Upsilon}
such that
\bm{\Upsilon^{o}}:=\bm{\Upsilon}-\mathbf{I}
is strictly causal, where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity operator.
Then, the causal operator
\bm{\Upsilon}^{-1}
exists, and the sequence
\mathbf{b}=\bm{\Upsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
satisfies, for
t=0,1,\dots
:

\displaystyle b_{t}=a_{t}-\Upsilon^{o}_{t}(b_{t-1:0}).

We start by proving
T.1
, meaning that for any
\mathbf{v}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{y}}
and
\mathbf{r}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{u}}
, one has
\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{y}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{u}}
.
We denote the input to the operator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
by
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
. Then, from the right panel of
Fig. 1
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}-\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\circ})=\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{K}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})+\mathbf{v}\bigr)-\mathbf{K}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})\bigr).

We now upper bound its norm:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\bm{\omega}}\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\bigl\|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{K}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})+\mathbf{v}\bigr)-\mathbf{K}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})\bigr)\bigr\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|\mathbf{r}\bigr\|_{p}+\bigl\|\mathbf{K}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})+\mathbf{v}\bigr)-\mathbf{K}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})\bigr)\bigr\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|\mathbf{r}\bigr\|_{p}+\gamma_{K}\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})+\mathbf{v}-\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})\bigr\|_{p}=\bigl\|\mathbf{r}\|_{p}+\gamma_{K}\|\mathbf{v}\bigr\|_{p},

where the second line is due to the triangle inequality and
\gamma_{K}>0
is the i.f.g. of
\mathbf{K}
.
Since
\mathbf{v}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{y}}
and
\mathbf{r}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{u}}
, we have
\hat{\bm{\omega}}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{u}}
.
Additionally, since
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
,
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})+\mathbf{v}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{y}}
. Consequently, since
\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})+\mathbf{r}\in\ell_{p}^{d_{u}}
.
Therefore, the mapping from
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v})
to
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y})
is an
\mathcal{L}_{p}
operator.

Next, we prove

.
Given a strictly causal system
\hat{\mathbf{G}}

Additionally, set the initial condition of

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{0}(\varnothing-K_{0}(\varnothing))=\hat{G}_{0}(\varnothing).

(9)

In

29

\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\textit{SC}}
.

We apply the same input
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
to both
\hat{\mathbf{G}}
and the closed-loop system formed by
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
and
\mathbf{K}

\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}{{}^{\textit{des}}}=\hat{y}^{\circ}_{t}
