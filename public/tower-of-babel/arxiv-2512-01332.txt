Counting Lattice Points in Generalized Permutohedra From A to B
Warut Thawinrak

Abstract
We derive a formula for the number of lattice points in type B generalized permutohedra, providing a concise alternative to the formula obtained recently by Eur, Fink, Larson, and Spink as a result from a study of delta-matroids. Our approach builds upon the existing framework and techniques introduced by Postnikov in his work on type A generalized permutohedra, a family of polytopes interconnected with many mathematical concepts such as matroids and Weyl groups. In particular, we express the number of lattice points in type B B generalized permutohedra in terms of Postnikov’s notion of G-draconian sequences, from which their Ehrhart polynomials and volume formula follow as consequences.



Keywords—
polytope, generalized permutohedra, lattice point, Ehrhart polynomial

1
Introduction

Type A generalized permutohedra form a rich class of polytopes that arise naturally in contexts such as the theory of matroids and Weyl groups, lying at the intersection of several areas of mathematics. Their geometric and combinatorial properties have been the subject of extensive study. In particular, Postnikov, Reiner, and Williams
[
11
,
10
]
established a comprehensive framework for type A generalized permutohedra, leading to extensive investigations of their lattice points, facial structures, various combinatorial interpretations, and applications.

Type B generalized permutohedra, by contrast, form a broader class of polytopes encompassing those of type A but remain comparatively less explored. Given the depth and maturity of the results and techniques in the type A setting, it is natural to seek generalization or, in some instances, direct adaptations of these tools to the type B context, thereby uncovering properties that both parallel and extend those known for type A. In this paper, we employ Postnikov’s framework
[
11
]
for type A generalized permutohedra to count the lattice points in type B generalized permutohedra. Specifically, by viewing a type B generalized permutohedron in each octant as a type A generalized permutohedron, we can apply Postnikov’s approach to derive analogous lattice-point enumerations via polytopal subdivisions into cells that correspond bijectively to both their lattice points and the so-called
G
-draconian sequences
. This yields a concise alternative to the formula recently obtained by Eur, Fink, Larson, and Spink
[
6
, Theorem A]
from their study of delta-matroids. As a consequence, we also obtain formulas for their Ehrhart polynomials and volume. Moreover, this approach suggests that many aspects of type B generalized permutohedra may be effectively analyzed using existing notion and techniques developed for their type A counterparts.

Paper Organization

We begin by introducing type
A
and type
B
generalize permutohedra, along with a review of existing techniques for enumerating lattice points in these polytopes. We then develop a method for realizing part of type B generalized permutohedron as a type
A
generalize permutohedron. Using this realization, we show how the number of lattice points in type
B
generalized permutohedra can be computed in terms of the so-called
G
-draconian sequences. Lastly, we propose questions for future work on how the combinatorial properties of type B generalized permutohedra might be deduced from those of their type A counterparts.

Preliminaries

2.1
Polyhedra

A
polyhedron

P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the solution to a finite system of linear inequalities, that is,

(2.1)

\displaystyle P=\left\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \Big|\ \mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}\leq b_{i}\text{ for }i\in I\right\}

for some
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R},
where the dot
\cdot
is the usual dot product and
I
is a finite set of indices. The dimension of
P
, denoted by
\dim(P),
is defined to be the dimension of
\mathrm{aff}(P)
the affine span of
P
.

A
polytope

P
is a bounded polyhedron. By the Minkowski-Weyl Theorem
[
9
,
12
]
, we can equivalently define a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the convex hull of finitely many points in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
i.e.,

P=\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{k}):=\{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\cdots+\lambda_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}\ |\ \lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}=1,\lambda_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i\in[k]\}.

We denote by
\mathrm{Vol}(P)
the volume of
P
with respect to the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathrm{aff}(P)
in the affine span of
P.
The
normalized volume
of a
d
-dimensional polytope is defined to be
\mathrm{NVol}(P):=d!\mathrm{Vol}(P).

For two nonempty polytopes
P_{1},P_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
Minkowski sum
of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, denoted by
P_{1}+P_{2}
, is the set
\{\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{1}\in P_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in P_{2}\}
. If
U
and
V
are the set of vertices of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, respectively, then
P_{1}+P_{2}=\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\ |\ \mathbf{u}\in U,\mathbf{v}\in V)
. This implies that a Minkowski sum of polytopes is a polytope. The
Minkowski difference
of
P_{2}
in
P_{1}
, denoted by
P_{1}-P_{2}
, is the set
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\mathbf{x}+P_{2}\subseteq P_{1}\}
. Since the vectors that translate
P_{2}
to lie in
P_{1}
form a polytope, it follows that a Minkowski difference of two polytopes is also a polytope. It is important to note that, in general, the Minkowski difference on polytopes is neither a commutative nor an associative operator. For example, while
(P_{1}+P_{2})-P_{2}=P_{1}
always holds, the expression
(P_{1}-P_{2})+P_{2}
may not equal
P_{1}
, or may even be undefined if the difference
P_{1}-P_{2}
is empty. Thus, it is crucial to clearly specify the order of the sum (and difference). Given nonempty polytopes
P_{i}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and signs
\delta_{i}\in\{1,-1\}
for
i\in[m]
, we define the Minkowski sum

\sum^{m}_{i=1}\delta_{i}P_{i}:=Q_{1}-Q_{2}\text{ where }Q_{1}:=\sum_{\delta_{i}=1}P_{i}\text{ and }Q_{2}:=\sum_{\delta_{i}=-1}P_{i}.

A subset
F
of a polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a
face
of
P
if there exists a hyperplane
H
such that
P
lies on one side of
H
and
F=P\cap H.
A face of dimension
\dim(P)-1
is called a
facet
, a face of dimension
1
is called an
edge
, and a face of dimension 0 is called a
vertex
. The partially ordered set
\mathcal{F}(P)
of all faces of
P
ordered by inclusion is called the
face poset
of
P
.

A
cone

\sigma
is a polyhedron defined by a system of homogeneous linear inequalities, i.e. inequalities of the form
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}\leq 0
. Given a nonempty face
F
of a polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
normal cone
of
P
at
F
is the set

\mathrm{ncone}(F,P):=\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{y}\text{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in F\text{ and all }\mathbf{y}\in P\},

that is,
\mathrm{ncone}(F,P)
is the set of all
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}
attains maximum value at
F
over all points in
P
. The
normal fan
of
P
, denoted
\Sigma(P)
, is the set of normal cones of
P
at all of its nonempty faces. We say that a polytope
Q
is a
deformation
of another polytope
P
if
\Sigma(Q)
is a coarsening of
\Sigma(P)
, i.e., every cone in
\Sigma(Q)
is a union of cones in
\Sigma(P)
. A deformation
Q
of
P
can be obtained by parallel translations of the facets of
P
.

A (
polyhedral
)
subdivision
of a polytope
P
is a collection
\mathcal{C}
of polytopes of dimension
\dim(P)
, called
cells
, such that
P
equals the union of all cells in
\mathcal{C}
, and any two cells intersect only at their common face.

A
lattice point
is a point whose coordinates are integers. A polytope is said to be
integral
if all of its vertices are lattice points. For a polytope
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a non-negative integer
t,
the
t^{\mathrm{th}}
-dilation
tP
is the set
\{tx\,|\,x\in P\}.
We define

i(P,t):=|\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap tP|
to be the number of lattice points in the
t^{\mathrm{th}}
-dilation
tP.
It follows from Ehrhart theory
[
5
]
that when
P
is an integral polytope, the function
i(P,t)
is a polynomial in
t
of degree
\dim(P)
. We call
i(P,t)
the
Ehrhart polynomial
of
P
. It encodes various geometric and combinatorial properties of
P
: its leading coefficient equals the volume of
P
, the second coefficient equals half of its surface area, and its constant term is one.

Two integral polytopes
P,Q
such that
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
are said to be
integrally equivalent
if there exists an invertible affine transformation from
\mathrm{aff}(P)
to
\mathrm{aff}(Q)
that preserves the lattice points in the two polytopes. When two integral polytopes are integrally equivalent, they have the same face poset, volume, and Ehrhart polynomials.

2.2
Type A generalized Permutohedra

In this section, we introduce the family of polytopes known as
type A generalized permutohedra
, following the notation and framework established by Postnikov in
[
11

. For a more comprehensive treatment beyond what is presented here, we refer the reader to
[

A
type A generalized permutohedron

P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a polytope whose edges are parallel to
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
for some
1\leq i<j\leq n,
where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
We note that it is also known simply as a
generalized permutohedron
. We include “type A” in the name to emphasize the fact that the edge directions of these polytopes are parallel to some vectors in type A positive root system
\{e_{i}-e_{j}\ |\ 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
.

Let
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a point satisfying
w_{1}>\cdots>w_{n}\geq 0.
The
permutohedron

\Pi(\mathbf{w})
, defined as the convex hull of all permutations of the coordinates of
\mathbf{w}
, is an example of a type
A
generalized permutohedron. See Figure
1
for examples of permutohedra and other type A generalized permutohedra. The normal fan of any permutohedron, called the
braid fan
and denoted by
\Sigma_{A_{n-1}}
, is composed of maximal cones defined by the chambers in the arrangement of the hyperplanes

H_{i,j}=\{(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ c_{i}-c_{j}=0\}\text{ for all }1\leq i<j\leq n,

known as the
braid arrangement
. We note that one can alternatively define a type A generalized permutohedron as any deformation of a permutohedron, i.e., a polytope whose normal fan coarsens the braid fan.

The family of type A generalized permutohedra is a widely studied class of polytopes that interconnect with various combinatorial objects, such as matroids and Weyl groups. Beyond permutohedra, many well-known polytopes in the literature can be realized as type A generalized permutohedra, including zonotopes, associahedra, cyclohedra, and the Pitman-Stanley polytopes (see
[
11
, Setion 8]
). Note that every type A generalized permutohedron
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
lies on a hyperplane
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}=a
for some real number
a.
For instance, the permutohedron
\Pi(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
lies on the hyperplane
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}=w_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}.
Thus, the dimension of
P
is at most
n-1.

2.3
Type B generalized Permutohedra

A
type B generalized permutohedron
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a polytope whose edges are parallel to
\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j},
or
\mathbf{e}_{i}
for some

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{e}_{i}\pm\mathbf{e}_{j}\ |\ 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
. We note that these polytopes are also known as
generalized signed permtohedra
(see
[
6
]
). By definition, every type A generalized permutohedron is a type B generalized permutohedron. Other notable examples in the family include parking function polytopes
[
1
]

y_{S}

-draconian sequences, and denote by

. It is well-known that any fundamental parallelipiped has no common interior with its integral translation. Thus, the conclusion of the lemma follows from this claim. Hence, it remains to only prove the claim.

Let
K=\{I_{i}\ |\ i\in[m]\text{ and }|I_{i}|\geq 2\}
. Then,

\hat{\Pi}:=\sum_{I\in K}\Delta_{I}.

For each

, let us write

, and define the corresponding set of intervals

\binom{I}{2}^{*}:=\{[\mathbf{e}_{j_{1}},\mathbf{e}_{j_{2}}],[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{e}_{j_{3}}-\mathbf{e}_{j_{1}}],\dots,[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{e}_{j_{|I|}}-\mathbf{e}_{j_{1}}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

where we denote by
[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
the line segment connecting
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
(the interval from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
), and set
\mathbf{e}_{0}=\mathrm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is easy to see that every vertex of the simplex
\Delta_{I}
lies in the zonotope

\Diamond_{I}:=\sum_{\Delta\in\binom{I}{2}^{*}}\Delta

spanned by the intervals in
\binom{I}{2}^{*}.
This implies that
\Delta_{I}\subseteq\Diamond_{I}
for all
I\in K
. In addition, let

B_{K}:=\bigcup_{I\in K}\binom{I}{2}^{*}

be the set of intervals from all of
\binom{I}{2}^{*}
for
I\in K
. Thus,
\hat{\Pi}
lies in the zonotope

\Diamond_{K}:=\sum_{\Delta\in B_{K}}\Delta=\sum_{I\in K}\Diamond_{I}.

Since
P_{T}
is
n
-dimensional, by Remark
4.6
,
\hat{H}
is a tree and a bipartite graph with
n
left and
n+1
right vertices. Because
|\binom{I}{2}^{*}|=|I|-1
, it follows that

\sum_{I\in K}\Big|\binom{I}{2}^{*}\Big|=n.

Thus, one sees that
B
contains translations of lattice points that form an integral basis for
\mathbb{Z}^{n}.
Hence, the polytope
\Diamond
is an integral translation of a fundamental parallelepiped in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\Pi
is an integral translation of
\hat{\Pi}
, it follows that
\Pi
lies in an integral translation of a fundamental parallelepiped as claimed.
∎

Let
S\in\mathbf{AdS}
be an admissible subset. We define
\nabla_{S}
to be the simplex closely related to
\Delta_{S}
by

\nabla_{S}:=\mathrm{conv}\left(\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{e}_{i},\sum_{i\in J}\mathbf{e}_{i}\ |\ J\subset S\text{ and }|J|=|S|-1\right).

The next lemma characterizes the fine mixed cells that are zonotopes, i.e., those fine mixed cells whose corresponding bipartite graphs are trees having left vertices of degree at most two.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
P=\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}
where
S_{i}\in\mathbf{AdS}
for all
i\in[m]
. Suppose that
T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}
is an admissible subset in which
P_{T}
is
n
-dimensional. Let
\Pi
be a fine mixed cell of
P_{T}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be the lattice point satisfying
\mathbf{x}+\hat{\Pi}=\Pi
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

1.

The fine mixed cell
\Pi
satisfies
\nabla_{T}\subseteq\hat{\Pi}.

2.

\mathbf{x}
is the unique lattice point in
(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{x}+\nabla_{T}\subseteq\Pi
.

3.

The fine mixed cell
\Pi
is a zonotope.

To prove this lemma, we need the following result regarding the existence of a perfect matching of a certain bipartite graph.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Suppose that
B
is a bipartite graph on
n
left vertices and
n
right vertices. Then, there is a perfect matching in
B
if and only if
B
satisfies the following two conditions:

1.

Every left and right vertex of
B
has degree at least one.

2.

Every left and right vertex of
B
is adjacent to at most one vertex of degree one.

Proof.

(
\Longrightarrow
) Clearly, if there is a perfect matching in
B,
then the two conditions must be met. (
\Longleftarrow
) Conversely, suppose that
B
satisfies the two conditions. We will proceed by induction on
n\geq 1.
When
n=1,
there is a perfect matching in
B
, since the left and right vertices are adjacent. This establishes the base case for induction. Now suppose that the statement is true for some
n\geq 1.
Consider a bipartite graph
B
with
n+1
left vertices
\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n+1}\}
and
n+1
right vertices
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{n+1}\}
satisfying the two conditions. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
r_{n+1}
is a right vertex of the least degree in
B
, and that the left vertex
\ell_{n+1}
is adjacent to
r_{n+1}
and has the least degree among other left vertices adjacent to
r_{n+1}
.

Let
\hat{B}
be the induced bipartite subgraph of
B
obtained by removing the vertices
\ell_{n+1}
and
r_{n+1}
of
B.
Then, it is easy to see that
\hat{B}
is a bipartite graph on
n
left and
n
right vertices satisfying the two conditions. Thus, by the induction hypothesis, there is a perfect matching in
\hat{B}
. By matching
\ell_{n+1}
with
r_{n+1}
and matching other vertices of
B
using a perfect matching in
\hat{B}
, we obtain a perfect matching in
B.
Therefore, by induction, the statement holds for all
n\geq 1.

∎

Proof of Lemma
4.8
.

Due to symmetry, we may assume for simplicity of notation without loss of generality that
T=[n]
. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
be the lattice point satisfying
\mathbf{x}+\hat{\Pi}=\Pi
.

Firstly, we show that (
1
) implies (
2
). Suppose that
\nabla_{[n]}\subseteq\hat{\Pi}.
Then,
\mathbf{x}+\nabla_{[n]}\subset\mathbf{x}+\hat{\Pi}=\Pi.
In particular, we have
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\mathbf{e}_{n}\in\Pi\subseteq P_{[n]}
. By Lemma
3.2
, we must have
\mathbf{x}+\square_{[n]}\subseteq P_{[n]}.
Thus,
\mathbf{x}\in(P_{[n]}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}.
Hence,
\mathbf{x}\in(P_{[n]}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is an integral vector such that
\mathbf{x}+\nabla_{[n]}\subset\Pi
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{y}\in(P_{[n]}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfies
\mathbf{y}+\nabla_{[n]}\subset\Pi.
Since
\Pi=\mathbf{x}+\hat{\Pi}
, we have
\nabla_{[n]}\subseteq\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}+\hat{\Pi}.
Because
\nabla_{[n]}\subseteq\hat{\Pi}
, we deduce that
\hat{\Pi}
has common interior with
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}+\hat{\Pi}
. By Lemma
4.7
, we must have
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}.
This shows the uniqueness of the lattice point
\mathbf{x}
.

Next, we show that (
2

). Suppose that
\mathbf{x}\in(P_{[n]}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and

\Pi
is a zonotope. Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\Pi
is not a zonotope. Then,
\hat{\Pi}
is also not a zonotope. This implies that
\hat{H}
is a bipartite graph that is also a tree with at least one left vertex having degree at least three. Together with Remark

\hat{H}
must have less than
n
left vertices. Thus, the set

of the right vertices cannot be a transversal of the neighbors of the left vertices of

Thus, By Lemma
2.7
and Remark

In particular, we must have
\nabla_{[n]}\not\subseteq\hat{\Pi}.
Therefore,

a contradiction. This shows that (
2
) implies (
3
).

Lastly, we show that (
3
) implies (
1
). Suppose that
\Pi
is a zonotope. Then,
\hat{\Pi}
is also a zonotope. Thus,
\hat{H}
is a bipartite graph that is also a tree on
n+1
right vertices in which every left vertex has degree two. Consequently, by Remark
4.6
,
\hat{H}
must have exactly
n
left vertices. To see that
\nabla_{[n]}\subseteq\hat{\Pi}
, it suffices to show
\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\mathbf{e}_{n}\in\hat{H}
and

for all

. For every

, let

be the induced bipartite subgraph of

obtained by removing the right vertex

of

while a matching of

. This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to give a proof of Lemma

.

We first show that
|\mathrm{D}(P_{T})\cap\square_{[m]}|=|\mathcal{C}^{*}|
. Suppose that
\mathcal{C}^{*}=\{\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{q}\}
. Since

n

3
/
3

. Let

be the fine mixed subdivision of

in which

3
/

. This implies that the corresponding spanning tree of

\Pi

Therefore,

Next, we show that
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\leq|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|.
By Lemma
4.8
/(
2
), the map
\Pi_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{x}

Lastly, we show that
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|.
Let
\mathbf{x}\in(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}.
Due to symmetry, we may assume for simplicity of notation without loss of generality that
T=[n]
. Let
\Pi\in\mathcal{C}
be a fine mixed cell that has a common interior interior with
\mathbf{x}+\nabla_{[n]}.
Note that such a fine mixed cell
\Pi

. We claim that
\Pi
is a zonotope (
\Pi\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
) and that
\mathbf{x}
is the unique lattice point in which
\mathbf{x}+\nabla_{[n]}
is contained in
\Pi
. This claim readily implies that
|\mathcal{C}^{*}|\geq|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|
, since it establishes the injectivity of the map from
(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
\mathcal{C}^{*}
defined by
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\Pi
where
\mathbf{x}+\nabla_{[n]}\subseteq\Pi
. Thus, it only remains to prove the claim.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\Pi
is not a zonotope. Then,
\hat{\Pi}
is also not a zonotope. This implies that
\hat{H}
is a bipartite graph that is also a tree with at least one left vertex having degree at least three. Together with Remark
4.6
, we conclude that
\hat{H}
must have less than

left vertices. Thus, the set

By Lemma
2.7
and Remark

, we deduce that
\hat{\Pi}
lies in the half-space

Because
\hat{\Pi}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, it follows that
\hat{\Pi}
lies in the polytope

\displaystyle Q:=\{\mathbf{x}\ |\ x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}\leq n-1\ \text{ and }0\leq x_{i}\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}=(n-1)\Delta^{0}_{[n]}.

Note that

Thus, for

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}+\nabla_{[n]}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\ |\ x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}\geq z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}+n-1\ \text{ and }x_{i}\leq z_{i}+1\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}.

Since

, a contradiction to our choice of

Now suppose that

.

The next theorem is a consequence of Lemma
4.2
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Suppose that
P=\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}
where
S_{i}\in\mathbf{AdS}
for all
i\in[m]
. Then,

(4.1)

\displaystyle|(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}|\mathrm{D}(P_{T})\cap\square_{[m]}|.

Proof.

We first observe that

(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\bigsqcup_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}(P_{T}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

Thus,

|(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}|(P_{T}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|.

Since
\square_{T}
is a translation of
\square_{[n]}
by an integral vector, we have
|(P_{T}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|
for all
T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}
. Consequently, we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle|(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|.

For
T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}
such that
P_{T}
is not
n
-dimension, we have that
|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=0=|\mathrm{D}(P_{T})\cap\square_{[m]}|
. Also, for
T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}
such that
P_{T}
is
n
-dimensional, we have by Lemma
4.2
that
|(P_{T}-\square_{T})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=|\mathrm{D}(P_{T})\cap\square_{[m]}|
. Thus, formula (
4.1
) follows from (
4.2
).
∎

Theorem
4.10
implies that

(4.3)

|(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(P_{T})}\binom{1}{a_{1}}\cdots\binom{1}{a_{m}}\right),

since only those
G
-draconian sequences
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
with

make the summands in equation (
4.3
) nonzero, and equal to one. Together with a simple binomial identity, we derive the following key result as a consequence.

Corollary 4.11
.

Suppose that
P=y_{1}\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+y_{m}\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}
where
y_{i}
are integers and
S_{i}\in\mathbf{AdS}
for all
i\in[m]
. Then, the number of lattice points in
P-\square_{[n]}
is given by

(4.4)

|(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(P_{T})}\binom{y_{1}}{a_{1}}\cdots\binom{y_{m}}{a_{m}}\right).

Proof.

As noted at the begining of the section, it suffices to show that the formula holds for positive integers
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
. Clearly, we can write any
y\Delta^{0}_{S}
where
S\in\mathbf{AdS}
and
y
is a positive integer as the Miknowski sum of
y
copies of
\Delta^{0}_{S}.
By writing

P=\underbrace{\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}}_{y_{1}\text{ terms }}+\cdots+\underbrace{\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}+\cdots+\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}}_{y_{m}\text{ terms }}

and applying formula (
4.3
) to
P
, one can express the right-hand side of (
4.3
) as

\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(P_{T})}\binom{y_{1}}{a_{1}}\cdots\binom{y_{m}}{a_{m}}\right)

using the binomial identity

\binom{y}{a}=\sum_{\underset{b_{1},\dots,\ b_{y}\,\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}}{b_{1}+\cdots+b_{y}=a}}\binom{1}{b_{1}}\cdots\binom{1}{b_{y}}\ \ \text{ for all }a,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.\qed

Remark 4.12
.

Let

D(P):=\bigcup_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}D(P_{T}).

Then, formula (
4.4
) can also be expressed as

(4.5)

\displaystyle|(P-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in D(P)}|\{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}\ |\ \mathbf{a}\in D(P_{T})\}|\binom{y_{1}}{a_{1}}\cdots\binom{y_{m}}{a_{m}}.

One observes that formula (
4.5
) bears a resemblance to formula (
2.7
) from Lemma
2.20
by Eur, Fink, Larson, and Spink. However, (
4.5
) is written in a more compact form: each summand in (
4.5
) consolidates
\frac{(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m})!}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{m}!}
individual terms that appear in (
2.7
).

The Ehrhart polynomial of
P=y_{1}\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+y_{m}\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}
can be computed simply by replacing
P
in (
4.4
) with
tP+\square_{[n]}=ty_{1}\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+ty_{m}\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}+\Delta^{0}_{\{1\}}+\cdots+\Delta^{0}_{\{n\}}
. This allows us to obtain a formula for the volume of
P
by computing the leading coefficient of its Ehrhart polynomial as stated in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.13
.

Suppose that
P=y_{1}\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+y_{m}\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}
where
y_{i}
are real numbers and
S_{i}\in\mathbf{AdS}
for all
i
. Then, the volume of
P
is given by

(4.6)

\mathrm{Vol}(P)=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(P_{T})}\frac{y_{1}^{a_{1}}}{a_{1}!}\cdots\frac{y_{m}^{a_{m}}}{a_{m}!}\right).

Proof.

To see that formula (
4.6
) gives the volume of
P
, it suffices to show that the formula holds for nonnegative integers
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
. Let

Q^{(t)}:=tP+\square_{[n]}=ty_{1}\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+ty_{m}\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}+\Delta^{0}_{\{1\}}+\cdots+\Delta^{0}_{\{n\}}.

We note that
\mathrm{D}(Q^{(t)}_{T})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}_{\geq 0}
. Then, the Ehrhart polynomial of
P
is given by

i(t,P)=|(Q^{(t)}-\square_{[n]})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(Q^{(t)}_{T})}\binom{y_{1}t}{a_{1}}\cdots\binom{y_{m}t}{a_{m}}\binom{1}{a_{m+1}}\cdots\binom{1}{a_{n+m}}\right).

Since the volume of
P
equals the leading coefficient of
i(P,t)
, it follows that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(P)=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{AdS}_{n}}\left(\sum_{\underset{a_{i}=0,\ \forall\,i\,>\,m}{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(Q^{(t)}_{T})}}\frac{y_{1}^{a_{1}}}{a_{1}!}\cdots\frac{y_{m}^{a_{m}}}{a_{m}!}\right).

One observes that
\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{D}(Q^{(t)}_{T})
satisfies
a_{i}=0
for all
i\,>\,m
if and only if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\mathrm{D}(P_{T})
. Thus, the volume formula (
4.6
) is equivalent to (
4.7
).
∎

More Problems from A to B

Our approach for computing the number of lattice points suggests that there seem to be many aspects of type B generalized permutohedra that can be explored using existing techniques and tools from the study of their type A counterparts. The following problems highlight some potential research directions.

Problem 5.1
.

In
[
11
, Section 7]
, Postnikov introduces building sets and nested complexes to describe the face posets of some type A generalized permutohedra. Can we give a combinatorial description of the faces of type B generalized permutohedra using similar combinatorial models as building sets and nested complexes?

Problem 5.2
.

In
[
10
]
, Postnikov, Reiner, and Williams compute the
f
and
h
-polynomials of a family of simple type A generalized permutohedra using building sets and the corresponding preposets. Can we employ a similar approach to compute the
f
and
h
-polynomials of type B generalized permutohedra?

Problem 5.3
.

Bastidas shows in
[
3
]
that every type B generalized permutohedron can also be written as the Minkowski sum of the simplices
\Delta_{S}
and
\Delta^{0}_{S}
where
S\in\mathbf{AdS}
are admissible subsets such that
\min(|i|\ |\ i\in S)\in S.
That is, the family of admissible subsets
\Delta_{S}
and
\Delta^{0}_{S}
where
\min(|i|\ |\ i\in S)\in S
is a “basis” for the type B generalized permutohedra. Find a formula for the Ehrhart polynomial of type B generalized permutohedra with respect to this basis (directly without transforming this basis to the basis used in this chapter).

Problem 5.4
.

An integral polytope is said to be
Ehrhart positive
if every coefficient of its Ehrhart polynomial is positive. Postnikov’s formula (
2.5
) implies that every type
A
generalized permutohedron of the form
y_{1}\Delta_{I_{1}}+\cdots+y_{m}\Delta_{I_{m}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Ehrhart positive provided
y_{i}
are positive integers for all
i\in[m]
. Our formula (
4.4
) in Corollary
4.11
does not make it immediately clear whether Ehrhart positivity holds for type
B
generalized permutohedra in a similar situation. This leads to the question: Is every type
B
generalized permutohedron of the form
y_{1}\Delta^{0}_{S_{1}}+\cdots+y_{m}\Delta^{0}_{S_{m}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
Ehrhart positive provided
y_{i}
are positive integers for all
i\in[m]
?
