Restoring macrophage iron homeostasis by natural carrier-free nanoplatform for the therapy of rheumatoid arthritis syndromes
Authors: Na Jia, Jia Cui, Lei Wang, Zifeng Huang, Xingru Tao, Qianqian Dong, Jingwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Focal iron overload frequently induce macrophage polarization towards pro-inflammatory subtypes, leading to joint inflammation, cartilage damage and anemia in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients. Faced with the complex and lingering pathogenesis of RA, there is an urgent need to develop safe and effective strategies. The active ingredients from traditional herbal pair, namely roburic acid (RBA) and tetramethylpyrazine (TMP), have potential values in the anti-inflammatory and antiplatelet aggregation biological activities. However, their poor water solubility and low bioavailability limit their strong clinical application. To break this bottleneck, we developed a carrier-free nanomedicine (RTNs) through the self-assembly of RBA and TMP for RA syndromes treatment. RTNs formed through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding exhibited strong inflammatory joint accumulation and long-term circulation in adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) rodents model. At the same time, RTNs outperformed the same doses of free RBA and TMP in the terms of good therapeutic effects in inhibiting joint inflammation, improving cartilage destruction, and alleviating RA secondary anemia. What's more, RTNs demonstrated good safety and biocompatibility. Finally, with the help of single-cell sequencing analysis and in vitro and in vivo research, we elucidated that RTNs restored macrophage iron homeostasis via facilitating ferroportin (FPN) level, and ultimately regulated macrophage function to treat RA syndromes. Overall, this study provides new insights into the application of carrier-free self-assembly of natural product medicines in the efficient and safe therapy of RA involving complex syndromes.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by synovitis, cartilage damage, bone destruction and anemia, ultimately leading to disability in patients [1]. At present, first-line drugs for treating RA neither strongly improve the immune imbalance of RA pathology, nor effectively prevent RA complications, including corticosteroids (such as dexamethasone) [2] and anti-rheumatic drugs (such as methotrexate) [3]. In addition, RA medication often involves high doses and frequent use, which lead to serious and persistent adverse reactions as well as economic burden on patients [4]. The use of herbal medicine to treat RA is effective, safe, and economically applicable. But it often faces the dilemma of low bioavailability and low targeting. In order to increase the homing and sustained release of natural products to specific sites, many methods including nanotechnology have attracted attention. In previous study, our team had conducted in-depth research on intelligent delivery systems hitchhiking natural products for the treatment of RA [5,6]. Although these methods improved the therapeutic effect of drugs, there were also some objective drawbacks [7]. On the one hand, the uneven half-life of these natural or non-natural carriers is difficult to eliminate, which may pose potential risks to patients. On the other hand, the acquisition of carrier materials and the complexity of drug loading procedures increase the cost of medication. The self-assembled carrier-free nanomedicines with the characteristics of simple preparation, safety, and high efficiency provides a new approach for targeted therapy of RA.

As a common herbal pair, Gentiana macrophylla and Ligusticum chuanxiong are the core components of the traditional Chinese medicine formula Daqinjiao decoction for treating rheumatism [8,9]. Roburic acid (RBA) is a representative active compound of Gentiana macrophylla, which has been proven to have good therapeutic effects in improving arthritis [5,10]. Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP) is a characteristic alkaloid derived from Ligusticum chuanxiong, which has biological activities such as improving microcirculation [11], anti-inflammatory [12], and anti-platelet aggregation [13]. However, both components hold poor water solubility, low bioavailability, and poor targeting, which limits their practical application as potential anti-RA agents. Therefore, if RBA and TMP self-assemble into carrier-free nanomedicines to improve their solubility and bioavailability, it will greatly enhance their therapeutic effects on RA while reducing systemic toxicity caused by vehicle.

There is focal iron metabolism disorder in RA, characterized by excessive iron deposition in the joints and peripheral iron deficiency [14]. Previous studies shown that treating anemia with iron dextran in RA patients could exacerbate synovitis [15]. When experimental autoimmune arthritis rats were given iron chelators to reduce systemic iron levels, joint inflammation was alleviated [16]. These results manifested a link between iron accumulation and the pathogenesis of RA. Recent research suggested that iron overload in inflamed joints exacerbated RA by inducing macrophage ferroptosis, and there were differences in susceptibility to iron dysregulation among specific macrophage subtypes [17]. As the main pro-inflammatory factor in the immune microenvironment, M1 subtype macrophages secrete interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) to induce inflammation and tissue damage in RA. On the contrary, M2 subtype macrophages secrete transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), arginase-1 (Arg-1), interleukin-10 (IL-10) to reduce inflammation and promote tissue repair [18]. Interestingly, iron overload induce macrophages to polarize towards M1 subtype, increase the production of inflammatory factors [19,20]. Therefore, based on the plasticity of macrophages, manipulating the subtype transformation of joint associated macrophages by regulating iron export to exert related functions has great therapeutic potential for RA syndromes.

Herein, we designed a carrier-free roburic acid-tetramethylpyrazine nanomedicines (RTNs) formed by self-assembly of RBA and TMP, which was mainly mediated by electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding (Scheme 1). Then, we explored the self-assembly process of RBA and TMP using molecular dynamics simulations and validated it through various characterizations. By utilizing fluorescent probes, RTNs exhibited strong inflammatory joint accumulation. In adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) model of mice, RTNs outperformed the same doses of free RBA and TMP in the terms of good therapeutic effects in inhibiting joint inflammation, improving cartilage destruction, and alleviating RA secondary anemia, while also demonstrating good safety. Mechanistic research enunciated that RTNs could restore iron export homeostasis by facilitating ferroportin (FPN) expression, which leaded to the transformation of macrophages from pro-inflammatory M1 subtype to anti-inflammatory M2 subtype, thereby regulating macrophage function to treat RA syndrome. We believe that this carrier-free nanomedicine based on classic natural medicine and modern drug delivery technology provide an appealing strategy for RA syndromes treatment with high efficiency, safety, and cost-effectiveness.Scheme 1Schematic diagram of the self-assembly process of RTNs and its anti-RA mechanism.Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Preparation of RTNs

Roburic acid (RBA, CAS: 6812-81-3, catalog B20723) and tetramethylpyrazine (TMP, CAS: 1124-11-4, catalog B20203) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). To prepare self-assembled RTNs, RBA and TMP were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with a 1: 1 M ratio, resulting in final concentrations of RBA and TMP of 3.2 mg/mL and 1.0 mg/mL, respectively. The mixed solution was sonicated with a probe type ultrasonic instrument (Scientz, Ningbo, China) at 200W for 10 min (working for 5 s with an interval of 5 s) to obtain RTNs. Then, the RTNs were loaded into deionized water dialysis bags (MWCO = 500 Da) for 24 h to remove excess DMSO solvent. The RTNs nanoparticle solution was freeze-dried using a MODULYOD-230 freeze dryer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at 4 °C for subsequent experiments. DiR (1,1’ -dioctadecyltetramethyl indotricarbocyanine) was purchased from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA).

Characterization of RTNs

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Tecnai G2, FEI, USA) was used to observe the morphology of RTNs. The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of RTNs were detected by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Malvern ZetaSizer Nano ZS90, UK). The ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–vis) of RBA, TMP and RTNs were obtained by full wavelength scanning using a UV visible spectrophotometer (Hitachi UH5300, Japan). The fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) of RBA, TMP and RTNs were recorded with an FT-IR spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 400∼4000 cm−1. RBA, TMP and RTNs were dissolved in DMSO-d6 and analyzed using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (Bruker-600 MHz, Bruker). The stability assessment aimed to evaluate the stability of RTNs stored at 4 °C for 7 days. Freshly prepared RTNs were stored in a constant temperature stability test chamber at 4 °C, and their particle size and PDI were measured daily for 7 days.

Self-assembly molecular dynamics simulation

We simulated the self-assembly mechanism of roburic acid and tetramethylpyrazine by GROMACS 2023.3 software package and GAFF force field. Four simulation systems were constructed with their initial structure composed of molecule. The RBA and TMP molecules were mixed at 1: 1 ratio, and the constructed mixed model was placed in a pre-equilibrated TIP3P aqueous phase within a cube box size of 6 × 6 × 6 nm. The RESP charge was calculated at the B97-3c level in all structures by ORCA software and Mutifwn software. A step size of 2 fs was employed, and the LINCS approach was used to rigidly hold all covalent bonds to hydrogen atoms. Through the PME method, electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were truncated at 1.4 nm. Following this, a short equilibration of 100 ps was conducted, succeeded by a production phase with a step size of 2 fs in an NPT ensemble using the same protocols. Under the conditions of 300 K and 1 atm, each of the four simulation systems was run for 100 ns. Finally, the systems and trajectories were visualized using VMD v1.9.3 and PyMOL v3.0.

AIA model and drug administration

C57BL/6 mice (five weeks, male, 18–20 g) and sprague-dawley rats (SD, male, 180–220 g) were purchased from Animal Research Center of Air Force Medical University (Xi'an, China). The animal experiments involved in this study were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Fourth Military Medical University Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee (No. KY20243034-1). The tail of the mice was subcutaneously injected with Complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA, 20 μL) (#7027, Chondrex, USA). On the 14th day after injection, the AIA model was successfully constructed.

Treatment was administered on days 14, 17, 20, 23, and 26 after CFA induction in the AIA model. On the 28th day, mice were euthanized by anesthesia (pentobarbital sodium, 65 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). The mice randomly divided into six groups received intravenous injections of different drugs according to the scheme: Normal (an equal volume of saline), AIA (an equal volume of saline), MTX (2.0 mg/kg), RBA (5 mg/kg), TMP (5 mg/kg), RTNs (dose of 5 mg/kg for RBA and 5 mg/kg for TMP). From the 14th day during this period, the body weight of each group mice was measured every other day. The joint diameter change were detected by toe volume measuring instrument (YLS-7C). The arthritis index were examined every two days. Each limb of the mouse was scored on a scale of 0–4 and added up, with the following criteria: 0 for normal; mild swelling was counted as 1; moderate swelling was counted as 2; severe swelling was counted as 3; ankylosis and inability to bear weight was counted as 4. The maximum score was 16. The levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α) and lactate in the serum of mice in each group were investigated using ELISA and lactate assay (Abcam, ab83429, USA) kits.

Micro-CT imaging

All mice were euthanized on the 28th day after AIA induction. After fixation in 4 % concentration of paraformaldehyde, the ankle joint was scanned using an ex vivo micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT, SCANCO MEDICAL VivaCT 80, Switzerland) at a resolution of 70 kV and 113 μA, with a resolution of 15 μm. The 3D images of the distal femoral joint and trabecular bone were produced by reconstructing the dataset.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

The histopathological investigation in this study included hematoxylin-eosin staining (HE), safranin o-fast green staining and toluidine blue staining. The removed ankle joint was fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, followed by chronic decalcification and fixed with 15 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tetrasodium for 2 months. The processed joints were embedded in paraffin and sliced to a thickness of 3 μm, followed by the above three types of staining. Finally, the pathological progression was observed under an optical microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequently, the slices were incubated with Ferroportin polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-64232, 1 μg/mL) at 4 °C overnight (12 h). Detection was performed using a goat anti-rabbit HRP secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 31460, 0.8 μg/mL). The expression was immunohistochemistry labeled using a streptavidin-biotin complex kit.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

After removing the fat and connective tissue from the mouse joints, the sample was digested with collagenase II at 37 °C for 1 h. The obtained cell suspension was centrifuged at 800×g for 5 min and the supernatant was removed. Subsequently, the single-cell suspension is subjected to quality inspection and counting, generally requiring a cell survival rate of over 85 %. The cells that passed the test were washed and resuspended to prepare suitable cell concentrations of 700–1200 cells/μL for 10 × Genomics Chromium System detection. After sample loading, GEMs (Gel Beam in Impulse) were constructed for single-cell isolation. After normal formation of GEMs, they were collected and labeled by reverse transcription in a PCR instrument. Next, the GEMs were subjected to oil extraction, and a strand of cDNA was purified and enriched using magnetic beads for cDNA amplification and quality inspection. Finally, we constructed a second-generation sequencing library of cDNA and used the Illumina Hiseq platform to perform PE150 sequencing on the constructed library.

Animal experimental methods related to anemia

PHZ-induced anemia: Mice were pretreated with 200 mm butyrate in drinking water for two days, followed by i.p. injection of 40 mg/kg PHZ (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) daily for two consecutive days.

Low-iron diet model: Mice were fed a low-iron diet containing 8 ppm iron (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) or a control diet containing 45 ppm iron (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering) after weaning for four weeks.

Macrophage depletion: To achieve macrophage depletion, mice were i.p. injected with 150 μL clodronate liposomes (FormuMax Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) on days −2, 2, and 5 of the AIA model.

Iron and hemoglobin measurement

After anesthetizing mice with pentobarbital sodium, blood was collected from the heart using a syringe. The blood (100 μL) was added to a centrifuge tube containing EDTA anticoagulant, and the remaining whole blood was left at room temperature to wait for coagulation before separating the serum. The coagulated and stratified whole blood was centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 10 min in a centrifuge, and then the serum from the upper layer was taken for serum iron and hemoglobin measurement. The iron content in synovium and serum was detected by iron assay kit (Abcam, ab83366, USA). Hemoglobin levels were measured using an automatic analyzer (Mindray BC-2800Vet, China). The prussian blue staining was performed on iron in the synovial tissue of AIA mice as described previously.

Macrophages polarization

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. Next, cells were incubated with different concentrations of free RBA, free TMP, and RTNs for 24 h. After detecting RAW264.7 cells with cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8), the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader to calculate the cell survival rate. Polarization of M1 subtype macrophages was achieved by treating RAW264.7 with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) + interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, 20 ng/mL) for 24 h at 37 °C. Polarization of M2 subtype macrophages was achieved by treating RAW264.7 with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) + IL-13 (20 ng/mL) for 24 h at 37 °C. The levels of M1 subtype macrophages (iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β) and M2 subtype macrophages (Arg-1, IL-10, TGF-β) markers were appraised using ELISA method. The ratio of M1 and M2 subtypes of macrophages was revealed by immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analysis

After administration, each group cells were blocked with PBS solution containing 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA), and then incubated with FITC-labeled anti-mouse CD86 antibody (Biolegend, 105005, 5 μg/mL) and APC-labeled anti-mouse CD206 antibody (Biolegend, 141707, 5 μg/mL) in a dark environment at 4 °C for 1 h. With stopping antibody incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. On the one hand, cells were prepared as a suspension and detected using flow cytometry (BD FACSCelesta, USA), and the results were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA). On the other hand, macrophages in the adherent state were stained with DAPI and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 800, Zeiss, Germany). The fluorescence intensity was quantified by Image J (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Safety evaluation

Healthy mice (20 ± 2 g) were intravenously injected with RBA (5 mg/kg), TMP (5 mg/kg), or RTNs (dose of 5 mg/kg for RBA and 5 mg/kg for TMP) to inspect the biosafety of drugs. The mice were euthanized according to the medication regimen. The blood of each group of animals was collected and centrifuged (2296 g) for 8 min to obtain serum. Subsequently, the levels of relevant indicators were measured using a automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, 7020, Japan). The HE staining was used to investigate the pathological changes of RTNs on major organs of mice. After the completion of drug administration according to the protocol, the animals were euthanized and the main organs of each group of rats were taken out: heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. The tissues were cleaned with physiological saline and soaked in 4 % paraformaldehyde for fixation. After being embedded in paraffin, the slices were frozen and sliced into 5 μm sections. Then perform H&E staining to observe the pathological status of the main organs in each group of animals.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were analyzed by SPSS software (version 20.0) and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent Student's t-test (two-sided) was used to evaluate the data between two groups. One way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate multiple sets of data. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of RTNs

RTN was formed by self-assembly of nature products RBA and TMP in aqueous solution. In brief, we prepared RTNs through a one-step self-assembly process, where the molar ratio of RBA to TMP was 1:1. A homogeneous solution was obtained using ultrasonic method in aqueous solution (Fig. 1A). Obvious Tyndall light-scattering phenomenon was observed in RTNs solution (Fig. 1B). TEM image showed that the self-assembled RTNs was roughly spherical in shape (Fig. 1C). The particle size of RTNs were about 167.52 ± 12.85 nm as measured by DLS, and the PDI was 0.21 ± 0.07 (Fig. 1D). The zeta potentials of RTNs were −6.83 ± 0.39 mV (Fig. 1E). The TEM and DLS results mutually confirmed the size of RTNs. RTNs was characterized by UV–vis and fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and fluorescence analysis. The UV–vis absorption spectra showed that the maximum absorption peak of RTNs was 296 nm, while the maximum absorption peaks of RBA and TMP were 203 nm and 270 nm, respectively. The emergence of absorption shift suggested that RBA and TMP had self-assembled into a new complex structure. The changes of functional groups were preliminarily displayed by FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 1G). Compared with RBA's carboxyl stretching vibration at 1713 cm−1, the infrared absorption intensity of the carboxyl band from RTNs reduced. This might be due to the electrostatic interaction between the lone electron pairs of the nitrogen atom from TMP and the carboxyl group from RBA during the self-assembly process of RTNs, resulting in a decrease in the stretching vibration frequency of the C=O double bond in RTNs. Finally, the self-assembly characteristics of RTNs were determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 1H). The single peak at the 9.09 ppm chemical shift in RBA weakened in the corresponding RTN, indicating electrostatic interactions between some carboxyl groups and lone pair electrons in TMP, which was consistent with the results of FT-IR spectroscopy. In addition, the peak at the chemical shift of 2.65 in TMP shifted to 2.53 in the corresponding RTNs, which might be due to the interference of methyl hydrogen by TMP after forming hydrogen bonds with RBA. In summary, the carboxyl group of RBA and the lone electron pair of TMP generated electrostatic interactions, forming stable self-assembled structures of RTNs through hydrophobic interactions.

Self-assembly mechanism of RTNs

The self-assembly process of RBA and TMP was studied through molecular dynamics simulation. The molecular structure of a frame every 20 ns is described as a representative conformation in the RBA-TMP system (Fig. 2A). RTNs was found to be tightly interlinked and formed a spherical structure within 100 ns (Fig. 2B). It was worth noting that TMP molecules are firmly embedded within RBA molecules via hydrogen bonds (Fig. 2C). The root mean square deviation (RMSD) was used in molecular simulations to measure the degree of trajectory stability. From Fig. 2D, the results suggested that the system was stable during the simulation process with the average RMSD value was 4.752 ± 0.374 nm. Moreover, the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) decreased sharply from 0 to 20 ns, indicating that the surface area of nanoparticles exposed to solvents gradually decreased and stabilized after 20 ns, showing that the system formed more intensive and more stable nanoclusters after reducing solvent exposure. The mean SASA of RBA-TMP system was 78.96 ± 15.27 nm2 (Fig. 2E). The RBA-TMP system displayed an mean of 1 ± 0.52 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 2F). The radius of gyration (Rg) of the RBA-TMP system was analyzed, which displayed the spatial compactness and morphological changes of the molecular system (Fig. 2G). Finally, we explored the driving factors of RBA and TMP self-assembly nanoparticles. The dominant forces in the RBA-TMP system were hydrogen bonding. The strength of ‌Van der Waals force‌ was measured by LJ-SR, while the strength of coulomb force was measured by Coul-SR [21]. The mean values of Coul-SR and LJ-SR in the RBA-TMP system were −12.18 ± 1.24 and −412.64 ± 63.52 kJ/mol, respectively, indicating that the ‌Van der Waals force‌ was substantially stronger than that of coulomb force (Fig. 2H). Taken together, molecular dynamics simulation revealed that hydrogen bonding and ‌Van der Waals force were the main forces driving self-assembly in RBA-TMP system [22].

Assessment of the in vivo distribution of RTNs

DiR was a lipid soluble near-infrared fluorescent dye with strong lipophilicity and excellent membrane fusion ability [23], which was used for visualizing the distribution of nanocarriers in vivo. DiR was encapsulated into the RTNs to obtain DiR@RTNs. The DiR@RTNs solution and free DiR were injected into the tail vein of AIA rat model. Subsequently, we used an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) to survey the fluorescence intensity of DiR in the joints at 6, 12, 24 and 48 h after administration. According to Fig. 3A and B, the average fluorescence intensity of DiR@RTNs in the AIA joint was prominently stronger than that of free DiR at 6, 12 and 24 h. And at the 24h time point, there was still significant fluorescence distribution in the AIA joint. However, the fluorescence at the joints almost completely disappeared within 48 h. We also evaluated the distribution of DiR in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys and paws of AIA rats at 6, 12 and 24 h after intravenous injection (Fig. 3C and D). It was obvious that compared to the heart, lungs, and kidneys, DiR@RTNs exhibited greater fluorescence in inflamed joints at three time points. This might be related to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect driven by inflammatory factors. The solid tumor environment consists of “leaky” vasculature from gaps in the endothelial cell lining and fenestrations that allow for higher permeability of relatively large molecules and particles. This enhanced permeability paired with an impaired lymphatic drainage system in tumor tissues is known as the EPR effect. The process of monocyte recruitment from circulation and the development of endothelial gaps that facilitate plasma leakage into the injured site are characteristic of inflamed joints. In RA inflammatory conditions, inflammatory mediators can be overexpressed and persistent, leading to ‘leaky’ vasculature similar to that seen in solid tumors. The use of EPR phenomenon for targeted drug delivery can promote the passive targeted accumulation of nanoscale therapeutic agents in RA joints compared to free drugs and reduce the systemic toxicities of potent anti-RA agents [[24], [25], [26]]. Moreover, the higher distribution of DiR@RTNs in the liver and spleen were due to the liver being a drug metabolism organ and the spleen being the main immune organ. According to known data, the metabolism of TMP is mainly converted into hydroxylation or glucuronic acid conjugates by the liver CYP450 enzyme system, and ultimately excreted by the kidneys [27]. The metabolic data of RBA is still unclear and requires further research. The spleen is the largest immune organ in the human body, playing a crucial role in immune regulation and inflammatory response. RA, as an autoimmune disease, may involve the spleen in its pathological process through mechanisms such as abnormal immune cell activation, inflammatory cytokine release, and imbalanced blood filtration function [28]. Perhaps due to these reasons and the EPR effect, RTNs significantly aggregate in the spleen.

In a word, RTNs represented outstanding inflammatory aggregation, which might contribute to their therapeutic effects in RA joints.

RTNs inhibit inflammation and relieve cartilage lesion in AIA mice

To evaluate the efficacy of RTNs in treating RA, we injected Freund's complete adjuvant into the ankle joints of mice to establish an AIA mouse model and investigate the therapeutic effects of different treatment groups. The therapeutic schematic of RTNs were shown in Fig. 4A. Methotrexate (MTX), a first-line treatment drug for RA [29], was used as a positive control for comparative efficacy. The study first dynamically monitored the body weight of all groups of mice, and the results showed that there was a small fluctuation in body weight throughout the treatment period, but overall it was relatively stable (Fig. 4B). We could visually observe abnormal joint swelling in the AIA group (Fig. 4C and E). After MTX and RTNs treatment, the joint diameter of AIA mice underwent prominent changes, indicating successful inhibition of joint swelling in AIA mice. Similarly, compared to the AIA group, MTX and RTNs treatment groups sensibly diminished the arthritis index (Fig. 4D). In addition, the organ indices (spleen and thymus indices) of the AIA group significantly increased, while RTNs could effectively reduce these two indices (Fig. S1A–B). Articular cartilage degeneration was evaluated using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) score. In Fig. S2, RTNs strikingly reduced the OARSI score of AIA mice. However, RBA and TMP did not show the same effects. Then, micro CT analysis was used to further validate bone erosion in a more intuitive way (Fig. 4F). These images represent the anterolateral ankle joint of mice. Arrows are used to mark the location of bone tissue damage. The AIA group showed that the inflamed ankle bone had a rough surface and severe bone erosion. The relief of bone erosion by free RBA and TMP was limited, but MTX and RTNs treatment strikingly smoothes the bone surface, approaching that of normal mice. The H&E staining results revealed that RTNs effectively improved the pathological microenvironment of the ankle joint in AIA mice, and significantly inhibited synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4G). Toluidine blue staining and Safranin O-fast green staining assays were used to clearly label the morphology of chondrocytes and distinguish between normal and degenerated cartilage tissues [6]. In Fig. 4H and I, the staining results elucidated that RTNs relieve cartilage lesion in AIA mice. The tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining also confirmed the cartilage protective effect of RTNs (Fig. 4J). The MTX still showed good cartilage protection in RA. During the pathological process of RA, a large number of immune cells accumulate in the joint cavity. These immune cells, especially pro-inflammatory macrophages, use glycolysis metabolism to rapidly provide energy [30,31]. Lactic acid, as an important product of this process, can lead to acidification of the joint cavity environment in RA [32]. Therefore, this study detected lactate to evaluate the glycolytic activity and development process of RA in different treatment groups. In Fig. 4K, the level of lactic acid in AIA group was 3.9 times higher than that in normal group. After treatment with MTX and RTNs, the lactate levels were almost close to normal. Meanwhile, RBA and TMP didn't have these therapeutic effects. Inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, are important indicators for measuring the degree of inflammation in AIA mice. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of AIA mice after RTNs treatment

We then explored the therapeutic mechanisms of RTNs by performing single-cell RNA sequencing analysis on the joints of the AIA group and the RTNs treatment group. The uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for visualizing cells and identifying 15 major clusters, including B cells, basophils, chondrocytes, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, erythrocytes, fibroblasts, granulocytes, macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, NK cells, stem cells, stromal cells (Fig. 5A). RTNs reduced the number of pro-inflammatory macrophages while increasing the number of chondrocytes and erythrocytes. As was well known, macrophages and erythrocytes were related to inflammation, while chondrocytes were associated with cartilage tissue. The results were consistent with the conclusion that RTNs alleviated synovitis and cartilage tissue damage in AIA mice (Fig. 4). Based on our previous research manifesting the function of macrophages that regulated immune inflammation and bone tissue damage [5], we attempted to focus on the role of macrophages in the treatment of RA with RTNs. We set differential expression analysis on all genes with p-values <0.05 and FC ≥ 2 to compare the differences between the two groups. The results displayed that there were 362 genes with significant differences in expression after RTNs treatment. Among them, 218 genes were significantly up-regulated (red dots), and 144 genes were significantly down-regulated (blue dots) (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, the rich factors of disease enrichment revealed that significantly different genes were associated with diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune diseases, liver cirrhosis, and so on (Fig. 5C). In KEGG enrichment (Fig. 5D), both in terms of p-values and rich factor, the role of RTNs was mainly concentrated in HIF-1 signaling pathway, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, rheumatoid arthritis and ferroptosis, which were usually closely related to signal transduction, autoimmune diseases, energy metabolism, and iron metabolism processes. Abnormal accumulation of iron within cells can generate oxygen free radicals through the Fenton reaction, thereby damaging various cellular components and affecting biological functions [33]. Previous research showed that regulating ferroptosis and iron metabolism might be potential candidates for RA treatment [34,35]. Thus, we were interested in the pathway of iron metabolism disorder and investigated whether it involved the anti-RA effect of RTNs in subsequent pharmacological studies. In summary, single-cell sequencing data suggested that the relief of RA symptoms by RTNs might involve interventions in various biological processes and signal transduction, such as immune regulation and metabolic imbalance.

RTNs restore macrophage iron homeostasis by facilitating FPN expression

Iron disorder refers to the anomalous absorption, release, and metabolism of iron, manifested externally as anemia, iron overload, and hereditary hemochromatosis. RA patients often experience symptoms of focal iron overload and inflammatory anemia [17,36]. Especially, the single-cell sequencing analysis suggested that the therapeutic effects of RTNs on RA might involve regulating the imbalance of iron metabolism in macrophages (Fig. 5). The Fe3+ in iron containing hemoglobin in the synovial tissue of AIA mice reacted with potassium ferrocyanide to form Prussian blue ferrocyanide precipitate [37], as shown in Fig. 6A, where the blue part represented the ferrocyanide precipitate. In Fig. 6A and B, the joint synovium of AIA mice showed iron deposition, and RTNs significantly improved this symptom. Upon analysis of Fig. 6C–E, compared with healthy mice, the serum iron, hemoglobin, and transferrin levels of AIA mice were decreased. It was worth noting that RTNs notably improved these RA anemia symptoms. To determine whether the anti-RA anemia effect of RTNs was associated with the alleviation of RA inflammation, we used a phenylhydrazine (PHZ)-induced inflammation independent anemia model [38]. PHZ destroyed membrane proteins on the surface of red blood cells, followed by a subsequent reduction in serum iron and hemoglobin levels (Fig. 6F). Interestingly, RTNs did not significantly mitigate the decline of hemoglobin and serum iron induced by PHZ in mice. In addition, we administered a low-iron diet to mice to establish an anemia model. Similarly, RTNs didn't effectively increase the serum iron levels in mice given a low-iron diet (Fig. 6G). These results indicated that RTNs could only restore iron homeostasis in RA inflammatory state, rather than all iron disturbances. Macrophages play a dominant role in regulating the iron homeostasis of the body. The single-cell sequencing analysis showed that RTNs significantly intervened in the number of macrophages during the treatment of RA. We depleted macrophages with clodronate liposomes in AIA mice, and then treated them with RTNs. As shown in Fig. 6H, RTNs had no effects on improving hemoglobin and serum iron. Next, we further investigated how RTNs regulated iron metabolism in macrophages. According to KEGG enrichment of single-cell sequencing analysis, the significantly differentially expressed genes enriched in Ferroptosis pathway mainly include: slc3a2, slc7a11, acsl1, sat1, trf, slc40a1, cybb, fth1. Among the genes related to iron export, RTNs significantly improved the expression of ferroportin (FPN, encoded by the SLC40A1 gene) (Fig. 6I). FPN is a membrane protein belonging to the major transporter superfamily, and is the only known protein that transports iron to the extracellular space. It mainly exists on the surface of cells such as macrophages and liver cells [39]. After internalization and degradation in macrophages, FPN restricted the transport of intracellular iron into the bloodstream, causing iron utilization disorders, affecting red blood cell production, and ultimately leading to the occurrence of RA and inflammatory anemia [40]. The dual results of flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry demonstrated that RTNs regulated iron transport by upregulating FPN expression in RA inflamed joint sites (Fig. 6J and K, Fig. S3). In other words, RTNs restored macrophage iron homeostasis by facilitating FPN expression.

According to surveys, about 30–70 % of RA patients experience anemia in clinical practice, with inflammatory anemia being the most common type of anemia among hospitalized patients [41]. Although both inflammatory anemia and iron deficiency anemia have a decrease in serum iron levels, their pathogenesis is different [42]. In short, we found that RTNs could rebalance iron homeostasis under RA inflammatory state. And this is a new unexpected discovery, our research not only expanded the therapeutic scope of RTNs, but also developed a nanomedicine for treating RA secondary anemia. Nonetheless, the detailed degradation mechanism of FPN has always been a hot and difficult topic in the field of iron metabolism. How RTNs specifically regulate the degradation of FPN to improve iron metabolism disorders in RA joints needs further investigation.

RTNs regulate macrophage function through FPN-mediated iron export for RA treatment

Previous reports had confirmed that iron overload in RA joints exacerbated arthritis by altering the iron function of macrophages, and local high levels of iron were positively correlated with the severity of RA inflammation and joint damage [43,44]. Of note, M1 and M2 macrophage subtypes not only exhibited different iron metabolism states, but also showed different sensitivities to high concentrations of iron [35]. Iron disturbance leading to polarization switch of macrophage subtypes and subsequent inflammation and immune microenvironment disruption was a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for RA. Given that the results of Fig. 6 had validated that RTNs could restore macrophage iron homeostasis for the treatment of RA, this section focuses on elucidating how RTNs intervened in macrophage function and joint inflammatory microenvironment through iron metabolism.

We subjected the in vitro and in vivo experiments. The flow cytometry was used to inspect the switch of M1/M2 macrophage subtypes. From Fig. 7A and B, we found that after treatment with 80 μM RTNs, the proportion of CD86+ macrophages in live cells significantly decreased, from 54.7 % to 17.77 %. At the same time, the proportion of CD206+ macrophages prominently increased to 45.19 %. The dose of 40 μM RTNs exhibited weaker repolarization ability. We had verified that RTNs restored macrophage iron homeostasis by facilitating FPN expression (Fig. 6). Therefore, we used FPN siRNA to knock out FPN and investigated the regulatory effects of RTNs on macrophages. The ratio of M1 macrophages after Si FPN treatment showed no significant difference compared to the model group, still reaching 52.7 %. In the FPN knockout state, RTNs did not alter the switching of macrophage subtypes, which was consistent with the previous findings that RTNs regulated macrophage iron homeostasis through FPN mediation (Fig. 7A and B, Fig. S4). The determination of M1/M2 macrophage subtype markers also supported this conclusion. From Figs. 7C and 80 μM RTNs dramatically down-regulated the levels of M1 macrophage markers (included iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β), while promoting the expression of M2 macrophage markers (included Arg-1, IL-10, TGF-β) in a dose-dependent manner. However, after FPN knockout, the intervention effects of 80 μM RTNs on M1 and M2 biomarker levels was minimal. Similar results have also been validated in vivo. To validate this possibility, we further identified M1 and M2 macrophages subtype by immunofluorescent staining of joints in mice using M1 maker CD86 (green) as well as M2 maker CD206 (green) (Fig. 7D). Strikingly, the high expression of CD86 in M1 macrophages was restrained by RTNs, while the content of CD206, which was originally less expressed in RA joints, increased effectively after RTNs treatment. After Si FPN treatment, the effects of RTNs reducing M1 phenotype and enhancing M2 phenotype disappeared. Local iron overload occurs in the synovium of RA joints, and excessive iron induce monocytes to differentiate into M1 pro-inflammatory macrophage subtypes, thereby disrupting the M1/M2 balance and joint immune homeostasis [35,45]. According to previous research reports, the concentration of free iron in RA-SF was higher than the serum level of RA patients, which might be due to the following two main factors. On the one hand, the upregulation of hepcidin caused by inflammatory stimulation limited the efflux of iron recovered from cells, leading to a disorder in iron distribution between blood and tissue organs. Another source of excessive iron in RA joints was the proliferation of RA vascular opacities, and hypoxia increased vascular permeability and leakage of hemosiderin [[46], [47], [48]]. The M1/M2 immune imbalance in RA joints had long been recognized, and our research revealed that RTNs regulated macrophage function and reshaped the RA immune microenvironment by restoring iron balance. Our data provided a new explanation for this.

Biosafety evaluation of RTNs

RBA and TMP are representative active ingredients of the classic formula Daqinjiao decoction [5,8], and this study aims to use them for synergistic administration research. Biosafety is crucial for evaluating the clinical efficacy and targeting ability of RTNs. RTNs is a carrier-free self-assembled drug delivery system consisting only of two natural products, RBA and TMP, theoretically proving the biological safety of the nanomedicine.

In this study, a brief evaluation was conducted on the in vitro and in vivo biosafety of RTNs. The cytotoxicity of RBA, TMP and RTNs were evaluated in RAW264.7 cells in vitro. We exposed the cells to different concentrations of drugs for 24 h. The cytotoxicity of RBA, TMP, and RTNs was dose-dependent, with the RTNs group showing a decrease in cell viability from 89 % at a dose of 40 μM–78 % at a dose of 80 μM (Fig. S5). The data showed that the concentration of RTNs used in this research was within a safe and reasonable range. Then, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys of mice from RBA, TMP, and control groups were collected and stained with H&E. From Fig. 8A, it could be seen that there were no significant pathological changes observed after treatment with RTNs, indicating that RTNs appeared to be safe and biocompatible. Moreover, the white blood cells (WBC) and red blood cells (RBC) were used to observe spleen and blood-routine-test (Fig. 8B and C), alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) were used for liver function (Fig. 8D and E), lung W/D weight ratio was used to observe pulmonary tissue edema (Fig. 8F), creatine kinase (CK) and lactic dehydrogenase (LDH) were used for heart function (Fig. 8G and H), and uric acid (UA) were used for kidney function. The above detection proved that the fluctuations of these indicators after RTNs treatment were within the normal reference ranges. Conclusion

In this study, a carrier-free nanomedicine (RTNs) was prepared through self-assembly of RBA and TMP for drug delivery, aiming to achieve the multi-objective of improving drug bioavailability for efficient and safe treatment of RA syndrome. The RTNs spherical nanostructures formed by electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding between RBA and TMP exhibited strong inflammatory joint accumulation. RTNs effectively inhibited joint swelling and arthritis index in AIA mice, reversed elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α), improved RA joint pathological microenvironment, promoted cartilage damage repair, restored macrophage iron homeostasis via facilitating FPN expression, and ultimately regulated macrophage function to treat RA syndrome and its associated inflammatory anemia. Meanwhile, RTNs exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy against RA compared to the same doses of free RBA and TMP, and were demonstrated to be safe and biocompatible. Additionally, the preparation process of RTNs was simple and reproducible, with the potential to achieve clinical translation. To sum up, RTNs extended an effective and safe strategy for drug optimization and treatment of RA.
