Research advances in the pathogenesis and photodynamic therapy of pathological scars
Authors: Jingyu Liu, Hongxiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review summarizes recent advances in the pathogenesis of pathological scars, including hypertrophic scars and keloids, and their photodynamic therapy (PDT). The formation of pathological scars involves fibroblast dysfunction, imbalanced ECM metabolism, chronic inflammation, aberrant activation of multiple signaling pathways (e.g., TGF-β/Smad, JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt/mTOR), epigenetic regulation, and biomechanical factors, against a background of genetic susceptibility. As a minimally invasive and targeted therapy, PDT acts through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light activation of photosensitizers, leading to fibroblast death, suppression of collagen synthesis, interruption of key signaling pathways, and modulation of immune responses and angiogenesis, thereby effectively improving scars. Recent developments in novel photosensitizers and nanotechnology-based delivery systems have significantly enhanced the penetration and efficacy of PDT, offering new directions for its precise clinical application.Keywords: Pathological scar, Hypertrophic scar, Keloid, Pathogenesis, Photodynamic therapy

Introduction

Pathological scars predominantly consist of hypertrophic scars (HS) and keloids. The pathogenesis involves a vicious cycle process that occurs against a background of genetic susceptibility, driven by persistent chronic inflammation and mediated through complex signaling networks. This dysregulated process induces fibroblast hyperactivation, impairs extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and culminates in keloid collagen disorganization and fibrotic nodule formation. Notably, emerging evidence indicates a paradigm shift from the ‘homogeneous’ model towards a ‘heterogeneity’-centered perspective [1], fostering a novel biological interpretation of these scars as quasi-tumors.

Pathogenesis of pathological scar

Core cellular mechanism

Primary pathogenic cells-dysfunctional fibroblasts

Fibroblasts are the core executors of scar formation. They are not a single cell type but are composed of multiple subpopulations with different functions. These subpopulations differ in embryonic origin, dermal layer localization, gene expression profiles, and functional characteristics [2]. Solé-Boldo, et al. [3] performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on skin samples from sun-protected areas (inguinal-iliac region) of healthy male donors to systematically identify and characterize human dermal fibroblast subpopulations. They defined four main subtypes: secretory-papillary fibroblasts and secretory-reticular fibroblasts, which highly express various collagen genes (e.g., COL1A1, COL3A1, COL5A1, COL6A1) and ECM-related genes, primarily responsible for collagen and ECM production. Pro-inflammatory fibroblasts highly express chemokines (CCL19, CXCL3, CXCL4) and inflammation-related genes (APOE) but show low expression of ECM-related genes, playing a role in regulating inflammatory responses and chemotaxis. Mesenchymal fibroblasts highly express genes associated with skeletal development, ossification, and chondrogenesis (ASPN, POSTN, GPC3, TNN, SFRP1). Notably, mesenchymal fibroblasts (which are characterized by high POSTN expression) are the primary contributors to keloid formation, with TGF-β regulating POSTN expression and promoting mesenchymal activation of endothelial cells.

Keloids are hyperproliferative scars that extend beyond the original wound boundaries and show no tendency for spontaneous regression. Serror et al. [4] analyzed the gene expression and functional properties (proliferation, migration, traction forces) of fibroblasts derived from different keloid regions, assessing inter-keloid (nodular vs. extensive) and intra-keloid (center vs. periphery, papillary vs. reticular) heterogeneity. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed that nodular and extensive keloids are distinct entities, with 834 differentially expressed genes between them. ECM-related gene expression revealed that central reticular fibroblasts in nodular keloids serve as the core region of ECM production, highly expressing mature collagens, TGF-β, HIF1α, and αSMA. Functional analysis demonstrated that peripheral fibroblasts from extensive keloids exhibit higher migration capacity and traction forces, which may explain their characteristic expansion into surrounding healthy tissue. This study confirmed both inter- and intra-keloid heterogeneity.

Almet et al. [5] integrated multiple scRNA-seq datasets to construct a high-resolution spatiotemporal atlas of fibroblast dynamics during skin wound healing, revealing three core dimensions of fibroblast heterogeneity: spatial location (upper/lower dermis), temporal progression (healing stage), and functional output (ECM/signaling factors). Scar tissue is not composed of a uniform fibroblast population but is stratified into molecularly distinct upper (Col25a1+) and lower (Pamr1+) fibroblasts. These upper and lower fibroblasts differentiate from a common progenitor pool in early wound healing and subsequently do not interconvert, forming two independent lineage compartments. Prg4 + FIB-IV fibroblasts represent an early wound-responsive population expressing chemokines and inflammatory genes, potentially playing a key role in initiating repair. The composition of fibroblast subpopulations changes dynamically over the healing timeline, whereas other cell types (e.g., keratinocytes, endothelial cells) remain relatively stable. Using cluster analysis, the authors demonstrated that fibroblast heterogeneity is primarily driven by two major functional gene sets: ECM synthesis genes (e.g., Col25a1) and signaling ligand genes. Keloid fibroblasts possess intrinsic defects, exhibiting autonomous and invasive behavior. Even after wound healing is complete, these cells continue to proliferate and resist apoptosis. They do not depend on persistent inflammation or mechanical tension stimuli, instead invading the surrounding normal tissue in a tumor-like fashion.

The persistent role of immune cells

Chronic inflammation serves as the cornerstone for the initiation and persistence of keloids. A diverse array of immune cells infiltrates the lesion, establishing a pro-fibrotic microenvironment. Macrophages transmit pro-fibrotic signals to fibroblasts through multiple signaling pathways, including TGF-β, PDGF, and Wnt. These pathways are precisely coordinated in a spatiotemporal manner to collectively drive fibroblast activation, proliferation, myofibroblast differentiation, and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. The dynamic phenotypic switch of macrophages from M1 to M2 is a key regulatory mechanism controlling the intensity of these pro-fibrotic signals [6]. Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) promote M2 macrophage polarization and fibroblast activation, thereby exacerbating tissue fibrosis [7]. Increased Th2-associated cytokines are observed in hypertrophic scars from burn patients. IL-17 induces the expression of SDF-1, TGF-β, collagen, and α-SMA in keloid fibroblasts via the STAT3 signaling pathway, implicating Th17 cells in fibroblast activation [8]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) play a crucial role in maintaining immune balance during wound healing. Highly activated FOXP3 + Tregs are significantly increased in injured skin, where they attenuate the accumulation of M1 macrophages and pro-inflammatory T cells (such as Th1 and Th17). Specific ablation of Tregs leads to delayed wound re-epithelialization, indicating that Tregs promote cutaneous wound healing by suppressing inflammatory responses [9]. However, a genetic study utilizing a two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization approach identified a potential causal relationship between immune cells and hypertrophic scar risk. Analyzing 731 immune cell traits, this study found that the CD45RA+CD39 + Treg subset was potentially strongly associated with an increased risk of hypertrophic scar, as suggested by the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method (PFDR < 0.05), a finding supported by weighted median, weighted mode, and MR-Egger methods [10].

Neutrophils are the first leukocytes to arrive at the injury site in response to DAMPs (Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns)/PAMPs (Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns). They capture and kill microorganisms by forming neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), whose components include neutrophil elastase, myeloperoxidase, and MMPs [11]. While controlled NET release is protective, excessive release can damage the wound matrix, impair angiogenesis, and harm reparative cells, leading to uncontrolled inflammation and delayed healing. Fetal wounds, which heal scarlessly, exhibit minimal neutrophil recruitment, partly due to lower P-selectin expression on fetal endothelial cells. In patients developing hypertrophic scars after cardiac surgery, P-selectin mRNA expression in the wound is significantly higher than in those with normal healing. P-selectin mediates leukocyte adhesion to endothelial cells, potentially facilitating the recruitment of other immune cells to the hypertrophic scar. Mast cells are resident inflammatory cells in the skin. Following skin injury, mast cells release mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes and chymase [12], increasing vascular permeability and recruiting immune cells. Mast cell-deficient mice show reduced neutrophil recruitment and impaired wound healing. In vitro co-culture systems demonstrate that mast cells can induce α-SMA expression and enhance the contractile force of fibroblasts.

Unique function of melanocytes

Elevated melanocyte activity is an important factor of the high incidence of keloids in dark-skinned populations. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis demonstrated that melanocytes within keloids exhibit a significant enrichment of pathways associated with pigment synthesis (e.g., melanosome signaling), with upregulated expression of key genes (MITF, PMEL, TYR, TYRP1, TYRP2). The melanin content in keloid tissue and affected patient skin is significantly higher than in normal controls, with a positive correlation between melanin levels and the clinical severity index of keloids (KASI). Melanin has been confirmed as a pivotal driver of fibroblast activation. Melanin substantially elevates the expression of iron storage proteins (FTH1, FTL) and iron import protein (TFRC) in fibroblasts, leading to a notable increase in intracellular iron ion levels (total iron, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺) [13]. Nevertheless, fibroblasts treated with melanin demonstrate marked resistance to ferroptosis. This protective effect stems from melanin’s ability to enhance the expression of critical ferroptosis suppressors (GPX4, SLC7A11, SLC3A2) while simultaneously suppressing ferroptosis-promoting factors (ACSL4). This coexistence of iron overload and resistance to ferroptosis, on one hand, triggers signals for the continuous activation of fibroblasts, while on the other hand, enables fibroblasts to escape apoptosis. Consequently, this results in uncontrolled fibroblast proliferation, enhanced migratory capacity, and excessive collagen accumulation (COL1A1, α-SMA) [14].

Molecular signaling pathway network

An intricate network of multiple signaling pathways operates in a highly coordinated manner to collectively govern the formation and progression of scars [15].

TGF-β/smad signaling pathway

The TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway is widely recognized as the principal and most extensively studied pathway driving the development of pathological scar. Its persistent activation leads to prolonged hyperactivation of both fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, serving as a primary driver for the excessive synthesis and accumulation of collagen. The type II receptor localized on the plasma membrane is initially targeted by the TGF-β ligand, after which the type I receptor (mainly ALK5) is recruited and phosphorylated, leading to the establishment of an activated receptor complex. Following activation, the type I receptor phosphorylates Smad2 and Smad3, which subsequently form complexes with Smad4 upon binding. These phosphorylated Smad2/3-Smad4 complexes translocate into the nucleus, where they function as transcriptional regulators that specifically recognize and bind to promoter elements (e.g., the CAGACA motifs within collagen gene promoters), thereby initiating the transcription of target genes. The activated Smad complex directly upregulates the transcriptional expression of Type I and III collagen, the main components of ECM in scars [16]. Furthermore, the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts is induced by the TGF-β/Smad pathway. Myofibroblasts exhibit α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression, demonstrate robust contractile capability and elevated collagen secretion level, collectively contributing to tissue contracture and the progression of fibrosis.

While stimulating collagen synthesis, the TGF-β/Smad pathway concurrently suppresses the expression or functional activity of collagen-degrading proteases (e.g., matrix metalloproteinases, MMPs), thereby disrupting the balance between collagen synthesis and degradation and ultimately resulting in extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation. Smad7 can interfere with R-Smad phosphorylation and recruit E3 ubiquitin ligases (e.g., Smurf) to degrade receptors and Smad proteins. However, within pathological scar tissue, the compensatory negative feedback loop governing this signaling pathway may exhibit insufficiency or undergo suppression, thereby sustaining persistent activation of the pathway [17, 18]. The TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway is hyperactivated in both hypertrophic scars and keloids. However, in hypertrophic scars, TGF-β signaling may downregulate as the healing process progresses. In contrast, in keloids, the pathway remains persistently “switched on” due to the loss or functional inhibition of Smad7 (a negative feedback inhibitor), such as through targeted suppression by miR-21.

The JAK/STAT signaling pathway: a critical molecular link between inflammatory signaling and cellular proliferation

The activation of the STAT signaling pathway, with a particular emphasis on STAT3, represents a pivotal nexus in the transmission of both pro-inflammatory signals (e.g., IL-6, IL-17) and pro-fibrotic signals [19]. IL-6 exhibits high expression levels in scar tissue. Following its binding to the specific IL-6 receptor, IL-6 initiates the activation of Janus kinase (JAK) family members. Subsequently, JAK kinases phosphorylate and thereby activate signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). Once activated, STAT3 dimers undergo nuclear translocation, and functions as a transcriptional regulator to initiate the transcription of its downstream target genes. The IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis synergistically enhances collagen biosynthesis, facilitates extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, stimulates fibroblast transition into the active cell cycle to promote proliferation, and induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby potentially contributing to the generation of additional myofibroblasts. Th17-derived IL-17 exhibits elevated expression levels in scar tissue. This cytokine can directly or indirectly (via the induction of IL-6 production) activate STAT3. IL-6 and IL-17 establish a positive feedback loop, wherein they mutually enhance their expression and sustain the activation of STAT3, thereby amplifying both inflammatory and fibrotic signaling pathways.

Multiple studies have confirmed that the expression and phosphorylation levels of STAT3 are significantly elevated in keloid tissues and keloid fibroblasts [18, 19]. Moreover, JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3 are all upregulated in keloids, with JAK2 mediating the phosphorylation of STAT3 [20, 21]. Yin et al. [22] in a systematic review, included 13 preclinical studies investigating the use of JAK and/or STAT inhibitors (JAK inhibitors: AG490, Ruxolitinib. STAT3 inhibitors: Cucurbitacin I, ASC-J9, STA-21, Niclosamide, S3I-201, among others). Utilizing in vitro cultured human keloid fibroblasts (KFs), keloid keratinocytes (KKs), as well as some three-dimensional skin models or animal models, these studies validated that inhibiting the JAK/STAT pathway (particularly STAT3) via pharmacological or genetic means can effectively reverse the pathological phenotype of keloid fibroblasts. This is evidenced by reduced collagen deposition, inhibited hyperproliferation and migration, regulated cell cycle and apoptosis, and attenuated inflammatory and oxidative stress responses.

The PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway: the central hub regulating cellular survival and metabolic processes

In keloid fibroblasts, the Akt/PI3K/mTOR pathway exhibits hyperactivation, as evidenced by upregulated expression of Akt, mTOR, and their phosphorylated isoforms, leading to significantly accelerated proliferation of KFs compared to that of normal fibroblasts. KFs exhibit resistance to apoptosis, thereby enabling their long-term survival and the continuous synthesis of collagen. The activation of this signaling pathway directly or indirectly upregulates the transcriptional and translational levels of Collagen I and III, facilitating the excessive deposition of ECM. The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway modulates cytoskeleton reorganization and the expression of motility-related proteins, enhancing fibroblasts invasiveness. The phenomenon clinically manifests as the “invasive growth” phenotype characteristic of keloids. Simultaneously, the hyperactivation of mTORC1 serves as a principal suppressor of autophagy.; in KFs, pathway activation induces decreased autophagy levels [23].

Other key pathways

In keloid fibroblasts, the MAPK/ERK pathway is persistently activated, evidenced by increased phosphorylation levels of critical signaling proteins, namely p-c-Raf, p-MEK, and p-ERK1/2. The activated MAPK/ERK pathway interacts and synergizes with the core pro-fibrotic TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway. Pathway activation can upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2) and inhibit pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bax), conferring resistance to programmed cell death and promoting long - term survival of keloid fibroblasts (KFs). Upon activation, ERK translocates into the nucleus where it phosphorylates a variety of transcription factors, including c-Fos and c-Myc. This phosphorylation event drives the transition of cells from the G1 to the S phase of the cell cycle, thereby accelerating the proliferation of KFs. Additionally, the MAPK/ERK pathway regulates cytoskeleton rearrangement, focal adhesion dynamics, and matrix metalloproteinase expression, enhancing fibroblast migration and invasion capabilities [24].

Integrins are a class of transmembrane receptor proteins located on the cell surface. Integrin subunit alpha V (ITGAV) is a key α subunit responsible for recognizing the RGD motif within the integrin family, functioning as a central switch connecting the extracellular microenvironment to intracellular kinase signaling networks, and is situated upstream in the signaling cascade. Upon activation, it not only directly promotes fibrogenesis and migration but, more importantly, serves as a key trigger; its high expression is one of the initial causes of the overactivation of downstream AKT/ERK and SMAD pathways. The ITGAV/integrin signaling activates the AKT/ERK pathway via GAB1, subsequently triggering the NF-κB pathway. This leads to the release of inflammatory factors (such as CCL2 and CCL5), thereby creating a pro-fibrotic inflammatory microenvironment, while also participating in the regulation of fibrogenesis and cell migration. Chen et al. [25] elucidated that miR-3606-3p, by targeting ITGAV, GAB1, and TGFBR2, regulates the integrin/FAK, AKT/ERK, and TGF-β/SMAD pathways. This inhibits collagen deposition, inflammation, migration, and proliferation in fibroblasts, ultimately alleviating skin fibrosis. Its multi-target, multi-functional, and multi-stage regulatory characteristics position it as a highly promising therapeutic target.

Epigenetic regulation

Epigenetic mechanisms offer a novel insight into the “pathological memory” of keloids. These mechanisms govern crucial biological processes in keloid fibroblasts, including proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and collagen synthesis, via three primary pathways: DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNAs [26].

DNA methylation and histone modification

The alterations in the methylation status of specific gene promoters (e.g., CDC2L1) affect their expression (e.g., leading to CDK11p58 downregulation) to inhibit apoptosis. Hypermethylation of the SFRP1 promoter promotes Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway activation, thereby accelerating fibrosis. The upregulated expression of histone deacetylases (HDACs) facilitates the transcription of fibrotic genes. In contrast, HDAC inhibitors, such as trichostatin A (TSA) and CUDC-907 can suppress fibroblast proliferation, migration, and invasion, diminish collagen synthesis and reverse TGF-β1-induced fibrosis [26].

Non-coding RNAs

The overexpression of miR-21 enhances TGF-β1 signaling through the inhibition of Smad7. Conversely, miRNAs that function as negative regulators of collagen synthesis, such as the miR-29 family and miR-376b, are downregulated. Collectively, these changes release the inhibitory constraint on fibrosis. lncRNAs and circRNAs, such as H19 and HOXA11-AS, regulate fibroblast function indirectly through the competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) mechanism by sponging miRNAs. Intricate interactions exist among these epigenetic mechanisms, constituting a regulatory network [27]. Furthermore, the reversibility of epigenetic alterations presents potential therapeutic avenues.

Biomechanical factors

Mechanical stress is an essential factor underlying the frequent occurrence of keloids in high-tension areas, such as the presternal region and shoulders. Fibroblasts are the primary mechanical sensing cells, capable of perceiving tension and matrix stiffness, differentiating into myofibroblasts, and excessively synthesizing collagen and ECM. Myofibroblasts express α-SMA, possess contractile ability, and exhibit prolonged survival and reduced apoptosis under mechanical stimulation. Integrins establish a connection between the ECM and the intracellular cytoskeleton, sensing external forces. Piezo1 represents a mechanosensitive cation channel highly expressed in scar fibroblasts. Mechanical stress, through mechanoreceptors like integrins and Piezo1 channels, activates the downstream YAP/TAZ signaling pathway. Activated YAP/TAZ enters the nucleus, directly initiating the expression of genes related to cell proliferation and fibrosis, and engages in crosstalk with the TGF-β pathway, forming a positive feedback loop [28]. This mechanism also underpins the theoretical foundation for mechanical interventions including tension-reducing sutures, botulinum toxin injections, and Embrace devices in scar prevention and treatment.

The pathogenesis of pathological scars is intricate, stemming from the synergistic interplay of genetic susceptibility, diverse cell types, molecular signaling pathways, epigenetic regulation, and biomechanical factors (Fig. 1).

The pathogenesis of hypertrophic scars and keloids

Photodynamic therapy for pathological scars: clinical efficacy evaluation

Pathological scars have a notably high incidence and recurrence rate, which poses significant challenges to their clinical management. Conventional treatments include surgical and non-surgical methods. While surgical excision offers a straightforward and effective solution for scar removal, it is associated with a notably high postoperative recurrence rate. Non-surgical methods include intralesional injections (e.g., corticosteroids, botulinum toxin type A, anti-tumor drugs), laser therapy (e.g., pulsed dye laser, fractional laser), cryotherapy, radiotherapy, pressure therapy, and topical silicone gels.

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) represents a minimally invasive, precisely targeted therapeutic modality characterized by high selectivity, low cytotoxicity, treatment repeatability, and the capacity to synergistically integrate with diverse therapeutic approaches. PDT can be administered either as a monotherapy or in conjunction with other therapeutic modalities. In clinical settings, PDT is frequently integrated with surgical excision, laser therapies (such as CO₂ fractional laser), pharmacological agents (including corticosteroids and 5-fluorouracil), or radiotherapy to augment treatment efficacy and mitigate the risk of recurrence. Mechanism of action of PDT in the treatment of pathological scars

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) for pathological scars (including hypertrophic scars and keloids) is based on three core elements: a photosensitizer, light of a specific wavelength, and oxygen. Its mechanisms of action can be categorized into four dimensions: cellular level, molecular pathways, microenvironment regulation, and tissue remodeling (Fig. 2).

Mechanism of action of PDT in the treatment of pathological scars

PDT induces fibroblast apoptosis, necrosis, autophagy, or ferroptosis

The primary characteristic of pathological scars is the excessive proliferation and apoptosis resistance of fibroblasts. PDT induces fibroblast apoptosis by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby inhibiting their over-proliferation and collagen synthesis.

Mitochondrial pathway

The core of PDT involves photosensitizers (PSs) generating ROS, such as singlet oxygen, upon light irradiation. Due to the high reactivity and short diffusion distance of singlet oxygen, its damaging effects occur near the localization site of the PSs. Mitochondria, with their high membrane potential and oxygen concentration, become the primary accumulation sites for many PSs (e.g., cationic rhodamine, cyanine dyes). The localized, high production of ROS within mitochondria can directly cause oxidative damage to mitochondrial membrane lipids, proteins (e.g., cytochrome c oxidase complex), and mitochondrial DNA, disrupting their structural and functional integrity. ROS attack and photodamage from the PSs can lead to the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP), disrupting the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). The collapse of membrane potential and increased membrane permeability result in the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c and apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) from the mitochondrial intermembrane space into the cytoplasm. The released cytochrome c forms an apoptosome with proteins like Apaf-1, subsequently activating caspase-9 and downstream effector caspases such as caspase-3, ultimately triggering irreversible apoptosis. PDT can also target and degrade anti-apoptotic proteins located in mitochondria (e.g., Bcl-2), thereby relieving their inhibitory effect on apoptosis and further enhancing the apoptotic signal [40, 41]. PDT can also interfere with mitochondrial metabolism. Research has found [42] that targeting hexokinase II (HKII) not only inhibits glycolysis but also disrupts the interaction between HKII and the voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) on the mitochondrial outer membrane, thereby promoting apoptosis.

Death receptor pathway

Singlet oxygen is a direct activator of the Fas pathway. The mechanism begins with singlet oxygen triggering Fas receptor oligomerization, which is then linked by the Fas-associated death domain (FADD) to form the Death-Inducing Signaling Complex (DISC), activating caspase-8. Subsequently, activated caspase-8 directly activates downstream caspases-3/7 on one hand, and on the other hand, activates the mitochondrial pathway by cleaving the pro-apoptotic protein Bid, amplifying the apoptotic signal. Ultimately, the widespread proteolytic action of caspase-3 and DNA fragmentation mediated by Caspase-Activated DNase (CAD) collectively execute the apoptotic program [43].

Endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway

PDT acts on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) primarily through its phototoxic products (mainly ROS), disrupting protein folding and calcium homeostasis, thereby inducing ER stress. This leads to upregulated expression of GRP78 and activation of sensors like PERK. Sustained stress shifts the signaling towards pro-apoptosis via pathways such as PERK-ATF4-CHOP, ultimately activating effector molecules like caspase-3, resulting in apoptosis [44].

Other pathways

Research by Liu et al. found [45] that the anti-keloid effect of 5-ALA-PDT is not primarily mediated through traditional apoptotic pathways but rather by inducing a form of autophagy-dependent cell death. Its core mechanism involves the downregulation of a SIRT1-SIRT3-SOD2-mROS dependent autophagy pathway. Using the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA significantly inhibited PDT-induced cell death, whereas the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK was ineffective. Research has also found that 5-ALA-PDT can significantly increase intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation levels, while downregulating key anti-ferroptosis proteins xCT and GPX4, disrupting the cell’s antioxidant defense, ultimately leading to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and death of keloid fibroblasts [46].

Regulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism

Another characteristic of pathological scars is the excessive deposition of ECM (primarily collagen). PDT can upregulate the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) while downregulating or balancing the levels of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), increasing the MMPs/TIMPs ratio, thereby accelerating the degradation of collagen and other ECM components [47]. Conversely, Shi et al. [48] used a low concentration of 2% ALA and optimized wavelength LED (630 nm) light (energy density of 4.5 J/cm²) in a “biomodulation” therapy based on photochemical stimulation. This generates moderate levels of ROS, activating regenerative functions in cells like skin fibroblasts, positively regulating collagen metabolism (increased synthesis, decreased degradation), thereby structurally and functionally reversing skin aging. The effect of PDT on collagen metabolism is dualistic; treatment parameters determine the boundary between “damage/destruction” and “stimulation/regeneration.” Furthermore, PDT can precisely target and interrupt the most critical TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway in scar formation. PDT induces K48-linked polyubiquitination, leading to the degradation of Smad4, a key transcription factor in the pathway. The degradation of Smad4 blocks the downstream transmission of TGF-β1 signaling, thereby inhibiting the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts (manifested as decreased α-SMA expression) and fundamentally reducing the synthesis of type I and III collagen [49].

Inhibition of microvascular angiogenesis

Angiogenesis plays an important role in scar hyperplasia [50]. PDT induces apoptosis in vascular endothelial cells, reduces vascular density, and decreases the blood supply to scar tissue. Experiments confirmed [51] that Ph1-PDT significantly inhibited the expression of vascular endothelial cell markers (CD31/CD34), thereby reducing vascular density within the scar tissue, diminishing local nutrient and oxygen supply, inhibiting fibroblast activity and collagen synthesis, leading to atrophy/volume reduction of keloid grafts.

Modulation of inflammatory response

Chronic inflammation is a driving force behind the continuous development of pathological scars. PDT bidirectionally modulates inflammation by inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors and influencing macrophage polarization. Ph1-mediated PDT significantly reduced the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-8 and IL-1β in scar tissue [51]. Macrophages are central regulators of the inflammatory process. In the initial stage of tissue injury, local macrophages are primarily polarized to the M1 phenotype. They secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, induce local inflammation, and clear pathogens and cellular debris. In the later stages of normal repair, macrophages polarize to the M2 phenotype, exerting anti-inflammatory effects, promoting tissue repair, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition, ultimately contributing to scar formation and gradual resolution [52]. Multiplex immunofluorescence microscopy confirmed that the number of total macrophages (CD68+) and M2 macrophages (CD68 + CD206+), as well as the M2/M1 ratio, were significantly higher in keloid tissue compared to healthy skin. M2 macrophages, especially the M2a subtype (wound-healing type), secrete a large number of cytokines such as transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and IL-4. These factors continuously stimulate fibroblast over-proliferation and excessive synthesis of collagen proteins (e.g., types I and III), while inhibiting collagen degradation, leading to excessive deposition and disordered arrangement of the extracellular matrix [53].

Other synergistic mechanisms of PDT

PDT possesses strong broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. Researchers incorporated lignin-copper sulfide nanocomposites into a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogel to fabricate an LS-CuS@PVA composite hydrogel. Under combined near-infrared (NIR) light and hydrogen peroxide, treatment for 10 min achieved bactericidal rates of 3.8-log and 4.8-log against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, respectively (i.e., killing > 99.99% of bacteria). Its high efficacy stems from the synergistic effect between the hyperthermia generated by photothermal action and the ROS produced by photodynamic/enzyme-like catalysis. This hydrogel significantly inhibited biofilm formation [54]. A clinical study [55] involved patients aged ≥ 20 years with skin ulcers infected with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA). They were treated with a macrogol ointment containing 0.5% ALA-HCl and 0.005% EDTA-2Na, followed by irradiation with 410 nm light (energy density 10 J/cm²) for ALA-PDT on consecutive days. Six out of seven patients (85.7%) showed a clear reduction in ulcer area to 60% or less of the baseline value, indicating a positive healing trend. The treatment was judged to be completely safe in all patients, with no treatment-related adverse reactions reported.

Innovations in PDT targeting pathological scar pathogenesis

Strategies for promoting the penetration of PSs/drugs

PDT is a promising light-activated drug therapy for pathological scars. Nevertheless, the comparatively limited penetration depth of both PSs and red light poses a significant obstacle to the application of PDT. To enhance the absorption of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), a series of pretreatment methods, including chemical enhancers, iontophoresis, sonophoresis, microneedling [56], and CO₂ fractional laser (CO₂FL), are employed on the scar prior to therapy. These pretreatments are designed to facilitate drug delivery, thereby improving the penetration of the PS. Physical techniques such as cross-hatching or punch biopsy can further optimize drug delivery and alleviate scar tension.

It is widely recognized that Hyaluronic Acid (HA) constitutes a major component of the ECM and forms tight bonds with collagen fibers, thereby generating a highly viscous gel barrier that significantly impedes drug diffusion. Hyaluronidase (HAase), a specific enzyme that degrades HA, has been clinically utilized as a penetration enhancer. Research has confirmed [57] that Metformin (Met) activates the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway in PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cells, downregulating the expression of the profibrotic cytokine TGF-β, thereby inhibiting the activity of pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs). This leads to the suppression of PSC-mediated profibrotic responses (production of α-SMA and collagen), effectively disrupting the dense tumor stroma. By employing Met to remodel the tumor microenvironment and combining it with a pHLIP peptide-modified magnetic nanodrug delivery system, a sequential treatment strategy of “breaking the barrier first, then targeted attack” was established. In both subcutaneous and orthotopic tumor mouse models, this strategy demonstrated significant tumor growth inhibition. Huang et al. [58] ingeniously selected MNs based on HAase with improved mechanical strength and toughness to function as a “spear”, facilitating their effortless penetration into rigid hypertrophic scar (HS) tissue. Simultaneously, they introduced Met as “scissors” to further enhance the anti-scarring efficacy of PDT by blocking the autophagy pathway and downregulating proteins associated with collagen deposition. PDT technology is undergoing continuous innovation, transitioning its drug delivery methods from conventional topical administration to advanced systems such as nanogels and microneedle patches, which substantially enhance drug penetration and prolong retention duration.

The development of photosensitizers

Classification of PSs

The PS is the core element of PDT. New PSs are primarily designed to tackle the drawbacks of first-generation (crude hematoporphyrin) and second-generation PSs (such as 5-ALA and MAL), including limited water solubility, poor targeting specificity, and inadequate tissue penetration depth. 5-ALA represents the most comprehensively investigated PS, undergoing intracellular conversion into the photosensitive protoporphyrin IX (PpIX). MAL is an esterified derivative of 5-ALA, characterized by enhanced lipophilicity. This property facilitates deeper penetration into tissues, although it is associated with a marginally reduced efficiency of converting into protoporphyrin IX (PpIX). Hematoporphyrin Monomethyl Ether (HMME) displays the characteristic of rapidly targeting vascular endothelial cells [59]. Natural PSs extracted from Chinese herbal medicine, such as Hypocrellin A, can suppress cellular autophagic activity [60]. Phenalen-1-one (Ph1) extracted from Scutellaria baicalensis, when employed in Ph1-PDT, inhibits angiogenesis by suppressing the expression of vascular density markers (CD31 and CD34) in scar tissue [51]. Additionally, it can suppress inflammatory mediators, thus slowing keloid formation.

Novel PSs in PDT for pathological scars

Commonly used PSs in PDT include 5-ALA, Methyl Aminolevulinate (MAL), Hematoporphyrin Monomethyl Ether (HMME), and Indocyanine Green (ICG), among others. These therapeutic agents are employed in combination with light sources that emit wavelengths within the range of 600–800 nm, specifically red or near-infrared light, to maximize the depth of tissue penetration. ZHANG et al. [61] confirmed through in vitro cell experiments that a novel PDT PS, p1, induced a satisfactory pro-apoptotic effect on keloid fibroblasts by increasing the expression of ROS, caspase-3, caspase-8, and cytochrome c, and decreasing the Bcl-2/Bax ratio. However, this effect was partially inhibited by Z-IETD-FMK (a specific inhibitor of caspase-8), suggesting that caspase-8 might be one of the targets through which p1 achieves its pro-apoptotic effect. Targeted delivery strategies based on nanomaterials

Traditional photodynamic drugs/photosensitizers are characterized by their small particle size and poor water solubility, which hinders effective accumulation in target tissues. Employing biocompatible macromolecular nanomaterials as carriers to encapsulate the drugs for targeted delivery in vivo represents an effective solution. Common nanomaterial carriers include liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and metal nanoparticles, among others [70].

Nano-engineered and functionally modified PSs, including 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA)-loaded nanovesicle, IR-808-ES [63], and A/A-ES (a complex of 5-ALA and gold nanoclusters) [64], are especially well-suited for the encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs. Utilizing the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect of nanocarriers and transdermal drug delivery technologies, they can more effectively deliver PSs to deep-seated dermal scar tissue. Tseng et al. [71] constructed folate-targeted fluorinated polymeric micelles for the delivery of the hydrophobic photosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6). The Ce6-loaded targeted micelles were selectively internalized by HeLa cells and exhibited enhanced cell growth inhibition upon light irradiation.

Nanocarriers enable spatiotemporally controlled drug release. Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs/NMOFs) possess high loading capacity and can prevent self-quenching of PSs, thus improving ROS generation efficiency [66]. Inorganic nanoparticles, such as the gold nanoclusters in A/A-ES which exhibit catalase-like activity, can utilize the catalytic activity of nanozymes to generate oxygen in situ within the hypoxic scar tissue, enhancing ROS production and overcoming PDT resistance caused by hypoxia [63]. Up-conversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) can convert near-infrared light (NIR, e.g., 980 nm) into ultraviolet or visible light, thereby exciting photosensitizers (PS) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS). This enables non-invasive treatment of deep tissues with high penetration depth. Their advantages include: high tissue penetration depth (NIR window), low phototoxicity, narrowband emission, ease of matching with PS absorption, as well as good photostability and biocompatibility [65].

Novel Aggregation-Induced Emission luminogens (AIEgens) effectively resolve the problems of fluorescence quenching and decreased ROS yield in traditional PSs during aggregation. Conversely, their luminescence and ROS production are enhanced upon aggregation [72]. Near-infrared PSs, for example, IR-808, possess excitation wavelengths (approximately 800 nm) that facilitate deeper penetration into tissues. Additionally, IR-808-ES can both generate ROS (PDT effect) and convert light energy into thermal energy (PTT effect). The thermal effect of PTT not only directly induces cellular damage but also enhances cellular uptake of the PS and the generation of ROS, thereby attaining a synergistic effect of PDT/PTT (photodynamic/photothermal) [64].

The development of new PSs is evolving from mere chemical screening into a comprehensive integration that merges nanotechnology, materials science, and molecular biology. Through the improvement of drug delivery, the enhancement of ROS yield, the overcoming of tumor-like microenvironments (e.g., hypoxia), and the development of synergistic treatment strategies, nano-PSs are anticipated to substantially propel photodynamic therapy (PDT) forward, making it a more efficacious, in-depth, and secure clinical approach for the treatment of pathological scars.

Adverse reactions of PDT and their management

The adverse reactions of PDT are primarily localized, with systemic reactions being relatively uncommon. The occurrence of these adverse reactions is closely associated with the type of photosensitizer, treatment site, light parameters, and individual patient differences. The most common acute adverse reactions during and after treatment include a burning sensation, stinging, or severe pain, which are related to the direct damage caused by the localized accumulation of the photosensitizer and the reactive oxygen species generated upon light irradiation. Such side effects have been reported in dermatological treatments, such as for actinic keratosis and basal cell carcinoma. To manage this side effect, analgesic measures can be implemented during treatment, such as local cooling, oral analgesics, local anesthesia, or even intravenous sedation. Post-treatment pain typically subsides within hours to days [73]. Additionally, erythema and swelling of the skin or mucosa in the treated area are manifestations of phototoxicity. These symptoms usually appear immediately after treatment and persist for several days.

A randomized, double-blind, phase III multicenter trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of BF-200 ALA (Ameluz®) combined with BF-RhodoLED® red light irradiation in photodynamic therapy (PDT) for mild to moderate actinic keratosis (AK). A total of 87 participants were randomized (55 in the BF-200 ALA group and 32 in the placebo group). All patients in the BF-200 ALA group experienced at least one adverse event, predominantly local reactions such as pain, erythema, pruritus, and edema, most of which were mild to moderate in severity and resolved within two weeks. Pain scores (VAS) averaged 5.5 after the first PDT session and 5.8 after the second session, compared with 0.9 and 0.3 in the placebo group [74]. Shi et al. [75] retrospectively analyzed adverse reactions associated with 5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) in the treatment of skin diseases, including 439 patients. Among them, skin appendage disorders (e.g., acne) accounted for 41%, HPV-related diseases (e.g., condyloma acuminatum) for 31%, skin cancer and precancerous lesions for 24%, and other conditions for 4%. The incidence of classic adverse reactions was as follows: intraoperative pain (98.8%), erythema (92.4%), edema (35.0%), exudation (23.0%), and hyperpigmentation (27.3%). Edema was more common in female patients. Patients with HPV-related diseases were more prone to erythema, edema, and exudation, while hyperpigmentation was more frequent in those with skin appendage disorders. Two commonly overlooked adverse reactions were fever (2.4%), predominantly observed in young women (mean age 31.2 years), typically occurring on the day of treatment with temperatures up to 39 °C, self-limiting and possibly associated with the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6; and hypopigmentation (1.9%), more common in elderly women (mean age 73.5 years), primarily occurring in patients with skin cancer or precancerous lesions, with an average onset time of 3.1 months post-treatment, possibly related to immune-mediated melanocyte injury. Herpes simplex occurred in six patients following treatment. Ibbotson et al. [76] concluded in their review that no significant long-term risks of PDT have been identified, and there is currently no evidence of cumulative toxicity from repeated PDT sessions or any indication that PDT increases the risk of skin cancer development.

Systemically administered photosensitizers, such as hematoporphyrin derivatives, can induce systemic photosensitivity reactions. Patients may experience severe sunburn-like reactions (redness, blistering, pain) if their skin or eyes are exposed to sunlight or intense indoor light during a certain period (days to weeks) after administration. Therefore, patients are required to strictly avoid light exposure during the photosensitizer clearance period (duration varies by drug), including wearing protective clothing and using light-blocking curtains [75].

Most adverse reactions of PDT are localized, transient, and self-limiting, and are predictable and manageable. By adjusting parameters such as photosensitizer dose, light exposure, and treatment intervals, adverse reactions can be mitigated while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. With advancements in novel photosensitizers, PDT is progressively moving toward more precise, minimally invasive, and efficient treatments. Future efforts should focus on multicenter, large-scale clinical trials to further standardize its application and expand its clinical utility.
