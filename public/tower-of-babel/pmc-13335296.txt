Clinical value and safety of liver biopsy in patients with liver failure
Authors: Jingran Jiao, Rui Zhao, Yongping Zhang, Manman Xu, Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the safety, etiological diagnostic value, and impact on clinical treatment decisions of transjugular liver biopsy (TJLB) and percutaneous liver biopsy (PLB) in patients with liver failure.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with liver failure who underwent liver biopsy between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2025. Demographic characteristics, clinical data, post-procedural adverse events, and pathological diagnoses were collected and subjected to statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 53 patients with liver failure were included, comprising 40 patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) and 13 patients with acute or subacute liver failure. 13 patients received TJLB, all of whom had ACLF. Compared with the PLB, patients in the TJLB group had a higher international normalized ratio (INR) [1.61 (1.32, 1.98)], a lower platelet count (PLT) [89.00 (73.00, 129.00) × 10⁹/L], and were more frequently complicated by moderate to large ascites. Both PLB and TJLB were successfully performed, with a low overall complication rate and no serious adverse events. Even among patients with marked coagulation abnormalities or concomitant ascites, TJLB demonstrated acceptable safety. Histopathological examination following liver biopsy enables reclassification of both chronic liver disease etiologies and acute precipitating factors in patients with ACLF, as well as etiological diagnoses in patients with acute liver failure (ALF) or subacute liver failure (SALF). A substantial proportion of drug- or toxin-related acute liver injury was identified by histopathological examination (n = 32, 60%), leading to a decreased proportion of liver failure with unclear etiology. Based on the pathological diagnosis, treatment strategies were modified in 11% of patients, including the initiation or continuation of specific therapeutic regimens.ConclusionsIn patients with liver failure, liver biopsy provides crucial information for determining the etiology and guiding clinical decision-making. With appropriate selection of the biopsy approach and patient population, TJLB can serve as a safe and feasible option, particularly for patients with coagulation disorders or severe ascites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04863-w.

Liver failure is a severe form of liver injury caused by a variety of etiologies, leading to marked impairment or decompensation of hepatic synthetic, detoxification, metabolic, and biotransformation functions [1, 2]. It is characterized by a rapid clinical course and high short-term mortality, with patients often deteriorating to severe hepatic failure within days to weeks [3, 4]. Depending on the presence of underlying liver disease and the pattern of disease progression, liver failure can be classified into different clinical types, including acute liver failure (ALF) and acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) [3, 5]. The former typically develops in individuals without underlying chronic liver disease, whereas the latter occurs in the setting of an acute episode of hepatic dysfunction in patients with pre-existing chronic liver disease [6]. Previous studies have shown that prognosis, particularly in patients with ACLF, is closely related to the type of underlying chronic liver disease and the acute precipitating event, with significant differences in mortality risk across different etiological backgrounds [7]. Therefore, identification of the etiology of liver failure is of critical clinical importance for guiding therapeutic decision-making. Despite continuous advances and widespread clinical application of diagnostic modalities such as serological marker testing and imaging techniques, histopathological assessment obtained through liver biopsy remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of liver diseases and plays a crucial role in etiological determination [8]. Percutaneous liver biopsy (PLB) remains the preferred diagnostic approach for elucidating the etiology of diagnostically challenging liver diseases. Nevertheless, in patients with significant coagulation disorders, high bleeding risk, or massive ascites, the risk of severe complications associated with PLB is substantially increased [9, 10]. Under these circumstances, transjugular liver biopsy (TJLB), which enables hepatic tissue acquisition via a vascular route without breaching the liver capsule, is regarded as a safer alternative for high-risk patients [11, 12]. In this retrospective study, patients with liver failure—including those with ACLF and acute or subacute liver failure—were analyzed to systematically compare the safety profiles of PLB and TJLB and to assess their value in etiological diagnosis, to provide evidence-based guidance for the rational selection of liver biopsy techniques in this population.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study collected data from liver failure patients who were hospitalized at Beijing You’an Hospital, Capital Medical University, and underwent liver biopsy between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2025. During this period, all liver biopsies were performed by the department of interventional radiology. Regarding the diagnostic criteria, ALF is defined as acute severe liver injury occurring in the absence of pre-existing chronic liver disease, characterized by coagulopathy (international normalized ratio [INR] ≥ 1.5) and hepatic encephalopathy of any degree [13, 14]. Based on the interval between the onset of jaundice and the development of hepatic encephalopathy, ALF can be further classified as acute and subacute forms. In contrast, ACLF is defined as an acute deterioration of liver function precipitated by various factors in patients with previously diagnosed or undiagnosed chronic liver disease, typically characterized by total bilirubin (TBiL) ≥ 5 mg/dL and INR ≥ 1.5 [5].

The diagnosis of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) relies on a comprehensive, multifaceted evaluation. Liver biopsy may serve as an adjunctive tool, and causality is assessed by integrating drug exposure history and clinical manifestations while excluding other etiologies of liver injury. The Rousell Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) scoring system is applied when necessary [15, 16].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a clinical diagnosis of ACLF, ALF, or subacute liver failure (SALF), without prior liver transplantation; (2) liver biopsy performed during hospitalization, including PLB or TJLB; (3) availability of a complete liver biopsy pathology report with analyzable findings; and (4) liver biopsy performed within 90 days after the diagnosis of liver failure. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients without liver failure; those with concomitant hepatic malignancies or focal hepatic lesions; incomplete clinical data; failure to meet the predefined time window; or the presence of concomitant hematologic disorders.

Data collection

All study data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system. Variables collected included patients’ demographic characteristics and clinical information, such as age, sex, known etiology of chronic liver disease, and acute precipitating factors. Biopsy-related data were also collected, including the biopsy approach, and the interval between the diagnosis of liver failure and the performance of liver biopsy. Based on laboratory results obtained before and after biopsy, relevant biochemical and coagulation parameters were extracted, and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score was calculated. For safety assessment, adverse events occurring within 48h after liver biopsy were recorded, including fever, pain, and blood product transfusions. The source of bleeding was determined using imaging or ultrasonography in conjunction with medical records to assess whether the hemorrhage originated from the liver. Adverse events (AEs) were defined according to the guidelines of the Society of Interventional Radiology (SIR) [17] and classified based on the severity grading criteria specified in these guidelines. Based on these criteria and previous studies, major hepatic hematoma, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, hemothorax, pneumothorax, and ventricular arrhythmia were defined as serious or life-threatening events. Pathological reports of liver biopsy specimens were reviewed to extract information such as the length and diameter of the liver tissue samples documented in the pathological descriptions, and compile the final pathological diagnoses. In addition, the impact of liver biopsy findings on clinical management was evaluated by recording whether treatment strategies were modified based on pathological diagnoses.

Liver biopsy procedures

Liver biopsy was performed based on clinical indications following multidisciplinary assessment, and was primarily indicated in patients with liver failure of unknown etiology despite comprehensive clinical evaluation, laboratory testing, and imaging studies, as well as in selected patients to further assess the severity of hepatic injury. The choice between TJLB and PLB was determined by the patient’s bleeding risk and clinical condition. Prior to the procedure, all patients underwent routine laboratory and abdominal imaging. Liver biopsy was performed when feasible if platelet count (PLT) ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L and INR < 2.5. Patients and their families were informed of the purpose and potential risks of liver biopsy, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient. For the procedure, patients were placed in the left lateral decubitus position (PLB) or supine position (TJLB). Routine disinfection and local anesthesia were performed. PLB was carried out under ultrasound guidance using a biopsy needle. In a few patients at increased bleeding risk, a plugged liver biopsy technique was used to reduce hemorrhagic complications. TJLB was performed via the right internal jugular vein using the Seldinger technique to establish vascular access. Under guidewire guidance, a catheter was advanced into the hepatic vein, and venography was performed to confirm venous patency. A dedicated biopsy needle was then introduced to obtain liver tissue specimens. After the procedure, local pressure was applied to the puncture site for 10 min, followed by a compression dressing. Specimen adequacy was evaluated by experienced pathologists based on the number of portal tracts contained in the samples.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were presented as median (P25, P75), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (%), with intergroup comparisons conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), and a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Figure 1 depicts the patient inclusion process of the study. A total of 98 patients who underwent liver biopsy were initially assessed, and after exclusion of those not meeting the inclusion criteria, 53 patients were ultimately enrolled, including 40 with ACLF, 7 with ALF, and 6 with SALF. Liver biopsy was primarily performed to clarify the underlying etiology and, in a minority of cases, to further evaluate the pathological characteristics of hepatic lesions. The general clinical characteristics of the overall cohort and of patients with different types of liver failure are summarized in Table 1. In the overall population, patients were predominantly middle-aged, with a median age of 44.0 (33.0, 54.0) years, and females accounted for 66%. Liver cirrhosis was common, with 22 patients having confirmed cirrhosis prior to liver biopsy, and some patients presented with varying degrees of ascites. The median interval from definitive diagnosis of liver failure to liver biopsy was 18.0 (12.0, 27.0) days. Prior to liver biopsy, the hemoglobin (Hb) level was 111.00 (100.00, 119.00) g/L, the PLT was 135.00 (86.00, 227.00) × 10⁹/L, the INR was 1.18 (1.02, 1.42), and the prothrombin time activity (PTA) was 67.50 (57.35, 92.35) %. Liver failure patients in this study more frequently underwent PLB than TJLB (75% vs. 25%). All patients who underwent TJLB had ACLF and more commonly presented with moderate to massive ascites. Compared with the PLB group, patients in the TJLB group exhibited more severe coagulation dysfunction. Specifically, PTA was significantly lower in the TJLB group (52.00 [40.00, 64.90] % vs. 79.00 [63.00, 97.00] %, p < 0.001). Stratified analysis showed a higher proportion of patients with PTA < 50% in the TJLB group (38%, p = 0.003). In addition, the TJLB group had significantly prolonged prothrombin time (PT) (p < 0.001) and higher INR [1.61 (1.32, 1.98), p < 0.001]. Further stratified analysis revealed that the proportion of patients with INR ≥ 1.5 was significantly higher in the TJLB group than in the PLB group (p < 0.001). Regarding hematological parameters, patients undergoing TJLB had significantly lower PLT levels [89.00 (73.00, 129.00) × 10⁹/L] than those undergoing PLB (p = 0.005). However, no significant differences were observed in the proportions of patients with PLT < 50 × 10⁹/L or < 80 × 10⁹/L (p = 0.057 and p = 0.140, respectively). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in white blood cell (WBC) count or Hb levels (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Liver biopsies

In the overall study population, liver biopsy was successfully performed in all patients, yielding histological specimens adequate for pathological evaluation. The median specimen length was 1.60 (1.40, 2.00) cm, and most specimens (83%) had a diameter of 0.2 cm. Post-biopsy PLT and Hb levels remained overall stable. However, statistically significant differences in post-biopsy PLT and Hb were observed between the TJLB and PLB groups. With regard to puncture-related adverse events, transient fever occurred in two patients undergoing PLB, one of whom developed fever potentially related to the heat-absorbing effect of gelatin sponge granules used during the procedure. One patient in the TJLB group also experienced transient fever. In addition, mild localized pain at the puncture site was reported in one patient after PLB. Bleeding-related complications were rare, with only one case of visceral hemorrhage, which was confirmed by imaging to have originate from the liver. Notably, no serious adverse events occurred during the study period. Following liver biopsy, one patient in the PLB group required treatment with vasoactive agents; this case was independent of the patient who developed post-biopsy bleeding. Overall, liver biopsy findings led to changes in clinical management in 6 patients (11%). All of them initiated new treatment regimens, and no patients discontinued existing therapies as a consequence of the biopsy findings (Table 3; Supplementary Table 3).

Clinical diagnosis and pathological diagnosis

A comparison between pre-biopsy clinical diagnoses and post-biopsy pathological diagnoses in 40 patients with ACLF revealed discrepancies in the etiology of underlying chronic liver disease in 17 cases (42.5%) and in acute precipitating factors in 18 cases (45.0%). In most patients, the primary indication for liver biopsy was an unclear etiology. Among pre-biopsy clinical diagnoses, the chronic etiology could not be identified in 10 patients (25%), and the acute precipitating factor remained undetermined in 22 patients (55%). Following histopathological evaluation, two patients with previously unclassified chronic etiologies were diagnosed with hereditary metabolic liver diseases, including one confirmed case of Wilson’s disease and one requiring further genetic testing for definitive diagnosis. No statistically significant differences were observed between clinical and pathological diagnoses regarding either chronic etiologies or acute precipitating factors in ACLF (p > 0.05). In two cases initially classified as SALF, pathological findings suggested underlying chronic liver disease, leading to reclassification as ACLF (Fig. 2). In patients with ALF and SALF, the proportion of cases with an unclear etiology decreased significantly after pathological evaluation, and all such cases achieved etiological clarification (p = 0.005). Among these, two cases initially diagnosed as hepatitis B infection alone were further reclassified after pathological assessment, with additional etiological factors identified (Fig. 3). Drug- or toxin-related liver injury was the most common acute factor in this cohort, with histopathology confirming this suspected etiology in 32 of 53 patients (60.4%). These cases included patients in whom drug- or toxin-induced injury was identified as the acute precipitating event in ACLF, as well as those in whom it was determined to be the primary etiology in ALF or SALF. We used the open-source pathology software Qupath [18] to delineate the pathological features of liver biopsy specimens from two representative cases of drug-induced ACLF (Fig. 4). The delineation included the following six characteristic pathological findings for diagnosing this disease: interface hepatitis; portal mixed inflammatory cell infiltration; (centrilobular) hepatocanalicular cholestasis; ductular reaction; lobular hepatitis; and necrosis. The complete annotation file is available in the Supplementary Material and can be accessed using the Qupath software. Overall, pathological diagnosis reduced the proportion of cases with unidentified acute precipitating factors from 54% to 28% in ACLF and from 54% to 0% in ALF or SALF, highlighting the complementary role of liver biopsy in refining etiological assessment (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study, 53 patients diagnosed with ACLF and acute or subacute liver failure were included. All patients underwent TJLB or PLB during hospitalization, primarily to clarify the etiology of liver failure.The findings demonstrate that both TJLB and PLB can be performed safely in patients with liver failure, with complications predominantly consisting of mild to moderate adverse events that were manageable with symptomatic treatment. Importantly, TJLB remained feasible and safe for obtaining liver histological specimens even in patients with significant coagulation dysfunction, including reduced PTA, elevated INR, and decreased PLT. These findings indicate that, under strict indication assessment and standardized procedural protocols, liver biopsy in patients with liver failure has an acceptable safety profile. These results are consistent with previous studies in the broader hepatology population, which have reported low rates of serious adverse events (approximately 1% across all patients) for both biopsy techniques [19].

The liver plays a pivotal role in the synthesis of multiple coagulation factors, and patients with liver failure frequently exhibit varying degrees of coagulation dysfunction. However, no unified consensus has been established in international guidelines regarding pre-biopsy coagulation assessment and risk stratification for liver biopsy. Guidelines jointly issued by the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) and the Royal College of Physicians of London in 1995 proposed a relative safety threshold for PLB of an INR ≤ 1.5 and PLT ≥ 80 × 10⁹/L [20]. The 2017 guidelines from the Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiological Society of Europe (CIRSE) recommended an INR < 1.5 and PLT > 50 × 10⁹/L [21]. In 2019, the SIR advised prophylactic platelet transfusion for patients undergoing liver biopsy with a pre-procedural INR > 1.5–1.8 or PLT < 50 × 10⁹/L. Hemostasis in cirrhosis is characterized by a rebalanced state, and conventional coagulation parameters, such as prolonged PT/INR and thrombocytopenia, do not reliably correlate with bleeding risk. Consequently, more liberal thresholds have been adopted in clinical practice, with procedures generally considered acceptable at INR < 2.5 and PLT > 30 × 10⁹/L [22]. The 2020 joint guidelines from the BSG, the Royal College of Radiologists, and the Royal College of Pathologists stated that an INR < 2.0 should be ensured before PLB; for non-lesional biopsy, TJLB is preferred when the INR exceeds 1.4 [8]. In China, relevant guidelines recommend INR < 1.5 and PLT > 50 × 10⁹/L as reference thresholds for liver biopsy [23]. Of particular interest, the 2009 American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines on liver biopsy suggested that traditional coagulation parameters, such as INR and PLT, do not demonstrate a clear correlation with post-biopsy bleeding risk [24]. In the present study, one patient who developed hepatic bleeding had a pre-procedural PLT above 50 × 10⁹/L, suggesting that isolated conventional coagulation parameters may be insufficient for bleeding risk stratification after liver biopsy.

Patients undergoing TJLB in this cohort were predominantly those with ACLF and more severe clinical conditions, including advanced ascites and more pronounced coagulation abnormalities. This difference is most likely attributable to confounding by indication, as TJLB is preferentially selected in higher-risk patients rather than reflecting intrinsic differences between biopsy techniques. Accordingly, the higher INR and greater disease severity observed in the TJLB group are more plausibly explained by selection bias. In this context, TJLB serves as a safer alternative when PLB is contraindicated or considered high risk, and is therefore more frequently performed in patients with severe ACLF or other critically ill liver disease populations [25–27]. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that TJLB provides a reliable diagnostic yield in the histological evaluation of ALF [28], supporting its role in etiological assessment and disease classification.

The prevalence of ACLF in high-risk populations can reach 20%-30% [29], primarily occurring in patients with chronic liver diseases such as hepatitis B or C, excessive alcohol consumption, or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [30, 31]. Multiple acute precipitating factors may lead to the development of ACLF, most commonly including reactivation of chronic hepatitis B virus infection, hepatitis A or E virus infection, short-term heavy alcohol consumption, acute bacterial infections, and drug- or toxin-induced liver injury [32]. As different etiologies are associated with distinct prognostic outcomes and mortality risks, accurate etiological identification is essential for optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient prognosis. However, previous studies have shown that nearly half of ACLF cases fail to obtain a definitive etiological diagnosis through routine clinical evaluation [33], highlighting the limitations of relying solely on clinical features and conventional ancillary investigations. In this context, liver biopsy not only provides direct histopathological assessment of hepatic injury but also plays a critical role in refining and clarifying etiological diagnoses [34, 35]. In this study, discrepancies were observed between clinical and pathological diagnoses, involving both underlying chronic liver disease etiologies and acute precipitating factors, which were reclassified after histopathological evaluation. In addition, most cases of drug- or toxin-induced liver injury were confirmed at the pathological level, contributing to a reduction in the proportion of cases with indeterminate etiology. Notably, some patients clinically diagnosed with SALF were reclassified as ACLF based on pathological findings, underscoring the value of liver biopsy in distinguishing subacute liver failure from acute exacerbation of chronic liver disease [6].

These findings indicate that liver biopsy plays an important role in clinical decision-making in patients with liver failure. Histopathological results often provide timely and actionable information that informs subsequent therapeutic management. Once the etiology is clarified, clinicians may refine treatment strategies by integrating pathological findings with the patient’s clinical context. Such adjustments primarily include initiating or optimizing existing empirical treatment regimens, or undertaking further necessary diagnostic investigations. A retrospective study of 365 patients with liver disease reported that liver biopsy resulted in changes in management in 12.1% of cases [36]. Similarly, a multicenter retrospective cohort study of ICU patients undergoing liver biopsy demonstrated that treatment strategies were modified in 58% of cases, among which 82% received newly initiated therapies, while the remainder continued or discontinued prior regimens [37]. All patients in the present cohort received standard medical management, including etiology-directed therapy when applicable, along with guideline-based management of complications. None underwent liver transplantation during hospitalization. The distinction between ALF/SALF and ACLF has important implications for transplantation, as eligibility and prioritization differ across these conditions. In this study, disease classification was based primarily on clinical criteria, and liver biopsy was not intended for diagnostic reclassification. However, histological assessment may provide additional evidence of underlying chronic liver disease and thereby contribute to diagnostic refinement. In a subset of patients, pathological findings led to reclassification, which may have potential implications for transplant decision-making. The impact of liver biopsy on transplant eligibility or prioritization was not systematically evaluated and warrants further investigation. In patients of suspected drug-induced liver injury, no medications were discontinued as a direct consequence of liver biopsy findings, likely because most suspected agents had already been withdrawn based on clinical suspicion prior to biopsy. Nevertheless, liver biopsy remains valuable in supporting the diagnosis, excluding alternative etiologies, and characterizing the pattern and severity of liver injury, rather than necessarily demonstrating a “textbook” DILI histological pattern [38].

This study primarily included patients with ACLF, while also encompassing a proportion of patients with ALF and SALF, thereby providing data on liver biopsy and its pathological findings across different types of liver failure. In addition, we report the complication rates associated with this invasive procedure, which may assist physicians in evaluating its potential role in clinical practice. TJLB was first introduced into clinical practice by Hanafee et al. in 1967 and has since been widely adopted internationally [39]. In contrast, its clinical application in China has lagged behind that of PLB, largely due to the later introduction of relevant equipment and limited accumulated experience. Within this context, these findings suggest that TJLB may be associated with acceptable safety and diagnostic performance in patients with liver failure.

This study is a retrospective analysis, which inherently carries certain limitations. The relatively small sample size, particularly the limited number of patients undergoing TJLB, may restrict the statistical power. In addition, selection bias cannot be excluded, as only a subset of patients with liver failure who underwent liver biopsy based on clinical indications were included, while many patients with ACLF or ALF/SALF were either too critically ill to undergo biopsy or could be adequately managed without histological confirmation. Despite these limitations, the present findings suggest that liver biopsy may be feasible and potentially safe in carefully selected patients during the acute phase of liver failure, although larger prospective studies are required to further validate these observations.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the use of TJLB and PLB in patients with ACLF as well as acute or subacute liver failure. The results suggest that liver biopsy may be feasible in carefully selected patients with liver failure. With appropriate selection of biopsy techniques and careful patient selection, liver biopsy can be performed safely and may provide additional diagnostic information for etiological clarification and support clinical decision-making.
