Bridging the Digital Divide: Real-World Efficacy of Commercial and Open-Source Automated Insulin Delivery Systems in a Diverse, Low-Resource Pediatric Population
Authors: Karin Dreifuss, Floris Levy-Khademi, Carmit Avnon Ziv, Adi Auerbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Achieving optimal glycemic control in type 1 diabetes (T1D) is challenging. Automated insulin delivery (AID) systems have improved outcomes, yet data on open-source systems—particularly in low-resource, low technological literacy settings—remain limited. This study compared the efficacy and safety of an open-source system with a regulatory-approved system in a heterogeneous real-world pediatric cohort.Methods:This longitudinal observational study included 61 children with T1D treated at Shaare Zedek Medical Center: 30 using the regulatory-approved Medtronic 780G and 31 using the open-source AndroidAPS AID system. Socioeconomic status (SES) was determined by residential address. Glycemic outcomes were compared between AID use and prior sensor-augmented pump therapy. Primary endpoints included changes in time in range (TIR), average glucose, glucose management indicator (GMI), and hypoglycemia/hyperglycemia duration. Safety outcomes included rates of severe hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).Results:Both groups demonstrated significant glycemic improvements after transitioning to AID, including an increase in %TIR (56.6 ± 14.1 vs 69.3 ± 8.8) and reduction in average blood glucose (173.3 ± 31.7 vs 149.9 ± 18.3), GMI and % time in hyperglycemia. No significant differences in glycemic outcomes were observed between groups, although a trend toward greater reduction in average blood glucose and GMI was observed in the regulatory-approved group. No episodes of severe hypoglycemia or DKA occurred in either group.Conclusions:In a heterogeneous real-world pediatric cohort including a low-resource population with limited technological access, both AID systems significantly improved glycemic outcomes without compromising safety. These findings suggest AID benefits transcend socioeconomic barriers, supporting patient autonomy in system selection.Keywords: type 1 diabetes, automated insulin delivery (AID), time in range (TIR), glycemic control, low-resource populations, open-source AID system

Introduction

The incidence of type 1 diabetes (T1D) is steadily increasing, particularly among young children.1,2 Despite technological advances, only 38.8% of pediatric patients achieve the recommended glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) target of <7%. 3

Automated insulin delivery (AID) systems combine an insulin pump, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), and a control algorithm that continuously adapts insulin delivery to glucose trends, representing a major advance in diabetes management. These systems have substantially improved glycemic outcomes and quality of life, increasing time in range (TIR) by 8.7% to 14% (about 2-2.6 hours per day).4,5

In parallel with the development of regulatory-approved systems, “do-it-yourself” communities have developed open-source AID algorithms that can be used with compatible insulin pumps and CGM devices. 6 These self-installed systems require technical expertise and active engagement with online support networks.

While observational studies of open-source AID systems demonstrated improvements in TIR, HbA1c, and quality of life,7-9 most were limited by the absence of control groups, small sample sizes, and self-reported data. 7

A multicenter randomized controlled trial confirmed the safety and efficacy of an open-source AID system. 10 Subsequent real-world studies have further supported the effectiveness and feasibility of open-source AID systems in routine clinical practice11-13; however, data remain limited regarding their use in diverse pediatric populations, particularly those facing socioeconomic and technological barriers.

Open-source systems pose unique challenges, including limited clinician experience, lack of structured training, and the absence of manufacturer-supported helpdesk. Accordingly, rigorously conducted real-world studies are essential to assess their efficacy and safety.

Our diabetes clinic serves a large proportion of patients from the ultra-Orthodox Jewish community, a population characterized by limited access to technology, including smartphones and internet. These factors may hinder the use of open-source AID systems. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of open-source versus commercially approved AID systems in a heterogeneous real-world pediatric population with T1D.

Materials and Methods

This observational real-life case-control study included children aged 1 to 18 years with T1D treated with an AID system at the Pediatric Diabetes Clinic at Shaare Zedek Medical Center.

The study included 2 groups:

The approved system group: Treated with the Medtronic 780G.

The approved system group: Treated with the Medtronic 780G.

The open-source group: Treated with AndroidAPS using Omnipod DASH pumps and Dexcom G5/G6 CGMs.

The open-source group: Treated with AndroidAPS using Omnipod DASH pumps and Dexcom G5/G6 CGMs.

System selection was made independently by patients prior to study enrollment.

Inclusion Criteria

Diagnosis of T1D according to American Diabetes Association guidelines, at least 1 year since diagnosis, use of an AID system for at least 3 months, and prior use of an insulin pump and CGM systems for at least 3 months.

Exclusion Criteria

Use of the AID systems for <75% of the time or missing baseline glycemic data for the 6 months preceding AID initiation.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Shaare Zedek Ethics Committee (SZMC-0032-23). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Data Collection

Glycemic data were retrieved from medical records, insulin pump, and CGM platforms. For each participant, data were collected from the 2 clinic visits immediately preceding AID initiation and 2 consecutive visits after at least 12 weeks of AID use. For each visit, glycemic and insulin delivery data from the preceding 14 days were analyzed.

Each patient served as their own control, comparing glycemic control during AID use with the preceding sensor-augmented pump period. The approved and open-source groups were compared regarding changes in glycemic outcomes.

The following glycemic control parameters were assessed: average glucose, % TIR (70-180 mg/dL), % time in hypoglycemia (<70 mg/dL), % time in severe hypoglycemia (<54 mg/dL), % time in hyperglycemia (>180 mg/dL), glucose management indicator (GMI)/estimated HbA1c, % time with CGM use, mean daily insulin dose, divided to basal and bolus compartments, HbA1c within 1 year before and at least 3 months after AID initiation, occurrence of severe hypoglycemia (defined as events causing confusion, loss of consciousness, seizure, glucagon use, or medical attention), and hospitalization due to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

For each continuous variable, the average of the 2 visits prior to AID initiation was compared with the average of the 2 visits following initiation.

Categorical variables were constructed based on target glycemic control thresholds: TIR ≥70%, time in hypoglycemia <4%, time in severe hypoglycemia <1%, time in hyperglycemia ≤25%, HbA1c <7%, and HbA1c <6.5%. 14

For categorical variables, the proportion of patients meeting each target at the first pre-initiation visit was compared with the proportion meeting the target at the final post-initiation visit.

Clinical and demographic data—including age, sex, age at diabetes diagnosis, duration of diabetes, presence of comorbidities, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), residential address, religious affiliation, access to internet and computers, difficulties encountered while using the system, level of satisfaction with the system (rated on a 1-10 scale), number of clinic visits during the 6 months before and after AID initiation, follow-up duration (defined as the interval between the first pre-initiation visit and the second post-initiation visit)—were collected via medical records and a structured questionnaire.

Socioeconomic position (SEP) was calculated based on residential address and analyzed according to the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics’ Characterization and Classification of Statistical Areas within Municipalities and Local Councils by the Socio-Economic Level of the Population15,16 This classification includes 2 measures:

The SEP cluster classifies neighborhoods and localities into clusters, with one being the most deprived and 10 the most affluent.

The SEP index is an adjusted score based on 14 variables that measure social and economic levels in the domains of demographics, education, standard of living, and employment.

A score of zero represents the national average, with positive or negative values indicating deviation in standard deviation (SD) units above or below the average (values ranging from −2.797 to 2.590).

A 3-point “technological index” was calculated for each patient, based on self-reported access to a computer, smartphone, and internet (1 point per component; range 0-3), with higher scores indicating greater technological orientation.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.

Comparisons between the 2 groups were made for all variables, and associations between glycemic control and system-related parameters were analyzed.

For categorical variables, absolute frequencies and percentages were calculated. For continuous variables, means and SDs were reported.

Baseline differences were assessed using chi-square tests with Yates correction for categorical variables and independent sample t tests for continuous variables. To assess the magnitude of the change over time within and between groups, a 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test for repeated measures was used. To test the magnitude of the change, between technological and non-technological groups, a 1-way ANOVA test for repeated measures was performed.

A 2-tailed P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data and Resource Availability

The data sets generated during and/or analyzed in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Differences were observed in age, BMI, and diabetes duration, all of which were higher in the approved system group. In addition, the proportion of male participants was significantly higher in the approved system group.

No statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of religious or ethnic affiliation, parental education levels, or presence of comorbidities. Socioeconomic data showed similar SEP cluster and index scores for both groups, reflecting a lower socioeconomic status (SES) of the study participants compared with the general population.

A “technological index” was developed to assess access to technology, based on the participant-reported ownership of a computer or smartphone and availability of internet access. Each technological factor contributed one point to the index. Eventually, reported smartphone ownership was extremely low and therefore excluded from the index. Reporting patterns may have been influenced by sociocultural factors, given cultural and religious norms that discourage smartphone use. All participants who reported having a computer also reported having internet access, resulting in an index score of 2 for these patients. No significant difference was found between the groups in the technological index.

In the whole cohort, which consisted predominantly of ultra-Orthodox participants, 20% to 30% reported no access to a computer or internet at home, reflecting the sociocultural characteristics of this population.

Across the entire cohort, initiation of AID therapy was associated with significant improvements in glycemic control parameters: % TIR, average glucose, % time in hyperglycemia, and GMI (Table 2). Time in range increased from 56.6% to 69.3%, representing a 12.7% absolute increase (~3 additional hours/day in range) (P < .0001). Time in hypoglycemia also decreased, although this change did not reach statistical significance (P = .05).

Time spent in severe hypoglycemia decreased numerically, although the change did not reach statistical significance (P = .089). Interestingly, the SD of average glucose increased significantly, indicating a rise in glucose variability.

No significant change was observed in laboratory-measured HbA1c, although data were incomplete for approximately one-third of participants, which limited the power of this comparison.

Total daily insulin dose increased significantly following AID initiation, with no change in the basal-to-bolus ratio.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the observed improvements in glycemic control, we assessed the proportion of patients who achieved recommended treatment targets, based on international guidelines for pediatric T1D management (Table 2).

When analyzing both study groups together, the initiation of AID therapy resulted in a statistically significant increase in the proportion of patients meeting these targets: TIR >70%, time in hyperglycemia ≤25% and GMI/estimated HbA1c <7%. A trend toward improvement was also observed in the proportion of patients with time in hypoglycemia <4%.

When comparing outcomes between the 2 system types, no statistically significant differences were observed in any glycemic metric or in the proportion of patients achieving clinical targets (Table 3). However, a modest trend toward greater improvement in average glucose and GMI was observed in the approved system group.

To further assess the safety of the systems, the occurrence of life-threatening events such as severe hypoglycemia and DKA was examined. Due to the low incidence of these events in both groups, statistical comparisons were not feasible; however, overall, no increase in these events was observed following AID initiation.

Follow-up measures were compared before and after treatment initiation with the AID systems. The mean number of clinic visits during the 6 months preceding initiation was lower in the open-source group (2.45 ± 0.81) compared with the approved system group (2.97 ± 1.15, P = .049). Following initiation, visit frequency further decreased to 2.13 ± 0.92 in the open-source group versus 2.72 ± 0.75 in the approved group (P = .008). The overall follow-up duration was significantly longer among open-source users (46.34 ± 17.24 vs 37.96 ± 12.44 weeks, P = .033).

Patients were asked about the difficulties they encountered while using the system, including disappointment with suboptimal results, technical difficulties, lack of knowledge, and fear of hypoglycemia. No significant differences were found between the groups in these domains. However, a trend toward higher overall satisfaction was observed among users of the approved system (9.09 ± 1.00 vs 8.46 ± 1.55, P = .051), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Larger studies are needed to further characterize differences in user experience between AID systems.

Discussion

This study assessed glycemic outcomes in children with T1D who initiated treatment with either a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved AID system or an open-source AID system. Changes in glycemic control were evaluated within individuals and compared across groups.

Preliminary differences in age, BMI, diabetes duration, and gender were observed between the groups. Children in the approved system group were older, had a higher BMI, and a longer disease duration. These differences are largely explained by the age eligibility restriction of the approved system (≥7 years), whereas the open-source system can be used by younger patients. As age increases, both BMI and diabetes duration naturally rise; therefore, the older age of the approved system group inherently contributes to their higher BMI and longer disease duration.

A difference in gender distribution was also observed: girls were more likely to choose the open-source system which is compatible with the tubeless Omnipod DASH pump and may reflect a preference for more discreet and convenient pump configurations. Such baseline differences are expected in real-world, nonrandomized settings where treatment choice reflects clinical eligibility and patient preference.

Previous research has shown mixed findings regarding the influence of age and sex on glycemic outcomes. Some studies report poorer glycemic control among girls and adolescents, and a retrospective analysis of the 780G system found smaller improvements among adolescents and those with longer diabetes duration.16,17 Conversely, a large population-based study found no sex-related differences in HbA1c. 18 Therefore, the independent impact of age and sex and diabetes duration on AID outcomes remains uncertain.

No significant differences were observed between the groups in socioeconomic or technological measures, both of which were low across the cohort.

Both systems led to statistically significant improvements in glycemic control.

The magnitude of improvement did not differ significantly between systems, although a trend toward greater reduction in average glucose and estimated HbA1c/GMI was noted in the approved system group.

The observed 11% increase in TIR with the open-source system, equivalent to an additional 2 hours and 38 minutes per day in range, is consistent with prior reports on open-source systems.7-9,14,19,20 Nevertheless, previous studies typically included highly motivated users with strong technological proficiency and access to online resources or clinical support. In contrast, many open-source system users in our cohort were unable to install the system independently due to limited access to computers, internet or technological knowledge and relied on paid third-party assistance for system setup, frequently without continued support.

A study similar to ours, published in 2024, compared an open-source AID system with the Medtronic 780G and demonstrated significant improvements in glycemic control in a technologically oriented, socioeconomically advantaged population. 19 The cohort in that study was essentially the mirror image of ours, characterized by higher-than-average SES (SEP cluster 8.0-8.5). Despite these differences, the magnitude of improvement in TIR (10%-11%) was comparable with that observed in our cohort. These findings suggest that AID systems can improve glycemic control across populations with very different socioeconomic and technological backgrounds.

We initially hypothesized that the approved system would yield superior outcomes in our cohort, as its operation is independent of user involvement or technological ability. Unexpectedly, our findings showed that even patients with limited technological orientation achieved significant improvements with the open-source system. These findings highlight the importance of preserving patient autonomy in treatment selection regardless of socioeconomic or technological background.

High levels of satisfaction were reported in both groups, consistent with previous studies.21,22 A trend toward higher satisfaction was seen among approved system users. While no significant differences were found in the types of difficulties encountered during AID use, larger studies are needed to better characterize user experience.

The open-source group had fewer clinic visits both before and after system initiation. Pre-initiation differences likely reflect the longer initiation process required for approved systems which necessitate several visits to the clinic. Post-initiation differences may indicate greater independence among open-source users or reliance on external support networks. Because visit frequency was lower, the follow-up period required to complete the 2 post-initiation visits for study inclusion was longer in the open-source group.

This study has several limitations. Its nonrandomized design allows for potential confounding due to patient self-selection of treatment systems. In addition, the cohort was derived from a single clinic serving predominantly families of low socioeconomic and technological status, limiting generalizability. The relatively small sample size also limited the power to detect group differences. Some glycemic parameters had missing data, particularly time in severe hypoglycemia, which may have limited the precision of the corresponding analyses.

In addition, the duration of AID use was not directly collected as a separate variable. However, the overall follow-up period—from the first pre-initiation visit to the second post-initiation visit—was less than 1 year on average in both groups and did not exceed 83 weeks. Since this interval began before AID initiation, the available data indicate that post-initiation assessments were performed relatively early in the course of AID use. Nevertheless, the potential impact of differences in AID experience and longer-term adaptation to the system could not be fully assessed.

The CGM metrics collected also imposed certain limitations. Hyperglycemia was recorded only as total time above 180 mg/dL, without separately documenting time spent in the high (180-250 mg/dL) and very high (>250 mg/dL) glucose ranges. Consequently, retrospective calculation of the glycemia risk index (GRI) was not possible. Future studies should distinguish between these 2 hyperglycemia ranges to enable calculation of the GRI and other emerging CGM metrics.

Another limitation is that direct engagement metrics (eg, bolusing frequency or carbohydrate entry) were not available from the device reports used in this study. Nevertheless, all participants met the inclusion criterion of AID use >75% of the time, indicating consistent use of the AID system. Furthermore, the observed basal-to-bolus insulin distribution (35% basal/65% bolus) is consistent with frequent meal bolusing and carbohydrate entry, providing indirect evidence of active engagement with the system.

Despite these limitations, this study reinforces the value of AID systems in improving glycemic control and quality of life. Importantly, it provides data from a population that has been largely underrepresented in prior AID research.

In summary, this study demonstrated significant improvements in glycemic control in children using both approved and open-source AID systems, with no major differences between systems in outcomes or safety profile. Importantly, the open-source system proved effective even in a population characterized by low SES and limited technological access, achieving outcomes comparable with those of an FDA-approved system.

Our findings support the inclusion of open-source systems as a legitimate and effective option in pediatric diabetes care and encourage further research in this field. The better we understand and characterize the population that chooses to adopt open-source systems and the impact of these systems on glycemic control, the more informed and supportive we can be in guiding patients through this process.
