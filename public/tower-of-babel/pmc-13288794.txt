Peptic ulcer disease in hospitalized patients: a comprehensive risk factor analysis using a validated checklist
Authors: Noorollah Tahery, Alireza Baghrobehbahani, Razieh Mohammadzadeh, Amirhossein Alaviyan, Rasool Paygozar, Mona Ebrahimzadeh, Mohsen Shafiee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeptic ulcer disease (PUD) remains a significant public health concern globally, particularly in regions with high prevalence of risk factors such as Helicobacter pylori infection and Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drug (NSAID) use. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of PUD and its associated risk factors among hospitalized patients in a tertiary hospital in southwest Iran.MethodsA mixed-methods design was employed, including a comprehensive literature review, checklist development and validation via a modified Delphi process, a cross-sectional prevalence study, and a hospital-based case-control study. Data were collected from 43,324 patient records (2019–2023) at Abadan University Teaching Hospital. Risk factors were assessed using a validated 21-item checklist, and multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of PUD.ResultsAmong all admissions, 6,874 cases of PUD were identified, indicating a point prevalence of 15.9% (95% CI: 15.6–16.2). NSAID use (75.3%), H. pylori infection (70.1%), smoking (46.9%), and corticosteroid use (30.2%) were highly prevalent among PUD patients. Significant independent risk factors included age ≥ 60 years (AOR: 1.65), NSAID use (AOR: 2.58), H. pylori positivity (AOR: 2.41), smoking (AOR: 1.45), and ulcer size ≥ 5 mm (AOR: 2.19). Despite the high rate of NSAID use, only 26.4% received gastroprotective therapy.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the high burden of PUD in hospitalized patients in southwest Iran, with modifiable risk factors such as NSAID use, H. pylori infection, and smoking playing a critical role. Targeted interventions, including Proton Pump Inhibitor (PPI) co-prescription, H. pylori eradication, and lifestyle modifications, are essential to reduce PUD incidence and its complications.Graphical abstractThe presented figure 1 provides a graphical abstract summarizing the main findings of this study.

Introduction

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is a common gastrointestinal disorder characterized by damage to the gastrointestinal lining, resulting in a mucosal tear larger than 3 to 5 mm, with a visible depth that extends into the submucosa [1]. PUD primarily occurs in the stomach and the upper part of the small intestine, known as the proximal duodenum. It has an estimated lifetime prevalence of 5% to 10% and an annual incidence of 0.1% to 0.3% among the general population in Western countries [2]. Gastric ulcers occur when gastric acid damages the gastric and duodenal mucosa, leading to erosion of the mucosal lining and exposing the underlying tissues to the digestive effects of gastric and duodenal secretions [3]. This condition was traditionally associated with a hypersecretion of acid, dietary factors, and stress [4]. However, the increasing prevalence of Helicobacter pylori infections, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor use, the widespread use of NSAIDs, lifestyle habits and genetic characteristics, and the rising abuse of alcohol and tobacco have altered the epidemiology of the disease [3, 5]. Symptoms of PUD include abdominal pain, discomfort in the upper abdomen, bloating, and a sensation of fullness [5]. If PUD worsens and leads to perforation, gastric juices and gas can enter the peritoneal cavity, causing chemical peritonitis [6]. PUD occurs due to an imbalance between protective and damaging factors affecting the gastric and duodenal mucosa [1]. The damage caused by PUD extends beyond the individual, primarily impacting their social life and career. This condition can also diminish the quality of life for both the individual and their family members and caregivers [7]. Factors affecting PUD should be assessed in an individual’s living conditions and environment. It is important to investigate local environmental influences, in addition to factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and infections, that may contribute to the development of this condition [8, 9]. This study aims to fill a methodological gap by combining evidence synthesis, expert consensus-based checklist validation, and multi-phase quantitative analysis within a unified mixed-methods framework. This approach allows for a standardized and comprehensive assessment of the risk factors associated with peptic ulcer disease (PUD) in a hospital setting. A literature review to identify factors

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify risk factors associated with peptic ulcer disease (PUD) and to compile a complete list of factors involved in its pathogenesis. Searches were performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar without language or geographic restrictions, using combinations of keywords such as “peptic ulcer,” “risk factors,” “etiology,” and “predictors” with Boolean operators. Original studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses addressing PUD risk factors were included, while studies with methodological limitations Non-English studies, those with methodological issues (according to the CASP checklist), or incomplete/ambiguous data were excluded. Study selection was carried out in three stages—title, abstract, and full-text screening—by two independent reviewers, with disagreements resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Data were extracted using a standardized form, and identified risk factors were categorized according to frequency of reporting, strength of association, and clinical relevance.

Development and validation of the checklist

Checklist development

Drawing from the insights gathered during the literature review, a preliminary checklist was crafted to delineate the primary risk factors and pertinent variables associated with PUD. The items on this checklist were meticulously developed to encompass factors that are commonly referenced in existing studies. Each item is defined with precision to maintain consistency and clarity in subsequent evaluations.

Delphi study design

A modified Delphi method was utilized to evaluate and enhance the initial checklist. The objective of the Delphi study was to achieve expert consensus regarding the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the proposed items.

Panel experts

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to assemble a panel of experts, which included gastroenterologists, epidemiologists, specialists in internal medicine, and clinical researchers. The selection of panel members was based on their publication records, clinical experience, and recognized expertise in the field of PUD. The study engaged an expert panel comprising 20 members, to whom a tailored checklist was emailed for evaluation.

Delphi rounds

The Delphi process was conducted over two iterative rounds:

Round 1: Experts were solicited to evaluate each item on the checklist using a 4-point Likert scale based on its relevance (1 = not relevant, 4 = highly relevant). Additionally, they were encouraged to provide qualitative feedback regarding the clarity, necessity, and potential additions or deletions of checklist items [10].Round 2: Following the feedback from the initial round, the checklist was revised and redistributed for re-evaluation to confirm consensus on the refined items. Consensus was defined as achieving a minimum of 80% agreement among panel members for each item.

Content validity assessment (CVI calculation)

To quantitatively evaluate the content validity of the checklist, we computed the Content Validity Index (CVI) at both the item and scale levels. For the item-level CVI (I-CVI), we determined the proportion of experts who rated each item as either 3 (quite relevant) or 4 (highly relevant) from the total number of experts. An I-CVI threshold of 0.78 was established, indicating adequate content validity. Regarding the scale-level CVI (S-CVI), we employed two distinct methods: S-CVI/UA (universal agreement) and S-CVI/Ave (average of I-CVIs). A S-CVI/Ave score of 0.90 or higher was deemed indicative of excellent content validity. Items that did not meet the minimum I-CVI criterion underwent revisions for clarity or were excluded based on expert feedback and group consensus. Ultimately, the final checklist consisted solely of items that demonstrated robust content validity metrics.

Data analysis

Quantitative data collected from each Delphi round were subjected to analysis utilizing descriptive statistics, which encompassed mean scores, standard deviations, and CVI values. Additionally, qualitative feedback was thematically analyzed to facilitate the iterative refinement of the checklist content across the various rounds.

Cross-sectional study to determine the prevalence of peptic ulcers

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted to estimate the prevalence of PUD among patients admitted to Abadan University Teaching Hospital. This study evaluated patients who were admitted to Admitted Hospital over five years, from January 2019 to December 2023. For key study variables, missing data were minimal (< 2%). Cases lacking essential information were excluded from the analyses, and no imputation was applied, ensuring that results reflect available, verified clinical records.

Study population and setting

In this study, we obtained patient hospitalization records from the Hospital Information System (HIS), a registry-based platform commonly utilized for documenting clinical and administrative data in hospital settings. We conducted a systematic review of all inpatient records from teaching hospitals over five years. Patients were identified and included in the analysis if they were either admitted with a primary diagnosis of peptic ulcer or if they developed a peptic ulcer during their hospital stay. The inclusion criteria for this study required the availability of complete clinical and diagnostic data, while the exclusion criteria consisted of diagnosed gastrointestinal malignancies and incomplete medical records.

Diagnostic criteria

Peptic ulcers are characterized as mucosal defects measuring five mm or greater in diameter, exhibiting visible depth, and confirmed through endoscopic examination in either the stomach or duodenum. In cases where endoscopy is not performed, a diagnosis is determined based on comprehensive clinical evaluation, supplemented by relevant imaging and laboratory findings.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to determine the point prevalence of PUD, presented as a percentage along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Case-control study to identify risk factors for peptic ulcers

Study design

Following an investigation into the prevalence of PUD, a case-control study was designed and conducted to further explore the potential risk factors associated with its development. This methodology was chosen to examine the relationship between a defined set of exposures and the incidence of PUD within the hospitalized population identified during the earlier cross-sectional phase.

Study population

The study population comprised two groups. First, the Cases included patients diagnosed with PUD, identified from the HIS database between January 2019 and December 2023. A total of 6,874 cases were identified, and their medical records in the HIS were thoroughly reviewed. Diagnostic confirmation was based on endoscopic findings or, in the absence of these, on relevant clinical, laboratory, and imaging criteria as previously outlined. The second group, the Controls, consisted of patients with no documented history or clinical evidence of PUD during the same hospitalization period. These patients were selected from the same hospitals and matched to the cases based on age (± 5 years), sex, and year of hospital admission. Their medical records, also accessible in the HIS, were reviewed. Their number was considered to be twice the number of cases, and the medical records of 13,748 people were reviewed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the control group were individuals aged 18 years or older, availability of complete clinical data, and hospital admission during the designated study period. The exclusion criteria included diagnosed gastrointestinal malignancies, major systemic diseases unrelated to gastrointestinal pathology, incomplete medical records, and recurrent admissions with a prior diagnosis of PUD to prevent selection bias.

Exposure assessment and data collection

Utilizing the finalized checklist developed through the Delphi process, we extracted potential risk factors from patient records. These factors included demographic characteristics such as age, sex, and education level, as well as lifestyle factors like smoking and alcohol consumption. Additionally, we considered clinical history, which encompassed the use of NSAIDs or corticosteroids and any previous gastrointestinal symptoms, along with comorbidities such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, and the presence of Helicobacter pylori infection. All data were gathered using a standardized data collection form to ensure consistency across the records. In cases of missing or ambiguous data, we conducted manual chart reviews or followed up with hospital records staff for verification. Records with unresolved missing essential variables after this process were excluded from the final analysis. No imputation methods were applied.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were utilized to characterize both the case and control groups. Bivariate analyses were performed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables, and depending on the normality of the distribution. Multivariate logistic regression modeling was applied to identify independent predictors of PUD. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.02 in the univariate analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Variable selection was guided by both statistical significance and clinical relevance, with particular attention to potential overlap among related predictors to minimize redundancy in the final model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Multicollinearity among independent variables, including NSAID use, corticosteroid co-administration, and gastroprotective therapy, was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and no significant collinearity was detected (all VIF < 2).

Results

Literature review

The literature review identified 94 eligible studies from 1,264 records, including 58 original articles, 23 systematic reviews, and 13 meta-analyses, providing high-quality evidence on PUD risk factors and clinical features. Based on these findings, a preliminary checklist was developed, which includes the following main classes: behavioral and substance use (NSAID use, corticosteroid or anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, smoking, opium, and alcohol consumption); diagnostic and clinical indicators (endoscopic confirmation, H. pylori positivity, gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, and persistent epigastric pain); drug characteristics (NSAID administration route and duration, and use of gastroprotective agents); ulcer characteristics (size, number, and location of ulcers); and demographics and comorbidities (age, sex, relevant comorbidities, and socioeconomic status).

Development and validation of the checklist

Using a modified Delphi methodology, we developed and evaluated a comprehensive checklist comprising 22 items across five domains: behavioral and substance use, diagnostic and clinical indicators, drug use characteristics, ulcer characteristics, and demographic/comorbidity profiles. Following two rounds of expert review, 21 items met the predefined content validity criteria (I-CVI ≥ 0.78) and were retained, resulting in an excellent overall scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave = 0.94). Diagnostic and clinical indicators demonstrated the highest levels of consensus, particularly endoscopic confirmation of ulcer (I-CVI = 1.00) and positive Helicobacter pylori testing (0.97). Key behavioral factors, including cigarette smoking (0.93), opium use (0.85), and alcohol consumption (0.82), also showed strong agreement. In contrast, the item related to general steroid use failed to reach consensus after the second round and was excluded. Overall, the retained items exhibited strong expert agreement and content validity, supporting their inclusion in the final checklist (Table 2).

Determine the prevalence of peptic ulcers

Between January 2019 and December 2023, a total of 43,324 hospitalized patients were recorded, among whom 6,874 patients were diagnosed with peptic ulcer disease (PUD) and included in the analysis. The mean age was 60.3 years, with 44.7% of patients aged ≥ 60 years, and 56.8% were male. Most diagnoses were confirmed endoscopically (88.7%). Ulcers were predominantly gastric (63.1%) or duodenal (34.5%), and the majority measured ≥ 5 mm (82.2%). Helicobacter pylori infection was identified in 70.1% of cases. Gastrointestinal bleeding occurred in 30.4% of patients, while clinical signs of perforation were observed in 7.1%. Regarding behavioral and medication-related factors, 75.3% of patients reported NSAID use, 30.2% received concomitant corticosteroids, and 22.6% used antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents. Cigarette smoking was reported by 46.9% of patients, opium use by 18.8%, and alcohol consumption by 14.9%. Among NSAID users, only 26.4% received gastroprotective therapy. Comorbid conditions were common, including diabetes mellitus (31.5%), cardiovascular disease (27.9%), and chronic kidney disease (13.8%). Most patients had a low or moderate socioeconomic status (83.5%) (Table 3).

Case-control study to identify risk factors for peptic ulcers

This case-control study included 20,622 participants, comprising 6,874 patients diagnosed with PUD and 13,748 controls. The mean age of participants was 57.3 years, 44.7% were ≥ 60 years, and 56.8% were male. Among PUD cases, NSAID use (75.3%), corticosteroid co-administration (30.2%), cigarette smoking (46.9%), opium use (18.8%), alcohol consumption (14.9%), and H. pylori infection (70.1%) were highly prevalent, with 88.7% confirmed endoscopically. Univariate analysis identified several significant risk factors, including age ≥ 60 years, NSAID and corticosteroid use, cigarette smoking, opium and alcohol consumption, and H. pylori positivity. Multivariate logistic regression confirmed independent predictors of PUD, including age ≥ 60 years (OR 1.65), male sex (OR 1.27), NSAID use (OR 2.58), corticosteroid co-administration (OR 1.48), antiplatelet/anticoagulant use (OR 1.25), smoking (OR 1.45), opium use (OR 1.37), alcohol consumption (OR 1.24), H. pylori positivity (OR 2.41), gastric ulcer size ≥ 5 mm (OR 2.19), diabetes mellitus (OR 1.42), and cardiovascular disease (OR 1.19). Gastroprotective agent use remained protective (OR 0.71), while socioeconomic status was no longer significant after adjustment. These findings highlight the key behavioral, pharmacological, and clinical determinants of PUD risk, consistent with the results of both univariate and multivariate analyses (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

This study employed a comprehensive mixed-methods approach to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of PUD among hospitalized patients at Abadan University Teaching Hospital. Among 43,324 admissions between January 2019 and December 2023, 6,874 patients were diagnosed with PUD, corresponding to a prevalence of 15.9%. While this rate exceeds global estimates reported by the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 [2], it remains lower than previous national-level assessments. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 cross-sectional studies conducted in Iran between 2002 and 2016 reported an overall prevalence of approximately 34% (CI: 0.25–0.43), with substantial regional variation: 13.6% in Qazvin (2005) and 54.5% in Sanandaj (2016) [11]. These discrepancies likely reflect heterogeneity in healthcare access, population demographics, and local exposure to risk factors, emphasizing the importance of context-specific epidemiological assessment. H. pylori infection was identified in 70.1% of cases, reaffirming its central role as the primary infectious determinant of PUD pathogenesis [12, 13]. This finding is concordant with current guideline recommendations, including the Kyoto Global Consensus and 2024 ACG Clinical Guideline, which advocate for first-line eradication therapy [14, 15]. Pharmacological exposures also remained prominent: NSAID use was reported in 75.3% of patients, and corticosteroid co-administration in 30.2%, highlighting the ongoing contribution of these medications to ulcer risk [16, 17]. Notably, despite strong evidence supporting gastroprotective therapy, only 26.4% of NSAID users received PPIs, indicating a persistent gap in adherence to prophylactic standards [18]. Behavioral factors were prevalent and likely synergistic. Cigarette smoking (46.9%) and alcohol consumption (14.9%) are established contributors to impaired mucosal defense and delayed ulcer healing (19–21). The concurrence of H. pylori infection, NSAID exposure, and these lifestyle factors may collectively increase mucosal vulnerability and the probability of complications, including bleeding and perforation (22, 23). Comorbid conditions, particularly diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease, were also common and may exacerbate mucosal susceptibility and ulcer recurrence, consistent with previous national observations (24–26). Socioeconomic status appeared to modulate disease burden: 43.8% of PUD patients were from low-income backgrounds. Limited healthcare access, suboptimal nutrition, and increased behavioral risk exposures may partially account for this association (27–29). Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that psychological stress and high-strain occupational contexts may influence ulcer pathophysiology via hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation and increased gastric acid secretion (30). Taken together, these findings underscore the multifactorial etiology of PUD, integrating infectious, pharmacologic, behavioral, comorbid, and socioeconomic determinants. By synthesizing regional, national, and global prevalence data, this study provides a nuanced perspective on PUD epidemiology in southwestern Iran. Importantly, it identifies modifiable risk factors that could guide targeted prevention and clinical management strategies, reinforcing the need for context-specific interventions and adherence to evidence-based prophylactic measures. These findings carry important implications for public health strategies and clinical policy in Iran. The high prevalence of H. pylori infection and the suboptimal use of gastroprotective therapy among NSAID users indicate opportunities for both population-level and clinical interventions. National screening and eradication programs for H. pylori, particularly targeting high-risk groups, could substantially reduce disease burden. In parallel, strengthening guideline-based prescribing practices, including wider prophylactic use of PPIs among NSAID and corticosteroid users, could mitigate preventable ulcer complications. Behavioral risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption underscore the need for integrated health education campaigns, while attention to socioeconomic disparities may inform targeted interventions to improve access to care and adherence to preventive measures. Collectively, these insights suggest that a combination of evidence-based clinical protocols, public health initiatives, and socioeconomically sensitive policies could meaningfully reduce the incidence and severity of PUD in Iran.

Clinical Implications and recommendations

Based on our findings and supported by current literature, several clinical and public health strategies are warranted:

H. pylori screening and eradication should be a priority in high-risk populations. Eradication therapy not only reduces recurrence but also lowers the risk of bleeding and perforation [11–13].Rational prescribing of NSAIDs with PPI co-therapy must be enforced, especially in elderly patients and those with comorbidities [14, 15].Lifestyle modification interventions, including smoking cessation and reducing alcohol consumption, should be integrated into primary care and gastroenterology services [16, 17].Addressing health inequities through improved healthcare access, patient education, and targeting deprived neighborhoods can reduce the burden of PUD in vulnerable populations [18, 19].

Limitations

This study has some limitations due to its retrospective design and single-center setting, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. However, the use of a registry-based HIS and standard diagnostic definitions helped reduce data variability. The retrospective design could introduce bias, limiting our ability to establish causality, and reliance on hospital HIS data may have led to incomplete or inaccurately recorded information. Efforts were made to follow up on missing data, when possible, to enhance data verification. Additionally, excluding patients with incomplete medical records may have introduced selection bias, further limiting the generalizability of the results. To validate these findings and explore the changing epidemiology of peptic ulcer disease (PUD) across different demographic settings, prospective multicenter studies are recommended.
