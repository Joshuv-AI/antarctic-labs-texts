Integration of phytohormones, miRNAs and transcriptome sequencing in cucumber fruit development under high-temperature stress
Authors: Zhong Dan, Kanghua Du, Da Zhang, Weiwu Lv, Jie Zhang, Lingfeng Bao, Xiaomei Li, Long Yang, Jixian Ma, Wanfu Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The molecular mechanisms by which heat stress compromises cucumber yield and quality, particularly the epigenetic regulatory networks, remain largely unknown. Here, we show that miRNAs act as key epigenetic mediators that integrating environmental and hormonal signals to coordinate stress adaptation with fruit development. Using a heat-sensitive cucumber line, we performed a multi-omics analysis, including fruit phenotyping, hormone profiling, and transcriptome and sRNA sequencing across development under heat stress. Our results reveal that heat stress induces regional imbalances in endogenous hormones, especially JA and GA₃, among the medial, proximal, and distal regions of the fruit. Integrated analysis RNA-seq and sRNA-seq data from four developmental stages under normal and high-temperature conditions, we identified 92 DE-miRNAs and 8171 DEGs. Notably, 11 consistently down-regulated DE-miRNAs formed 65 miRNA–mRNA pairs with 24 DEGs (Cor > 0.7, P < 0.01). These regulatory modules participate in maintaining JA and GA₃ homeostasis and signaling, thereby contributing to fruit thermal adaptation and morphogenesis under heat stress. Our findings uncover a potential miRNA-hormone regulatory network that orchestrates fruit adaptation and morphogenesis under heat stress, providing a foundation for understanding stress-induced fruit deformity and offers potential targets for breeding thermotolerant cucumber varieties.

Introduction

As a pivotal trait determining the commercial value of cucumbers, the optimal morphological characteristics, including geometric symmetry, vibrant coloration, and surface glossiness, constitute fundamental visual parameters governing market acceptance. Standardization of fruit morphology significantly extends shelf life and enhances efficiency in mechanical harvesting, packaging and transport logistics, whereas malformed fruits incur supply chain loss rates as high as 22% (Lin et al., 2023). However, under protected cultivation, prolonged exposure of ovaries to high temperatures often induces developmental abnormalities, including localized medial swelling and pronounced axial curvature. Although elevated CO2 (enhancing photosynthesis), selenium (scavenging ROS) and MSDI (regulating root-zone conditions) mitigate heat stress, they cannot fully reverse the damage to reproductive development caused by prolonged heat (Du et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2023). Consequently, deciphering the heat-responsive molecular networks governing cucumber fruit development is essential for breeding thermotolerant cultivars that maintain high productivity under heat stress.

Temperature stress influence cucumber fruit morphogenesis by altering the frequency and orientation of cell divisions. Although phytohormones have been proposed to act as central signaling hubs that translate environmental cues into regulated programs of cell division, differentiation, and stress adaptation (Wang et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020), the regulatory network underlying hormone action and its upstream environmental sensors remain largely unclear. Recent evidence suggests that small RNAs (sRNAs), particularly microRNAs (miRNAs), may participate in regulating hormone-related transcription factors and genes, thereby potentially linking stress responses to fruit development. For example, bioinformatic analysis suggests that miRNAs may be implicated in modulating key transcription factors involved in IAA and GA3 biosynthesis, or in responding to upstream cytokinin signals to regulate genes controlling cell elongation and parthenocarpy (Sharif et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2019). However, it is worth noting that most of these findings derive from correlation analysis or heterologous expression systems, and direct causal evidence remains scarce. Similar observations have been reported in other species. In tomato, low-temperature stress induces miR166 overexpression, which suppresses SIHB15A expression and disrupts the auxin-ethylene balance, thereby altering cell division and expansion programs and influencing fruit set (Clepet et al., 2021; da Silva & Nogueira, 2021). Likewise, temperature variations drive the expression of miR156/157-SBP-box module, which influences melon fruit morphogenesis (Ma et al., 2014). Collectively, these studies suggest that hormone signaling may be governed by an integrated network involving miRNAs and transcription factors, and that this network can dynamically reorganize in response to changing temperatures and developmental cues (Correa et al., 2018). Nevertheless, the post-transcriptional regulation by miRNAs is often species-, tissue-, and stress-specific. Whether the miRNAs identified thus far are indeed involved in fruit morphogenesis under heat stress requires further experimental validation.

Although the above studies have provided important clues, there is still a lack of systematic research on miRNA-mediated transcriptional reprogramming during the development of cucumber fruit under high-temperature stress. In this study, we used a European-type cucumber line that has numerous fruits per stem, and that is sensitive to heat stress and prone to fruit deformity under high-temperature conditions. Therefore, we aim to characterize the physiological responses of cucumber fruit development to heat stress, by comparing fruit phenotypes and phytohormone dynamics under different temperature regimes. Furthermore, to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying miRNA involvement in heat-induced fruit malformation, we performed transcriptome and sRNA sequencing to quantitatively analyze the expression levels of key miRNAs and related genes involved in hormone biosynthesis and signaling pathways. This work provides an initial exploration of the miRNA-mediated regulatory network of fruit morphogenesis under heat stress, rather than a comprehensive elucidation of all mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

‘75–2’ cucumbers (gynoecious, European-type) were pot-grown in a mixture of coir pith, sphagnum moss and perlite (60:30:10) under a controlled environment (25 ± 1 °C/20 ± 1 °C day/night, 16 h light, 80% RH) at Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

At first flower anthesis, high-temperature plants were transferred to 32 ± 1 °C/28 ± 1 °C (day/night), while normal-temperature plants remained under the control conditions (25 ± 1 °C/20 ± 1 °C). All plants were maintained continuously under their respective temperature regimes from the first anthesis throughout all sampling stages. Under each temperature condition (normal and high temperature), 15 individual cucumber plants were grown.

Fruit samples from both treatments were collected at 0 (ovary stage), 5 (division stage), 10 (enlargement stage), and 15 days post anthesis (DPA, commercial maturity stage). Three representative fruits were randomly selected from different plants at each developmental stage. Each fruit was considered as one biological replicate, and each biological replicate sample was subjected to three technical replicates. Each fruits sample was divided into four portions: one for paraffin sectioning, one for endogenous hormone measurements, and the two others for sRNA and transcriptomic analysis. Samples for subsequent RNA extraction and hormone analysis were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Thus, all experimental analyses were derived from the same biological replicates, ensuring direct comparability.

Paraffin section and fruit histology analysis

From each fruit (or ovaries), tissue blocks (0.5 cm thick) containing the full pericarp (exocarp, mesocarp and endocarp) were excised from the proximal, medial, and distal equatorial regions. These tissues blocks were fixed in immediately formalin-acetic-alcohol (FAA) at 4 °C for 48 h, dehydrated (ethanol series, 15%, 30%, 50%, 75%, 85%, 95%, 100%, each for 50 min), cleared (ethanol: xylene, 1:1, v/v), and embedded in paraffin following standard histological protocols. Serial 6-μm sections were cut using a Leica RM2235 microtome (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany), stained with safranin O-fast green (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and imaged under a Zeiss Axiophot microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) to assess cellular and structural changes. This paraffin embedding and sectioning protocol was optimized for the specific morphological analyses required in this study (Liu et al., 2020). Cell number and cell size in each given section were calculated using Infinity Capture 6.0 and Image-Pro plus 5.1 software according to the method reported previously (Yang et al., 2013). All measurements were made in three regions per section, and three random sections per tissue block.

Endogenous hormones measurements

For absolute quantification of nine key phytohormones, fruit samples were collected at developmental stages from ovary to commercial maturity and analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The analytes included indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), zeatin (ZT), salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), isopentenyl adenosine (IPA), gibberellic acid (GA3), abscisic acid (ABA), kinetin (KT), and the ethylene precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ETH).

Hormones were extracted from frozen fruit tissue followed by a modified protocol based on Šimura et al. (2018). Briefly, 25 mg frozen tissue (fresh weight equivalent, pre-determined by weighing fresh samples before freezing) was subjected to low-temperature extraction (−20 °C, isopropanol/water 4:1, v/v, containing a mixture of stable isotope-labeled internal standards)followed by centrifugation (4 °C, 11,180 x g, 15 min), solvent evaporation and purification. Quantification was conducted using a SCIEX Triple Quad™ system (SCIEX, Beijing, China), with three technical replicates per biological sample. For hormone quantifications, 9 authentic standards at concentrations ranging from 0.025 to 1000 ng·mL−1 were used to obtain calibration curves based on the peak area of each standard. Detailed information of calibration curves and limits of detection and quantification (LOD/LOQ) of each standard are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Transcriptome analysis, abundance estimation and differentially expressed testing

To systematically analyze mRNA expression dynamics during fruit development under temperature stress, total RNA from frozen fruit samples was extracted and purified using FlaPure Plant Total RNA Extraction Plus Kit (Genesand Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). For each biological replicate, RNA from different fruit regions (proximal, medial and distal) was pooled in equal amounts to obtain a representative RNA sample per fruit. This pooling strategy was adopted to obtain an integrated fruit-level transcriptional profile and to minimize intra-fruit spatial variability. For each developmental stage, three representative fruits were randomly selected for RNA extraction, yielding a total of 24 libraries for subsequent transcriptome sequencing.

The cDNA libraries sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), following the vendor's recommended protocol. Fastp software (version 0.23.4; https://www.bsc.es/marenostrum/marenostrum/available-software/fastp) was used to filter low-quality sequence reads from raw sequencing reads to obtain clean sequence reads (Chen, 2023). Quality control was assessed with FastQC (version 0.12.0; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/; last accessed on 03 January 2023). Clean reads were aligned to the cucumber reference genome Cucumber CLV4.0 (http://www.cucumberdb.com/) using HISAT2 software (version 2.0.4; https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/) (Kim et al., 2015) with default parameters.

Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) using StringTie (version 3.0.3; https://github.com/gpertea/stringtie) (Pertea et al., 2015). Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 using an independent filtering approach. Genes with criterial of |log2 (fold change) | > 1 and adj. P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed (Love et al., 2014). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the Cucumber-DB online tool (http://www.cucumberdb.com/).

miRNA library construction, sequencing and analysis

sRNAs were extracted from frozen tissue using the miRcute Plant miRNA Isolation Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The experimental design was identical to that used for transcriptome sequencing. Each pooled RNA sample was used to prepare an independent library, yielding a total of 24 libraries. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), following standard protocols. Raw reads of sRNAs were processed using fastp software (version 0.23.4; https://www.bsc.es/marenostrum/marenostrum/available-software/fastp) with the following criteria: reads containing 3′ end adapter were removed; low-quality reads were trimmed from each sample; reads with more than 10% ambiguous bases (N) and shorter than 18 nt or longer than 30 nt after trimming were discarded.

Clean reads were aligned to the cucumber reference genome Cucumber CLV4.0 (http://www.cucumberdb.com/) using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.5; https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/index.shtml) with not more than two mismatches and 15 valid alignments allowed. Mapped reads were compared against mature miRNA sequences in miRbase (version 22.1) to identified known miRNA (Meyers et al., 2008). Unannotated reads that could form a stable hairpin structure were predicted using miRDeep2 software (version 0.1.3; https://github.com/rajewsky-lab/mirdeep2) though setting the length of sequence for predicting RNA secondary structure to 250, and a plant-specific scoring system was added to miRDeep2 (Friedländer et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2014). miRNAs expression levels were normalized to TPM (transcripts per million). Differential expression was analyzed using DESeq2 with criterial of |log2 (fold change) | ≥ 0.58, FDR (false discovery rate) < 0.05. Putative target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs) were predicted using the TargetFinder software (version 1.7.0; https://github.com/carringtonlab/TargetFinder) with the default score cutoff of 4. This cutoff has been widely adopted in plant miRNA studies and has been shown to achieve high precision (89%) and recall (97%) for true-positive target prediction (Srivastava et al., 2014).

WGCNA R package (version 4.4.3) (Chen et al., 2019) was used for the construction of interaction network for DEGs involved in hormone biosynthesis and signal transduction. The correlation coefficients (Cor) between the expression levels of DE-miRNAs (TPM) and DEGs (FPKM) were calculated with SPSS (version 25.0). Correlations with Cor > 0.6 and adj. P < 0.01were considered significant. Only negatively correlated pairs (Cor < −0.6) were prioritized as potential miRNA-mRNA regulatory interactions.

Validation of RNA-Seq and sRNA-Seq by qRT-PCR

To validate the expression patterns of selected DEGs and DE-miRNAs, qRT-PCR was performed on the same biological samples used for RNA-seq and sRNA-seq. Reverse transcription was carried out with the UnionScript First-strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) for mRNA and the UnionScript First-stand cDNA Synthesis Mix (Genesand Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) for miRNA. qRT-PCR reactions were run on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with GS AntiQ qPCR SYBR Green Fast Mix (Genesand Biotech, Beijing, China). Each reaction was performed in three technical replicates per biological sample.

Primers for DEGs (listed in Table S16) were designed using Primer Premier 6.0. miRNA primers (listed in Table S15) were designed on the mature miRNA sequences using Vazyme miRNA Design Software (v1.01). Expression levels of miRNA were normalized to the internal reference genes U6, and mRNA levels were normalized to CsEF1. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Fruit samples at 0 DPA were used as the calibrator for all comparisons.

The choice of U6 and CsEF1 as reference genes was validated by previous studies. U6 is a highly conserved and widely used reference for plant miRNA expression (Wang et al., 2018), and CsEF1 has been demonstrated to exhibit the highest expression stability among candidate reference genes in cucumber under various abiotic stress conditions (Wan et al., 2010).

Statistical analysis

All data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test. The coefficient of variation (CV% = SD/mean×100%) was calculated for each variable among three biological replicates. CV% < 20% represented low variability. Significance testing was conducted using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test and One-Way or Two-Way ANOVA with post hoc multiple comparisons, with threshold for statistical significance set at adj. P < 0.05 (significant, marked with *) or adj. P < 0.01 (very significant, marked with **). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0) and data visualization was conducted using R (version 4.4.3).

Results

High temperature treatment results in morphological changes of cucumber fruits

High temperatures induced abnormal development at 5 DPA compared to normal conditions (Fig. 1A). Moreover, high-temperature fruit exhibited progressively slower growth in the medial regions during development, whereas proximal and distal growth remained similar to normal-temperature fruits. To elucidate the cellular basis of these morphological changes, we observed cell number and size in the inner epidermis, outer epidermis and carpels of high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits from ovary stage to commercial maturity (0, 5, 10 and 15 DPA). At the ovary stage (0 DPA), cell numbers showed no significant differences between normal-temperature and high-temperature fruits across all regions (Fig. S1A). During the division and elongation stages (5 and 10 DPA), fruit cells underwent rapid division and exponential growth, driving significant expansion. Histological analysis revealed that heat stress severely disrupted organization in the medial fruit region. Cells lost their radial order and were irregularly arranged (Fig. 1B). At the cellular level, epidermal cells were markedly atrophied, with a significantly reduced volume, in contrast to the plump cells of controls (Fig. 1B). Although more cell layers were present in the observed region, the total cell number per unit area was lower than in control fruits. Furthermore, proximal and distal pericarp cell showed no significant changes in both groups (Fig. S1B). The asymmetric distribution of endogenous hormones controls abnormal fruit under high temperature

Fruit cell division and expansion are primarily governed by genes regulating hormone signaling, transcription factors, and cell cycle pathways (Huang et al., 2009; Qi et al., 2013; Su et al., 2021). We measured nine key phytohormones in the high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits throughout development (ovary to commercial maturity) using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS; Table S1). As shown in Supplementary Table S1, the majority of measured hormones (> 85%) exhibited CV values below 20%, indicating good consistency among assay data and low biological variability. The developmental trajectories of GA3, JA, ZT, and IPA from ovary to commercial maturity (0–15 DPA) were altered under high temperature (Fig. 2A). Notably, the timing of these hormonal shifts was closely temporal coupling with the progression of fruit developmental abnormalities observed under the same conditions. Furthermore, all hormones were elevated, whereas GA3 showed a different pattern, in high-temperature versus normal-temperature fruits. However, a pronounced shift in hormone profiles emerged at commercial maturity. This shift at the commercial maturity stage suggests stage-specific adaptation mechanisms under thermal stress.Fig. 2Analysis of phytohormone contents. GA3 peaked during division and elongation stages (5–10 DPA) then declined in both groups (Fig. 2B, Table S17). Notably, high-temperature fruits exhibited significant spatiotemporal divergence: GA₃ accumulation was consistently lower in the medial region than in proximal and distal regions at 5–10 DPA compared to normal-temperature. At 5 DPA, distal GA₃ in high-temperature fruits exceeded both medial and proximal GA3 by 2.4fold (adj. P < 0.01) and surpassed that normal-temperature fruits by 1.4fold (adj. P < 0.01). These results revealed a patterned shift in GA3 distribution under high temperature. Specifically, accumulation was reduced in the medial region but enhanced in the distal region during 5–10 DPA, whereas the proximal region remaining less affected. This spatial pattern of hormonal change closely coincided with observed phenotypic alterations in both timing and location.

Cell division-related hormones (IPA, KT) showed markedly higher levels in the proximal and medial regions of high-temperature fruits than normal-temperature fruits during the division and enlargement stages (Fig. 2C). At the commercial maturity stage, IPA and KT levels decreased by 35–54% in high-temperature proximal and medial regions compared to normal-temperature fruits. Stress-response hormones (JA, SA) were markedly reduced in distal regions of high temperature fruits versus normal temperature controls (Fig. 2D and E). JA and SA levels decreased by >10% in high temperature distal regions versus controls (adj. P < 0.01). These findings also suggested that the morphological differences between the high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits may be partially attributable to spatial variations in hormone composition and levels.

DE-miRNAs identification, analysis of miRNA targets and miRNA expression analysis

Through sRNAs sequencing, we identified 220 miRNAs in all the samples. A total of 3 known miRNAs and 217 novel miRNAs belonging to 76 families were identified based on miRbase (v 22.1) through BLAST method. The identified miRNA families in various plants are conserved, such as miR159, miR166 and miR399. In these libraries, miR166 was the largest family with 9 members (Table S7-S9). To investigate the roles of miRNAs on fruit development under heat stress, we evaluated the expression patterns of miRNAs. A total of 92 DE-miRNAs (1 known and 91 novel) were identified between high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits. Among these, 7 were up-regulated and 17 were down-regulated across multiple developmental stages (Fig. 5A, S3). Notably, miR398 (Novel_chr5_36931) demonstrated persistent down-regulation at ovary (0 DPA), elongation (10 DPA) and commercial maturity (15 DPA) stages (Table S10 and S11). To understand the potential biological functions of DE-miRNAs, 1106 potential targets genes were predicted (Table S12 and S13). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed on these target genes. Specifically, the target genes of up-regulated miRNAs (334 genes) were enriched in response to hormone (GO:0009725) and glycerolipid metabolism process (GO:0046486), whereas those of down-regulated miRNA (371 genes) were enriched in cell wall modification (GO:0042545), cell wall organization (GO:0071555) and regulation of signaling (GO:0023051). KEGG analysis of these predicted target genes revealed predominant enrichment in fatty acid metabolism (ko01212), carotenoid biosynthesis (ko00906), and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940). Among the pathways, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis was the most significantly enriched pathway in target genes of DE-miRNAs during the early to middle stages of fruit development (ovary, division, and elongation stages) under high-temperature stress (Fig. S3). These results suggest that phenylpropanoid biosynthesis may play an important role in fruit morphogenesis under high temperature.

Co-expression network construction of miRNAs and mRNAs

To better understand the potential miRNA-mRNA regulatory network associated with hormone biosynthesis and signaling under heat stress, we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network (WGCNA) based on hormone quantification and RNA-seq data. Following a scale-free topology criterion, a soft-thresholding power of 9 was selected, which partitioned 1402 DEGs into 10 co-expression modules (excluding the grey module; Fig. 5B, C). Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between the expression patterns of DEGs in these modules and the levels of ZT, IPA, JA and GA3 (|PCC| > 0.5, adj. P < 0.05). JA was negatively correlation with darkturquoise and grey60 modules, whereas GA3 positively correlated with darkgrey modules. Considering the dynamic changes in hormone levels during fruit development, we focused on DEGs involved in JA and GA3 biosynthesis and signaling, and constructed a regulatory model (Fig. S6, Table S14). This model identified 47 and 459 DEGs participating in JA and GA3 biosynthesis and signaling, respectively. Importantly, ABC-2 family transporter protein (CsaV4_11G000232) showed strongest JA association (|MM| > 0.9 and |GS| > 0.5), whereas gibberellin regulated protein (CsaV4_2G002868), LRR receptor-like serine threonine-protein kinase (CsaV4_1G001067) and G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase (CsaV4_1G001226) were most tightly linked to GA₃ (|MM| > 0.9 and |GS| > 0.5).

To gain deeper insight into the regulatory mechanisms by miRNAs and hormone related genes mediate high-temperature-induced fruit deformity in cucumber, we performed an integrative analysis of RNA-seq and sRNA-seq data. This analysis revealed that 11 consistently down-regulated DE-miRNAs formed 65 miRNA-mRNA pairs with 24 DEGs (Cor > 0.7, adj. P < 0.01) (Table S18). Notably, all 11 DE-miRNAs exhibited significant positive correlations with DEGs. Further analysis indicated that two DE-miRNAs (novel_chr5_32739 and novel_chr5_32738) co-regulated CsaV4_4G002214. The remaining DE-miRNAs regulated multiple DEGs, and conversely, individual DEGs were also regulated by several DE-miRNAs. These results suggest the possible existence of a complex regulatory network under heat stress, in which multiple miRNAs and hormone-pathway genes cooperatively modulate fruit morphogenesis.

Validation of transcriptomic data and sRNA sequencing data by qRT-PCR

To evaluate the reliability of the RNA-seq and sRNA-seq data generated from high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits at four development stages, we quantified the relative expression levels of random selected DE-miRNAs and DEG. Compared to the relative expression levels determined by qRT-PCR, the FPKM value from RNA-seq data and TPM value from sRNA-seq data exhibited a similar trend (Fig. S4, S5). Further analysis using Pearson correlation showed that the expression patterns correlated significantly with the sequencing data (R2 = 0.87, adj. P < 0.01; R2 = 0.76, adj. P < 0.01), suggesting a high degree of confidence in the RNA-seq and sRNA-seq results.

Discussion

Reproductive organs are high sensitivity to heat stress, which disrupts anther development, style elongation, pollen release and germination, pollen tube growth, pollen-pistil interactions, fertilization, and embryogenesis, ultimately compromising fruit set and development (Erickson & Markhart, 2002; Firon et al., 2006; Sato et al., 2000; Zinn et al., 2010). Cucumber, as a typical parthenocarpy vegetable, is highly susceptible to temperature stress during fruit development, which is manifested as poor ovary pollination, inhibited fruit growth, and a markedly increased rate of malformed fruits (Shari et al., 2022). Fruit morphology in cucumber is primarily determined by the rates of cell division and expansion, and the interactions between genetic and environmental factors play a crucial role in the fruit development process (Colle et al., 2017). Despite this foundational knowledge, the post-transcriptional regulating high-temperature-induced fruit morphogenesis in cucumbers remain unclear. Here, we compared tissue morphology and hormone levels between high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits across development stages. After entering the division stages (5 DPA), heat stress significantly reduced cell division and expansion rates in the ventral pericarp compared with the proximal and distal regions, whereas no such regional differences were observed in normal-temperature fruits. These findings suggest that heat-stress-induced fruit deformity arises from regional asynchrony in cell division and expansion rates among pericarp regions. Phytohormones, including IAA, CK, and GA3, directly or indirectly regulate cell division and expansion during early fruit development, ultimately determining fruit size and shape (Boonkorkaew et al., 2008). In addition, endogenous hormones such as IAA, SA, JA, and brassinosteroids (BRs) enhance plant self-protection under environmental stress by modulating processes including protein modification, DNA repair, and macromolecular metabolism (El-Remaly, 2023; Yan et al., 2016). Based on these established roles, our study revealed that compared with control fruits, the fruit deformity induced by high-temperature stress may be attributable to severe spatial deviations in hormones such as GA₃ across different developmental stages and fruit regions. However, whether the pronounced changes in SA and JA under heat stress contribute to thermal adaptation and fruit morphogenesis in cucumber requires further investigation.

miRNAs have been reported as pivotal regulators of fruit development and stress response (Liu et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2014; Zuo et al., 2019). Evolutionarily conserved miRNAs exert similar effects on their target genes across diverse plant species, including tomato, cucumber, melon and pepper, where they govern developmental patterning, such as organ size and shape (Du et al., 2026). By contrast, species-specific miRNAs primarily regulate unique fruit traits, including pigmentation, volatile biosynthesis, flavor development, and stress resistance (Liang et al., 2018). To explore the regulatory mechanisms of miRNAs in under high-temperature-induced fruit deformity, we performed an integrated analysis of transcriptomic and sRNA sequencing data. The expression levels of both miRNAs and mRNAs varied significantly among different temperature conditions and development stages. Notably, we identified 92 DE-miRNAs between high-temperature and normal-temperature fruits. Although only one known miRNA was annotated in the cucumber genome, a large proportion of the identified novel miRNAs belong to highly conserved families, such as miR156, miR166 and miR398. These conserved miRNAs may regulate fruit morphology by modulating cell differentiation and may also influence fruit set and development under abiotic stress through hormone signaling pathways (Clepet et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2014). However, a limitation of this study is that RNA pooling from different fruit regions precludes assessment of spatial heterogeneity within the pericarp. Future studies using spatially resolved transcriptomics or laser capture microdissection are needed to dissect the region-specific roles of these DE-miRNAs and DEGs.

Because MIR gene promoters harbor conserved cis-regulatory elements responsive to hormones and environmental stresses, they function as critical hubs that integrate hormonal signals to regulate miRNA transcription (Betti et al., 2021; Curaba et al., 2013; Zhao & Li, 2013). Consequently, far from being passive victims of temperature stress, plant fruits actively orchestrate their acclimation by constructing multi-layered regulatory networks through miRNAs and multiple hormone pathways (Delarue, et al., 2025; Jin, et al., 2024; Chen, et al., 2022; Asakura, et al., 2023). In this study, we identified 11 miRNAs that were consistently downregulated in at least two fruit developmental stages under heat stress. By integrating WGCNA and correlation analyses, we associated these DE-miRNAs with DEGs involved in JA and GA₃ biosynthesis and signaling, yielding 65 miRNA–mRNA potentially pairs. Among the identified DEGs, several belong to families known to regulate hormone biosynthesis and signaling. For example, LRR receptor-like serine threonine-protein kinase (LRR-RLK) and G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase (G-LecRLK) are subfamilies of plant transmembrane receptor kinases. In rice, members such as LRK1 and SRK1 modulate GA3 biosynthesis and signaling by either suppressing the key synthetic enzyme OsKO2 or interacting with the GA receptor GID1(Li et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2013). Gibberellin-regulated proteins (GASA/GAST) act as important integrators downstream of the GA signaling pathway and may participate in promoting or inhibiting cell elongation and division (Li et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020). We also observed that miR166 and a novel miRNA (Novel_chr3_18259) positively correlated with the expression of CsaV4_11G000232, suggesting co-regulation of an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter. Several ABC transporter subfamilies (e.g., COMATOSE, SIABCG23 and OsPDR1) function as carriers for the JA precursor 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (OPDA) or as direct JA transporters, thereby participating in JA signaling (Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020).

Previous studies have reported that LRR-RLK is a bona fide target of miR159a in rice. Moreover, GASA and G-LecRLK, as essential components of the GA signaling network, are also directly or indirectly regulated by miRNAs (Fan et al., 2018; Rubinovich & Weiss, 2010). Based on these findings and our identified DEGs and DE-miRNAs, we propose that under high-temperature stress, a miRNA-mediated regulatory network involved in hormone biosynthesis and signaling may contribute to heat stress responses in cucumber fruit (Fig. 6). Specifically: (1) heat stress represses miR159 expression, leading to reduced LRR-RLK expression, which may derepress GA biosynthetic enzymes (e.g., OsKO2) and promote GA₃ synthesis; (2) down-regulation of miR166 and miR8657 coordinately regulates key factors such as GASA and G-LecRLK, which interact with core GA signaling components (e.g., GID1), subsequently triggering downstream pathways (e.g., phenylpropanoid biosynthesis) involved in heat adaptation and fruit morphogenesis; and (3) miR166 and a novel miRNA jointly regulate an ABC transporter, thereby participating in JA biosynthesis and signaling. Conclusion

In summary, morphological and physiological analyses indicated that high-temperature-induced fruit deformity in cucumber primarily attributable to regional differences in endogenous hormone (e.g., JA and GA₃) levels, which cause an imbalance in cell division and expansion rates among the medial, proximal, and distal regions. Jointly analyzing RNA-seq and small RNA-seq data from four developmental stages under high-temperature and normal-temperature conditions, we identified 92 DE-miRNAs and 8171 DEGs. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that both DE-miRNAs and DEGs were predominantly enriched in secondary metabolic pathways, including phenylalanine metabolism and fatty acid metabolism. Further analysis uncovered 65 miRNA–mRNA pairs formed by 11 consistently down-regulated DE-miRNAs and 24 DEGs (Cor > 0.7, adj. P < 0.01). Among these, the miR159 family (Novel_chr1_32997, Novel_chr5_29837) significant positive correlations with LRR-RLK (CsaV4_1G001067); the miR166 family (Novel_chr6_40005, Novel_chr3_16617) together with the miR8567 family (Novel_chr5_37304) co-regulates GASA (CsaV4_2G002868) and G-LecRLK (CsaV4_1G001226); and a novel miRNA (Novel_chr3_18259) together with the miR166 family (Novel_chr2_10575) co-regulates an ABC transporter (CsaV4_11G000232). These miRNA-mRNA pairs may participate in maintaining JA and GA₃ homeostasis and signaling, thereby contributing to fruit thermal adaptation and morphogenesis. The precise mechanisms by which these miRNA–mRNA pairs mediate high-temperature-induced fruit morphogenesis in cucumber require further investigated through genetic or molecular experiments.
