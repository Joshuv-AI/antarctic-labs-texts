Developing an AI-driven multimodal approach to visualising resilient team performance: joint attentional engagement with gaze and speech in simulated emergency scenarios
Authors: Atsushi Miyazaki, Frank Coffey, Hitoshi Sato, Andrew K Mackenzie, Kyota Nakamura, Kazuya Bise, Takeshi Saitoh, Takeru Abe, Miharu Fuyuno, Stephen Timmons, Keiko Tsuchiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealthcare team performance directly impacts the quality and safety of medical care. However, measuring the performance of teams is challenging and requires methodologies to investigate different contributing elements. This study proposes an AI(artificial intelligence)-driven multimodal approach to visualising gaze (ie, joint visual attention) and speech in medical team performance and examines how these might differ across medical expertise, using eye-trackers and our own automatic gaze annotation programme.MethodFour simulation sessions, two in Japan and another two in the UK, were filmed with eye-trackers worn by a clinician and a nurse. In each site, one session was conducted with an experienced pair (UK_Ex and JP_Ex) and the other with a less experienced pair (UK_LessEx and JP_LessEx). The scenarios were a difficult intubation in Japan and a urine infection, with a family member present, in the UK. The numbers of occurrences and the time lengths of joint attention and individuals’ speeches in the four data sets were compared in total and in 15 s time ranges to see the correlations.ResultThe Ex pairs in both contexts paid joint visual attention more frequently and longer and spoke more than the LessEx pairs. In the JP_Ex, the positive correlation was found between the numbers of joint attention and the total speech durations (r=0.81). That indicates the team members verbally coordinated each other’s attentional objects, which is termed coregulative attentional engagement. In contrast, in the UK_Ex, the correlation was negative (r=−0.70), where they visually monitored each other’s actions while talking to the patient’s family, which we call coinfluential attentional engagement. These tendencies were weak in the LessEx pairs.ConclusionAlthough the accuracy of automatic annotation (approximately. 40%–60%) should be improved before applying it to medical training, the research method could provide preliminary insight into elements of good team performance.Keywords: Communication, Teamwork, Simulation, Medical education, Quality measurement

Introduction

In any social organisation with professional teams, accurately measuring their performance is important for devising strategies to achieve team goals and managing teams efficiently.1 The same is true for healthcare teams, where improving team performance can directly improve the quality and safety in healthcare.2 3 However, it is challenging to objectively evaluate the performance of a team, which is collective and transcends individual skills. Traditional team performance metrics tend to focus on the outcomes achieved by the team or the contributions of its members, potentially failing to adequately capture the cognitive and social processes of the team interactions. To fill the gap, we draw on the concept of joint attention, which has been studied in the field of developmental psychology and cognitive science. It refers to a state where multiple individuals direct their attention towards the same object and mutually recognise the sharing of that attention.4 The notion of visual joint attention is of our central interest, which is a social practice where two or more individuals simultaneously pay visual attention to an object.5 Healthcare professionals’ visual attention is recognised as key to efficient healthcare team performance and studied with an eye-tracking device, for example, neonatal team leaders’ gaze behaviour in real operations6 and anaesthetists’ gaze behaviour during central venous catheter insertion.7

In a similar vein, this study proposes a research method to visualise how healthcare professionals coordinate joint engagement with gaze and speech in team interactions. The research project team comprises senior physicians, linguists, psychologists and information scientists in the UK and Japan. Two research questions are addressed:

RQ1: Do healthcare professionals use couplings of gaze and speech to achieve visual joint attention during simulated operations?

RQ2: Do healthcare professionals with different levels of clinical experience use verbal and gaze signals differently to coordinate joint attentional engagement?

We recorded healthcare team interactions in small-scale simulation sessions recorded in two sites with two different emergency scenarios, an emergency care setting in the UK and simulated anaesthesia emergency in Japan, capturing healthcare professionals’ gaze with two mobile eye-tracking devices. By so doing, we captured clinical practices in different disciplines and cultures. For the gaze analysis, we used a programme developed by the AI scientists in our project team to automatically detect occurrences of joint attention. The results are discussed, drawing on the enactive theory in embodied cognition, which distinguishes two types of joint attentional engagement: mutual influence (eg, two children doing a puzzle together) and coregulation (eg, an adult helping a young child’s holding a fork).8 This study is exploratory with a small dataset, and the findings cannot be generalised. Its main contribution is to assess the feasibility of a methodology to visualise team interactions with multimodal sensory data, which can inform future research.

Data and methods

Research data

Four simulation sessions, two in Japan and another two in the UK, were filmed with eye-tracking glasses (Pupil Invisible in Japan and Tobii Pro Glasses 2 in the UK) worn by a clinician and a nurse to capture their first-person view input. To extract gaze data from the eye-trackers, a multimodal software product, iMotions,9 was used. In each site, one session was conducted with an experienced pair (Ex) of a clinician and a nurse and the other with a less experienced pair (LessEx). Two simulated anaesthesia sessions were recorded in an operating room at the Yokohama City University Medical Centre in Japan (JP_Ex and JP_LessEx), with a scenario of a difficult intubation (using a high-fidelity mannequin as a patient). The JP_LessEx pair consisted of a less experienced anaesthetist and an experienced anaesthetist acting as a nurse. While in the UK, emergency care simulation sessions were filmed in DREEAM, an emergency medicine academic unit at the Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust in the UK, with a scenario of urine infection (UK_Ex and UK_LessEx), where a participant patient, a participant patient’s family and a participant nurse also attended. In the UK_LessEx, a nurse practitioner participated in the simulation as a less experienced clinician. Along with eye-trackers, a 360° camera (GoPro Max) was placed in the room to capture the whole image of the simulation sessions. The first 6 min of each session was used for comparison in the preliminary analysis.

Research methods

Some studies on healthcare team performances employed conversation analytic and video reflexive ethnographic approaches, which have strengths in a micro analysis, observing and qualitatively describing healthcare interactions,10 but a weakness in the quantification of interactions. This study fills the gap and proposes a research method to capture global patterns in gaze-speech (de)couplings in healthcare team performances, employing a multimodal corpus analysis.11 A multimodal corpus comprises multiple human communication signals time-aligned for analysis, that is, speech and gaze in interactions in this study. The numbers of occurrences (count) and the time lengths (duration) of joint attention and individuals’ speeches in the four datasets were juxtaposed and compared in total and in 15 s time ranges to see the correlations.

Our previous study of a simulated emergency care interaction with two mobile eye-trackers classified three types of joint gaze behaviours: joint attention (two people look at the same part of an object), narrow joint field view (two people look at different parts of an object) and broad joint field view (two people share a view field but look at different objects).12 Joint attention in this study refers to the first two. For the analysis, a multiple-gaze annotation system was developed by the AI specialists in our project team at Kyushu Institute of Technology, applying computer vision technology.13 It automatically detects instances of visual joint attention between a clinician and a nurse in image frames extracted from two sets of eye-tracking data, using feature-point detection technology. The programme was used to annotate the two JP data. The annotated joint attention data (text files and video images of time-aligned two-gaze movements) were imported into a multimodal analysis tool, ELAN (EUDICO Linguistic Annotator),14 and manually checked and refined (the accuracy rate of the automatic gaze annotation on the JP_Ex data was 42.0 %, and that of JP_LessEx 58.7%).

The gaze behaviours of the anaesthetists and the nurses in the UK data were manually annotated on ELAN, using the video images extracted from the eye-trackers. In the coding process, individual gaze data were manually annotated first in terms of the timing and the objects they looked at, and the clinician’s annotated gaze data and that of the nurse were compared with identify instances of joint attention. The individual healthcare professionals’ utterances were also manually transcribed. In so doing, the joint attention coding in both sites was validated.

In the speech data, the numbers of speaking turns were counted. In the UK data, the clinicians’ and the nurses’ utterances addressed to the patient family present were excluded from the data in order to see the patterns in verbal interactions among the team.

Their total speech durations, which is an addition of the clinician’s and nurse’s speech time, are approximately twice those of the LessEx counterparts. In the anaesthesia simulation sessions, more time was spent talking between the anaesthetist and a nurse than the emergency care scenarios, especially in the JP_Ex data, where the total speech duration was higher than the total operation time (6 mins) because of the overlapping speeches. The clinicians spoke more than the nurses in all the data, but the discrepancy was larger in the context of the emergency care scenarios in the UK.

Gaze and speech (de)couplings

To see the global patterns in the cooccurrences of joint attention and speech during the simulation interactions, the counts and durations of joint attention and speech in the four datasets were bundled into 15 s time ranges and juxtaposed in online supplemental table 1. The correlations of the factors listed in each set were calculated and illustrated in figure 1, using an online tool for the exploratory factor analysis.15 In the JP_Ex, the positive correlation was found between the numbers of joint attention and the total speech durations (r=0.81). This means that the use of speech was likely to increase when the anaesthetist and the nurse achieved joint attention. In other words, gaze-speech couplings tended to be attributed to the JP_Ex interactions. In contrast, the numbers of joint attention and the total speech durations are negatively correlated in the UK_Ex data (r=−0.70). To put it differently, their joint attention and speeches were likely to be decoupled in the simulated emergency care interactions because in the UK data, the clinicians were engaged in talking to the patient’s family while observing the patient and monitoring the nurse’s actions. The difference in the distribution of their visual attention and speech is discussed further in the following section. The correlation between the numbers of joint attention and the total speech durations is positive and moderate in both LessEx pairs (r=0.56 in the UK_LessEx and r=0.50 in JP_LessEx), indicating weak gaze-speech couplings in interactions. Discussion

The AI-driven multimodal approach visualised the global patterns of gaze and speech (de)couplings in the different simulated scenarios. The section discusses the preliminary results from the analysis based on the notions of coregulation and mutual influence in the enactive theory to show the potential of the methodological contribution. Problems of AI-driven approaches to gaze analysis are also delineated.

RQ1: joint attention and gaze-speech couplings

From the global patterns analysis of the visual and verbal behaviours in the simulated interactions, different types of joint attentional engagement practices were identified between the two distinctive scenarios. In the anaesthesia emergency scenario, especially in the JP_Ex, their speeches coregulated each other’s visual attention, closely coordinating their joint attentional activities both verbal and visual cues. In figure 3 from the JP_Ex data, for instance, the anaesthetist asks the nurse to check if there is any leak in the line while looking and pointing at the intravenous access on the patient arm. This is an example of a coupling of the anaesthetist’s verbal and visual behaviours. Hearing the anaesthetist’s request, the nurse shifts his gaze from the machine to the patient and checks the intravenous access, holding the patient’s arm. This led to a joint attention to the patient’s arm between the anaesthetist and the nurse. In reference to the enactive theory, we shall call this coregulative attentional engagement.

Still image from the JP_Ex data: joint attention on the patient’s body. IV, intravenous.

In the emergency care scenario, on the other hand, the Ex data showed the pattern not of couplings but decouplings of joint visual attention and speech. In the context, the experienced clinician (and the nurse) seemed to distribute their speech and gaze to different attentional objects. Since the participating family member was present in the sessions, the healthcare professionals were engaged in two conversation frames: interactions among the team members and the other with the patient’s family member (the patient also responded to the clinician’s speech, but it was limited because their level of consciousness was low). Figure 4 depicts the moment when the experienced clinician gazed at and monitored the nurse’s gaze direction towards the patient’s oxygen mask while talking to the patient family member. The clinician then heard the nurse’s utterance, ‘oh, you lost your oxygen, darling’, shifting his gaze to the patient’s face, which leads to their joint visual attention to the patient’s face and the mask. In this excerpt, the clinician’s utterance belongs to the conversational frame between the clinician and the family member, not between the medical team members. Similarly, the nurse’s utterance framed a conversation between the nurse and the patient. Thus, both utterances were not directly used to coordinate or coregulate each other’s actions. In other words, their verbal signals were decoupled from their subsequential joint visual attentional engagement. We shall call this as coinfluential attentional engagement.

RQ2: joint engagement and the levels of medical experience

The global pattern analysis of their gaze and speech suggests possible differences in team performances for joint attentional engagement between simulated emergency care and anaesthesia interactions. The distinctive practices were described in reference to the two concepts, mutual influence and coregulation, drawing on the enactive theory. The JP_Ex data attributes to coregulative attentional engagement, where the clinician and the nurse regulated each other’s actions by coordinating their verbal and visual cues. In the UK_Ex data, on the other hand, the doctor’s speech was allocated to the interaction with the patient’s wife, but his gaze was distributed to the nurse’s actions, decoupling his verbal and visual behaviours. In a similar manner, the nurse interacted and administered medical procedures on the patient while monitoring the doctor’s actions with gaze. Thus, their joint attentional engagement was coinfluential, simultaneously participating in multiple-layered frames of interactions, for example, a conversation with a patient family and with a team member.

This tendency is, however, not seen clearly in the data of the less experienced pairs. In contrast to the JP_Ex, the JP_LessEx’s use of verbal signals to coordinate and coregulate their joint visual attentional practices was limited. The UK_LessEx showed a weak positive correlation between the counts of joint attention and the speaking time. This means that they used verbal cues to achieve joint visual attention. It contradicts the UK_Ex data with a negative correlation between their joint attention and speaking. This indicates that the UK_LessEx may have failed to distribute their multiple sensory attention, ie, gaze and speech, to multiple conversation frames simultaneously occurring in the simulated emergency care interactions.

The two labels, coregulative attentional engagement and coinfluential attentional engagement, were used in the analysis to distinguish the behaviours observed in the team interactions in the anaesthesia simulation data from the other in the simulated emergency care scenario, but the findings are preliminary. The dataset we used for the analysis is small (two data sets in each site), and the differences could have simply been caused by the contexts and the scenarios of the individual training sessions. The data should be further investigated with a larger dataset, but the global pattern analysis highlights the areas to be examined. More descriptions could be added with a qure-alitative approach, for example, a linguistic analysis on verbal cues for joint attentional engagement. Future study could also involve measurement of visual attentional performance and cognitive processing, drawing on the knowledge accumulated in experimental and behavioural psychology, for example, visual attention in driving and sports.16

AI-driven multimodal approach to visualising resilient team performance

Automatic gaze coding software is incorporated in some gaze analysis system products, for example, the automatic area-of-interest module in iMotions, yet those are not applicable for analysing multiple gaze datasets filmed in a dynamic interaction. Manual gaze annotation is unavoidable to capture gaze behaviour of a medical team interaction at this stage, but AI-driven approaches would increase the efficiency. Two known problems of the gaze analysis of medical interactions using mobile eye-tracking devices would be: the processing time for manual annotation and validation, and the potential disturbance in the operation caused by wearing an eye-tracker for filming.

The UK_Ex data, for example, includes 361 instances of the clinician’s gaze and 306 of the nurse’s. Assuming that it takes 2 min to annotate one instance of gaze, it would take 22 hours to complete the individual gaze coding for the 6 min data in addition to the time for detecting joint attention based on the individual gaze annotations (approximately several hours). With the AI programme to automatically detect joint attention developed for this project, we could significantly reduce the time for coding (approx. half of the time spent for the manual coding), using the automatically annotated data as a guide for manual coding. However, improving the accuracy of automatic gaze annotation is required before moving onto the full automatisation of gaze coding.

As for the second issue, to collect human visual sensory data, healthcare professionals have to wear a mobile eye-tracking device, which could be distracting regardless of the recent advancement in the feasibility of mobile eye-tracking devices. To address this, the use of remote sensing technology could be another solution, for exaple, head pose estimation technology to detect joint attentional engagement from 360 video footages.17

Conclusion

Healthcare team interaction is a complex phenomenon. Capturing multimodal sensory signals of multiple healthcare professionals who are engaged in joint activities and analysing the data in an integrated manner is not an easy task. However, it could be a way to visualise good team performance in action. It is still at an early stage, but the AI-driven multimodal research method we reported here would suggest a possibility of visualising key elements of team performance, while significantly reducing the time and manual labour required for the annotation of multiple sensory data to analyse dynamic and fluid healthcare team interactions. Although the accuracy of automatic annotation needs to be improved, before applying the technology to medical simulation in education and training, the research methodology offers a valuable way of visualising important facets of team performance using multimodal human sensory data.18

Applications of the research methodology to simulated or real-life interactions would be possible in a range of healthcare contexts such as surgeon–nurse interactions during an operation, communication among a rapid response team or information transfer between nurses at handover in individual departments. What healthcare professionals see and say in each context would differ, depending on the priority given in the particular context. The identification of good practice in their utterances and gaze behaviours in distinctive settings would likely benefit healthcare professionals, especially those with less experience, to perform better as team members in real situations.

The challenge to the methodological application of the findings is twofold, that is, the feasibility and the scalability of the analysis. Mobile eye-trackers are becoming accessible to researchers (and lay persons) in disciplines outside the field of information science, in terms of the production and operability. Yet, the analysis of multiple human sensory data currently requires manual coding, which is labour-intensive. This affects the size of the data to be analysed. To process a large dataset, automated annotations on the recorded gaze and spoken data are necessary, and the accuracy of AI-driven annotations should also be validated. These are the areas to be addressed further in future research. Accumulating and sharing the knowledge of good practice in team interactions in different disciplines would benefit medical education and training. AI-driven annotation systems could also enable healthcare professionals to receive immediate feedback on their own gaze behaviours and spoken interactions at briefings during simulation sessions in the future, which would be a useful education tool for enhancing team performance.

As a final note, our considerations on sociocultural contexts of the simulation sessions filmed for this study should be briefly elucidated. This study could not attribute differences in findings in the Japanese and UK scenarios to cultural difference, as the clinical scenarios and specialty settings were different in the two countries and any differences might have been the result of this. Instead, we focused on distinctive team practices in the different medical disciplines, leaving further research on cultural differences in healthcare practice for future investigations.
