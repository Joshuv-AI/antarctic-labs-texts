Photon rest mass from localized fast radio bursts with improved distribution of dispersion measure from extragalactic gas
Yuchen Zhang, Yang Liu, Hongwei Yu, Puxun Wu

Abstract
The assumption that photons are massless is a foundational postulate of modern physics, yet it remains subject to experimental verification. Fast radio bursts (FRBs), with their cosmological distances and precisely measured dispersion, offer an excellent laboratory for testing this hypothesis. In this work, we propose an improved distribution function for the dispersion measure arising from extragalactic gas and demonstrate that it provides an excellent fit to mock data. We then apply this distribution to constrain the photon rest mass under the Λ \Lambda CDM, w w CDM, and w 0 ​ w a w_{0}w_{a} CDM cosmological models, the last of which is favored by recent DESI baryon acoustic oscillation observations. The corresponding 1 ​ σ 1\sigma upper limits on the photon mass are found to be 4.83 × 10 − 51 ​ kg 4.83\times 10^{-51}\,\mathrm{kg} , 4.71 × 10 − 51 ​ kg 4.71\times 10^{-51}\,\mathrm{kg} , and 4.86 × 10 − 51 ​ kg 4.86\times 10^{-51}\,\mathrm{kg} , respectively, which are the most stringent constraints derived from FRBs to date. These results indicate that the choice of cosmological model has only a minor impact on photon-mass bounds, demonstrate that FRBs provide robust and reliable constraints, and offer strong empirical support for the massless nature of the photon.

Introduction

A central postulate of Einstein’s special relativity is the invariance of the speed of electromagnetic waves
19
: the speed of light in vacuum is constant, independent of the motion of either the source or the observer. This principle implies that photons must have zero rest mass since a non-zero photon mass would result in a frequency-dependent group velocity of photons, causing higher-frequency components to travel faster than the lower-frequency ones. Consequently, photons emitted simultaneously from the same source will exhibit a frequency-dependent time delay upon arrival, providing an opportunity to experimentally test whether photons are massless.

Numerous ground tests have been conducted to measure variations in the speed of light in order to examine the photon rest mass

II
DM from photon mass

If photons possess a non-zero rest mass
m_{\gamma}
, the energy of a single photon is

\displaystyle E=\sqrt{p^{2}c^{2}+m_{\gamma}^{2}c^{4}},

(1)

where
p
is the photon momentum and
c
is the speed of light. From Eq. (
1
), the corresponding group velocity of a photon with frequency
\nu
becomes

\displaystyle v=\frac{\partial E}{\partial p}=c\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{\gamma}^{2}c^{4}}{h_{p}^{2}\nu^{2}}}\approx c\left(1-\frac{1}{2}A\nu^{-2}\right),

(2)

where
A\equiv\frac{m_{\gamma}^{2}c^{4}}{h_{p}^{2}}
and
h_{p}
is the Planck constant. The approximation is valid only for a very small photon mass,
m_{\gamma}\ll E/c^{2}\approx 7\times 10^{-42}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mathrm{GHz}}\right)\mathrm{kg}

71
. Eq. (
2
) indicates that if
m_{\gamma}\neq 0
, the speed of light becomes frequency-dependent, with higher-frequency photons propagating faster.

We now consider an astrophysical source at redshift
z
emitting radiation with high and low frequencies
\nu_{h}
and
\nu_{l}
. If the high-frequency photons arrive at
z=0
, the low-frequency photons, traveling more slowly, arrive at time
-\Delta z
(with
\Delta z\ll 1
). The comoving distances traveled by the two components are

x_{\nu_{h}}=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}A\nu_{h}^{-2}(1+z^{\prime})^{-2}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}{\tilde{E}(z^{\prime})}

and

x_{\nu_{l}}=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{-\Delta z}^{z}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}A\nu_{l}^{-2}(1+z^{\prime})^{-2}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}{\tilde{E}(z^{\prime})},

where
H_{0}
is the Hubble constant,
\tilde{E}(z)=H(z)/H_{0}
is the dimensionless Hubble parameter. The factor
(1+z)^{-2}
accounts for cosmic redshift of the photon frequency. Since both photon components originate from the same source, their comoving distances must be equal, i.e.,
x_{\nu_{h}}=x_{\nu_{l}}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\Delta z\simeq\frac{A}{2}(\nu_{l}^{-2}-\nu_{h}^{-2})H_{\gamma}(z),

(3)

where

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}(z)\equiv\int_{0}^{z}\frac{(1+z^{\prime})^{-2}}{\tilde{E}(z^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.

(4)

The corresponding time delay induced by the photon mass is then

\displaystyle\Delta t_{m_{\gamma}}=\frac{\Delta z}{H_{0}}\simeq\frac{A}{2H_{0}}(\nu_{l}^{-2}-\nu_{h}^{-2})H_{\gamma}(z).

(5)

The time delay due to
m_{\gamma}\neq 0
can be interpreted as an effective DM

57
;
75
;
71
;
63
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM_{\gamma}}=\Delta t_{m_{\gamma}}\frac{8\pi^{2}m_{e}\epsilon_{0}c}{e^{2}}(\nu_{l}^{-2}-\nu_{h}^{-2})^{-1}=\frac{4\pi^{2}m_{e}\epsilon_{0}c^{5}}{h_{p}^{2}e^{2}}\frac{H_{\gamma}(z)}{H_{0}}m_{\gamma}^{2}.

(6)

Here
\epsilon_{0}
represents the permittivity of vacuum,
e
is the electron charge, and
m_{e}
is the mass of electron.

By comparing the observed DM with Eq. (
6
), we may constrain the photon rest mass.
Since
H_{\gamma}(z)
depends on the expansion history, adopting a cosmological model is essential. Motivated by recent DESI BAO results favoring dynamical dark energy, we consider three flat cosmological models, i.e.,
\Lambda
CDM,
w
CDM and
w_{0}w_{a}
CDM. Their dimensionless Hubble parameters are

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(z)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{r0}(1+z)^{4}+(1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0})}&\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}\\[8.0pt]
\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{r0}(1+z)^{4}+(1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0})(1+z)^{3(1+w)}}&w\mathrm{CDM}\\[8.0pt]
\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{r0}(1+z)^{4}+(1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0})(1+z)^{3(1+w_{0}+w_{a})}e^{-3w_{a}\frac{z}{1+z}}}&w_{0}w_{a}\mathrm{CDM}.\end{cases}

(7)

Here
\Omega_{m0}
and
\Omega_{r0}
are the present matter and radiation density parameters, and
w
,
w_{0}
and
w_{a}
are constants characterizing dynamical dark energy. The radiation density is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{r0}=\Omega_{\gamma 0}(1+0.2271N_{eff}),

(8)

where
\Omega_{\gamma 0}=\frac{3}{4h^{2}\cdot 31500}\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{CMB}}}{2.7\mathrm{K}}\right)^{4}
is the present photon energy density with
h\equiv H_{0}/100~\mathrm{km}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
and
T_{\mathrm{CMB}}=2.7255~\mathrm{K}

13
, and
N_{eff}=3.044
represents the effective number of relativistic species.
To break degeneracies between cosmological parameters and the photon mass, we include complementary datasets: SN Ia, CMB and BAO.

III
Observational Data

III.1
FRB

For FRBs, the observed dispersion measure,
\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, contains contributions from several astrophysical sources as well as a potential contribution from a non-zero photon mass:

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{obs}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM_{astro}}+\mathrm{DM_{\gamma}}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{ISM}}^{\mathrm{MW}}+\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{halo}}^{\mathrm{MW}}+\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(z)+\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{host}}(z)+\mathrm{DM_{\gamma}},

where the superscript MW denotes the Milky Way, and the subscripts refer to contributions from the interstellar medium (ISM), the Milky Way halo, the cosmic baryon contribution (including the IGM and intervening halos
16
), the FRB host galaxy, and the photon-mass term.

Due to significant fluctuations in the distribution of baryons along the signal path, only the average cosmic dispersion measure
\mathrm{\langle DM_{cosmic}\rangle}
can be computed reliably. Its theoretical expression is
27
;
26
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\langle{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3c\Omega_{b0}{H_{0}}f_{\mathrm{{d}}}}{8\pi Gm_{p}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{(1+z^{\prime})\chi_{e}(z^{\prime})}{\tilde{E}(z^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}

(10)

where
\Omega_{b0}
is the present cosmic baryon density fraction parameter,
m_{p}
is the photon mass, and
f_{\mathrm{d}}
is the baryon mass fraction in the diffuse state. Following
41
;
38
, we treate
f_{\mathrm{d}}
as a constant,
despite the expectation of an increasing trend with redshift
52
;
48
. For redshifts
z<3
, hydrogen and helium are fully ionized, giving
\chi_{e}=7/8

4
;
49
, which describes the number of free electrons per baryon. The Milky Way ISM contribution
\mathrm{DM_{ISM}^{MW}}
can be subtracted from the observed dispersion measure using electron-density models such as NE2001 and YMW16
17
;
78
. In this work, we adopt NE2001. For the Milky Way halo, Prochaska et al.
52
estimates
\mathrm{DM_{halo}^{MW}}
to lie between 50 and 80
\mathrm{pc~cm^{-3}}
. Therefore, we impose a Gaussian prior
\mathcal{N}(65,~15^{2})~(\mathrm{pc~cm^{-3}})
truncated to this interval.

Even after removing these Milky Way contributions, degeneracies remain among the cosmic, host, and photon-mass components. Following Macquart et al.
47
, we define
\mathrm{DM_{ext}^{\prime}}
as
\mathrm{DM}
corrected for contributions from the ISM and the halo:

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM_{ext}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM_{obs}-DM_{ISM}^{MW}-\mathrm{DM_{halo}^{MW}}}

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}+\mathrm{DM_{host}}+\mathrm{DM_{\gamma}}\equiv\mathrm{DM_{ext}}+\mathrm{DM_{\gamma}}.

The probability distribution function (PDF) of
\mathrm{DM_{ext}}
, including cosmic and host contributions, is

\displaystyle P(\mathrm{DM_{ext}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathrm{DM^{\prime}_{ext}-DM_{\gamma}}}\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{\langle DM_{cosmic}\rangle}}P_{\mathrm{host}}(\mathrm{DM_{host}})

(12)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{DM^{\prime}_{ext}-DM_{host}-DM_{\gamma}}}{\mathrm{\langle DM_{cosmic}\rangle}}\right)\mathrm{dDM_{host}}.

This expression originates from Macquart et al.
47
, although they omitted the normalization factor
\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{\langle DM_{cosmic}\rangle}}

82
;
85
.
Let
\Delta\equiv\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}/\mathrm{\langle DM_{cosmic}\rangle}
, the cosmic-component PDF,
P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(\mathrm{\Delta})
, is found to satisfy

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(\Delta)=A\Delta^{-\beta}\exp\left[-\dfrac{(\Delta^{-\alpha}-1)^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}^{2}}\right],~~~~~\Delta>0,

(13)

where
A
is the normalization constant, and
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
are model parameters. This formula differs slightly from that proposed by Macquart et al.
47
(see Eq. (
24
)) as we fix
C_{0}=1
. In
47
,
\alpha
and
\beta
were both set to 3 and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}=F\times z^{-0.5}
, with
F
being a free parameter. Based on the continuous
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
catalog, Konietzka et al.
29
found that the PDF of
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
takes a similar form to that of Eq. (
13
), with parameters
\alpha\approx 1
and
\beta\approx 3.3
, which fits well with their simulation data. In our analysis, we treat
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
as free parameters and calibrate them with mock data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicates that allowing all three parameters to vary provides a significantly better description of
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
(see Appendix.
A
for details).

We model the host-galaxy DM,
P_{\mathrm{host}}(\mathrm{DM_{host}})
, as a log-normal distribution

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{host}}(\mathrm{DM_{host}})=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{DM_{host}\sigma_{host}}}\times\exp\mathrm{\left[-\dfrac{(\ln{DM_{host}}-\mu_{host})^{2}}{2\sigma_{host}^{2}}\right]},

(14)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{host}}
are obtained from the IllustrisTNG simulation results
80
over
0.1<z<1.5
. Values at arbitrary redshift are obtained via cubic-spline interpolation.

From the FRB likelihood function,
\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FRB}}=\ln P(\mathrm{DM_{ext}})
, we can derive constraints on the photon mass and cosmological parameters from FRBs. Recently, a collection of 115 localized FRBs was compiled in
23
, with the highest redshift
z=1.354
. This large dataset significantly enhances our ability to constrain free parameters.
In this compilation, FRB190520B and FRB220831A are excluded due to their extremely high
\mathrm{DM_{obs}}
, and FRB220319D is discarded because it has a negative
\mathrm{DM_{ext}}
. Additionally, FRB20221027A is excluded for its ambiguity in the host galaxy localization
58
. Considering that
\mathrm{DM_{halo}}
is restricted to
(50,80)\,\mathrm{pc~cm^{-3}}
, we impose the additional condition:
\mathrm{DM_{obs}-DM_{ISM}^{MW}>80}~\mathrm{pc~cm^{-3}}
as filters to ensure physical consistency. We also require
z>0.02
, because the parameters in the PDF start at
z=0.02
. After applying these cuts, 104 FRBs remain for our analysis.

III.2
SN Ia

The SN Ia data utilized in our analysis come from the Pantheon+ compilation, which consists of 1701 supernovae with redshifts in the range
z\in[0.00122,~2.26137]

56
. To reduce the impact of peculiar velocities on very nearby events
9
, we discard all objects with redshift
z\leq 0.01
, leaving 1590 supernovae in our final sample. The distance module
\mu
of SN Ia is defined as
\mu=m-M
, where
m
is the SN apparent magnitude and
M
is the absolute magnitude, treated as a nuisance parameter that is marginalized over in the analysis. The theoretical distance module for a SN Ia can be evaluated using the following formula:

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{th}}=25+5\log_{10}\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{L}}(z)}{\mathrm{Mpc}}\right),

(15)

where
D_{\mathrm{L}}
is the luminosity distance, calculated as

D_{\mathrm{L}}=\frac{c(1+z)}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{\tilde{E}(z^{\prime})}
in a spatially flat universe. The log-likelihood function for SN Ia can be expressed in terms of the observed distance modules and the corresponding model predictions:

\displaystyle\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SN}}\propto-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{obs}}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{th}})^{\mathrm{T}}~\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{SN}}^{-1}~(\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{obs}}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{th}})

(16)

with
\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{SN}}
the covariance matrix of the observed distance moduli.

CMB

For CMB data, we use the Planck 2018 results
2

l_{\mathrm{A}}
, the shift parameter
R
and

The theoretical expression of

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{A}}(z^{*})=(1+z^{*})\frac{\pi D_{\mathrm{A}}(z^{*})}{r_{s}(z^{*})},

(17)

where
z^{*}=1089.92

\displaystyle r_{s}(z)=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{1/(1+z)}\frac{da}{a^{2}\tilde{E}(a)\sqrt{3\left(1+\frac{3\Omega_{b0}h^{2}}{4\Omega_{\gamma 0}h^{2}}a\right)}}.

Here

\displaystyle R(z^{*})=(1+z^{*})\frac{D_{\mathrm{A}}(z^{*})\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}}H_{0}}{c}.

IV
Results

Within the framework of three cosmological models, we run 32 independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains for each model. The number of iterations (
\mathrm{N_{steps}}
) for each chain is automatically controlled by the sampler. Convergence is assessed using the integrated autocorrelation time
\tau
for all parameters, with the stopping criterion: (1)
\mathrm{N_{steps}}>50\times\tau
and (2)
\left|\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{old}}-\tau}{\tau}\right|<0.01
, where
\tau_{\mathrm{old}}
is the value from the previous checkpoint. The convergence test is performed every 1,000 iterations.

In the context of the

V
Conclusions and discussions

The DMs of cosmological and well-localized FRBs have become powerful tools for estimating cosmological parameters. In this paper, we first improved the DM distribution from extragalactic gas proposed in
47
by restoring a missing normalization factor and by allowing the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
in the probability distribution function for the DM of cosmic origin to vary with redshift. We then utilized this improved distribution to test whether the photon has a non-zero rest mass, since any such mass would contribute an additional effective DM term. Using 104 localized FRBs, combined with SN Ia, CMB, and BAO data,
we obtain the following
1\sigma
upper limits on the photon rest mass:
m_{\gamma}\leq 4.83\times 10^{-51}~\mathrm{kg}
for
\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}
model,
m_{\gamma}\leq 4.71\times 10^{-51}~\mathrm{kg}
for the
w\mathrm{CDM}
model and
m_{\gamma}\leq 4.86\times 10^{-51}~\mathrm{kg}
for the
w_{0}w_{a}\mathrm{CDM}
model. These values differ only minimally among the three cosmological models, indicating that the photon-mass constraint is largely insensitive to assumptions about dark energy. All three results provide strong support for the massless nature of the photon.

Appendix A
Modified probability distribution function of
\Delta

Since the distribution of free electrons in the IGM is inhomogeneous, the actual value of
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
fluctuates around its average value
\langle\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle
. To account for this variation, Macquart et al.
(
47
)
introduced
\Delta=\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}/\langle\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle
and proposed a probability distribution function (
Macquart distribution
) for
\Delta

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(\Delta)=A\Delta^{-\beta}\exp\left[-\dfrac{(\Delta^{-\alpha}-C_{0})^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}^{2}}\right].

(24)

Here both
\alpha
and
\beta
are fixed to 3,
A
is the normalization constant,
C_{0}
is a parameter determined by the requirement that the mean value of
\Delta
equals 1, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
is assumed to follow
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}=Fz^{-1/2}
with
F
being treated as a free parameter.
Later, Zhang et al.
83
treated
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
in Eq. (
24
) as a free parameter and employed the IllustrisTNG cosmic simulation to determine the optimal values for different redshift points. Their results have since been widely adopted in subsequent studies. Recently, Konietzka et al.
29
proposed a new method for measuring
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
in the IllustirsTNG cosmic simulation and found Zhang et al.
83
had misestimated the standard deviation and higher moments of the
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
distribution by over 50%. Based on the continuous
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
catalog acquired through their new method, Konietzka et al.
29
found that the PDF of
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
takes a form similar to that of Eq. (
24
), with parameters
\alpha\approx 1
and
\beta\approx 3.3
(
Konietzka distribution
), which fits well with their simulation data. However, since the Konietzka distribution
29
does not explicitly include the average
\langle\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle
, which encodes cosmological information, it is therefore incapable of using in FRB cosmology.

To address this problem, we assume that
P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(\Delta)
takes a form similar as Eq. (
24
), but
C_{0}
is fixed to 1,

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(\Delta)=A\Delta^{-\beta}\exp\left[-\dfrac{(\Delta^{-\alpha}-1)^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}^{2}}\right].

(25)

Since Konietzka et al.
29
found that
\alpha
and
\beta
taking values different from
\alpha=\beta=3
provide better fit, we treat
\alpha
and
\beta
as free parameters and determine their values using the continuous
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
catalog. In Eq. (
25
),
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
is also assumed to a free parameter, determined along with
\alpha
and
\beta
. To derive the values of
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
, we first calculate the
\langle\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle
from Eq. (
10
) for each redshift points, adopting
H_{0}=67.74~\mathrm{km/s\cdot Mpc^{-1}}
,
\Omega_{m0}=0.3089
,
\Omega_{b0}=0.0486
and
f_{eb}=f_{d}\cdot\chi_{e}=0.83
, which are consistent with the assumptions made in generating the continuous catalog in
29
. Next, we employ a MCMC process to determine
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
by fitting
P(\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}})
to the simulated
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
distribution. Here we emphasize that the PDF for
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
is given by
P(\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}})=\frac{1}{\langle\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle}P_{\mathrm{cosmic}}(\Delta)
as investigated in
82
;
85
. The obtained parameters values for each redshift point are listed in Table.
2
.
In Fig.
5
, we plot the variation of
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
with redshift. These parameters exhibit more significant variations at low redshift, while their evolution becomes flatter at higher redshifts. Therefore, we adopt finer redshift intervals at low
z
and coarser intervals at high
z
.

To evaluate the validity of
P(\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}})
proposed here, we apply the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test through using the
SciPy
python package
61
.
The KS test is defined as,

\displaystyle D_{n}(p)=\displaystyle\sup_{x}|F_{p}(x)-F_{n}(x)|,

(26)

where
F_{p}(x)
and
F_{n}(x)
are cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) corresponding to
P(\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}})
and the simulated
\mathrm{DM_{\mathrm{cosmic}}}
distribution, respectively. The KS statistic
D_{n}(p)
quantifies how far the samples deviate from the expected PDF. The KS test has a null hypothesis that the samples are drawn from
P(\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}})
. We reject the null hypothesis if the difference
D_{n}(p)
exceeds a critical value
D_{\mathrm{crit}}
, which can be calculated as
D_{\mathrm{crit}}\approx\frac{c(\tilde{\alpha})}{\sqrt{n}}
, where
\tilde{\alpha}
is the significance level that represents the probability of wrongly rejecting a true null hypothesis, and
n
is the number of samples. For a specific redshift point, the continuous catalog has 120000
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
samples, and we adopt
\tilde{\alpha}=0.05
by convention. Thus we obtain
c(\tilde{\alpha})=1.36
(from
Real Statistics Using Excel

1
1

1

https://real-statistics.com/statistics-tables/kolmogorov-smirnov-table/
), leading to
D_{\mathrm{crit}}\approx 0.0039
. After applying the KS test to
P(\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}})
with the parameters listed in Table.
2
, we plot the KS statistics for each redshift point in Fig.
6
. The mean value of the KS statistic is 0.0042, which slightly exceeds the
D_{\mathrm{crit}}
. We also include the KS statistics of the Macquart distribution
47
and the Konietzka distribution
29
in Fig.
6
. It is evident that the PDF in
29
performs better than the one in
47
, which is consistent with the finding reported in
29
. However, the KS statistics for both distributions clearly exceed
D_{\mathrm{crit}}
, indicating that they are inadequate for describing the distribution of
\mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}
. Furthermore, we find that the parameter
F
in Eq. (
24
) varies from
1.15
to
0.16
as the redshift changes from
0.1
to
3.0
, suggesting that
F
should not be treated as a constant. This implies that the relation
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}=Fz^{-1/2}
may be unsuitable.
