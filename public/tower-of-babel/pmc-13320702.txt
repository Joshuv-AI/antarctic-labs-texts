General practice care models and satisfaction in Australian residential aged care homes: a national survey of GPs, practice nurses, and managers
Authors: Kate H Marshall, Mary A Burns, Christopher J Poulos, Roslyn G Poulos, Andrew Montague, Ebony T Lewis, Russell Pearson, Joel J Rhee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResidential aged care homes (RACHs) in Australia support a growing population with complex healthcare needs. General practitioners (GPs), supported by practice nurses (PNs) and practice managers (PMs), play a central role providing medical care. Care models vary widely in these settings, and evidence on provider satisfaction and workforce sustainability is fragmented.ObjectiveTo describe general practice care models in Australian RACHs, and examine satisfaction and workforce intentions among GPs, PNs, and PMs.MethodsA national online survey of GPs, PNs, and PMs involved in RACH care was conducted between June and August 2023. Descriptive and bivariate analyses examined associations between demographic and practice characteristics, satisfaction across care elements, and GP intentions to continue working in aged care.ResultsOf 105 GP respondents, 87% were currently providing RACH care (58% aged 40–59 years; 53% female). Care was predominantly delivered through unstructured models (70%). Over half (53%) reported high satisfaction with their care model, while 59% were dissatisfied with remuneration; satisfaction did not differ by model type. Twenty-four PNs and 23 PMs participated, reporting high satisfaction with GP collaboration yet lower satisfaction with RACH staff relationships. Younger, less experienced GPs were more likely to plan to cease RACH work within one year (both P = .001, Cramér's V = 0.33–0.39), and metropolitan GPs were less likely to anticipate long-term involvement (P = .016, Cramér's V = 0.32).ConclusionGeneral practice remains central to aged care but faces persistent workforce and structural pressures. Addressing remuneration, workload, and cross-sector collaboration is critical to sustaining high-quality general practice in RACHs.

Introduction

Residential aged care homes (RACHs) are an important and resource-intensive component of Australia's aged care system, accommodating around 190 000 people in recent years, a number that has grown substantially over the past decade [1]. Residents typically present with multiple chronic, neurocognitive, and psychological conditions, creating complex care needs that require ongoing medical input from general practitioners (GPs) [2], alongside coordinated support from practice managers (PMs), administrative staff, and, in some cases, general practice nurses (PNs). General practice services are central to healthcare delivery in this setting; however, provision in Australia remains inconsistent and under pressure [3].

Persistent barriers continue to limit the provision of general practice care in RACHs, including inadequate funding, heavy administrative demands, difficulties accessing resident information, and challenges maintaining continuity of care [4–8]. National data also suggest declining GP participation, with only around 10% undertaking RACH work in the past month compared with substantially higher rates only a decade earlier [9, 10]. The Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety (hereafter the Royal Commission) highlighted these systemic shortfalls and called for more sustainable and coordinated models of primary medical care [9]. While a variety of models operate across Australia, ranging from continuity arrangements with community GPs to designated or provider-employed GPs and team-based approaches supported by nursing staff [11], evidence on their effectiveness is limited, and it remains unclear which approaches offer most benefit for residents and their families, or support sustainability [12].

The introduction of the Standards for General Practice Residential Aged Care, developed by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP), reflects growing recognition of the need for clearer structures, stronger integration, and greater support for general practice in aged care [13]. Despite these developments, limited evidence exists on how models of care are enacted in practice or how GPs define their role within them. Characterizing existing approaches is important to clarify how care is delivered, while understanding GP satisfaction is equally critical to identifying features that support quality and workforce sustainability [14]. Existing evidence on GP satisfaction related to models of care remains fragmented, focusing largely on discrete challenges such as remuneration or continuity [8], with little exploration of whether experiences differ across models or influence intentions to remain in the workforce [9]. Perspectives from PNs and PMs, who coordinate and support general practice care, are also largely absent from research. Together, these gaps constrain efforts to develop models that are both acceptable to providers and feasible within the aged care system.

This study aims to (i) describe the different models of general practice care operating within RACHs in Australia and their core features; (ii) determine GPs’, PNs’, and PMs’ reported satisfaction with these elements; (iii) examine whether GPs’ satisfaction varies by model type; and (iv) explore factors associated with GPs’ satisfaction and their intention to continue providing care in the RACH setting.

Materials and methods

A national, cross-sectional online survey of GPs, PNs, and PMs was conducted between June and August 2023. Eligible participants were aged ≥18 years, employed in general practice, and working in practices involved in the care of residents in RACHs. GPs were identified through the Australasian Medical Publishing Company (AMPCo) database, which listed 2740 GPs with an interest in geriatric medicine; AMPCo distributed email invitations to a random sample of 1000. PNs and PMs were identified through AMPCo's database of 658 practices listing geriatric medicine as a focus; AMPCo distributed fax invitations, including a QR code linking to the online survey, to all relevant practices. The survey was additionally promoted by the research team through Primary Health Networks (Australian regional organizations that support primary care), professional organizations (e.g. Australian Primary Health Care Nurses Association, Australian Association of Practice Management), and social media platforms, including Twitter and relevant Facebook groups (e.g. GPs Down Under and Doctors in Aged Care), supplemented by paid, targeted Facebook advertising. Information sheets and consent forms were accessible via the survey link, and participation was voluntary and implied by completion. The survey was administered using the Qualtrics platform and completed anonymously, with duplicate entries minimized using browser cookies. Respondents could optionally provide an email, stored separately, to enter a draw for 1 of 20 AU$100 VISA gift vouchers.

Questionnaire development was guided by a published scoping review [12] and relevant Australian primary care quality frameworks [15, 16] and was refined through team discussion. Content validity was assessed by GPs and primary care researchers within the research team. The resulting questionnaires were tailored to the roles of participants and included items on demographic and practice characteristics [e.g. age, sex, rurality (classified using the Modified Monash Model) [17], medical qualifications, experience, hours worked, practice accreditation, practice size, and intention to continue RACH care], as well as items on practice arrangements across nine domains: model of care, remuneration, routine care, accompanied visits, note-taking, prescribing, urgent issues (both during business hours and after hours), and leave arrangements. Satisfaction with these domains was measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Very unsatisfied” to “Very satisfied.” Free-text questions captured suggestions for improvement, though these data are not reported here. The average time to complete the questionnaire was 10–15 minutes. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (HC220807).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. GP satisfaction was categorized as high (satisfied/very satisfied ratings), neutral, or low (unsatisfied/very unsatisfied ratings). Models of care were classified as structured (scheduled, team-based, or GP employed directly by a RACH; n = 24, 30%) or unstructured (ongoing care following a patient's transition to a RACH or practices accepting all residents; n = 55, 70%). Bivariate analyses were conducted to test associations between model type and satisfaction with key care elements, as well as between participant (age, sex, rurality, registrar training, and GP experience) and practice (number of RACH patients, model type, remuneration, routine scheduling) characteristics and the primary outcomes of overall model satisfaction [low, neutral, and high; also examined in dichotomized form (high/neutral vs low)] and intention to continue in RACH care (≤1 year, 2–5 years, >5 years; also tested in sensitivity analyses as ≤5 years vs >5 years). Associations were examined using χ2 or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate. Both P-values and effect sizes (Cramér's V for larger tables, Φ for 2 × 2 tables) were reported to convey statistical and practical significance [18], with 0.05 used as a guideline rather than strict evidence of significance [19], and values of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 denoting small, medium and large effects [20]. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was made due to the exploratory design. Analyses were performed on complete cases, with denominators varying across outcomes. Ordinal logistic regression was considered but not retained because of small cell sizes and unstable estimates. Variables with limited variation (e.g. accompanied visits, prescribing arrangements) were excluded from inferential testing. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.x) [21].

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 105 GP respondents, 91 (87%) reported visiting RACHs (Table 1). Fourteen of 24 PNs (58%) and 19 of 23 PMs (83%) indicated that their practice was involved in aged care. Among GPs, more than half (58%) were aged 40–59 years, 53% were female and 74% had obtained their primary medical qualification in Australia. Most (82%) had >10 years’ general practice experience, 53% worked in metropolitan areas, 57% had cared for RACH patients during registrar training, and 25% had completed further aged care training. All PNs and PMs were female. PNs were most often aged <40 years (50%) with two-thirds (67%) having ≤10 years’ experience; almost all (92%) held a registered nurse qualification. PMs were most often aged 40–59 years (53%), with 41% reporting ≤10 years’ experience. Intended length of future RACH service varied, with 23% of GPs planning to cease within one year, 41% to continue for 1–5 years, 10% for 6–10 years, and 26% for >10 years.

General practice characteristics

Among GPs providing care in RACHs, the most common model involved continuing care for existing practice patients (36%). Other models included care of all residents within a facility or acceptance of new RACH patients (17%), scheduled care across a small number of RACHs (14%), team-based care including PNs (5%), and employment or contracting arrangements with an aged care provider (4%; Table 2). Most cared for ≤30 residents (57%). Remuneration was most commonly via contractor arrangements (40%) or direct patient billing (21%). Routine care was usually scheduled on set days or times (43%), and documentation was most often completed using practice (47%) or RACH (45%) clinical software. After-hours arrangements were typically managed by direct GP contact (38%) or deputizing/on-call services (26%), while leave was most often covered by GP colleagues (45%).

Among those working in RACHs, half of PNs were employed in practices with 1–4 GPs, while most PMs (56%) were in practices with 5–9 GPs (Supplementary Table S1). Care for RACH residents was often provided by a single GP (50% PN; 35% PM), although one-third of PMs reported ≥5 GPs were involved. The most common PN activities were triaging staff requests (50%), recalls and reminders (38%) and annual health assessments (25%), while PMs most often reported nurse contributions to annual health assessments (67%), care plan documentation (44%), and advance care planning (33%).

Satisfaction with elements of general practice in aged care

GPs’ satisfaction with aged care practice elements.

Over half of GPs (53%) expressed high satisfaction with their current model of care, while 24% reported low satisfaction. Most GPs (60%) were also highly satisfied with their approach to scheduling routine care, with 15% reporting low satisfaction. Remuneration arrangements received the lowest satisfaction ratings, with 59% of GPs indicating low satisfaction and 18% reporting high satisfaction. For documentation processes, 52% reported high satisfaction and 30% with low satisfaction. High satisfaction was also noted for processes related to the communication for urgent medical issues, both during and after-hours, among 55% of GPs and low satisfaction from 10%. Almost half of GPs (49%) were highly satisfied with leave cover arrangements, while 23% reported low satisfaction. Levels of satisfaction with specific elements of aged care practice did not differ significantly between GPs practicing either structured or unstructured models of care (all P > .05).

High satisfaction with nurse involvement in aged care was reported by 80% of PNs and 75% of PMs, while 13% of PMs reported low satisfaction (Supplementary Table S1). For remuneration related to PNs’ aged care work, 80% of PNs reported high satisfaction, compared with 17% of PMs, of whom 66% reported low satisfaction. For clinical note-taking processes, 40% of PNs reported high satisfaction and 20% low satisfaction; this item was not assessed for PMs. Working relationships with visiting GPs were rated most positively, with 85% of PNs reporting high satisfaction and none reporting low satisfaction; this item was not assessed for PMs. Relationships with RACH staff were more variable, with 28% of PNs and 50% of PMs reporting high satisfaction, and 43% of PNs and 36% of PMs reporting low satisfaction. For relationships with allied health professionals, 43% of PNs and 35% of PMs reported high satisfaction, while 14% of PNs and 29% of PMs reported low satisfaction.

Factors associated with GP satisfaction and intention to continue

Among GPs working in RACHs, 30% operated in structured models of care (scheduled care, team-based approaches, or employment by aged care providers) and 70% in unstructured models (continuing care for existing patients or accepting new residents; Table 2). No participant or practice characteristics were associated with satisfaction with model of care (Supplementary Table S2, n = 76; all P > .05, all Cramér's V = <0.20), and findings were unchanged when satisfaction was examined using dichotomized categories (high/neutral vs low). Intention to continue providing care to RACHs varied by age and general practice experience (Supplementary Table S3, n = 80); younger GPs were more likely to plan to cease within 1 year [P = .001, Cramér's V = 0.33 (medium effect)], and those with fewer years of practice experience were less likely to intend long-term involvement [P = .001, Cramér's V = 0.39 (medium effect)]. No significant associations were observed for sex, practice location, registrar training, model of care, or remuneration (all P > .05, Cramér's V = <0.20). Sensitivity analyses (≤5 vs >5 years) found rurality was also associated with intention to continue [χ2 = 8.28, df = 2, P = .016, Cramér's V = 0.32 (medium effect)], with metropolitan GPs less likely to report long-term involvement compared with those in regional or rural/remote areas.

Discussion

This study provides a national overview of general practice care in RACHs from the perspectives of GPs, PNs, and PMs. Most GPs surveyed reported working in unstructured models, such as continuing care for existing patients or accepting new residents on an ad hoc basis, while structured arrangements (e.g. scheduled or team-based care) were less common, mirroring broader workforce trends in Australian aged care [11]. Over half of GPs were satisfied with their current model of care, a higher proportion than previously reported among Australian GPs overall, where 38% expressed high job satisfaction [22]. Compared to other aspects of aged care practice, remuneration attracted the lowest satisfaction ratings, aligning with evidence that financial barriers remain a persistent challenge for GPs in RACHs [4–6, 8, 23]. This pattern also reflects international data, with only 17% of Australian GPs across all practice settings reporting high satisfaction with income, the lowest among comparable high-income countries (e.g. United Kingdom and Switzerland) [22]. Overall, our data indicate that while GPs expressed generally positive engagement with RACH care, ongoing dissatisfaction with remuneration underscores structural pressures that may compromise the sustainability of aged care practice.

Overall GP satisfaction did not differ between structured and unstructured models of care, suggesting that factors beyond model type may shape GPs’ experiences of RACH practice. Large-scale international studies echo this finding, showing that broader structural and organizational features of care, including how services are modeled or delivered, have less influence on GP satisfaction than workplace factors, such as administrative burden and workload [24–26]. Commonwealth Fund data similarly indicate that only 12% of Australian GPs were satisfied with their workload, with 94% reporting excessive administrative demands [22]. These findings suggest there is unlikely to be a “one-size-fits-all” approach to general practice in Australian aged care, with previous evidence further highlighting flexibility and workload balance as key determinants of GP satisfaction [27]. Additionally, GP intentions to remain in RACH care varied by age, experience, and location, with younger, less experienced, and metropolitan GPs less likely to anticipate long-term involvement, signaling potential risks to workforce sustainability in aged care. Comparable trends are seen in community general practice, where younger age predicts intention to leave [28–30]. Global evidence also shows greater dissatisfaction among middle-aged and urban-based GPs, whereas higher satisfaction among rural GPs, often linked to broader scope and task diversity [25, 31], may support retention; however, these patterns were not evident in this dataset. More broadly in Australia, nearly half (46%) of GPs report wanting to reduce their clinical hours [26], suggesting our findings may mirror wider workforce pressures influencing intentions to remain engaged in RACH care.

Across all participant groups, satisfaction was moderate overall but tended to be higher for direct, interpersonal aspects of care (e.g. communication in urgent situations, teamwork between GPs and PNs) and comparatively lower for elements linked to broader organizational or administrative systems (e.g. remuneration and engagement with RACH staff). For example, around half to two-thirds of GPs were highly satisfied with communication and scheduling but rated remuneration lowest, while PNs reported high satisfaction with teamwork and communication with visiting GPs but lower satisfaction with relationships with RACH staff. This pattern may reflect the quality of relationships among GPs, PNs, and PMs, as evidence suggests that when formal systems are limited, good communication, shared goals, and mutual trust become key to maintaining teamwork and satisfaction [32, 33]. Given most GPs in this study worked within unstructured models of care, effective coordination in these settings may rely more on these relational processes than on formalized systems. Yet, our data indicate opportunities to strengthen collaboration across organizational boundaries remain, particularly between PNs and RACH staff, where satisfaction was lower.

Strengths of this study include its national scope and inclusion of GPs, PNs, and PMs, providing a broad understanding of general practice care in RACHs. Comparison of structured and unstructured models offers novel insights into how organizational arrangements may influence satisfaction and sustainability. Several limitations should be noted. Cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, underscoring the need for longitudinal research to track how satisfaction and workforce outcomes respond to policy and remuneration reforms. Sample sizes for PNs and PMs were modest, limiting subgroup analyses, and the anonymous survey design prevented linking responses within practices. The GP sample was skewed toward mid-career practitioners, with few younger participants, potentially limiting representativeness across career stages. Participation may also have been influenced by non-response bias, as the overall response rate was relatively low and respondents may differ from clinicians less engaged in, or no longer providing, RACH care. If clinicians disengaged from aged care were less likely to respond, the sample may under-represent these perspectives and therefore underestimates workforce challenges in this setting. Not all respondents completed every survey item, resulting in some missing data across analyses; this may reflect survey burden or the variable relevance of items across participants, and analyses were conducted using available data for each outcome. Small cell sizes required the use of Fisher's exact test, which may have reduced power to detect moderate effects. Larger, more representative samples are needed to confirm these findings and strengthen subgroup analyses. Findings may be relevant to other universal healthcare settings; however, their generalizability to other countries is limited. Data were collected immediately prior to the introduction of new government incentives for GPs working in aged care [34]; future research should examine whether GP experiences change following their implementation. While satisfaction was examined across multiple domains, validated measures of job satisfaction and wellbeing were not included.

Conclusion

General practice remains central to medical care within RACHs, yet ongoing workforce and structural pressures threaten the sustainability of this role. Over half of GPs reported high satisfaction with their model of care, reflecting strong engagement and commitment to aged care practice; however, persistent dissatisfaction with remuneration and workload may continue to deter participation. Strong collaboration between GPs and PNs also emerged as a key strength, supporting coordinated and responsive care within practices. In contrast, weaker relationships with RACH staff point to the continuing challenges of communication and integration across organizational boundaries. Addressing these relational and structural barriers through strengthened funding arrangements, workforce support, and targeted training will be essential to maintain GP engagement and ensure sustainable, high-quality general practice in aged care.
