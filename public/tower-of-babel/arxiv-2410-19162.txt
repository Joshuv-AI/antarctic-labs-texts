Absorption of a twisted photon by an electron in strong magnetic field
A.A. Shchepkin, D.V. Grosman, I.I. Shkarupa, D.V. Karlovets

Abstract
The work investigates absorption of a twisted photon, which possesses quantized total angular momentum (TAM), by a relativistic electron with the Lorentz factor γ ∼ 1 − 10 \displaystyle\gamma\sim 1-10 in a strong magnetic field up to the Schwinger limit, H c = 4.4 ⋅ 10 13 \displaystyle H_{c}=4.4\cdot 10^{13} G. We examine the absorption cross sections and their dependence on the parameters of the incident photon and the initial Landau electron. It is found that total absorption cross sections decrease as angular momentum of the incident photon increases and increase as angular momentum of the initial electron grows. The process is also compared across different magnetic field strengths, and the contribution of various electron spin transitions to total absorption cross section is analyzed. We also find that the processes without an electron spin flip dominate and, on top of that, an asymmetry in the ‘‘spin-down’’ → \displaystyle\rightarrow ‘‘spin-up’’ and the ‘‘spin-up’’ → \displaystyle\rightarrow ‘‘spin-down’’ transitions is observed. Specifically, the cross sections for the ‘‘spin-down’’ → \displaystyle\rightarrow ‘‘spin-up’’ transition are larger, which can be interpreted as an analogy of the Sokolov-Ternov effect present for photon emission. Finally, the cross sections are found to be almost constant as the transverse momentum of the photon varies from 0.1 \displaystyle 0.1 eV to 100 \displaystyle 100 keV. Our findings can help to improve the understanding of the QED processes in critical fields, typical for astrophysical environments, e.g. magnetospheres of neutron stars.



Key words
: magnetic field, Landau state, twisted photon, absorption

2
Relativistic Landau states

In a constant and uniform magnetic field
\displaystyle\bm{H}=\{0,0,H\}
a charged particle (an electron, from hereon) is described by the Dirac equation

\left(\gamma^{\mu}(i\partial_{\mu}-e\mathcal{A}_{\mu})-m_{e}\right)\Psi(x)=0.

(1)

In the symmetric gauge, corresponding to the following vector potential of the magnetic field

\mathcal{A}^{\mu}=\frac{H}{2}\{0,-y,x,0\},

(2)

the solutions to (
1
) can be chosen in the from of the well known Landau states with a definite value of total angular momentum (TAM), comprising of spin and orbital contributions, which are described by Laguerre-Gaussian functions
[
32
]
. Alternatively, one can choose a different gauge for the vector potential
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\mu}=H\{0,0,y,0\}
and obtain a basis of solutions in the form of Hermite-Gaussian functions
[
33
]
, which are also commonly referred to as Landau states. In this work, we will use the Laguerre-Gaussian packets as we consider the initial electron to be possessing the TAM. Moreover, these states are normalized in both transverse directions (relative to the particle propagation axis) and allow for a simpler and clearer analysis of the TAM transfer. They can be constructed from the scalar wave functions that satisfy the following equation

\left[(i\partial_{\mu}-e\mathcal{A}_{\mu})^{2}-m_{e}^{2}+eH\Sigma_{z}\right]\Phi(x)=0,

(3)

where

\Sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{z}&0\\
0&\sigma_{z}\end{pmatrix}=\text{diag}\{1,-1,1,-1\}.

(4)

One can see
[
34
]
for the detailed derivation of (
3
). The solution to (
3
) can be presented as the following:

\Phi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\Phi_{+}(x)\\
c_{2}\Phi_{-}(x)\\
c_{3}\Phi_{+}(x)\\
c_{4}\Phi_{-}(x)\end{pmatrix},\;\;\Phi_{\pm}(x)=N_{s,j\mp 1/2}\psi_{s,j\mp 1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{-it\varepsilon_{s,j}+i(j\mp 1/2)\varphi+ip_{z}z},\;\;\psi_{s,l}(\tilde{\rho})=\tilde{\rho}^{|l|}L_{s}^{|l|}(\tilde{\rho}^{2})e^{-\tilde{\rho}^{2}/2}.

(5)

Here
\displaystyle s=0,1,2,...,j=\pm 1/2,\pm 3/2,...
are the radial quantum number of the electron and its TAM projection to the magnetic field respectively,
\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}=\rho/\rho_{H}
, where

\rho_{H}=\sqrt{2/|e|H}

(6)

is the coherence length of the ground Landau state. The energy of the electron in a Landau state is related to the quantum numbers via

\varepsilon_{s,j}^{2}=m_{e}^{2}+p_{z}^{2}+4/\rho_{H}^{2}(s+j+1/2),

(7)

where
\displaystyle p_{z}
is the longitudinal momentum of the electron. Now, to obtain the solution to the Dirac equation (
1
) we have to act on (
5
) with the projection operator

\Psi(x)=\left[\gamma^{\mu}(i\partial_{\mu}-e\mathcal{A}_{\mu})+m_{e}\right]\Phi(x).

(8)

The Dirac equation and the corresponding solutions account for the electron spinor degrees of freedom. However, spin and angular momentum separately are not proper quantum numbers in a relativistic theory as it is only the TAM that is a conserved quantity. Thus, there is an ambiguity as to how TAM is separated into spin and OAM constituents,

\hat{j}_{z}=\hat{l}_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\Sigma}_{z}.

(9)

The general solution (
8
) is an eigenfunction of the TAM operator, but depending on the coefficients
\displaystyle c_{i}
its spinor structure is different. Since the
discussion of spin-related phenomena is not the aim of this work (see for details
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
), let us choose two orthogonal spin states obtained by either setting
\displaystyle c_{i=1}=1,c_{i\neq 1}=0
or
\displaystyle c_{i=2}=1,c_{i\neq 2}=0
in (
5
). Such a choice is in accordance with the previous work
[
38
]
and also with
[
14
]
, where different solutions of the Dirac equation in a magnetic field were examined. The explicit expressions for the electron bispinors are

\Psi_{\uparrow}=N_{s,j-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}(\varepsilon+m_{e})\psi_{s,j-1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{-i\varphi/2}\\
0\\
p_{z}\psi_{s,j-1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{-i\varphi/2}\\
2\rho_{H}^{-1}i\psi_{s,j+1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{i\varphi/2}\end{pmatrix}e^{i(p_{z}z+j\varphi-\varepsilon t)},

(10)

\Psi_{\downarrow}=N_{s,j+1/2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
(\varepsilon+m_{e})\psi_{s,j+1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{i\varphi/2}\\
-2\rho_{H}^{-1}i\left(s+j+\frac{1}{2}\right)\psi_{s,j-1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{-i\varphi/2}\\
-p_{z}\psi_{s,j+1/2}(\tilde{\rho})e^{i\varphi/2}\end{pmatrix}e^{i(p_{z}z+j\varphi-\varepsilon t)}.

(11)

The indices
\displaystyle\uparrow
and
\displaystyle\downarrow
stand for the states where the electron spin
\displaystyle z
-projection,
\displaystyle\sigma_{z}
, equals
\displaystyle 1/2
and
\displaystyle-1/2
respectively. The normalization constants are determined from
\displaystyle\int\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi dV=1
and are obtained to be

N_{s,j\mp 1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{s!}{2\pi L(s+|j\mp 1/2|)!\varepsilon(\varepsilon+m_{e})\rho_{H}^{2}}},

(12)

with
\displaystyle L
being the normalization length. As mentioned before, the states (
10
), (
11
) posses a definite value of TAM, and in the limit of a weak magnetic field
\displaystyle H\ll H_{c}
they become the eigenstates of the Pauli spin operator

\hat{\bm{s}}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma},\;\;\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}),

(13)

where
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices, with the eigenvalues
\displaystyle\pm 1/2
. For this reason, we will refer to them as ‘‘spin-up’’ and ‘‘spin-down’’ states, respectively.

3
Vector potential of the vortex photon

Recently, it has been shown that an electron transition between different Landau states in a magnetic field results in the emission of a vortex photon having the form of a Bessel beam, which is described in detail in
[
10
]
. Such a photon state is characterized by a definite energy,
\displaystyle\omega
, longitudinal momentum,
\displaystyle k_{z}
, absolute value of the transverse momentum,
\displaystyle\kappa
, and a definite TAM,
\displaystyle m
, consisting of the orbital part and helicity. The vector potential of a Bessel photon can be represented as a superposition of plane waves, whose momenta lie on the surface of a cone with an opening angle
\displaystyle\tan\theta_{k}=\kappa/k_{z}

\bm{A}_{\kappa mk_{z}\Lambda}(\bm{r})=\int a_{\kappa mk_{z}}(\bm{q})\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\Lambda}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}-i\omega t}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}},\;\;a_{\kappa mk_{z}}(\bm{q})=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{\kappa}\delta(q_{\perp}-\kappa)\delta(q_{z}-k_{z})e^{im\varphi_{q}},

(14)

where
\displaystyle\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\Lambda}
is the polarization vector of a plane wave with momentum
\displaystyle\bm{k}
, which in a Coulomb gauge satisifes

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\Lambda}\cdot\bm{k}=0,\;\;\bm{e}_{\bm{k},\Lambda}\cdot\bm{e}^{*}_{\bm{k},\Lambda^{\prime}}=\delta_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}
. The explicit expression for the vector potential in a Coulomb gauge reads

\bm{A}_{\kappa mk_{z}\Lambda}(\bm{r})=\sqrt{\frac{\pi\kappa}{3\omega LR}}e^{ik_{z}z-i\omega t}\sum\limits_{\sigma=0,\pm 1}d_{\sigma,\lambda}^{(1)}(\theta_{k})e^{i(m-\sigma)\varphi_{r}}J_{m-\sigma}(\kappa\rho)\bm{\chi}_{\sigma}

(15)

Here
\displaystyle d^{(1)}_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(\theta_{k})
are the Wigner d-matrices
[
39
,
40
]
and

\bm{\chi}_{\pm}=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\pm i\\
0\end{pmatrix},\bm{\chi}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}

(16)

are the eigenvectors of the photon spin operator, corresponding to eigenvalues
\displaystyle\pm 1,0
respectively. The vector potential is normalized as follows:

\int\frac{\bm{E}^{2}+\bm{H}^{2}}{8\pi}dV=\omega.

(17)

The factor proportional to
\displaystyle 1/\sqrt{LR}
in (
15
) arises since the vector potential is normalized in a large but finite volume. Thus,
\displaystyle L,R
are the longitudinal and transverse scales of the quantization cylinder (the height and the radius of the cylinder base, respectively).

One of the quantum numbers of a twisted photon is
\displaystyle\Lambda=\pm 1
, often referred to as helicity. However, we should note that a twisted photon does not possess a definite value of momentum. Thus, the notion of helicity as of the photon spin projection onto the direction of its momentum loses meaningfulness. From (
14
) it is clear that
\displaystyle\Lambda
is the helicity of each plane wave in the decomposition of a twisted photon vector potential. However, different plane waves (with momenta
\displaystyle\bm{k}
and
\displaystyle\bm{k}^{\prime}
) in the decomposition propagate in different directions. From Eq.(
15
) we see that the vector potential of a twisted photon is a superposition of eigenstates of the
\displaystyle z
- projection spin operator

\hat{s}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

It possesses a spin projection onto the average propagation direction
\displaystyle\sigma=0,\pm 1
and OAM
\displaystyle m-\sigma
. The relative contribution of different spin-eigenstates with
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
to the vector potential of a twisted photon is proportional to
\displaystyle d_{\sigma,\Lambda}^{(1)}(\theta_{k})/d_{\sigma^{\prime},\Lambda}^{(1)}(\theta_{k})
. Therefore, for a given longitudinal momentum and energy of a twisted photon we can think of
\displaystyle\Lambda
as a quantity that determines the contribution of a spin-eigenstate to the vector potential. For example, for
\displaystyle\Lambda=1
it can be checked that
\displaystyle d_{1,\Lambda}^{(1)}(\theta_{k})/d_{-1,\Lambda}^{(1)}(\theta_{k})>1
. This can be seen even more clearly if we consider the vector potential (
15
) in the paraxial approximation (
\displaystyle k_{z}\gg\kappa)

\bm{A}_{\kappa mk_{z}\Lambda}(\bm{r})\propto J_{m-\Lambda}(\kappa\rho)e^{i(m-\Lambda)\varphi+ik_{z}z}\bm{\chi}_{\Lambda},

(19)

In (
19
) we can explicitly see that
\displaystyle\Lambda
describes the photon spin and simply determines the direction of the vector potential rotation in the transverse plane (relative to its propagation axis). The sign of helicity determines the direction of rotation with
\displaystyle\Lambda=1
corresponding to counterclockwise rotation and
\displaystyle\Lambda=-1
to clockwise rotation. Also, from the simple paraxial expression we can see that vortex photons with a given TAM
\displaystyle m
but different helicities have different spatial profiles (and, hence, energy distributions) as they are proportional to
\displaystyle J_{m\mp 1}^{2}(\kappa\rho)
. In particular, the first ring in the spatial energy distribution of a photon with
\displaystyle\Lambda=-1
will be located further from the photon propagation axis.

4
Matrix element of vortex photon absorption

Let us now obtain the S-matrix element for the absorption of a vortex photon by an electron in a Landau state. In the first order of perturbation theory the matrix element reads:

S^{(1)}_{fi}=\langle e_{f}|\hat{S}|e_{i},\gamma\rangle=-ie\int d^{4}xj^{\mu}_{fi}(x)A_{\mu}(x).

(20)

Here,
\displaystyle|e_{i},\gamma\rangle=|e_{i}\rangle\otimes|\gamma\rangle
is the vector describing the state of the initial electron and the incident photon,
\displaystyle|e_{f}\rangle
is the final state of the electron after the photon absorption,
\displaystyle A^{\mu}=(0,\bm{A})
is the incident photon vector potential (
15
). The transition current is given by:

j^{\mu}_{fi}(x)=\overline{\Psi}_{f}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\Psi_{i}(x),

(21)

with
\displaystyle\Psi_{i,f}(x)
being the corresponding wave functions of the initial and the final electron states, respectively, in the coordinate representation. The explicit expressions for the transition current are given in Appendix
A
.

For an electron transition between the Landau states with quantum numbers
\displaystyle s_{i},j_{i}
and
\displaystyle s_{f},j_{f}
for the ‘‘spin-up’’ initial and final electrons the
\displaystyle S
-matrix element is

S_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{(1)}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\kappa}{3\omega LR}}ieN_{i}N_{f}(2\pi)^{3}\rho_{H}\delta(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{f}+\omega)\delta(p_{zi}-p_{zf}+k_{z})\delta_{j_{f},j_{i}+m}\times\\
\times\Bigg[2\sqrt{2}\left((\varepsilon_{i}+m_{e})d^{(1)}_{-1\Lambda}(\theta_{k})\mathcal{F}_{s_{f},s_{i}}^{j_{f}+1/2,j_{i}-1/2}(\kappa\rho_{H})-(\varepsilon_{f}+m_{e})d_{1\Lambda}^{(1)}(\theta_{k})\mathcal{F}_{s_{f},s_{i}}^{j_{f}-1/2,j_{i}+1/2}(\kappa\rho_{H})\right)+\\
+\rho_{H}d^{(1)}_{0\Lambda}(\theta_{k})\left(m_{e}(p_{zi}+p_{zf})+p_{zf}\varepsilon_{i}+p_{zi}\varepsilon_{f}\right)\mathcal{F}_{s_{f},s_{i}}^{j_{f}-1/2,j_{i}-1/2}(\kappa\rho_{H})\Bigg],

(22)

where

\mathcal{F}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}_{s,s^{\prime}}(y)=\frac{(s^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime})!}{s!}\frac{1}{2^{2(s-s^{\prime})+\ell-\ell^{\prime}+1}}y^{2(s-s^{\prime})+\ell-\ell^{\prime}}L_{s^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime}}^{s-s^{\prime}+\ell-\ell^{\prime}}(y^{2}/4)L_{s^{\prime}}^{s-s^{\prime}}(y^{2}/4)e^{-y^{2}/4}.

(23)

The function (
23
) can be found in
[
41
]
. Notice, that this edition contains a typo:
\displaystyle m\Leftrightarrow n
. The corrected expression (
23
) is also provided in
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
. In (
22
)
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i},p_{zi}
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{f},p_{zf}
are the energy and the longitudinal momentum of the initial and the final electrons, respectively,
\displaystyle k_{z}
and
\displaystyle\kappa
refer to the longitudinal momentum and the absolute value of the transverse momentum of the absorbed photon, respectively. For the
\displaystyle S
-matrix elements with other configurations of the initial and the final electron spins one is referred to Appendix
B
.

Notice that the function
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s,s^{\prime}}^{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(y)
and therefore the entire
\displaystyle S
-matrix includes an exponentially decreasing factor. The same expressions (with the only difference being the complex conjugation of
\displaystyle A^{\mu}
) are obtained for photon emission by an electron in a strong magnetic field
[
13
,
14
]
. This indicates that the probabilities of absorbing (and emitting) a vortex photon include a damping factor

|S_{fi}^{(1)}|^{2}\propto\exp(-\frac{(\kappa/\kappa_c)^2}{2}),\;\;\kappa_{c}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\rho_{H}}=m_{e}\sqrt{\frac{H}{H_{c}}}

(24)

and are larger for smaller absolute values of the photon transverse momentum,
\displaystyle\kappa\ll\kappa_{c}
. The critical value
\displaystyle\kappa_{c}
is of the order
\displaystyle 100\,\text{keV}-1\,\text{MeV}
for
\displaystyle H\sim(0.01-1)\,H_{c}

5
Kinematic requirements

Let us now briefly discuss the kinematic properties of the absorption process. The
\displaystyle\delta
-functions and the Kronecker symbol in (
22
) reflect the conservation laws:

\varepsilon_{f}=\varepsilon_{i}+\omega,

(25)

p_{zf}=p_{zi}+k_{z},

(26)

j_{f}=j_{i}+m.

(27)

For a transition between the electron quantum states characterized by quantum numbers
\displaystyle s_{i},j_{i}\rightarrow s_{f},j_{f}
with a given energy and longitudinal momentum of the incident photon
\displaystyle\omega,k_{z}
we can solve the equations (
25
) - (
27
) for the longitudinal momentum of the initial electron. We find it to be

p_{zi}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\left[k_{z}\zeta_{s_{f}s_{i}m}\pm\omega\sqrt{\zeta_{s_{f}s_{i}m}^{2}-4\kappa^{2}\left[m_{e}^{2}+\frac{4}{\rho_{H}^{2}}\left(s_{i}+j_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]}\right],

(28)

where

\zeta_{s_{f},s_{i},m}=4\rho_{H}^{-2}(s_{f}-s_{i}+m)-\kappa^{2}.

(29)

There are two possible solutions, such that
\displaystyle p_{zi}^{+}>k_{z}
and
\displaystyle p_{zi}^{-}<k_{z}
. From this simple consideration it follows that absorption cross sections as functions of the longitudinal momentum of the initial electron should represent a discrete spectrum. This is reasonable because the transverse energy of the electron states is quantized and the conservation laws (
25
) can not be satisfied for an arbitrary electron longitudinal momentum. However, this is not the case for the photon emission problems, where the photon energy is not a fixed parameter, but is determined by the transition energy
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{f}-\varepsilon_{i}
. Alternatively, one could consider the initial momentum of the electron to be fixed and obtain a restriction on the photon energy that would allow absorption accompanied by the electron transition
\displaystyle s_{i},j_{i}\rightarrow s_{f},j_{f}
. In the reference frame corresponding to
\displaystyle p_{zi}=0
(from hereon referred to as the electron ‘‘rest frame’’), we find the photon energy required for absorption to be

\omega^{(a)}=(s_{f}-s_{i}+m)\omega_{c}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{i}}=\frac{2(s_{f}-s_{i}+m)\omega_{c}}{\sqrt{1+\displaystyle\frac{2\omega_{c}\sin^{2}\theta_{k}}{\varepsilon_{i}}(s_{f}-s_{i}+m)}+1},

(30)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{c}=eH/\varepsilon_{i}
is the cyclotron frequency and
\displaystyle\theta_{k}
is the conical angle of the incident photon. We can see that
\displaystyle\omega^{(a)}
are not exact multiples of the cyclotron frequency due to the electron recoil caused by the photon non-zero transverse momentum (see, e.g.,
[
31
]
). However, as the incident photon becomes more delocalized, the recoil decreases. In the limit
\displaystyle\theta_{k}\rightarrow 0
, the frequency
\displaystyle\omega^{(a)}
approaches
\displaystyle N\omega_{c}
, where
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
.

6
Results

We aim to analyze absorption cross sections for various parameters such as the TAM of the initial electron,
\displaystyle j_{i}
, the TAM of the photon,
\displaystyle m
, the transverse photon momentum,
\displaystyle\kappa
, and others. It should be noted that the cross section here is generalized to the states of the initial electron and the incident photon possessing no fixed value of their momenta (see Sec.
C
in Appendix for detailed information). We will focus on two specific quantities. The first one is the absorption cross section summed over the spin states of the final electron,
\displaystyle\sigma_{z}
, and averaged over the incident photon helicity,
\displaystyle\Lambda
,

\sigma_{\text{tot}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\Lambda=\pm 1}\sum\limits_{\sigma_{zf}=\pm 1/2}\int d\sigma.

(31)

The second one is the absorption cross section only averaged over the incident photon helicity,

\sigma_{\text{spin}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\Lambda=\pm 1}\int d\sigma.

(32)

Here
\displaystyle d\sigma
is defined by (
63
). Notice, that in a set up with the given longitudinal momentum of the initial electron and the given energy and longitudinal momentum of the incident photon the quantum numbers of the final electron,
\displaystyle s_{f},j_{f},p_{zf}
, are fixed by the conservation laws (
25
)-(
27
). Therefore,
\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{tot}}
in Eq.(
31
) represents total absorption cross section. Alternatively, the quantity
\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{spin}}
(
32
) allows us to compare absorption processes involving different spin transitions and analyze their contribution to total cross section. In this work we primarily focus on how TAM of the particles affects absorption processes and are not interested in polarization related effects. On top of that polarization resolved measurements are often challenging not only in astrophysical observations, but also in terrestrial experiments. Therefore, we take averages over
\displaystyle\Lambda
in Eq.(
31
),(
32
).

Since the cross section is a Lorentz invariant quantity
[
42
,
43
]
, it is convenient to analyze it in the ‘‘rest frame’’ of the initial electron. The energy of the incident photon is then determined by the conservation laws and changes for various parameters such as the TAM of the initial electron and the incident photon. For the parameters we use below the photon energy lies in the range
\displaystyle 10\,\text{keV}-10\,\text{MeV}
, which corresponds to hard X-rays and gamma rays.

In Fig.
1
we investigate the dependence of total absorption cross section
\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{tot}}
on the transverse momentum of the incident photon. From hereon, cross sections are expressed in barns (
\displaystyle 1\,\text{b}=10^{-24}\,\text{cm}^{-2}
). As discussed earlier in Sec.
4
the matrix elements inclide an exponential damping factor
\displaystyle\exp( - \kappa^2 / (2\kappa_{\text{c}}^2))
. Therefore, the emission and the absorption of photons with
\displaystyle\kappa\gtrsim\kappa_{\text{c}}
(0.511 MeV for
\displaystyle H\sim H_{\text{c}}
) is suppressed. For smaller values of
\displaystyle\kappa
absorption cross sections do not depend on
\displaystyle\kappa
. Note that although cross sections in Fig.
1
are given only for
\displaystyle H=H_{c}
their behaviour is also the same for
\displaystyle H<H_{c}
. Cross sections are also influenced by the other parameters, increasing when TAM of the incident photon is lower and the TAM of the initial electron is higher. For large radial quantum numbers (
\displaystyle s\geq 20
) the cross sections for the same photon TAM group together with the separation between these groups growing as
\displaystyle s
increases.

7
Conclusion and outlook

In this work we have addressed the problem of a twisted photon absorption by an electron in a strong magnetic field. The absorption cross sections have been studied and their dependence on the quantum numbers of the initial particles has been analyzed. It has been found that for a given TAM of the initial electron and incident photon absorption cross sections remain independent on the absolute value of the incident photon transverse momentum,
\displaystyle\kappa
, as it varies from
\displaystyle 0.1
eV to
\displaystyle 100
keV. However, for the larger values,
\displaystyle\kappa\gtrsim m_{e}\sqrt{H/H_{c}}
, absorption cross section is exponentially suppressed. On top of that cross sections corresponding to larger radial quantum numbers and the same incident photon TAM begin to group into distinct bunches.

Next, it has been shown that total absorption cross sections grow for larger values of the TAM of the initial electron and decrease for larger values of the TAM of the incident photon. In the critical magnetic field (
\displaystyle H\sim H_{c}
) the cross section of the absorption of a photon with
\displaystyle m=1
exceeds the Thompson scattering cross section (
\displaystyle\sigma_{T}\sim 1
b) by
\displaystyle 1-2
orders. It has also been noted that as the magnetic field strength increases, the difference between the cross sections for opposite spin states of the initial electron disappears, though the overall values of the cross sections remain largely unchanged.

Next, we have analyzed the absorption of a photon for given spin states of the initial and final electrons and compared the cross sections for different spin transitions. First, the cross sections for processes where the electron’s spin remains the same (no spin flip) are several orders of magnitude higher than those where the electron’s spin changes. On top of that, there is much less difference in the absorption cross sections without electron spin transition (‘‘spin-up’’
\displaystyle\rightarrow
‘‘spin-up’’ and ‘‘spin-down’’
\displaystyle\rightarrow
‘‘spin-down’’) compared to those for the absorption with a spin flip (‘‘spin-down’’
\displaystyle\rightarrow
‘‘spin-up’’ and ‘‘spin-up’’
\displaystyle\rightarrow
‘‘spin-down’’). It has been shown that spin-flip cross sections are always larger for the ‘‘spin-down’’
\displaystyle\rightarrow
‘‘spin-up’’ transition, which could be interpreted as the analogy of the Sokolov-Ternov effect present in photon emission
[
35
,
44
]
. It has also been found that the cross section for the reverse ‘‘spin-up’’
\displaystyle\rightarrow
‘‘spin-down’’ transition sharply decreases at certain values of the initial electron TAM.

We believe that studying the absorption of twisted photons by electrons in a strong magnetic field could help improve our understanding of radiative transport in astrophysical environments. Unlike plane-wave photons, twisted photons can excite electrons to states with higher angular momentum. The emission and absorption of twisted photons by electrons could play a key role in processes occurring in neutron star magnetospheres, kilonovae, jets of active galactic nuclei and others. Additionally, accounting for these processes could help explain certain features in the spectra and light curves of objects with pulsed emission, such as X-ray transients and soft gamma repeaters (see, e.g.,
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
).

There could also be the several following extensions to this research. First, one could look into the absorption of a photon approaching an electron at a specific impact parameter and further investigate an absorption by a localized ensemble of electrons described by a specific distribution function. Moreover, it could be of interest to study absorption cross sections in a different gauge of the magnetic field vector potential, namely, the Hermite-Gaussian solutions. Also, notice that the absorption of the photon by an electron in a magnetic field is only the specific (resonant) case of the broader process of the electron-photon scattering (Compton effect). Therefore, the analogous treatment for the Compton effect stimulated by a magnetic field and accounting for a photon vorticity also takes place for the future works.
