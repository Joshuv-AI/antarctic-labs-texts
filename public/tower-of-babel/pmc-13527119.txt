Impact attribution of anthropogenic forcing on lake surface temperature
Authors: Xiwen Wang, Kun Shi, Boqiang Qin, R. Iestyn Woolway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global lakes are important sources of freshwater, supporting biodiversity and providing vital ecosystem services. While widespread lake surface warming has been well-documented, formal attribution of this trend to specific anthropogenic drivers has remained out of reach. Here, we provide the attribution of global lake surface warming to human activities using a dataset that integrates satellite-constrained lake temperature estimates with climate model simulations. From 1950 to 2020, we find that the observed 0.47 °C increase in lake surface temperature is primarily driven by greenhouse gas emissions, which contributed a warming trend of 0.95 °C, partially offset by aerosol-induced cooling of −0.28 °C. A dominant greenhouse-gas fingerprint is consistently detected across 23 of 44 studied regions, with regional variations reflecting the influence of other anthropogenic and natural forcings. Our findings provide the direct evidence of global lake surface warming induced by greenhouse-gas emissions, offering scientific foundations for climate accountability and future adaptation in a warming world.

Introduction

There is clear evidence that human influence has warmed the Earth’s atmosphere, oceans, and land, driving widespread and rapid changes across the Earth system1. A large body of detection and attribution research has established that anthropogenic forcings, primarily greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, have altered the global climate1. Yet, a critical gap remains in understanding the human fingerprint in freshwater lake systems, which comprise millions of water bodies and represent an essential component of the terrestrial hydrosphere. Lakes are sentinels of climate change and cornerstones of societal and ecological well-being2. They provide freshwater for drinking, agriculture, and industry; support aquatic biodiversity; and regulate regional climates3–7. Given their outsized importance, understanding how lakes respond to external climate forcings is vital for securing water resources and preserving ecosystem integrity under accelerating climate change.

Global observations show that lake surface temperatures are rising8,9. This warming drives a cascade of ecological consequences in lakes10; it strengthens thermal stratification, reduces oxygen solubility, inhibits oxygen replenishment in deep waters11–13, and facilitates the spread of harmful algal blooms, including toxin-producing cyanobacteria14–17. These changes are already degrading water quality, disrupting food webs, and causing fish die-offs, with especially severe implications for communities dependent on lakes for food and livelihoods10,18,19.

To manage and mitigate these risks, it is no longer sufficient to monitor change, we must attribute it. Specifically, we need to detect whether observed lake warming exceeds natural variability—that the occurrence of this change is unlikely due to internal variability by chance, and then attribute these changes to their causal drivers. Attributions seek to evaluate the relative contributions of multiple factors, including natural (e.g., volcanic activity, solar variability) and anthropogenic (e.g., GHG, aerosols, land use change) forcings1. A foundational study demonstrated that recent lake changes could not be explained by pre-industrial variability alone, successfully detecting a combined signal from all external forcings20. However, formal attribution to specific human drivers remained out of reach due to two major limitations: (1) the absence of a global, observationally constrained lake temperature dataset spanning multiple decades20, and (2) the lack of simulated lake temperature responses to individual climate forcings, the so-called “fingerprints” necessary for attribution.

Climate modeling initiatives such as the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP)21 have long provided such fingerprints for the atmosphere, land, or oceans – but not for lakes. While the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project (ISIMIP) has advanced lake model applications using bias-adjusted and statistically downscaled (BASD) CMIP6 data20,22,23, it does not include the single-forcing experiments required for a formal detection and attribution analysis. As a result, it has not been possible to quantify how much of observed lake warming is specifically due to GHGs versus other influences, a critical omission for both science and policy.

In addition to its scientific significance, attributing lake surface warming to anthropogenic forcing carries important legal and ethical implications. On July 23, 2025, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) issued a landmark advisory opinion affirming that states may bear international responsibility for inadequate action on climate change, and recognized a healthy environment as a fundamental human right. While not legally binding, the opinion has been widely interpreted as a pivotal moment for climate justice, offering a framework that could support future state-to-state litigation and claims for reparations. By providing system-specific, quantitative evidence of human-induced warming in lake ecosystems, this study contributes to the growing body of knowledge needed to support accountability and redress for nations disproportionately affected by climate change.

Here, we present a global-scale impact attribution analysis of lake surface warming, focusing specifically on the pathway through which anthropogenic forcing influences lake thermal states via surface air temperature. This approach aligns with the consensus that surface air temperature is the primary driver of lake surface temperature changes at global scales24. We generate a satellite-constrained reconstruction of lake surface temperature for ~600,000 lakes worldwide (Supplementary Fig. 1) from 1950 to 2020 (hereinafter referred as OBSrecon), offering an observational proxy for attribution. Leveraging this dataset alongside BASD climate data from the Detection and Attribution Model Intercomparison Project (DAMIP)25 and Land Use Model Intercomparison Project (LUMIP)26 within CMIP6, we generate a complete set of fingerprints for lake surface temperature, quantifying the responses to GHG, aerosols (AER), land-use and land cover change (LULCC), and natural (NAT) forcings. Using formal multi-signal optimal fingerprinting, we quantify the contributions of anthropogenic forcings to observed lake surface warming at global and regional scales, providing a comprehensive assessment of human influence on global lake temperature.

Results

Historical lake surface temperature changes

We further evaluate the reconstructed trends by comparing summer lake surface temperature (July–September for the Northern Hemisphere and January–March for the Southern Hemisphere) trends derived from OBSrecon with satellite-based datasets9,27 at matched lake locations (Supplementary Fig. 2). The results show that OBSrecon captures the long-term warming signals consistent with observations from moderate resolution satellites; for example, over the period 2001–2024, the summer warming rate is 0.25 °C decade−1 in OBSrecon, closely matching the 0.26 °C decade−1 derived from MODIS observations9. In comparison, the ALL-forcing simulations exhibit reduced interannual variations but retain the overall warming tendency. In addition, global lake surface warming rates during summer exhibit clear spatial variations with latitude and lake characteristics, with enhanced warming at mid- to high latitudes (Supplementary Fig. 3), consistent with previous studies28.

To quantify the lake response to atmospheric forcing, we calculated the thermal sensitivity, defined as the ratio of lake warming to air warming trends (Fig. 2a, b). Globally, the average thermal sensitivity is less than 1.0, reflecting that air warming generally outpaces lake surface warming. The thermal sensitivity during summer is higher than that throughout the year, which is consistent with previous research29. This is most likely due to the heat exchange being concentrated within a shallower mixed layer during summer stratification, making the surface temperature more responsive to atmospheric forcing30. As a global average, air temperature warmed at a rate of 0.20 °C decade for the full year, suggesting a thermal sensitivity of 0.41. During the summer season, the warming rates for lake surface and air temperature increased to 0.11 and 0.16 °C decade−1, respectively. The notably higher thermal sensitivity during summer (0.67) suggests that lakes track atmospheric changes more closely during the stratified period. This estimate is broadly consistent with previous results based on surface energy budget, which suggests a thermal sensitivity of 70–85% during the open-water season24. While summer thermal sensitivity decreases with depth and remains higher overall, full-year sensitivity exhibits a non-monotonic relationship with depth, peaking at 25–50 m (Fig. 2c). The OBSrecon exhibit a wide range of change rates across the studied lake grids, from a distinct cooling along the southern flank of the Himalayas (−0.13 °C decade−1) to intense warming in some hotspot regions (0.55 °C decade−1). The MME reproduces the rapid warming in central and western Europe, but overestimates the change rates in southern North America, northern South America, and southern Africa, which contributes to the positive bias in the global-averaged change rate. In addition to this temporal evolution, the MME also depicts the large-scale pattern in climatological mean lake surface temperature with high accuracy, achieving a spatial correlation coefficient (weighted by grid cell area) of 0.99 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Global lake surface temperatures have increased most strongly under GHG forcing, with minimal trends under natural forcing (Fig. 1c). The ALL simulations successfully reproduce the reconstructed temporal changes, including both the long-term warming and the interannual variability in lake surface temperature. The GHG simulations generate a monotonic warming trajectory that aligns with OBSrecon post-1970. In contrast, the AER simulations generate a cooling trajectory that persisted until the late 20th century. The NAT simulations show no significant long-term change but introduce decadal variability; for instance, the pronounced cooling effect of the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption, a feature also evident in the OBSrecon. The NOLU simulation (an all-but-land-use-change scenario) resembles the reconstructed observed record, particularly during the post-1970s warming period. The spatial patterns of these trends are broadly consistent with the global means (Supplementary Figs 5–8). GHG and NOLU simulations produce widespread warming across nearly all regions, whereas AER simulations exhibit widespread cooling with the most pronounced rates over East Asia. In contrast, NAT simulations display a mixed pattern of weak regional warming and cooling.

Discerning the anthropogenic fingerprint

To determine whether the observed lake surface warming is attributable to specific external forcings, we conducted a detection and attribution analyses globally and across 44 regions predefined in the Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)31 (Supplementary Table 1). For these analyses, we used the Regularized Optimal Fingerprinting (ROF) method32, which is based on the generalized linear regression model, in a one-, two-, and three-signal framework to separate the influences of different drivers (see “Methods”), where the regression coefficients (scaling factors) quantify the amplitude of the simulated response required to match the observations. Values close to 1 indicate consistency between simulated and observed responses, values significantly larger (smaller) than 1 suggest underestimation (overestimation) by the model, and values not significantly different from zero indicate that the signal is not robustly detected. We further performed sensitivity experiments to assess the robustness of these results to observational and model uncertainties (see “Methods”). In the following, we report the baseline estimates and whether they remain robust under these perturbations.

In the one-signal analysis, the scaling factor—that is, the regression coefficient for the response to the ALL forcing—is 0.78 [0.61, 0.95] (brackets indicate 95% confidence intervals), representing the best estimate and its uncertainty (Fig. 3). This scaling factor and its confidence intervals exclude zero, indicating a detectable influence of the ALL forcing. However, it is less than unity, suggesting that the MME overestimates the magnitude of observed warming. This conclusion is robust to uncertainties in both OBSrecon and model fingerprints. One-way regression models for GHG, AER, and NAT signals all fail the residual consistency test (RCT; p < 0.1; see “Methods”), suggesting that a single forcing is insufficient to explain the observed changes. These findings point to the necessity of decomposing potentially confounding drivers within the combined signal. Notably, our results are consistent with a previous study using an ensemble of one-dimensional process-based lake models, which also reported a global-scale scaling factor for the ALL forcing below unity20. This inter-model consistency provides a robust basis for our subsequent multi-signal attribution analysis.

In contrast of the global result, a robust (defined as the regression model passes the RCT and robustness under observational and fingerprint uncertainties) attribution of the ALL signal is achieved in 33 regions (Supplementary Figs. 9–10). These regions are mainly located in the mid-high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere, as well as in Oceania, Central America, and several parts of Southern Africa and Southern America. In most of these regions (31 out of 33), the best-estimate scaling factor is below one. One-signal analyses for GHG, AER, and NAT demonstrate the dominant role of GHG emissions, with robust attribution to GHG forcing alone achieved in 18 regions (Supplementary Figs. 11–13). Nevertheless, the GHG-only analysis fails the RCT in many regions, while the AER and NAT analysis are statistically robust in even fewer regions. This insufficiency of any individual forcing suggests that a multi-signal analysis is required to properly attribute the observed changes.

A two-signal analysis separating the ANT and NAT forcings clarifies the source of the lake surface warming (Fig. 3). The ANT signal is robustly detected with a scaling factor of 0.83 [0.65, 1.02], which is consistent with unity after separating the ANT signal from the NAT forcing component. In contrast, the NAT signal cannot be detected. These results suggest that global lake surface warming in past decades is attributable to anthropogenic factors.

The regional two-signal analysis distinguishes (where the regression model passes the RCT and at least one forcing is detectable) between ANT and NAT signals in 31 regions (Supplementary Fig. 14). In most of these regions (n = 24), changes in reconstructed lake surface temperature are robustly and solely attributed to the ANT forcing (Supplementary Figs. 14a and 15), indicating a predominantly ANT fingerprint. The analysis reveals exceptions where the NAT forcing plays an important role, such as in Western Central Asia and Western Siberia, but their attribution claims are questionable. The best-estimate scaling factors in these regions are exceptionally large, a discrepancy that suggests a severe underestimation of the NAT-forced response in the models or to unresolved confounding factors, rather than a physically plausible attribution1.

A three-signal analysis that simultaneously regresses the OBSrecon onto GHG, AER, and NAT simulations further disentangles the components of the ANT signal (Fig. 4). The warming effect of GHG is robustly attributed with a scaling factor of 0.95 [0.75, 1.17]. The cooling effect of AER is also detected and consistent with unity (1.15 [0.55, 1.84]). The fact that the AER scaling factor is greater than one suggests that the MME likely underestimates the magnitude of aerosol-induced cooling. This underestimation of the cooling component is possibly the reason that the ALL-signal analysis overestimates lake surface warming. This bias may be related to the inability of the models to fully represent radiative changes associated with aerosols, which can directly modulate surface cooling. Attributable trends

To quantify the contribution of different forcings to the reconstructed observed warming, we calculated attributable trends by first determining the total change from each forcing (change rate multiplies by period length) and then scaling this value by its corresponding optimal scaling factor, which is derived from the most comprehensive multi-signal analysis available for each forcing (Fig. 7). The results demonstrate that the global lake surface warming of 0.47 °C in the OBSrecon during the 1950–2020 period is dominated by the ANT forcing, which contributes a warming of 0.71 [0.56, 0.87] °C (ranges represent the 95% confidence interval derived from the scaling factor in the ROF analysis). At the regional scale, the results of attributable trends confirm that the widespread ANT-induced lake surface warming is dominated by a strong GHG-induced warming in most of the studied regions (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 17). The attributable lake surface warming from GHGs ranges from 0.39 to 1.59 °C. The attributable trends from other signals indicate considerable regional complexity, with both promoting and offsetting effects on GHG-induced warming (Supplementary Fig. 17). The influence of AER is highly variable, with attributable trends ranging from −0.08 °C to −1.32 °C in the nine regions with a detectable signal. Similarly, NAT-attributable trends range from −0.24 to 0.01 °C across three regions.

As a comparison, the GHG-attributable air temperature trend over the same period is 1.87 [1.33, 2.49] °C, suggesting that only about 50% of the GHG-induced atmospheric warming is transferred to lake surface temperatures (Fig. 8). Note that these estimates may differ substantially from those reported in climate attribution studies, as we use the ERA5-Land reanalysis—providing the meteorological forcing for the lake simulations—as the observational reference in the detection and attribution analysis. In addition, our analysis is restricted to grid cells containing the studied lakes. These choices are intended to ensure a consistent and direct comparison between air and lake temperature responses. At the regional scale, in most regions the GHG-induced lake warming is smaller than the corresponding air temperature warming. Discussion

Our study attributes global lake warming over the past seven decades to anthropogenic forcing, which contributed a warming of 0.71 °C. This ANT-driven warming is primarily explained by a stronger warming of 0.95 °C from GHG, partially offset by a cooling effect from AER. At the regional level, the GHG fingerprint is clear, though in certain regions its warming effect is accompanied by other drivers, including moderate negative contributions from the AER forcing in mid-latitude Northern Hemisphere (e.g., Mediterranean), as well as from the NAT forcing in South Asia and LULCC in Southeastern Africa.

Our approach of using a satellite-constrained lake model with a comprehensive fingerprinting dataset reduces key uncertainties relative to previous work and offers insights into the drivers of lake warming. We acknowledge, however, two simplifications in the modeling framework. A key limitation of the air2water model lies in its reliance on air temperature as the sole explicit meteorological forcing. While the calibration against historical observations allows model parameters to implicitly capture the combined effects of multiple drivers, such as trends in wind speed and solar radiation, which are crucial elements of the lake surface energy budget and drivers of lake temperature variation28,30,35–37, this empirical relationship assumes stationarity under changing forcing conditions. This assumption may be violated in single-forcing experiments (e.g., hist-GHG and hist-aer), where the balance among physical processes is altered. Specifically, if the air–lake temperature sensitivity learned from historical data implicitly absorbs the effects of historical wind stilling38,39 or global dimming/brightening40–43, this relationship may be misapplied in scenarios where these variables decouple from air temperature, potentially inflating or masking the inferred thermal response to specific forcings.

Despite these theoretical risks, global sensitivity analyses based on the energy balance equation suggest that air temperature remains the primary driver of lake surface temperature trends24. The combined influence of other meteorological variables is estimated to offset the air-temperature-driven warming by ~10%, though the effects of other variables can be equally important at the local scale. This scale-dependency is supported by results of RCT; while the attribution is robust at the global level, it is frequently not satisfied at regional scales. Such failures likely reflect the heightened importance of unresolved processes at smaller spatial scales and for specific lakes, which leads to a localized mismatch between the reconstructed observations (OBSrecon) and the fingerprints. This suggests that while structural model biases may influence the absolute magnitude of attributed trends, they are unlikely to fundamentally alter the identification of the dominant anthropogenic signal at the global scale. However, we acknowledge that at the regional scale, where trends in other climate variables are more pronounced, the uncertainty in both the direction and magnitude of the attributed response may increase.

Beyond the issue of non-stationary between climate variables, an additional methodological consideration arises from the use of a shared structural framework of the reconstructed observations (OBSrecon) and the simulated fingerprints. Because both datasets are generated using the air2water model, the scaling factors derived from ROF should be interpreted as compensating for both GCM and lake model structure biases. While this inevitably reduces the physical interpretability of the scaling factors, the primary quantity of interest in attribution remains the magnitude of warming trend attributable to external forcings. As long as the large-scale spatiotemporal evolution of the signal is correctly captured, the ROF framework yields a robust estimate of attributable change, even if the scaling factor absorbs contributions from multiple sources of structural uncertainty. Future work could benefit from incorporating multiple lake models to construct multi-model fingerprints, which may help further constrain attribution estimates and provide an additional perspective on structural uncertainty.

Second, the lake model does not resolve the temporal evolution of lake-specific properties that may affect water temperature, such as changes in water transparency and inflows30,44–46. Long-term changes in water clarity can alter the vertical distribution of solar energy absorption, thereby amplifying or dampening changes in surface temperature47. Similarly, fluctuations in water level and volume48 can modify a lake’s thermal inertia. Despite this, existing numerical evidence suggests that the omission of time-dependent light attenuation has a limited effect on the model performance when simulating surface water temperature49,50. Our parameter sensitivity experiments further indicate that global-scale attribution remains robust to moderate changes in lake thermal characteristics. Moreover, the optimal fingerprinting method is inherently designed to distinguish large-scale, externally forced signals from localized or transient noise. While the convergence of atmospheric warming with significant shifts in water level or clarity may increase uncertainty at specific lakes, these processes are unlikely to fundamentally alter or obscure the dominant anthropogenic signal at the global scale. Future research integrating long-term satellite-derived lake characteristics into physical modeling frameworks will be essential for refining attribution at the level of individual lakes.

Our quantitative attribution of GHG contributions establishes a direct physical link between human emissions and changes in thermal states in global lakes. Given that lake surface warming is a primary regulator of freshwater ecosystem health10, these findings highlight the critical necessity of integrating lake responses into global climate mitigation and adaptation policies. Identifying an anthropogenic fingerprint in lake surface warming has broad implications beyond the physical climate system. Lakes support critical ecosystem services, sustain fisheries and water supplies, and are vital to the livelihoods of communities, particularly in low- and middle-income regions. Formal attribution to human-induced warming provides a scientific basis for incorporating freshwater ecosystems into climate adaptation and mitigation planning, and may also inform emerging legal and policy frameworks for climate accountability and environmental justice.

Methods

HydroLAKES

Lake characteristics including mean depth, surface area, and polygon were retrieved from the HydroLAKES51 dataset; we computed coordinates of centroids from the lake polygons.

Meteorological data

Reanalysis data

The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5-Land (ERA5-Land)56 provides land variables at hourly time intervals and a spatial resolution of 0.1° over the decades. Daily mean 2 m air temperature from the grid closest to the lake center during 1950–2020 was extracted and used as the meteorological forcing for the lake model to reconstruct lake surface temperature (OBSrecon).

Model data

This study investigates the drivers of lake surface temperature change using a multi-signal detection and attribution framework. We analyzed responses to several external forcings: ALL, NAT, GHG, and AER. The ALL signal was constructed from historical simulations (1950–2014) extended to 2020 using the SSP2-4.5 scenario. The GHG, AER, and NAT signals were taken from the corresponding single-forcing experiments (hist-GHG, hist-aer, hist-nat) of the DAMIP. Two additional signals were derived to isolate specific anthropogenic components. The ANT signal was calculated as the difference between the ALL and NAT simulations. To isolate the impact of LULCC, we used the hist-noLu (all forcings except land-use change; NOLU) experiment from the LUMIP, with the LU signal calculated as ALL minus NOLU. Finally, pre-industrial control (piControl) simulations were used to estimate internal climate variability. As CMIP6 models do not explicitly resolve lake–atmosphere coupling, the near-surface air temperature fields are used here as a representation of the large-scale atmospheric response to external forcing. Given that large5,57 and densely distributed lakes58 can modulate regional climate, improving the representation of lakes and their coupling with the atmosphere in climate models remains an important direction for future model development, particularly for applications at regional scales and for individual lakes.

The climate forcing data used in this study were prepared following the BASD protocol of the ISIMIP3b23,59–61. We prioritized the use of pre-existing ISIMIP3b datasets where available, and for the large number of models required for our analysis but not included in their archive, we manually processed the raw CMIP6 data to generate a comprehensive and consistent dataset. For each selected model, we began by using the first available ensemble realization, converting its calendar to proleptic Gregorian, and filling any gapped data via linear interpolation and extrapolation. The time series were then regrided to a regular lon/lat grid with the nearest resolution (0.5°, 1.0°, or 2.0°) using first-order conservative remapping, after which they were bias-adjusted against the W5E5 v2.0 observational dataset59,62. Finally, data on grids coarser than 0.5° were sequentially downscaled to the target 0.5° resolution in stages: first from 2.0° to 1.0°, and subsequently to 0.5°, with W5E5 serving as the reference at each stage.

Due to differences in model availability and the temporal coverage between the DAMIP (ending in 2020) and LUMIP (ending in 2014) experiments, we compiled two model ensembles for our attribution analyses. The DAMIP-based ensemble includes models that provided a complete set of daily near-surface air temperature data for the historical, SSPs (1-2.6, 2-4.5, 3-7.0, and 5-8.5), hist-GHG, hist-aer, and hist-nat simulations. This ensemble forms the basis of our main attribution analysis. The LUMIP-based ensemble includes models providing both historical and hist-noLu simulations, used specifically to isolate the impact of LULCC. To ensure model independence, only one model was selected from any single modeling center. This selection process resulted in a final ensemble of ten GCMs for the DAMIP-based analysis and seven GCMs for the LUMIP-based analysis (Supplementary Table 2). For the estimation of internal variability, we used all available piControl runs from the ISIMIP3b archive (spanning over 6000 model years), without downscaling additional model outputs due to the high computational cost.

It is important to note that different datasets are used for BASD (W5E5) and for driving the lake model to generate OBSrecon (ERA5-Land). W5E5 combines WFDE5 over land with ERA5 over the ocean, where WFDE5 itself is derived from ERA5 through bias correction using station-based observations62, while ERA5-Land provides a higher-resolution land-only reanalysis product. As a result, the two datasets share a common origin but differ in spatial resolution and processing methodology.

The use of ERA5-Land as the forcing for the lake model is motivated by its higher spatial resolution and improved representation of land surface conditions. Given the large number of lakes included in this study, using coarser-resolution datasets would result in multiple lakes sharing identical forcings, of the forcings used to drive the model were derived from a remote grid, substantially reducing the reliability of the reconstructed lake temperature. High-resolution forcing is therefore essential for generating a spatially explicit reconstruction (OBSrecon) that can serve as a substitute for lake temperature observations.

The BASD method involves conservative remapping, which is designed to preserve global integrals, necessitating the use of a global dataset such as W5E5. ERA5-Land, being limited to land areas, is therefore not suitable as a reference for this purpose.

Importantly, the BASD procedure is designed to preserve the long-term trends simulated by GCMs, ensuring that bias adjustment does not alter the externally forced signal23 (Supplementary Fig. 18). As a result, the differences in trends between OBSrecon and the CMIP6 simulations (such as in Fig. 1) are not attributable to the use of different datasets (W5E5 versus ERA5-Land), but instead reflect differences between modeled and reconstructed responses. This combination of datasets therefore represents a practical compromise, allowing for both reliable bias adjustment of climate model outputs and high-resolution lake temperature reconstruction.

The air2water model only requires air temperature and lake mean depth to predict lake surface temperature, yet it yields comparable accuracy with more complexed 1D lake models69. It performs well even with large amounts of missing data in observation datasets used for model calibration, as long as the data covers long time periods68. The model has been shown to satisfactorily reproduce long-term trends, interannual and interdecadal variations in lake surface temperature across a wide range of lakes with diverse morphological and hydrological characteristics64,65,67. Its combination of physical constraints and statistical, data-driven calibrated parameters allow it to provide a physically consistent representation of main processes governing air–water heat exchange, while maintaining robustness in lake surface temperature projections regardless of the air temperature dataset used to drive the model22,65. Given these strengths, the model has been a well-suited companion to satellite lake surface temperature records in large-scale lake analysis29,70. While fully coupled lake–atmosphere simulations offer a comprehensive view of lake responses, their application at global scale is often constrained by the small scales of lakes and the substantial computational demands relative to the coarse resolution of climate models. The input air temperature fields for the lake model are derived from a reanalysis dataset56 and fully coupled GCM simulations, and thus inherently encapsulate the large-scale circulation changes and radiative feedbacks associated with external forcing.

We computed a priori ranges of eight model parameters from mean water depths and selected lakes that had missing data not exceeding 99% during the entire satellite data collection period and offered a minimum of ten years of data for model calibration (n = 879,249). We used particle swarm optimization as the parameter tuning algorithm and iterated 50,000 times to find the optimized parameters that produced the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) with lake temperature observations from Landsat surface temperature products, within the priori ranges computed from mean water depths. MAE was chosen as the objective function because of its resilience to potential anomalies in observed temperature. Landsat surface temperature data during 1982–2009 and 2010–2023 were used to calibrate and validate model parameters, respectively. To enhance the generalizability of the model parameters across diverse forcing datasets, we exclusively selected lakes for analysis whose MAEs were less than 2 °C in both the calibration and validation phases, and the absolute difference in MAE between these two phases was restricted to 0.5 °C or less, resulting in 626,436 lakes (Supplementary Figure 1). It is understandable that certain lakes did not succeed in the calibration process due to (1) discrepancies in air temperature derived from the ERA5-Land grid nearest to lake centroids, particularly for lakes adjacent to shorelines (zonal differences between the ERA5-Land grid and the lake) or in complex terrains (altitudinal variations); (2) potential inaccuracies in mean water depths obtained from the HydroLAKES dataset, resulting in inappropriate prior ranges for model parameters; (3) inherent limitations of the lake model itself, where the impact of omitted processes (such as precipitation, inlets/outlets) cannot be rectified by adjusting the eight parameters.

The accuracy of the air2water model in simulating lake surface temperature was evaluated using the MAE and the Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) index (Supplementary Fig. 19). While MAE measures the absolute errors between simulated and observed data, NSE assesses the prediction skill of model, with values nearer to 1 indicating higher performance. Across the study sites, the MAEs had an average value of 1.6 °C, with a 2.5th–97.5th percentile range of [1.3, 1.9] °C. The NSE values averaged 0.8 [0.6, 0.9], exceeding 0.8 for 73% of the studied lakes. The lowest NSE values were found in tropical lakes, where observations have small variances. We also compared simulation results with lake surface temperature observations from daily moderate-resolution satellite products71,72 and daily or summer-mean in situ measurements11,73,74 (Supplementary Fig. 20). The results showed an average MAE ranging from 1.3 to 1.6 °C across the observational datasets, which is comparable to the errors calculated against Landsat products. This indicates the robustness of using Landsat observations as a reference for calibrating the lake model. Despite the coarse temporal resolution of the reference data, the inherent representation of lake thermal dynamic in model allows for a reasonable reproduction of day-to-day variations.

Time series analysis and spatial aggregation

To derive annual mean lake surface temperature, a key issue is the treatment of ice-covered periods. Previous studies often restrict the analysis to fixed seasonal windows (e.g., specific months) or to the minimum open-water period across years, in order to ensure temporal comparability for individual lakes. However, such approaches lead to inconsistent temporal sampling across lakes. This inconsistency is particularly problematic in this study, as the detection and attribution analysis is conducted at regional and global scales, requiring aggregation across lakes. Inconsistent temporal coverage across lakes would introduce artificial heterogeneity and reduce the physical interpretability of aggregated temperature changes.

To address this, we include ice-covered periods in the annual mean calculation by assigning a temperature of 0 °C during these periods. This approach ensures consistent temporal sampling across both lakes and years, and allows the annual mean metric to reflect both open-water temperature and variations in ice duration. We test the robustness of our main conclusions by repeating the analysis with ice-covered periods excluded. While the attribution of lake surface warming to GHG forcing remains strong at the global scale, the resulting scaling factors are generally reduced, and the spatial extent of detectable regions decreases (Supplementary Figs. 21–22). This behavior is reasonable, given the increased interannual variability, i.e., lower signal-to-noise ratio, associated with shorter periods used to calculate annual means, especially for lakes at mid-high latitudes.

We calculated the rate of change in annual mean lake surface temperature using the Theil-Sen estimator, a non-parametric method that computes the median of the slopes between all pairs of data points, thus increasing its robustness against potential outliers in time series data75.

We aggregated properties of individual lakes to regional and global scales following the steps: Each lake was assigned to a 1° grid cell according to locations of lake centroids, and the average (or other statistics) of all lakes within each grid was calculated to obtain grid-level properties. The regional and global properties were calculated using means of grid data weighted by grid cell area to avoid the emphasizing high-latitude grids, which have smaller areas than other places.

We conducted one-, two-, and three-signal detection and attribution analyses. The one-signal analysis enables detection of whether the observed changes are consistent with any single forcing taken in isolation, but it does not account for potential confounding among multiple forcings. The two-signal analysis allows us to separate and compare the effects of two combined signals (e.g., ANT vs NAT, or LU vs NOLU), especially when one component cannot be directly simulated and must be inferred through decomposition. The three-signal analysis provides a more detailed partitioning of external influences (e.g., GHG, AER, and NAT), while accounting for potential collinearity among the fingerprints. By jointly estimating their scaling factors, the three-signal regression helps mitigate attribution biases that may arise when signals are analyzed independently, offering a more robust assessment of the relative contributions and uncertainties associated with different forcings.

Uncertainty quantification

Uncertainty in fingerprints

To account for model parameter uncertainties, we quantified the sensitivities of the fingerprints (the right-hand side of Eq. 4) through a parameter perturbation experiment. Since conducting a comprehensive sensitivity analysis for all ~600,000 lakes across every GCM and forcing experiment was computationally intractable, we employed a stratified sampling strategy.

Lakes were stratified based on four primary physical characteristics: latitude (0°, 23.5°, 40°, 60°, 90°), surface area ([0, 1, 100, ∞] km2), mean depth ([0, 5, 20, ∞] m), and elevation [−∞, 500, ∞] m). This design aimed to capture the full spectrum of lake types rather than replicating the global lake distribution, which is numerically dominated by small, shallow lakes. After excluding non-existent strata combinations, 986 representative lakes were selected for the perturbation experiments (Supplementary Fig. 23).

For each representative lake, 1000 parameter sets were generated via bootstrap sampling, with each model parameter perturbed by ±10%. Daily lake surface temperatures were simulated, and annual means were subsequently derived. To ensure consistency with our main analysis, we calculated two sets of annual means: one including ice-covered periods (with temperatures fixed at 0 °C) and another excluding them for supplementary validation. This choice was informed by the fact that absolute errors exhibited a strong latitudinal dependence, likely due to the inherently lower temperature variability in tropical lakes; relative errors provided a more spatially stable metric of model uncertainty.

To propagate these localized uncertainties to the global scale, we mapped the error distributions from the 986 representative lakes to the entire dataset. For every individual lake in the global database, we identified its ten nearest neighbors within the four-dimensional feature space (latitude, area, depth, elevation) using a K-Dimensional (KD) Tree algorithm (Supplementary Fig. 24). These neighbors formed an empirical error pool, from which 1000 bootstrap samples were drawn and applied multiplicatively to the baseline GCM fingerprints.

For both fingerprint and observational sources, 1000 unique realizations were generated and processed through the ROF framework. By repeating the detection and attribution analysis for each realization, we derived a robust uncertainty envelope for the scaling factors. This envelope is defined by the absolute minimum lower bound and the absolute maximum upper bound of the 95% confidence intervals across all 1,000 realizations, providing a conservative yet comprehensive estimate of the attribution confidence. The robustness of the attribution results was evaluated based on scaling factor intervals across all uncertainty realizations; specifically, a conclusion is considered robust only if it remains consistent across the entire ensemble of 1000 bootstrap simulations for both observational and fingerprint uncertainty tests.
