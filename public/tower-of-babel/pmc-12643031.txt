Impulse oscillometry-defined airway abnormalities as a treatable trait for mild-to-moderate COPD: a prospective cohort study with 3-year follow-up
Authors: Lifei Lu, Fan Wu, Zihui Wang, Gaoying Tang, Qi Wan, Zhishan Deng, Jieqi Peng, Cuiqiong Dai, Kunning Zhou, Xiaohui Wu, Guannan Cai, Shuqing Yu, Yongqing Huang, Changli Yang, Shengtang Chen, Pixin Ran, Yumin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatable traits (TTs) can effectively conduct personalised assessment and treatment for patients with mild-to-moderate COPD. After excluding the classic TTs, do some neglected TTs remain? We evaluated associations of impulse oscillometry (IOS)-defined airway abnormalities (AAs) with respiratory health outcomes and prognoses in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD.MethodsWe analysed the baseline and 3-year follow-up data from a prospective cohort in China. Patients with mild-to-moderate COPD were enrolled with excluded classic TTs (COPD Assessment Test ≥10, modified Medical Research Council score ≥2, frequent acute exacerbations (AEs) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) % pred <60%). AAs were defined based on IOS parameter resonant frequency greater than the upper limit of normal. Differences in lung function decline and AEs were examined among groups with normal spirometry, AAs and without AAs in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD.Results1328 participants were finally enrolled (799 normal spirometry, 230 without AAs and 299 with AAs in mild-to-moderate COPD). The AA group was older and exhibited worse lung function, higher symptom scores and worse image abnormalities than the other groups. During the 3-year follow-up, compared with the group with normal spirometry, a faster FEV1 decline and a higher risk of total AEs were observed in the AA group, but no differences in FEV1 decline and total AEs were found in groups with normal spirometry and the group without AAs.ConclusionThe AA group in mild-to-moderate COPD experienced worse image abnormalities, a higher risk of AEs and a faster FEV1 decline. These results suggest that AAs may be a special TT in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD.

Introduction

COPD, a heterogeneous disorder characterised by persistent airflow limitation, represents a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [1]. While advanced COPD imposes profound clinical burdens, ​epidemiological data highlight that mild-to-moderate (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 1–2) cases constitute the majority (60–80%) of patients with COPD [2]. Individuals with mild-to-moderate COPD exhibit measurable physiological impairment with an increased morbidity and a higher risk of mortality compared with healthy nonsmoking individuals. Additionally, COPD patients with GOLD 1–2 experience a more rapid decline in lung function compared with those with advanced COPD [3, 4]. These findings suggest that early intervention in mild-to-moderate COPD patients may effectively slow disease progression. Several studies from Zhou et al. [5–7] have provided evidence-based medical support for this assumption. Nevertheless, mild-to-moderate COPD patients are usually underdiagnosed and undertreated, and personalised treatment strategies for mild-to-moderate COPD remain significantly inadequate.

A treatable trait (TT) is defined as a “therapeutic target identified by phenotypes or endotypes through a validated biomarker”. Traits are categorised into pulmonary, extrapulmonary and behaviours/lifestyle risk factor domains [8–11]. The benefits include limiting exposure to unnecessary treatments, using objective measures to identify illness burdens, providing the most appropriate treatments for the most appropriate patients, engaging all parties in a person-centred approach and improving health outcomes. Among the classic TTs in COPD are dyspnoea (modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score ≥2), COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score ≥10, frequent acute exacerbations (AEs) in the previous year (moderate AEs ≥2 or severe AEs ≥1) or forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) % predicted <60% [12–16]. However, some studies have found that except classical treatable therapies, there are still some TT subtypes in mild-to-moderate COPD.

Small airways (diameter <2 mm) were considered the primary area of early pathological changes in COPD. Several clinical techniques are employed to evaluate small airway dysfunction, including spirometry, impulse oscillometry (IOS), nitrogen washout, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and body plethysmography [17, 18]. Among these, IOS stands out as a noninvasive, effort-independent method for assessing respiratory impedance, with its specific parameters proving particularly valuable for detecting airway abnormalities (AAs) [17, 19–21]. Existing evidence suggests that IOS-defined AAs were closely associated with symptom burden, increased airway resistance, reduced lung dynamic compliance and gas trapping, and the risk of AEs in the prior year [22–25], potentially offering greater sensitivity than traditional spirometry metrics in reflecting disease activity [26]. Notably, targeted interventions for small airways have shown promise in improving IOS parameters and patient-reported outcomes, indicating that IOS may guide treatment stratification [27–29]. However, the relationships between IOS-AA subtypes, except for classic TTs and disease progression in mild-to-moderate COPD, were unclear.

The present study aims to explore whether IOS-defined AAs can be used as a TT in mild-to-moderate COPD using a prospective cohort in China with 3-year follow-up: ​1) cross-sectional associations between IOS-AAs subtypes and baseline respiratory health (symptoms and computed tomography (CT) phenotypes); and 2) longitudinal relationships with annual FEV1 decline and moderate–severe AE incidence. This study seeks to provide new evidence for the precise classification and personalised intervention of COPD, advancing the clinical translation of the “TT” framework in early disease management.

Methods

Study design

The Early Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (ECOPD) study is a prospective study aimed at exploring the onset and development of COPD. It enrolled individuals aged 40–80 years old, including never, former and current smokers, from July 2019 to August 2021. This study included individuals with normal spirometry (post-bronchodilator FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ≥0.70 and FEV1 % pred ≥80%), mild–moderate COPD (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 and FEV1 % pred ≥50%) in ECOPD study. The exclusion criteria and ethical considerations have been outlined in the original ECOPD protocol [30]. In brief, individuals were excluded who met the criteria as follows: 1) <40 years old or >80 years old; 2) had incomplete IOS tests; 3) had classic TTs (including mMRC score ≥2, CAT ≥10, frequent AEs and FEV1 % pred <60%) [15, 31]; 4) preserved ratio impaired spirometry (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ≥0.70 and FEV1 % pred <80%) [32, 33]; and 5) respiratory infection or exacerbations within 4 weeks prior to screening. More details were observed in the ECOPD design [30]. These individuals underwent a 3-year follow-up with three visits. At baseline, they filled in the questionnaire and data collection involved demographic information, the mMRC score and CAT score, family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure and biomass exposure. Then, these individuals underwent pre-bronchodilator IOS tests, pre-and post-bronchodilator spirometry tests and chest CT scans. Meanwhile, the questionnaire, spirometry tests and IOS tests were performed each visit during follow-up.

To avoid airway spasm after spirometry tests, pre-bronchodilator IOS (CareFusion, Hochberg, Germany) tests should be performed based on European Respiratory Society (ERS) 2003 guidelines before the spirometry test [34]. IOS tests require more than 30 s during tidal breath. The coefficient of variation of resistance at 5 Hz (R5) needs to be less than 10% for three measurements. IOS parameters included R5, resistance at 20 Hz (R20), difference between 5 Hz and 20 Hz (R5–R20), area under the reactance curve (AX), reactance at 5 Hz (X5) and resonant frequency (fres). According to the ERS and American Thoracic Society 2005 guidelines, subjects with lung function (CareFusion, Hochberg, Germany) who reached grade B quality control or above were included in the analysis [35]. Lung function predicted values were calculated by the Global Lung Function Initiative global spirometry reference equations [36].

Emphysema was defined as the percentage of voxels with attenuation values < −950 HU at maximal inspiration (LAA−950), while air trapping was quantified as the percentage of voxels with values < −856 HU at end-expiration (LAA−856) [37]. Pi10, a standardised measure of airway wall thickness representing a theoretical airway with a 10-mm lumen perimeter, was derived from CT imaging to assess small airway remodelling [38].

Definitions and outcomes

Our previous study showed that the proportion of AAs defined by fres is higher than those defined by other parameters in patients with COPD, and fres assessing AAs was more strongly associated with the risk of AE in the previous year than other indices [22]. So, we firstly considered using fres to define AAs. AAs were defined as IOS parameters greater than the upper limit of normal (ULN). The ULN was determined based on predicted values derived from healthy Chinese subjects in the IOS study by Liang et al. [39] and our definition was consistent with established methods [19, 21]. Meanwhile, we performed sensitivity analysis using fres greater than a fixed cut-off (11.23 Hz). Li et al. [40] reported that a fixed cut-off of fres (11.23 Hz) was recommended to detect small airway dysfunction in Chinese subjects with chronic respiratory symptoms and preserved pulmonary function. The individuals were divided into three groups: 1) normal spirometry; 2) mild–moderate COPD without AAs; and 3) mild–moderate COPD with AAs.

Outcomes of interest included total AEs (mild, moderate and severe), moderate–severe AEs and decline in post-bronchodilator FEV1. We defined the AE as the onset or aggravation of at least two of the following five symptoms: cough, sputum, purulent sputum, dyspnoea and wheeze for more than 2 days after excluding other diseases based on questionnaires at each visit. Moderate–severe AEs were defined as those resulting in outpatient or emergency department visits, or hospitalisation, or the need for COPD medications at each visit [31].

Statistical analysis

We compared demographics (age, body mass index (BMI), pack-years and smoking status), risk factors (family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure and biomass exposure), lung function (FEV1, FEV1 % pred, FVC, FVC % pred and FEV1/FVC), IOS parameters (R5, R20, R5-R20, X5, AX and fres) using ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis tests or chi-squared tests. Then, multivariable linear regression models were performed to explore the associations of AAs with symptoms score and CT abnormalities, adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure and biomass exposure. In longitudinal 3-year follow-up analysis, the primary outcomes included a decline in post-bronchodilator FEV1 and total AEs. The rates of AEs at the 3-year follow-up visit were compared among AA groups using a negative binomial regression model, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure and biomass exposure. Total AEs in the previous year were considered a significantly important risk factor for AEs and was also adjusted as a confounding factor in the negative binomial regression model analysing variations in AEs among the IOS-defined AA groups. Secondly, the linear mixed-effects model was applied to explore the associations between decline in post-bronchodilator FEV1 and IOS-defined AAs. Baseline post-bronchodilator FEV1 was additionally included as a confounding factor for adjustment to analyse the rate of FEV1 decline. Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis (defining AAs as fres >11.23 Hz) to assess the association of AAs with FEV1 decline and AEs, thereby validating the robustness of our findings. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Data analysis was performed using R (v.4.4.2) and IBM SPSS v.27.0.

Results

In total, 1328 participants were included in the analysis (799 normal spirometry, 230 without AAs and 299 with AAs in mild–moderate COPD) (figure 1). Participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD were older and exhibited worse lung function, higher symptom scores, worse image abnormalities (air trapping, emphysema and airway wall thickness) and total AEs in the previous year than other groups. For the IOS parameters, our results showed that participants without AAs in mild–moderate COPD had lower airway resistance, higher proportions of respiratory symptoms, current smokers, family history of respiratory diseases and higher pack-years than individuals with normal spirometry (table 1).

AAs and symptom score CT images

After adjusting for confounding factors of age, sex, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure and biomass exposure, we found that participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD were associated with higher CAT scores and mMRC scores. However, no differences in CAT score and mMRC score were observed between participants without AAs in mild–moderate COPD and normal spirometry. In CT images, compared with normal spirometry, there was more severe emphysema, air trapping and thicker airway walls in participants with and without AAs in mild–moderate COPD (table 2).

Associations of AAs with a decline in FEV1

Figure 2 shows the rate of decline in post-bronchodilator FEV1 at the 3-year follow-up. Our findings suggested that the rate of FEV1 decline among normal spirometry, participants without AAs in mild–moderate COPD and participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD was −32.6±2.2 mL·year−1, −33.9±4.0 mL·year−1 and −43.9±3.6 mL·year−1, respectively. Adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure, biomass exposure and baseline post-bronchodilator FEV1, we found that compared with participants with normal spirometry, a faster FEV1 decline was observed in participants with AAs (mean difference −11.1 mL·year−1, 95% CI −21.8– −0.4; p=0.042) in mild–moderate COPD, but no difference was found in participants without AAs in mild–moderate COPD (mean difference −0.8 mL·year−1, 95% CI −12.5–10.9; p=0.887).

Associations of AAs with AEs

Figure 3 shows associations of AAs with total AEs. Our findings observed that the rate of AEs among normal spirometry, participants without AAs in mild–moderate COPD and participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD was 0.38±0.02 patients·year−1, 0.33±0.03 people·year−1 and 0.55±0.04 patients·year−1. Compared with participants with normal spirometry, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, pack-years, family history of respiratory diseases, occupational exposure, biomass exposure and AEs in the previous year, the incidence rate ratio (IRR) for the presence of total AEs of participants without AAs in mild–moderate COPD and participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD were 0.93 (95% CI 0.74–1.17; p=0.521) and 1.52 (95% CI 1.24–1.87; p<0.001), respectively. Sensitivity analysis

We performed sensitivity analysis of AAs using fres greater than a fixed-value cut-off (11.23 Hz) with lung function decline and AEs. Similarly, we observed that compared with participants with normal spirometry, participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD had higher symptoms scores, worse lung function and more severe CT abnormalities (supplementary tables S2 and S3). In follow-up visits, a higher risk of AEs and faster lung function decline was observed in participants with AAs in mild–moderate COPD than those with normal spirometry (supplementary table S4).

Discussion

This study provides critical insights into the role of AAs as measured by IOS in mild–moderate COPD. We found that patients with AAs with mild–moderate COPD experienced worse respiratory health, image abnormalities, higher rates of AEs and faster lung function decline compared with those with normal spirometry. These findings highlight the potential of IOS-based AAs as a TT in mild–moderate COPD, offering a novel approach to early intervention and personalised management.

COPD is a heterogeneous condition with varying clinical presentations and disease trajectories. The concept of “TTs” has emerged as a personalised approach to managing COPD, focusing on identifying and targeting specific clinical features that contribute to disease burden and progression [41]. The classic TTs in COPD were dyspnoea (mMRC score ≥2), health status impairment (CAT score ≥10), frequent AEs (defined as moderate AEs ≥2 or severe AEs ≥1 in the previous year) and significant airflow limitation (FEV1 % predicted <60%). These TTs are well-established indicators of disease severity and are associated with worse outcomes, making them critical targets for therapeutic intervention.

A TT is characterised by the following criteria: clinical relevance, association with specific outcomes (such as symptoms, health status and risk of future events), ease of identification and measurement and the ability to be treated [10, 42]. Previous studies have shown that IOS-defined AAs in asthma were associated with AEs and poor symptom control [43]. Increasing evidence suggests that IOS-defined AAs are TTs in asthma [44]; however, its clinical value in COPD remains unclear. Our previous results showed that the proportions of IOS-defined (R5, R5-R20, X5, AX and fres) AAs in patients with COPD were 52.9–67.3% in the baseline [22]. Furthermore, we found that the fres parameter identified a higher proportion of AAs compared with other parameters in mild–moderate COPD patients. Meanwhile, for AEs in the previous year. Additionally, fres was more closely associated with the risk of AEs in the previous year compared with other parameters. In clinical practice, we have simplified the assessment of AAs by using fres > ULN. After excluding classic TTs, our findings show that patients with AAs in mild–moderate COPD exhibited more severe symptom scores and imaging abnormalities compared with normal spirometry group. This result suggests that AAs may be associated with early structural changes, such as gas trapping and emphysema, even in mild–moderate COPD.

AEs were major driver of hospitalisation and mortality in COPD [15]. We examined the relationship between AAs and the risk of AEs over a 3-year period. After adjusting for some confounders, we found that patients with AAs in mild–moderate COPD had a significantly higher risk of total AEs or moderate–severe AEs compared with those with normal spirometry. However, there was no significant difference in AE risk between the group without AAs in mild–moderate COPD and those with normal spirometry. These findings suggested that AA played a key role in exacerbation risk during the early stages of COPD. Interestingly, we observed that the group without AAs had a trend towards fewer AEs compared with the normal spirometry group, indicating that other factors might contribute to increased exacerbation risk. We suspected that airway resistance played a certain role in the normal spirometry group, and found that the normal spirometry group had higher airway resistance compared with patients without AAs in mild–moderate COPD at baseline, which is not surprising. Specifically, our previous research demonstrated that a substantial proportion of individuals with normal lung function still present with AAs [21], likely reflecting subclinical airway dysfunction, and the previous study also confirmed that IOS parameters were associated with risk of AEs [20]. In addition, the ECLIPSE study reported that only 22% patients with moderate COPD experienced frequent exacerbations [45]. Not all mild–moderate COPD patients had AEs.

Lung function decline is a well-established prognostic marker for COPD progression. After adjusting for confounding variables, our results revealed that patients with AAs in mild–moderate COPD experienced a faster decline in FEV1 compared with those with normal spirometry. However, no significant difference in FEV1 decline was observed between the group without AAs and those with normal spirometry. These findings reinforce the concept that AAs are a TT in mild–moderate COPD, influencing both exacerbation risk and the rate of lung function decline. The presence of AAs, particularly in early COPD stages, suggests a need for more targeted interventions. As AAs are associated with worse clinical outcomes, its identification and management could substantially reduce exacerbation frequency and slow the rate of lung function deterioration. These findings provide strong support for incorporating AAs into a personalised approach to COPD management.

Our study has several limitations. First, the “gold standard” for diagnosing AAs remains unclear. There are various tools available for assessing AAs, such as nitrogen washout, body plethysmography, exhaled nitric oxide fraction and HRCT [28, 43, 46, 47]. Future studies should explore the use of multiple methods in combination to assess the relationship between AAs and disease prognosis in mild–moderate COPD. Second, the crucial aspect of TTs is their “treatability” [10]. Ideally, treatment efficacy should be established through randomised controlled trials (RCTs), yet there is a lack of RCTs examining the therapeutic effects of treating AAs in mild–moderate COPD. Further investigation is needed to validate this concept. Finally, TTs are influenced by a range of factors, including pulmonary, extrapulmonary and environmental elements. While our study focused on classic TTs in mild–moderate COPD, future research should evaluate the impact of other known TTs, considering these additional factors.

Conclusion

AAs, as assessed by IOS, play a crucial role in the exacerbation risk and lung function decline in mild–moderate COPD. By identifying and addressing AAs as part of personalised management strategies, we can potentially improve disease outcomes and delay the progression of COPD, thereby improving the quality of life for affected individuals.
