Optimizing Georgia’s public health workforce: a study on demographics, engagement, and capacity building
Authors: Smriti Ridhi, Binita Adhikari, Ekaterine Cherkezishvili, Elene Godziashvili, Tamar Gabunia, Tushar Singh, Sara Bennett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA well-functioning public health system relies on a robust workforce. Comprehensive data on the workforce, such as number, distribution, and key characteristics, are crucial for evidence-based workforce planning and development. However, few comprehensive public health workforce assessments exist, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Public health reforms over the years and needs identified during the COVID-19 pandemic prompted this assessment in Georgia.MethodsA survey of the core public health workforce, including employees at central and regional units of the National Center for Disease Control and Public Health (NCDC) and Municipal Public Health Centers (MPHC), was conducted online between June and September 2023. The survey collected data on workforce demographics, education, on-the-job training, and time spent across different program areas and job functions, along with questions on career progression, job satisfaction, and motivation.ResultsThe response rate was 81.3%. Findings showed that the median age was 48 for NCDC and 56 for MPHC employees. Over 80% of NCDC and 90% of MPHC employees are women. More than 50% of the workforce hold a master’s degrees or higher, and over half of degree-holders specialized in public health or medicine. Mean years of service are 14.9 (NCDC) and 18.0 (MPHC), but career mobility is limited, only 33.3% of NCDC and 10.5% of MPHC staff have ever been promoted. NCDC employees spend most time on administration and surveillance/response, while MPHC staff focus on communicable disease management, administration, and immunization. Training participation is limited, with employees in key positions having better access. Despite limited advancement and relatively low pay, the workforce reported high job satisfaction and strong intrinsic motivation.ConclusionsThese findings are pivotal in identifying workforce planning and development bottlenecks and developing targeted strategies. Key interventions include addressing an aging workforce through targeted recruitment and succession planning, providing competitive salaries to attract a younger workforce, and strengthening training offerings. This effort to profile the public health workforce could guide similar assessments in the future and in other countries to prevent, detect, and respond to public health threats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12960-026-01072-0.

Background

The public health workforce is the foundation of the public health infrastructure within a country [1]. Having the right people with appropriate skills and competencies is critical to addressing complex public health challenges [2]. Assessing public health system capacity therefore requires quantifying and understanding key characteristics of the public health workforce, including its size, composition, distribution, motivation and opportunities for skills development and career progression [3]. While substantial work has been done to map the health care workforce [4, 5], there is comparatively little evidence, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and post-Soviet settings, on the core public health workforce and how workforce conditions may shape the delivery of essential public health functions (EPHFs).

Building this understanding and guiding strategic investments in workforce development hinges on the availability of comprehensive data [6]. However, enumerating and characterizing the health workforce can be challenging due to limited or unreliable Human Resource Information Systems [7]. The situation is even more complicated for the public health workforce, where clear definitions of who constitutes the workforce are often lacking. The WHO and the Association of Schools of Public Health in the European Region (ASPHER) “Roadmap to Professionalizing the Public Health Workforce in the European Region” define the core public health workforce as all the individuals who provide EPHFs as their primary role [8]. Gebbie et al. characterize core public health professionals as individuals employed by government public health agencies who work closely with other public and private organizations, while other approaches define the public health workforce by employing institutional or occupational classification [9].

Using these approaches, a limited number of national studies have mapped the public health workforce through surveys, reviews of routine data, and other data collection techniques [10]. In the United Kingdom, the Centre for Workforce Intelligence mapped the public health workforce in England to guide policy decisions for workforce planning by drawing on administrative records to estimate workforce numbers, distribution, and skills [11]. In South-Eastern Europe, Bjegovic-Mikanovic et al. synthesized qualitative data from workforce development success stories reported by the South-Eastern Europe Health Network (SEEHN) to provide a high-level view of the public health workforce and the EPHFs it performs [12]. In Georgia, a 2004 study assessed public health workers’ demographics, employment history, education, and on-the-job training, as well as views on HR management practices, municipal policies, and funding, using a survey of directors and one employee from 30 randomly selected municipal public health centers (MPHCs) and focus group discussions with public health professionals, center directors, and policymakers [13]. In the United States, a nationally representative survey of public health employees, the Public Health Workforce Interests and Needs Survey (PH WINS) has captured workforce demographics, characteristics, training needs, retention metrics, and engagement and satisfaction [14, 15]. These dimensions matter because workforce capacity depends not only on staffing levels and distribution, but also on retention, motivation, and career development. Evidence from public sector and health workforce research suggests that supportive management, access to training, and clear advancement pathways are associated with job satisfaction and motivation and may influence retention and organizational performance [16–21].

Since the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, Georgia’s public health system has undergone structural changes and shifted from the sanitary-epidemiological model to a more holistic approach [22]. The apex public health agency in the country, the National Center of Disease Control and Public Health (NCDC), established in 1996, oversees all EPHFs within the country and coordinates with nine regional public health divisions [23]. NCDC is supported by 65 MPHCs spread across 11 administrative regions that deliver public health services at the local level [24, 25]. Together, employees across NCDC, its regional units, and MPHCs constitute the core public health workforce in Georgia.

Strengthening the public health workforce was identified as a priority by Georgia’s government in the aftermath of the COVID-19 response. The government recognized the critical role of the public health workforce in pandemic response and understood that while there were clear historic strengths, there were also potential vulnerabilities, particularly in terms of sustaining public health workforce capacity into the future. The government sought to understand the extent to which the public health workforce was motivated and engaged; had relevant and up-to-date skills matched to the functions they performed; and was organized efficiently. Our study aimed to inform the development of a national public health workforce strategy and advance Georgia’s capability to perform essential public health functions by mapping the existing core public health workforce in Georgia, and assessing workforce composition and demographics, distribution by job function and geography, and levels of satisfaction and workforce engagement.

Methods

Aim and setting of the study

We conducted a public health workforce survey in Georgia between June and September 2023. This was a cross-sectional, descriptive census of the core public health professionals (permanent and contract staff) in Georgia employed at the central and regional units of the NCDC and all MPHCs rather than a sample. It excluded personnel in the Department of Technical Support and the Legal Department in the NCDC, as well as drivers, janitors, and administrative assistants across the NCDC and MPHCs who were determined to be support staff rather than core public health workers. The survey was administered to 390 of 446 employees in the NCDC central and regional offices and 734 of 974 employees in MPHCs, excluding support staff.

NCDC organogram outlining the departments and divisions is included as Additional file 1.

Questionnaire development and survey process

The survey was developed by the Johns Hopkins University team and refined based on feedback from workforce experts in NCDC Georgia and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) country office in Georgia. The instrument drew upon existing public health workforce surveys, including the PHWINS [26], the Council on Linkages Public Health Workers Survey Instrument [27], and other validated workforce engagement questions [28]. Most of the questions in the survey were closed questions, but a few questions offered opportunities for explanation.

The survey (Additional file 2) collected data on workforce demographics, including name, age, sex, work location, work unit, position, nature of employment, and hours worked. It also included fields on education level and focus area, as well as participation in on-the-job training. Employees were asked to estimate, on average, the percentage of their weekly work time allocated across a set of defined program areas and across different types of job functions. The program area was intended to collect time spent by domain or topic and the function represented the type of work performed (e.g., administration, program management, research, etc.). Responses were based on the individual’s understanding and were not verified by a supervisor. The survey also included questions on career progression, job satisfaction and motivation. For job satisfaction and motivation, we used a 5-point Likert scale and statements from well-established tools [28] where respondents agreed or disagreed with statements where is extremely dissatisfied and is extremely satisfied with different aspects of work, including pay and benefits, job duties, work culture (working hours, supervisory support, acknowledgment), and job security. The analysis used simple means on a scale of 1–5. Workforce engagement was also explored through questions relating to reasons for joining and staying, the nature of performance review, perceived organizational support, and retirement plans.

Survey administration

The bilingual (Georgian and English) survey was administered online (Qualtrics). NCDC staff received individualized links and MPHC staff received a link via center heads. Four reminders were sent during the field period.

Data analysis

Survey responses with less than 40% completion and duplicates were removed from the data set. Open fields were translated from Georgian to English using Google Translate, and sample translations were reviewed by a team member fluent in both Georgian and English to ensure accuracy. Such open fields were also cleaned through a manual review, correcting misspellings and typos. In some cases, open fields were converted into categories of responses to allow easier analysis. The dataset was checked for outliers and erroneous entries in numerical fields (e.g., age, monthly salary, number of hours worked), and these errors were excluded from data analysis. The public health workforce distribution analysis was conducted using the 2023 Georgia population estimates published by the National Statistics Office of Georgia [29].

Analysis was conducted using RStudio Version—2023.09.1 + 494 and Microsoft Excel.

Findings were presented to and discussed with local stakeholders to verify key results and enhance interpretation.

Results

The overall response rate was 81.3% (914/1124), 69.7% for NCDC and regional centers (n = 272) and 87.5% for municipal public health centers (n = 642). Response rates by NCDC departments and MPHC regions are included as Additional files 3 and 4. Findings are elaborated in detail below.

Distribution of public health workers

The NCDC workforce is distributed across several functional areas. The Lugar Center for Public Health Research, Georgia’s reference laboratory for public health, employs 18.2% of staff. Core disease-focused departments include Communicable Diseases (10.0%), Non-Communicable Diseases (5.6%), and Environmental Health (1.5%), while Medical Statistics accounts for 4.9%. Finance, administration, public relations, and quality control divisions account for 7.5%. Risk Preparedness and Continuing Professional Training and Development divisions employ just 1.3% of staff.

The Department of Public Health State Programs Central and Regional Management, which supports MPHCs through nine regional offices, employs 22.3% of staff. An additional 28.7% work in vertical state programs funded by the state budget and administered by the NCDC. These programs focus on domains such as HIV/AIDS, COVID-19, hepatitis, tuberculosis, blood safety, and disease prevention.

The overall density of public health workers is 30.1 per 100,000 population, and 78.7% of NCDC employees and 84.4% of MPHC employees are in permanent positions.

The density of the public health workforce in municipal units varies from 9 to 114 workers per 100,000 population (Table 1). A higher density of public health workers is seen in mountainous regions such as Svaneti. Key positions at MPHCs include epidemiologists responsible for disease surveillance and preventive care services (20.8% of MPHC public health workforce); immunization program employees (11.7%); medical-statistical informaticians (7.6%); preventionist/prophylactic doctors (7.6%); parasitologists (5.6%); entomologists (5.0%); lab and other assistants (10.2%); there are several other positions at the MPHCs that make up a smaller proportion of the workforce. The workforce across most MPHCs except two (Adjara and Tbilisi) have similar key positions. However, the number of employees working in these positions varies and is not correlated with the population served. Workforce characteristics

Half of the workforce is above the age of 48 in NCDC and 56 in MPHCs. The mean years of service for NCDC employees are 14.9 years, and for MPHC employees are 18.0 years. Despite long tenures, only 33.3% of NCDC and 10.5% of MPHC staff have ever received a promotion. On average, fewer than 20 employees join NCDC and the MPHCs annually. Hiring is usually driven by organizational restructuring or increased public health needs, such as during the COVID-19 pandemic. There were surges in recruitment associated with the NCDC establishment (1996), the public health system reorganization (2007), the establishment of the Lugar Center (2012), and the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Historical patterns of hiring based on survey response

The workforce is predominantly female, with 81.6% of the employees at NCDC and 90.3% at MPHCs being women.

At the time of the survey, the median salaries for key positions at NCDC ranged from 750 Georgian Lari (GEL) (approximately 277 USD) per month to 2420 GEL (893 USD) per month. For MPHCs, median salaries were between 484 GEL (179 USD) per month and 1611 GEL (594 USD) per month. Among MPHC employees, we observed significant regional differences in salary for people working in the same position. The salary in some regions was half that of others.

Educational background and qualifications of public health professionals

Public health employees in Georgia have robust pre-service education (Table 2). Most NCDC employees have a master’s degree or higher, and more than half of the employees with master’s and bachelor’s degrees specialized in public health or medicine. In MPHCs, one-quarter of staff have master’s degrees, and a similar proportion have medical degrees, and the domain of specialization shows a similar trend to that of NCDC. Continuing professional development

Around 79.7% of NCDC employees and 80.2% of MPHC employees reported participating in some form of on-the-job training since assuming their positions. Most training opportunities focused on COVID-19, contact tracing, epidemic surveillance and response, and immunization. The frequency of participation in training differed between departments. Biosecurity and Especially Dangerous Pathogens, as well as Virology and Molecular Biology Departments, had higher participation in training among employees, while the Medical Statistics Department reported fewer training engagements. Within NCDC departments, recent training participation was evenly distributed among key positions. Participation in training by cadres reflected a higher median age among trainees. At the MPHCs, epidemiologists, employees focused on immunization, and Directors attended a larger number of trainings compared to other cadres. The average age of employees who attended the FETP frontline training across both NCDC and MPHC was ~ 50 years.

Time spent across program areas

In NCDC, staff reported spending the most time on administrative program areas (21.9%) and public health surveillance and response (21.1%), followed by communicable disease management (19%). MPHC staff reported spending the most time on communicable disease management (25.3%), administration (16.3%), and immunization (14.9%). In both settings, relatively little time was allocated to NCDs/health promotion and to workforce development and policy/legislation. Top reasons for staying were challenging work, mission, and supervisor satisfaction among NCDC staff, and job satisfaction, supervisor satisfaction, and a sense of mission among MPHC staff. Very few respondents cited a lack of other opportunities. Reasons to stay in the job did not show any specific patterns when reviewed by age groups. The most important reason for joining the organization for both NCDC and MPHC employees was their interest in job functions, followed by the reputation of the organization. Around half of NCDC employees and 70.6% of MPHC employees reported receiving some form of performance feedback, and overall, they found these useful. However, there is no formal performance review process at NCDC or in the municipal offices, except at the Lugar Center at NCDC.

Discussion

Survey findings suggest many positive factors: the current workforce in Georgia is stable as most of the employees are in permanent positions; the workforce is well-trained in terms of pre-service education; and the longer tenure of employees suggests a wealth of knowledge gained on the job. Furthermore, the public health workforce appears satisfied and finds substantial intrinsic motivation in its work. At the same time, the government’s concerns about potential risks for Georgia’s future public health system capacity appear warranted. Georgia has an aging public health workforce, with limited advancement pathways, a large portion of time spent on administrative functions and a perception of constrained access to training. Without concerted succession planning and improved training opportunities Georgia’s ability to sustain essential public health functions, especially as needs evolve, may be undermined.

There have been very few other public health workforce surveys in middle-income countries, limiting comparisons; however, some findings align with what has been found in high-income countries. Sellers et al. found that the US state public health workforce is also heavily female (72%) and has a median age of 50 [14]. Similarly, Robin et al. found that 81% of local public health workers were female and also trended older [31]. In Georgia, the workforce is similarly female-dominated and older. Unlike the US, where most public health workers had been in their positions for 5 years or less [31], public health workers in Georgia have had long tenures within their organization but limited career mobility.

The core public health workforce density of 30.1 employees per 100,000 population in Georgia is lower than other countries in the region, such as Poland (44.7), Moldova (57.1), and Slovenia (58.0), and falls significantly short of high-income countries such as Switzerland (102.6) and the United States (104.2) [32, 33]. As the definition of public health workforce across these countries is not consistent, however, comparisons are difficult. Within Georgia, we found surprisingly high variation in the density of MPHC workers. An earlier study in Georgia also noted the unequal distribution of public health workers but observed higher concentrations in urban rather than rural areas [13]. Explanatory factors that we considered, such as the presence of an NCDC regional office, which might substitute for MPHC staff, or MPHCs with a higher density of staffing having an older workforce (who were disinclined to retire), were not supported by the data. The particularly low workforce density in Adjara is likely explained by a different staffing model in this autonomous region, where there is far greater centralization of public health staff, which may present a more efficient model for the country to consider in the future. Otherwise, we believe regional variations may be due to idiosyncratic staffing practices within MPHCs that could be addressed through clearer central guidelines on staffing. Since these findings were presented, the Ministry of Health (MoH) and NCDC have issued such guidance.

Compared with the US, Georgian public health workers appear to have higher levels of formal education. Bogaert et al. reported that only 19% of US state health employees had a degree in a public health domain at any educational level [16]. A significant percentage of Georgian public health employees have public health or preventative medicine degrees. Also, 56% of the workforce in Georgia have a master’s degree compared to 31% in the US [16]. However, this comparison is nuanced due to the structure of the education system in Georgia until 1994, when single-cycle programs were offered and considered equivalent to a master’s degree [30]. Nonetheless, this observation of a more highly educated workforce than other countries aligns with other studies on the general health workforce that note that Georgia has a high availability of qualified physicians, even compared to other countries in the region [25].

Public health workforce salaries in Georgia are relatively low compared to other similar roles within the country. The National Statistics Office in Georgia reported an average monthly salary of GEL 1,823 for public administration and social security workers [34], approximately twice that of many key public health positions. Our discussions with universities offering public health courses suggested that lower salaries compared to private sector employees deter graduates from opting for government positions. Both satisfaction and motivation among Georgian public health workers appeared high despite limited prospects for promotion and relatively low pay. Other studies of health worker motivation in Georgia have also found this [35] and noted that factors such as good relationships with supervisors, and a sense of meaning in work are key. However, it should be noted that US studies have shown high job satisfaction but also a high intent to leave [14, 31]. Since the survey was employer-led, we avoided questions about employees’ intention to leave, but turnover among public health workers in Georgia appears very low. The findings may reflect a combination of genuine public service motivation, limited alternative employment opportunities in public health, and possible social desirability bias. These competing explanations cannot be disentangled with the current data, and future research should use independent survey channels and include questions on intention to stay.

We faced challenges in collecting accurate data on training conducted during the past 2 years, but responses suggest that improved training opportunities may enhance workforce motivation. This resonates with an earlier study that also concluded that improved opportunities for continuing education were key [13]. The higher median age among trainees suggests that younger members of the workforce may require more targeted training interventions to support long-term career development goals.

The finding that the largest shares of NCDC employee time are spent on administration (21.9%) and surveillance and response (21.1%), while only minimal time is devoted to workforce development, health promotion, and policy work, also warrants attention. A workforce spending more than one-fifth of its collective effort on administrative tasks may face structural inefficiencies that might hinder its capacity to deliver EPHFs. Efforts to streamline administrative processes and rebalance time allocation could meaningfully expand the workforce’s impact.

This survey indicates several areas in which NCDC may intervene to sustain public health workforce strength into the future: most notable is a need to recruit public health workers to address areas of emerging importance such as environmental health and health promotion (especially for NCDs), to provide competitive salaries to attract a younger workforce, and to strengthen the array of training opportunities offered. This profile of Georgia’s public health workforce allows the government to understand better its capabilities to prevent, detect, and respond to public health threats. Understanding training needs would help the government and partners tailor capacity-building, strengthen career pathways, and allocate resources more efficiently.

A draft public health workforce strategy reflecting most of these recommendations is currently being processed by the MoH for finalization and approval. Salaries for the public health workforce were increased by approximately 10% for each cadre in early 2024 after this survey was conducted, and several other changes are also underway.

Limitations of the study

This survey was sponsored and administered by NCDC, and while respondents were assured that their responses would remain anonymous, there may nonetheless be a social desirability bias in responses, leading for example to participants over-stating job satisfaction and motivation. The survey also received lower responses from NCDC compared to MPHC staff, and the low NCDC response rate (69.7%) may introduce nonresponse bias. Self-reporting of key variables including time allocation across program areas, training participation, and promotion history may be subject to recall bias or self-motivated reporting.

We also recognize that by focusing on the core public health workforce, our survey failed to cover many health workers who contribute to public health as part of their jobs (albeit not their primary focus). Accordingly, the survey provides an incomplete picture of how public health functions are carried out in Georgia. Finally, the cross-sectional design means the survey captures a single point in time and cannot assess trends in workforce composition over the years, nor can it establish causal relationships between workforce conditions (such as training access or promotion) and outcomes (such as satisfaction or performance). These limitations should be weighed when interpreting and applying the findings.

Conclusions

This is the first national survey of Georgia’s core public health workforce. It provides an overview of workforce distribution by job function and geography, key workforce characteristics, and levels of satisfaction, motivation, and engagement. The insights are critical in identifying and addressing workforce planning and development bottlenecks. First, understanding how the current workforce spends their time aids in aligning workforce planning to public health needs and the country's disease profile. Second, the findings indicate the importance of accelerating investment in training, especially for younger members of the workforce.

Finally, this initial effort to profile the public health workforce could guide similar assessments in the future in Georgia, providing an opportunity to track how human resource policies are affecting the workforce. Georgia’s experience could also serve as a reference to other post-Soviet countries in the region as they advance their public health workforce to address country and regional priorities. These efforts contribute to improving health security and pandemic preparedness. Similar assessments of the public health workforce elsewhere could help provide an evidence-based approach to workforce planning and development.
