Multidimensional cognitive deficit in logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia: a case report
Authors: Vivien Puspitasari, Yetty Ramli, Diatri Nari Lastri, Pukovisa Prawiroharjo, Jeremiah Hilkiah Wijaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLogopenic variant primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA) is a language-led presentation of Alzheimer’s disease that is easily overlooked in older patients with vascular and systemic comorbidities.Case presentationWe report a 78-year-old right-handed Asian woman with hypertension, diabetes, hepatic cirrhosis, and coronary artery disease who developed insidious word-finding difficulty progressing to severe anomia, impaired sentence repetition, and multidomain cognitive decline. Serial Indonesian MMSE, MoCA-Ina, Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD), and Tes Afasia untuk Diagnosis, Informasi, dan Rehabilitasi (TADIR) assessments showed early amnestic and executive deficits followed by dominant phonological and naming impairment, while MRI revealed left-predominant temporo-parietal and medial temporal atrophy with small-vessel white matter disease, consistent with mixed Alzheimer and vascular pathology. She received donepezil, low-dose clobazam, and targeted speech-language and cognitive rehabilitation with partial symptomatic benefit.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of detailed language assessment and culturally adapted neuropsychological batteries to identify lvPPA within mixed dementia in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia, Alzheimer’s disease

Introduction

Primary progressive aphasia (PPA) is a clinical syndrome characterized by a gradual and predominant impairment of language, accounting for roughly 20–40% of focal cortical degenerative syndromes presenting before 65 years of age [1, 2]. The logopenic variant (lvPPA), most strongly associated with underlying Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology, is marked by impaired word retrieval and sentence repetition with relatively preserved grammar and motor speech, and represents approximately one-third to one-half of PPA cases in specialist cohorts [3]. In contrast, typical amnestic AD remains far more common, constituting around 60–70% of all dementia diagnoses worldwide, which contributes to the under-recognition of language-led AD phenotypes such as lvPPA [3]. lvPPA is frequently overlooked, particularly in settings where cognitive screening tools and clinical heuristics are oriented toward memory-predominant presentations of dementia [4]. Coexisting small-vessel disease and systemic comorbidities in older adults further obscure the clinical picture and complicate etiological attribution.

We report the case of an elderly woman with mixed dementia in whom lvPPA emerged as the dominant and most disabling clinical phenotype, characterized longitudinally using an Indonesian neuropsychological battery. This case is notable for the intersection of Alzheimer-type and vascular pathology, hepatic cirrhosis, and a language-led presentation in a resource-limited context without access to cerebrospinal fluid or positron emission tomography (PET) biomarkers, which remain unavailable to most patients globally. It highlights the clinical relevance of detailed language assessment and serial functional evaluation in patients initially labeled as having “typical” AD or vascular cognitive impairment. By describing the clinical course, neuroimaging findings, and targeted management of this patient, we aim to refine diagnostic reasoning for lvPPA within mixed dementia and to emphasize its occurrence beyond high-income, highly specialized centers. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines [5].

Case description

A 78-year-old right-handed Asian woman, whose primary language is Bahasa Indonesia, was referred to our neurology clinic for progressive language and cognitive difficulties. She had worked as a housewife throughout adulthood and lived with her husband and adult daughter. Her medical history included long-standing hypertension (20 years), type 2 diabetes mellitus (15 years), hepatic cirrhosis related to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, and coronary heart disease status post coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in 2018, with reasonably controlled vascular risk factors (home blood pressures 120–140/70–80 mmHg, HbA1c 6.8–7.2%). She had no history of stroke or transient ischemic attack, did not smoke, denied alcohol use, and had no family history of dementia or other neurodegenerative disease. Chronic medications included an angiotensin receptor blocker, beta-blocker, low-dose aspirin, statin, metformin, basal insulin, and low-dose spironolactone for portal hypertension.

Her symptoms began insidiously around early 2018, when she was evaluated preoperatively for CABG and reported about one year of word-finding difficulty. She experienced frequent “tip-of-the-tongue” episodes for low-frequency nouns and used circumlocutions (for example, “the thing for cutting paper” instead of “scissors”), while phrase length, grammar, and articulation remained preserved; there were no problems in everyday comprehension, behavior, or basic activities of daily living (ADL). Initial screening with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment–Indonesia (MoCA-Ina) showed mild impairment, predominantly in attention and delayed recall, with only subtle language involvement (Table 1). She was advised to optimize vascular risk factor control and attend cognitive therapy but was lost to follow-up.

During this approximately 5-year interval, the patient was managed at home without formal neurocognitive tracking. While this gap limits our ability to chart the precise early trajectory of her cognitive decline, collateral history from her daughter confirmed a slow, insidious progression of language and memory difficulties typical of a neurodegenerative disease.

In March 2023, she re-presented with clearly progressive symptoms: prominent word-finding pauses in spontaneous speech, difficulty understanding long or syntactically complex instructions, and increasing forgetfulness for recent information. She remained independent in basic ADL and simple household tasks, although she worked more slowly and relied on written reminders. Comprehensive neurocognitive assessment revealed an amnestic profile with impaired immediate and delayed recall, reduced recognition, executive dysfunction (slowed processing speed, reduced flexibility, and calculation/abstraction errors), and language deficits characterized by impaired confrontation naming with phonemic and semantic paraphasias and reduced verbal fluency; MMSE and MoCA-Ina scores were in the mild dementia range (Table 1). A diagnosis of probable Alzheimer’s dementia was made, with mixed dementia (Alzheimer’s disease plus vascular cognitive impairment) considered, and donepezil 5 mg/day was initiated and titrated to 10 mg/day.

One month later, she showed further decline in language and global cognition: slower, more hesitant speech with shorter utterances, increased word-finding pauses, and social withdrawal due to communication difficulties. Basic ADL were preserved, but instrumental ADL (IADL), such as managing finances and medications, now required supervision. Cognitive testing documented progression of orientation and processing speed deficits and a more pronounced decline in naming and semantic fluency (Table 1). By September 2024, she needed help with more complex IADL, including operating appliances and planning multi-step activities, and experienced disorientation in previously familiar environments. MMSE and MoCA-Ina scores had fallen into the moderate–severe range with marked memory and executive dysfunction (Table 1), but the most striking feature remained severe anomia with frequent pauses, circumlocutions, and phonological errors, along with impaired repetition of long, syntactically complex sentences and preserved motor speech and grammar, raising strong suspicion for lvPPA on top of Alzheimer-type dementia.

General physical examination showed a blood pressure of 132/78 mmHg, heart rate 72 beats/minute, and body mass index of 24 kg/m2. There were mild stigmata of chronic liver disease (palmar erythema and spider angiomas) but no jaundice or asterixis, and cardiovascular examination was unremarkable with no signs of heart failure. Neurological examination revealed no cranial nerve deficits, hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, extrapyramidal signs, cerebellar ataxia, or abnormal gait; muscle tone, strength, and reflexes were normal with flexor plantar responses bilaterally, and there were no features suggestive of motor neuron disease, corticobasal syndrome, or Lewy body disease. Baseline laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, electrolytes, renal function, thyroid function, vitamin B12 and folate, and HIV and syphilis serology, were unremarkable; liver tests showed mild transaminase and GGT elevation with preserved synthetic function (albumin 3.8 g/dL, INR 1.1) and normal ammonia, arguing against hepatic encephalopathy.

Non-contrast brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated generalized cerebral volume loss with mild left temporo-parietal predominance, medial temporal lobe atrophy compatible with Alzheimer-type pathology, and moderate periventricular and deep white matter hyperintensities consistent with chronic small-vessel ischemic disease, without acute infarcts, cortical strokes, microbleeds, or cortical superficial siderosis (Fig. 1). These findings supported a mixed pattern of neurodegeneration and small-vessel disease. Carotid Doppler ultrasound showed non-hemodynamically significant atherosclerotic plaques, and transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function without intracardiac thrombus or shunt. Cerebrospinal fluid and PET biomarkers were not available at our center, limiting pathophysiologic confirmation, but the clinical–radiologic profile strongly suggested underlying Alzheimer’s disease.

In February 2025, detailed language and cognitive profiling was performed exclusively by the neurology team using a standardized Indonesian battery analogous to the Tes Asesmen Dini dan Interpretasi Risiko (TADIR), as a formal, dedicated evaluation by a certified Speech-Language Pathologist to characterize motor speech, prosody, and grammatical production in detail was not available at our center (Table 1) [6]. The assessment confirmed severe anomic aphasia with both phonemic and semantic paraphasias, marked impairment in confrontation naming and sentence repetition and reduced verbal fluency, with relative preservation of single-word comprehension and repetition of short, high-frequency phrases. While additional isolated measures of phonological working memory were not available in our standard clinical battery, we relied on the forward and backward digit span (FDS/BDS) and sentence repetition tasks as clinical proxies. Reading aloud was slowed with occasional phonological errors. Since this February 2025 evaluation, the patient has continued to experience a slow functional decline. She currently requires maximal assistance for instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs) and moderate assistance for basic ADLs, remaining at home under her daughter's care.

Functionally, she declined from fully independent ADL and IADL at baseline to requiring supervision for medication management, financial tasks, and transportation, although she continued to feed, bathe, and dress herself with minimal assistance until late in the course (Table 2). Her daughter, the primary caregiver, reported increasing burden due to communication difficulties and the patient’s anxiety when unable to find words or follow conversations. There were no persistent hallucinations, delusions, or disinhibition; apathy and social withdrawal were mild and appeared related to frustration with her language deficits. Standardized mood screening indicated clinically significant anxiety without major depression.

She continued donepezil 10 mg/day, which was well tolerated and associated with transient subjective stabilization. She was also started on low-dose clobazam 5 mg at night to address severe, distressing anxiety and sleep disturbance that were significantly impacting her quality of life. The choice and dosage of clobazam were made cautiously, with careful consideration of her underlying hepatic cirrhosis and potential alterations in drug metabolism. While benzodiazepines can exacerbate cognitive impairment, the acute benefit of managing her severe agitation and sleep fragmentation was deemed to outweigh these risks at that clinical stage. Furthermore, the patient’s progressive cognitive decline was already well-documented prior to the initiation of this medication, suggesting that clobazam was not the primary driver of her worsening neurocognitive status.

The patient underwent tailored speech and language therapy incorporating semantic feature analysis to systematically aid word retrieval, and spaced retrieval training to reinforce functional communication. These specific approaches were utilized because they are established compensatory strategies for the phonological and lexical retrieval deficits characteristic of lvPPA, along with cognitive rehabilitation using compensatory tools such as written prompts and caregiver-mediated cueing. Daily home-based cognitive stimulation was encouraged, and caregiver education sessions aimed to optimize communication, reduce frustration, and maintain safety and autonomy as far as possible.

Discussion

Logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA) is characterized by impaired word retrieval and sentence repetition with relatively preserved grammar and motor speech, and typically associated with left temporo-parietal atrophy on imaging [7]. In published series, lvPPA accounts for roughly one-third to one-half of PPA cases and is strongly linked to Alzheimer's pathology on biomarkers or autopsy, in contrast to the FTLD spectrum seen more often in nonfluent and semantic variants [8]. Crucially, our patient’s presentation mapped directly onto the established 2011 Gorno-Tempini consensus criteria for lvPPA (Table 3). She exhibited both core clinical features: impaired single-word retrieval in spontaneous speech and naming, and markedly impaired repetition of sentences. Furthermore, she met the necessary supportive features, including the presence of phonological paraphasias, with relatively spared motor speech, spared single-word comprehension, and an absence of frank agrammatism. In our patient, the prominent anomia, frequent phonemic and semantic paraphasias, disproportionate difficulty with long sentence repetition, and preserved grammar and articulation, together with left-predominant temporo-parietal atrophy and medial temporal involvement, align closely with this theoretical profile [8].

The coexistence of global atrophy, medial temporal atrophy, and moderate small-vessel white matter disease supports a mixed AD and vascular substrate, which is common in older adults where mixed pathology is found in up to 50% of dementia brains at autopsy. While small-vessel disease frequently contributes to the observed executive dysfunction and slowed processing speed, the specific emergence of prominent phonological paraphasias and disproportionate difficulty with sentence repetition are classic cortical features highly specific to neurodegenerative lvPPA. This distinguishes her primary language deficit from the subcortical profile typical of isolated vascular cognitive impairment. Recognizing lvPPA within this mixed pathological context is essential, as it reframes a seemingly “typical” dementia into a language-led AD phenotype with specific management needs.

Our case illustrates how lvPPA may initially present with subtle word-finding difficulty that can be misattributed to normal aging or nonspecific cognitive decline, particularly when standard screening tools emphasize memory. Over several years, the patient’s evolution from mild word-finding pauses to severe anomia and impaired sentence repetition, alongside progressive but initially less striking memory and executive deficits, mirrors longitudinal descriptions of lvPPA trajectories in the literature. The anatomical correlation of her language profile with left temporo-parietal atrophy and small-vessel disease emphasizes the vulnerability of dorsal language and phonological working memory networks in this syndrome [9, 10]. At the same time, the presence of hepatic cirrhosis and vascular risk factors underscores a key pitfall: clinicians may over-attribute cognitive changes to metabolic or vascular encephalopathy, potentially delaying recognition of a primary neurodegenerative language disorder [9]. Our case reinforces the value of systematic language assessment and repeated, domain-specific testing (including local tools like TADIR) to disentangle overlapping etiologies and to avoid under-diagnosing lvPPA in complex medical patients. Furthermore, while baseline ammonia levels were normal, we acknowledge that a single measurement does not definitively exclude episodic or minimal hepatic encephalopathy, representing a limitation in fully ruling out metabolic contributions.

Several limitations should be considered in the interpretation of this case. First, while the clinical-radiological profile strongly favors an underlying Alzheimer's pathology, the lack of advanced biomarker confirmation means that alternative pathologies cannot be definitively excluded. Therefore, our diagnosis remains a "clinically probable" syndrome rather than a pathologically definitive one. Second, although baseline ammonia levels were normal, a single measurement does not definitively rule out episodic or minimal hepatic encephalopathy, representing a limitation in fully excluding metabolic contributions. Finally, formal evaluation by a certified Speech-Language Pathologist and isolated, standardized measures of phonological working memory were unavailable; our language profiling relied exclusively on the neurologist-administered TADIR battery, using digit span and sentence repetition as proxies for working memory.

From a practical standpoint, this case highlights both the possibilities and limitations of managing lvPPA with mixed dementia in resource-limited settings. Although advanced biomarkers such as CSF amyloid/tau and amyloid PET remain unavailable to many patients worldwide, careful clinical phenotyping, standardized neuropsychological testing, and basic MRI can still yield a syndromic diagnosis consistent with AD-related lvPPA, as demonstrated here. The partial stabilization of daily function with a cholinesterase inhibitor and targeted speech-language therapy aligns with data suggesting symptomatic benefit of AD-directed and compensatory interventions even when disease-modifying options are lacking [11]. However, while the clinical-radiological profile strongly favors an underlying Alzheimer's pathology, the lack of biomarker confirmation means that frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) cannot be definitively excluded. Therefore, our diagnosis remains a "clinically probable" syndrome rather than a pathologically definitive one.

The partial stabilization of daily function with a cholinesterase inhibitor and targeted speech-language therapy aligns with data suggesting symptomatic benefit of AD-directed and compensatory interventions even when disease-modifying options are lacking [12, 13]. However, our case also exposes pitfalls: caregiver burden, communication-driven anxiety, and the risk that lvPPA is overlooked in epidemiological and service-planning statistics, where language-led dementias remain under-represented compared with the 60–70% of cases labeled as “typical” amnestic AD [12, 13].

Conclusion

This case illustrates how logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia can emerge as the dominant and most disabling clinical phenotype in an elderly patient with mixed Alzheimer and vascular pathology, multiple systemic comorbidities, and limited access to advanced biomarkers. The patient’s progressive anomia, impaired sentence repetition, preserved grammar and motor speech, and left temporo-parietal atrophy are highly consistent with the theoretical lvPPA profile, demonstrating that careful clinical phenotyping and basic MRI can still yield a robust syndromic diagnosis. Our experience underscores the importance of systematic language assessment, longitudinal neuropsychological follow-up, and close caregiver involvement to avoid misattributing symptoms solely to “typical” amnestic dementia, vascular cognitive impairment, or metabolic encephalopathy. Recognizing lvPPA in such complex presentations allows more tailored pharmacological and rehabilitative strategies, and highlights the need for greater awareness of language-led Alzheimer phenotypes in routine clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings.
