Impact of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol on cardiovascular diseases and all-cause mortality: a large-scale cohort study in Korea
Authors: Youn Huh, Ki Young Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the association of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and all-cause mortality and to determine differences based on the presence or absence of a history of CVDs. This retrospective cohort study included 343,321 adults aged ≥ 40 years whose survey data were recorded in the Korean National Health Insurance Service database from 2009 to 2010. HDL-C levels were divided into five groups. The association among HDL-C levels, CVDs, and all-cause mortality was examined using multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. During a mean follow-up period of 9.5 ± 2.0 years, CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes occurred in 8.3%, 5.9%, and 13.2% of the study population, respectively. HDL-C levels were inversely associated with CVDs and composite outcomes, with a U-shaped association between HDL-C levels and all-cause mortality. Compared with normal HDL-C levels, very low HDL-C levels and very high HDL-C levels were associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality. Individuals without preexisting CVDs showed similar patterns. Very low HDL-C and low HDL-C levels in individuals with preexisting CVDs were associated with a higher risk of outcomes. Our results suggest that very high HDL-C levels are associated with a decreased risk of CVDs and an increased risk of all-cause mortality. These associations varied according to preexisting CVDs status, sex, and age, challenging the notion that a higher HDL-C level is universally beneficial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50885-5.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 30% of all deaths in 20161. In Korea, the number of hospitalizations for CVDs increased 4.5-fold in 2018 compared to 20022, with CVDs being the second leading cause of death1. Furthermore, approximately 70% of Korean adults had at least one risk factor for CVDs such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, or dyslipidemia, whereas approximately 20% had three or more risk factors3. Thus, the burden of CVDs is continually increasing, underscoring the importance of primary and secondary prevention against CVDs.

High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) transports cholesterol from atherosclerotic arteries to the liver or steroidogenic organs4 and is known to exhibit an inverse association with CVDs5. In atherosclerotic CVDs risk estimators, high HDL-C levels are considered to exert a protective effect against atherosclerotic CVDs6,7. Nevertheless, recent results have raised questions regarding the protective effect of HDL-C. In particular, a previous study revealed that cholesteryl ester transfer inhibitors increased the HDL-C levels and decreased the low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels but did not reduce the risk of CVD outcomes8. Additionally, some recent cohort studies concluded that the association between very high HDL-C levels and CVD outcomes was different from previous results9–11. One previous study involving approximately 400,000 UK individuals without preexisting coronary artery diseases showed that HDL-C levels > 80 mg/dL were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality9. Another study involving 19,945 Western populations with preexisting coronary artery diseases demonstrated that HDL-C levels > 80 mg/dL were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality10. A previous study on relatively healthy Korean adults reported a U-shaped association between HDL-C levels and CVD mortality11. However, some studies showed that very high HDL-C levels were not associated with the risk of CVD outcomes12–14. A study including 17,802 individuals who were taking statins to lower LDL-C levels in the JUPITER trial revealed that high HDL-C levels did not decrease the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, unstable angina, and CVD mortality12.

Few studies have distinguished between CVDs and all-cause mortality. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association of HDL-C levels with CVDs and all-cause mortality and to determine the differences based on the presence or absence of a history of CVDs using nationally representative cohort data. Due to the limited evidence stratified by a history of CVDs, we aimed to clarify the association of HDL with both primary and secondary prevention of CVDs.

Methods

Data source and study population

Our study used nationally representative data from the South Korean National Health Insurance Service-Health Screening Cohort (NHIS-HEALS; https://nhiss.nhis.or.kr/), which included information on participants who underwent national health screening provided by the Korean NHIS at least once every 2 years15. Participants were aged 40–79 years in 2002, representing approximately 10% of all health screenings during this period, and were followed up until 2019. The NHIS-HEALS database contains sociodemographic information, health risk factors, laboratory results, medical diagnosis (based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] codes), and treatment.

The initial sample consisted of 462,610 adults, aged ≥ 40 years in 2002–2003, who underwent a health examination between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2010. Among them, 118,575 individuals with missing study variables and 714 individuals with HDL-C levels > 200 mg/dL were excluded. A final sample of 343,321 participants (185,086 men and 158,235 women) was included in the analyses.

HDL-C categories and study covariates

The Korean NHIS-HEALS database contains data on participants’ smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity obtained from a self-reported questionnaire survey. Smoking status was categorized according to smoking history into never smoker, ex-smoker, or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was categorized into non-drinker or drinker. In accordance with the World Health Organization recommendations16, regular exercise was defined as the performance of moderate or vigorous activity for ≥ 150 or ≥ 75 min/week, respectively. The participants’ height and weight were recorded, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Based on the definition of obesity for the Asia-Pacific region from the World Health Organization17, obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (obesity). Place of residence was categorized into urban (metropolitan and city) and rural areas. Low-income level was defined as a group that included participants in the lowest 20th percentile of income level. Disability was defined based on national disability registration data.

Blood samples were obtained after overnight fasting to measure the fasting plasma glucose, lipid battery, and creatinine levels. HDL-C levels were divided into five groups: very low (≤ 30 mg/dL), low (31–40 mg/dL), normal (41–60 mg/dL), high (61–80 mg/dL), and very high (> 80 mg/dL) reported in previous population-based study18. Type 2 diabetes was identified based on a fasting plasma glucose concentration of ≥ 126 mg/dL or at least one medication prescription claim per year with ICD-10 codes E11–E14. Hypertension was defined as BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg or at least one medication prescription claim per year with ICD-10-CM codes I10–I13 or I15. Chronic kidney disease was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease Study equation. Lipid-lowering agents such as hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA reductase inhibitors were defined as those prescribed from 2009 to 2010.

Study outcomes and follow-up

Outcomes included incident CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes. CVDs were identified from hospital discharge claims data containing ICD-10 codes for angina (I20), acute myocardial infarction (I21–I22), and stroke (I63–I66). Composite outcomes were defined as CVDs and all-cause mortality. A history of CVDs was defined as a diagnosis before the 2009–2010 health examination. Information on all-cause mortality was obtained from the records of the Korea National Statistical Office. Individuals were tracked until December 31, 2019, or until the occurrence of any of the study outcomes. The mean follow-up duration was 9.5 ± 2.0 years.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were stratified by HDL-C categories and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables or as number (proportion) for categorical variables. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using analysis of variance and chi-square test, respectively. The cumulative incidence probability of the study outcomes was calculated according to HDL-C categories using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences in HDL-C categories were analyzed using the log-rank test. Associations between continuous HDL-C levels and outcomes were investigated using log hazard ratio (HR) curves that permitted nonlinear associations based on the lowest HDL-C level as the reference in each population using restricted cubic spline models with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of HDL-C distribution. The incidence rates of CVDs were calculated by dividing the number of events by 1,000 person-years. The HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes were analyzed using multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models according to HDL-C categories. The following four models were used in our study: Model 1 was not adjusted for any variables; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, income, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, LDL-C levels, and triglyceride levels; and Model 4 was adjusted for lipid-lowering agents in addition to the variables in Model 3. Analyses were performed based on the presence or absence of a history of CVDs. Additionally, analyses stratified by age, sex, and statin use were conducted. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and the R package, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Association between HDL-C categories and study outcomes

During a mean follow-up period of 9.5 ± 2.0 years, CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes occurred in 8.3% (n = 28,481), 5.9% (n = 20,416), and 13.2% (n = 45,276) of the study population, respectively.

Figure 1 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for the incidence probabilities of the study outcomes according to HDL-C categories in all participants. The very low HDL-C group had the highest incidence probabilities of CVDs and composite outcomes, whereas the high HDL-C group had the lowest incidence probabilities (all log-rank p < 0.001). The incidence probability of all-cause mortality was the highest in the very low HDL-C group, followed by the low HDL-C, very high HDL-C, normal HDL-C, and high HDL-C groups (log-rank p < 0.001).

Table 2 shows the association between HDL-C levels and study outcomes. Among total participants, the high HDL-C group had lower HRs for CVDs and composite outcomes. Compared with the normal HDL-C group, the HRs for CVDs were 1.36 (95% CI, 1.25–1.47) and 0.79 (95% CI, 0.74–0.85) in the very low HDL-C and very high HDL-C groups, respectively. Furthermore, compared with the normal HDL-C group, the HRs for composite outcomes were 1.44 (95% CI, 1.35–1.53) and 0.94 (95% CI, 0.89–0.99) in the very low HDL-C and very high HDL-C groups, respectively. Compared with the normal HDL-C group, the HRs for all-cause mortality were 1.56 (95% CI, 1.44–1.70) in the very low HDL-C group, 1.16 (95% CI, 1.11–1.20) in the low HDL-C group, and 1.18 (95% CI, 1.10–1.27) in the very high HDL-C group. Individuals without preexisting CVDs showed similar patterns. In individuals with preexisting CVDs, very low HDL-C and low HDL-C levels were associated with a higher risk of outcomes. Very low HDL-C levels were associated with 1.3-, 1.5-, and 1.4-fold higher risk of CVDs (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.10–1.48), all-cause mortality (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.23–1.77), and composite outcomes (HR, 1.35; 95% CI, 1.19–1.52). Subgroup analysis

Figure 3 shows the association between HDL-C categories and the risk of outcomes after stratification by sex, age, and lipid-lowering agents in all participants. These trends were observed in most subgroups. Significant interactions with sex (p for interaction < 0.001) and age (p for interaction < 0.001 for CVDs and p for interaction = 0.010 for composite outcomes) were observed in the association between HDL-C categories and the risk of outcomes. These associations were more prominent in men and in adults aged 40–64 years than in women and in older adults, respectively. Additionally, among individuals without preexisting CVDs, the association between HDL-C categories and outcomes was more pronounced in men and younger adults aged 40–64 years than in their counterparts (Supplementary Fig. S1). In individuals with preexisting CVDs, the association between HDL-C categories and the risk of all-cause mortality was more pronounced in women than in men (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated the association of HDL-C levels with CVDs and determined the differences based on the presence or absence of a history of CVDs. The association of HDL-C levels with CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes showed different patterns. The risk of CVDs and all-cause mortality was inversely associated with HDL-C levels, with a U-shaped association; composite outcomes were not associated with very high HDL-C levels. These associations were stronger in men and younger adults, excluding those with preexisting CVDs. These results indicated that the association of HDL-C levels with CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes differed based on the presence or absence of preexisting CVDs.

Some studies reported an inverse association between HDL-C levels and CVDs19–22. A previous study involving 53,813 adults showed that a 1 SD increase in HDL-C levels was associated with a 38% decreased risk of myocardial infarction (odds ratio, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.58–0.66)19. A previous cohort study of 8,665,841 Korean adults aged ≥ 19 years reported that the inverse association between HDL-C levels and MACEs (including myocardial infarction, stroke, and all-cause mortality) and the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and all-cause mortality increased in individuals with lower HDL-C levels compared to those with the highest HDL-C quartile21. In this study, the highest HDL-C quartile cutoffs were 59 mg/dL in men and 67 mg/dL in women, respectively21. One meta-analysis of 24 studies involving 69,145 individuals from the Asia-Pacific region demonstrated that low HDL-C levels were associated with an increased risk of coronary heart disease compared to normal HDL-C levels (HR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.31–1.87)22. Some possible mechanisms of these associations have been identified: low HDL-C levels induce endothelial cell dysfunction, activate antithrombotic production, and inhibit platelet aggregation23, whereas high HDL-C levels play an important role in the immune system through pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory particle24. In addition, individuals with high HDL-C levels tended to have better health condition such as lower BMI and lower prevalence of smoking and comorbidities9. Thus, high HDL-C levels may decrease the risk of CVDs through these probable mechanisms.

However, our study revealed different results for CVDs and all-cause mortality. Our results were consistent with previous studies reporting an U-shaped association between HDL-C and all-cause mortality25,26. An observational study including 116,508 adults without preexisting CVDs demonstrated a U-shaped association between HDL-C and all-cause mortality for both men and women26. Men and women with very high HDL-C levels exhibited a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with those with normal HDL-C levels (HR, 2.63; 95% CI, 1.84–3.75 for men and HR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.29–3.04 for women)26. Unlike our results, another study including 14,478 UK adults and 5467 US adults with preexisting CVDs identified that very high HDL-C levels were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality after adjusting confounding variables (HR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.42–2.72 in UK and HR, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.09–2.43), compared with normal HDL-C levels10. Our results indicated that high and very high HDL-C levels were not significantly associated with the risk of all-cause mortality in individuals with preexisting CVDs, although the analysis using a continuous scale (Fig. 2C) is indicative of a trend in this group that is similar to that observed in previous studies. However, further studies are required to confirm these associations. The mechanisms underlying these results also remain unclear. The first possible mechanism is that high HDL-C increases some genetic polymorphisms, and some variants increase the risk of CVDs27. Another possible explanation is that very high HDL-C levels may lead to dysfunctional HDL-C, resulting in the frailty of endothelial progenitor cells28.

In the present study, very high HDL-C levels were associated with a decreased risk of CVDs and an increased risk of all-cause mortality, but was not associated with the risk of composite outcomes after adjustment for confounding variables. Among all participants and individuals without preexisting CVDs, these relationships were stronger in men and younger adults. These tendencies were more prominent in men than in women in previous studies because of differences in sex hormone administration and plasma lipid metabolism11,26. In addition, previous studies demonstrated that younger adults had more prominent associations than older adults because of higher genetic risk factors29 and decreased risk of CVDs30. However, these associations were more pronounced in women than in men among individuals with preexisting CVDs. Therefore, when setting an appropriate HDL-C level to reduce the risk of CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes, it may be necessary to consider not only the presence or absence of preexisting CVDs but also sex and age.

The present study had some limitations. First, our study might have demonstrated a reverse causality between the HDL-C category and CVDs based on the retrospective nature of our study. Second, the Korean NHIS-HEALS database was used for prescription purposes, and our definitions of outcomes might have been under-diagnosed or over-diagnosed. In addition, CVDs were defined using ICD-10 codes derived from administrative data, which were subject to misclassification bias. In addition, information regarding the validation of these diagnostic codes was not available, which may affect the accuracy of outcome ascertainment. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution. Third, this study included relatively healthy individuals who were eligible for health checkups. Therefore, additional studies using hospital or external data are needed to circumvent this limitation and confirm our results. Fourth, given that the Korean NHIS-HEALS database does not include several potentially relevant variables, such as genetic factors27, dietary patterns31, and inflammatory markers, residual confounding may have influenced the observed associations. Furthermore, our database did not include measures of cholesterol efflux or HDL particle function; therefore, we were unable to assess the association between HDL functionality and our outcomes. Further studies addressing this issue are warranted. Another limitation of this study is the possibility of residual confounding and reverse causality in the observed U-shaped association between HDL-C and all-cause mortality. Although we adjusted for multiple covariates, unmeasured or incompletely measured factors such as underlying chronic illness, frailty, and alcohol consumption patterns may have influenced the results. Finally, this study only included Korean adults.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. Using a large-scale nationwide database, we demonstrated the association of HDL-C levels with CVDs, all-cause mortality, and composite outcomes, as well as the differences in such association according to the presence or absence of preexisting CVDs. In addition, we performed analyses adjusted for important confounding variables and conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, and lipid-lowering agents. The present study is the first to investigate the association between HDL-C levels and the risk of outcomes according to the presence or absence of preexisting CVDs in an Asian population.

Conclusion

Very high HDL-C levels were associated with a decreased risk of CVDs and an increased risk of all-cause mortality among total population and individuals without preexisting CVDs. Furthermore, very high HDL-C levels were not associated with the risk of outcomes among individuals with preexisting CVDs. Our findings revised to state that although appropriate HDL-C levels may be associated with CVD risk, clinically applicable thresholds remain to be determined, and further research is required to evaluate their role in risk prediction. In addition, our results challenge previous findings that higher HDL-C levels are better among all individuals, including those with preexisting CVDs.
