Evaluation and Comparison of Antifungal Efficacy and Mechanical Properties of Acrylic-based Tissue Conditioner when Incorporated with p-Cymene and Triphala
Authors: Rajesh Shetty, Kevin J. Lasrado, Amar S. Lobo, Mallikarjuna Ragher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Tissue conditioners play a vital role in managing denture stomatitis, but alone may not inhibit Candida albicans, the primary causative organism. Plant-derived agents, like p-Cymene and Triphala, offer antimicrobial benefits with lower resistance risks.Materials and Methods:Forty-two samples (n = 14 per group) were divided into Control, p-Cymene, and Triphala groups. Antifungal efficacy was tested via agar diffusion at 24 hours and four days. Hardness, tensile strength, and water sorption were evaluated on day 4.Statistical Analysis:Paired t-test assessed changes over time; one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD were used for intergroup comparisons.Results:Triphala showed the highest antifungal activity at both time points. The control group had the greatest hardness, while tensile strength improved slightly in the Triphala group. Water sorption was lowest in the control group.Conclusion:Incorporation of p-Cymene and Triphala enhanced antifungal efficacy of tissue conditioners, with Triphala being superior, and maintained acceptable mechanical properties.

INTRODUCTION

Denture stomatitis is a prevalent mucosal condition in denture wearers, marked by inflammation or erythema of the palatal mucosa due to poor denture hygiene and maintenance. Candida species are the primary etiological agents, though factors, such as immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, anemia, HIV, prolonged corticosteroid use, and nutritional deficiencies, also contribute to its onset.[1] Tissue conditioners are temporary soft liners used to alleviate mucosal inflammation by cushioning occlusal forces.[2] Conventional antifungal agents, like fluconazole and ketoconazole, have been associated with adverse effects, drug resistance, and reduced efficacy due to salivary dilution when used topically.[3] Medicinal plants offer a promising alternative, being rich in bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and phenolics with proven antimicrobial properties.[4] Among these, p-Cymene, an aromatic hydrocarbon found in essential oils of plants like thyme and cumin, and Triphala, an Ayurvedic blend of Amla, Bibhitaki, and Haritaki, have shown antifungal activity. However, synthetic antifungals have been reported to affect the dimensional stability of tissue conditioners.[5]

METHODOLOGY

Study setting, design, and sampling

This in vitro experimental study was conducted in the Department of Prosthodontics and Department of Microbiology after obtaining ethical clearance from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (Approval No. YEC2/2023/037). A total of 42 samples were included based on a sample size calculation using G*Power for one-way ANOVA, considering an effect size of 0.5, 80% power, and 1% significance level. The samples were divided into three groups (n = 14 per group): Control (Coe Comfort), Coe Comfort with 9% p-Cymene, and Coe Comfort with 12.5% Triphala. Simple random sampling was employed to allocate the samples.

Method for candidal testing

For antifungal evaluation, three test solutions were prepared: 25 ml of tissue conditioner monomer served as the control; the second solution involved incorporating 9% p-Cymene diluted with distilled water into 25 ml of monomer; the third involved dissolving Triphala powder in distilled water to achieve a 12.5% concentration, mixed with 25 ml of monomer. The test organism used was Candida albicans ATCC 10231, standardized using BHI broth and SDA media to match a 0.5 McFarland standard (10⁶–10⁸ organisms/ml). Further dilution was performed to achieve 10≥–10⁴ organisms/ml. A lawn culture was prepared on Sabouraud agar plates, and three wells (8 mm × 2 mm) were punched into each plate. The test mixtures were placed into the wells and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours and stored further for up to four days. Antifungal efficacy was determined by measuring the maximum inhibition diameter using a divider.

Method for testing mechanical properties

Mechanical testing included hardness, tensile strength, and water sorption. Rectangular specimens for hardness were fabricated following ISO 10139-1 standards and immersed in Shelli’s artificial saliva at 37°C for four days. Hardness was measured using a Shore A durometer at five different points per sample. Dumbbell-shaped samples were used to assess tensile strength according to ASTM D412 standards, using a universal testing machine with a crosshead speed of 40 mm/min to record the maximum load. For water sorption, disk-shaped specimens (15 mm × 2 mm) were prepared, weighed initially (W1), immersed in artificial saliva for four days, dried, and reweighed (W2). Water sorption was calculated using the formula: (W2 − W1)/Surface Area, as per ADA Specification No. 12. All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS software version 25.

RESULTS

Antifungal testing showed that the control group exhibited no zone of inhibition at both 24 hours and four days, indicating no antifungal activity. Among the test groups, Triphala demonstrated the highest antifungal effect, with a mean inhibition zone of 24.33 ± 4.23 mm at 24 hours and 26.00 ± 4.60 mm at four days. In contrast, p-Cymene showed moderate inhibition zones of 14.00 ± 1.41 mm and 14.67 ± 1.21 mm at the respective time points [Table 1].

For water sorption, Triphala recorded the highest absorption (1.932 ± 1.160%), followed by p-Cymene (1.801 ± 0.383%), while the control group showed the lowest (1.224 ± 0.000%). A statistically significant difference was observed between the control and Triphala groups (P = 0.030), while the difference between the control and p-Cymene approached significance. There was no significant difference between p-Cymene and Triphala in water sorption values [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

The antifungal action of Triphala is believed to stem from phenolic compounds like tannic acid, chebulic acid, and flavonoids. These bioactives exert their effect by disrupting fungal cell metabolism through hydroxyl group toxicity, thereby inhibiting fungal growth. Similar findings have been reported by authors, where Triphala exhibited superior antimicrobial action compared to its individual components.[123] However, p-Cymene also demonstrated antifungal potential, though to a lesser degree, likely due to its ability to disrupt microbial membranes and inhibit biofilm formation.[4]

In terms of mechanical properties, the control group exhibited the highest hardness values, followed by Triphala and then p-Cymene. The reduced hardness observed in the p-Cymene group may be due to its liquid–liquid interaction with the monomer, forming a weaker gel matrix compared to the solid-liquid interaction seen in the Triphala formulation. This observation is consistent with previous research by Balahbib A et al., where incorporation of natural antifungal agents like terpineol resulted in reduced hardness of the modified tissue conditioner.[5] Despite these variations, all groups maintained hardness within the clinically acceptable range of 25–30 Shore A units, as noted in the literature by Sheldon Winkler, supporting their short-term clinical applicability.[6] Tensile strength was highest in the Triphala group, likely due to favorable cross-linking and polymer structure formation during storage in simulated saliva. This condition mimics the moist oral environment and contributes to leaching of ethanol esters, which then interact with the tissue conditioner powder, enhancing its strength and elasticity.[7]

CONCLUSION

Triphala and p-Cymene, when incorporated into acrylic-based tissue conditioners, exhibited effective antifungal activity against Candida albicans, with Triphala showing superior efficacy.
