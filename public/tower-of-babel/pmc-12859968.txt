Current concepts of Helicobacter pylori infection
Authors: Oluwakemi Opeyemi Akinpelu, Humphrey Sam Samuel, David Akpanke Undie, Francis Arinze Ibekwe, Onimisi Princewill Onotu, Emmanuel Edet Etim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Helicobacter pylori infection is a major cause of chronic gastritis, which can progress to a more serious gastroduodenal disease, such as a peptic ulcer, gastric cancer, or gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue. Typically acquired during childhood, H. pylori infection persists throughout life if left untreated, affecting nearly half of the world population, with prevalence varying based on geographic location and sanitation standards. The bacterium exhibits unique adaptations that enable colonization of the gastric epithelium in acidic environments. Its pathogenesis of H. pylori involves bacterial virulence factors, host immune interactions, and environmental influences, leading to varied disease outcomes. The established role of H. pylori in gastric cancer underscores the importance of screening and treatment. Diagnosis relies on invasive tests, such as endoscopy and the non-invasive tests including stool, breath and serological methods” with test selection based on patient history and resource availability. Standard treatments combine acid suppression with antibiotics and/or bismuth. However, rising antibiotic resistance poses a major challenge, emphasizing the need for antibiotic susceptibility testing, resistance surveillance, and improved antibiotic stewardship in managing H. pylori infections.

Introduction

Approximately half of the world's population is afflicted with H. pylori, making it one of the most prevalent illnesses worldwide, though prevalence rates differ by area. In wealthy countries, prevalence may be less than 20%, but in some developing countries, rates might reach 80–90%. The frequency is higher in low-income environments, packed homes, and specific minority groups. Social variables, such as socioeconomic position, living conditions, and ethnicity, have a substantial impact on transmission rates. Oral–oral and fecal–oral pathways are the main ways that it is transmitted [1]. Over their lifetime, a sizable proportion of infected people will develop clinically serious disease, even while the majority remain asymptomatic. Numerous gastrointestinal disorders are linked to Helicobacter pylori, a gram-negative, spiral-shaped bacterium that colonizes the human stomach. A complex interaction between host genetic vulnerability, environmental conditions, and bacterial virulence factors determines its pathogenicity. Urease, an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of urea to ammonia and neutralizes gastric acid locally, is the first way that H. pylori survive the acidic environment in the stomach after entering it [2]. The bacteria use its flagella for movement after establishing this protective niche, which enables it to pass through the mucous layer and approach the stomach epithelial cells. H. pylori use adhesins, which are surface molecules that adhere to host cell receptors specifically and ensure close contact with the stomach lining, to create a persistent infection. Several virulence factors, including the vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA) and the cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) protein, are used in the subsequent stage of disease. Through a type IV secretion system, CagA is injected into gastric epithelial cells, where it disrupts cellular signaling pathways, increases inflammation, and contributes to cellular alterations that may result in gastric cancer [1–3]. Conversely, VacA promotes tissue damage and immune evasion by inducing the creation of intracellular vacuoles and interfering with other cellular functions. Intense immune cell recruitment to the stomach mucosa caused by a persistent H. pylori infection causes chronic gastritis, which in turn can develop to peptic ulcer disease, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphomas, or gastric cancer [3].

The rationale for H. pylori treatment is rooted in its strong association with peptic ulcer disease, gastric MALT lymphoma, and non-cardia gastric adenocarcinoma. Eliminating the bacteria promotes ulcer healing, lowers the likelihood of ulcer recurrence, and is thought to be a preventive measure against stomach cancer, particularly when done prior to the development of significant premalignant alterations. According to national and international guidelines, individuals with a family history of gastric cancer, gastric MALT lymphoma, active or past peptic ulcers, and populations at high risk for gastric cancer should be tested for and treated for H. pylori. Alongside conventional regimens like bismuth-based quadruple therapy and adjuvant adjuncts like probiotics, new techniques and a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying bacterial resistance continue to direct advancements in treatment strategies, particularly as antibiotic resistance becomes a growing concern [4, 5].

The discovery of Helicobacter pylori dates to the 1980s, when Barry Marshall and Robin Warren identified this bacterium while examining gastric biopsies from patients suffering from chronic gastritis and peptic ulcers [6]. Prior to this discovery, the medical community widely believed that peptic ulcers and gastritis were caused by stress, spicy foods, and excessive stomach acid production. However, Marshall and Warren's groundbreaking work showed that a bacterial infection could also be a primary cause of these conditions. To prove the link between H. pylori and gastric disease, Marshall famously performed a self-experiment in 1984, where he ingested a culture of the bacteria. Within days, he developed symptoms of gastritis, including nausea and vomiting. In the following years, extensive epidemiological studies and clinical trials confirmed the association between H. pylori and several gastrointestinal diseases, such as gastritis and peptic ulcer disease (PUD). Symptoms include bloating, burning stomach pain, and, in severe cases, vomiting blood or passing black stools [6, 7]. This study aims to investigate the prevalence, virulence factors, and antibiotic resistance profiles of H. pylori by understanding its pathogenic mechanisms and treatment challenges. This study seeks to provide insights into improving diagnostic approaches and therapeutic interventions for H. pylori-related diseases. This article will focus on studying the connections between H. pylori infection and various systemic conditions, the trends of the infection in various parts of the world and establishing correlations between clinical manifestations and the presence of specific virulent factors or antibiotic-resistant strains.

Epidemiological studies emphasize the importance of global surveillance to monitor H. pylori prevalence and guide treatment strategies. As antibiotic resistance becomes a growing concern, understanding the geographical distribution and demographic factors influencing H. pylori infections will be essential for effective management and prevention efforts [8–14]. Research has shown possible connections between H. pylori infection and various systemic conditions, such as reduced hemoglobin in the blood, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, and cardiovascular diseases [76]. Public health must focus their efforts on creating awareness, reducing infections, and exploring different treatment choices such as vaccines and probiotics to address this increase in rates of infection. Current statistics and trends in H. pylori infection rates worldwide.

For the past 40 years now, a notable decrease in the occurrence of Helicobacter pylori infection worldwide has been reported. According to recent estimates, H. pylori infections affect approximately 4.4 billion people globally, making it a serious public health concern, especially given its links to peptic ulcer disease and stomach cancer [22, 77]. The global prevalence of H. pylori remains widespread around the world but has declined in more recent infants born between 2023 and 2025 on an average of 0.39–0.83% per year between 2011 and 2022. A large 2017 meta-analysis estimated that 4.4 billion individuals were infected in 2015, with notable differences or variations between regions. More recent estimates suggest a global prevalence of 43% from 2011 to 2022, with a more dramatic decline since 2011, especially in the WHO African region. Country-specific prevalence varies by year of study and method of diagnosis. Switzerland has been estimated at 18.9% in population-based data assembled. Historically, Pakistan estimates have reached 81% in earlier data, but recent urban series show declines, adding temporal and between-country heterogeneity. For India, pooled adult estimates from 12 studies suggest 59.5%, although local rates vary by setting and method. In Africa, studies suggest a frequency of up to 70.1%, with the US falling between 30 and 40%, 77.2%. in Turkey, and Switzerland recording a lower rate of 18.9%. Prevalence of this H. pylori is lower in high-income settings than in underdeveloped countries. The association between H. pylori and general anemia is inconsistent; a 2024–2025 meta-analysis had an extremely weak overall effect on the prevalence of anemia. In contrast, treatment trials show that eradication with iron improves ferritin and hemoglobin in the iron deficiency population, and guidelines endorse non-invasive testing for H. pylori in idiopathic unexplained iron deficiency and Anemia (IDA) after formal assessment [23–27]. We demarcate this by distinguishing population-level association (uncertain) from therapeutic benefit in specifically selected IDA populations.

Transmission and risk factors

Transmission from oral to oral happens through saliva, which can be exchanged through sharing utensils, kissing, or close oral contact. The importance of family contact in spreading the virus is shown by studies that reveal infected mothers are frequently the main source of transmission to their offspring [28].

Transmission through faecal–oral, particularly in places with inadequate sanitation, contaminated food and water supplies are important routes [28, 29].

Gastro-oral transmission, H. pylori can survive in stomach secretions, vomiting can also help spread the infection. This pathway is particularly common in children, whose shared living arrangements and close contact raise the risk of infection. Iatrogenic transmission occurs less frequently, if appropriate sterilizing standards are not followed, transmission may happen during medical procedures, such as endoscopy or dental care. In addition, unhealthy practices such as overcrowding, inadequate sanitation, and restricted access to medical treatments and people from lower socioeconomic backgrounds are more vulnerable. These circumstances greatly increase the frequency of H. pylori infection in poor countries [30, 31].

Helicobacter pylori infection risk is significantly influenced by a range of environmental factors, particularly socioeconomic status, exposure to heavy metals, and oral health conditions. A comprehensive understanding of these determinants is essential for the formulation of effective prevention strategies. Research indicates that H. pylori are commonly found in environmental waters, with an overall prevalence rate of 21.76%. This rate is notably elevated in rural areas, where it reaches 42.62%, in contrast to urban environments. The correlation between socioeconomic status and H. pylori prevalence suggests that individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds may experience increased infection risks due to inadequate hygiene and sanitation practices. In addition, exposure to heavy metals, specifically lead (Pb) and cadmium (Cd), has been linked to heightened rates of H. pylori infections. Those in populations with significant exposure to these metals demonstrate a markedly higher prevalence of infection [32]. Oral health also plays a critical role, as dental conditions such as periodontitis and dental caries have been recorded as significant risk factors for H. pylori colonization. Notably, rural populations show increased rates of infection, underscoring the necessity of oral health in the transmission dynamics of H. pylori [33, 34]. In addition, factors related to genes play a crucial role in determining one's susceptibility to Helicobacter pylori infection through various mechanisms that include the modulation of immune responses and interactions with bacterial virulence. Investigating these genetic influences can provide insights into why certain individuals remain asymptomatic, while others develop serious conditions, such as gastric cancer. Host genetic factors include variations in toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR1, which have been traced to heightened levels of anti-H. pylori immunoglobulin G (IgG) titters, indicating their involvement in response to the infection. In addition, polymorphisms in cytokine genes, specifically IL-2 and IL-6, have been associated with increased susceptibility to H. pylori and the progression of similar diseases [35]. On the bacterial side, certain strains of H. pylori carry virulence factors, such as the Cag-Pathogenicity Island, which significantly enhance their pathogenic potential. Genetic variations in these virulence factors can result in diverse disease outcomes [36]. Furthermore, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in bacterial genes, such as HtrA, have been linked to the development of gastric cancer, highlighting the intricate relationship between host genetics and bacterial pathogenicity [37, 38]. The prevalence of Helicobacter pylori infection is significantly affected by various lifestyle factors that differ across demographics. Notable contributors include dietary practices, alcohol intake, smoking habits, and socioeconomic conditions, which exhibit considerable variation between urban and rural populations. A comprehensive understanding of these factors is essential for identifying vulnerable groups and formulating effective preventive strategies. Dietary habits play a crucial role, as high dairy consumption (≥ 200 g daily) has been linked to an increased likelihood of H. pylori positivity (OR: 1.37). A preference for spicy and grilled foods, coupled with a low intake of fruits and vegetables, is associated with elevated infection rates. In addition, frequent consumption of hot tea has been shown to heighten risk (OR: 1.58), while the use of serving chopsticks appears to offer a protective effect (OR: 0.30). Alcohol consumption also correlates with higher infection rates among drinkers. Regarding alcohol and smoking, both current and former smoking are associated with increased odds of infection (OR: 1.34 and 1.38, respectively). Socioeconomic factors further influence H. pylori prevalence, with a higher incidence observed in rural areas (80.6%) compared to urban settings (67.3%), suggesting a relationship between socioeconomic status and infection rates. Key determinants such as income, education, and access to clean water are particularly relevant in urban populations [39–41].

Clinical manifestations and health consequences

Helicobacter pylori infection is linked to various clinical manifestations, predominantly affecting the gastrointestinal tract. The intensity and nature of symptoms can differ based on the virulent factors of the bacterium and the individual's immune response. Common gastrointestinal symptoms associated with H. pylori include chronic gastritis, which presents with epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting, particularly in the presence of virulent strains, such as CagA + and VacA + [42]. Peptic ulcers are another frequent manifestation, where patients often report pain occurring after meals or during the night, which has a notable correlation with H. pylori infection [42, 43]. In addition, functional dyspepsia, characterized by bloating and discomfort, may overlap with symptoms of gastritis. The relationship between H. pylori and gastro-esophageal reflux disease (GERD) remains contentious, although it has been suggested that H. pylori might aggravate symptoms in certain individuals [42, 44]. Furthermore, a higher prevalence of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) has been observed in patients harboring virulent strains of the bacterium. Finally, there is a significant link between chronic gastritis and H. pylori infection, particularly in the context of gastric cancer, including adenocarcinoma [43, 44].

H. pylori infection is a factor contributing to serious long-term health issues, particularly gastric cancer and peptic ulcers. The presence of this bacterium leads to chronic inflammation and various pathological alterations in the gastric epithelium, which may evolve into severe health conditions. A comprehensive understanding of these underlying mechanisms is essential for mitigating the associated health risks. The carcinogenic processes associated with H. pylori include chronic inflammation, which can result in DNA damage and the emergence of precancerous lesions [45]. In addition, virulence factors such as the CagA protein play a significant role by disrupting host cell signaling and inducing cellular changes that can lead to dysplasia and cancer. The cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) protein, delivered into gastric epithelial cells via the type IV secretion system, undergoes tyrosine phosphorylation and interacts with host signaling pathways, such as SHP-2 phosphatase, leading to cytoskeletal rearrangements, disruption of tight junctions, and aberrant cell proliferation. These changes contribute to gastric epithelial injury and may promote carcinogenesis. In addition, CagA can interfere with immune signaling, enabling the bacterium to evade host immune clearance. The vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA) induces the formation of intracellular vacuoles, disrupts mitochondrial function leading to apoptosis, modulates autophagy, and impairs antigen presentation by immune cells. This immunomodulatory role of VacA helps H. pylori establish chronic infection by dampening T-cell activation [45, 46]. Furthermore, the infection can trigger genetic alterations, including hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes and DNA double-strand breaks, thereby enhancing the risk of carcinogenesis. In terms of peptic ulcers, H. pylori are recognized as a primary etiological agent, causing mucosal damage and increased acid secretion that can lead to ulcer formation [46, 47]. Research indicates that individuals infected with H. pylori face a significantly elevated risk of developing gastric ulcers compared to those who are uninfected [48]. Untreated infections caused by Helicobacter pylori present considerable public health challenges, particularly due to their strong correlation with severe gastrointestinal disorders, including gastric cancer and peptic ulcers. The enduring presence of H. pylori in populations, especially in developing countries, highlights the urgent need for effective public health interventions to address this infection. Health risks linked to untreated H. pylori infections. Furthermore, untreated H. pylori infections often persist indefinitely, heightening the likelihood of severe complications over time. However, the rising antibiotic resistance associated with eradication efforts necessitates vigilant monitoring and responsible management [49–52]. It is worth noting that, despite the serious health risks associated with H. pylori, some research indicates that it may confer protective effects against certain childhood diseases, suggesting a complex relationship that merits further exploration.

Current approaches to diagnosis and treatment

There are several approaches to diagnosing Helicobacter pylori infection. The various methods for the diagnosis of H. pylori have been broadly divided into invasive and non-invasive methods. Urea breath test (UBT), serology, and stool antigen test (SAT) are non-invasive methods of diagnosis, while endoscopic imaging, histology, rapid urease test (RUT), and culture are invasive methods of diagnosis [53]. There are several diagnostic approaches used, which can be divided into invasive and non-invasive methods. Non-invasive methods include urea breath test (UBT), stool antigen test (SAT) and serology, while invasive methods include endoscopy, rapid urease test, culture and polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The urea breath test (UBT) detects whether H. pylori-produced urease, an enzyme, is present in the stomach. Patients consume a urea solution that has been marked with either 13C or 14C, the carbon isotope. The urea is broken down into carbon dioxide by H. pylori, which is then found in the breath. It has an 84–100% sensitivity and 93–100% specificity. For both initial diagnosis and post-treatment confirmation, the stool antigen test (SAT) identifies H. pylori antigens in stool samples with about 80–100% sensitivity and 86–95% specificity. Blood tests are used in serology to measure antibodies against H. pylori. Serology test specificity can vary greatly, especially in areas with low prevalence, despite its high sensitivity (90–100%). The invasive approaches, such as endoscopy with biopsy, which include taking tissue samples for additional testing as well as visually examining the stomach lining. The gold standard for diagnosis involves staining biopsy samples to see H. pylori, usually using Giemsa or hematoxylin and eosin. Also, the rapid urease test measures urease activity directly on biopsy samples. The presence of urease is indicated by a color shift with sensitivity of 90% to 95% 100% specificity [54]. In addition, culturing H. pylori from biopsies enables antibiotic susceptibility testing, even though the technique is time-consuming and susceptible to false negatives because of specimen handling problems. Although it is less commonly used for initial diagnosis, chronic cases may benefit from its use.

Recent surveillance also highlights the alarming rise in resistance to H. pylori. According to global treatment guidelines, clarithromycin resistance has been above 15% in most parts of the world, and that is the point above which standard triple therapy (amoxicillin, PPI, clarithromycin) should no longer be used. Metronidazole resistance is greater than 40–50% across much of Africa and Asia, and resistance to levofloxacin is nearing 30% in Europe and East Asia. These rates are consistent with declining eradication efficacy: recent meta-analyses illustrate triple therapy success often < 70%, prompting adoption of bismuth-containing quadruple regimens and molecular resistance-directed treatment in most guidelines [55–57]. Although no H. pylori vaccine has received regulatory approval to date, research has continued into diverse strategies. Recent reviews summarize progress in candidate antigens (e.g., CagA and VacA antigens) sometimes given with mucosal adjuvants, which have reached phase I/II trials and elicited systemic and mucosal immunity, but protection has been inconsistent. Difficulties are presented by H. pylori immune evasion strategies and in eliciting long-term gastric mucosal responses. New adjuvants, nanoparticles, and recombinant viral vectors are being investigated, but yet, there is no licensed vaccine. [58, 59]. Besides antibiotics, research explores probiotics to reduce bacterial load and toxicity, antimicrobial peptides and phytochemicals as adjuvants, and nanoparticle-mediated delivery to enhance antibiotic stability and gastric mucosal permeation. Preclinical experimental modalities such as bacteriophage therapy and host-directed immunomodulators are under development but represent nascent concepts under exploration [60, 61].

H pylori diagnostic methods also include molecular approaches like the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), which targets DNA of the bacteria if present in the stool or biopsy samples [62]. Antibiotic resistance and treatment failure are two of the most pressing therapeutic challenges that are making managing Helicobacter pylori infections more difficult. As clarithromycin, levofloxacin, and metronidazole resistance continue to rise worldwide, the standard of care has become less effective [67, 68]. Over the past 10 years, it has consistently relied on regimens like triple therapy, which combines a proton pump inhibitor with clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole. As a result, eradication rates have significantly decreased, and in many areas, clarithromycin resistance alone has made this standard treatment less effective. It is difficult to choose the best regimen empirically, because resistance is not uniform and varies greatly by region and even within groups. The concern increase of H. pylori strain resistance rates has also been attributed to the improper use of antibiotics for unrelated diseases [69].

Clinical recommendations are placing more emphasis on antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) to direct medication considering these problems, especially when prior treatment has failed. However, widespread adoption is constrained by practical obstacles such the requirement to collect representative samples for correct assessment and the availability of AST that is quick, precise, and affordable, particularly in environments with limited resources. Researchers have suggested two different approaches to AST in H. pylori infection, the use of the common routine culture method (AST) and the advanced and more sensitive molecular method [70]. Due to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) H. pylori, which can occasionally even be resistant to bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT), physicians are being forced to adopt increasingly complicated, expensive, and occasionally poorly tolerated treatment plans. In addition to increasing the risk of the disease and prolonging patient symptoms, treatment failures also increase secondary resistance and restrict the options for future therapies [70, 71]. Newer treatment substances and techniques are being studied or have recently started clinical usage to combat the diminishing effectiveness of traditional regimens. In comparison with conventional proton pump inhibitors, the acid blocker vonoprazan is attracting interest for its ability to increase eradication rates by boosting antibiotic activity, lowering susceptibility to genetic differences in drug metabolism, and establishing a more favorable gastric pH. Although there are still issues with possible myelotoxicity and expense, rifabutin-based triple treatments provide an alternative for patients who have had extensive pretreatment. Although probiotic adjuvants are being researched to reduce side effects associated with medication and possibly improve eradication, more solid clinical evidence is needed before they can be used consistently [72].

In addition to antibiotics, non-traditional approaches are given a lot of attention. The search for a long-term strategy to stop infection and the subsequent consequences of chronic gastritis and stomach cancer has led to research into developing a vaccine against H. pylori. The bacterium's immune evasion capabilities and the difficulty of generating long-lasting mucosal immunity are two reasons why effective human vaccines have not yet entered clinical practice despite decades of effort. In addition, there is growing interest in targeted strategies that interfere with H. pylori's capacity to colonize or survive in the gastric niche. Examples of these strategies include drugs that alter the microenvironment (e.g., local oxygen manipulation), interfere with urease function, or damage the integrity of the cell wall.

Though still in their early phases, innovative technologies such as biomaterial-based or antibiotic-free strategies possibly utilizing nanotechnology or bacteriophage therapy show promise for addressing current resistance mechanisms [72, 73]. To effectively design treatments, future research will probably need to incorporate better, faster diagnostic testing, continuous monitoring of resistance trends at the national and regional levels, and cautious antibiotic stewardship to maintain their effectiveness. Especially in high-burden, low-resource countries, this multimodal strategy may necessitate strong international cooperation to enable equal access to innovative medicines and diagnostics. The eradication of H. pylori appears to be on the horizon thanks to new antimicrobials, next-generation proton pump inhibitors, and alternative approaches, but putting these developments into practice will cost a lot of money, because rising global resistance is still a serious challenge [70–74].

Management of Helicobacter pylori in countries with high prevalence and antibiotic resistance

An efficient management approach must be thorough and adapted to local resistance patterns in nations, where Helicobacter pylori infection is very common and antibiotic resistance is a major problem. When resistance to clarithromycin and other commonly used antibiotics is high or susceptibility testing is not available, optimized bismuth-based quadruple therapy which consists of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI), bismuth, tetracycline, and metronidazole administered for 14 days is frequently advised as the first-line regimen [75]. Patients with known drug allergies or those who have not responded to first treatment attempts may benefit from alternative therapies such vonoprazan–amoxicillin dual therapy or rifabutin-based triple therapy [78–80]. Public health initiatives are crucial in addition to individual care. Reinfection rates can be decreased, patient adherence to treatment can be improved, and early diagnosis can be improved by implementing national screening programs and community education campaigns. To inform treatment decisions and revise clinical recommendations when resistance patterns shift, ongoing monitoring of regional antibiotic resistance profiles is crucial. To further reduce transmission, it is critical to incorporate infection control measures at the home and community levels [81–83]. For eradication efforts to be successful, patient education and adherence support are essential. In areas where antibiotic resistance is most prevalent, focusing on these tactics along with stewardship to cut down on needless antibiotic use and the creation of innovative treatments such as probiotics and botanical extracts guarantees a more reliable and long-lasting strategy to control Helicobacter pylori.

Limitations of current knowledge and future research

This review highlights several limitations in knowledge about H. pylori. Epidemiological modelling continues to be constrained by heterogeneity in diagnostic practices and a lack of longitudinal data, particularly in under-represented nations, such as Africa and Latin America. Resistance surveillance studies are single-center and underpowered, making it hard to extend the application (a method or conclusion) to the national and global levels. In addition, most therapeutic trials are being conducted in high-income settings, and fewer data originate from low-resource countries. Upcoming research needs to bridge these gaps by establishing robust global surveillance systems and multicenter randomized controlled trials comparing novel treatment regimens and diagnostics.

Conclusion

H. pylori infection is a worldwide public health issue, with very high prevalence in developing nations due to a lack of sanitation, high rates of overcrowding, and limited access to medical centers. Its close association with gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric cancer makes it an area that requires sustained public health interventions. Enhancement in diagnostic technologies, encompassing convolutional neural networks, deep learning, and gastrointestinal endoscopic image analysis, holds bright prospects for increased precision in discrimination and detection between active, history, and absence of infection. Such technologies would be valuable in increasing surveillance of disease, guiding pinpoint interventions, and establishing public health policy.
