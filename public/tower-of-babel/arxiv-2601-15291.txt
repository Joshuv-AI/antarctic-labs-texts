Public transport challenges and technology-assisted accessibility for visually impaired elderly residents in urban environments


Abstract
Independent navigation is a core aspect of maintaining social participation and individual health for vulnerable populations. While historic cities such as Edinburgh, as the capital of Scotland, often feature well-established public transport systems, urban accessibility challenges remain and are exacerbated by a complex landscape, especially for groups with multiple vulnerabilities such as the blind elderly. With limited research examining how real-time data feeds and developments in artificial intelligence can enhance navigation aids, we address this gap through a mixed-methods approach. Our work combines statistical and machine learning techniques, with a focus on spatial analysis to investigate network coverage, service patterns, and density through live Transport for Edinburgh data, with a qualitative thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews with the mentioned target group. The results demonstrate the highly centralised nature of the city’s transport system, the significance of memory-based navigation, and the lack of travel information in usable formats. We also find that participants already use navigation technology to varying degrees and express a willingness to adopt artificial intelligence. Our analysis highlights the importance of dynamic tools in terms of sensory and cognitive needs to meaningfully improve independent travel.

Introduction

As ageing populations increase, the number of elderly individuals experiencing sight loss continues to grow, impacting their ability to confidently navigate urban environments
(
Schulz et al., 2015
;
Wood et al., 2022
)
. In historic cities such as Edinburgh, public transport systems are crucial for mobility, but infrastructure obstructions and a lack of accessible real-time travel information discourage independent navigation for visually impaired people, increasing reliance on others and reducing quality of life
(
Campisi et al., 2021
)
. With 10% of Edinburgh’s population being older adults, inclusive mobility solutions are highly relevant for the city’s fastest-growing demographic
(
EHSCP, 2022
)
. In this context, artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly being explored for navigational utility, supporting independence and accessibility for age-related vision impairments
(
Ma et al., 2023
;
Wong et al., 2025
)
.

The purpose of this work is to understand the navigational challenges encountered by the visually impaired elderly relying on public transport networks, and to assess the potential of technological advances to address these barriers. We make use of data from Transport for Edinburgh, the organisation overseeing public buses and trams in Edinburgh, Scotland, and use a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative analysis of transport data and qualitative insights from semi-structured phone interviews. Our aim is to understand whether, and how, these kinds solutions can address accessibility challenges and enhance navigation. We explore the question how existing infrastructure impacts independence, identify key day-to-day barriers, and explore attitudes and expectations regarding AI-assisted navigation.

In doing so, our work enters multiple original contributions into the literature. While public transport for the visually impaired and elderly has been investigated separately, including for Edinburgh by
Montarzino et al., 2007
and
Gorman et al., 2003
, respectively, through qualitative research and surveys, the literature on the overlap with multiple vulnerabilities remains sparse. An exploration of the potential of artificial intelligence with an approach focussed on the target group’s perception furthers these insights. The quantitative parts of the related literature also generally rely on traditional descriptive statistics for insights, whereas the quantitative part of our work strongly focusses on spatial analysis through machine learning approaches to gain a different angle on a public transport network.

Background

Barriers to inclusive urban accessibility

Accessibility is essential to urbanisation and has been characterised as a fundamental human right central to maintaining social equity and inclusion, especially for marginalised populations limited by mobility
(
Solá et al., 2018
;
United Nations, 2016b
)
. The United Nations (UN) define urban accessibility as the extent to which an individual can navigate their environment and access essential services without hindrance
(
United Nations, 2016b
;
United Nations, 2016a
)
. This is reinforced as a fundamental right in the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities
(
Nykiforuk et al., 2021
)
. As urban areas expand and ageing populations increase, insufficient accessibility continues to pose obstacles to achieving equity
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
.

These environments serve as centres of economic activity, entertainment, social engagement, and innovation, yet often fall short in that regard
(
Pineda, 2024
)
. Public transport plays an important role, with
Wang and Loo, 2024
’s analysis of Hong Kong finding that, despite the city’s transport-oriented design, location-based characteristics and overcentralisation reasults in uneven accessibility. With approximately 800 million people, or around 10% of the world’s population, being seniors, this number is projected to double by 2050
(
United Nations, 2023
, see)
, while more than half of the elderly live in cities
(
Padeiro et al., 2022
)
. Recent studies by
Asiedu-Ampem et al., 2024
and
Eisenberg et al., 2024
describe barriers such as inconsistent and overcentralised transport services, as well as outdated or unmaintained pedestrian infrastructure.

The number of people with vision impairments is expected to increase by about 60% in 25 years, with an increased likelihood of developing vision loss through ageing
(
Wahl, 2013
)
. Vision loss varies in severity and can impact the ability to navigate cities independently, with everyday tasks relying on sight becoming daunting, placing blind elderly people amongst the most marginalised groups in society
(
Fraser et al., 2019
)
.

Vision impairment and ethical considerations

The experience of navigating urban environments is a cognitive task relying on sensory information for spatial awareness
(
Meneghetti et al., 2021
)
. These tasks become more complicated for ageing vision-impaired populations as they must rely on alternative sensory cues and adaptive strategies
(
Schinazi et al., 2015
)
.
Cimarolli et al., 2012
examine 364 individuals and reveal that over 60% associate navigating in built environments with fear and insecurity. The inability to engage with surroundings leads to social isolation and reduced interpersonal relationships, which can exacerbate health issues
(
Barnes et al., 2022
)
.

Vision plays a key role, as humans navigate using two main strategies for spatial orientation, egocentric (self-centred) reliance on sensory cues and allocentric (environment-centred) navigation dependent on locations relative to oneself
(
Ruggiero et al., 2022
, see, for example,)
, with age-related shifts towards the latter. For the visually impaired elderly, this dependence increases cognitive strain due to reduced memory capacity and slower cognitive processing, with assistive technology having been proposed to ease increased cognitive strain and sensory overload
(
Muhsin et al., 2024
)
.

Independence is central to well-being as a key aspect of the World Health Organization’s Quality of Life (WHOQoL) framework
(
World Health Organization, 1998
)
. For the elderly, especially those with additional challenges, maintaining independence can be challenging, impacting decisions such as domicile and level of environmental engagement
(
Remillard et al., 2023
)
. The inconsistencies of cities introduce obstacles through heavy foot traffic, vehicle movement, and sensory overstimulation
(
Vincent and Hartt, 2024
)
.

Artificial intelligence in urban navigation

Current AI methods are experiencing rapid growth with the potential to transform traditional operations across sectors like healthcare and transport
(
Dwivedi et al., 2023
)
. In this context, smart cities are urban areas that apply modern technology to enhance quality of life, improve efficiency in urban operations, and maintain sustainable growth
(
Batty et al., 2012
, see)
, with prior research demonstrating how they can enhance urban mobility through real-time navigation technology and sensor-based infrastructure to mitigate challenges connected to disabilities
(
Elassy et al., 2024
;
Kolotouchkina et al., 2022
)
.

Aligning with Edinburgh’s smart city initiative, the City of Edinburgh Council has trialled accessible technologies to assist visually impaired users by activating pedestrian crossings via Bluetooth through a mobile app
(
Kirkpatrick, 2015
)
. Here, a major drawback is the need to retrofit pedestrian crossings with specific hardware, which presents cost and scalability concerns. Similar trials in Malaysia and Australia are observed by
Deverell et al., 2022
but raise further concerns about user training requirements, a lack of focus on existing personal navigation aids, and the prerequisite that users own and confidently use smartphones. As
Wong et al., 2025
suggest, this assumption might not extend to elderly individuals.

Despite advancements demonstrating the utility of AI in healthcare
(
Ma et al., 2023
, see, for example,)
, ethical considerations must be addressed for handling sensitive information. Aside from robust security measures and the prevention of data misuse, data collection and usage should be transparent and the associated benefits and risks made clear to patients
(
Dankwa-Mullan, 2024
)
. Safeguards around consent and algorithmic fairness are essential to ensure that AI-driven solutions are accessible, equitable, and trustworthy.

Existing tools for the visually impaired elderly

Conventional navigation aids such as the well-established white cane have long been essential tools for visually impaired and elderly individuals
(
Rasouli Kahaki et al., 2023
)
. When examining strengths and weaknesses of existing aids,
Abidi et al., 2024
note that the latter are also not as effective at detecting non-ground-level features of urban landscapes, such as dangers above, in front, and parallel to one’s head.

Guide dogs provide not only mobility but also emotional support, but drawbacks include, for example, the inability to interpret signs or traffic signals and overstimulation in extremely busy areas
(
Whitmarsh, 2005
)
. While traditional aids have been effective, they fall short in adapting to fast-paced situations requiring real-time information and dynamic alerts
(
Prandi et al., 2023
)
.

AI research for accessibility and transport is not a novel concept; wearable technology and real-time AI devices have been adapted to complement existing systems, with smart sticks being one example.
Singh et al., 2024
develop and trial such a tool with ten elderly participants, featuring obstacle detection, GPS functionality, and mobile app integration. Device personalisation in their work is limited to changing sensor sensitivity levels, with haptic or audible navigation feedback, showing this to be a cost-effective and user-friendly alternative to guide dogs in developing countries. However, limitations include the inability to detect pits in roads and elevation changes, which are key challenges to accessibility in Edinburgh’s landscape.

Research on real-time AI solutions includes
Kulkarni et al., 2023
, who use use computer vision to label accessibility features of bus stops, identifying whether they are enclosed by shelters or open, and whether they have timetables, signage, or seating. However, the tool relies on a crowd-sourcing network of users to contribute updates and verify live information, similar to how Google Maps asks its users questions to update live data and provide real-time crowd tracking
(
Parady et al., 2023
)
.

Methodology

Research approach and data collection

We adopt a mixed-methods approach combining a quantitative analysis of transport system data with qualitative insights from participants in that system, reflecting related works
(
Wong, 2018
;
Aberle et al., 2025
)
. Quantitative data is collected over eight days in five-minute intervals through the TfE Open Data API
5
5

5

https://tfe-opendata.readme.io
. We delete rows with null values indicating inactive vehicles, verified via null travel direction values as well as coordinates for the seven Lothian Bus depots, the tram depot at Gogar, and the maintenance centre at Seafield. Rows with inactive routes through missing next stops and destination values are removed.

Qualitative data is collected through semi-structured phone interviews as a non-intrusive method, which reduces the stigma and discomfort associated with formal research settings, which can influence responses
(
Emara, 2025
;
Akram, 2021
)
. Our contact with the Royal National Institute of Blind People (RNIB) Scotland and North East Sensory Services, after ethics approval, enabled access to participants fitting the study criteria, and only individuals contacted through these organisations were included in the study.

Participants were required to be 65 years and over, registered partially sighted or blind under UK and Scottish certification standards, and living in the Edinburgh region or use its public transport system frequently. All participants were informed of the study through a verbal explanation of the participant information sheet and consent form, and verbal consent was obtained and recorded separately. Interviews were transcribed and cleaned manually using verbatim-style transcription
(
McMullin, 2023
, see)
.

Interviews targeted their experiences with public transport, including specific challenges or barriers, familiarity with technology, and perceptions and expectations of AI-based solutions. Questions are adapted from multiple studies, including
Wong et al., 2025
and
Al Shehri et al., 2022
. Our research involves vulnerable participants and Level 3 ethics approval was granted by the university’s corresponding ethics committee. Guidance on data retention and GDPR compliance was followed and detailed in the participant information sheet. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose and how their responses would be used, and any personal information that could be traceable to participants was redacted in the transcripts.

Quantitative data analysis

We apply nearest neighbour analysis (NNA) to data on stops to assess the spatial distribution of bus stops across Edinburgh. Introduced by
Clark and Evans, 1954
, this method examines whether the distribution trend of points is due to clustering, randomness, or dispersion calculated by

R=\frac{\bar{r}_{A}}{\bar{r}_{E}}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \bar{r}_{A}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}r}{N},\ \bar{r}_{E}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\rho}},

(1)

where
R
is the Nearest Neighbour Index (NNI),
\bar{r}_{A}
is the average distance to the nearest stop,
\bar{r}_{E}
is the expected, average distance under a random distribution, and
r
represents each nearest-neighbour distance (in metres). Lastly,
\rho
is the point density, calculated as the number of stops
N
over the area of interest
A
,

\rho=\frac{N}{A}.

(2)

We compute the nearest-neighbour distances between all stop coordinates to calculate the NNI, which is then compared against the theoretical value. Additionally, we employ a
Z
-test to assess the strength of
R
, using the formulation proposed by
Clark and Evans, 1954
,

Z=\frac{\bar{r}_{A}-\bar{r}_{E}}{\sigma_{\bar{r}_{E}}},\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \sigma_{\bar{r}_{E}}=\frac{0.26136}{\sqrt{N\rho}}.

(3)

Kernel density estimation (KDE), pioneered by
Rosenblatt, 1956
and
Parzen, 1962
, is a nonparametric method that is useful when assumptions about the underlying probability distribution cannot easily be made
(
Silverman, 1986
)
. We use vehicle data to analyse city-wide usage, with vehicle coordinates assigned to their nearest stop and the cumulative number of vehicle positions representing usage intensity. We make use of a bivariate kernel function to estimate the density of vehicle stop activity
\hat{f}(x,y)
at a given point
(x,y)
,

\hat{f}(x,y)=\frac{1}{nh_{x}h_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{x_{i}-x}{h_{x}},\frac{y_{i}-y}{h_{y}}\right),

(4)

where
K
is a kernel function
(
Silverman, 1986
, typically Gaussian, see)
),
h_{x}
and
h_{y}
are bandwidth (smoothing) parameters for the horizontal and vertical
x
and
y
dimensions, and
(x_{i},y_{i})
are the coordinates of the
n
observed vehicles from the data
(
Yu et al., 2014
)
.

Lastly, we apply
k
-means clustering, originally established by
MacQueen, 1967
and later formalised by
Lloyd, 1982
, to data on stops and incorporate the vehicle–stop assignment data used in KDE. We section stops into
K
clusters through similarity grouping
(
Chen et al., 2024
, see)
, which minimises the total squared Euclidean distances between each data point and its randomly assigned centre point (centroid),

\min\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{x_{i}\in c_{k}}\left\|x_{i}-\mu_{k}\right\|^{2},

(5)

where
x_{i}
is a transport stop,
\mu_{k}
is the centroid of cluster
k\in K
, and
c_{k}
represents the subset of stops assigned to cluster
k
. We use three features; latitude and longitude from of stops and vehicle stop frequency. These features are standardised using
Z
-score normalisation to remove the mean and scale to unit variance,

x^{\prime}=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{\sigma},

(6)

where
x^{\prime}
is the original feature vector, and
\bar{x}
and
\sigma
are the mean and standard deviation across these vectors, ensuring equal weighting. Incorporating stop usage frequency in addition to two-dimensional coordinates allows the algorithm to consider physical stop distribution and service intensity, distinguishing between high and low-frequency stops that are geographically close but have different patterns
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
.

Methodological limitations

The TfE API and data availability presented several challenges. Stops and live vehicle location data are available but lack attributes such as vehicle delays, stop congestion or service disruptions. This restricts the ability to analyse live accessibility barriers, whereas a more granular version with information on stop accessibility, service updates and service delays was previously offered. This live data became unavailable in February 2025 due to maintenance and has, at the time of writing, not been restored, impacting the ability of the research community to fully explore the transportation system.

Moreover, a Freedom of Information request under the Freedom of Information (Scotland) Act 2002
6
6

6

https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2002/13/contents
to the City of Edinburgh Council has been answered with the clarification that accessibility data for the public transport system is not stored and, as such, remains unavailable retroactively for research. As a result, the level of inclusivity cannot be assessed, further complicating the analysis of vulnerable groups for policy analysis, and it is uncertain whether the council consistently achieves an adequate standard in providing accessible services. At the time of writing, the TfE API has also stopped providing any accessibility information, real-time or otherwise, which exacerbates this challenge.

For our quantitative data collection, the chosen observation period also clashed with the northbound North Bridge closure in Edinburgh, introducing some abnormal service patterns. Although disruptions reflect realistic variability, these may not fully represent Edinburgh’s transport efficiency and accessibility. Recruiting participants is also challenging in this area of research due to the sensitive nature of interacting with vulnerable persons willing to participate
(
Ellard-Gray et al., 2015
)
.

After measuring the distances between stops, we apply KDE to the combined data on stops and vehicle locations. The resulting values correspond to the cumulative number of recorded transport vehicles over time at their closest assigned stops, overlaid with key locations such as transport hubs, landmarks, and hospitals as areas of concentrated activity, in Figure

KDE for transport stop density in Edinburgh. The left-hand panel shows the Greater Edinburgh Area, while the right-hand panel depicts a zoomed-in view of the City Centre. Key locations for transport and public life are marked separately.

The highest density values exceed 1,000 and fall around major transport hubs such as Waverley Station, Haymarket, and Princes Street. Similarly, regions such as Leith, North Bridge, Abbeyhill, and the West End exhibit moderate values ranging from 110 to 464. In contrast, we observe lower density (less than 77) on the outskirts of Edinburgh in areas such as Balerno, Wester Hailes, and Dalkeith.

We run preliminary experiments for different smoothing bandwidths
h\in[0.1,0.3,0.5,0.8,1.0]
to evaluate their influence on the density interpretation, as lower
h

h>0.5
leads to excessive smoothing that misses discrepancies between the central and suburban areas, while
h<0.3
introduces excessive granularity, making the results harder to interpret. As a result, we set
h=0.3
for the granularity of suburban areas and central stop-dense areas.

Cluster analysis of stops and usage

Finally, we employ
k
-means clustering to identify stop accumulations based on geographical coordinates and usage frequency. The choice of
k=4
is the result of iterative tests for cluster stability, showing that
k>4
results in oversegmentation, where some clusters contain too few stops to provide meaningful insights. Conversely,
k<4
leads to overly broad groupings that fail to accurately differentiate usage frequency levels by location. The resulting Cluster 0 features 2,198 stops, Cluster 1 422 stops, Cluster 2 86 stops, and Cluster 3 1,094 stops. We analyse their characteristics through boxplots and geospatial scatter plots for both the Greater Edinburgh Area and the City Centre, as depicted in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Cluster analysis results for geographical locations and usage frequency (activity) of stops. The left-hand panel shows a boxplot representing the distribution of activity within each cluster, measured by the number of vehicles frequenting each stop. An extreme outlier in Cluster 2 with a value of 8,708 vehicles is excluded from the plot, corresponding to the central area where many key interchange stops are located. The upper right-hand panel depicts the clustering of transport stops in the Greater Edinburgh Area based on both location and activity, while the lower right-hand panel shows the same for the City Centre.

While Cluster 0 contains the greatest number of stops, Cluster 2 exhibits far higher service frequency, demonstrating that a higher stop count does not correspond to higher transport activity. Cluster 3 represents moderate-frequency stops, while Cluster 1 exhibits the lowest activity, and outliers for very-high-frequency stops are especially notable in Cluster 2. Incorporating vehicle counts provides insights into stop usage frequency and how activity levels are physically distributed compared to clustering solely based on geographic proximity. This results in more meaningful clusters for transport accessibility and service coverage analysis, where high-traffic and low-traffic central stops can be differentiated even if they are located near each other.
