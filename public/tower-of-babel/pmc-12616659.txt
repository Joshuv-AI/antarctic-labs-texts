Higher soluble CD8 correlates with poor prognosis in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients
Authors: Yamei Li, Niying Zhao, Yangjuan Bai, Yunfei An, Xinhua Dai, Hanjing Liu, Hua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary infections pose significant challenges in accurate prognosis due to the lack of reliable biomarkers. This study aims to explore the prognostic potential of soluble CD4 (sCD4) and sCD8 for adverse outcomes in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients.MethodsThis study enrolled 112 hospitalized pulmonary infection patients and 62 healthy controls (HCs). Hospitalized patients were grouped into survivors (n = 87), who recovered and were discharged, and non-survivors (n = 25), who either died or discontinued treatment due to severe conditions. Plasma sCD4 and sCD8 levels were quantified by ELISA. T cell subsets were determined using flow cytometry. IL-6 and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were detected using electrochemiluminescence, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured using rate nephelometry. Inflammatory markers derived from complete blood count (CBC) were calculated accordingly. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, Cox proportional hazard models and correlation analyses were conducted.ResultsNon-survivors were significantly older than survivors and HCs. Compared to survivors, non-survivors presented significantly higher levels of sCD8, PCT, IL-6, CRP and CBC-derived inflammatory markers, along with significantly reduced CD3+ T and CD8+ T cell counts. ROC analyses demonstrated that sCD8 had the highest area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.85 (0.78–0.92) with sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 66.67%, respectively. Other markers showed weak to moderate prognostic power with AUCs ranging from 0.64 to 0.75. Multivariate Cox hazard regression analysis indicated that higher sCD8, lower CD8+ T cell counts and the presence of mixed-type pathogenic infections remained independently associated with undesirable outcomes. sCD8 was positively correlated with all inflammatory markers but negatively correlated with T cell subset counts.ConclusionsOur data suggest that a plasma sCD8 level above the optimal cut-off value is independently associated with poor outcomes. Elevated sCD8 may be a novel prognostic biomarker in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients.

Introduction

Pulmonary infections caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, mycoplasma and other infectious agents are major health problems worldwide associated with high morbidity and mortality. Data from the 2021 Global Burden of Diseases study showed that lower respiratory tract infections were the seventh leading cause of death globally, accounting for 28.7 deaths per 100,000 population [1]. Early identification of infections, accurate evaluation of prognosis, and timely implementation of effective treatment are imperative to reduce the death rate. Although various pathogen-specific biomarkers (such as glutamate dehydrogenase and galactomannan) and host-response biomarkers (such as C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cells (WBCs) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)) assist in monitoring, risk stratification and prognosis evaluation of pulmonary infections in clinical settings [2], none are completely reliable in their prognostic performance [3]. Identifying novel potential biomarkers is essential for optimal and personalized management of pulmonary infections.

T-cell immunity, particularly CD8+ T cells, is crucial for defending against various pathogens. Recent studies highlighted that dynamic changes in CD8+ T cell subsets, such as activated CD8+CD38+ T cells, correlate with worse clinical outcomes in COVID-19 patients [4,5]. A skewed CD4/CD8 ratio has been associated with higher risks for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and mortality during viral infections [6]. Notably, severe infections often result in drops in absolute numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes due to the apoptotic depletion at the end stage of infection. Studies have shown that T-lymphopaenia is an independent risk factor for survival in patients with critical illness, sepsis, influenza virus and COVID-19 [7–10], indicating the prognostic value of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in various infections. As typical markers of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells, CD4 and CD8 glycoproteins exist not only in membrane-bound form, but also in soluble form. Early studies have shown that when T cells are activated, the release of soluble CD4 (sCD4) and sCD8 increases via different mechanisms [11]. sCD4 is produced mainly through cell surface shedding by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)-like proteinases [12], while sCD8 is generated from both alternative splicing of CD8 mRNA and ectodomain shedding of the membrane CD8 molecule [13,14]. sCD4 and sCD8 are generally considered surrogate markers of immunological activation [15,16]. Various studies have shown their close relationship with disease severity and prognostic value in multiple diseases [12,16–18]. For instance, serum sCD4 levels in patients with rheumatoid arthritis positively correlate with disease activity, and higher sCD4 is associated with poor prognosis [12]. In advanced non-small cell lung cancer patients, lower sCD8 levels predict better outcomes in patients receiving anti-PD-L1 treatment [16]. However, whether sCD4 and sCD8 are associated with the prognosis of hospitalized pulmonary infection patients remains unknown.

In this study, we hypothesized that sCD4 and sCD8 levels provide informative data for evaluating the prognosis of hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. Therefore, we determined the concentrations of plasma sCD4 and sCD8, quantified CD3+, CD4+, CD8+ T cell subsets, measured clinically used inflammatory markers PCT, IL-6 and CRP, and along with complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory markers such as NLR and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), to investigate the prognostic potential of sCD4 and sCD8 for adverse outcomes in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. We also compared the prognostic values among these markers in this study.

Materials and methods

Study population and ethical consideration

This is a cross-sectional study that enrolled 112 hospitalized pulmonary infection patients and 62 healthy controls (HCs) from West China Hospital of Sichuan University between June and July 2024. The inclusion criteria for pulmonary infection patients are as follows: (1) adults; (2) severe infection; (3) hospitalized in our medical centre. The inclusion criteria for HCs were individuals who underwent health examinations in our hospital and showed no abnormalities in their laboratory test results (including routine blood tests, urine tests, biochemical tests, tumour biomarkers and thyroid function) and imaging results. Infection patients were grouped into survivor group if they recovered and were discharged from the hospital. In the non-survivor group, patients died or discontinued treatment due to the severe clinical conditions. Pathogen identification was performed using a combination of microbiological cultures, PCR assays, serological tests, clinical symptoms, thoracic computed tomography and effectiveness of clinical treatment. Mixed pathogen infection was defined as the concurrent detection of two or more types of pathogens (bacterial, viral or fungal). The detailed study flowchart is displayed in Supplementary Figure 1. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital (No. 2024 (1053)) and was performed according to the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrolment.

Blood sampling and measurement of T cell subsets, sCD4 and sCD8

Fresh EDTA anticoagulated peripheral blood was collected from study populations following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [19]. Samples were processed and stored within 12 h of collection. Flow cytometry was used to determine the percentage and absolute number of CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets. Briefly, 50 μL whole blood was stained with the following antibodies: CD45-PerCP, CD3-FITC, CD4-APC and CD8-PE (all antibodies were purchased from BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Then, 500 μL haemolytic agent (NH4CL, 0.15 mol/L) was added to lyse erythrocytes for 10 min. The cells were then detected on a FACS Canto II instrument (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA). Counting beads were used to calculate the absolute number of T cell subsets. Data analysis was performed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR). Plasma was collected after centrifugation and stored in aliquots at −80 °C until required to determine the concentrations of sCD4 and sCD8. sCD4 and sCD8 were quantified strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Jianglai Industrial Limited by Share Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Data collection and processing

Clinical and laboratory examination data were collected from the hospital information system and the laboratory information system, respectively. The following items were recorded for each patient: gender, age, identity document number, clinical diagnosis, infection pathogens, sampling date, admission and discharge dates, outcomes, IL-6, CRP, PCT and routine blood test results. If certain laboratory data were missing from the information system, frozen plasma samples were used for supplementary testing to ensure data integrity. IL-6 and PCT were detected using the electrochemiluminescence method (e801, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). CRP was determined using the IMMAGE 800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, CA). Routine blood tests were performed using an automated haematology analyzer Sysmex (Kobe, Japan). Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis strategy is similar to our previously published work [20]. Data were presented as numbers (%), means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile ranges) according to data type. One-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis H-tests were performed for comparisons among three groups. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were utilized to compare categorical variables between groups. Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney’s U-test was used to compare continuous variables with normal and skewed distributions, respectively. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the predictive performance of the markers of interest. The optimal cut-off value was determined based on the maximum value of Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Patients were dichotomized into two groups based on the optimal cut-off values of the markers. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were used to identify whether the potential biomarkers were independent risk factors for adverse outcomes in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. Kaplan–Meier’s survival plots were generated for sCD8, CD8+ T cell count and IL-6, and compared by log-rank tests. Spearman’s correlation matrix was generated to explore the correlations between biomarkers. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and a two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism version 6.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Results

The baseline characteristics of participants are outlined in Table 1. In general, hospitalized pulmonary infection patients were significantly older than HCs, and non-survivors were older than survivors. In the non-survivor group, 84% of patients suffered from mixed pathogen infections, which was significantly higher than in survivors (48.3%). For routine blood test results, non-survivors showed significantly higher levels of WBC, percentages and absolute numbers of neutrophils, and significantly lower percentages and absolute numbers of lymphocytes and haemoglobin when compared to survivors. There was no difference in gender distribution, monocytes, platelets or RBC among HCs, survivors and non-survivors.

Predictive values of T cell subsets, sCD8 and clinically used inflammatory markers for adverse outcomes in infection patients

To assess the potential of biomarkers in predicting the prognosis of hospitalized pulmonary infection patients, ROC curves are plotted in Figure 3. The analysis identified that sCD8 showed the highest area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.85 (0.78–0.92), with the best calculated cut-off value of 1307.00 U/mL and the sensitivity and specificity were 100% and 66.67%, respectively (Figure 3(A)). Other markers including PCT, IL-6, CRP, CD3+ T cell count, CD8+ T cell percentage, CD8+ T cell count, NLR, dNLR, MLR, PLR, SII and SIRI, showed weak to modest predictive potential, with the AUC values ranging from 0.64 to 0.75. In addition, compared to sCD8 alone, the predictive performance of combining sCD8 with other markers has not significantly improved (Supplementary Table 1).

Correlation analyses between sCD4, sCD8 and inflammatory markers

Spearman’s correlation matrix was generated to determine the associations between sCD8, T cell subsets and inflammatory markers (Figure 4). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analyses were performed to thoroughly evaluate whether sCD8 was independently associated with adverse outcomes in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. Continuous variables, including sCD8, CD8+ T cell count, NLR and haemoglobin, were converted to binary variables based on the optimal cut-off values obtained from Figure 3(C). As shown in Table 3, older age, mixed pathogenic infection, higher sCD8, NLR levels, lower haemoglobin and lower count of CD8+ T cells were associated with mortality in the univariate Cox proportional hazard model. In the multivariate Cox hazard regression analysis, higher sCD8, lower CD8+ T cell count and the presence of mixed pathogenic infections remained independently associated with undesirable outcomes in patients (Table 2). Patients with sCD8 ≥ 1307 U/mL had significantly shorter survival than those with <1307 U/mL (Figure 5(A)), while patients with CD8+ T cell counts of 98 cells/μL or above showed better prognosis (Figure 5(B)). Patients with mixed-pathogen infections had worse survival (Figure 5(C)). In addition, we observed that sCD8 < 1307 U/mL seemed to be more efficient than CD8+ T cell count ≥ 98 cells/μL at predicting recovery in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the prognostic potential of sCD4 and sCD8 as indicators of unfavourable outcomes in hospitalized patients with pulmonary infections. We reported five major findings. First, we found that non-survivors showed significantly higher level of plasma sCD8 than that in survivors, while no significant difference was observed between groups regarding sCD4 levels. Second, sCD8 demonstrated superior discriminatory power in evaluating prognosis of hospitalized pulmonary infection patients when compared to other clinically utilized makers and inflammatory markers. Third, the combination of sCD8 with additional biomarkers augmented the prognostic potential of individual biomarkers but did not surpass the predictive performance of sCD8 alone, supporting the strong prognostic power of sCD8 in severe pulmonary infection scenarios. Fourth, sCD8 was positively correlated with all assessed inflammatory markers, implicating an ‘inflammatory feature’ of sCD8. Finally, higher sCD8 level, reduced count of CD8+ T cells and mixed types of infection pathogens were independently associated with poor outcomes in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients.

The CD8 antigen, a co-receptor for TCR on CD8+ T cells, enhances TCR signalling and CD8+ T cell activation by facilitating the interaction between HLA-I and TCR. Unlike CD4+ T cells, which indirectly combat infections by modulating the activity of other immune cells, naïve CD8+ T cells become activated and perform direct killing function quickly upon encountering virus, bacteria and other pathogens [4]. During chronic infections, CD8+ T cells, on the one hand, loss effector functions and become exhausted due to the persistence of antigen and inflammation, on the other hand, the number of CD8+ T cells decreases due to apoptotic depletion [21–23], supporting the pivotal role of CD8+ T cells in responding to infections. In line with the above theory, our study suggests that plasma sCD8 (potentially serving as a surrogate marker of CD8+ T cell activation) [11], rather than sCD4, might serve as a clinically valuable prognostic biomarker for severe pulmonary infection patients. The rationale behind sCD8’s notably superior prognostic capacity in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients remains to be elucidated. It is possible that higher sCD8 may be the result of a stronger CD8+ T cell activation when facing pathogens. The strong inflammatory signals and extended exposure to antigens are favour in CD8+ T cell exhaustion or loss, which is ineffective in controlling infection [24]. Therefore, sCD8 may serve as a pure biomarker for prognosis indication in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. Our multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression data revealed that both higher sCD8 level and lower CD8+ T cell count were independent risk factors for unfavourable outcomes, which to some extent supports this speculation and suggests that sCD8 can be considered as a complementary immune monitoring marker independent of the absolute number of CD8+ T cells. Another possibility is that sCD8 may play an important immunosuppressive function by blocking CD3ζ-chain phosphorylation [25] or (and) competing with cellular CD8 to interact with HLA-I/TCR complexes, interfering with the interaction between CD8+ T cells and target cells, leading to ineffective antigen presentation and failed activation of CD8+ T cell [26]. All these hypotheses require further detailed basic experiments to clarify in infection models. Notably, while a recent study by Hong Tang’s team indicated that higher serum sCD4 was significantly associated with sepsis-induced hyperinflammation, and could prevent excessive inflammatory activation of macrophages and alleviate sepsis via interrupting MHCII-TLR signalling in mice [27], our clinical data showed that sCD4 concentration did not display prognostic potential for adverse outcome in the particular setting of hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. Future studies should explore the predictive and prognostic potential of sCD4 in other specific infection settings.

To compare the prognostic ability of sCD8 with clinically useful markers for adverse outcomes, we also analysed the prognostic potential of T cell subpopulations, PCT, CRP, IL-6 and CBC-derived markers, which have been reported as promising predictors in various infection settings [28–31]. Consistent with previous findings, most of these markers showed weak to moderate prognostic values (AUC values ranging from 0.64 to 0.75) for predicting adverse outcomes in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. However, combining sCD8 with other biomarkers via binary logistic regression analysis did not significantly improve the prognostic ability compared to sCD8 alone. This may be associated with the fact that sCD8 and other assessed inflammatory markers were highly positively correlated, as demonstrated in Spearman’s correlation matrix in Figure 4. Despite this, considering that sCD8 showed perfect sensitivity (100%) but relatively low specificity (66.67%) at the optimal cut-off value of 1307.00 U/mL, it is still recommended to combine sCD8 with other biomarkers which possess higher specificity (such as IL-6 and CD8+ T cell count) in a different way to reduce the false positive rate. In addition, we observed that sCD8 was highly and positively correlated with currently recognized inflammatory markers such as IL-6, CPR and NLR. We also found that when mice were treated with vesicular stomatitis virus or lipopolysaccharides, the serum sCD8 concentration significantly increased within 24 h after treatment (unpublished data). These clinical and experimental data indicate that sCD8 is a sensitive biomarker of immune activation and might be an inflammatory molecule, as evidenced by previously published studies [11,15].

Interestingly, unlike the positive correlation between sCD8 and the absolute number of CD8+T cells in HIV-infected patients [32] and the lack of correlation in Sjögren’s syndrome patients [33], our study found a negative correlation between sCD8 and CD8+ T cell count with moderate correlation coefficient of −0.48 in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. These inconsistencies indicated that the behaviours of CD8+ T cells and the generation of sCD8 may perform differently in different disease contexts, which should be taken into account in the future functional investigations.

There are several limitations that merit attention in this study. First, this single-centre study with a limited sample size restricted extensive subgroup analyses, such as based on age, pathogens of infection. Second, we only included hospitalized pulmonary infection patients, and whether sCD8 could serve as a valuable prognostic biomarker in other clinical infection settings requires further larger, prospective and multi-centre investigations. Third, we cross-sectionally collected the one-point plasma samples during hospitalization, which may lead to the insufficient exploration and integration of the prognostic value of sCD8. Serial determinations of sCD8 are definitely warranted to provide more information on making the prognosis and assessing the response to medical therapy.

Conclusively, our study found that elevated plasma sCD8 levels are independently associated with poor prognosis in hospitalized pulmonary infection patients. Notably, sCD8 outperformed other immune markers (such as CRP, CD8+ T cell counts) in prognostic accuracy, highlighting its critical clinical value. However, these findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations, including the single-centre design, limited sample size, cross-sectional sampling and the inability to perform robust subgroup analyses. Future studies should prioritize multi-centre collaborations, larger cohorts, longitudinal sampling and stratification by infection types and comorbidities to validate sCD8’s generalizability. Additionally, mechanistic investigations into sCD8’s role in immune dysregulation during infections are warranted to elucidate its functional significance in infection pathogenesis.
