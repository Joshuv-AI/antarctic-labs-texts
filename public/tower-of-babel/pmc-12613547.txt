A review on health system-based surveillance for acute flaccid paralysis: technological advancements, challenges, and outlooks
Authors: Honey Gemechu, Gelane Biru, Eyerusalem Gebremeskel, Hundessa Daba Nemomssa, Kokeb Dese, Efrem Wakjira, Gashaw Demlew, Dessalew Yohannes, Ketema Lemma Abdi, Hamdia Murad, Elbetel Taye Zewde, Bontu Habtamu, Mesfin Tefera, Mikias Alayu, Netsanet Workneh Gidi, Filimona Bisrat, Tenager Tadesse, Legesse Kidanne, Se-woon Choe, Jude Kong, Gelan Ayana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoliomyelitis is a severe viral infection that can lead to permanent paralysis or death, primarily affecting children under 15 years of age. Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP) surveillance is essential for global polio eradication efforts, enabling the rapid detection, reporting, and investigation of suspected cases. Implementing various technological solutions has significantly advanced AFP surveillance, which is the gold standard for early polio case identification. These technologies include short message alert systems for better case reporting, the Open Data Kit (ODK) for efficient data collection and management, digital specimen tracking systems for improved sample handling, geospatial mapping tools for monitoring surveillance coverage, and awareness campaigns to engage healthcare workers and communities. Despite these advancements, there remains a notable absence of comprehensive analyses evaluating the cumulative progress, challenges, and policy implications of AFP surveillance.Main bodyThis study presents a targeted literature review of English-language publications from 2002 to 2024, focusing on challenges, technological advancements, and practical recommendations for AFP surveillance. Only empirically grounded, methodologically sound studies centered on AFP within health systems were included, with preference for research conducted in polio-endemic countries. Studies lacking methodological rigor or direct relevance were excluded. The review highlights how digital reporting platforms, specimen tracking, geospatial monitoring, and awareness campaigns have strengthened surveillance operations. At the same time, it identifies persisting weaknesses in surveillance methodologies and gaps in coverage, underscoring the need for further innovation and integration into health systems.ConclusionBy synthesizing evidence on the latest developments, this review addresses a critical gap in the literature and provides actionable recommendations to improve AFP surveillance. The findings inform both practice and policy by identifying innovative solutions and future directions, ultimately contributing to stronger global health surveillance systems and advancing polio eradication strategies.Keywords: Acute flaccid paralysis, Poliomyelitis, Surveillance, Health system

Introduction

Poliomyelitis, commonly shortened to polio, is an acute viral infection caused by the wild or vaccine-derived poliovirus [1]. It only affects humans and is transmitted through the fecal-oral route, and occasionally through contaminated food and water [2]. Poliovirus belongs to the family Picornaviridae. The Picornaviridae family consists of tiny, non-enveloped viruses (lacking an outer lipid membrane), which makes them more resistant to environmental conditions. These viruses have a genome composed of ribonucleic acid (RNA) and measure approximately 22 to 30 nanometers in size. The outer structure of the virus forms a symmetrical, 20-sided shape known as an icosahedral capsid, which helps protect the viral RNA. The single-stranded positive-sense RNA genome of the poliovirus is about 7500 nucleotides long. Poliovirus is commonly recognized as the most basic major virus due to its tiny genome and basic makeup, which consists solely of RNA and a protein coat that encases it [3, 4]. Poliovirus has three major strains (serotypes 1, 2, 3), which differ slightly in their capsid proteins. All three serotypes are responsible for causing poliomyelitis infection, which can lead to irreversible paralysis or death [1, 5]. Polio is an extremely contagious disease. The virus enters the body through the mouth and multiplies in the intestine. Then it penetrates the nervous system and can cause complete paralysis in a matter of hours. The incubation period typically ranges from 7 to 10 days, but may extend from 4 to 35 days. The infection is asymptomatic in approximately 75% to 90% of individuals, while others may experience symptoms such as fever, fatigue, headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, and limb discomfort. In up to one in every two hundred infections, the virus invades the central nervous system and causes paralytic disease. The severity of paralysis tends to increase with age and compromised immune status. Mortality rates among paralytic cases vary, with most estimates ranging between 5% and 10%, though it can reach up to 20% when respiratory muscles are affected and no ventilatory support is available [6, 7].

Before the establishment of the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI), polio rendered more than a thousand children disabled each day worldwide [8]. GPEI was established in 1988 to eradicate all polioviruses, ensuring that no child would suffer from paralytic poliomyelitis again [9]. Since its inception, there has been a dramatic decline in polio cases, from more than 350,000 cases in 1988 to just 33 cases in 2018 [10]. Despite this remarkable progress, wild poliovirus type one (WPV1) remains endemic in Afghanistan and Pakistan. Afghanistan reported 29 WPV1 cases in 2019, which rose to 56 in 2020, while Pakistan recorded 147 cases in 2019, decreasing to 84 in 2020 [11]. Continued efforts have further reduced the numbers, with only 12 cases reported in both countries in 2023 [12]. However, concerns persist. In 2024, wild poliovirus type 2 was detected in sewage samples from Gaza, and the first confirmed case was identified in an unvaccinated 10-month-old child from the central Gaza Strip [13–16].

To achieve global eradication of poliovirus, the World Health Organization (WHO) has outlined four interrelated strategies that form the foundation of its approach [17]. The first strategy emphasizes the importance of routine immunization for infants, especially in polio-endemic regions. This includes administering at least three doses of the oral polio vaccine (OPV), along with a birth dose, to ensure strong early protection against the virus [18]. In addition to routine immunization, WHO recommends conducting supplemental immunization activities (SIAs). These mass vaccination campaigns are designed to reach all children under the age of five, regardless of their previous vaccination status, with at least two additional doses of OPV. Rather than replacing routine immunization, SIAs serve to strengthen immunity in communities where access to regular healthcare may be limited [18]. Moreover, Surveillance plays a vital role in detecting and stopping poliovirus transmission. This involves investigating cases of acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) among children under 15 years old and analyzing stool samples in specialized laboratories to identify and analyze polioviruses [19]. Finally, in areas where polio cases are found, WHO recommends implementing targeted house-to-house mop-up vaccination campaigns. These focused efforts aim to quickly vaccinate children in high-risk zones to prevent localized transmission from spreading further [20].

Surveillance of polio is crucial for early detection and rapid response to outbreaks, preventing the spread of the virus and ensuring the success of global eradication efforts. There are two main types of surveillance: environmental surveillance and AFP surveillance [21]. Environmental surveillance involves monitoring wastewater or other environmental samples to detect the presence of poliovirus, often identifying transmission even before clinical cases emerge [22, 23]. AFP surveillance, considered the gold standard for monitoring polio cases, is a widely recognized case-based syndromic surveillance method [24]. It plays a central role in the polio eradication program by tracking the implementation of other strategies and providing critical data for decision-making. Without surveillance, it would be difficult to pinpoint the locations and patterns of transmission of wild poliovirus and circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (VDPV), or to verify the eradication of the virus [25]. The AFP surveillance system involves the identification and reporting of children presenting with AFP, followed by the collection and laboratory analysis of stool samples to confirm or rule out poliovirus infection [26, 27]. This process not only helps in identifying outbreaks and new cases but also verifies the presence of the virus through specialized polio laboratories. In addition, AFP surveillance relies on the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of data, including demographic variables such as age and sex, clinical signs and symptoms, vaccination status, location, and the timing of stool specimen collection [10, 28].

AFP surveillance is a sensitive approach for promptly detecting, reporting, and investigating confirmed poliomyelitis infections [2, 29]. However, the AFP surveillance system encounters difficulties in ensuring that cases are reported promptly and accurately. There are also challenges in maintaining high-quality specimen collection, transportation, and testing, as well as sustaining community participation and awareness regarding the importance of reporting AFP cases. Another limitation is the lack of access to a robust public health infrastructure and specialized laboratories [30–33].

Ongoing efforts, such as providing training and capacity building are implemented to overcome the limitations in AFP surveillance activities. Furthermore, there have been many efforts to extend AFP surveillance activities to hard-to-reach and conflict-affected areas while maintaining the sensitivity and specificity to mitigate the challenges in AFP surveillance. Moreover, recently, various technologies and approaches employed to bridge the limitations and challenges in the AFP surveillance system have been observed [34–36]. These include the use of mobile health applications for prompt and accurate data collection and reporting [37], Short Message Service (SMS) alerting system [31, 38] implementation of digital specimen tracking systems to ensure high-quality specimen collection and transportation [39, 40], and utilizing geospatial mapping and data visualization tools to identify hard-to-reach areas and plan surveillance activities [41]. Additionally, leveraging educational and awareness campaigns through social media and other digital platforms also helped to sustain community participation and awareness about the importance of reporting AFP cases [42]. Furthermore, continuous training programs using e-learning platforms contributed significantly to capacity building for healthcare workers [43, 44].

AFP cases are detected through two main strategies, health system-based and community-based surveillance, Fig. 1 The health system-based surveillance, which relies on information from healthcare facilities further categorized into active and passive surveillance [45]. Active surveillance involves trained personnel making regular visits to health facilities to identify, report, and investigate instances of diseases. These personnel are typically not part of the health facility and gather data from individual cases, registers, medical records, or logbooks at reporting sites to ensure that no cases are overlooked [46]. Active surveillance for AFP involves a highly sensitive system that enables rapid detection, reporting, investigation, and response to confirmed cases of poliomyelitis [47]. Passive surveillance involves the routine reporting of diseases or relevant conditions from designated reporting sites, such as health facilities and hospitals [48]. Community-based surveillance (CBS) is a surveillance strategy in which trained community members are engaged to report suspected AFP cases to a designated focal person based on the AFP case definition [26]. When there is population displacement, CBS is frequently developed in addition to surveillance in medical facilities. In hard-to-reach communities or locations with limited health center availability and accessibility, it facilitates the early detection of diseases prone to epidemics [49].

Despite the widespread implementation of these strategies and substantial progress in polio eradication, critical research gaps remain in understanding the evolving challenges and innovations within health system-based AFP surveillance. Existing studies often provide limited analysis of how health systems integrate technologies, maintain data quality, and respond effectively in high-risk or resource-constrained settings. Few offer comparative assessments of how variations in surveillance strategies influence case detection, timeliness, and response outcomes. While surveillance tools and practices have advanced notably, the literature still lacks a comprehensive synthesis focused specifically on health system-based AFP surveillance.

This review addresses these gaps by providing updated insights into system performance, technological integration, and operational challenges. It focuses on the crucial role of health system-based AFP surveillance in identifying potential polio cases, detecting outbreaks, and informing eradication strategies, Fig. 2 The review explores methods such as case identification, specimen collection, and laboratory analysis, while also examining the system’s challenges and limitations. Furthermore, the paper investigates innovative technological solutions designed to address the shortcomings of routine surveillance and outlines prospects for enhancing system performance. This review is significant as it fills the literature gap in the subject matter by providing fresh insights and innovative outlooks in research, policy, and operation. This paper advances research by pinpointing gaps in AFP surveillance methodologies, guiding targeted investigations for enhanced detection and response. Moreover, it informs policy by detailing innovative solutions and future directions for effective polio eradication. It also provides operational insights into overcoming practical challenges with technological advancements, thus improving global health surveillance systems.

Methodology

This study conducted a comprehensive literature review to identify the most relevant research on health system-based surveillance for AFP. The literature search was performed across multiple databases, including Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, JSTOR, DOAJ, IEEE Xplore, and ResearchGate. To refine and expand the search results, the strategy incorporated Boolean operators (e.g., AND, OR) to combine and structure key terms such as “polio AND AFP,” “poliomyelitis OR acute flaccid paralysis,” “AFP surveillance AND health system,” “wild poliovirus OR vaccine-derived poliovirus,” and “disease detection AND reporting OR case investigation.” This approach ensured comprehensive coverage of literature aligned with the review objectives. The search was further limited to articles published in English over the past 22 years (from 2002 to 2024), aligning with technological advancements and global efforts to enhance AFP surveillance systems. This targeted approach ensured that the review included the most relevant and up-to-date findings on the topic. PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the process of identifying, screening, assessing, and including studies in the review

Selection criteria were established to ensure the inclusion of impactful studies that specifically addressed health system-based AFP surveillance. Studies were prioritized based on their relevance to the role of health systems in identifying and managing AFP cases. The selection emphasized countries historically classified as polio-endemic, particularly those reporting cases of wild poliovirus (WPV) in recent years, such as Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Palestine. This assessment aimed to identify best practices and gaps within current surveillance frameworks. Additionally, the review included literature that provided insights into technological advancements and challenges within the AFP surveillance framework. During the review process, abstracts, methodologies, challenges, and technological advancements highlighted in the literature were systematically evaluated. This evaluation aimed to pinpoint studies that significantly contributed to enhancing health system-based surveillance. Conversely, studies focusing solely on AFP surveillance without integrating systemic roles were excluded to maintain alignment with the review’s primary focus. Non-empirical studies, subjective opinions, and research lacking methodological rigor were omitted to ensure the credibility and relevance of the review. This thorough selection process resulted in a robust collection of literature encompassing diverse case studies, reports, and analyses that represent various economic and geographic contexts. This work identified studies showcasing innovative approaches to health system involvement, demonstrating proven impacts on surveillance outcomes, and highlighting best practices for effectively detecting and reporting AFP.

Relevant data from the selected articles were carefully extracted, including study aims, methodologies, key findings, challenges, and practical recommendations for improving health system-based AFP surveillance. A manual, inductive thematic analysis was then conducted to synthesize and interpret this data. The process began with open coding of key ideas and recurring concepts found within the literature. These codes were then grouped and refined into broader emergent themes that reflected common patterns, contrasts, and insights across studies. No pre-existing coding framework was applied, allowing themes to emerge naturally from the data. Themes such as technological integration, barriers to effective surveillance, and country-specific challenges were iteratively developed and revised based on the frequency and relevance of issues discussed in the studies. This process facilitated the extraction of critical information, leading to meaningful insights, actionable recommendations, and well-founded conclusions.

AFP surveillance

AFP surveillance is particularly significant as it detects possible AFP cases in children under 15, including those caused by poliovirus [50]. According to WHO, an AFP case is defined as any child under the age of 15 who experiences a sudden onset of floppy paralysis or muscle weakness, regardless of the underlying cause. Additionally, it includes individuals of any age who exhibit paralysis if a clinician suspects poliomyelitis as a possible cause [51]. AFP can occur in a child who is highly suspected of having polio based on clinical symptoms or who has either received less than three doses of the polio vaccine or has recently traveled to a country where polio is prevalent (having returned up to 35 days before the onset of paralysis) or belongs to a high-risk group [2]. This sensitive case definition can include Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS), transverse myelitis, traumatic neuritis, encephalitis, brain tumors, acute poliomyelitis, and other conditions with comparable clinical manifestations, as illustrated in Fig. 4, which summarizes the differential diagnoses and causes of AFP [52, 53]. GBS is a rapid and severe inflammatory nerve disorder characterized by progressive limb weakness and generalized areflexia or hyporeflexia [54]. Transverse myelitis is an acute inflammatory disorder that typically affects the thoracic/dorsal region of the spinal cord, resulting in rapid progressive weakness, sensory deficits, and potential bowel and bladder dysfunction [55, 56]. Traumatic neuritis is a serious condition commonly resulting from injuries to the upper or lower extremities, often leading to physical disability. It primarily affects young adults [57]. Furthermore, encephalitis is the inflammation of the brain’s active tissues caused by infection or autoimmune response, resulting in symptoms such as headache, stiff neck, sensitivity to light, confusion, and seizures [58]. A brain tumor is an abnormal growth of cells in the brain or surrounding tissues, and its symptoms may include persistent headaches, neurological changes, increased intracranial pressure, as well as fatigue and sensory changes [59].

Common differential diagnoses and causes of AFP, including infections, inflammation, injuries, and tumors

For the purpose of conducting AFP surveillance, the WHO advises taking four steps: locating and notifying children who have AFP; sending stool samples for examination; isolating and detecting poliovirus in a lab; and mapping the virus to ascertain the strain’s origin [60]. When a patient who meets the AFP case description is examined at a health facility, healthcare practitioners do extensive studies to rule out poliovirus as the cause of paralysis [61]. These include obtaining a detailed medical history, performing a systematic examination, and collecting two stool specimens in 24 to 48 h apart, within 14 days of the onset of symptoms, Fig. 5 The case investigation form, which includes demographic, clinical, and vaccination information, as well as the dates of stool specimen collection, is completed and sent to the WHO-accredited laboratory for viral isolation and identification [61, 62].

The AFP surveillance system’s goal is to provide critical information for developing effective polio eradication strategies, identifying specific areas where poliovirus transmission is occurring, investigating all cases of AFP, confirming the absence of wild poliovirus in the country, and evaluating the impact of interventions such as routine immunization and supplementary immunization activities on polio [28]. Goals of AFP surveillance: detect, investigate, confirm absence of poliovirus, and assess eradication progress

To achieve these goals, effective AFP surveillance depends on a robust and well-coordinated network of district surveillance officers and health facility surveillance focal persons. These individuals are responsible for rapidly detecting, reporting, and investigating cases of AFP. Both public and private healthcare providers are involved in this network, and they often receive support from organizations like the WHO [34]. There are specific performance indicators in place to evaluate the effectiveness of AFP surveillance systems [63]. A sensitive AFP surveillance system network should detect at least two cases of nonpolio AFP (NPAFP) annually per 100,000 children under 15 years of age. In addition, performance indicators for AFP surveillance require that reporting be timely and complete, representing the country’s geography [64].

Surveillance performance indicators

Performance indicators in AFP surveillance are specific measures that help evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of surveillance systems in detecting and monitoring cases of AFP. These indicators provide quantitative data to assess various aspects of surveillance, including case detection, laboratory testing, and data quality [53, 65].

AFP cases identified and reported during surveillance can be either polio-related or non-polio-related. NPAFP is a type of AFP caused by conditions other than poliovirus, such as GBS, transverse myelitis, encephalitis, traumatic neuritis, and other non-polio enteroviruses. These conditions are determined based on clinical assessment or laboratory testing [66, 67].

AFP surveillance adequacy and quality are assessed using key performance indicators recommended by WHO, Table 1. These indicators include the sensitivity of surveillance, with at least two cases of NPAFP should be detected annually per 100,000 population under the age of fifteen years, during outbreaks, this threshold increases to three cases per 100,000 children under fifteen years to enhance the sensitivity of the surveillance, the collection of 2 sufficient stool samples (≥ 8 g) 24 h apart and within 14 days of the onset of paralysis, the adequacy of stool specimens, timeliness of the cases, and follow-up examination for residual paralysis within 60–90 days of onset [61, 68–72].

Surveillance for poliovirus depends on two main factors. The first factor is the detection, reporting, and stool testing of AFP in children or paralysis in anyone suspected of having poliomyelitis. Prompt detection and reporting of AFP cases is crucial for initiating the investigation and response process, and collecting stool samples from these cases and testing them for the presence of poliovirus is essential for confirming the diagnosis and determining the poliovirus type. The second crucial factor is the availability of a specialized laboratory that can accurately identify poliovirus and differentiate between wild poliovirus and VDPV. Identifying the specific type and strain of poliovirus is critical for understanding the source and origin of the virus, and laboratories that perform intratypic differentiation (ITD) testing can distinguish between wild-type poliovirus and VDPV, which is essential for determining the appropriate public health response [73, 74]. This combination of robust AFP surveillance and a strong laboratory network capable of poliovirus identification and characterization is the foundation of the global polio surveillance system, enabling the early detection of poliovirus circulation, facilitating prompt outbreak response, and supporting the ultimate goal of polio eradication worldwide [75].

In addition to AFP surveillance, wastewater-based surveillance (WWS), also referred to as environmental surveillance, has become a vital tool in poliovirus monitoring. This method involves sampling and testing sewage for polioviruses excreted in human feces, enabling the detection of virus circulation even in the absence of clinical cases. Since more than 90% of poliovirus infections are asymptomatic, WWS serves as an early warning system by identifying silent transmission in high-risk or under-reported areas and regions with weak health infrastructure. The GPEI and WHO have emphasized the integration of WWS to detect both wild poliovirus and VDPV, validate polio-free status, and prompt targeted immunization responses [76]. Recent studies highlight how WWS has been successfully applied not only for poliovirus but also for emerging pathogens like COVID-19 and Monkeypox, showcasing its potential as a scalable and sensitive public health surveillance tool [77, 78].

Diagnosis process

The laboratory diagnosis of poliovirus involves three main procedures: viral isolation to confirm the presence of poliovirus in stool samples, intratypic differentiation to distinguish vaccine strains from wild poliovirus strains, and sequencing for molecular epidemiology [79]. Sequencing provides detailed molecular genetic data that not only confirms the poliovirus type but also reveals genetic relationships between virus isolates. This information is essential for tracking the evolution and movement of polioviruses. By comparing nucleotide sequences, health authorities can identify chains of transmission, detect importation events, and distinguish between endemic circulation and reintroduced strains [80]. These procedures are aligned with the three tiers within the Global Polio Laboratory Network (GPLN). GPLN consists of global specialized laboratories (GSLs), national laboratories (NLs), and regional reference laboratories (RRLs). Some countries have established subnational laboratories, which are designed to operate to the same standards as a national laboratory and carry out some or all of the duties of a national laboratory [81]. The GPLN consists of accredited laboratories in the six WHO regions, including the African region, the Region of the Americas, the South-East Asia Region, the European Region, the Eastern Mediterranean Region, and the Western Pacific Region [82–84]. These laboratories are monitored through a standardized quality assurance program involving annual onsite audits and proficiency testing. National laboratories isolate polioviruses, regional laboratories conduct intratypic differentiation to identify WPV, VDPV, and Sabin polioviruses, and global specialized laboratories perform genomic sequencing [85]. Figure 7 illustrates the three-tiered laboratory system of the GPLN, which consists of National Laboratories, Regional Reference Laboratories, and Global Specialized Laboratories. Each level has specific roles: NLs conduct virus isolation, RRLs perform intratypic differentiation and confirm the virus type, distinguishing between wild poliovirus, vaccine-derived poliovirus, and Sabin strains, and GSLs conduct sequencing for molecular epidemiology to identify transmission pathways and determine the origins of the virus. The data generated by these laboratories inform national and global decisions about outbreak response, immunization efforts, and surveillance strategies.

Structure and function of the Global Polio Laboratory Network in tracking and analyzing poliovirus

Poliovirus sequencing is a crucial method used to track poliovirus transmission pathways. This is achieved by comparing the nucleotide sequence of poliovirus isolates. The results of this sequencing technique help detect connections between polio cases, identify local sources of polio, recognize imported polioviruses, and monitor progress toward eradicating the disease. Monitoring the decreasing diversity of virus lineages in circulation is one way to gauge progress toward eradication. Sequencing data also play a key role in characterizing VDPVs, which are mutated strains of the oral polio vaccine virus that have undergone genetic changes over time and regained neurovirulence [86]. This molecular information enables polio program managers at the local, regional, and global levels to make critical decisions about outbreak response, immunization, and to assess progress toward eradication. Moreover, sequencing data aids in evaluating surveillance sensitivity [87].

The virological data provided by the GPLN, which is based on the confirmed circulation of WPV or VDPV, helps make decisions about where targeted immunization and surveillance activities should take place. This data is also used to monitor progress toward the eradication of polio by tracking the decreasing genetic diversity and identifying transmission links of poliovirus isolates [79]. The laboratory is essential to make sure that the goal of polio eradication is achieved. Since AFP can be caused by substances other than polioviruses, a virological examination is necessary for all suspected cases [88]. Only a small percentage of poliovirus infections exhibit clinical symptoms, while most infections are asymptomatic. Because of this, stool samples from each AFP case that has been discovered need to be thoroughly and methodically examined for the presence of poliovirus, as missing the virus in one case could result in the missed infection of a thousand [81].

Laboratory procedures

Stool samples are first treated with chloroform to remove bacteria, fungi, and potentially harmful lipids, as well as separate viral clumps [20]. The stool suspension from all AFP cases is then added to human rhabdomyosarcoma (RD) and mouse L cells transfected with the human cellular receptor gene for poliovirus (L20B) cell cultures [89]. These cell cultures are incubated at 36 °C in a stationary inclined position. Isolates that only show a cytopathic effect (CPE) in the RD cell line are considered non-polio enteroviruses [90]. Meanwhile, isolates that exhibit CPE on both the RD and L20B cell lines are thought to be polioviruses and are then subjected to intratypic differentiation using real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction. Through this process, polioviruses are classified as Sabin-like poliovirus types 1, 2, or 3, VDPV, nonenterovirus, or nonpolio enterovirus [90–92].

The health system-based AFP surveillance involves an extended process of case investigation, specimen collection, and data analysis, Fig. 8. When a suspected polio case is identified, an AFP case investigator collects stool samples and relevant case information, which are then sent to the NL. At the NL, virus isolation and analysis are performed, and the virus isolates and typing data are forwarded to the RRL for sequence analysis. The sequence analysis result is then sent to the GSL. Concurrently, the national polio program receives the ITD test result from the NL and updates the national line lists of AFP cases, which are shared with the WHO regional office, along with the sequence analysis result from the GSL. The WHO headquarters then receives the national summaries of AFP cases from the WHO regional office, enabling centralized monitoring and reporting of the AFP surveillance system.

Challenges in the health system-based AFP surveillance

Infectious diseases are the major contributors to higher mortality rates. Effective epidemiological surveillance systems are crucial for identifying potential outbreaks and taking necessary action. However, the surveillance systems have been criticized for failing to detect outbreaks promptly. The public health institutions lack timely and comprehensive data, hindering effective response measures. Furthermore, the knowledge and capacity of health workers to assess and interpret available information are limited, leading to inadequate investigation of epidemic trends and potential missed outbreaks [93]. The same is true when it comes to AFP surveillance. AFP surveillance has a wide range of limitations that are unique to each country implementing health system-based AFP surveillance. The following sections present a brief description of challenges unique to some countries across the world and general challenges in health system-based AFP surveillance.

Challenges of AFP surveillance by country

Pakistan

Polio eradication efforts in Pakistan face significant challenges that hinder progress. The cases of wild poliovirus type 1 are concentrated in insecure areas, and there is resistance to vaccination within the community, making it difficult to carry out immunization efforts. Moreover, there is a risk of cross-border transmission from Afghanistan, where WPV1 continues to circulate, posing a serious threat [94, 95]. Operational issues, such as inaccuracies in caregiver recall and fake finger-marking by some vaccinators, result in many missed children during supplementary immunization activities. The safety of frontline health workers is also a concern due to targeted attacks, which create an environment of fear and discourage community engagement [94].

Afghanistan

Polio eradication efforts in Afghanistan are encountering significant challenges. House-to-house vaccination campaigns in some areas are restricted, limiting the effectiveness of supplementary immunization activities. The shared border with Pakistan, where polio transmission persists, complicates these efforts due to population movement [96]. Moreover, approximately 54% of Afghan children suffer from malnutrition, which impairs their immune response to the oral poliovirus vaccine. Concerns about the accuracy of vaccination coverage reporting and a shift in the age of polio cases, with older children now being more susceptible, further hinder progress [97].

Palestine

Polio eradication efforts in Palestine are encountering several significant challenges. One major issue is inadequate AFP surveillance. Despite being certified polio-free since 2006, there remains a risk of poliovirus importation from regions where polio is still reported, particularly in the Eastern Mediterranean region, necessitating continuous monitoring and rapid response capabilities. The absence of a national laboratory for primary poliovirus isolation and identification further complicates the healthcare system’s ability to effectively respond to and manage AFP cases [98]. Maintaining high immunization coverage rates is crucial, as any decline in vaccination could increase the risk of polio resurgence [99]. Amid these ongoing challenges, the situation in Gaza has become even more dire with the emergence of new polio cases in 2024. This alarming development compounds the existing hardships faced by the population, including limited access to healthcare, ongoing conflict, and severe humanitarian crises. The reemergence of polio not only threatens public health but also underscores the vulnerability of communities already struggling to cope with a multitude of adversities [100–102].

Ethiopia

In Ethiopia, the health system-based AFP surveillance faces unique challenges due to the country’s vast and inaccessible terrain, such as the remote highlands and arid lowlands, which hinder timely case detection and reporting. Furthermore, ongoing conflicts in the country disrupt surveillance efforts and limit health workers’ access to affected areas [103]. The presence of nomadic populations, particularly in the Somali and Afar regions, further complicates tracking and follow-up on AFP cases due to their constant movement across borders. These challenges are compounded by cultural barriers, where traditional beliefs in rural areas may lead to underreporting or delays in seeking medical care. Moreover, the country has encountered difficulties in maintaining stool condition, timely detection, and transportation of specimens from remote and hard-to-reach areas [50].

China

Poliomyelitis has historically been widespread in China. To prevent the spread of the virus, China relied on routine immunization and supplementary immunization activities, supported by surveillance. However, the risk of poliovirus importation from regions with ongoing transmission requires high-quality surveillance [104, 105]. Unfortunately, the country has faced challenges in collecting stool specimens from cases of AFP, underreporting due to limited coverage, logistical issues in sample collection, and the need for increased involvement of clinicians to ensure prompt reporting and adherence to surveillance protocols [106].

South Sudan

Polio eradication activities in South Sudan began in 1998 in collaboration with WHO and the Sudan Peoples’ Liberation Movement while the country was part of Sudan. These efforts were initially part of the Sudan polio program until South Sudan officially joined the African Region [107]. South Sudan was declared wild poliovirus-free by the Africa Regional Certification Commission in June 2020. However, the country has faced civil war, political instability, and armed conflicts since gaining independence in 2011. These challenges have severely impacted the healthcare system and surveillance efforts for AFP. Geographical inaccessibility and insufficient stool adequacy in certain areas further complicate surveillance, impeding timely testing and data collection. Suboptimal data quality also creates barriers to accurate surveillance [108].

Nigeria

The AFP surveillance system plays a crucial role in Nigeria’s effort to eradicate polio. It provides important information to alert healthcare managers and clinicians so they can take timely action to stop the spread of poliovirus. Nigeria’s surveillance strategy includes passive and active components, with data collected from reporting sites at primary healthcare facilities and hospitals. Local Government Area Disease Surveillance and Notification Officers (LGA DSNOs) are responsible for gathering and documenting surveillance activities at the community level, ensuring that accurate information flows up to higher levels of the health system [109]. However, there are several challenges in documentation and knowledge among healthcare workers. These include discrepancies in documentation, such as incorrect birthdates and dates of paralysis onset, incomplete feedback to caregivers, and a lack of awareness among community leaders about reporting AFP cases. DSNOs may also have insufficient documentation and archiving issues, which can result in missed cases of wild poliovirus outbreaks, especially in areas hosting displaced persons. Furthermore, capacity challenges such as chronic insecurity and lack of infrastructure have been observed, further complicating surveillance efforts [110].

Ghana

Ghana’s health system-based surveillance is an important component of its public health infrastructure. It is designed to monitor and respond to infectious diseases through the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework. The system operates at national, regional, and district levels, with a focus on community-based surveillance to ensure early detection and response to health threats. The National Technical Coordination Committee (NTCC) coordinates these efforts, bringing together experts from various sectors to address public health emergencies. Key activities include monitoring diseases like poliovirus and evaluating routine immunization coverage [111]. Despite its strengths, the system faces challenges such as inadequate knowledge among health staff regarding AFP surveillance, which is crucial for detecting poliovirus cases. Many health facilities lack IDSR technical guideline booklets, which hinder effective surveillance practices. Additionally, there is insufficient evidence of regular record reviews and limited supervisory visits, which are important for maintaining data accuracy and adherence to protocols. The surveillance system struggles to effectively reach mobile populations, such as nomadic pastoralists, despite high reported vaccination coverage. Additionally, logistical challenges, including poor road networks and delays in coordination, can hinder timely outbreak investigations and responses [112].

Iran

Iran has had an AFP surveillance system in place as part of its public health strategy to eliminate poliomyelitis since 1990. This system, in line with the WHO’s GPEI, focuses on identifying poliomyelitis cases among children under fifteen, as AFP is a crucial indicator of the disease. With almost 100% coverage of the OPV since 2002, Iran has successfully maintained its status as polio-free since the last wild poliovirus case was reported in 2000. The surveillance system plays a critical role in monitoring the potential re-importation of the virus, particularly considering the endemic nature of poliomyelitis in neighboring countries. However, Iran’s AFP surveillance system faces several challenges. Geographical barriers, such as mountainous regions and remote areas, hinder access to healthcare and timely reporting, potentially leading to underreporting. The proximity to endemic countries like Pakistan and Afghanistan increases the risk of virus re-importation due to travel and migration [113, 114].

General challenges in the health system-based AFP surveillance

Effective surveillance of AFP cases is critical for monitoring and responding to outbreaks, particularly in areas where polio remains endemic. However, AFP surveillance faces several key challenges globally.

Lack of timeliness

Timeliness in AFP surveillance refers to the prompt detection, reporting, investigation, and response to suspected cases by the public health system [115, 116]. Maintaining high timeliness is essential to prevent further transmission of poliovirus, enable early outbreak intervention, and ensure accurate surveillance data. Delays at any stage can lead to missed or late identification of cases, increasing the risk of outbreaks, especially in areas with low immunization coverage, and undermining efforts to track and interrupt virus transmission. Ultimately, delays can hinder progress toward polio eradication by allowing undetected transmission to continue [117, 118].

Lack of awareness

Awareness among healthcare workers is crucial for an effective surveillance system [119]. Healthcare workers must comprehend the importance of AFP surveillance, understand case definitions and reporting protocols, demonstrate proficiency in clinical diagnosis and management, be familiar with investigation and follow-up processes, and be dedicated to timely reporting and follow-up. However, many healthcare workers lack adequate training, which leads to underreporting and misidentification of cases [28, 120].

Poor data quality

Public health surveillance data is vital for monitoring disease trends, identifying outbreaks, and informing response activities. It also plays a crucial role in allocating resources and evaluating public health interventions, policies, and strategies. Policymakers and public health agencies depend on high-quality data to make informed decisions. In infectious disease surveillance, high-quality data, defined by its reliability, timeliness, completeness, and accuracy, is essential for early outbreak detection and effective control [121]. However, AFP surveillance faces challenges in data quality and completeness. Incomplete or incorrect data can lead to underreporting or misreporting of AFP cases, making it difficult to track the true incidence and spread of the disease [25]. Additionally, poor data quality can delay the identification of outbreaks, leading to slow public health responses and potentially allowing the diseases to spread further [118, 122].

Inaccessibility

Ensuring effective surveillance for AFP in hard-to-reach areas poses significant challenges for public health authorities. These regions often encounter geographical barriers such as remoteness, mountainous terrain, or dense forests, making it hard for healthcare workers to reach potential AFP case sites [52, 123]. Socioeconomic factors, such as limited financial resources and access to healthcare services, including the associated costs of transportation, training, and maintaining cold chains for vaccines, Additionally, cultural beliefs and practices also affect the willingness of communities to engage with healthcare workers or report cases of AFP [124]. Infrastructural deficiencies, such as the absence of functional healthcare facilities, lack of trained personnel, and inadequate supply chains for essential medical equipment and supplies, are also limiting factors [125]. Implementing effective AFP surveillance in conflict-affected areas is quite challenging. This is due to security risks, disrupted healthcare infrastructure, restricted population access, broken communication, and eroded community trust [70, 126].

Inadequate stool specimen

It is important to emphasize the significance of collecting and handling stool specimens properly in the AFP surveillance system. The adequacy of stool specimens is a crucial aspect of the global AFP surveillance system, which is used to identify and track polio cases worldwide [127]. Important factors that determine the adequacy of stool specimens include the timing of collection, sample quality, appropriate labeling, and packaging, as well as timely transportation and testing [128, 129]. The presence of inadequate practices can significantly hinder global efforts to eradicate polio. Without reliable data from stool specimens, accurately identifying and characterizing the poliovirus strains becomes challenging. This in turn makes it difficult to detect new outbreaks and monitor eradication progress. Furthermore, compromised data quality and reliability of surveillance make it hard to pinpoint virus transmission locations and modes, and to verify complete eradication. Ultimately, poor management of stool specimens undermines the effectiveness of the AFP surveillance system, a critical element in the WHO’s polio eradication strategies [130, 131].

Technologies used to bridge the gap in AFP surveillance

Technology has greatly enhanced healthcare delivery. The internet, mobile phones, remote sensing, geographic information systems, geospatial mapping tools, and open data kits have all had an impact on public health practice. Digital technology has improved epidemiological analysis, illness prevention, control, and elimination, as well as health education and health promotion. Disease surveillance has benefited from technological improvements, notably in the areas of data collection and analysis of suspected diseases [93].

In 2016, Datta et al. [31] conducted a study in Papua New Guinea to enhance AFP surveillance by implementing a Short Message Service (SMS) alert system. The approach involved sending bi-monthly SMS reminders to healthcare professionals, which included pediatricians and provincial disease control officers. These messages prompted the recipients to report any AFP cases they encountered by replying with “1” for yes and “0” for no. Frontline SMS software was utilized to manage the SMS system, which operated through the Digicel mobile service provider in Papua New Guinea. Throughout the study, indicators such as participation rates, SMS response times, changes in reported AFP cases, and stool adequacy were monitored to assess the impact of the SMS alert system on AFP surveillance.

The study conducted by Njeru et al. [132] offers a comprehensive assessment of the impact of training on surveillance indicators during Kenya’s transition from a standalone web-based system to the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2). DHIS2 is a powerful open-source software platform tailored for health information management. It allows for seamless collection, validation, and aggregation of health data from various sources. Users can customize data entry forms to capture a wide range of health indicators and program-specific data elements. DHIS2 ensures data quality and accuracy through its validation mechanisms and allows users to generate insightful reports, charts, graphs, and maps for trend analysis. The platform’s user-friendly interface, multilingual support, and mobile data collection capabilities enhance accessibility, particularly in remote settings. Its interoperability with other health systems, scalability, flexibility, and active community engagement makes it pivotal in strengthening health information systems and facilitating data-driven decision-making processes globally.

The study conducted by Bassey et al. [37] found that the implementation of the Open Data Kit (ODK) significantly improved the AFP surveillance system in South Sudan. ODK allows real-time data collection and analysis, providing immediate access to crucial information for timely decision-making. Geocoding of AFP cases enhanced outbreak tracking, addressing a significant gap in previous reporting. Furthermore, ODK standardized data collection processes, resulting in more accurate reporting and improved monitoring of health facilities through organized supervision visits. This system has guided targeted health interventions, enabling effective resource allocation and training.

In a study carried out by Shuaib et al. [133] in Nigeria, the SMS-based smartphone application Auto-Visual AFP Detection and Reporting (AVADAR) provides significant advantages for detecting and reporting cases of acute flaccid paralysis. The system, aimed at enhancing AFP surveillance, improves sensitivity by allowing swift case detection and reporting through a user-friendly smartphone interface, ultimately boosting the efficiency of the surveillance network. Additionally, AVADAR raises awareness of AFP among community informants and healthcare workers through educational videos, resulting in better case identification and reporting. The application facilitates effective communication by enabling instant notification of AFP cases with minimal information collected and sent directly to the appropriate authorities for investigation. Furthermore, AVADAR’s geolocation capabilities and feedback loop mechanisms ensure accurate case tracking and follow-up, enhancing the overall surveillance process. Cost-effective and innovative, AVADAR harnesses mobile technology to detect more AFP cases, particularly in security-compromised and silent reporting areas, compared to traditional surveillance systems, demonstrating its potential to significantly improve AFP surveillance in Nigeria and support global public health initiatives.

Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have become a cornerstone in disease surveillance and immunization campaigns. Tools such as eSurv and Integrated Supportive Supervision (ISS) mobile applications enable real-time outbreak detection, active case reporting, and on-the-ground monitoring. These applications help identify surveillance gaps and disease trends, empowering health programs to respond proactively. Additionally, satellite imagery and digital elevation models allow for detailed mapping of inhabited and uninhabited areas, tracking population movements, and defining surveillance catchment zones. Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking of vaccination teams ensures campaign coverage completeness, reduces the number of missed children, and supports targeted interventions in hard-to-reach areas [134].

Parallel to these spatial tools, laboratory network enhancements, especially within the GPLN, have significantly strengthened diagnostic capacity. Key technological innovations include the integration of real-time reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) for serotype-specific identification of poliovirus strains. These molecular assays enable rapid differentiation between wild polioviruses, vaccine-derived polioviruses, and other variants, thereby allowing for swift public health responses. Furthermore, genetic sequencing technologies now offer granular insights into virus evolution and transmission dynamics, enabling early detection of emerging clusters. Automation and standardized protocols across laboratories have further improved the efficiency, consistency, and reliability of test results, making GPLN a critical pillar of the global polio eradication strategy [135].

Complementing these developments, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning have emerged as powerful tools in epidemiological surveillance. AI systems are capable of processing vast, complex datasets from sources like electronic health records, social media, airline travel logs, and news reports, allowing for real-time disease tracking and outbreak forecasting [136]. Notable platforms like BlueDot and HealthMap exemplify this capability. BlueDot, developed in Canada, utilizes machine learning to detect outbreaks early, famously providing an advanced alert about the COVID-19 outbreak in Wuhan [137]. HealthMap, originating from Boston Children’s Hospital, uses natural language processing to visualize outbreaks on interactive maps, supporting rapid identification and response to global health threats [138]. In the U.S., the CDC’s BioSense platform analyzes emergency department and health record data to enhance national situational awareness and guide public health interventions [136].

The integration of AI has also shown particularly promising results. A notable example is the WOA-RVFL (Whale Optimization Algorithm – Random Vector Functional Link) hybrid model. This AI-based method combines a randomized neural network with a bio-inspired optimization algorithm to predict VDPV outbreak incidence accurately. By analyzing diverse epidemiological datasets, including immunization records and demographic information, WOA-RVFL has demonstrated superior predictive performance over traditional machine learning models. Its validation using real-world data from VDPV outbreaks in Lao PDR, as well as benchmark datasets, confirms its potential to inform vaccination strategies, identify immunity gaps, and support targeted health responses in high-risk areas [21].

Outlook

To this point, this survey emphasized the importance of AFP surveillance, discussed the challenges faced, and explored the technologies used to bridge the gap. This section covers the recommendations to enhance the health system-based AFP surveillance.

First, leveraging mobile health technologies and community health workers can facilitate better outreach and ensure that health services reach underserved populations. Strengthening logistics and supply chain management for specimen handling, along with setting clear timelines for collection and processing, will improve the overall efficiency of the surveillance system. Collaborating with local organizations, such as community-based NGOs, religious institutions, and women’s groups, can facilitate rapid mobilization and logistics support. To raise community awareness and promote early case detection, targeted initiatives such as SMS-based education campaigns, community dialogue forums, radio spots in local languages, and school-based education programs should be employed. These strategies will improve knowledge of polio symptoms and increase reporting rates in high-risk or low-surveillance areas [103].

Second, integrating AFP surveillance with routine immunization will improve overall health service delivery. Establishing robust data collection systems is essential for tracking AFP cases, monitoring vaccination coverage, and identifying gaps in surveillance. This will enable regular assessments and effective resource allocation [139].

Third, the integration of advanced technologies such as deep learning, machine learning, and data analytics should be actively pursued to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of surveillance systems. AI can process and analyze large volumes of data, including demographic information (age, vaccination history), geospatial data (case clustering, surveillance coverage gaps), mobility patterns, and health system indicators, to detect anomalies and predict potential outbreak zones with high accuracy [140]. Additionally, predictive models can help prioritize resource allocation by flagging high-risk populations or regions with declining immunization rates. Automating routine processes such as data validation, case classification, and risk scoring reduces the workload for health personnel, enabling faster response times and allowing healthcare professionals to focus on strategic decision-making and frontline care [141].

Fourth, community engagement efforts must go beyond general awareness-raising. Specific strategies could include school-based education programs to teach students and parents about polio symptoms and prevention; community dialogues led by trusted local figures to dispel vaccine myths; and SMS-based campaigns in local languages to encourage prompt reporting of AFP cases. These approaches not only increase early detection but also build public trust in the surveillance system and vaccination campaigns.

Conclusion

This comprehensive survey on health system-based AFP surveillance highlighted the crucial role of this strategy in the global effort to eradicate poliovirus. It provided valuable insights and innovative approaches in research, policy, and operational practices related to AFP surveillance that resulted in enhancing detection and response capabilities for polio eradication. This work also identified key challenges in the health system-based AFP surveillance, including maintaining high-quality data collection, transportation, and testing, as well as sustaining community participation and awareness among healthcare workers. Moreover, the review discussed the various technological solutions that have been implemented to overcome these challenges, such as short message alert systems, open data kits for data collection, digital specimen tracking, geospatial mapping, and awareness campaigns. These innovative interventions were deemed crucial for the success of the global polio eradication effort.

The findings of this study filled a notable gap in the literature and suggested practical applications for strengthening AFP surveillance. Future research directions include the integration of advanced technologies and data analytics to further refine surveillance strategies. Exploring the integration of AFP surveillance with other disease monitoring systems and analyzing the impact of these innovations on polio eradication outcomes, such as timely case detection, outbreak response, and interruption of virus transmission, were also identified as promising avenues for future research.

The implications of this work extend beyond polio eradication, highlighting the need for continuous improvement in surveillance systems and the integration of innovative technologies. The lessons learned and technological advancements realized in the health system-based AFP surveillance can be applied to other infectious diseases prevention and control, such as tuberculosis, malaria, and emerging zoonotic diseases. Key components like digital reporting platforms, structured case detection frameworks, and robust laboratory networks can be adapted to enhance early detection, data accuracy, and response speed across disease areas. These elements collectively strengthen surveillance infrastructure, enabling broader public health resilience and preparedness.

Limitation

One key limitation of this research is its restriction to literature published in English between 2002 and 2024. This language and time-frame constraint may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages or earlier works that could provide valuable historical context, alternative perspectives, or additional insights into the challenges and evolution of AFP surveillance systems.

Definition of key terms

AFP

a clinical syndrome characterized by the sudden onset of weakness or paralysis with reduced muscle tone (flaccidity) in one or more limbs, typically without obvious sensory loss.

Polio

a highly contagious infectious disease caused by the poliovirus, which mainly affects nerves in the spinal cord or brain stem.

Disease surveillance

the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of health data for monitoring and preventing disease, guiding public health decisions, and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions.
