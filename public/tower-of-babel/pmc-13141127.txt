Intraoperative endoscopic pylorus dilatation during minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy to prevent delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE): protocol for the WIDE randomised controlled trial
Authors: Lucien Cron, Jennifer M Klasen, Kerstin Neuschütz, Amanda Dirnberger, Alia Maaser, Johannes Baur, Henriette Heinrich, Ines Gockel, Martin Bolli, Beat P Müller, Marko Kraljević
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Oesophageal cancer has a poor prognosis and oesophagectomy has increasingly adopted minimally invasive techniques, but delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE) still occurs in ~10–50% of patients, depending on the used diagnostic criteria and definitions. DGCE can cause serious complications, such as aspiration pneumonia, and it prolongs hospital stay and the overall morbidity of patients. Surgical pyloric drainage procedures (pyloroplasty or pyloromyotomy) have not clearly improved outcomes. Endoscopic pyloric balloon dilatation (EPBD) effectively treats established DGCE, and emerging evidence suggests that prophylactic EPBD may reduce its incidence. However, no randomised trial has evaluated intraoperative EPBD during minimally invasive oesophagectomy until now. The WIDE trial will test whether adding an EPBD during oesophagectomy can prevent early postoperative DGCE.Methods and analysisWIDE is a single-centre, double-blind randomised controlled trial, in which 116 adult patients undergoing minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy for oesophageal cancer are randomly assigned to intraoperative endoscopic pyloric balloon dilatation (30 mm EPBD) versus no pyloric intervention. Patients with prior gastric surgery, non-curative disease or American Society of Anesthesiologists class V are excluded. The EPBD is following induction of general anaesthesia and endotracheal intubation, and immediately prior to or concomitant with the start of the surgical procedure in the operating room. Control patients will receive standard surgery without any pyloric procedure. Patients and outcome assessors are blinded to the groups. The primary outcome is early DGCE after 5 and 10 days post-op, defined as nasogastric tube output ≥500 mL on postoperative day 5 or later and/or >100% increase in gastric conduit width on imaging (vs immediate post-op baseline). Key secondary outcomes include DGCE at 3 months, postoperative complication rates (eg, anastomotic leak, pneumonia, aspiration), recovery metrics (time to first bowel movement, time to resume solid food, vomiting episodes), length of hospital and ICU stay, and 3-month quality of life. Analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle. A sample size of 104 patients (52 per arm) provides 80% power to detect a meaningful reduction in DGCE incidence (α=0.05). The target enrolment is 116 to allow for ~10% attrition.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol (V.1.3) has been approved by the Northwestern and Central Switzerland Ethics Committee (EKNZ; BASEC 2025-01877). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. The trial is classified as a Category A (minimal-risk) study under the Swiss Human Research Act and Clinical Trials Ordinance (ClinO). Findings will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations, as well as in our in-hospital journal in a lay version for patients.Trial registration numberNCT07355374.Keywords: Randomized Controlled Trial, Oesophageal disease, Cancer, SURGERY, Thoracic surgery, Adult surgery

Introduction

Oesophageal carcinoma is a significant global health problem, with an estimated 604 000 new cases worldwide in 2020.1 The prognosis remains poor in most cases.2 Surgical resection (oncologic oesophagectomy) is a cornerstone of curative treatment for localised oesophageal cancer. The adoption of minimally invasive oesophagectomy techniques in recent years has reduced perioperative morbidity compared with open surgery.3 However, delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE), also referred to as delayed gastric emptying or pyloric dysfunction (spasm), is a common postoperative complication, occurring in approximately 10–50% of patients.4 DGCE is characterised by impaired emptying of the gastric conduit after oesophagogastric anastomosis, typically manifesting as high nasogastric (NG) output, intolerance of oral intake, nausea and vomiting in the first postoperative week. Importantly, retained conduit contents increase the risk of aspiration pneumonia (a serious complication that can worsen postoperative mortality5 and often prolong intensive care unit and hospital stays). Furthermore, by increased pressure on the oesophagogastric anastomosis due to gastric outlet obstruction, it might lead to increased anastomotic dehiscence/insufficiency. This is the most feared complication in oesophageal surgery with known severe consequences, such as mediastinitis, sepsis, bronchial/tracheal fistula and aortic arrosion bleeding, all associated with a reasonable mortality.

The aetiology of DGCE is multifactorial. Intraoperative vagus nerve disruption (vagotomy) during oesophagectomy, which is an obligatory step in oncologic procedures, is believed to contribute to pyloric sphincter dysfunction, often leading to pyloric spasm and, in addition, it is well-known that antro-duodenal motility is disturbed by vagal discontinuity. The transposition of the stomach into the negative-pressure thoracic cavity and local ischaemia or oedema of the gastric conduit, often caused by the intraoperative trauma of tube formation, lymphadenectomy and pull-up, may also impair gastric motility. Various strategies have been explored to mitigate or prevent DGCE. Historically, many surgeons performed a pyloric drainage procedure at the time of oesophagectomy (pyloroplasty or pyloromyotomy) to facilitate gastric emptying by permanently widening the pylorus. In many cases, the pylorus was also dilated using forceps to dilate and a gastrotomy to access the pylorus. In the era of minimally invasive surgery, the direct intraoperative access to the pylorus has changed. However, evidence for routine pyloric drainage is inconclusive. A systematic review found no significant difference in DGCE rates with routine pyloric drainage vs no drainage.6 Moreover, a small randomised study likewise found no difference in outcomes, whether a pyloromyotomy, an intraoperative pyloric dilation or no drainage procedure was performed.7 Thus, surgical drainage has not clearly demonstrated a relevant benefit and can introduce its own risks (eg, added operative time, bile reflux gastritis, dumping syndrome). Thus, preoperative or intraoperative pyloric interventions have—due to inconsistent data—not been implemented into current clinical guidelines so far. In view of the significant consequences for patients’ postoperative course and outcomes, this gap has to be closed by clear clinical data.

Endoscopic pyloric dilatation has emerged as an alternative to surgical drainage. Endoscopic balloon dilatation of the pylorus is an established therapy for treating gastric outlet obstruction and has been used as a rescue treatment for DGCE after oesophagectomy. Beyond balloon dilatation, other endoscopic approaches have been explored for pyloric intervention. Gastric peroral endoscopic myotomy, which involves endoscopic division of the pyloric muscle fibres, has shown promise in treating refractory gastroparesis. Similarly, botulinum toxin injection into the pylorus has been investigated as a method to temporarily reduce pyloric tone and facilitate gastric emptying, though its efficacy remains debated due to variable response rates and transient effects.8 By mechanically stretching the pylorus, it can relieve established delayed emptying without surgical re-intervention. This observation prompted interest in performing a prophylactic pyloric dilation at the time of oesophagectomy to prevent DGCE from occurring. A recent meta-analysis of non-randomised studies suggested that intraoperative mechanical stretching of the pylorus was associated with a significantly lower incidence of postoperative DGCE.9 Another systematic review focused on prophylactic endoscopic balloon dilatation prior to oesophagectomy and indicated a significant reduction in DGCE and even anastomotic insufficiency rates. The study reported a DGCE incidence of 13.2% with prophylactic dilation vs 37.5% without.10 However, these analyses were based on retrospective data with inherent selection biases and inhomogeneous patient populations.

To date and to our knowledge, according to the current literature, no prospective randomised trial has specifically evaluated performing the endoscopic pylorus dilatation intraoperatively during minimally invasive oesophagectomy as a prophylactic measure against DGCE. One randomised trial in the literature examined pyloric interventions during open oesophagectomy and found no significant benefit to either surgical pyloroplasty or intraoperative dilation compared with no intervention,11 but its applicability to minimally invasive surgery and modern perioperative care is uncertain. There remains a clear need for high-level evidence to guide optimal pyloric management in oesophagectomy. The WIDE trial (With or Without Intraoperative Dilation of the Pylorus in Oesophagectomy) is designed to fill this gap. We hypothesise that performing an endoscopic balloon dilatation of the pylorus during minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy will significantly reduce the incidence of early postoperative DGCE. Additionally, we will assess whether prophylactic dilation improves related outcomes, such as pulmonary and anastomotic complications as well as recovery quality and time. If effective, this simple intraoperative adjunct with a limited spectrum of potential complications, disturbing the operative procedure and time only minimally, could be widely adopted to improve patient outcomes after oesophagectomy.

Objectives

Primary objective

To determine whether an intraoperative endoscopic balloon dilatation of the pylorus during minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy reduces the incidence of early DGCE (at 5 and 10 days after surgery) - compared with no pyloric intervention. Early DGCE is defined in this trial as NG output ≥500 mL on postoperative day (POD) five or later and/or >100% enlargement of the gastric conduit on upright chest X-ray on POD 5, or later relative to the immediate post-surgical baseline. We define DGCE according to the Delphi round by Konradsson et al.12

Methods and analysis

Trial design and setting

The WIDE trial is a single-centre, investigator-initiated randomised controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio between intervention and control. It is designed as a prospective superiority trial to test whether adding pyloric dilatation yields superior outcomes (a lower DGCE incidence) as compared with standard care. The study is conducted at Clarunis – University Digestive Health Care Center Basel, Switzerland, a high-volume oesophageal surgery centre performing ~50–70 esophagectomies annually. Patient recruitment will take place over approximately 30 months. There is no planned interim efficacy analysis, but trial safety will be monitored on an ongoing basis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design of this study, the definition of the research question, or the selection of outcome measures. Randomisation and blinding

After consent and final eligibility confirmation, patients are randomised using block randomisation to the two study groups. Randomisation is performed after informed consent is provided using a randomisation programme in our Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) Database. Randomisation will be stratified according to age (<65 years vs ≥65 years) and disease stage (Union Internationale Contre le Cancer (UICC)) stage <III vs ≥III) in order to ensure balance between treatment groups with respect to these prognostically relevant variables. Within each stratum, randomisation will be carried out using permuted blocks of variable size. The randomisation sequence will be generated and implemented centrally within REDCap, and allocation will occur after confirmation of eligibility and completion of baseline assessments. Allocation concealment will be ensured, as the randomisation sequence will not be accessible to investigators prior to participant assignment. The trial is patient and observer-blinded: Patients and postoperative care providers/outcome assessors are blinded to group assignment. Ward physicians responsible for assessing NG tube output, medication dosing, coding and recording of complications and all further perioperative data—which are documented in the clinical information system (CIS)—are blinded to treatment allocation, as are the radiologists interpreting postoperative chest X-rays. Data are subsequently transferred from the CIS to the REDCap electronic data capture system by the study team. The operating surgeons and endoscopist cannot be blinded (since they perform or omit the dilatation), but they do not participate in outcome determination. Details of the pyloric intervention are documented in a separate study record, not in the routine operative report, where only ‘esophagectomy WIDE’ is written. Postoperative care is standardised for all patients to avoid unblinding. Emergency unblinding is permissible only if knowledge of the allocation is essential for patient management (an unlikely scenario, as management of DGCE or complications is similar regardless of group). Any instances of unblinding will be recorded.

Intervention and control

All patients receive standard robotically assisted minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy and systematic two-field lymphadenectomy, which presents our internal clinical standard for oesophageal cancer surgery.

Intervention (pyloric dilatation)

Following induction of general anaesthesia and endotracheal intubation, and immediately prior to or concomitant with the start of the surgical procedure, a flexible endoscope is introduced orally into the stomach. Endoscopic balloon dilatation of the pylorus is then performed using a through-the-scope pneumatic balloon dilator, which is positioned across the pylorus under direct endoscopic visualisation and inflated to a diameter of 30 mm. The balloon is maintained in the inflated state for 1 min to achieve controlled dilation of the pyloric ring, after which it is deflated and removed. Subsequently, the endoscope is withdrawn and the surgical procedure proceeds according to the standard minimally invasive Ivor Lewis technique, including access to the abdominal cavity and adhesiolysis if required, followed by construction of the intrathoracic oesophagogastric anastomosis. No surgical pyloroplasty or pyloromyotomy will be performed in any patient.

Control (no pyloric intervention)

Patients in the control arm undergo the same minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy, without any prophylactic pyloric procedure. No endoscopic dilatation, surgical pyloric incision or pyloric botulinum toxin injection is performed. All other aspects of intraoperative and perioperative management are identical between the two groups according to our institutional recovery protocol for oesophagectomies.

Performing the endoscopic dilation adds approximately 20–30 min to the operation. This intervention is considered low-risk: in therapeutic endoscopy series, pyloric balloon dilation has a reported perforation risk of 1–2%, and a minor bleeding risk of 2–7%, which are mostly self-limiting.15 Crucially, in this trial, the procedure is done under direct vision during surgery, so any complication (eg, a pyloric tear or perforation) can be immediately recognised and repaired surgically (essentially converting to a pyloroplasty on the spot).

Postoperative care and follow-up

After surgery, all patients are managed in the intensive care unit and on the ward with a standardised enhanced recovery pathway. A NG tube is placed intraoperatively in all patients for conduit decompression. The NG tube will be maintained until predefined removal criteria are met. All patients will be allowed oral intake of clear fluids on POD 1. A stepwise dietary progression will be initiated with a liquid diet on POD 2. Advancement to solid food will occur once the NG output is <500 mL per 24 hours and the patient is clinically stable. Removal of the NG tube will be performed once the daily NG output is <500 mL per 24 hours, the patient does not experience nausea while the NG tube is clamped and no reflux occurs during a 1-hour period after unclamping the tube. All patients receive proton pump inhibitors and prokinetic medications per protocol. All patients receive metoclopramide (Paspertin) 10 mg three times daily intravenously, transitioning to oral administration once a liquid diet is tolerated, continued throughout the entire hospitalisation in the absence of contraindications; additionally, all patients receive magnesium (Magnesio Card 300 mg) once daily orally on initiation of oral intake. Aggressive pulmonary physiotherapy and early mobilisation are implemented to reduce pulmonary risk. Patients are usually discharged by POD 10–14, if recovery is uncomplicated. A routine outpatient follow-up visit is scheduled at 3 months post-surgery. At this 3-month visit, patients are evaluated for late DGCE (via symptom questionnaire and a barium swallow transit study) and complete the EORTC QLQ-OES18 quality-of-life survey. Any patient with evidence of significant delayed emptying at that time is managed appropriately (including endoscopic dilatation as a therapeutic measure in accordance with internal standards, if indicated). Additional follow-ups are as per standard oncologic care.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Incidence of early DGCE assessed on POD 5 and 10. As defined above, a patient is considered to have early DGCE if on POD 5 or later, he/she meets at least one of the following criteria: (1) NG tube output ≥500 mL in 24 hours (with the NG tube still in place), or requiring reinsertion of an NG tube due to repetitive vomiting; and/or (2) >100% increase in the transverse width of the gastric conduit on upright chest X-ray compared with the immediate postoperative baseline film (typically obtained on POD 0–1). Radiologists (blinded to group) will assess conduit width on routine chest X-rays on POD 5 and POD 10. A doubling of the conduit’s width on any film is considered indicative of significant conduit retention.13 The primary endpoint will be determined by personnel blinded to the intervention (nursing staff recording NG outputs and independent radiologic review of imaging).

Sample size and statistical analysis

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on the primary endpoint (incidence of early DGCE). We anticipated an absolute reduction in early DGCE from approximately 48% in the control group to 22% in the intervention group, based on prior data regarding perioperative pyloric interventions in the context of oncologic oesophagectomy.16 Using a two-sided α=0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.80, it was calculated that n≈52 patients per arm would be required to detect such a difference. To account for potential drop-outs or exclusions, the target sample size was set at 116 patients (58 per group). This provides an ~10% margin to preserve power even if some patients do not complete the 3-month follow-up or are excluded post-randomisation.

Statistical analysis

Analyses will adhere to the intention-to-treat principle, analysing patients in the groups to which they were randomised regardless of actual intervention received. The primary outcome (binary DGCE incidence) will be compared between groups using a χ2 test (or Fisher’s exact test, if expected counts are small). The absolute risk difference will be reported with a 95% CI. A two-sided p<0.05 will be considered statistically significant for the primary endpoint. Relative risk or OR estimates will also be provided for context. Furthermore, per protocol analysis will be performed to compare the groups, which actually received the intervention to the group without intervention. If necessary, more specific per protocol analysis for subgroups will be performed if any differences appear.

Secondary categorical outcomes (eg, incidence of anastomotic leak, pneumonia, any further surgical or general complication) will likewise be compared by χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous outcomes (eg, length of stay, days to first bowel movement) will be compared using Student’s t-test, if approximately normally distributed, or the Mann-Whitney U test if distributions are skewed. Time-to-event variables in this study (such as time to resume oral intake) are expected to be fully observed (all patients ultimately reach these milestones before discharge), so formal survival analysis is not necessary. They will be analysed as continuous time measures. Statistical significance will be set at p<0.05.

To adjust for any potential imbalances in baseline characteristics (and to improve precision), a multivariable logistic regression may be performed for the primary outcome including relevant covariates, such as age, tumour histology, neoadjuvant therapy, etc. This will yield an adjusted OR for DGCE in the intervention vs control group with 95% CI. However, given randomisation and stratification for age and tumour stage, major baseline imbalances are unlikely in a sample of this size.

No interim efficacy analysis is planned. A safety review will be conducted after approximately 20 patients per arm have completed their hospital course, to ensure that there is no unexpected excess of adverse events in the intervention group. This check is for monitoring only and not for early stopping. The trial will be stopped prematurely only if a clear safety concern arises or if external evidence emerges that definitively answers the research question.

Recruitment and consent

Potential participants are identified at the multidisciplinary tumour board, when oesophagectomy is recommended. Treating surgeons or research staff approach eligible patients during their preoperative clinic visits. Patients receive detailed verbal and written information about the trial (‘Participant Information Sheet’ and ‘Consent Form’), and are given ample time (several days to a few weeks, often during the commonly administered neoadjuvant therapy) to consider participation and discuss with family. Consent is obtained at a subsequent visit, where consent for surgery is obtained. Informed consent is documented by the patient’s signature and a study investigator’s countersignature; a copy is given to the patient. After consent is obtained, randomisation will be performed using our solely for the study created REDCap database.

Ethical considerations

The participants’ confidentiality is maintained at all times. Participation in the trial is entirely voluntary. Patients are assured that declining or withdrawing will not affect their standard care. Participants may withdraw from the study at any time without negative effects. If a patient withdraws consent before surgery (ie, prior to randomisation), they will not undergo any study procedures and will receive standard treatment off-trial. If a patient withdraws after randomisation or after surgery, they will continue to be followed with standard postoperative care. Data collected up to the point of withdrawal will be retained and may be included in the analyses (unless the patient requests complete data removal). Withdrawals (and reasons, if provided) will be reported in the results.

Data management and monitoring

All study data are captured in a secure, study-specific electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) using REDCap. Participants are identified in the database only by a coded study ID. Personal identifiers are kept separately in the database and can only be seen by selected study staff. The eCRF includes built-in validation checks and range limits to minimise entry errors. Study documents and source data will be securely stored for at least 20 years after trial completion or termination, per Swiss regulations.

Patient confidentiality is rigorously protected. No names or identifying information appear in the study database or any reports. Publications will use anonymised data only. Data handling complies with the Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection17 and institutional privacy policies. The trial will be monitored for compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and the approved protocol. An independent monitor (not otherwise involved in the trial) will conduct periodic on-site reviews of informed consent forms, eligibility criteria and data accuracy by comparing eCRF entries against source medical records. The monitor’s findings will be documented and communicated to the Sponsor-Investigator, who will ensure that any issues are addressed. The study may also be subject to inspection by regulatory authorities. Participants provide consent for such auditing of their records. All study data are archived for 20 years after study termination or premature termination of the study.

Data availability statement: De-identified individual participant data and the study protocol will be made available on reasonable request after publication of the primary results. Requests should be directed to the corresponding author. Data will be shared in accordance with institutional and ethical approvals.

Safety considerations

The WIDE trial is classified as a Category A (minimal risk) clinical trial under the Swiss Human Research Act and Clinical Trials Ordinance (ClinO).18 19 The study is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki20 and International conference on harmonisation (ICH) GCP guidelines.12 The protocol (V.1.3 dated 18 December 2025) received ethics approval from the Northwestern and Central Switzerland Ethics Committee (EKNZ, BASEC ID 2025-01877) prior to patient enrolment. Any protocol amendments will be submitted for ethics approval before implementation. All participants provide written informed consent. Emergency unblinding is available, if knowledge of the allocation is absolutely necessary for management, although in practice, medical management of DGCE or common complications is the same regardless of group.

Discussion

Post-esophagectomy DGCE is an important clinical challenge that impacts patient recovery and quality of life and causes consecutive complications. To date, practices to prevent DGCE have varied widely, reflecting the lack of high-level evidence and consensus. The WIDE trial will provide prospective, randomised data on whether prophylactic endoscopic pyloric dilatation at the time of minimally invasive oesophagectomy can effectively reduce the incidence of early DGCE. If positive, this would establish a simple intraoperative step as part of the standard surgical protocol for oesophagectomy. Such a change could potentially reduce postoperative morbidity by decreasing aspiration events, reducing hospital stay and expediting return of oral nutrition. Conversely, if the trial shows no benefit, it will indicate that routine dilation is not necessary, sparing patients an unnecessary procedure and focusing attention on alternative strategies.

A major strength of this study is its focused design in a homogeneous patient population: All patients undergo the same surgical approach (Ivor Lewis robotically assisted minimally invasive oesophagectomy) by a single specialised team, which standardises many perioperative factors. This homogeneity enhances internal validity. The study is also double-blinded, which is uncommon in surgical trials but achievable here due to the nature of the intervention (performed endoscopically and not evident to the patient or the ward staff). Blinding patients and outcome assessors should minimise bias in measuring subjective outcomes, like time to diet or length of stay. We also use objective criteria for DGCE (quantified NG outputs and radiographic measurements), as defined in the study by Konradsson et al,9 and have central blinded review for those measures, further strengthening the outcome assessment.

The WIDE trial addresses a question not fully answered by prior research. A related ongoing study is the PROPPER trial (Prophylactic endoscopic pylorus dilatation prior to oesophagectomy for oesophageal cancer to prevent delayed gastric emptying) testing preoperative EPBD 1 day before oesophagectomy.21 PROPPER uses a sham endoscopy control and will clarify if dilating the pylorus prior to surgery improves outcomes. In contrast, our trial tests dilation during surgery, which is logistically convenient (no extra procedure day, no extra day in the hospital for patients before surgery), and ensures any complication can be handled immediately. The combination of PROPPER and WIDE will provide complementary evidence. If both trials demonstrate a clear benefit, they will strongly support routine pyloric dilatation in combination with oncologic oesophagectomy (whether done preoperatively or intraoperatively). If one trial was positive and the other negative, the timing of dilation (preoperative vs intraoperative) may be critical, and clinical practice would adopt the timing shown to be effective.

We also note that other prophylactic strategies have been attempted, such as intraoperative botulinum toxin (Botox) injection into the pylorus. However, prior studies and meta-analyses have not shown a clear benefit of Botox for preventing DGCE, and only mechanical stretching of the pylorus is beneficial in preventing DGCE.9 Pharmacologic pyloric relaxation is transient and adds cost, whereas mechanical dilation is immediate and uses standard equipment. The results of WIDE will help to determine if mechanical intervention at the pylorus is worthwhile. If so, future work could identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

By focusing on preventing a common complication, the WIDE trial has the potential for immediate clinical impact. Should prophylactic pyloric dilatation significantly reduce DGCE and associated problems, implementing this procedure was straightforward—as it requires no new devices (standard dilation balloons) and adds minimal operative time only, while not increasing patients’ potential perioperative risks. It could be readily adopted in oesophagectomy protocols worldwide to enhance postoperative recovery. On the other hand, if no benefit were observed, a refocus on quality improvement efforts on other aspects of perioperative care was needed. Either outcome will advance knowledge in oesophageal surgery. In summary, WIDE aims to provide high-level evidence to guide pyloric management in oesophagectomy, ultimately striving to improve the safety and recovery of patients undergoing surgery for this challenging disease.

Trial status

As of the submission date, recruitment has just started after the approval of the Ethics Committee from 16 October 2025. The first patients were enrolled in December 2025. The trial is registered on the Deutsches Register Klinischer Studien (DRKS00038237) and on ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07355374. Recruitment is expected to continue until approximately January 2028 to reach the target sample size. We aim to complete primary endpoint follow-up for the last patient by late 2028, with analysis and dissemination of results to follow promptly.
