Immunogenicity and safety of inactivated TZ84- vs. HM175-based hepatitis A vaccines in pediatric populations: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ritabrata Kundu, Apurba Ghosh, Archana Toppo, Usha Rani H Patted
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIndividual studies indicate stronger and persistent long-term immunogenicity with TZ84-based than HM175-based hepatitis A vaccines. This systematic review and meta-analysis compared immunogenicity and safety of inactivated TZ84- and HM175-based hepatitis A vaccines in children and adolescents. Studies involving adults, immunocompromised populations, non-comparative designs, non-English publications, and studies with non-extractable outcomes were excluded. MEDLINE/PubMed, Google Scholar, other electronic databases, and grey literature were searched (September–October 2025). Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, and meta-analyses were performed where appropriate. Seven publications arising from four randomized cohorts (total participants: 3,282) reporting seroconversion rates (SCRs), geometric mean concentrations (GMCs), long-term seroprotection, and adverse events (AEs) were identified. Pooled analyses demonstrated higher SCRs and GMCs with TZ84 than HM175-based vaccines following both one and two doses. SCRs at 1 month [odds ratio (OR): 3.05; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.88–4.96] and GMCs at 7 months [standardized mean difference (SMD): 0.88; 95% CI: 0.18–1.58; p = .01] both favored TZ84-based vaccines. Long-term follow-up (up to 186 months/15.5 y) showed higher reported anti-HAV antibody concentrations for TZ84-based vaccines. The AEs were comparable between vaccine types (RR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.76–1.20; p = .69). Evidence was limited by the small number of unique randomized cohorts, geographic concentration in China, heterogeneity across studies, and reliance on surrogate immunogenicity outcomes. Overall, available evidence suggests that TZ84-based hepatitis A vaccines (HAPIBEV™/HEALIVE®) are associated with higher reported antibody responses and greater reported antibody persistence than the HM175-based vaccine (HAVRIX®) in children and adolescents, with comparable safety profiles.Registration: PROSPERO CRD420251154480.KEYWORDS: Hepatitis A, HEALIVE®, HAPIBEV™, HAVRIX®, immunogenicity, safety, children

Introduction

Hepatitis A is an acute, self-limiting disease caused by the hepatitis A virus (HAV).1,2 It remains a significant public health concern, particularly in regions with poor sanitation,3 with transmission primarily occurring through the fecal-oral route via direct contact or contaminated food and water.2–5

Globally, HAV is the leading cause of acute viral hepatitis, accounting for an estimated 100 million infections annually,1,6 with the highest burden in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and South Asia.7 In 2019, about 158.9 million new infections were reported worldwide, with India and China accounting for nearly one-third of cases.8 Children and adolescents are most affected, accounting for 82.7% of HAV cases, with about 129.38 million new pediatric infections reported globally.9 In highly endemic regions, up to 90% of children are infected before 10 y of age, usually with asymptomatic or mild infection.3,4,10 Severe symptoms, including jaundice, pruritus, fever, weight loss, diarrhea, and malaise, occur more often in adults.3,11 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends introducing hepatitis A vaccination into national immunization schedules for individuals aged ≥ 12 months when indicated by an increasing trend of acute hepatitis A disease, including severe disease, among older children, adolescents, or adults, changes in endemicity from high to intermediate, and considerations of cost-effectiveness.12 Vaccination remains the most effective option for preventing hepatitis A and reducing associated disease burden.3,10,13 Both live attenuated and inactivated HAV vaccines, available since the early 1990s, are safe and immunogenic in children and adults.14 Live attenuated vaccines, derived from wild-type viruses, are licensed for use in individuals aged ≥ 18 months and administered as a single subcutaneous dose according to the WHO position paper (2022).12,13,15

Long-term data of live attenuated vaccines show that approximately 80% of recipients retain detectable anti-HAV IgG titers for up to 15 y.13 However, they present certain limitations, including the theoretical risk of reverse mutation of live strains, possible viral shedding with secondary transmission, and lack of post-exposure protection due to delayed immune response, making them unsuitable for emergency immunization to control outbreaks.16–18 These are also contraindicated during pregnancy, in severely immunocompromised patients, and in children below 1.5 y.13,19 Furthermore, research indicated significantly lower GMC of anti-HAV IgG in the live attenuated vaccine group than in the inactivated group at 12 months (p < .05).20

Inactivated HAV vaccines are produced using whole-virus strains cultured in human MRC-5 diploid cells and inactivated with formaldehyde.14 They are licensed and widely available for use in both pediatric (≥1 y) and adult populations.13,21 The WHO position paper states that inactivated hepatitis A vaccines are safe and highly immunogenic and can be administered intramuscularly in children from ≥12 months of age as a two-dose schedule; when a two-dose schedule is used, the interval between doses is flexible, usually 6–18 months.12 In India, the Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) Advisory Committee on Vaccines and Immunization Practices (ACVIP) also recognizes the inactivated hepatitis A vaccine based on the TZ84 strain as an option for immunization in children aged ≥ 12 months as two-dose regimen.22 Several inactivated strains are used globally, including HM175, TZ84, CR-326, GBM, YN5, KRM003, Lv-8, and RG-SB.2,14,23–25 Inactivated vaccines have demonstrated established safety, strong immunogenicity, and reliable large-scale production.25–27 HAVRIX® and HEALIVE® are based on different hepatitis A virus strains.26 HAVRIX® is derived from the HM175 strain, whereas HEALIVE® is derived from the TZ84 strain. HAPIBEV™, developed by Biological E Limited, is also a TZ84-based inactivated hepatitis A vaccine fill-finished in India from WHO-prequalified HEALIVE® bulk, and one eligible Indian trial directly compared HAPIBEV™ with HAVRIX®.24,28 HAVRIX® (0.5 mL) contains 720 ELISA units (EL.U) of antigen and 0.25 mg alum, with 2-phenoxyethanol as preservative, while HEALIVE® (0.5 mL) and HAPIBEV™ (0.5 mL) each contain 250 U HAV antigen (1 IU = 13 U) adjuvanted with aluminum-based compounds and no preservative.24,29–31 While both demonstrate high seroconversion rates, comparative studies have reported that TZ84-based vaccines produce significantly higher and more persistent geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) of anti-HAV antibodies up to 15 y post-vaccination than HM175-based vaccines.26,32 Additionally, long-term prediction data also shows that HEALIVE® maintained predicted anti-HAV GMCs with a lower 95% CI ≥ 20 mIU/mL up to 30 y after full vaccination and showed higher predicted seroprotection than HAVRIX® at 35.5 y.26 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis also reported higher antibody responses with HEALIVE® compared with HAVRIX® at 6 months (SMD = 0.85, 95% CI 0.57–1.07) and 7 months (SMD = 0.55, 95% CI 0.41–0.70), while noting comparable safety and the need for standardized assay reporting and further studies in intermediate-endemic settings.33

The present systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken to synthesize comparative evidence on seroconversion, antibody concentrations, antibody persistence/predicted seroprotection, and safety of inactivated TZ84- and HM175-based hepatitis A vaccines in children and adolescents.

Methods

Searching strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and followed the procedures outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. The protocol for this study was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under the registration ID CRD420251154480. The search was carried out in multiple databases, including MEDLINE/PubMed, Cochrane Database of Clinical Trials, ClinicalTrials.gov, Google Scholar, and Grey literature searches from September 2025 to October 2025. Additionally, manual review of the bibliographies of all retrieved articles and citation tracking of relevant primary studies and reviews were examined to identify any further relevant studies.

The search strategy used a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords covering the study population, interventions, comparators, and outcomes. Key search terms included “children,” “adolescents,” “TZ84,” “Healive,” “HAPIBEV,” “HM175,” “Havrix,” “immunogenicity,” “seroconversion,” “antibody response,” “safety,” “tolerability,” “adverse events,” and “long-term protection.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were employed to combine terms effectively and enhance the sensitivity of the search.

Screening and study eligibility

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of identified studies for relevance. Full-text articles were retrieved when abstracts suggested that the study evaluated the immunogenicity or safety of hepatitis A vaccines in pediatric populations. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: published before January 2010; published in a language other than English; focused on objectives unrelated to hepatitis A prevention; conducted exclusively in adult populations or in immunocompromised or clinically unstable populations; or used live attenuated hepatitis A vaccines, combination vaccines (unless data for the hepatitis A component were reported separately), or comparators other than TZ84 or HM175 hepatitis A vaccines. Additionally, reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, theses, letters, expert opinions, editorials, animal or in vitro studies (including pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic studies), unpublished data, duplicate data, studies with full texts not retrievable, or studies with non-extractable or duplicate outcomes were excluded. Studies from which key outcomes such as seroconversion rates, GMCs, duration of seroprotection, or adverse events could not be obtained from the text, tables, or figures were also excluded.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized form. The first reviewer manually entered the extracted data into a pre-specified Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and 20% of the entries were randomly checked for accuracy by the second reviewer. The disagreements between reviewers regarding the study inclusion were resolved through discussion between them, and a third reviewer was consulted if consensus could not be reached.

The extracted information included the author’s name, year, country of publication, study design, sample size, participant demographics (age, sex), vaccine strain used (TZ84 or HM175), immunogenicity outcomes (seroconversion rates and geometric mean concentrations), and safety outcomes (local and systemic adverse events). Additionally, the duration of follow-up was noted.

Additional methodological details, including seroconversion/seroprotection thresholds, assay methods, HAV-naivete or baseline serostatus assessment, adverse-event collection procedures, endemicity context, and funding or conflict-of-interest information, were extracted where reported. Details not available in the published reports were recorded as not reported.

For long-term antibody persistence and predicted seroprotection, observed anti-HAV GMCs and model-predicted seroprotection estimates were extracted from the included long-term follow-up publications and summarized descriptively. No new prediction model was developed for this review.

Assessment of risk of bias

The Version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) was used to assess the quality and potential bias of RCTs.34 This tool evaluates five key domains of potential bias: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was assessed at the outcome level, and judgments were classified as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.” An overall risk of bias judgment for each study was then derived from the individual domain assessments according to the RoB 2 guidelines. All evaluations were conducted independently by two reviewers and cross-checked for consistency. Differences in judgment were resolved by discussion and consensus, or arbitration by a third author. Publication bias was planned to be assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically through Egger’s test for small-study effects if at least 10 studies were available for an outcome.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

This meta-analysis was conducted using an inverse variance weighted average or Mantel–Haenszel method, using the software Cochrane’s Review Manager (RevMan Web). Dichotomous outcomes, including seroconversion rates (SCRs) and adverse events (AEs), were synthesized using odds ratios (ORs) and risk ratios (RRs), respectively, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes, including geometric mean concentrations (GMCs), were summarized using standardized mean differences (SMDs) or mean differences (MDs), as appropriate, with corresponding 95% CIs. In addition, heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic. If the I2 value > 50% or the p-value for the Q test was ≤ 0.05, it indicated significant heterogeneity, and a random effects model was utilized; otherwise, a fixed effect model was used. I2 < 25% was considered mild, 25–75% was considered moderate, and above 75% was considered substantial heterogeneity. P < .05 was deemed statistically significant. Only published summary statistics available in the text, tables, or figures were included. Outcomes/time points with missing or non-extractable data were excluded, and authors were not contacted for additional information.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially eliminating individual included studies and comparing the combined results with the individual study results to assess robustness. No subgroup analysis was performed.

Results

Search results

A total of 509 citations were identified through the search strategy, including 66 from PubMed, 348 from Google Scholar, 24 from the Cochrane database, 10 from Google, 46 from ClinicalTrials.gov, and 15 from the bibliographies of reviews and meta-analyses. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, seven publications with summed up sample size of 3,282 were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. However, because three publications represented sequential follow-up reports from the same original cohorts, the number of unique randomized participants was 2,039. Included studies characteristics

The seven included publications were based on randomized controlled study designs, but they represented four independent randomized cohorts. Zheng et al. (2011) and Zhang et al. (2016) represented early and extended follow-up reports from the same single-dose vaccine cohort, whereas Yu et al. (2016), Wang et al. (2020), and Wang et al. (2023) represented sequential follow-up reports from the same two-dose HEALIVE® vs. HAVRIX® cohort.26,29,32,35,36 Zhang et al. (2012) and Thuluva et al. (2021) represented independent randomized cohorts.24,37 These publications were retained because they contributed data at distinct outcome time points; however, dependence between publications was considered during interpretation. Sample sizes in the cohorts ranged from 303 participants37 to 841 participants35 and study durations ranged from 28 d35 to 186 months/15.5 y.26 Across the included publications, the enrolled pediatric populations included children with reported ages ranging from 1 to 15 y. Most studies were conducted in China, with one multicenter trial from India.24 All vaccines were administered intramuscularly. Two publications assessed single-dose vaccination schedules,35,36 while five publications evaluated two-dose regimens administered at 0 and 6 months.24,26,29,32,37 The majority of trials compared the inactivated hepatitis A vaccines HEALIVE® and HAVRIX®,26,29,32,35–37 while one study compared HAPIBEVTM with HAVRIX®.24 Two studies additionally included the live attenuated H2 vaccine and/or hepatitis B vaccine as control groups.35,36 A summary of the included studies, including study design, vaccine types, routes of administration, vaccination schedules, and sample sizes, is presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Pre-vaccination GMCs and baseline serostatus were not consistently reported across all included publications. Several cohorts enrolled anti-HAV-seronegative children at baseline. Long-term follow-up publications also showed participant attrition over time; however, demographic changes among retained participants were not uniformly reported across follow-up time points.

Included studies quality evaluation/risk of bias

Risk-of-bias assessments were conducted at the publication/outcome level. Of the seven included publications, three were assessed as having a low overall risk of bias and thus considered of “good quality.” The remaining four studies raised some concerns, primarily due to open-label designs and lack of allocation sequence concealment. However, given the use of objective, predefined quantitative methods for outcome assessment (SCR and GMCs), these issues were not deemed to substantially affect the validity of the findings.26,29,32,35–37 All studies except one adhered to an intention-to-treat (ITT) analytical approach. Thuluva et al. (2021) employed a per-protocol (PP) analysis for immunogenicity, which is a widely used method in vaccine trials, particularly those involving multiple-dose schedules, as it better reflects immunogenicity under ideal adherence conditions.24,38,39 A summary of the risk of bias assessments across all domains is summarized in Figure 2.

Meta-analysis results

Seroconversion rates

Early immune response (≤1 month)

One publication35 reported SCR at 7 and 14 d, with numerically higher proportions in the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group compared to HAVRIX®. While a pooled meta-analysis was not feasible, single-study ORs showed a numerical trend favoring the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.72 to 2.91, p = .30 at day 7; OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 0.59 to 5.63, p = .29 at day 14; Supplementary Figures 1 and 2). Because seroconversion percentages could not be added directly to the forest plots, the corresponding study-level seroconversion proportions for each seroconversion analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Two publications32,35 reported SCR at approximately 1 month (28–31 d), with a pooled analysis demonstrating significantly higher odds of seroconversion in the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group compared to HAVRIX® (OR = 3.05, 95% CI: 1.88 to 4.96, p < .00001; I2 = 0%; Figure 3a).

Forest plot showing seroconversion rates in HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® vs. HAVRIX® groups at a) 1 month (28–31 d) and b) 6 months. CI: confidence interval.

Mid-term immune response (6–7 months)

Two publications32,37 reported seroconversion rates at 6 months, with a pooled OR of 13.70 (95% CI: 1.42 to 132.38), significantly favoring the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group (p = .02; Figure 3b). At 7 months, three publications24,32,37 reported 100% seroconversion in both HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® and HAVRIX® groups; therefore, a pooled effect estimate could not be calculated.

Geometric mean concentrations

Early immune response (≤1 month)

Similar to seroconversion rates, GMCs at 7 and 14 d were reported by Zheng et al. (2011)35 At both time points, antibody titers were numerically higher in the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group compared to the HAVRIX® group, with single-study MD favoring the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group (MD = 1.90, 95% CI: −1.53 to 5.33; p = .28 at day 7; MD = 31.30, 95% CI: −2.93 to 65.53; p = .07 at day 14; Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). At 1 month (28–31 d), pooled analysis from two publications32,35 showed SMD of 0.25 (95% CI: −0.11 to 0.60), numerically favoring the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group, although this was not statistically significant (p = .17; I2 = 64%; Figure 4a).

Forest plot showing pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) for geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) in HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® vs. HAVRIX® groups at a) 1 month, b) 6 months, and c) 7 months. SD: Standard Deviation, CI: Confidence Interval.

Mid-term immune response (6–7 months)

At 6 months, two publications32,37 reported GMCs, both demonstrating significantly higher antibody titers in the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group compared to HAVRIX®. The pooled SMD was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.57 to 1.08; p < .00001; I2 = 53%), indicating a statistically significant advantage in favor of the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group (Figure 4b).

At 7 months, three publications24,32,37 reported GMCs. The pooled SMD was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.18 to 1.58; p = .01), significantly favoring the HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group. However, there was substantial heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 96%; τ2 = 0.36; χ2 = 28.76, df = 2; p < .00001), suggesting considerable variability in effect estimates across studies (Figure 4c).

Durability of seroprotection post-vaccination

All long-term time-points (24, 66, 112, 138, and 186 months) reported had data available from sequential follow-up publications; therefore, pooled effect estimation was not feasible. Single publication based MD in GMCs across all time points significantly favored HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group over HAVRIX® group (Table 2). Adverse events

Three publications24,35,37 reported AEs, allowing for pooled analysis. The meta-analysis demonstrated no significant difference in the risk of AEs between HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® and HAVRIX® recipients, with a pooled RR of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.76 to 1.20; p = .69; Figure 5). Individual study RRs ranged from 0.86 to 1.89, none of which reached statistical significance. These findings suggest comparable safety profiles for the two vaccines.

Forest plot showing pooled risk ratio (RR) for adverse events (AEs) in HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® vs. HAVRIX® groups. CI: Confidence Interval.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the pooled effect for all outcomes, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted for outcomes that had data from more than two studies, i.e., GMC at 7 months and AEs. For GMC at 7 months, excluding Zhang et al. (2012) improved homogeneity of the analysis, suggesting it may have disproportionately influenced heterogeneity and should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Figure 10). For AE, the sensitivity analysis showed consistent results across exclusions, with no statistically significant difference in AE risk between HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® and HAVRIX® (RR range: 0.88–1.02). Heterogeneity varied moderately (I2: 0–72%), suggesting some influence of individual studies (Supplementary Figure 11).

Assessment of reporting biases

Publication bias was not assessed as fewer than 10 trials were available for analysis.

Discussion

Despite improvements in sanitation and vaccination, HAV infection persists as a major burden, particularly in developing regions with frequent outbreaks.3,4 Among various high-risk populations, children are particularly vulnerable to HAV infection in low-resource settings.40 The WHO recommends hepatitis A vaccination for individuals aged ≥ 12 months when severe cases rise or endemicity shifts from high to intermediate.41 An anti-HAV antibody titer of 20 mIU/ml or higher is considered the threshold for seroprotection.18,42

This meta-analysis included seven eligible publications arising from four randomized cohorts that compared inactivated hepatitis A vaccines based on the TZ84 strain (HAPIBEV™/HEALIVE®) with the HM175 strain (HAVRIX®). TZ84-based vaccines across these publications showed higher immunogenicity at most time points while maintaining a safety profile comparable to HAVRIX®. These findings are broadly consistent with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis comparing Healive® and Havrix®, which also reported higher antibody responses with Healive® at 6 and 7 months and comparable safety between the vaccines; however, the present review additionally contextualizes these findings in relation to HAPIBEVTM, cohort overlap, and long-term antibody persistence.33 The findings of the current systematic review and meta-analysis have important implications for pediatric immunization, particularly in regions like India, where a transition from high to intermediate endemicity has increased susceptibility among older children and adolescents.43

Early seroconversion (28–31 d) was significantly higher in HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® recipients compared with HAVRIX® (pooled OR 3.05; 95% CI: 1.88 to 4.96), indicating a rapid onset of seroconversion following priming, which is clinically relevant because anti-HAV antibody seroprotection is used as a surrogate of protection. This is clinically valuable in endemic regions where exposure may occur shortly after vaccination. At 7 and 14 d, Zheng et al. (2011) also reported numerically higher seroconversion and GMCs for HEALIVE® (TZ84), though differences were not statistically significant.35 These findings align with the results of Ren et al. (2002) that demonstrated seroconversion rates of 70% and 87.5% in low-dose (500 units) and high-dose (1000 units) TZ84-based vaccine groups, compared with 50% for HAVRIX®, with anti-HAV titers far exceeding the protective threshold after 1 month of primary dose.44 Further, evidence demonstrates that HEALIVE® induces high early immunogenicity, with seroconversion rates above 90% in 1 month after the first dose, including 92.65% at 2 weeks,35 99.4% at 3 weeks,45 and 100% at 4 weeks.35 The consistent early advantage of TZ84-based vaccines aligns with known early seroprotection kinetics of inactivated HAV vaccines.46,47 The high seroconversion rates observed in HEALIVE® group also resonate with a study of 193 young adults (18–30 y), where vaccination with HEALIVE® led to all seronegative participants (12% with <20 IU/L) achieving protective titers, with a mean post-vaccination antibody level of 60 IU/L, demonstrating 100% seroconversion and the vaccine’s effectiveness in young adults.48 Supporting these findings, an emergency vaccination program using HEALIVE® during a 2006 outbreak in rural China prevented new infections among 3,365 vaccinated students, compared with four cases in 2,572 unvaccinated controls (p = .035), demonstrating 100% protective efficacy in a real-world setting.49 On the other hand, administering a single dose of hepatitis A live attenuated vaccine to exposed schoolchildren did not reduce infection risk.50 New infection rates during the first 18 d post-vaccination were similar between vaccinated and unvaccinated groups (5.43% vs. 7.43%), with no significant difference during the 40 d follow-up.50 These findings indicate that the live attenuated vaccine did not provide effective post-exposure seroprotection during emergency settings.50

Mid-term findings (6–7 months) showed a more pronounced and statistically robust benefit with TZ84-based vaccines. Across included studies, HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® induced a more rapid and higher early anti-HAV antibody response than HAVRIX®, with GMCs exceeding the seroconversion threshold as early as 14 d and remaining higher through 12 months (Figure 6a).32,35–37 At 6–7 months post-vaccination, GMCs were approximately two- to four-fold higher in HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group than those observed in the HAVRIX® group.24,32,37 Pooled data in the present study also demonstrated that GMCs significantly favored HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® at 6 months (SMD 0.83; 95% CI: 0.57 to 1.08; p < .00001) and 7 months (SMD 0.88; 95% CI: 0.18 to 1.58; p = .01). A trial by Jiang et al. (2008) also showed that children aged 1–8 y receiving the preservative-free inactivated vaccine HEALIVE® had significantly higher GMCs and seroconversion rates at months 1 and 6 compared with a control inactivated hepatitis A vaccine of formulation similar to HAVRIX® (0.5 mL/dose, 720 EL.U, 0.25 mg alum, 2-phenoxyethanol as preservative).51 Sensitivity analysis for GMCs at 7 months in our meta-analysis, confirmed its robustness despite Zhang et al. (2012) study heterogeneity. Higher second dose antibody titers predict long-term seroprotection,15,52 suggesting superior memory B-cell priming with the TZ84 strain.6 Even as circulating antibody levels decline over time, long-lived plasma cells and memory immune cells persist, enabling vaccinated individuals to mount a protective anamnestic response upon HAV exposure.53 Upon antigen re-exposure, memory B cells rapidly proliferate and differentiate into plasma cells, facilitating a swift and robust secondary antibody response that underpins durable immunity.54

Long-term follow-up (24 to 186 months/2 to 15.5 y) consistently favored HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE® group, with MDs consistently positive and statistically significant. These long-term findings were primarily derived from sequential follow-up publications of the same original two-dose cohort; therefore, they provide valuable longitudinal evidence rather than discrete evidence from different randomized studies. As limited data was available at each time point, pooled analysis was not possible, though the uniform trend supports seroprotection durability advantage of TZ-84 based vaccines. These findings align with long-term persistence studies demonstrating up to 20 y of observed and over 30 y of predicted seroprotection with inactivated hepatitis A vaccines.26,29,52 In Wang et al. (2020 and 2023), HEALIVE® maintained higher GMCs than HAVRIX® at both 138-months (11.5 y; GMC: 166.2 mIU/mL vs. 117.1 mIU/mL) and 186 months (15.5 y; GMC: 164.8 mIU/mL vs. 105.7 mIU/mL) (Figure 6b).26,29 Mathematical modeling further quantified the long-term differences between HEALIVE® and HAVRIX®, with model projections estimated maintenance of GMCs (lower CI of ≥20 mIU/ml) for ≥30 y with HEALIVE® and ~25 y with HAVRIX® (Figure 6b).26,29 Together, these results indicate slower antibody decline and longer-lasting seroprotection with HAPIBEV™/HEALIVE® relative to HAVRIX®. Furthermore, the two-dose HEALIVE® regimen demonstrates substantially greater long-term immunogenicity than the single-dose live attenuated hepatitis A vaccine.26,55 The observed 15 y seroprotection data for HEALIVE® show 97.9% seroprotection, while model-based projections estimate 96.3% seroprotection at 35 y.26 In comparison, the single-dose live attenuated vaccine shows 62% observed seroprotection at 17 y.55 These findings indicate longer durability of antibody persistence with the two-dose inactivated HEALIVE® vaccine schedule compared with the live attenuated vaccine.26,55

Apart from the pediatric populations, evidence from adult studies also provides important context for dosing strategies. Inactivated hepatitis A vaccines are licensed for both children and adults, typically administered in a two-dose schedule at 0 and 6–12 months, which achieves near-universal seroconversion and durable seroprotection for decades.56,57 A single priming dose has demonstrated rapid onset of seroprotection, making it valuable in outbreak settings, for travelers, or in populations requiring urgent coverage.12 However, long-term follow-up consistently shows that the second dose substantially enhances antibody persistence, with modeling studies predicting seroprotection for 30 to 40 y in adults receiving two doses, compared with shorter duration after a single dose.56 WHO notes that adults, particularly those over 40 y or with comorbidities, benefit from the two-dose regimen to ensure reliable immune memory and sustained seroprotection.12 The rationale for adopting two doses is multifactorial, including environmental exposure risks in endemic regions, variability in individual immune responses such as age, body mass index, or HIV status, and the need for long-term seroprotection in populations with shifting endemicity.56 Thus, while one dose provides immediate benefit, especially in emergency or high-risk contexts, the two-dose schedule remains the standard for ensuring robust and durable seroprotection in both pediatric and adult populations.

Across the included studies, the risk of AEs was comparable between TZ84-based (HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE®) and HM175-based (HAVRIX®) vaccines. Most reported AEs were mild to moderate, such as injection site pain, cough, or fever.24,26,29,32,35–37,51 No serious adverse events (SAEs) leading to withdrawal or hospitalization were reported. Pooled analysis and sensitivity analyses confirmed the consistency of these findings, indicating that higher immunogenicity of TZ84-based vaccines was not associated with an increased risk of AEs, supporting their safe use in children and adolescents. These results also resonate with a 10 y (2002–2011) review of marketing experience with HEALIVE® vaccine, confirming its excellent safety, with reported AE frequency below 0.25 per million doses among approximately 15 million vaccinated subjects.58

The present systematic review and meta-analysis possess several strengths. It provides a comprehensive assessment of the immunogenicity and safety of inactivated TZ84- (HAPIBEVTM/HEALIVE®) and HM175- (HAVRIX®) based hepatitis A vaccines in pediatric populations. The protocol was preregistered in PROSPERO, and an extensive search across multiple databases ensured a comprehensive evidence base. The analysis also incorporated long-term follow-up data (up to 186 months/15.5 y), supporting durability of the TZ84-based vaccines. However, there were certain limitations to this systematic review and meta-analysis as well. The number of eligible trials was limited, with most conducted in a single country (China), potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Participant ethnicity was not consistently reported, and immune responses may vary according to age, baseline HAV serostatus, endemicity, prior natural exposure, nutritional status, and other population-level factors. Another key limitation was that the seven included publications did not correspond to seven independent participant cohorts. Three reports represented extended follow-up publications from the same original randomized cohorts, reducing the number of unique randomized populations and limiting the independence of long-term observations. The inclusion of only English-language publications introduces potential linguistic bias. Heterogeneity in study design, sample size, and follow-up duration contributed to variability in effect estimates. In addition, immunoassay methods and reporting of specific assay-antigen details were not uniform across studies. As assay configuration can influence measured anti-HAV antibody concentrations, particularly GMC estimates, this should be considered when interpreting the comparative immunogenicity findings. The included studies assessed immunogenicity and safety outcomes but did not directly evaluate clinical protection against hepatitis A disease, such as laboratory-confirmed hepatitis A infection or outbreak prevention. Therefore, seroconversion, seroprotection, anti-HAV antibody concentrations, and predicted seroprotection should be interpreted as surrogate immunological outcomes rather than direct clinical effectiveness data. In addition, pooled analyses for long-term seroprotection were not feasible because the available long-term data were reported at different follow-up durations across sequential follow-up publications. Despite these limitations, the results provide pooled evidence comparing the immunogenicity and safety of TZ84 and HM175 hepatitis A vaccines.

Conclusion

Available evidence from this systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that inactivated TZ84-based hepatitis A vaccines (HAPIBEVTM and HEALIVE®) are associated with higher reported antibody responses and greater antibody persistence compared with the HM175-based vaccine (HAVRIX®) in pediatric populations, while maintaining sustained seroprotection and a comparable safety profile. Importantly, the included Indian HAPIBEVTM trial evaluated its immunogenicity and safety in children, supporting its use as a TZ84-based hepatitis A vaccine for immunization in Indian pediatric populations. Given the limited geographic scope and number of available studies, further large-scale, multicenter randomized trials are warranted to confirm these findings and support evidence-based hepatitis A immunization policies.
