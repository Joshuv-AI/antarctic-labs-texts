Performance evaluation of the automated haematology analyzer XN-31 for malaria diagnosis in Plasmodium vivax-dominant regions of Thailand
Authors: Miki Kawaguchi, Kanako Komaki-Yasuda, Masami Nakatsu, Nattha Kerdsakundee, Mieko Hamana, Takahiro Tougan, Mina Kamei, Aya Konishi, Srivicha Krudsood, Shigeyuki Kano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe innovative automated haematology analyzer XN-31 has demonstrated comparable or, in some settings, superior performance to microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), and good concordance with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in various endemic areas, particularly where Plasmodium falciparum is prevalent. The XN-31 applies the principle of flow cytometry to measure cell size and nucleic acid content to generate a two-dimensional cytogram in approximately one minute. This technique identifies P. falciparum and other species and provides % parasitaemia (MI-RBC%). This study evaluated the diagnostic performance of the XN-31 in Thailand, where Plasmodium vivax is predominant.MethodsFrom November 2019 to June 2022, 349 patients suspected of having malaria were enrolled at the Hospital for Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University, Bangkok. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture and analysed using the XN-31, thick and thin film microscopy, RDTs, and PCR. The qualitative diagnostic accuracy of the XN-31 for detecting malaria parasites and identifying their species was compared with other methods. The quantitative diagnostic ability of the XN-31 was assessed by correlating the MI-RBC% values with % parasitaemia from thin film microscopy.ResultsAmong the 349 samples, 125 were positive according to thick film microscopy (103 P. vivax, 17 P. falciparum, 3 Plasmodium knowlesi, 1 Plasmodium ovale, and 1 Plasmodium malariae). The XN-31 demonstrated 98.4% sensitivity relative to thick film microscopy as the reference method, outperforming the RDT (88.0%). All XN-31-positive samples were confirmed via PCR. The information on the parasite species, flagged as ‘Malaria? (P.f)’ or ‘Malaria? (others)’ by the XN-31, which depicts P. falciparum or other Plasmodium species, respectively, matched 100% with microscopic determination. The quantitative performance of the XN-31 was strongly correlated with that of thin film microscopy (correlation coefficient [r] = 0.903).ConclusionsThis is the first study to confirm the accuracy of the XN-31 for both qualitative and quantitative diagnoses in a malaria-endemic region dominated by P. vivax. The XN-31 is expected to be a useful diagnostic tool in similar endemic regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05763-2.

Background

Thailand aims to eliminate malaria by 2025; however, the number of malaria cases, particularly those due to Plasmodium vivax, increased from 2022 to 2023. Traditionally, the diagnosis of malaria relies on microscopic observation of Giemsa-stained blood films as the gold standard. However, this is time-consuming and requires special training. Moreover, in low-prevalence settings, there are fewer opportunities for observation, making microscopic examination more difficult and increasing the risk of missing the parasite [1]. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) provide quicker results and require less training than microscopy but cannot quantify parasites or differentiate their stages [2]. Most RDTs detect common malaria antigens such as Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH), aldolase, or Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 (PfHRP2), but have lower sensitivity for non-P. falciparum species [3, 4]. Although polymerase chain reaction (PCR) offers high sensitivity and specificity, it remains impractical for routine clinical use [2]. These limitations highlight the pressing need for innovative, sensitive, and user-friendly malaria diagnostic tools to complement existing methods in endemic regions.

The automated haematology analyzer XN-31 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) is a novel malaria diagnostic device that shares physical characteristics, hardware configuration, and reagent requirements with the preliminary research-focused XN-30 model [5, 6]. This analyzer can detect malaria parasite-infected red blood cells (iRBCs), calculate % parasitaemia (referred to as “MI-RBC%”), and measure the complete blood count within approximately one minute [5, 6]. Moreover, the analyzer generates a two-dimensional cytogram, an “M scattergram,” capable of identifying P. falciparum and other Plasmodium species. Previous studies across various malaria-endemic settings, particularly where P. falciparum is prevalent, have demonstrated the qualitatively comparable or superior ability of the XN-31 to thick film microscopy and RDTs, along with good quantitative concordance with thin film microscopy and PCR [7–9]. Its utility has also been demonstrated for the diagnosis of imported malaria and therapeutic monitoring in nonendemic settings [10–13].

This study evaluated both the qualitative and quantitative diagnostic performance of the XN-31 using microscopy as the primary reference and RDTs and PCR as comparators for patients visiting the Hospital for Tropical Diseases, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. In addition, the validity of the XN-31 flags for identifying P. falciparum and non-P. falciparum species was verified. This is the first study to assess the diagnostic utility of the XN-31 in a setting where P. vivax predominates.

Methods

Study design and site

A diagnostic evaluation study was conducted at the Hospital for Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University, Bangkok, from November 2019 to June 2022. A total of 349 patients with suspected malaria infection were enrolled. Informed consent was obtained from all the patients prior to inclusion. Venous blood samples were collected by venipuncture before the initiation of any antimalarial treatment and were subsequently subjected to diagnostic evaluation via XN-31, microscopy, RDTs, and PCR.

Procedures for sample collection

Venous blood (3 mL) was collected using a plain syringe for routine testing. Residual blood from this collection was used for diagnostic procedures. For PCR, approximately 200 μL of blood (dispensed as approximately 100 μL/spot in two spots) was applied onto filter paper from a needle, and the remaining blood was transferred to a blood collection tube (BD K2-EDTA Microtainer blood collection tube, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Several microliters of blood from this tube was used to prepare one thick and two thin films. For RDTs (BinaxNOW™ Malaria test kit and SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag P.f./Pan), 15 μL and 5 μL were used, respectively. For XN-31 analysis, 280 μL of blood was used.

Diagnostic procedures

XN-31

The automated haematology analyzer XN-31 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) employs flow cytometry to detect and quantify iRBCs. It calculates the ratio of iRBCs (represented as "MI-RBC#" and "MI-RBC%," respectively) within approximately one minute. The XN-31 generates a two-dimensional cytogram (M scattergram), which is based on cell size and nucleic acid content. The analyzer also identifies parasite developmental stages, such as ring forms, trophozoites, schizonts, and gametocytes, as well as species (P. falciparum and other species), by providing information on malaria parasite species through flags. Such flags as "Malaria? (P.f), "Malaria? (others)," and "Malaria? (UNC)" indicate the presence of P. falciparum, other Plasmodium species, and unclassified Plasmodium parasites, respectively. When the XN-31 detects indefinite particles rather than RBCs on the M scattergram, the analyzer flags the MI-RBC Abn Scattergram, denoting an abnormal scattergram. The XN-31 offers three analytical modes: low malaria (LM), whole blood (WB), and predilution (PD). The analyzer requires 60 μL of blood in LM and WB modes and 20 μL of blood diluted with 120 μL of the dedicated reagent CELLPACK DCL (a diluent and sheath fluid) in PD mode. In this study, the blood samples were analysed in LM mode, which is the default for the XN-31 and has a lower limit of quantitation than the other modes.

Microscopy

For each participant, 1 thick and 2 thin blood films were prepared and stained with Giemsa according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. In Thailand, thick film microscopy is routinely used for parasite detection and species identification in clinical settings; the same approach was applied in this study to follow the standard diagnostic workflow at the Hospital for Tropical Diseases. Plasmodium infection and species identification were determined based on microscopic observations of both thick and thin films. For thick film examination, a negative infection was defined by observing 100 fields under 100 × magnification. Through thick film examination, the number of asexual and sexual parasites was counted per 200 white blood cells (WBCs) (when the count was > 100 parasites/μL) or per 500 WBCs (when the count was < 99 parasites/μL), as previously outlined. The % parasitaemia value was calculated by the proportion of iRBCs out of 10,000 RBCs counted in a thin film [13], which was specifically prepared for this study to evaluate the quantitative performance of the XN-31. For the routine thick film examination, all slides were examined by microscopists who had achieved Level 1 in the External Competence Assessment of Malaria Microscopists conducted by the WHO and the Ministry of Public Health (MOPH), Thailand. According to the institutional standard operating procedure (SOP), two microscopists independently read each thick film, and when the parasite density differed by more than 20%, a third reader was consulted.

RDT

EDTA-anticoagulated venous blood samples were tested immediately after venipuncture in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions. A total of 231 samples were analysed with the BinaxNOW™ Malaria test kit (HRP2/aldolase) (660–000, Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA), and 118 samples were analysed with the SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag P.f/Pan (HRP2/pLDH) (05KF60, Abbott). The switch from the BinaxNOW™ Malaria test kit to the SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag P.f/Pan was necessitated by the expiration of the BinaxNOW™ import license in Thailand. A positive HRP2 line indicated P. falciparum infection, whereas positive aldolase or pLDH lines indicated infection with any Plasmodium species.

PCR

DNA was extracted using the Maxwell DNA FFPE Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and the automated Maxwell RSC Instrument (Promega) from a punched dried blood spot of 5 mm in diameter (approximately equivalent to 20–30 µL of whole blood). The DNA was eluted in 100 μL of elution buffer. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting P. falciparum and P. vivax—the two most prevalent species in Thailand—was performed on all 349 samples to screen for these major species. A PCR reaction mix, consisting of 20 µL, was prepared with SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA). The concentration of both the forward and reverse primers was 0.125 µM, and 2 µL of the respective template DNA was used. For the detection of P. falciparum DNA, the PFF1 and PFR1 primers were used, and the PCR conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 38 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 20 s. For the detection of P. vivax DNA, PVF11 and PVR7 primers were used, and the PCR conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 38 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 62 °C for 20 s. The detailed primer sequences can be found in a previous report [14]. PCR and subsequent detection were performed via the CFX96 Real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

For samples showing discordant or ambiguous results among microscopy, XN-31, and qPCR findings, additional nested PCR was performed to confirm the species identity, including rare species, such as Plasmodium knowlesi, Plasmodium ovale, and Plasmodium malariae. Among these, five samples that tested negative by qPCR but positive by thick film microscopy were further analysed by nested PCR with species-specific primers. Nested PCR was used to target the 18S rRNA gene of four human Plasmodium species and P. knowlesi to confirm and accurately identify the infecting species [15].

Validation of the qualitative capacity of XN31

The ability of the XN-31 to detect malaria parasites and identify Plasmodium species via its flagging system was evaluated by comparison with results from thick film microscopy and PCR.

Validation of the quantitative MI-RBC% value from the XN-31

The MI-RBC% values obtained from the XN-31 were validated by comparison with the % parasitaemia values determined via thin film microscopy (see the Microscopy section). The agreement between the two methods was assessed via Deming regression analysis by calculating the regression line and correlation coefficient (r). Plasmodium species were classified based on the results of PCR.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using Analyse-it 5.30.4 (Analyse-it Software, Ltd., Leeds, UK). Participant characteristics are represented as medians, interquartile ranges (IQRs), and percentages. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated and compared with those of thick film microscopy as reference methods to illustrate the detection performance of the XN-31 and RDT. The breakdown of Plasmodium species between the XN-31 and thick film microscopy was analysed using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). For the comparison of % parasitaemia values from thin film microscopy and MI-RBC% values from the XN-31, ordinary Deming regression was used to calculate the slope, intercept, and correlation coefficient (r). The log conversion of each % parasitaemia value used in these calculations was determined using Excel (Microsoft).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 349 patients with suspected malaria were enrolled at the Hospital for Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University, Bangkok. Most cases were presumed to have been acquired in rural forested areas along the borders of Myanmar, Cambodia, and Malaysia, with patients having traveled to Bangkok from these malaria-endemic regions. All participants presented with fever suggestive of malaria. Their ages ranged from 2 to 88 years, with a median age of 33 years (IQR 22–49 years). The median body temperature was 38.4 °C (IQR 37.9–38.7 °C), and the median duration of fever was 2 days (IQR 1–3 days) (Table 1). Twelve patients reported a prior history of malaria consistent with their treatment records. Of these, six had a history of international travel to destinations including Myanmar (three cases), Nigeria, Laos, and India (one case each). Evaluation of the qualitative diagnostic performance of the XN-31

The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of XN-31, thin film microscopy, and RDTs were evaluated using thick film microscopy as the reference standard. Among the 349 samples analysed, 10 flagged as “MI-RBC Abn Scattergram” by the XN-31 were excluded. These excluded samples were all determined to be negative by thick and thin film microscopy, as were RDTs. The remaining 339 samples were included in the comparative evaluation. All the values of the XN-31, such as the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV (98.4%, 99.1%, 98.4%, and 99.1%, respectively), were greater than 95% and were comparable to those of thin film microscopy (96.0%, 100.0%, 100.0%, and 97.7%, respectively). These values exceeded those of RDTs (88.0%, 98.1%, 96.5%, and 93.3%, respectively) (Table 2). Among the 103 P. vivax–positive cases identified by microscopy, two were negative by XN-31; both were confirmed as P. vivax–positive by PCR. Conversely, two microscopy-negative samples were flagged as positive (“Malaria? (UNC)”) by the XN-31. PCR analysis revealed that one of these was P. falciparum and the other was P. vivax. These results indicate that the XN-31 provides diagnostic accuracy nearly equivalent to that of thick film microscopy and superior to that of RDTs in detecting malaria infection. Validation of flags indicated by XN-31

The XN-31 generates flags such as "Malaria? (P.f)," "Malaria? (others)," and "Malaria? (UNC)" based on scattergram patterns. The results from 125 samples were compared with species identified via thick film microscopy to evaluate the accuracy of these flags. Among them, 15 were flagged as "Malaria? (P.f)," 96 as "Malaria? (others)," 9 as both "Malaria? (P.f)" and "Malaria? (others)," and "Malaria? (UNC)" (Table 3). Among the 15 samples flagged as "Malaria? (P.f)," all were identified as P. falciparum (Table 3, "Malaria? (P.f)"). Among the 96 samples flagged as "Malaria? (others)," 93 were identified as P. vivax, 1 as P. ovale, 1 as P. malariae, and 1 as P. knowlesi, with no samples flagged as P. falciparum (Table 3, "Malaria? (others)"). Conversely, in all 9 samples flagged as both "Malaria? (P.f)" and "Malaria? (others)," no mixed infection was observed; 1, 6, and 2 samples were identified as infection with P. falciparum, P. vivax, and P. knowlesi, respectively (Table 3, "Malaria? (P.f)" & "Malaria? (others)"). Five samples were flagged as "Malaria? (UNC), "1, 2, and 2 samples were identified as P. falciparum, P. vivax, and noninfected (negative), respectively (Table 3, "Malaria? (UNC)"). These five samples were likely flagged due to very low parasitaemia, anaemia, or other unidentified factors. Of these, four were confirmed as Plasmodium-positive by microscopy, with parasitaemia levels below 0.1%, while one was negative by PCR. Among 214 samples flagged as "negative" by the XN-31, 2 were determined to be P. vivax by thick film microscopy (Table 3, "Negative"), though both were also negative by thin film microscopy and RDT. These findings suggest that the parasitaemia was equal to or lower than the detection limit of the XN-31. Overall concordance rates of the flags "Malaria? (P.f)," "Malaria? (others)", and "Negative" were 100.0%, 100.0%, and 99.0%, respectively, indicating that the XN-31 provides highly reliable flag-based identification of Plasmodium species. A very high level of agreement was observed between species identification by XN-31 and microscopy (κ = 0.925). Confirmation of Plasmodium species by PCR

To confirm the presence and species of Plasmodium parasites, PCR analysis was conducted on all 349 samples. Initial screening was performed via qPCR targeting P. falciparum and P. vivax, as these species are the most commonly encountered in the study region. qPCR detected Plasmodium DNA in 122 samples (104 P. vivax, 17 P. falciparum, and 1 mixed infection of both species). Notably, 1 P. falciparum-positive sample and 1 P. vivax-positive sample were identified by qPCR among the negative samples by thick film microscopy. Additionally, 1 P. falciparum-positive sample initially identified by thick film microscopy was revealed to be a mixed infection with P. vivax by qPCR. These discrepancies are likely attributable to extremely low parasite densities below the microscopic detection threshold. Conversely, 5 samples that were microscopy positive, tested negative by qPCR. Nested PCR of these 5 samples revealed 1 case of P. ovale and 4 cases of P. knowlesi. The species identified by nested PCR were consistent with the microscopy results, except for 1 sample, which was initially identified as P. malariae by thick film microscopy but was finally determined to be P. knowlesi by nested PCR. These minor discrepancies between thick film microscopy and nested PCR did not affect the overall qualitative comparison between microscopy and analysis by the XN-31. Further details are provided in Table 3.

M scattergrams depicted by XN-31

The XN-31 generates M scattergrams in which dots are scattered on the basis of the size (vertical axis, forward scattered light [FSC]) and nucleic acid content (horizontal axis, side fluorescent light [SFL]) of cells containing nucleic acids. In samples with no malaria parasite infection, RBCs are not depicted. Instead, only WBCs and debris from lysed RBCs are depicted as light blue and blue dots, respectively (Fig. 2a). In cases of P. falciparum infection, the characteristic one or more red dot clusters of the ring forms appeared (Fig. 2b), suggesting that each cluster corresponds to either single or multiple parasite infections per RBC [6]. In P. vivax infection, the characteristic dot clusters of the ring forms, which are relatively larger in size than those of P. falciparum, trophozoites, schizonts, and gametocytes, are algorithmically depicted as red, orange, purple, and green, respectively (Fig. 2c). P. ovale infection is flagged as "Malaria? (others)," The cluster pattern was similar to that of P. vivax on the M scattergram (Fig. 2d). The cell shape becomes uniform due to the reaction of a dedicated lysis solution (Lysercell M reagent) that transforms the cell shape into a sphere for iRBCs [6], making it more difficult to distinguish between P. vivax and P. ovale by this approach than by microscopy. In P. knowlesi, infections flagged as "Malaria? (P.f)" and "Malaria? (others)," the cluster pattern on the M scattergram was similar to that of P. falciparum. However, the SFL value indicating the nucleic acid content was lower than that of P. falciparum, whereas the FSC value indicating the cell size was similar to that of P. falciparum. This relationship between SFL and FSC values was consistently observed in all four P. knowlesi cases, and their M scattergrams are presented in Fig. S1. Validation of the MI-RBC% value from XN-31

The MI-RBC% values from the XN-31 were compared with % parasitaemia obtained via thin film microscopy to evaluate the quantitative accuracy of the XN-31. A strong overall correlation was observed across all samples (Fig. 3, solid line; y = 1.028x + 0.853, r = 0.903), as well as in the P. falciparum samples (Fig. 3, dotted line: y = 0.8897x + 0.1804, r = 0.975), and in the other Plasmodium samples (Fig. 3, dotted-dashed line; y = 0.8241x + 0.0584, r = 0.863). Discussion

Previous studies evaluating the XN-31 have focused primarily on regions with a high prevalence of P. falciparum. In contrast, the present study was conducted in Thailand, where P. vivax was more prevalent. When XN-31 data were compared with thick film microscopy data, the analyzer demonstrated sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV comparable to those of microscopy and superior performance to RDTs. The detection performance of the XN-31 was largely comparable to that of microscopy, although minor discrepancies in species identification were observed. Nevertheless, because it does not rely on expert microscopists, it may be more practical for routine use. However, the XN-31 showed slightly lower accuracy in species identification in comparison to microscopy, as discussed below.

Unlike P. falciparum, P. vivax and P. ovale preferentially infect reticulocytes and often present with low parasitaemia. Accurate quantification by microscopy under such conditions is challenging, and the results can vary among microscopists. Although % parasitaemia of P. vivax and P. ovale was less than 1% in this study (see Fig. 3), the MI-RBC percentage strongly correlated with the microscopic % parasitaemia determined by highly skilled microscopists. These findings indicate that the XN-31 can reliably detect low-level parasitaemia in non-P. falciparum species.

The XN-31 presented three flags: "Malaria? (P.f)," "Malaria? (others)," and "Malaria? (UNC)." Among the 15 samples flagged as "Malaria? (P.f)," one was identified as a mixed infection of P. falciparum and P. vivax by qPCR (Table 3). In this mixed infection case, both thin and thick film microscopy detected only P. falciparum, while qPCR revealed a threshold cycle (Ct) of 22.34 for P. falciparum and 34.83 for P. vivax, suggesting that P. falciparum was dominant and that the density of P. vivax was below the detection threshold of the XN-31.

Four P. knowlesi infections were detected in this study. Of these, two were flagged as both "Malaria? (P.f)" and "Malaria? (others)," and the other two as "Malaria?(others)" These results reflected the limited reliability of current XN-31 flagging for P. knowlesi, highlighting the need for further sample collection to refine its detection capabilities. Interestingly, the consistently lower SFL values observed in P. knowlesi compared with P. falciparum may provide a potential basis for improving the future discrimination of P. knowlesi by the XN-31 algorithm. Because P. knowlesi is difficult to distinguish morphologically from P. falciparum and P. malariae by microscopy and often requires species-specific PCR for accurate identification, it is conceivable that future refinements of the XN-31 algorithm could further improve its ability to differentiate this species automatically.

In this study, only one case of P. ovale infection could be differentiated by microscopy, and one case identified as a single P. falciparum infection by microscopy was revealed to be a mixed infection with P. vivax by PCR. Accurate detection of P. ovale and mixed infections using the XN-31 remains an important challenge for malaria diagnosis in Southeast Asia, and future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to address this issue.

Among seven samples flagged with both "Malaria? (P.f)" and "Malaria? (others), "one was confirmed as P. falciparum and six as P. vivax by both microscopy and PCR. In these cases, MI-RBC clusters on the M scattergrams deviated from the typical patterns shown in Fig. 2, suggesting that RBC abnormalities or blood preservation conditions may have affected the morphology of parasite-infected RBCs.

The XN-31 may also flag "MI-RBC Abn scattergram" and/or "Malaria? (UNC)" due to interference from certain RBC abnormalities. Such flags have been observed in peripheral blood samples containing Howell–Jolly bodies or sickle cells [7, 11]. In this study, five samples were flagged as "Malaria? (UNC)" could not be identified at the species level (see Table 3); among them, two were determined to be malaria-negative by thick film microscopy. Although the course of these unclassified flags is unclear, the high prevalence of G6PD deficiency in Thailand, partially along the Thai–Myanmar border and among specific ethnic groups [16], raises the possibility of underlying congenital or acquired RBC abnormalities.

MI-RBC Abn scattergrams were observed in 10 samples, all from malaria-negative individuals, confirming that no true malaria cases were missed. Empirical observations suggest that such MI-RBC Abn scattergrams arise under various conditions: (i) anemia and structurally abnormal RBCs such as sickle-shaped RBCs or inclusions in RBCs such as Howell–Jolly bodies, (ii) degeneration of RBCs due to storage at temperatures below 2 °C, and (iii) unknown factors. In the present study, the frequency of these conditions followed the order (i) > (iii) > (ii). Future improvements in the XN-31’s algorithm to automatically distinguish Howell–Jolly bodies from malaria parasites may help minimize such false-positive flags and improve diagnostic accuracy. The effects of the above hematologic and storage-related conditions on the M scattergram of the XN-31 should be investigated with the inclusion of a larger number of such cases in future studies to enhance the interpretability of abnormal flags.

Conclusions

This is the first study to demonstrate the utility of the XN-31 for both qualitative and quantitative diagnoses in a malaria-endemic region where P. vivax is predominant. Furthermore, this study validated the accuracy of the species-specific flagging provided by the XN-31. These findings support the practical applicability of the XN-31 in such settings and highlight its potential role in advancing malaria elimination efforts.
