DKC1 promotes colorectal cancer progression and therapy resistance by dysregulating sphingolipid biosynthesis
Authors: Umar K Khan, Ayush Goel, Shivansh Nigam, Nazia Chaudhary, Ayush Praveen, Anjan Roy, Fabeha Fatima, Dilpreet K Sawhney, Afiya Dalwai, Akshay Shendre, Shannon Carskadon, Anjali Bajpai, Abhimanyu Kapoor, Anjali Tewari, Abhijit Chandra, Avanish Saklani, Sorab N Dalal, Nallasivam Palanisamy, Bushra Ateeq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDKC1, a key coordinator of RNA modification and telomerase activity, has been implicated in colorectal cancer (CRC), yet its role in disease pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. We show that DKC1 drives CRC by promoting cell cycle progression, suppressing apoptosis, conferring stemness and drug resistance. Elevated DKC1 in CRC associates with poor prognosis and WNT-enriched Consensus Molecular Subtype 2 gene signature. Mechanistically, canonical WNT-signaling forms a feedback loop with DKC1, driving its expression and oncogenic activity. Comprehensive transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses reveal perturbed sphingolipid metabolism and abundance of specific very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides. Further, a regulatory axis involving DKC1 and SOX2 drives the expression of SGPP2, a critical sphingolipid metabolism mediator. Importantly, DKC1-mediated sphingolipid dysregulation promotes first-line chemoresistance, and FOLFOX-resistant patient-derived organoids effectively respond to DKC1 and WNT signaling inhibitors. Conclusively, we identify the DKC1/WNT axis as a therapeutic target in therapy-resistant CRC and underscore complex sphingolipids as promising plasma-based biomarkers.Subject terms: Colon cancer, Cancer metabolism

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) patients harboring RAS wild-type tumors often receive targeted therapies against EGFR, and most of them show a good response, as documented in the CRYSTAL, PRIME, and TAILOR trials1–3. Nonetheless, many exhibit poor outcomes owing to mutations in the KRAS (45%) or NRAS (4%) genes4,5. Besides EGFR, aberrantly active WNT signaling also plays a causal role in CRC, therefore, targeting catalytic production of WNT ligands, antagonists for its receptors, or inhibitors of transcriptional messengers, such as β-catenin, have been rigorously exploited against aggressive cancers, including CRC, some of which are in clinical trials6,7. However, mutations in the WNT signaling components, such as APC, AXIN, CTNNB1, and WNT ligands, pose challenges8,9. Furthermore, a significant fraction of CRC patients frequently receive multimodal treatment such as folinic acid, 5-fluorouracil, and irinotecan (FOLFIRI), or folinic acid, 5-fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX), as a part of first-line therapy coupled with anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies. Though many patients fail to respond or rapidly relapse after treatment due to intrinsic or acquired resistance mechanisms10,11. Notably, clinical and preclinical studies show that FOLFIRI/FOLFOX-resistant tumors often exhibit transient response to WNT pathway inhibitors due to compensatory signaling activation, underscoring the need for combinatorial therapeutic strategies12–14.

The molecular heterogeneity associated with CRC often leads to a variable clinical response that impedes the selection of effective therapeutics and challenges the accurate evaluation of the prognostic and predictive indicators. Thus, CRC Subtyping Consortium (CRCSC) devised criteria for the categorization of patients into four consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) with distinct features: CMS1 (microsatellite instability immune, 14%) which is hypermutated, microsatellite unstable, and linked with strong immune activation; CMS2 (canonical, 37%), epithelial features with marked WNT and MYC signaling; CMS3 (metabolic, 13%), also epithelial, displaying striking metabolic dysregulation; and CMS4 (mesenchymal, 23%), characterized by prominent TGF-β activation, stromal invasion, angiogenesis, and an inflammatory, immunosuppressive phenotype15. Although CMS classification offers potential for clinical utility in predicting prognosis and therapy response16,17, further refinement is warranted for a detailed understanding of these molecular subtypes. For example, single-cell characterization of the CMS subtypes revealed high heterogeneity associated with CMS2, wherein substantial variability and enrichment of pathways regulating metabolism and cell cycle were noted18.

The Dyskerin pseudouridine synthase 1 (DKC1) gene is known to be mutated in an X-linked human disease known as dyskeratosis congenita19, and has been involved in pseudo-uridylation of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and telomere maintenance20. Moreover, high DKC1 expression in hepatocellular and breast cancers has been associated with poor relapse-free survival and shorter survival probability, respectively21,22. Further, the multipronged role of DKC1, such as in the regulation of cell cycle and rRNA processing, makes it an ideal therapeutic target for cancer23. In this regard, targeting DKC1 along with the MEK/ERK pathway has been shown to have a synergistic effect in CRC24. Nevertheless, owing to the multiple cellular functions, DKC1 demands more insightful and elaborate elucidation of its oncogenic function, the underlying mechanism involved in its upregulation, and the associated molecular pathways involved in CRC progression.

Deregulation of lipids has been associated with tumor proliferation and progression by affecting protein-protein interactions and signal transduction25,26. Sphingolipids are a class of lipids important in both membrane composition and signaling pathway regulation that are generated by de novo synthesis and metabolized by the salvage pathway27. Further, sphingolipids serve as substrates for ceramide synthases (CerS) to produce ceramides, complex sphingolipids that act in a chain-length specific and context-dependent manner28,29. Notably, long-chain ceramides such as Cer d18:1/18:0 and Cer d18:1/20:0 are known to be reduced in colorectal cancer30, whereas other long-chain ceramides such as C16-Cer, C16-HexCer, and SM34 and very-long-chain ones like Cer d18:1/22:0, Cer d18:1/24:0, and Cer d18:1/24:1 are known to be elevated in tumor tissues30–32. While specific ceramides in plasma have been used as biomarkers for molecular subtyping33, the complex interplay between the ceramides and cell signaling proteins that may establish their effector function in cancer pathogenesis, and a potential drug target, is not well understood.

Here, we decipher the molecular mechanism underlying the upregulation of DKC1 in CRC and uncover the oncogenic traits associated with it. We report that CRC patients harboring high DKC1 exhibit similarity with CMS2 subtype enriched with WNT signaling, and represent an accumulation of specific very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides, highlighting their utility as lipid-based diagnostic biomarkers. Further, we unravel the role of DKC1 in promoting first-line therapy resistance in advanced-stage CRC patients. Importantly, using mice xenografts and patient-derived organoids (PDOs) established from therapy-resistant patients, we demonstrate the efficacy of combinatorial treatment using DKC1 and WNT inhibitors. Collectively, this study offers a resolution for therapy-resistant patients harboring high DKC1 levels and emphasizes the importance of plasma-based lipid signatures for the diagnosis of CRC.

Results

High DKC1 levels associate with poor prognosis in colorectal cancer patients

DKC1 is an essential component of the telomerase complex known to be highly expressed in multiple cancer types21,22. Hence, to examine DKC1 expression in CRC, we immunostained tissue microarrays (TMAs) comprising CRC patient specimens (N = 75) and a high expression was noted in ~ 31% of primary tumors across all stages and grades, while medium intensity was observed in ~45% of the cases, and only 24% exhibited low DKC1 expression (Fig. 1a–c). We also analyzed the publicly available RNA-seq dataset for the CRC patients of Korean ethnicity (GSE6988), and higher DKC1 expression was observed in primary tumors compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1d). A similar trend was observed in specimens (N = 13) derived from Indian CRC patients (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Data 1a). To further strengthen our findings, immunofluorescence staining for DKC1 was performed on additional tumor (N = 6) and normal (N = 5) tissues derived from Indian CRC patients, as expected elevated DKC1 expression was noted in the tumors (Fig. 1f, g, Supplementary Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Data 1b).

Next, we asked whether DKC1 qualifies as a therapeutic target in CRC, and analyzed the publicly available pan-cancer RNA-seq dataset (GDC-PANCAN). Higher DKC1 levels were observed specifically in CRC specimens compared to normal solid tissues across different organs, projecting DKC1 as a potential drug target with minimal off-target effects (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Previously, a kinase-enriched shRNA screen in CRC cell lines identified DKC1, along with nine key genes, as regulators of β-catenin–mediated transcriptional activity and cell proliferation34. In line with this, we reanalyzed gene expression profiles of these genes in the publicly available TCGA-Colorectal Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COADREAD) dataset and observed that DKC1 exhibited the highest expression in primary tumors, highlighting its significance as a critical mediator in CRC pathogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 1d). To assess the expression of DKC1 in tumors with different mutational backgrounds, we categorized CRC patients based on KRAS and BRAF mutations (GSE2138 and TCGA COADREAD). Notably, high DKC1 expression regardless of the mutational status was observed, thus emphasizing the potential of DKC1 as a putative therapeutic target in a broader spectrum of CRC patients (Supplementary Fig. 1e). To determine if DKC1 holds clinical relevance, we performed survival analysis using TCGA-COAD dataset to generate Kaplan-Meier curve, and poor recurrence-free survival for patients with higher DKC1 expression was observed (Fig. 1h). Next, to explore the downstream signaling pathways regulated by DKC1, we performed a comprehensive analysis using multiple publicly available CRC datasets comprising 1538 patients, categorized based on high to low DKC1 expression, and the intergroup variability was examined between first and fourth quartiles of these patients (N = 770). Notably, 265 genes were found to be significantly upregulated and 51 genes exhibited low expression in the DKC1-high group (Supplementary Fig. 1f and Supplementary Data 2a, b), thereby establishing a positive association of these upregulated genes with the DKC1-high group (Supplementary Fig. 1g). To identify the biological pathways associated with DKC1, gene ontology analysis was performed and enrichment of the processes linked with cancer pathogenesis, such as regulation of cell cycle and proliferation, etc, was observed (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Data 2c, d). Collectively, these findings indicate that high DKC1 associate with poor prognosis in CRC irrespective of the KRAS or BRAF mutational backgrounds and patient ethnicity.

DKC1 elicits oncogenic properties and drives colorectal cancer progression

To understand the functional significance of DKC1 in CRC, we examined the expression of DKC1-associated genes in cancer cell lines (Supplementary Data 2a), where SW620 and HT-29 (WiDr) showed higher expression of these genes (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Likewise, the transcriptome profile of CRC cell lines obtained from the cancer cell line encyclopedia (CCLE) was examined and SW620 cells showed the highest expression of DKC1, followed by Colo320HSR and WiDr cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Hence, for loss-of-function studies, we performed lentiviral shRNA-mediated stable silencing of DKC1 in SW620, Colo320HSR, and WiDr cell lines, and the knockdown was confirmed at both transcript and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Since DKC1 is known to regulate IRES-mediated ribosomal RNA translation by deregulating ribosome biogenesis and pseudouridylation activity35, we sought to confirm the functional implications of suppressing DKC1 activity by employing a fluorescence-based click chemistry L-homopropargylglycine (HPG) labeling assay, utilizing a methionine analog to directly visualize and quantify nascent protein synthesis in isogenic SW620 cells with DKC1-knockdown (shDKC1−2 and shDKC1−3) against controls. Notably, silencing of DKC1 markedly impaired nascent protein synthesis, as evident by a pronounced reduction in green fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Owing to the multifaceted role of DKC1 and its relatively less explored function in CRC pathogenesis, we next performed transcriptome profiling of DKC1-silenced SW620 cells (shDKC1−2 and shDKC1−3) and the scramble control (shSCRM) (Supplementary Fig. 2e and Supplementary Data 3a, b). Pathway enrichment analysis using Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) revealed different cancer-related biological processes, notably, the processes related to sterol and cholesterol biosynthesis were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 3c, d).

To elucidate the functional relevance of DKC1 in colorectal cancer, we conducted cell proliferation assays, which revealed a striking decline in CRC cell growth upon DKC1 silencing, highlighting its pivotal role in sustaining tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2 f). Moreover, a remarkable reduction in the foci formation ability of DKC1-silenced CRC cells was observed (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2g). Next, to examine the self-renewal capability of these cells, tumor sphere assay was performed, and a significant reduction in the sphere-formation efficiency and the surface area of the spheres was observed in DKC1-silenced cells compared to control (Fig. 2d). We also noted reduced stemness in DKC1-silenced cells, confirmed by a decrease in the expression of stemness-associated markers (Fig. 2e). In addition, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), also revealed significant negative enrichment of G2/M cell cycle transition upon DKC1 silencing (Supplementary Fig. 2h). To validate this, we examined the expression of genes involved in G2/M phase transition and performed propidium iodide-based flow cytometry to evaluate cell cycle progression. Aligning with the previous findings, a significant downregulation of genes involved in the G2/M phase transition and a marked accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase with DKC1 knockdown was observed (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2i). To further decipher the underlying mechanism associated with the oncogenic potential of DKC1, we examined the expression of apoptotic markers and found a subsequent reduction in the anti-apoptotic protein, Bcl-xL, while the expression of pro-apoptotic markers, such as cleaved Caspase-3 and cleaved PARP was significantly elevated upon DKC1 suppression (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2j).

Previously, DKC1 has been shown to augment the telomerase activity and genomic integrity20,36. Moreover, DNA damage response (DDR) and checkpoint pathways often lead to cell cycle arrest37, thus, we sought to explore if silencing DKC1 can induce DNA damage. Towards this, we examined the expression of DDR genes, and a significant decrease was observed upon DKC1 silencing (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Next, we checked the phosphorylation of H2AX protein, γH2AX, a highly specific marker for double-strand DNA breaks and an increase in γH2AX was observed upon DKC1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d).

To establish the role of DKC1 in CRC tumorigenesis, we subcutaneously implanted stable SW620-shSCRM and SW620-shDKC1 cells in NOD/SCID mice and monitored the tumor growth. A remarkable reduction (~81%) in tumor burden was observed in the mice implanted with SW620-shDKC1 cells compared to the control (Fig. 2h, i). Furthermore, a significant decrease in the abundance of Ki67-positive nuclei, a marker for actively dividing cells, was observed in tumors excised from the DKC1-silenced group (Fig. 2j). To further delineate the context-dependent role of DKC1 in CRC, we employed a doxycycline (dox)-inducible lentiviral shDKC1 system, enabling precise temporal control of gene silencing in cancer cells. This approach allowed us to uncouple the effects of DKC1 suppression on in vivo tumor progression from its tumor-initiating and proliferative functions, observed in vitro. Towards this, dox-mediated temporal induction of shDKC1 activity was first confirmed at both transcript and protein levels in dox-inducible isogenic SW620 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f), and a significant reduction in the proliferation rate of shDKC1 cells was noted upon dox induction compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 3g).

Further, dox-inducible SW620-shSCRM and shDKC1 cells were subcutaneously implanted into athymic nude mice to establish tumor xenografts. Once the tumors reached a palpable size (~ 40–50 mm3), dox was administered to induce DKC1 silencing. As anticipated, a significant reduction in tumor volume (~78%) was observed in the Dox + shDKC1 group compared to controls (Fig. 2k, l). Moreover, a significant decrease in the abundance of DKC1 levels and Ki67-positive nuclei was observed in the tumors excised from Dox+ shDKC1 group compared to controls (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 3h), with no notable changes in mice body weights monitored throughout treatment course (Supplementary Fig. 3i). Taken together, we show that elevated DKC1 in CRC promotes tumor growth, maintenance, and progression by modulating cell cycle regulation and cell survival pathways.

DKC1-positive colorectal cancer associates with consensus molecular subtype 2

Considering the highly heterogeneous nature of CRC, we sought to examine whether the elevated DKC1 expression is linked with a defined molecular signature or subtype. The widely accepted classification system for CRC is Consensus Molecular Subtypes (CMS), thus we employed the gene signatures for different CMS classes as CMS-UP or -DOWN (Supplementary Data 4), and analyzed against the combined dataset of CRC patients (N = 770) categorized as DKC1-high and -low groups. The Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) showed upregulation of the ‘CMS2 UP’ gene signature in DKC1-high patients, while a downregulation in DKC1-low patients (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Further, we utilized ColoType, a 40 gene signature for defining CMS class based on the global transcriptome data38, and found a similar association with ‘CMS2 UP’ gene signature (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Subsequently, to validate these findings, we examined the publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data for unsorted single cells (N = 91,103) from two different CRC patient cohorts of Belgian and Korean ethnicities, respectively (GSE144735 and GSE132465)39, and evaluated the average expression and percentage of cells expressing DKC1 in different CMS subtypes. A higher percentage of cells aligning to the CMS2 subclass and relatively high mean expression of DKC1 compared to other subclasses was found in both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). To further strengthen our findings, we conducted a meta-analysis of single-cell RNA-seq datasets from three independent studies (GSE132465, GSE144735, and GSE200997), and applied a pseudo-bulk approach with DESeq2-based differential expression modeling under a random effects framework. Notably, DKC1 emerged as significantly upregulated in the CMS2 subtype compared to CMS1, CMS3, and CMS4, reinforcing its subtype-specific relevance in colorectal cancer (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

Next, we analyzed RNA-seq count data from TCGA-COADREAD cohort by stratifying patients into four CMS categories using expanded representative gene sets (~ 20–25 genes per subtype) to capture hallmark biological processes associated with each subclass, as previously defined15. Expression levels of selected CMS2-associated genes, including DKC1, were standardized across samples. Notably, DKC1 exhibited distinctly elevated expression in the majority of patients classified under the CMS2 subtype, further underscoring its potential role in this molecular context (Fig. 3a). To corroborate large-scale transcriptional findings with protein-level evidence, we analyzed proteomic profiles of CRC patients from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC). This analysis revealed a strong association between elevated DKC1 protein expression and the CMS2 molecular subtype, further reinforcing its subtype-specific relevance (Fig. 3b). A notable fraction of cells with high DKC1 expression also exhibited a stem-like/TA (Transit Amplifying) phenotype, corroborating with our results, where a remarkable reduction in stemness was noted upon DKC1 silencing (Fig. 2e). To further validate this, we investigated the association of differentially expressed genes between the comprehensive patient data (N = 770) categorized based on DKC1 expression and the cell-type gene signature proposed in an earlier CRC classification40. A remarkable 14% overlap between the gene signature upregulated in TA cells and the genes upregulated in DKC1-high patients was observed (Supplementary Fig. 4f and Supplementary Data 5), while a notable ~7% overlap of the downregulated stem cell gene signature was noted with the genes downregulated in DKC1-high cases, indicating an association of DKC1-positive patients with TA/stem-cell phenotype and CMS2 molecular subtype.

A positive feedback loop exists between DKC1 and canonical WNT signaling in colorectal cancer

Since the CMS2 subclass of CRC invariably shows WNT signaling activation15, we sought to explore if DKC1 levels are regulated via Wnt/β-catenin signaling. The majority of CRC patients display activation of WNT signaling via inactivating mutation(s) in the APC tumor suppressor; thus, we analyzed TCGA-COAD dataset and stratified patients based on APC mutation status and compared the expression of DKC1 along with other WNT target genes. Interestingly, most of the patients harboring APC mutations exhibit higher expression of DKC1 and WNT target genes (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Furthermore, colorectal polyps from mice harboring inactivating mutations in Apc (Apcmin) showed significantly high Dkc1 expression compared to adjacent normal colorectal tissues, which progressively increased upon induction of Apc deletion in Apc-floxed mice (Fig. 3c, GSE65461). A similar trend was observed in colorectal polyps from Apc+/Δ716 mice, which showed significantly higher Dkc1 expression compared to normal colorectal tissues derived from both Apc+/+ and Apc+/Δ716 genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 4h, GSE89077). We further re-analyzed RNAseq data (GSE98496) obtained from Apcmin, and the mice treated with azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium, known to induce a spontaneous activating Ctnnb1 mutation41. Interestingly, increased expression of Dkc1 and Wnt signaling mediators was observed in the adenoma tissues compared to non-adenoma tissues in both groups, emphasizing the role of WNT signaling in modulating DKC1 in CRC (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

To further delineate the direct control of WNT signaling on the DKC1 expression, we analyzed a publicly available dataset (GSE44097), and a significant reduction in the DKC1 expression was noted upon silencing of CTNNB1 in CRC cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b). To confirm these findings, siRNA-mediated silencing of CTNNB1 was performed in the panel of CRC cell lines and a significant decrease in DKC1 expression was observed (Fig. 3d, e). In addition, stimulation of organoids established from CRC patients, with the canonical WNT ligand, namely WNT3A, led to a remarkable upregulation of DKC1 expression, concomitant with nuclear accumulation of phosphorylated-β-catenin, indicating robust activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling (Fig. 3f). Similar results were obtained upon stimulating immortalized Mouse Small Intestinal Epithelium (MSIE) and Young Adult Mouse Colonic epithelium (YAMC) cells42 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Contrariwise, treating SW620 cells with a tankyrase inhibitor (XAV939), known to impair WNT signaling, markedly reduced DKC1, thus highlighting the importance of WNT signaling in DKC1 regulation (Supplementary Fig. 5d). To further delineate the underlying mechanistic circuitry involving WNT signaling-mediated transcriptional regulation of DKC1, we examined the upstream promoter/enhancer region of DKC1 for the presence of TCF/LEF binding motifs using JASPAR43. Several putative TCF7L2 binding sites or Catenin Binding Motifs (indicated as CBM1-CBM4) were found in the proximal and distal promoters of DKC1. To confirm the occupancy of β-catenin/TCF complex, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR using β-catenin antibody. A significant enrichment of β-catenin/TCF complex was observed at all four CBMs (Fig. 3g). In conjunction with these findings, analysis of ChIP-seq data (GSE31477) revealed enrichment of TCF7L2 binding along with RNA polymerase II on the promoter region of DKC1, indicating that WNT signaling directly regulates the transcription of DKC1 (Supplementary Fig. 5e).

Of note, previously Firestein et al. reported DKC1 as one of the upstream modulators of β-catenin activity; however, our findings show that DKC1 is the downstream target of WNT/β-catenin signaling. Taking cues from these findings, we went ahead to check if a positive feedback loop exists between the effectors of WNT signaling and DKC1. Notably, upon DKC1 silencing, we observed a significant reduction in the expression of downstream effectors of the canonical WNT signaling, such as β-catenin and TCF7L2 (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). Taken together, our findings show a direct control of canonical WNT signaling in the transcriptional regulation of DKC1 and a feedback loop between DKC1 and canonical WNT signaling in CRC (Fig. 3h).

DKC1 perturbs sphingolipid biosynthesis in colorectal cancer

Lipid deregulation is known to be a targetable metabolic vulnerability in CRC, and specific lipid moieties are utilized as non-invasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers44. GSEA analysis of our transcriptomics data revealed downregulation of biological processes, such as sphingolipid biosynthesis in DKC1-silenced cells, signifying the involvement of DKC1 in sphingolipid metabolism (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Hence, we performed lipid profiling of DKC1-silenced and control cells using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). The principal component analysis revealed two separate clusters highlighting the distinct lipidome of DKC1-silenced versus control cells (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Interestingly, the differential lipid profile revealed downregulation of various ceramides and sphingosine, while a concurrent upregulation of several phospholipids and sphingomyelins in DKC1-silenced cells (FDR ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 6a). Next, we sought to identify the most significantly deregulated lipid moieties, where several phospholipid species, including phosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylethanolamines, and various plasmalogen isoforms, were found to be upregulated, while several ceramide species, including Gb3(d18:1/16:0), HexCer(d18:1/16:0) and Hex2Cer(d18:1/24:1) were downregulated (VIP score > 1) (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Data 6b). Furthermore, Lipid Ontology network analysis (LION) revealed a marked downregulation of sphingolipid metabolism-related ontologies following DKC1 silencing, while glycerophospholipid-associated ontologies were conversely upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 6c and Supplementary Data 6c). These findings highlight a pivotal role of DKC1 in modulating fatty acid metabolic pathways, potentially driving CRC progression.

High DKC1 defines a CRC patient subtype enriched in very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides

Taking cues from our cell line-based lipidomics data, we next sought to determine DKC1-mediated lipid dysregulation in CRC patient specimens. Towards this, DKC1 levels were examined using the tumor specimens and paired adjacent normal tissues (N = 9), categorizing them as DKC1-high or -low tumors (Supplementary Fig. 6d), followed by lipidome analyses as described elsewhere. The lipid profile revealed increased levels of very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides in patients with higher DKC1 levels, accompanied by low sphingomyelins (FDR ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 7a). Previously, these fatty acid signatures have been shown to confer oncogenic properties and are utilized as clinical biomarkers26. We identified very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides, Cer(d18:2/23:0) and Cer(d18:1/24:0), as some of the most important variables in the differential lipid profile, wherein both of these ceramides had VIP scores of > 1.7, highlighting their importance in diagnostics and prognostics of DKC1-high cases (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Data 7b). Furthermore, the Lipid Ontology Network analysis revealed upregulation of ontologies related to sphingosine and very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides and downregulation of sphingomyelin-related ontologies in DKC1-high tumors (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Data 7c). We subsequently examined whether the identified ceramides could be used as potential biomarkers in CRC, and the abundance of the same was found in tumors with elevated DKC1 (Fig. 4g). Importantly, both of these ceramides show high sensitivity and specificity with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve of >0.9 (Supplementary Fig. 6e). To further confirm this, ROC curve-based model evaluation was performed using the Biomarker analysis module of Metaboanalyst 5.0 using the partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) for the performance evaluation. When assessing sensitivity and specificity for both ceramides together, they exhibit an AUROC curve of 0.855, thus strengthening their utility as lipid-based non-invasive biomarkers for diagnosing DKC1-positive CRC patients (Supplementary Fig. 6f).

Building on our findings wherein very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides were enriched in tumor tissues, we next investigated whether these elevated ceramide levels could also be detected in the peripheral blood of DKC1-high CRC patients relative to healthy controls, thereby assessing their potential utility as a non-invasive biomarker for disease burden and prognostic stratification. Thus, we established a competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for quantitative measurement of C24 Ceramide in human serum samples. Interestingly, we found significantly elevated C24 Ceramide levels in the serum samples of DKC1-positive CRC patients (N = 5) compared to healthy individuals (N = 5) (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Data 7d), signifying the importance of C24 Ceramide as a promising non-invasive diagnostic biomarker for DKC1-positive CRC patients. Collectively, our findings underscore a crucial role for DKC1 in regulating sphingolipid metabolism, specifically in modulating very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides, which may serve as a clinically relevant CRC biomarker.

DKC1-driven SGPP2 confers oncogenic traits in colorectal cancer

Thus far, we have shown that DKC1 exhibits oncogenic properties in CRC by upregulating canonical WNT signaling and dysregulating the sphingolipid metabolism, resulting in the accumulation of very-long-chain ceramides. However, the underlying molecular mechanism involved in the upregulation of ceramide levels needs to be explored. To investigate the significance of altered lipid metabolism in the context of DKC1-mediated carcinogenesis, we performed integrative pathway analysis using the transcriptome and lipid profiling data of DKC1-silenced and control cells. In line with our patient lipidomics data, sphingolipid metabolism was noted to be the most significantly affected pathway with a high impact score (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In addition, several metabolic hits such as sphingosine, lactosylceramide, digalactosylceramide, and sphingomyelin were observed in the sphingolipid metabolism. Importantly, a single gene hit, namely Sphingosine-1-phosphate phosphatase 2 (SGPP2), an enzyme that is involved in the metabolism of sphingosine-1-phosphate and a sphingolipid metabolite involved in regulating various cell functions, was also observed (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

To cross-examine the regulatory interplay between DKC1 and SGPP2 in clinical patient specimens, we checked their expression in CRC biopsy specimens (N = 49) (Supplementary Data 8). A strong positive correlation was observed between DKC1 and SGPP2 transcripts, and higher SGPP2 levels were associated with increase in tumor grade (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Likewise, immunostaining was performed for SGPP2 expression using TMAs, which were stained for DKC1 (Fig. 1a). About ~ 67% of patients showed high to medium expression of both SGPP2 and DKC1 (Fig. 5b, c). Furthermore, ~82% of the DKC1-high cases showed either high or medium expression of SGPP2, while patients with low DKC1 expression harbored either low or negative SGPP2 (Fig. 5d). In concordance with the role of DKC1 as a transcriptional regulator of SGPP2, its expression was primarily found in the nucleus, and to some extent in the cytosol. Albeit owing to the enzymatic function of SGPP2, its expression was predominantly cytoplasmic. Furthermore, SGPP2 expression was detected in the majority of CRC tumors (~ 91%), with high levels observed in ~ 32% cases, moderate in ~ 47%, and low in ~ 12%, while only ~9% of specimens exhibited negative staining (Supplementary Fig. 7d–f). To validate these findings, immunofluorescence analysis was performed on the same tumor tissues previously assessed for DKC1 expression (Fig. 1f). As anticipated, SGPP2 levels were markedly elevated in tumor tissues compared to their adjacent normal counterparts (Fig. 5e, f), emphasizing its potential role in CRC pathobiology. Considering the strong association between DKC1 and SGPP2, and its pragmatic role in sphingolipid metabolism, we examined the expression of SGPP2 in DKC1 knockdown CRC cells. A significant reduction in SGPP2 levels was observed in DKC1-silenced cells (Fig. 5g), which corroborates our lipid profile data using patient specimens, wherein a significant reduction in sphingosine and ceramide levels was noted (Fig. 4f). Previously, DKC1 has been shown to form a complex with SOX2/OCT4 and regulate the expression of NANOG in embryonic stem cells45. Therefore, we conjectured that this transcriptional circuitry might also regulate the expression of SGPP2. To examine this, DKC1 immunocomplex was pulled down and immunoblotted for SOX2 expression, which displayed a strong interaction with DKC1 (Fig. 5h). Furthermore, the regulatory region of SGPP2 was examined for transcription factor binding sites, and two distinct SOX2 binding motifs (SBM1 and SBM2) were identified within a 5 Kb upstream promoter region of the transcription start site (TSS). To check the occupancy of DKC1/SOX2 complex on SGPP2 promoter, ChIP-PCR was performed, and a significant reduction of SOX2 binding was observed in DKC1-silenced cells compared to control SW620 cells (Fig. 5i).

To investigate whether SGPP2 has a possible role in DKC1-mediated oncogenicity, we generated stable SGPP2 knockdown SW620 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Upon assessing the phenotypic effect, a remarkable reduction in the proliferation as well as foci formation ability of SGPP2 knockdown cells was observed compared to control (Fig. 5j, k). Further, to delineate whether DKC1-associated tumorigenicity is explicitly via SGPP2, we performed a rescue experiment, wherein SGPP2 was ectopically expressed in DKC1-knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig. 8b). As expected, the diminished oncogenic properties upon DKC1 knockdown were rescued upon SGPP2 overexpression in shDKC1 SW620 cells (Fig. 5l, m). Taken together, we established that the DKC1-SOX2 complex drives upregulation of SGPP2 expression, which potentiates DKC1-mediated tumorigenicity in CRC.

DKC1-driven SGPP2 perturbs sphingolipid biosynthesis and transactivates canonical WNT signaling

The role of SGPP2 has been previously established in the salvage pathway of ceramide biosynthesis, wherein it catalyzes the dephosphorylation of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) to sphingosine, which gets converted to different ceramides by various ceramide synthases (Supplementary Fig. 8c). To examine whether reduced expression of SGPP2 upon DKC1 silencing is due to dysregulation of the salvage pathway, we detected S1P levels in DKC1 knockdown and control SW620 cells, and an increased accumulation of S1P in DKC1-silenced cells was observed (Fig. 5n). Similar results were obtained in SGPP2 knockdown cells, included as positive controls (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Conversely, ectopic re-expression of SGPP2 in DKC1-knockdown cells led to a significant reduction of S1P levels (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Furthermore, ELISA-based quantification of C24 Ceramide revealed a significant decrease in its levels in DKC1-silenced cells compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 8f).

To demonstrate that the conversion of sphingosine to ceramides downstream of the DKC1–SGPP2 axis occurs via the salvage pathway, we examined the expression of key ceramide synthases upon SGPP2 knockdown. This perturbation is expected to reduce sphingosine influx as a substrate, thereby eliciting metabolically adjusted levels of ceramide synthases46. Notably, SGPP2-silenced CRC cells exhibited a marked upregulation of CERS1 and CERS4, while DKC1 knockdown led to a significant increase in these enzymes, whereas SGPP2 rescue reversed this effect, resulting in a concomitant decrease in CERS1 and CERS4 levels (Supplementary Fig. 8g), suggesting that ceramide synthase regulation governs ceramide biosynthesis downstream of the DKC1–SGPP2 axis in CRC. Of note, CERS2 levels, which is typically involved in very-long chain fatty acid ceramide biogenesis, did not change significantly upon perturbing this circuitry47. Our data reveal an inverse regulatory interplay among very-long-chain, long-chain, and short-chain fatty acid ceramides, indicating a compensatory feedback mechanism that safeguards ceramide homeostasis under conditions of altered SGPP2 and DKC1 expression. These findings underscore the crucial role of DKC1 in orchestrating SGPP2-mediated sphingosine metabolism through the salvage pathway of ceramide biosynthesis. Of note, ceramides are known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) via perturbed mitochondrial activity48. Our lipid ontology network analysis also revealed accumulation of very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides along with enriched mitochondrion in DKC1-high compared to low cases (Fig. 4f). Therefore, to confirm the downstream effect of ceramide accumulation on ROS generation and mitochondrial activity, we stained the DKC1-silenced and control cells with dihydroethidium and MitoTracker dyes respectively, and a significant reduction in the ROS and mitochondrial activity was observed in DKC1 knockdown cells compared to control (Supplementary Fig. 8h, i). These findings imply the dysregulation of sphingolipid biosynthesis upon DKC1 perturbation in CRC.

Loss of Apc function has been reported as one of the early events in CRC progression, although this lesion alone is insufficient in driving colon carcinogenesis in mice49,50. However, the downstream oncogenic pathways could be amplified by altering sphingolipid metabolism along with APC inactivation, resulting in enhanced tumor-promoting effect51. Furthermore, very-long-chain ceramides such as C24 have been reported to transactivate the WNT-signaling in CRC via Gb352, which is also discovered as one of the DKC1 targets in our lipidomics data (Fig. 4b, c). Therefore, we sought to investigate whether perturbing sphingolipid metabolism by silencing SGPP2 can affect WNT signaling in CRC. To investigate this, we examined the status of canonical WNT signaling upon SGPP2 knockdown in SW620 cells, and a remarkable reduction in CD44 levels was observed, a direct read-out for the WNT signaling pathway (Fig. 5o). To further confirm that the observed effect operates in the context of DKC1-SGPP2 mechanistic circuitry, we checked for WNT-signaling in DKC1-silenced CRC cells ectopically expressing SGPP2, and a remarkable increase was observed in active β-catenin and CD44 levels (Fig. 5p–r). Conclusively, our findings suggest that elevated DKC1 dysregulates sphingolipid metabolism that may potentiate CRC progression by transactivating canonical WNT signaling.

DKC1 elicits chemoresistance to first-line therapies in Colorectal Cancer

We uncovered a positive regulatory feedback loop between DKC1 and WNT signaling in colorectal cancer, wherein pharmacological inhibition of WNT signaling led to a significant downregulation of DKC1 expression (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 5d). Building on this, we next evaluated the impact of XAV939 treatment on DKC1-driven oncogenicity, and observed a modest reduction in proliferation and foci formation capacity in DKC1-positive CRC cells (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), indicating the evidence of a compensatory signaling mechanism, a characteristic also common in therapy-resistant patients, particularly that fail to respond to first-line therapy53.

Of note, mounting evidence links dysregulated cholesterol biosynthesis to therapy resistance across multiple malignancies54. In line with this, transcriptomic profiling of DKC1-silenced CRC cells revealed significant downregulation of genes involved in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 2a). Therefore, we hypothesized that CRC cells with high DKC1 may elicit resistance to standard first-line therapies. To examine this, we performed a chemosensitivity assay, and an enhanced sensitivity towards 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) was noted in DKC1-silenced cells compared to control (Supplementary Fig. 9c), indicating a plausible role of DKC1 in imparting chemoresistance. Furthermore, we also generated chemoresistant CRC models, wherein HCT116 cells were treated with 5-FU alone or in combination with Irinotecan (IR) and Oxaliplatin (Ox), recapitulating FOLFIRI and FOLFOX treatment regimens, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9d). The IC50 values for each treatment conditions confirmed the desired level of chemoresistance (Supplementary Fig. 9e). Interestingly, a panel of chemoresistant CRC cells derived from HCT116 showed higher expression of DKC1 compared to control, with the highest levels observed in the 5-FU and Ox-resistant (5-FU+Ox)R cells (Supplementary Fig. 9f).

To establish the role of DKC1 in mediating chemoresistance, we generated stable DKC1-silenced HCT116-(5-FU+Ox)R cells, and knockdown was confirmed at both transcript and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 9g). A marked reduction in proliferative rate of DKC1-silenced cells was observed compared to control (Supplementary Fig. 9h). Moreover, chemosensitivity assay revealed a striking reduction in cell viability in shDKC1-HCT116-(5-FU+Ox)R cells compared to controls upon treatment with 5-fluorouracil and Oxaliplatin, both by individual drugs and in combination (Supplementary Fig. 9i). Next, we investigated the role of DKC1 in promoting drug resistance using preclinical mice model, and implanted shDKC1-HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R and shSCRM-HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R cells subcutaneously in the NOD/SCID mice, followed by administration of the FOLFOX treatment. Notably, while the FOLFOX treatment was ineffective in the chemoresistant cells, a remarkable reduction in tumor burden was observed in shDKC1-HCT116-(5-FU+Ox)R compared to controls (Fig. 6a, b). Immunohistochemical analysis of the resected tumors confirmed decreased DKC1 and SGPP2 levels in the shDKC1-HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R cells only, with no significant change in their levels upon FOLFOX treatment (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9j). In addition, reduced Ki67 nuclear staining and high levels of Caspase-3, an apoptotic marker was observed in shDKC1-HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R cells (Fig. 6c).

Dual targeting of DKC1 and WNT signaling attenuates tumorigenicity and chemoresistance in colorectal cancer

Having established the WNT-dependent role of DKC1 in driving CRC tumorigenesis and therapy resistance through dysregulated sphingolipid biosynthesis, we next sought to identify pharmacological strategies to disrupt this oncogenic circuitry and overcome treatment refractoriness. While previous findings showed that WNT inhibition alone yields only modest inhibition of oncogenic properties, we hypothesized that targeting WNT signaling in isolation may be insufficient, as DKC1 could reinitiate pathway transactivation. In contrast, concurrent inhibition of both DKC1 and WNT signaling was anticipated to abrogate the compensatory feedback loop. To test this, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of dual targeting in chemoresistant CRC models. Hence, we observed that targeting WNT signaling alone exerted only a marginal effect on the viability of chemoresistant CRC cells, while DKC1 inhibition using pyrazofurin elicited a more pronounced response in chemoresistant cells relative to their sensitive counterparts. Notably, combinatorial pharmacological inhibition of both DKC1 and WNT signaling led to a substantial reduction in cell viability in chemoresistant models compared to parental controls (Supplementary Fig. 10a), highlighting the synergistic potential of dual-targeting strategies to overcome therapeutic resistance.

To validate these findings in preclinical mice models, we assessed the therapeutic efficacy of single-agent versus combinatorial inhibition of DKC1 and WNT signaling in HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R cells xenografted mice. Notably, dual-targeted treatment elicited a significantly greater reduction in tumor volume compared to either monotherapy or vehicle controls (Fig. 6d, e). Further, reduced DKC1 expression was confirmed in the excised tumors following drug administration (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Importantly, no overt toxicity was observed at the administered dosages, as evidenced by stable body weights of the mice throughout the treatment period and unaltered blood biochemical profiles at the termination of the study (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d).

To explore the translational relevance of dual targeting of the DKC1–WNT axis in therapy-resistant CRC, we established patient-derived organoids from DKC1-high/SGPP2-high CRC cases that had failed to respond to first-line chemotherapeutic regimens (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 10e). These patient-derived organoids were subjected to graded concentrations of four treatment regimens: 5-FU alone; 5-FU + Pyrazofurin; 5-FU + XAV939; 5-FU+Pyrazofurin+XAV939. As expected, these organoids exhibited higher sensitivity towards 5-FU + Pyrazofurin and 5-FU + XAV939 compared to 5-FU treatment alone (Supplementary Fig. 10f). Furthermore, the combinatorial treatment (5-FU+Pyrazofurin+XAV939) showed a remarkable reduction in tumor viability, and a synergistic effect was noted at around 1 μM drug concentration (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 10g). Collectively, our findings uncover a pivotal role for DKC1 in imparting therapy resistance in CRC and offer a promising combinatorial strategy, targeting DKC1 and WNT signaling, as an effective therapeutic intervention for overcoming resistance in CRC patients.

Discussion

The routine treatment modalities for CRC patients offer limited clinical benefits owing to their non-specific mechanism of action, off-target effects, and resistance acquired over time55,56. Recent advances in screening methods and medicaments have improved substantially57, however, a comprehensive tumor molecular profiling and functional characterization of the genetic aberrations have been advantageous in devising a personalized treatment regimen(s) for better disease management58. In this study, we decipher the functional significance of DKC1 in promoting CRC progression, show its association with the WNT-associated CMS2 subtype, discover the underlying molecular mechanism via perturbed sphingolipid metabolism, and its role in imparting therapy resistance that can be addressed by using a dual targeting strategy against the DKC1-WNT axis.

Earlier, DKC1 has been reported to be essential for the assembly and stabilization of the telomerase complex, where it is known to add telomeric repeats to the chromosomal ends and maintain genomic integrity, thereby preventing chromosomal fusions and DNA damage response20,59. Moreover, DKC1 attenuation has been linked with G1/S mitotic checkpoint and G2/M DNA damage checkpoints arrest in lung adenocarcinoma and sarcoma, respectively23,60. Our data suggest a contextual association of DKC1 with G2/M cell cycle phase transition. In addition, mutations in DKC1 lead to a pathological condition known as Dyskeratosis congenita, characterized by a hypomorphic body phenotype, which manifests defective telomerase and ribosome activity resulting in increased susceptibility to premature cellular senescence and tissue dysfunction61–63. Broadly, mutations in DKC1 are not reported to cause sporadic cancers64, except for a few instances, such as breast, where an inactivating mutation in DKC1 has been linked with reduced expression of p53 via defective IRES-mediated mRNA translation65. Nevertheless, high DKC1 expression has been associated with poor prognosis in multiple cancer types22,66. Previously, Kan et al. showed that elevated DKC1 associates with poor overall survival in Chinese CRC patients, and elicits oncogenicity by deregulating ribosome biogenesis and pseudouridylation activity, highlighting its effectiveness as a combinatorial therapeutic target along with the RAS/RAF pathway24. In addition, Hou et al. demonstrated the involvement of elevated DKC1 in CRC patients in promoting tumor progression and metastasis through hypoxia-induced cancer dissemination pathways67. Corroborating with these reports, our findings suggest that elevated DKC1 in CRC is a poor prognosis, irrespective of the ethnicity and/or RAS/RAF mutational status. Moreover, targeting DKC1 in lung adenocarcinoma has been shown to cause telomere-associated senescence and apoptosis60. In line with the previous report24, no notable side-effects of pharmacological inhibition of DKC1 in preclinical mice studies were observed. Nonetheless, a patient suffering from Dyskeratosis congenita due to a germline mutation in the DKC1 is known to exhibit progressive pulmonary fibrosis, myelodysplastic syndrome, and pulmonary fibrosis68, and hence an in-depth clinical evaluation is warranted.

Distinct CMS classification requires specific delineation of molecular and phenotypic features for identifying candidate drug targets. Our findings highlight a remarkable overlap in the molecular signatures of DKC1-positive CRC with the CMS2, characterized by active WNT signaling and MYC amplification. Earlier, the WNT-MYC axis has been shown to modulate the ribosomal biogenesis in pancreatic and colorectal cancers69. Firestein et al. demonstrated the involvement of DKC1 in the regulation of β-catenin activity34. However, we showed that DKC1 is transcriptionally regulated via WNT signaling, emphasizing a feedback loop between DKC1 and WNT signaling. Thus, we propose the importance of targeting WNT signaling in abrogating DKC1-mediated carcinogenesis. The CMS2 class has also been associated with high chromosomal ploidy and activation of the homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathways70,71. Likewise, upon DKC1 silencing, we found downregulation of PRKDC, a key player in NHEJ, thereby leading to enhanced DNA damage. Moreover, CMS2 also exhibits a poor immunogenic signature, and patients belonging to this class show an association with tubular adenomas and epithelial signatures. This specific class is known to harbor TA cell signature, abundance of APC and TP53 mutations, low methylation pattern, and enhanced ribosomal translation15. Similarly, we found an overlap in the gene signature enriched in TA cells and DKC1-high patients, who often harbor APC alterations. Hence, our data reveals a strong correlation between elevated DKC1 expression and CMS2 subtype of CRC, it may have a broader association with other CRC categories in a context-dependent manner.

Interestingly, we found dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism due to upregulation of SGPP2 via DKC1-mediated transcriptional circuitry. SGPP2 is known to metabolize its substrate Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) to ceramides via the salvage pathway72, which has been reported to induce ulcerative colitis and promote tumor growth and metastases in gastric cancer46,73. Furthermore, we showed DKC1 is a transcriptional co-activator that regulates SGPP2, thereby confirming its involvement in sphingolipid metabolism. Although S1P has been shown to play a pleiotropic role in cell survival and metastasis74, our results highlight the context-dependent role of S1P in DKC1-mediated carcinogenicity. Our data also negatively correlates the expression of SGPP2 with ceramide synthase 1 (CERS1) and ceramide synthase 4 (CERS4) levels. While CERS1 and CERS4 have been known to shift the substrate of the salvage pathway, sphingosine towards C16-C20 (short/long ceramides)75,76, previous reports suggest that this upregulation may result in a reciprocal reduction in the levels of very-long chain ceramide species77. Moreover, DKC1 knockdown results in reduced sphingosine and Hex2Cer(d18:1/24:1) levels, a glycosylated product of very-long chain fatty acid ceramides, indicating that the salvage pathway is operative and CERS2 activity is maintained in DKC1 high tumors. This complex interplay largely recapitulates the physiological consequences of altered long-chain/very-long chain ceramides ratios having opposite effects in breast and colon cancer29,78,79. Furthermore, our integrative analysis revealed the presence of lactosylceramide and galactosylceramides, indicating their pro-survival roles in CRC. Other complex sphingolipids, such as ceramides, exhibit a pro-apoptotic effect80, however, ceramides of varying chain lengths confer diverse effects in cancer predisposition28. Of note, we found an accumulation of C24 Ceramide in tumor specimens of DKC1-positive patients, signifying its possible role in evading programmed cell death81. Previously, DKC1 has been shown to regulate the intrinsic ribosomal translational activity in immortalized breast epithelial cells82. In corroboration with this, our findings also highlight the critical role of DKC1 in enhanced protein synthesis, hence opening avenues for an in-depth investigation of its impact on CRC progression.

Impaired ceramide metabolism is unequivocally known to contribute to multidrug resistance. Ceramides have implications in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and increased oxidative stress by signaling mechanisms such as special cell membrane rafts, providing an important response to mediate the crosstalk between ceramide and redox signaling83. Long and very-long-chain pro-inflammatory ceramides, such as C24, have been shown to induce mitochondrial activity mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation84. Corroborating this, our data also reveal DKC1-mediated dysregulation of lipid metabolism, resulting in higher C24 ceramides levels and ROS generation. Notably, targeting ceramide metabolism can reverse the therapeutic resistance in cancer. Specifically, ceramide glycosylation by the enzyme glucosylceramide synthase (GCS), which forms the noncytotoxic metabolite glucosylceramide, has been noted in some multidrug-resistant cell lines that may be an important pathway for bypassing apoptosis85. Thus, targeting ceramide metabolism can help in enhancing tumor clearance by routine therapeutics.

Previously, DKC1 has been reported as a biomarker for late-stage colorectal cancer86. However, lipid-based plasma biomarkers are more reliable and non-invasive26, here, we offer very-long-chain ceramides such as C23 and C24 as potential lipid-based biomarkers for DKC1-positive CRC. However, we do not neglect the probability of finding other ceramide and sphingomyelin species for further clinical validation in a larger cohort.

Cancer relapse after administration of first-line therapy is the major roadblock in the management of cancer patients. In our study, high DKC1 is associated with stemness and stem-like/transit-amplifying phenotype, which is well-characterized in conferring therapy resistance in cancer87,88. Notably, FOLFIRI has demonstrated efficacy against CRC subtypes characterized by stem-like or transit-amplifying phenotypes. However, this class exhibits a differential response to cetuximab and has been further stratified into two distinct subclasses, namely cetuximab-sensitive and -resistant40. Furthermore, WNT pathway inhibition elicited a limited therapeutic response, particularly in therapy-resistant colorectal cancer. This phenomenon reflects both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms frequently encountered in clinical settings, especially among CRC patients unresponsive to first-line regimens. Although WNT pathway resistance does not universally define first-line therapy failure, WNT-driven stemness and prosurvival programs have been implicated in resistance to FOLFIRI and FOLFOX regimens in the clinic53. Taken together, our findings highlight DKC1 inhibition as a plausible treatment option for overcoming chemotherapy resistance in CRC patients. Hence, targeting DKC1 along with WNT signaling could provide a better treatment strategy in patients with therapy-resistant as well as stem-like/transit amplifying features.

Conclusively, this study unraveled the molecular mechanism associated with DKC1-mediated CRC tumorigenesis and chemoresistance towards FOLFOX/FOLFIRI treatment regimen (Fig. 7). We provide an insight into the DKC1 potentiating oncogenic and stem cell-like phenotype in CRC. The association of DKC1-positive tumors with CMS2 classification highlights the clinical significance in predicting patients’ prognosis and response to adjuvant therapies. Lastly, our findings indicate the clinical utility of specific very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides as lipid-based biomarkers in DKC1-positive CRC patients. We demonstrate the role of DKC1 in orchestrating first-line chemoresistance and provide the pre-clinical validation of DKC1 and WNT inhibition as a combinatorial therapeutic strategy, thus providing a compelling rationale for targeting the DKC1/WNT axis in chemotherapy non-responding CRC patients.

Human Colorectal Carcinoma patient specimens

Surgically resected colorectal cancer specimens and paired adjacent normal tissue samples were collected from King George’s Medical University, Lucknow, India; Regency Hospital, Kanpur, India. Ethical clearance were obtained from the respective Institutional Ethics Committees and Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur (81st ECM II-A/P6; RHL-IEC-16015, and IITK/IEC/2020-21/II/32, respectively). For CRC specimens collected from the ACTREC, Tata Memorial Center (TMC), approval was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee III (IEC III, reference no. 900912). Counseling followed by informed consent was obtained from each patient before the surgical procedure.

For metabolic experiments, fresh CRC samples were collected and snap frozen using liquid nitrogen. Patient tissue microarray (TMA) slides (CO1502) comprising colorectal cancer specimens (N = 75) with two cores for each patient were procured from Tissuearray.com LLC.

H-score-based IHC scoring quantification of CRC patient TMAs

Staining intensities of all CRC specimens were measured using ImageJ and quantified using the H-score method89. For each sample, two cores with approximately four representative fields were selected, and the following parameters were measured: total area, % area, integrated density, and mean gray values. Normalized % area was calculated for each representative field. Staining intensity categories were defined based on the mean gray value, wherein lower mean gray values corresponded to higher staining intensity. Next, the samples were assigned scores of 3 (high), 2 (medium), 1 (low), or 0 (negative) using quartile-based distribution thresholds. A weighted score was calculated by multiplying the normalized percent area by the corresponding intensity score. The weighted H-score across all the fields was summed to generate an overall H-score for all the samples. For subsequent analysis, patients were stratified into three intensity groups based on final H-scores: low (0–75), medium (76–225), and high (226–300).

Survival analysis

To evaluate the association between DKC1 expression and overall survival in colorectal cancer patients, the TCGA–Colon Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COAD) dataset was retrieved from the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net). R packages Survival, Survminer, and SurvMisc were used for survival data analysis, cutoff optimization was done according to the Cox proportional hazard regression model and a Kaplan-Meier curve. Statistical significance was measured using the log-rank test.

Differential expression analysis of patient transcriptome data

Publicly available gene expression data for primary tumor samples (n = 1644) in colorectal cancer microarray datasets (GSE2109, GSE13067, GSE13294, GSE14333, GSE17536, GSE20916, GSE33113, GSE35896, and GSE39582) from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were subjected to normalization using single sample frozen RMA method90 from ‘frma’ package R/Bioconductor. These datasets were next subjected to outlier sample detection for quality assessment using ‘arrayQualityMetrics’ package from R91. The outliers were excluded based on MA plots, pairwise sample distances, and gene expression value distribution. The Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) score was calculated for every probe in the remaining samples (n = 1538). While converting probes to gene symbols, duplicate symbols were removed by keeping the probe with the highest MAD score and rejecting others for each gene. Next, samples were subjected to quartile classification based on the expression of DKC1 and the first and fourth quartiles were designated as DKC1-high and DKC1-low cases (n = 770). ‘limma’ package from R/Bioconductor was used to identify differentially expressed genes between DKC1-high and DKC1-low patients. Genes with log2(fold change) ≥ 1 were designated as upregulated, and genes with log2(fold change) ≤ − 1 were called downregulated. Genes showing a positive association with DKC1 expression were subjected to functional annotation clustering analysis using The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)92, and Gene Ontology analysis was performed to analyze upregulated biological processes and pathways.

CCLE data analysis

Publicly available expression data for DKC1 was downloaded from the DEPMAP database Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (https://depmap.org/portal/). Chronos and CERES scores for DKC1 were also retrieved from DEPMAP and plotted in GraphPad Prism 7.0.

Cell culture and generation of engineered colorectal cancer cell lines

Colo320HSR cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and WiDr cells were obtained as a generous gift from Dr. Eric Fearon’s laboratory at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, USA. SW620, L-Wnt3a, and LM cells were obtained from the cell line repository at the National Center for Cell Sciences, Pune, Maharashtra, India. Colo320HSR cells were maintained in RPMI1640 media, whereas WiDr and SW620 cells were maintained in MEM and Leibovitz’s L-15 medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.5% Penicillin/Streptomycin and grown in culture conditions as per ATCC recommendations. MSIE and YAMC cells were generously gifted by Prof. Robert H Whitehead, Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research, Melbourne. Both the murine epithelial cells were cultured and maintained as per the conditions described previously42. All cell lines were validated by STR profiling (DNA Forensics Laboratory, New Delhi; DNA Labs India, Hyderabad, India; Recombinant DNA facility, ACTREC, Navi Mumbai, India) and routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the PlasmoTest mycoplasma detection kit (InvivoGen).

Lentiviral packaging was performed using ViraPower Lentiviral Expression System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. For stable silencing of DKC1, human lentiviral short-hairpin RNA-targeting DKC1 (shDKC1) (Cat# VB170928-1264dkz, Cat# VB170928-1281mcr) and non-targeting scrambled short-hairpin RNA (shSCRM) were cloned into pLV vectors (Cyagen Biosciences). For establishing genetically-modified doxycycline-inducible shRNA-mediated knockdown of DKC1, SW620 cells were transduced with commercially available Tet-On TRIPZ inducible lentiviral shRNA. Lentiviral particles were generated using a non-silencing control vector (Cat# RHS4743) and a plasmid harboring DKC1 targeting sequence (Cat# RHS4696-201901845). Stable cells were maintained under selection pressure with 1 μg/mL puromycin. For generating a stable SGPP2 knockdown, a pLKO vector containing the SGPP2 targeting sequence was used, while for ectopic SGPP2-expressing cell line, the SGPP2 cDNA cloned into the pLV vector was considered. Stable cells transfected with vector backbone (EV) (Cat# VB230128-1150etb), and shDKC1 with ectopic expression of SGPP2 (VB230124-1164gak) were maintained under selection pressure using 2.5 μg/mL puromycin and 5 μg/mL blasticidin. We acknowledge the shRNA consortium (Dr. Subba Rao), IISc Bangalore, India, for providing shRNA constructs against SGPP2.

Protein translation assay

Global protein translation was examined using the Click-iT™ Protein Synthesis Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, shSCRM and shDKC1 SW620 cells (~ 40,000) were grown on a coverslip in a 4-well culture dish, and were placed in a methionine-free medium the next day, after serum starvation for 4 h. L-homopropargylglycine (HPG) was added in a 1:1000 dilution, and incubated for 30 mins. Cells were washed with PBS, and fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde, followed by 15 mins incubation at room temperature. Subsequently, the Click-iT reaction was performed, followed by HPG detection using Alexa Fluor® 488. Images were acquired using confocal microscopy (Nikon, AX/AXR) and percent positive GFP cells were quantified using Fiji software.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Real-time expression of genes was analyzed using quantitative PCR as per the established protocol93,94. Briefly, RNA was isolated from the colorectal cancer cells using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Life Technologies) as per the instructions provided by the manufacturer, and 1 μg of RNA was converted to cDNA using Random primers. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR mastermix (Genetix Biotech Asia Pvt. Ltd) on the QuantStudio Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences are provided in the Supplementary Data 9.

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA was isolated as described above and exported to Macrogen Inc. (South Korea). Quality of RNA was assessed using 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), followed by library preparation (using Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library method) and paired-end sequencing on NovaSeq with a 150 bp read length, and more than 25 M reads were recorded for each sample. Samples were subjected to differential expression analysis using DESeq295 to identify dysregulated genes (> or < 0.6 log2(fold-change) and adjusted p-value < 0.05). This gene list was subjected to functional annotation clustering-based Gene Ontology analysis using The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)92.

For performing the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)96, samples were normalized, and the batch correction was performed using Combat in R. Genesets with Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) of > 1 or < − 1 were considered significant, with GSEA being run with signal-to-noise-ratio as per the default parameters.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed as per the established protocol97. Briefly, radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer with Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail Set-II (Calbiochem) and protease inhibitor (VWR) was used to prepare whole cell protein lysates. Protein quantification was performed using BCA, and proteins was separated on SDS-PAGE followed by transfer to PVDF membrane (GE Healthcare). Next, the membrane was incubated with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for an hour at room temperature. Antibody exposure was provided at the respective dilutions in TBS-T containing 2% non-fat dry milk: 1:1000 diluted DKC1 (CST, 53234), Caspase-3 (CST, 9662), Cleaved PARP (CST, 9541), γH2AX (CST, 9718), SOX2 (Abcam, ab97959), SGPP2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-42767), phospho-β-catenin (CST, 4176), Histone-3 (CST, 14269), Alpha-Tubulin (Abcam, Ab-7291), Bcl-xL (CST, 2764S), CD44 (CST, 3570S), Lamin A (Abcam, Ab-26300), 1:3000 diluted β-catenin (CST, D10A8) and 1:5000 diluted β-actin (Abcam, ab6276) followed by overnight incubation at 4˚C. Post incubation, the antibody was removed, and the membrane was washed with 0.1% TBS-T and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with respective secondary anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (1:5000, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Cat # 115-035-003 and Cat # 111-035-144). Again, the membrane was washed with 0.1% TBS-T, and the signal was captured using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell proliferation assay

Isogenic shSCRM control and shDKC1 cell lines for SW620, WiDr cells (2 × 103), and Colo320HSR (1 × 103) cells were plated in a 96-well plate, and a proliferation assay was performed. The media was replenished every alternate day. Resazurin or WST-1 was added to the cells, and fluorescence intensity was measured at the indicated time points at 530–590 nm excitation-emission using the SpectraMax® Mini Multi-Mode Microplate Reader. The relative cell proliferation rate was plotted. For experiments with WNT inhibitor, media was supplemented with XAV939 (MedChemExpress, HY-15147) and replaced every 24 h. For doxycycline induced DKC1 silencing experiment, SW620 shSCRM and shDKC1 cells were pretreated with doxycycline (MedChemExpress, HY-N0565B; 1.5 µg/mL) and seeded in a 96-well culture dish. Serum starvation of cells was done for 4–6 h before dox treatment. After 24 h, treatment was repeated, and cells were harvested 48 h after the initial induction. RNA and protein were extracted from the Dox-induced cells to confirm the DKC1 knockdown. For shSCRM and shDKC1 HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R proliferation assay, 2 × 103 cells per well were seeded in a 96-well culture dish. The method as mentioned above, was used for proliferation assays.

Flow cytometry

For cell cycle analysis, 1 × 106 cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 70% ethanol. Subsequently, they were stained with propidium iodide (BioLegend, Cat # 421301) as per the manufacturer’s protocol before acquisition. Cell cycle distribution was assessed using an in-built univariate model of FlowJo software. The gating strategy has been provided in Supplementary Fig. 11. The flow cytometry experiment was performed on the BD Accuri™ C6 Plus Flow Cytometer and the acquired data was analyzed using FlowJo v.10.6.1.

Immunofluorescence

2 × 104 cells were plated per coverslip per well of 4-well cell culture dishes. The next day, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in the dark. Cells were washed post-fixation, permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, and washed with 0.1% Tween-20 containing PBS (PBS-T). Subsequently, 5% normal goat serum (NGS) in PBS-T was used for masking and was removed an hour later followed by washing with PBS-T. Antibodies against DKC1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-28922), SGPP2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-42767), γH2AX (Santacruz, sc517348) was diluted (1:50) in 2% NGS in PBS-T and cells were incubated in it overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the primary antibody was removed, and cells were washed with PBS-T to remove the non-specifically bound antibody. Cells were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with Alexa-fluor-555 conjugated secondary antibody against mouse heavy chain (dilution 1:600) in 2% NGS in PBS-T. Next, the secondary antibody was washed with PBS-T, and the cells were incubated with Hoechst nuclear staining dye (5 μg/mL) for 45 mins. Cells were washed and coverslips were mounted on slides (Vectashield) followed by imaging using Carl Zeiss Axio Z1 observer epifluorescence microscope with an Apotome device attached.

Tumor sphere assay

Tumor sphere assay was performed as per the protocol described earlier98. Briefly, cells were plated in an ultra-low attachment cell-culture dish (Corning) at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well and cultured in DMEM-F12 serum-free medium supplemented with growth factors (EGF and FGF), Insulin, and B-27 (all these from Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were allowed to grow, and spheres formed were dismantled by pipetting every third day. The experiment was terminated after three weeks, and spheres were counted and imaged using the brightfield microscope (Leica). The diameter (d) of spheres was quantified using ImageJ, and the surface area was calculated using the formula πd2. Post-image acquisition, RNA was isolated from these tumor spheres for the quantification of stemness markers.

Chemosensitivity assay

SW620 control (shSCRM) and DKC1 knockdown (shDKC1) cells were plated (5 × 103) in a 96-well dish. Cells were treated with 5-Fluorouracil (MedChemExpress, HY-90006) in a range of 400 μM to 0.39 μM administered for 48 h. For HCT116 parental and chemoresistant derivatives cell lines (3 × 103 per well) were seeded. Treatment was done for up to 3 days with varying 0.1 µM Pyrazofurin (MedChemExpress, HY-122502) and 10 µM XAV939, alone and in combination. Media containing the drug was replenished after 48 hours. SCRM control and DKC1-silenced HCT116 (5-FU + Ox)R cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate, and treated with 5-FU, Oxaliplatin (MedChemExpress, HY-17371) and combination (5-FU+Oxaliplatin) at varying concentrations ranging from 0.1 µM to 10 µM. The drug-containing medium was replenished every other day, and the experiment was terminated after 96 h of treatment. At the endpoint, Resazurin was added, and fluorescence intensity was measured at 530–590 nm excitation and emission using the Synergy Z4 fluorescence reader (Biotek). The data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0, and IC50 values of the drugs in the cell lines were calculated with the Non-linear regression method.

Foci formation assay

For the foci formation assay, 2 × 103 cells were plated in a six-well culture dishes in cell line-specific recommended culture media supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C. The media was changed on alternate days. For assessing the effect of WNT inhibitor on foci formation ability, media supplemented with XAV939 was used and replaced on alternate days. The assay was terminated after 10 days, and the culture dish containing foci were stained with crystal violet solution (0.05% w/v). For quantification, foci were destained using 10% glacial acetic acid solution, and absorbance was measured at 550 nm.

Mice xenograft studies

NOD.CB17-Prkdcscid/J (NOD/SCID) mice were commercially purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (RRID:IMSR_JAX:001303), and athymic NU-Foxn1nu nude mice were commercially purchased from Hylasco Biotechnology (India) Pvt. Ltd. (licensee of Charles River Laboratory). The experiment was performed as per the standard procedure, and the guidelines of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA) were strictly followed. Regulatory standards of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur were precisely maintained during the experiment, including a maximum tumor burden of less than 1500 mm3. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection using a Ketamine (50 mg/kg) and Xylazine (2.5 mg/kg) cocktail. For in vivo assessment of constitutive DKC1 knockdown, SW620 shSCRM control, shDKC1−2, and shDKC1−3 cells were trypsinized, and 1.5 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 0.9% saline containing 15% Matrigel and subcutaneously implanted into the flanks of 4-6 weeks old NOD/SCID male mice (N = 6 for each type). For temporal in vivo silencing of DKC1 in the mice xenografts, doxycycline-inducible SW620 shSCRM and shDKC1 cells were used. Briefly, 1.5 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 0.9% saline containing 30% Matrigel for subcutaneous implantation into the flanks of 4–6 weeks old female athymic NU-Foxn1nu nude mice. Once the tumor volume reached ~ 40 mm3, mice were randomized (N = 5 per group) and treated with either vehicle control or doxycycline (MedChemExpress, HY-N0565B) during the treatment period. Doxycycline (10 mg/kg) was dissolved in a vehicle solution containing 30% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and administered every third day via the intraperitoneal route. To maintain a sustained transgene induction, doxycycline (2 mg/mL) supplemented with 5% sucrose was provided in regular drinking water and replenished every 5 days for the duration of the study.

To check the DKC1-mediated chemoresistance in mice model, shSCRM and shDKC1 HCT116-(5-FU + Ox)R cells (2 × 106) were subcutaneously implanted in 6–8 weeks old male and female NOD/SCID mice as described above. Once the tumor volume reached ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomized (N = 5 per group) and treated with either vehicle control or a combination of 5-FU (MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-90006) and Oxaliplatin (MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-17371). The drugs were resuspended in a vehicle solution containing 30% PEG300 and 5% Tween-80. Mice were treated with 5-FU (30 mg/kg) twice a week and Oxaliplatin (2.5 mg/kg) once a week via intraperitoneal injection.

For dual targeting of DKC1 and WNT signaling in FOLFOX-resistant cell line, HCT116-(5-FU+Ox)R cells were implanted in 6–8 weeks old male CD1 Nude mice (Crl: CD1-Foxn1nu) as described99. Once the tumor volume reached ~ 50 mm3, mice were randomized into four groups (N = 6 per group) and treated with the vehicle control, Pyrazofurin (1 mg/kg) treated, XAV939 (10 mg/kg) treated, and combination (Pyrazofurin + XAV939). Both drugs were administered twice a week via intraperitoneal injection for the entire treatment period. For all experiments, tumor burden was monitored every alternate day using a digital Vernier Caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (π/6) (L × W2), (L = length; W = width). All mice used in the study were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility as per the institutional guidelines. The ambient temperature of the mice facility room was maintained at 20–24 °C with ~ 40–60% humidity and a 12:12 h light–dark cycle.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Tumors were first fixed in 10% buffered formalin, paraffinized, and 3μm sections were taken using a microtome (Leica). Tumor xenograft slides were incubated at 60 °C for 2 h followed by antigen retrieval in a PT Link instrument (Agilent DAKO, PT200) using EnVision FLEX Target Retrieval Solution, High pH (Agilent DAKO, K800421-2) for DKC1 slides and EnVision FLEX Target Retrieval Solution, Low pH (Agilent DAKO, K800521-2) for Ki67 slides. 1X EnVision FLEX Wash Buffer (Agilent DAKO, K800721-2) was used to wash slides, followed by treatment with Peroxidazed 1 (Biocare Medical, PX968M) and Background Punisher (Biocare Medical, BP974L) for 5 mins and 10 mins respectively with wash after each step. Immunostaining was performed using antibodies against DKC1 (Abcam, ab156877; Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-28922), SGPP2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-42767), Ki67 (clone MIB-1, DAKO M7240) and Caspase-3 (CST,9662) overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, slides were washed and incubated in Mach2 Doublestain 1 (DKC1 slides) (Biocare Medical, MRCT523L) or Mach2 Doublestain 2 (Ki67 slides) (Biocare Medical, MRCT525L) for 30 mins at room temperature, rinsed in 1X EnVision wash buffer 3 times, and treated with a Betazoid DAB solution (Biocare Medical, BDB2004H) for 5 mins. Next, slides were rinsed in distilled water followed by treatment with EnVision FLEX Hematoxylin (Agilent DAKO, K800821-2). After rinsing in tap water, slides were dried completely and then dehydrated using xylene. EcoMount (Biocare Medical, EM897L) was added to each slide, which was then mounted with coverslips.

The quantification was performed by calculating the integrated density for 15 random histological fields using the IHC Toolbox plugin of ImageJ software. For Ki67 positive nuclei quantification, a ratio of Ki67 positive to total nuclei was calculated for 10–15 random histological fields using Fiji’s H&DAB color deconvolution or ImageJS Ki-67 online module100.

Patient tissue microarray (TMA) slides (CO1502, Tissuearray.com LLC) were subjected to immunohistochemical staining as per the mentioned protocol. Except for SGPP2 IHC, where the TMA slide was incubated with an antibody against SGPP2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-42767) overnight at 4 °C. The SGPP2 slide was washed and incubated in Mach2 Doublestain 1 for 30 mins at room temperature. For DKC1 staining of TMA, the same protocol was followed as mentioned above.

In silico analysis for CMS classification

The count data for RNA-seq for COADREAD samples were obtained from TCGA via the GDC portal using TCGA biolinks R package. Raw counts were normalized to log2 counts per million (log2 CPM) with a pseudo-count of 1 added to avoid log-transform issues. CMS subtype gene signature was defined based on the published classification by Guinney et al. 15. Four CMS subtypes (CMS1–4) were defined using representative gene sets expanded to ~ 20–25 genes per subtype to capture key biological processes relevant to each molecular class. Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was performed on the normalized expression matrix using the GSVA R package, calculating enrichment scores for each CMS gene signature per sample. Each sample was assigned a CMS subtype corresponding to the gene set with the highest enrichment score. DKC1-high and DKC1-low patients were subjected to Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) for up- and downregulated CMS signatures derived from the CMS classification15 or a smaller signature proposed for the same as the ‘ColoType’38. For proteomics data analysis, CPTAC proteomics data for colorectal cancer was utilized to assess different parameters.

Single-cell expression data analysis

Single-cell expression matrices and metadata were downloaded from GEO datasets GSE132465 and GSE144735. The normalized expression data and metadata were combined into an anndata object using Scanpy. Further analysis and visualizations, such as dot plots, were done using Scanpy functions. The metadata from the three independent datasets GSE132465, GSE144735, and GSE200997 were downloaded from GEO. The counts data and metadata were used to create the three Seurat objects for the three datasets, respectively. Using a sum of aggregates approach, counts from individual cells in Seurat objects were aggregated to form Pseudo Bulk. The native Seurat function AggregateExpression was used to create Pseudo Bulk samples. The counts data and metadata exported from Pseudobulks were used to create three independent DESeq2 datasets. Meta-analysis was performed using a custom script based on the metafor package. The forest plots were created using ggplot2.

siRNA transfection

Cells were plated to ~50% confluence in 6-well plates and 30pmol of siRNA against CTNNB1 (siCTNNB1) or non-targeting siRNA (siNT) (Dharmacon; Cat#D-001810-10-05) were transfected using XtremeGENE™ siRNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, Merck) as per manufacturer’s instructions (for SW620, 150pmol siRNA was used). Cells were transfected again after 24 h, and protein or RNA was isolated post 36 h of the second transfection and were analyzed by qPCR and immunoblotting as mentioned above.

WNT3A stimulation

WNT3A-conditioned media was collected as per the ATCC recommendations. Briefly, L-Wnt-3A cells or control LM cells were plated at ~ 50–60% confluency, and two batches of the conditioned media were collected on the fourth and seventh day, processed, and stored at 4 °C. The target benign colonic murine cells and organoids were serum starved for 4–6 h, followed by supplementation with 50% conditioned medium. The media was replaced every 24 h and the experiment was terminated after 60 h. since the start of the treatment. Cells were harvested and immunoblot assay was performed to confirm signaling stimulation.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation

Subcellular fractionation of cell line and patient-derived organoids was performed as per the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78833) manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were trypsinized, washed with chilled PBS, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 mins. The cell pellet was then incubated in a chilled CER I and CER II buffer (provided with the kit), vortexed, and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected in a fresh tube and used as a cytosolic fraction. The remaining pellet was resuspended in a chilled NER buffer, vortexed intermittently (15 sec pulses) for 40 mins. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 16000 × g for 10 mins, 4 °C, and the nuclear fraction was used.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Sphingosine-1 phosphate levels were measured using ELISA as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Universal SIP ELISA kit, Maker: ImmunoTag, G bioscience, ITLK09484). Briefly, cells in 100 mm dishes were lysed using a buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) along with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysis was followed by centrifugation at 15000 × g at 4 °C for 10 mins. The supernatant was collected, and the protein amount was estimated using the bicinchoninic acid assay and was incubated in 96-well strips pre-coated with antibody against S1P, followed by incubation with biotinylated antibody. Next, Streptavidin-HRP solution was used, followed by TMB substrate. The reaction was stopped using ‘Stop solution’, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. S1P levels normalized to protein expression were used for the final assessment. For C24 Ceramide, an indirect ELISA was done following the standard Abcam protocol. C24 ceramide (Sigma-Aldrich-860524P) was coated in the wells overnight. Standard curve was set up using a serial dilution of C24 Ceramide antigen starting from 1 mM. Cell supernatants were processed using Folch’s method101 for lipid enrichment which were subsequently used to coat 96-well plates. A standard curve was set up using serial dilution of C24 Ceramide antigen starting from 1 mM. For the in vitro experiment, enriched lipids from shSCRM and shDKC1 lysed cells were used for coating the wells. Ceramide monoclonal antibody (C8104) was used as a primary antibody at 1:100 concentration. Secondary anti-mouse was used at a concentration of 1:1000. For in vivo experiment, mice tumor tissues were mechanically dissociated and homogenized using Qiagen TissueLyser, and lysates were subsequently extracted.

ChIP-sequencing data analysis

Publicly available ChIP-Seq data (GSE31477) for TCF7L2 in HCT116 cells was used to explore the regulation of DKC1 under the control of WNT signaling. Quality assessment for raw single-end reads was done using FASTQC, trimmed with FASTQ Trimmer, with default settings of the Galaxy web platform (usegalaxy.org). Reads were aligned to the reference genome (hg19) using Bowtie to generate Sequence Alignment/Map (SAM) files. Unaligned or unmapped reads were filtered using FilterSAM, a utility of SAMtools. The SAM files were converted to its Binary Alignment/Maps (BAM) files using SAMtools. Further, ChIP-Seq peaks for RNA PolII and TCF7L2 were called using Model-based analysis of ChIP-seq (MACS; P < 10−5) with default settings against Input. BAM and BED files obtained were visualized by Integrative Genome Browser (IGB).

Immunoprecipitation

SW620 cells were cultured in a 100 mm dish and were harvested at 90% confluence; cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed with ice-cold lysis buffer containing NP-40 (2%, ab142227, Abcam), Triton X-100 (2%), NaCl (100 mM), Tris-Cl (50 mM, pH 8.0), EDTA (1 mM), 1X protease inhibitor (Genetix) and 1X phosphatase inhibitor (524625, Millipore) and kept at 4 °C on rotor for 1 h, centrifuged at 15000 × g, 4 °C for 10 mins. The supernatant was incubated with 4 µg of primary DKC1 (Santacruz, sc-373956-X, mouse monoclonal antibody) or isotype control antibody (mouse IgG, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10400C) at 4 °C overnight. Simultaneously, protein A/G coated agarose beads (SantaCruz, sc-2003) were blocked with bovine serum albumin (HiMedia) followed by incubation at 4 °C overnight. Blocked beads were incubated for 24 h at 4 °C with the lysate to make antibody-bead conjugates. Next, these conjugates were washed and immunocomplex was eluted using elution buffer (2X dye containing SDS). For immunoblotting, anti-mouse or Veriblot (Abcam, ab131366) secondary antibodies were used.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation

ChIP experiments were performed as previously described102. Briefly, SW620 cells or SW620 scrambled control and DKC1 knockdown cells were plated in a 100 mm cell culture dish and were harvested at 90% confluence. First, cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 mins, quenched with 125 mM glycine, washed with PBS, and lysed using lysis buffer containing 1% SDS, Tris-Cl (50 mM, pH 8.0), EDTA (10 mM) and 1X protease inhibitor (Genetix) followed by DNA fragmentation through sonication to an average fragment length of ~ 500 bp using Bioruptor (Diagenode) and sheared chromatin was incubated at 4 °C overnight with 4 µg of primary β-catenin (CST, D10A8) or SOX2 (Abcam, ab97959), or isotype control antibodies (rabbit IgG, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10500C). Concomitantly, Protein G-coated Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were blocked with bovine serum albumin (HiMedia) and sheared salmon sperm DNA (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by incubation at 4 °C overnight. Blocked beads were incubated for 8 h. at 4 °C with the lysate to make antibody-bead conjugates. Next, these conjugates were washed and the immunocomplex was eluted using elution buffer [1% SDS, 100 mM NaHCO3, Proteinase K (Sigma-Aldrich), and RNase A (500 µg/ml each) (Sigma-Aldrich)]. Phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction method was used to isolate DNA from the immunocomplex. JASPAR and MatInspector softwares were used to predict binding sites for β-catenin/TCF complex on the DKC1 promoter or SOX2 on the SGPP2 promoter.

Lipidomics analysis

To analyze the global lipid profiles, patient tumor specimens were homogenized in 150 mM Ammonium bicarbonate solution in a 1:10 ratio (w/v, mg/µL) with a TissueLyser system, 2 cycles for 2 mins at 28 Hz frequency at 4 °C with one 5 mm stainless steel beads, followed by sonication for lysis. Following this, 200 µL of each tissue homogenate was taken from every sample, and UHPLC-grade methanol was added to it in a 1:5 ratio (v/v, µL/µL) and snap frozen using liquid nitrogen stored at -80 °C. Similarly, for SW620 control and DKC1 knockdown cells, ~ 15 million cells were used in triplicate. The cells were detached using trypsin, neutralized and the supernatant was removed and resuspended in 150 mM Ammonium bicarbonate. Next, lysis was performed using sonication and protein concentration was estimated directly for each homogenate using the BCA method. Cells were again centrifuged, and supernatant was removed, fixed with 100 μl of 100% MeOH, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and immediately stored at -80 °C. The samples were shipped to the Singapore Lipidomics Incubator, National University of Singapore, where they were processed further for downstream analysis.

Briefly, 100 μl chilled butanol containing internal standards was added to the samples, vortexed for 2 mins, shaken, and sonicated at 4 °C for 30 mins, respectively. Lysates were centrifuged twice at 18000 g, 4 °C for 10 mins, and the supernatant (BuMe) was collected for LC-MS analysis. For UHPLC: Mobile phase A: 40% acetonitrile + 60% water + 10 mM ammonium formate, and Mobile phase B: 10% acetonitrile + 90% isopropanol + 10 mM ammonium formate with Column: Poroshell 120 C18 (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.9 µm) were used at a column temperature of 40 °C. The LC gradient information is provided in the Supplementary Table 1.

The positive ionization spray voltage and nozzle voltage were set at 3000 V and 1000 V, respectively. The drying gas and sheath gas temperatures were both maintained at 250 °C. The drying gas and sheath gas flow rates were 14 L/min and 11 L/min, respectively. The nebulizer nitrogen gas flow rate was set at 35 psi. The iFunnel high and low-pressure RF were 150 V and 60 V, respectively. Quantification data was extracted using Agilent MassHunter Quantitative Analysis (QQQ) software. The data was manually checked to ensure that the software integrated the right peaks.

QC filtering is based on raw peak areas for all the measured lipid species using the (pooled) TQC samples. The filtering criteria were as follows:

Low Signal variation (CoV < 20%);

High Sample/Blank ratio (> 10);

Good Signal linearity (R2 > 0.8).

The lipid species that didn’t pass QC filtering are marked as “FAILED” and excluded from the downstream analysis.

Using lipid class-specific standards, the relative concentration of an analyte in the sample is calculated as shown in Eq. (1):

cAnalyte: Molar [M or µmol/µL] concentration of the analyte in the sample,

AreaAnalyte: Peak area of Analyte,

AreaISTD: Peak area of ISTD,

VISTD mix: Extraction solvent volume (ISTD mixture)

Sample Amount: Quantity of the sample (e.g. µL or mg)

cISTD: Concentration of the ISTD in the extraction solvent (ISTD mixture)

Multivariate analysis for statistical significance

The false discovery rate for lipidomics data was calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure applied to the list of ranked raw p-values. To ensure valid monotonicity, a cumulative minimum correction was applied. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Projection of Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis (OPLSDA), providing Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores for dysregulated lipid species were performed using ‘Statistical analysis (one factor)’ module of Metaboanalyst v5.0103.

Integrative pathway analysis

Differential genes and lipids obtained from RNAseq and UHPLC-MS/MS analysis for scrambled control and DKC1-silenced SW620 cells were subjected to the Joint-pathway analysis module of Metaboanalyst v5.0103. Enrichment analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test, and degree centrality was used as the topology measure to combine queries. The analysis was performed to identify significantly impacted metabolic pathways.

Biomarker analysis

The lipid profile of CRC patient tumors was used to identify biomarkers using the Biomarker Analysis module of Metaboanalyst v5.0103. The data was Pareto-scaled for normalization. Classical univariate analysis was performed to generate a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and compute the area under the curve for different ceramides. ROC curve-based model evaluation (Tester) module was used to assess the effectiveness of proposed ceramides as biomarkers for DKC1-High tumors.

Detection of reactive oxygen species and mitochondrial activity

Dye-based fluorescent staining was performed using DHE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D11347) and Mitotracker (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M22425) to detect the presence of ROS and mitochondria, respectively, inside the cells. Briefly, ~ 2.5×104 cells were seeded and grown on a cover slip in a 4-well culture dish. Once the cells get confluent and attain native morphology, the media was removed and the cover slips were gently rinsed with 1X PBS. DHE and Mitotracker dye were added at a dilution of 1:4000 and 1:2000, respectively. Stained cells were then incubated at room temperature for 30 mins in the dark, the residual dye was removed by washing with 1X PBS, and the cover slips were mounted on a glass slide using Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica-SP5). For quantification, mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified using ImageJ software.

Generation of HCT116 chemoresistant cell line

Chemoresistant cells were generated as described previously99. Briefly, on day 0, HCT116 cells were subjected to IC90 dosage (causing 90% inhibition) of the 5-FU, 5-FU + Iri (FOLFIRI), or 5-FU + Oxa (FOLFOX) treatment regimens, respectively. The drug was replenished every third day until day 9 for selecting the chemoresistant cells. The cells were then allowed to expand clonally in the drug-free media. On day 18, IC50 values were determined to confirm the chemoresistance.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs)

The patient-derived organoids were established using CRC biopsies obtained from Indian patients enrolled at ACTREC, TMC, Mumbai, using protocols described previously104. Briefly, mature organoids were collected in a T15 tube using cut tips and centrifuged at low speed (100–200 × g) for 5 mins. The pellet was resuspended in 500 μl of complete medium per well of a six-well plate. One well of a 96-well plate was divided into quadrants. 50 μl of the PDOs suspension was added to the wells. Organoids were viewed under the microscope and carefully counted. Organoid concentration was calculated by dividing the count by the volume used. The total sphere count was calculated by multiplying the concentration by the total volume.

Drug treatment of PDOs

Three-dimensional drug screening of PDOs was performed as described104. Briefly, mature organoids were counted and collected as per the procedure given above. 200 organoids were seeded per well in 96-well tissue culture plates, each comprising a solidified 20 μl Matrigel base layer and 90 μl of the PDO medium with 5% (by volume) Matrigel was added. In a separate 96-well plate, 16 wells were seeded similarly to generate day 0 seeding controls and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 overnight. For the control plate, 90 μl of the culture medium was added and assayed with Cell Titer Glo 3D (CTG-3D) to generate a seeding control value at the dosing time. A separate drug plate was prepared with an eight-point serial dilution of 90 μl of each condition in technical quadruplicate, to a seeded 96-well plate. Treated PDO plates were incubated for 96 h (37 °C and 5% CO2) and assayed with CTG-3D. Raw luminescent values from each condition were normalized to the day 0 seeding control value. Combination index was calculated using CompuSyn 1.0 software, which uses Chou and Talalay’s algorithm105.

Mice blood biochemical analysis

At the experiment endpoint, mice were anesthetized, and blood was collected through cardiac puncture in the clot activator tube, followed by refrigerated centrifugation at 1500 × g for 15 mins. Supernatant containing serum was collected and subjected to biochemical examination for liver and kidney toxicity studies using Siemens Dimension® EXL™ 200.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism 7.0 was utilized for statistical analysis. For all comparisons, two-tailed Student t-tests or One-Way ANOVA were used unless otherwise stated in the respective figures. FDR and p-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.
