Plant mediated synthesis of sustainable magnetite nano adsorbent for efficient cadmium (II) removal from aqueous media
Authors: Werku Aweke Teso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The need to eliminate hazardous heavy metals, such as cadmium (Cd (II)), has become increasingly evident. In this study, magnetite nanoparticles (Fe3O4-NPs) supported on teff straw and modified with a green approach were synthesized, characterized, and tested for their ability to remove Cd (II) ions. The characterization of the teff straw-supported Fe3O4-NPs was carried out using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), thermo gravimetric analysis (TGA), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Both FT-IR and TGA analysis confirmed effective surface functionalization of the Fe3O4-NPs. The XRD analysis confirmed the presence of magnetite in the synthesized teff straw-supported Fe3O4-NPs, with an average crystalline size of 18.83 nm. SEM analysis revealed that the Fe3O4-NPs were nearly spherical, with an average particle size of 23.85 nm. Adsorption studies were conducted to examine the adsorption of Cd(II) on the teff straw-supported magnetite nanoparticles and the influence of various adsorption parameters. The findings indicated that the solution pH had the greatest impact on the removal of Cd(II) ions. Optimal experimental conditions were found to be a pH of 6, contact time of 45 min, adsorbate concentration of 40 mg/L, and adsorbent amount of 1 g. Under these conditions, a maximum removal efficiency of 98.04% for Cd(II) was achieved. FT-IR analysis also showed that surface functional groups such as C = C, –OH, and C–C on the newly produced Fe3O4-NPs contributed to the enhanced removal of Cd(II) ions from the aqueous solution. The Langmuir model best described the adsorption isotherm data, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 101.01 mg/g. Therefore, teff straw-supported Fe3O4-NPs are an efficient and promising adsorbent for Cd(II) ions removal.

Introduction

The contamination of water by toxic heavy metals (HMs) has got great concern owing to their extreme toxicity, non-biodegradability, and carcinogenicity. The rapid advancement of urbanization and industrialization has made heavy metal contamination as a serious concern and a risk to the health of the population1,2 studied fossil fuel combustion as one of the causes of heavy metal release to the environment. These HMs contaminate the water, which then leads to severe toxicity. The toxicity of HMs is fatal to living things. The fatality of toxic HMs is caused due to the formation of free radicals that cause oxidative stress, damage of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), and main biological molecules like nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and enzymes3. Cd(II), recognized as one of the most toxic heavy metal ions in wastewater and aquatic systems, is predominantly discharged from industries such as oil refining, battery production, chloralkali manufacturing, paint production, paper and pulp processing, plastic manufacturing, cement production, gold mining, textiles, electronics, and rubber processing1.

Various treatment technologies, such as chemical precipitation, phytoremediation, membrane filtration, ion exchange, adsorption, electrochemical treatment, reverse osmosis, coagulation-flocculation, and biological decomposition, have been utilized to remove Cd(II)2,3. However, most of these techniques are costly, time-intensive, inefficient, and demand large quantities of chemicals and high energy to effectively remove Cd(II) from the aquatic environment4. As an illustration, chemical precipitation creates unwanted sludge, resulting in secondary pollution that requires additional treatment5. Researchers have also noted that membrane technology is highly effective in separating Cd(II) from water/wastewater; however, the process appears costly due to its high energy demands6. Aziz and co-workers reported coagulation/flocculation technique generates large sludge volume and uses non-reusable coagulants and flocculants7. Similarly, the ion-exchange method is challenging to remove concentrated heavy metal solutions and partially remove specified metal ions8. However, of all the methods listed, the adsorption technique, especially when using functionalized adsorbents, shows superior performance in removing heavy metals. It offers benefits such as the ability to regenerate adsorbents, high efficiency, low cost, and straightforward operational processes9.

Recently, the rapid progress in nanotechnology, particularly in nanoscale science and engineering, has created significant opportunities to develop simple, alternative, flexible, and environmentally friendly remediation methods. Nanomaterials, as part of this advancement, have garnered considerable attention. They offer promising solutions for the efficient removal of heavy metals in water and wastewater through the adsorption method, owing to their ease of surface modification, high adsorption capacity, and exceptional properties derived from the nanoscale effect10,11. Among nanometer-sized materials, metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) stand out for their remarkable potential in water treatment, particularly in effectively removing heavy metals and offering promising applications in the biomedical field.

Metal oxide nanoparticle adsorbents offer numerous benefits and are highly sought after. They are favored for their simple preparation methods, excellent stability, ease of engineering to desired sizes, surface functionalization capabilities, high adsorption efficiency, relatively large surface area, ability to shape control, and reusability12,13. Nano-sized metal oxide nanoparticle adsorbents exhibit high selectivity for the adsorbate. Commonly used metal oxide nanoparticles, including aluminium oxide, magnesium oxide, nano-sized iron oxides, and zinc oxide, are particularly selective towards heavy metal pollutants13,14. showed that the sensitivity of NPs chiefly relies on the morphology of the synthesized nanoparticle adsorbents. The sensitivity of metal oxide NPs is highly dependent on their shape and sizes than the compositions of NPs adsorbents. However, metal oxide NPs adsorbents have disadvantages such as oxidizing with oxygen and water in aqueous solutions (mainly bare metal oxide nanoparticle adsorbents), difficulty to separate from aqueous solutions effectively because of their small size, having aggregation property, having weak affinity to some organic pollutants/or adsorbates15,16.

Iron oxide NPs have good advantages in combating environmental contamination from the existing metal oxide NPs. Magnetite NPs (Fe3O4-NPs) are gaining significant attention in environmental remediation, moreover, in the removal of HMs owing to their exceptional characteristics viz., great biocompatibility, large surface area to volume ratio, high removal capacity, elevated reactivity, surface modifiability, ease of separation, high selectivity and small particle size which are promising to develop new or improve existing technologies in water/wastewater treatment17. Nevertheless, Fe3O4 NPs made through chemical and physical means result in the loss of reactivity towards the adsorbate, cause the risk of toxicity to the environment, are difficult for reusability, and the protocol uses very reactively and toxic reducing agents18. One of the explicit characteristics of nano-sized materials is a high atomic percentage of the nano-sized particles are on the surface. The surface atoms that are not saturated can link with extra atoms and hold enhanced chemical activity19. The other difficulties with the use of bare Fe3O4-NPs include; passivation/oxidation, corrosion by non-target compounds, rapid sedimentation that confines the mobility of fine-sized particles in water, and rapid aggregation of the smaller particles that reduce the performance in catching pollutants). For example, Qiao et al. fabricated superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs and reported that naked Fe3O4-NPs spontaneously aggregate and have limited applications20. Another study also showed bare Fe3O4-NPs have poor biodegradability, non-specific interaction, and high chemical instability21,22.

Therefore, to address the challenges associated with using Fe3O4 nanoparticles for the removal of Cd(II), there is a need for comprehensive research into safe and cost-effective methods for surface modification of Fe3O4 nanoparticles23,24. The synthesis of Fe3O4-NPs by the eco-friendly method is considerably simple and secure, and plant-mediated synthesis of NPs using polyphenol as a reducing agent is still a new scheme. The findings of18,25 demonstrated that plant mediated synthesis of metal oxide NPs adsorbents is considered a cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and feasible method for HMs removal. Therefore, using cheaper, and porous materials as supporting materials can tackle some problems of Fe3O4-NPs more26. These materials can be sourced from agricultural waste biomass. Locally available teff straw, as an agricultural byproduct, requires minimal processing, and alternative technologies can be employed to utilize it as a supportive material27,28. The integration of teff straw with Fe3O4 nanoparticles can significantly enhance the adsorption efficiency and physicochemical properties of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. However, research on the green synthesis of teff straw-supported Fe3O4 nanoparticles for use as nano-adsorbents remains limited. Therefore, utilizing polyphenol-based green-synthesized teff straw-supported Fe3O4 nanoparticles can address some of the inherent challenges of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, such as improving stability, reducing passivation and corrosion in acidic and basic environments, and minimizing or eliminating nanoparticle aggregation.

In this study, a functionalized teff straw-supported Fe3O4-NP was successfully synthesized using an eco-friendly synthesis method. Teff straw, an abundant but underutilized agricultural residue in Ethiopia, presents a sustainable and renewable surface-functionalization material distinct from commonly used rice or wheat straw. Owing to its rich silica, lignin, and hydroxyl functional groups, it enhances the stability and reactivity of magnetite nanoparticles. To the best of my knowledge, the application of teff straw for magnetite nanoparticle functionalization and cadmium removal has not been previously reported The synthesized adsorbent was systematically characterized. The adsorbent was then used to adsorb Cd(II) ions from an aqueous solution in batch mode. The effects of various parameters, such as pH, contact time, initial adsorbate concentration, and adsorbent dosage, on adsorption efficiency were explored. The mechanisms involved in the removal of Cd(II) were explained comprehensively.

Materials and methods

Preparation of teff straw porous material sample

The teff straws were collected from the agricultural farm field of Horro Guduru wollega Zone, Hareto Woreda, using plastic bags. The collected raw materials were reduced in size (1–5 mm) by cutting them into smaller pieces, cleaned several times with distilled water (H2O) to remove dust particles, and sun-dried followed by drying in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h to eliminate residual moisture and obtain a sample with constant mass. The dried teff straws were then ground into fine particles using an electric mill and sieved through a 0.1 mm sieve to eliminate unwanted components. The prepared porous material was stored in a closed plastic bag at room temperature until the next stage of the experiments.

Polyphenol preparation from plant extract

Fresh leaves of Aloe vera were collected from Horo Guduru Wollega Zone. The collected leaves were washed with tap water repeatedly to remove foreign particles and dirt, then rinsed with distilled water (H2O) for further purification. The leaves were then sun-dried for two weeks. The dried leaves were reduced in size and ground into a powder of 0.1–0.2 mm using an electric mill to prepare the leaf extract. After grinding, 15 g of Aloe vera leaf powder was added to 100 ml of double-distilled water and heated in a water bath at 60 °C for 30 min. Following vacuum filtration, the resultant extract containing polyphenols was further filtered using Whatman filter paper. The filtrate was collected and stored in a refrigerator at an adjusted temperature to prevent any degradation. The extract was then used as a reducing agent to synthesize magnetite and teff straw-supported Fe3O4-NPs.

Synthesis of magnetite nanoparticles

Fe3O4-NPs were prepared by the reduction of iron sulfate (FeSO4·7H2O) and iron chloride (FeCl3·6H2O) solution in the presence of Aloe vera polyphenol extract. Iron sulfate and iron chloride were used in a 3:1 w/w ratio as reported by29 A solution of iron sulfate (FeSO4·7H2O) and iron chloride (FeCl3·6H2O) was prepared by dissolving it in 200 mL of deionized water in a 500 mL beaker. Then, 10 mL of Aloe vera polyphenol extract was added to the mixture and stirred gently using a magnetic stirrer. A 2 M NaOH (99.8%) solution (ATDM, Turkey) was prepared and added dropwise to adjust the pH of the solution to 11. The mixture was stirred continuously until it turned black, indicating the formation of precipitates. The solution was left undisturbed, allowing the black precipitates to settle at the bottom of the beaker. The precipitates were separated by decanting the solution and then washed twice with deionized water. The mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 25 min, and the washing and centrifugation steps were repeated three times. Afterward, the precipitates were carefully transferred into an oven-dried Petri dish and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Finally, the synthesized Fe3O4 nanoparticles were stored properly for further characterization.

Synthesis of teff straw supported magnetite nanoparticles

Teff straw-assisted Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fe3O4-NPs) were synthesized by reducing a solution of iron sulfate (FeSO4·7H2O) and FeCl3·6H2O (99%) using Aloe vera polyphenol extract. The iron sulfate and FeCl3·6H2O were mixed in a 3:1 w/w ratio and dissolved in 200 mL of deionized water in a 500 mL beaker. To this solution, 2 g of ground teff straw and 15 mL of Aloe vera polyphenol extract were added, and the mixture was heated at 80 °C while gently stirring with a magnetic stirrer (equipped with a hot plate). A 2 M NaOH (99.8%) solution (ATDM, Turkey) was prepared and added dropwise until the pH of the solution reached 11. The solution was stirred continuously until it turned black, indicating the formation of the teff straw-assisted Fe3O4-NPs (TSA). The mixture was left to settle undisturbed, allowing the black precipitates to form at the bottom of the beaker. The precipitates were then isolated by decanting the solution and washing twice with deionized water. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min, and the centrifugation and washing steps were repeated three times. Finally, the precipitates were carefully transferred into a Petri dish and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The resulting adsorbent was labeled as Teff straw-assisted magnetite (Fe3O4-TSA) NPs and stored in a suitable place for further characterization and adsorption studies.

The synthesis of Fe3O4 NPs was confirmed by UV–visible spectral analysis. A small aliquot of the supernatant of the synthesized Fe3O4 or Fe3O4-TSA NPs was used for analysis purposes. The crystallite size and crystallinity of synthesized Fe3O4-TSA NPs were examined with XRD (X-ray diffraction,DW-XRD-Y7000) analysis. For this, well dried and powdered samples were used. It was operated at 30 kV and 25 mA with CuKα radiation (λ = 0.154021 nm). The scanning was done from 10 to 80° with steps of 0.03°s-1 to collect 2θ data. Phase and pattern analysis of the XRD data was studied using Match! Software version 3.10.2.173 and Origin Pro2019b version 9.65. The average crystalline size of Fe3O4-TSA NPs was calculated using Scherrer’s Eq. (1).

The surface functionalization of the synthesized Fe3O4-TSA NPs was determined with the help of FT-IR spectroscopy (Fourier transform infrared- spectroscopy, Perkins Elmer L1600300). The synthesized adsorbent was dried, ground, and mixed with KBr powder in appropriate ratios to make a pellet for FT-IR spectroscopy analysis before and after the adsorption experiments. These measurements were executed, and the spectra were collected in the range of 4000–400 cm-1. The structure and morphology of synthesized Fe3O4-TSA NPs were examined by Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (JCM-6000Plus). The acceleration voltage for the SEM was set at 15 kV. Origin Pro 2019b version 9.65 and ImageJ version 1.44 software were used to analyze the synthesized Fe3O4-TSA NPs further using the results of SEM analysis. Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis of Fe3O4-WSS NPs was executed for powder samples (~ 8 mg) with a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 25 °C up to 800 °C using Perkin-Elmer TGA-4000 TG in a nitrogen gas atmosphere. Adsorption experiments

A stock solution of 1 g/L Cd(II) was prepared by dissolving 1 g of analytical-grade cadmium chloride (CdCl2 (99%) (Finkem, Italy) in 1 L of deionized water (H2O). A series of diluted Cd(II) solutions (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mg/L) were used for experimental purposes. Various batch experiments were conducted using wheat straw-supported Fe3O4 nanoparticles (MNP-TSA) at different dosages (0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 1, and 1.3 g), initial pH values (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10), initial contact times (15, 35, 55, 75, and 95 min), and initial Cd(II) concentrations (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mg/L). All adsorption experiments were carried out at a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, with pH adjustments made using 0.1 M NaOH and 0.1 M HCl (36.46%) (LOBA Chemie, India). In each experiment, a specified dosage of MNP-TSA was added to the corresponding concentration of Cd(II) solution in a 250 mL conical flask. The solution volume for each experiment was 100 mL. The mixture was agitated at 250 rpm for varying contact times. After the specified contact time, each sample was centrifuged for 5–10 min, filtered through a membrane filter, and analyzed for Cd(II) residuals. The absorbance of Cd(II) residuals was measured using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 490 nm, and the Cd(II) concentration was determined based on the calibration curve. UV–visible spectrophotometer analysis of Cd(II) concentration

The residual concentration of Cd(II) was determined by creating a calibration curve, as described by30,31. A 1 g/L stock solution of Cd(II) was prepared by dissolving 1.253 g of analytical-grade CdCl₂ (99%) in 1 L of deionized water (DH₂O), along with 2 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃, 69.5%) (LOBA Chemie, India) in a volumetric flask. A series of diluted Cd(II) solutions (10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mg/L) were prepared from the stock solution. A 1,5-Diphenylcarbazide (98%) solution was prepared by dissolving 0.4 g of diphenylcarbazide in 100 mL of methanol (99.9%) (Merck, Germany) in a 150 mL volumetric flask. To each diluted Cd(II) solution, 1–1.5 mL of the diphenylcarbazide solution was added to produce a colored complex, and the pH of the mixture was adjusted to 5–6 using 0.1 M NaOH (99.8%) and HCl (36.46%) to minimize background interference during UV–Visible spectrophotometer analysis (only for calibration curve). Finally, the absorbance of each diluted Cd(II) solution was measured using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer at 490 nm. For each experimental batch, 1 mL of the prepared diphenylcarbazide solution was used for UV–Visible spectrophotometric analysis.

Experimental results on adsorption isotherm studies

The parameters of an isothermal equation that describes the adsorption isotherm typically express the affinity and surface property of the sorbent. The theoretical models, primarily the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Timken models, can be utilized to construct the adsorption isotherm. They research the connection between the concentration of solute still present in the solution and the sorbate on the surface of the sorbents, which is the number of species adsorbed per unit mass of sorbent. The correlation coefficients determine the adsorption isotherm’s potential for explaining the obtained data (R2). The adsorption data can be interpreted using the relationships which describe the distribution of Cd ion between aqueous solution and solid phases.

Determination of point of zero charge

A 0.010 M NaCl (99.5%) salt solution was prepared in a conical flask to determine the point of zero charge. A total of 0.10 g of MNP-TSA was mixed with 50 mL of the 0.010 M NaCl solution in nine separate conical flasks at ambient temperature (25 °C) and tightly sealed. The pH of the mixtures was adjusted to initial values of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 by adding a few drops of 0.01 M HCl or NaOH solution and agitating manually. The mixtures were allowed to stand for two days, and the final pH was carefully measured using a digital pH meter. A plot was constructed of the difference between the final pH and the initial pH versus the initial pH, and the pH at the point of zero charge (pHpzc) was determined as the intercept on the x-axis of the plot.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum analysis

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was used to identify surface functional groups and confirm functionalization of the synthesized Fe3O4-TSA nanoparticles. The FT-IR spectra (Fig. 1b) display characteristic peaks corresponding to various functional groups on the nanoparticle surface. The spectrum includes results from Aloe vera leaf extract (black curve) and teff straw powder (red curve). A broad absorption band in the region of 3660–3250 cm⁻1 is attributed to –OH stretching vibrations, which likely originate from phenolic compounds and cellulose components present in both the extract and the straw. Peaks observed at 1694 cm⁻1 and 1650 cm⁻1 are associated with C = C stretching vibrations typical of alkenes. The absorption range between 1250–1010 cm⁻1 corresponds to C–O stretching, indicating the presence of phenols, lignin, cellulose, alcohols, carboxylic acids, and ether groups. Additionally, the bands at 2921 cm⁻1 and 2909 cm⁻1 are assigned to C–H stretching in alkanes, while those at 610 cm⁻1 and 612 cm⁻1 suggest the presence of C–Cl bonds, indicating alkyl halide groups. These findings are in good agreement with previously reported literature of33, This confirms that Fe3O4 nanoparticles can be effectively functionalized and modified using biomass-derived materials.

Figure 2a displays the FT-IR spectrum of the biosynthesized Fe3O4-TSA nanoparticles. A broad absorption band between 3640 and 3200 cm⁻1, with a notable peak at 3456 cm⁻1, is attributed to –OH stretching vibrations originating from phenolic compounds and/or cellulose. The sharp peak at 1636 cm⁻1 corresponds to C = C stretching vibrations, while the distinct peak observed at 581 cm⁻1 is assigned to Fe–O bonds, confirming the presence of magnetite on the nanoparticle surface. Peaks at 2893 cm⁻1 and within the range of 1250–1010 cm⁻1 indicate C–H stretching and C–O stretching vibrations, respectively. These spectral features validate the successful functionalization of the magnetite nanoparticles and the strong interaction between teff straw-derived components and the Fe3O4.surface.Phenolic compounds play a dual role in nanoparticle synthesis — they not only act as reducing agents but also contribute to nanoparticle stabilization through capping. This is due to their hydroxyl functional groups, which exhibit strong binding affinity toward metal ions during nanoparticle formation34. Furthermore, the presence of these functional groups on the surface of the synthesized Fe3O4-TSA nanoparticles indicates that both Aloe Vera extract and teff straw played a stabilizing role. This stabilization helps prevent nanoparticle agglomeration and adhesion, thereby enhancing their effectiveness in the removal of heavy metals from aqueous solutions33. A comparison of the spectra reveals slight shifts in key absorption peaks after Cd(II) adsorption. Specifically, the peaks initially observed at 3456, 1636, and 581 cm⁻1 shifted to 3449.5, 1632, and 579 cm⁻1, respectively, upon Cd(II) loading. These shifts correspond to the stretching vibrations of –OH groups, C = C bonds, and Fe–O bonds, suggesting their involvement in the adsorption process.

The shift in the –OH band to 3449.5 cm⁻1 suggests the participation of hydroxyl groups in binding Cd(II) ions. Meanwhile, the reduction and slight shift of the Fe–O vibration band to 579 cm⁻1 indicate an interaction between iron oxide and cadmium, highlighting Fe–O as a key contributor in the adsorption process. Additionally, the C = C peak shift from 1636 to 1632 cm⁻1 implies that alkene groups serve as active sites for metal ion binding, further confirming the effective interaction between Cd(II) and the functionalized nanoparticle surface35. Similarly, the observed shift of absorption bands from 1250–1010 cm⁻1 to 1185–1000 cm⁻1 indicates the involvement of C–O functional groups in the adsorption of Cd(II) ions. This shift suggests that the C–O groups serve as active sites facilitating heavy metal binding through mechanisms such as ion exchange and ionic interactions. Consistent with previous reports, these functional groups demonstrate considerable potential in the removal of heavy metals from aqueous solutions28,36.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

Figure 3 presents the X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns of teff straw-supported Fe3O4-TSA nanoparticles (top) and unsupported Fe3O4 nanoparticles (bottom). The characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 30.52°, 35.64°, 43.3°, 53.38°, 57.48°, and 62.87° correspond to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystal planes, respectively, confirming the formation of magnetite (Fe₃O₄) nanoparticles. These peaks align well with the standard cubic spinel structure of Fe3O4 as referenced in the JCPDS database (PDF No. 85–1436). The XRD patterns of the teff straw-supported Fe3O4 nanoparticles closely resemble those of the unsupported Fe3O4, indicating that the interaction with teff straw did not alter the crystalline phase or cause degradation of the magnetite core. Determination of point of zero charge

The point of zero charge (pHₚzc) is a key parameter that determines the net surface charge of an adsorbent in a given solution. It represents the pH at which the surface of the adsorbent exhibits a neutral charge. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the pHₚzc of Fe₃O₄-TSA nanoparticles was determined using the salt addition method and found to be 4.35. This indicates that the surface of Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs is positively charged at pH values below 4.35 and negatively charged at pH values above this point. Consequently, Cd(II) adsorption is expected to be more favorable at pH levels higher than the pHₚzc, where electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged adsorbent surface and the positively charged Cd(II) ions is enhanced.Fig. 6Point of zero charge.

Cadmium (II) removal studies

Effect of the pH solution on Cd(II) removal

The influence of pH on Cd(II) removal was investigated at room temperature using 100 mL of a 40 mg/L Cd(II) solution across a pH range of 2 to 12. The experiments were conducted with an adsorbent dosage of 0.7 g, a shaking speed of 250 rpm, and a contact time of 75 min. As illustrated in Fig. 7a, the adsorption capacity of Cd(II) onto Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs was significantly affected by the solution pH. The lowest adsorption occurred at pH 2, while the maximum uptake was observed at pH 6. This trend can be attributed to the speciation of cadmium in aqueous media, where Cd(II) exists primarily as Cd2⁺ at lower pH values and as Cd(OH)⁺ and Cd(OH)₂ at higher pH levels42. These species have a considerable impact on the adsorption processes. At low pH, the electrostatic repulsion between the positive charges of Fe3O4-TS nano-adsorbent and the cationic species of Cd(II) leads to poor removal efficiency of Cd(II). A competition occurs between hydrogen ions (H+) and cationic Cd(II) species for binding sites on Fe3O4-TSA NPs. This happens because the excessive presence of H+ ions occupies the active surface sites of Fe3O4-TSA NPs, resulting in competitive adsorption between Cd(II) and H + , which reduces Cd(II) adsorption. As the solution pH increases, more electronegative charges are created on the surface of the nano-adsorbents, promoting electrostatic attraction between the cationic species of Cd(II) and the adsorbent. Consequently, the adsorption capacity of Fe3O4-TSA NPs continues to rise. However, as shown in Fig. 7a, at higher solution pH, the removal of Cd(II) ions decreases, possibly due to the formation of Cd2+ ions as hydroxide complexes.Fig. 7(a) Effect of the pH on the removal of Cd (II) (b) Effect of the contact time on Cd (II) removal (c) Effect of adsorbent dosage on Cd (II) removal (d) Effect of initial concentration on Cd(II) removal.

(a) Effect of the pH on the removal of Cd (II) (b) Effect of the contact time on Cd (II) removal (c) Effect of adsorbent dosage on Cd (II) removal (d) Effect of initial concentration on Cd(II) removal.

Alternatively, in an aqueous medium, the surface of Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs acquires charge through the protonation or deprotonation of surface hydroxyl groups (Fe–OH). This surface charge plays a pivotal role in metal ion adsorption, as it governs the electrostatic interactions between the adsorbent and the metal ions present in the solution43. The protonation or deprotonation process of the FeOH surface sites is influenced by the pHpzc of the Fe3O4-TS nano-adsorbent and the solution pH. At pH < pHpzc, protonation occurs, making Fe-(OH2)n + the dominant species on the surface of Fe3O4-TS NPs. As a result, the protonated adsorption sites hinder Cd(II) adsorption due to electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged sites and the positively charged metal ions. On the other hand, at pH > pHpzc, Fe–O- + H+/Fe–O-(OH)n becomes predominant, and the deprotonated adsorption sites favor Cd(II) adsorption due to electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged sites and Cd(II) ions. These findings align well with previous studies on similar adsorption mechanisms44, 45 This demonstrates that the pH of the solution significantly affects the Cd(II) removal process, as it governs both the speciation of Cd(II) in the solution and the surface charge of the synthesized nanoadsorbent. Based on this, the optimum pH for Cd(II) ion adsorption by Fe3O4-TSA NPs was determined to be pH 6.

Effect of the contact time on Cd(II) removal

The effect of contact time on Cd(II) removal was investigated at room temperature over a range of 5 to 95 min, with 10-min intervals. The experiments were conducted using 100 mL of 40 mg/L initial Cd(II) solution, an adsorbent dose of 0.7 g, a shaking speed of 250 rpm, and a solution pH of 6. The experimental results, presented in Fig. 7b, shows that the adsorption capacity of Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs increased rapidly with contact time, particularly within the first 25 min. Notably, approximately 73.71% of Cd(II) ions were removed within the initial 5 min, indicating a rapid adsorption process at the early stage. This rapid uptake is attributed to the abundance of available negatively charged active sites on the surface of the Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs, which readily interact with the cationic Cd(II) ions46, thus resulting in the rapid removal of Cd(II). The effective binding of the surface functional groups of Fe3O4-TSA NPs with Cd(II) ions enhanced the adsorption processes. But, it was found that beyond 45 min, increasing the contact time had no substantial effect on the adsorption of Cd(II) ions. As contact time was increased beyond 45 min, there is a negligible change in percentage removal of Cd(II), which may result due to the unavailability of vacant active sites on the adsorbent surface above the equilibrium. This means at these contact times, the adsorbed Cd(II) onto the surface of Fe3O4-TSA NPs is in equilibrium with the desorbed Cd(II) from the adsorbent. The studies reported by48,49 confirmed that the availability of active sites of the surface adsorbents significantly affects the binding of Cd(II) ions with contact time.

Effect of the adsorbent dose on Cd(II) removal

The effect of adsorbent dose on Cd(II) ion removal from aqueous solution was investigated using varying amounts of Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs (0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 1, 1.3, and 1.6 g) with an initial Cd(II) concentration of 40 mg/L. The solutions were agitated for 75 min at a shaking speed of 250 rpm and a pH of 6. After the adsorption process, each sample was centrifuged for 5–10 min and filtered using a membrane filter for residual Cd(II) analysis.

As illustrated in Fig. 7c, the adsorbent dose had a significant effect on Cd(II) removal. The results revealed that increasing the adsorbent dose from 0.1 g to 1 g enhanced the removal efficiency from 74.74% to 98.04%. This improvement is attributed to the increased number of available active sites on the adsorbent surface, which promoted stronger electrostatic interactions between Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs and Cd(II) ions, thereby enhancing the adsorption efficiency.

However, beyond 1 g of adsorbent, the removal efficiency plateaued, showing negligible improvement with further dosage increases. This behavior suggests that the synthesized Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs exhibited minimal aggregation, ensuring a consistently accessible surface area. In contrast, highly aggregated adsorbents typically exhibit reduced surface area, which can diminish the adsorption performance. Thus, the nearly constant removal efficiency beyond the optimal dose reflects the high dispersion and surface accessibility of Fe₃O₄-TSA NPs50 also reported the effect of adsorbent dosage on various adsorbates, confirming that increasing the dosage beyond the optimal amount leads to aggregation of nanoadsorbents, which reduces the available surface area for metal ion binding ions.

Effect of the initial concentration onto Cd(II) removal

The impact of initial Cd(II) concentration on the removal efficiency of Cd(II) ions by Fe3O4-TSA NPs was evaluated at room temperature by varying the initial concentration (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mg/L) while keeping other parameters constant: adsorbent dose of 1 g, contact time of 75 min, and pH of 6. The solutions were shaken at 250 rpm for 75 min, then centrifuged for 5–10 min and filtered through a membrane filter before analyzing the residual Cd(II) concentration.

As shown in Fig. 7d, increasing the initial Cd(II) concentration caused a decrease in the percentage removal of Cd(II) ions. The highest removal efficiency of 99.51% occurred at 20 mg/L, while the lowest efficiency of 81.68% was observed at 100 mg/L. This behavior is attributed to the greater availability of active adsorption sites relative to the Cd(II) ions at lower concentrations, allowing more complete adsorption. Conversely, at higher initial concentrations, the fixed number of active sites on the adsorbent becomes saturated, limiting further adsorption and thus reducing the removal percentage. Therefore, for a given adsorbent dose, the number of active sites remains constant and can become fully occupied beyond a certain initial adsorbate concentration, resulting in a plateau in adsorption capacity.

Adsorption kinetic studies

The study of adsorption kinetics evaluates how effectively an adsorbent utilizes its capacity over the contact time between the liquid and solid phases. In this work, adsorption kinetics were investigated by measuring the uptake of cadmium ions from aqueous solutions at different time intervals (10–90 min), while keeping all other parameters constant. The solution pH was maintained at 7.0 using 0.1 M NaOH or 0.1 M HCl. The adsorbate–adsorbent mixtures were stirred at a constant speed of 250 rpm with an adsorbent dosage of 0.75 g per 100 mL. Based on the experimental results, the adsorption mechanism of Cd(II) onto magnetite nanoparticles was analyzed using pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intra-particle diffusion models. The pseudo-fist-order, pseudo-second-order, and intra-particle diffusion model plots revealed that the adsorption of Cd (II) onto the magnetite NPs with their respective correlation coefficients. In comparison to the pseudo-first-order and the pseudo-second- order model kinetic model of Cd (II) up-take showed not linearity with correlation coefficient (R2).

Pseudo first order kinetic model

The R2 value (0.97721) obtained for the pseudo-first-order model was not high and the experimental qe did not agree with the calculated value obtained from the linear plot. This finding shows that the adsorption of Cd2+ on the adsorbent does not follow a pseudo first- order kinetic model.

Pseudo-second-order

The pseudo-second-order model provided the best fit for describing the Cd(II) bio sorption process, indicating that chemisorption was the dominant mechanism onto magnetite nanoparticles. The correlation coefficient for the pseudo-first-order model was lower compared to the pseudo-second-order model (R2 = 0.9988), and a significant discrepancy was observed between the calculated and experimental equilibrium adsorption capacities (qₑ), demonstrating that the pseudo-first-order model poorly described the experimental data.

Adsorption kinetics plays a crucial role in understanding the adsorption process, as it reveals the mechanism by which the adsorbate interacts with the adsorbent. More importantly, it describes the influence of contact time on the adsorption efficiency and the rate at which the adsorbate is removed from the solution. Conclusions

In this study, eco-friendly synthesized teff straw-supported Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fe3O4-TSA NPs) demonstrated excellent physicochemical properties and high efficiency for Cd(II) removal from aqueous solutions. XRD and SEM analyses confirmed average crystalline and particle sizes of 18.83 and 21.48 nm, respectively. Adsorption experiments revealed that pH, contact time, adsorbent dosage, and initial metal concentration significantly influence Cd(II) removal, with pH having the most pronounced effect. Maximum removal efficiency (96.84%) was achieved at pH 6, while low efficiency (36.8%) occurred at pH 2 due to electrostatic repulsion. Adsorption equilibrium was reached within 45 min, and the Langmuir model described the isotherm data with a maximum adsorption capacity of 101.01 mg/g.

Although the results demonstrate the high potential of Fe3O4-TSA NPs as sustainable nano-adsorbents, this study was conducted using aqueous solutions, and real wastewater systems may introduce additional complexities. Future studies should focus on scaling up the synthesis, evaluating long-term reusability, and testing performance in practical wastewater applications. The use of teff straw, an underutilized agricultural residue, combined with Aloe vera extract, highlights a sustainable and low-cost approach for producing efficient magnetite-based nano-adsorbents for heavy metal remediation.
