Preferences and predictors driving opioid-involved polysubstance use profiles and trajectories: a prospective cohort study protocol
Authors: Maya Campbell, Josephine I Lafayette, Karina Vallejo Vasquez, Ava Rivard, Jordan Diamond, Clarence Nguyen, Dominick Caputo, Sahana Lothumalla, Chavez R Rodriguez, Devin C Tomlinson, Erin E Bonar, Jason E Goldstick, Maureen Walton, Sunghee Lee, Nathan Menke, Lewei (Allison) Lin, Lara N Coughlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPolysubstance use (PSU), particularly opioid-involved and stimulant-involved PSU, is a growing issue in the USA. PSU increases the risk of negative health consequences, including infectious diseases, worsening physical and mental health conditions, and overdose-related deaths. These consequences occur in the context of varying health risk behaviours, substance-related preferences, and treatment engagements among people with PSU. To inform improvements in prevention, harm reduction, and substance use disorder (SUD) treatment, additional research is needed to comprehensively understand the current context and drivers of PSU preferences, motivations, and behaviours.Methods and analysisHerein, we describe the protocol for a prospective cohort study designed to capture detailed patterns, profiles, and trajectories of PSU, with the aim of comprehensively examining the drivers of PSU behaviours and SUD treatment utilisation. Adults (ages 18–75; n=400) who engage in PSU will be recruited from healthcare institutions, an established participant database maintained by an adjacent SUD research team, and online advertisements. Study assessments will capture dynamic patterns, choice preferences, and motivators of PSU via behavioural economic (BE) measures, detailed Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB) interviews, and self-administered surveys. The assessment timeline will include a baseline survey and TLFB interview, weekly TLFB interviews for 4 weeks post-baseline, and follow-up surveys and TLFB interviews at 4-, 8-, and 12-months post-baseline.Ethics and disseminationThe study is funded through the National Institutes of Health Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) initiative and was approved by the University of Michigan Medical Institutional Review Board. Findings will be disseminated to academic, clinical, and community partners through the Michigan Innovations in Addiction Care through Research and Education programme. Results from this study will inform actionable and practical insights relevant to the delivery of personalised care in the context of PSU.

Introduction

Background

The drug overdose epidemic remains a significant issue in the USA. Although overdose risk is dependent on many factors,1 a consistent driver in the drug overdose epidemic is polysubstance use (PSU): the simultaneous or sequential use of multiple substances.24 Although PSU can vary from person to person, there are specific behaviours and patterns related to PSU that increase the risk of overdose, including the use of opioids and stimulants, using illicitly manufactured fentanyl (IMF), using substances without a bystander present, and intravenous routes of substance administration.257 Given the dynamic nature of the drug overdose epidemic, more research is needed to comprehensively understand the context and drivers of PSU in order to tailor and increase the uptake of prevention and treatment interventions, which remain low.8

There are specific individual, social, and community factors related to PSU that may impact different health outcomes. Individual factors related to worse health outcomes include older age and male sex.914 Social factors related to worse health outcomes include adverse childhood experiences, comorbid health disorders, family histories of substance use disorders (SUD), and race.91518 Most pointedly, Black and/or African Americans are subject to systemic inequities (eg, structural racism) that contribute to an increased risk of PSU, treatment stigma, and fatal overdoses.1928 Community factors related to worse health outcomes include unemployment, low income, and reduced access to treatment, including medications for opioid use disorders (MOUD), mental health care, and medical care.29 30 Substance use patterns that contribute to worse health outcomes also vary across communities, with prescription opioid and methamphetamine overdoses occurring more commonly in rural communities and heroin and cocaine overdoses occurring more commonly in urban communities.13 31 Ultimately, an integrated understanding of these individual, social, and community factors is necessary to determine how to better engage different individuals in PSU treatment.

In addition to the influence of individual, social, and community factors, there are many different motives for PSU that may impact different health outcomes. Prior research has found that people engage in PSU to enhance feelings of intoxication, reduce undesired effects of other substances, alleviate withdrawal symptoms, and attempt to cope with physical and mental health symptoms.32 What is poorly understood, however, is how these motivations vary within and between individuals. In particular, there is a need to determine which patient characteristics are associated with different motives, in what context these different motives occur, and which motives contribute to high-risk PSU and worse outcomes. As such, a nuanced understanding of the motivations behind different patterns of PSU within and between individuals can inform personalised interventions to mitigate substance use risks and improve health outcomes.

Substance-related choice preferences and decision making also play a role in PSU patterns and associated health risks. Behavioural economics (BE), though rarely applied in PSU research, provides insight into the choice preferences central to substance use behaviours.3335 While it is widely understood that choice preferences underlie many substance use behaviours, little is known about the BE of individual-level choice preferences and how these impact overdose risk and other consequences of PSU.3335 BE provides a framework for evaluating how individuals assess the risks, rewards, and consequences of their substance use in complex environments (ie, drug valuation and overdose devaluation).36 37 Such insights have been used to inform effective SUD treatments (eg, contingency management) and can continue to be used to predict conditions that could decrease the likelihood of PSU.36 37 Ultimately, by clarifying underlying decision-making processes, BE can enrich our understanding of the choice preferences central to PSU.3843

Lastly, improvements in health outcomes for people with PSU are complicated by barriers in the healthcare system. Although individuals with PSU frequently interact with the healthcare system prior to an overdose, few receive the preventive services they need. For instance, over 85% of individuals involved in fatal overdoses interact with the healthcare system in the year leading up to their death.84446 During this time, these individuals receive SUD diagnoses, but few receive overdose prevention tools or are effectively linked to SUD care.84446 Additionally, individuals with comorbid SUDs are less likely to receive MOUD and other effective treatments.8 47 Furthermore, stigma is a major barrier to care for people with PSU, especially Black and/or African Americans.1921 26 4851 Manifestations of experienced, expected, and internalised stigma decrease the likelihood of seeking treatment among people with PSU.52 Together, these findings underscore a continued need to address missed opportunities to engage people with PSU in these healthcare settings.

Goal of the current study

This study will illuminate detailed patterns, profiles, and trajectories of PSU. Specifically, this study will compare individual, social, and community factors and BE measures to examine the drivers of different PSU behaviours and treatment (figure 1). Additionally, this study will compare healthcare utilisation and BE measures within and between individuals to identify or further characterise modifiable risk factors of PSU and treatment determinants. Furthermore, this study will employ BE measures to assess how PSU preferences are influenced by drug valuation and overdose devaluation, aiming to gain insight into the delivery of personalised PSU care.5357 Finally, this study will compare PSU patterns and healthcare utilisation to address opportunities for interventions in healthcare systems. Herein, we focus on the following aims: (1) elucidating within-person and between-person patterns and predictors of high-risk PSU behaviours and (2) examining predictors and trajectories of healthcare utilisation across a 1-year follow-up among people with opioid-involved PSU. In this way, this study will integrate an understanding of the individual, social, and community factors related to PSU outcomes; the motivations behind different patterns of PSU within and between individuals; the BE choice preferences related to PSU; and healthcare utilisation to inform the timing, type, and tailoring of interventions and policies effective in treating PSU.

Methods

Community involvement

Patient and public involvement

Although there was no direct public involvement in the development of this study, our aims and methodology were heavily informed by existing public health data on high-risk PSU behaviours and tailored to accommodate varied PSU experiences and patterns of use.

Recruitment procedures and eligibility

Recruitment

We will recruit 400 adults, ages 18–75, with opioid-involved PSU in urban and rural settings across the USA. To increase the representation of specific subgroups who are disproportionately affected by the drug overdose epidemic, we will recruit an enriched sample of people who co-use stimulants and are Black and/or African American. To promote a diverse cohort of adults with opioid-involved PSU, recruitment will include patients from the Michigan Medicine healthcare system (for local perspectives), an established participant database maintained by an adjacent SUD research team (to supplement these local perspectives), and online advertisements from a third-party recruitment platform (for national perspectives and to increase our study’s reach).

Prescreening

Prior to recruitment, potential participants will be prescreened via electronic health records and self-reports to assess if they might be eligible to continue with study screening. Potential participants will prescreen eligible if they: (1) are ages 18–75 and (2) have an indication of past-year substance use in their ICD-10 codes, problem summary lists, social histories, or self-reports. Individuals will prescreen ineligible if they: (1) are in end-of-life care; (2) are hearing-impaired or have substantial problems communicating that would prevent them from being able to complete study assessments; (3) do not have a valid email address, phone number, or address that we can use to contact them; (4) are not able to provide informed consent; or (5) do not speak English.

Screening

After prescreening, eligible potential participants will be invited to screen by email, text, phone, or in-person correspondence. Barring any participation refusals, each potential participant will be contacted up to 10 times over the course of 2 weeks before research staff will end recruitment efforts. When a potential participant agrees to screen, they will be asked to provide informed consent to screen before completing a brief (~10 min) screening survey online, over the phone, or in person. For completing the screening survey, individuals will receive a $15 gift card.

Eligibility

Potential participants will screen eligible if they: (1) endorse past-month opioid misuse (indicated by a Tobacco, Alcohol, Prescription medication, and other Substance use (TAPS-2) score ≥1 for prescription or non-prescription opioids)58; (2) endorse past-month misuse of another substance (indicated by a TAPS-2 score ≥2 for alcohol or a TAPS-2 score ≥1 for other non-opioid substances); (3) are ages 18–75; and (4) have reliable access to a phone. Individuals will screen ineligible if they: (1) are not able to provide informed consent; (2) do not speak English; or (3) fail an authenticity check (characterised by the failure of an attention check and/or the use of invalid identification information).59

Study procedures and data collection

Study assessments

Eligible individuals who elect to participate in the full study will be invited to complete an additional informed consent form for the full study, a baseline survey and Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB) interview,60 four weekly TLFB interviews, and three follow-up surveys and TLFB interviews at 4-, 8-, and 12-months post-baseline (figure 2). Each study assessment will take approximately 45 min to complete and can be done online, over the phone, or in person.

Study flow. Note: Figure 2 depicts the study flow from the eligible completion of the screening survey to the completion of the 12-month follow-up assessments.

Study consent

Prior to participation, eligible individuals will be asked to provide informed consent to the full study. A copy of the consent form will be provided to participants, and research staff will be available to answer any questions that arise. Research staff will ensure that individuals can describe the essential elements of the study and assess whether they are able to provide informed consent before proceeding.

Baseline assessments: survey and TLFB interview

After providing informed consent to the full study, participants will complete a baseline survey and TLFB interview. The completion of the baseline survey and the baseline TLFB interview will constitute full enrolment in the study.

The baseline survey will collect demographic information, including age, housing status, level of education, employment status, household income, number of children, medical insurance, and military affiliation. Additionally, the baseline survey will include measures of lifetime and past 4-month substance use,61 interest in reducing substance use,62 self-efficacy to change substance use,63 64 substance use motives,65 experiences of stigma,52 mental/physical health and functioning,6668 experiences of adversity,69 healthcare utilisation and use of harm reduction strategies,62 70 and treatment barriers/facilitators.71 72 Notably, the baseline survey will use BE measures to gain insight into the decision-making processes underlying specific PSU behaviours. Several BE measures were modified to fit the scope of this project. A summary of these BE measures is available for reference in table 1.

Behavioural economic measures

The baseline TLFB interview will assess patterns, frequencies, quantities, social contexts, and high-risk behaviours of PSU using a detailed 30-day TLFB structure to capture these measures at a monthly level over the past month. In addition to the TLFB, these interviews will evaluate medications prescribed for PSU (eg, MOUD),61 other SUD treatments,61 and social networks73 across the lifetime and over the past four months.

Weekly TLFB Interviews

Following the baseline assessments, participants will complete four weekly TLFB interviews. Adapted from validated assessments, the weekly TLFB interviews will also assess patterns, frequencies, quantities, motives, social contexts, and high-risk behaviours of PSU using a detailed 7-day TLFB structure to capture these measures at an episodic level over the past week (figure 3).72 Building on traditional TLFB interviews, we will operationalise an episode as any period during the day in which a substance is used. To track PSU and otherwise isolated instances of substance use at an episodic level, we will categorise substance use as either single, sequential, or simultaneous where single use is defined as the use of a substance >4 hours after the use of another substance, sequential use is defined as the use of a substance 2–4 hours after the use of another substance, and simultaneous use is defined as the use of a substance <2 hours after the use of another substance. As an alternative to these temporal markers, participants will have the option to describe their substance use as occurring a long time after, a short time after, or at the same time as the use of another substance (descriptions that will be coded as single, simultaneous, and sequential use, respectively). In this way, we will be able to establish the approximate temporality of use while reducing participant recall burden. In line with these definitions, if an individual only uses one substance one time in the span of a day, that isolated instance of substance use will also be coded as single use.

In Episode 3, the use of heroin is single because it is used more than 4 hours after the previous use of heroin.

Follow-up assessments: surveys and TLFB interviews

At 4-, 8-, and 12-months post-baseline, participants will complete follow-up surveys and TLFB interviews. To evaluate measures longitudinally, these follow-up assessments will assess the same measures that were captured at baseline, evaluating changes over the past four months.

Compensation

For completing study assessments, participants will receive up to $390 in gift cards: $60 for the baseline assessments, $25 for each weekly TLFB interview, $60 for the 4-month follow-up assessments, $65 for the 8-month follow-up assessments, and $75 for the 12-month follow-up assessments. Additionally, participants will have the opportunity to receive a $10 bonus at each follow-up assessment for confirming their appointments.

Community-level data

To further supplement study assessments, community-level data will be linked to the available individual participant data collected during the baseline assessments. Such data will include local demographics, unemployment rates, income reports, and other socioeconomic measures. Additionally, we will procure place-based data on various types of treatment availability, overdose rates, and other social determinants of health. These measures will be sourced from publicly available databases, such as the US Census Bureau, the National Survey of Substance Abuse Treatment Services (N-SSATS), and Area Health Resources Files (AHRF), to further our understanding of the community factors that influence PSU. We will geocode participant home addresses and link place-based data using the smallest areal unit practicable (eg, census tracts for demographic and socioeconomic status data).

Statistical analysis

Aim 1

The primary analytic goals of Aim 1 are to (a) characterise the patterns of PSU both between and within individuals, and (b) disaggregate the between-person and within-person effects of predictors of high-risk PSU. For goal (a), we will examine descriptive statistics to assess between-person and within-person variability in PSU frequency and characteristics. We will use generalised linear mixed models to analyse effects of time-varying covariates on high-risk PSU behaviours (eg, a Yes/No indicator of intravenous drug use, on a given day, based on the TLFB). To separate between-person and within-person effects of time-varying covariates (eg, motives) on outcomes, we will calculate person-level means for each covariate and mean-centred versions of each covariate, entering each as a separate covariate in the models; the coefficient for the former estimates the between-person effect, and the coefficient for the latter estimates the within-person effect.74 75 For non-time-varying covariates (eg, community-level variables), effect estimates will be purely between-person. For goal (b), we will use latent trajectory modelling to characterise trends in PSU frequency across follow-ups.7679 This model will identify ‘trajectory types,’ allowing post-hoc analysis of what baseline characteristics predict different trajectory types (eg, using multinomial logistic regression). We will select the number of latent classes using information criteria (eg, Akaike Information Criterion/Bayesian Information Criterion) and will consider class separation (eg, quantified by relative entropy) as part of the model selection process.

Aim 2

The Aim 2 analysis will focus on predictors of treatment utilisation in the year following baseline. We will use the same framework described above for decoupling within-person and between-person effects, focusing instead on healthcare utilisation outcomes (eg, MOUD initiation). Focal predictors in this aim will include variables expected to reduce the likelihood of treatment (eg, discriminatory experiences; stigma) and those expected to increase treatment likelihood (eg, social support). Place-based factors, as described above, will also be analysed as between-person predictors.

Sample size

To address our first aim, we estimated the power for the analysis of daily data, assuming: (1) a 60% response rate for the weekly assessments80; (2) our population would report a high-risk PSU episode on 25% of days during the 4-week measurement period; and (3) on days with high-risk PSU episodes, there would be only one episode. As the episode is the unit of analysis, this would result in approximately eight data points per person. Under these assumptions, we simulated from the model described above with a large within-person correlation (0.5), and determined how large the odds ratio had to be (in standard deviation units) to reliably detect (ie, >80% power based on 1000 simulation replications) a between-person (ie, person-level, non-time-varying) predictor, and a within-person effect, respectively. Under these specifications, given the planned sample size of n=400, the minimum detectable odds ratio for a between-person predictor is approximately 1.12, and the corresponding effect for a within-person predictor is approximately 1.18, both of which are small effect sizes.81

The planned longitudinal analyses in our second aim involve the n=400 sample at baseline, 4-, 8-, and 12-months. The assumptions underlying this power analysis were: (1) participants will complete an average of 2 follow-ups (conservative relative to the estimated 80% follow-up rate); and (2) large (0.5) within-person residual correlations on primary outcomes. Similarly to above, the power analysis was calibrated to determine minimum detectable effects (>80% power) via simulation for both the within-person and between-person effects. Our simulations showed that we can detect a between-person effect of a quantitative outcome if the adjusted correlation is ≥0.13, with a corresponding minimum detectable effect of 0.18 for the within-person effect, both small-to-medium effect sizes.82 An analogous analysis for binary outcomes showed minimum detectable effects (odds ratios) of 1.21 (between-person effect) and 1.29 (within-person effect), both corresponding to small effect sizes.

Discussion

A comprehensive understanding of PSU is necessary to inform improvements in prevention, harm reduction, and SUD treatment. This study aims to achieve this comprehensive understanding by: (1) elucidating within-person and between-person patterns and predictors of high-risk PSU behaviours and (2) examining predictors and trajectories of healthcare utilisation across a 1-year follow-up among people with opioid-involved PSU.

This study has multiple strengths. One strength of this study is its integrative approach to PSU research. There has been limited research to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the contexts and drivers of PSU both within and between individuals and contextualised through community-level data. Another strength of this study is its longitudinal design, which captures changes in PSU behaviours, preferences, and healthcare utilisation over time. This study also benefits from its oversampling of populations who face a disproportionate burden of the drug overdose epidemic, namely, individuals who co-use stimulants and Black and/or African Americans. Given the increased risk of negative PSU outcomes, it is essential that each of these subgroups is represented. Lastly, this study benefits from the use of multiple recruitment sources. This use provides clinical relevance as it enables us to engage diverse perspectives in PSU research.

One limitation of this study is a potential self-selection bias. Study participation is voluntary; therefore, results may not be representative of the overall PSU population, which faces systematic barriers to research engagement due to historic criminalisation and stigma in research settings. Likewise, this study relies on retrospective self-reports, which may introduce recall biases; however, our decision to conduct weekly assessments in between the baseline and follow-up assessments helps to mitigate this bias. Moreover, there is ambiguity in defining specific PSU behaviours, particularly in operationalising the timing of co-use (eg, simultaneous and sequential).80 83 There is no consensus definition in the field around co-use, but the detailed person-level data from this study may help to further inform future approaches and provide a necessary first step in guiding the field.

Ultimately, by integrating an understanding of the individual, social, and community factors related to PSU outcomes; the motivations behind different patterns of PSU within and between individuals; the BE choice preferences related to PSU; and healthcare utilisation, this study will inform the timing, type, and tailoring of interventions and policies effective in treating PSU.

Ethics and dissemination

The study is funded through the National Institutes of Health Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) initiative. The study protocol was approved by the University of Michigan Medical Institutional Review Board (HUM00229563), which included a participating site at the University of Michigan (HUM00234294). The study commenced in September 2022 and is scheduled to conclude in September 2027. The participant-facing activities described herein began in August 2023 and will run until December 2026. At the conclusion of this study, our findings will be disseminated to academic, clinical, and community partners through the Michigan Innovations in Addiction Care through Research and Education programme.
