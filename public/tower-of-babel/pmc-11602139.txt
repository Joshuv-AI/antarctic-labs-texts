PCNA-binding activity separates RNF168 functions in DNA replication and DNA double-stranded break signaling
Authors: Yang Yang, Deepika Jayaprakash, Satpal S Jhujh, John J Reynolds, Steve Chen, Yanzhe Gao, Jay Ramanlal Anand, Elizabeth Mutter-Rottmayer, Pablo Ariel, Jing An, Xing Cheng, Kenneth H Pearce, Sophie-Anne Blanchet, Nandana Nandakumar, Pei Zhou, Amélie Fradet-Turcotte, Grant S Stewart, Cyrus Vaziri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNF168 orchestrates a ubiquitin-dependent DNA damage response to regulate the recruitment of repair factors, such as 53BP1 to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). In addition to its canonical functions in DSB signaling, RNF168 may facilitate DNA replication fork progression. However, the precise role of RNF168 in DNA replication remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that RNF168 is recruited to DNA replication factories in a manner that is independent of the canonical DSB response pathway regulated by Ataxia-Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) and RNF8. We identify a degenerate Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA)-interacting peptide (DPIP) motif in the C-terminus of RNF168, which together with its Motif Interacting with Ubiquitin (MIU) domain mediates binding to mono-ubiquitylated PCNA at replication factories. An RNF168 mutant harboring inactivating substitutions in its DPIP box and MIU1 domain (termed RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1) is not recruited to sites of DNA synthesis and fails to support ongoing DNA replication. Notably, the PCNA interaction-deficient RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 mutant fully rescues the ability of RNF168−/− cells to form 53BP1 foci in response to DNA DSBs. Therefore, RNF168 functions in DNA replication and DSB signaling are fully separable. Our results define a new mechanism by which RNF168 promotes DNA replication independently of its canonical functions in DSB signaling.

Introduction

Ubiquitin signaling cascades play central roles in the sensing and repair of DNA damage. Failure to sustain DNA damage-inducible ubiquitin signaling compromises DNA repair resulting in increased sensitivity to genotoxic agents and genomic instability. DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) such as those induced by ionizing radiation (IR) or radiomimetic agents are a potent inducer of the ubiquitin-dependent cellular DNA damage response (Ub-DDR), which is mediated primarily by the E3 ligases, RNF8 and RNF168. The mechanism of RNF8–RNF168 activation in response to DSBs is well-established (1–3) and is initiated by the DSB-responsive checkpoint kinase ATM which phosphorylates the histone H2A variant H2AX located in chromatin proximal to a break. Phosphorylated H2AX (termed γH2AX) recruits Mediator of DNA damage Checkpoint 1 (MDC1), which is also phosphorylated by ATM. The phosphorylation of MDC1 facilitates the recruitment of RNF8 to sites of damage, whereby it catalyzes the ubiquitylation of H1 and H2A-type histones surrounding the break. RNF8-dependent ubiquitylation of histones stimulates the recruitment of another E3 ligase complex, RNF168/UBC13 via direct interaction. Once recruited, RNF168/UBC13 mediates additional K63-linked poly-ubiquitylation of the ubiquitylated H2A histones. The sequential RNF8 and RNF168-dependent ubiquitylation of histones, together with other post-translational modifications already present at the break creates a platform for the recruitment of repair factors such as 53BP1, RNF169 and the BReast CAncer gene 1 (BRCA1) protein (4–6). 53BP1 is a critical DNA repair factor that dictates the pathway choice between NHEJ (non-homologous DNA end-joining) and homologous recombination (HR)-dependent DSB repair through its ability to suppress DNA end-resection, a process that is essential for initiating HR (7,8). The importance of the RNF168-dependent Ub-DDR in genome maintenance is evident from the existence of patients with ‘RIDDLE’ syndrome, which is caused by bi-allelic mutations in RNF168. Patients with RIDDLE syndrome are immunodeficient and unable to produce Immunoglobulin G (IgG) owing to defects in class switch recombination (CSR). Cells from RIDDLE syndrome patients are hypersensitive to IR and fail to recruit 53BP1 to sites of DNA DSBs, recapitulating phenotypes of cells lacking H2AX, RNF8 and MDC1 (4,9).

In addition to the vital roles of ubiquitin signaling in the DSB response, ubiquitylation also plays key roles in sensing and processing DNA damage or ‘stress’ that arises during DNA replication. DNA replication stalling can result from exogenously induced bulky DNA lesions or DNA synthesis through highly repetitive regions of chromatin. The uncoupling of replicative DNA helicase and polymerase activities at stalled DNA replication forks generates ssDNA tracts (10) that are prone to breakage, a process often described as ‘fork collapse’.

To sustain S-phase and avert the lethal consequences of fork collapse, eukaryotic cells employ genome maintenance processes termed ‘DNA damage tolerance’ and ‘DNA damage avoidance’ which are also regulated by ubiquitin signaling cascades (11). The DNA damage tolerance pathway is triggered by activation of RAD18, an E3 ligase that interacts with ssDNA at stalled replication forks (12,13). RAD18 cooperates with the E2 ubiquitin conjugating enzyme RAD6 to mono-ubiquitylate PCNA at stalled replication forks (14). PCNA mono-ubiquitylation may help recruit specialized Y-family trans-lesion synthesis (TLS) DNA polymerases to stalled replication forks (15). TLS polymerases are damage-tolerant when compared with the replicative DNA polymerases (Polδ, Polϵ) and are able to sustain DNA synthesis in cells harboring damaged genomes (16). The Y-family DNA TLS polymerases (Polκ, Polη, Polι and REV1) have ubiquitin-binding domains and PCNA-interacting peptide motifs (termed ‘PIP boxes’). Although there is some debate regarding the extent to which TLS polymerases depend upon PCNA mono-ubiquitylation for their recruitment to DNA (17,18), the general consensus is that monoubiquitylated PCNA can promote TLS.

TLS-deficient cells are intolerant of DNA damage and typically accumulate excessive ssDNA in S-phase which leads to checkpoint activation and lethal abortive mitoses (19,20). However, although TLS confers DNA damage tolerance, Y-family polymerases are error-prone and can increase the risk of mutagenesis. Some Y-family polymerases have a preferred cognate lesion that is bypassed with relative accuracy when compared with other TLS polymerases. For example, Polη bypasses UV-induced cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) in an error-free manner and represses sunlight-induced mutagenesis (21). In Polη-deficient individuals (a congenital disease termed xeroderma pigmentosum-Variant or XPV), error-prone compensatory bypass of CPD adducts by alternative TLS polymerases leads to mutations and skin cancer-propensity (22).

Interestingly, RAD18-induced PCNA mono-ubiquitylation can also promote a relatively error-free DNA damage-avoidance pathway termed ‘Template Switching’ (TS) to sustain S-phase in cells with damaged genomes (23,24). TS uses fork-reversal as a mechanism for the leading strand to avoid damaged DNA and instead use the intact replicated lagging DNA strand as a template. In mammalian cells, the template-switching process requires two E3 ubiquitin ligases, Helicase like Transcription factor (HLTF) and Snf2 histone linker PHD RING helicase (SHPRH), which further extend the K164-mono-ubiquitin moiety on PCNA, generating K63-linked poly-ubiquitin chains. K63-poly-ubiquitylated PCNA recruits helicases and chromatin-remodeling factors that mediate fork reversal and the template-switching process (25,26). Thus, ubiquitin signaling is crucial for normal responses to both DNA DSB and DNA replication stress.

Increasingly, it is becoming clear that E3 ubiquitin ligases are versatile factors that can promote ubiquitylation of multiple substrates and can also function as chaperones or scaffolds in ubiquitylation-independent biological processes. For example, RAD18 responds to DSB and potentiates HR via a mechanism involving chaperoning of the recombinase RAD51D to DSB sites (27). RAD18 was recently shown to promote mono-ubiquitylation of histones in response to DSB (28). Although RAD18-RAD6 is the main PCNA-directed E3-E2 ubiquitin ligase complex in most cells, other E3 ligases including HLTF (29) and RNF8 may also promote PCNA mono-ubiquitylation (30). RNF168 was recently detected at DNA replication forks (31–33) and shown to promote normal S-phase progression (31). Therefore, E3 ligase-directed Ub-dependent signaling cascades are rarely specific to an individual type of DNA damage but rather form a complex network of integrated pathways that respond to a variety of different cellular stresses.

The mechanisms by which E3 ubiquitin ligases are targeted to different pathways potentially have an enormous impact on DNA damage tolerance in cancer cells. Inappropriate expression levels of E3 ubiquitin ligases or their inappropriate deployment to potential downstream substrates could lead to an imbalance in DNA repair pathway choice that results in genetic instability and/or therapy-resistance. Interestingly, RNF168 and RAD18 are both aberrantly overexpressed in many cancers (34–37), although the impact of altered RNF168/RAD18 on tumorigenic phenotypes is poorly understood.

Given the impact of E3 ubiquitin ligases on DNA damage tolerance and genome stability in both normal development and pathological states, it is important to understand how these enzymes are targeted to different genome maintenance pathways. Therefore, we sought to elucidate the mechanisms that target RNF168 to sites of DNA synthesis. Here, we identify a degenerate PIP box (DPIP) located within the C-terminus of RNF168 that mediates its recruitment to replication forks and facilitates DNA replication fork progression independently of its roles in DSB signaling to 53BP1.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Cancer cell lines H1299, U2OS and 293T were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and used for the described experiments without further authentication. U2OS RNF168−/− and U2OS RNF8−/− cell lines were gifted by Dr Daniel Durocher, University of Toronto, Canada. U2OS cells harboring Doxycycline-inducible human RNF168 were generated using the pINDUCER20 lentiviral vector (Addgene plasmid #44012; http://n2t.net/addgene:44012;RRID:Addgene_44012). All cell lines were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin–streptomycin (1%). All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma contamination as determined using the ATCC Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit (ATCC 301012K). Plasmid DNA and siRNA oligonucleotides were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, except that plasmid DNA and Lipofectamine 2000 concentrations used in each transfection reaction were decreased by 50% to reduce toxicity.

Adenovirus construction and infection

All other adenoviruses were constructed and purified as described previously (38). In brief, cDNAs encoding RNF168 variants were subcloned into the pACCMV shuttle vector. The resulting shuttle vectors were co-transfected with the pJM17 adenovirus plasmid into 293T cells. Recombinant adenovirus clones were isolated by plaque purification. The empty vector AdCon (used to control for adenovirus infections) was derived similarly but by co-transfection of the parental pACCMV shuttle vector with pJM17. Adenovirus particles were purified from 293T cell lysates by polyethylene glycol precipitation, CsCl gradient centrifugation and gel filtration column chromatography. Adenovirus preparations were quantified by A260 measurements. Cells were typically infected with 0.1−3 × 1010 pfu/ml by direct addition of purified virus to the culture medium.

RNA interference

siRNAs were delivered to cells using reverse-transfection or electroporation. For reverse-transfection, siRNAs were incubated with Lipofectamine 2000 and serum-free Optimem for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Cells were then trypsinized, resuspended in 1 ml of Optimem and added directly into the siRNA/Optimem/Lipofectamine solution to give a plating density of 50%, then incubated for 48 h. Electroporation was performed with a GenePulser Xcell (Bio-Rad), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Typically, electroporation was performed in a 0.2 cm cuvette containing 200 ml PBS, 2 × 106 cells and 5 nM siRNA using 150 V (10 mS, 1 pulse).

siRNA sequences used here were: control non-targeting siRNA, 5′-UAGCGACUAAACACAUCAA-3′ (Thermo Fisher Scientific); RNF168 siRNA #1,siGENOME Smartpool Dharmacon (Cat# M-007152–03); RNF168 siRNA #2, 5′-GAGAAUAUGAAGAGGAAAUUU-3′; RNF168 siRNA #3, 5′-GAAGAGUCGUGCCUACUGAUU-3′ (described by Fradet-Turcotte et al., (5)); RAD18, 5′-GAGCAUGGAUUAUCUAUUCAAUU-3′; RNF8, 5′-GAGAAGCUUACAGAUGUUU-3′.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

To prepare extracts containing soluble and chromatin-associated proteins, monolayers of cultured cells typically in 10 cm plates were washed twice in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in 500 μl of ice-cold cytoskeleton buffer (CSK buffer; 10 mM Pipes pH 6.8, 100 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (EGTA), 1 mM dithiothreitol, 0.1 mM ATP, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM NaF and 0.1% Triton X-100) freshly supplemented with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and Phostop (Roche). Lysates were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 4 min, to recover the CSK-insoluble nuclei. The detergent-insoluble nuclear fractions were washed once with 1 ml of CSK buffer and then resuspended in a minimal volume of CSK before analysis by sodiumdodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblotting. For all immunoprecipitation experiments, input samples were sonicated and normalized for protein concentration. Magnetic beads containing covalently conjugated antibodies against the HA tag (M180-11, MBL) were added to the extracts and incubations were performed 30min at 4 °C using rotating racks. Immune complexes were recovered using magnetic stands. The beads were washed five times with 1 ml CSK (1 min per wash), to remove nonspecifically associated proteins. The washed immune complexes were boiled in protein loading buffer for 10 min, to release and denature for SDS-PAGE.

For immunoblotting, cell extracts or immunoprecipitates were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes and incubated overnight with the following primary antibodies: PCNA (sc-56), Chk1 (sc-7898), β-actin (sc-130656), GAPDH (sc-32233), RNF8 (SC-134492), pRPA32 S4/8 (A300-245A) and RAD18 (A301-340A) from Bethyl Laboratories (Montgomery, TX); p-Chk1 S317 (2344) and H2A (2578) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology; γH2AX (05–636) and H2A K15ub (MABE11119) were from Millipore; H2B (Ab1790) antibody was from Abcam. Antibody dilutions used for immunoblotting were 1:1000, with exceptions for the following antibodies: PCNA (1:500), β-actin (1:5000), GAPDH (1:5000) and γH2AX (1:10 000).

Live-cell imaging and analysis

A high-content imager (InCell Analyzer 2200; Cytiva) was used to collect 12 fields of view from each well and the whole plate was imaged at 15-min intervals for 20 h with a Nikon 40×/0.95 Plan Apo objective. The 4-megapixel CMOS sensor acquired the FITC (ex 490/20, em 525/36) and Texas Red channels set to a 1.5 s exposure and 2.5D imaging method set to two steps to deconvolute a 2-micron z-range around the target plane. For the digital image, each pixel is equivalent to 0.162 μm of the sample, the pixel size in the camera itself is 6.5 μm. Images were then analyzed and segmented using INCell Developer, cell tracking was performed using Visual Basic for Applications, and the resultant feature data were stored in a custom MySQL database.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Twenty-four hours after adenoviral infection or plasmid transfection cultures were pulse labeled with EdU (Fisher #C10340) for 1 h, fixed with 2% PFA for 15 min, washed three times with PBS (5 min/wash), permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 5 min, washed three times with PBS (5 mi/wash) and blocked in PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 5% Donkey serum for 1 h. Next, the Edu click reaction was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions, followed by three 5 min washes in PBS. Staining with the primary antibodies (1:200 dilution) was performed for 1 h. Then primary antibody solution was removed by washing three times in PBS (5 min/wash). Secondary antibody staining (1:300 dilution) was performed for 1 h. Secondary antibody was removed using three washes in PBS (5 min/wash). Finally, Vectashield with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (#H-1200 Vector laboratories) was applied to the stained cells prior to imaging. For experiments in which cells were stained with PCNA antibody, cells were fixed and permeabilized with ice-cold methanol for 20 min prior to antibody staining. For proximity ligation assay (PLA), the cells were fixed and stained according to manufacturer’s instructions.

For co-localization analyses in Figure 1B and 1G, the images were acquired with Zeiss LSM 710/700 Spectral Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope with 40×/60× objective to obtain a total of 100 nuclei/condition/replicate. The images were subsequently analyzed in Fiji and scored by eye. Nuclei containing five or more co-localizing foci were considered ‘positive’.

DNA fiber analysis

Sub-confluent cells were labeled with 25 μM chloro-deoxyuridine (CldU) (Sigma-Aldrich) before media was removed and replaced with fresh media containing 250 μM iodo-deoxyuridine (IdU) (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were harvested at the indicated time, washed in PBS, added to glass slides and lysed in spreading buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 50 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS). DNA fibers were spread across the slides by tilting at an angle following lysis. Slides were air dried before fixation in methanol and acetic acid at a 3:1 ratio for 10 min. Prior to immunostaining, DNA was denatured using 2.5 M HCl for 1 h, 15 min before addition of blocking solution (1 % BSA, 1 % Tween-20 in PBS) for 30 min. Incorporated CldU and IdU were detected using mouse anti-BrdU (clone B44; BD Biosciences, 347583) and rat anti-BrdU (clone BU1/75, ICR1; Abcam, ab6326) primary antibodies, respectively, for 1.5 h. The mouse anti-BrdU and rat anti-BrdU primary antibodies are known to specifically cross-react with CldU and IdU and are routinely used by many labs that perform DNA fiber analysis. Slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min before addition of anti-rat Alexa Fluor 555 and anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescently labeled DNA fibers were imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ni microscope with NIS-Elements software (Nikon Instruments). Fiber track lengths were measured and recorded using ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health; NIH). A minimum of 250 fibers were counted for each of three independent experiments.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC; GE AutoITC 200) was conducted by titrating synthetic peptide (p21, GRKRRQTSMTDFYHSKRRLIFS-amide where underlined residues denote PIP box; syringe, 150–300 mM) into a solution of PCNA (cell; 30 mM) in TBS. Control experiments using peptide injected into buffer alone showed minimal heats with no evidence of titration. Analysis of the isotherm yielded Kd = 76.3 nM and stoichiometry = 0.93 (Microcal Origin software).

In vitro ubiquitylation assay

Ubiquitylation assays were performed in 25 μl reactions in which the components were added in the following order: dd H2O, 1× Energy regeneration solution (#B-10 R&D systems), 75 mM ubiquitin (#U-100H R&D systems), 1.6 mM FLAG-PCNA substrate (expressed and purified in bacteria), 0.1 mM E1 Ubiquitin Activating Enzyme (#E-304 R&D systems), 0.2 mM E2 conjugase (UbcH5c, #E2-627 R&D systems or RAD6 #E2-613 R&D Systems), 0.2 mM E3 ligase (recombinant bacterial RAD18-RAD6 complex or RNF168 both purified in-house). Reaction mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 15 or 30 min, then inactivated by adding 1x Laemelli buffer and boiling for 10 min. Reaction products were then resolved on SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting.

Results

RNF168 is recruited to DNA replication factories and DSB via independent mechanisms

To elucidate mechanisms of RNF168 recruitment to replication forks, we first asked whether the requirements for RNF168 localization to DSBs and replication factories were separable. It has been shown that the sustained accumulation of RNF168 at IR-induced foci (IRIF) requires both the Motif Interacting with Ubiquitin 2 (MIU2) and the UMI (UIM [Ubiquitin-Interacting Motif] and MIU-related) ubiquitin binding domains (4). Therefore, we compared the subcellular localization of WT RNF168 and a mutant lacking its MIU2 domain (ΔMIU2) in the absence of exogenous DNA damage and following exposure to hydroxyurea (HU) or IR. Consistent with previous observations (31), WT RNF168 co-localized with PCNA and this co-localization was enhanced in U2OS cells treated with HU but not IR (Figure 1A and B). Interestingly, the RNF168 ΔMIU2 mutant which is compromised for recruitment to IRIF (4) displayed enhanced co-localization with PCNA when compared to the WT protein (Figure 1A, B). Therefore, the mechanisms that recruit RNF168 to DSBs and sites of DNA replication stress are separable.

Although it is known that RNF8 functions to promote RNF168 recruitment to sites of DSBs (4), it has been suggested that RNF8 can also promote PCNA ubiquitylation and is therefore expected to be present at replication forks (30). However, while both RNF8 and RNF168 readily co-localized with γH2AX following exposure to neocarzinostatin (NCS), only RNF168 was recruited to replication forks in the presence/absence of replication stress (Figure 1C and D). Despite this lack of detectable localization of RNF8 to sites of DNA synthesis, we hypothesized that the ATM–MDC1–RNF8 DDR axis could still potentially function to facilitate the recruitment of RNF168 to replication forks. To investigate upstream determinants of RNF168 recruitment to forks, we monitored the cellular localization of RNF168 in cells lacking RNF8. Interestingly, RNF8−/− cells still retained the ability to recruit RNF168 to replication forks (Figure 1E–G). Therefore, the canonical Ub-dependent DDR pathway controlled by RNF8 and RNF168 is specific to DSB signaling but the relationship between RNF8 and RNF168 diverges during DNA synthesis.

The RNF168 C-terminus contains a novel PCNA-interacting motif

We sought to define the non-canonical mechanism of RNF168 recruitment to sites of DNA replication. It has been previously demonstrated that RNF168 can bind PCNA in cultured cells (31). However, it is unclear whether the RNF168–PCNA interaction is direct or mediated through its association with other replisome components. PCNA-binding proteins often associate with PCNA via PIP motifs sharing the consensus sequence Q-X-X-L/V/I/M-X-F-F, where X represents any amino acid (39). We noticed that the C-terminus of RNF168 contains a degenerate PIP-like sequence Q-K-S-V-F-Q-M-F (DPIP, Figure 2A) that could potentially serve as a PCNA-interacting motif. Q558-F566 resides in the C-terminal region of RNF168 that is highly disordered (Figure 2A) and therefore likely to mediate protein-protein interactions. Since PIP boxes tend to be located in disordered regions of PCNA-binding proteins (40,41) we reasoned that the PIP-like sequence may mediate the localization of RNF168 to replication forks. Indeed, when the RNF168 DPIP was modeled in the presence of PCNA using the ColabFold implementation of the AlphaFold2 multimer, it formed a convergent complex with PCNA (Figure 2B), with an overall similar binding mode to that of the reported PCNA-Polk PIP complexes (PDB: 2zvl).

To test the functionality of the putative DPIP box in RNF168 we transfected cells with FLAG-tagged RNF168 WT or an RNF168 mutant in which M565 and F566 were substituted with di-Alanine (hereafter designated ‘RNF168 ΔDPIP’). Then, we utilized immunofluorescence microscopy and proximity ligation assays (PLA) to ascertain whether loss of the DPIP compromised recruitment of RNF168 to replication forks. As shown in Figure 2C and 2D, ablating the DPIP box significantly compromised the re-localization of RNF168 to sites of ongoing DNA synthesis as judged by both immunofluorescence and PLA.

Consistent with our immunofluorescence microscopy experiments showing RNF168-PCNA co-localization, WT RNF168 co-immunoprecipitated with PCNA. The interaction between RNF168 and PCNA was moderately enhanced following exposure to HU but not CPT or a WEE1 inhibitor (Figure 2E, compare lanes 2, 3, 4 and 6). Interestingly, disruption of the RNF168 DPIP box compromised its interaction with PCNA in unperturbed cells (Figure 2E, compare lanes 2 and 8) and significantly reduced its binding following HU treatment (Figure 2E, compare lanes 3 and 9). These data indicate that the DPIP box in RNF168 primarily mediates its interaction with PCNA in unstressed cells but that in the presence of replication stress, PCNA interaction also relies on other RNF168 domains.

Co-immunoprecipitation of RNF168 with PCNA was unaffected by pharmacological ATM inhibition (Figure 2E, compare lanes 2 and 5). Moreover, the association of RNF168 with PCNA was robust and readily detected in cells lacking RNF8 (Figure 2F). PCNA-binding also was not reduced by the loss of the RNF168 MIU2 domain (Figure 2F, compare lanes 2 and 4). Therefore, essential regulatory proteins (ATM, RNF8) and domains (MIU2) required to elicit the Ub-dependent DDR in response to DSBs are dispensable for RNF168-PCNA association during S-phase. Of note, we observed that the RNF168 ΔMIU2 mutant bound more strongly to PCNA than WT RNF168 (Figure 2F, compare lanes 2 and 4), suggesting that the balance between the cellular pools of RNF168 is a binary choice dictated by binding either ubiquitylated histone or PCNA.

We reasoned that if RNF168 binds to PCNA via a PIP-box-mediated mechanism, the interaction would be competitively disrupted by a PIP box-containing peptide. We synthesized small peptide corresponding to the 20 amino acids sequences spanning the p21 PIP box and used ITC to confirm its high-affinity (Kd= 76.3 nM) association with PCNA (Figure 3A). As shown in Figure 3B, the p21 PIP box peptide inhibited the amount of PCNA-co-immunoprecipitating RNF168 in a dose-dependent manner (lanes 2–5). In similar experiments, a peptide corresponding to the Polη PIP box (which binds PCNA with low affinity, Kd ∼400 nM) (42) when compared with the p21 PIP domain (Kd ∼76 nM) did not disrupt PCNA–RNF168 interactions as effectively (Figure 3B, lanes 6–9). We conclude that the C-terminus of RNF168 contains a functional PIP box that mediates interactions with PCNA.

Interestingly, we noticed that the RNF168–PCNA interaction (as determined by co-IP) in HU-treated cells was more refractory to inhibition by the p21 PIP box peptide than the basal interaction in unstressed cells (Figure 3C, compare lanes 4 and 8). Because HU induces PCNA mono-ubiquitylation (Figure 3C), and RNF168 contains multiple ubiquitin-binding domains, we hypothesized that covalent modification of PCNA by ubiquitin may further stabilize the RNF168–PCNA complex via tandem affinity. To test the contribution of PCNA K164 ubiquitylation to RNF168 binding we used co-IP to compare association of RNF168 with wild-type PCNA and a K164R mono-ubiquitylation-resistant PCNA mutant. As shown in Figure 3D, RNF168 WT showed reduced binding to PCNA K164R when compared with PCNA WT (compare lanes 3 and 5) indicating that PCNA modification at K164 promotes RNF168 binding. We also performed co-IP experiments to determine association of RNF168 with a PCNA-ubiquitin fusion protein that mimics mono-ubiquitylated PCNA (16). As also shown in Figure 3D, RNF168 WT showed increased binding to PCNA-ubiquitin fusion protein when compared with PCNA WT (compare lanes 3 and 7). We conclude that RNF168 associates with PCNA via a degenerate PIP-box and that PCNA K164 mono-ubiquitylation further stimulates this binding.

RNF168 binds ubiquitylated proteins via its UMI, MIU1 and MIU2 domains (4,43). To identify ubiquitin-binding domains that cooperate with the DPIP sequence to mediate PCNA association, we generated RNF168 mutants in which the DPIP motif was inactivated individually or in combination with UMI, MIU1 or MIU2 domains. As shown in Figure 3E, UMI and MIU2 mutations had no effect on the PCNA–RNF168 interaction (compare lanes 3, 4 and 6). However, combining DPIP and MIU1 mutations largely abrogated the basal association between RNF168 and PCNA (compare lanes 3 and 5). In HU-treated cells (in which PCNA mono-ubiquitylation is increased above the basal levels found in proliferating cells) we observed similar cooperative effects of MIU1 and DPIP domains in mediating interactions between RNF168 and PCNA (Supplementary Figure S1). Taken together, the results of Figures 2–3 indicate that RNF168 is a bona fide PCNA-interacting protein, and that the DPIP and MIU1 domains of RNF168 mediate its association with mono-ubiquitylated PCNA.

RNF168–PCNA interactions promote PCNA mono-ubiquitylation

To date, the only substrates reported to be ubiquitylated by RNF168 are H2A(X), BLM, 53BP1 and JMJD2A (44–47). In RNF168-overexpressing cells, we observed robust mono- and poly-ubiquitylation of PCNA even in the absence of exogenous replication stress and this ubiquitylation was dependent on an intact DPIP box (Figures 2 and 3; Supplementary Figure S2).

To define the mechanism of RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation, we expressed WT RNF168 and mutants lacking either the DPIP box or the UMI domain (Δ121–157) and monitored the ubiquitylation of H2A and PCNA. As a complementary approach to testing the effect of RNF168–PCNA association on PCNA ubiquitylation, we also expressed an RNF168 mutant in which the DPIP was replaced with the canonical high-affinity PIP box from p21 (QKSVFQMF > QTSMTDFY, hereafter termed ‘RNF168 super-PIP’). The WT and mutant forms of RNF168 were expressed in both RNF8+/+ and RNF8−/− cells. Consistent with our previous observations, expression of WT RNF168 induced robust PCNA ubiquitylation that was ablated by inactivating mutations in the DPIP box (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 3 and 4). In contrast, mutating the DPIP to a high-affinity PIP box (super-PIP) significantly enhanced the RNF168-dependent ubiquitylation of PCNA (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 3 and 5). Therefore the PCNA-binding ability of RNF168 is essential for RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation. Strikingly, loss of the UMI domain of RNF168 (which prevents its binding to RNF8-ubiquitylated histone H1 and further recruitment of 53BP1 to DSBs) induced comparable levels of PCNA ubiquitylation to the super-PIP mutant in RNF8−/- cells (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 11 and 12). As expected, RNF8−/− cells failed to localize 53BP1 to foci in response to DSB (Supplementary Figure S2B). The results of our mutational analyses (Supplementary Figure S2) strengthen the idea that the DSB- and replication-associated functions of RNF168 exist in balance and disrupting DSB signaling (e.g. by deleting the UMI domain) enhances DNA replication functions. The ability of WT and ΔUMI RNF168 to stimulate PCNA ubiquitylation was enhanced in cells lacking RNF8, further indicating a competition between DSB- and DNA replication-related functions of RNF168. Interestingly, RNF168-dependent ubiquitylation of H2A was unaffected by loss or enhancement of the DPIP (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 3–5) or the presence/absence of RNF8 (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 3–6 with 9–12), highlighting that the DPIP box is required for PCNA-related functions but not DSB signaling events involving H2A ubiquitylation. As expected, RNF8−/− cells failed to localize 53BP1 correctly to sites of DSB unless complemented with a wild-type RNF8 cDNA (Supplementary Figure S2B). Ectopically expressed RNF168 induced ubiquitylation of both H2A and PCNA in RNF8−/− cells, fully consistent with previous findings that RNF168 can override the effects of an RNF8 deficiency (4).

Since PCNA mono-ubiquitylation is a well characterized marker of replication stress, we investigated whether the increased ubiquitylation of PCNA following the expression of RNF168 arose indirectly through activation of the ATR-dependent replication stress response. In contrast with the robust phosphorylation of Chk1 and RPA2 in response to UV irradiation (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 1 and 2), Chk1 and RPA2 phosphorylation were not induced following the over-expression of WT or mutant RNF168 (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 2 with 3–6). However, we noticed that the expression of RNF168 was associated with the increased recruitment of RAD18 to chromatin (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 2 with 3–6). Chromatin-binding of RAD18 did not correlate with levels of induced PCNA ubiquitylation, since the ΔDPIP, super-PIP and ΔUMI mutants of RNF168 all induced comparable levels of RAD18 recruitment (Supplementary Figure S2A, compare lanes 2 with 3–6) suggesting that the RNF168-induced increase in H2A ubiquitylation triggers RAD18 recruitment rather than promoting RAD18-mediated PCNA ubiquitylation. Importantly, ectopically expressed WT RNF168 did not induce defects in DNA replication licensing or DNA synthesis (as determined by flow cytometry, see Supplementary Figure S2C), indicating that RAD18 recruitment was not a secondary effect caused by DNA replication stress.

To test the role of RAD18 in mediating RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation, we ectopically expressed WT and mutant forms of RNF168 in RAD18−/− H1299 cells with or without co-expressed RAD18. As expected, the patterns of PCNA ubiquitylation induced by WT RNF168 relative to mutant RNF168 variants were similar in RAD18-replete cells (Supplementary Figure S2D, lanes 8–13) and RNF8+/+ and RNF8−/− U2OS cells (Supplementary Figure S2A). Most unexpectedly however, RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation was also readily detectable in RAD18−/− cells, particularly for the super-PIP mutant (Supplementary Figure S2D, lanes 1–6). Similar to results of experiments in RAD18-expressing cultures, PCNA ubiquitylation levels were lower in RNF168 ΔDPIP-expressing cells when compared with cells expressing RNF168 WT (and higher in RNF168 super-PIP-expressing cells when compared with cells expressing RNF168 WT). RAD18 co-expression clearly augmented RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation (Supplementary Figure S2D), but we conclude that RNF168 is also capable of promoting PCNA ubiquitylation independently of RAD18. Moreover, although RAD18-RAD6 is the predominant PCNA K164-directed E3 ligase complex, RAD18-dependent PCNA ubiquitylation can be augmented by the ligase activity of RNF168, in a manner that is governed by the affinity of its DPIP box.

The results of Supplementary Figure S2D suggested that RNF168 activates a PCNA-directed E3 ubiquitin ligase other than RAD18, or that PCNA is a direct target of RNF168-induced ubiquitylation. Other E3 ligases reported to promote PCNA ubiquitylation include RNF8 (30) and HLTF (29). Since RNF8 is dispensable for RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation (Supplementary Figure S2A), we tested a possible relationship between HLTF and RNF168. However, siRNA-mediated depletion of HLTF did not affect RNF168-induced PCNA ubiquitylation in either WT or RAD18−/− cells (Supplementary Figure S2E). Therefore, we performed in vitro ubiquitylation assays to test whether PCNA is a direct target of RNF168.

Given that RNF168 has been reported to form a complex with RAD6 (48), we initially used RAD6 as the E2 conjugating enzyme in the in vitro ubiquitylation reactions. However, we did not observe any significant ubiquitylation of PCNA by RNF168 in the presence of RAD6. Since RNF168 has also been shown to utilize UBCH5 as an E2 enzyme in vitro, we carried out the ubiquitylation reactions using UBCH5 instead of RAD6. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2F, purified RNF168 promoted mono-ubiquitylation of recombinant PCNA in the presence of UBCH5 indicating that RNF168 may target E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes such as UBCH5 to PCNA. Interestingly, a combination of RAD18–RAD6 and RNF168 led to a synergistic increase in PCNA mono-ubiquitylation in vitro (Supplementary Figure S2F), recapitulating the results obtained when we co-expressed RAD18 and RNF168 in cultured cells (Supplementary Figure S2D). Taken together, the results of Supplementary Figure S2 identify RNF168 as a new potential mediator of PCNA ubiquitylation.

RNF168-dependent S-phase progression requires PCNA-interacting motifs

It has been shown that RNF168 is required to maintain efficient DNA replication in the absence of exogenous replication stress (31). It was suggested that this function of RNF168 requires not only its E3 ligase activity and ubiquitin binding capabilities but also key members of the canonical Ub-DDR including H2AX, ATM, RNF8 and 53BP1. In light of our finding that RNF168 is specifically targeted to sites of DNA replication via its DPIP box, we reasoned that loss of PCNA-binding activity could potentially compromise the ability of RNF168 to sustain DNA synthesis.

To investigate the role of PCNA-binding in RNF168-dependent replication fork movement, we reconstituted RNF168−/− U2OS cells with either WT RNF168 or with RNF168 mutants lacking the DPIP box and measured DNA replication dynamics using DNA fiber analysis. For comparison, we also complemented RNF168-null cells with an MIU2 domain mutant of RNF168. Consistent with previous observations (31), complementation of RNF168-null cells with WT RNF168 but not a mutant lacking the MIU2 domain restored replication fork speed (Figure 4A). Interestingly, the RNF168 ΔDPIP, ΔMIU1 and Super-PIP mutants fully corrected the reduced fork speed of RNF168−/− cells, conferring fork velocities comparable to those of WT RNF168-complemented cells (Figure 4A). However, the RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 double mutant failed to correct the reduced fork velocity of RNF168−/− cells. Moreover, cells expressing the RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 double mutant also displayed increased fork stalling comparable to the vector complemented line (Figure 4B), suggestive that the ability of RNF168 to bind PCNA plays a role in regulating replication fork stability.

We also determined the effect of different RNF168 mutations on recovery from HU-induced replication fork stalling. As shown in Figure 4C and D, Wild-type, RNF168, RNF168 ΔDPIP and RNF168 ΔMIU1 fully corrected the defective recovery of stalled replication forks RNF168−/- cells after HU treatment. However, the RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 double mutant did not support efficient recovery of stalled DNA replication forks. We conclude that both domains involved in mediating association with PCNA (namely DPIP and MIU1) are necessary for RNF168 functions in DNA replication fork progression and recovery from fork stalling.

Many of the factors that confer DNA replication stress tolerance have roles in preventing degradation of reversed DNA replication forks (49). Therefore, we determined the effect of RNF168 and its PCNA association on the stability of DNA replication forks that are reversed following an HU-induced DNA replication block. As shown in Figure 4E, HU-reversed forks were aberrantly degraded in RNF168-deficient cells when compared with wild-type controls, which could be corrected by ectopic expression of WT RNF168 or RNF168ΔMIU2, but not RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1. These results suggest that the association of RNF168 with PCNA is necessary for the protection of reversed forks. Interestingly, the effect of an RNF168-deficiency on fork degradation, or an inability of RNF168 to bind PCNA, was epistatic with 53BP1 loss (Figure 4E). We conclude that PCNA-associated RNF168 and 53BP1 act in the same pathway for preventing the excessive degradation of stalled DNA replication forks.

We also performed live cell imaging to determine the precise kinetics with which RNF168-dependent 53BP1 foci form in NCS-treated U2OS cells (Figure 5B and D). Our experiments established that RNF168-dependent formation of 53BP1 foci plateaued 1 h following treatment with NCS (Figure 5C). Discussion

RNF168 has recently been implicated in regulating DNA replication in times of stress (31,50,51), but its mechanism of recruitment to DNA replication forks has not been addressed. Importantly, our study identifies the mechanism by which RNF168 utilizes different motifs to target its E3 ligase activity to distinct replication stress and DSB-responsive branches of the DDR. In relation to this, we have identified a previously unrecognized degenerate PIP box that is critical for mediating RNF168 recruitment to DNA replication forks. Moreover, we have shown that the MIU1 domain of RNF168 stabilizes its localization to replication forks by binding to ubiquitylated PCNA. Crucially, we show that the PCNA-targeting DPIP and MIU1 motifs are essential for the protection and restart of stalled replication forks.

Penengo and colleagues previously demonstrated that RNF168 is present at sites of ongoing replication and that both its RING domain and ubiquitin-binding ability are required for unperturbed fork progression (31). However, it was not clear from their study whether the requirement for RNF168 to maintain replication progression in the absence of exogenous genotoxin exposures was related to its role in responding to spontaneously arising DSBs or replication stress. Moreover, since the RNF168 mutant utilized in that study had mutations in all three ubiquitin-binding domains (i.e. UMI, MIU1 and MIU2), the contribution of the individual ubiquitin-binding domains to the replication function of RNF168 was not determined. In relation to this, it was recently shown that a truncated form of RNF168 containing only its RING, UMI and MIU1 domains is capable of mediating DNA DSB-induced 53BP1 foci despite being unable to localize to foci itself (52). This suggests that the slowing of forks that we observed in cells expressing the RNF168 ΔMIU2 mutant is not related to a failure to recruit 53BP1.

Consistent with this, we showed that cells expressing RNF168 ΔMIU2 are proficient for RNF168/53BP1-dependent fork protection. In contrast, whilst cells expressing the RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 mutant also displayed a decrease in unperturbed fork progression (similar to the RNF168 ΔMIU2 mutant), they additionally displayed increases in fork stalling and reduced replication fork restart. Importantly, we demonstrated that cells expressing the RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 mutant exhibited increased fork degradation, which is epistatic with loss of 53BP1. Combined, our results suggest that RNF168 recruitment to damaged forks is mediated by its DPIP motif and MIU1 domain interacting with ubiquitylated PCNA. This allows RNF168 to trigger localized recruitment of 53BP1 to protect stalled replication forks from degradation and facilitate their restart. Given that the reduction in unperturbed replication in cells expressing the RNF168 ΔMIU2 mutant is not associated with an increase in stalled forks, a reduction in replication fork restart or increased replication fork degradation, we postulate that the MIU2 domain is linked to loss of another replication-associated RNF168 function, perhaps related to the direct ubiquitylation of replisome components to regulate fork progression, or to the resolution of other types of replication stress, for example, replication-transcription conflicts (53).

Significantly, our experiments with the PCNA-interaction-deficient RNF168 ΔDPIP/ΔMIU1 mutant suggest that replisome-associated RNF168 cooperates with RAD18 to promote PCNA mono-ubiquitylation. This then raises the question ‘what is the basis for the cooperative PCNA ubiquitylation by RNF168 and RAD18?’ Our experiments using RAD18−/− cells (Supplementary Figure S2) suggest that RAD18 may be necessary for the initial ubiquitylation of one PCNA molecule within the PCNA trimer which further stimulates RNF168 binding via the MIU1 motif, promoting ubiquitylation of other unmodified PCNA subunits.

Since PCNA monoubiquitylation occurs spontaneously during S-phase, even without exogenously induced DNA damage or stress (54–56), we consider it most likely that S-phase-associated RAD18 activity initiates the feed-forward cascade of PCNA-ubiquitylation, which is subsequently amplified by the combined actions of RAD18 and RNF168. Given that PCNA mono-ubiquitylation can also be induced via several other E3 ubiquitin ligases including RFWD3 (57), CRL4 (58) and HLTF (29), it is plausible that these may also cooperate with RNF168 to stimulate PCNA ubiquitylation.

It is important to note that RING-finger E3 ligases such as RAD18 and RNF168 lack catalytic activity but act as specificity factors that target E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes to their substrates (59). Therefore, it is possible that the synergistic effect of RAD18 and RNF168 on PCNA mono-ubiquitylation could be mediated by RAD6 exchanging between RAD18 and RNF168. Potentially consistent with this, RNF168 has been previously reported to associate with RAD6 (48). Alternatively, it is possible that the direct interaction of RNF168 with PCNA via its DPIP promotes RAD18-RAD6 recruitment which in turn mediates PCNA ubiquitylation. Future efforts will be needed to define the detailed mechanism by which RAD18, RAD6 and RNF168 cooperate to ubiquitylate PCNA.

While the synergistic effects of RAD18 and RNF168 on PCNA ubiquitylation can be recapitulated in an in vitro system with a limited number of components, it is likely that regulation of PCNA for modification in the context of a damaged replication fork, which is surrounded by components of the replisome and chromatin, is far more complicated. In this respect, it should be noted that RNF168 and RAD18 both specifically bind histone H2A K13/15-ubiquitylated nucleosomes (60). Therefore, the interactions of RAD18 and RNF168 with damaged chromatin might also influence PCNA ubiquitylation. For example, RNF168-dependent H2A-ubiquitylation in the vicinity of the replication fork could amplify the local concentration of RAD18 to ensure optimal PCNA ubiquitylation. Cipolla et al. show that Polη is recruited directly to ubiquitylated H2A (32). Since Polη is part of the RAD18 complex in cells, RAD18-associated Polη might also be recruited to RNF168-modified chromatin. The RNF168 and RAD18-mediated feed-forward loop that amplifies PCNA ubiquitylation is analogous to the cooperativity between RNF8 and RNF168 which amplifies histone ubiquitylation at DNA DSBs (1,2,61).

PCNA ubiquitylation at DNA replication forks is also amplified via a cooperative interaction between RAD18 and Polη (62). Similar to Polη and other Y-family TLS polymerases, we show that RNF168 interactions with PCNA are dependent on cooperation between a PIP box and a ubiquitin-binding motif (which in the case of RNF168 is MIU1).

Our study reveals some interesting similarities and differences with respect to the mechanisms by which RAD18 and RNF168 are recruited to sites of DNA replication stress. RAD18 has long been recognized as the major PCNA-directed E3 ligase in mammalian cells. Although an N-terminal region 350 amino acid region of RAD18 was shown to interact with PCNA (63), to our knowledge this region lacks a PIP box. In response to replication stalling, RAD18 is first recruited to the vicinity of PCNA via an interaction with RPA (12), and subsequently associates with PCNA via its binding partner Polη (62). In this context, Polη serves as a scaffold which provides a PIP box (which binds PCNA in a ubiquitylation-independent manner), as well as UBZ domains (which link RAD18 to PCNA in its mono-ubiquitylated state). Here, we show that RNF168, which has not been recognized previously as a PCNA-directed E3 ligase, has both a PIP box and a MIU domain that link RNF168 to PCNA in its unmodified as well as ubiquitylated forms, respectively. In other words, PCNA-binding features that are present in RNF168 (PIP box and ubiquitin-binding domain) are lacking in RAD18, but provided by Polη.

Our results also reveal yet another layer of intersection and crosstalk between the genome maintenance pathways that respond to DSB and DNA replication stress. For example, RAD18 was first described as a PCNA-directed E3 ligase that promotes TLS. However, subsequent studies showed that RAD18 also promotes DSB repair via HR (27,64), facilitates the chromatin retention of 53BP1 in G1 (65) and directly ubiquitylates histones (28). Conversely, RNF8 and RNF168 were first identified as mediators of the DSB response (4), yet both may also mono-ubiquitylate PCNA directly (see Zhang et al. (30) and this study) and thereby influence genome maintenance at the replication fork independently of DSB signaling.

Given that RNF168 is a rate-limiting mediator of histone ubiquitylation following the generation of DSBs, there has been tremendous interest in determining the downstream targets and consequences of excessive RNF168 activity. RNF168 is overexpressed in neoplastic cells (36) including a subset of BRCA1-mutant cancers (37). Overabundance of RNF168 shifts DSB repair pathway choice from HR toward the relatively error-prone NHEJ (36). Remarkably, ectopically expressed RNF168 promotes mutagenic NHEJ but does not affect physiological NHEJ-mediated repair of DSBs that arise during immunoglobulin CSR (37). It was also shown recently that ectopic RNF168 expression promotes break-induced DNA replication in BRCA1-deficient cells (50). Clearly therefore, pathological changes in RNF168-mediated DSB repair are likely to contribute to the DNA damage tolerance and genetic instability of cancer cells (36,37).

Based upon our results showing that RNF168 stimulates PCNA mono-ubiquitylation, it is possible that overactive RNF168 in cancer cells would not only confer chromosomal rearrangements and other forms of genetic instability due to deregulated NHEJ but could also increase SNV burden due to aberrant activation of error-prone Y-family TLS polymerases. In support of this, RAD18, is also pathologically overexpressed and/or aberrantly activated in tumors (34,35). Moreover, it has been shown that the levels of RAD18 mRNA correlate directly with overall SNV counts in many cancer types (35). It will be interesting to determine whether similar correlations exist between RNF168 expression and SNV burden in cancer. Clearly further experiments are necessary to determine the impact of excessive RNF168 on mutability of cancer genomes.

Our work has not addressed the functional role if any of RNF168-induced PCNA mono and poly-ubiquitylation. For example, in Supplementary Figure S2A, we show that inactivating mutations in the DPIP box ablates the RNF168-dependent enhancement of PCNA ubiquitylation yet these same mutations still fully support RNF168’s ability to promote replication fork progression (Figure 4D) and replication fork protection (Figure 4E). Thus, while RNF168 has the potential to promote RAD18–RAD6-dependent PCNA monoubiquitylation, this activity is not required for the RNF168 functional roles in DNA replication that are discovered in the current manuscript.

However, it is possible that RNF168-dependent PCNA-ubiquitylation participates in alternative genome maintenance pathways. Raschle et al. performed mass spectrometry to study the dynamic recruitment of DNA repair factors to chromatin undergoing ICL repair (66). Those workers showed that Polη, RNF168 and RAD18 were coincident on chromatin during the TLS-mediated phases of ICL repair. Therefore, the RAD18–RNF168-mediated amplification of PCNA ubiquitylation described here might influence the kinetics/efficiency of ICL repair. While PCNA mono-ubiquitylation is central to TLS polymerase activation, several proteins other than Y-family DNA polymerases associate with mono-ubiquitylated PCNA including Spartan (67) and DNA cross-link repair 1A (DCLRE1A) (68). Moreover, the mono-ubiquitylated K164 residue of PCNA is further ubiquitylated by human RAD5 homologues SHPRH and HLTF to promote TS and DNA damage avoidance (29). Therefore, it is probable that RNF168-mediated PCNA ubiquitylation impacts not only TLS but also additional genome maintenance processes including spartan-mediated repair of DNA–protein crosslinks, DCLRE1A-mediated interstrand cross-link (ICL) repair and TS. Notably, the PCNA poly-ubiquitylation that mediates TS is a rare and low-abundance post-translational modification that is typically challenging to detect using PCNA immunoblotting of whole chromatin extracts (29). In our experiments, RNF168 induced an unusually robust PCNA poly-ubiquitylation response, perhaps hinting at a role for RNF168 in TS.

A limitation of our study is that several experiments presented here relied on ectopically expressed and epitope-tagged proteins to detect association of RNF168 with its binding partners and subcellular distribution of DDR proteins. In our experiments with appropriate positive and negative controls, none of the commercial, or home-made antibodies available to us were sufficiently sensitive to allow co-IP or co-localization analyses. Therefore, out of necessity we have worked with tagged forms of RNF168. Nevertheless, these approaches are routinely used within the DDR field. For example, over the last few decades most papers in the TLS field have studied regulation and protein–protein interactions of RAD18 and Y-family polymerases using ectopically expressed tagged proteins. Thus historically, with the use of appropriate controls, studies with ectopically expressed proteins have generated mechanisms of action and accepted paradigms for ubiquitin signaling and genome maintenance.

In this regard the PCNA-interaction-deficient RNF168 ΔDPIP ΔMIU1 allele described here is a clean separation-of-function mutant that will be a valuable tool for helping to define additional contributions of the RNF168–PCNA signaling axis to DNA damage tolerance and genome maintenance.
