CSF proteomic quantitative trait loci mapping reveals genetic insights into Alzheimer’s disease

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the identification of numerous genetic risk variants for Alzheimer's disease (AD), mechanisms through which these variants act remain unclear. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder, pathologically defined by extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular tau tangles. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on AD have identified up to 80 genetic risk loci for AD, revealing insights into the genetic architecture of AD [1–6]. With APOE-ε4 being the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, there is a substantial role of genetic factors in the development of AD (h2-AD = ~ 58–79%) [7]. A key challenge has been interpreting the functional impact of these genetic loci and studying ongoing (patho)physiological processes in the living brain of patients. Identifying specific protein levels affected by genetic variation can provide valuable insights into the underlying biological pathways implicated in AD. The identification of these pathways may help in finding novel treatment targets, which has been shown to be more successful when they are supported by genetic evidence [8].

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is in close contact with the brain and contains thousands of proteins [9]. CSF protein levels reflect ongoing biological processes in the brain, and its use has proven useful in monitoring as well as diagnosing disease. Genome-wide protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) mapping studies assess the relationship between SNPs and CSF protein levels and have identified specific loci related to CSF levels of AD biomarkers [10, 11] and other markers of neurodegeneration [12, 13]. Further pQTL efforts on larger proteomics panels have identified many genetic variants that provide important insights into the etiology underlying disease and traits [14–18]. One example is the genetic AD risk region MS4A, which is related to altered CSF sTREM2 concentrations [19]. Since the time of these first studies, mass spectrometry techniques have improved and can now detect more than 3000 proteins in CSF. Furthermore, pQTL studies so far investigated associations in cohorts with control and patient samples, and so it remains unclear if there are pQTLs that act differently in the presence or absence of amyloid-beta pathology [17]. The identification of pQTL effects specific to the presence or absence of amyloid-beta could offer valuable insights into genetic factors that may influence amyloid-beta accumulation (relevant for AD protection), as well as downstream effects that occur once amyloid-beta pathology forms (AD progression).

In this study, we aimed to improve our understanding of the biological pathways underlying the genetic risk for AD, by conducting a genome-wide protein quantitative trait locus (pQTL) study encompassing 2,215 CSF proteins measured using untargeted TMT mass spectrometry in a cohort of N = 487 subjects along the clinical spectrum of AD (n = 220 control subjects, n = 87 MCI, n = 180 AD-type dementia). We identified 399 CSF pQTLs with study-wide significance (P < 5 × 10–8 / 2,215 proteins = 2.26 × 10–11), representing 222 CSF proteins and 394 unique independent loci, of which 69% have not been detected before in CSF QTL studies [12–17]. Using untargeted CSF proteomic data from the independent EMIF-AD MBD cohort, we replicated 227 CSF pQTL loci. By integrating CSF pQTLs with AD GWAS summary statistics we provide genetic support for a pathophysiological role of several CSF proteins in AD, including novel (i.e., CSF levels of FCGR2A, GALNT6, HLA-DPB1, HS3ST1 and PLXDC2) and previously observed CSF pQTLs (i.e., CSF levels of ACE, CD33, CTSH, SIRPA, BSN). We identified 107 putative CSF pQTLs associated with amyloid pathology (P < 5 × 10–8), of which none exceeded the study-wide Bonferroni threshold (P < 2.26 × 10–11). Moreover, only one signal, CSF LRP1 levels with gene CECR2, showed nominal replication in an independent cohort. Context-dependent findings for amyloid pathology should therefore be considered exploratory and suggest that larger studies are needed to determine whether a subset of pQTL effects is genuinely dependent on amyloid-beta pathology. Altogether, this study provides insight into the effects of GWAS-based risk associations in AD on disease pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Cohorts

The discovery study sample included cognitively healthy controls and patients with dementia, from three cohorts including the Amsterdam Dementia Cohort (ADC) [20], the EMIF-AD 90 + study [21] and the Twin Study [22]. The ADC started collecting samples in the year 2000 and is an ongoing, observational follow-up study of patients who visited the memory clinic of the Alzheimer Center Amsterdam [20]. All patients receive a standardized multidisciplinary assessment as part of the diagnostic workup, consisting of medical history, informant-based history, neurological and medical examination, neuropsychological investigation, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), standard laboratory work-up and lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assessment. Dementia was diagnosed according to diagnostic guidelines for MCI or AD [23, 24]. The EMIF-AD 90 + study aimed to identify factors associated with resilience to cognitive impairment in the oldest-old [21]. For the Twin Study, monozygotic twins were recruited from the Netherlands Twin Register to participate in the PreclinAD study, a component of the EMIF-AD project (http://www.emif.eu/). In this study, one subject per twin pair was included [25].

As replication cohort, we used the European Medical Information Framework for Alzheimer's Disease Multimodal Biomarker Discovery (EMIF-AD MBD) [26]. The aim of EMIF-AD MDB is to improve the access to, and use of, health-related data, with emphasis on AD-related biomarker research. The EMIF-AD MBD study retrospectively combined several types of data from 1221 subjects that had been collected across different centers [26]. This cohort includes individuals with normal cognition, MCI, AD-type dementia. In total, 242 subjects had both genetic and CSF proteomic data available.

Human ethics and consent to participate

All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All studies were approved by the ethics committee of the Amsterdam UMC (location VUmc) and the Biobank Research Ethics Committee of the Amsterdam UMC (location VUmc). For EMIF-AD MBD, local institutional review boards approved the procedures for this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cerebrospinal fluid data

CSF was collected by lumbar puncture with a 25-gauge needle and syringe and stored at –80 °C in polypropylene tubes, according to international guidelines [20, 26]. For the discovery cohort (ADC), CSF levels of amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42), total tau (t-tau) and hyperphosphorylated 181 tau (p-tau) were determined as part of the diagnostic work-up using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) (Innotest: Fujirebio, Ghent, Belgium) [20]. CSF Aβ42 values were adjusted for drift as described previously [27]. Biomarker abnormality cut-offs for the ADC cohort are CSF Aβ42 < 813 pg/ml, CSF t-tau > 375 pg/ml, ratio t-tau/Aβ42 > 0.52 [28]. For the replication cohort (EMIF-AD MBD), CSF levels of Aβ42 and Aβ40 were measured using V-PLEX Plus Aβ Peptide Panel 1 (6E10) Kit from Meso Scale Discovery (MSD, Rockville, MD) and a CSF Aβ42/40 ratio < 0.06 was defined as abnormal [26]. For EMID-AD MBD, CSF p-tau abnormality status was provided by the local cohorts (as derived in clinical laboratory practice [29]). In both cohorts, proteomics was determined with tandem mass tag (TMT) liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using a 16-plex for the discovery cohort, and an 11-plex for the replication cohort [30]. Prior to mass spectrometry, CSF protein content was enzymatically digested into peptides, labeled with TMT and fractionated and analyzed with LC–MS/MS. Details on TMT procedures can be found in [30].

Genotyping, quality control and imputation

Discovery samples were genotyped on the Illumina Genome Screening Array (GSA) v1, human genome build 37. Standard quality control methods were performed, as described in depth elsewhere [31]. Briefly, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.5%, significant deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1 × 10−6) in the total sample of founder individuals, or a variant call rate < 98% were excluded from analyses. Individuals with sex mismatches or an individual call rate less than 98% were excluded from analyses. SNPs were imputed to the TOPMED reference panel (https://imputation.biodatacatalyst.nhlbi.nih.gov). To identify ethnic outliers, a principal component analysis of ancestry (PCA) was performed (based on 1000Genomes clustering) using EIGENSOFT. Individuals of non-European ancestry were excluded from analysis. Relatedness was assessed through identity by descent, and family relations up to second-degree (i.e., identity-by-descent ≥ 0.3) were excluded.

Replication samples from EMIF-AD MBD were genotyped using GSA array and SNPs were imputed using Minimac 3 to the Haplotype Reference Consortium reference (HRC) panel [32], as described in detail previously [30, 33]. Since this genetic data of EMIF-AD MBD was in build hg19/37, we lifted over all significant lead SNPs from the discovery phase to build hg19/37. For lead SNPs unavailable in the replication data, we selected a proxy SNP in strong LD (R2 > 0.8) within ± 1 kb of the strongest signal.

Functional annotation independent pQTL SNPs

For each protein we selected significant SNPs by using a significance threshold of P < 2.26 × 10–11. To only select independent genetic regions, SNPs were then clumped with LD R2 < 0.2 (–clump in Plink). All remaining SNPs for any protein were then concatenated, de-duplicated, and reformatted for annotation. The GENCODE v.41 basic gene annotation GTF database (https://www.gencodegenes.org/human/stats_41.html) [34] was used to interpret the functional consequences of significant pQTL SNPs. R package “ChIPseeker” (Bioconductor version: Release 3.17) was used for the annotation [35]. Promoter regions were defined as done in Watanabe et al., (2017), 250 bp up- and 500 bp down-stream of transcription start site (TSS) [36].

Assessment of previous CSF pQTL literature

To assess the relevance and novelty of the identified pQTLs, we examined whether the 399 independent lead SNPs were previously reported in CSF pQTL GWAS studies. We searched PubMed before August 2023 and found a total of six genome-wide studies on CSF proteomic data [12–17]. For each study, we downloaded the significant SNPs and lifted them over to hg38 using LiftOver (Lift Genome Annotations (ucsc.edu)). CSF pQTL signals were identified as novel if the association has not been reported within ± 1 Mb of the strongest signal for the exact same protein (based on Uniprot ID). We performed an additional filter on rsID, to examine whether previously reported pQTLs included similar lead SNPs.

Assessment of Alzheimer’s disease risk loci as pQTL

We assessed the overlap between AD risk loci and CSF pQTLs to gain mechanistic insight into biological pathways of genetic risk for AD. We selected AD risk SNPs from AD GWAS literature [1–5, 37] and the GWAS catalog (term 'Alzheimer’s disease', April 20, 2023) and SNPs in LD with these (r2 > 0.8, LD-window 1000000, TOPMED European ancestry) resulting in 24,102 AD-related SNPs.

Mendelian randomization

To evaluate potential causal effects of CSF protein levels on AD risk, we performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using the TwoSampleMR R package. For each AD-related CSF protein with at least one genome-wide significant pQTL (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), we selected SNPs as instrumental variables (IVs) and tested their associations with AD risk using GWAS summary statistics from the Bellenguez et al. 2022 meta-analysis (N = 1,126,563 individuals, 111,326 clinically diagnosed / proxy AD cases and 677,663 controls) [1], and AD pathology using neuropathological summary statistics on Braak stage and CERAD score (N = 7,804 autopsied participants) [38]. Exposure (pQTL) and outcome (AD/AD pathology GWAS) datasets were harmonized using harmonise_data() to align effect alleles and ensure consistent orientation of SNP effects. Palindromic SNPs were excluded unless allele frequency information allowed strand resolution. MR was performed using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary analysis. To assess the robustness of findings, we also applied MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode estimators. Potential horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analysis to examine the influence of individual SNPs on overall MR estimates. Proteins were considered to have evidence for a causal effect on AD/AD pathology risk when the IVW estimate reached nominal significance (P < 0.05) and showed consistent direction and magnitude of effect across sensitivity methods.

Proteome-wide association analysis (PWAS)

To identify proteins whose local-regulated CSF level is associated with AD and three other neurological phenotypes, we performed PWAS using FUSION software (http://gusevlab.org/projects/fusion) [39]. First, we generated weights for all CSF proteins with at least one genome-wide significant signal (P < 5 × 10–8). We restricted to loci ± 500 Kb around the lead SNPs for each protein, with the note that some proteins were polygenic and had more than one associated genetic loci. For polygenic proteins, any significant SNPs outside of the ± 500 Kb range of another genome-wide significant SNP were included as a separate model. Age, sex and the first three genotype PCs were included as covariates in the –covar flag of the FUSION.compute_weights.R script.

We used the FUSION.assoc_test.R script (default settings) to test for association between the CSF protein weights and GWAS for neurodegenerative disorders, including AD [1], dementia with Lewy bodies [40], stroke [41] and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [42]. To account for LD structure we used 1000 Genomes data (all ancestries, build 38) as LD reference panel. The –coloc flag was included to perform colocalization on any proteins that had an association with the trait of interest with P-TWAS < 0.05 [43]. Colocalization analysis further narrowed down the protein-trait associations to those with a single variant influencing both the CSF protein level and trait, and associations with a PP4 > 0.8 were classified as having supported colocalization evidence.

Results on gene–tissue associations per phenotype were corrected for multiple comparisons using a 5% FDR significance threshold. Significant PWAS loci were identified as novel if the strongest associated SNP was not nominally significant (P > 1 × 10–5) in the corresponding GWAS within ± 1 Mb of the transcriptional start site of the gene’s region.

Statistical analysis

Association analyses between genetic variants and CSF protein levels were performed using PLINK version 2.0 [44, 45]. Among the 2,472 proteins that passed initial quality controls, GWAS was performed using linear regressions with covariate adjustment for sex, age and the first three genotype principal components (PC1-3) to account for population stratification. To ensure robustness, we excluded 257 proteins (10%) from downstream analysis due to evidence of genomic inflation (λ < 0.95 or λ > 1.05). In addition, quantile–quantile (QQ) and Manhattan plots of all pQTL associations were visually inspected for the presence of analytical artifacts, such as major deviations from the null distribution and atypical genome-wide association patterns. The final analysis included 2,215 CSF proteins (Table S2). Genetic variants with MAF < 0.5% or a imputation quality R2 < 0.3 were excluded. To examine whether pQTL effects varied by amyloid-beta pathology, we repeated the GWAS including an interaction term for CSF amyloid-beta abnormality status (–gxe parameter in PLINK). A CSF pQTL signal was considered novel if no prior CSF pQTL study reported a genome-wide significant association [12–17], within ± 1 Mb of the lead SNP for the same protein. All follow-up statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using RStudio (version 4.0.3, Bunny-Wunnies freak out, R Development Core Team).

Results

Study sample

Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table S1. The discovery sample consisted of N = 487 subjects of European ancestry (280 [57%] women, average age 65.5 ± 9.7 years), including 220 controls, 87 subjects with MCI and 180 patients with AD-type dementia. After QC, the final GWAS included 2,215 CSF proteins (Table S2). For replication we used the European EMIF-AD MBD cohort, which included N = 242 subjects with genetic and TMT mass spectrometry CSF proteomics available (131 [53.9%] women, average age 66.8 [SD = 8.1] years). The discovery and replication cohorts were comparable in terms of age, sex, APOE-ε4 carriership, and the prevalence of amyloid-beta and p-tau abnormalities (all P > 0.05). An overview of the cohorts and follow-up analyses performed for downstream analysis after CSF pQTL mapping is provided in Fig. 1.

Identification of 399 pQTLs in untargeted mass-spectrometry CSF proteomics

Genome-wide CSF pQTL analysis across 2,215 proteins identified 27,789 significant SNP-protein associations after Bonferroni correction (P < 5 × 10–8 / 2,215 proteins = 2.26 × 10–11) (Fig. 2, Table S3). After clumping SNPs within 1 Mb and applying an LD threshold (R2 < 0.2), we identified 399 independent lead CSF pQTL signals, representing associations between 394 unique independent loci and 222 CSF proteins (Table 1). Six out of the 394 genetic loci were previously identified as risk loci in AD GWAS studies [2, 6, 46–49], which included cis effects for the genes HLA-DQA1/DRB1, CTSH and CD33 (with their respective CSF protein), and trans effects for gene HSPA6 with CSF FCGR2A, BSN with CSF MST1, and APOE with CSF HS3ST1 (Table S4). The majority of pQTLs were classified as cis-acting. Using a strict definition of cis pQTLs (< 1 Mb from the encoding gene), we classified 188 (47.8%) pQTLs as cis, whereas 156 (38.4%) met a broader (1–5 Mb from the encoding gene) cis definition. The remaining 55 (13.8%) pQTLs were classified as trans-acting. Most CSF proteins had a 'monogenic' architecture, with 145 proteins (65.3%) associated with a single genetic locus [14]. Similar to other QTL studies [16, 50], pQTL SNPs resided often in intronic regions and exonic regions (32.5%, 8.06% respectively), which most likely represents effects of pQTLs on post-translational regulations and coding sequences, respectively (Table S5). Forty-one CSF proteins (18.5%) had two independent pQTL signals, while 16 (7.2%) had three and 20 (9.0%) had four or more. Here, pQTLs with a ‘polygenic architecture’ comprised loci originating from complex LD regions, such as the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region on chr6:25,119,106–33854733. Four genetic loci influenced levels of more than one CSF protein, including the genes LAMC1, FCGR3A, FUCA2 and HLA-DRB1. As often observed in GWAS studies on molecular traits, these mostly included cis pQTLs in addition to trans effects of proteins that were involved in similar biological pathways as the cis protein.

Of the significant pQTLs identified in the discovery cohort, 270 gene-protein associations were available for replication (148 overlapping CSF proteins). Out of the 270 CSF pQTLs, 230 (85%) reached at least nominal significant signals in the EMIF-AD MBD cohort (i.e., P < 0.05), of which 71 with a strict genome-wide threshold of P < 5 × 10–8 (Table S6, Table S7). We also examined our CSF pQTLs in relation to previously published genome-wide CSF pQTL studies, which used different proteomic platforms (SOMAmer, Olink and immunoassays) [12–16]. Out of the 399 identified pQTLs in the discovery cohort, 125 (31%) have been reported before in CSF pQTL studies (Table S8), of which forty-nine (12%) included identical lead SNPs (Table S9).

Genetically predicted CSF protein levels associate with Alzheimer’s disease

Next, we performed proteome-wide association study (PWAS) analyses using the TWAS framework from FUSION [39], to identify proteins whose genetically predicted CSF levels associated with genetic risk for AD and other brain-related disorders. This framework offers greater power than traditional GWAS, enabling the detection of novel genetic risk regions and novel candidate CSF proteins for the neurodegenerative traits of interest [51]. Genetically predicted CSF levels of proteins were tested for association with genetic risk for AD [1] and three other neurodegenerative diseases that show genetic overlap and/or comorbidity with AD (and have a large enough sample size in their GWAS), including dementia with Lewy bodies [40], stroke [41] and ALS [42].

Among the 208 CSF proteins with sufficiently predictable genetic regulation, the R2 between the predicted and actual protein levels ranged from 0.005 to 0.68 (mean = 0.16, SD = 0.14) (Fig. S1). Heritability estimates for CSF protein levels ranged from h2SNP = 5%−78% (mean = 26%, SD = 16%), with the highest reported heritability estimates for CSF protein levels of CTSH (77%), CFHR2 (72%), MST1 (69%) and FCGR3B (66%). We identified eighteen significant (P FDR < 0.05) protein-phenotype associations, representing fifteen CSF protein levels (Fig. 3, Table S10). All five PWAS associations supported by colocalization analysis (posterior probability (PP)4 > 0.8) were related to AD, of which two included novel AD risk regions: SIRPA-CSF SIRPA P-FDR = 0.001 and GALNT6-CSF GALNT6 P-FDR = 0.002. One of the known associations for AD included CSF PLXDC2, whereas this association was novel for ALS (P-FDR = 3.79 × 10–14, P-FDR = 0.001 respectively). The other known AD regions included CTSH with CSF CTSH (P-FDR = 5.5 × 10–4) and ACE with CSF ACE (P-FDR = 2.68 × 10–11).

Amyloid-beta dependent CSF pQTLs

We next examined whether pQTL effects were modulated by amyloid-beta pathology, by repeating genome-wide pQTL analysis with an interaction term for abnormal amyloid-beta CSF status (158 subjects with normal amyloid and 329 with abnormal amyloid status, further referred to as A- and A +, respectively). We identified 107 significant (P < 5 × 10–8) CSF pQTL interaction signals (representing 92 CSF proteins and 103 unique independent loci) for which the association between genetic variant and CSF protein level differed between A + and A- (Table S11). None of the pQTLs were significant after additional Bonferroni-correction for the number of proteins tested (P < 5 × 10–8 / 2,215 proteins = 2.26 × 10–11). We found that 76 of the 107 amyloid-beta dependent pQTLs had opposite effects in A + and A- subjects. The other 31 pQTLs were only present in subjects with A- and not in those with A + (using a P value cut-off of 0.01). No pQTLs were identified that were only significant in A + versus A-.

The EMIF-AD MBD cohort (54 A- and 188 A +) had 57 out of the 92 CSF proteins available for replication analysis (corresponding to 62 AB-dependent pQTLs in the discovery phase). None of 62 CSF pQTLs significant in the discovery phase showed genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10–8) signals in the EMIF-AD MBD cohort, but three CSF pQTLs had a P < 0.05 (Table S12). The only association reaching significance in both, the discovery and replication cohorts (with consistent effect direction) included CSF LRP1 with rs12170778 from gene CECR2, with rs12170778-A associating with increased levels of CSF LRP1 in subjects with abnormal amyloid-beta and with decreased CSF LRP1 levels in subjects with normal amyloid-beta (Fig. S2).

Mendelian randomization

We performed MR analyses by integrating CSF pQTL results with AD and AD neuropathology GWAS results (i.e., Braak stage and CERAD score) (Table S13, Fig. S3-S11). Notably, PLXDC2, SIRPA, CTSH, and CD33 were consistently associated with increased AD risk across all MR methods (IVW, weighted median, weighted mode, simple mode, range β = 0.021–0.063, range P = 3.05 × 10⁻214 – 1.27 × 10–5). Neuropathological MR analyses further provided consistent evidence that levels of SIRPA, CTSH and CD33 associated with AD pathology, as estimated by Braak stage (CD33 IVW: PBraak = 2.39 × 10–7, CTSH IVW: PBraak = 8.35 × 10–9) or CERAD score (SIRPA IVW: PCERAD = 4.67 × 10–7).

Conversely, CSF levels of ACE, GALNT6, and FCGR2A demonstrated robust protective effects against AD. For example, ACE showed a strong inverse association with AD (IVW β = –0.118, P = 3.6 × 10⁻35), and AD pathology (CERAD IVW: β = –0.082 P = 2.30 × 10–11). Similarly, GALNT6 (IVW β = –0.029, P = 1.9 × 10⁻45, IVW Braak β = –0.069, P = 1.76 × 10⁻11) and FCGR2A (IVW β = –0.014, P = 2.4 × 10⁻1⁸) were associated with reduced AD risk. MST1 associated with Braak stage (IVW β = 0.019, P = 7.4 × 10⁻9) but not with AD risk or CERAD score (all P > 0.23).

Discussion

To gain more insight into biological pathways relevant to the genetic architecture of Alzheimer’s disease, we conducted a genome-wide study of 2,215 CSF protein levels, measured with TMT mass spectrometry. We identified 399 CSF pQTLs, representing 222 CSF proteins and 394 unique independent loci, which included 69% novel CSF pQTLs. We further integrated our pQTL results with AD genetics to characterize functional mechanisms by which genetic risk loci for AD exert effects on AD pathogenesis. Here, we provide genetic support for the functional role of known and novel candidate proteins for AD (summarized in Table 2). In context-specific analysis for amyloid abnormality status, we identified 107 genome-wide, nominal significant (P < 5.0 × 10–8), CSF pQTLs that may be dependent on the presence or absence of amyloid pathology. However, as none of these signals exceeded the more stringent study-wide Bonferroni threshold (P < 2.26 × 10⁻11), it remains unclear if these results may have been underpowered or if they may reflect noise. As such, replication in independent future CSF pQTL studies that measure both genetics and CSF untargeted mass spectrometry proteomics remain required.

In line with previous pQTL studies, we report that the genetic risk for AD (partly) influences disease risk through the modulation of the proteome [13–18]. We extend this body of literature by identifying novel CSF pQTL effects for AD risk genes. Specifically, we report novel pQTL effects for AD risk genes RPS23P10/HSPA6 with CSF levels of FCGR2A, BIN2 with CSF GALNT6, APOE with CSF HS3ST1, and the HLA region with CSF levels of HLA-DPB1 and PLXDC2.

One of the novel AD-related pQTL proteins is FCGR2A (Fc Gamma Receptor IIa, also known as CD32), a cell surface receptor expressed on macrophages and neutrophils, playing a key role in phagocytosis [52]. Gene discovery studies have linked FCGR2A to many immune-related traits and Parkinson's disease. FCGR2A has been implicated in AD, with upregulated FCGR2A gene expression levels in the prefrontal cortex of AD patients compared to controls [53]. Elevated FCGR2A gene expression levels further associate with increased infiltration of M2 macrophages (class of macrophages typically involved in tissue repair and extracellular matrix formation) and increased Aβ42 levels, suggesting a potential mechanism by which FCGR2A contributes to dysregulated inflammatory processes in AD.

Another AD-related pQTL included PLXDC2 (plexin domain containing 2 or TEM7R), a surface transmembrane protein receptor involved in angiogenesis, neurodevelopment and the blood–brain barrier function. While most literature on PLXDC2 is on cancer [54], some studies have reported a role for PLXDC2 in AD. For instance, Plxdc2 gene expression has been shown to be upregulated in AD mouse models [55]. Additionally, PLXDC2 gene expression has also been shown to be elevated in the presence of AD pathology [55].

We successfully replicated previously reported pQTL effects for AD risk genes, including cis pQTL effects for ACE, CD33, CTSH, and SIRPA, as well as trans pQTL effects for BSN with CSF MST1. ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme), an enzyme regulating blood pressure, has been shown to influence AD pathogenesis by specifically breaking down amyloid-beta [56]. Further, the (genetically proxied) inhibition of blood serum ACE relates to a higher risk for AD and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) [57]. CTSH (Cathepsin H), a protein-cleaving enzyme important in the degradation of lysosomal proteins, shows increased gene expression in AD patient brain tissue (i.e., hippocampus, temporal cortex and frontal cortex) as well as in AD transgenic mice carrying familial mutations [58].

Proteins such as CTSH, CD33, and SIRPA, all cis pQTLs with supportive colocalization and PWAS evidence, emerged as the most robust AD candidates, showing consistent risk-increasing effects in MR analyses and mapping to established AD genetic loci. Conversely, proteins like ACE, GALNT6, and FCGR2A showed protective effects and may represent genetically regulated mechanisms of resilience. While trans pQTLs such as PLXDC2 and HS3ST1 also demonstrated strong MR signals, the potential for horizontal pleiotropy and indirect regulatory effects highlights the need for cautious interpretation. Notably, HLA-DPB1 exhibited evidence of directional pleiotropy, despite supportive MR and pQTL results. Taken together, our integrative framework highlights genetically regulated proteins in CSF that contribute to AD pathogenesis and nominates several for functional validation and biomarker development.

While most QTL effects are independent of disease processes [17], certain QTL effects appear context-specific, as seen in dementia [59], but also other traits, such as treatment-dependent eQTLs in obesity [60], disease-dependent eQTLs in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [61]) and sex-dependent pQTLs in brain tissue [62]. Identifying context-specific QTL effects could uncover biological pathways involved in disease progression and protection. One putative context-dependent association identified in our study was the trans pQTL of CECR2 with CSF LRP1 levels, which showed differential effects according to amyloid-beta abnormality status. While this interaction did not reach genome-wide significance (P = 1.55 × 10⁻⁸ and P = 0.036 for the discovery and replication cohort, respectively), it showed a consistent pattern in both cohorts, with opposite effects observed in amyloid-beta abnormal and normal individuals. As such, this finding should be considered exploratory and requires validation in larger independent cohorts. LRP1 (low density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1) plays a critical role in AD pathology by facilitating amyloid-beta clearance, synaptic signaling, and inflammatory processes [63]. CECR2 (Cat Eye Syndrome Critical Region Protein 2) acts as an epigenetic regulator, particularly in chromatin remodeling, and is essential for neurulation— the transformation of the neural plate into the neural tube. CECR2 may further influence the DNA damage response, particularly affecting gene expression of proteins involved in microglial function [64]. Notably, CECR2 expression is reduced in the microglia of AD patients [65]. Similar to the novel identified AD pQTL FCGR2A, CECR2 is also associated with increased infiltration of M2 macrophages, highlighting how genetic risk factors for AD may modulate the inflammatory response to (tissue) damage [66].

This study identified and replicated various CSF pQTLs, but several limitations should be considered. This study had a relatively small sample size compared to traditional GWAS approaches that study genetic associations with clinical outcomes. The notion that we were able to replicate over 80% of identified CSF pQTL in an independent cohort, indicates a strong relationship between genes and protein levels, suggesting that for this type of analysis our sample size was sufficient. Moreover, we identified 107 CSF pQTLs (P < 5.0 × 10–8) that may act differently in the presence or absence of abnormal amyloid-beta. These findings require cautious interpretation, as most signals could not be evaluated in the replication cohort due to limited detection of CSF proteins in our replication cohort. In addition, both the discovery and replication cohorts showed an imbalance in amyloid-beta abnormality groups, with fewer individuals classified as amyloid-beta normal (A −) compared with amyloid-beta abnormal (A +) individuals. Since interaction analyses require substantially larger sample sizes, this imbalance may have reduced statistical power to detect these effects particularly in the smaller A − group. Therefore, larger independent cohorts with balanced amyloid-beta groups and untargeted mass spectrometry CSF proteomics will be required to validate these exploratory findings. Furthermore, our study consisted solely of European-ancestry individuals. Future pQTL studies must prioritize ancestral diversity to ensure that CSF biomarker signatures and genetic mechanisms are representative across populations. This is particularly critical as many known AD risk loci exhibit ancestry-specific effects.

Our study highlights that certain genetic risk loci for AD function as pQTLs, regulating protein levels in CSF. While preliminary, we were able to identify amyloid-beta-dependent pQTL effects, providing insights into specific proteins through which genetic variants may either contribute to or mitigate the development of AD. Together, our results highlight how genetic variation shapes protein expression in the central nervous system, offering mechanistic insight into AD and support the importance of proteogenomic studies in other neurodegenerative diseases.
