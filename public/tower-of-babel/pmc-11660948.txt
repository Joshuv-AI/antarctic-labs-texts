Multiple Displacement Amplification Facilitates SMRT Sequencing of Microscopic Animals and the Genome of the Gastrotrich Lepidodermella squamata (Dujardin 1841)
Authors: Nickellaus G Roberts, Michael J Gilmore, Torsten H Struck, Kevin M Kocot, Matthew Webster
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObtaining adequate DNA for long-read genome sequencing remains a roadblock to producing contiguous genomes from small-bodied organisms, hindering understanding of phylogenetic relationships and genome evolution. Multiple displacement amplification leverages Phi29 DNA polymerase to produce micrograms of DNA from picograms of input. However, multiple displacement amplification's inherent biases in amplification related to guanine and cytosine (GC) content, repeat content and chimera production are a problem for long-read genome assembly, which has been little investigated. We explored the utility of multiple displacement amplification for generating template DNA for High Fidelity (HiFi) sequencing directly from living cells of Caenorhabditis elegans (Nematoda) and Lepidodermella squamata (Gastrotricha) containing one order of magnitude less DNA than required for the PacBio Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow. High Fidelity sequencing of libraries prepared from multiple displacement amplification products resulted in highly contiguous and complete genomes for both C. elegans (102 Mbp assembly; 336 contigs; N50 = 868 kbp; L50 = 39; BUSCO_nematoda_nucleotide: S:96.1%, D:2.8%) and L. squamata (122 Mbp assembly; 157 contigs; N50 = 3.9 Mbp; L50 = 13; BUSCO_metazoa_nucleotide: S:80.8%, D:2.8%). Coverage uniformity for reads from multiple displacement amplification DNA (Gini Index: 0.14, normalized mean across all 100 kbp blocks: 0.49) and reads from pooled nematode DNA (Gini Index: 0.16, normalized mean across all 100 kbp blocks: 0.49) proved similar. Using this approach, we sequenced the genome of the microscopic invertebrate L. squamata (Gastrotricha), the first of its phylum. Using the newly sequenced genome, we infer Gastrotricha's long-debated phylogenetic position as the sister taxon of Platyhelminthes and conduct a comparative analysis of the Hox cluster.Keywords: genomics, Gastrotricha, invertebrate, sequencing, genome

Significance

Despite recent advances, obtaining sufficient DNA from minute organisms has hindered genome sequencing efforts, and thus understanding of their evolutionary history. This study introduces a novel combination of methods leveraging multiple displacement amplification and High Fidelity sequencing to produce high-quality genomes from minute organisms directly from living cells. By successfully applying this approach to model and nonmodel organisms, we address the long-standing challenge of limiting amounts of high molecular weight DNA from microscopic eukaryotes. Using the genome of Lepidodermella squamata, the first for phylum Gastrotricha, we resolve its position in the animal tree of life and show that it has a significantly reduced Hox gene cluster. The L. squamata genome will be a valuable resource to the field of evolutionary developmental biology as this species is an emerging model system.

Background

Availability of high-quality genomes provides unprecedented opportunities for resolving elusive phylogenetic relationships. It is of little debate that for most applications leveraging genomes, assemblies that are both contiguous and well-annotated are ideal (reviewed by Laumer [2018]). In recent years, long-read technologies have become the industry standard for de novo genome sequencing. PacBio's multiple pass circular consensus sequencing High Fidelity (HiFi) has stood out as a popular choice due to its high accuracy (base-level resolution of >99%) and relatively long reads (~10 kbp) able to span complex and repetitive genomic regions (Hon et al. 2020). Using adequate high molecular weight (HMW) DNA with as little damage as possible for sequencing library preparation is crucial to obtain reads that are both highly accurate and of adequate length (Pollard et al. 2018). Avoiding pooling multiple individuals serves to increase the assembly quality of genomes by reducing heterozygosity (Birky 1996; Flot et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2017; Simion et al. 2018). It follows that de novo genome projects are most likely to succeed when a large amount of HMW DNA with little to no exogenous contamination is available from a single individual.

Unfortunately, obtaining adequate starting material meeting the above requirements is not always possible. PacBio's Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow yields data volumes comparable to standard input libraries from as little as 5 ng of HMW template. This approach has successfully been used to produce high-quality genomes from individual small animals such as mosquitos (Kingan et al. 2019) and springtails (Schneider et al. 2021). However, even the Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow is not applicable to very small animals (e.g. meiofauna <1 mm in total length). For example, a single hermaphrodite specimen of Caenorhabditis elegans contains 959 somatic nuclei (Cohen-Fix and Askjaer 2017) and has a genome size of roughly 100 Mbp (C. elegans Sequencing Consortium 1998), meaning that a single specimen may contain as little as 200 picograms of DNA with comparable DNA content in other small-bodied animal phyla also ranging in the hundreds of picograms of maximal yield. Thus, obtaining sufficient starting material meeting even the greatly reduced input requirements for the PacBio Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow is not possible for many organisms. Further, if researchers want to avoid pooling multiple individuals or if the organism of interest is both small and rare, reduced input strategies are not an option thus far.

Due to the challenges associated with sequencing genomes of small-bodied animals, metazoan genome sequencing efforts have been biased toward large-bodied organisms. Of the roughly 30 metazoan phyla, nearly all those that are still lacking high-quality genome assemblies—or any genomic data whatsoever in some cases—are small-bodied (Worsaae et al. 2023). For example, within Lophotrochozoa (=Spiralia) all phyla that are lacking in genomic data are mostly or exclusively small-bodied (Fig. 1, supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). With an average body size of <1 mm, these organisms are underrepresented in genomic and phylogenomic studies (Kocot et al. 2017; Laumer et al. 2019), hindering understanding of genome evolution in small animals and deep metazoan phylogeny.

Whole-genome amplification (WGA) is a strategy for generating large amounts of DNA from limiting sample material through techniques such as multiple displacement amplification (MDA; Dean et al. 2001), degenerate-oligonucleotide-primed-PCR (DOP-PCR; Telenius et al. 1992), primer extension preamplification PCR (PEP-PCR; Xu et al. 1993), ligation-mediated PCR (LM-PCR; Mueller and Wold 1989), transfer PCR (T-PCR; Grothues et al. 1993), multiple annealing and looping based amplification cycles (MALBAC; Chapman et al. 2015), and protocols with a mixture of preamplification and amplification methods (PicoPLEX; Takara Bio). WGA methodologies are widely used in diagnostic medicine and single-cell research (Evrony et al. 2015; Fu et al. 2015; Li et al. 2018; Zhou et al. 2020) for the detection of structural variants, large deletions and inversions, and amplicon sequencing from degraded (e.g. formalin-fixed paraffin embedded) material (Evrony et al. 2015; Zhou et al. 2020). However, the utility of WGA for amplifying DNA for de novo genome sequencing of very small-bodied organisms in the age of long-read sequencing technologies has received little attention (but see Yao et al. 2021; Hård et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2023; Lord et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2023).

WGA methodologies typically make use of PCR, isothermal amplification, or both (reviewed by Wang et al. 2020). PCR-based protocols make use of specific primers, degenerate oligos, and/or repetitive genomic regions for priming. These strictly PCR-based amplification strategies are exponential in their amplification and thus uneven, or nonspecific amplification may occur due to variations in template guanine and cytosine (GC) content and amplicon length. PCR-based methodologies have proven highly useful for medical and diagnostic applications due to their ability to analyze copy number variation and identify small structural variants or small stretches of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), but their utility for long-read sequencing is limited due to typical product fragment sizes only being within 100 to 1000 bp. Long-range PCR-based approaches have been demonstrated to be successful for de novo genome sequencing of specimens in which material is limited. Picogram input multimodal sequencing (PiMmS; Laumer 2022) involves DNA purification using a salting out procedure to provide HMW ethanol-precipitated DNA that is then sheared to ∼10 kbp, enzymatically treated to perform DNA damage repair, end-polishing, and adapter ligation, amplified with long-range PCR, purified, and then used for PacBio or Oxford Nanopore sequencing library preparation (Laumer 2022). As PiMmS makes use of PCR, a qPCR step is required to estimate the cycle number to reduce artifacts produced during PCR amplification. PiMmS has performed well in producing relatively contiguous genomes from small-bodied animals (Heligmosomoides nematodes; Stevens et al. 2023), but involves increased handling of genomic DNA and a technically challenging protocol.

Isothermal amplification in the case of MDA uses Phi29 DNA polymerase to amplify long fragments of linear or circular DNA (Dean et al. 2001; Dean et al. 2002; Lasken 2007; Lu et al. 2023). Amplification using Phi29 polymerase is highly accurate and efficient due to high strand-displacement activity, extremely high processivity, and strong proofreading activity (Garmendia et al. 1992). MDA, as opposed to PCR-based WGA approaches, has the benefit of producing highly accurate fragments with an average fragment length >10 kbp (up to 100 kbp). Like other WGA approaches, MDA can produce tens of micrograms of DNA from picograms of starting material. It is also widely commercially available in user-friendly kits from multiple manufacturers. MDA typically makes use of short, random exonuclease-resistant primers to allow the reaction to proceed isothermally. Like PCR-based methods, MDA is exponential leading to potential biases in coverage based on GC content (Lasken and Egholm 2003; Borgström et al. 2017). In GC-rich regions, there may be reduction of polymerase processivity leading to the underrepresentation of that region during amplification. However, differential binding affinities of GC- and AT-rich primers can also lead to GC-rich regions being preferentially amplified (Benita et al. 2003; Sahdev et al. 2007). Further, the denaturation strategy used prior to MDA (alkali, thermal, or otherwise) may affect access and efficacy of DNA binding in GC-rich regions. For example, KOH alkali denaturation has been shown to increase primer access to GC-rich template DNA (Pinard et al. 2006). Amplification bias with respect to GC richness is a function of reaction gain, defined as the ratio of DNA product mass over DNA template mass. Compared to MALBAC and PicoPLEX single-cell, increased reaction gain in the case of MDA more negatively influences amplification uniformity and the detection of copy number variants. Fortunately, lowering amplification time reduces reaction gain, thus reducing the overamplification of certain genomic regions (Dean et al. 2001). Also, unlike both MALBAC and PicoPLEX, MDA reactions with high gain do not have a meaningful increase in the rate of single nucleotide errors (de Bourcy et al. 2014), making MDA protocols with more modest amplification time preferable to generate template for de novo sequencing. While MDA does have better accuracy than MALBAC or PicoPLEX it has higher allelic dropout, and potentially less uniform coverage. Finally, unlike the other WGA methods discussed above (with the exception of PiMmS), MDA produces very large fragment sizes during amplification (10 to 20+ kbp; Borgström et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2020), making it most suitable for generating template DNA for long-read sequencing approaches.

Another potential challenge to using MDA to generate template for long-read sequencing is chimera formation. During MDA, chimeric DNA fragments can form when 3′ termini are displaced and annealed to nearby displaced 5′ strands, forming chimeric DNA fragments after random hexamer annealing and extension (Lasken and Stockwell 2007). Previous studies have suggested a rate of up to 1 chimera/10 kbp for MDA, which is potentially problematic for the recovery of specific genomic regions, the analysis of copy number variation, and de novo assembly, although these problems can be reduced with adequate coverage (Rodrigue et al. 2009). Proper preservation of samples and careful HMW DNA extraction resulting in more intact template DNA and thus fewer 3′ termini can reduce chimera formation (Nelson 2014), especially when combined with lower reaction gain. Moreover, as MDA can be applied directly to lysed cells (Hosono et al. 2003), shearing of DNA during extraction can be avoided altogether when aliquots of cell cultures or entire microscopic organisms are lysed followed by immediate amplification in the same tube. Taken together, MDA's isothermal nature, high accuracy, production of fragments longer than typical PacBio HiFi reads, ease of use, and ability to amplify DNA directly from freshly lysed cells make it an attractive choice for template preparation for HiFi sequencing. Moreso, under low reaction gain and in low volumes, sequencing of MDA DNA can achieve high data uniformity and coverage (Li et al. 2018).

Here, we investigated the utility of MDA for generating adequate template DNA for PacBio long-read sequencing from very small animals (<1 mm). We demonstrate the utility of this approach using the model organism C. elegans, which was selected because a high-quality reference genome and HiFi reads generated from unamplified DNA extracted from a pool of worms with extremely low heterozygosity are already available for comparison. We additionally chose the gastrotrich Lepidodermella squamata (Dujardin 1841) as a test of MDA's ability to enable de novo long-read genome sequencing of a nonmodel organism. Gastrotricha Metschnikoff, 1865, colloquially referred to as “hairy bellied worms,” is a phylum of ∼831 species of microscopic benthic invertebrates found in aquatic habitats worldwide (Margulis and Schwartz 1998). Of these, 346 are found in freshwater habitats, with the rest inhabiting marine and brackish water. Living mostly in sandy, benthic habitats, key taxonomic characters of Gastrotricha include the variable presence of scales, spines, or hooks on the body wall and the presence of adhesive tubes for attaching to substrate. Adult gastrotrichs range from 70 µm to 1 mm in size, although they may reach up to 3 mm in the case of the genus Megadasys (Schmidt 1974; Fontaneto et al. 2015). Additionally, gastrotrichs are flattened along their dorso–ventral axis reducing their volume even further in comparison to similar-sized animals that are rounder in cross-section. The ∼250 µm freshwater gastrotrich L. squamata has proven to be a useful species for the study of Gastrotricha due to its ease of culture (Bennett 1979). Gastrotricha is of great interest to evolutionary biologists as its phylogenetic position has long been debated (Edgecombe et al. 2011; Struck et al. 2014; Laumer et al. 2015; Kocot 2016; Bleidorn 2019). Availability of a high-quality reference genome for L. squamata also benefits the field of evolutionary developmental biology in which L. squamata is an emerging model species (Hejnol 2015).

Results

Genome Assembly and Sequencing

MDA performed on one-half specimen of C. elegans yielded 8.74 µg of DNA with an average fragment size of 28,268 bp after purification with the PacBio Short Read Eliminator kit. MDA performed on one whole specimen of L. squamata yielded 14.92 µg of DNA with an average fragment size of 24,288 bp after purification with the Short Read Eliminator kit.

Sequencing of a HiFi library generated from MDA DNA for C. elegans (C. elegans [MDA]) resulted in 708,811 HiFi reads (96x coverage of the 102.5 Mbp assembly) with an average HiFi read length of 13,885 bp (Table 1). The de novo C. elegans assembly based on MDA DNA was 102 Mbp contained in 336 contigs with an N50 of 868 kbp and an L50 of 39 (Table 2). The de novo C. elegans assembly was screened for contamination with BlobTools (Laetsch and Blaxter 2017) but all 336 contigs had hits only to Metazoa and thus none were removed. The de novo assembly had a genome nucleotide BUSCO metazoa_odb10 completeness score of 69.9% (Single copy BUSCOs [S]:67.5%, Duplicate BUSCOs [D]:2.4%) and a genome nucleotide BUSCO nematoda_odb10 score of 96.1% ([S]:93.3%, [D]:2.8%) compared to 73.3% ([S]:72.6%, [D]:0.7%) and 99.4% ([S]:98.9%, [D]:0.5%), respectively, for the reference C. elegans genome (NCBI GCA000002985.3). Base-level sequencing accuracy and k-mer completeness of the de novo assembly based on MDA DNA were analyzed with Merqury (Rhie et al. 2020). Just 3,204 k-mers were found only in the assembly while 102,473,629 were found in both the assembly and the read set, leading to a consensus error rate score (QV) of 57.6019 and a base call consensus error rate of 1.734e−06. The k-mer completeness of the genome is 96.86% indicating that ∼97% of the solid k-mers (distinct k-mers after filtering of erroneous, low-copy k-mers) found in the read set are present in the assembly. The combination of both haplotypes without phasing, has a k-mer completeness of 98.31% indicating that ~98% of the solid k-mers found in the read set are present in one haplotype and/or the other (Table 2).

An assembly was also generated based on a publicly available HiFi sequencing run of pooled C. elegans of the same strain used in our experiments (NCBI SRR22507561; C. elegans [pooled]) for purposes of comparison. The HiFi dataset based on non-MDA amplified DNA extracted from pooled C. elegans had 880,513 HiFi reads (125x coverage of the 102.5 Mbp assembly) with an average HiFi read length of 15,157 bp (Table 1). The unamplified pooled genome assembly was 106.3 Mbp contained in 49 contigs with an N50 of 6.69 Mbp and an L50 of 6 (Table 2). The assembly had a genome nucleotide BUSCO metazoa_odb10 score of 71.2% ([S]:70.3%, [D]:0.9%]) and a genome nucleotide BUSCO nematoda_odb10 score of 99.4% ([S:98.9%,D:0.5%]). For the unamplified pooled assembly, 794 k-mers were found only in the assembly, with 106,323,756 found in both the assembly and the read set, leading to a consensus error rate score (QV) of 64.49 and a base call consensus error rate of 3.556e−07. The k-mer completeness of the genome is 99.69% indicating that 99.61% of the solid k-mers found in the read set are present in the assembly (Table 2).

Sequencing of L. squamata resulted in 917,258 HiFi reads with an average HiFi read length of 13,358 bp (83x coverage of the 147 Mbp genome; Table 1). The L. squamata assembly was 147 Mbp contained in 527 contigs with an N50 of 2.9 Mbp and an L50 of 17. After filtering with BlobTools (Challis et al. 2020), 370 contigs were removed based on best hits to nonmetazoans. The resulting assembly was 122 Mbp contained in 157 contigs with an N50 of 3.9 Mbp and an L50 of 13. The BUSCO completeness score of the decontaminated assembly using the metazoa_odb10 dataset in genome mode was 80.8% ([S]:78.0%, [D]:2.8%). In the decontaminated L. squamata dataset, 3,508 k-mers were found only in the assembly, with 122,826,690 found in both the assembly and read set, leading to a consensus error score (QV) of 58.0 denoting a base call consensus error rate of 1.587e−06. The k-mer completeness percentage for the genome is 72.37%, indicating that ∼72% of the solid k-mers found in the read set are present in the decontaminated assembly. The combination of both haplotypes without phasing has a k-mer completeness of 99.44% indicating that >99% of the solid k-mers found in the read set are present in one haplotype or the other (Table 2).

Coverage and Amplification Bias Estimation

Due to MDA's potential for amplification bias with respect to GC and repeat content (Lasken and Egholm 2003; Borgström et al. 2017), we investigated the relationship between GC and repeat content and read coverage using the model organism C. elegans and its reference genome. To understand the level as to which MDA may have resulted in the overamplification of genomic regions with respect to GC content, we compared mean coverage in 100 kbp blocks of reads from our dataset based on MDA (C. elegans [MDA]) and reads from unamplified DNA (C. elegans [Pooled, SRR22507561]) extracted from a pool of worms mapped to the reference genome (Fig. 2a and b). Due to the potential for repetitive regions to have GC content far outside the mean, we also investigated coverage with respect to repeat percentage (Fig. 2b). The reference C. elegans Bristol N2 genome has a GC content of 35% and, according to RepeatModeler (Flynn et al. 2020), a repeat content of 18.40%. Mean coverage was normalized for each dataset by subtracting the minimum mean coverage from each datapoint (representing a 100 kbp region) divided by the range (maximum coverage − minimum coverage) of the dataset [(x − min(x))/range(x)), x = coverage of 100 kbp region]. Resulting normalized mean coverage, repeat content, and GC content in 100 kbp blocks across all six chromosomes were plotted to visualize potential overamplification of genomic regions during MDA (Fig. 2). The C. elegans reads based on MDA DNA, when mapped to the reference genome, showed 0.71x lower coverage in highly repetitive 100 kbp blocks (>45% repeats, more than double the genome's average repeat content) and 0.86x lower coverage in moderately repetitive 100 kbp blocks (>20% but <45% repeats), compared to 0.62x and 0.77x lower coverage, respectively, for the reads based on unamplified DNA from a pool of worms. Regions high in repeats do not have higher-than-average GC content (100 kbp blocks >45% repeats, GC content 35.19% SD ± 1.31, 100 kbp blocks >20% repeats, 35.86 SD ± 1.45). We see similar results when investigating coverage of 100 kbp blocks below mean repeat content (18.40% repeats) with the amplified dataset having 1.07x higher coverage and the unamplified dataset 1.14x higher coverage (Figs. 2b,d and 4b).

We also investigated the number of misassemblies between samples amplified with MDA (C. elegans [MDA]) and samples from a pool of worms (C. elegans [Pooled, SRR22507561]) to the reference genome (C. elegans [Reference, GCA000002985.3]) using the dnadiff function of MUMmer3 (Kurtz et al. 2004). The C. elegans genome assembly based on MDA DNA had a total of 15,713 breakpoints (nonmaximal alignment endpoints, i.e. places where an alignment stops before it reaches the end of the sequence) when compared to the reference genome. Surprisingly, the number of breakpoints was more than twice as high, 36,572, for the genome assembly based on a pool of worms. The assembly based on MDA DNA had a combined total of 206 relocations, translocations, and inversions (relocations: 45, translocations:17, inversions: 144) compared to a similar total of 207 for the assembly based on a pool of worms (relocations: 90, translocations: 0, inversions: 117; supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online).

Without a reference genome or a dataset from a pool of individuals, no mapping comparisons could be made for L. squamata, but reads were mapped to the primary assembly after removal of contamination with BlobTools to assess coverage with respect to GC content and repeat content (Fig. 3). The decontaminated genome assembly of L. squamata has a GC content of 43% and a repeat content of 21.98%. The L. squamata genome has 2.25x higher coverage in highly repetitive (>45% repeats, more than double the genome's repeat content) 100 kbp blocks (Fig. 3b), which also have higher mean GC content than the genome does on average, albeit by only 2% (45% SD ± 1.96 GC richness in 100 kbp blocks with >45% repeat content). The 100 kbp blocks with higher-than-mean GC content have 1.2x higher coverage than the mean and those with lower than mean GC content having 0.79x lower coverage than the mean, suggesting a tendency for GC-rich regions of the genome to have higher coverage on average. Only 0.4% of 100 kbp blocks with lower than mean GC content and >45% repeats have higher fold coverage compared to 6.6% in those blocks with higher-than-average GC content and >45% repeats (Fig. 3).

Lorenz curves, used here to plot the cumulative share of reads against the cumulative share of genomic positions covered by those reads, ordered from highest to lowest (Zong et al. 2012), were calculated (Fig. 4a). The Gini index is calculated as the area between the calculated Lorenz curve and a straight Lorenz curve, representing perfectly uniform coverage. In our case, a Gini index of zero would indicate perfect uniform coverage and a Gini index of 1 would indicate maximally nonuniform coverage. The Gini index values for the amplified and unamplified C. elegans datasets were similar, 0.14 and 0.16 respectively (Fig. 4a). The value for L. squamata (based on the decontaminated genome assembly) was 0.29 (Fig. 4a).

Chimeric Read Screening and Removal

The program Alvis (Martin and Leggett 2021) was used to investigate the presence of chimeric contigs in both assemblies. Alvis interprets a sequence to be chimeric if it has two different alignments to either different reference sequences or the same reference sequence in multiple nonoverlapping positions. Alvis only found one contig in the C. elegans assembly to be a potential chimera and none were detected in the assembly of L. squamata.

To further investigate the presence of chimeric reads, pseudo-short reads were created from each HiFi dataset by taking the first and last 500 bp of each read. These pseudo-short reads were then aligned to their respective assemblies and their parent long reads were removed from the dataset if their pseudo-short reads aligned to different contigs. As mapping scores can be variable in terms of their quality (MAPQ score), we chose to implement this approach at two different levels of stringency based on MAPQ score, mismatched reads (MMRs) in which a parent read was removed if its pseudo short reads mapped to different contigs, and reliably mismatched reads (RMMRs), in which reads were only removed if they mapped to different contigs with a MAPQ >30 (Table 2). In the case of L. squamata, all MMRs were RMMRs as all mapping scores were >30. The filtered HiFi read sets were reassembled as described above and BUSCO and Merqury QC analyses were performed to understand the effect of removing potentially chimeric HiFi reads on assembly quality. Quality metrics for assemblies in which all HiFi reads with pseudo-short read mapping mismatches were removed (MMRs) and those in which only reliably mapped pseudo-short read mismatches were removed (RMMRs) are summarized in Table 2. We found a low number of potential chimeras in C. elegans (39,791/708,811, 5.6% of reads, MMR, and 3,131/708,811, 0.44% of reads, RMMR) and L. squamata (29,347/917,258, 3.1% of reads, all of which were RMMRs). Removing potential chimeric reads from the dataset, increased BUSCO completeness scores by a marginal amount in the case of C. elegans MDA (Table 2) but led to decreases or little change in other statistics. However, for L. squamata this approach increased duplicate and fragmented BUSCO's for the RMMR dataset over the de novo assembly (Table 2). Due to little variation in genome statistics post removal and reassembly, we chose to proceed with the original dataset (L. squamata [MDA] Post Decontamination) decontaminated using the BlobTools pipeline for analysis in the case of L. squamata, and the original MDA amplified dataset (C. elegans [MDA]) without putative chimera removal for C. elegans.

Annotation of L. squamata genome

The final, decontaminated L. squamata assembly was estimated to be 21.98% repetitive. Of the 122 Mbp assembly, ~16 Mbp (13.74%) was composed of unclassified repeats, while the remaining ∼26 Mbp (8.24%) was composed of simple repeats (supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online). BRAKER predicted 28,377 gene models with a BUSCO metazoa_odb10 protein completeness score of 79.80% (Single copy BUSCOs [S]: 76.4%, Duplicate BUSCOs [D]: 3.4%, Fragmented BUSCOs [F]: 4.5%).

Phylogenetic Analysis

To infer the phylogenetic position of Gastrotricha within Lophotrochozoa, we identified orthologs among the genomes of 33 lophotrochozoans (plus five outgroup taxa) broadly spanning the diversity of the group (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). We assembled a supermatrix of 2,779 genes totaling 676,632 amino acids with 18.68% missing data. Maximum likelihood analysis using the posterior mean site frequency model (PMSF; Wang et al. 2018) fitting the C60 profile mixture model resulted in a tree with full bootstrap support for all nodes (Fig. 5). Gastrotricha was recovered as the sister taxon of Platyhelminthes with maximal support, recapitulating the proposed sister-group relationship recovered previously (Struck et al. 2014) and dubbed Rouphozoa. Rotifera, the only representative of Chaetognathifera (Park et al. 2024) in this study, was recovered as the sister group to all other Lophotrochozoa with Rouphozoa as the next branching lineage and the sister taxon of all remaining taxa (Trochozoa) consistent with Struck et al. (2014). Within Trochozoa, we recovered Mollusca as the sister taxon of a clade comprising, Annelida, Nemertea, and the lophophorate phyla (Brachiopoda, Bryozoa, and Phoronida), consistent with several previous studies (Struck et al. 2014; Kocot et al. 2017; Laumer et al. 2019). Due to the tree being based exclusively on genomes, several lineages of the superphylum, specifically most chaetognathiferan phyla (Chaetognatha, Gnathostomulida, and Micrognathozoa), Cycliophora, and Entoprocta could not be included in this analysis.

Hox Gene Analysis

Homeobox domain-encoding genes were extracted from the L. squamata gene annotations using Easel and HMMER (Finn et al. 2011). Hox genes were identified through a maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis including Hox gene sequences from diverse other lophotrochozoan taxa and Drosophila melanogaster. This approach recovered only four Hox genes: Hox2 (LSQUAM g6522.t1), Hox3 (LSQUAM g26458.t1), Lox4 (LSQUAM g24235.t1), and Post2 (LSQUAM g3662.t1; Fig. 6; supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). Additionally, a possible Post1 gene (LSQUAM g18500.t1) was recovered in our phylogenetic analysis as the sister sequence to all other sampled Post1 sequences; however, a BLAST (v.2.61.1) search against the NCBI nonredundant protein database did not return any known Post1 sequences. Engrailed (LSQUAM g26599.t1) was also identified. Notably, each of the recovered hox genes was located on a different contig. We also used HbxFinder (Zhong and Holland 2011; Mulhair et al. 2023) to attempt to extract any homeodomain-containing proteins missed by our structural annotation strategy from the decontaminated genome assembly, but none of the sequences identified by HbxFinder as homeobox domain-containing could be annotated as Hox genes not present in our gene models. Only an adult transcriptome is available for this species, and our investigation into these data also failed to recover any additional hox genes.

Discussion

Here, we investigated the utility of MDA for generating adequate template DNA from small-bodied organisms for PacBio HiFi sequencing. Although coverage uniformity with respect to GC and repeat content are known biases of MDA, our results show that MDA of DNA obtained directly from lysed cells can be used to generate template for HiFi sequencing with limited coverage bias and artifacts in the resulting data.

Given existing high-quality genomic resources, C. elegans was chosen to explore the uniformity of coverage with respect to GC and repeat content by comparing our HiFi data generated from MDA DNA to HiFi data generated from unamplified DNA extracted from a pool of worms. Compared to HiFi reads generated from unamplified DNA (Gini Index of 0.16), reads generated from MDA DNA (Gini Index of 0.14) performed similarly in terms of coverage, including repetitive 100 kbp blocks with >20% repeats and >45% repeats demonstrating that starting with samples as small as one-half C. elegans may not result in amplification bias (Fig. 4). While our de novo C. elegans assembly is not chromosome-level, it performed well in terms of BUSCO completeness and k-mer completeness (Table 2). The de novo C. elegans assembly based on reads from unamplified DNA from a pool of worms (49 contigs, N50 = 6.69 Mbp) did result in a more contiguous assembly than that generated from MDA DNA (336 contigs, N50 = 0.86 Mbp; Fig. 2b). However, it is unclear if this is due to biases in the dataset generated from MDA DNA or simply due to a longer mean HiFi read length of 15,157 bp for the dataset based on a pool of worms compared to 13,885 bp for the MDA DNA-based dataset. Notably, the two assemblies have a similar number of total insertions, relocations, and inversions when compared to the reference (C. elegans [MDA]: 206, C. elegans [Pooled]: 207; supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online). As the two assemblies have a similar number of assembly errors with respect to the reference, we conclude MDA amplification of a single individual did not introduce enough artifacts to hinder the assembly of a genome resembling the reference. It should be noted that our C. elegans assembly has a heterozygosity of (0.4705%); while sequencing a pool of many worms of this species resulted in a rather contiguous genome assembly, we speculate that such a strategy applied to e.g. marine invertebrates with higher heterozygosity would not perform as well. When compared to PacBio Ultra-Low Input assemblies from single organisms, such as springtails (Schneider et al. 2021), our C. elegans assembly has comparable completeness, albeit with lower contiguity. While our assembly performed well in terms of completeness (BUSCO Nematoda score of 94.9%), N50 was notably lower (868 kbp) and the assembly consisted of a higher number of contigs (336) when compared to assemblies of similarly sized springtail genomes (N50 = 5.6 Mbp, 142 contigs, 211 Mbp genome size for Desoria tigrina and N50 = 8.7 Mbp, 79 contigs, 165 Mbp genome size, for Sminthurides aquaticus). This suggests that the PacBio Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow is likely a better choice than our MDA-based strategy for animals that can provide adequate HMW template DNA (>5 ng) or when pooling is possible, but obtaining enough HMW DNA would not be possible in the case of a single C. elegans, and meiofaunal animals where a whole animal contains roughly an order of magnitude less DNA than required.

Application to De novo Genomics of Single Meiofaunal Organisms

Our results demonstrate that this approach can be successfully used on small-bodied organisms without a reference genome to produce assemblies that are both rather contiguous and complete. The L. squamata assembly consists of 157 contigs with an N50 of 3.9 Mbp, approaching the level of contiguity produced using the PacBio Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow (Schneider et al. 2021; Fig. 3). The inferred BUSCO nucleotide completeness score of 80.8% for the decontaminated L. squamata genome based on the metazoa_odb10 set suggests that this genome is lacking nearly 20% of the nuclear protein-coding genes thought to be single copy in all metazoans. However, L. squamata is a long-branched taxon in our phylogenomic analysis and Gastrotricha as a phylum has been shown to be long branched in previous phylogenomic studies (Struck et al. 2014; Kocot et al. 2017; Laumer et al. 2019; Marlétaz et al. 2019). Also, there are no gastrotrichs or early branching flatworms in the metazoa_odb10 reference dataset (Manni et al. 2021). Like the reference genome of C. elegans (BUSCO metazoa_odb10 score of just 73.3%), L. squamata may have performed poorly in this BUSCO analysis due to sequence divergence rather than the absence of these genes, although the loss of BUSCOs in compact invertebrate genomes has been reported previously (Deutekom et al. 2019; Cunha et al. 2023). Regardless, the score recovered here represents a relatively high level of completeness when compared to the proteomes of other rouphozoan taxa (supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online).

Annotation of the decontaminated L. squamata genome with transcriptome evidence from an adult transcriptome resulted in 28,377 genes, which protein BUSCO (metazoa_odb10) found to be 79.8% complete. This is comparable to the BUSCO score for the genome assembly, suggesting that the predicted gene model set is of high quality given the data. The shared k-mer percentage between the two L. squamata haplotypes is 66.24%, indicating a substantial amount of genetic sequence divergence between the haplotypes. This is consistent with the genome's heterozygosity of 2.987%, where frequent SNPs and structural variations contribute to the lower overlap. The divergence between haplotypes is reflected in the individual haplotype completeness scores (L. squamata [MDA] Haplotype I, L. squamata [MDA] Haplotype 2), which are around 76% or lower. Despite this, the combined haplotypes reach 99% completeness, demonstrating that the assembly successfully captures most of the genome's content. The lower haplotype completeness scores likely reflect true biological variation between haplotypes rather than a technical limitation of the method or sequencing strategy. This contrasts with a scenario where the genome has low heterozygosity and a high percentage of shared k-mers between haplotypes, in which a low completeness score would more likely indicate some sort of error in amplification or sequencing. This contrasts with the results for the MDA amplified C. elegans genome, whose haplotype phased assembly has a Merqury completeness of 96.8643% and a completeness of 98.3141% when both haplotypes are included. The shared k-mer percentage between the two C. elegans haplotypes is 90.43%, which reflects lower sequence divergence between the haplotypes, and is consistent with the genome's heterozygosity of 0.4705%. As a result, the k-mer completeness for both haplotypes is high (>90%), as there is minimal genetic variation between haplotypes. This high shared k-mer percentage and completeness highlight the lower level of biological divergence and the overall robustness of the assembly process for this species. This result likely is due to the lower heterozygosity of the C. elegans genome (0.4705%) compared to that of L. squamata. Taken together, these results demonstrate that MDA combined with highly accurate HiFi sequencing can result in genome assemblies of similar quality to those of other nonmodel invertebrates (Rayko et al. 2020).

Despite the contiguity of the L. squamata assembly, more bias with respect to repeat content and GC richness was observed in these data than in those from C. elegans (Figs. 2a,b and 4). When compared to the C. elegans reference genome (18.40% repeats, 35% GC content), the genome of L. squamata (21.98% repeats, 43% GC content) has a slightly higher repeat content. We observed increased coverage in regions with >45% GC content, particularly in 100 kbp blocks containing more than 45% repeats and in relatively short contigs, as well as lower coverage of highly repetitive sequences not exhibiting a high GC content (Fig. 4). Amplification bias in GC-rich regions is a known artifact of MDA (Dean et al. 2002; de Bourcy et al. 2014). However, in the absence of a reference genome for L. squamata to map to, it is unclear the extent to which repetitive regions are assembled, leading to potentially more coverage in repetitive regions due to reads mapping to fragmented repetitive contigs.

MDA has been explored in the context of Oxford Nanopore (ONT) sequencing to produce relatively contiguous genomes from a meiofaunal priapulid, Tubiluchus corallicola (Lord et al. 2023) and single nematodes (Lee et al. 2023). For C. elegans, Lee et al. generated an assembly consisting of 499 contigs with an N50 of 656.2 kbp and a nucleotide BUSCO (eukaryota_odb10) completeness score of 97.6%, compared to 336 contigs with an N50 of 868.1 kbp and a nucleotide BUSCO (metazoa_odb10) score of 92.2% in the present study. However, applying this approach to 13 other nonmodel nematodes, they recovered genome assembles ranging from 136.6 Mbp to 738.8 Mbp in 1,557 to 32,219 contigs with N50 values of 26.3 to 441.0 kbp. Drops in coverage were observed around highly repetitive regions but coverage with respect to GC content was not examined in detail. Notably, T7 endonuclease digestion was needed in the case of ONT sequencing, as MDA's secondary branching products can clog sequencing pores, reducing yield. Due to the nature of library preparation following shearing and size selection, endonuclease digestion is not needed for HiFi sequencing, a benefit of the approach presented here.

Chimeric Reads

We were also concerned about the potential of MDA to form chimeras. However, investigation of inverted sequences and tandem regions with Alvis identified only one suspected chimeric contig in our C. elegans assembly and none in our L. squamata assembly. Pseudo-short reads produced from the raw HiFi reads further aided in potential chimera identification. These reads are defined as pseudo-short reads as they were produced in a way that does not reflect an artificial short read library. The pseudo-short reads were only made using the first and last 500 bp of each read and are not made by randomly distributing paired 500 bp forward and reverse reads across all HiFi reads. It is assumed that if a chimeric read was formed by joining DNA fragments end-to-end then the sequenced ends of a given read would map to different regions in the genome as that read reflects two true reads joined into a chimera. Any pseudo-short read pair (MMRs and RMMRs) that spanned a contig–contig gap resulted in the removal of their parent read from the original read set and the dataset was reassembled. While the removal of these suspected chimeric reads did subtly improve contig N50, this strategy also slightly reduced the BUSCO completeness of the de novo L. squamata genome (Table 2). Whether or not L. squamata HiFi reads in which their 500 bp ends map to two different contigs in the final assembly do in fact represent chimeric sequences or simply represent reads that fail to span contig–contig gaps remains unknown, this technique is a useful, albeit an aggressive, method to check for potential chimeric sequences in a dataset generated using this approach. As we lysed cells and immediately proceeded with MDA in the same tube without a DNA extraction procedure, our template DNA underwent little handling that could cause breakage. Less handling may produce less breakage which would result in fewer free 3′ ends of strands that could anneal to another strand and act as a primer to initiate chimera formation. A low level of suspected chimeras in our data could also be due to the relatively low reaction gain of our amplification reactions; however, this could not be measured as cells were directly lysed and DNA input was not quantified (Lasken and Stockwell 2007; de Bourcy et al. 2014).

As chimeric reads may be more problematic in data obtained from more fragmented template DNA, paired-end Illumina data generated from e.g. half of the specimen lysate taken before MDA is performed could be used in future studies to aid in the detection of chimeras (see Lee et al. 2023). Examining the mapping of true paired-end short reads to postamplification HiFi reads could be used to reveal chimeric reads as one of the reads in a pair would be expected to map on one side of the chimeric junction while its mate would be expected to map elsewhere, as in the case of our pseudo-short read dataset.

Phylogenomic Analysis

The recovery of the sister taxon relationship of Gastrotricha and Platyhelminthes (Fig. 5) here has seen support in recent studies involving transcriptomes (Laumer et al. 2015; Kocot et al. 2017). Rouphozoa, the clade comprising Gastrotricha and Platyhelminthes has been noted to have few characters exclusively uniting the two phyla (Struck et al. 2014). Notably, members of both phyla ancestrally possess a simple pharynx (referred to as a pharynx simplex in Platyhelminthes), which dilates to ingest food. However, this simple feeding structure (contrasted with e.g. the radula in molluscs or the jaws in some annelids) is somewhat dubious as a synapomorphy. Additional detailed morphological comparisons are needed to better understand the close relationship of these phyla. While long-branch attraction has been regarded as a potential driver of the inferred phylogenetic positions of platyzoan phyla in previous studies, transcriptome-based studies that have specifically attempted to ameliorate this artifact have also recovered the clade (Struck et al. 2014; Laumer et al. 2015, 2019; Kocot et al. 2017). As genomes reach chromosome scale, alternative sources of phylogenetic signal such as chromosome rearrangements may provide increased support for relationships among phyla who exhibit fast evolutionary rates (Schultz et al. 2023).

Hox Gene Analysis

We recovered four Hox genes in the genome of L. squamata, Hox2, Hox3, Lox4, and Post2 (Fig. 6, supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). We also recovered a potential Post1 gene. However, BLAST (v.2.61.1) results were inconclusive with respect to its identity and thus more investigation, ideally with the inclusion of more full-length Hox gene sequences from representatives of Platyhelminthes and Chaetognathifera, is needed to help confirm its identity. Hox genes, responsible for anterior–posterior body axis patterning during development, have been suggested to be responsible for the diversity of body plans among lophotrochozoan taxa, resulting from variations in linkage and spatial and temporal expression (Hejnol et al. 2021). The presence and absence of Hox genes have been shown to be variable in lineages that are fast evolving, such as Platyhelminthes (Barucca et al. 2016). No studies to date have investigated the Hox gene complement in Gastrotricha, which does not allow for investigation of gain and loss within the phylum. As is the case in Platyhelminthes, some gastrotrich species may have more Hox genes in their genome, and thus our findings in L. squamata should not be taken as representative of the entire phylum as it is a freshwater representative of a mostly marine phylum and represents only one of the two major clades within the phylum. The apparent absence of several Hox genes within the L. squamata genome may be due to our inability to recover fast-evolving Hox genes, but given the highly conserved nature of homeobox domains (Ferrier and Minguillón 2003), this seems unlikely. Several fast-evolving phyla have also seen losses in several Hox genes beyond Gastrotricha and Platyhelminthes, such as the parasitic Dicyemida, which is suspected to be related to their extremely reduced body plan (Zverkov et al. 2019). While gastrotrichs are decidedly not as simple as dicyemids, their relatively simple body plan could be due, at least in part, to a reduction of the Hox cluster, or a symptom of body plan simplification. As the genome is potentially incomplete, we note that this may not be the full Hox complement of L. squamata, but the available data suggest significant Hox gene loss.

Applications

Here, we show that the use of MDA to generate template for PacBio HiFi sequencing can result in relatively contiguous and complete genomes from microscopic animals. Although we detected bias in our MDA-based data, combination of this approach with other low-input sequencing techniques, such as PiMmS (Laumer 2022), maybe a valuable strategy as these techniques are expected to differ in their biases with respect to amplification uniformity. In contrast to long-range PCR amplification used in PiMmS and the PacBio Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow, the isothermal amplification used in MDA eliminates PCR duplicates, albeit at the potential cost of reduced uniformity across GC-rich regions, particularly those high in repeats (Figs. 2 and 3a,b). Here, DNA was amplified directly from lysed cells, bypassing HMW DNA extraction that may result in substantial DNA loss from an already low starting amount of DNA and reducing DNA fragmentation caused by pipetting, which we view as a beneficial characteristic of this approach.

As low-input (<10 μg tissue) chromatin conformation sequencing approaches become more widely available (Hughes and Davies 2024), a combination of approaches using amplification that is linear rather than exponential such as LIANTI WGA (Chen et al. 2017) after construction of an ultra-low-input Hi-C library may prove to further increase the contiguity of genomes produced from a minute number of cells. Extremely low-input Hi-C methods that have been used for small amounts of tissues, or from single diploid human cells such as in the case of Dip-C may prove extremely useful for organisms composed of only thousands of cells, allowing scaffolding of genome produced via MDA (Díaz et al. 2018; Tan et al. 2018).

Careful consideration of the expected amount of DNA in a sample and DNA quality should be considered when deciding between DNA amplification with MDA directly from lysed cells, as performed here, versus HMW DNA extraction prior to amplification with either MDA or long-range PCR-based approaches. Library diversity and complexity are important considerations following any amplification protocol. Low complexity libraries, resulting from degraded or too little template DNA, and libraries with fragments rich in chimeras, which are more likely to arise when working with degraded DNA, can lead to poor quality assemblies. Given the performance of MDA on roughly one order of magnitude less DNA than required for the PacBio Ultra-Low DNA Input Workflow from directly lysed, living cells, this strategy may be particularly advantageous when such samples are available. Finally, MDA's yield of large amounts of DNA from limiting starting material allows multiple sequencing libraries to be produced from a single round of amplification, making it possible to increase coverage across under-amplified genomic regions, such as those with lower GC content.

Given the successful application of this method to the organisms studied here, an important direction for future work would be to apply this strategy to other small-bodied phyla that are currently lacking published genomes (e.g. Gnathostomulida, Micrognathozoa, Entoprocta, and Cycliophora). Increased genomic sampling of small-bodied phyla will enable phylogenomic analyses that stand to resolve long-debated questions about deep metazoan phylogeny. Such genomic resources would also lead to an improved understanding of the evolution of small-bodied organisms and assist in uncovering signatures and commonalities underlying the genomic architecture of secondarily miniaturized animals and small-bodied animals in general. Presently, the genomic consequences of miniaturization are poorly understood given the dearth of genomes from microscopic animals. Work to date has revealed that while some small-bodied lineages exhibit simple genome architecture with significant reduction in gene and repeat content with respect to their large-bodied relatives (Seo et al. 2001; Mikhailov et al. 2016), others have taken a more conservative route in regards to genome compaction (Martín-Durán et al. 2021; Worsaae et al. 2023) and some small-bodied organisms, such as entoprocts, appear to have large (∼2 Gbp) genomes (Roberts NG, Kocot KM, unpublished data). While our results demonstrate the utility of an MDA-based genome sequencing strategy for small-bodied animals with relatively small genomes, the efficacy of this approach for organisms that are small-bodied, but with larger genomes needs rigorous examination.

Conclusion

The amount of DNA has long been a limiting factor in de novo genome projects and, despite recent advances, many organisms still contain too little DNA for genome sequencing using available long-read sequencing library preparation protocols. In this study, the efficacy of whole-genome sequencing following MDA with Phi29 DNA polymerase was assessed. We demonstrate that HiFi sequencing data produced from MDA DNA can successfully lead to a contiguous and complete assembly for both model organisms such as C. elegans (Nematoda) and for nonmodel organisms such as L. squamata (Gastrotricha) with limited coverage bias and an extremely low incidence of chimeras. This methodology could be further expanded upon or combined with other scaffolding techniques or amplification strategies to increase the contiguity of genomes from amplified material. This strategy has the potential to greatly expand the availability of whole-genome sequencing data from a wide variety of DNA-limited sources including but not limited to other microscopic metazoans.

Materials and Methods

Laboratory Methods

Live cultures of C. elegans (Bristol N2) and L. squamata were purchased from Carolina Biological Supply Co. and kept alive in the laboratory under manufacturer-recommended conditions. Half of a single individual C. elegans hermaphrodite (∼500 µm) cut with a sterile razor blade and a single individual L. squamata (∼190 µm) were added to 4 µl of Qiagen REPLI-g Advanced Single-Cell Storage Buffer using a sterilized Irwin Loop as input for amplification using the Qiagen REPLI-g Advanced Single-Cel kit following the manufacturer's protocol (i.e. lysis with SDS and denaturation at room temperature, incubation at 30 °C for 2 h, 60 °C for 10 min, and 4 °C until purification). Resulting amplified DNA, 14.92 µg from L. squamata and 8.74 µg from C. elegans were sent to Discovery Life Sciences for sequencing on a shared single PacBio Sequel II flow cell, resulting in ½ a flow cell each.

Assembly

HiFi reads were extracted using the pbbioconda extracthifi (v.1.0) package and each set of reads was assembled with Hifiasm (v.0.15.2; Cheng et al. 2021). Genome statistics were generated using QUAST (v.5.0; Gurevich et al. 2013), and completeness was measured with BUSCO (v.4.0.2; Simão et al. 2015; Table 2); completeness of all assemblies was analyzed with the metazoa_odb10 BUSCO set, and additionally, both the de novo and reference guided C. elegans assemblies were assessed with the nematoda_odb10 BUSCO set. All assemblies' k-mer completeness was measured with Merqury (v.1.3) and Meryl (v.1.4.1) with a k-mer size of 21 (Rhie et al. 2020). The percentage of shared k-mers between haplotypes was estimated using Merqury (v.1.3) and Meryl (v.1.4.1). All distinct (nonredundant) k-mers from both haplotypes were estimated using the following command: meryl union output union_kmers genome_hap_1 genome_hap_2. The amount of distinct (nonredundant) shared k-mers was estimated using the following command: meryl intersect [equal-to 1 genome_hap_1] equal-to 1 genome_hap_2 output intersect_kmers. The percentage of shared k-mers was calculated as ((shared distinct k-mers between haplotypes)/(all k-mers present in both haplotypes)) ×100 (supplementary table S5, Supplementary Material online). Genome heterozygosity was measured using GenomeScope (v.2.0; Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020). HiFi reads were mapped back to their respective assemblies using Minimap2 (v.2.22; Li 2018). The C. elegans and L. squamata assemblies were searched against the UniProt reference proteomes database (2021 June 2) with Diamond (v.2.0.14; Buchfink et al. 2015) using the following settings: diamond blastx --db ∼/databases/uniprot_taxonmap.dmnd --query assembly.bp.p_ctg.fasta --outfmt 6 qseqid staxids bitscore qseqid sseqid pident length mismatch gapopen qstart qend sstart send evalue bitscore --sensitive --max-target-seqs 1 --evalue 1e-25 --threads 32. The output of these tools as well as BUSCO were used as input for BlobTools (v.2.6.1). Further, the de novo C. elegans assembly was aligned to the publicly available reference using Minimap2 (v.2.22) C. elegans N2 genome (GCA_000002985.3) to assess completeness and screen for the presence of chimeric reads as identified by Alvis (v.1.0; Martin and Leggett 2021), a program that identifies chimeras in the assembly based on mapping statistics from mapping raw reads to the assembled genome. All de novo assemblies (C. elegans and L. squamata) were screened for contamination using BlobTools (v.2.6.1) by removing all contigs that had top BLAST (v.2.16.0) hits to taxa other than Metazoa or “no-hit” when searched against the NCBI nonredundant protein database.

Annotation

The repeat content of the contaminant-filtered L. squamata genome was assessed using RepeatModeler (v.2.0.1; Flynn et al. 2020) and soft masked using RepeatMasker (v.4.1.2; Smit et al. 2015). Raw transcript reads from the publicly available L. squamata transcriptome (SRR1273732) were downloaded, quality trimmed with TrimGalore (v.0.6.10) from NCBI and mapped against the soft masked genome using STAR (v.2.4.0; 96.83% reads too short for mapping, 33.63% of filtered reads uniquely mapped, 37.09% of filtered reads mapped to multiple loci; Dobin et al. 2013). The soft masked genome and mapped transcripts were given to the BRAKER (v.2.1.6; Hoff et al. 2019) pipeline as input to produce gene annotations with the following command: braker.pl --useexisting --cores 12 --softmasking --UTR = on --crf --makehub --gff3 --species = Lepidodermella_squamata --genome Lepidodermella_sp.asm.bp.p_ctg_filtered.fasta.masked --bam RNAseq.bam. Gene annotations were measured for completeness using the BUSCO metazoa_odb10 database.

Read Coverage Estimation and Pseudo Read Mapping

Assemblies of all three data sources (L. squamata and amplified and nonamplified C. elegans) were used as subjects to map HiFi reads as a query to estimate genome coverage and coverage bias. Following read mapping with Minimap2 (v.2.22; Li 2018), and removal of multimappers with SAMtools (v.1.10.0; Danecek et al. 2021; samtools view -F 2308), Bedtools (v.2.30.0; Quinlan 2014) was used to separate the genomes into independent 100 kbp windows in which both overall read coverage, mean coverage, repeat content and GC content were calculated. Resulting datasets and graphs were modified and constructed in the R computing environment (v.4.3.3; R Core Team 2022). Lorenz graphs were constructed utilizing the package gglorenz. Additionally, we assessed the coverage uniformity of our C. elegans HiFi data mapped against the reference genome, as well as PacBio HiFi reads generated from unamplified DNA extracted for a pool of worms of the same strain (SRR22507561). Read depth, breadth, and mapping rate for C. elegans to the reference assembly were calculated using SAMtools (v.1.10.0).

For data visualization, and further mapping comparisons, both C. elegans assemblies (pooled and MDA amplified) were aligned to the reference using the dnadiff function in MUMmer3 (v.3.0; Kurtz et al. 2004). One-to-one mappings, combined with datasets computed as described above were used as input for visualization in Circos (v.0.69-9; Krzywinski et al. 2009). Summary outputs from each analysis (C. elegans [MDA] to the reference, C. elegans [Pooled] to the reference) are presented in supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online.

To test for the presence of possible chimeric sequences, pseudo-short reads were created by taking the first and last 500 bp of each read from the dataset using seqkit (v.2.7.0; Shen et al. 2016). Next these pseudo-short reads were mapped to the final assembly using Minimap2 (v.2.22) to identify which long reads may be chimeric. If two of these pseudo-short reads originating from the same long read mapped to different nonoverlapping contigs, the original read used to produce the pseudo-short reads was removed from the original read set using seqkit (v.2.7.0). These reads in which any pseudo-short read mapped to different nonoverlapping contigs were referred to simply as MMRs. To further filter for reads that mapped “uniquely” across contigs, the alignment was filtered by a MAPQ score of 30 using SAMtools (v.1.10.0), and the original long read from which the short reads originated was removed from the dataset, these reads are referred to as RMMRs. For C. elegans this process was done for all (MMRs) and for (RMMRs), but for L. squamata all MMRs were RMMRs as all reads had MAPQ scores higher than 30. Thus, only one read set is shown in Table 1 to reduce redundancy.

Orthology Inference and Supermatrix Construction

To investigate the phylogenetic position of Gastrotricha, orthology determination, and supermatrix assembly were performed following a modification of the pipeline of Kocot et al. (2017) implementing new versions of the programs used if applicable. Lophotrochozoan genomes (including L. squamata) broadly spanning the diversity of the group were selected along with five outgroup taxa resulting in 38 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). Homologous sequences were identified using OrthoFinder (v.2.4.0; Emms and Kelly 2019) and sequences shorter than 100 amino acids were removed, keeping the longest sequence. Homogroups were retained if they included >75% of the total of all taxa, with those with less being removed. Fasta files were aligned with MAFFT (v.7.310; Katoh et al. 2002), suspected mistranslated regions were removed with HmmCleaner (v.0.180750; Di Franco et al. 2019), and regions high in gaps or ambiguity were trimmed with BMGE (v.1.12.2; Criscuolo and Gribaldo 2010). Maximum likelihood gene trees were generated using IQ-Tree2 (v.2.0.4; Minh et al. 2020) with the best-fitting model of amino acid substitution for each gene. Subsequent fasta files and trees were used as input for PhyloPyPruner (v.0.9.5; https://pypi.org/project/phylopypruner) to retrieve a set of strict [1:1] orthologs with the settings phylopypruner --min-taxa 15 --min-support 0.9 --mask pdist --trim-lb 3 --trim-divergent 0.75 --min-pdist 0.01 --prune LS. The --min-taxa flag guaranteed the removal of pruned alignments that did not include at least 15 taxa from the original dataset.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The final supermatrix from PhyloPyPruner (v.0.9.5) of 676,632 amino acid positions was used for phylogeny reconstruction in IQ-Tree2 (v.2.0.4). Using maximum likelihood with the simplest profile mixture model available, LG + C20 +F, a guide tree for PMSF (Wang et al. 2018) was produced. The PMSF analysis fitted the C60 profile mixture model, LG + C60 + F, to the data and reconstructed the phylogeny assessing nodal support with 1,000 rapid bootstraps.

Hox Gene Identification

A profile hidden Markov model was constructed from the homeodomain database seed alignment (PF00046) using HMMER (v.3.3; Finn et al. 2011) using command: hmmbuild PF00046_uniprot.hmm PF00046.alignment.seed. Easel (v.0.49; https://github.com/EddyRivasLab/easel) was used to index the annotated proteins of L. squamata and hmmsearch was used to retrieve hits based on the seed hmm: hmmsearch --tblout myhits.tbl PF00046_uniprot.hmm L_squam.aa.fasta. Easel was then used to retrieve the protein hits: esl-sfetch -f L_squam.aa.fasta hitlist.txt>myhits.fa. Resulting sequences were aligned with additional Hox and Engrailed gene sequences from diverse other lophotrochozoans and D. melanogaster retrieved from NCBI using MAFFT (v.7.310; Katoh et al. 2002) with the --auto setting. A gene tree was constructed based on this alignment using IQ-Tree 2 (iqtree2 -s hoxes_v_3.1_pruned_aligned.fasta -m MFP -T AUTO -bb 1000). Engrailed was used to root the tree. Hox genes were identified based on the results of the phylogenetic analysis. Further, an adult transcriptome of L. squamata was initially used to search for Hox genes using the above approach, however as the transcriptome was from an adult, few Hox genes, all of which were recovered using the genome, were found.

We also used HbxFinder (Commit number: 125; Zhong and Holland 2011; Mulhair et al. 2023) to attempt to extract any homeodomain-containing proteins missed by our structural annotation strategy from the decontaminated genome assembly. Sequences identified by HbxFinder were aligned to the dataset described above using the same approach described above and were manually filtered to exclude aberrant sequences without clear homology to the consensus homeobox domain sequence reported by (Bürglin and Affolter 2016). The resulting alignment was then realigned, and a phylogenetic analysis was performed as described with identifications based on the maximum likelihood tree. The identity of Lox4 (recovered as sister to the clade comprising Lox2 and other Lox4 sequences) was confirmed based on a BLAST (v.2.61.1) search against the NCBI NR database on February 9, 2024.
