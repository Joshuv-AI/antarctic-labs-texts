Obstetric violence and associated factors among women who gave birth at public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, 2024: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mitiku Desalegn, Aman Endris Shikur, Erstie Nigusse, Yidnekachew Dedachew, Tewoderos Shitemaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstetric violence during labor and delivery is a significant contributor to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Developing respectful maternity care during childbirth has been identified as an effective approach to reducing perinatal mortality. This study aimed to assess the magnitude of obstetric violence and associated factors among women who gave birth at public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among mothers who gave birth at public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia from November 1 to 30, 2024. Four hospitals were randomly selected, and a systematic sampling method was employed to enroll 409 postpartum women. Data were collected using structured face-to-face interviews and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with obstetric violence. Statistical significance was declared at p-value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 308 (75.3%) mothers with 95% CI (71.84–79.67) experienced at least one form of obstetric violence during institutional childbirth. The three most commonly reported forms were non-consented care (62.3%), non-confidential care (44.5%), and physical abuse (39.6%). Induction of labor (AOR = 3.56, 95% CI: 1.89–7.79), cesarean delivery (AOR = 0.304, 95% CI: 0.18–0.82), and female birth attendants (AOR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.76–6.47) were significantly associated with obstetric violence.ConclusionThe magnitude of obstetric violence was high, with four out of five mothers experiencing at least one form of mistreatment during childbirth. Induction of labor, mode of delivery, and sex of birth attendant were significant predictors. Introduction

Obstetric violence (OV) refers to the mistreatment of women during childbirth or pregnancy by healthcare providers [1]. It encompasses seven forms: physical abuse, non-confidential care, non-dignified care, non-consented care, abandonment/neglect, discrimination, and detention [2–4]. Globally, approximately 140 million births occur annually, and while women are advised to give birth in healthcare facilities to ensure access to skilled providers, facility-based delivery does not guarantee respectful care [5, 6].

OV is a significant contributor to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, influencing women’s future birth place choices [7, 8]. Developing respectful maternity care (RMC) during childbirth, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, has been identified as more effective in reducing perinatal mortality than antenatal or postnatal care approaches alone [9].

According to the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey, institutional delivery remains low at 48% [10]. Lack of respectful care leads to physical, emotional, and psychological harm and is among the factors contributing to low facility delivery rates [11]. Despite Addis Ababa having high institutional delivery rates (95%) [10, 12], studies reveal substantial mistreatment. A study on HIV-infected women in Addis Ababa found that over three-quarters experienced OV, with failure to obtain informed consent, privacy violations, neglect of life-threatening complications, and physical abuse being the major forms [13].

Evidence from Ethiopian public health facilities shows OV prevalence ranging from 75.1% in Gondar to 79.7% in Gedeo Zone [14, 15]. Such abuse compromises women’s rights to quality healthcare and freedom from violence. However, limited evidence exists on OV in Addis Ababa’s public hospitals. This study aimed to assess the magnitude of obstetric violence and identify associated factors among women giving birth in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from November 1 to 30, 2024, in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital city. Addis Ababa has approximately 5 million inhabitants, with 12 public hospitals providing maternity services. Four hospitals were randomly selected: St. Peter’s TB Specialized Hospital, St. Paul Millennium Medical College, Zewditu Memorial Hospital, and Menelik II Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. These facilities provide maternal and neonatal services with average monthly deliveries of 320, 900, 580, and 430 respectively (total 2,230 births).

Source and study population

The source population comprised all mothers who gave birth in Addis Ababa public hospitals. The study population included mothers who gave birth at the four selected public hospitals during the study period and met inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women who gave birth at the selected public hospitals and who had completed their hospital stay after a discharge decision had been made were included in the study. This approach was adopted to minimize potential underreporting of obstetric violence that might occur if women were interviewed while still admitted. Women who experienced loss of consciousness or were unable to recall their labor and delivery process were excluded.

Sample size determination for the first objective

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula for the first objective (magnitude of OV), based on a previous study in Gedeo Zone, South Ethiopia [15] reporting non-dignified care at 40.5%. With 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and adding 10% for non-response, the final sample size was 409. This was larger than sample sizes calculated for associated factors (ranging from 318 to 400) from previous studies [13, 15, 16].

Sample size determination for the second objective

The sample size for the second objective (identifying factors associated with obstetric violence) was calculated using the double population proportion formula, considering various exposure variables from previous studies. Sampling procedure

Four public hospitals were randomly selected from Addis Ababa. Proportional allocation was performed based on each hospital’s average monthly deliveries: St. Peter’s TB Specialized Hospital (320/2,230 × 409 = 59), St. Paul’s Millennium Medical College (900/2,230 × 409 = 165), Zewditu Memorial Hospital (580/2,230 × 409 = 106), and Menelik II Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (430/2,230 × 409 = 79). A systematic random sampling technique was employed to select study participants. A separate sampling frame was created for each hospital based on the expected number of deliveries during the study period. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing each hospital’s average monthly deliveries by its allocated sample size. This resulted in facility-specific intervals: approximately 5 for each hospital (St. Peter’s: 320/59 ≈ 5.4; St. Paul’s: 900/165 ≈ 5.5; Zewditu: 580/106 ≈ 5.5; Menelik II: 430/79 ≈ 5.4). In all facilities, k was rounded to 5.

In each hospital, a random start number between 1 and 5 was selected. Subsequently, every 5th postpartum woman who met the inclusion criteria was interviewed consecutively until the allocated sample size for that facility was achieved (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Data were collected using face-to-face interviews with a structured questionnaire adapted from relevant literature [14, 15, 17]. The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated to Amharic (local language), and back-translated to English to ensure consistency. Four health professionals collected data, supervised by two health professionals who received training on study objectives and procedures.

Operational definition

Obstetric violence was measured using seven performance standards with 25 verification criteria adapted from previous studies [4, 18]. Women who answered “yes” to at least one criterion were considered to have experienced obstetric violence.

Normalization of violence (accepted violence):

Refers to the extent to which a woman perceives mistreatment during childbirth as a normal or expected part of the delivery process, often resulting from repeated exposure to or cultural acceptance of disrespectful care [4, 15].

Involvement in decision-making:

Refers to the degree to which a woman was actively engaged in discussions and choices regarding her labor and delivery care, including being informed about procedures, asked for consent, and given the opportunity to express preferences [17, 19].

Data quality assurance

Data collectors and supervisors received training on study objectives and interview techniques. A pre-test was performed on 5% of the calculated sample size at Alert Specialized Hospital, a facility not included in the main study, to assess the consistency and clarity of the data collection questionnaire. Regular meetings were held between data collectors and the principal investigator to address issues arising during data collection. Questionnaires were reviewed daily for completeness, consistency, and accuracy by the supervisors.

Before analysis, all collected data were thoroughly checked for completeness, outliers, and missing values. Incomplete questionnaires were reviewed for potential correction during data entry; those with critical missing data on the outcome or key exposure variables were excluded from the final analysis. Data were double-entered and cross-verified to minimize entry errors. Descriptive statistics were used to identify anomalies, and any inconsistencies were resolved by referring back to the original questionnaires.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered into SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations) were computed.

Variable selection was guided by a conceptual framework developed from the literature on respectful maternity care, mapping potential socio-demographic, obstetric, provider-related, and individual factors onto the outcome of obstetric violence. All independent variables from this framework were first examined using bivariable logistic regression. To avoid over-reliance on pp-value thresholds while maintaining model parsimony given the sample size (n = 409, events = 308), variables with p < 0.25p < 0.25 in bivariable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression. This approach balanced confounder identification against the risk of overfitting. Key theoretical confounders (e.g., parity, education) were retained for sensitivity checks regardless of bivariable significance.

Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ2 = 8.32χ2 = 8.32, df = 8, p = 0.411p = 0.411). Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were computed, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05p < 0.05.

All 409 invited postpartum women participated (100% response rate). The mean age was 27.88 years (± SD 4.864), with 229 (56%) aged 25–34 years. Most participants (66.0%) were permanent residents of Addis Ababa, and 371 (90.7%) were married. Regarding education, 218 (53.3%) had secondary education or above, while 63 (15.4%) had no formal education. Magnitude and forms of obstetric violence

Among 409 interviewed mothers, 308 (75.3%) with 95% CI (71.8–79.7) reported experiencing at least one form of obstetric violence. The three most commonly experienced forms were non-consented care (255, 62.3%), non-confidential care (182, 44.5%), and physical abuse (162, 39.6%) (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with obstetric violence

In bivariable analysis, educational status, number of ANC contacts, induction of labor, mode of delivery, involvement in decision-making, sex of main birth attendant, and profession of main birth attendant were associated with OV (p < 0.25). In multivariable logistic regression, induction of labor, mode of delivery, and sex of main birth attendant remained significantly associated with OV (p < 0.05).

Mothers whose labor was induced were more than three times more likely to experience OV compared to those with spontaneous labor onset (AOR = 3.56, 95% CI: 1.89–7.79; p = 0.002). Women who delivered via cesarean section were 69.6% less likely to report OV compared to those with spontaneous vaginal delivery (AOR = 0.304, 95% CI: 0.18–0.82; p = 0.001). Mothers attended by female birth attendants were three times more likely to experience OV compared to those attended by male attendants (AOR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.76–6.47; p = 0.003) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study assessed the magnitude and associated factors of obstetric violence among women giving birth in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The finding that 75.3% (95% CI: 71.8–79.7) of women experienced at least one form of OV indicates a high prevalence of mistreatment during facility-based childbirth, despite Ethiopian Ministry of Health efforts to promote compassionate care.

The magnitude is consistent with previous Ethiopian studies: Gondar University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital which reported (75.1%, 95% CI: 70.2–80.0) [14], Gedeo Zone (79.7%, 95% CI: 75.1–84.3) [15], and South Wollo (79.4%, 95% CI: 74.6–84.2) [4]. It also aligns with international studies from Mozambique (80%, 95% CI: 75.4–84.6) [20], Italy (78.4%, 95% CI: 73.1–83.7) [21], and Europe (76.3%, 95% CI: 71.8–80.8) [22]. However, this finding exceeds rates reported in Kenya (20%, 95% CI: 16.1–23.9) [23], sub-Saharan Africa systematic review (44.1%, 95% CI: 38.5–49.7) [24], India (32.4%, 95% CI: 27.3–37.5) [25], and Brazil (18.3%, 95% CI: 15.1–21.5) [26] .Conversely, the prevalence is lower than studies from Peru (97.4%, 95% CI: 95.1–99.7) [27], Pakistan (99.7%, 95% CI: 98.9–100) [28], Hawassa in Ethiopia (85.6%, 95% CI: 81.2–90.0) [29], and North Showa in Ethiopia (84.3%, 95% CI: 79.8–88.8) [16].

These wide variations can be attributed to several methodological differences across studies. First, data collection methods vary; the face-to-face interviews potentially lead to underreporting due to social desirability bias, whereas Kenyan and Brazilian studies using self-administered anonymous questionnaires or community-based designs may have captured more accurate or lower estimates depending on context. Second, the study setting; in high-volume public hospitals patient loads and staff-to-patient ratios vary. In Ethiopian public hospitals, midwife-to-patient ratios often exceed 1:15 during peak hours, a workload level shown to correlate directly with rushed care and non-consented procedures. Lower-prevalence settings typically have better staffing. Third, and most critically, the verification criteria and operational definitions differ substantially. Our study employed a broad definition of obstetric violence, encompassing seven domains and 25 verification criteria, and classified any woman reporting at least one form as having experienced OV. This inclusive approach likely yields a higher prevalence than studies using a narrower set of criteria. Fourth, differences in sample size affect the precision of estimates; smaller studies are more susceptible to random variation, producing less stable point estimates, contributing to apparent discrepancies.

Finally, the recall period; the time between childbirth and interview can influence what women are willing to disclose and how they remember their experience. Our study’s timing (after discharge) differs from those interviewing women immediately postpartum or months later, each with its own potential for recall bias. The consistency in prevalence across other Ethiopian studies may be due to use of similar measurement tools and operational definitions of obstetric violence, structural and systemic similarities across Ethiopian public hospitals and shared cultural norms and provider attitudes toward childbirth.

Non-consented care was the most prevalent form (62.3%) (95% CI: 57.6–67.0%), consistent with Gondar (63.6%) [14] and Gedeo Zone (66%) [15] but higher than Hadiya Zone (43.7%) [30] and sub-Saharan Africa (16.87%) [24]. However, it was lower than Kazakhstan (88.9%) [31], central Ethiopia (97.2%) [16], and a previous Addis Ababa study (78.3%) [13]. The most reported criterion under non-consented care (40.8%) was providers not obtaining consent before procedures, highlighting a critical gap in patient autonomy and informed decision-making.

This study found that women whose labor was induced were more than three times more likely to experience obstetric violence compared to those with spontaneous labor onset (AOR = 3.56, 95% CI: 1.89–7.79). This finding is consistent with a study from Gedeo Zone, South Ethiopia, which reported that women with induced labor had 2.5 times higher odds of experiencing mistreatment [15]. Similarly, research from Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, found that augmentation of labor was significantly associated with disrespect and abuse [8]. Several mechanisms may explain this association. First, induced labor often involves a cascade of medical interventions, and each additional intervention creates more opportunities for non-consented procedures and physical discomfort [32, 33]. Second, induced labor is frequently associated with more intense pain, which may reduce women’s capacity to participate in decision-making, thereby increasing vulnerability to mistreatment [33]. Third, from a healthcare providers perspective, induction cases are often perceived as higher risk, potentially leading to provider stress and curtailed communication with the patient [32]. These findings suggest that women with induced labor should be considered a priority group for respectful maternity care interventions.

Mothers attended by female birth attendants were three times more likely to experience obstetric violence compared to those attended by male attendants (AOR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.76–6.47). This finding is consistent with previous Ethiopian studies. Research among HIV-infected women in Addis Ababa found that those attended by female providers had 2.4 times higher odds of obstetric violence [13]. Similarly, a study in central Ethiopia reported that female birth attendants were more frequently associated with disrespectful care [9].

This counterintuitive finding requires careful, evidence-informed interpretation beyond the superficial claim that male providers are simply more respectful. First, female data collectors may have encouraged disclosure of mistreatment by female attendants, while underreporting of mistreatment by male providers may occur due to shame, fear, or cultural taboos against criticizing men [1]. Second, female midwives bear the highest patient loads in understaffed, high-volume wards, leading to burnout and rushed care [7]. Male attendants are fewer, often in supervisory roles with shorter patient interactions. Thus, the association likely reflects differential exposure to stressful environments, not inherent gender differences [6]. Third, Ethiopian women expect greater empathy from female caregivers. When unmet due to systemic pressures, mistreatment is reported more acutely. Male attendants face lower emotional expectations, so similar behaviors may go unreported [1]. Finally, unmeasured provider-level variables (workload, training, experience, staffing ratios) may explain the association [2]. Female attendants predominate in high-risk labor wards; male attendants in operating theatres. Future research should adjust for these confounders.

The finding that cesarean delivery was protective against OV (69.6% less likely) (AOR = 0.304, 95% CI: 0.18–0.82) aligns with Peruvian research [27] and may reflect that women undergoing cesarean sections experience fewer procedures than those delivering vaginally. Vaginal deliveries involve repeated examinations, potential episiotomies, and longer labor durations, creating more opportunities for mistreatment [30].

Implications of the study

The high magnitude of obstetric violence documented in this study has several implications for policy, practice, and research. For policy, the findings underscore the need for the Ethiopian Ministry of Health to strengthen the implementation and monitoring of respectful maternity care guidelines across public hospitals, with particular attention to ensuring informed consent for all procedures. For practice, healthcare facilities should prioritize targeted interventions for women undergoing labor induction, who are at higher risk of mistreatment, and provide additional support and supervision for female birth attendants to address the unexpected finding of higher OV rates among this group. Regular audits of patient experiences, coupled with confidential reporting mechanisms, could help identify mistreatment and promote accountability. For research, future studies should employ mixed methods to explore the contextual and systemic drivers of obstetric violence, including provider workload, training adequacy, and institutional culture, to inform evidence-based interventions that promote dignified and respectful care for all childbearing women.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal relationships between independent variables and OV. Recall bias may affect responses, as women were interviewed postpartum. Using health professionals as data collectors for a study on mistreatment by health professionals may lead to social desirability bias and power differentials which might result underreporting of mistreatment. Consequently, the true magnitude of obstetric violence is probably higher than the 75.3% reported here and future research should employ non-clinical data collectors with no affiliation to the study facilities to reduce this bias. The study was conducted in public hospitals in one city, limiting generalizability to private facilities or rural settings. This study did not assess provider-related factors such as job satisfaction, training history, work experience, or provider-to-client ratios, which may influence the quality of maternity care and the occurrence of obstetric violence. Their omission means the observed associations may be confounded by unmeasured systemic factors (e.g., female-staffed wards having higher patient loads). Consequently, these findings should be interpreted as associative, not causal, and future research should include such variables to clarify mechanisms. The use of face-to-face interviews alone may not capture all dimensions of mistreatment. Additionally, while a multi-hospital sampling procedure was used, the sample size calculation and statistical analyses did not incorporate a design effect or adjust for potential clustering by hospital. This may lead to an underestimation of standard errors and confidence intervals that are narrower than they would be under a fully clustered design.

Conclusion and recommendations

The magnitude of obstetric violence in Addis Ababa public hospitals is high, with three-quarters of women experiencing at least one form of mistreatment during childbirth. Non-consented care, non-confidential care, and physical abuse were the most prevalent forms. Induction of labor, mode of delivery, and sex of birth attendant were significant predictors.

These findings underscore the urgent need for interventions to promote respectful maternity care. The Ministry of Health should strengthen maternity services by incorporating healthcare provider training on respectful care, ensuring all procedures obtain informed consent, and addressing systemic factors contributing to mistreatment. Healthcare providers should prioritize women with induced labor, provide adequate information about indications, risks, and complications, and ensure individualized care. Female birth attendants require particular support to meet respectful maternity care standards.

Future research should explore women’s experiences using mixed methods to understand the contextual factors driving mistreatment. Implementation research evaluating interventions to reduce OV is needed, along with studies examining the long-term psychological impacts of obstetric violence on women and their families.
