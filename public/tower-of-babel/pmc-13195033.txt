New lineages provide insights into the convergent evolution of extreme salt adaptation within symbiotic Archaea
Authors: Joshua N Hamm, Nina Dombrowski, Luis E Valentin-Alvarado, Chris Greening, Tom A Williams, Anja Spang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental genomics has led to the discovery of many new lineages of archaea, including “DPANN” (or Nanobdellati), comprising organisms with small genomes, reduced gene content, and potentially symbiotic or parasitic lifestyles. DPANN live in various environments, and several lineages have been identified that are adapted to extremely high-salt concentrations, including the Nanohaloarchaeota. Since it was long thought that the Haloarchaea (within “Euryarchaeota”) were the only high salt-adapted archaea, the origins of these genome-reduced halophiles have been debated. Here, we used phylogenetic, comparative genomic, and gene tree-species tree reconciliation approaches to resolve the evolution of halophily within DPANN, making use of recently published genomes that help to inform the phylogenetic placement and genome evolution of salt-adapted lineages. Phylogenetic analysis placed Nanohaloarchaeota sister to a previously uncharacterized lineage, which we here refer to as Terrarchaeota. Terrarchaeota appear to be predominantly anaerobic thermophiles that are not adapted to high-salt concentrations, indicating that adaptation to high salt evolved after their divergence from Nanohaloarchaeota. Furthermore, our analyses identified genomic hallmarks of salt adaptation in another recently discovered halophilic DPANN lineage within Aenigmatarchaeota, the Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae. We found that the Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae have distinct sets of proteins that enable life at high salt concentrations but share a common mechanism of evolutionary adaptation, in which niche-relevant genes were acquired horizontally from their halophilic hosts. This work provides the first detailed investigation into the enigmatic Terrarchaeota, and new insights into the convergent evolution of high salt adaptation within symbiotic clades of Archaea.

Introduction

wThe DPANN archaea, named after the first member clades (Rinke et al. 2013; Castelle et al. 2015; Castelle and Banfield 2018; Dombrowski et al. 2019, 2020), comprise a diverse archaeal group consisting of at least 10 phyla, many representatives of which are predicted to be symbionts (Rinke et al. 2021). For instance, the first cultivated members of the DPANN belonging to the Nanoarchaeota (Huber et al. 2002; Wurch et al. 2016; St John et al. 2019; Kato et al. 2022), Nanohaloarchaeota (Hamm et al. 2019; La Cono et al. 2020; Reva et al. 2023), and Micrarchaeota (Golyshina et al. 2017; Krause et al. 2017; Sakai et al. 2022) are obligately dependent on archaeal hosts such as from the Sulfolobales, Halobacteriales, and Thermoplasmatales. Among these, the Nanohaloarchaeota (symbionts of Halobacteriales) represent the first DPANN lineage (Ghai et al. 2011; Narasingarao et al. 2012; Rinke et al. 2013) that comprises organisms with adaptations to extremely high salt concentrations (ie salt concentrations of >100–150 g/L (Oren 2024)).

Initial phylogenetic analyses varied as to the placement of these halophilic symbionts—either within DPANN (Rinke et al. 2013) or as a sister to the Halobacteriales (Narasingarao et al. 2012; Aouad et al. 2018; Feng et al. 2021). However, recent work using better-fitting phylogenetic models supports their placement within DPANN (Aouad et al. 2018; Castelle and Banfield 2018; Dombrowski et al. 2020; Rinke et al. 2021; Baker et al. 2024). Indeed it has recently been suggested that the DPANN form at least two major clades: Cluster 1, which includes the Altiarchaeota, Iainarchaeota, and Micrarchaeota and cluster 2, including Nanohaloarchaeota, Nanoarchaeota, Aenigmatarchaeota, EX4484-52, SpSt-1190, and Undinarchaeota (Dombrowski et al. 2020; Rinke et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024). The signal for a relationship with Halobacteriales might derive from shared amino acid usage patterns associated with salt adaptation (Baker et al. 2024), and/or from horizontal acquisition of niche-relevant genes from other halophiles (Mahendrarajah et al. 2023). Although several previous analyses have investigated the evolution of Nanohaloarchaeota (Xie et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2022), their closest sister group within DPANN and the genes underpinning salt adaptation remain to be elucidated.

In this study, we set out to investigate the mechanisms of salt adaptation in DPANN archaea through comparative genomics, drawing on recently published genome data (Dombrowski et al. 2017; Hou et al. 2020; Reysenbach et al. 2020; Vázquez-Campos et al. 2021) that proved relevant for understanding when and how Nanohaloarchaeota transitioned to a halophilic lifestyle. We then investigated common underpinning mechanisms of salt adaptation across DPANN, comparing the Nanohaloarchaeota clade with an independently evolved, recently described halophilic DPANN lineage, the Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae (Gutiérrez-Preciado et al. 2024). Our analyses placed the Nanohaloarchaeota within DPANN cluster 2 archaea and allowed us to characterize their closest sister lineage EX4484-52, for which we propose the name Terrarchaeota. Furthermore, we were able to reconstruct the genome evolution of DPANN cluster 2 lineages including Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae to assess genomic signatures associated with the adaptation to a halophilic lifestyle. Our work identifies the Terrarchaeota as primarily nonhalophilic terrestrial thermophiles, and highlights DNA repair and potassium transport as likely key to extreme halophily in DPANN. Increased rates of horizontal gene transfer between Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae and the Halarchaeoplasmatales point to a putative symbiont–host relationship between members of these clades.

Methods

Generation of dataset for phylogenetic and genomic analyses

To investigate the placement of halophilic lineages a previously established representative set of 607 archaeal genomes was used as a backbone (Dombrowski et al. 2020). To this, 44 publicly available genomes associated with the Nanohaloarchaeota–Aenigmatarchaeota clade, Hikarchaeota, and Thermoplasmatales were added (Table S1) resulting in a final dataset of 651 genomes.

Selection of phylogenetic marker genes for species tree reconstruction

To generate a reliable species tree and accurately place Aenigmatarchaeota, Terrarchaeota, and Nanohaloarchaeota (ATN) lineages for downstream analyses, we applied a phylogenetic approach similar to Dombrowski et al. (2020) to identify suitable marker genes from a set of 151 possible markers (Table S13). HMM profiles specific to the marker genes were used to identify homologs in the 651 genomes using a custom script Selection_of_Phylogenetic_Markers/Selecting_best_markers_151set.sh (Hamm et al. 2025) that incorporates the hmmsearch (Steinegger et al. 2019) algorithm from the HMMER v3.1b2 package. To reduce the possibility of distant paralogs being extracted during this process, the set of HMM profiles used as query was expanded to include the entire TIGRFAM (Haft et al. 2003) database (4,528 profiles total). Subsequently, homologs to each of the 151 marker genes were parsed from the output based on best hit evaluated by e-value and bit-score and the top hit for each marker and genome extracted from their respective genomes. Single gene alignments were performed for all 151 marker genes using MAFFT L-INS-i v7.407 (Katoh and Standley 2013) (settings:reorder) and trimmed using BMGE-1.12 (Criscuolo and Gribaldo 2010) (settings:-tAA -m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55). Phylogenies were inferred for all genes using IQ-tree v2.1.2 (Nguyen et al. 2015) (settings: -m LG+G -bb 1000 -wbtl -bnni). Single gene phylogenies were assessed using a custom python script to rank markers based on their capacity to resolve established monophyletic taxa as previously described (Dombrowski et al. 2020). Reliability of markers was scored according to the number of total splits (total number of taxa placing outside the expected taxonomic member clade) as well as the number of splits normalized to species count within a specific clade. Marker genes in which Archaea were previously found to not be monophyletic (Dombrowski et al. 2020) were removed. Split counts were used to rank the marker genes and concatenated alignments of the 25% and 50% top and lowest-ranked genes were generated (Table S13).

Sequences on long branches were flagged and removed using a custom python script Selection_of_Phylogenetic_Markers/cut_gene_tree_v2.py (Hamm et al. 2025) applied to trees generated for marker gene reliability assessment. Following long-branch removal, remaining sequences were realigned and trimmed using MAFFT L-INS-i v7.407 (Katoh and Standley 2013) and BMGE-1.12 (Criscuolo and Gribaldo 2010) as described above. Marker gene sets were concatenated using catfasta2phyml.pl (https://github.com/nylander/catfasta2phyml). Annotation of genomes

All genomes included in the reference dataset (651) were annotated using our annotation pipeline to ensure consistency of annotations Annotation_Tables/1_Input/Annotations.txt (Hamm et al. 2025). Coding sequences were predicted using Prokka v1.14 (Seemann 2014) (settings: –kingdom Archaea –addgenes –force –increment 10 –compliant –center UU –cpus 20 –norrna –notrna). For functional annotation of proteins several additional databases were used including COGs (Galperin et al. 2015) (downloaded October 2020), arCOGs (Makarova et al. 2015) (2014 version), KO profiles from the KEGG Automated Annotation Server (Aramaki et al. 2020) (downloaded April 2021), the Pfam database (Bateman et al. 2004) (release 34.0), the TIGRFAM database (Haft et al. 2003) (release 15.0), the Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy) database (Yin et al. 2012) (v7, downloaded August 2020), the Transporter Classification Database (Saier et al. 2006) (downloaded April 2021), the Hydrogenase database (Sondergaard et al. 2016) (HydDB, downloaded July 2020), and NCBI_nr (downloaded August 2021). In addition to this, protein domain predictions were carried out using InterProScan (Jones et al. 2014) (v5.62-94.0, setting: –iprlookup –goterms).

Annotations for the respective databases were carried out as follows. COGs, arCOGs, KOs, PFAMs, TIGRFAMs, and CAZymes were all identified using hmmsearch v3.1b2 (Steinegger et al. 2019) (settings: −E 1e−5). The Transporter Classification Database and Hydrogenase Database were queried using BLASTp v2.7.1 (Altschul et al. 1997) (settings: e-value 1e−20). For database searches, the best hit was selected based on the highest e-value and bit-score and summarized for Terrarchaeota in Table S3 and for the full set Annotation_Tables/1_Input/Annotations.txt (Hamm et al. 2025). Multiple hits were allowed for InterProScan domain annotations using a custom script for parsing results Annotation_Tables/parse_IPRdomains_vs2_GO_2.py (Hamm et al. 2025). Best blast hits against the NCBI_nr database were identified using DIAMOND v0.9.22..123 (Buchfink et al. 2021) (settings: blastp –more-sensitive –e-value 1e−5 –no-self-hits). CRISPR systems were identified in Terrarchaeota genomes using the CRISPRCasTyper v1.8.0 tool (Russel et al. 2020). Terrarchaeota genomes were taxonomically classified using GTDB-Tk (setting: –skip_ani_screen) (Chaumeil et al. 2019). Optimal growth conditions were predicted using genomeSPOT v1.0.1 (Barnum et al. 2024).

Analysis of NADH- [NiFe]- and [FeFe]-hydrogenases

To confirm annotation of putative hydrogenases the predicted catalytic subunits from Terrarchaeota genomes were identified and aligned to reference sets of previously published NiFe and FeFe hydrogenase catalytic subunits (Spang et al. 2019; Greening et al. 2024) using MAFFT-LINSI v7.407 (settings: reorder). Alignments were trimmed using BMGE-1.12 (settings:-tAA -m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55) and manually screened for presence of conserved active sites in putative Terrarchaeota hydrogenases. Terrarchaeota sequences missing conserved active site residues were removed from the dataset and remaining sequences were realigned with MAFFT-LINSI v7.407 (settings: reorder) and trimmed with BMGE-1.12 (settings:-tAA -m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55). Phylogenetic trees were inferred using IQTree v2.1.2 (settings: -m LG+C20+F+R -nt AUTO -ntmax 25 -bb 1000 -alrt 1000).

Comparative genomics

Output from the annotation pipeline was used for comparative genomics. Most genomes were clustered into class-level lineages, whilst DPANN and most uncultivated taxa were defined at the level of phylum. Presence/absence patterns of proteins encoded by these clades were determined using a custom R script Annotation_Tables/Count_Tables.r (Hamm et al. 2025). Briefly, the number of occurrences of each gene in every genome in the dataset was tallied and used to generate summary tables using the ddply function within the plyr (v1.8.8) package. Heatmaps were then plotted by converting the summary tables into presence/absence matrices followed by application of the ddply function to summarize counts across the different phylogenetic clusters. Data were visualized as a heatmap using ggplot2 (v3.4.2). Genome reconstruction for Figure S21 was carried out manually using KEGG annotations. Genes were classified as present (found in more than 50% of genomes, indicated with filled in circle), partially present (found in 0–50% of genomes, indicated with half-filled circle), or absent (missing from all genomes) for Figure S21.

Amino acid frequency analysis

To investigate signatures of halophily across our reference set of genomes, amino acid frequencies were calculated using a custom R script Amino_Acid_Analysis/AA_Freq_analysis.R (Hamm et al. 2025). Briefly, predicted protein sequences from each genome were concatenated and the frequency of each amino acid (ie number of its occurrence) was calculated and divided by the total length of the concatenated sequence. Principal components analysis was carried out using prcomp from the stats package (v4.4.0) on a matrix of calculated amino acid frequencies per individual genome across the entire dataset. Statistical significance of amino acid frequency variation between lineages within the reduced dataset was calculated using one-way ANOVA tests from the stats package (v4.4.0) with a Games–Howell post hoc evaluation of significance from the rstatix (v0.7.2) package. P-values were used to generate groups with letter designations to simplify representation with multcompview (v0.1-9). Acidity of predicted protein sequences was calculated using the peptides package (v2.4.5) isoelectric point (pI) command applied on a protein-by-protein basis to each genome. Calculated pI values were then plotted in density plots on a genome-by-genome basis.

Gene tree-species tree reconciliation

Gene tree-species tree reconciliation analyses were performed using amalgamated likelihood analysis (ALE) v1.0 (Szöllősi et al. 2012, 2013), ALE/ALE_arCOGs_final.md (Hamm et al. 2025). Protein clusters were generated using arCOG annotations produced using hmmsearch as described above (section Genome Annotation). To account for gene fusion of arCOGs in some taxa, proteins with more than 50 aa remaining after removal of the sequence part assigned to an arCOG were screened for secondary arCOG annotations and split up, with this procedure being repeated up to four times. Clusters were then cleaned by removing sequences containing Xs prior to alignment. Clusters with 1,000 sequences or less were aligned using MAFFT L-INS-I v7.407 (settings: reorder) whilst those with more than 1,000 sequences were aligned with MAFFT E-INS-I v7.407 (settings: reorder). All alignments were trimmed using BMGE-1.12 (settings:-t AA -m BLOSUM30 -h 0.55). Clusters with three or fewer sequences were removed from further analysis.

To evaluate gene clusters, initial trees were inferred from trimmed alignments using FastTree v2.1.11 (settings: -lg -gamma) and KEGG annotations mapped to sequences in the tree. A custom script ALE/Split_Count_v2.py (Hamm et al. 2025) was used to screen these phylogenies for long branches and the monophyly of KEGG annotations within trees. Long branches were defined as branches that have lengths greater than five times the interquartile range for the tree in which they were found, and corresponding sequences were flagged for removal. Instances where multiple KEGG annotations were assigned to one arCOG cluster were flagged for splitting if more than 10% of sequences in the tree were annotated with the relevant KEGG number and all of these were monophyletic. A total of 424/9,481 (4.5%) of clusters were identified as potential candidates for splitting which were then manually reviewed resulting in 74 clusters (0.8%) being split for a total of 9,555 individual clusters. Following long-branch removal and splitting of clusters, retained sequences were realigned with either MAFFT L-INS-I v7.407 (≤1,000 sequences) or MAFFT E-INS-I v7.407 (>1,000 sequences) and trimmed with BMGE-1.12 as described above.

Once final alignments were produced guide trees were inferred (settings: -m LG+G+F –score-diff all -nt 1 -wbtl -B 1000 -bnni) and model tests carried out (settings: -m MF -mset LG -madd LG+C10,LG+C10+G,LG+C10+R,LG+C10+F,LG+C10+R+F,LG+C10+G+F,LG+C20,LG+C20+G,LG+C20+F,LG+C20+G+F,LG+C20+R,LG+C20+R+F –score-diff all -T 1) using IQ-TREE v2.1.2. Model test results were summarized and alignments were distributed into two categories: category one comprised alignments where the best fitting model was a C-series model. For computational feasibility, trees for category 1 alignments were inferred using the rapid PMSF method, which approximates full profile mixture models (Wang et al. 2018). Nonmixture models were chosen as best model in category 2 alignments, for which trees could be inferred without PMSF. Trees were inferred for all alignments using IQTree v2.1.2 (settings: -T 1 -wbtl -B 1000 -pers 0.2 -nstop 500) and for category 1 alignments the additional command to run using a PMSF model (-ft Phylogenies/single/iqtree_lg_guide/{1}.treefile).

Following inference of single gene trees, ALE reconciliation was performed for all 9,555 arCOG clusters against the species tree inferred using the 50% best marker genes described above. Because the archaeal root is unresolved (Williams et al. 2017; Baker et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025), and to assess the impact of root position on our ancestral genome reconstructions, we performed reconciliations under the three most supported rooting scenarios: a root between the DPANN and all other archaea (Williams et al. 2017), a root between Euryarchaeota and all other archaea (Raymann et al. 2015; Huang et al. 2025), or a root between TACK and Asgard archaea and all other archaea (Baker et al. 2025). A first round of reconciliations was performed using ALEm1_undated v1.015 for each gene family including CheckM2 v0.1.3 estimates of genome completeness to correct for incomplete genomes using the fraction_missing flag. In order to carry out ancestral genome reconstructions, the probabilities that gene families originate at the root of the species tree was optimized for each arCOG category by maximum likelihood (ML) using a python script ALE/setup_OR_estimation.py (Hamm et al. 2025) and summary statistics extracted using another python script ALE/parse_or.py (Hamm et al. 2025) for each arCOG. This origination model implies that the prior probability of gene family origination is the optimized value, OR, at the root, and (1-OR) divided by the number of nonroot nodes elsewhere. Following optimization of root origination rate, a second run of ALE was performed with ALEm1_undated v1.0 including the ML origination rate as a correcting factor with the flag O_R and completeness scores with the flag fraction_missing as above. Inferred gene statistics were parsed using a custom python script (ale_parser.py). Data were filtered for nodes of interest using another custom python script (Ale_summary.py) and genome reconstruction carried out manually. arCOGs were classified as likely present (PP ≥ 0.8, filled in circle), possible present (0.8 > PP ≥ 0.5, half-filled circle), or likely absent (PP < 0.5, empty circle) for genome reconstructions.

Sisterhood analysis

To investigate occurrences of sisterhood relationships between archaeal taxa, single gene trees produced for ALE analyses were processed using a custom python script HGT_Analysis/Tree_Analyser.py (Hamm et al. 2025). Briefly, GTDB taxonomy was mapped to leaves in the trees and for all Order and Family level lineages the relative frequency of sisterhood was calculated per tree. To calculate relative frequency of sisterhood each instance of the target lineage in the tree was identified and progressive searches conducted of nodes upstream until a member of a different lineage was found. Maximum branch length (3) and minimum branch support (0.7) cutoffs were applied and sisterhood frequencies were not calculated for trees in which length or support values fell outside these cutoffs. Once a sister clade was identified the relative frequency of different lineages within the sister clade was calculated per tree. Following this, the relative frequencies across all trees for each lineage were summed together. Plots were then produced using a custom R script HGT_Analysis/Sisterhood_Analyses.R (Hamm et al. 2025).

Results and discussion

Phylogenetic analyses placed the Nanohaloarchaeota as a sister lineage to the Terrarchaeota

To establish a framework for investigating the evolution of salt adaptation in DPANN, we first investigated the phylogenetic position of the salt-adapted lineages beginning with Nanohaloarchaeota. To do so, we built upon a previously established approach (Dombrowski et al. 2020) to identify a set of universal single copy marker genes least affected by HGT (25% and 50% top ranked markers). All concatenated phylogenies resolved a monophyletic clade within the DPANN consisting of Aenigmatarchaeota, Nanohaloarchaeota and the uncharacterized EX4484-52 phylum, which we here refer to as Terrarchaeota (see Classification of Terrarchaeota) (Fig. 1, Figures S1–S4). Terrarchaeota were recovered as the sister lineage of the Nanohaloarchaeota in phylogenies inferred using the 25% and 50% top marker genes (100/100 and 99.8/100 SH-like aLRT/ultrafast bootstrap support, respectively) (Fig. 1, Figures S1 and S2). Their placement sister to a monophyletic clade consisting of Nanohaloarchaeota and the Aenigmatarchaeota lineage PWEA01 (recently proposed as Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae (Gutiérrez-Preciado et al. 2024)) (26/69 and 89.2/95 SH-like aLRT/ultrafast bootstrap support, respectively) in phylogenies using the 25% and 50% lowest-ranked markers (Figures S3 and S4) is likely an artifact of concatenating incongruent markers affected by horizontal transfer and/or phylogenetic artifacts. Removal of the 10, 30, or 50% fastest evolving (Kostka et al. 2008) (Figures S5–S10) or compositionally biased (Viklund et al. 2012) (Figures S11–S16) sites had little to no impact on the placement of the Terrarchaeota in either of the top 25% or 50% marker gene phylogenies. Likewise, addition of genomes from the recently described Asbonarchaeaceae lineage (Baker et al. 2024) (a sister lineage to the Nanosalinaceae within the Nanohaloarchaeota) further supported the sister relationship of Terrarchaeota and Nanohaloarchaeota (Figures S17–S20). Together, this provides strong support for the monophyly of Nanohaloarchaeota and Terrarchaeota and the placement of both of these lineages sister to Aenigmatarchaeota within DPANN cluster 2 archaea, consistent with previous analyses (Gutiérrez-Preciado et al. 2024). We refer to this clade consisting of Aenigmatarchaeota, Terrarchaeota, and Nanohaloarchaeota as ATN.

Genomic analyses suggest that Terrarchaeota comprise nonhalophilic members but identify a second halophilic clade within DPANN cluster 2 archaea

PCA analyses of amino acid frequencies, pI profiles, and amino acid frequencies inferred for all representatives in our taxon set, revealed that Terrarchaeota are not extreme halophiles. Specifically, their proteomes clustered with nonhalophilic Aenigmatarchaeota (Fig. 2a) and pI profiles showed no signs of increased proteome acidity characteristic for halophilic Archaea (Fig. 2b). Likewise, we did not observe statistically significant variation in individual amino acid frequencies for Terrarchaeota except for tryptophan and proline (Fig. 2c, Table S7). Metabolic predictions based on the 11 publicly available Terrarchaeota MAGs (genome completeness and contamination ranging from 64 to 91% and 0 to 2.19%, respectively) (Tables S1 and S2) derived from hydrothermal vent (Dombrowski et al. 2017) and deposit (Reysenbach et al. 2020), methane seep, and radioactive site metagenomes (Vázquez-Campos et al. 2021), revealed that members of this group have limited catabolic and anabolic potential similar to other DPANN cluster 2 archaea (Table S5, Figures S21–S25, Information). This is consistent with metabolic reconstruction for the Terrarchaeota MAG GCA_902384675.1 (Vázquez-Campos et al. 2021), which was predicted to represent a fermentative symbiont with a higher than typical number of transporters compared to other DPANN. Specifically, our analyses revealed that Terrarchaeota representatives, while encoding a glycolytic pathway and enzymes for the degradation of complex carbohydrates, lack TCA cycle genes and genes for any of the known carbon fixation pathways of archaea (Berg et al. 2010). Many biosynthetic pathways such as for amino acids, vitamins, purine and pyrimidine, and archaeal lipids are incomplete. However, similar to other DPANN, representatives encode the capacity for interconversion of nucleotides as well as ribose 1,5-bisphosphate isomerase (K18237, 8 MAGs) and ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase (RbcL; RuBisCo, K01601, 7 MAGs), which are hypothesized to function in the salvage of nucleosides (Dombrowski et al. 2020). Further, all Terrarchaeota seem to encode a complete A/V-type ATP synthase, and some representatives encode group A [FeFe]-hydrogenases (2 MAGs), a group 3 [NiFe]-hydrogenase (two MAGs), putative group 4 [NiFe]-hydrogenase (three MAGs) and a putative NADH-dehydrogenase (Text, Tables S3 and S6, Figures S26–S28). Altogether, these analyses identify Terrarchaeota as a symbiotic sister clade of the Nanohaloarchaeota whose members are not adapted to high salt concentrations.

In contrast, our amino acid composition analyses identified hallmarks of adaptations to high salt conditions (Figs 1 and 2a) in the PWEA01 clade within Aenigmatarchaeota, recently named Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae (Gutiérrez-Preciado et al. 2024). Isoelectric profiles of Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae protein-coding sequences showed signs of a shift toward higher acidity in the proteome, and statistically significant variations in amino acid frequency were observed for nine amino acids, including aspartate and glutamate, which are typically elevated in salt-in halophiles (Oren 2002; Andrei et al. 2012). Other amino acids that significantly vary in frequency amongst Nanohaloarchaeota, such as cysteine and serine, do not differ in Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae. These results are consistent with the recent discovery of Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae representatives, in hypersaline brines from the Danakil Depression (Gutiérrez-Preciado et al. 2024).

The evolution of metabolic gene repertoires and parallel adaptation to halophilic lifestyles within DPANN cluster 2

Our findings, indicating that the Nanohaloarchaeota form a sister lineage of the Terrarchaeota and that Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae emerge from within Aenigmatarchaeota (Fig. 3a), raise the question as to how these lifestyles evolved within DPANN cluster 2 archaea. To elucidate the origin of extreme halophily and gain insight into metabolic adaptations in ATN lineages, we performed gene tree-species tree reconciliation using ALE (Szöllősi et al. 2012, 2013; Williams et al. 2024). We rooted our species tree between DPANN and the rest of the archaea in agreement with several publications (Williams et al. 2017; Dombrowski et al. 2020; Moody et al. 2022). Since the root of Archaea is currently uncertain, we also tested recently recovered alternative root positions (Petitjean et al. 2015; Raymann et al. 2015; Baker et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025), such as between Euryarchaeota or TACK/Asgard Archaea and all other archaea respectively, but these have only marginal effects on gene family presence probabilities in reconstructed ancestral nodes (Table S8). We therefore base our discussion below on inferences using the DPANN root. Ancestral genome content was inferred based on posterior probabilities (PP) of arCOG family presence in any node of interest (Table S8). We considered gene families with PP values between 0.5 and 0.8 to have low support and >0.8 to be of moderate support for having been present at the nodes examined (Fig. 3b).

Reconstructed metabolic processes support an ancestral fermentative lifestyle for all last common ancestors examined (Fig. 3a); ie Aenigmatarchaeota (LCA-A), Terrarchaeota (LCA-C), Nanohaloarchaeota (LCA-N), Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae/PWEA01 (LCA-P), Terrarchaeota–Nanohaloarchaeota (LCA-TN), and Aenigmatarchaeota–Terrarcheota–Nanohaloarchaeota (LCA-ATN) with acetate the likely end-product for all ancestors except for the Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae (Discussion; Metabolism). All ancestors examined, with the exception of LCA-N, were inferred to have complete or near-complete phospholipid biosynthesis pathways allowing production of archaeol from acetyl-CoA indicating their capability of synthesizing membrane lipids (Fig. 3, Table S8). In contrast, the majority of quinone and carotenoid biosynthesis pathways were predicted to be absent from all ancestral genomes (Fig. 3). Absence of quinone synthesis pathways is unsurprising considering that most cluster 2 DPANN are generally thought to lack respiratory complexes that require quinones. The analyzed ancestors were also predicted to encode pili/archaellum subunits, although individual KOs and arCOGs showed differential distributions across the various lineages (Fig. 3, Table S8, Discussion; Cell surface structures).

Several arCOGs implicated in adaptation to hypersaline environments displayed predicted presence/absence patterns consistent with extreme halophily evolving along the branch leading to halophilic ancestors (ie LCA-N and LCA-H) rather than being an ancestral state. Subunits of the sodium-hydrogen antiporter (arCOG03076, arCOG03079, arCOG03082, arCOG03099, arCOG03121, arCOG01537, and arCOG01962), which is particularly important in regulating osmotic balance in extreme halophiles, were predicted to be present in all individual ancestors of the various ACN lineages but not in LCA-ATN, with LCA-A and LCA-TN predicted to encode different KOs (Table S8). Absence of these genes from LCA-ATN may indicate that each lineage acquired their antiporter complexes via HGT. Consistent with this scenario, single gene trees resolved Nanohaloarchaeota sequences as either sister to or branching within halobacterial clades for all antiporter subunits whilst Aenigmatarchaeota and Terrarchaeota sequences typically form monophyletic clades sister to Thermoplasmatota. However, it is also possible that the amino acid biases associated with adaptation to high salt conditions has caused artificial attraction of the nanohaloarchaeal and halobacterial sequences. LCA-N was predicted to encode at least four different redox regulation proteins including two glutaredoxins (arCOG02608: PP = 0.94 and arCOG01297: PP = 0.98), one peroxiredoxin (arCOG00314: PP = 0.88) and a thioredoxin reductase (arCOG01296: PP = 0.97) but not rubrerythrin. None of these protein families have high posterior probabilities for being present in the proteomes of any of the ancestors, though there is moderate support for rubrerythrin in some of them (K19824: LCA-A: PP = 0.55; LCA-T: PP = 0.54; LCA-N: PP = 0.11, LCA-TN: PP = 0.50, LCA-ATN: PP = 0.55, Fig. 3). The absence of rubrerythrin from LCA-N is consistent with a transition into an oxic environment alongside their haloarchaeal hosts whilst the putative presence of it in some of the other lineages may indicate their adaptation to an anoxic environment. LCA-N is the only DPANN ancestor predicted to encode both photolyase (arCOG02840: LCA-A: PP = 0.04; LCA-T: PP = 0.07; LCA-N: PP = 0.99, LCA-TN: PP = 0.49, LCA-ATN: PP = 0.28) and RecB (arCOG00802: LCA-A: PP = 0.12; LCA-T: PP = 0.06; LCA-N: PP = 0.99, LCA-TN: PP = 0.47, LCA-ATN: PP = 0.27), which both may play a role in DNA repair. This is particularly relevant as many hypersaline environments experience exposure to high UV-radiation (Jones and Baxter 2017) necessitating adaptation to UV-induced DNA damage.

Finally, gene copy number changes characterizing the evolution of DPANN cluster 2 lineages provided further insights into adaptations to extreme salt conditions (Tables S9 and S10). While the Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae seem to have distinct protein families with multiple copies per proteome, the Kef K+ transporter (arCOG01955) has the highest copy number increase along the branches leading to both lineages. The Kef-type potassium transporters play an important role in halophiles that use a salt-in strategy by facilitating the uptake of K+ ions allowing to maintain osmotic balance whereas Na+ and Mg2+ ions are excluded from the cytoplasm (Oren 2002). Expansion of Kef-type transporter family proteins may have allowed members of both the Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae to adjust to hypersaline systems in the absence of additional protective measures (eg osmoprotectants such as glycine betaine) that are generally present in Halobacteriales (Oren 2013).

The hosts of uncultivated DPANN

It has previously been shown that some DPANN archaea exchange genes more frequently with their hosts than with other taxa, such as Nanohaloarchaeota with Halobacteriales, Micrarchaeota and Parvarchaeota with Thermoplasmatales, Huberarchaeales with Altiarchaeales, and Nanoarchaeales with Sulfolobales (Dombrowski et al. 2020). To investigate potential hosts of the ATN clades, especially the newly identified Terrarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae lineages, we calculated the relative frequency of sisterhood relationships for all trees used in the ALE analysis at the level of Order and Family from the GTDB taxonomy (Fig. 4, Tables S11 and S12, Figure S29). While we could not identify a clear signal for Terrarchaeota, Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae representatives displayed an increased frequency of sisterhoods with PWKY01 (Halarchaeoplasmatales), suggesting that members of the halophilic PWKY01 clade may interact with or serve as hosts for some representatives of the Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae clade.

Related lineages have been clustered together using colors and specific lineages that show higher rates of sisterhood with DPANN lineages highlighted with unique colors. The data used to generate this plot are available in Table S10.

It is conspicuous that all DPANN symbiont–host partnerships with observable increase in sisterhood rates (except Huberarchaeales and Altiarchaeales) occur in extreme environments (hypersaline, acidic, and/or high-temperature). It seems possible that the selective pressures involved in adapting to these environments favor the acquisition of genes related to the specific extremophily from the host at rates higher than those seen in other DPANN symbiont–host partnerships from less extreme environments. Consistent with this, several copies of the KefB potassium-efflux system gene (the highest increase in copy number for both Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae) from halophilic DPANN genomes placed as sister to their respective/putative hosts (Halobacteriales and PWKY01) in single gene trees (Hamm et al. 2025). Alternatively, given that extreme environments typically support less diverse microbial communities, symbiont–host gene transfers may be more easily detected from background HGT. Additionally, amino acid composition biases, such as those displayed by extreme halophiles and thermophiles, are known to cause compositional attraction artifacts in trees, particularly when limited signal is available as is the case for single gene trees. Thus, a fraction of sisterhood relationships between extremophilic DPANN and their putative hosts may be the result of erroneous gene trees rather than genuine gene transfer (Dombrowski et al. 2020; Baker et al. 2024). Finally, we note that the trees used for this analysis were limited to archaeal taxa only. However, many DPANN lineages occupy environments dominated by Bacteria and some representatives have been proposed to associate with bacterial and/or eukaryotic hosts (Ortiz-Alvarez and Casamayor 2016; Hamann et al. 2017; Dombrowski et al. 2020; Reysenbach et al. 2020; Bruto et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2025). The absence of bacterial and eukaryotic sequences from our trees due to computational limitations of the gene tree-species tree reconciliation workflow, limits the confidence with which sisterhood relationships can be determined. Therefore, whilst our results suggest a possible relationship between the Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae and the Halarchaeoplasmatales which remains to be confirmed experimentally, they do not allow us to identify hosts for DPANN that do not interact with other Archaea.

Classification of Terrarchaeota

Classification of the Terrarchaeota genomes with GTDB-Tk (Chaumeil et al. 2019) indicated that all 11 MAGs included in this analysis belong to the existing candidate phylum referred to as EX4484-52. The included genomes represent 6 families, 7 genera, and 10 species. Consistent with the SeqCode Registry requirements for designation of type material we designate Candidatus Terrarchaeum hikurangii (Assembly ID: GCA_013138765.1, estimated completeness ∼90%, estimated contamination: 1.6%, tRNAs: 20/20, 5S and partial 16S rRNA genes present) as type material for the phylum EX4484-52, with the new proposed name Terrarchaeota, and the corresponding family (Terrarchaeaceae), order (Terrarchaeales), and class (Terrarchaeia), following the principle of propagation from type material as outlined in SeqCode (Section 3, Rule 15). Additionally, we designate Candidatus Marinivulcanus frater (Assembly ID: GCA_015520605.1, estimated completeness: ∼91%, estimated contamination: 0%, tRNAs: 15/20, 5S rRNA gene present) as type material for the novel family Marinivulcanaceae.

Concluding remarks

Extreme halophily in archaea, once thought limited to the Halobacteriales, is now known to have evolved independently at least five times including twice within the DPANN archaea (ie Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaeceae) (Oren 2002; Ghai et al. 2011; Narasingarao et al. 2012; Sorokin et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2022; Gutiérrez-Preciado et al. 2024). While some adaptations to high-salt are known to be conserved across lineages (such as acidic shifts in amino acid content), the evolution of genome content in extremely halophilic DPANN archaea and the degree to which these lineages adopt convergent strategies for adapting to high-salt environments has been unclear. We find that the Nanohaloarchaeota (including both Nanosalinaceae and Asbonarchaeaceae) form a sister lineage to the Terrarchaeota, which we infer to be predominantly fermentative thermophiles, while the Haloaenigmatarchaeaeceae are nested within the Aenigmatarchaeota. Our ancestral gene content reconstructions revealed that heightened tolerance to DNA damage and increased capacity for import and export of salt ions were key in the evolution of both the Nanohaloarchaeota and Haloaenigmatarchaeaeceae. However, our results also indicated important differences between lineages such as the adaptation to oxic environments in the Nanohaloarchaeota and preferences for different protein families involved in the same functions e.g. S-layer proteins, Na+/H+ antiporter, and citrate metabolism enzymes. Furthermore, the predicted acquisition of genes from host species via HGT highlights the potential importance of the symbiotic interactions these DPANN engage in for their adaptation to hypersalinity. Future work is necessary to confirm the potential partnership between Haloaenigmatarchaeaceae and Halarchaeoplasmatales and to assess the degree to which compositional biases impact the predicted rates of HGT between halophilic archaea. Moreover, the mechanism identified for the acquisition of high salt adaptation in symbionts, HGT from halophilic hosts, suggests that the evolution of halophily in symbionts may be facile whenever suitable high salt-adapted hosts are available, suggesting that more cases of halophilic ectosymbiotic Archaea and, potentially, Bacteria remain to be discovered.
