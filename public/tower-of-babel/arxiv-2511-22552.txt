Graphical Tests of Causality Journal: Special Issue on Quantum Physics and Logic
Ämin Baumeler, Eleftherios-Ermis Tselentis, Stefan Wolf

Abstract
Bell inequalities limit the possible observations of non-communicating parties. Here, we present analogous inequalities for any number of communicating parties under the causal constraints of static causal order, definite causal order, and bi-causal order. All derived inequalities are remarkably simple. They correspond to upper bounds on the winning chance in graphical games: Given a specific directed graph over the parties, the parties are challenged to communicate along a randomly chosen arc. In the case of definite causal order, every game that we find is specified by a kefalopoda digraph. Based on this we define weakly causal correlations as those that satisfy all kefalopoda inequalities. We show that the problem of deciding whether some correlations are weakly causal is solvable in polynomial time in the number of parties.

Generally, it is difficult

]
to decide whether a given distribution

belongs to a polytope.
A naïve approach is to enumerate all

and to verify that

satisfies each one of them.
In our case, however, complete enumerations are unknown.
Moreover, numerical computations suggest that in the case of

the more parties are considered, the more nontrivial limitations are encountered.

1.2
Outlook

In the next section we present some basic notation, and the required preliminaries on polytope theory and graph theory.
After that, we introduce the
single-output scenario
and establish a map from polytopes to sets of digraphs.
This is followed by the definition of graphical tests, and the study of each of the causal constraints.
After that, we define
weakly causal correlations
and present the efficient algorithm for deciding membership.
Finally, we conclude and discuss some open questions.

3.1
Geometric Representation

For
n

P_{A|S,R,X}
is fully specified by the

p(0|s,r,x)
for the receiver producing output

Therefore, a specific

\bm{p}:=(\bm{p}^{0},\bm{p}^{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}

\bm{p}^{x}:=(p(0|s,r,x))_{s,r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

If we request that
P_{A|S,R,X}
must satisfy some causal constraint, then we focus on the body
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
of such vectors in agreement with that constraint.
The dimension of the body in this scenario scales
at most quadratically
with the number of parties.
This is in radical contrast to the usual Bell scenarios, where the dimensions grow exponentially in
n
.

3.2
Basic Properties

As we show below, all causal constraints of interest produce bodies
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
that satisfy some useful properties.

Definition 2

(Operational 0/1 polytope,
\Delta_{n}
)

The body

\displaystyle(\bm{p}^{0},\bm{p}^{1})\in\ext(\mathcal{P})\Longrightarrow\forall(s,r)\in[n]^{2}_{\neq}:(\bm{p}^{0}\oplus\bm{1}_{(s,r)},\bm{p}^{1}\oplus\bm{1}_{(s,r)})\in\ext(\mathcal{P})\,.

For some

means that the set of single-output correlations is closed under convex combinations,

The last property (Eq. (

In order to see that, focus on some specific sender
\hat{s}
and receiver

In particular, the case

\hat{s}
is the sender.
The remaining two cases, i.e., when

outputs a constant

\hat{s}
is the sender.
Now, suppose some correlations

After all, this amounts to a basic relabeling of party

3.4
Polytope of Digraphs

The map

Just as before, where we geometrically represent a set of single-output correlations as a polytope

we now geometrically represent a set
\mathcal{D}
of digraphs as a polytope
\mathcal{Q}:=\conv(\bm{\alpha}(\mathcal{D}))\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
As we show in Ref.
[

, in certain cases, the facet-defining inequalities of
\mathcal{Q}
can be lifted to represent facet-defining inequalities of
\mathcal{P}
.
With this, this detour to sets of digraphs helps us to derive the facet-defining inequalities of
\mathcal{P}
by solving the same problem
on half of the original dimension.

Lemma 1

(Lifting
[
18
]
)
.

If an operational 0/1 polytope
\mathcal{P}
and its digraph polytope
\mathcal{Q}:=\conv(\bm{\alpha}(\Gamma(\mathcal{P})))
are full-dimensional,
and if
(\bm{w},c)
is a non-negative and nontrivial facet-defining inequality of
\mathcal{Q}
,
then
((\bm{w},-\bm{w}),c)
is a facet-defining inequality of
\mathcal{P}
.

For completeness and as a warm-up, we restate the proof here.

Proof.

The validity of
((\bm{w},-\bm{w}),c)
for
\mathcal{P}
follows directly from the validity of
(\bm{w},c)
for
\mathcal{Q}
, i.e., for some
\bm{p}\in\ext(\mathcal{P})
,
we have
(\bm{p}^{0}\oplus\bm{p}^{1})\in\ext(\mathcal{Q})
, and

(\bm{w},-\bm{w})\cdot\left(\bm{p}^{0},\bm{p}^{1}\right)=\bm{w}\cdot\left(\bm{p}^{0}-\bm{p}^{1}\right)\leq\bm{w}\cdot\left(\bm{p}^{0}\oplus\bm{p}^{1}\right)\leq c\,.

(8)

In order to show that
((\bm{w},-\bm{w}),c)
is facet-defining for
\mathcal{P}
, we generate a set of
2d
affinely independent extremal points of
\mathcal{P}
that saturate the inequality.
Since
(\bm{w},c)
is facet-defining for
\mathcal{Q}
, there exists a set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\ext(\mathcal{Q})
of
d
affinely independent vectors that saturate the inequality, i.e., with

\forall\bm{q}\in\mathcal{T}:\bm{w}\cdot\bm{q}=c\,.

(9)

These correspond to
d
affinely independent vectors
\mathcal{S}_{0}:=\{(\bm{q},\bm{0})\mid\bm{q}\in\mathcal{T}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}

that saturate the
lifted inequality:

\forall\bm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}:(\bm{w},-\bm{w})\cdot\bm{p}=c\,.

(10)

The remaining
d
extremal points are obtained from taking elements in
\mathcal{S}_{0}
and by flipping certain bits, producing a
diagonal
in the last
d
dimensions.
First note that, if for some
\bm{p}=(\bm{q},\bm{0})\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
, the vector
\bm{q}
has a
0
entry at position
i\in[d]
, then

(\bm{w},-\bm{w})\cdot(\bm{p}\oplus(\bm{1}_{i},\bm{1}_{i}))=(\bm{w},-\bm{w})\cdot(\bm{q}\oplus\bm{1}_{i},\bm{1}_{i})=\bm{w}\cdot\bm{q}+w_{i}-w_{i}=c\,.

(11)

The remaining
d
extremal points that saturate the lifted inequality are thus

\mathcal{S}_{1}:=\{(\bm{q}^{(i)}\oplus\bm{1}_{i},\bm{1}_{i})\mid i\in[d]\}\,,

(12)

where
\bm{q}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{T}
has a
0
entry at position
i
.
The set
\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}
describes
2d
affinely independent vectors that saturate the lifted inequality,
unless there exists some
i_{0}\in[d]
for which we cannot find any
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{T}
with a
0
entry at position
i_{0}
.
But this latter situation cannot arise.
Firstly, if there is a second index
j_{0}\in[d]
with
\forall\bm{q}\in\mathcal{T}:\bm{q}_{j_{0}}=1
,
then
\mathcal{T}
(which defines a
(d-1)
-dimensional space) does not fit into the remaining
d-2
coordinates.
Lastly, if there is no such second index, then
\mathcal{T}
occupies the whole remaining space.
This, by assuming without loss of generality
i_{0}=0
, means that

\forall\ell\in[d-1]:\bm{w}\cdot(1,\bm{1}_{\ell})=c\,,

(13)

which in turn implies
\bm{w}=(c,\bm{0})
, i.e., the inequality is trivial.
∎

In fact, the symmetry induced by the operational closure allows us to
generate an exponential number
of facet-defining inequalities.
We will need this lemma for the second part of the present article.

Lemma 2

(Facet generation)
.

If
((\bm{w},-\bm{w}),c)
is a facet-defining inequality of a full-dimensional operational 0/1 polytope
\mathcal{P}
,
then
((\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w}),c)
is a facet-defining inequality of
\mathcal{P}
for any
\bm{\phi}\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
, where the product is taken element-wise.

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider
\bm{\phi}
with a single
-1
at coordinate
i_{0}\in[d]
.
The full statement follows from the iterative application of this case.
This case holds because flipping the signs according to
\bm{\phi}
is tantamount
to flipping the corresponding entries in the extremal point
\bm{p}=(\bm{p}^{0},\bm{p}^{1})
:

\displaystyle(\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w})\cdot\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\bm{\phi}\bm{w}\cdot\left(\bm{p}^{0}-\bm{p}^{1}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}\bm{w}_{i}\left(\bm{p}^{0}_{i}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i}\right)-\bm{w}_{i_{0}}\left(\bm{p}^{0}_{i_{0}}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i_{0}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}\bm{w}_{i}\left(\bm{p}^{0}_{i}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i}\right)+\bm{w}_{i_{0}}\left(\bm{p}^{1}_{i_{0}}-\bm{p}^{0}_{i_{0}}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=(\bm{w},-\bm{w})\cdot\left(\bm{p}^{0}\oplus\bm{1}_{i_{0}},\bm{p}^{1}\oplus\bm{1}_{i_{0}}\right)\,.

(17)

Thus, the “rotated” inequality
((\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w}),c)
is valid for
\mathcal{P}
, and there are
2d
affinely independent vectors in
\mathcal{P}
that saturate it.
∎

Before we consider specific causal constraints, we discuss graphical games in general.
Take some digraph polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

The recipe is always the same:

4.1

The causal constraint of

4.2
Definite Causal Order

The assumption of
definite causal order
captures the idea that the future does not influence the past.
This causal constraint relaxes the constraint of static causal order insofar that an event may influence
everything
within its future.
Thus, an event may also specify the causal order among the events in its future.
Tailored to the scenario we consider, this means that the sender cannot signal to the receiver whenever the receiver is the
first
in some causal order.

Definition 7

(Definite causal order,
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\text{causal}}
)
.

The
n
-party correlations
P_{A|S,R,X}
agree with
definite causal order
if and only if their probabilities decompose as

p(a|s,r,x)=p_{\text{first}}(r)p_{0}(a|s,r)+(1-p_{\text{first}}(r))p_{1}(a|s,r,x)\,,

(37)

where
p_{\text{first}}(r)
is the probability for the receiver to be the first in the causal order.
The set of such correlations is
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\text{causal}}
.

4.2.1
Source-Digraph Polytope

The set
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\text{causal}}
forms an operational 0/1 polytope
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\text{causal}}
.
Therefore, we are in position to use the map
\Gamma
and study the resulting set of digraphs instead.

Theorem 3

(Source digraphs)
.

The set of digraphs
\Gamma(\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\text{causal}})
is the set of all digraphs on
n
vertices with at least one source vertex:

\Gamma(\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\text{causal}})=\{D\mid\mathcal{V}(D)=[n],\exists i\in[n]:\deg_{D}^{\text{in}}(i)=0\}\,.

(38)

Proof.

For the
\subseteq
-inclusion it is sufficient to note that since at least one party
i_{0}
is causally before all others, then that party cannot receive any signal:
Party
i_{0}
describes a source.
For the opposite inclusion, suppose that
D
is a digraph with a source
i_{0}
.
The following correlations
P_{A|S,R,X}
with the probabilities

p(0|s,r,x)=\begin{cases}1-x&(s,r)\in\mathcal{A}(D)\\
1&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(39)

respect that signaling structure (we have
p(0|s,r,0)\oplus p(0|s,r,1)=1
if and only if
(s,r)\in\mathcal{A}(D)
)
and are causal (the probability
p(0|s,i_{0},x)
is independent from
x
).
∎

4.3
Bi-Causal Order

Some multi-party correlations may be incompatible with any definite causal order simply because the correlations among few of them are.
The constraint of
bi-causal order,
or rather the violation thereof, captures this
genuinely multi-party character
of correlations incompatible with any definite causal order.
This is similar to the notions of genuinely multi-party nonlocality
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
.
Here, the parties are partitioned into two non-empty groups.
Within each group communication is unrestricted.
Also, one group (the “past”) can communicate to the other (the “future”).
It is only that the parties from the “future” group cannot signal to any party in the “past.”

Definition 10

(Bi-causal order,
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\text{bi}}
)
.

The
n
-party correlations
P_{A|S,R,X}
agree with
bi-causal order
if and only if
their probabilities decompose as

p(a|s,r,x)=\sum_{\mathcal{F}:s\not\in\mathcal{F},r\in\mathcal{F}}p(\mathcal{F})p^{\bot}_{\mathcal{F}}(a|s,r)+\sum_{\mathcal{F}:r\in\mathcal{F}\rightarrow s\in\mathcal{F}}p(\mathcal{F})p^{\top}_{\mathcal{F}}(a|s,r,x)\,,

(46)

where
\mathcal{F}\subsetneq[n]
is a non-empty strict subset of
[n]
.
The set of such correlations is
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\text{bi}}
.

5
Weakly Causal Correlations

The kefalopoda inequalities are possibly the only nontrivial facet-defining inequalities of the source-digraph polytope.
This is in stark contrast to the other digraph polytopes studied here, i.e., the DAG polytope and the not-strong polytope.
There, we
know
that additional facet-defining inequalities exist in general; the sets of facets given by Theorem
2
and Theorem
6
are incomplete.
This observation motivates us to turn Theorem
4
into a definition of a
weak
version of the source polytope.
This then then translates to a causal constraint, the constraint of
weakly causal correlations.

We then present an efficient algorithm to decide whether some single-output correlations
P_{A|S,R,X}
are weakly causal or not.

Definition 13
.

The polytope
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{\text{src}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}
is the set of vectors that satisfy the following inequalities:

\displaystyle(-\bm{1}_{i,j},0)

\displaystyle\text{(for any $(i,j)\in[n]^{2}_{\neq}$; trivial)}\,,

(58)

\displaystyle(\bm{1}_{i,j},1)

\displaystyle\text{(for\penalty\ $n\geq 3$ and any $(i,j)\in[n]^{2}_{\neq}$; trivial)}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle(\bm{\alpha}(\kappa_{f}),n-1)

\displaystyle\text{(for any fixed-point-free function\penalty\ $f:[n]\rightarrow[n]$)}\,.

(60)

We know that
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{\text{src}}=\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\text{src}}
for
n\leq 4
.
In general, this identity is unsolved.
3
3

3

If for some
n
these polytopes differ, then
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{\text{src}}
will have
non-integer
extremal points.

We now define the corresponding polytope of correlations.
This polytope is motivated by the lifting lemma (Lemma
1
) and the facet-generation lemma (Lemma
2
).

Definition 14
.

The polytope
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{Q})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is the set of vectors that satisfy the following inequalities:

\displaystyle(-\bm{1}_{i},0)

\displaystyle\text{(for any $i\in[2d]$; trivial)}\,,

(61)

\displaystyle(\bm{1}_{i},1)

\displaystyle\text{(for any\penalty\ $i\in[2d]$; trivial)}\,,

(62)

\displaystyle((\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w}),c)

\displaystyle\text{(for any $(\bm{w},c)\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{Q})$,\penalty\ $\bm{\phi}\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}$)}\,,

(63)

where

is the set of facet-defining inequality for
\mathcal{Q}
.
The polytope of
n

is

In fact, the signs from

Luckily for us, the polytopes

There exists a projection of the vectors in

This lemma, that we state next, is complementary to the lifting lemma used in the previous parts.
The projection is given by the map

Lemma 3
.

If all nontrivial facet-defining inequalities a polytope
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
are non-negative,
then, whenever
\bm{p}=(\bm{p}^{0},\bm{p}^{1})\in[0,1]^{2d}

\bm{p}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{Q})\Longleftrightarrow|\bm{p}^{0}-\bm{p}^{1}|\in\mathcal{Q}\,,

(64)

where the absolute values are taken element-wise.

Proof.

We prove both directions by contradiction.
For the
\Leftarrow
direction, suppose that
\bm{p}
is
not
in
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{Q})
.
This means there exists a
violated
lifted inequality
((\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w}),c)
, i.e.,

(\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w})\cdot\bm{p}>c\,,

(65)

where
(\bm{w},c)
is a nontrivial and non-negative facet-defining inequality of
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\bm{\phi}\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
.
The left-hand side of this equation is

\sum_{i\in[d]}\bm{\phi}_{i}\bm{w}_{i}\left(\bm{p}^{0}_{i}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in[d]}\bm{w}_{i}\left|\bm{p}^{0}_{i}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i}\right|=\bm{w}\cdot\left|\bm{p}^{0}-\bm{p}^{1}\right|\,.

(66)

Therefore, the projected vector is also not in
\mathcal{Q}
.
For the opposite direction, suppose that
|\bm{p}^{0}-\bm{p}^{1}|
for some
\bm{p}\in[0,1]^{2d}
is
not
in
\mathcal{Q}
.
Again, this means there exists a nontrivial and non-negative inequality
(\bm{w},c)
that is violated:

\bm{w}\cdot\left|\bm{p}^{0}-\bm{p}^{1}\right|>c\,.

(67)

Now, the can express the left-hand side as

\sum_{i\in[d]}\bm{w}_{i}\left|\bm{p}^{0}_{i}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i}\right|=\sum_{i\in[d]}\bm{\phi}_{i}\bm{w}_{i}\left(\bm{p}^{0}_{i}-\bm{p}^{1}_{i}\right)=(\bm{\phi}\bm{w},-\bm{\phi}\bm{w})\cdot\bm{p}\,,

(68)

where we define
\bm{\phi}_{i}:=1

\bm{p}^{0}_{i}\geq\bm{p}^{1}_{i}
, and
\bm{\phi}_{i}:=-1
otherwise.
Thus, also
\bm{p}
is not in
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

5.1
Efficient Digraph Algorithm

By the above lemma, the problem of deciding whether some single-output correlations belong to the polytope of weakly causal correlations is reduced
to the problem whether some vector belongs to
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}^{\text{src}}
.
Towards an algorithm and complexity-theoretic treatment, we need to specify how the input is encoded.
Machines cannot treat real numbers.
So, in the following, we always consider

Moreover, each rational number

We use

The decision problem

6
Conclusions and Open Questions

We derive particularly simple inequalities for static causal order, definite causal order, and bi-causal order.
These inequalities, described by digraphs, serve as device-independent tests of different degrees of causality for any number of parties.
This result is made possible through a tight connection between single-output correlations and directed graphs.
Here, the causal constraints are mirrored in graph-theoretic properties, and the study of correlations is reduced to the study of graphs.
It is immediate that all graphical games for
n\geq 3
parties can be won perfectly using classical or quantum processes without definite causal order
[
35
,
20
]
.
This follows from the Ardehali-Svetlichny type process with genuinely multi-party “acausality” derived in Ref.
[
23
]
.
This means that winning these games is not at odds with logical consistency.
In fact, the “Ardehali-Svetlichny” process is a resource with which
any
graphical game for
n\geq 3
parties can be won perfectly.
This, in turn, implies that the single-output scenario studied here seems to be unsuitable for the study of
antinomicity

[
36
,
37
]
,
which has been proposed as an ultimate level of ‘‘acausality.’’
4
4

4

But then, does antinomicity require the output of two or more parties?

Nevertheless, as we show, this single-output scenario is sensitive to different degrees of causality.

The present research raises a series of immediate open problems.
One such problem that can be formalized precisely, is:
Is the set of weakly causal correlations the same as the set of causal correlations,
i.e., does the identity
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\text{causal}}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{\text{causal}}
hold in general?
Another open question is the derivation of all facet-defining inequalities of the bi-causal inequalities (see Fig.
8
).
The observations on the Hamiltonian-cycle polytope (see Section
4.4
) also initiate further research.
Firstly, does the Hamiltonian game represent some “essence” of causal order?
Secondly, is the
converse
of the present study also feasible?
In the present article we derive digraphs from causal constraints.
Instead, one may derive causal constraints from digraphs.
It is possible that such an approach would lead to a fine-grained specification of causal constraints.
This then again, may lead to a hierarchy of computational power and information-processing capabilities in general.
Note also that further causal constraints are described in Ref.
[
22
]
, that lead up to the bi-causality constraint.
Finally, studying scenarios beyond the single-output scenario may be of interest.
There are two reasons for that.
The first reason is that for two or more outputs, the scenario may become sensitive to quantum-over-classical advantages and the study of antinomicity.
The second reason is that the Hamiltonian game is a
constraint version
of the guess-your-neighbor’s-input game
[
38
,
21
]
.
Here, the input of a single neighbour must be guessed.
There, all parties must guess their neighbour’s input simultaneously.
This points to a series of intermediate problems that may share similar qualities.
