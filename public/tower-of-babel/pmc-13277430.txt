Targeted Delirium Screening in At-risk Older Adults in the Emergency Department Improves Screening Rates
Authors: Saket A. Saxena, Emily H. Weaver, Evelyn C. Brady, David Bruckman, McKinsey Muir, Stephen Meldon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDelirium is prevalent yet underrecognized in the emergency department (ED). Timely identification of delirium is a priority, but results from systematic implementation of targeted delirium screening in the ED are limited.MethodsThis study analyzes delirium screening rates, positivity rates, and distribution of screening scores before and after implementation of an electronic medical record (EMR)-based risk stratification at ED triage. In the baseline period, delirium screening was based on age (65+ years) and clinical concern for altered mental status (AMS). In the intervention period, eligibility for delirium screening was based on an automated EMR-based risk stratification. The 4AT was used as a delirium screening tool in both periods.ResultsIn the baseline period, only 3267 (3.0%) of patient encounters were noted to have AMS and were eligible for delirium screening; 2241 patients received the screening; 1901 screenings resulted in a positive 4AT. In the intervention period using EMR-based targeted screening, 39,065 encounters were eligible for delirium screening; 14,125 patients received the screening, resulting in 2016 positive 4AT.ConclusionThis multicenter study demonstrates the feasibility of EMR-based risk stratification to target encounters for delirium screening at ED triage. The clinical judgment approach resulted in lower screening rates and a greater percentage of high-positive 4AT scores. The risk-stratified approach resulted in a 6-fold increase in number of screenings and identification of low-positive 4AT scores, detecting more patients with subtle delirium. An approach that leverages risk-stratified screening, complemented by screening based on clinical judgment, could capture a wider range of patients with delirium.

The Bottom LineTimely delirium screening is crucial, yet screening all older adults in the emergency department (ED) can be challenging. This multicenter study shows that stratifying risk using electronic medical records and targeting at-risk patients for delirium screening increased screening by 600% compared with symptom-based methods, yielding a 14.3% delirium detection rate. Implementing 4AT as a screening tool in the ED helps identify patients with possible cognitive impairment. Detecting delirium at triage and reporting results across the health system make this study unique and create opportunities for the earliest recognition, prevention, and management of delirium.

Introduction

Background

Older adults have the highest rate of emergency department (ED) use in the United States and are some of the most complex patients due to frequent multimorbidity, polypharmacy, cognitive impairment, and mobility dysfunction.1, 2, 3, 4 Notably, up to 40% of older ED patients have some form of cognitive impairment, including short-term cognitive dysfunction (delirium) and long-term neurodegenerative disorders (dementia).5 Unlike dementia, delirium is acute, transient, and presents with altered mental status (AMS), with fluctuating awareness and attention.6 Delirium is associated with poor long-term cognitive outcomes and is also closely associated with mortality (at 7 days, 30 days, and 6 months), falls, prolonged hospital stays, acute functional decline, need for rehabilitation services, distress in both patients and family members and has an estimated annual attributable cost of $38 billion to $152 billion in the United States.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Importance

With the growing older population and increasing burden of multiple long-term conditions in the United States, delirium estimates are expected to increase exponentially.4,14 Delirium is estimated to affect 10% to 30% of older ED patients and has been referred to as the quiet epidemic.15 Despite this prevalence and increases in morbidity, mortality, and associated costs, delirium remains underrecognized, with emergency medicine providers failing to detect delirium in an estimated 57% to 83% of cases.16 Three decades of ED research have consistently shown low delirium recognition rates of 0% to 35%.17 Multiple screening instruments have been used for delirium screening in older adults in the ED with variable success.18,19 Traditional ED-based delirium screening, most frequently targeting all encounters or a subset of patients who show signs of abnormal mentation or AMS, has been limited by time constraints and front-line knowledge and beliefs about screening.20 Qualitative research continues to show that variations in delirium detection instruments, the time required to perform delirium assessment, a poor understanding of pathophysiology, and an unclear responsibility for managing delirium are key factors attributable to poor delirium identification in the ED.21 To address the abovementioned limitations, recent studies have started to investigate how to identify the patients who might most benefit from delirium screening in ED.22 Risk-stratified approaches to screen for delirium are promising, but the feasibility and effectiveness compared with more traditional approaches have not been demonstrated.

Goals of This Investigation

Our baseline 2-step process for identifying patients for delirium screening used age (≥65 years) and presentation with concerning change in mental status or confusion or AMS on a preliminary screening by a triage nurse or by asking “If there is any concerns about mental status changes” from the patient or caregiver, if available. For encounters with AMS noted to be present at triage, a formal delirium screening was then completed using the 4AT, a standardized screening instrument with high sensitivity and specificity.23 The 4AT was chosen because of its validity in ED settings, minimal interpersonal variability in administration, and shorter time to complete.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 The 4AT generates a score ranging from 0 to 12. A 4AT score of zero indicates delirium or severe cognitive impairment unlikely, scores of 1 through 3 indicate possible cognitive impairment, and scores of ≥4 indicate possible delirium with or without cognitive impairment.29 For this study, we considered a score of ≥4 as a positive delirium screening. In our intervention process, we aimed to study changes in delirium screening in ED using an automated EMR assessment to target high-risk patients for delirium screening. We developed automated EMR-based risk stratification criteria using evidence-based criteria to identify high-risk geriatric patients (HRGPs) who are at risk for presenting with delirium The HRGP risk-stratification criteria were determined using evidence-based risk factors30,31 for delirium. This method included patients presenting with falls, a history of dementia, use of ≥10 medications, and frequent ED visits (≥5 in a year). Geriatric patients were defined as high risk if they were aged ≥80 years with ≥1 risk factors or aged 65 to 79 years with ≥2 risk factors (Supplementary Appendix 1). This approach of identifying HRGPs by leveraging available EMR-based discrete data has demonstrated feasibility while saving valuable nursing time in this first step for our system.32,33

Once patient was identified as HRGP by EMR, triage nurse received EMR prompt to complete 4AT. Figure 1B depicts the enhanced delirium screening process during the intervention period.

In both periods, simultaneous training modules on delirium, including pocket cards and case-based training, were developed using the situation, background, assessment, and recommendation (SBAR) framework to facilitate effective communication with nursing teams.34 Separate case-based education was provided for physicians, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants. All EDs in both periods used delirium screening using the 4AT. We sought to examine whether our intervention of risk-stratified delirium screening improved screening rates and detected more subtle cases of delirium.

Methods

Study Design

This pre-post retrospective implementation study aimed to measure the success of a process change supported by EMR automation to increase systemwide screening for delirium in the ED among at-risk adults aged ≥65 years.

Study Setting

This study was conducted in a large, integrated health care system, comprising 27 diverse academic, urban, suburban hospital-based and freestanding EDs in 2 states; 17 EDs were included in the study, with a combined annual volume of >640,000 ED visits. Geriatric patients (≥65 years) accounted for 27.5% of all ED patients. Thirteen of these 17 EDs were accredited by the American College of Emergency Physicians Geriatric Emergency Department Accreditation (GEDA) program, which requires tailored education and care processes for geriatric patients.35 Although 4 EDs did not have GEDA accreditation, the delirium screening process and education did not differ between GEDA and non-GEDA sites.

Selection of Participants

All adults aged ≥65 years presenting to eligible EDs during the study period were included in the analysis. Virtual encounters, encounters at ineligible sites, and encounters with missing age, missing or negative length of stay (LOS), and obvious errant discharges were excluded (Supplementary Appendices 2 and 3).

Study Period

This analysis included older adult ED encounters between January 1, 2023, and January 6, 2024. The baseline period was from January 1, 2023, to July 6, 2023, and the intervention period was from July 7, 2023, to January 6, 2024. This period was selected to ensure a sufficient number of patient encounters for statistical analysis.

Site Selection

All 27 EDs serving adults were eligible for the study. Ten EDs that did not provide encounter information for both study periods were excluded; 17 EDs with complete data over the study period were included in the study (Supplementary Appendices 2 and 3).

Outcome Measures

The main outcome measures were 4AT delirium screening completion rates, score, and screen positivity reported for both the baseline and intervention periods. Patient encounter characteristics, including patient demographics (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), HRGP status, ED LOS, and the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) are also reported in both the baseline and intervention periods. Race and ethnicity were self-reported by patients. CCI is a calculated measure of the burden of patients with multiple chronic conditions and reflective of mortality risk within 1 year of hospital discharge.36,37 Documentation of delirium was assessed by the presence of an International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision (ICD-10) code grouper for delirium in the clinical impressions of the ED chart. The ICD-10 grouper code for delirium included the codes R41.0, R41.82, G93.40, and F05. All data were extracted from the EMR.

Statistical Methods

Screening completion rates, screen positivity, and other patient characteristics from baseline were compared with those of intervention using Pearson χ2 tests (proportion testing) and Student t test with Sattherthwaite method (means with unequal variances), Wilcoxon rank sum or Kruskal-Wallis tests (medians or ranks). Positive 4AT scores (4-12) were compared across periods using the Jonckheere-Terpstra (JT) test.

Groups were considered independent, although a minority of patients had encounters in both periods. Means are reported with SD, and medians are reported with an IQR (Table 1). SAS software was used to perform all analyses (SAS Institute). Results

During the study period, records for 283,827 patient encounters were extracted, of which 217,627 older adult encounters were included in the analysis. Further, 23,575 (17.8%) encounters in the baseline period and 40,489 (26.7%) encounters in the intervention period were excluded from the analysis because they were from ineligible ED sites that did not report data in both periods. The 2136 encounters were excluded during data cleaning, resulting in 107,886 encounters in the baseline period and 109,741 in the intervention period (Supplementary Appendices 4 and 5). Both unique and repeated encounters were considered for delirium screening in both periods and were included in the study. Approximately one-third of encounters in both periods were repeat visits: 35,394 in the baseline period, 36,139 in the intervention period, and 21,767 unique patients had encounters in both periods.

Sample Characteristics

No significant differences in patient characteristics were found between the baseline and intervention periods. The mean age of the patient encounters was 76.6 years; 44.4% of patient encounters were men; 72% were Whites, 20% Blacks, 5% Hispanics (any race), 1% Asian and Pacific Islanders, 1% multiracial/ethnic, and 0.13% American Indians or Alaskan Native, and <0.5% patients identified as other race. The mean CCI score was marginally higher (0.1 difference) among baseline encounters (P < .0001). In both periods, the median time between ED arrival and delirium screening was ∼10 minutes. Delirium documentation recorded by ICD-10 codes was 3.4% in both periods. The median LOS was longer during the intervention period (4 minutes) than during the baseline period. Approximately one-third of patients in both periods arrived by ambulance (Table 1).

Screening Results

During the baseline period, all 107,886 patient encounters were eligible for AMS screening by the triaging nurse, and 83,487 (77.4%) had AMS documentation completed. Only 3267 (3.0%) of these encounters documented a positive AMS concern. In this group, 2241 (68%) encounters received the 4AT screening. Limitations

This is a descriptive pre-post implementation study focusing on process change to increase delirium screening in the ED. Changes in health outcomes resulting from the process change were not measured.

The study design did not allow for a washout period after the process change, which could have influenced provider attitudes toward delirium screening in HRGP. Observed changes in screening could also have resulted from other factors, such as differing patient characteristics. Because delirium screening was based on encounters, both unique and repeat encounters were included in the analysis for both periods. Excluding repeated encounters or conducting a smaller paired analysis would not have met the study’s primary focus. Finally, not all eligible patients received delirium screening, which could introduce selection bias and limit the validity of the prevalence rates; this was not the primary focus of this implementation study.

Discussion

Delirium in older adults in the ED remains underrecognized, underreported, and often missed,39 resulting in poor patient outcomes. Delirium, when identified in the ED, increases the opportunities for timely intervention and escalation of care.

This is one of the largest studies to date using risk stratification to assess feasibility of targeted delirium screening at triage in the ED. In contrast, existing research comprised primarily small, single-site studies in academic settings.14,16,18, 19, 20,40,41

Our initial approach focused on provider and nurse education and training on delirium recognition and its importance. The 2-step process of only screening patients with AMS resulted in low overall screening rates, but the identification of severe delirium in a small but significant number of patients. This approach aligned well with standard interventions20,42 but did not screen most at-risk patients.

Conducting additional screenings in the ED and at triage is challenging due to time constraints. Our data show that when screening for delirium relied on triage-nurse recognition of AMS, just 3% of encounters noted this concern, which is significantly lower than anticipated in older adults in the ED and suggests that a substantial number of patients with AMS were not recognized. To address this major barrier, we implemented EMR-based solutions with systemwide scalability to identify high-risk patients.32,33

Our intervention, which used EMR alerts to prompt delirium screening at triage regardless of AMS, successfully increased screening rates by >600%, suggesting greater feasibility and uptake with this new approach. In both periods, delirium screening was typically conducted within 10 minutes of the patient’s arrival at the ED, and the median ED LOS was only 4 minutes longer in the postperiod, which, while statistically significant, had no meaningful impact on ED clinical operations. Implementation of the EMR algorithm modified the eligibility criteria for delirium screening from symptom based to risk factor based, resulting in a significantly higher number of patients having the 4AT completed (2241 vs 14,125). A risk factor-based approach resulted in significantly different delirium detection rates (1.8% vs 14.3%). This reported prevalence of 14.3% is similar to that reported in other recent studies, including a recent meta-analysis of several smaller studies.43,44

Our implementation study parallels the growing calls for automated risk-stratification methods to identify high-risk populations for Geriatric Emergency Department interventions40 and represents a scalable, sustainable strategy for screening geriatric patients in the ED without adding workflow burden to staff. By drawing on existing evidence on delirium risk factors, we automated this process, which had historically relied on a subjective assessment of AMS. Use of EMR records to assess patient risk is dependent on EMR data, and EMR data in our system were very complete, with <1% of encounters dropped during the data cleaning process. The number of patients new to our system (first-time encounter) with potentially limited EMR data during the intervention period was <5%. With EMR crossconnectivity and the adoption of federal EMR standards, this barrier may be overcome in the future.

Any EMR-based risk stratification relies on the accuracy of the information; further improvements in EMR validity may be needed to overcome such barriers. Because the implementation of EMR-based risk stratification of HRGP uses the Healthcare Management and Information System (HIMSS) Electronic Medical Record Adoption Model (EMRAM) stage 7, EMRAM stage 6 measures might be needed to effectively use a similar proposed algorithm to identify HRGP and delirium screening triggers in real-time.45

There are other EMR-based risk-stratification methods, and recently published GRADE-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines suggest using risk stratification for a better screening approach for delirium; however, the overall strength of any risk-stratification method is still limited, and real-world implementation data are still lacking.46 A recent study compared 4 risk-stratification tools.47 However, many of the subcomponents used in these tools require screening by health care personnel, are time consuming, might be unreliable or not available at triage, or are not functional in an EMR-based screen.

Our study focuses on patients known to have poor outcomes,32 who may benefit from a comprehensive geriatric assessment and are at high risk of delirium, and adds delirium detection as part of their risk profile. This study uses the most readily available risk factors for delirium and demonstrates how delirium screening can be implemented on a large scale and sustained without a complex validation process.48 Ongoing advocacy specifically calls for quick, accurate, equitable, acceptable, and integrable delirium screening49; our study demonstrates all these qualities.

Acute changes or fluctuating course and alertness are the 2 most heavily weighted domains in 4AT. The 4AT scoring patterns, as reflected in comparison of 4AT score distribution in both periods (Figure 3), suggest that the symptom-based baseline delirium screening approach may be more sensitive in identifying the most obvious cases of delirium, whereas the risk-based intervention period screening approach may be more sensitive in identifying more subtle symptoms, for example, when acute mental changes or impaired alertness are not the presenting feature and could be missed.

In this study, 7% and 15% of patients in the baseline and intervention periods, respectively, had a 4AT score between 1 and 3, indicating possible cognitive impairment (Table 2). Although these patients do not meet the cutoff for delirium, they may be at increased risk for delirium, and scores in this range could be a way to efficiently screen for cognitive impairment in ED.41 This is the only study, to our knowledge, that identified a simultaneous cohort of individuals with possible cognitive impairment in the ED at triage.

Despite EMR alerts and significantly increased delirium screenings in the intervention period, EMR documentation of delirium by ED providers was consistently low and remained unchanged over the time of study. A lack of consistency between delirium ICD-10 diagnosis capture and actual delirium screening positivity in acute care is a known issue,50 and evidence on approaches to reliably capture delirium diagnoses through ICD-10 coding is lacking. We suspect that further education and EMR prompts are needed for ED providers on how and why to document delirium diagnoses consistently. This conclusion aligns with a key call to action by the American Delirium Society to improve documentation and ICD-10 coding for delirium.51, 52, 53

Given the high incidence and poor outcomes associated with older adults experiencing delirium in the ED, the implementation of successful delirium screening programs is imperative. Although screening patients who present with AMS is common and feasible, we demonstrate the feasibility of an EMR-enabled targeted screening approach during triage that increased both the frequency of screenings and the detection of milder cases of delirium, which may otherwise go undetected. A method that uses risk-stratified screening with the option to screen additional patients based on clinical judgment could maximize identification of ED patients with delirium.
