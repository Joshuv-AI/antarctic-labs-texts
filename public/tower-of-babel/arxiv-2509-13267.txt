Learning Discrete Bayesian Networks with Hierarchical Dirichlet Shrinkage
Alexander Dombowsky, David B. Dunson

Abstract
Discrete Bayesian networks (DBNs) provide a broadly useful framework for modeling dependence structures in multivariate categorical data. There is a vast literature on methods for inferring conditional probabilities and graphical structure in DBNs, but data sparsity and parametric assumptions are major practical issues. In this article, we detail a comprehensive Bayesian framework for learning DBNs. First, we propose a hierarchical prior for the conditional probabilities that enables complicated interactions between parent variables and stability in sparse regimes. We give a novel Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm utilizing parallel Langevin proposals to generate exact posterior samples, avoiding the pitfalls of variational approximations. Moreover, we verify that the full conditional distribution of the concentration parameters is log-concave under mild conditions, facilitating efficient sampling. We then propose two methods for learning network structures, including parent sets, Markov blankets, and DAGs, from categorical data. The first cycles through individual edges each MCMC iteration, whereas the second updates the entire structure as a single step. We evaluate the accuracy, power, and MCMC performance of our methods on several simulation studies. Finally, we apply our methodology to uncover prognostic network structure from primary breast cancer samples.



Keywords
: Directed acyclic graphs; Bayesian inference; structure learning; variable selection; causal discovery

Introduction

Graphical models convey the conditional independencies in a group of random variables and are used routinely in many fields. The focus of this article is on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for categorical observed variables, which are
also known as belief networks, a type of
Bayesian network

(
Pearl 1985
;
Pearl 1988
)
. Such models express the conditional independencies in random variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
in terms of a DAG
G=(V,E)
. The DAG has nodes corresponding to the
p
variables,
V=[p]=\left\{1,\dots,p\right\}
. The parent set of a node, defined as
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\left\{j^{\prime}<j:(j^{\prime},j)\in E\right\}
, is essential to understand the relationship of
G
to
\bm{x}
. By definition, we require
x_{j}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\left\{\bm{x}_{[j-1]\setminus\textrm{Pa}(j)}\right\}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
, where for any
U\subset V
, we set
\bm{x}_{U}=(x_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\in U}
. For discrete data, conditional independence arises via the joint probability mass function of the variables,
\Pr(\bm{x})
, and the resulting model is referred to as a discrete Bayesian network (DBN). An example DAG is given in Figure
1
, which implies the following factorization:
\Pr(\bm{x})=\Pr(x_{1})\Pr(x_{2}\mid x_{1})\Pr(x_{3}\mid x_{2})\Pr(x_{4}\mid x_{1},x_{3})\Pr(x_{5}\mid x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
. We can extract further information on the conditional dependence relations between
x_{j}
and
\bm{x}_{[p]\setminus\left\{j\right\}}
using a Markov blanket of node
j
, which is defined as the union of
\textrm{Pa}(j)
, its children
\left\{j^{\prime}\in[p]:(j,j^{\prime})\in E\right\}
, and the parent set of its children
(
Pearl 1988
)
. In practice, we focus on inferring (a) the conditional probabilities under the DAG and (b) the DAG itself, which is known as
structure learning

(
Kitson et al. 2023
)
.

Contemporary Bayesian models for DBNs often use multinomial likelihoods with conjugate Dirichlet priors on the conditional probabilities
(
Bishop 2006
;
Scutari and Denis 2021
)
. A posteriori, the conditional probabilities of a node given its parent set are also Dirichlet-distributed, with a high-dimensional array of concentration hyperparameters updated by the conditional cell counts. Importantly, conditional cell counts are frequently sparse, so the hyperparameters of the Dirichlet prior often have a substantial impact on the posterior. Yet, there is little practical guidance on how to choose these hyperparameters despite their influence. Alternately, one can use a regression to calculate conditional probabilities
(
Rijmen 2008
;
Kratzer et al. 2023
)
or infer the structure of the network
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2006
)
, but this requires making assumptions on how to model interactions while remaining stable in sparse regimes.

In this paper, we propose a flexible and fully Bayesian method to infer conditional probabilities and graphical structures for DBNs. We show that, when conditioning on the cell counts of its parents, the cell counts of any node follow a Poisson-multinomial distribution
(
Lin et al. 2022
)
. That is, conditioning on the parent set of any node separates the data into distinct groups based on the parent categories. This fact motivates the shrinkage of conditional probabilities toward node-specific
latent prior means
, bypassing the need to specify high-dimensional hyperparameters. Therefore, there are two layers to the model: (i) high-dimensional conditional probabilities and (ii) low-dimensional latent prior means. After marginalizing the first layer, we obtain an embarrassingly parallel Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler for posterior inference that uses the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA). Interestingly, these novel computational techniques are applicable beyond DBNs; for example, for models built from
hierarchical Dirichlet processes (HDP)
(
Teh et al. 2006
)
. We show that the full conditional distribution of the prior means factorizes across the categories of each node and, under certain conditions on our hyperparameters and if the marginal cell counts are positive, is almost surely log-concave.

We extend our model to account for the uncertainty in the DAG, allowing inference on
\Pr(G\mid\bm{n})
and applicability to causal discovery
(
Pearl 2009
)
. We initially focus on the problem of inferring a single parent set. The first algorithm we propose assumes all possible parent sets have positive probability and iteratively adds and removes edges. A second algorithm is detailed for the case in which the user would like to evaluate a moderate collection of candidate parents. Both allow for the incorporation of prior knowledge and regularization (i.e., restricting the degree of a node) through hyperparameters. We show how these algorithms can also be used to model Markov blankets, then modify the aforementioned algorithms to produce MCMC samples of DAGs. The output is used to obtain a point estimate of
G
and express uncertainty with posterior edge probabilities
\Pr((j^{\prime},j)\mid\bm{n})
.

There is a rich literature on hierarchical Dirichlet models and their uses. A notable contribution is the HDP
(
Teh et al. 2006
)
, which was originally motivated by modeling dependence in group-specific distributions and clusterings. Hierarchical Dirichlet distributions are also widely used in topic modeling. For example,
Perotte et al. 2011
extend latent Dirichlet allocation
(
Blei et al. 2003
)
to shrink towards a common Dirichlet measure across a corpus of documents. However, none of these methods are intended for modeling probability distributions that factorize according to a DAG, nor do they address structure learning. Other works propose versions of the HDP that incorporate a DAG structure
(
Zhang et al. 2010
;
Alam et al. 2019
;
Chakrabarti et al. 2024
)
, although these models differ greatly from ours.

Azzimonti et al. 2017
and
Azzimonti et al. 2019
apply a finite version of the HDP to DBNs, while
Azzimonti et al. 2022
develop a graph score for related models. Their methodology is extended to account for multi-source data in
Catalán Cerezo 2023
. In contrast to our methods, these works focus on classification and meta-analysis. Furthermore, they use variational methods to estimate the concentration parameters and use a variational surrogate in place of the marginal likelihood; such variational approaches are subject to massive approximation error to the true posterior. We make major advances in the ability to conduct accurate Bayesian inferences by developing efficient exact posterior sampling using MCMC. Furthermore, our approach enables structure learning through fully Bayesian models, whereas the aforementioned approaches rely on deterministic search algorithms over the DAG space. There is also a literature on recursive Dirichlet priors for tree-structured DAGs
(
Petitjean et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, though these incorporate a markedly different structure than the hierarchical Dirichlet model we propose.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
details the graph-theoretic and contingency table notation, motivates hierarchical modeling for DBNs, and introduces our method, which we call Hierarchical Directed Dirichlet Networks (HiDDeN). A MALA-within-Gibbs sampler for HiDDeN is described in Section
3
, and we propose several algorithms for structure learning in Section
4
. Section
5
evaluates HiDDeN’s performance for parameter and structure learning on synthetic data, while Section
6
uses HiDDeN to uncover network structures related to breast cancer treatment. Methodological and theoretical extensions are discussed in Section
7
.

Constructing Dirichlet Networks for Known DAGs

2.1

Discrete Bayesian Networks

Let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
be a vector of
p
variables, where
x_{j}\in[k_{j}]=\left\{1,\dots,k_{j}\right\}
with
2\leq k_{j}<\infty
for all
j\in[p]=\left\{1,\dots,p\right\}
. If
k_{j}=2
,
x_{j}
is binary and we follow the convention
x_{j}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. The vector
\bm{x}
takes values in
\chi=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{p}):x_{j}\in[k_{j}]\right\}
. We are interested in modeling the joint probability mass function
\Pr(\bm{x})
, which corresponds to an unknown probability tensor of dimension
K=\prod_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}
. In general, the massive dimension of the probability tensor is unwieldy, motivating the more interpretable approach of describing
\Pr(\bm{x})
through its
conditional independencies
. We say that
x_{j_{1}}
and
x_{j_{2}}
are conditionally independent given a set of variables
\bm{x}_{j_{3}}
if
\Pr(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}}\mid\bm{x}_{j_{3}})=\Pr(x_{j_{1}}\mid\bm{x}_{j_{3}})\Pr(x_{j_{2}}\mid\bm{x}_{j_{3}})
for all choices of
x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}},\bm{x}_{j_{3}}
.

A
Bayesian network
is a framework for describing the conditional dependence structure between variables comprising
\bm{x}
, characterized by a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
G=([p],E)
and parameters
\bm{\pi}

(
Pearl 1985
;
Pearl 1988
)
. The graph
G
has the vertex set
V=[p]=\left\{1,\dots,p\right\}
and edges
E\subseteq\left\{(j,j^{\prime}):j\neq j^{\prime}\in[p]\right\}
. A DAG is a specific type of graph that has (a) directed edges and (b) no cycles (i.e., a non-trivial path in
G
from a node to itself).
A discrete Bayesian network (DBN) models the joint distribution of a set of categorical variables
\bm{x}
as

\begin{gathered}\Pr(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\pi},G)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\Pr(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},G)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\textrm{ for all }\bm{x}\in\chi,\end{gathered}

(1)

where
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\left\{j^{\prime}<j:(j^{\prime},j)\in E\right\}
indexes the
parents
of
j
,
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}=(x_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)}
is the vector of variables with nodes in
\textrm{Pa}(j)
, and
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})=\Pr(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},G)
. We use
E_{j}=\left\{(j^{\prime},j)\in E:j^{\prime}<j\right\}
to represent the edges associated with the parents of node
j
. By convention, any node with no parents is a
root node
, and if node
j
is a root, this implies
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})=\bm{\pi}_{j}(x_{j})
.

DBNs essentially describe a connection between a graph
G
and the joint probability of a set of variables. Formally, we are assuming a relationship between the conditional independencies in
\bm{x}
and the edges in
G
. This requires that
G
be an
independence map
(I-map)
(
Pearl 1988
)
, i.e., that if the nodes corresponding to
j_{3}
intercept all directed paths between
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
, then
x_{j_{1}}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp x_{j_{2}}\mid\bm{x}_{j_{3}}
. To ensure consistency between
G
and (
1
), we further assume that
G
is a
minimal I-map
; if
H\subset G
is the subgraph of
G
defined by deleting any edge in
E
, then
H
is not an I-map for
\bm{x}
.

2.2

Modeling of the Cell Counts

Let
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{ip})
for
i=1,\dots,n
be categorical observations, i.e.
x_{ij}\in[k_{j}]
for all
j=1,\dots,p
, which we collect in
\left\{\bm{x}_{i}\right\}
. In a multinomial likelihood, the sufficient statistics for the cell probabilities are
cell counts
. For any subset
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[p]
, the cell count of
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}}
is
\bm{n}_{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{1}_{\bm{x}_{i\mathcal{J}}=\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}}}
, that is, the number of samples
\left\{\bm{x}_{i\mathcal{J}}=(x_{ij})_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\right\}
generated from the cell
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}}
. Key properties of the cell counts include:
\sum_{\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}}}\bm{n}_{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}})=n
; and for any
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{J}
,
\bm{n}_{\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}^{\prime}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{n}_{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}})\textbf{1}_{\bm{x}_{i\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}=\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}}
. The discrete tensor
\bm{n}=(\bm{n}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\in\chi}
is called the
contingency table
, and we can obtain contingency tables for any
\mathcal{J}\subset[p]
with
\bm{n}_{\mathcal{J}}=(\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}}))_{\bm{x}_{\mathcal{J}}\in\chi_{\mathcal{J}}}
, with
\chi_{\mathcal{J}}=\left\{(x_{j}):j\in\mathcal{J},x_{j}\in[k_{j}]\right\}
.

DBNs focus on modeling cell counts for each node and its parent set
(
Scutari and Denis 2021
)
. Let
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
be the cell count of
(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
; for any root
j
, these are the marginal cell counts
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})=\bm{n}_{j}(x_{j})
. Given any
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
, let
\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})=(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}))_{x_{j}\in[k_{j}]}
be the conditional cell counts for the
j
th node.
Under (
1
), the multinomial likelihood of the global contingency table factorizes according to

f(\bm{n}\mid\bm{\pi},G)\propto\prod_{j=1}^{p}\prod_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}}\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})^{\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})},

(2)

where proportionality is conveyed in terms of
(\bm{\pi},G)
. Note that (
2
) makes no further assumptions on the conditional PMF for each node, i.e. we do not assume an additive relationship between
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
and
x_{j}
. Conditionally on
G
, the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) for any cell with non-zero counts are

\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})=\frac{\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})}{\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})},

(3)

that is, the proportions of cell counts for the subgraph
H_{j}=(\textrm{Pa}(j)\cup\left\{j\right\},F_{j})\subset G
, where
F_{j}=\left\{(j^{\prime},j):j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)\right\}
. These cell counts are important quantities for testing I-map conditions via chi-square tests
(
Agresti 2002
)
.

Estimating the conditional probabilities can be brittle to
sparsity
, i.e. when
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
are small, motivating
a Bayesian approach. As (
2
) is an exponential family, there exists a conjugate prior: assign independent Dirichlet distributions to
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
for all categories
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}

(
Lindley 1964
;
Dawid and Lauritzen 1993
;
Castelletti and Peluso 2021
)
. The posterior mean of
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
, conditional on
G
, is a shrinkage estimator, taking values between the cell proportions (
3
) and the prior mean.
Hence, we can think of the Dirichlet hyperparameters as being “prior cell counts" for variable
j
and its parents. Beyond this interpretation, it is difficult to choose their values in practice, in part due to the vast dimension of the hyperparameters. The conjugate prior for (
2
) introduces
\sum_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j^{\prime}}
prior cell counts, one for each cell in
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}
. Of course, one could simply set the prior cell counts to be equal across all the cells, reducing the problem to just one hyperparameter, but this choice can lead to brittleness.

, which has

, and the MLEs for
\Pr(x_{j}=1\mid x_{j-1})
are
(3/4,1/3,0/5,3/7,0/3,3/3)
. We assume that
(\bm{\pi}_{j\mid j-1}(0\mid x_{j-1}),\bm{\pi}_{j\mid j-1}(1\mid x_{j-1}))\sim\textrm{Dir}(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j})
independently, and we vary
\alpha_{j}\in\left\{0.01,1,2,5,10,100\right\}
. For comparison, the first column of Figure

are the true simulated conditional probabilities,
\Pr(x_{j}=1\mid x_{j-1})
. The sparse counts make
\alpha_{j}
very influential on the fitted values. A small value of
\alpha_{j}
leads to a better estimate of
\Pr(x_{j}=1\mid x_{j-1}=6)
, but a worse estimate of
\Pr(x_{j}=1\mid x_{j-1}=2)
. In addition, letting
\alpha_{j}
grow very large results in trivial behavior, setting
\widehat{\Pr}(x_{j}=1\mid x_{j-1},\bm{n})=1/2
.

We pursue an alternate strategy, retaining the nonparametric likelihood in (
2
) but circumventing the high dimensionality and brittleness of the Dirichlet-multinomial. First, we relax the assumption that all of the conditional probabilities are statistically independent. Returning to the example in Figure
1
, suppose that
k_{j}=2
for all
j=1,\dots,5
. Since
\textrm{Pa}(4)=\left\{1,3\right\}
, the conditional probabilities of
x_{4}
can be visualized as

\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(0\mid 0,0)&\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(1\mid 0,0)\\
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(0\mid 1,0)&\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(1\mid 1,0)\\
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(0\mid 0,1)&\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(1\mid 0,1)\\
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(0\mid 1,1)&\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}(1\mid 1,1)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

The rows of
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}
are all probability vectors that describe the
same events
, i.e., whether
x_{4}
takes a value of
0
or
1
, but across different possible values of the parents. Under the standard Dirichlet-multinomial model, the rows are assumed to be independent before observing the cell counts. Instead, it is more realistic to generate dependent conditional probabilities, since each row of
\pi_{4\mid\{1,3\}}
describes the same phenomena but conditioned on different events. A natural way to induce prior dependence is through a hierarchical model in which conditional probabilities are independent conditional on a common prior mean. In sparse regimes, the conditional probabilities for cells with small counts will shrink towards the common prior mean, combining information across the rows of
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}
. The Dirichlet-multinomial model would shrink these probabilities towards row-specific prior means that are entirely specified by the user, such as in Figure
2
.

The above intuition about a hierarchy is supported by a key mathematical property of DBNs: the parents of node
j
partition the samples into
K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}=\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j^{\prime}}
groups, with the size of each group given by
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
for all categories
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
. We demonstrate this by focusing on the transition kernel of the stochastic process of cell counts, defined by advancing from node-to-node on the DAG. We show that the transition kernel for
\bm{n}_{j}
conditional on the cell counts of its parents is a Poisson-multinomial distribution
(
Chen and Liu 1997
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
, a generalization of the multinomial distribution that allows for
distinct
event probabilities. For an experiment with
k
outcomes, we denote the Poisson-multinomial distribution as
\textrm{P-Mult}(n,P)
, where
n
is the number of trials (as in the typical multinomial set-up), and
P
is a
n\times k
matrix whose rows are probability vectors. The Poisson-multinomial simplifies to the multinomial when the rows of
P
are all equal.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
be a DAG and
\left\{x_{i}\right\}
a multinomial sample from a DBN defined by
G
. The conditional distribution of
\bm{n}_{j}
given its parent contingency table
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
is

\bm{n}_{j}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},\bm{\pi},G\sim\textrm{P-Mult}(n,P_{j}),

where
P_{j}=(\bm{p}_{j1},\dots,\bm{p}_{jn})^{T}
and
\bm{p}_{ji}=\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{i\textrm{Pa}(j)})
.

The rows of
P_{j}
in Theorem
1
have a specific form: they must be one of the rows of
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}
by construction. In the context of Figure
1
, this means that
\bm{p}_{ji}
must be one of the rows of
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Hence, we expect certain rows of
P_{j}
to be identical, resulting in clustering of the observations into
K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
groups, similar to Bayesian hierarchical models for grouped data
(
Gelman et al. 2013
)
. In light of this property, we specify an appropriate hierarchical framework for learning the conditional probabilities in DBNs.

2.3

Hierarchical Model of Dirichlet Kernels

Following the group structure we discussed in the previous section, we assume that the conditional probabilities of each node are drawn from hierarchical Dirichlet kernels:

\begin{gathered}\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\overset{ind}{\sim}\textrm{Dir}(\bm{t}_{j});\textrm{ for all }\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\in\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)};\;\>\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})\overset{ind}{\sim}\textrm{Gamma}(\rho_{j}/k_{j},b_{j});\end{gathered}

(5)

where
\bm{t}_{j}=(\bm{t}_{j}(1),\dots,\bm{t}_{j}(k_{j}))\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}}
and
\rho_{j},b_{j}>0
. We view
\bm{t}_{j}
as an unnormalized latent prior mean, which defines a common prior expectation for all
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
. If
j
is a root, we specify (
5
) as a hierarchical prior for
\bm{\pi}_{j}(\cdot)
. We highlight that (
5
) can be applied to any DAG and for any number of categories in the different categorical variables. We refer to the prior for
\bm{\pi}
in (
5
) as a Hierarchical Directed Dirichlet Network (HiDDeN).

Let
\beta_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{j}}\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})
and
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}=\bm{t}_{j}/\beta_{j}
. Returning to our running example in Figure
1
, HiDDeN will introduce prior means for each non-root:
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{2}
,
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{3}
,
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4}
, and
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{5}
. A priori, the rows of
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}
in (
4
) are centered on
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4}
, meaning
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\pi}_{4\mid\left\{1,3\right\}}\mid\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4}]=(\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{4})^{T}
. One can show that
\beta_{j}
controls variation in the conditional probabilities from their common prior means; as
\beta_{j}\to\infty
, then
\pi_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\to\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}
. Furthermore, our representation in (
5
) is equivalent to specifying Dirichlet and gamma priors for
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
, respectively
(
Das et al. 2024
)
. For roots, (
5
) can be interpreted as a prior distribution for a concentration parameter in the typical Dirichlet prior. While we focus on non-roots in this and the following sections, introducing latent variables
\bm{t}_{j}
for all nodes is relevant to our approach to structure learning in Section
4
.

Let
\bm{x}^{\textrm{new}}_{j}
be a new observation from the
j
th node
after
observing the data. Then, the posterior predictive probability of
\bm{x}^{\textrm{new}}_{j}
is

\begin{gathered}\Pr(\bm{x}_{j}^{\textrm{new}}=x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},\bm{n},G)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})}{\beta_{j}+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})}\bigg|\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j},G\bigg].\end{gathered}

(6)

For small values of
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
, (
6
) shrinks toward
\mathbb{E}[\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})/\{\beta_{j}+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\})\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j},G]
, concatenating information on all conditional probabilities
\{\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\}_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}
. In the context of the example conditional probabilities in (
4
), HiDDeN learns common structure in the rows of
\bm{\pi}_{4\mid 1,3}
via
\bm{t}_{4}
, which is then used to infer sparse cells and avoid hyperparameter sensitivity. We explicitly show how information is shared in the following section.

3

MALA-Within-Gibbs Sampler

Standard MCMC samplers for finite approximations to the HDP, such as that of
Teh et al. 2006
, could be applied to HiDDeN by reparameterizing (
5
). However, these algorithms are not motivated by the setting of DBNs and assume that the cell counts are derived from latent variables. Instead, we propose a novel marginal Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler that uses the setting of DBNs to ensure efficiency. Importantly, our approach does not rely on variational inference, which has previously been applied to hierarchical Dirichlet kernels on DAGs
(
Azzimonti et al. 2019
;
Azzimonti et al. 2022
)
.

As we do not need
\bm{\pi}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
to compute the fitted values (
6
), we marginalize these variables out from the likelihood, which in turn reduces the size of the parameter space. After marginalizing over these quantities, the likelihood contribution of the nodes are
f(\bm{n}\mid\bm{t},G)\propto\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},\bm{t}_{j})
, where

\begin{gathered}f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},\bm{t}_{j})=\prod_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\in\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\frac{\Gamma(\beta_{j})}{\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})+\beta_{j})}\prod_{x_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\frac{\Gamma(\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})}{\Gamma(\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j}))}.\end{gathered}

(7)

If
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\emptyset
, we introduce a dummy variable for the parents of
j
so that
\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}=\left\{1\right\}
. Hence, our algorithm is applicable to both roots and non-roots. By construction of our prior, the MCMC updates of each
\bm{t}_{j}
can be carried out independently. The full joint distribution for a single node is obtained by multiplying the prior distribution for
\bm{t}_{j}
in (
5
) to its associated term in (
7
):

\begin{gathered}f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)},\bm{t}_{j}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},G)\propto\prod_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\beta_{j}\right)}{\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})+\beta_{j})}\\
\times\prod_{x_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\frac{\exp\left\{-b_{j}\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})\right\}\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})^{\rho_{j}/k_{j}-1}}{\Gamma(\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j}))^{K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}}\left\{\prod_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})+\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j}))\right\},\end{gathered}

(8)

where
K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}=|\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}|=\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j^{\prime}}
. The distribution in (
8
) is split into two terms. The first is a function of the sum
across
categories in the
j
th node,
\beta_{j}=\sum_{x_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})
. The second term factorizes as a product over these categories. Using elementary properties of the Beta function, we obtain that the first term can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\beta_{j}\right)}{\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})+\beta_{j})}=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})^{\beta_{j}-1}(1-\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}))^{\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})-1}d\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})}{\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}))}

for all
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
. This decomposition suggests the Gibbs sampler given in Algorithm
1
to sample
\bm{t}_{j}
.

Each step in the Gibbs sampler can be done using parallel computing. The auxiliary variables
\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
are independent across the values of
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
; the entries of the latent prior means
\bm{t}_{j}
are independent across the values of
x_{j}
. Furthermore, information is shared across the parent set by the product in (
9
): the cell counts
\{\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})\}_{\forall\>\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}
contribute to the expression of
h_{j,x_{j}}
.
We show that
h_{j,x_{j}}(t)
is log-concave under mild conditions on marginal cell counts and hyperparameters.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\textrm{Pa}(j)\subseteq[p]\setminus\{j\}
. The function
h_{j,x_{j}}(t)
is log-concave in
t
for all values of the auxiliary variables
\{\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\}_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\in\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}
if
\rho_{j}\geq k_{j}
and
\bm{n}_{j}(x_{j})>0
.

Log-concavity is shown by taking derivatives of the logarithm of (
9
). The first two derivatives are

\displaystyle\frac{d\log h_{j,x_{j}}(t)}{dt}=-b_{j}+\frac{\rho_{j}/k_{j}-1}{t}+\sum_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\{\psi(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})+t)-\psi(t)+\log\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\}

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\log h_{j,x_{j}}(t)}{dt^{2}}=\frac{1-\rho_{j}/k_{j}}{t^{2}}+\sum_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\{\psi^{(1)}(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})+t)-\psi^{(t)}(t)\},

(11)

where
\psi(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\log\Gamma(t)
and
\psi^{(1)}(t)=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\log\Gamma(t)
are the digamma and trigamma functions, respectively. The condition on
\rho_{j}
is needed to make the first term in (
11
) negative, while
n_{j}(x_{j})>0
is a sufficient condition for the second term in (
11
) to be negative since the trigamma function is decreasing. These conditions are minimal are practice. We typically set
\rho_{j}
to be some value larger than
k_{j}
, such as
\rho_{j}=k_{j}+1
, to satisfy Theorem
2
. The second condition states that we require that category
x_{j}
is represented by at least one observation, which is trivial to verify.

In light of Theorem
2
, we propose using the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) to simulate from (
9
), leveraging the appealing properties of MALA for sampling log-concave densities
(
Dwivedi et al. 2019
)
. The Supplemental Material explains in detail the MALA implementation for the HiDDeN model. Given a current value
\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})
, the Langevin proposal generates
\bm{t}_{j}^{*}(x_{j})
with

\bm{t}_{j}^{*}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})+\frac{\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})^{2}}{2}\frac{d\log h_{j,x_{j}}(\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j}))}{dt}+\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})z^{(r)},

(12)

where
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})>0
is the step size and
z^{(r)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. MALA pools information across the parent categories using the derivative (
10
): all of the node-parent cell counts
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
are added to
\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})^{(r)}
and summed over. In addition, we also achieve pooling of information across the values of node
j
with
\bm{u}_{j}(x_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
. The algorithm requires the specification of step size parameters
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})>0
for all
x_{j}\in[k_{j}]
. The step size cannot be naively chosen, e.g. setting
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})
too large can result in negative Langevin proposals, which are automatically rejected in the Metropolis step. We adaptively adjust the step size using the acceptance probabilities for
\bm{t}(x_{j})
and MCMC diagnostics for the log-posterior, favoring a balance between successful proposals and a sufficient exploration of the parameter space.

The complexity of Algorithm
1
depends on multiple factors. The contingency tables can be derived in advance and stored as
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
(parent cell counts) and
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}
(parent-child cell counts). The computation time for
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}
is bounded above by
\mathcal{O}(nK_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j})
, and
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
is obtained by summing the entries in
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}
, which is
\mathcal{O}(K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j})
.
The number of categories in the parents,
K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
, determines the number of auxiliary variables to sample and the computation time for the gradient in (
10
).
K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
is influenced both by the cardinality of the parent set and by the number of categories in each parent. For example,
K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}=16
when node
j
has
4
parents with
4
categories each, and when node
j
has
2
parents with
2
categories and
8
categories, respectively. Similarly, the number of categories in
j
, or
k_{j}
, influences the calculation of
\beta_{j}
and the sampling of
\bm{t}_{j}
. Therefore, the complexity of doing one sweep of Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{O}(K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j})
, assuming the tables are made before running MCMC.

Structure Learning

4.1

Edge Indicators

We first address the problem of deriving an unknown parent set of a single node
j
with little prior information. For nodes
j
and
j^{\prime}
, let
z_{j^{\prime}j}=\textbf{1}_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)}
be the indicator of
(j^{\prime},j)\in E
. In addition, suppose that we have access to a set of candidate parents
\textrm{Ca}(j)\subset[p]\setminus\left\{j\right\}
, so that
f\left\{\textrm{Pa}(j)\subseteq\textrm{Ca}(j)\right\}=1
, meaning that
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\left\{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j):z_{j^{\prime}j}=1\right\}
. Conditional on
\textrm{Ca}(j)
, we model
z_{j^{\prime}j}\sim\textrm{Ber}(\gamma_{j})
, where
0<\gamma_{j}<1
controls the size of the parent set, or
in-degree
, of node
j
, since
|\textrm{Pa}(j)|=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)}z_{j^{\prime}j}\sim\textrm{Bin}(|\textrm{Ca}(j)|,\gamma_{j})
. We collect the indicators into a vector
\bm{z}_{j}=(z_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)}
. To complete the specification, we also assume
\gamma_{j}\sim\textrm{Beta}(c_{j},d_{j})
for
c_{j},d_{j}>0
. Importantly, our formulation allocates non-zero prior probability to all subsets of
\textrm{Ca}(j)
.

The MCMC update for
z_{j^{\prime}j}
is a sample from a Bernoulli distribution, with probabilities

\begin{gathered}f(z_{j^{\prime}j}=0\mid-)\propto\bigg\{\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}}(1-z_{j^{\prime\prime}j})+d_{j}\bigg\}f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}},\bm{t}_{j})\\
f(z_{j^{\prime}j}=1\mid-)\propto\bigg\{\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}}z_{j^{\prime\prime}j}+c_{j}\bigg\}f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},\bm{t}_{j});\end{gathered}

(13)

where
\textrm{Ca}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}=\textrm{Ca}(j)\setminus\left\{j^{\prime}\right\}
,
z_{j}^{-j^{\prime}}=(z_{j^{\prime\prime}j})_{j^{\prime\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}}
,
\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}=\{j^{\prime\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)\setminus\left\{j^{\prime}\right\}:z_{j^{\prime\prime}j}=1\}
, and
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j}\cup\left\{j\right\}
. By convention, if
\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j}=\emptyset
, we set
\bm{\chi}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}}=\left\{1\right\}
and
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j^{\prime}}}(1)=n
. With
\gamma_{j}
marginalized out in (
13
), the hyperparameters
c_{j}
and
d_{j}
influence the distribution of the in-degree of
j
; for example,
if
c_{j}/d_{j}\to\infty
then
z_{j^{\prime}j}\to 1
. Sampling from (
13
) for all
j\prime\in\textrm{Ca}(j)
adds or removes edges from
\textrm{Pa}(j)
.

A single MCMC update of
\bm{z}_{j}

is detailed in Algorithm
2
. Once each entry of
\bm{z}_{j}
is updated, we proceed according to the Gibbs sampler in Algorithm
1
with the updated parent set
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\left\{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j):z_{j^{\prime}j}=1\right\}
. Each iteration has complexity bounded by
\mathcal{O}(nK_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}k_{j}+k_{j}+K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}+|\textrm{Ca}(j)-1|)
, assuming that the contingency tables are recalculated during every loop through
\textrm{Ca}(j)
. If the previous
z_{j^{\prime}j}=1
, updating that edge indicator is more efficient since
\textrm{Pa}(j)^{-j}
is obtained by summing the entries in the contingency table.

4.2

Bayesian Parent Selection

In contrast to the setting of Algorithm
2
, suppose that we prespecify a moderate number of candidate parents
\textrm{Pa}(j)_{1},\dots,\textrm{Pa}(j)_{M_{j}}
and prior probabilities
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{1}),\dots,\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{M_{j}})
, which can be chosen to favor certain nodes as parents or restrict the degree of
j
. For example, in medicine,
\{\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M_{j}}
can represent different combinations of interventions for a disease, while node
j
is an outcome. Default prior probabilities can be chosen as
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m})=1/M_{j}
for all
m\in[M_{j}]
. Unlike using edge indicators, this setup allows for direct modeling of the parent set and can lead to a markedly smaller space of network structures for the MCMC algorithm to explore over. Also, under this model we update the entire parent set in a single step rather than passing through all possible edges.

We generate draws from the posterior
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)\mid\bm{n})
by iteratively updating
\textrm{Pa}(j)
within the set of candidate parents.
We compute the conditional probability of the
m
th candidate parent as
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}\mid-)\propto\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m})f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}},\bm{t}_{j}).

We then set
\textrm{Pa}(j)=\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}
with probability
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}\mid-)
. Taking the logarithm of
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}\mid-)
returns the Bayesian Dirichlet (BDE) graph score
(
Heckerman et al. 1995
;
Friedman 1998
)
of
\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}
with the latent prior means in place of the prior sample sizes. Algorithm
3
shows the update of
\textrm{Pa}(j)
in our MCMC sampler, which we then use to update
\bm{t}_{j}
in Algorithm
1
. In contrast to Algorithm
2
, we can store the parent and parent-child contingency tables
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}}
and
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m},j}
beforehand, increasing efficiency. This makes the complexity of one pass through Algorithm
3

\mathcal{O}(M_{j}k_{j}K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
.

4.3

MCMC Estimators for Graphical Quantities

Alternating Algorithms
1
and
2
or
3
for
R>0
iterations returns posterior draws of the parent sets, denoted
\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)}
for
r=1,\dots,R
; the binary edge indicators
\bm{z}_{j}^{(r)}
, where
z_{j^{\prime}j}^{(r)}=1
if and only if
j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)}
; and the latent prior means
\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}
. Importantly, the conditions of Theorem
2
depend only on the marginal cell counts and hyperparameters, not the makeup of the parent set. Therefore, we retain efficient sampling of
\bm{t}_{j}
even when the parent set is treated as unknown.

Posterior edge probabilities can be used to represent posterior uncertainty in graph learning; for example, using line widths that increase with edge probabilities. In addition, there is often particular interest in the directed relationships between specific variables. For example, in the causal inference setting, one may want to infer the weight of evidence of a directed edge between an exposure or treatment and an outcome variable.
The posterior probabilities are estimated using the binary edge indicators,
\Pr(z_{j^{\prime}j}=1\mid\bm{n})\approx\widehat{\Pr}(z_{j^{\prime}j}=1\mid\bm{n})=(1/R)\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{j^{\prime}j}^{(r)}
. We approximate the posterior probability of an entire parent set
\textrm{Pa}(j)
with
\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)\mid\bm{n})\approx\widehat{\Pr}(\textrm{Pa}(j)\mid\bm{n})=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\textbf{1}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)}=\textrm{Pa}(j)}
. Under appropriate loss functions, estimators
\widehat{\textrm{Pa}}(j)
can be calculated from
\widehat{\Pr}(\textrm{Pa}(j)\mid\bm{n})
. Reasonable estimators include the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator, which chooses the combination of parents having the highest posterior probability, or the median probability model
(
Barbieri and Berger 2004
)
, which chooses all parents having
\widehat{\Pr}(z_{j^{\prime}j}=1\mid\bm{n})>0.5
.

Finally, we obtain the predictive PMF of
\bm{x}^{\textrm{new}}
by taking the expectation of (
6
) with respect to the posterior distribution of
\textrm{Pa}(j)
:

\begin{gathered}\Pr(\bm{x}^{\textrm{new}}_{j}=x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Ca}(j)},\bm{n})\approx\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)},j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)}},x_{j})}{\beta_{j}^{(r)}+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)}}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)^{(r)}})}.\end{gathered}

(14)

Here, we adopt the notation
\Pr(\bm{x}^{\textrm{new}}_{j}=x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Ca}(j)},\bm{n})
to reflect that
\textrm{Pa}(j)
is unknown but constricted to be contained in a candidate set
\textrm{Ca}(j)
. Note that
\textrm{Ca}(j)
is prespecified by the user when applying Algorithm
2
, and we can define
\textrm{Ca}(j)
from Algorithm
3
via
\textrm{Ca}(j)=\{j^{\prime}:\exists m\in[M_{j}]\textrm{ with }j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}\}
. (
14
) can be used to create a classifier for node
j
that takes into account uncertainty in the conditional dependencies.

4.4

Inferring Markov Blankets and DAGs

The algorithms and MCMC estimators detailed in the previous subsections can be generalized to infer Markov blankets and the overall graph
G
. The extension to Markov blankets is straightforward: let
\textrm{Ca}(j)=[p]\setminus\{j\}
, then run Algorithm
2
or
3
(depending on the availability of prior information and the problem setting) to produce MCMC samples of the Markov blanket, denoted
\textrm{Mb}(j)
, which we display as an undirected graph; see Section
5.2
for an example. This process is then repeated for each node, resulting in posterior probabilities for
p
undirected graphs defined on
p-1
nodes.

To infer a DAG, we can use a similar strategy: conditional on the rest of the graph
G
, we define a set of candidate parents
\textrm{Ca}(j)
for a particular node
j
, update the associated parent set using Algorithms
2
or
3
, and repeat these steps for each of the different nodes within the Gibbs sampler. In applying this approach, it is crucial to rule out cycles in defining the possible parents of node
j
taking into account the current graph structure in other nodes. That is, if adding the edge
(j^{\prime},j)
to
E
results in a cycle, we automatically set
z_{j^{\prime}j}=0
in Algorithm
2
and
\max_{m:j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}}\Pr(\textrm{Pa}(j)_{m}\mid-)=0
in Algorithm
3
. In the special case in which there is a fixed stochastic ordering of the variables, we can apply HiDDeN in parallel across the nodes with
\textrm{Ca}(j)=[j-1]
.

Alternatively, if we can narrow down consideration to a small-to-moderate number of candidate DAGs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{M}
, we can calculate posterior probabilities for these DAGs updating prior probabilities
\Pr(G_{1}),\dots,\Pr(G_{M})
.
The posterior probabilities of each
G_{m}
provide a model-based alternative to other graph scores such as BDE. We can update
G
in a Gibbs sampler by computing

Discussion

This article proposes a new Bayesian method for parameter and structure learning in DBNs. We specify a hierarchical Dirichlet model for the conditional probabilities of a node,
j
, given the categories of its parents,
\textrm{Pa}(j)
. A key to our approach is learning latent prior means,
\bm{t}_{j}
, adaptively from the data. We derive a novel marginal MALA-within-Gibbs sampler to generate posterior samples of
\bm{t}_{j}
. Our computation strategy is motivated by a key property: the full conditional distribution of the latent prior means is factorizable and almost-surely log-concave. We generalize our approach to infer parent sets, Markov blankets, and DAGs, then construct appropriate estimators and uncertainty quantification metrics. In several simulation studies, we demonstrate the strong performance of our method in parameter estimation, structure learning, and graph selection. Finally, our approach infers insightful networks describing the relationships between genetic and non-genetic factors with various outcomes in breast cancer.

There are many possible extensions to our work. For instance, existing adaptive algorithms for selecting
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}

(
Atchadé 2006
;
Marshall and Roberts 2012
;
Biron-Lattes et al. 2024
)
could be applied to our MCMC scheme and circumvent tuning. More generally, gradient based MCMC methods, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) or the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)
(
Hoffman et al. 2014
)
, could be used to sample from the posterior of
\bm{t}_{j}
. In addition, other priors besides the gamma distribution may be considered for
\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})
, though some choices do not retain the log-concavity property. With the gamma prior, our MALA-within-Gibbs algorithm can be used for posterior inference in finite approximations to the HDP, and could be adapted to address extensions of the HDP
(
Catalano and Del Sole 2025
)
, though log-concavity will fail if there are empty mixture components. We propose several algorithms to obtain MCMC samples of graphical structures, but HiDDeN can be adjusted to incorporate other MCMC DAG algorithms; see
Liang et al. 2023
and references therein.

A promising area of future research is to extend HiDDeN to different settings than those considered in this article, such as weighted Bayesian networks and undirected graphs. With regard to the latter models, our approach can infer
local
undirected graphs via the Markov blankets, thus our strategy could be extended to model an entire undirected graph. Alternatively, we can construct a single undirected graph by iteratively moving from node-to-node, fitting HiDDeN, and keeping nodes in the candidate set based on the point estimate, say, the median probability model, from the previous node. In a broader sense, HiDDeN can be used to infer network structure in latent discrete variables, such as those in Bayesian pyramids
(
Gu and Dunson 2023
)
and discrete hierarchical models
(
Kong et al. 2024
)
. This motivates using HiDDeN in a deep generative model for categorical data. As an example, if we start with a variable
j
and its parent variables
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
, we could model the conditional cell counts in the following multi-level structure. Let
\bm{y}_{j}=(y_{j1}\dots,y_{jL})
be latent variables with
y_{jl}\in[k_{y}]
, where the conditional probabilities are of each
y_{jl}
are modeled according to the HiDDeN prior. Sampling the latent variables creates latent counts, say,
\bm{n}_{\bm{y}_{j}}
. From these latent counts, we then generate
x_{j}
via HiDDeN, i.e. we set
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}=\bm{n}_{\bm{y}_{j}}
in our notation. This process effectively creates a latent layer between
\textrm{Pa}(j)
and
j
, in which unobserved nodes
1,\dots,L
characterize the dependence structure. It is straightforward to see how this setup could be extended to multiple layers of latent variables, resulting in a novel multi-layer model for discrete data.

Appendix B
MALA-Within-Gibbs Sampler

In this section, we discuss the technical details for the MALA-within-Gibbs sampler that we propose for HiDDeN. The initial value
\bm{t}_{j}^{(0)}(x_{j})
must be supplied for the algorithm. Too small a value of
\bm{t}_{j}^{(0)}(x_{j})
often leads to low acceptance probabilities. In practice, we find that setting
\bm{t}_{j}^{(0)}(x_{j})\geq 1
avoids this pathology. For subsequent iterations, we generate a candidate
\bm{t}_{j}^{*}(x_{j})
from the current value
\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})
, then accept or reject that candidate. The Langevin proposal is given by

\bm{t}_{j}^{*}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})+\frac{\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})^{2}}{2}\frac{d\log h_{j,x_{j}}(\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j}))}{dt}+\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})z^{(r)}

(18)

where
z^{(r)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and the derivative of
h_{j,x_{j}}
is defined in (
16
). Note that the gradient will depend on the current value of
\bm{u}_{j}
. We will then set
\bm{t}_{j}^{(r+1)}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{*}(x_{j})
with probability

\bm{\kappa}_{j}^{(r+1)}(x_{j})=\min\left\{1,\frac{h_{j,x_{j}}(\bm{t}_{j}^{*}(x_{j}))\psi_{j,x_{j}}(\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})^{(r)};\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})^{*})}{h_{j,x_{j}}(\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j}))\psi_{j,x_{j}}(\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})^{*};\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})^{(r)})}\right\},

where

\psi_{j,x_{j}}(t^{*};t)=\phi\left(t^{*};t+\frac{\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})^{2}}{2}\frac{d\log h_{j,x_{j}}(t)}{dt},\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})^{2}\right).

Otherwise, we set
\bm{t}_{j}^{(r+1)}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})
. Any proposals from (
18
) that violate the positivity constraint on
\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})
will automatically set
\bm{\kappa}_{j}^{(r+1)}=0
and
\bm{t}_{j}^{(r+1)}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})
. Hence, the user should be careful not to set
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})
to be too large, as this choice will likely cause more rejections. We also record the acceptance indicator of the iterate,
\textbf{1}_{\bm{t}_{j}^{(r+1)}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})}
for all
r>0
. The acceptance proportion of the MCMC samples is
(1/R)\sum_{r}\textbf{1}_{\bm{t}_{j}^{(r+1)}(x_{j})=\bm{t}_{j}^{(r)}(x_{j})}
, which is used to select
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}(x_{j})
, e.g., by aiming for the asymptotically optimal rate of
0.574

(
Roberts and Rosenthal 1998
)
. MCMC pathologies are monitored by computing samples from the log-posterior, which has the form

\displaystyle\log f(\bm{t}_{j},\bm{z}_{j}\mid\bm{n})\propto\sum_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\in\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\left\{\log\Gamma(\beta_{j})-\log\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})+\beta_{j})\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{x_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\left\{-b_{j}\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})+(\rho_{j}/k_{j}-1)\log\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j})-K_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\log\Gamma(\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j}))\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{x_{j},\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\log\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})+\bm{t}_{j}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle+\log\Gamma\bigg(\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)}z_{j^{\prime}j}+c_{j}\bigg)+\log\Gamma\bigg(\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\textrm{Ca}(j)}(1-z_{j^{\prime}j})+d_{j}\bigg).

The setup of this simulation study can arise when one wants to evaluate whether there is an association between
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
conditional on
x_{1}
, i.e. testing
H_{0}:x_{2}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp x_{3}\mid x_{1}
. We assume that the dependence between
x_{1}
and
(x_{2},x_{3})
is known to the statistician, such as when
x_{1}
is a set of confounders for two variables. Hence, we propose two possible DAGs,
G_{m}
for
m=1,2
. Both DAGs include the edges
(1,2)
and
(1,3)
, but
G_{2}
also includes the edge
(2,3)
, i.e., an association between
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
conditional on
x_{1}
. For HiDDeN, we evaluate the DAGs by choosing the
G_{m}
that maximizes the posterior probability. Recall that the posterior probability of
G_{m}
is

\Pr(G_{m}\mid\bm{n})=\int_{\bm{t}_{3}}\frac{\Pr(G_{m})f(\bm{n}_{3\mid\textrm{Pa}(3)_{m}}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(3)_{m}},\bm{t}_{3})}{\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{2}\Pr(G_{m^{\prime}})f(\bm{n}_{3\mid\textrm{Pa}(3)_{m^{\prime}}}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(3)_{m^{\prime}}},\bm{t}_{3})}\textrm{d}f(\bm{t}_{3}\mid\bm{n}).

The term in the numerator is the exponentiated BDE of
G_{m}
with
\bm{t}_{j}
as the prior sample size, motivating a comparison to the standard BDE, as well the AIC and BIC, all of which are commonly used as DAG scores
(
Kitson et al. 2023
)
.

For the values of the confounder categories
k_{1}
, we use stepsizes
(.9,.3)
,
(.65,.25)
,
(.45,.15)
,
(.4,.1)

b_{3}=1
and
\rho_{3}=2
, and initializations
\bm{t}_{3}^{(0)}=(1,1)
. These values are constant across the
100
replications. BIC, AIC, and BDE are implemented with the
bnlearn
package and evaluated at both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, and all score hyperparameters are set to their defaults. For the competitors, we set the estimator of
G
to be the
G_{m}
with the highest score, e.g., for BDE this rule is:
\widehat{G}=G_{2}

Berger 1985

Bernardo and Smith 1994
)
, a MAP estimation approach when
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}\sim\textrm{Dir}(1,\dots,1)
a priori, as well as an empirical technique for selecting the hyperparameters in the BDE graph score. We force the constraint that
\sum_{x_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}(x_{j})=1
and the negative log-likelihood for
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}
is

g_{j}(\bm{\omega})=-\sum_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\sum_{x_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\left\{\log\Gamma(\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})+\beta_{j}\bm{\omega}(x_{j}))-\log\Gamma(\beta_{j}\bm{\omega}(x_{j}))\right\},

(19)

where we are interested in minimizing
g_{j}
over all
\bm{\omega}
values in the probability simplex of dimension
k_{j}
, here denoted as
C_{k_{j}}
. Fixing
\beta_{j}
results in the following property for
g_{j}
.

Proposition 1
.

For any
\beta_{j}>0
,
g_{j}(\bm{\omega})
is strictly convex over
C_{k_{j}}
if
\bm{n}_{j}(x_{j})>0
for all
x_{j}\in[k_{j}]
.

Thus, there is a unique MMLE for all choices of
\beta_{j}
, derived from the optimization problem
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\textrm{MMLE}}_{j}=\underset{\bm{\omega}\in C_{k_{j}}}{\textrm{argmin }}g_{j}(\bm{\omega})
. The proof of Proposition
1
, displayed below, utilizes similar results to the proof of Theorem
2
.

In practice, one can solve for
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\textrm{MMLE}}_{j}
across all nodes in
G
and use projected gradient descent. The algorithm iteratively updates
\bm{\omega}
by moving along the gradient of
g_{j}(\bm{\omega})
:
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}^{t+1}=\textrm{proj}_{C_{k_{j}}}(\bm{\omega}^{t}-\eta_{j}\nabla g_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\pi}}^{t}_{j}))
,
where
\eta_{j}>0
and
\textrm{proj}_{C_{k_{j}}}(\bm{z})
is the projection of any
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}}
onto
C_{k_{j}}
. One can project iterates onto the simplex using the technique in
Chen and Ye 2011
. The gradient is evaluated via

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bm{\omega}(x_{j})}=-\beta_{j}\sum_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\left\{\psi(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{2})+\beta_{j}\bm{\omega}(x_{j}))-\psi(\beta_{j}\bm{\omega}(x_{j}))\right\},

(20)

where
\psi(z)=\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
is the digamma function. Since
\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}\Gamma(y+z)/\Gamma(z)=\infty
for all
y>0
,
\eta_{j}
must be sufficiently small in order to avoid a projection in which
\bm{\omega}^{t+1}(x_{j})=0
for some
x_{j}\in[k_{j}]
.

G.2

Selecting
\beta_{j}

The predictive probability of transitioning from
\textrm{Pa}(j)
to any category in
[k_{j}]
is given by

\Pr(x_{j}^{\textrm{new}}=x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}^{\textrm{new}},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j},\beta_{j},\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j})=\lambda_{j}\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}(x_{j})+(1-\lambda_{j})\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}),

(21)

where
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}(x_{j}\mid\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})=\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})/\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})
is the conditional cell probability MLE and
\lambda_{j}=\beta_{j}/\left\{\beta_{j}+\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})\right\}
. This estimator is an extension of the James-Stein estimator for contingency table analysis
(
Hausser and Strimmer 2009
)
to the setting of directed graphical modeling. Hence, one can adapt commonly used Dirichlet hyperparameters associated with Jeffrey’s prior (
\beta_{j}=k_{j}/2
), the Bayes-Laplace uniform prior (
\beta_{j}=k_{j}
), and Perks prior (
\beta_{j}=1
), as in
Hausser and Strimmer 2009
. Observe that both Jeffrey’s prior and the Bayes-Laplace uniform prior will result in more pronounced shrinkage towards
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}
for nodes with a large number of categories. This property may be appealing in this regime; higher
k_{j}
will often result in more sparsity in
\bm{n}_{j,\textrm{Pa}(j)}
. Other possible methods would be to estimate
\beta_{j}
using optimization methods on the marginal likelihood, e.g. the Newton-Raphson algorithm for Dirichlet concentration parameters
(
Ronning 1989
)
, which is often employed for LDA
(
Blei et al. 2003
)
, though this involves fixing
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}
. By alternating between these methods and the projected gradient descent, one can create estimates of
(\bm{\beta}_{j},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j})
.

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

Clearly, for any
x_{j}\neq y_{j}\in[k_{j}]
, we have that

\frac{\partial^{2}g_{j}}{\partial\bm{\omega}(x_{j})\partial\bm{\omega}(y_{j})}=\frac{\partial^{2}g_{j}}{\partial\bm{\omega}(y_{j})\partial\bm{\omega}(x_{j})}=0,

i.e., the Hessian is diagonal. Therefore, convexity is ensured so long as the diagonals of the Hessian are positive. We have that

(22)

for all
x_{j}
. As
\psi^{(1)}(z)
is decreasing for all
z>0
, we have that
\frac{\partial^{2}g_{j}}{\partial\bm{\omega}(x_{j})^{2}}\geq 0
. Observe that (
22
) is positive when
\forall m_{2}\in[k_{2}]\>\exists m_{1}\in[k_{1}]\textrm{ so that }\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})>0

Appendix H
Structure Learning with Marginal Likelihood

H.1

Marginal Likelihood

This section focuses on both deriving and computing
f(\bm{n}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},G)
, the marginal PMF of the contingency table after integrating out
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}
, under the assumption that
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{j}\sim\textrm{Dir}(\alpha_{j},\dots,\alpha_{j})
. The marginal likelihood can be used to compute the Bayes Factor (BF)
(
Kass and Raftery 1995
)
for multiple candidate DAGs. Below, we show that the likelihood contribution is available in a closed form by summing across the discrete auxiliary variables introduced by
Teh et al. 2006
for computation in the HDP.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f_{j}(G)=f(\bm{n}_{j\mid\textrm{Pa}(j)}\mid\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},\beta_{j},\alpha_{j},G)
be the likelihood contribution of any non-root node in
G
. Then,

f_{j}(G)=H_{j}(G)\sum_{\bm{w}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}}\beta_{j}^{w_{j}}\hskip-14.22636pt\prod_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}}\hskip-8.53581pts(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}),\bm{w}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}))|\times\frac{\prod_{x_{j}}\Gamma(\bm{w}_{j}(x_{j})+\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(w_{j}+\alpha k_{j})},

(23)

where
\bm{w}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}=(\bm{w}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}))
are contingency tables such that
1\leq\bm{w}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})\leq\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
for all
\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}\in\chi_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}
and
x_{j}\in[k_{j}]
,
w=\sum_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j}}\bm{w}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)},x_{j})
,
s(c,d)
are the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind, and
H_{j}(G)
is a constant depending on
G
, taking the form

H_{j}(G)=\prod_{\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}}\frac{\Gamma(\beta_{j})}{\Gamma(\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{Pa}(j)})+\beta_{j})}\times\frac{\Gamma(k_{j}\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})^{k_{j}}}.

Theorem
3
shows that we can compute the marginal likelihood of the HiDDeN model exactly by looping over the entries of
\bm{n}_{\textrm{Pa}(j),j}
in parallel for all
j
. This can be straightforward in some cases. For example, in the binary rooted tree, this can be implemented by applying four nested loops through the integers
\bm{n}_{j-1,j}(0,0)
,
\bm{n}_{j-1,j}(0,1)
,
\bm{n}_{j-1,j}(1,0)
, and
\bm{n}_{j-1,j}(1,1)
, since every node has no more than one parent. More general cases greatly increase the computation time of
f_{j}(G)
, motivating the more efficient methods that we propose in Section
4
.
