Spatiotemporal evolution and obstacles of tourism socioecological system sustainability from pressure and resilience perspective in arid regions
Authors: Tian Wang, Zhaoping Yang, Xin Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTourism destinations in arid regions are increasingly facing multifaceted pressures, including intensifying resource and environmental constraints and growing tourism development demands. The sustainability of these destinations largely relies on the collective resilience of ecological, economic, and social systems. This study develops a conceptual framework and an indicator system for assessing the sustainability of Tourism Socioecological Systems (TSES) in arid regions from the perspective of pressure and resilience. Employing the improved TOPSIS method, State Classification Functions, Coupling Coordination Models, and Obstacle Degree Models, this study evaluated the sustainability levels of TSES in Xinjiang from 2010 to 2020. Additionally, the spatiotemporal evolution, coupling coordination relationships, and obstacle factors were analyzed. The results reveal that: (1) The overall sustainability of TSES in Xinjiang improved during the study period. Spatially, sustainability was highest in key tourism-economic cities such as Urumqi and gradually decreased in surrounding areas, with significant regional disparities. (2) The number of regions in a sustainable surplus state increased, while those in a load state decreased, indicating enhanced resilience and reduced pressures. However, improvements in some prefecture-level units, such as Turpan and Hami, remained limited. (3) The coupling coordination dynamics of Xinjiang’s 14 prefectures improved steadily, with most regions transitioning from passive coordination to low/medium-level coordination. However, achieving high-level coordination remains challenging, suggesting the need for further optimization of pressure-resilience interactions. (4) Obstacle factors to sustainability showed significant regional heterogeneity, with spatial occupancy pressure from tourists and population carrying pressure being universal constraints. These findings provide scientific guidance for the sustainable development of TSES in Xinjiang and other arid regions.Keywords: Tourism socioecological system, Pressure-resilience, Sustainability, Arid regions, XinjiangSubject terms: Socioeconomic scenarios, Sustainability

Introduction

The social-ecological system (SES) provides a comprehensive scientific framework for understanding the complex processes and interactions between natural and human systems1. As an extension of this framework in the field of tourism research, the tourism social-ecological system (TSES) breaks through the limitations of traditional single-factor analysis, emphasizing the coupling of social, economic, and ecological subsystems within tourism destinations2. Numerous studies have shown that the TSES framework offers unique advantages in researching destination vulnerability, safety, and resilience3,4. The application of TSES is particularly critical in arid regions5. Arid zones, covering approximately 35% of the global land area, constitute a critical component of terrestrial ecosystems and support the socio-economic activities of nearly one-third of the global population6. Characterized by their unique natural-human landscape combinations, the development trends and significance of arid zones have become increasingly prominent. These regions not only serve as a core driver for socio-economic development but also represent an important pathway for reconciling economic growth with environmental protection7,8. However, unlike general TSES, TSES in arid regions exhibits more complex and dynamic characteristics. Arid zone TSES faces disturbances and pressures from tourism development drivers as well as natural environmental and social crises. At the same time, due to water scarcity, fragile ecosystems, and often underdeveloped socioeconomic foundations, these systems exhibit higher inherent vulnerability7,9. This characteristic makes it difficult for the system’s internal resilience to effectively respond to external pressures, thereby threatening its long-term sustainability5. Consequently, sustainable management of arid zone TSES urgently requires moving beyond traditional empirical models and establishing a scientific foundation. Accurately assessing the sustainability levels and states of arid zone TSES and analyzing their internal interaction mechanisms can provide a scientific basis for sustainable management, as well as serve as a necessary prerequisite for formulating sustainable development strategies and promoting effective sustainability governance.

Scientific assessment of the sustainability of TSES in arid regions is a prerequisite for analyzing the sustainability levels and mechanisms of TSES. It is of critical significance for formulating sustainable development strategies and mitigating conflicts between development and conservation. In terms of assessment frameworks, scholars have made progress by drawing on various theories, including the adaptive cycle theory1, complex systems theory10, the human–environment regional system11, the tourism destination life cycle theory12,13, and resilience theory14. For example, based on the theoretical perspective of the human–environment regional system, an assessment framework was constructed from the perspectives of stakeholders in protected areas and different environmental system elements to evaluate the sustainability level of tourism destinations in protected areas15. Arzoumanidis drew on the tourism destination life cycle theory to develop a sustainability assessment framework for tourism destinations, emphasizing that the assessment of destination sustainability should be closely linked to factors such as energy and water at various stages of destination development12. Liu proposed an adaptive cycle-based framework for assessing the sustainability of oasis tourism in arid regions, analyzing the stages of system evolution and quantifying the relationship between system evolution and its scale suitability1. Notably, the introduction of the DPSIR (Driving Force-Pressure-State-Impact-Response) framework provided a new perspective for understanding external pressures and system responses, and its integration with the regional system theory of human–environment relationships facilitated a shift from static assessment to dynamic simulation4,16.

In terms of methods for assessing the sustainability of TSES, approaches such as spatial mapping experiments17, tourism ecological footprint models13, system dynamics18, and indicator systems19 have been widely used. Among these, the indicator system method has become one of the most common and widely used approaches due to its ability to integrate multiple indicators and its high operability20–22. The selection of indicators is a critical step in this method, directly influencing the scientific validity and practical utility of the assessment results. In existing studies, TSES sustainability evaluation indicators generally encompass economic, institutional, environmental, and cultural dimensions23–25, and are primarily categorized into single-dimension and multi-dimension indicators, demonstrating an evolution from single-dimensional to systematic multi-dimensional assessments. Single-dimension indicators focus on the critical role of a specific element in sustainability. For example, in a study on community tourism sustainability in Spain, scholars used participatory research to highlight the importance of stakeholder engagement in the sustainability of tourism destinations, thus constructing sustainability evaluation indicators based on the willingness of local groups such as women26. On the other hand, multi-dimension indicators place greater emphasis on the overall sustainability of the system. For instance, a study on the sustainability evaluation of water conservancy scenic areas in China developed a multi-dimensional indicator system from the perspectives of ecological environment, facility conditions, and management, assessing the sustainability levels of 83 national water conservancy scenic areas27.

While existing research has made important advances in assessing TSES sustainability, several key limitations remain. First, most current studies focus on static evaluations of system components, without properly accounting for how resilience responses dynamically affect sustainability under multiple pressures. Second, existing indicator systems often fail to include region-specific factors—particularly for unique areas like arid zones—missing critical local pressure factors and resilience indicators that could reveal sustainability weaknesses. Developing evaluation indicators specifically for arid region TSES is therefore both academically valuable and practically significant. Additionally, most studies simply calculate sustainability levels without further analyzing system states, coordination relationships, or specific obstacles. This significantly limits how useful the results are for decision-making. To address these research gaps, this study takes Xinjiang, China as a representative arid region case study, innovatively constructing a pressure-resilience interaction framework for TSES sustainability assessment in arid areas. The research systematically reveals the sustainability characteristics of arid TSES through a comprehensive approach, ultimately promoting sustainable development. Specifically, the study includes: First, theoretically deconstructing the sustainability mechanism of TSES under pressure-resilience interactions, clarifying how their dynamic interplay determines system state evolution, thereby establishing the theoretical foundation. Building on this framework, the study then develops a novel dual-dimensional (pressure-resilience) indicator system, employing an improved TOPSIS model to quantify indicator weights and composite sustainability levels for scientific assessment. Building upon this foundation, our study further examines the sustainability characteristics of arid TSES through three integrated analytical components. First, we systematically analyze spatiotemporal evolution patterns to identify regional disparities and dynamic trends across Xinjiang’s 14 prefectures from 2010 to 2020. Second, employing state classification methodology, we categorize systems into three distinct states—surplus and overloaded—based on quantitative assessments, thereby achieving an in-depth qualitative interpretation of sustainability. Third, we utilize coupling coordination modeling to quantitatively characterize the interactive coordination between pressure and resilience dimensions, revealing the system’s intrinsic operational mechanisms. Finally, the research culminates with obstacle degree modeling to precisely identify key limiting factors, distinguishing between pressure-side constraints and resilience-side deficiencies, which informs targeted strategy recommendations for sustainability enhancement. This comprehensive study follows a rigorous logical progression from theoretical framework construction to empirical analysis and finally to policy recommendations, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The findings not only provide an empirical basis for sustainable development of Xinjiang’s TSES but also contribute a novel analytical framework and methodological approach for tourism social-ecological system research in arid regions globally.

Methodology and data

Evaluation of TSES sustainability

Conceptual framework for TSES sustainability

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development explicitly highlights the importance of promoting sustainable tourism through SDG 8.9 and 12.b28, making destination sustainability a critical component in attaining these global objectives. As complex adaptive systems, TSES encompass dynamic interactions between human activities and natural environments29. Ostrom’s foundational framework for analyzing social-ecological system sustainability revealed that its essence lies in complex interactions and feedback mechanisms among system components, establishing crucial theoretical groundwork for subsequent research30. While existing studies demonstrate that sustainable tourism development requires balanced consideration of environmental protection, social equity, and cultural preservation, understanding the dynamic human–environment interaction processes remains paramount31. Building on these foundations, this study conceptualizes TSES sustainability as a system’s capacity to achieve long-term stability and prosperity through dynamic equilibrium between pressures and resilience across ecological, social, economic, and cultural dimensions11,20,32. This definition both incorporates the core principles of social-ecological systems theory and emphasizes the distinctive dynamic balance characteristics of tourism systems, where sustainability emerges from the ongoing interaction between stressors and adaptive capacities. The pressure-resilience perspective offers particular analytical value by recognizing that sustainability outcomes are jointly determined by external disturbances and internal response capacities. In tourism contexts, pressure subsystems quantify the intensity of impacts from factors like climate change and visitor overload on multiple system dimensions, while resilience subsystems capture the system’s ability to maintain functionality through resistance, adaptation, or transformation33,34. This approach not only enables comprehensive assessment of tourism impacts and destination responses but also permits diagnostic evaluation of system states through analyzing pressure-resilience coupling coordination, thereby generating actionable insights for sustainable management. Particularly in ecologically vulnerable arid regions, this approach effectively reveals dynamic balance mechanisms between pressures and resilience, offering precise intervention pathways for sustainable development that address the urgent need to reconcile tourism growth with ecological constraints5.

The theory of pressure originates from ecology and environmental science, describing the processes and consequences of external disturbances on systems. In TSES, pressure refers to the internal and external negative forces exerted on the system during tourism development, which directly impact ecological, social, economic, and cultural dimensions. The sources of pressure are complex and diverse, including climate change, environmental pollution, tourist overcrowding, community conflicts, economic fluctuations, and cultural erosion35,36. These pressures can be broadly categorized into ecological pressures, social activity pressures, and infrastructure pressures. Ecological pressures involve the impact of environmental degradation on tourism resources and environments, which is particularly severe in ecologically fragile arid regions. Human activities, especially overtourism, resource overexploitation, and infrastructure overload, often exert significant pressure on ecological and social structures. These pressures alter the system’s resource utilization patterns, social structures, and economic models, leading to negative state changes such as ecological degradation, social conflicts, economic decline, and cultural loss. The accumulation of pressure may exceed the system’s threshold, threatening its stability and sustainability.

TSES resilience captures the system’s capability to sustain its core functions, structures, and identity in the presence of internal and external challenges, disturbances, or shocks, and to promote sustainable development through adaptation or transformation33,37. The core of this concept lies in the system’s dynamic response mechanisms to uncertainty. Building upon the dimensional understandings from both ecological resilience and social-ecological systems resilience theories38,39, while incorporating insights from tourism social-ecological system resilience research, the resilience response process consists of four stages—resistance, recovery, adaptation, and renewal—each reflecting different capacities and manifesting at different stages of pressure impact38,40,41. In TSES, resilience ensures that the system can return to its original state or transition to a new stable state after disturbances, or even achieve higher levels of sustainable development through fundamental changes, by coordinating the interactions of ecological, social, economic, and cultural subsystems.

As shown in Fig. 2, TSES pressure and resilience are interdependent, and their dynamic interactions shape sustainability42,43. The relative strength of their interplay can even influence the system’s state and future. As a multidimensional composite system integrating ecological, social, economic, and cultural elements, TSES sustainability requires attention to the roles and connections of multiple dimensions in pressure effects and resilience responses32. Pressure can destabilize the system and disrupt its functional structure, while resilience enables the system to respond to these disruptions. Under low to moderate pressure, resilience can effectively regulte and restore the system to its normal state, maintaining ecological, social, and economic balance. However, excessive pressure may exceed the threshold of resilience, leading to system imbalance or collapse44. For example, in arid regions, ecological pressures such as water scarcity and land desertification pose severe threats to TSES45. If not addressed through measures like optimized water resource management and vegetation restoration, these pressures may lead to ecosystem collapse, tourism decline, and socio-economic instability, thereby undermining the system’s health and sustainability. The interaction between pressure and resilience indicates that TSES sustainability does not rely on a single factor but depends on their dynamic balance and interplay, which collectively determine the system’s future.

The evaluation indicator system for TSES sustainability

Guided by the aforementioned conceptual framework and fully incorporating the distinctive features of arid regions, such as vulnerable ecosystems, limited water resources, and abundant cultural assets, this study adheres to the principles of objectivity and feasibility to construct a sustainability evaluation index system for the TSES in arid areas. This system approaches the sustainability evaluation of TSES from the perspectives of pressure and resilience, dividing it into a pressure subsystem and a resilience subsystem, which collectively consist of 7 criterion layers and 32 indicator layers. Evaluation indicator system for TSES sustainability in Xinjiang.

The pressure dimension of the TSES involves multiple factors. This study selects three aspects from natural and social pressures: ecological environmental pressure, tourism social pressure, and tourism facility pressure46–48. The focus on ecological environmental pressure arises from the vulnerability of arid region ecosystems, which are highly susceptible to external disruptions like pollution, vegetation loss, and water scarcity, all of which directly affect the sustainability of tourism development. Specific indicators include air pollution levels, vegetation coverage change rate, ecological environmental quality, and water resource pressure. These indicators respectively reflect the status of air quality, the health of the ecosystem, and the pressure exerted by water resources as a critical limiting factor in arid regions on the TSES.

Tourism social pressure reflects the impact of tourism activities on local social structures and residents’ livelihoods, revealing the stability and coordinated development capacity of the system under population load, tourist disturbances, and social space competition. Population load pressure reflects the distribution of population per unit area, indicating the carrying capacity of the regional social system under population growth, and is a key parameter for measuring social pressure. Tourist disturbance pressure quantifies the degree of interference of tourism activities on residents’ daily lives, directly reflecting the balance between tourism development and social harmony. Tourist space occupancy pressure measures the extent to which tourists occupy public spaces and resources, highlighting the competitive pressure of tourism development on the allocation of social space resources. The tourist growth threat pressure indicator captures the potential risks of rapid growth in tourist populations.

Regarding tourism facility pressure, indicators most closely related to tourism development, such as transportation, accommodation, and sanitation services, were selected. The tourism transportation pressure index was chosen as an indicator reflecting the intensity of transportation facility usage, revealing the potential pressure of rapid tourism development on the transportation network. Tourism accommodation pressure reflects the intensity of tourists’ demand for accommodation facilities, revealing the pressure of tourism development on such facilities. Tourism sanitation facility pressure reflects the pressure of tourism activities on public service capacity, which is a critical factor in ensuring the social stability of tourist destinations.

The resilience subsystem in the indicator system includes resistance capacity, recovery capacity, adaptation capacity, and renewal capacity, with indicators covering ecological, economic, social, and cultural elements within the TSES49,50.

Resistance capacity refers to the system’s ability to withstand external destructive factors. Water resources are the foundation for maintaining ecosystems and socio-economic activities, especially in arid regions, where water availability directly impacts ecological health and sustainable tourism development. Vegetation health and biodiversity reflect the stability and disturbance resistance of ecosystems. Economic development levels and domestic tourism income indicate local economic strength and tourism scale, serving as the economic and industrial foundation for resisting pressure disturbances. The richness of scenic and cultural resources reflects resource endowment conditions, demonstrating the diversity and attractiveness required for resistance capacity.

Recovery capacity refers to the TSES’s ability to restore its functions and structure after external disturbances. Desertification is a prominent environmental challenge in arid regions, and desertification control efficiency reflects the system’s ability to recover from environmental issues. Forest health management demonstrates the system’s capacity to restore forest ecosystems. Environmental governance investment levels reflect financial support for recovery. Well-developed tourism facilities can quickly meet tourist demands after pressure events, helping the tourism system regain vitality. Sufficient transportation infrastructure facilitates the rapid flow and dispersion of tourists, ensuring the recovery and development of tourism activities. Investment in cultural initiatives promotes the protection and utilization of cultural resources, providing spiritual motivation and economic support for system recovery.

Adaptation capacity refers to the TSES’s ability to proactively adjust its structure and functions to respond to external environmental changes. Environmental greening capacity reflects the system’s ability to cope with climate change and ecological degradation. More advanced industrial structures and diversified economies enhance the system’s adaptability and stability to external shocks. Sufficient industrial investment supports the development of eco-friendly industries, promoting the coordinated adaptation of socio-economic activities and the environment. High-quality human resources meet diverse tourism demands, enhancing the innovation capacity and market adaptability of the tourism industry.

Renewal capacity refers to the TSES’s ability to enhance its development potential and competitiveness through innovation and resource renewal. Renewal capacity is measured through talent, technology, and digitalization, showcasing the system’s innovation potential and modernization level, which are key guarantees for continuous renewal. Sufficient reserves of tourism professionals provide intellectual support for innovation development and service quality improvement. Investment in technological innovation drives the development of smart tourism and ecological protection technologies, providing technological momentum for system renewal. Digitalization levels reflect the tourism industry’s digital transformation capability, enhancing market competitiveness and operational efficiency through technological means.

State classification

The resilience/pressure ratio intuitively reflects the sustainability level of the system. The TSES sustainability index can be divided into three scenarios: TSES sustainability = 1, TSES sustainability > 1, and TSES sustainability < 1. When resilience/pressure = 1, it indicates that the system’s resilience precisely offsets the pressure, and the system is in a critical equilibrium state. In this state, the system can maintain basic functions, but its buffer capacity against external disturbances is weak. Any additional pressure may lead to system imbalance. When resilience/pressure > 1, it indicates that the system’s resilience exceeds the pressure, and the system has strong adaptability and carrying capacity, belonging to a surplus state. In this state, the system can effectively respond to external disturbances and support long-term sustainable development. When resilience/pressure < 1, it indicates that the system’s resilience is insufficient to cope with the pressure, and the system may be in a loaded state. In this state, the pressure on the system exceeds its resilience capacity, potentially leading to functional degradation, resource depletion, or unsustainable development. Referring to the study by41, we further classify the TSES sustainability state using three threshold lines, as shown in Fig. 3. These thresholds provide a clearer framework for assessing the system’s sustainability status and guiding targeted interventions to enhance resilience or reduce pressure, ensuring the long-term viability of the TSES.

Coupling coordination degree model

The Coupling Coordination Degree Model is a quantitative model for measuring and analyzing interactions and coordination between different systems, widely applied in multiple fields65–67. Coupling degree is an indicator to measure the closeness of coupling between two subsystems, reflecting their interdependence68. However, the coupling degree model only reflects the intensity and direction of interactions between systems, essentially comparing their consistency. Coupling coordination degree not only considers the tightness of coupling but also evaluates whether systems can maintain coordinated states, typically used to assess overall sustainability and stability. This study employs the Coupling Coordination Degree Model (CCDM) as an advanced analytical step following initial evaluation and state classification, to deeply examine the coupled coordination relationship between sustainability pressures and resilience. This approach allows for deconstructing the intrinsic characteristics of sustainability, moving beyond the binary question of 'whether it is sustainable or not’ to better address the underlying mechanisms of 'why it is sustainable or unsustainable. Study area

Situated in the northwestern part of China, Xinjiang covers an expansive area of around 1.665 million square kilometers, acting as a crucial access point to China’s northwest and a strategic center for the “Belt and Road” initiative72 (Fig. 4). Thanks to its unique geographical environment and profound historical and cultural heritage, Xinjiang boasts abundant natural tourism resources, including deserts, mountains, lakes, and glaciers, as well as cultural tourism resources characterized by the Silk Road and multi-ethnic traditions73. Statistics from the Xinjiang Culture and Tourism Department reveal that in 2024, Xinjiang attracted 302 million tourists, contributing 359.542 billion yuan in tourism revenue, which accounted for approximately 17.51% of its GDP. Tourism has become a pillar industry for its socio-economic development. As a typical arid region, Xinjiang’s tourism development currently faces dual challenges of fragile ecosystems and regional development imbalances74. The unique natural conditions of arid regions, such as water scarcity, variable climate, and low vegetation coverage, make ecosystems particularly sensitive to external disturbances. At the same time, the rapid development of tourism has intensified resource consumption and environmental pressure, further highlighting the vulnerability of TSES in arid regions. With increasing pressures, the adaptation process of Xinjiang’s TSES is undergoing changes, and the demand for sustainable development has become a critical issue. To overcome these challenges, it is essential to enhance the resilience of Xinjiang’s TSES by focusing on ecological protection, resource management, and balanced regional development. Strategies such as promoting eco-friendly tourism practices, investing in sustainable infrastructure, and fostering community participation can help mitigate environmental pressures and ensure the long-term sustainability of tourism in Xinjiang. Furthermore, utilizing technological innovation and fostering digital transformation can enhance the tourism sector’s productivity and competitiveness, amplifying its impact on the region’s socio-economic growth.

Data sources

The study spans the period from 2010 to 2020, incorporating a comprehensive dataset that includes statistical data, natural geographic data, and socio-economic data. Statistical information was collected from the Xinjiang Statistical Yearbook and the statistical yearbooks and bulletins of prefecture-level administrative units. Ecological and environmental data were sourced from the Resource and Environmental Science and Data Center, affiliated with the Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://www.resdc.cn/Default.aspx). Habitat quality data were acquired from the National Earth System Science Data Center (http://www.geodata.cn). PM2.5 concentration data were derived from the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group at Washington University in St. Louis (https://sites.wustl.edu/acag/datasets/surface-pm2-5/). Data on national key cultural heritage sites and historic villages were obtained from the National Cultural Heritage Administration’s comprehensive management platform (https://gl.ncha.gov.cn/#/public-service). Vector boundary data of Xinjiang were downloaded from the National Standard Map Service website (https://bzdt.ch.mnr.gov.cn/). Missing values for certain years were addressed using the multiple imputation method.

Results

Spatiotemporal evolution of TSES sustainability in Xinjiang

Temporal evolution trends

As shown in Fig. 5, the sustainability level of the TSES across Xinjiang’s 14 prefecture-level administrative units exhibited significant annual variations from 2010 to 2020. Overall, the sustainability level of Xinjiang’s TSES demonstrated a clear upward trend during the study period, although this process was nonlinear and accompanied by periodic fluctuations. Specifically, the period from 2011 to 2017 marked a phase of steady growth, followed by fluctuations after 2018 and a notable decline in 2019. Despite this, the sustainability level in 2020 remained significantly higher than the baseline of 2010, as confirmed by the annual averages across prefecture-level units, reflecting overall progress in TSES sustainability over the decade. The evolution of TSES sustainability levels across prefecture-level units exhibited significant differences. Based on trend characteristics, these units can be categorized into three types: continuous growth type, fluctuating type, and declining type. The continuous growth type is represented by Ili and Altay, where sustainability levels showed significant improvement from 2010 to 2020, particularly between 2010 and 2016. The fluctuating type includes cities such as Urumqi and Turpan, where sustainability levels exhibited multi-peak fluctuations. The declining type is exemplified by Kizilsu Kirghiz Autonomous Prefecture, which experienced a brief rise from 2013 to 2016 but saw a continuous decline after 2017, returning to near 2010 levels by 2020. Notably, 2017 marked the peak year for sustainability levels in most prefecture-level units, while 2019 saw a widespread decline.

Temporal evolution of TSES sustainability levels in Xinjiang from 2010 to 2020.

A deeper examination of the temporal dynamics of the TSES Pressure Index (PI) and Resilience Index (RI) subsystems (Fig. 6) indicates that between 2010 and 2020, the overall pressure on Xinjiang’s TSES declined, whereas resilience showed steady enhancement. Specifically, during the period from 2010 to 2015, the system experienced high pressure, with many prefecture-level units reaching peak pressure levels that generally exceeded resilience levels, indicating significant stress on the TSES. After 2016, pressure levels in most prefecture-level units declined significantly, while resilience levels began to rise steadily. By comparing the temporal changes in pressure and resilience indicators within the same prefecture-level units, the study further elucidates the interaction mechanisms between the two subsystems and their impact on sustainability. The findings reveal distinct pressure-resilience evolution patterns across different units: for example, Urumqi and Ili exhibit a “pressure decline-resilience rise” pattern, reflecting a virtuous development trend. Taking Ili as an example, in 2010, its pressure level was 0.339, and its resilience level was 0.386, indicating a relatively balanced state. After 2016, pressure levels continued to decline, while resilience levels improved significantly, forming a virtuous cycle. In contrast, Turpan remained in a fragile state of “high pressure-low resilience” for an extended period, while regions such as Hotan displayed a “low pressure-low resilience” development pattern. These differentiated patterns highlight significant gaps in pressure management and resilience-building among prefecture-level units. In some regions, the improvement in resilience lagged behind the decline in pressure, leaving the system exposed to substantial sustainability risks.

TSES pressure, resilience, and sustainability levels in Xinjiang’s 14 prefecture-level units from 2010 to 2020.

Spatial differentiation characteristics

Based on the assessment results of TSES sustainability levels across 14 prefecture-level administrative units in Xinjiang from 2010 to 2020, these units were categorized into high, medium, and low sustainability levels. The study reveals significant spatial heterogeneity in Xinjiang’s TSES sustainability (Fig. 7). In 2010, the overall sustainability level in Xinjiang was relatively low, but a preliminary spatial pattern had already emerged. High sustainability regions were primarily concentrated in Urumqi, Ili, and Altay, which held advantageous positions due to their status as economic hubs and abundant tourism resources. Medium sustainability regions were widely distributed across most parts of Xinjiang, particularly in Bayingolin and Aksu in southern Xinjiang. Low sustainability regions were mainly located in Turpan City and Hami City. Over time, the number and coverage of high sustainability regions gradually expanded. By 2015, Urumqi, Changji, and Ili Prefecture had further consolidated their high sustainability status, while Tacheng and Bortala also entered the high-level category. Northern Xinjiang formed a diffusion pattern centered around Urumqi, while some prefecture-level units in southern Xinjiang, such as Bayingolin and Hotan, also showed continuous improvement. However, a temporary fluctuation occurred in 2019, with a reduction in high sustainability regions and an overall decline in sustainability levels. Nevertheless, core high sustainability regions remained concentrated in economic and tourism hubs such as Urumqi and Ili. Overall, the spatial pattern of Xinjiang’s TSES sustainability is centered around the capital city of Urumqi and the tourism-economic core areas of northern Xinjiang, exhibiting a trend of diffusion from the core areas to the periphery. Most regions in northern Xinjiang gradually entered the high sustainability level, while some tourism core areas in southern Xinjiang also showed improvement. Despite this, significant disparities between regions persist, indicating that the sustainable development of Xinjiang’s TSES continues to face challenges of imbalance.

Spatial distribution of TSES sustainability in Xinjiang from 2010 to 2020.

State classification of TSES sustainability driven by pressure-resilience dynamics

According to the state classification, Fig. 8 delineates the distribution and progression of TSES sustainability states in 14 prefecture-level administrative units in Xinjiang between 2010 and 2020. The number of cities in a surplus state expanded from 4 to 11, while the number of cities in an overloaded state contracted from 10 to 3. This indicates significant progress in enhancing resilience and alleviating pressure within Xinjiang’s TSES. Notably, around 2014, the number of cities in a surplus state surpassed those in an overloaded state for the first time. After 2015, the number of cities in a surplus state stabilized at a relatively high level. Examining specific cities, some exhibited clear positive trends from 2010 to 2020. For instance, Urumqi transitioned from a critical state in 2010 to a stable surplus state by 2020. Similarly, Kashgar gradually shifted from an initial overloaded state to a surplus state, achieving significant sustainability improvements. Other cities, such as Aksu and Changji, also showed similar positive trends, transitioning from overloaded to surplus states. Some cities maintained relatively stable states with minimal fluctuations during the study period. For example, Ili Prefecture has consistently remained in a surplus state since 2010, highlighting the high stability and resilience of its TSES. Tacheng experienced relatively mild state changes, remaining close to a critical balance state in most years without significant improvement or deterioration. However, a few cities exhibited unfavorable trends. For instance, Turpan, despite some alleviation of pressure, remained primarily in an overloaded state and failed to achieve a surplus state. In summary, from 2010 to 2020, Xinjiang’s TSES underwent significant transformation and optimization, with most cities achieving positive state transitions and a continuous increase in the number of cities in a surplus state. Nevertheless, limited improvements or even adverse changes in some cities indicate that, despite overall regional system improvements, specific areas still face persistent challenges and state fluctuations. Targeted attention is needed to ensure balanced development across the entire region.

Coupling relationships between pressure and resilience in sustaining TSES

From 2010 to 2020, the TSES coupling coordination degree across Xinjiang’s 14 prefectures and cities showed an overall gradual upward trend. This intuitively reflects the regulatory role of the dynamic interaction between the pressure subsystem and the resilience subsystem on overall sustainability. As shown in Fig. 9, an increasing number of regions transitioned from unilateral coordination or low-level coordination to intermediate-level coordination. Before 2014, the TSES coupling relationship in Xinjiang was primarily characterized by unilateral and low-level coordination. Urumqi, Ili, Aksu, and Kashgar exhibited relatively higher coupling coordination levels compared to other regions during this period. Low-coordination areas still dominated, indicating weak overall system coordination. This spatial differentiation suggests the significant influence of regional development foundations on systemic coordination capacity. In 2015, Urumqi’s coupling coordination level surpassed 0.7, moving from low-level to intermediate-level coordination. This marked the first entry of the pressure-resilience system into a positive feedback phase. Concurrently, the extent of low-level coordination areas expanded across Xinjiang, reflecting a general improvement in coupling coordination. Between 2018 and 2019, all prefectures and cities except Bortala and Kizilsu transitioned from unilateral coordination to low-level coordination. This signified the enhanced role of improved system resilience in promoting sustainability. By 2020, Urumqi and Ili maintained intermediate-level coordination, continuing to lead the region. Kashgar, Hotan, Aksu, Hami, and Turpan gradually entered the low-level coordination stage. However, Altay, Tacheng, and Bayingolin remained in a state of unilateral coordination, highlighting persistent disparities in systemic coordination between regions. Notably, as of 2020, no prefecture or city in Xinjiang had achieved high-level coordination. This indicates significant room for improvement in Xinjiang’s TSES coordination level and further confirms that neither unilateral pressure control nor resilience enhancement alone can achieve deep sustainability; synergistic evolution of both is essential. Overall, while Xinjiang’s TSES coupling coordination degree exhibited a steady upward trend over the past decade, with multiple regions achieving leaps to higher coordination levels, regional disparities persist. Achieving high-level coordination remains a future objective.

Obstacle factors to TSES sustainability enhancement

Based on the principle of cumulative obstacle degree exceeding 50%, significant regional differences and common characteristics were identified among the key obstacle factors (Fig. 10a). Tourism space occupancy pressure emerged as the most prevalent obstacle factor across all cities, particularly in tourism-intensive regions such as Changji and Turpan, where its obstacle degree was significantly higher than other factors. Population load pressure was another widely observed major obstacle, ranking high in cumulative obstacle degree in 12 prefecture-level cities, especially in tourism hotspots like Ili and Turpan. In northern Xinjiang, the primary obstacle factors were concentrated in traffic pressure, tourism space occupancy pressure, and population load pressure. For instance, in Urumqi, traffic pressure (60.47%) far exceeded other factors, highlighting the strain on infrastructure in this key transportation hub and major tourist destination. In Karamay, tourism reception facility pressure (24.46%) and tourism space occupancy pressure (22.30%) dominated, reflecting the tension between reception capacity and resource development intensity. In contrast, southern Xinjiang faced different challenges, primarily centered on tourism space occupancy pressure and tourism sanitation facility pressure. In Kashgar and Hotan, tourism space occupancy pressure accounted for 27.53% and 23.50%, respectively, making it the top obstacle factor. Tourism sanitation facility pressure ranked third in these regions, indicating an urgent need for infrastructure improvement. Kizilsu also faced tourism space occupancy pressure and population load pressure, with infrastructure deficiencies being more severe compared to northern Xinjiang.

Major obstacle factors of TSES sustainability (a) obstacle factors in the pressure layer (b) Obstacle Factors in the Resilience.

In the resilience subsystem, tourism technology innovation investment level was the most prevalent obstacle factor (Fig. 10b). Core regions in northern Xinjiang, such as Urumqi (23.89%), Ili (26.52%), and Changji (23.34%), exhibited significant gaps in technological investment. In southern Xinjiang, tourism transportation infrastructure capacity was a critical obstacle, with obstacle degrees of 25.66% and 13.55% in Kashgar and Turpan, respectively, highlighting the impact of lagging infrastructure on resilience. Additionally, tourism resource richness emerged as a key obstacle factor in multiple cities, such as Karamay (15.80%) and Kashgar (17.15%). In northern Xinjiang, including Urumqi, Karamay, and Ili, resilience obstacles were primarily concentrated in tourism technology innovation investment level and tourism resource richness. In contrast, southern Xinjiang regions such as Kashgar, Hotan, and Kizilsu faced major obstacles in tourism transportation infrastructure capacity and tourism talent reserves, reflecting significant lags in infrastructure development and human resource allocation in these areas. In summary, while tourism space occupancy pressure and population load pressure were common obstacles across Xinjiang, regional disparities were evident in the specific challenges faced by northern and southern Xinjiang. Addressing these obstacles requires targeted strategies tailored to the unique characteristics and needs of each region to enhance the overall sustainability and resilience of Xinjiang’s TSES.

Discussion and policy recommendations

Discussion

Proposal of a sustainability evaluation framework for TSES from the pressure-resilience perspective

This study develops a dual-dimensional pressure-resilience evaluation index system, achieving theoretical, methodological, and regional innovations. Unlike traditional sustainability assessment models constrained by unidirectional pressure-response logic4,16, the proposed framework integrates the dynamic interactions between the pressure subsystem and resilience subsystem. The pressure subsystem quantifies tourism-induced disturbances in arid regions through three dimensions: ecological-environmental pressure, tourism-social pressure, and tourism-infrastructure pressure. Conversely, the resilience subsystem captures the system’s capacity to resist, recover, adapt, and transform under stress, thereby revealing how their dynamic interplay drives TSES evolution. To address the overreliance on universal indicators in existing studies42, the indicator design prioritizes region-specific characteristics of arid zones, including ecological fragility, water resource constraints, and cultural resource endowments75,76. Key ecological indicators such as water resource carrying capacity and desertification degree directly measure environmental thresholds critical to TSES sustainability. Socio-cultural dimensions are integrated through metrics like cultural heritage inventory and management, which assesses systematic preservation of intangible assets, and fiscal expenditure on cultural public services, reflecting governmental efforts to enhance community-based cultural resilience. These tailored indicators not only improve analytical precision but also offer scalable methodologies for TSES studies in similar ecosystems. Methodologically, an improved TOPSIS model is employed to objectively evaluate subsystem heterogeneity, combined with an obstacle degree model to diagnose sustainability barriers. This approach transcends conventional evaluation paradigms, providing a robust toolkit for policymakers to prioritize interventions in arid TSES.

Spatiotemporal evolution and coupling relationships

From 2010 to 2020, the sustainability level of TSES significantly improved, particularly during the continuous growth phase from 2010 to 2016, reflecting notable policy achievements and practical outcomes in the field of tourism sustainability8,77. In recent years, driven by national policies such as the “Belt and Road” Initiative and regional strategies like “Tourism Enriches the People” and “Tourism Revitalizes Xinjiang,” the region has received strong policy support and resource investments, further promoting the enhancement of TSES sustainability74. However, the study found that Xinjiang’s sustainability level declined in 2019, primarily due to the disruptive impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on tourism and socio-economic activities78. As Adams, Choe79 pointed out, the sustainability of TSES is vulnerable to external shocks, a conclusion validated by the decline in Xinjiang’s sustainability in 2019. This reaffirms the critical interaction between external pressures and sustainability, emphasizing the necessity of incorporating disturbance-induced pressure factors into TSES assessments80.

The study revealed significant spatial heterogeneity in the sustainability of Xinjiang’s TSES, with the number of high-sustainability regions gradually increasing over time. Urumqi, as the economic and tourism hub of Xinjiang, maintained a high level of sustainability due to its strategic location, advanced infrastructure, and mature tourism market. The notable improvements in Ili and Altay reflected the developmental advantages of northern Xinjiang, where abundant tourism resources and a stronger economic base better supported TSES sustainability. These findings align with the research of Yang, which highlighted the significant sustainability improvements in Urumqi and Ili, attributed to their economic centrality and infrastructure investments74. In contrast, southern Xinjiang regions such as Bayingolin and Aksu progressed more slowly, constrained by historical economic disparities and underdeveloped tourism resources. This spatial imbalance underscores the need for targeted policies and investments to bridge regional gaps, echoing Yuan’s observations on how resource endowments and economic inequalities influence tourism sustainability19.

The coupling coordination relationship between pressure and resilience further reveals the deeper dynamics of TSES sustainability, particularly in analyzing how pressure and resilience interact to maintain sustainability. Urumqi, Ili, and Changji exhibit “dual improvement” characteristics, achieving enhanced coupling coordination (progressively reaching moderate coordination) alongside increased resilience levels. This synergistic effect stems from their economic advantages, well-developed tourism infrastructure, and effective policy implementation77, enabling them to alleviate pressure while strengthening adaptive capacity. In contrast, southern Xinjiang regions like Kizilsu Kirghiz Autonomous Prefecture and Hotan present a distinct disparity: despite improved coupling coordination, they remain trapped in a “low resilience-high pressure” dilemma. Local socioeconomic development lags behind, compounded by ecological constraints81. This mismatch highlights the urgent need for resilience-oriented interventions to reconcile systemic vulnerabilities with sustainable development goals. Notably, although Xinjiang’s TSES pressure-resilience relationship has trended toward greater coordination over the decade, the overall level remains suboptimal. This underscores persistent challenges in arid-region TSES—particularly mounting ecological pressures and socioeconomic development delays constraining resilience enhancement. Wang and Cao’s research has confirmed this issue’s critical impact on arid-region tourism development8,48.

Policy recommendations

Strategies for enhancing system resilience

This study finds that the sustainability level of TSES is vulnerable to external disturbances. Therefore, enhancing the system’s capacity to resist interference and respond effectively is a critical pathway to achieving sustainable development. First, improving the risk resilience of key infrastructure such as transportation, communications, healthcare, and emergency facilities is essential to ensure rapid recovery of tourism activities during external disturbances. Simultaneously, developing diversified tourism products can reduce dependence on single resources, thereby lowering systemic vulnerability. Furthermore, establishing regional collaborative emergency mechanisms and risk monitoring and early warning systems will enable effective responses to sudden events and promote rapid recovery of the tourism economy. These measures collectively enhance the adaptability and stability of TSES, mitigating the negative impacts of external shocks.

Region-specific governance approaches

Based on findings regarding sustainability states, internal coupling relationships, and obstacle factors, targeted recommendations are proposed. For regions such as Kizilsu, Hotan, Turpan, and Hami, which are in a “high-pressure load” state with significantly higher pressure levels than resilience levels and low coordination, future efforts must prioritize ecological conservation to provide a sound environmental foundation for tourism resource development and industrial growth. These regions should also increase market scale and financial investments to enhance systemic resilience and further advance sustainable development. For regions fluctuating between “low-surplus” and “low-load” states, such as Altay, Bayingolin, Tacheng, and Bortala, where coupling coordination remains passive, future strategies should simultaneously focus on reducing pressure and improving resilience. In particular, addressing the widespread obstacle of insufficient ecological protection investments requires intensified conservation efforts. Additionally, region-specific strategies should be implemented: Altay should emphasize the development of cultural tourism resources to fully leverage its unique cultural heritage; Bayingolin needs to enhance the construction level of tourism infrastructure to strengthen facility support for sustainable tourism; Tacheng and Bortala should prioritize the diversification of tourism resource offerings, addressing gaps in resource supply, facility capacity, and industrial scale to comprehensively improve TSES sustainability and internal coordination.

Limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations that need to be further explored in future research. Firstly, due to data availability constraints, the indicators selected in this study cannot fully capture spatial heterogeneity at a finer scale. Future research could integrate big data, remote sensing technologies, and other advanced tools to obtain higher-resolution and multi-dimensional datasets, thereby more accurately characterizing the regional features of TSES. Secondly, although this study analyzed the interaction between pressure and resilience through state classification and coupling coordination models, it lacks an in-depth exploration of the subtle interaction mechanisms and causal pathways between these two dimensions. Investigating how pressure and resilience synergize or conflict in driving system sustainability holds significant theoretical and practical importance. Future research should employ multivariate interaction models or causal inference frameworks, such as structural equation modeling and agent-based simulations, to analyze how these factors co-evolve and shape the sustainable development trajectory of TSES.

Conclusion

This study developed a sustainability assessment framework for the TSES in arid regions, with its applicability verified using Xinjiang as a representative example. By employing improved TOPSIS methods, state classification functions, coupling coordination degree models, and obstacle degree models, the study assessed and analyzed the sustainability level, spatiotemporal characteristics, state transitions, coupling coordination relationships, and obstacle factors of Xinjiang’s TSES from 2010 to 2020. The results indicate that during the study period, the sustainability level of Xinjiang’s TSES experienced a growth phase from low to high, accompanied by temporary fluctuations. Spatially, sustainability exhibited a radiating pattern centered around Urumqi and the tourism economic hubs of northern Xinjiang, gradually decreasing toward the periphery, yet significant regional disparities remained. Overall sustainability improved, with an increase in regions achieving a “surplus state” and a decrease in those under a “load state,” reflecting enhanced system resilience and reduced pressure. However, progress in some cities, such as Turpan, remained limited. The coupling coordination degree of TSES in Xinjiang’s 14 prefectures gradually improved, transitioning from “passive coordination” to “low-level” or “medium-level coordination,” though no region reached “high-level coordination,” indicating substantial potential for further system synergy optimization. The obstacle factors to TSES sustainability exhibited significant regional heterogeneity: issues such as spatial crowding pressure from tourists and population carrying pressure were widespread. Northern Xinjiang primarily faced challenges like insufficient technological innovation and transportation bottlenecks, while southern Xinjiang was constrained by inadequate infrastructure and talent shortages.

This study provides important insights and references for sustainable tourism development in arid regions beyond Xinjiang. Firstly, the proposed “pressure-resilience” assessment framework and evaluation indicators exhibit universal applicability, providing innovative tools for assessing TSES sustainability in other arid regions. Secondly, the research logic framework of “theoretical construction-empirical analysis-policy recommendations” established in this study serves as a valuable reference for similar regional studies. Thirdly, the revealed differentiated development pathways demonstrate that achieving sustainable tourism development in arid regions requires synergistic strategies of “pressure management and resilience cultivation.” This study not only extends the application of social-ecological system theory in tourism research but, more importantly, offers a transferable assessment framework and decision-support tools for sustainable tourism development in arid regions. It provides practical references for achieving coordinated advancement of tourism development and ecological conservation in global arid regions.
