Dynamic structures of dengue virus serotype 2 secreted NS1 and their interactions with heparan sulfate
Authors: Qunfei Zhou, Thiam-Seng Ng, G. Suthershinii, Aaron Wai Kit Tan, Victor A. Kostyuchenko, Guntur Fibriansah, Shee-Mei Lok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue secreted non-structural protein 1 (sNS1) contributes to the vascular permeability symptom of severe dengue hemorrhagic fever. Previous flavivirus sNS1 structures suggest that they predominantly exist as loose tetramers. Here, we report two stable tetramer structures (3.1–3.6 Å) together with loose tetramers. Formation of the stable tetramers involves a dramatic rearrangement of their N-terminal regions compared to the loose tetramers. We observe a higher molecular weight complex (HMWC) comprising dimeric sNS1 and heat shock proteins, which exhibits much lower endothelial hyperpermeability activity than the tetramer-enriched samples. We also determine high-resolution structures of the sNS1 complex with heparin, an analogue of the attachment factor heparan sulfate, showing that heparin binds to a conserved basic groove on the outer surface of the dimer, at the intra-dimer interface. Pre-incubation of sNS1 with heparin reduces its endothelial hyperpermeability activity. Our findings provide important structural information for future sNS1-based drug or vaccine design.

Introduction

Flaviviruses, such as dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV), yellow fever virus (YFV), and West Nile virus (WNV), are important human pathogens. They are positive-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses1. The flavivirus genome, approximately 9.5-12 kb in length, encodes three structural proteins (capsid [C], premembrane/membrane [prM/M], and envelope [E]) and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, NS5)1. DENV, the most prevalent flavivirus, causes an estimated 390 million infections annually in over 100 countries2. It has four serotypes (DENV1–DENV4) and can cause severe diseases such as dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) or dengue shock syndrome (DSS)3,4. Currently, there are no specific antiviral drugs for DENV, and the development of effective vaccines remains a challenge due to the need for balanced immunity against all four serotypes to avoid antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) of infection5.

DHF is characterized by vascular leakage, thrombocytopenia, and hemorrhagic manifestations, which can progress to life-threatening DSS6. Non-structural protein 1 (NS1), a ~ 45 kDa glycoprotein, plays a key role in endothelial dysfunction7 and DHF progression8. NS1 is synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and then undergoes glycosylation in the trans-Golgi network9. It is then either transported to the plasma membrane or secreted as extracellular NS1 (sNS1). Intracellular NS1 (iNS1) forms dimers and is part of the viral replication complex10, while extracellular sNS1 exists in multiple oligomeric forms11 and circulates in the bloodstream of infected patients12,13. Recombinant sNS1 has been shown to directly cause endothelial hyperpermeability14,15, facilitating viral dissemination15 and inducing vascular leakage in mice14. sNS1 proteins from different flaviviruses show specificity for different endothelial cell types, consistent with the affected organs observed during infection7. Studies have shown that sNS1 binds to heparan sulfate (HS) and chondroitin sulfate E on the surface of endothelial cells16. Upon binding, sNS1 triggers degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx layer and disrupts tight junction integrity by activating host enzymes, including sialidase, cathepsin L, and heparanase17. However, how sNS1 interacts with host factors to mediate cell binding and induce hyperpermeability remains unknown.

Full-length NS1 structures from various flaviviruses, including DENV18–23, WNV18, ZIKV22,24–26, JEV22, and YFV27, have been determined previously. Crystal structure of iNS1 shows that it exists as a dimer18,25. Each protomer consists of three distinct domains: (1) the N-terminal domain comprising β1- β3 (residues 1-35), (2) the wing domain (residues 38-151), and (3) the β-ladder domain (residues 180-352), with linker regions connecting the domains (residues 36-37 and 152-179). The N-terminal ends of the two protomers within a dimer interact with each other, forming a β-roll structure. Based on the highly hydrophobic characteristics of this region, the β-roll has been proposed to interact with cellular membranes18. The wing domain was observed to be the most flexible region24. The β-ladder domain consists of 10 β-strands stacked together. In contrast, recombinant sNS1 exhibits diverse oligomeric states, including dimers, loose and stable tetramers, and hexamers19–22,26,27, with the loose tetramer being the predominant conformation22,26,27. In loose tetramers, the two dimers are largely stabilized by hydrophobic interactions between their β-roll domains22,26,27. The angles between the two dimers within a loose tetramer are variable, leading to an overall asymmetric conformation.

We have previously determined different oligomeric states of the recombinant sNS1 of DENV2 clinical strain PVP94/0721 – a loose tetramer, a stable tetramer, and a loose hexamer. The stable tetramer structure is distinct compared to the other flavivirus structures. The tetramer has a D2 symmetry, with the two dimers tightly packed together. This involves the rearrangement the N-terminal ends of both dimers into elongated β-sheets21.

In this study, we further optimize the purification of the recombinant DENV2 PVP94/07 sNS1 sample and improve the cryo-EM classification and reconstruction process. This leads to the identification of a tetrameric assembly – a distinct stable tetramer (named stable tetramer 2). Stable tetramer 2 exhibits completely different inter-dimer interactions in the N-terminal region compared to the loose tetramer and the previously identified stable tetramer (named stable tetramer 1). This further demonstrates the high structural plasticity of the N-terminal regions of sNS1. We also observe a higher molecular weight complex (HMWC) comprising sNS1 dimers interacting with host heat shock proteins (HSPs). However, this HMWC has lower endothelial hyperpermeability activity compared to the tetramer-enriched sNS1 sample. To investigate how sNS1 engages cell surface attachment factors such as HS, we determined the cryo-EM structures of the sNS1:heparin (Hp) complex at high resolutions. Hp, a highly sulfated structural analog of HS, is found to bind a conserved basic groove on the outer surface of the intra-dimer interface. Consistent with this interaction, the addition of free Hp reduces sNS1-induced endothelial hyperpermeability. These findings show 1) that sNS1 tetramers are important in causing endothelial hyperpermeability activity, 2) how stable tetramers are formed, and 3) how they bind to cell surface receptor HS. Structural and functional characteristics of sNS1, as well as its interactions with host factors, are important for understanding its complex roles in dengue pathogenesis, and the results could advance the development of NS1-based therapies.

Results

Purification of DENV2 sNS1 oligomers

Our previous DENV2 sNS1 structures21 revealed a range of oligomeric forms, including loose tetramers at ~8 Å resolution, a stable tetramer at ~4 Å resolution, and a loose hexamer at ~8 Å resolution. In this study, we optimized the purification protocol and performed a more rigorous classification of sNS1 structural states during cryo-EM analysis. We observed a high molecular weight complex (HMWC) and determined a high-resolution structure (3.1 Å) of the sNS1 dimer in this complex. We also observed a distinct stable tetramer structure (stable tetramer 2) (Fig. 1a). For previously observed classes, such as loose tetramers and stable tetramer 1, we also achieve significantly improved resolutions (Fig. 1a). Previously, we purified recombinant His-tagged DENV2 sNS1 using nickel affinity chromatography combined with gel filtration21. In this study, we introduced an additional purification step using a Resource Q anion-exchange column after the nickel affinity step. Resource Q chromatography of sNS1 yielded two fractions (Supplementary Fig. 1a): Fraction 1 (eluting at 136 mM NaCl) and Fraction 2 (eluting at 318 mM NaCl), hereafter referred to as RQfraction 1 and RQfraction 2, respectively. Subsequent size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex S200i column showed that RQfraction 1 eluted at a retention volume of 11.1 mL (Supplementary Fig. 1b), while RQfraction 2 eluted at 9.8 mL (Supplementary Fig. 1c), indicating that RQfraction 2 has a higher molecular weight.

SDS-PAGE analysis showed that RQfraction 1 contains predominantly sNS1, whereas the RQfraction 2 contains sNS1 and two additional bands at ~90 kDa and ~70 kDa (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and In-gel MS analysis showed that the RQfraction 2 contains host proteins HSP70 and HSP90 as minor components, as the intensities of the bands are weak in the SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 1d, Supplementary Table 1). Gel filtration, semi-denaturing SDS-PAGE, and chemical crosslinking assays show that RQfraction 2 indeed has a higher molecular weight than RQ fractions 1 (Supplementary Fig. 1e–g); therefore, we designate the protein complex in RQfraction 2 as a high molecular weight complex (HMWC).

Cryo-EM 2D averages of the HMWC showed that the large assemblies contain dimeric forms of sNS1 (Supplementary Fig. 2), with additional sparse densities adjacent to the dimers. However, we are unable to resolve the whole complex structure, likely due to the heterogeneity of the overall structure. In contrast, RQfraction 1 contains predominantly sNS1 tetramers, as shown by cryo-EM reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 3), with low levels of dimers. Hence, we named RQfraction 1 as the tetramer-enriched sNS1 fraction. Lipidomics analysis did not detect enrichment of any specific lipid species in either the HMWC or the tetramer-enriched sNS1 fraction.

Overall, this two-step chromatographic strategy effectively separates different NS1 oligomeric states. The resulting enriched fractions enabled us to assess their endothelial hyperpermeability properties and facilitated high-resolution cryo-EM reconstruction of additional oligomeric forms.

Tetramer-enriched fraction induces greater hyperpermeability

sNS1 can cause endothelial cell hyperpermeability in tissue culture, simulating the vascular leakage symptom in DHF7,8. We used a trans-endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay to quantitatively measure sNS1 endothelial hyperpermeability activity in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) and dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMEC).

The oligomerization states of the HMWC and tetramer-enriched fraction were determined using a combination of gel filtration, semi-denaturing SDS-PAGE, and cryo-EM. However, in the TEER assay, the samples are diluted into different media, which may alter their oligomeric state. Therefore, the HMWC and tetramer-enriched fractions were diluted in buffer and different endothelial cell culture media to mimic the assay conditions, and their oligomerization profiles were analyzed by semi-denaturing SDS–PAGE. The resulting gel profiles (Supplementary Fig. 4) were similar to those of the undiluted samples used for cryo-EM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1e), indicating that the oligomeric states remained unchanged.

Our results show that the HMWC induced significantly less hyperpermeability in both endothelial cell lines than the tetramers-enriched fraction at the same sNS1 concentration (10 μg/ml) (Fig. 1c).

Cryo-EM structures of five sNS1 oligomeric states

We determined cryo-EM structures of five distinct oligomeric states (Fig. 1a): (1) A dimer structure at 3.1 Å resolution reconstructed from 183,923 particles (HMWC fraction); (2) Two loose tetramer conformations reconstructed from tetramer-enriched fraction — loose tetramer 1 at 4.7 Å from 25,540 particles and loose tetramer 2 at 4.5 Å from 27,103 particles, with different inter-dimer angles; (3) Two stable tetramers also from tetramer-enriched fraction — stable tetramer 1 at 3.6 Å from 40,652 particles and stable tetramer 2 at 3.1 Å from 55,807 particles. The dimer and loose tetramers 1 and 2 were reconstructed without imposing symmetry (C1) (Supplementary Figs. 2–3). For stable tetramers 1 and 2, C2 symmetry was applied during reconstruction, with the axis passing through the center of the tetrameric structure (Supplementary Fig. 3).

All models were fitted into their respective cryo-EM density maps (Fig. 1b). For the lower-resolution loose tetramers (4.7 Å and 4.5 Å), we performed rigid-body fitting using two dimer subunits. In contrast, the dimer and stable tetramers, resolved at higher resolutions (3.1–3.6 Å), allowed reliable fitting of side chains (Supplementary Fig. 5). We define the interaction between two protomers within a dimer as the intra-dimer interface, and the interaction between dimers as the inter-dimer interface (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Following previous nomenclature22, the two protomers within the intra-dimer region are designated as chains A and a, while the two protomers from the second dimer are labeled as B and b (Fig. 1b). Superposition of our intra-dimer structure onto the previously reported crystal structure of DENV2 iNS118 shows a largely conserved conformation, with a Cα RMSD of 0.839 Å. The dimer thus serves as a fundamental building block for all oligomeric forms.

Structures of protomers in different oligomers

sNS1 protomers from all oligomers largely retain the typical flavivirus NS1 topology, comprising an N-terminal region, followed by the connector, the wing domain, the “greasy finger” and finally the β-ladder domain (Fig. 2a). The differences between protomers of dimers, loose tetramers and stable tetramers 1 and 2 are mainly located in the N-terminal β1-β3 regions (Figs. 2a, 3a). We previously determined the stable tetramer 1 structure21, and have now produced a density map at higher resolution (Supplementary Fig. 5d). As a result, we can now observe side chain densities of the N-terminal region (Supplementary Fig. 5d). The 3.1 Å resolution stable tetramer 2 is a distinct structural class identified through more extensive classification of particles. Stable tetramer 2 exhibits two-fold symmetry: protomer A is identical to protomer b, and protomer B is identical to protomer a (Fig. 2a). We observed densities corresponding to the entire N-terminal region containing β1-β3 strands in protomers A and b (Supplementary Movie 1), somewhat similar to those in dimer and loose tetramer structures (Figs. 2a, 3a). In the protomers B and a (Fig. 3a), we could only observe part of β2 strand and the entire β3 strand densities, which suggests that the entire of β1 strand and the N-terminal end of β2 are likely disordered.

Structures of the N-terminal regions in different oligomers

The β1-β3 strands in protomers A/a in the dimer and loose tetramer conformations interact with each other at the intra-dimer interface, forming the β-roll structure (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 6). In the stable tetramer 1 state, part of the β2 strand forms an antiparallel β-sheet structure with the same strand from the other protomer at the intra-dimer interface (Figs. 2a, 3a). In the stable tetramer 2, these N-terminal strands of protomer A/a do not associate with each other, and therefore, they no longer interact at the intra-dimer interface (Fig. 2a).

Structures at the inter-dimer interface of tetramers

Inter-dimer interactions between the dimers of loose tetramers 1 and 2 are mostly hydrophobic interactions between their β-roll structures (Fig. 3b). In the stable tetramer 1, β sheets assemblies from the opposite dimers interact with each other via mostly hydrophobic interactions21, forming an antiparallel, four-stranded β-sheet stack with crisscross topology (Fig. 3b).

Stable tetramer 2 is distinct compared to the other oligomers, as there are no intra-dimer interactions between the protomers at the N-terminal β1-β3 region (Figs. 2a, 3b). Instead, there is a “handshake” conformation formed at the inter-dimer interface between protomers A-to-B or a-to-b (Figs. 2b, c and 3b). Analysis of surface charges shows that this “handshake” interaction is mediated by electrostatic interactions, as the interaction partners have opposite charges (Fig. 2d). The list of interactions (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Movie 2) stabilizing this “handshake” includes: (1) electrostatic/hydrogen bonds between β1-β2 hairpins of protomer A and protomer B (K14A-D23B, F20A-T22B, T21A-T21B, T22A-F20B), (2) an electrostatic interaction between β1-β2 hairpin of protomer A to the wing domain of protomer B (D23A-K69B), (3) a hydrogen bond between the β1-β2 hairpin of monomer B to the connector of monomer A (T29B-Q31A); (4) and an electrostatic interaction between the β-ladder of protomer A and the wing domain of protomer B (E281A-K122B).

Filamentous sNS1 structure in the tetramer-enriched fraction

In our cryo-EM micrographs of the tetramer-enriched fraction, in addition to the isolated tetrameric particles, we also observed filamentous assemblies ~90 Å in diameter (Fig. 4a). These filaments were not observed in the HMWC fraction (Fig. 4a). Filament Tracer analysis in the program CryoSPARC28 with subsequent 2D classification showed that these assemblies consist of tetramers (combination of loose and stable tetramers) stacked together through their outer surface (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 6c). Cryo-EM structures of heparin complexed with sNS1 oligomers

Heparan sulfate (HS) has been identified as one of the attachment factors used by dengue sNS116. We therefore determined the cryo-EM structures of sNS1 complexed with Heparin (Hp), a structural analog of HS (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Figs. 7–8). Stacked NS1 tetramers remained detectable in the presence of Hp (Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting that Hp binding does not abolish filamentous NS1 assembly. In the dimeric, loose and stable tetrameric states, we observed densities corresponding to Hp (Supplementary Figs. 7–8). The Hp densities on the dimer and stable tetramer states appear stronger than that on the loose tetramer (Supplementary Figs. 7–8). This difference might be due to the varying angles of dimers in the loose tetramer state, resulting in a lower-resolution overall map. Nonetheless, Hp binds to all oligomeric states largely in the same manner (Supplementary Fig. 8), at the outer surface of the intra-dimer interface. We therefore focus on the Hp-bound structure of stable tetramer 2, which was determined to the highest resolution (3.1 Å) (Fig. 5a–d, Supplementary Fig. 7). We observed an elongated density which corresponds well to part of Hp (~3 sugar rings) (Fig. 5c), as we used a heterogeneous mixture length of Hp (mostly 17–19 kDa). mVenus tag at C-terminal reduces sNS1 activity

As the Hp binding site is on the outer surface of the sNS1 tetramer near the C-terminal end (Fig. 5a–d, Supplementary Figs. 7–8), we hypothesized that placing a mVenus tag at the C-terminal end might reduce its interaction with heparan sulfate. Cryo-EM micrographs showed that the mVenus-tagged sNS1 did not form filamentous assemblies, unlike those in the wild-type (WT) sNS1 sample (Figs. 4a, 6a). Since the WT sNS1 filaments are likely formed by tetramers stacking via their outer surfaces (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 6c), the mVenus tag would abolish this interaction, and this is indeed what we observed in the sNS1-mVenus sample. Cryo-EM reconstruction of NS1-mVenus with C2 symmetry produced a structure with high local resolution at the sNS1 tetramer (3.8 Å) but much lower resolution density for the mVenus tag (~10 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). mVenus molecules have weak interactions with each other and therefore can exist either as dimers or monomers29. The low-resolution cryo-EM density observed for mVenus may reflect this variability, with the monomeric form likely being highly flexible on the surface of the sNS1 tetramers. Focus classification of the sNS1–mVenus cryo-EM maps, using a mask on the mVenus density, confirmed the presence of both monomeric and dimeric forms of mVenus (Supplementary Fig. 9). Superposition of the NS1-Hp complex structure onto the NS1-mVenus stable tetramer map shows that the Hp binding site would be either partially or fully occluded in these structural classes (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Consistent with this, we conducted ELISA assays, demonstrated that NS1-mVenus had reduced Hp binding compared to WT NS1 (Fig. 6d). Discussion

The secreted recombinant sNS1 structures showed a mixture of different oligomeric states – dimeric and tetrameric and in some cases, hexameric states, with tetramers being the predominant form across flaviviruses18–27. High-resolution hexameric sNS1 structures from DENV4 have indicated a relatively large proportion of hexamers in that preparation22. In contrast, in this study, even after extensive 2D classification, only a very small number of hexamer-like particles (<1%) were observed (Supplementary Fig. 10), consistent with our previous findings that hexamers are a minor component in DENV2 sNS1 preparations21. Both our DENV221 and the DENV4 sNS1 reported by Qi Pan et al.22 were expressed in the Expi293F mammalian cell system and purified using similar procedures, suggesting that their oligomeric distributions likely reflect intrinsic differences between the virus serotypes and strains.

Here, we identified a new fraction (HMWC) comprising a complex between sNS1 dimers and host chaperone HSPs. Its function and biological significance remain unclear, as this fraction exhibits low endothelial hyperpermeability activity. In contrast, sNS1 tetramers are more effective at inducing endothelial hyperpermeability. We speculate that tetramers, and their higher-order filamentous conformations (stacked tetramers), may engage multiple receptors simultaneously, leading to more effective activation of downstream signaling pathway30,31.

Compared to other flavivirus (DENV4, JEV, ZIKV, and YFV) sNS1 proteins, which exhibit a conserved N-terminal β-roll structure, the N-terminal regions of our DENV2 sNS1 displays tremendous structural diversity. The dimers and loose tetramers both have a β-roll conformation, whereas the stable tetramers 1 and 2 contain different β-sheet structures. Stable tetramer 2 shows the most dramatic structural changes from the β-roll conformation, as the N-terminal end no longer makes intra-dimer interactions, and instead forms extensive inter-dimer interactions. In both stable tetramer forms, the dimer pairs pack more closely than in loose tetramers (Fig. 7a, Supplementary Fig. 11), likely due to a combination of N-terminal structural plasticity and complementary electrostatic interactions at the inter-dimer interface. Electrostatic analysis of the inter-dimer interface of DENV2 sNS1 showed complementary charges at all four corners (Fig. 7b, Supplementary Fig. 11). In contrast, other flavivirus sNS1 structures showed less electrostatic complementarity (Supplementary Fig. 11). For example, the inter-dimer interface of the DENV4 sNS1 dimer have weak electrostatic complementarity, while those of JEV and YFV sNS1 show no or even repulsive electrostatic complementarity, respectively. This might explain their loose tetramer conformations. Although ZIKV sNS1 likely can form slightly more peripheral interactions via weak complementary charges and/or hydrophobic interactions, structural studies showed that they largely assembled into the loose tetramer state22,26. The lack of stable tetramer states in ZIKV sNS1 might also be due to the limited structural flexibility of its N-terminal end β-region. Our previous work demonstrated that a specific antibody (5E3) can inhibit the endothelial hyperpermeability activity of sNS1 in loose oligomeric status21. The stable tetramer 1 structure on the other hand is resistant to this antibody21. Although the 5E3 epitope is partially exposed in all oligomeric forms, the loose tetramer can transiently expose its epitope due to the dynamic motions between the dimers. The stable tetramer 1 exhibits tight dimer-dimer interactions, so the 5E3 epitope remains partially hidden, preventing antibody binding. This observation supports the hypothesis that the stable tetramer may represent a structural mechanism of immune evasion. The distinct stable tetramer 2 was determined to even higher resolution than the stable tetramer 1, and the dimer-dimer “handshake” structure also involves strong electrostatic interactions, thus suggesting that stable tetramer 2 is also tightly packed and may likewise be resistant to antibody binding. Superimposition of Fabs 5E321, 1G5.332, and 2B733 (previously demonstrated to have endothelial hyperpermeability inhibition activity) onto our stable tetramer 2 structure showed clashes with the Fabs, indicating that these antibodies likely cannot bind to this conformation (Supplementary Fig. 12a). To date, the NS1 stable tetrameric assemblies have only been observed for DENV2 sNS1. An interesting question for future studies is whether the abundance of stable sNS1 tetramers across different flaviviruses, serotypes, and strains influences virulence, pathogenesis, and clinical outcomes in patients.

Our cryo-EM analysis of DENV2 sNS1 revealed several conformational states—a dimer, two loose tetramers, two stable tetramers, and a very small number of hexamer-like particles. These conformations may represent either discrete end-state assemblies or dynamic intermediates in a structural equilibrium. For the latter possibility, we propose a stepwise assembly model (Fig. 7c). During assembly, two individual dimers dissociate from membranes and oligomerize via interactions between their hydrophobic β-roll domains, forming the loose tetramers. Complementary electrostatic charges at the inter-dimer interface then bring the dimers into closer proximity, while the structural plasticity of the sNS1 N-terminal region facilitates its reorganization into β-sheet conformations, ultimately leading to the formation of stable tetramers 1 and 2. We propose that stable tetramer 1 represents an intermediate in the transition toward stable tetramer 2, as the β-roll to β-sheet transformation in stable tetramer 1 is less dramatic than in stable tetramer 2. We performed a 3D variability analysis in cryoSPARC28, focusing on the stable tetramer cluster population in our reconstruction. The resulting conformational landscape reveals a continuous transition between stable tetramers 1 and 2, characterized by large movements at the N-terminal region and slight rotations of their outer dimer surfaces (Supplementary Movie 3).

The DENV4 sNS1 assembly model proposed by Qi Pan et al.22 suggests that loose tetramers represent intermediate states, with the hexamer being the final stable conformation. The differences between the DENV4 and our DENV2 model may, in part, arise from distinct interactions at the inter-dimer interface within the tetramer. In the DENV4 loose tetramer, only one corner of each dimer exhibits complementary charges at the inter-dimer interface (Supplementary Fig. 11), whereas in DENV2, complementary charges are present at all four corners (Fig. 7b, Supplementary Fig. 11). This charge distribution could favor the tetramer as a stable end state in DENV2. In contrast, in the DENV4 hexamer structure, a triacylglycerol molecule stabilizes three dimers through interactions with their β-roll domains, potentially promoting the hexamer as the preferred end state. Consistently, the high-resolution determination of the DENV4 hexamer structure (2.9-3.0 Å) suggests that this assembly is highly stable. By comparison, in our DENV2 sample, only a small population of hexamer was detected, and the 2D class averages show that the three dimers in the hexamer are flexible, precluding high-resolution structural determination.

Our cryo-EM structures demonstrate that Hp binds to a positively charged groove formed by the spaghetti loops at the outer surface of the dimers in all oligomeric forms of the DENV2 sNS1 structures (Supplementary Fig. 8). Electrostatic charge comparisons of the outer surface of other flavivirus sNS1 showed that only DENV serotypes 1-4 have this conserved positively charged groove while others do not (Supplementary Fig. 13). This suggests the other flaviviruses might not use HS as a receptor or they employ different binding sites. Interestingly, a previous study7 using TEER assays across different endothelial cell lines derived from specific organs showed that DENV sNS1 could induce endothelial hyperpermeability in a range of human endothelial cell lines (pulmonary, brain, umbilical, liver), while sNS1 proteins from other flaviviruses exhibit greater specificity (WNV to brain-derived and YFV to liver-derived endothelial cell lines). This might suggest different receptor usage by different flavivirus sNS1.

We observed some filament-like structures in the DENV2 sNS1 sample (Supplementary Fig. 3). 2D averages suggests that these filament assemblies might form from the stacking of the stable tetramers or loose tetramers on top of each other via their outer dimer surfaces (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 6c). Similar tetramer–tetramer stacking has been observed in ZIKV26 and DENV422. Notably, these filaments were absent in the NS1-mVenus construct (Fig. 6a), in which the mVenus tag occupies the proposed stacking interface on the outer surface of the tetramer. Interestingly, sNS1-mVenus showed reduced endothelial hyperpermeability activity compared to wild-type sNS1. Superimposition of our sNS1:Hp structure with the sNS1-mVenus model suggests that the mVenus tag may sterically hinder the Hp binding site (Supplementary Fig. 9b). This is consistent with reduced Hp binding observed in ELISA assays (Fig. 6d) and may explain the reduced hyperpermeability activity (Fig. 6e). However, one caveat is that filament assembly is also abolished by the mVenus tag. The role of sNS1 filaments in causing endothelial hyperpermeability remains unknown. Interestingly, when we mapped previously reported sNS1 residues important for binding33 and internalization34 into endothelial cells and the activation of the downstream signaling pathways33, we observed that all these residues would be exposed in the filaments, i.e, none are localized to the tetramer-stacking interface (outer surfaces of tetramers) (Supplementary Fig. 12b). This might suggest that the filamentous assembly could have a role in causing endothelial hyperpermeability.

In summary, we showed that the N-terminal region of sNS1 exhibits high structural plasticity, enabling the formation of highly stable tetrameric states. These tetramers are important for endothelial hyperpermeability and can further assemble into higher-order filamentous structures. All sNS1 oligomeric forms can bind Hp, and addition of Hp to sNS1 likely blocks HS binding, thereby reduces endothelial hyperpermeability. We observed Hp binding to positively charged groove at the intra-dimer interface in all sNS1 oligomeric forms. This groove might also mediate interactions with other negatively charged glycans such as sialic acid and chondroitin sulfate E16, facilitating sNS1 attachment to the endothelial cell surface. Thus, one potential therapeutic strategy for DENV-induced vascular leakage could involve blocking HS binding by targeting the basic groove of sNS1 with glycan-mimicking molecules, engineered heparin derivatives, or antibodies. Consistent with this idea, sulfated β-glucans have been reported to block NS1 binding to endothelial cells and reduce NS1-induced endothelial dysfunction for both DENV and ZIKV35, and treatment with the heparan sulfate mimetic PG545 has been shown to reduce systemic vascular leakage caused by DENV infection in vivo36. While sNS1 has been considered as a potential flavivirus vaccine candidate, its intrinsic ability to induce vascular leakage raises safety concerns. Our NS1-mVenus structural data reveal that occlusion of the Hp-binding groove, while preserving the overall fold, could provide a template for engineering safer sNS1 immunogens that retain immunogenicity without causing vascular leakage.

Methods

Cell culture

Expi293F suspension cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) used for the expression of the recombinant sNS1 were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and maintained in a CO₂ incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ on an orbital shaker at 120 rpm. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Lonza Bioscience, catalog no. C2517A) and human dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMEC) (Lonza Bioscience, catalog no. CC-2543) used in the TEER assay, were cultured in EGM-2™ BulletKit™ Medium supplemented according to the manufacturer’s instructions and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Expression and purification of recombinant DENV2 sNS1

Recombinant sNS1 from DENV2 strain PVP94/07 was expressed using a previously described plasmid21, without further modification. The plasmid was transfected into Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the ExpiFectamine™ 293 Transfection Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell culture supernatants were harvested four days post-transfection by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by further clarification at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and loaded onto a HisTrap column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with binding buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 10% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Bound protein was eluted with the same buffer containing 500 mM imidazole. The eluant was buffer-exchanged using a PD-10 desalting column into 50 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and further purified by anion-exchange chromatography using a Resource Q column (Cytiva) with a linear gradient to 500 mM NaCl over 20 column volumes. Two peak fractions were collected separately (Supplementary Fig. 1a) and subjected to size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva) equilibrated in 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Construct, expression, purification of sNS1-mVenus

The mVenus-N1 plasmid was a gift from Michael Davidson and Atsushi Miyawaki (Addgene plasmid #54640; http://n2t.net/addgene:54640; RRID: Addgene_54640)37. The mVenus tag was inserted into the DENV2 PVP94/07 NS1-HisTag plasmid between NS1 and the His-tag, separated by a 5-residue glycine-serine (GS) linker (NS1–GS linker–mVenus–His). The recombinant plasmid was transfected into Expi293 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the supernatant was harvested 5 days post-transfection. Purification followed the same protocol as for NS1-His, yielding a single peak in both Resource Q and size-exclusion chromatography. The peak fraction was used for cryo-EM analysis.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3 grids were glow-discharged at 5 mA for 1 min using an Emitech K100X Glow Discharge Unit. A volume of 2.1 μL of NS1 RQfraction 1 or RQfraction 2 (Supplementary Fig. 1), each at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL, was applied to the glow-discharged grids. For the sNS1-mVenus sample, 2.1 μL of protein at a concentration of 0.12 mg/mL was applied to the cryo-EM grids in the same manner. For preparation of the sNS1:Hp complex, purified sNS1 protein (buffer exchanged to 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4) was mixed with Hp sodium salt from porcine intestinal mucosa (Mw 17-19 kDa, Sigma-Aldrich) at a 1:2 molar ratio (assuming an Hp Mw of 18 kDa) and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The final concentration of sNS1:Hp complex was 0.2 mg/mL. A volume of 2.1 μL of the sNS1:Hp complex was then applied to glow-discharged grids. Grids were blotted using Ted Pella standard Vitrobot filter paper (Grade 595) with the following settings in the Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI, Netherlands): blot force 2, blot time 2 s, drain time 0.5 s, 100% humidity and at 4 °C, and then plunge-frozen in liquid ethane.

Cryo-EM image acquisition and reconstruction procedure

Cryo-EM micrographs of sNS1 and sNS1:Hp complexes were collected using a Titan Krios transmission electron microscope operated at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector and a Gatan GIF post-column energy filter in zero-loss mode (slit width: 20 eV). Images were acquired at a nominal magnification of 130,000× with a pixel size of 0.671 Å, using SerialEM for automated data collection. Data were recorded in movie mode with a total exposure time of ~4.0 s, generating 57–67 frames per movie and a cumulative electron dose of ~80 e⁻/Å². Movies were collected at defocus values ranging from −1.0 to −2.2 μm. A total of 4,845, 10,236, 9,464, and 6,328 movies were collected for the high molecular weight complex (HMWC) fraction, the sNS1 tetramer-enriched fraction, the sNS1:Hp complex, and the sNS1-mVenus sample, respectively. Motion correction was performed using Patch Motion Correction in CryoSPARC28, and the resulting full-dose images were used for particle picking and orientation determination. Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using Patch CTF estimation in CryoSPARC28 and incorporated into the reconstruction workflow. All subsequent reconstructions were performed using various modules in CryoSPARC28.

For the high molecular weight complex (HMWC) fraction, a total of 1,319,545 particles were auto-picked and extracted. 2D classification was performed to remove junk and damaged particles, resulting in 453,784 high-quality particles. Ab initio reconstruction was carried out with three classes. Only one class, comprising 183,923 particles, displayed an intact dimeric morphology and was selected for further refinement. Non-uniform refinement was performed in parallel with either no imposed symmetry (C1) or two-fold symmetry (C2). This resulted in final maps at 3.09 Å resolution (C1) and 2.97 Å resolution (C2). Comparison of the C1 and C2 reconstructions using cross-correlation coefficients (CCC), calculated in UCSF Chimera38 at a contour level of 3.0σ, yielded a CCC of 0.97 for the dimer, indicating that the structure possesses inherent C2 symmetry.

For the sNS1 tetramer-enriched sample, a total of 4,545,536 particles were auto-picked and extracted. After 2D classification, 1,267,205 high-quality particles were retained. Heterogeneous refinement was then used to separate the particles into six distinct classes, resulting in 211,983 particles corresponding to dimers, 372,693 to stable tetramers, and 221,979 to loose tetramers. For the dimer subset, non-uniform refinement was performed with both no imposed symmetry (C1) and two-fold symmetry (C2), yielding final maps at 3.40 Å (C1) and 3.27 Å (C2) resolution. The resulting structure was consistent with the dimer obtained from the HMWC fraction. For the loose tetramer subset, 3D classification without alignment was used to further resolve structural heterogeneity. Eight classes were generated, and two with clearer overall density were selected for further non-uniform refinement under C1 symmetry. This yielded maps at 4.67 Å and 4.53 Å resolution, corresponding to loose tetramer 1 and loose tetramer 2, respectively. For the stable tetramer subset, 3D classification into three classes was performed, identifying 236,464 particles with the best-defined tetramer morphology. To further resolve structural heterogeneity—evident as we observed diffused density in the central region of the initial reconstructions—3D classification without alignment was performed, yielding five classes. Two of these classes showed improved central density and were selected for non-uniform refinement. One class (stable tetramer 1) matched our previously published structure featuring an elongated β-sheet at the central interface, while the other (stable tetramer 2) lacked this elongated β-sheet. The remaining three classes likely represent intermediate conformations between stable tetramers 1 and 2. Final non-uniform refinements were carried out under both C1 and C2 symmetry. The final maps reached 3.78 Å (C1) and 3.63 Å (C2) for stable tetramer 1, and 3.17 Å (C1) and 3.14 Å (C2) for stable tetramer 2. Comparison between the C1 and C2 reconstructions of each structural class showed high similarity, with cross-correlation coefficients (CCC) calculated in UCSF Chimera38 at a contour level of 3.0σ: 0.88 for stable tetramer 1 and 0.97 for stable tetramer 2. In addition, we conducted 3D variability analysis on a tetramer cluster containing 236,464 particles to observe their conformational transitions.

To investigate the oligomeric composition of sNS1 within the filamentous structures observed in the micrographs, Filament Tracer was applied with parameters set to a filament diameter of 70–90 Å, a segment separation distance of 0.2, and a minimum filament length of 3. The selected filament segments were extracted using a box size of 800 pixels (corresponding to 536.8 Å). We then conducted multiple rounds of 2D classification.

For the sNS1:Hp complex, a total of 3,697,637 particles were auto-picked and extracted. After 2D classification, 973,231 high-quality particles were retained. Ab-initio reconstruction was performed to generate three good distinct classes: 165,594 particles was used to reconstruct dimers, 304,449 particles for stable tetramers, and 163,202 particles for loose tetramers. To further refine the dimer population, 3D classification was performed on the dimer subset, resulting in one class with improved morphology (98,664 particles). Non-uniform refinement with two-fold symmetry (C2) yielded maps at 3.20 Å (C2). Extra density corresponding to bound Hp was observed. For the stable tetramer class, 3D classification into three classes was performed, identifying 140,550 particles with the best-defined tetramer morphology. Non-uniform refinement using C2 symmetry produced final reconstructions at 3.11 Å for stable tetramer 2:Hp complex and 3.89 Å for stable tetramer 1:Hp complex. Additional density corresponding to the bound Hp was observed on the sNS1 outer surface. For the loose tetramer class, non-uniform refinement without imposing any symmetry (C1) resulted in a 3.42 Å map. HP density was not clearly observed, likely due to the intrinsic flexibility between the two dimer units. Focused refinement on a single dimer with C2 symmetry applied yielded a 3.23 Å resolution map, revealing a small extra density on the dimer’s outer surface, consistent in location with the Hp-binding site identified in the dimer and stable tetramer structures.

For the sNS1-mVenus sample, a total of 197,319 particles were auto-picked and extracted. After 2D classification, 82,370 high-quality particles were retained. Ab initio reconstruction was performed with three classes, however, only one class (61,580 particles) displayed tetrameric morphology and was selected for further refinement, while the other classes were mainly composed of broken particles. Non-uniform refinement was carried out with C2 symmetry, consistent with the symmetry observed for the sNS1 tetramer. To further classify and refine the mVenus density, 3D classification without alignment was performed using a mask focused on one side of the mVenus-tagged outer surface of the NS1 tetramer, generating five distinct classes. Non-uniform refinement was subsequently applied to the resulting classes.

The cryo-EM reconstruction workflow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 2 (HMWC fraction), Supplementary Fig. 3 (tetramer-enriched fraction), Supplementary Fig. 7 (sNS1:Hp complex), and Supplementary Fig. 9 (sNS1-mVenus). Map resolutions were determined using the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) criterion, and local resolution estimates were obtained using the Local Resolution Estimation tool in CryoSPARC. Refinement statistics are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Protein model building

Our DENV2 sNS1 (containing the sequence of strain PVP94/07) homology model was made by using the DENV2 iNS1 dimer (PDB: 4O6B)18 as an initial template in the program “SWISS-MODEL”39. The homology model was then used for rigid-body fitting into the cryo-EM density maps of the dimer, loose tetramers, and stable tetramers. For the stable tetramer 1 and stable tetramer 2 structures, due to the structural rearrangements in the β1–β3 region, residues 1–40 were manually fitted into the density in the program “Coot”. We also separately conducted automated fitting using the program ModelAngelo40, and the resultant structures are similar to our manually fitted structures.

For the high-resolution maps of the dimer and stable tetramers, model refinement was carried out in Phenix41 using the real_space_refine tool with the following options: minimization_global, local_grid_search, simulated_annealing, morphing, and nqh_flips, with secondary structure restraints. Refinement was followed by manual model adjustment in Coot42. For the loose tetramer models, only minimization_global and rigid_body refinement were applied in Phenix, with secondary structure restraints. The geometry of all refined models was validated using MolProbity. Structural figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX43.

Electrostatic potential calculations

Electrostatic surface potentials were calculated using the Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (APBS) via the PBE server (server.poissonboltzmann.org)44. Input structures were first processed with PDB2PQR, applying the PARSE force field and setting the pH to 7.0 for analyzing Hp-binding surfaces, and pH to 8.0 for evaluating electrostatic interactions involved in tetramer formation. PROPKA was used to assign protonation states based on the specified pH, enabling the calculation of atomic charges and radii. The resulting PQR files were submitted to APBS using default parameters to compute the electrostatic surface properties of the proteins.

Measurement of inter-protomer distances in sNS1 tetramers

The distances between sNS1 protomers were measured using UCSF ChimeraX43. To assess the overall inter-dimer spacing, centroids were defined for protomers A, B, a, and b, and the distances between selected pairs (e.g., A–B and A–b) were measured (Fig. 7). As some flavivirus sNS1 tetramers exhibit asymmetric “loose” conformations – where one side of a protomer is closer to its opposing protomer than the other side of the same protomer – additional measurements were made using centroids defined for the wing domain (purple circles) and the β-ladder domain (orange circles) of each monomer to assess domain-specific opening distances (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay

HUVEC and HDMEC monolayers were cultured in T75 flasks until reaching 80–90% confluency and then detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. For Transwell seeding, 60,000 cells in 300 μL of medium were added to the apical side of Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size), which were placed into 24-well plates containing 1.5 mL of endothelial cell culture medium in the basolateral chamber. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for approximately 72 h to allow a confluent monolayer with tight junctions to form. To compare sNS1-induced hyperpermeability, tetramer-enriched sNS1 fraction and high molecular weight complex (HMWC) fraction were added to the apical chamber. Because the HMW sNS1 assemblies contain co-purifying host proteins, the amount of sNS1 in each fraction was first calibrated by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Accordingly, 3 μg of tetramer-enriched sNS1 or 5 μg of HMWC (containing an equivalent amount of sNS1) were applied. To assess the effect of Hp on sNS1-induced hyperpermeability, the following conditions were tested: 3 μg sNS1 alone (tetramer-enriched fraction); 3 μg sNS1 mixed with 1 μg Hp; 3 μg sNS1 mixed with 3 μg Hp; and 3 μg Hp alone. To compare sNS1-mVenus and sNS1 tetramer-enriched fractions, equal molar amounts were added to the apical side: 3 μg sNS1 tetramer or 5.15 μg sNS1-mVenus. In all experimental conditions, TNF-α (0.03 ng) was included as a positive control. Transendothelial electrical resistance is measured in Ohms (Ω) and was recorded every 2 h after treatment, by using an epithelial volt-ohm meter with STX2 “chopstick” electrodes. To maintain cell health, 50% of the culture medium in each well was replaced 48 h after initial seeding. Endothelial permeability was expressed as relative TEER, calculated using the following formula:

(Ω experimental condition − Ω medium alone) / (Ω untreated endothelial cells − Ω medium alone).

ELISA assay

Equal molar amounts of tetramer-enriched sNS1 fraction (20 µg/mL), HMWC fraction (33.3 µg/mL), sNS1-mVenus (34 µg/mL), or BSA (34 µg/mL, negative control) were diluted in 50 µL of coating buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4) and applied to Nunc MaxiSorp 96-well ELISA plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C. The wells were washed three times with PBST (PBS containing 0.01% Tween-20) and then blocked with 5% BSA in PBST for 2 h at room temperature. The plates were then washed three times with PBST before adding biotinylated heparan sulfate (heparin-biotin sodium salt, Sigma-Aldrich) at concentrations of 250, 50, or 10 µg/mL in PBST. Biotin alone (25 µg/mL) was included as a negative control to assess non-specific biotin binding. The plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature to allow binding. Wells were washed again three times with PBST, and bound Hp was detected by incubating with streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) polymer (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. After a final washing step, TMB substrate (3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine) was added to each well. The reaction was stopped by adding 1 M HCl, and absorbance was read at wavelength 450 nm using a Tecan microplate reader (Tecan Life Sciences).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0. Variability is represented as standard error of the mean (SEM), indicated by error bars. All experiments were independently repeated at least twice with similar outcomes. Source data are provided in the accompanying Source Data file.
