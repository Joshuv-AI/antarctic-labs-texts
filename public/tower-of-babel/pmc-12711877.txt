An intuitionistic fuzzy automated negotiation model for personalized and efficient shared decision-making
Authors: Ping Lu, Han Lu, Ying Wei, Bin Dai, Keda Lin, Sijie Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Shared decision-making (SDM) is a healthcare decision-making model that integrates patient preferences with medical expertise. It seeks to foster active involvement, enhance satisfaction, and strengthen the doctor-patient relationship. However, SDM applications face three main challenges: the complexity of medical information, the ambiguity of patient preferences, and the uncertainty of treatment outcomes. These challenges lead to a negotiation process that is frequently time-consuming and complex. Although existing SDM tools have made progress in improving efficiency, they still face challenges in addressing all of these issues simultaneously. In response to these challenges, this paper introduces an Agent-based Auto-negotiation Model based on Intuitionistic Fuzzy Sets (AN-IFF). Specifically, AN-IFF tackles the uncertainties inherent in preference assessment and negotiation decision-making using intuitionistic fuzzy sets and fuzzy inference systems. The model also includes a time-discounting mechanism to dynamically adjust the concession strategy, generating an optimal counter-offer set. To further accommodate varying decision-making behaviors, AN-IFF introduces three distinct negotiation strategies that simulate the concessionary behaviors of decision-makers with optimistic, balanced, and pessimistic personalities. The model rationalizes the consultation process by fuzzy modeling physician and patient preferences while integrating multiple personality traits. Experimental results based on negotiation preference data show that AN-IFF effectively models the differences in concession behavior among decision-makers with diverse personalities in SDM contexts. Furthermore, AN-IFF demonstrates significant improvements in joint satisfaction, fairness, and the number of negotiation rounds compared to baseline methods, such as FCAN.

Introduction

With the ongoing advancement of medical information digitization, patients’ understanding of their conditions has markedly improved1. Consequently, the traditional healthcare-driven decision-making model is no longer adequate to meet the increasing demand for personalized diagnosis and treatment in modern healthcare services. In this context, Shared decision-making (SDM), a patient-centered healthcare decision-making paradigm, has attracted considerable attention in both medical and academic circles. The SDM model requires the collaborative involvement of both healthcare professionals and patients in developing treatment plans2. In addition to presenting risk-benefit assessments and relevant clinical indicators, healthcare providers must guide patients in making informed trade-offs based on their values, ultimately leading to the final treatment plan. Empirical studies have shown that SDM significantly reduces patient anxiety3, enhances treatment adherence, and mitigates doctor-patient conflicts arising from information asymmetry4,5.

Although the SDM model offers many advantages, its implementation faces complex systemic challenges. The primary challenge stems from the diversity of decision-making stakeholders involved. SDM typically involves multiple stakeholders, such as healthcare professionals, patients, and their families, each with distinct knowledge backgrounds, values, and decision-making preferences. Furthermore, the complexity of decision-making increases during the evaluation process, as it requires a comprehensive consideration of factors such as therapeutic efficacy, treatment duration, economic burden, and potential adverse reactions. This complexity places greater demands on the decision-making capabilities of all parties involved. The third challenge concerns uncertainty in information exchange. The disparity in medical knowledge and health literacy between doctors and patients hinders patients from fully comprehending disease mechanisms and treatment risks, while simultaneously making it difficult for doctors to promptly grasp patients’ values and genuine preferences6. In addition, privacy concerns and limited consultation time further restrict comprehensive information exchange. These factors collectively result in information asymmetry during the decision-making process, manifested in patients’ difficulty in accurately assessing treatment benefits and risks and in doctors’ difficulty in comprehensively understanding patients’ individual needs and concerns. Finally, resource constraints, time pressures, and limitations in medical resources in clinical practice pose significant challenges, directly impacting the thorough discussion and optimal selection of treatment options.

Researchers have developed tools for patients, such as the outcome prioritization tool (OPT)7 and the advance care planning (ACP) tool8, designed to help patients clarify their values and decision-making criteria. However, studies indicate that these tools have limited effectiveness in alleviating time pressure9. Furthermore, existing treatment decision aids often present singular, precise values and fail to address potential hesitancy and ambiguity in patient preferences. This limitation may lead to a mismatch with real-world decision-making, where uncertainty about medical options and ambiguity due to limited medical knowledge are common. Multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods have demonstrated significant value in applications such as medical knowledge base construction, disease diagnosis, and pathology image classification10. However, the MCDM approach has two key limitations: first, existing methods often assume that decision-makers’ preferences are static, which contrasts with the dynamic nature of preferences in SDM11; The second challenge is that, although extensions of MCDM—such as consensus-reaching models—can partially mitigate information asymmetry by reconciling inconsistent evaluations, they operate primarily at the score and weight level. These approaches align expressed judgments but provide limited insight into the underlying trade-offs and unarticulated values that arise in real-time SDM. In clinical settings, patients often lack adequate medical knowledge to articulate treatment risks and benefits, while physicians face time and privacy constraints that hinder recognition of patient values. As a result, information asymmetry remains unresolved, and traditional MCDM methods, even with consensus extensions, demonstrate clear limitations in SDM scenarios.

In view of the dynamic nature of SDM, this study proposes agent-based negotiation as an auxiliary approach to resolve decision conflicts. Agent-based negotiation not only models the beliefs, desires, and intentions (BDI) of decision-makers but also adapts to environmental changes12,13. This approach has been widely applied in fields such as healthcare14, energy markets15, and cloud computing16 to address complex decision-making conflicts. In the context of SDM, Chen et al.17 and Lin et al.18 modeled SDM as an agent-based multi-issue negotiation framework, simulating the negotiation process between agents representing doctors and patients to quickly reach a mutually acceptable solution. Compared to traditional manual negotiation, agent-based negotiation can significantly reduce time and effort while enhancing the satisfaction of both parties.

Although agent-based negotiation has shown considerable potential in intelligent decision-making, existing studies continue to present notable limitations. The AutoSDM-DDPG framework proposed by Chen et al.19 and the ACANM-SDM model developed by Wen et al.20 represent valuable explorations of automated doctor–patient negotiation, employing fuzzy membership functions to capture the vagueness of decision-makers’ preferences. However, these approaches remain confined to traditional fuzzy number representations and do not account for hesitation, thereby limiting their ability to fully capture decision-makers’ uncertainty. This limitation imposes stricter requirements on the design of negotiation mechanisms. To address this issue, the present study adopts Intuitionistic Fuzzy Set (IFS) theory and establishes a three-dimensional evaluation framework comprising membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees to comprehensively formalize decision-makers’ treatment preferences21,22.

In summary, the primary contribution of this study is the development of a multi-agent negotiation model based on IFS and Fuzzy Reasoning, referred to as agent negotiation using IFS and Fuzzy Reasoning (AN-IFF). This model is designed to generate highly satisfactory treatment plans for SDM, improve negotiation efficiency, and facilitate the effective implementation of SDM in clinical practice. Related work

This chapter reviews related work on SDM, agent negotiation, and intuitionistic fuzzy decision-making, highlighting the innovation and sophistication of this research.

Shared decision-making

Veatch introduced SDM in 1972, emphasizing equal doctor-patient participation in medical decision-making23.SDM is widely recognized, especially in developed Western countries, as an ideal model for promoting patient autonomy24. Research mainly focuses on theoretical SDM models25,26, clinical applications27, and patient decision aids (PDAs).

The three-stage shared decision-making model, proposed by Elwyn et al.28, offers a systematic framework for clinical practice with three components: Choice Talk, Option Talk, and Decision Talk. Clinical studies confirm SDM’s positive impact on patient satisfaction, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes. For instance, Song et al.4 showed that SDM participation increased patient trust in physicians and improved prognostic indicators in inflammatory bowel disease. Similarly, Chen et al.29 showed that SDM enhances decision-making quality and patient prognosis in oncology treatment. However, SDM adoption faces challenges such as limited time, information asymmetry, and inadequate patient health literacy24.

PDAs facilitate evidence-based SDM by presenting disease information and quantifying patient preferences. Core tools include the OPT and ACP, which use scales7,30 and multimedia to reduce information asymmetry. However, studies reveal a gap between the clinical outcomes of PDAs and expectations. Summeren et al.9 found that OPT improved physicians’ understanding of patient preferences but failed to reduce SDM time costs. Similarly, Austin et al.8 showed that decision tools enhanced patient perception but could not replace patient-physician communication. Existing PDAs overly focus on patients, neglecting the physician’s role. Their design requires improvement to optimize SDM holistically.

Advances in healthcare AI have propelled clinical decision support systems (CDSS), which integrate medical knowledge with patient data to improve clinical decision-making. Studies show that CDSS improves diagnostic efficiency, reduces medication errors, and standardizes care processes31,32. Core functions include building medical knowledge bases, providing diagnostic support, and generating prognostic predictions33–35. For example, Zhou et al.36 proposed an e-health system to improve diagnostic efficiency in diabetes care. However, most systems focus on pathology-driven recommendations, neglecting patient preferences and contradicting SDM principles37.

Despite the effectiveness of SDM and PDA tools in improving patient engagement and decision-making quality, clinical time pressure, information asymmetry, and health literacy gaps hinder their widespread adoption. Existing CDSS over-rely on pathological data and overlook patients’ preferences, leading to an authoritarian decision-making model. The proposed agent negotiation model integrates doctor and patient preferences using intuitionistic fuzzy sets (IFS) and fuzzy inference, alleviating time pressure and balancing decision-making. Compared to traditional tools, the model quantifies patient preferences, offers optimal solutions through multi-party participation, enhances SDM efficiency and satisfaction, and drives theoretical and practical innovation.

Agent negotiation

Wooldridge et al.12 introduced the concept of the agent in 1995, defining it as a computer system that operates in complex, dynamic environments. The BDI model is widely used to enable autonomous decision-making in agents. It functions through three dimensions: belief (environmental information), desire (goal motivation), and intention (execution plan). The standard BDI workflow includes four stages38: (1) environmental perception and belief updating, (2) goal assessment and alignment, (3) intention generation, and (4) action execution. This cycle continues until the goal is achieved or resources are exhausted. Agents, with their autonomous decision-making capabilities, have significant potential in complex domains like medical decision-making. In medical decision-making, Elghamrawy14 proposed a multi-agent coordination model that resolves diagnostic disagreements and improves efficiency by using junior and senior physician agents. This application has encouraged researchers to optimize inter-agent negotiation mechanisms.

Kiruthika et al.39 proposed a life-cycle model dividing agent negotiation into three phases: pre-negotiation (preference extraction), negotiation (strategy and utility modeling), and post-negotiation (optimal offer evaluation). While foundational, the utility model’s applicability requires improvement. For issue types, Amini et al.40 proposed a temporal utility function for linear issues by integrating adversary states based on the Boulware strategy41, while Yang and Luo42 developed a priority-based discrete evaluation method for nonlinear issues. To address uncertainty, Hsu et al.43 introduced a fuzzy membership function, while Yi et al.44 proposed a Bayesian improvement model integrating fuzzy linguistic probabilities. Raouia et al.45 designed adaptive concession curves using rivals’ historical quotes, but their method is limited to linear topics and struggles to handle complex scenarios.

While existing research has made significant progress in offering evaluation and negotiation strategies for agent-based negotiation, limitations persist in addressing the complexity of human preferences. These challenges are particularly evident in large-scale and unknown negotiation domains, where uncertainty in decision-makers’ preferences becomes more pronounced. Simplifying or omitting this uncertainty in existing Agent-based negotiation models can result in deviations between negotiation outcomes and decision-makers’ expectations, thereby limiting their applicability in real-world scenarios.

This study builds on current research by integrating ambiguity and hesitancy into the agent construction process. It maps uncertainty to the agent’s concession behavior using an intuitionistic fuzzy utility model and negotiation strategies. This approach improves the representation of decision-makers’ fuzzy preferences and enhances negotiation efficiency through optimal strategies, strengthening the intelligence and generalizability of agent-based negotiation models.

Intuitionistic fuzzy sets

Atanassov’s IFS theory21 describes uncertainty through membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees, offering a more comprehensive approach than traditional fuzzy sets.IFS metrics, ranking, and aggregation methods are widely applied in MCDM to address information asymmetry. Ejegwa et al.46 proposed a similarity-distance approach to combine IFS features and reduce information loss. Zhang et al.47 introduced a ranking method based on the ideal solution to aid optimal selection. Additionally, Nur?ah et al.48 developed an aggregation method combining the utility and satisfaction of multiple decision-makers.In multi-issue negotiation, Roszkowska49 proposed an IFS-based framework, while Zhan et al.50 introduced prioritized fuzzy constraints to address uncertainty.

In recent years, researchers have actively applied and developed advanced fuzzy set theories to enhance the modeling capabilities of IFS and integrate them into fuzzy MCDM frameworks. For example, Farid et al.51 introduced and applied Circular Intuitionistic Fuzzy Sets (CIFS) in a comprehensive evaluation of sustainable logistics in the electronics industry. By integrating CIFS with LOPCOW, RANCOM, and MARCOS methods, they improved decision stability and interpretability under uncertain conditions. Similarly, Jameel et al.52 combined CIFS with entropy-weighted SWARA and CoCoSo for the comprehensive evaluation of renewable energy systems. Al-Quran et al.53 applied Cubic Pixel Fuzzy Sets (CPFS) to enhance the evaluation of tropical artificial forest ecosystems by introducing a novel fair aggregation operator. Jameel et al.54 also developed a hybrid MCDM framework using Linear Diophantine Fuzzy Sets (LiDFS), demonstrating its applicability in energy priority ranking. Additionally, T-spherical fuzzy sets (T-SFSs)55, integrated with the Dubois–Prade interactive aggregation operator, were employed within the CRITIC-EDAS framework for assessing environmental impacts and supporting mitigation strategies for low-carbon technologies.

However, the core research focus of these methods remains within static decision-making paradigms—primarily emphasizing one-time ranking and selection of alternatives based on predefined criteria and expert-assigned weights. In contrast, scenarios such as medical negotiation—which involve individualized linguistic interactions, evolving preferences, and psychological hesitation—require more than structural expansions of fuzzy MCDM models, which remain insufficient in addressing such interactive complexity. Although advanced fuzzy set theories such as CIFS, CPFS, and LiDFS have shown strong adaptability in macro-level MCDM domains—such as sustainable development, energy planning, and ecological management—their primary contribution lies in enhancing the expressive capacity and aggregation precision within indicator-based evaluation systems. However, they do not directly address the core requirements of healthcare negotiation—namely, dynamic interaction modeling and the representation of individual preference dynamics. Moreover, the structural and parametric complexity of these models often limits their interpretability and practical applicability in real-world clinical environments.

In contrast, IFS has demonstrated effectiveness in modeling ambiguity and hesitation, while maintaining a concise and interpretable structure. Furthermore, IFS has been applied in medical contexts to manage diagnostic uncertainty. For example, Jemal et al.56 used IFS in an ICU monitoring system to represent expert hesitation, while Ali et al.57 proposed a similarity-based approach that improved diagnostic accuracy. These findings highlight the robust modeling capacity and interpretability of IFS in clinical applications, thereby providing a solid foundation for its integration into dynamic multi-agent negotiation frameworks. Building on this foundation, we propose the AN-IFF framework, which is grounded in IFS theory and incorporates fuzzy inference mechanisms alongside dynamic adjustment strategies. This approach addresses a key gap in existing research, which has largely focused on static MCDM methods and lacks a thorough exploration of IFS in agent-based negotiation. The proposed framework effectively manages the complexity and uncertainty inherent in shared decision-making (SDM) by integrating patient preferences with medical recommendations, thereby improving consultation efficiency and enhancing decision satisfaction.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiamen Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The preference data were not generated through distribution simulation but were obtained from semi-structured interviews with pediatricians, clinical specialists, and parents of children with asthma at Xiamen Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The interviews focused on key aspects of pediatric asthma treatment to elicit authentic physician–patient preference information. All data were de-identified before analysis, with personally identifiable information removed and responses aggregated to prevent re-identification. The use of anonymized data complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and legal requirements.

SDM is a collaborative process in which doctors and patients engage in two-way negotiation to align individual preferences with treatment plans. SDM is a dynamic multi-party decision-making problem aimed at resolving doctor-patient conflicts and achieving a mutually satisfactory treatment plan. This study proposes a multi-agent negotiation framework using IFS and reasoning to help doctor-patient decision-makers reach agreement in SDM. The framework uses IFS to represent the uncertain preferences of doctors and patients and incorporates offer evaluation methods to enable agents to quantify user benefits effectively. The framework incorporates a fuzzy inference system into the negotiation strategy to adjust the agent’s concession behavior based on user preference uncertainty, thereby enhancing negotiation efficiency. The following sections discuss the problem addressed in this study and outline the components of the proposed model.

Negotiation framework

Significant divergences in treatment preferences exist in clinical medical decision-making due to individual differences and information asymmetry. This paper proposes an automated negotiation framework to reduce divergence. It models SDM as a distributed fuzzy constraint satisfaction problem. And by integrating IFS and fuzzy inference, it tackles uncertainty and multi-party preference challenges. This section details the core components of the proposed negotiation model, including SDM process modeling and agent behavior modeling.

SDM processing modeling

Doctors and patients often have differing treatment preferences due to individual differences, social backgrounds, and knowledge levels, combined with inherent uncertainty. Consequently, the SDM problem can be formulated as a distributed fuzzy constraint satisfaction problem and addressed using an automated negotiation model.

Agents in negotiation are influenced by external constraints, from the environment and adversaries, and internal constraints. These constraints may cause conflicting goals or reduce the solution space, increasing negotiation complexity and uncertainty. This paper addresses these challenges by reformulating SDM as a distributed intuitionistic fuzzy constraint satisfaction problem (DIFCSP) and developing a context-aware automated negotiation model.IFS provides an accurate representation of ambiguity and uncertainty in healthcare decision-making by capturing constraint satisfaction degrees. This approach improves both consultation efficiency and decision-making accuracy. Agent model

In each negotiation round, an agent selects an action from the set {Accept, Reject, Send}and communicates its choice to the opposing party. This decision-making process is guided by two key components of the agent: offer evaluation and negotiation strategy. The structure and decision-making process of the agent is shown in Fig. 2 and further detailed in Subsections “Offer evaluation” and “Negotiation strategy”.The agent evaluates offers based on satisfaction, dissatisfaction, and hesitation defined by IFS preferences. The aggregation function combines satisfaction, dissatisfaction, and hesitation into a single real number, representing the offer’s utility, called the agent’s aggregate satisfaction value (ASV). If the ASV exceeds the accept threshold (AT), the agent selects the “Accept” action, indicating mutual agreement and a successful conclusion of the negotiation. If the ASV falls below the AT, the agent adjusts its concession behavior relative to the AT using the negotiation strategy. The negotiation strategy calculates the concession value using a fuzzy inference system and a time discount factor, then updates the AT for the current round. The agent then identifies offers with utilities between the acceptance thresholds of the two rounds to construct a counteroffer set. These offers are ranked by their similarity to the opponent’s initial offer, and the most similar one is selected to be sent back.Fig. 2Agent decision-making process for a round in the consultation.

Offer evaluation

Offer evaluation aims to determine the decision-maker’s satisfaction value for a given offer. This is typically achieved using a utility function that maps the outcome space to a specific real number. The most common method for offer evaluation is the multi-issue weighted average sum, calculated as shown in Equation (1). As shown in the formula, the utility assessment of an offer depends on the evaluation function and the weight distribution of each issue. Value functions quantify the satisfaction level a decision-maker derives from the candidate values of an issue. Linear and radial models are common forms of these functions. However, traditional evaluation functions capture only a single satisfaction rating and rely on the decision-maker’s ability to provide precise assessments of issue values. In practice, evaluations are often multi-dimensional, and individuals may hesitate due to insufficient information. This approach enables the agent to evaluate topic values beyond a single real number, incorporating three components: membership (satisfaction), non-membership (dissatisfaction), and hesitation (uncertainty). The agent must aggregate satisfaction, dissatisfaction, and hesitation to generate offer evaluations, ensuring a more accurate representation of the decision-maker’s preferences.

Compared with other extended fuzzy environments (such as Pythagorean Fuzzy Sets (PFS) and Spherical Fuzzy Sets (SFS)), IFS explicitly distinguishes satisfaction, dissatisfaction, and hesitation using a ternary structure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\pi _A,\mu _A, \nu _A)$$\end{document} at the theoretical level, while also adhering to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _A+\mu _A + \nu _A=1$$\end{document} principle. This characteristic closely aligns with clinical communication in SDM scenarios, as patients’ assessments and questionnaires frequently capture not only clear satisfaction or dissatisfaction but also marked hesitation and uncertainty. In contrast, PFS and SFS enhance theoretical expressiveness through squared or norm-based constraints; however, their practical application frequently requires additional nonlinear constraints and calibration procedures. This increases computational complexity and intensifies data calibration demands, thereby hindering the rapid deployment of multi-agent negotiation and medical SDM scenarios. Therefore, this study prioritizes IFS as the primary modeling tool, while the discussion section suggests that PFS and SFS may be incorporated as extensions in future work to evaluate their potential advantages under conditions of higher uncertainty.

Negotiation strategy

A negotiation strategy is a key mechanism that enables agents to resolve conflicts through moderate concessions on benefits. To help agents adapt to diverse negotiation environments, most negotiation strategies incorporate adaptive concession mechanisms based on factors like time and negotiator behavior. In SDM negotiation, both aspects are equally important. However, in a fuzzy negotiation environment, the impact of fuzzy information (i.e., uncertainty) on concession behavior must also be considered to improve the agent’s adaptability and achieve better negotiation outcomes.

To address this, this paper proposes a negotiation strategy that integrates fuzzy information and time to regulate concession behavior using a fuzzy concession factor (FCF) and a time discount factor (TDF). The FCF determines the degree of concession by assessing the decision-maker’s attitude toward fuzzy information and capturing how uncertainty affects their willingness to concede. Meanwhile, the TDF prevents the agent from conceding too quickly, potentially causing unnecessary losses, or too slowly, potentially resulting in negotiation failure. Fig. 6 illustrates the flow of the negotiation strategy. The left side shows the generation of the FCF based on satisfaction and hesitancy, while the right side details the process of combining the FCF and the TDF to calculate the concession value and generate the counteroffer. Step 2: Fuzzy Inference-The inference mechanism relies on a rule base to map the fuzzy values of each attribute to their corresponding resultant fuzzy set. The fuzzy inference system matches input variables to the fuzzy rules in the rule base, generating output variables that represent a willingness to concede. This output variable, referred to as the “Concession Score,” is divided into five fuzzy sets: Very Low (VL), Low (L), Medium (M), High (H), and Very High (VH). Each fuzzy set is represented by a triangular membership function, with specific forms shown in Fig. 8 and detailed in Table 3. The design of fuzzy rules considers the varying attitudes of decision-makers with different personalities toward hesitation. Three concession strategies are proposed to reflect these subjective preferences: optimistic, balanced, and pessimistic. Optimistic decision-makers tend to interpret uncertainty as shifting toward satisfaction. Their rule design is based on optimism bias, which posits that individuals overestimate the probability of positive outcomes when facing future events6061. Pessimistic decision-makers, by contrast, interpret uncertainty as shifting toward dissatisfaction. Their rule design is informed by risk preference and aversion research, with experimental evidence showing significant risk-averse behavior under incentives6263. Balanced decision-makers adopt a middle ground between optimism and pessimism, taking a compromise stance toward uncertainty. Their rule design is grounded in risk neutrality and rational choice, assuming relative neutrality under uncertainty. This aligns with expected utility theory64 and is supported by risk perception research, which identifies individuals with relatively balanced risk attitudes beyond optimism and pessimism65.

In constructing the three personality-based strategies, this study draws inspiration from advances in behavioral game theory and decision psychology. Specifically, the fuzzy rule matrices were designed to simulate differentiated concession patterns of risk-seeking, risk-neutral, and risk-averse negotiators under varying levels of satisfaction and hesitation. This modeling approach is inspired by the work of Shen et al.66, who developed a consensus adjustment mechanism for group decision-making based on the Nash bargaining game and regret theory. Their findings demonstrate that regret aversion and risk attitudes significantly influence strategy selection and the consensus-reaching process. Building upon these insights, the present study extends this conceptual foundation to the context of bilateral doctor–patient negotiations, formalizing the concession responses of individuals with different risk attitudes to signals of satisfaction and uncertainty through fuzzy rule encoding. Experiment

This section starts with a detailed explanation of the experimental design, covering the experiment’s objectives and parameter settings. Next, the evaluation metrics used in the experiment are described, followed by a presentation and analysis of the results.

Experiment design

In this study, two experiments were conducted to assess the model’s sophistication and the effectiveness of the three proposed negotiation strategies:

Experiment 1: Three types of negotiation strategies tailored to different decision-maker profiles—optimistic, balanced, and pessimistic—are introduced. To evaluate the effectiveness of the three strategies, this experiment was conducted between PA and DA, employing either identical or differing strategies. The strategies used by both parties in a negotiation are referred to as strategy pairs.

Experiment 2: This experiment compares the proposed model with a baseline model and a state-of-the-art fuzzy constraint-based negotiation model. Negotiation simulation experiments were conducted between agents of the same model class, with each scenario simulated 50 times under varying time constraints. Additionally, the outcomes of all three AN-IFF strategies were included in the comparative analysis. Since this experiment focuses on comparing the outcomes of different negotiation models, only the performances of strategy pairs of the same type were analyzed. The details of all models are presented in Table 4.

Experiment 3: To evaluate the robustness and stability of the proposed model under varying time discount settings, both agents’ negotiation strategies were fixed to the Balanced type, with the negotiation deadline set to 30 rounds. Experiment in AN-IFF strategies

Building on the design of Experiment 1, this experiment simulates negotiations between multiple AN-IFF policy pairs to assess the effectiveness of the three AN-IFF strategies proposed in this paper.

First, this experiment compares concession behaviors during negotiations across AN-IFF strategies tailored to different personality types. Fig. 10 shows the threshold descent process of the PA under different AN-IFF strategies, with the same adversary strategy, when the time constraint is set to 10 rounds. As negotiations progressed, agents with pessimistic, balanced, and optimistic strategies made varying concessions, reaching agreements in the 6th, 8th, and 10th rounds, respectively. The results indicate that the optimistic strategy has the slowest threshold decline, while the pessimistic strategy shows the fastest, with the balanced strategy in between. This difference stems from the distinct interpretations of hesitancy inherent to each strategy. Optimistic decision-makers perceive a greater portion of hesitancy as likely to convert into satisfaction. Consequently, they hesitate to concede potential benefits too quickly, reducing the magnitude of their concessions and slowing the threshold decline. Conversely, pessimistic decision-makers perceive a greater likelihood of hesitancy converting into dissatisfaction. As a result, they choose to concede quickly, relinquishing uncertain benefits and causing a faster threshold decline.Fig. 10Threshold change process of the three strategy types of AN-IFF.

Conclusion

SDM is gaining recognition as a patient-centered medical decision-making model. However, its practical implementation encounters several challenges, such as time constraints, limited medical literacy among patients, and insufficient humanistic care literacy among medical staff. Building on the SDM implementation model, this study conceptualizes it as a dynamic, multi-party, distributed decision-making problem and proposes a multi-agent negotiation model grounded in IFS and fuzzy inference. The aim is to achieve optimal decision-making for both doctors and patients. The main innovations of this study include: (1) Developing an automated negotiation mechanism for DA and PA to enhance the efficiency of resolving doctor-patient decision-making conflicts; (2) Designing an offer evaluation method based on intuitionistic fuzzy set theory to enhance the agent’s ability to represent fuzzy information and accurately quantify user satisfaction with potential solutions; (3) Introducing a negotiation strategy based on a fuzzy inference system, enabling the agent to make concessions according to the uncertainty of decision preferences. Three distinct strategies are proposed to simulate decision-makers with varying characteristics, enhancing the realism of agents in doctor-patient communication; Three distinct strategies are proposed to simulate decision-makers with varying characteristics, enhancing the realism of agents in doctor-patient communication; The results show that the model outperforms existing methods in terms of negotiation efficiency, joint satisfaction, and fairness, while significantly contributing to the maximization of social welfare.

Ethics consideration

This study proposes the Agent-SDM system to simulate language interaction and strategy evolution in SDM. Due to the lack of publicly available SDM datasets, scenario data were collected based on pediatric asthma through semi-structured interviews with clinicians and families at Xiamen Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (XHMH). All participants provided informed consent, and the process followed medical ethical standards. No personal data was collected. All materials were anonymized and semantically desensitized, used solely for modeling role preferences and communication patterns within a virtual consultation context. No real clinical interventions were involved. Thus, this study does not constitute human or clinical research. Future clinical deployment will follow institutional ethics approval procedures and comply with data protection regulations, including the PIPL, HIPAA, and GDPR.

To construct the experimental negotiation domain, we abstracted key decision-making factors (e.g., cost, side effects, effectiveness, convenience, etc.) from the real-world pediatric asthma scenarios obtained during interviews. The domain design refers to typical issue structures used in prior healthcare SDM studies, while customizing value ranges and weights based on aggregated role preferences. Although the experiments focus on a representative negotiation instance, the proposed Agent-SDM framework is generalizable and can be extended to other medical conditions or decision contexts by adapting issue sets and role parameters accordingly.

Discussion

This study employs IFS to model preferences and hesitation in doctor–patient negotiations. Compared with many extended fuzzy frameworks, IFS offers a concise representation with strong interpretability and a clear semantic structure, making it particularly suitable for embedding into multi-agent dynamic negotiation processes in clinical settings. These advantages explain its adoption as the theoretical foundation of this study.

Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that other advanced fuzzy frameworks—such as PFS and SFS—provide greater theoretical expressiveness, especially in representing extreme uncertainty and high-dimensional preference information. Their main strength lies in expanding the dimensionality of evaluation and enhancing aggregation precision. However, such approaches often involve nonlinear constraints and complex parameter calibration, which substantially increase computational and calibration costs, thereby limiting their feasibility for real-time clinical decision support.

To address this, future research will explore incorporating PFS and SFS into the current framework as modular extensions. This will enable systematic comparisons across different fuzzy environments with respect to negotiation efficiency, satisfaction, and stability. Such efforts would help to clarify the applicability of these advanced fuzzy approaches to healthcare SDM while maintaining the interpretability and operational feasibility required in practice.

Limitations

Although the present study provides a solid foundation for IFS-based automated negotiation in SDM, several limitations should be noted: The applicability of the model is currently confined to specific disease scenarios.
