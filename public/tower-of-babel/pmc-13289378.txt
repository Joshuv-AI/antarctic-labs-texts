Retrospective analysis of the epidemiological characteristics, risk factors, survival rate, and drug resistance of candidemia and construction of a logistic regression prediction model (2013–2024)
Authors: Zhineng Xu, Yuanhong Wang, Xue Duan, Dehua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare differences between candidemia and non-candidemia in the past 12 years and to construct a predictive model of candidemia to enrich clinical data and improve the diagnosis and treatment of candidemia.MethodsA matched case-control study design was used to collect the clinical data of inpatients in a tertiary hospital in Yunnan from 2013 to 2024. The patients were divided into candidemia group and non-candidemia group (control group), and accurately matched at 1:1. SPSS was used to compare the differences in epidemiological characteristics, risk factors and survival. Drug sensitivity analysis of candidemia was performed by broth dilution method. Logistic regression analysis was performed using R language and a clinical prediction model was constructed.ResultsA total of 134 cases were collected, including 67 with candidemia and 67 without candidemia. Elderly men were significantly more susceptible to candidemia and non-candidemia. The time to positivity of blood cultures, hospitalization duration, urolithiasis distribution rate, and mortality rate of patients with candidemia were significantly higher than those of patients without candidemia (all P < 0.05). Five types of Candida were isolated from patients with candidemia, and the antifungal drug sensitivities of amphotericin B, anidulafungin, caspofungin, and micafungin to all detected Candida strains were 100%. Chronic kidney disease, hepatorenal syndrome, tigecycline and amikacin use, abdominal infection, and invasive pulmonary fungal infection may be potential risk factors for candidemia. Logistic regression analysis showed that the time to positivity of blood cultures (≥ 2 days) (odds ratio [OR] = 121.03, P < 0.001), number of concurrent infections during hospitalization (OR = 3.9, P < 0.01), and blood transfusion treatment (OR = 6.91, P < 0.05) were risk factors, whereas fibrinogen levels (OR = 0.77, P < 0.01) and C-reactive protein levels (OR = 0.98, P < 0.01) were protective factors. The receiver operating characteristic curve, calibration curve, and clinical decision curve showed that the model was meaningful.ConclusionThis study found that there were differences in multiple outcomes between candidemia and non-candidemia. The five potential risk factors analyzed can be used as predictors of candidemia and may provide a reference for the diagnosis and treatment of clinical candidemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13510-4.

Introduction

Fungal bloodstream infection (BSI) is a systemic infectious condition resulting from fungal invasion of the bloodstream. This condition progresses rapidly, is associated with a poor prognosis, and exhibits a high mortality rate [1, 2]. It predominantly affects hospitalized elderly patients [3]. Candidemia, defined as the presence of one or more positive Candida cultures in blood samples, is the most prevalent clinical manifestation of invasive candidiasis [4, 5]. The initial symptoms in patients are often subtle, and the disease progresses insidiously. Without timely diagnosis and intervention, candidemia can lead to complications such as multiple organ dysfunction and septic shock [6]. Studies have shown that there are significant differences in the prevalence of candidemia in different regions[7–9]. Because of the differences in epidemiology, geographical location and population distribution of patients [10, 11], the incidence rate also has obvious regional differences [12–14]. The patient’s condition is influenced by various factors, including underlying diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and cerebrovascular accidents[11]. Additionally, invasive procedures such as postoperative intubation, tracheal intubation, venous catheterization, surgery, hemodialysis, and blood transfusion, as well as the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and glucocorticoids, are significant risk factors [15, 16].

At present, more than 40 types of pathogenic Candida have been identified in candidemia, among which Candida albicans is the most common, and the number of infection cases is increasing[17]. Candida tropicalis, Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, and Candida krusei are also prominent species associated with candidemia. Regarding antifungal drug resistance, C. glabrata and C. krusei exhibit reduced sensitivity, and in some cases, resistance to antifungal agents such as fluconazole [18, 19]. The restricted availability of antifungal medications, coupled with the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains [20], raises significant concerns regarding the susceptibility of Candida spp. to these drugs [21]. Prompt and appropriate therapeutic intervention is critical to enhance the prognosis of patients with candidemia [22], necessitating the timely administration of targeted antifungal treatments [23]. Furthermore, detailed local epidemiological data and a thorough understanding of antifungal drug susceptibility profiles and trends are imperative for the informed selection of initial antifungal therapy [24].

Although the epidemiology, risk factors and predictive models of candidemia in China have also been reported in the literature [25–27], the content of these reports is limited and there is a big gap, In particular, there is no relevant literature in Yunnan. This study investigated the epidemiological characteristics, risk factors, survival rates, and drug resistance of patients with candidemia over the past 12 years in a tertiary hospital in Yunnan. Clinicians should attach great importance to the risk factors that may affect the prognosis of patients [28, 29]. Intervention measures should be taken as soon as possible, antifungal drugs should be rationally used, and patients should be provided with positive and effective diagnostic and treatment programs [12, 30].

Materials and methods

Retrospective investigation of medical records

All positive blood culture results from the First Hospital of Kunming between 2013 and 2024 were collected. Patients with Candida detected by blood culture were set as candidemia group, and patients with bacteremia caused by other pathogens except Candida were set as non-candidemia group. This study employed a case-control design. To control confounding factors as much as possible, the frequency matching method was used to randomly select 67 patients from the eligible patients as the control group, namely “patients with fever or suspected infection, positive blood culture, and non-candidemia during hospitalization,” with reference to the admission area and similar admission time of the case group, to improve the comparability of cases and controls in the clinical examination background. Simultaneously, the electronic medical records of the enrolled patients were queried. Patients with incomplete electronic medical records, no infection symptoms such as fever, chills, dyspnea, and hypotension, and possible skin contamination were excluded. Finally, the proportion of patients in the candidemia and non-candidemia groups reached 1:1. If the same bacteria were detected in multiple blood cultures, only the first-detected strains were recorded.

Epidemiological data and risk factors of the participants were collected and sorted

Detailed information on the patients’ medical records was collected, including general information (sex, age, and department) and basic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, and cerebral infarction). Clinical interventions (central venous catheter insertion, mechanical ventilation, indwelling catheter, drainage tube use, intensive care unit [ICU] treatment, and the use of glucocorticoids and antibiotics) were also recorded. In addition, the prognosis of the subjects was recorded; that is, whether they were discharged or died.

Collect laboratory test results from the participants

Laboratory test results of the enrolled patients during the period from admission to the detection of pathogenic bacteria by blood culture were collected, including routine blood test results using a blood analyzer (Sysmex, Japan), liver and kidney function test results using a biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland), and coagulation function test results using an automatic coagulation analyzer (STA-R Max, France).

Microbial identification, drug sensitivity test related instruments and reagents, drug sensitivity breakpoint judgment method

Blood culture was performed using the BD BACTEC 9240 and Mérieux BacT/Alert 3D systems. Positive samples were transferred to Columbia blood plates, MacConkey plates, and Sabouraud ‘s medium. After 24–48 h of culture at 35°C and 5% CO2, the strains were isolated and identified using the Vitek-2 Compact and Autof MS1000 mass spectrometer. The results of the two identification methods were mutually verified to ensure consistency. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Candida was determined using the YST08 card or Antu-Bio Candida kit. The results of the two methods were mutually verified to ensure that the results were consistent. MICs were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M60, M27, and M59 [31–33] guidelines. The quality control strains were C. albicans ATCC 14053, C. parapsilosis ATCC 22019, and C. krusei ATCC 6258. Specific operations are detailed in the supplementary materials.

Survival analysis of patients

Survival status data of patients with candidemia 60 days after admission were collected, and the survival curve was drawn using the Kaplan–Meier method in SPSS version 26.0.

Logistic regression analysis and clinical prediction model

The continuous variables in the risk factors collected were analyzed using the t-test or rank sum test using RStudio version 4.3.0 [34], whereas the categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-squared test to identify the relevant risk factors. Subsequently, univariate and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analyses were performed on the identified risk factors to determine the factors suitable for constructing a multifactorial model. A BSI model was developed using logistic regression in RStudio version 4.3.0. The performance of the model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel (2019 edition) and IBM SPSS26.0 were used to analyze the data. The t-test, chi-square test, and rank-sum test were used to compare the two groups of data. A two-tailed test was used to determine statistical significance, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. RStudio 4.3.0 was used to construct the clinical prediction model and draw the graph, and GraphPad Prism 9.0 was used for visual analysis.

Result

We analyzed the basic characteristics of sex, age, time to positive blood culture, and hospitalization time

A total of 7174 positive blood culture results were obtained between 2013 and 2024, of which 67 cases met the criteria and were identified as having candidemia. Of the remaining 7103 results, 67 cases that did not have candidemia were selected for the non-candidemia group. When comparing the intra-group distribution of candidemia and non-candidemia, it was found that the proportion of males was significantly higher than that of females; however, there was no significant difference in sex composition between the two groups (Fig. 1A; Table 1). At the same time, it was found that the age structure in the two groups was dominated by people over 64 years old (p < 0.05). The average age of patients with candidemia was 65.22 ± 22.85 years, and the average age of patients without candidemia was 67.39 ± 17.30 years. There was no significant difference in age composition between the two groups (Fig. 1B; Table 1).

The time to positive blood culture and hospitalization duration for candidemia and non-candidemia were compared. The average time to positive blood culture in the former group was 2.52 ± 1.30 days, and there was no significant difference in the group. The latter was 1.12 ± 0.37 days, mainly concentrated in < 3 days (P < 0.05). The average time to positive blood culture in patients with candidemia was significantly longer than that in patients with non-candidemia, as shown in Fig. 1C; Table 1. In terms of hospitalization time, the average hospitalization time of patients with candidemia was 30.8 ± 39.5 days, mainly concentrated in 7–21 days (P < 0.05). Non-candidemia was 15.3 ± 12.26 days, mainly concentrated in < 7 days (P < 0.05). The comparison between the two groups showed that the average hospitalization time of patients with candidemia was significantly longer than that of patients without candidemia, as shown in Fig. 1D; Table 1.

Distribution of inpatient departments

In the distribution of inpatient departments, the number of patients with candidemia was the highest in the ICU, with 22 cases, accounting for 32.84% of the total. Urology was the second most common specialty, with nine cases (13.43%). Geriatrics, respiratory medicine, and nephrology accounted for 11.94%, 10.45%, and 7.46%, respectively. The incidence of candidemia in these departments was relatively high. There were significant differences between departments, as shown in Fig. 2A; Table 2. The number of patients with non-candidemia was the highest in the ICU, with 13 cases (19.40%). The respiratory department was the second most common department, with 11 cases (16.42%). Subsequently, it was distributed in hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery, oncology, and neurosurgery, accounting for 8.96%, 7.46%, and 7.46% of the total, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2B; Table 2. The distribution of patients with non-candidemia was wider than that of patients with candidemia, which was distributed in the rehabilitation, orthopedics, infection, cardiology, neurology, and cardiothoracic surgery departments. The distribution of candidemia patients in the urology department was significantly higher than that of non-candidemia patients, and the difference was significant, as shown in Fig. 2C; Table 2.

Analysis of risk factors in patients with candidemia and non-candidemia

Before bacteremia, these patients had various underlying diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cerebral infarction, and cerebral embolism. The total number of underlying diseases is shown in Fig. 3A. The intra-group comparison of candidemia and non-candidemia showed that the proportions of hypertension, diabetes, and cerebral infarction/cerebral embolism were the highest (P < 0.05). Comparing the two groups, it was found that the number of underlying diseases in candidemia was higher than that in non-candidemia. Comparing the basic diseases, it was found that there were significant differences between the two groups in chronic kidney disease and hepatorenal syndrome (Fig. 3B; Table 1). Invasive operations were performed in both candidemia and non-candidemia, including surgery, tracheal intubation, thoracic puncture, arterial puncture, and subclavian central venous catheterization. The total number of specific statistics is shown in Fig. 3C. The intra-group comparison of the two groups showed that surgery, blood transfusion, arterial catheterization, subclavian central venous catheterization, and tracheal intubation accounted for the most in candidemia (P < 0.05), while surgery accounted for the most in non-candidemia (P < 0.05). By comparing the two groups, it was found that patients with candidemia underwent more invasive operations than patients with non-candidemia. Compared with each invasive operation, it was found that the number of subclavian central venous catheterization, blood transfusion history, arterial catheterization, and hemoperfusion therapy in candidemia was significantly higher than that in non-candidemia (Table 1; Fig. 3D). For the total number of co-infections during hospitalization, see Fig. 3E. The number of concurrent infections during hospitalization was compared between the two groups, and the number of pulmonary infections was the highest (P < 0.05). The number of intra-abdominal infections and invasive pulmonary fungal infections in candidemia was significantly higher than that in non-candidemia (Table 1; Fig. 3F).

Among cases of candidemia and non-candidemia during hospitalization, prophylactic use of more than two antibiotics is quite common, and the use rate of the former is much higher than that of the latter. Concurrently, the use of tigecycline, amikacin, biapenem, and cefmenoxime in candidemia was much higher than that in non-candidemia, whereas the use of meropenem was the opposite. Prophylactic antifungal drugs are more common in candidemia, mainly voriconazole, fluconazole, and caspofungin (Table 1).

Mortality and survival analysis

The mortality rate in patients with candidemia is high. The mortality rate of 67 patients with candidemia 60 days after admission was determined, and 21 of the 67 patients died, with a mortality rate of 31.34%. Of the 67 patients with non-candidemia, only two died, with a mortality rate of 2.99%. There was a significant difference between the two groups (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 3. For the 60-day survival curve of 21 dead patients with candidemia, as shown in Fig. 4, the median survival time was 14.50 days, and 5–20 days in the hospital were the risk periods for death in patients with candidemia.

Clinical prediction model of candidemia

Five predictors with high correlation coefficients were screened using a single factor and the LASSO method. The TTP of blood culture (≥ 2 days) (OR = 121.03, 97.5%CI = 22.61-647.95, P < 0.001), number of concurrent infections during hospitalization (OR = 3.9, 97.5%CI = 1.57–9.69, P = 0.003), blood transfusion therapy (OR = 6.91, 97.5%CI = 1.42–33.6, P = 0.017), FIB (OR = 0.77, 97.5%CI = 0.064–0.92, P = 0.003), and CRP (OR = 0.98, 97.5%CI = 0.97–0.99, P = 0.002). The ROC curve of the clinical prediction model is shown in Fig. 7B. The AUC value was 0.971, and the 95% confidence interval was 0.949–0.994. The calibration curve of the model is shown in Fig. 7C, and the predicted probability was in good agreement with the actual probability of the training set. It always maintains good consistency in the repeated 1000 sampling verification. The deviation between the predicted and actual values was small, and the average standard error was 0.021. The clinical decision maker curve (DCA curve), as shown in Fig. 7D, is outside the All curve, above the ‘None’ curve, and the prediction model between 0 and 1.0 has obvious clinical use value.

Discussion

The incidence of candidemia in the world is increasing worldwide [35]. This article retrospectively analyzed the situation of candidemia in the previous 12 years and systematically sorted out the key data of candidemia in our hospital, such as general data, distribution of admission departments, basic diseases, combined other site infections, survival analysis, strains, antifungal drug resistance, and candidemia prediction model.

It was observed that the susceptibility of males in both groups was higher than that of females. Liu et al. [11]. also reported that male patients were more likely to be infected with Candida. The average age of patients with candidemia was 65.22 years old, which was consistent with the average age of 65.7 years old reported by Saeed, N, K, et al. [36]. The hospitalization time of patients with candidemia was significantly longer, mainly due to the complexity and difficulty of treatment [37]. Although scholars such as Salmanton-Garcia have questioned the dogma of “at least 14 days of treatment” they have not denied the complexity of candidemia [38]. Therefore, the hospitalization arrangement for patients with candidemia should be reasonably formulated according to the actual situation. In addition, this study found that the TTP of blood culture of candidemia was generally longer than that of non-candidemia, which was consistent with the results of Ransom, E, M, et al. [39]

This study found that patients with candidemia were mainly concentrated in the ICU. Many researchers have pointed out that candidemia is particularly common in ICU [40–42]. Interestingly, the incidence of urinary candidemia in our hospital was also high. Many studies have found that Candida is an important pathogen causing urinary tract infections[43, 44]. Candida urinary tract infection may induce BSI [45–47]. Similarly, candidemia can also cause urinary tract infections through hematogenous spread or the retrograde urethral route[48].

This study found that the underlying diseases of patients with candidemia were hypertension, diabetes, and stroke. Similar studies have shown that the common underlying diseases in candidemia are hypertension and diabetes [49–52]. The number of cases of candidemia with underlying diseases was more than that of non-candidemia cases, and patients with hypertension and diabetes accounted for a large proportion regardless of whether they were complicated with candidemia. Some scholars have also pointed out that candidemia is also accompanied by other underlying diseases (such as cardiovascular disease, kidney disease and kidney transplantation, cancer and cirrhosis, etc.) [14, 51, 53, 54]. It is necessary to focus on and prevent the risk of candidemia in patients with underlying diseases.

This study found that surgery, bronchial intubation, central venous catheterization (CVC), blood transfusion, and arterial intubation were the most common invasive procedures performed in patients with candidemia. A number of studies have confirmed that CVC and surgery are risk factors for candidemia [13, 55–57]. The proportion of surgery in candidemia and non-candidemia is large, although there is no difference; surgery is still an important risk factor for candidemia [36].

Candidemia is often not a single infection, often accompanied by other parts of the infection, such as pneumonia, intraocular infection, urinary tract infection and septic shock [41, 47, 58–60]. The use of antibiotics is also an important risk factor for candidemia [61]. This study also showed the same results as above. Therefore, it is necessary to be alert to co-infection after infection, continue to pay attention to the patient’s condition, and implement reasonable antibacterial treatment. Some patients require long-term or life-long use of antibiotics to suppress infections that are considered incurable, which may increase the risk of candidemia [62]. Reducing the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics may reduce the incidence of hospital-acquired candidemia [63].

A number of studies have shown that the mortality rate of candidemia is at a high level [11, 12, 49, 64]. This study found that the 60-day mortality rate of patients with candidemia was 31.34%, which was significantly higher than that of patients without candidemia (2.99%), and the median survival time of patients with candidemia was 14.5 days. Hayashi, K, C et al. pointed out that antifungal therapy improved the 60-day mortality of candidemia within 72 h after fever [65]. Studies have also shown that for every additional day of hospitalization for candidemia, the adjusted in-hospital mortality rate increases by 3% [66]. Clinically, it is necessary to recognize the high mortality risk of candidemia, pay close attention to the disease progression of patients, and prevent it.

It has been reported that C. albicans and C. glabrata are the main pathogens of candidemia [67–69]. This is consistent with the distribution of pathogens in this study. Low resistance levels to fluconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and itraconazole were also observed. Aydin et al. reported that the overall resistance rates of candidemia to fluconazole, voriconazole, caspofungin, micafungin, and flucytosine were 3.7%, 0%, 2.5%, 1.8%, and 1.8%, respectively [70]. However, Dai et al. found that the resistance rates of C. albicans to fluconazole, voriconazole, and itraconazole were 32.32%, 23.53%, and 31.31%, respectively [41]. There are significant differences in the drug resistance rates of different Candida species in different regions. For Candida with high drug resistance, new drug therapy or combination therapy can be used, such as rezafenib or rezafenib combined with caspofungin in the treatment of candidemia [71, 72].

In this study, according to the epidemiological characteristics of candidemia, a total of five highly correlated predictors were analyzed. Studies have shown that longer TTP may predict C. glabrata, while shorter TTP may predict C. tropicalis [73]. Blood transfusion during the same hospitalization was also found to be an independent risk factor for candidemia [74]. In this study, we found that TTP of blood culture (> 2 days) and blood transfusion therapy were predictors of candidemia. We also found that the number of concurrent infections during hospitalization was a predictor of candidemia. This is closely related to an increase in the number of infections and the use of antibiotics for treatment. Studies have pointed out that reducing the chance of patients contacting these antibiotics can help prevent the occurrence of candidemia [75]. Many studies have found that CRP may be a predictor of candidemia [76–78]. The Kokkoris team found that the level of CRP in patients with candidemia was significantly reduced, and CRP was a protective factor for the predictive model of candidemia [78]. Interestingly, this study found that FIB was also a protective factor for the prediction model of candidemia, but there was no relevant literature reported.

Based on the above content data, a clinical prediction model was successfully constructed to predict the possibility of patients suffering from candidemia in Yunnan, China. Through the evaluation of ROC curve, calibration curve and DCA curve, the whole prediction model has certain feasibility. The model is helpful for clinical staff in the region to quickly determine whether potential patients may develop candidemia. In this way, the number of cases of candidemia can be reduced and its harm can be reduced. For patients who may be sick, intervention measures can be taken as soon as possible, and antifungal drugs can be used rationally to provide positive and effective diagnosis and treatment programs. However, when the missing data are supplemented, the average number supplement method may be used. Because the missing data are not randomly distributed, the average value supplement may introduce bias[79]. Although only LASSO analysis can achieve variable screening, the results are susceptible to data disturbance, lack of clinical prior integration, and coefficient deviation. Therefore, the robustness and generalization ability of the model must be confirmed by combining clinical knowledge and independent external verification [80, 81]. In addition, incomplete data and the possible deviation caused by missing data are limitations of our model.

Conclusion

To better diagnose and treat candidemia in clinical practice, this study found that elderly men, patients in ICU wards, patients with underlying diseases such as hypertension and diabetes, patients with chronic kidney disease and hepatorenal syndrome, and patients with multiple-site infections were more likely to develop candidemia. The TTP of blood cultures and the average hospitalization time for candidemia were generally higher than those for non-candidemia patients. The 60-day mortality rate in patients with candidemia was significantly higher than that in patients without candidemia. Antifungal drugs detected in vitro were generally effective against Candida. Logistic regression analysis showed that the TTP of blood culture (≥ 2 days), the number of concurrent infections during hospitalization, and blood transfusion treatment were risk factors. FIB and CRP levels were protective factors. The prediction model has certain value in the diagnosis of candidemia. However, owing to the data from a single-center retrospective study, there is a lack of some data. Therefore, more research is needed to verify.

Limitations of research

The model still has shortcomings. This study is retrospective in nature and the data source was limited to single-center data. Future research should conduct multi-center joint prospective research to obtain more representative data to construct the model and realize its robustness and generalization ability. In this study, the missing values were supplemented by the average, and only LASSO fitting analysis was used. The model may have a certain offset. At the same time, there was no external validation set or external hospital data to test the accuracy and stability of the model. Therefore, the results of this study are mainly applicable to risk prediction in this specific clinical scenario, and further verification should be conducted when used in other scenarios.
