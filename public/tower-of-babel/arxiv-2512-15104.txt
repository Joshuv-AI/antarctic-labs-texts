Abstract
We consider Markov chains on general state spaces in stationary random environment which are defined by a random mapping that is contractive up to a bounded perturbation. We prove their convergence to a limiting law, providing convergence rates. We also show that these processes are strongly mixing and estimate their mixing coefficients. Our results significantly extend those available in the literature. In particular, for some additive autoregressive processes with exogenous covariates we achieve mixing rates that are optimal up to logarithmic factors.

Remark 4.4
.

We now compare the technical details of our approach to the standard
drift condition
+
small set approach, used in a number of papers,

[
15
,
14
,
17
,
16
,
12

.

The drift condition guarantees that there is a sequence of stopping times at which the MCRE
returns to a given small (compact) set. Minorization condition on this compact set then permits
to couple two copies of the MCRE.

Our approach is quite different in this paper. To construct a coupling in
n

we use the first
n/2
steps to guarantee that the two copies of the MCRE get close

with high probability
. Then we start coupling the two chains at

It is possible, however, that at these time moments the chain is far away from a given
compact, due to the environment, e.g. look at the term

in (

). So the coupling
is not realized from minorization on a fixed compact small set, and the non-standard minorization condition
of Assumption
2.2
becomes useful as it concerns only the relative positions of the two chains.

For any
y_{1},y_{2}\in E
,
e\in E
, and
x\in F
, we have

|\mu(y_{1},x)-\mu(y_{2},x)|\leq(1-2ah)|y_{1}-y_{2}|+2hJ,

which implies that Assumption
2.1
holds with
\rho=1-2ah\in(0,1)
and
R=2hJ
.

To verify Assumption
2.2
, the minorization condition, let
x\in F
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(E)
be arbitrary. Consider two arbitrary points
y_{1},y_{2}\in E
such that

|y_{1}-y_{2}|\leq\frac{2R}{1-\rho}=\frac{2J}{a}.

Then we can write

\mathbb{P}(f(y_{i},x,\varepsilon_{0})\in A)=\int_{E}\mathds{1}(g(x,y_{1},y_{2},z)+\Delta f_{i}\in A)\,\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

(24)

where
\varphi
is the density of the standard
m
-dimensional normal distribution, and

g(x,y_{1},y_{2},z)=\frac{1}{2}(f(y_{1},x,z)+f(y_{2},x,z)),\quad\Delta f_{i}=f(y_{i},x,z)-g(x,y_{1},y_{2},z),\ i=1,2.

Note that, for
i=1,2
, the shift term
\Delta f_{i}
does not depend on
z
due to the definition of
f
, and it satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\Delta f_{i}|&\leq\frac{1-2ah}{2}|y_{1}-y_{2}|+\frac{h}{2}|H(y_{1},x)-H(y_{2},x)|\\
&\leq\frac{J}{a}(1-ah)\leq\frac{J}{a}.\end{split}

(25)

Applying the substitution
z=u-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2h}}\Delta f_{i}
in (
24
) and using the estimate (
25
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(f(y_{i},x,\varepsilon_{0})\in A)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\mathds{1}(g(x,y_{1},y_{2},u)\in A)\varphi\left(u-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2h}}\Delta f_{i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{J}{\sqrt{2h}a})^{2}}\int_{B_{1}(0)}\mathds{1}(g(x,y_{1},y_{2},u)\in A)\,\mathrm{d}u.

From this, one can choose the measure
\nu(x,y_{1},y_{2})
to be the uniform distribution over the ball of radius
\sqrt{2h}
centered at the midpoint

\frac{1}{2}\left(-2ah\,y_{1}-h\,H(y_{1},x)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(-2ah\,y_{2}-h\,H(y_{2},x)\right).

In this case, the minorization coefficient
\eta
in Assumption
2.2
can be chosen to be constant. Moreover, since
|y_{1}-y_{2}|\leq\frac{2J}{a}
, we have
\nu(x,y_{1},y_{2},B_{K}(y_{i}))=1
for
i=1,2
, for any
K>0
satisfying
K\geq\frac{J}{a}+\sqrt{2h}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

It was proved in
[
15
]
, under weaker assumptions, that
\mathcal{L}(Y_{n})
converges to a limiting law in total variation at a rate
O(e^{-cn^{1/3}})

for some
c>0
. Our present technology enables us to establish convergence at an exponential rate under the current assumptions.

Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR), also known as expected shortfall, are among the most widely used tail risk measures in finance
[
19
]
.
The remainder of this section is devoted to a financially motivated example illustrating the recursive computation of these risk measures via the SGLD iteration (
23
).
We show that the assumptions of Proposition
4.5
are satisfied in this setting, which allows us to apply the main results of the paper.

Let
X
denote the loss of a financial instrument over a given time horizon. For a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the Value at Risk (VaR) is defined as the lower
\alpha
-quantile of
X
:

\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X):=\inf\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(X\leq\xi)\geq\alpha\right\}.

For simplicity, we assume that
X
admits a strictly positive continuous density
f_{X}:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
. Under this assumption,
y_{*}=\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)
is the unique solution of

\mathbb{P}(X>y)\leq 1-\alpha.

Further, assuming
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR), also referred to as the expected shortfall, at level
\alpha
is defined by

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid X\geq\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)].

The CVaR is often favored in practice when a more robust risk measure is required, as it is coherent and satisfies properties such as subadditivity
[
2
]
, unlike VaR.

Rockafellar and Uryasev in
[
24
]
introduced the objective function

b(y)=y+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\big[(X-y)_{+}\big],\quad y\in\mathbb{R},

(26)

and showed that the risk measures
\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)
and
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)
can be characterized, respectively, as the set of minimizers and the minimum value of the convex function
b
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{y\in\mathbb{R}}b(y),

\displaystyle\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}}b(y).

Building on this observation, Bardou et al.
[
3
]
introduced a recursive stochastic approximation scheme for the numerical computation of
\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)
and
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)
.
In
[
13
]
, almost sure convergence of this scheme was established under decreasing step sizes and certain Lyapunov-type conditions.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a stationary sequence with
X_{0}\sim X
, and let
a>0
be a fixed parameter.
It is straightforward to verify that

b^{\prime}(y)=\mathbb{E}\big[H(y,X_{0})\big],

where

H(y,x)=1-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathds{1}_{\{x\geq y\}},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, one clearly has the uniform bound

|H(y,x)|\leq J:=\max\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},\,1\right),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, by Proposition
4.5
, for any step size
0<h<\frac{1}{2a}
,
Assumptions
2.10
and
2.2
are satisfied for the model under consideration with a suitable constant
\eta
.
Furthermore, for
\tilde{y}=0
we have

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big|f(\tilde{y},X_{0},\varepsilon_{1})\big|\right]\leq hJ+\sqrt{2h}\,\mathbb{E}\big[|\varepsilon_{1}|\big]<\infty.

As a consequence, by point 5 of Theorem
2.7
, for any initial condition
Y_{0}=y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{L}(X_{n},Y_{n})\longrightarrow\mu,\quad n\to\infty,

at an exponential rate, independently of the
\alpha
-mixing properties of the sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Here,
\mu
denotes the probability distribution of the pair
(X_{0},Y_{0}^{*})
, where
(Y_{n}^{*})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a stationary solution of the MCRE associated with the Langevin recursion (
23
).
The decay properties of the mixing coefficients
\alpha_{X,Y}(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are governed by the conclusions of Theorem
2.8
.

In order to apply the iteration (
23
) to the approximation of
\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)
, additional considerations are required.
Indeed, the limiting distribution
\mathcal{L}(Y_{0}^{*})
does not concentrate on the global minimizer of the objective function
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Instead, one obtains a probability measure with density proportional to
\exp(-U(y))
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
, where

U(y)=ay^{2}+b(y)

is the
regularized
objective.
While the introduction of an inverse temperature parameter into the scheme (
23
) enforces concentration of this measure, the regularization term remains a source of bias, in addition to the discretization error inherent in the scheme.

4.2
Stochastic volatility models

A prominent member of the class of models (
20
) describes asset prices with stochastic volatility.
Let
d:=1
, for simplicity, see
[
11
]
for a more general, multi-asset setting. Let
F:=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let

\zeta_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
be i.i.d. in
\mathbb{R}
with mean
0
and finite variance, independent of
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
b_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
be a real sequence with
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}^{2}<\infty
,
define the (causal) linear process
Z_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}\zeta_{n-k}
.
Define
X_{n}:=(\zeta_{n+1},Z_{n})\in F
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Z_{n}
is thought to describe the log-volatility of a financial asset. Certain empirical studies suggest that this
might be best described by non-Markovian processes, see Part II of
[
1
]
.

Fix
\rho\in(-1,1)
, this is to represent the correlation of the asset price with its volatility process.
There are two (independent) driving noises: that of the volatility (
\zeta_{n}
) and the asset’s own source of randomness,
(
\varepsilon_{n})
.

We define
\tilde{\sigma}(x_{1},x_{2})=e^{x_{2}}
. Finally, we consider the log-price of a financial asset to be described by
Y_{n}
at time
n
, satisfying the recursion

Y_{n+1}=\mu(Y_{n})+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}(X_{n})\varepsilon_{n+1}+\rho\tilde{\sigma}(X_{n})\zeta_{n+1},

that is, we set
\ell(x_{1},x_{2}):=\rho e^{x_{2}}x_{1}
and
\sigma(x_{1},x_{2}):=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}e^{x_{2}}
in (
20
).

It was proved in
[
11
]
that such models converge to a stationary state as
n\to\infty
but no convergence rates were
given. In
[
12
]
convergence rates were given in Example 3.4, but only under rather stringent conditions (the tail of
\varepsilon_{n}
had to be thick enough).
Now, using Theorem
2.7
, we may provide convergence estimates under mild conditions.

Notice that, if
Z
is an invertible linear process (see Chapter 3 of
[
4
]
for the concept of invertibility), then

\alpha_{X}(n)\leq\alpha_{Z}(n-1)
and there are results in the literature about
\alpha_{Z}
since

Z
is a linear process, see
e.g.
[
23
,
8
]
.

Proposition
4.1
, Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
imply that, depending on the decay rate of
\alpha_{Z}(\cdot)
, we may estimate
\alpha_{Y}(\cdot)
and also provide a convergence rate for
Y_{n}
to its limiting law.

4.3
Threshold autoregressive models

We now consider a particular case of model (
20
) with
d=1
and the multiple threshold AR
(1)
model. We keep the same assumptions for
\ell,\sigma
and the probability distribution of
\varepsilon_{1}
.
Let
-\infty=r_{0}<r_{1}<\cdots<r_{m-1}<r_{m}=\infty
be some real numbers and set
\mathcal{R}_{i}=(r_{i-1},r_{i}]
for
0\leq i<m
and
\mathcal{R}_{m}=\left(r_{m-1},\infty\right)
. Set also

\mu(x,y)=\mu(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(a_{i}y+b_{i}\right)\mathds{1}_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{m}}.

In this case, (
20
) is an extension of a model studied by
[
5
]
that allows exogenous covariates.

Proposition 4.7
.

If
\max\left(|a_{1}|,|a_{m}|\right)<1
, then (
21
) is valid
and the conclusion of Proposition
4.1

holds true.

Proof.

We only have to check condition (
21
). Set
\rho=\max\left(|a_{1}|,|a_{2}|\right)
,
b=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|b_{i}|
,
a=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|a_{i}|
and
r=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|r_{i}|
. Let us consider the following cases.

•

Suppose first that
y,y^{\prime}\in\cup_{i=2}^{m-1}\mathcal{R}_{i}
. Then

\left|\mu(y)-\mu(y^{\prime})\right|\leq\left|\mu(y)\right|+\left|\mu(y^{\prime})\right|\leq 2(ar+b).

•

Suppose next that
y\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
. If
y<0
and
y^{\prime}>0
, we have

\left|\mu(y)-\mu(y^{\prime})\right|\leq|a_{1}|\cdot|y|+|a_{m}|\cdot|y^{\prime}|+2b\leq\rho\left(|y|+|y^{\prime}|\right)+2b=\rho|y-y^{\prime}|+2b.

If now
y\geq 0
, then
r_{m-1}
is positive,
|y|\leq r
and
\left|\mu(y)\right|\leq ar+b
. we deduce that

\left|\mu(y^{\prime})-\mu(y)\right|\leq\rho|y^{\prime}|+ar+2b\leq\rho|y^{\prime}-y|+2ar+2b.

We have a similar bound in the reverse case
y^{\prime}\leq 0
.

•

Suppose next that
y\in\cup_{i=2}^{m-1}\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
. Then

\left|\mu(y^{\prime})-\mu(y)\right|\leq\rho|y^{\prime}|+ar+2b\leq\rho|y-y^{\prime}|+2ar+2b.

•

Finally, if
y^{\prime}\in\cup_{i=2}^{m-1}\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
y\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
, we get the same bound as in the previous point.

We conclude that (
21
) is satisfied with
R=2ar+2b
.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

Our condition
\max\left\{|a_{1}|,|a_{m}|\right\}<1
is somewhat stronger than the conditions

\max(a_{1},a_{m})<1\mbox{ and }a_{1}a_{m}<1

used for getting geometric ergodicity in Theorem
2.3
of
[
5
]
. On the other hand, our result allows to consider exogenous
covariates with nearly optimal mixing rates.

A multivariate autoregressive model

Finally we consider the multivariate model (

) with

\mu(x,y)=\mu(y)=Ay+r(y),

(27)

where

Proposition 4.9
