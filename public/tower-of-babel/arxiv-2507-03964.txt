Rigid spheres rising at constant speed through collections of hydrogels.
T. Mullin 1, T. Pettinari 2, J.A. Dijksman 2

Abstract
We present the results of an experimental investigation into buoyant rigid spheres rising through concentrated collections of hydrated hydrogel particles. The volume fraction of particles is such that the mechanical properties of the material are intermediate between a very viscous fluid and a soft solid. Despite the non-Newtonian character of these hydrogels, we find that when the surface of the material is free, an immersed buoyant sphere rises with a constant speed. The effects of the motion in the surrounding material are observed to be highly localized around the sphere. When the mass of the sphere is varied, its terminal velocity is observed to depend exponentially on the buoyancy. Qualitatively distinct behavior is found when a lid is present on the surface of the material, and in this case, a sublinear time dependence of the displacement is found. Linear motion of the sphere is accompanied by flow at the surface of the material whereas fluid movement is suppressed when a lid is applied. Other boundary condition variations are consistent with the perspective that constant speed motion is induced by the presence of a free boundary.

The use of a falling-bob viscometer to make estimates of the viscosity of Newtonian fluids is a standard laboratory procedure. It involves applying Stokes law for the motion past a sphere
(
Stokes 1851
)
to the terminal speed of rigid particles falling or rising in very viscous fluids or at very small lengthscales. Stokes law applies when
Re<<1
where the Reynolds number
Re=\frac{Ur}{\nu}
with
U
, the terminal speed of the sphere of radius
r
and
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity of the fluid. Typically, the law is valid for many viscous oils and fluids such as glycerine and syrups. The falling bob viscometer technique is nowadays highly refined
(
Tang 2016
;
Patramanis-Thalassinakis et al. 2023
)
where effects from the walls of the container
(
Ladenburg 1907
;
Matthieu Brizard et al. 2005
)
or the finite length of the apparatus
(
Tanner 1963
)
are taken into account. Moreover, this configuration has been used to investigate the motion of rigid spheres through suspensions of neutrally buoyant spheres over a range of volume fractions
(
Reardon et al. 2007
)
and suspensions of fine rods
(
Powell et al. 1989
)
. The method has also been extended to falling cylinders in narrow tubes
(
Průša et al. 2012
)
.

Typically, it is assumed that the fluid properties are independent of time and speed for falling-bob viscometers to be used effectively. However, these techniques can also be used to provide deeper insights into the flow behavior of some non-Newtonian fluids, such as viscoelastic materials
(
Cho et al. 1983
)
. A study using a shear thinning fluid found that the distance the sphere travels is
\propto t^{1/2}

(
McKinley 2002
)
. An explanation for this non-linear behaviour is provided by
(
Beris et al. 1985
)
who demonstrate that a build up of stagnant fluid around the sphere leads to an increase in its effective size and hence Stokes drag increases with time. In shear thickening fluids, a similar coupling has been observed between the effective size of the intruder and the viscous response of the material surrounding it for the non-linear slowdown of an intruder
(
Brassard et al. 2021
)
.

The focus of our current study is on a class of non-Newtonian materials, namely mixtures of hydrated hydrogel particles. Indicative of the ambiguous mechanical nature of these materials, we also refer to them as hydrogel suspensions when we discuss their overall fluid-like properties, or hydrogel packing when we discuss the static properties of the sedimented hydrogel particles. They have many remarkable features, including sensitivity to the frictional properties of the hydrogels, non-Newtonian flow profiles, critical packing fraction behavior in their high flow rate rheology and various forms of creep behavior
(
Workamp and Dijksman 2019
;
Jewel et al. 2018
;
Tapia et al. 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2024a
)
. Control of the properties of hydrogel suspensions in water can be achieved via the friction of hydrogel particles
(
Workamp and Dijksman 2019
)
, packing density
(
Shewan and Stokes 2015
;
Shewan et al. 2021
;
Tapia et al. 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
)
and even using the valency of added ions
(
Budtova et al. 1994
)
. In previous studies, the non-Newtonian fluid properties of dense hydrogel suspensions is paramount. For example, hydrogel suspensions have been shown to behave as Herschel-Bulkley fluids
(
Workamp and Dijksman 2019
)
when probed with standard rheological methods under steady state and constant driving rate conditions.

I.1
Constant speed motion

Our primary goal is to use the conceptually simple geometry of a falling-bob viscometer to investigate our counterintuitive observation that under some circumstances a buoyant, rising sphere can travel at a constant speed through samples of hydrated hydrogel suspensions. Results are obtained over a wide variety of sample conditions and sphere sizes and are surprising since hydrogel suspensions are materials with well-established non-Newtonian properties. Indeed, our previous work revealed a creep flow motion in which the speed typically decreases with the square root of time
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2024a
)
, indicating the presence of significant non-Newtonian behavior at these constant stress driving conditions. Hence, observation of constant speed motion motion over a wide parameter range is a new and surprising result.

In our investigations, we systematically vary the boundary condition of the hydrogel particle mixture to find that the difference between the constant speed motion observed in the current work and the nonlinear creep motion observed previously originates in the boundary conditions applied to the material. Despite the rheology of hydrogel suspensions being an intrinsic property of the microscopics of the material, we establish that its global mechanical response can be affected by the boundary conditions, which is highly unusual. Our work suggests that further theoretical work is required to describe the constitutive behaviour of hydrogel particle suspensions in the presence of specific boundary conditions.

I.2
Dependence on material properties and boundary conditions

The motion of rigid intruders is described using concepts based on the observed constant terminal speed of the spheres. We confirm that the material flows in a continuous way and the intruder moves smoothly as resolved using high speed video imaging. We use these observational facts to assign an effective constant viscosity
\eta_{\rm eff}
at a prescribed value of the stress, even though it is clear that the material under investigation is non-Newtonian. In fact, the effective viscosity characterization of the material reveals an exponential stress dependence of this property on the buoyancy stress exerted by the intruder. This suggests Arrhenius or Eyring-type
(
Eyring 1935
)
mechanisms to be a leading factor in the dissipative mechanisms of the material, albeit that they occur at much lower stress levels than the traditional pressure sensitivity of ordinary viscous fluids
(
Bridgman 1925
)
. Even so, the exponential stress dependence is also observed in the non-Newtonian, time-dependent creep behavior previously observed in hydrogel suspensions
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2024a
)
.

The dependence on temperature of the observed suspension flow behavior is observed to be significantly greater than the temperature dependence of water alone. This finding indicates that the effective viscosity of the suspension is not dominated by the solvent viscosity alone e.g. via a local mechanism such as a lubrication layers between the particles, as these would scale sinking or rising speeds proportionally with the water viscosity. In fact, particle image velocimetry analyses reveal that the deformation field of the hydrogel suspension is highly localised around the intruder.

Despite the localization of the flow, we show that the constant speed motion is consistently changed to a sub-linear time dependence of the displacement when a buoyant sphere rises towards a rigid lid floating on the upper boundary of the hydrogel suspension. We therefore systematically explore different boundary conditions of the hydrogel suspension to find that constant speed motion is found for all intruder motions towards a free boundary, including a boundary formed with a liquid on which the hydrogel suspension is floating.

Experimental set-up

A front view of the apparatus used in the majority of the experiments is shown in Fig.
1
. It comprises a
130
mm o.d.,
200
mm long plexiglass cylinder which has a
3
mm thick wall. The cylinder is aligned vertically in the laboratory frame of reference and is mounted on a
10
mm thick aluminium base which has a machined
129

Butler et al. 1994
) and hence help prevent the formation of bubbles over the periods of the experiment, which were typically months. It is observed that the presence of bubbles can significantly affect the reproducibility of the experimental results, probably as a result of the effect of compressibility and surface tension on the packing mechanics
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
)
and the relatively high Laplace pressure in small bubbles in comparison to the other stress scales that apply in the material. Repeatable results are obtained over the entire period of the experiment using this sample preparation protocol.

II.1
Upper Boundary Conditions

The cylinder is sealed at the top by a tight fitting lid containing a neoprene ’O’ ring. The sealed container helps to minimise any evaporation of the water. There is a
1
cm gap between the upper surface of the sample and the underside of the external lid. The upper boundary of the sample is a free surface for
\sim 20\%
of the experiments and a printed plastic lid is placed on the surface of the sample in the remainder of the experiments. The lid is 3D printed in plastic (PLA), hence it is partially aerated and weighs
\sim 50.3
g. Its bottom surface is smooth since it is formed in contact with the heating element of the 3D printer, and is hence comparatively free of defects. It is placed on the surface of the hydrogel packing and a thin layer of water is squeezed on onto the upper surface. The lid is lightweight and the results of an investigation into the effects of confinement
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2024a
)
indicate its contribution to the bulk stress on the material is minimal.

Finally, a third upper boundary condition is imposed using a steel mesh placed on the surface of the sample. The stainless steel mesh was circular and contained an array of approximately square holes formed by
0.5
mm wires which cross orthogonally. Meshes of this type are used in devices such as cafetieres. It has a diameter of
100
mm, thickness
1.0
mm and a pore size of
0.2
mm and weighs
\approx 3.6
g. Hence water may pass through the mesh easily, while hydrogels are held in position by the mesh. The surface of the mesh is very rough, providing a no-slip boundary condition to the hydrogels.

A time lapse set of images are superposed on the frontview of the apparatus in Fig.
1
to give an impression of the upward motion of a ping-pong ball. To initiate the experiment a buoyant ping pong ball is pushed down to the centre of the base of the cylinder using a
1
mm diameter metal rod which has a hoop at one end. Smaller spheres were manipulated with a similarly shaped
0.5
mm diameter rod. A settling period of
10
minutes is allowed before the ball is released on the centre line and the hoop is carefully moved to the side of the cylinder. The timescale for the insertion and release of the sphere are typically much shorter than the transit time through the sample.

II.2
Particle tracking

The motion of the sphere is tracked by capturing images using a computer controlled camera at set intervals ranging from
5
to
100
seconds depending on the buoyancy of the ball. In the case shown in Fig.
1
the images are taken at
40

400

II.3
Sample preparation

Sample preparation proceeds as follows. Dry hydrogel powder is slowly mixed with the water which is gently stirred and left to settle for two hours between additions. The particles swell as they absorb water and sink to the bottom of the cylinder as they are slightly more dense than the fluid. The sample is gently stirred between each charge. The hydrogels absorb the water efficiently and form a layer of swollen particles at the bottom of the cylinder with a clear layer of fluid above. Measurements of the depth of the hydrogel layer as a function of the added mass of dry powder is linear as in previous experiments
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
)
. This finding is consistent with the hydrogel particles being essentially frictionless spheres
(
Gong 2006
;
Workamp and Dijksman 2019
;
Shakya et al. 2024
)
, which settle to a random close packing. The hydrogel particle packing reaches the surface of the water at a dry hydrogel content
\phi\approx 6
g/L where
\phi
is defined as the weight of the dry powder divided by the total volume of water in grams per Litre (g/L). Once the layer has reached the surface, the addition of further quantities of dry hydrogels does not give any detectable change in the layer height and presumably there is a small compression of the particles throughout the layer. The average size of the swollen particles is measured to be
\sim 1
mm. The value of
\phi
used in the majority of the experiments reported here is set to
6.35
g/L.

The experiments with rising spheres were performed over a period of approximately one year in a laboratory constructed in TM’s home which is a well-insulated property with a thatched roof. This provides a good short-term stable temperature environment. The temperature of the sample is recorded at regular intervals and for the majority of the period it is found to be
19.0\pm 1.0
°C. Over the summer months the temperature varied outside of this range and the effects of this are reported below. However, the majority of the data given below is collected with the temperature within the given range. We will see below that this temperature window gives at most a
10\%
variation of measured travelling speeds, which is insignificant for the effects considered, given the large range of travelling speeds explored via the variation of buoyancy stress and intruder size.

Experiments were also performed in a laboratory environment at the University of Amsterdam, under standard laboratory temperature control. In these cases a suspension of hydrogel spheres was formed in a square plexiglass box of dimensions
25\times 25\times 40
cm following our established protocol using dry hydrogel spheres with triply boiled Amsterdam tap water at a density of
6.09
g/L unless otherwise noted. To initiate the experiments, a rod with a hoop was mainly used to place the sphere at the bottom, and in other cases a small magnet was placed inside the ball. An external magnet was used to hold and release the ping pong ball from the bottom of the container.

In the Amsterdam experiments the software TroublePix was used with a Lumenera Lt225C high speed camera to record a video of the motion at a frame rate of
71
frames/second. Still images were captured using a Nikon D5200 camera with ball tracking imaging performed using MaxTraq and Matlab. The flow field was measured using Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV). In this, the plane of motion of the sphere was illuminated by two vertical green laser sheets. Spatial averaging which co-moves with the intruder was used. Matlab integrated software “PIVlab” was used to analyse the sequences of images.

II.4
Intruder specifications

Ping pong balls are manufactured from Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene (ABS) plastic, are
40\pm 0.1

2.73\pm 0.2

(

Changing the buoyancy of a ping-pong ball is relatively straightforward as water can be injected into the ball using a hypodermic needle, the injection hole sealed and the weight of the ball measured using an electronic balance. The buoyancy force can then be estimated using the difference in mass between the sphere and the displaced water. We assume that the stress
\sigma_{S}
that an intruder exerts on the hydrogel suspension is its effective weight divided by its projected cross sectional area.
The main set of experiments were performed using ping-pong balls with a hydrogel sample of 6.35 g/L. A second set of experiments were carried out using a hollow 20mm diameter polypropylene ball (Precision plastic ball co.). Injecting water and weighing was again used to change and estimate the buoyancy. The value of
\phi
was 6.0 g/L. A third set of experiments were performed with hollow plastic balls. The balls again came from the Precision Ball Co., were made from a range of plastics (polyamide, polypropylene, PTFE, polyethylene) and had diameters
25.0,20.0,19.0
mm. These hollow plastic balls were weighed using an electronic balance and the the weight was used to compute the buoyancy stress. The value of
\phi
used was 6.15 g/L for these experiments. Finally, in one experiment, a glass sphere with a diameter of 25mm was used.

III.1
Time Histories with a Free Surface

As discussed above, the buoyancy of the ping-pong ball can readily be changed by adding small measured quantities of water to its interior. The time histories obtained over a range of buoyancies are shown in Fig.
3
(a). It can be seen that a straight line provides a satisfactory least squares fit to each time history with typically only a 3% root mean squared error, and hence there is a linear dependence of distance on time. A wide range of buoyancies was explored using this approach, from a sphere without added water (
\sigma_{S}=240
Pa) and the total rise time is
\sim 2
minutes to one with
\sigma_{S}=130
Pa, where the sphere takes
\sim 2
hours to rise through the sample. We show all the
\delta(t)
behaviour for the experiments with ping-pong balls in a sample with
\phi=6.35
g/L and a free upper surface in Fig.
3
(a).
A fixed terminal velocity is found for each value of
\sigma_{S}
in the specified sample. This suggests an interpretation of the results using the laws of standard Stokes viscometry might be possible. Of course, the connection cannot be complete since we shall see that the rise velocity depends exponentially on buoyancy; the hydrogel sample has clear non-Newtonian properties. Even so, models of rigid particles in suspensions
(
Cheng and Law 2003
)
suggest that an effective viscosity can be ascribed to suspensions of particles in fluids. Following this approach we extract an effective viscosity of the hydrogel medium from the constant intruder velocity data with

\eta_{\rm eff}=\frac{2\Delta\rho R^{2}g}{9U},

where
\eta_{\rm eff}
is the effective dynamic viscosity of the fluid,
R
is the radius of the sphere,
\Delta\rho=\rho_{I}-\rho_{h}
is the difference in density between the intruder and hydrogel medium respectively,
g
is gravity and
U
is the terminal velocity of the sphere. Strictly speaking, this formula is only valid for a Stokes flow in an unbounded domain. If the fluid medium used here was a Newtonian viscous fluid, wall and end effects would be significant for the geometry used here. However, as was found in
(
Pal and Kudrolli 2021
)
and reported below, the hydrogel suspension motion is only significant close to the sphere, so we choose the simplified analysis for the present study. We will show PIV measurements below which also indicate that the flow field is localised around the sphere.

The effective viscosity approach allows us to rescale all the acquired displacement data for one sample onto a master curve that follows
\delta\propto t
. We show the result of this analysis in Fig.
3
(b). An excellent collapse over three orders of magnitude confirms the constant speed dynamics of an intruder rising towards a free surface.

The collapse of the data provides an important message. It is possible that the time dependence observed previously
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2024a
)
could arise from a gradient in the properties of the sample resulting from concentration variations or the effects of hydrostatic pressure, for example, or other gradients because of the sensitivity of the viscosity to packing density around the random close packing limit
(
Shewan and Stokes 2015
)
. The constant speed motion, instead, highlights that concentrated samples of hydrated hydrogels which do not display spatiotemporal density variation effects can be consistently produced.

A graph of the effective viscosities of the time histories presented in Fig.

\sigma_{S}

)
and global confinement stress

)
, which is consistent with material response previously reported for these materials.

IV
Flow structure within the hydrogel suspension

Figure 6:

(b-d) Visualisation of the flow field: (b) the horizontal velocity clearly shows a quadrupolar nature, with a slight vertical asymmetry; (c) the vertical velocity of the ball, marked by the red arrow in the color bar on the side, is an order of magnitude bigger than the backflow, marked by the dark blue arrow, and a factor 5 bigger than the small clump of hydrogel particles dragged along by the ping pong ball; (d) details of the flow revealed by constraining the color scale. The constant speed motion of the rising intruder begs the question if the flow field around the slowly moving sphere bears similarity to a Stokes flow. To investigate this, we perform experiments with ping pong balls rising to a free surface in a hydrogel suspension in the square container illuminated with a pair of green laser sheets as shown in Fig.
6
. We analyze the measured flow fields around the sphere using the software PIVlab
(
Thielicke and Stamhuis 2014
)
applied to the videos recorded with the high-speed camera. The videos were pre-processed using
intensity capping
to reduce the noise from the brightest spots which arise from scattering of the laser illumination by impurities in the hydrogel suspension. We also remove the image of the ping pong ball from the region of interest for the PIV analysis using a mask.

The flow field analysis is performed using a
Multipass FFT window deformation algorithm
. In particular we used three passes with an interrogation area of 80x80, 40x40 and 20x20 pixels respectively (corresponding to approximately 18x18, 9x9 and 5x5 mm) with 50% overlap and with the
“Extreme” correlation robustness
option. This option has been shown to yield results with high signal-to-noise ratio in the presence of noise
(
Thielicke and Sonntag 2021
)
. We also employ a standard approach of using different frame jumps to measure the slow velocities far away from the moving intruder
(
Gollin et al. 2017
)
.

Finally, we remove the few outliers (which is a result of out-of-plane motion or poorly lit areas) through two velocity validation algorithms, automatically implemented in PIVlab. We first filter the velocity vectors based on the magnitude of each component, requiring that it does not deviate more than 8 standard deviations from the mean, and then we apply the
local median filter

(
Westerweel and Scarano 2005
)
. This procedure yields a Valid Detection Probability (VDP) of higher than 98%. A typical PIV result from an experimental video is available as Supplementary Information.

The resulting PIV fields are averaged within a domain that co-moves with the steadily rising sphere obtained over 700 frames. The velocity field
u
representing left-right motion is shown in Fig.
6
(b). The quadrupolar flow structure as required by mass conservation can be clearly distinguished, but there is a weak vertical asymmetry in the profile. The velocity component parallel to gravity
v
is shown in Fig.
6
(c). The colour scheme highlights that the upward speed of the sphere is significantly higher than the return downward speed of the water in the hydrogels. This is reasonable since the downward counterflow required by conservation of mass is spread over a wide area around the sphere. To highlight the structure of the additional flow field induced by the shear between the sphere and the hydrogel sample, we constrain the colour scale in Fig.
6
(d). There is a large wake flow dragged upwards with the sphere, and there is also a large volume of hydrogel particles pushed ahead of the sphere. However, adjacent to the sphere, the hydrogels are mainly stationary, even though a no-slip boundary condition exists between the water and the sphere. Combining the velocity components into
|\textbf{v}|
allows for a comparison of PIV results with tracking measurements and of the equatorial velocity component
|\textbf{v}|(r)
with the Stokes solution, Fig.
6
(e). We observe that the hydrogel fluid velocity profile has a very short decay length when compared with the long range Stokes solution.

Implicit in our arguments thus far is that the medium flows in a continuous way. In practice our sample comprises a mixture of polydisperse, deformable, hydrogel beads and water which could give rise to discrete displacement steps from e.g. stick-slip or other intermittent dynamics. We show such intermittency cannot be detected, even it high spatiotemporal resolution. In Fig.
7
the results of an experiment on the motion of a ping-pong ball recorded using the high speed imaging system described above that there is no evidence for discrete steps in the motion. Note that these results were obtained using the square container setup. The same qualitative behaviour was observed in separate experiments with a circular container, indicating that side-wall effects on the motion are also not significant.

V
Temperature and Diffusion effects

The constant speed behaviour suggests that a water-based lubrication mechanism may play a role in the dynamics of the rising sphere. We can test this hypothesis by measuring the effective viscosity of a sample under varying environmental temperature. The sample temperature is recorded in all experiments and the buoyancy stress is kept fixed for this particular investigation. We plot in Fig.
8
the effective viscosity of a hydrogel sample versus temperature with
\phi=6.35
g/L with a buoyancy stress of
\sigma_{S}=213
Pa. The temperature range covered is
17
to
27
°C. Also included in Fig.
8
are data points for water taken from tabulated values
(
Mareš 2009
)
. The data sets are normalised to be unity at
20
°C. The fitted lines have an Arrhenius form; it can be seen that the hydrogel sample has a significantly stronger dependence on temperature than water. We therefore conclude that a simple water-based lubrication mechanism, for example in the observed slip between the sphere and the hydrogel sample found here and reported elsewhere
(
Pal and Kudrolli 2021
)
, cannot be the only factor that plays a role in the effective viscosity of the two-phase medium. We can speculate on other temperature dependent mechanisms that contribute to the effective viscosity of the hydrogel sample. One of these can be the temperature dependence of the stiffness of the hydrogels
(
Dijksman et al. 2017
)
, which controls their poroelastic behavior
(
Chan et al. 2012
)
. However, poroelastic effects typically have a timescale that is the ratio of the stiffness and the viscosity, which for the system observed here both decrease with temperature, partially compensating this effect.

Another local mechanism that can drive motion of an intruder is local particle diffusion in the hydrogel suspension. It is thus interesting to understand how fast the intruder moves with respect to diffusive processes in the hydrogel suspension. The intruder motion is of the order of
10^{-6}
to
10^{-3}
m/s. There are two types of diffusive processes involved. At the molecular level motion within the water is one, but also the hydrogel particles close to the sphere are affected by passage of the intruder
(
Hsiau and Hunt 1993
)
. More intensive Particle Tracking Velocimetry could aid in extracting the mean squared displacment
D
of the hydrogel particles. With the intruder speed
U
and a characteristic lengthscale
L
, one can estimate a Peclet number
Pe=UL/D
. From the available dyed tracer particle measurement we estimate that hydrogel particles do not diffuse more than a millimeter during the course of an experiment, putting an upper bound on their mean squared displacement
D<10^{-8}
m
2
/s. The diffusion constant of water is around
10^{-6}
m
2
/s, suggesting that the Peclet number should be larger than one for both the linear and sublinear scenarios. This simplified interpretation may be more subtle in practice: for example we expect
D
to be
U
dependent, as the diffusive motion of the hydrogels is driven not by thermal motion of the solvent molecules, but by the movement of the intruder
(
Hsiau and Hunt 1993
)
, involving a self-fluidization process that is commonly observed to be present for dry granular materials
(
Campbell 1997
;
Dijksman et al. 2011
;
Kamrin et al. 2024
)
. The typical lengthscale involved is also not easily identified. We thus see several avenues for future research in exploring the role of microscopic mechanisms that may contribute to drive the slow flow in hydrogel suspensions.

VI
End boundary conditions

All the data we have discussed thus far displays a linear dependence of distance on time for spheres moving towards a free boundary. As discussed in the introduction, previous results for the motion of heavy spheres through hydrated hydrogels
(
Dijksman and Mullin 2022
;
Dijksman and Mullin 2024a
)
which correspond to cumulative 200+ measurements show
d\propto t^{1/2}
. The main difference between the two configurations is that in the present study, the ping-pong balls rise towards a free surface whereas the heavy spheres and cylinders used in earlier work were descending towards a rigid boundary. A question which may be asked is whether the approached end boundary condition plays a role in the motion of the intruder.

We therefore compare the example intruder motion data from Fig.
1
obtained from an intruder moving towards a free boundary, with observations of the same intruder motion moving in the same hydrogel sample towards a light rigid lid floating on the hydrogel sample. We observe that the motion of the buoyant sphere moving towards the floating lid is clearly sub-linear. The example data for sub-linear motion shown in Fig.
9
is best fitted with a power law of
\delta\propto t^{\alpha}
with
\alpha=0.73
, as can be seen in the double logarithmic inset in Fig.
9
.

After systematically exploring this positive buoyancy case for a hydrogel suspension confined between two solid boundaries (a lid at the hydrogel surface and the container wall at the bottom), we also investigate two other boundary conditions. Firstly, we measure the motion of an intruder with a positive buoyancy and a packing confined by a stiff, porous metal mesh. Secondly, we investigate the motion of intruders with both positive and negative buoyancies with a hydrogel sample confined between two fluid boundaries (air at the top, heavy liquid at the bottom). Finally, we consider flow field aspects that pertain to the boundary conditions, visualizing the strength of the displacement field close to the boundary at the top of an unconstrained hydrogel packing. These additional observations force us to conclude that the end boundary conditions play a crucial role in setting the overall flow behaviour of intruders in the hydrogel packing.

Sphere moving towards a rigid floating lid.

To systematically address the issue of the effects of end boundary conditions, a sequence of experiments are performed where a printed plastic lid sits on top of the swollen hydrogels. The sample used as before has
\phi=6.35

As outlined above in the discussion of the results in Fig.

(

Thus, we systematically explore the effects of a lid on
\delta(t)
behavior. The results of tests with ping pong balls of various weights are shown in Fig.

Here,
\eta_{\rm eff}
is again a prefactor that sets the speed of motion. Despite the non-Newtonian nature of the flow behavior, we refer to it as the effective viscosity. We can verify which rescaling coefficient provides the best collapse of the data; plotting
(\delta\eta_{\rm eff})^{1/\alpha}(t)

\alpha=0.75
provides the best collapse of the data and show this result in Fig.

\eta_{\rm eff}
that we use to characterize the strength of the sublinear behavior in Fig.

We show in Fig.

This boundary condition can is rough and hence suppresses vertical and radial motion of the hydrogels at the top of the packing. However, the mesh is also very light. The results indicate that the mesh has very little effect on the constant speed motion of the ping-pong ball. Moreover, the surface of the sample remains flat during the motion of the ball, suggesting that the constant speed motion is not an end effect imposed by having a surface structure imposed, suggesting that flexible rough lids would also not create sublinear motion. We cannot exclude the possibility that a smooth yet flexible boundary such as the surface tension boundary conditions from

Sinking towards a free, fluid boundary.

Experiments were performed to investigate whether the constant speed behavior exists for heavy spheres that

VII
Conclusions

We performed falling-ball viscometry tests of hydrogel suspensions. We used hollow plastic spheres to observe the dynamics of a rising sphere as a function of time. The spheres rise with
\delta\propto t
in cases with a free surface on the hydrogel suspension. Each run of the experiment has this feature. The ascent rate is found to depend exponentially on the buoyancy stress i.e.
\propto\exp{(\sigma_{S})}
and therefore there is clear evidence for non-Newtonian behavior in the hydrogel suspension. We confirm the non-Newtonian behavior with temperature tests and flow field visualization, both of which reveal non-Stokesian dynamics. We contrast the linear rise dynamics with creep flow found earlier to confirm a dependence of the flow behavior on the boundary conditions. An intruder sinking towards a free boundary also shows linear displacement dynamics, and we find that when a freely floating lid is placed on the surface of the material,
\delta=t^{\alpha}/\eta_{\rm eff}
, with
\alpha\neq 1
. The qualitative difference between the two types of motion is accompanied by clear evidence of flow at the free surface. The consistency of the results indicates that theoretical progress may be possible using continuum models in these flows that include boundary conditions as an effective way to tune the rheology of the bulk material.
