Metabolic cost of information processing in Poisson variational autoencoders
Hadi Vafaii, Jacob L. Yates Redwood Center for Theoretical Neuroscience, UC Berkeley

Abstract
Computation in biological systems is fundamentally energy-constrained, yet standard theories of computation treat energy as freely available. Here, we argue that variational free energy minimization under a Poisson assumption offers a principled path toward an energy-aware theory of computation. Our key observation is that the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence term in the Poisson free energy objective becomes proportional to the prior firing rates of model neurons, yielding an emergent metabolic cost term that penalizes high baseline activity. This structure couples an abstract information-theoretic quantity—the coding rate —to a concrete biophysical variable—the firing rate —which enables a trade-off between coding fidelity and energy expenditure. Such a coupling arises naturally in the Poisson variational autoencoder ( 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -VAE)—a brain-inspired generative model that encodes inputs as discrete spike counts and recovers a spiking form of sparse coding as a special case—but is absent from standard Gaussian VAEs. To demonstrate that this metabolic cost structure is unique to the Poisson formulation, we compare the 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -VAE against 𝒢 relu \mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}} -VAE, a Gaussian VAE with ReLU rectification applied to latent samples, which controls for the non-negativity constraint. Across a systematic sweep of the KL term weighting coefficient β \beta and latent dimensionality, we find that increasing β \beta monotonically increases sparsity and reduces average spiking activity in the 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -VAE. In contrast, 𝒢 relu \mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}} -VAE representations remain unchanged, confirming that the effect is specific to Poisson statistics rather than a byproduct of non-negative representations. These results establish Poisson variational inference as a promising foundation for a resource-constrained theory of computation.

Introduction

Modern artificial intelligence (AI) has achieved impressive breakthroughs with no slowdown in sight. However, this achievement comes at a serious cost: mainstream AI models are energetically inefficient, posing a global sustainability threat
(
21
)
. Powering models like ChatGPT consumes gigawatt-hours, and energy is quickly becoming the unavoidable bottleneck for AI progress
(
73
)
. This is a fundamental physical constraint we cannot engineer our way around—there is an urgent need to address this from first principles.

The energy inefficiency of mainstream AI systems originates from a critical design principle:
the decoupling of energy and computation

(
35
;
15
)
. There are no mechanisms internal to architectures like transformers that relate computation to energy expenditure. For a transformer, every token is created equal, as far as voltage goes. This is wasteful by design.

In sharp contrast, energy-efficiency is a core principle of biological computation
(
58
;
43
;
47
)
. Brains run on
\sim\!\!20
watts
(
6
)
, yet perform computations that require megawatt-scale data centers to approximate. This efficiency is likely driven by the
efficient coding hypothesis

(
7
;
8
;
9
)
, which states that brains adapt to the statistics of their environments
(
55
)
, minimizing metabolic cost
(
5
;
42
;
44
)
. A complementary possibility is that the brain’s representational form—discrete spiking events—determines the cost structure of the computation itself.

Neuromorphic computing aims to bridge biological and artificial computation
(
40
)
, where event-driven architectures like Intel’s Loihi
(
14
)
already “think” in spikes and energy. But we still lack rigorous theoretical foundations to inform future algorithm and hardware co-design. This motivates the need for an energy-aware theory of computation that goes beyond the current frameworks, which are limited to time and space complexity
(
56
;
71
)
.

1
recently approached this from the hardware perspective. He argued that neuromorphic computing has fundamentally different energy scaling compared to the von Neumann architecture. In conventional systems, energy is proportional to total algorithmic work: every operation incurs a fixed cost regardless of what is actually being computed. In neuromorphic systems, energy is instead proportional to the cumulative change of state across the computational graph: if a neuron does not spike and its state does not change, no energy is expended.
1
further showed that the dominant energy terms all scale with the average firing rate, making sparsity the primary lever for efficiency. However, this analysis addresses only the hardware side. The algorithmic and theoretical foundations remain missing.

Here, we demonstrate how
Poisson variational inference

(
64
;
65
)
naturally leads to the emergence of an energy-aware objective that learns to trade computational accuracy for energy expenditure. We contrast this with standard Gaussian variational inference
(
31
;
16
;
17
;
18
)
, revealing that such a metabolic cost term is critically absent from the Gaussian formulation. We provide a theoretical explanation using information geometry
(
4
)
: Poisson and Gaussian distributions have fundamentally different geometries, and only Poisson realizes the kind of energy-computation coupling that
1
argues for. We then conduct comprehensive experiments that confirm these theoretical predictions.

1.0.1
Contributions.

We establish from probabilistic first principles that variational inference under Poisson assumptions naturally produces an emergent metabolic term that
makes silence cheap
, and
couples information rate to firing rate
. This cost structure is strikingly similar to what
1
arrives at from hardware principles: that energy-efficient neuromorphic computation requires algorithms where energy scales with change-of-state rather than total work, and that sparsity (silence) should be free. The convergence of these two independent lines of reasoning—hardware and information theory—positions Poisson variational inference as a promising foundation for resource-constrained theories of computation that treat energy expenditure as a core consideration.

To fully specify

The latent representational form.

The choice of prior and the approximate posterior is particularly important, because it determines the

Residual parameterization in the

The

3.1
KL divergence: Poisson versus Gaussian

Despite the local similarity (
eqs.

10
and
11
), the approximate KL terms (
eqs.

12
and
13
) reveal two fundamental structural differences.

3.1.1
The first difference concerns
scaling
.

For the Poisson, the KL is proportional to the prior rate
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
; for the Gaussian, the mean-shift cost is scaled by the precision
1/{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}
, which is independent of the prior mean. This has a direct consequence for what each model considers “cheap”: the Poisson objective can reduce coding costs by lowering the absolute firing rate (
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}\to 0
), i.e., “silence is cheap”; while the Gaussian can reduce costs by inflating the prior variance (
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}\to\infty
), i.e., “ignorance is cheap”. Additionally, the Poisson scaling (
eq.

8
) is reminiscent of the L1 penalty in sparse coding
(
42
)
, but with an adaptively modulated penalty strength
f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})
rather than a fixed coefficient.

3.1.2
The second difference concerns
translation invariance
.

The Gaussian mean-shift term depends only on the difference
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\mu}}^{2}=({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}
, so the cost is identical whether
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0}
is 0 or 1000. In other words, the Gaussian framework has no metabolic floor and is indifferent to the absolute magnitude of activity. In contrast, the Poisson KL breaks this invariance: its linear dependence on
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
grounds the computation in a regime where zero activity is the unique lowest-cost state.

3.1.3
The differences arise from information geometry.

Both differences are consequences of information geometry
(
4
)
. The KL divergence is locally quadratic in the natural (canonical) parameters of the exponential family. For the Poisson, the natural parameter is
\log\lambda
, and the Fisher information metric in this natural space is
I=\lambda
. This yields a curvature scaled by the rate itself:
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}\,(\log{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})^{2}
. For the Gaussian mean variable
\mu
, the natural parameter is scaled by variance, but the metric is determined by precision (
I=1/\sigma^{2}
), which is, crucially, independent of the mean. This specific coupling between the metric and the parameter magnitude in the Poisson distribution is what gives rise to the emergent metabolic cost (but is absent in the location-scale Gaussian family).

3.1.4
Summary so far.

Unlike the Gaussian case, the Poisson KL admits a metabolic cost interpretation, owing to its linear dependence on the prior rate:
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\approx{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}(\log{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})^{2}
:

Poisson KL: a metabolic cost term interpretation

To encode information efficiently, maintain low baseline firing rates whenever possible.

3.2
The Poisson KL divergence term couples firing rate to information rate

The previous section established that the Poisson KL is proportional to the prior firing rate
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
. We now argue that this mathematical structure carries a deeper significance, by examining what the KL term itself measures.

3.2.1
Is KL “just” a regularizer?

In much of the machine learning literature, the KL term in the ELBO is treated as a regularizer: a penalty that prevents the approximate posterior from straying too far from the prior, analogous to weight decay or an L2 penalty. Under this view, the KL is just another knob to tune. This framing, while operationally useful, obscures the profound meaning of the KL term. To address this, we ask:
What, precisely, does the KL term quantify in the context of inference?

3.2.2
KL as a measure of information gain.

24
addressed this question from first principles. He was interested in deriving a unique functional that measures the information gained when updating from a prior belief
P
to a posterior belief
Q
. Starting from a small set of “intuitively reasonable properties”—reparameterization invariance, additivity for independent sources, monotonicity, and a composition rule—he proved that the KL divergence is the
only
expression that quantifies information gain while satisfying those intuitive requirements. In sum:

KL as a unique measure of information gain

\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\!\left({Q}\,\|\,{P}\right)
is the unique expression for the information contained in a message which alters the probabilities from
P
to
Q

(
24
)
.

3.2.3
Abstract, information theoretic perspective: KL as information rate.

This result reframes the KL term from just a regularizer into something far more fundamental: it is the
information rate
—the number of bits per inference step that the stimulus-driven posterior contains beyond what was already present in the prior. Further, KL is not one of many possible measures, or just a definition: it is “
the
” measure, uniquely determined by basic desiderata.

3.2.4
Concrete, biophysical perspective:
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
as metabolic rate.

Now consider what appears on the right hand side in the Poisson KL expression (
eq.

8
). The prior rate
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
is not an abstract parameter. It represents a concrete biophysical quantity: the baseline number of spikes per unit time. In a potential neuromorphic implementation, it has energy units. As a model of spiking in the brain, it costs ATP (or at least, the synapses responding to those spikes cost ATP
(
5
)
). Thus, the prior rate
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
is—in this literal sense—a
metabolic rate
.

3.2.5
Merging the two perspectives:

The Poisson KL term is a joint cost over information and energy, that couples an abstract “Information Rate” (bits / inference step) to a concrete “Metabolic Rate” (spikes / inference step):

Poisson KL: Information Rate
\leftrightarrow
Metabolic Rate

\hskip-8.53581pt\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\Big({\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0}\odot{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{x}))}\,\big\|\,{\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0})}\Big)\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0}^{\top}f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\lambda}}})

Importantly, the variational free energy objective (
eq.

1
) was derived from purely probabilistic considerations—nowhere did we impose an explicit energy constraint. Yet, a metabolic cost term emerged from the mathematics of variational inference under Poisson (but not Gaussian) assumptions.

3.2.6
Interim conclusion (theory).

We have theoretically established that the Poisson KL term couples metabolic rate to information rate—a feature with no analog in the Gaussian framework. We explained this difference through the lens of information geometry, and traced it back to the mean-variance coupling in Poisson.

Beyond mean-variance coupling, another fundamental difference between Poisson and Gaussian distributions is their support: Poisson is nonnegative, whereas Gaussian is unconstrained. Could restricting the Gaussian to positive values induce a similar metabolic cost structure?

Recall from

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}~.

So far, we have focused solely on the KL term (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}
), and derived its closed-form expression for both Poisson and Gaussian latent variables (

5.0.1
The reconstruction term.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}
measures the quality of the latent representation: if the encoding is high fidelity, the data sample
\bm{x}
will have high expected likelihood under the approximate posterior. Throughout this work, we assume a Gaussian conditional likelihood with
2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\sigma}}}_{\text{dec}}^{2}=\mathbf{1}
, yielding the mean squared error reconstruction loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}

\displaystyle=\;\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{x})}\!\Big[{-\log p(\bm{x}|\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})}\Big]

(16)

\displaystyle=\;\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{x})}\!\Big[{\left\lVert\bm{x}-\mathrm{dec}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Big]~,

where
\mathrm{dec}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})
is the decoder neural network.

5.0.2
Linear decoders and closed-form reconstruction.

Consider a linear decoder
\mathrm{dec}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})={\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}

{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\equiv{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}}

42

48

{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}
is often called the

Given a linear decoder, the expectation in

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\EE_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{q}}}[{\bm{z}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{mean penalty}}}\,+\,{\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\diag({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\!{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})^{\top}\Var_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{q}}[\bm{z}]}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{variance penalty}}}~.

(17)

5.0.3
The variance penalty.

The second term in
eq.

17

\Var_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{q}}[\bm{z}]={\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
, linking variance directly to firing rate. This introduces a sparsity pressure distinct from the KL mechanism (
eq.

12

[{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\!{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}]_{ii}
) must maintain lower firing rates (
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}_{i}
) to minimize free energy. Biologically, this says that neurons with larger receptive fields should fire more sparingly.

For the standard Gaussian, variance is independent of the mean, so no such sparsity interpretation arises. But what about the rectified
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE?

For
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE, we need the moments of

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\;

(18)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{v}}}\;

7
Conclusions and Discussion

We showed that variational inference under Poisson assumptions yields a KL term with a metabolic cost interpretation, enabling a principled trade-off between computational accuracy and energetic cost. This structure is absent in Gaussian formulations—even when rectified—a result we explained using information geometry and verified empirically. Combined with the rate-distortion perspective developed below, these results position Poisson variational inference as a promising foundation for energy-aware theories of computation that could inform algorithm and hardware co-design for efficient AI.

7.0.3

\displaystyle=\;\EE_{z\sim q_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}}\!\Bigg[{z\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}}-({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0})}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}}-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}+{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}}\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}}-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}\,f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})~,

where we substituted the expected count
\EE_{q}[{z}]={\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}
, and identified the multiplicative residual
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}}\coloneqq{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}/{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0}
and the function
f(y)\coloneqq y\log y-y+1
.

For a
K
-dimensional latent space, we have:

\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\Big({\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0}\odot{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{x}))}\,\big\|\,{\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0})}\Big)\;\;=\;\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\lambda}}_{0i}f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}}_{i})\;\;=\;\;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0}^{\top}f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\lambda}}})$}

(29)

where
f(\cdot)
is applied element-wise.

B.2
Gaussian KL derivation

For a single latent dimension, let the approximate posterior be
q_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}(z|\bm{x})=\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})
and the prior be
p(z;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})=\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2})
. The KL divergence is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\Big({\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})}\,\big\|\,{\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\;\EE_{z\sim q}\!\Bigg[{\log\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}})^{2}}{2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}\right)}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}}{2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}\right)}}\Bigg]

(30)

\displaystyle=\;\EE_{z\sim q}\!\Bigg[{\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}}{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}}-\frac{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}})^{2}}{2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}+\frac{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}}{2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\;-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}\,-\,\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\EE_{z\sim q}\!\Bigg[{\frac{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}})^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}}\Bigg]}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1}}\,+\,\frac{1}{2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}\EE_{z\sim q}\!\Bigg[{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\;-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}\,-\,\frac{1}{2}\,+\,\frac{1}{2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Var_{q}[z]}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}}+({\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\EE_{q}[{z}]}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}+\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}-1-\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\mu}}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}}+g({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2})\right]~,

where we used the standard result
\EE_{q}[{(z-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}}]=\Var_{q}[z]+(\EE_{q}[{z}]-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0})^{2}
, substituted the residuals
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\mu}}={\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mu}}_{0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}={\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}/{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0}
, and identified the function
g(y)\coloneqq y-1-\log y
.

For a
K
-dimensional latent space with a factorized (diagonal) posterior, we have:

\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\Big({\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\mu}}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\sigma}}}^{2})}\,\big\|\,{\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\mu}}}_{0},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\sigma}}}_{0}^{2})}\Big)\;\;=\;\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left[\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\mu}}_{i}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\sigma}}_{0i}^{2}}+g({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}_{i}^{2})\right]\;\;=\;\;\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\mu}}}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\sigma}}}_{0}^{2}}+g({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\sigma}}}^{2})\right]$}

(31)

where
g(\cdot)
is applied element-wise, and the division in the precision-weighted term is understood to be element-wise.

B.3
KL Taylor expansions: Poisson and Gaussian

Here we derive the quadratic approximations for the nonlinear cost functions
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
used in the main text.

B.3.1
Poisson: expansion in log-rates

Let
u\coloneqq\log{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}}
denote the log-residual of the firing rate. Then
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}}=e^{u}
. Substituting this into the definition of
f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})
:

f(e^{u})\;=\;e^{u}\log(e^{u})-e^{u}+1\;=\;ue^{u}-e^{u}+1~.

(32)

We perform a Taylor expansion around
u=0
(corresponding to
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}}=1
, i.e., posterior equals prior):

\displaystyle F(u)\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;ue^{u}-e^{u}+1

(33)

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(u)\;

\displaystyle=\;(e^{u}+ue^{u})-e^{u}\;=\;ue^{u}

(34)

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(u)\;

\displaystyle=\;e^{u}+ue^{u}~.

(35)

Evaluating at
u=0
, we find
F(0)=0
,
F^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
F^{\prime\prime}(0)=1
. Thus, the second-order approximation is:

\framebox{$\displaystyle f({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})\;\approx\;\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}(\log{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\lambda}})^{2}$}

(36)

B.3.2
Gaussian: expansion in log-variance

Let
v\coloneqq\log({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2})
denote the log-residual of the
variance
. Then the variance ratio is
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2}=e^{v}
. Substituting this into the definition of
g({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2})
:

g(e^{v})\;=\;e^{v}-1-\log(e^{v})\;=\;e^{v}-1-v~.

(37)

We perform a Taylor expansion around
v=0
(corresponding to matched variances,
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2}=1
):

\displaystyle G(v)\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;e^{v}-1-v

(38)

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(v)\;

\displaystyle=\;e^{v}-1

(39)

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}(v)\;

\displaystyle=\;e^{v}~.

(40)

Evaluating at
v=0
, we find
G(0)=0
,
G^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
G^{\prime\prime}(0)=1
. Thus, the second-order approximation is:

\framebox{$\displaystyle g({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2})\;\approx\;\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}(\log{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\sigma}}^{2})^{2}$}

(41)

Appendix C
Derivation of the closed-form reconstruction loss for linear decoders

In this section, we derive the analytical expression for the reconstruction loss term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}
(
eq.

16
) under the assumption of a linear decoder.

Let the decoder be defined as
\hat{\bm{x}}={\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}
, where
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\in\RR^{M\times K}
is the dictionary matrix. We assume the likelihood is Gaussian with identity covariance (up to a scalar), leading to the mean squared error (MSE) loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}\;=\;\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}\!\Big[{\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Big]~.

(42)

We expand the squared Euclidean norm:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\;(\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z})^{\top}(\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z})

(43)

\displaystyle=\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}\,-\,\bm{x}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\,-\,({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z})^{\top}\bm{x}\,+\,({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z})^{\top}({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}\,-\,2\bm{x}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\,+\,\bm{z}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}~.

Now we take the expectation with respect to the approximate posterior
q_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{x})
. By linearity of expectation:

\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}\!\Big[{\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Big]\;=\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}\,-\,2\bm{x}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}[{\bm{z}}]\,+\,\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}\!\Big[{\bm{z}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}}\Big]~.

(44)

Let
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\coloneqq\EE_{q}[{\bm{z}}]
be the posterior mean. The second term simply becomes
-2\bm{x}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}
. For the third term (quadratic form), we use the trace trick (
\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{x}=\Tr(\mathbf{A}\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\top})
):

\displaystyle\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}\!\Big[{\bm{z}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\;\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}\!\Big[{\Tr({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top})}\Big]

(45)

\displaystyle=\;\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q}\!\Big[{\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}}\Big]\right)~.

Recall the definition of the covariance matrix:
\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]=\EE[{\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}^{\top}
. Thus,
\EE[{\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}}]=\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}^{\top}
. Substituting this back:

\displaystyle\text{Quadratic term}

\displaystyle=\;\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}(\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}^{\top})\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=\;\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]\right)\,+\,\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\;\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]\right)\,+\,{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}~.

We substitute the quadratic term back into the full expression:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}

\displaystyle=\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}\,-\,2\bm{x}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\,+\,{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\,+\,\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]\right)

(47)

\displaystyle=\;(\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}})^{\top}(\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}})\,+\,\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]\right)

\displaystyle=\;\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,+\,\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]\right)~.

Therefore, for a general (non-factorized) posterior, the closed for linear decoder reconstruction loss is given by:

\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}\;=\;\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,+\,\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{q}}[\bm{z}]\right)$}

(48)

Assuming a factorized posterior (mean-field approximation), the covariance matrix
\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]
is diagonal, with diagonal elements given by the variance vector
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{v}}}=\Var_{q}[\bm{z}]
. The trace of the product of a matrix
\mathbf{A}
and a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}
is the dot product of their diagonals:
\Tr(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D})=\sum_{i}A_{ii}D_{ii}
.
Therefore:

\Tr\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\Cov_{q}[\bm{z}]\right)\;=\;\diag({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{v}}}~.

(49)

This yields the final closed-form expression used in the text:

\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{m}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{mean penalty}}}\,+\,{\color[rgb]{0.6016,0.6016,0.6016}\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\diag({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\!{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{v}}}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{variance penalty}}}$}

(50)

Appendix D
Derivation of the rectified Gaussian moments

In this section, we derive the exact analytical expressions for the first and second moments of a Rectified Gaussian distribution. These moments are required to compute the closed-form reconstruction loss for the
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE (Table
1
).

Let a single latent variable
z
follow a Gaussian approximate posterior:

q_{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}(z|\bm{x})\;=\;\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})~.

(51)

The rectified representation is given by
h=\mathrm{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z)
. To evaluate the reconstruction term
\EE_{q}[{\log p(\bm{x}|h)}]
, we require the expected value
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{m}}=\EE_{q}[{h}]
and the variance
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{v}}=\Var_{q}[h]
.

D.0.1
Setup and change of variables.

We seek to compute the expectation of functions of
h
over the Gaussian density of
z
. Since
h=0
for
z<0
, the integration domain is effectively restricted to
z\in[0,\infty)
.
We perform a change of variables to the standard normal distribution using:

u\;=\;\frac{z-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}}\;\;\implies\;\;z\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}u~,\quad dz\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}du~.

(52)

The integration lower bound
z=0
maps to the standardized limit:

0\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}u\;\;\implies\;\;u\;=\;-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}}\;\coloneqq\;-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}~.

(53)

where we have defined the standardized mean
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}\coloneqq{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}/{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}
. The standard normal PDF is denoted by
\phi(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^{2}/2}
, and the CDF by
\Phi(u)
.

D.0.2
First Moment:
\EE[{h}]
.

The expected activation is given by:

{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{m}}\;=\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\max(0,z)\,\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})\,dz\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}z\,\mathcal{N}(z;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})\,dz~.

(54)

Substituting the standardized variables:

{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{m}}\;=\;\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}u)\,\phi(u)\,du\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}\underbrace{\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}\phi(u)\,du}_{\text{Term A}}\;+\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\underbrace{\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}u\,\phi(u)\,du}_{\text{Term B}}~.

(55)

Term A (probability mass).
Due to the symmetry of the Gaussian, the area under the curve from
-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}
to
\infty
is equivalent to the area from
-\infty
to
+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}
:

\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}\phi(u)\,du\;=\;1-\Phi(-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\;=\;\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})~.

(56)

Term B (tail expectation).
We use the identity
\frac{d}{du}(-\phi(u))=u\phi(u)
:

\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}u\phi(u)\,du\;=\;\Big[-\phi(u)\Big]_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}\;=\;0-\big(-\phi(-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\big)\;=\;\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})~,

(57)

where we used
\phi(\infty)=0
and the symmetry
\phi(-x)=\phi(x)
.

Combining Terms A and B yields the first moment:

\framebox{$\displaystyle\EE[{h}]\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{m}}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\,+\,{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})$}

(58)

D.0.3
Second Moment:
\EE[{h^{2}}]
.

The second raw moment is computed similarly:

\EE[{h^{2}}]\;=\;\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}u)^{2}\,\phi(u)\,du\;=\;\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}^{2}+2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}u+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}u^{2})\,\phi(u)\,du~.

(59)

We split this into three integrals:

\EE[{h^{2}}]\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}^{2}\underbrace{\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}\phi(u)\,du}_{\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})}\;+\;2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\underbrace{\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}u\phi(u)\,du}_{\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})}\;+\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}\underbrace{\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}u^{2}\phi(u)\,du}_{\text{Term C}}~.

(60)

Term C (Integration by Parts).
To solve
\int u^{2}\phi(u)du
, we use integration by parts. Let
w=u
and
dv=u\phi(u)du
. Then
dw=du
and
v=-\phi(u)
.

\displaystyle\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}u^{2}\phi(u)\,du\;

\displaystyle=\;\Big[-u\phi(u)\Big]_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}-\int_{-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}}^{\infty}\big(-\phi(u)\big)\,du

(61)

\displaystyle=\;\Big(0-\big[-(-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\phi(-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\big]\Big)+\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})

(62)

\displaystyle=\;\Big(0-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\Big)+\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})

(63)

\displaystyle=\;\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})~.

(64)

Substituting Term C back into the main equation:

\displaystyle\EE[{h^{2}}]\;

\displaystyle=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}^{2}\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\;+\;2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\;+\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}\Big(\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\Big)

(65)

\displaystyle=\;({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\;+\;2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\;-\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2}\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}}\right)\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})

(66)

\displaystyle=\;({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\;+\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})~.

(67)

Thus, the second raw moment is:

\framebox{$\displaystyle\EE[{h^{2}}]\;=\;({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}^{2})\Phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})\,+\,{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\sigma}}\phi({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\zeta}})$}

(68)

D.0.4
Variance.

Finally, the variance is obtained via the standard definition
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{v}}\,=\,\EE[{h^{2}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{m}}^{2}
.

Appendix E
Extended methodological details and supplementary results

Here we provide additional experimental details. We trained 128 models in total, comprising 64
\mathcal{P}
-VAE and 64
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE instances. Each family was evaluated over the full
8\times 8
grid of latent dimensionalities K and KL weighting coefficients
\beta
(
eq.

20
):

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\in\;{64,128,192,256,384,512,1024,2048},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\in\;{0.01,0.1,0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,4.0,8.0}.

Dataset.

We used whitened van Hateren natural image patches
(
69
)
following the preprocessing protocol from the
\mathcal{P}
-VAE paper
(
64
)
. The dataset consists of
16\times 16
pixel grayscale image patches extracted from the van Hateren natural image scenes dataset
(
69
)
. The preprocessing pipeline includes:

1.

Random extraction of
16\times 16
patches from natural images

2.

Whitening filter:
R(f)=f\cdot\exp((f/f_{0})^{n})
with
f_{0}=0.5
and
n=4

3.

Local contrast normalization with kernel size 13 and
\sigma=0.5

4.

Z-score normalization across spatial dimensions

This preprocessing ensures that the input statistics approximate natural scene statistics while removing low-frequency correlations, encouraging the model to learn sparse, localized features. For additional details on the dataset preparation and statistics, we refer readers to the original
\mathcal{P}
-VAE paper
(
64
)
.

Model architectures.

In this paper, we trained linear
\mathcal{P}
-VAE and
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE models. Both architectures share identical input/output dimensions but differ in their latent parameterization and decoding nonlinearity. Below we describe the specifications for a model with latent dimensionality
K=512
.

Encoder.

•

Input
\bm{x}
:
16\times 16=256
dimensional flattened image patches.

•

Poisson:
Single linear layer
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times 256}
. Output is
512
dimensional log-rate residuals
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}
.

•

Gaussian:
Single linear layer
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1024\times 256}
. Output splits into
512
dimensional mean residuals
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\mu}}}
and
512
dimensional log-scale residuals
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{v}}}
.

•

No bias terms, no nonlinearities.

Sampling.

•

Poisson:
Rates are computed as
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}={\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\lambda}}}_{0}\odot\exp({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}})
. Samples are drawn
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})
.

•

Gaussian:
Means are computed as
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\mu}}}={\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\mu}}}_{0}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{\mu}}}
, and scales are computed as
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\sigma}}}=\exp(\log{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\sigma}}}_{0}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\delta\bm{v}}})
. Samples are drawn
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\mu}}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\sigma}}}^{2})
.

Decoder.

•

Input: Sampled latent variables
\bm{z}
.

•

Single linear layer:
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{256\times 512}
(dictionary).

•

Poisson:
Reconstruction
\hat{\bm{x}}={\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}
.

•

Gaussian:
Reconstruction
\hat{\bm{x}}={\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\mathrm{relu}(\bm{z})
.

•

No bias terms, no nonlinearities.

Poisson training details.

We trained the
\mathcal{P}
-VAE models using the exponential arrival time (EAT) Poisson relaxation, with a cubic indicator approximation
(
25
)
. We used a relaxation temperature of
\tau=0.05

Learnable prior distributions.

To ensure a fair comparison, we learned the prior parameters for both
\mathcal{P}
-VAE and
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE. We initialized the
\mathcal{P}
-VAE prior as a uniform distribution in log-space, which corresponds to a scale-invariant Jeffrey’s prior. We initialized the
\mathcal{G}_{\scriptstyle{\text{relu}}}
-VAE prior as the standard normal distribution.

Training configuration.

•

Optimizer
: Adamax

•

Learning rate
:
\eta=0.005

•

Batch size
: 1000 samples per batch

•

Epochs
: 3000 training epochs + 5 warmup epochs

•

Weight decay
: 0.0 (no explicit L2 regularization)

•

Gradient clipping
: Maximum gradient norm of 500

•

Learning rate schedule
: Cosine annealing over all training epochs

Compute details.

We used NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPUs without mixed-precision training to ensure numerical stability. Training a single model takes approximately 2-3 hours. The full experimental sweep required training 128 models in total, which was parallelized across multiple GPUs using a custom sweep runner that manages GPU memory allocation and job scheduling.
