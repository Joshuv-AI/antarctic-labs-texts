ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract Overview of the study. IAT: inhaled antibiotic therapy.

Introduction

Inhaled antibiotic therapy (IAT) is increasingly used in patients with chronic airway diseases beyond cystic fibrosis, particularly in the context of recurrent exacerbations and persistent bronchial infection [1, 2]. Although its efficacy is well established in cystic fibrosis-related bronchiectasis, its use has progressively expanded to non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, selected patients with COPD and occasionally asthma. International guidelines and expert consensus documents acknowledge this broader application [3–9]. However, despite this expansion, there is limited information regarding how IAT is positioned and initiated in routine clinical practice.

The decision to initiate IAT represents a therapeutic escalation within the clinical and microbiological progression of chronic airway diseases. Clinicians typically integrate microbiological persistence, exacerbation burden, symptom severity, structural abnormalities and overall disease instability when considering treatment initiation. This process is further complicated by the frequent coexistence of overlapping airway conditions, such as bronchiectasis combined with COPD or asthma, which may generate distinct multidimensional clinical profiles [10–12].

Most available evidence derives from randomised clinical trials with restrictive inclusion criteria or from disease-specific registries that do not capture the precise clinical context at the time of IAT initiation [1, 2, 13]. Consequently, real-world data describing the baseline clinical, microbiological and phenotypic landscape at treatment start remain scarce. This gap is particularly relevant because IAT may be introduced not only in patients fulfilling strict criteria for chronic bronchial infection (CBI) [8], but also in those with early or recurrent isolations accompanied by substantial exacerbation burden or symptom impact.

To address this unmet need, the Spanish Society of Pneumology and Thoracic Surgery (SEPAR) established the National Registry of IAT in Chronic Airway Diseases (REPAIR), a prospective, multicentre initiative designed to systematically collect standardised data from patients at the moment of IAT initiation under real-world conditions [14].

The present study aimed to characterise the clinical, microbiological and functional profile of patients at the time of IAT initiation, and to describe prescribing patterns according to infection status and underlying airway disease phenotypes in routine practice.

Materials and methods

Study design

REPAIR is a national, multicentre, observational and prospective registry designed to collect real-world data from adult patients with chronic airway diseases in whom IAT is initiated under routine clinical practice conditions [14]. The present study corresponds to a descriptive cross-sectional baseline analysis including exclusively data recorded at the visit in which IAT was initiated. Patients were consecutively enrolled across participating tertiary centres in Spain between 2023 and 2025.

The study was approved by the corresponding institutional research ethics committees at each participating centre, and all patients provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. The registry was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and current regulations on personal data protection [15].

Study population

Adult patients (≥18 years) with bronchiectasis, COPD or asthma were eligible if initiation of IAT was decided according to the treating physician's clinical judgement. Inclusion criteria were: at least one documented isolation of a potentially pathogenic microorganism from respiratory samples; availability of clinical data for the preceding 12 months; and regular follow-up at the prescribing centre. Patients were excluded if IAT had been used in the previous 12 months, if IAT was indicated for conditions unrelated to chronic airway disease, or if microbiological documentation was unavailable.

No formal sample size calculation was performed, as REPAIR is an ongoing national registry designed to include consecutive eligible patients during the recruitment period.

To reduce selection bias, consecutive eligible patients were enrolled at each participating centre.

Definitions

Microbiological status was categorised according to respiratory isolations during the 12 months prior to IAT initiation. CBI was defined as the documented isolation of the same potentially pathogenic microorganism at least three times within a 12-month period, with each isolation separated by at least 1 month to ensure distinct episodes [16]. First isolation referred to the first-ever documented isolation of a given potentially pathogenic microorganism. Remaining cases were classified as recurrent non-CBI isolation.

Clinical status at IAT initiation was categorised as stable (≥4 weeks since the end of the last exacerbation), early convalescence (<4 weeks) or acute exacerbation, according to the treating physician's assessment.

Data collection

All data were entered prospectively into a centralised electronic case report form with predefined fields and validation rules to ensure data consistency. Data queries were generated and resolved with the recruiting centres when inconsistencies were detected. Baseline variables included demographics, smoking history, exacerbation history in the preceding year, symptom burden measured by the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score, lung function, radiological findings, microbiological profile, background respiratory treatments and IAT characteristics. Baseline systemic inflammatory biomarkers included leukocyte count, neutrophil count, blood eosinophil count and C-reactive protein, as available in routine practice at treatment initiation.

Classification of chronic airway disease phenotypes

At registry inclusion, investigators identified the main underlying chronic airway disease prompting IAT initiation, selecting bronchiectasis, COPD and/or asthma. Multiple selections were allowed when clinically appropriate, reflecting real-world overlap scenarios.

To ensure internal consistency, a predefined verification algorithm was applied. Bronchiectasis required radiological confirmation on chest computed tomography (CT) performed prior to or at inclusion. Patients with CT-confirmed bronchiectasis who had not been initially labelled as such were reclassified as overlap phenotypes (e.g., COPD–bronchiectasis or asthma–bronchiectasis).

COPD diagnosis required the presence of persistent airflow obstruction defined as forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.70 measured at the inclusion visit, before the first dose of IAT, together with a compatible exposure history, primarily tobacco smoking. Cases labelled as COPD without objective obstruction or without relevant exposure history were reviewed with the recruiting centres and excluded when inconsistencies could not be resolved.

Asthma diagnosis was accepted according to investigator report when consistent with clinical history and not contradicted by functional data, reflecting real-world clinical practice.

When discordance existed between investigator-reported diagnosis, radiological findings and spirometric data, cases were reviewed centrally and clarified with participating centres whenever possible. Patients with unresolved major inconsistencies were excluded from the final analytical cohort, as detailed in the study flowchart (figure 1). This approach enabled classification into mutually exclusive phenotypic groups while preserving the complexity of overlapping airway diseases encountered in routine clinical practice.

Radar plot scores were not derived from direct statistical transformation of individual-level data. Instead, they represent a semi-quantitative conceptual synthesis based on the relative distribution of key clinical domains across phenotypes observed in the dataset. Scores (1–5) were assigned to reflect comparative burden across phenotypes for each domain, with higher values indicating greater relative impact.

Inhaled antibiotics

The inhaled antibiotic agent, delivery device (nebuliser versus dry powder inhaler (DPI)), dosing schedule and date of initiation were recorded. Treatments included commercially available inhaled formulations and intravenous formulations adapted for inhalation.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were primarily descriptive. Continuous variables are presented as mean±sd and categorical variables as absolute and relative frequencies. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Between-group comparisons were performed using t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied and p-values should be interpreted descriptively. Missing data were not imputed; analyses were performed using available cases. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 or later.

Real-world patterns of IAT prescription

Antibiotic selection

Colistimethate sodium accounted for 78.6% of initial prescriptions (table 2; figure 3). Its use was strongly associated with P. aeruginosa isolation (92.0% versus 35.2%; p<0.001) (supplementary table S2). No significant differences were observed in age, lung function, exacerbation frequency or hospitalisation burden between patients receiving colistimethate and other inhaled antibiotics. Device selection (DPI versus nebulised)

Nebulised therapy was used in 73.0% and DPI in 26.3% (table 2). DPI users were younger (p<0.001), more frequently female (p=0.036), and had higher FEV1 and FEV1/FVC values (both p<0.05). Emphysema was less frequent among DPI users (p=0.016). Exacerbation frequency, hospitalisation rates, CBI prevalence and multidrug-resistant pathogens isolation did not differ significantly between device groups (supplementary table S3).

Infection burden (CBI versus non-CBI)

Patients meeting CBI criteria had higher CAT scores (p=0.008), lower FEV1 and FEV1/FVC (both p<0.05), and greater exacerbation frequency (p=0.022) compared with those without CBI. Long-term macrolide use and prior intravenous eradication therapy were more frequent in the CBI group (both p<0.05). Inhaled antibiotic class selection did not differ according to CBI status (supplementary table S4).

Prior eradication therapy

A total of 34.5% of patients had received intravenous eradication antibiotic therapy within the previous 12 months. Among them, 29.2% had received prior treatment, while in 5.4% intravenous therapy was administered concomitantly with inhaled antibiotic initiation. Those without prior eradication had higher CAT scores, lower lung function, and greater exacerbation and hospitalisation burden in the preceding year (all p≤0.003). CBI was also more frequent in this subgroup (p=0.024). Antibiotic class and device selection were similar between groups (table 3; supplementary table S5). Clinical status at initiation

The majority of patients (55.7%) initiated IAT under stable conditions. Compared with those initiating under stable conditions, these individuals had a significantly higher exacerbation burden and hospitalisation rate in the preceding year (both p<0.01) and were more frequently dyspnoeic (p=0.014). CBI prevalence did not differ significantly between groups (p=0.158). However, first isolation (p<0.001) and multidrug-resistant microorganisms (p=0.048) were significantly more frequent in the peri-exacerbation group. Lung function parameters and antibiotic class or device selection did not differ significantly between groups (table 4; supplementary table S6). Biomarkers

Baseline systemic inflammatory markers showed limited variation across subgroups. Neutrophil counts were higher in patients initiating IAT during exacerbation or convalescence, whereas C-reactive protein and blood eosinophil counts did not show consistent differences according to CBI status or prior eradication therapy.

Exploratory phenotype analysis of chronic airway diseases

Phenotypic distribution and descriptive comparisons across phenotypes are detailed in supplementary tables S7 and S8. The bronchiectasis–COPD overlap phenotype exhibited lower lung function, higher microbiological activity and greater background treatment intensity compared with single-disease phenotypes. Isolated bronchiectasis showed a predominantly infection-driven profile with relatively preserved airflow, whereas COPD displayed the opposite pattern, with dominant obstruction and lower microbiological intensity. These multidimensional patterns are graphically illustrated in figure 4, which integrates relative positioning across key clinical domains. Discussion

This baseline analysis of the REPAIR registry characterises patients at the moment of IAT initiation across chronic airway diseases. IAT was predominantly initiated in older patients with structural airway disease, substantial exacerbation burden and frequent P. aeruginosa isolation. Approximately half of the cohort fulfilled criteria for CBI, while a substantial proportion initiated therapy during exacerbation or early convalescence. Importantly, the objective of this analysis was not to evaluate treatment effectiveness but to describe the clinical and microbiological context in which IAT is initiated in routine practice. Together, these findings suggest that both chronic infectious persistence and acute microbiological events play a central role in positioning IAT in routine clinical practice.

A central finding of this study is that initiation of IAT appears predominantly influenced by microbiological factors. Isolation of P. aeruginosa was the only variable strongly associated with colistimethate prescription, whereas lung function impairment, exacerbation history and overall clinical severity did not substantially influence antibiotic class selection. These observations align with data from national and European bronchiectasis registries, where inhaled antibiotics are predominantly used in the context of chronic P. aeruginosa infection and recurrent exacerbations [2, 17–20]. In routine practice, IAT is not prescribed to treat COPD, bronchiectasis or asthma per se, but rather to address chronic or recurrent bronchial infection occurring within these diseases. The infectious substrate, rather than the diagnostic label, therefore emerges as the principal determinant of treatment initiation. In addition, local availability of formulations and longstanding familiarity with nebulised colistimethate within the Spanish healthcare system may further contribute to its predominance.

Although patients fulfilling CBI criteria exhibited greater symptom burden, worse airflow obstruction and higher exacerbation frequency, this increased severity did not translate into differentiated antibiotic or device selection. This therapeutic uniformity, despite the recognised prognostic impact of CBI [21–25], suggests that prescribing practices remain relatively homogeneous across different levels of clinical severity. Infection appears to function largely as a binary trigger for treatment rather than as a graded factor guiding therapeutic intensity or formulation choice, highlighting the limited integration of multidimensional disease profiling into therapeutic decision-making. Notably, a substantial proportion of patients without previous eradication attempts already fulfilled criteria for CBI at the time of IAT initiation, suggesting that escalation to inhaled therapy may occur after infectious chronicity is already established rather than during earlier, potentially modifiable stages of disease.

Although the majority of patients initiated IAT under stable conditions (55.7%), a substantial proportion (44.3%) started treatment during exacerbation or early convalescence. These individuals concentrated a higher exacerbation burden, greater hospitalisation rates and more severe dyspnoea. Importantly, CBI prevalence did not differ significantly between stability groups; instead, first isolation and multidrug-resistant microorganisms were more frequent among those initiating therapy around exacerbation. This pattern suggests that, while IAT is commonly initiated in stable clinical conditions, episodes of clinical deterioration often act as a key trigger for treatment initiation in a substantial proportion of patients. Exacerbations therefore remain an important driver of therapeutic decision-making in real-world practice. Although sustained exposure to inhaled antibiotics has consistently been associated with reductions in exacerbations and hospitalisations in real-world settings [2, 18, 26–29], their frequent initiation during periods of instability raises an important clinical question: could earlier positioning in selected high-risk but clinically stable patients maximise preventive benefit? This hypothesis warrants prospective evaluation. From a practical perspective, initiation during acute phases may also raise tolerability concerns, as inhaled antibiotics can induce bronchospasm or airway irritation [30], underscoring the need for careful supervision at treatment onset.

Device selection appeared to follow a different rationale. While antibiotic class was strongly microbiology-driven, the choice between nebulised formulations and DPI seemed more closely related to functional and usability factors than to microbiological burden, although statistical differences in microbiological distribution were observed. This divergence suggests that once the decision to treat infection is made, physiological and practical considerations outweigh microbiological gradation in determining therapeutic delivery [31, 32].

The exploratory multidimensional phenotype analysis further illustrates the heterogeneity of clinical profiles encountered in routine practice. Overlap phenotypes, particularly bronchiectasis–COPD, concentrated more severe airflow limitation, higher microbiological activity and greater treatment intensity, consistent with previous reports describing worse outcomes in combined disease states [13, 33–35]. Although these complex phenotypes are clearly identifiable in real-world cohorts, antibiotic class and device distribution remained broadly similar across groups, suggesting that multidimensional disease characterisation is not yet systematically incorporated into therapeutic decision-making.

The absence of meaningful associations between routinely measured systemic biomarkers and IAT initiation highlights the gap between emerging precision-medicine paradigms and current clinical practice. Although inflammatory and type 2 biomarkers have demonstrated prognostic value in chronic airway diseases [36–39], microbiological history remains the dominant determinant guiding treatment initiation.

REPAIR's prospective design offers the opportunity to determine whether this predominantly microbiology-driven and frequently reactive initiation strategy translates into meaningful modification of exacerbation trajectories and hospitalisation risk. Longitudinal analyses will be essential to clarify whether earlier or more phenotype-integrated approaches could improve patient outcomes.

REPAIR represents, to our knowledge, the first prospective nationwide registry specifically focused on the initiation of IAT across chronic airway diseases. Strengths include the inclusion of consecutive real-world patients at the exact moment of IAT start, standardised microbiological definitions, central verification of phenotypic classification and comprehensive multidimensional characterisation. Unlike disease-specific registries or randomised trials, REPAIR captures the heterogeneity and overlap of airway diseases encountered in daily clinical practice.

Limitations include the observational design, the absence of standardised initiation criteria across centres and the potential influence of local availability of formulations, including off-label nebulised intravenous antibiotics, which may limit external generalisability. In addition, the predominance of colistimethate and the distribution of inhaled antibiotic formulations observed in this study may reflect the availability and prescribing practices within the Spanish healthcare system, and may not be directly generalisable to settings where certain formulations, such as dry powder colistin, are not available. Furthermore, the classification of clinical status at treatment initiation may not fully capture the temporal relationship between exacerbations, microbiological sampling and treatment decisions. In some cases, IAT initiation after an exacerbation may have reflected confirmation of persistent infection following an acute event rather than a strictly proactive or reactive strategy. This nuance could not be fully disentangled within the current study design. However, this heterogeneity also reflects real-world clinical decision-making and allows characterisation of how IAT is actually positioned in routine practice across different clinical scenarios. Biomarker assessment was restricted to routinely collected variables, and exclusion of cases with unresolved diagnostic inconsistencies may have reduced representation of less clearly characterised phenotypes.

In conclusion, initiation of IAT in chronic airway diseases is primarily determined by microbiological isolation, particularly P. aeruginosa, rather than by multidimensional clinical or inflammatory stratification. CBI and exacerbation burden define a more severe clinical context but do not substantially modify therapeutic selection once treatment is indicated. IAT is frequently introduced during periods of clinical instability, although most patients initiate treatment under stable conditions, highlighting the dual role of chronic infection and acute clinical events in shaping treatment decisions in routine practice. Whether earlier and more phenotype-integrated strategies could improve long-term outcomes remains an important question for prospective investigation.
