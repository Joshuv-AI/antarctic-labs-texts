Hospital Admissions for Hepatitis A and E in Spain: Nationwide Trends and In‐Hospital Outcomes
Authors: Mario Martín‐Portugués, Jorge Esteban‐Sampedro, Alejandro Muñoz‐Serrano, Natalia Vicente‐López, Patricia Mills‐Sánchez, Laura Dorado‐Doblado, Carmen de Mendoza, Vicente Soriano, Víctor Moreno‐Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe prognosis of acute hepatitis A virus (HAV) and hepatitis E virus (HEV) in patients with immunosuppression (IS) or chronic liver disease (CLD) remains unclear. We aimed to identify predictors of severe outcomes in these patients. Using the Spanish National Hospital Discharge Database, we identified all admissions for acute HAV or HEV (January 2016–December 2023). Primary outcomes were intensive care unit (ICU) admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, and in‐hospital mortality. Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations between IS causes, CLD etiologies, and outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, and extrahepatic manifestations. Among 6477 unique admissions, 5306 had HAV (81.9%) and 1191 had HEV (18.4%), including 20 dual HAV/HEV coinfections counted in both virus‐specific groups. HEV admissions increased whereas HAV admissions declined over time. HAV admissions were younger (39.5 vs. 59.8 years) and had higher HIV coinfection rates (6.8% vs. 1.3%), but lower rates of IS (20.0% vs. 45.7%) and underlying CLD (12.4% vs. 32.7%) (p < 0.001). HAV admissions had lower rates of acute or subacute hepatic failure (2.4% vs. 4.5%) and mortality (1.4% vs. 3.1%). All admissions with coded acute or subacute hepatic failure had pre‐existing CLD. Multivariable analysis identified CLD as the principal mortality determinant (HAV: OR 2.8, 95% CI: 1.7–4.6; HEV: OR 5.2, 95% CI: 2.5–10.9). Alcoholic liver disease predicted both acute or subacute hepatic failure and mortality in both viruses; autoimmune hepatitis predicted hepatic failure only, and chronic hepatic insufficiency predicted ICU admission in both viruses, acute or subacute hepatic failure in HAV, and mortality in HEV. Despite increased mortality, IS did not predict acute or subacute hepatic failure. Severe outcomes in admissions with HAV and HEV infections are mostly driven by baseline hepatic reserve regardless of viral type. Thus, risk stratification should prioritize pre‐existing liver dysfunction, the major risk determinant for acute or subacute hepatic failure.Keywords: acute or subacute hepatic failure, acute‐on‐chronic liver failure, chronic liver disease, enterically transmitted viral hepatitis, hepatitis A, hepatitis E, immunosuppression, in‐hospital mortality

Introduction

With the introduction of effective treatments for hepatitis C virus (HCV) and universal hepatitis B virus (HBV) vaccination, the landscape of viral liver disease in high‐income countries has shifted. As these historically dominant etiologies recede, classically enterically transmitted hepatitis A virus (HAV) and hepatitis E virus (HEV) infections have emerged as increasingly relevant causes of hospitalization [1, 2, 3, 4]. In Spain, as in other Western countries, HAV transmission predominantly occurs through sexual contact among men who have sex with men (MSM), and, less often, through household contact or ingestion of contaminated food or water [5, 6, 7]. HAV hospitalizations have declined, although outbreaks continue, notably in 2016–2017 and among MSM and migrants from regions with suboptimal vaccination coverage, supporting targeted immunization [5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. In Europe, most clinically significant HEV infections are due to genotype 3 and linked to zoonotic or foodborne exposure, particularly among older people and those with comorbidities [1, 8, 10]. HEV hospitalizations in Spain have increased substantially from 0.22 per million inhabitants in 1997 to 2.95 in 2019 [10].

Both acute HAV and HEV can precipitate severe short‐term clinical events including intensive care unit (ICU) admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, in‐hospital mortality, and extrahepatic manifestations, with hematologic and neurologic complications particularly reported for HEV infection [1, 8, 13, 14]. The role of immunosuppression (IS) in short‐term outcomes among admissions with acute HAV or HEV is not clearly defined. Definitions of IS are heterogeneous, and distinct conditions are often pooled, obscuring whether excess mortality reflects hepatic decompensation or non‐hepatic systemic complications [15, 16].

Pre‐existing chronic liver disease (CLD) also increases the risk of clinical severity with either infection, which may present as acute liver failure (ALF) or as acute‐on‐chronic liver failure (ACLF) [10, 13, 14, 17, 18]. However, these are distinct syndromes; ALF occurs in the absence of pre‐existing CLD, whereas ACLF develops on established CLD and requires assessment of decompensation and organ failure [19, 20, 21]. Prognosis is particularly poor in the latter, for which HAV and HEV have been identified as important etiologic agents, accounting for up to one‐third of ACLF cases [22, 23]. Most evidence comes from single‐center cohorts, outbreak reports, or studies focused on a single virus, limiting direct comparisons [8, 9, 24]. Key questions remain, particularly whether specific CLD etiologies (alcoholic liver disease [ALD], metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease [MASLD], autoimmune hepatitis [AIH], and chronic hepatitis B or C) or advanced liver damage may predispose to acute or subacute hepatic failure. Likewise, it is unclear whether the major determinants of acute or subacute hepatic failure in the setting of CLD also drive mortality itself [18, 24, 25]. Consequently, clinicians lack adjusted estimates integrating age, sex, IS conditions, and CLD etiology to identify admissions that may need closer monitoring or ICU.

To generate contemporary estimates, we conducted a nationwide analysis of HAV and HEV hospitalizations in Spain from 2016 to 2023 using the Spanish National Hospital Discharge Database (SNHDD). We aimed to characterize the prognostic role of IS conditions and baseline CLD etiologies in hospital admissions with HAV or HEV [5, 10].

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We performed a nationwide, registry‐based study using the SNHDD, a government repository that captures administrative and clinical information for all public and most private hospitalizations in Spain. It has been previously validated in other studies [26, 27, 28]. The database contains hospital‐discharge records only and does not capture outpatient encounters. Records include demographics, admission/discharge data, diagnoses, and procedures coded with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10). As reference, data on the population living in Spain during the same period were obtained from the National Statistics Institute (INE) (www.ine.es).

Study Population

We included all discharge records in the SNHDD from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2023, containing an ICD‐10 code for acute HAV or HEV infection in any diagnostic position. The ICD‐10 codes are listed in Table S1. Because the database is episode‐based, results refer to hospital admissions rather than unique patients, and outpatient infections were not captured.

Exposure Definitions

The primary exposures were HAV and HEV infections, treated as mutually exclusive categories for comparative analyses. Admissions coded for both HAV and HEV were classified as coinfections, described separately, and excluded from virus‐specific multivariable analyses.

We defined IS as a prespecified administrative category of conditions associated with impaired immunity, identified from ICD‐10 codes recorded during the hospital admission (Table S1). Causes of IS comprised diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), HIV infection, solid‐organ transplant (with previous liver transplant specifically flagged), hematopoietic stem‐cell transplant, primary immunodeficiency, systemic autoimmune disease, solid organ neoplasm (SON), and hematologic disease. CLD etiologies included ALD, MASLD, AIH, chronic HBV infection, HCV infection, advanced liver damage (chronic hepatic insufficiency [CHI]), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). CHI was a composite category encompassing chronic hepatic failure, chronic hepatitis, fibrosis/sclerosis, cirrhosis, and portal hypertension (Table S1). We also captured extrahepatic manifestations grouped as hematologic, neurologic, renal, gastrointestinal, and others (Table S1).

Outcomes

Prespecified in‐hospital outcomes were ICU admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, and in‐hospital mortality. Length of hospital stay, ICU length of stay, and liver transplantation were recorded as secondary descriptive variables and were not modelled owing to the small number of events. All endpoints were ascertained by ICD‐10 codes aligned with clinical definitions used in prior literature. In admissions resulting in death, the principal discharge diagnosis, defined in the SNHDD as the condition established after study to be chiefly responsible for the admission, was reviewed and classified as hepatic or non‐hepatic. Diagnoses directly referring to viral hepatitis, liver disease, hepatic failure, primary hepatic malignancy, or liver‐transplant complications were classified as hepatic; all others were classified as non‐hepatic. In fatal admissions, this field derives from the discharge (death) report, and therefore reflects the condition chiefly responsible for death. Individual codes and classifications are provided in Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis proceeded in two stages. First, we performed a descriptive characterization of the study population and an epidemiological analysis. Baseline characteristics of admissions for HAV and HEV were compared using Pearson's χ 2 tests for categorical variables and Student's t‐tests for continuous variables. We assessed annual HAV and HEV hospitalizations across the study period using admission rates per 1 million inhabitants per year, based on the Spanish national population. In a secondary analysis, trends for mean age, outcomes, and the prevalence of each comorbidity were analyzed using the annual percent change (APC); mean age and baseline CLD were analyzed separately for HAV and HEV. We assessed temporal trends using the Joinpoint Regression Program version 6.1.0.

Second, we fitted multivariable logistic regression models to estimate associations with primary outcomes (ICU admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, and in‐hospital death) separately for HAV and HEV. These models incorporated condition‐specific indicators for IS and CLD, adjusting for a pre‐specified set of covariates based on clinical plausibility: age (per 10‐year increase), sex, all IS conditions, all CLD etiologies, and extrahepatic manifestations. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Where model convergence failed due to sparse data, estimates were labeled as not estimable. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27.0.

To explore the clinical context of death, we conducted a post‐hoc pooled analysis of HAV‐ and HEV‐coded admissions. Two separate binary logistic regression models were fitted for hepatic principal‐diagnosis death and non‐hepatic principal‐diagnosis death, each contrasted with all other admissions. The exposure was any IS, and models were adjusted for age (per 10‐year increase), sex, virus, and baseline CLD. Because malignancy could define both IS and the principal discharge diagnosis, a sensitivity analysis excluded admissions with solid‐organ or hematologic malignancy. These analyses were considered exploratory.

Ethics

The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Puerta de Hierro University Hospital Ethics Committee (PI185/24). SNHDD provided de‐identified records; no direct identifiers were available to investigators. In accordance with Spanish law, informed consent was not required for analyses of anonymized administrative data.

Results

Study Population and Epidemiological Trends

Between 2016 and 2023, 6477 unique hospital admissions for enterically transmitted viral hepatitis were recorded in Spain: 5306 (81.9%) had HAV and 1191 (18.4%) had HEV; 20 admissions (0.3%) had dual HAV/HEV coinfection and were included in both virus‐specific groups (Table 1). Overall, most HAV and HEV admissions occurred in men (70.4%), and the mean age was 43.2 years. Baseline characteristics included pregnancy in 70 admissions (3.6% of female admissions), IS in 1601 (24.7%), baseline CLD in 1041 (16.1%), and extrahepatic manifestations in 495 (7.6%). The overall mean hospital stay was 6.9 days, and 235 admissions (3.6%) required ICU; 183 admissions (2.8%) had acute or subacute hepatic failure, 17 of which (9.3%) underwent liver transplantation. All admissions with acute or subacute hepatic failure had pre‐existing CLD. The overall in‐hospital mortality was 1.7% (111 deaths). Among the 183 admissions with acute or subacute hepatic failure, 22 died in hospital (12.0%).

Principal discharge diagnoses were classified for all 111 in‐hospital deaths. Among the 72 deaths in admissions with IS, 56 (77.8%) had a non‐hepatic and 16 (22.2%) a hepatic principal discharge diagnosis. Among the 39 deaths in admissions without IS, the corresponding numbers were 19 (48.7%) and 20 (51.3%), respectively (p = 0.002). Relative to all admissions, hepatic principal‐diagnosis death occurred in 16 (1%) admissions with IS and in 20 (0.41%) without IS, whereas non‐hepatic principal‐diagnosis death occurred in 56 (3.50%) and in 19 (0.39%), respectively. After adjustment for age, sex, virus, and baseline CLD, IS was associated with non‐hepatic principal‐diagnosis death (OR 3.85, 95% CI: 2.21–6.72; p < 0.001), but not with hepatic principal‐diagnosis death (OR 0.68, 95% CI: 0.34–1.36; p = 0.278). After exclusion of all admissions with SON or hematologic malignancy, the association persisted for non‐hepatic principal‐diagnosis death (OR 2.03, 95% CI: 1.08–3.81; p = 0.027), while no association was observed for hepatic principal‐diagnosis death (OR 0.63, 95% CI: 0.29–1.34; p = 0.226) (Table S2).

Throughout the study period, hospital admission rates for HAV declined significantly (APC −20.48%, p < 0.05) despite a 2017 peak (Figure 1). By contrast, HEV admissions rose sharply between 2016 and 2018 (APC +34.22%, p < 0.05), followed by a steady increase from 2018 to 2023 (APC +5.83%, p < 0.05). The mean age at admission increased significantly among HAV admissions (APC +5.51%, p < 0.05) but remained stable among HEV admissions (APC +0.71%, p = 0.119) (Figure 1). The rate of baseline CLD followed the same pattern, increasing among HAV admissions (APC +15.13%, p < 0.05) but not among HEV admissions (APC +5.80%, p = 0.197). No significant temporal changes were identified for adverse clinical outcomes, including ICU admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, and death (Figure 2).

Comparison of HAV and HEV Admissions

HAV and HEV admissions had distinct demographic and clinical profiles (Table 1 and Figure 3). HAV admissions were younger (39.5 vs. 59.8 years, p < 0.001) and less frequently male (69.6% vs. 74.1%, p = 0.003). Pregnancy was less frequent in HEV than in HAV admissions (1.3% vs. 4.1%, p = 0.012). Compared with non‐pregnant female admissions, pregnant admissions were younger (mean age 31.7 vs. 45.1 years) and had a shorter mean hospital stay (3.2 vs. 7.1 days). There were no significant differences in ICU admission (2.9% vs. 4.0%, p = 1.000), acute or subacute hepatic failure (5.7% vs. 2.5%, p = 0.106), liver transplantation (0% vs. 0.3%, p = 1.000), or in‐hospital mortality (0% vs. 2.1%, p = 0.399).

IS was significantly less frequent in HAV than HEV admissions (20.0% vs. 45.7%) (Figure 3A). Regarding specific causes of IS, HAV admissions showed lower rates of DM (6.9% vs. 31.7%) and CKD (2.2% vs. 7.8%) (p < 0.001 for all). In contrast, HIV infection was significantly more frequent in HAV admissions (6.8% vs. 1.3%, p < 0.001). Other IS conditions were less prevalent in HAV admissions, including solid organ transplant (0.2% vs. 2.6%) and systemic autoimmune disease (1.7% vs. 3.6%) (p < 0.001 for all).

Documented CLD was also less frequent in HAV than HEV admissions (12.4% vs. 32.7%) (Figure 3B). This difference was mostly driven by lower rates of ALD (1.8% vs. 10.4%), MASLD (4.2% vs. 13.4%), AIH (0.4% vs. 1.3%), and CHI (2.7% vs. 12.9%) (p < 0.001 for all). Conversely, pre‐existing chronic hepatitis B was more common in HAV than HEV admissions (2.5% vs. 1.3%, p = 0.012).

Finally, extrahepatic manifestations were less frequent overall in HAV than HEV admissions (7.0% vs. 10.6%, p < 0.001), particularly hematologic and neurologic ones. HAV admissions also had a shorter mean hospital stay (6.0 vs. 11.1 days, p < 0.001) and lower rates of ICU admission (3.2% vs. 5.5%), coded acute or subacute hepatic failure (2.4% vs. 4.5%), and in‐hospital mortality (1.4% vs. 3.1%) (p < 0.001 for all). Liver transplantation was uncommon but less frequent in HAV admissions (0.2% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.034) (Figure 3C).

Determinants of Adverse Outcomes

Multivariable analyses evaluated associations between distinct demographic and clinical variables and three outcomes: ICU admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, and in‐hospital mortality. Outcomes were examined separately for each virus.

Hepatitis A

For ICU admission, age per 10‐year increase (OR 1.2, 95% CI: 1.1–1.3), SON (OR 2.0, 95% CI: 1.2–3.4), hematologic disease (OR 5.6, 95% CI: 2.6–12.0), CHI (OR 3.3, 95% CI: 2.0–5.7), and extrahepatic manifestations (OR 2.3, 95% CI: 1.5–3.4) were independent predictors (Figure 4). For acute or subacute hepatic failure, only ALD (OR 3.9, 95% CI: 1.9–8.3), AIH (OR 5.5, 95% CI: 1.4–20.8), and CHI (OR 4.4, 95% CI: 2.3–8.6) were independent predictors of acute or subacute hepatic failure. Lastly, for in‐hospital mortality, independent predictors were: age per 10‐year increase (OR 2.0, 95% CI: 1.7–2.4), SON (OR 5.6, 95% CI: 3.1–10.2), hematologic disease (OR 3.7, 95% CI: 1.2–11.3), ALD (OR 7.5, 95% CI: 3.4–16.6), and extrahepatic manifestations (OR 3.1, 95% CI: 1.8–5.4).

Independent predictors of ICU in HEV admissions included ALD (OR 2.3, 95% CI: 1.2–4.5), CHI (OR 2.3, 95% CI: 1.2–4.4), and extrahepatic manifestations (OR 3.1, 95% CI: 1.7–5.8) (Figure 5). Discussion

This nationwide analysis delineates two distinct clinical profiles and prognostic factors for admissions with HAV and HEV infection. Although the epidemiology of both viruses in Spain and, overall, in Europe is evolving [5, 10], HAV infection more often involves younger adults with a low burden of comorbidity, and correspondingly better short‐term outcomes [2, 29, 30]. HEV admissions, by contrast, occurred in older patients with substantially more comorbidity. Patient profile, rather than the virus itself, accounts for their consistently worse short‐term outcomes [31, 32]. Accordingly, in admissions for acute HAV or HEV infection, pre‐existing CLD was the principal determinant of critical care needs and death [18, 25, 33]. Importantly, the risk is not uniform across CLD etiologies and conditions, with ALD, AIH, and CHI carrying the highest risk [18, 33, 34]. In practice, triage at admission should be based on the underlying liver etiology and on prior advanced liver damage, rather than on the virus. Patients with these features warrant early escalation and assessment for transplantation [18, 29].

Prior population‐based studies have shown that whereas HAV hospitalizations have declined, despite periodic outbreaks in adults and other under‐vaccinated groups, HEV hospitalizations have re‐emerged and risen, largely reflecting autochthonous HEV genotype 3 infections in Spain [10, 11, 35, 36]. Our findings confirm these divergent trends in the Spanish population and extend previous nationwide observations through 2023. The sharp HAV peak observed in 2017 corresponds to a well‐documented European outbreak disproportionately affecting MSM, which prompted intensified vaccination campaigns in this population that likely explain the subsequent decline [5, 7, 10, 12, 14]. However, preliminary data suggest a renewed increase in HAV cases during 2024, underscoring the need for sustained vaccination efforts in at‐risk populations [37]. In addition, anecdotal cases of the new rat HEV (HEV‐C1) spilling over to humans have been documented in Spain, stressing that further emerging zoonotic threats should be expected [38, 39, 40, 41].

Molecular epidemiology provides additional context for these trends. In Spain, HAV subgenotypes IA and IB have historically circulated, whereas the large 2016–2017 outbreak was predominantly caused by genotype IA strains, particularly among MSM [7, 12, 42]. In contrast, autochthonous HEV infection is overwhelmingly caused by genotype 3; national molecular surveillance identified HEV‐3f as the predominant subtype (88.3%), with HEV‐3m and HEV‐3c circulating at lower frequencies [43]. HEV circulation has also been documented among asymptomatic blood donors. In Catalonia, HEV RNA was detected in approximately 1 in 3333 donations in 2013, with an anti‐HEV IgG seroprevalence of approximately 20%. Following implementation of universal screening, 151 of 655,523 donations (1 in 4341) were HEV‐RNA positive during 2017–2020, and all characterized isolates belonged to genotype 3 [44].

Beyond hospitalization trends over 8 years, our head‐to‐head analysis for HAV and HEV clearly shows distinct patient profiles. HAV admissions were mostly seen in younger patients with a disproportionately high rate of HIV infection [8, 30, 45, 46, 47], whereas HEV occurred predominantly in older men with a high burden of chronic comorbidities, especially IS and CLD [32, 35, 36]. These differences at clinical presentation are helpful to stratify patients at admission. Older age, male sex, and especially the presence of underlying liver disease can serve as early triage flags. Older patients and those with comorbidities who present with moderate to severe acute hepatitis should undergo immediate HEV testing, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR); patients with suspected HAV infection should also be isolated [45, 46, 48].

Among IS conditions, only SON and hematologic disease were independently associated with adverse outcomes. In admissions with HAV, both predicted ICU admission and mortality; in admissions with HEV, only SON remained associated with mortality once age and CLD were adjusted for. No IS condition independently predicted acute or subacute hepatic failure in either virus. The principal‐diagnosis analysis supports a predominantly non‐hepatic context for the excess mortality associated with IS, consistent with greater systemic vulnerability rather than hepatic decompensation [36, 49, 50, 51]. This interpretation was unchanged after excluding malignancy‐related admissions, although principal discharge diagnoses cannot establish the mechanism of death.

Pre‐existing CLD, rather than viral type, was the central prognostic factor in our study, consistently so across admissions that required ICU care and those that ended in death. This finding has already been noted by others [18, 31, 33]. All acute or subacute hepatic‐failure events occurred in admissions with pre‐existing CLD. This pattern is clinically consistent with acute‐on‐chronic presentation rather than classical ALF; however, formal ACLF criteria could not be applied. Recent international consensus statements further contextualize these findings. The Kyoto Consensus highlights the heterogeneity of current ACLF definitions and the importance of early recognition and prognostic stratification, whereas the recent APASL ACLF Beijing Position Paper emphasizes prompt identification and treatment of precipitating events, organ support, prognostic assessment, and timely evaluation for liver transplantation [21, 52]. These recommendations reinforce the clinical relevance of recognizing HAV and HEV as potential acute precipitants in patients with impaired baseline hepatic reserve. This interpretation is in line with results from other European series and registry analyses in which acute viral insults in patients with impaired hepatic reserve precipitate hepatic decompensation or ACLF, regardless of etiology [31, 53, 54]. However, the large size of our study population allowed us to assess the role of distinct CLD etiologies. Two conditions predicted outcomes differentially: ALD and AIH. As expected, the presence of baseline CHI was another major predictor [25, 55, 56]. ALD was the only etiology independently associated with both hepatic failure and mortality in the two viruses, and in HEV it also predicted ICU admission. AIH predicted hepatic failure only. CHI predicted ICU admission in both viruses, hepatic failure in HAV, and mortality in HEV. These findings point towards a common disease pathway from baseline liver harm to limited hepatic reserve, acute or subacute hepatic failure, and death [5, 10, 57, 58].

Our findings support the implementation of different but complementary strategies to better manage patients hospitalized with HAV/HEV infection. Vaccination programs for HAV that target at‐risk adults (those with CLD, people who inject drugs, MSM, and travelers to endemic regions) will remain the cornerstone of prevention [46]. For HEV, given that there is no licensed vaccine in Europe yet, emphasis must shift towards food safety messaging, especially regarding the avoidance of undercooked pork and game meat [14, 17, 47]. Alongside such measures, One Health surveillance integrating human, animal, and environmental surveys should inform prevention strategies, including assessment of HEV vaccine needs and the risk of the new rat HEV spillover to humans [38, 39, 40, 47].

Our study has limitations inherent to analyses based on a nationwide hospital‐discharge registry. Diagnoses and outcomes relied on administrative codes rather than direct clinical verification; therefore, misclassification between HAV and HEV or across CLD categories cannot be excluded. We mitigated this limitation by focusing on endpoints with established code definitions (ICU admission, acute or subacute hepatic failure, in‐hospital death). Etiologic confirmation, particularly using HEV PCR/serology, may occur after discharge. Since the SNHDD captures discharge codes, cases confirmed only after discharge may not have been captured, potentially introducing inclusion/ascertainment bias that could vary by hospital and calendar year.

A second limitation refers to generalizability of our data. The SNHDD captures inpatient admissions but not outpatient infections. The cohort may therefore overrepresent severe disease and underrepresent mild HAV or HEV infections. Consequently, incidence and community case‐fatality cannot be inferred from our data.

Third, clinical granularity was limited. We lacked information on disease activity, exposures, treatments, laboratory values, severity scores (e.g., bilirubin/INR, MELD‐Na/Child‐Pugh scores), viral genotype (e.g., HEV G3), vaccination status, and testing modality or timing (PCR vs. serology, days from onset). We therefore could not independently apply formal ACLF criteria or grade ACLF severity. Acute or subacute hepatic failure diagnoses were clinician‐recorded discharge diagnoses. Formally certified causes of death were unavailable. In fatal admissions, however, the principal discharge diagnosis derives from the discharge (death) report; we therefore used it to characterize the clinical context of death. The principal‐diagnosis models were post‐hoc and included relatively few events, particularly hepatic principal‐diagnosis deaths, and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Despite these limitations, the study's national scope provides a unique, large, contemporary, and population‐based view of hospitalizations with HAV and HEV. Our unified analytic approach allowed direct, etiology‐specific comparisons. Moreover, our findings are consistent with recent European reports, supporting the robustness and relevance of our analysis.

In conclusion, HAV and HEV infections showed distinct clinical profiles, but pre‐existing CLD was their shared prognostic determinant. The risk was not uniform across etiologies. ALD carried the greatest weight, being the only etiology associated with both hepatic failure and death regardless of the infecting virus, whereas AIH pointed specifically to hepatic failure and CHI mainly to the need for critical care. Clinical triage should therefore prioritize pre‐existing liver damage, especially ALD and AIH, and reduced hepatic reserve, over viral type, with early escalation for patients with these high‐risk conditions. Prevention strategies should reflect these findings. Catch‐up HAV vaccination is warranted for at‐risk groups, including patients with CLD, people who inject drugs, MSM, and travelers to endemic areas. For HEV, where no vaccine is licensed in Europe, food‐safety interventions and One Health surveillance should be strengthened instead. Aligning prevention and inpatient management with host‐related risk, rather than virus type alone, may offer the most effective strategy for reducing severe outcomes in enterically transmitted viral hepatitis.
