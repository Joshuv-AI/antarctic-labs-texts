Microcephaly-like phenotype triggered by novel reassortant and prototypic Oropouche virus strains in brain organoids
Authors: Gabrielle Brum, Vivian Grizente, Fábio Luís Lima Monteiro, Beatriz Luzia de Mello Lima Guimarães, Livia Goto-Silva, João Marcos de Azevedo Delou, Pedro Junior Pinheiro Mourão, Ismael Carlos da Silva Gomes, Enzo Oliveira Barone, Matheus Villanueva Andrade, Rafael Ferreira Lima, Izabela Mamede, Clarisse Reis, Fernanda Martins Marim, Helena Lobo Borges, Michael M Rosenblatt, Laura Cazañas Torres, Fábio César Sousa Nogueira, Gilberto Barbosa Domont, Victor Emmanuel Viana Geddes, Renato Santana Aguiar, Amilcar Tanuri, Carolina Moreira Voloch, Stevens Kastrup Rehen

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundOropouche virus (OROV) is an emerging arbovirus currently spreading across South America, with increasing reports of neurological manifestations, severe systemic disease, and congenital abnormalities. Although traditionally associated with mild febrile illness, the recent geographic expansion and surge in OROV outbreaks have prompted attention to its neurotropic potential.MethodsWe investigated the effects of Oropouche virus (OROV) infection on neural stem cells (NSCs) and brain organoids derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells, using both an emergent reassortant isolate and a prototypical strain.FindingsOROV infected NSCs, leading to cell death, depletion of proliferative progenitors, and disruption of neuroepithelial organisation. Transcriptomic profiling revealed reduction of antiviral response genes and enrichment of signalling pathways related to viral replication, apoptosis, and inhibition of stem cell maintenance and neuronal differentiation. These molecular signatures aligned with phenotypic collapse of progenitor pools and cortical structure observed in organoids, in which OROV infected progenitors, neurons and astrocytes. Proteomic analysis of brain organoids infected with OROV showed regulation of neurodevelopment pathways, which have been previously associated with ZIKV infection, suggesting convergent pathway disruption associated with reduced growth of organoids infected by ZIKV and OROV and highlighting their potential to impair brain development.InterpretationThese results reveal evidence consistent with a previously unrecognised neurodevelopmental pathogenic potential of OROV strains and provide mechanistic insight into their contribution to microcephaly-like outcomes.FundingD'Or Institute, ITpS, CNPq, FAPEMIG, FAPERJ.Keywords: Neurodevelopment, Neurotropism, Oropouche virus, Arbovirus infection, Brain organoids

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a PubMed search from 1961, corresponding to the first description of Oropouche virus, up to April 2026 using terms including “Oropouche virus”, “neural stem cells”, “brain organoids”, “congenital infection”, and “vertical transmission”. We included studies reporting OROV infection in neural cells or foetal tissues, regardless of language, and excluded studies limited to non-neural outcomes. The available evidence predominantly addresses OROV epidemiology, vector biology, and systemic infection, with limited mechanistic insights into its neuropathogenesis. Prior in vivo and ex vivo studies, mostly on the prototypic OROV strain, showed infection of neurons and microglia in animal models and adult human brain slices. Recent clinical observations detected OROV RNA and antigens in foetal tissues from microcephaly cases, suggesting vertical transmission and possible neurodevelopmental consequences. Mechanistic details of OROV neuropathogenesis remain scarce.

Added value of this study

Using human brain organoids, we show that OROV, including a recently emerged reassortant strain, impairs neural progenitor proliferation, disrupts neuroepithelial organisation, and infects neurons and astrocytes. Both strains induced similar pathogenic effects and transcriptomic alterations involving neuronal differentiation and apoptosis. These findings reveal the potential neurodevelopmental effects of OROV, including strains emerging outside its historic endemic region, and provide mechanistic insight into its microcephaly–like phenotypes in human organoids.

Implications of all the available evidence

The geographic expansion of OROV and the increasing number of cases underscore the urgent need to include this virus in arboviral surveillance and congenital infection screening programs. The disruption of foetal brain development and infection of differentiated neural cells suggest that OROV may be associated with microcephaly and other neurological complications. Intensified research into its neuropathogenesis is essential to guide public health strategies and mitigate the growing impact of this emerging neurotropic arbovirus.

Introduction

Oropouche virus (OROV) is an emerging arbovirus of the Orthobunyavirus genus (Peribunyaviridae family) historically associated with self-limiting febrile illness in South America.1 Recently, increasing reports of encephalitis, multiorgan failure, and notably, vertical transmission, have prompted urgent investigation into OROV neuropathogenesis.

In 2024, a new wave of outbreaks marked a shift in the epidemiological profile of OROV. Autochthonous transmission reported in Central America and south-eastern Brazil indicated ongoing geographic expansion, likely supported by favourable ecological conditions.2,3 Genomic analyses have identified reassortment events among circulating strains,4 with preliminary in vitro analysis suggesting altered replication dynamics and virulence.5 Although rare, severe systemic cases in adults have been documented, with OROV antigens detected in multiple tissues, including the central nervous system.6 A recent case series also reported OROV RNA and antigen in foetal tissues of newborns with microcephaly, reinforcing concerns about congenital infection and its potential contribution to neurodevelopmental disorders.7 This raises parallels with Zika virus (ZIKV), which causes a congenital syndrome driven by impaired neural progenitor function and disrupted cortical development.8

Despite these emerging concerns, the impact of OROV on the developing human brain remains poorly understood. Most available studies rely on animal models or adult human brain slice cultures, which have shown that OROV infects neurons and microglia and can trigger inflammatory responses.9, 10, 11 However, these systems do not capture the cellular composition and developmental dynamics of the early foetal brain, highlighting the need for human-relevant models that recapitulate early neurodevelopment. Human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived brain organoids fulfil this need by modelling key features of foetal brain development, including neural progenitor proliferation, differentiation, and early cortical organisation.12, 13, 14 Single-cell RNA sequencing of unguided organoids between 30 and 60 days in vitro reveals a transition from regionally specified neural progenitors to expanding populations of excitatory and inhibitory neurons, capturing the gradual emergence of early cortical identities.15,16 In addition, proteomic analysis of 45-day organoids demonstrates approximately 40% overlap with protein expression profiles from human foetal brain at 16–20 gestational weeks, reflecting molecular features of mid-gestation cortex, including progenitor zones, subplate, and cortical layers.17 Together, these data position organoids as a physiologically relevant system for human neurogenesis and a biological model to investigate viral tropism, cytopathic effects, and disruptions in neurogenic programs during early development.

The potential for OROV to affect human neural progenitors and early cortical tissue remains largely unexplored, even as reports of viral circulation and geographic expansion continue to rise. To address this gap, we used hiPSC-derived neural stem cells (NSCs) and brain organoids to investigate the infectivity and neuropathogenic potential of two genetically distinct OROV strains, including a recent clinical reassortant isolate. These human neural cellular models provide direct evidence of OROV-driven neurodevelopmental pathology in a tissue-like, physiologically relevant context. We show that OROV infects NSCs, astrocytes, and neurons, leading to increased cytotoxicity, impaired proliferation, and disruption of cortical-like tissue organisation. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses further reveal that OROV perturbs neurodevelopmental pathways and extracellular matrix components essential for cortical growth, consistent with the structural abnormalities observed in infected organoids. These findings provide mechanistic insight into OROV-induced neurodevelopmental pathology and highlight its potential contribution to congenital disease amid expanding viral transmission.

Methods

Ethics statement

The human induced pluripotent stem cell line GM23279A (RRID:CVCL_F178) was obtained from the Coriell Institute for Medical Research (NIGMS Human Genetic Cell Repository), collected under IRB-approved protocols with documented donor consent. The human embryonic stem cell line BR-1 (RRID:CVCL_C062) was derived in accordance with Brazilian legislation (Biosafety Law No. 11.105/2005), with approval from the Ethics Committee of the Instituto de Biociências, University of São Paulo, and with donor consent. All procedures involving human-derived materials were conducted in accordance with the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Copa D’Or Hospital (CAAE 60944916.5.0000.5249, approval 1.791.182).

Virus strains and propagation

All procedures involving the handling of viable viral particles—including inoculation, viral propagation, and titration—were performed within the BSL-3 facility of the Molecular Virology Laboratory at UFRJ. Two Oropouche virus (OROV) strains were used: BeAn19991 and RJ (LVM-2024). BeAn19991 is a reference strain obtained from the Evandro Chagas Institute, Brazil (GenBank accession numbers: L: KP052850; M: KP052851; S: KP052852). RJ (LVM-2024) is a clinical isolate collected in 2024 from the Cacaria district, Rio de Janeiro state, Brazil (GenBank accession numbers: L: PQ537324; M: PQ537328; S: PQ537332).5 This new isolate displays segmental reassortment in the L and S segments, showing phylogenetic similarity to Iquitos virus (IQTV), an orthobunyavirus associated with outbreaks in Peru. Viral stocks were propagated in Aedes albopictus C6/36 cells (ATCC CRL-1660) maintained in Leibovitz's L-15 (Gibco) medium supplemented with 2% foetal bovine serum (Gibco), non-essential amino acids (Merck), and 0.3% tryptose phosphate broth (Gibco), grown at a density of 7 × 104 cells per cm2. Infections were performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.005. After 72 h of infection at 28 °C, supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. Conditioned medium from uninfected C6/36 cells was used as mock control.

Neural stem cells

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs; GM23279A) and embryonic stem cells (hESCs; BR-1) were maintained on Geltrex-coated plates in StemFlex medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). hiPSCs were differentiated into NSCs according to the PSC Neural Induction Medium protocol (Gibco). Briefly, iPSCs were plated as cell clumps at a density of 2.5–3 × 105 cells per well on Geltrex matrix-coated 6-well plates. On day 1, the culture medium was substituted with PSC Neural Induction Medium (Neurobasal plus 1X Neural Induction Supplement - NIS). Medium changes were performed every other day. By day 7, P0 NSCs were harvested and subsequently expanded in Neural Expansion Medium (1:1, Neurobasal: Advanced DMEM/F12, 1X NIS).

NSCs infection

Neural Stem Cells (GM23279A and BR-1) were cultured on Geltrex-coated plates for infection assays with OROV strains BeAn19991 or RJ (LVM-2024) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. Cells were incubated for 2 h with the viral inoculum at 37 °C. Then, the medium was removed and replaced with fresh Neural Expansion Medium. For immunofluorescence analysis, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed in cells fixed at 24 h post-infection (hpi). Cells were rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature, and stored at 4 °C until further processing.

For transcriptomic analysis, cells were plated in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of infection, conditioned medium was discarded, cells were washed twice with PBS and RNA was extracted.

Neurosphere culture and infection

Neurospheres were generated from hiPSC-derived NSCs (GM23279A) and dissociated to perform infection in isolated neurons. Briefly, NSCs were dissociated to a single-cell suspension and seeded in 96-well low-attachment plates at a density of 90,000 cells per well in Neural Expansion Medium consisting of a 1:1 mixture of Neurobasal and Advanced DMEM/F12 (Life Technologies), supplemented with 1× NIS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Media were changed 72 h after plating to Differentiation Medium consisting of DMEM/F12 (Life Technologies) and Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 1× N2 (Invitrogen) and 1× B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Media were subsequently changed twice weekly. On day 10, neurospheres were dissociated with accutase and plated on poly-d-lysine-coated 96 well plates in differentiation medium with 10 μM ROCKi. Medium was changed 24 h after plating to remove ROCKi. After 3 days of plating, cultures were infected with OROV strains BeAn19991 or RJ (LVM-2024) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were fixed at 24 hpi for immunofluorescence analysis.

Astrocyte culture and infection

Human astrocytes (GM23279A) were cultured and infected to evaluate OROV tropism and cytopathic effects in glial cells. Astrocytes were maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For infection assays, cells were seeded in 96-well at a density of 10.000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Monolayers were then infected with OROV strains BeAn19991 or RJ (LVM-2024) at an MOI of 0.1 for 2 h at 37 °C. The inoculum was removed, cells were washed with PBS, and fresh astrocyte medium was added. Astrocytes were fixed at 24 h post-infection in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, washed with PBS and processed for immunofluorescence.

Cell culture and quality control

All cell cultures used in this study were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert™ detection kit (Lonza) at the time of plating for experiments and were confirmed to be negative.

Synthetic RNA standard curve

To enable accurate RNA quantification in supernatants from infected NSCs, synthetic viral RNA produced via in vitro transcription was used to construct a standard curve. Initially, viral RNA was purified from OROV RJ (LVM-2024) stock using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN), according to the manufacturer's protocol. This RNA was reverse transcribed with Superscript IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using specific primers targeting the S segment (BstBI F: 5′ TGTCTTCGAAAGTAGTGTGCTCCACAATTCA 3’; XhoI R: 5′ GTCTTCCTCGAGAGTAGTGTGCTCCACTATATGTCA 3′). Following cDNA synthesis, the S segment was amplified using the Platinum II Taq Hot-Start DNA Polymerase system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The amplicon was cloned into a TVT7R (0, 0)17 vector (AddGene) via restriction digestion and ligated using T4 DNA Ligase. The resulting plasmid, designated TVT7OROV-S, was used as the template for an in vitro transcription reaction with the MEGAscript™ T7 Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. Transcribed RNA was purified by lithium chloride precipitation and quantified using the Qubit™ RNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The synthetic RNA was then diluted to 2 × 108 copies/μL, yielding 109 copies per RT-qPCR reaction, followed by a serial 10-fold dilution (from 109 to 102 copies) in RNase-free water. All primers used were obtained from Extend.

Viral load quantification

To assess the viral load in the supernatant of OROV-infected NSCs, neurosphere-derived neurons and astrocytes, RT-qPCR was performed in parallel with a synthetic RNA standard curve. Briefly, supernatants were collected 24 h post-infection, and nucleic acids were extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer's protocol. Purified RNAs from both the supernatants and synthetic RNA were then amplified using the GoTaq Probe 1-Step RT-qPCR System, targeting the OROV S segment (OROV_FNF: 5′ TCCGGAGGCAGCATATGTG 3’; OROV_FNR: 5′ ACAACACCAGCATTGAGCACTT 3’; OROV_FNP: 5′ (FAM) CATTTGAAGCTAGATACGG 3′).4 Following amplification, cycle threshold (Ct) values were plotted against the synthetic RNA standard curve to determine the corresponding RNA copy number per millilitre in each sample using a simple linear regression method with GraphPad Prism 9.

RNA extraction, quantification, library preparation, RNA-sequencing and analysis

Cell monolayers were incubated with Buffer RLT from the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen), and total RNA was extracted following the manufacturer's procedures. Purified RNA was treated with TURBO DNA-free™ DNase to remove genomic contaminating DNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total RNA was quantified using Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit on a Qubit 4 fluorimeter (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA integrity was assessed by capillary electrophoresis on a 4150 TapeStation System using a High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies). Only samples with a RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of ≥8.7 were used. Sequencing libraries were prepared from 100 ng of total DNase-treated RNA from 5 replicates from 2 independent experiments for each group (non-infected cells, OROV BeAn19991 infected cells, or OROV RJ (LVM-2024) infected cells) using Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep, Ligation kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Ribosomal RNA was depleted by the Ribo-Zero Plus kit, followed by fragmentation and denaturation of RNA, synthesis of first and second cDNA strands, adenylation of 3′ ends, anchor ligation, clean-up of fragments, library amplification and clean-up. For the clean-up steps, Agencourt RNAClean XP and Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter) were used for RNA and for the libraries, respectively. Samples were indexed using IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes Set A. The prepared libraries were quantified using QIAseq Library Quant Assay kit (Qiagen), and the average size (350 bp) was verified using High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape reagents (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing was performed as paired-end using a P2 Reagent Cartridge 200 Cycles on the NextSeq 1000 platform (Illumina), with over 15 million reads per sample. Raw FASTQ files were quality-checked using FastQC and aligned to the human transcriptome (GENCODE v48) with Salmon,18 applying default parameters and the Gibbs sampling bootstrap method. Transcript-level quantifications were imported into R and summarised at the gene level. All downstream analyses and visualisations were performed entirely in R, with the full code available at: https://github.com/iza-mcac/2025-08-Oropouche-virus-strains-brain.

Brain organoids

Brain organoids were generated from hiPSCs (GM23279A) cultured in StemFlex medium on Geltrex-coated plates using an adapted version of the unguided Lancaster protocol.12,19 Embryoid bodies (EBs) were formed by plating 9000 cells/well in 96-well plates in StemFlex medium containing 50 μM ROCK inhibitor (Merck Millipore) and centrifuging to improve initial aggregation. After day 1, EBs were cultured with human Embryonic Stem Cell medium (hESC medium): DMEM/F12, 20% Knockout serum replacement (KSR), 3% certified foetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (P/S), 1% GlutaMAX (Life Technologies), 1% MEM-NEAAs and 100 μM 2-Mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 50 μM ROCKi, and 4 ng/mL bFGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific). On day 6, EBs were transferred to 24-well ultralow-attachment plates with neuroinduction medium: 1% N2 supplement (Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX, 1% MEM-NEAAs, 1% P/S (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1 μg/mL heparin (STEMCELL Technologies) in DMEM/F12. At day 10, organoids were coated with Matrigel (Corning) in a 60-mm non-adherent tissue culture plate. Following Matrigel coating, organoids were returned to 24-well ultra-low-attachment plates with neurodifferentiation medium without vitamin A: 50% neurobasal medium, 0.5% N2, 1% B27 supplement without vitamin A, 50 uM 2-Mercaptoethanol, 0.5% MEM-NEAA, 1% GlutaMAX, 1% P/S, and DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with media change after 48 h in culture. On day 15, brain organoids were transferred to 6-well plates on an orbital shaker at 90 rpm and transferred to neurodifferentiation medium with vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Medium was replenished twice a week until day 30 or 60.

Brain organoid infection

Human brain organoids differentiated for either 30 or 60 days were infected with 3 × 103 plaque-forming units (PFU) of OROV, using the strains BeAn19991 or RJ (LVM-2024), per organoid for 2 h at 37 °C under continuous orbital agitation (90 rpm). Infections were performed in 12-well low-attachment plates containing 800 μL of neurodifferentiation medium with vitamin A per well. After infection, organoids were washed three times with PBS and transferred to virus-free differentiation medium under continuous agitation.

Conditioned media from organoids infected at 30 days of differentiation were collected at 12 h and 1–5 days post-infection (dpi) for LDH assays (Promega), 10 μL, and viral titration (50 μL). At 5 dpi, organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 1 h at room temperature, washed with PBS, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose and stored for cryosectioning.

Organoids infected at 60 days were maintained in culture until 11 dpi with medium changes (neurodifferentiation medium with vitamin A) on days 3 and 7 post-infection. Endpoint analysis was performed at 11 dpi, when organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 1 h at room temperature, washed with PBS, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose and stored for cryosectioning.

Viral titration

Viral titres were measured by plaque assay in Vero Cells as described previously.5 Briefly, Vero CCL-81 cells (BCRJ-0245) seeded in 24-well plates at 90% confluency were inoculated with 10-fold serial dilutions of OROV stocks or organoids supernatant for 1 h in the incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). After viral adsorption, the inoculum was removed, and cells were cultivated for 3 days in a semisolid medium composed of Alpha-MEM supplemented with 1% foetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1.5% carboxymethyl cellulose (Merck). After this period, cells were fixed with 10% formaldehyde solution for 1 h, washed with distilled water and stained with 0.25% crystal violet and 20% ethanol solution for plaque visualisation. Viral titres were expressed as plaque-forming units (PFU) per millilitre. For all inoculations the MOI was calculated based on viral infectious units (PFU) per number of cells.

Cytotoxicity assay

OROV-induced cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into the culture medium as a marker of cell lysis. At each point, 10 μL of conditioned medium was collected and mixed with 90 μL of LDH storage buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.3; 10% glycerol; 1% BSA). Samples were stored at −80 °C and thawed simultaneously for batch processing. LDH levels were quantified using the LDH-Glo™ Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega), according to the manufacturer's protocol. For each condition, LDH values were normalised to the 12-h post-infection time point to calculate fold changes.

Immunofluorescence and image acquisition

After 24 h of infection, all 2D cultures (NSCs, neurosphere-derived neurons and astrocytes) cultured in 96-well plates were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, samples were washed for 5 min with PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and washed again with PBS for 5 min. Blocking was performed for 1 h at room temperature using 3% BSA and 5% NGS in PBS. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Depending on the experiment, the following primary antibodies were used: anti-OROV (mouse polyclonal anti-OROV, ATCC, antibody, in-house, 1:1000), anti-Nestin (RRID: AB_2155426, rabbit, Neuromics, 1:500), anti-MAP2 (RRID: AB_10982739, rabbit, Invitrogen, 1:100) and anti-GFAP (RRID: AB_880202, rabbit, Abcam, 1:250). Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times for 5 min each with PBS, fluorescently labelled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-rabbit; Invitrogen, 1:400) were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, protected from light. Nuclei were stained for 10 min with DAPI (300 nM solution in PBS), followed by two PBS washes and a final rinse with MilliQ water. A solution of 50% glycerol in PBS was added to the plates. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Agilent Cytation 1 Cell Imaging, and quantification was performed using Gen5's integrated analysis tools.

Brain organoids at 35 and 71 days in culture were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature, rinsed with PBS, cryoprotected overnight in 30% sucrose at 4 °C, and embedded in Tissue Freezing Medium (Leica). Cryosections of 10 μm were obtained using a Leica CM1860 cryostat. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer: 10 mM Sodium Citrate, 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20, pH 6.0. Sections were permeabilized in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, washed in PBS for 5 min, and blocked for 1 h in 3% BSA prior to overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer (3% BSA and 5% normal goat serum in PBS). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-cleaved caspase-3 (RRID: AB_91556, rabbit, Millipore, 1:100), anti-OROV (mouse, ATCC, 1:200), anti-PAX6 (RRID: AB_2533526, rabbit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:100), anti-Ki67 (rabbit, Neuromics, 1:100), anti-GFAP (rabbit, Abcam, 1:250), and anti-MAP2 (rabbit, Invitrogen, 1:100). After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse Alexa Fluor-conjugated, Invitrogen, 1:400), and nuclei were counterstained with 300 nM DAPI for 10 min. Slides were mounted with Aqua-Poly/Mount and stored at 4 °C after drying. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using either a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope or a Zeiss Cell Discoverer, with a 20 × objective lens. Image analysis was performed using Fiji and ZEN Blue software.

Proteomic analysis of 35-day-old brain organoids

Brain organoids at 30 days of differentiation were infected as described above. At 5 dpi, organoids were washed once with PBS, all residual liquid was removed, and the samples were frozen dry in microtubes. Protein extraction from frozen brain organoids (infected and control) was performed using a BeadRuptor 4 (Omni) with ceramic beads and a 2X lysis buffer (10% SDS, 60 mM DTT, and 100 mM TEAB, pH 8.5). A total of 150 μL of lysis buffer was added to each brain organoid sample. Samples were homogenised using the BeadRuptor with three cycles of 40 s on and off, followed by sonication with three cycles of 2 min on and off. Protein extracts were collected after centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C (Thermo Scientific Megafuge 8 R), then incubated at 95 °C for 10 min in a Thermomixer (Eppendorf) and cooled on ice. Protein precipitation was performed using the methanol/chloroform/water (MCW) method (4:1:3, v/v). The resulting pellet was resuspended in 2X lysis buffer (10% SDS, 60 mM DTT, and 100 mM TEAB, pH 8.5) and sonicated. Subsequently, an aliquot from each sample was precipitated with four volumes of cold acetone and used for protein quantification using the Qubit assay. Fifty micrograms of total protein were diluted 1:2 with 2X lysis buffer and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min in a Thermomixer. Protein disulfide bonds were alkylated with 120 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were acidified with 1.2% phosphoric acid, and seven volumes of binding/wash buffer (100 mM TEAB in 90% methanol) were added. The mixture was transferred to S-Trap mini filters (Protifi) and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 30 s to capture proteins within the S-Trap column. The binding/wash buffer (400 μL) was added and centrifuged again at 4000 × g for 30 s; this step was repeated four times for SDS removal. Enzymatic digestion was performed by adding 125 μL of digestion solution containing trypsin diluted in 50 mM TEAB (enzyme-to-substrate ratio 1:50). Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 17 h without agitation. Tryptic peptides were sequentially eluted using the following solutions: 80 μL of 50 mM TEAB, 80 μL of 0.2% formic acid, 40 μL of 50% ACN/0.2% formic acid, and 40 μL of 70% ACN/0.2% formic acid. The eluates were pooled, dried in a SpeedVac (Thermo Scientific), and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid for LC–MS/MS analysis.

Tryptic peptides (250 ng) were analysed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific). Samples were loaded onto a trap column (C18, 0.5 cm × 300 μm I.D., Thermo Scientific) and separated on an analytical column (PepMap RSLC C18, 75 cm × 75 μm I.D., 2 μm particle size, Thermo Scientific) maintained at 60 °C. Peptide elution was carried out using mobile phases consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (solvent B) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min, following the gradient: 2–7% B for 1 min, 7–14% B for 24 min, 14–40% B for 49 min, 40–60% B for 5 min, 60–99% B for 2 min, and 99% B for 8 min.

Data were acquired in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. MS1 parameters were set to a mass range of 375–1500 m/z, resolution of 30,000 at 200 m/z, RF lens at 50%, and standard mode for maximum injection time and AGC target. For DIA acquisition, the same mass range (375–1500 m/z) was divided into 59 overlapping isolation windows (1.0 m/z overlap) as follows: 15 m/z for 375.5–540.0 m/z; 9 m/z for 540.5–756.0 m/z; 14 m/z for 756.5–854.0 m/z; 19 m/z for 854.5–968.0 m/z; 39 m/z for 968.5–1319.0 m/z; and 90 m/z for 1319.0–1499.0 m/z. DIA parameters were set as follows: normalised AGC target of 500%, maximum injection time of 25 ms, resolution of 15,000, and normalised collision energy (NCE) of 28%. Raw data were processed using DIA-NN software (version 2.3.0)20 with an in silico spectral library generated from Homo sapiens (UP000005640_9606, 20,435 entries, 2024_05_01), Oropouche virus (UP000204424_118655_reference_4proteins_2025-10-29), and commons contaminant.21 Search parameters included tryptic digestion allowing up to three missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation (M), acetylation (M), and N-terminal methionine excision were included as variable modifications. A false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was applied at the precursor level. The “Match between runs” (MBR) option was disabled.

DIA-NN protein output (report.pg_matrix.tsv) was imported into Amica platform (version 3.0.1)22 using the custom format with a contaminant column. Data filtering steps included a minimum two out of three valid values in each groups. Data normalisation was performed using the median centring method, and no imputation was applied. Differential abundance analysis was conducted using the limma statistical framework, considering a log2 |fold change| ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value <0.05 as the significance threshold. Visualisation and statistics for proteomics comparisons were performed in R and the entire code is also available at https://github.com/iza-mcac/2025-08-Oropouche-virus-strains-brain.

Proteomic analysis of 60-day-old non-infected brain organoids

For 60-day-old organoids, proteins were extracted individually from three organoids using low-binding Eppendorf tubes (Eppendorf, cat. no. 5360000038) and 100 μL of lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 1% SDS, 1% glycerol, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Complete™ Mini, EDTA-free, Roche, cat. no. 11836170001) and Benzonase (50 units; Millipore, E8263). Samples were subjected to sonication (Bioruptor Pico, Diagenode, cat. no. B01080010) followed by determination of protein concentrations with the BCA Protein Assay (Thermo, 23,228). TCEP (5 mM) and Chloroacetamide (12.5 mM) were used to ensure reduction and alkylation of cystines to form acetyl modified thiols. Samples were processed using the Magnetic Proteomics Sample Prep (MPSP) Kit (Promega, cat. no. CS3325A04) to remove undesired surfactants followed by on-bead proteolysis (37 °C, overnight) with Trypsin/Lys-C (Promega, V5071) (enzyme/substrate ratio = 1:25). Following proteolysis, peptides were quantified using the Pierce Quantitative Fluorometric Peptide Assay (Thermo, 23,290).

Samples were analysed by LC–MS/MS on an Orbitrap Exploris 240 Mass Spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) coupled to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC (ThermoFisher Scientific). Digested peptides (500 ng) were first loaded onto a C18 Trap Column (Thermo PepMap Neo; particle size = 5 mm; 300 mm × 5 mm) to remove salts and buffers followed by loading onto a C18 analytical column (Thermo Acclaim PepMAP RSLC). A gradient of 0% A (0.1% Formic Acid in water) to 32% B (80% Acetonitrile/0.1 Formic acid/19.9% Water) over 120 min at a flow rate of 400 nL per minute was used for peptide separations. A temperature of 50 °C was used to reduce column backpressure and ensure consistency of separations. Data was acquired in Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode using a top12 method. The following MS1 parameters were used: MS1 resolution = 120,000; Scan range = 250–1500 m/z; MS1 AGC % = 300; MS1 AGC = 3 × 106; MS1 ion injection time = 50 ms; Intensity Threshold = 1 × 104; Dynamic exclusion = 60 s after 1 trigger. The MS2 parameters included: Isolation window = 4; HCD collision energy = stepped (27/30/33); MS2 resolution—15,000; MS2 AGC % = 100; MS2 AGC = 1 × 105; MS2 ion injection time = 25.

Data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (version 3.2, ThermoFisher Scientific) with the Chimerys search engine (MSAID). Protein identification was performed against the UniProt Human database (canonical sequences only, no isoforms). For each search, 2 missed cleavages (Trypsin/P) were allowed using a 20 ppm mass tolerance. Dynamic modifications included Methionine oxidation and static modifications included Cysteine carbamidomethylation (due to chloroacetamide modification). An FDR of 1% was used for filtering. Upon completion of the data search, search results were further analysed using the Mass Dynamics software platform (massdynamics.com) with normalisation across the total proteome and imputation using low abundance values. Analysis of Fold changes required a fold change (Log2) greater than 1 and a P value above 1.4 (FDR = 5%).

Statistical analysis

Replicates and number of organoids analysed are described in the specific results sections and figures subtitles. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results

OROV infects human NSCs and modulates neurodevelopmental markers

To assess the susceptibility of neural progenitor cells to OROV, we exposed human iPSC-derived NSCs to two viral strains: the prototypical BeAn19991 strain and a recently isolated clinical strain from Rio de Janeiro, RJ (LVM-2024), presenting genomic reassortment in the S and L segments associated with the recent virus expansion outbreaks in Latin America.5 At 24 hpi, immunofluorescence staining revealed robust intracellular accumulation of OROV antigens (green) in Nestin-positive NSCs (red), confirming infection of both strains (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis at 24 hpi showed a higher percentage of infected NSCs in the BeAn19991 condition (86.6% ± 9.8%) compared to the RJ (LVM-2024) strain (48.2% ± 8.8%), suggesting differences between strains (Fig. 1B). In addition, both OROV lineages displayed detectable viral loads in the NSC supernatants (Fig. 1C), reaching 3.39 × 104 ± 3.59 × 104 RNA copies per mL for BeAn19991 and 7.96 × 103 ± 7.20 × 103 for RJ (LVM-2024) strain. The infection of NSCs with both OROV strains was replicated in NSCs derived from embryonic cell line BR-1 (Supplementary Figure S1A–C). These findings demonstrated that OROV efficiently infects human neural progenitor cells at early stages of differentiation and raises the possibility of interference with neurodevelopmental programs.

To further characterise the molecular consequences of OROV infection in human NSCs, we performed transcriptomic analysis at 24 hpi. Differential gene expression analysis–applying thresholds of Log2FoldChange higher than 2 and p-value lower than 0.01–revealed many upregulated genes in both BeAn19991-and RJ (LVM-2024)-infected NSCs compared to mock controls, with 657 genes commonly induced across both strains (Fig. 1D). Downregulated genes also showed substantial overlap (94 shared). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed regulation of pathways related to viral replication, such as viral gene RNA expression, viral release from the host cell and viral budding, as well as biological processes critical for early neurodevelopment. Both strains showed enrichment for gene sets associated with inhibition of stem cell maintenance and differentiation, and negative regulation of neuron projection development and neuronal differentiation (Fig. 1E). Additionally, we observed enrichment of pathways related to the regulation of cell-cycle checkpoints and the intrinsic apoptotic signalling pathway in response to DNA damage, suggesting that OROV infection may trigger cell death mechanisms in NSCs (Fig. 1E). These results provide molecular evidence that OROV impairs fundamental programs of neural progenitor function.

The comparison between BeAn19991-and RJ (LVM-2024)-infected NSCs (Fig. 1F), revealed 4 genes upregulated in RJ (LVM-2024): CYP26A1, LSM6, RGS16 and SAXO2. These genes are associated with signalling pathways related to host defence, cellular stress responses, and structural remodelling. Interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as IL18, CCL5, APOBEC3A, ITGAL and ITGAM were downregulated in RJ (LVM-2024) compared to BeAn19991. These genes appear upregulated in BeAn19991 versus Mock, but not in RJ (LVM-2024) versus Mock (Supplementary Figure S1D, Supplementary Table S1). This suggests that RJ (LVM-2024) strain elicits a weaker interferon response in NSCs than the reference BeAn19991. Additionally, the collagen-encoding gene COL4A4 was downregulated in RJ (LVM-2024)-infected NSCs, while COL12A1 and COL1A1 showed a trend towards lower expression. These genes are essential for the development of the brain and the blood–brain barrier and were also downregulated in brains of newborns with congenital Zika syndrome.23,24

Oropouche virus strains infect brain organoids, leading to impaired growth and tissue organisation

Infection of neuroprogenitors and regulation of gene expression affecting the developmental program prompted us to investigate if OROV infection in brain tissue derived from iPSCs could reproduce some of the deleterious effects of in vivo infection. For that, 30-day brain organoids were infected with BeAn19991-and RJ (LVM-2024) and monitored for 5 dpi.

Viral progeny was quantified in supernatants at multiple time points after inoculation by plaque assay. Viral replication was detected for both strains as early as 12 hpi (Fig. 2A), with the BeAn19991 strain producing higher titres. After 24 h, viral titres increased with no significant differences between the strains. These data indicate that both OROV strains can infect and replicate efficiently in human brain organoids.

Morphological inspection of infected organoids revealed reduced growth and irregular contours compared to mock controls (Fig. 2B and C). Growth rate was reduced in both viral conditions when compared to mock treated cells, showing disruption of the development (Fig. 2C). Histological sections revealed architectural disarray, with collapse of the neuroepithelial surface (Fig. 2D). Cortical-like stratification, evident in controls, was severely disrupted in infected conditions. These observations indicate that OROV infection compromises both growth and structural organisation of developing brain tissue.

OROV infection induces widespread cell death in brain organoids

To determine whether cell death contributed to the observed collapse in organoid morphology, we performed cleaved caspase-3 immunostaining. At 5 dpi, both BeAn19991 and RJ (LVM-2024) strains triggered a marked increase in caspase-3 positive cells (Fig. 3A–C). Caspase-3 activation in infected organoids was not confined to superficial layers but extended throughout the neuroepithelium, including ventricular-like zones (Fig. 3B). Supplementary Figure S2A and B shows the colocalisation between OROV and caspase-3, indicating apoptosis in infected cells. Quantification (Supplementary Figure S2C) revealed no difference between strains, supporting comparable cytotoxic effects. These results suggest that OROV infection leads to widespread and strain-independent apoptosis in brain organoids.

Further evaluation for cell toxicity during infection was obtained by monitoring the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), a marker of cytotoxicity (Fig. 3D). Along the 5 days of infection BeAn19991 and RJ (LVM-2024) induced a progressive increase in LDH release compared to the mock controls. No statistically significant differences were observed between strains at any time point, indicating comparable cell damage with similar kinetics. Altogether, these results support a comparable cytotoxicity profile for the two strains.

Neural progenitor proliferation and identity are disrupted by OROV

Given the high infectivity in NSCs and the degenerative phenotype in organoids, we examined whether OROV impacts the neuroprogenitor pool in brain organoids. Immunostaining for Ki67 revealed a reduction in proliferating cells following infection (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis revealed a marked reduction of Ki67+ cells in ventricular-like zones, areas that during brain development contain organised neural progenitor cells (Fig. 4B). Similarly, the radial glial marker PAX6 was decreased and more disorganised in infected conditions (Fig. 4C and D), with discontinuous expression along the apical surface. These results indicate that OROV infection depletes both the proliferative and structural neuroepithelial scaffold, likely contributing to impaired neurogenesis and collapse of tissue architecture.

Proteomic profiling reveals coordinated suppression of translation, ECM and neuroglial programs upon OROV infection

To identify host pathways through which OROV perturbs early cortical development, we performed label-free quantitative proteomics on 35-day-old cerebral organoids infected for 5 days with BeAn19991 (BeAn) or RJ (LVM-2024; RJ) strains. Differential protein abundance analysis was performed using the limma statistical framework, considering a log2 ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value <0.05 as the significance threshold. In total, there were 5930 proteins identified in both datasets. From these, 211 proteins appeared downregulated and 47 upregulated, upon RJ (LVM-2024) infection. 259 proteins were downregulated and 38 were upregulated by BeAn19991 infection.

Gene Ontology enrichment of proteins modulated upon Oropouche virus infection, considering both strains together, revealed a convergent impact on host pathways typically targeted during viral replication. Significantly enriched categories included translation and cytoplasmic translation, ribosome biogenesis, type I interferon response, chemokine production, calcium-dependent signalling, positive regulation of synaptic transmission, long-term synaptic potentiation, and Wnt/canonical Wnt signalling pathways (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table S2).

Dot size indicates -log of the p. adjusted and colour indicates the Normalised Enrichment Score (NES). (B and C) Volcano plots of BeAn19991 vs Mock (B) and RJ (LVM-2024) vs Mock (C) of differentially present proteins named as their genes. Proteins that pass the cut-off values (p < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1) are shown in colour, and those associated with collagen, neuronal functions, or viral components are further highlighted. (D) Dot Plot indicating specific proteins (named as their genes) associated with ECM/adhesion, neurons and NSC differentially present in BeAn19991 vs Mock and RJ (LVM-2024) vs Mock.

At the protein level, volcano plots highlighted robust upregulation of viral structural and non-structural proteins (OROV_M, OROV_NSs, OROV_L, OROV_N; Fig. 5B and C), confirming the productive infection (Fig. 2A), accompanied by significant downregulation of key basement membrane and ECM components (COL18A1, COL6A1, COL4A5, COL4A6, LAMB1, ITGA2, SLIT2, SULF2) in both BeAn vs mock and RJ vs mock comparisons (Fig. 5B and C). Transcriptional and translational regulators, including the cortical patterning factor EMX2, the RNA polymerase subunit POLR2G and the ribosomal protein RPL39, were also consistently shifted toward negative log2 fold changes, indicating suppression of intrinsic neurodevelopmental and protein synthesis machinery.

To integrate these changes in a cell-type-oriented framework, we examined curated marker sets for ECM/adhesion, neurons and neural stem/progenitor cells. A heatmap of log2 fold changes showed predominantly negative regulation of astrocyte-enriched ECM markers (e.g., COL18A1, COL6A1, ITGA2, LAMB1, SLIT2, SULF2), neuronal markers (including APP and multiple synaptic/axonal proteins), and NSC-associated factors such as EMX2 (Fig. 5D). Together, these data indicate that OROV infection in early-stage brain organoids not only induces strong viral protein accumulation but also drives a coordinated depletion of translational, ECM and neuroglial signatures, consistent with disruption of both structural and transcriptional programs critical for cortical development. Despite robust accumulation of viral structural and non-structural proteins, proteins annotated to the “response to type I interferon” pathway did not display upregulation (Fig. 5A).

Oropouche virus infects astrocytes and neurons in brain organoids

To assess OROV tropism in neural tissues at later stages of development, we used 60-day organoids containing differentiated astrocytes and neurons. To confirm the maturation state of organoids at this stage, we performed proteomic profiling of non-infected organoids collected at days 35 and 60. Using curated neuronal and astrocytic marker sets, this comparison showed an increase in both neuronal and glial markers at 60 days, with neuronal markers significantly over-represented by Fisher's exact test (Supplementary Figure S3A). Functional pathway analysis further showed that neurodevelopmental processes such as synaptic plasticity, neuritogenesis, and neuronal differentiation were detectable at day 35 and were more strongly represented at day 60, with expanded coverage of neurobiological pathways (Supplementary Figure S3B). Together, these data indicate a progressive increase in molecular complexity and maturation between days 35 and 60, consistent with advancing neuronal and glial development.

Having confirmed the cellular composition of the 60-day organoids, we next infected them with BeAn19991 and RJ (LVM-2024) strains. After 11 days of infection, organoid sections exhibited extensive tissue damage. Haematoxylin–eosin staining revealed widespread cell death, consistent with the more pronounced cytopathic effects expected from a prolonged infection period (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence analysis of these sections showed colocalisation between OROV antigens and MAP2+ neurons as well as GFAP+ astrocytes, indicating that both cell types were susceptible to infection (Fig. 6B). However, the extended 11-day infection led to substantial cellular and structural destruction caused by both the BeAn19991 and RJ (LVM-2024) strains, resulting in a marked reduction in the number of detectable neurons and astrocytes. This loss prevented reliable quantification of infection in these populations within organoids. To confirm OROV susceptibility in differentiated cell types, we performed infections in 2D neuronal and astrocyte cultures. Immunofluorescence revealed colocalisation of OROV antigens with GFAP+ astrocytes and MAP2+ neurons (Supplementary Figure S4A). Infection quantification at 24 h and RT-qPCR detection of viral RNA in the supernatant confirmed productive OROV infection in both cell types, with astrocyte infection and strain-dependent differences observed in neurons (Supplementary Figure S4B and C).

Discussion

Here, we report that both a prototypical and a contemporary reassortant OROV strain infect hiPSC-derived neural stem cells, astrocytes, and neurons; trigger widespread apoptosis; deplete proliferating neural progenitors; and disrupt cortical-like tissue architecture in brain organoids. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses further reveal coordinated suppression of neurodevelopmental programs, extracellular matrix components, and translational machinery.

Recent epidemiological data from the Brazilian Ministry of Health report five confirmed deaths resulting from OROV infection, with additional two fatalities currently under investigation. In 2024, approximately 13,000 cases were reported nationwide, and a steep rise was evident in 2025, with nearly 12,000 cases documented by mid-year.25 The aforementioned outbreaks were associated with a recently emergent OROV reassortant lineage, raising concerns about changes in viral spread and pathogenesis associated with neurological complications, severe systemic disease, and suspected congenital infections.3,7,26

Pioneer work from our group showed microcephaly-like phenotype induced by ZIKV infection in human brain organoids.8 Now, we show neuroepithelial collapse and reduced growth in brain organoids infected with two genetically distinct OROV strains, the prototype BeAn19991 and the recently circulating RJ (LVM-2024) isolate. Comparative analysis of BeAn19991 and RJ (LVM-2024) revealed no major phenotypic differences in terms of cell death or tissue damage, although the prototypical strain displayed faster replication kinetics and higher titres at 12 hpi. This contrasts with in vitro data suggesting increased virulence of reassortant strains circulating in Brazil.27 While past reports raised concern over the potential emergence of more pathogenic variants,5 our findings do not support increased cytopathogenicity under the experimental conditions tested here. Nonetheless, we should consider that more nuanced differences in viral behaviour may only emerge when comparing RJ (LVM-2024) with other contemporary strains. Comparative studies with the emergent reassortant BR-2015-2024 reassortant lineage, identified during the 2023–2024 outbreaks in northern Brazil, may reveal subtle variations in tropism, replication dynamics, or immunoevasion.4,28

In this context, Martins et al. reported a case series of newborns with microcephaly, congenital malformations, miscarriages, and infant deaths reported between 2015 and 2021, in which anti-OROV IgM or viral RNA was detected, supporting the hypothesis of vertical transmission of Oropouche virus.29 Notably, a fatal case in a 47-day-old infant showed histopathological and immunohistochemical evidence of OROV involvement in brain tissue.29 Another study demonstrated that OROV can infect trophoblasts and human placental explants, contributing to the vertical transmission hypothesis.30 While previous investigations focused primarily on the BeAn19991 strain, a prototypical isolate from early Oropouche fever outbreaks, our study incorporates a representative strain from the current epidemic and shows that this reassortant OROV strain also infects human neural cells and disrupts key neurodevelopmental processes.

These findings suggest that the teratogenic potential of OROV may be an intrinsic aspect of its pathogenesis, historically underdiagnosed in regions such as the Amazon basin. Together, our results reinforce the need to consider OROV in the differential diagnosis of congenital infections, particularly for pregnant individuals and newborns with unexplained neurodevelopmental anomalies. It is important to note, however, that our experimental models do not permit direct causal inference in human congenital disease; the association between OROV infection and microcephaly in clinical settings remains observational, and larger prospective epidemiological and clinical studies are required to establish the true frequency and spectrum of congenital OROV disease.

This recent surge in severe cases might reflect the broader geographic spread and higher incidence of OROV infection across Brazil, rather than intrinsic changes in viral virulence. This hypothesis is further supported by the teratogenic effects observed in other Orthobunyaviruses of the Simbu serogroup, where OROV is included, such as Akabane virus (AKAV) and Schmallenberg virus (SBV), which are known to cause congenital malformations and miscarriages in ruminants.31

Transcriptomic profiling of infected NSCs reveals a direct impact of OROV on neurodevelopmental programs. Both viral strains triggered upregulation of viral replication pathways and downregulation of gene sets involved in stem cell maintenance and neuronal differentiation. Notably, gene sets such as “negative regulation of neuron projection development” and “regulation of cell-cycle checkpoint” were significantly enriched, indicating that OROV impairs the molecular machinery responsible for proliferation and maturation of neural precursors. These molecular effects align with the collapse of neuroepithelial zones and loss of PAX6+ and Ki67+ cells observed in brain organoids, strengthening the mechanistic link between viral infection and disrupted corticogenesis.

In parallel, infection coordinately impaired type IV collagen synthesis, an essential component of the pial and vascular basement membrane, as evidenced at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels. At the RNA level, the RJ (LVM-2024) strain reduced expression of COL4A4 and showed trends toward lower expression of COL12A1 and COL1A1, genes critical for brain development and blood–brain barrier integrity.23 Previous studies with brains from neonates affected by congenital Zika syndrome reported a significant reduction in collagen fibres compared to control brains of the same gestational age,24 reinforcing the potential teratogenicity of OROV strains. Proteomic analysis revealed reduced levels of COL4A5 and COL4A6, confirming that multiple collagen IV isoforms are affected. These findings suggest that OROV compromises basement membrane integrity, consistent with genetic models in which collagen IV defects lead to neurovascular fragility, cobblestone-like cortical malformations, and reduced brain size.32

In addition to weakening this extracellular scaffold, OROV also perturbs core dorsal telencephalic patterning: robust downregulation of EMX2 across both viral strains indicates interference with a transcription factor that controls cortical arealization, radial glial proliferation, and neuroepithelial organisation.33 Notably, OROV infection also downregulates the Wnt pathway, which is a key regulator of dorsal progenitor fate decisions and the balance between symmetric expansion and neurogenic divisions; Wnt dysregulation has likewise been proposed as a contributor to Zika-associated microcephaly.34 Together, the concurrent reduction of Wnt signalling and EMX2 is consistent with a loss of dorsal radial glial identity and impaired progenitor output, while the loss of collagen IV suggests destabilisation of the basement membrane niche that anchors these progenitors, representing a dual hit to intrinsic patterning and extrinsic structural support that could converge on a microcephaly-like phenotype.

Beyond these early patterning and niche effects, our OROV datasets also point to disruption of neuronal maturation, including pathways linked to neurogenesis and synaptogenesis, suggesting that OROV outcomes may extend beyond gross reductions in brain size to more diffuse impairments in circuit assembly and developmental trajectories. In this respect, prior work in ZIKV–infected human neural stem cells and neurons reported impairment of neurogenesis- and synaptogenesis-related pathways,35 providing a useful benchmark for convergent mechanisms across neurotropic arboviruses. Recent foetal abnormal events associated with OROV vertical transmission show encephalomalacia and multi-organ necrosis with high viral RNA loads and vasculitis, suggesting a stronger component of fulminant systemic and vascular injury compared to classical descriptions of congenital Zika syndrome, in which CNS malformations can occur without such widespread organ damage.29

OROV tropism for microglia and neurons and neuroinflammatory damage in adult human brain slice cultures was previously demonstrated by Almeida et al.11 Our data expand these observations to the developing brain, demonstrating that NSCs are highly permissive to infection, with robust induction of cell death. In contrast to previously reported findings in adult tissue, showing negligible astrocyte infection,11 we observed clear OROV tropism for both astrocytes and neurons in brain organoids, which was also observed in 2D cell cultures of each cell type. This suggests that the immature human brain may present a broader range of susceptible cell types, potentially contributing to the severity of congenital outcomes. Given the critical role of radial glia and astrocytes in scaffolding cortical development, the infection in these cells could have compounded effects on tissue organisation and neuronal maturation. Noteworthy, while brain organoids used in the present work capture key features of early corticogenesis and viral pathogenesis, they lack a functional immune system and placental interface.36

Proteomic analysis of day-35 infected organoids showed that the “response to type I interferon” pathway did not display the canonical upregulation typically associated with antiviral responses, which may be due to the limited immunocompetence of brain organoids. In this context, the absence of microglia, key cells in viral neuroinflammation, represents an important limitation of the model, preventing the assessment of immune-mediated contributions to the observed cytopathic phenotypes. Likewise, the differences observed between the two strains in the modulation of interferon-stimulated genes in NSCs warrant further investigation, but should be interpreted with caution given the lack of functional immune validation in the current experimental system. Future studies incorporating microglia-containing assembloids or maternal–foetal interface biological models may better capture the interplay between innate immunity and viral neurotropism.

The broader astrocyte susceptibility observed in this study, in contrast to findings in adult brain slices,11 likely reflects the cellular and immunological immaturity intrinsic to the organoid model. The viral doses employed were based on previous studies with Zika virus using the same brain organoid model, providing a comparative framework between neurotropic arboviruses and microcephaly.8 Nevertheless, these conditions may not quantitatively reflect the infectious burden reaching the foetal brain during natural vertical transmission. The experimental time points capture early-to-mid stages of corticogenesis and recapitulate molecular features associated with early human neurodevelopment, though a direct correspondence to specific gestational weeks should be interpreted with caution given the inherent limitations of organoid-based developmental staging. Furthermore, all organoid and NSC experiments were conducted using a single hiPSC donor line (GM23279A) as the primary model, with BR-1 used only for confirmatory NSC infections. This limits the generalisability of our findings across genetically diverse backgrounds. Future studies incorporating multiple iPSC lines from different donors will be important to assess the influence of host genetic variability on OROV susceptibility and neurodevelopmental outcomes.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that OROV infects and damages human developing brain cells, impairing neurogenesis and tissue architecture, thereby supporting its neurotropic and potentially teratogenic role. By leveraging hiPSC-derived brain organoids, we establish a mechanistic foundation to interpret congenital OROV cases. The convergence of transcriptomic, proteomic, and morphological evidence across two genetically distinct OROV strains extends mechanistic parallels with congenital Zika syndrome. Although these findings provide biological plausibility for a role of OROV in congenital disease, they are based on in vitro models and do not establish causality in humans, which will require confirmation through prospective clinical and epidemiological studies. Together, our results support enhanced surveillance efforts, consideration of OROV in the differential diagnosis of congenital infections, and further investigation of strain-specific virulence, host immune responses, and long-term neurological outcomes. Future studies incorporating microglia-containing organoids, maternal–foetal interface models, and multiple iPSC donor lines may help clarify immune-mediated mechanisms and host-specific determinants of disease. From a clinical standpoint, our data support the consideration of OROV testing in newborns presenting with microcephaly or other neurodevelopmental anomalies in endemic or recently affected regions, in conjunction with appropriate diagnostic and clinical evaluation. Moreover, the in vitro platforms developed here set the stage for the investigation of antiviral agents, offering tools for a rapid response to future neurotropic arboviral outbreaks.
