The role of significant others in the inadequate use of maternal healthcare among married women in Uganda: a secondary analysis of the 2016 demographic and health survey
Authors: Moses Festo Towongo, Olusegun S Ewemooje, Morrison Okezie Ogu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite global efforts to improve maternal health outcomes, many regions, including Uganda, continue to face challenges in achieving optimal maternal healthcare utilization. This study aims to investigate how restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others affects inadequate use of maternal healthcare services among married women in Uganda. Inadequate use of maternal healthcare services refers to not meeting all four WHO-recommended standards for ANC timing, ANC visits, place of delivery, and PNC timing.MethodsSecondary data from the latest 2016 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS 2016) were used for this study. Data from 7906 married women of childbearing age were analyzed using a multilevel binary logistic regression approach.ResultsThe inadequate use of maternal healthcare services was estimated to be 39.1%. The odds of inadequate use were 17% higher among women with moderate restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others compared to women with low restrictions [Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) = 1.17, 95% Confidence Interval (CI) = 1.02–1.34]. Inadequate use of MHCS decreases with an increase in the household wealth index. Women from the richest household [(AOR) = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.39,0.70] were less likely to report Inadequate use of maternal healthcare services compared to the poorest. Employed women were less likely [AOR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.62–0.84] to report inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Women who reside in the northern region of Uganda were significantly less likely [AOR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.52–0.99] to report inadequate use of maternal healthcare services compared to those who reside in the central region.ConclusionThe study concluded that the restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others plays a crucial role in the use of maternal healthcare services among married women in Uganda. Factors such as maternal age, household wealth index, employment status, and region of residence were also associated with inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Therefore, to achieve Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, the government and policymakers should engage significant others in maternal health programs, promote regional equity, female labor force participation, education, and autonomy in health decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01151-3.Keywords: Significant others, Maternal healthcare, Healthcare utilization, Married women, Restricted autonomy, Uganda

Background

Maternal healthcare is a fundamental element of public health, directly influencing maternal and child morbidity and mortality rates [1, 2]. Achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which aims to reduce maternal mortality to 70 deaths per 100,000 live births, depends on the utilization of maternal healthcare services [3]. Despite global efforts to enhance maternal health outcomes, many regions, including Uganda, still face challenges in optimizing maternal healthcare utilization [4, 5]. Inadequate use of recommended maternal health practices remains a major obstacle, especially among married women [6, 7]. Inadequate use of maternal healthcare services refers to failure to meet the four WHO-recommended standards of using antenatal care services in the first trimester, attending antenatal care services at least four times during the gestation period, choosing to deliver at a hospital or maternity home, and receiving postnatal care services within 48 h after delivery [1–3]. This issue is multifaceted, influenced by socio-cultural, economic, and familial factors [8–10]. In Uganda, traditional gender roles and cultural norms often assign men and extended family members, particularly spouses, a central role in healthcare decision-making [11]. Consequently, this involvement leads to a lack of autonomy in making health-related decisions and reliance on spouses or other family members for guidance and support [12–14]. Autonomy, defined as the personal capacity to control one’s own life and well-being, highlights an individual’s ability to make decisions, manage resources, and meet personal needs independently [15]. This dynamic can either promote or hinder adherence to maternal health recommendations [16, 17].

Also, several studies have identified various factors influencing maternal healthcare service utilization at both individual and community levels [18–22]. At the individual level, these include socio-economic factors such as income, education, and employment status, which significantly shape maternal healthcare decisions and outcomes. Financial constraints can prevent women from accessing healthcare by limiting their ability to afford transportation, medications, or quality care during pregnancy and childbirth [23, 24]. Limited health literacy, often linked to lower educational attainment, can impair women’s understanding of prenatal care, recognition of danger signs in pregnancy, and the importance of skilled birth attendance [25, 26]. Factors like exposure to media [16, 19], maternal age [19, 27], household head [19], parity [19, 23], pregnancy intention [28, 29], religion [19, 30], and spouse or partner’s level of education [19] are among those identified as influencing maternal health outcomes. These socio-economic disparities significantly contribute to unequal health outcomes, with poorer women facing higher risks of complications and mortality [16, 23, 24].

Moreover, the accessibility and quality of healthcare services also affect utilization; these barriers are further worsened by systemic issues such as inadequate infrastructure in low-income areas, social stigma, and lack of culturally sensitive care [18]. At the community level, regional disparities persist [19, 27], with notable differences among women based on place of residence. While overall rates of skilled birth attendance and facility-based deliveries have increased, their distribution remains uneven, with clear urban–rural gaps [23, 31, 32]. Socioeconomic and educational levels within communities heavily influence service use; women from wealthier households are more likely to deliver in facilities, and those with tertiary education have higher odds of facility delivery, whereas the number of ANC visits varies among women with no education and those with secondary or higher education [20–22, 29]. Although there is extensive literature on women’s autonomy and young married women’s utilization of maternal healthcare, there is limited research specifically focused on married women in Uganda. Furthermore, a lack of context-specific data on married women’s autonomy is necessary to develop effective policies for reducing maternal mortality and meeting the global agenda, given that married women represent a significant portion of the female population aged 18 and above, and most births occur within marriage [33]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate how restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others affects inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. It specifically focuses on the factors influencing the use of maternal healthcare among married women in Uganda.

Methods

Study design and sampling strategy

This study used secondary data from the 2016 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS). To obtain the data, a brief description of the study plan was submitted to the DHS Measure International website (www.dhsprogram.com), and upon the study’s approval, the data were extracted. There are several formats of datasets available, and the STATA version was selected for this study. The 2016 UDHS used a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design with rural-urban and regional components. The Uganda Bureau of Statistics provided a sampling frame for the 2014 Uganda Population and Housing Census (NPHC), which yielded a sample of 696 Enumerated Areas (EAs). In Uganda, an EA is a geographic area that covers an average of 130 households. Out of 20,791 households, 19,938 occupied households were successfully surveyed, yielding a response rate of 98%. All women aged 15–49 years from the households, including visitors who stayed overnight, were eligible to be interviewed. Of these women, only those who reported having given birth in the past five years preceding the survey were selected for this study (unweighted, n = 10263, weighted, n = 9630). This study included a weighted sample of 7,906 married women. Further information on the study design and sampling strategy can be found in the 2016 UDHS report [34].

Outcome variables

The outcome variable for this study is inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. In the context of this study, inadequate use is defined by the four measures of maternal healthcare service utilization being; (1) not using antenatal care services in the first trimester, (2) attending antenatal care services less than four times during the gestation period, (3) choosing to deliver at home or in another location instead of a hospital or maternity home, and (4) receiving postnatal care services after 48 h after delivery. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends four key measures of maternal healthcare services for women during pregnancy. These measures were recoded into dichotomous variables as follows: (1) Not using antenatal care services in the first trimester: coded as 1 if true, 0 otherwise. (2) Attending antenatal care services fewer than four times during the gestation period: coded as 1 if true, 0 otherwise. (3) Choosing to deliver at home or in a non-hospital setting instead of at a hospital or maternity home: coded as 1 if true, 0 otherwise. (4) Receiving postnatal care services more than 48 h after delivery: coded as 1 if true, 0 otherwise. These four outcomes were computed, and their output ranged from 0 to 4. Women who had inadequate use on 1–4 of the criteria were coded as 1 (inadequate) while women who had adequate use on all four criteria (score of 0) were coded as 0 (adequate) [33–36]. Therefore, the current outcome variable is specifically intended to assess whether women adhered to the full World Health Organization’s recommendations regarding maternal health service utilization or not.

Individual-level variables

The key predictor variable in this study is restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others. This was defined as having household decision-making power regarding four components: personal health care, household purchases, spending, and freedom of movement [37, 38]. During UDHS 2016, women were asked the following questions: (i) the person who usually decides on the respondent’s healthcare, (ii) the person who usually decides on large household purchases, (iii) the person who usually decides what to do with money the husband earns, and (iv) the person who usually decides on visits to family or relatives. For each of the questions, answers were recoded using five options: respondent alone, respondent and the husband/partner together, respondent and the other person, husband/partner alone, someone else [34]. These variables were assigned a value of 0 where women participated in decision-making, while the remaining responses were assigned a value of 1, where women did not participate or had restricted autonomy in decision-making. A final score was computed and the output ranged from 0 to 4 and was categorized into three categories of restricted autonomy. Women with a score of 4 were classified as having “High Restricted Autonomy”, meaning they did not participate in any of the four decision-making criteria. Women with scores of 1 to 3 were classified as “Moderate Restricted Autonomy”, meaning they did not meet al.l four criteria of non-participation but did meet at least one criterion of participation in decision-making. Women with a score of 0 were classified as “Low Restricted Autonomy”, meaning they participated in four criteria of decision-making [7, 16].

We also included 14 variables as other predictors in this study. These variables were selected because of their association with maternal healthcare service utilization from the literature as well as their presence in the UDHS 2016 dataset [6, 16, 27, 34]. These variables were categorized into individual and community-level variables. The individual level variables include: women’s age at the time of the survey, which was categorized into seven categories: 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34…0.44–49. Parity refers to the number of children a woman has had; it was classified as 1, 2–3, or 4 and above children. The household head is a person who was recognized as head of household by members of the household and was categorized into male and female. The level of education of the mother was categorized into none, primary, secondary, or higher education. The Household Wealth Index was derived from a composite measure of asset ownership and housing quality. The study specifically included the ownership of consumer goods, such as televisions, bicycles, and cars, alongside important indicators of living conditions. These indicators also included the main source of drinking water for the household, the availability and type of sanitation facilities, and the materials used for flooring. The 2016 UDHS used a 20% quintile classification, dividing it into five groups: poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest.

Also, the religious affiliation of the women was categorized into four categories: Catholic, Anglican, Muslim, and others (Pentecostal, Seventh Day Adventist, to name a few). Employment status was defined as women’s working status: not working (unemployed) or working (employed). Wanted pregnancy refers to whether the pregnancy was wanted when the woman became pregnant; the responses ranged from wanted then, later, and no more. The spousal/partners’ level of education refers to the highest level of education attained by the spouse, which ranged from no education, primary, secondary, or higher education.

Community-Level variables

Community-level variables were generated by aggregating individual-level characteristics at the Enumerated Area (EA) level, and these aggregate variables were categorized as high or low based on the distribution of proportion values computed for each EA. The aggregated community-level variables include socioeconomic status, community media exposure, community level of education, and community distance to health facilities. The other two community-level variables were place of residence (rural/urban) and region of residence. The community socioeconomic status is defined as the proportion of the poor and the poorest in the cluster. The variable was constructed by aggregating the proportion of the poor and poorest women in the Enumerated Areas (Cluster). The median was used as a cut-off point because the aggregated variable was not normally distributed; the proportion less than 50% was recoded as 0, otherwise 1 [20]. Community media exposure was generated by aggregating the proportion of women exposed to at least one medium (radio, newspaper, television) in an Enumerated Area. The median value was used as a cut-off point, which was set at 50% because the aggregated variable was not normally distributed. The proportion below 50% was recoded as low, otherwise high [39].

Community-level education can be defined as the proportion of women with at least secondary or higher education in the community. This variable was computed by aggregating the proportion of women who reported having attained at least secondary or higher. The variable was computed, but it was not normally distributed; due to a lack of normal distribution, a median was used as a cut-off point. The proportion below 50% was regarded as low and otherwise high. Community distance to health facility: this variable was computed by aggregating individual-level variables where women were asked whether getting medical help for themselves: distance to health facility was a problem; therefore, community distance to health facility was defined as the proportion of women from the Enumerated Areas (EA) who view distance as a big problem. The mean distance for those who view distance as a problem was used. The proportion below 50% was regarded as low; otherwise, and high. The place of residence refers to the place of dwelling where the woman was found during the survey, which was categorized into two: rural and urban. The region of residence refers to the geographic administrative areas where the women resided during the survey night. This information was collected from 15 sub-regions. we grouped the sub-regions into four main regions of Uganda: Northern (West Nile, Acholi, Lango, and Karamoja), Western (Kigezi, Ankole, Bunyoro, and Tooro), Eastern (Busoga, Bugishu, Teso and Bukedi), and Central region (South Central, North Central and Kampala).

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using Stata version 17 [40]. Univariate, bivariate, and multivariate regression were used to analyze the data. Pearson’s chi-square test of independence was utilized to examine the variables that significantly relate to inadequate use of maternal healthcare services at the bivariate level. This analysis was subsequently followed by a multilevel binary logistic regression analysis. Before performing the regression analysis, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among the predictor variables. This study found that the minimum, maximum, and mean VIF were 1.01, 2.25, and 1.48, respectively. Therefore, there is no evidence of high multicollinearity among the variables. This study used five modeled multilevel binary logistic regression analyses to examine the association between restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others and inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. This study employed multilevel binary logistic regression modeling because the DHS datasets were collected using a multistage sampling design that involved cluster sampling. Since the clusters are not homogeneous, ignoring them and using ordinary regression methods could result in Type I or Type II errors, leading to a fallacy of generalization [41].

Five multilevel binary logistic regression models were used to examine the relationship between individual and contextual variables and inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Model 0 serves as a baseline to estimate the variance in inadequate use associated with primary sampling units (PSUs). Model 1 adds the restricted autonomy variable. Model 2 incorporates individual-level variables in addition to restricted autonomy. Model 3 includes restricted autonomy and community-level variables. Finally, Model 4 is the full model, integrating restricted autonomy, individual-level, and community-level variables to comprehensively assess their combined effect on maternal healthcare inadequate use. Furthermore, the findings of the fixed-effect model were presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to assess the model fitness; the model with the lowest value was considered to be the best-fitted model. All the analyses were weighted, and the level of statistical significance was set at 5%. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. These guidelines include items that pertain to the article’s title and abstract, as well as the introduction, methods, results, discussion sections, and additional relevant information [42].

Results

Demographic characteristics

The distribution of the background characteristics of the sample for the married women aged 15–49 years who reported having given birth in the five years preceding the 2016 UDHS survey (see Table 1). Overall, the prevalence of inadequate use of maternal health services was 39.1%. More than half (55.3%) of women experienced moderate restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others, while 11.1% reported high restrictions. Over one-quarter (25.9%) of the women were aged 20–29 years, and the least proportion (2.1%) were women aged 45–49 years. Approximately half (48.9%) of the women had four or more children, while three in every five (60.2%) of the women had primary education, and more than half (54.0%) of their spouses/partners possessed primary education. The women from the poor household formed the highest proportion (21.3%), and more than two-thirds (70.2%) of the women belong to Anglican and Catholic denominations. Approximately four-fifths (78.9%) of the women were reported to be employed. Almost three out of every five (58.0%) women wanted pregnancy when they became pregnant. More than three-quarters (77.5%) of the women reside in rural areas, and more than half (55.8%) of the women viewed distance to the health facilities as not a big problem. More than half (55.9%) of the women belong to low socioeconomic status communities, with more than half (54.2%) of women belonging to communities with high access to media. More than three-quarters (77.7%) of the women belong to communities with low levels of education. The sample of women was almost evenly distributed across the major regions of Uganda (See Table 1).

At Community-Level

Women who reside in communities with high socioeconomic status (40.9%), low level of education (40.2%), and those who see community distance to health facilities as a problem (41.4%) have inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. A considerable proportion of women residing in the eastern (42.3%) and northern (40.2%) regions also exhibited inadequate use of maternal healthcare services (See Table 2).

Association between restricted autonomy, individual, and community-level influence on inadequate use of maternal healthcare services in Uganda

Random effects (measure of variation)

The random effect of restricted autonomy, individual, and community-level variation on inadequate use of maternal healthcare services in Uganda was assessed by interclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and model fitness was assessed using Akaike information criteria (AIC). The ICC of the base model showed that 14% of the variation in the inadequate use of maternal healthcare services was associated with restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others. After adjusting for restricted autonomy, individual and community-level, the full model showed statistically significant variation in inadequate use of maternal healthcare services across restricted autonomy, individual, and community-level at a 5% significance level. Comparing the empty model to subsequent models, the correlation coefficient dropped to 13% at the restricted autonomy and the community-level model. Therefore, the inadequate use of maternal healthcare services was associated with restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others and community-level characteristics. The Akaike information criteria values confirmed a subsequent decrease, significantly improving from the empty model to the restricted autonomy, individual, and community-level models. The goodness of fit was found in model 4. Therefore, model 4 was selected to predict the adequacy of maternal healthcare services among married women in Uganda (see Table 3, Fig. 1).

Discussion

Applying the multilevel approach, this study was conducted to examine the impact of restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others on maternal healthcare inadequate use among married women in Uganda. Maternal healthcare service utilization is not only a matter of concern to Uganda, but also a fundamental human right that cuts across Sub-Saharan Africa [7, 27, 43]. This study highlights the crucial role “significant others” play in influencing inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Maternal healthcare service utilization is critical to the mother and unborn baby’s health, as well as the achievement of the global Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1 Agenda of reducing the maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 per 100,000 by 2030 [44]. This study had two main findings: the prevalence of inadequate use of maternal healthcare services and the determinants of inadequate use of maternal healthcare services, with restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others.

The UDHS 2016 data showed that 39.1% of married women in Uganda had inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. The proportion of inadequate use is higher than it was in Ghana (34%), but lower than in Rwanda (66%) [45], Bangladesh (69%) [46], Ethiopia (74%) [31], Mozambique (84%) [47], and the broader sub-Saharan African average (75%) [48]. These differences may reflect variation in poor road networks, inadequate transportation, and great distances to facilities limit maternal healthcare use in Ethiopia, Uganda Rwanda, and other sub-Saharan countries. Even though services are free, indirect costs, including transportation, medical supplies, and informal levies, discourage use. Restrictive gender norms, restricted decision-making autonomy, and expectations of childbirth as a natural process without medical intervention further inhibit acceptance [10]. Healthcare issues, including personnel shortages, absenteeism, rude treatment, and poor facility conditions, also damage trust and service use.

In Uganda, the persistence of patriarchal norms and male-dominated decision-making structures likely contributes to inadequate use among married women, as husbands often influence or control reproductive health and maternal healthcare decisions [16, 49]. Addressing these sociocultural barriers through male engagement, empowerment of women, and culturally responsive health interventions is critical to improving maternal health outcomes. The findings from this study corroborate the hypothesis that there is a strong association between restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others and inadequate maternal healthcare service utilization among married women in Uganda [16, 27]. These findings are consistent with multiple studies that have established a strong association between women’s autonomy and the utilization of maternal healthcare services i.e. women with moderate restricted autonomy and high restricted autonomy restrictions on their decision-making autonomy had significantly higher odds of inadequate use of maternal healthcare services recommended by WHO compared to women with low restricted autonomy. In Nepal, Thapa and Niehof [50] found that women who have the ability to make decisions regarding their healthcare and household expenditures are significantly more likely to attend antenatal care and give birth in health facilities. This underscores the importance of women’s empowerment in influencing maternal health-seeking behaviour. Similarly, research conducted in Indonesia [23] demonstrated that women who actively participated in decision-making processes were more inclined to use skilled birth attendants and access postnatal care. These findings reflect the role of autonomy in enhancing women’s access to timely and appropriate maternal services. In Ethiopia, studies [13, 31] further supported this relationship by showing that women who had a say in household matters, such as purchasing decisions and visiting family, were more likely to engage with maternal health services. These studies also highlighted how social and cultural constraints limit women’s ability to exercise autonomy, particularly in rural settings.

Beyond individual country studies, regional analyses from developing countries and sub-Saharan Africa [27, 51] have consistently found that higher levels of women’s autonomy are associated with increased utilization of antenatal care, skilled delivery, and postnatal services in line with findings from this study. These studies suggest that autonomy not only affects health-seeking behaviour but also interacts with other factors such as education, media exposure, and partner support. Contrastingly, some studies have found no significant association between women’s autonomy and the use of maternal healthcare services. For example, Kamiya [52] reported that although some women had decision-making power, this did not necessarily translate into higher service uptake, possibly due to overriding financial or structural barriers. Similarly, Kruk et al., [53] found that internalized cultural norms and negative previous experiences with health systems might reduce the impact of autonomy on service use. Additionally, Ganle et al., [54] argued that community-level expectations and limited spousal support could override individual autonomy, diminishing its influence on maternal healthcare decisions.

A plausible explanation for the persistent noncompliance with maternal healthcare services among married women lies in their limited freedom to act independently. Specifically, many women face restrictions in their ability to: make decisions regarding their health; make significant household purchases; earn an income independently; and visit family or relatives without permission. These aspects of autonomy are often subject to the approval of others, including husbands, in-laws, or other household members. As a result, women’s exclusion from key household decision-making processes becomes a significant source of disempowerment, ultimately limiting their access to and utilization of essential maternal healthcare services. This is corroborated by other studies [16, 51, 55] that women who are empowered to make key decisions in their households are more likely to utilize antenatal care promptly and adequately, give birth in health facilities, and receive timely postnatal care services compared to those who are not empowered. Conversely, Rutaremwa et al. [18]., revealed that in Uganda, cultural values and norms influenced women’s behaviour towards home delivery. Similar studies by Kyomuhendo [45], show that Ugandan women often adhere to traditional birthing practices, viewing pregnancy as a test of endurance, with maternal death regarded as a tragic but accepted part of life. A study conducted in the Kiboga district of Uganda found that pregnancy and childbirth remain significant domains in which women hold power and status. Many women actively use this status to enhance their influence within both the household and the broader community [18, 19, 22].

We observed that some individual-level variables were associated with inadequate use of maternal healthcare service utilization. Our study established an inverse association between age and inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Married young adults aged 20 to 34 were significantly less likely to report inadequate utilization of maternal healthcare services compared to adolescents. Supporting this finding, Rizkianti et al. [23] identified a correlation between age and the use of maternal healthcare services. One possible explanation for this could be that older women have a greater level of autonomy due to their maturity, allowing them to make health-related decisions independently. In contrast, younger adolescent women may need permission from their partners or in-laws because of their inexperience with pregnancy and childbirth. Similarly, an inverse relationship was established between household socioeconomic status and inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Women from the poorer, middle to richest households were less likely to report inadequate use of maternal healthcare services compared to the poorest. This finding was in line with other studies, which established a positive relationship between household socioeconomic status and maternal healthcare service utilization [10, 56]. Contrary to these findings, other studies found mixed results between wealth and other components of maternal healthcare [6, 14, 57].

Additionally, women’s socioeconomic status is often associated with utilization of maternal healthcare services; this could be due to issues relating to distance and the affordability to pay for services. Although maternal healthcare services in Uganda are relatively free, indirect costs such as transportation expenses and unofficial payments due to drug shortages add up. Between 2002/03 and 2005/06, these costs led to an average increase of $21 in health expenditures per household, which continues to hinder access to care [57]. Similarly, married women who reported being employed were significantly less likely to report inadequate use of maternal healthcare services compared to their counterparts who were unemployed. Employment status is associated with the ability to pay for services as well as some level of autonomy. Employed married women often possess a certain level of education, enabling them to make informed decisions about their health. These women have the financial means to access quality maternal healthcare from private clinics and hospitals. They can cover their transportation and medical expenses, unlike unemployed women who often rely on their partners or husbands for financial support [15].

Our study also found a significant variation in inadequate use of maternal healthcare services at the community level. Women who reside in the northern region of Uganda were less likely to report inadequate use of maternal healthcare services compared to the women who reside in the central region of Uganda. This finding was consistent with other studies from Uganda [18, 21, 22]. Meanwhile, some studies found mixed results regarding the association between the region of residence and maternal healthcare service utilization [4, 5]. The level of socioeconomic development varies across countries and is often influenced by regional differences. In the context of Uganda, government programs aimed at specific regions play a crucial role in this development. For instance, the central region is relatively well-developed in terms of both socioeconomic factors and infrastructure. In contrast, northern Uganda faced significant challenges due to wars; peace was restored only in 2005. Therefore, both government programmes such as emergency and camp health coordination, Primary Health Care/District Health Services through the ministry of health and health sector reforms and international non-governmental organizations such as Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF/Doctors Without Borders) directed their efforts toward the northern region due to the high influx of internally displaced persons and refugees from neighboring countries such as the Democratic Republic of Congo and South Sudan. The displaced persons camp offers accessible healthcare services, and the presence of community healthcare workers significantly contributes to educating women about the importance of attending maternal healthcare services, thereby increasing the utilization of these services in the region [18, 20].

Strengths and limitations

The present study’s strength lies in its focus on “restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others” perceived as external environmental factors that shape women’s behavior towards the utilization of maternal healthcare services. In addition, this study only examines married and cohabiting women, who are subjects of sociocultural influence in their households. The study encompassed both individual and contextual-level factors, addressing methodological issues. The use of UDHS 2016 makes the findings generalizable in the context of Uganda due to the representativeness of the sample. The DHS boasts a renowned methodology that undergoes testing, documentation, and referencing. The cross-sectional designs limit causal inference because exposure and outcome are measured simultaneously, making it impossible to establish causality. The retrospective nature of respondents’ responses makes it impossible to control for recall bias. Also, this study could not account for unmeasured factors like the quality of local health facilities or deeply ingrained cultural attitudes that simultaneously influence both household decision-making dynamics and health-seeking behaviour. However, our study also focuses on married women, and it excludes unmarried women. The exclusion of unmarried women may limit the generalizability of the findings, as it overlooks a potentially vulnerable group with unique barriers to maternity care. This exclusion can also introduce bias by presenting a partial picture that reflects only the experiences of married women. The discussion attributes inadequate use of maternal healthcare services to “patriarchal norms” and “male-dominated decision-making”; however, it is important to note that the analysis does not directly measure these constructs due to a lack of available data.

The authors acknowledge that limitations may arise in the study due to the way the outcome variable, “inadequate use” of maternal healthcare services, is defined. Specifically, a woman missing just one component is grouped with a woman missing all four components, which does not reflect the varying degrees of inadequate use. Furthermore, the key predictor variable “restricted autonomy in household decision-making by significant others” may not fully capture the nuances and quality of the “contribution of significant others.” For example, supportive involvement may encourage care, while domineering involvement may hinder it. However, this outcome variable was developed to address the research objective and to assess adherence to the WHO-recommended standards for antenatal care (ANC).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the critical role that significant others play in shaping women’s behavior within the household when it comes to using maternal healthcare services. The study’s findings revealed a 39.1% inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Other factors showed that maternal age, household wealth index, employment status, and region of residence were associated with inadequate use of maternal healthcare services among the women. Therefore, to reduce the rate of inadequate maternal healthcare utilization and achieve Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which aims to decrease maternal mortality, the government should not only continue poverty alleviation programs but also implement targeted regional and education-based interventions.

These interventions may include Conditional Cash Transfers (CCTs) for maternal health, community-based health insurance schemes, women’s empowerment and economic inclusion programs, and the integration of maternal health into regional development initiatives. By addressing economic constraints as well as household power dynamics and structural barriers that limit women’s autonomy, these combined programs can help reduce inadequate use of maternal healthcare services. Achieving SDG 3.1 requires a multi-sectoral approach that goes beyond just alleviating poverty. It should include institutional, legal, social, and gender-transformative reforms that empower women to make independent decisions about their health. Community-level interventions are important and should focus on: engaging men and families to promote shared decision-making and support for women’s health, prioritizing empowerment programs that enhance women’s economic independence and self-efficacy, and providing training for healthcare workers to ensure they are sensitive to power dynamics within families and able to communicate directly with women.
