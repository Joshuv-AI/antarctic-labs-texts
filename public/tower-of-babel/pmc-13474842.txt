Efficacy and safety of nasal mask compared to nasal cannula during procedural sedation in gastroscopy: a systematic review, meta-analysis, and trial sequential analysis
Authors: Mohamed Yaseen Alfeetouri, Munder Lateiresh, Ayham Qatza, Engy Elkoury, Nuran Arif Benfaid, Muhammed Elhadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxemia is the most common adverse event during propofol-based procedural sedation for gastroscopy. Conventional nasal cannula oxygenation provides low flow rates, permitting room air entrainment and often proving insufficient. Nasal mask oxygenation delivers higher inspired oxygen concentrations, potentially enhancing respiratory safety during sedation.ObjectiveThis study compared the efficacy and safety of nasal mask versus nasal cannula oxygen delivery during propofol-based procedural sedation for gastroscopy.MethodsElectronic databases/registries were systematically searched from inception to January 24, 2026, for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Inclusion criteria encompassed adult patients undergoing propofol-based sedation for gastroscopy. The primary outcome was the incidence of hypoxemia. Secondary outcomes included severe hypoxemia, minimum oxygen saturation (SpO₂), need for airway or oxygenation interventions, procedure-related adverse events, and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Random-effects meta-analyses were performed using risk ratios (RRs) or mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Trial sequential analysis (TSA) assessed the sufficiency of the evidence. Risk of bias was evaluated using RoB 2, and certainty of evidence using GRADE.ResultsFive RCTs enrolled 1,267 patients, of whom 1,252 (98.8%) were included in the quantitative synthesis. Nasal mask oxygenation significantly reduced hypoxemia (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.58, p < 0.0001) and severe hypoxemia (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.67, p = 0.0007) compared with nasal cannula. Nasal masks also decreased the need for jaw thrust (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.52) and mask-assisted ventilation (RR 0.16, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.46), while elevating minimum SpO₂ (MD 3.78, 95% CI 1.39 to 6.16). No significant differences emerged in coughing, hiccups, or PONV.ConclusionsNasal mask oxygenation is associated with greater respiratory safety compared with nasal cannula oxygenation during propofol-sedated gastroscopy. This advantage manifests as reduced incidence of hypoxemia and fewer airway-related interventions, alongside a comparable overall safety profile.PROSPEROCRD420261291468.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03973-y.Keywords: Anesthesia, Nasal mask, Nasal cannula, Propofol, Gastroscopy, Hypoxemia, Randomized controlled trials, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Procedural sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy reduces patient anxiety and discomfort. It also improves endoscopist performance and minimizes unintended movements that compromise procedural safety and efficiency [1–3]. However, sedation is associated with adverse cardiopulmonary events, though mortality remains rare. These complications arise from sedative agents, dosing regimens, and administration routes, particularly during moderate-to-deep sedation [4].

Propofol provides rapid recovery, superior sedation quality, greater patient cooperation, and comparable cardiopulmonary safety compared with traditional agents [5]. Hypoxemia represents the most common adverse event during sedated gastroscopy, arising from endoscope-induced airway obstruction, sedation-related collapse, respiratory depression, and reduced lung volumes due to gas insufflation [6]. Patient- and procedure-related factors, including obesity [7], cardiovascular disease [8], and age over 60 years [9], also highlight the need to optimize oxygen delivery during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy [2].

Supplemental oxygen delivered via a conventional nasal cannula represents standard practice. However, low flow rates and room-air entrainment limit its efficacy by diluting inspired oxygen concentrations and impairing hypoxemia prevention [10]. Alternatives, including high-flow nasal cannula, nasopharyngeal airway devices, dual-channel laryngeal mask airways, and supraglottic jet oxygenation, variably mitigate hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy [11–14]. Nasal mask oxygenation delivers higher inspired oxygen concentrations without impeding gastroscopy, emerging as a promising approach to preventing hypoxemia during procedural sedation [15].

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing nasal mask and conventional nasal cannula oxygenation during gastroscopy have reported inconsistent results regarding hypoxemia incidence and the need for airway interventions. Heterogeneity arises from differences in study design, patient populations, sedation protocols, outcome definitions, and methodological quality, which limit the interpretability and generalizability [15–19].

No prior systematic synthesis has comprehensively evaluated randomized evidence on nasal mask versus nasal cannula oxygen delivery during procedural sedation for gastroscopy. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis assess their comparative effectiveness and safety in adult patients undergoing sedated gastroscopy.

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting format

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Tables S1-2) [20]. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the AMSTAR-2 measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR-2) (Table S3) [21]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under the registration number (CRD420261291468).

Search strategy and information sources

A comprehensive literature search was performed across five major electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library) from database inception through January 24, 2026. In addition, the ClinicalTrials.gov registry was searched to identify ongoing or unpublished trials. No restrictions were applied regarding language, publication year, or study design. The reference lists of eligible articles were manually screened, and citation tracking was conducted to identify additional relevant studies. The search strategy combined terms related to the intervention (nasal mask) and the procedure (gastroscopy). The complete search strategy is provided in Table S4.

Eligibility criteria and outcomes definitions

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: [1] RCT design; [2] comparison of oxygen delivery via a nasal mask versus a conventional nasal cannula during procedural sedation; [3] inclusion of adult patients (≥ 18 years) classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III undergoing diagnostic or therapeutic gastroscopy; and [4] use of propofol-based procedural sedation, either alone or in combination with other sedatives or analgesic agents. Studies were excluded if they: [1] failed to meet the predefined inclusion criteria; [2] involved procedures other than gastroscopy or were performed under general anesthesia with airway instrumentation; [3] were observational studies or non–peer-reviewed publications, including book chapters, editorials, letters, opinion articles, review articles, conference abstracts, or animal studies; or [4] reported irretrievable, overlapping, or incomplete data. No restrictions were applied regarding language, geographical region, or year of publication.

The primary outcome was the incidence of hypoxemia during procedural sedation for gastroscopy. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of severe hypoxemia, minimum oxygen saturation (SpO₂), need for airway or oxygenation interventions (including mask-assisted ventilation, jaw thrust, or increased oxygen flow), procedure-related adverse events (coughing and hiccups), and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Detailed outcome definitions across studies are provided in Table S5. Outcomes lacking standardized or comparable definitions across studies were excluded from quantitative analyses to ensure methodological consistency and validity.

Selection process and data extraction

Study selection was conducted using the Covidence platform in two sequential stages based on predefined eligibility criteria. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by three reviewers (A.Q., E.E., and N.A.B.), followed by a full-text assessment performed independently by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by a fourth reviewer (M.Y.A.) [22]. Data extraction was also independently performed by two reviewers (E.E. and N.A.B.) using a standardized Excel spreadsheet. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (M.Y.A.). The data extraction form comprised three domains. The first domain included study characteristics, such as the first author, year of publication, country, sample size, type of procedure, and details of the anesthetic regimen, including induction and maintenance agents, routes of administration, and dosages. The second domain captured baseline participant characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), baseline oxygen flow rate (L/min), and the sedation scale used. The third domain included all predefined primary and secondary outcomes.

Two included trials (Liu 2025 A, Liu 2025 B) [17, 18] were registered under the same clinical trial protocol identification number. However, these studies investigated distinct patient populations. To address potential concerns regarding participant overlap, the corresponding author was contacted for clarification and confirmed that the studies enrolled entirely separate populations with no overlap in participants. Both trials were therefore considered eligible and included independently in the meta-analysis.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

The risk of bias for all included studies was independently evaluated by two reviewers (M.Y.A. and A.Q.) using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [23]. Assessment encompassed five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Each domain, as well as the overall study, was classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (M.L.). The findings were synthesized and graphically presented using the Robvis tool [24].

The certainty of the evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [25]. Evidence was assessed across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Based on these criteria, the overall certainty of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. A Summary of Findings table was generated using the GRADEpro GDT software [26].

Data synthesis

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1; RStudio, Vienna, Austria) using the meta and metafor packages. Continuous outcomes reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) were converted to means and standard deviations (SDs) using the method described by Wan et al. [27]. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) method to address anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Dichotomous outcomes were summarized as risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. For dichotomous outcomes with zero-event data, a fixed continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to studies with zero events in one or both arms to permit the calculation of pooled effect estimates [28]. This facilitated the inclusion of such studies while averting exclusion due to computational constraints and minimizing bias. Studies with unresolved missing outcome data via author contact were excluded from the relevant quantitative synthesis, while other outcomes remained included. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.10 indicating significant heterogeneity) and quantified using the I² statistic. Heterogeneity was interpreted as unimportant (0–40%), moderate (30–60%), substantial (50–90%), or considerable (75–100%). Prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated to estimate the range of true intervention effects across different clinical settings. The robustness of pooled estimates was assessed using leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. Publication bias and small-study effects were not formally assessed due to the small number of included studies.

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was conducted to evaluate the reliability of cumulative evidence in the context of repeated significance testing and random error [29]. Analyses were performed using TSA software (version 0.9.5.10 Beta; Copenhagen Trial Unit, Copenhagen, Denmark). A two-sided type I error rate of 5% and a type II error rate of 20% (80% power) were prespecified. The required information size (RIS) for dichotomous outcomes was estimated based on the relative risk reduction observed in trials judged to be at low risk of bias. O’Brien–Fleming monitoring boundaries for benefit, harm, and futility were applied. Evidence was considered conclusive if the cumulative Z-curve crossed the TSA monitoring boundaries for benefit, harm, or futility, irrespective of whether the required information size was reached.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

An extensive literature search across five electronic databases and one trial registry identified 1138 records after removing 100 duplicates using EndNote software: title and abstract screening of 1038 records excluded 1023 irrelevant studies. A full-text review of the remaining 15 articles excluded 10 studies due to reasons detailed in Table S6. Five RCTs met the inclusion criteria for the meta-analysis. Quality assessment of included studies

None of the included studies exhibited a high overall risk of bias. Four trials showed low risk of bias [15, 17–19], whereas Chen et al. [16] did not raise concerns about the randomization process and deviations from intended interventions. A detailed summary of the risk-of-bias assessment is presented in Figures S1 and S2.

Primary outcome

A nasal mask is associated with greater efficacy compared with a nasal cannula in preventing hypoxemia during gastroscopy (RR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.28 to 0.58, 95% PI: 0.15 to 1.08, P < 0.0001; I² = 58.9%) (Fig. 2). The cumulative Z-curve crossed the TSA monitoring boundary for benefit before reaching RIS. This indicates that the available evidence suggests a statistically significant benefit of nasal cannula therapy. However, because RIS has not yet been fully achieved, some degree of uncertainty remains, and the findings should be interpreted as potentially supportive rather than definitively conclusive (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of the incidence of hypoxemia.

Trial sequential analysis for the incidence of hypoxemia

Secondary outcomes

Nasal mask oxygenation was associated with a reduction of approximately 61% in the risk of severe hypoxemia compared with nasal cannula oxygenation (RR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.67, 5% PI: 0.18 to 0.85, P = 0.0007; I² = 19.9%) (Fig. 4). Patients receiving nasal mask oxygen required fewer increases in oxygen flow during the procedure compared with those receiving nasal cannula oxygen (RR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.29 to 0.64, 95% PI: 0.13 to 1.36, P < 0.0001; I² = 59.6%) (Figure S3).

Forest plot of Incidence Severe hypoxemia

Nasal mask use decreased the need for airway interventions, including rescue jaw-thrust maneuvers (RR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.31 to 0.52, 95% PI: 0.28 to 0.58, P < 0.0001; I² = 0.0%) and mask-assisted ventilation (RR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.46, 95% PI: 0.01 to 2.90, P = 0.0008; I² = 60.5%) (Figure S4-S5). In addition, Nasal mask oxygenation significantly increased minimum SpO₂ compared with nasal cannula (MD = 3.78, 95% CI: 1.39 to 6.16, 95% PI: -4.45 to 12.00, P = 0.0019; I² = 93.9%) (Figure S6).

No statistically significant differences in adverse events, such as cough, hiccups, or PONV, emerged (Figures S7-S9). (Table 2) summarizes effect estimates, heterogeneity measures, and GRADE certainty of evidence for all outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of pooled estimates. Effects for any hypoxemia, severe hypoxemia, the need for jaw thrust, and the minimum SpO₂ remained stable and statistically significant across iterations, unaffected by any single study (Figures S10-S13). For the outcomes of increased oxygen flow and the need for mask-assisted ventilation, the direction and significance of the effect persisted consistently (Figures S14-S15). Adverse events, including coughing, hiccups, and PONV, showed stable null results (Figure S16-S18).

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE assessment showed moderate certainty for primary hypoxemia and high certainty for jaw thrust maneuvers. Certainty was also moderate for increased oxygen flow, severe hypoxemia, mask ventilation, and minimum SpO₂. In contrast, outcomes such as coughing, PONV, and hiccups were rated as low certainty (Table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 5 RCTs involving 1252 patients suggests that nasal mask oxygenation is associated with a lower incidence of hypoxemia than nasal cannula oxygenation during propofol‑sedated gastroscopy. Additional advantages included decreased rates of severe hypoxemia, fewer airway interventions such as jaw thrust and mask-assisted ventilation, and higher minimum peripheral oxygen saturation. These results enhance understanding of oxygenation strategies for sedated upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, a procedure routinely undertaken worldwide. Hypoxemia represents the predominant adverse event in these procedures, compromising patient safety and procedural efficiency [6, 30].

Nasal masks are associated with a more favorable respiratory safety profile, likely due to higher fractional inspired oxygen concentrations and minimal room-air entrainment, thereby maintaining alveolar oxygenation during sedation-induced respiratory depression. This 60% risk reduction in the incidence of hypoxemia carries substantial clinical significance. A network meta-analysis of oxygen therapies ranked non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation and Wei nasal jet tubes highest for preventing hypoxemia. It positioned nasal masks as a simpler, non-invasive alternative that achieves comparable odds reductions without requiring specialized equipment [31]. Cheng et al. reported that a nasal mask oxygen kit significantly lowered hypoxia incidence during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy compared with nasal cannulas (7.4% vs. 12.5%) [32]. Recent studies have evaluated alternative oxygenation methods for sedated gastroscopy, such as the procedural oxygen mask (POM). The POM delivers high inspired oxygen fractions and enables capnographic monitoring without obstructing endoscopic access [33]. A prospective observational study comparing POM with nasal cannula in sedated endoscopy patients reported more stable oxygenation and fewer respiratory complications with POM [34]. Integrated oropharyngeal airway systems, including the capnography monitoring bite block oxygenation (COMBO) endoscopy device, combine a bite block, supraglottic airway, oxygen delivery, and capnography sampling. These systems aim to mitigate hypoxia during propofol sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy [35]. A recent randomized trial protocol details the evaluation of the COMBO endoscopy oropharyngeal airway, with hypoxia as the primary outcome [36]. Although advanced devices provide superior oxygen delivery, airway support, and real-time monitoring, they demand complex setup and higher costs compared with nasal mask oxygenation. Thus, nasal mask oxygenation provides clinical improvements in respiratory safety without requiring specialized airway management.

The meta-analysis further revealed that nasal masks substantially decreased severe hypoxemia rates, achieving a 61% risk reduction. This effect averts critical SpO2 declines that demand advanced resuscitation. Moreover, profound desaturations beyond immediate safety are associated with delayed recovery and elevated 30-day readmission risk in endoscopy populations [37].

Nasal mask oxygenation elevated minimum SpO2 levels, a critical physiological marker of stable oxygen reserves during sedation. Despite high heterogeneity (I² = 93.9%) arising from divergent measurement protocols across RCTs, these findings remain consistent with observations in high-risk patient cohorts [38]. An elevated minimum SpO2 provides a broader safety margin against desaturation events that compromise organ perfusion.

The present meta-analysis showed that nasal masks are associated with a 60% reduction in jaw thrust use and an 84% reduction in the need for mask-assisted ventilation during procedural sedation. These findings underscore the efficacy of nasal masks in maintaining airway patency without manual interventions — especially given the high-certainty rating of the evidence. Clinically, fewer manipulations may decrease the risk of trauma and aspiration for patients, streamline procedures for endoscopists, and reduce overall procedural duration and resource utilization.

No significant differences emerged in coughing, hiccups, or PONV, supporting a favorable safety profile with low-certainty evidence that proved stable across sensitivity analyses.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis has several strengths. All included RCTs were recent and predominantly low-risk of bias, aligning with current sedation practice. Restricting procedural sedation to propofol minimized heterogeneity in sedative agents and depth, thereby increasing clinical applicability. Evaluation encompassed key respiratory adverse events, including hypoxemia, severe hypoxemia, minimum SpO₂, and airway rescue maneuvers, providing a practical measure of sedation-induced respiratory compromise. The included studies were conducted within a limited geographic region, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. In addition, the evidence base comprised only five RCTs, which may limit statistical power and the precision of pooled estimates. Variability in sedation protocols—including propofol dosing, use of adjunctive sedatives or opioids, and depth of sedation—as well as differences in oxygen flow rates and definitions of hypoxemia, likely contributed to residual heterogeneity across outcomes. Inconsistent BMI criteria across studies constrained subgroup analyses in obese patients: Chen et al. [16]. examined mixed populations, Liu et al. (A) [17] applied BMI ≥ 25 kg/m², Yan et al. [15] and Xu et al. [19]. utilized BMI ≥ 28 kg/m², whereas Liu et al. (B) [18] focused exclusively on elderly participants.

Conclusion

These meta-analyses suggest that nasal mask oxygenation is associated with greater respiratory safety compared with nasal cannula oxygenation during propofol-sedated gastroscopy. The intervention is associated with reduced hypoxemia and fewer airway interventions, alongside a comparable overall safety profile. However, given the small number of included RCTs and the moderate to high heterogeneity for some outcomes, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis‑generating.

Future studies should examine long‑term clinical outcomes, cost‑effectiveness, and the applicability of nasal mask oxygenation in diverse populations, including obese and elderly patients, across different international healthcare settings. These studies are needed to confirm the magnitude and generalizability of the observed effects.
