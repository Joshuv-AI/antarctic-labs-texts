Active conflict and national identity shape intergroup bias among Arabic and Hebrew speakers in Israel
Authors: Fady Khoury, Tamar Degani, Uri Hertz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProsocial decision-making reflects how individuals weigh outcomes for themselves versus others and is shaped by cognitive and social-identity factors, and intergroup conflict. We investigated how intergroup dynamics, language use, and conflict context influence in-group favoritism and out-group discrimination in such decisions, among native Arabic and Hebrew speaking Israeli citizens, representing national minority and majority in Israel, before and during Israel-Hamas war. Participants (N = 338) completed a Social Value Orientation (SVO) task presented in different languages (L1, L2), that concerned in-group and out-group recipients. Across the full sample, participants were more prosocial toward in-group than out-group members, and intergroup bias intensified during the war. Notably, language conditions did not produce significant effects on intergroup bias. Among Arabic speakers, but not Hebrew speakers, national identity was correlated with intergroup bias only during the war. These findings highlight the way intergroup relations, such as minority-majority and active conflict, shape social decisions, and the dynamic and multifaceted nature of intergroup bias.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55115-6.Keywords: Prosocial decisions, Minority and majority groups, Intergroup bias, Social identity, Language, MultilingualsSubject terms: Politics and international relations, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Social decisions have direct ramifications for oneself and others. In such decisions, people weigh outcomes for themselves and others. This relative weighting of expected outcomes for self and others has been described as a stable trait1–3. However, when these decisions involve prosocial behavior toward a known recipient, they have been shown to depend on the social context4–6. Previous research has also shown that cognitive demands – such as those induced when processing information in a non-native language, can influence decision-making7–9. Yet, while prosocial behavior and language-related cognitive effects have each been studied separately, their joint influence remains largely unexplored. This is particularly relevant in intergroup contexts, where people are typically more prosocial toward in-group than out-group recipients10–12. In the present study, we used a laboratory-based social-decision task to examine how social factors related to social identity and intergroup relations, together with cognitive factors associated with the language of presentation, jointly influence prosocial decision-making in the broader context of an ongoing intergroup conflict.

While the primary focus of research on social decision-making often concerns real-life contexts, laboratory-based tasks offer controlled insights into these behaviors. A widely used task in prosocial decision-making study is the dictator game13. This is a two-person game in which one participant, the “dictator,” decides how to allocate a certain sum of money between themselves and another participant who has no control over the distribution. By using variations of this game with multiple sharing decisions, some beneficial to both self and other, researchers can assess each participant’s social value orientation (SVO) – i.e., the extent to which individuals are willing to sacrifice personal gains for the benefit of others14,15.

Such social decisions can be affected by cognitive factors. Communicating and thinking in one’s native language (L1) is generally associated with cognitive fluency and emotional resonance, often encouraging intuitive–automatic processing8,9,16–18. By contrast, using a second language (L2) typically requires greater cognitive effort, evoking more controlled and analytic processing7–9. Social decisions were shown to be influenced by such processing modes: for example, when participants in cooperation games were forced to decide quickly, they tended to be more prosocial19. Thus, L1 use might foster more intuitive and potentially more prosocial decisions, whereas L2 use might encourage slower, more deliberative, and less prosocial choices. However, the degree to which language shapes prosociality may depend on the salience of other factors, particularly intergroup identity.

A well-documented bias in social decision-making concerns the role of intergroup relations and perceived similarity between the decision maker and the recipient. Prosocial behavior is typically stronger toward in-group members than toward out-group members, reflecting an intergroup bias rooted in social identity and group-based categorization processes10–12. According to social identity theory, individuals derive part of their self-concept from their group membership, and they tend to favor in-group members to maintain a positive social identity. Such favoritism often manifests in resource allocation tasks, in which participants share more with in-group recipients compared to members of an out-group5,6.

Recent work has emphasized that intergroup bias is not a uniform phenomenon but rather reflects complex, context-dependent markers of group identity, threat, and power dynamics20,21. When intergroup relations are tense or characterized by structural inequality, individuals may show heightened sensitivity to social identity cues, leading to stronger differentiation between in-group and out-group recipients22. Conversely, when relations are more cooperative or centered on shared goals, perceived threat decreases and intergroup boundaries become less salient, attenuating such biases23. In the context of majority-minority relations, power imbalances further shape these dynamics. Minority group members often face discrimination and social pressure, which may lead them to suppress or strategically manage their cultural and national identity when interacting with the majority group. One common example of such identity management is switching to the dominant group’s language rather than using their own native language24–27. These identity-management strategies may vary depending on the group’s sociopolitical status: cultural minorities, such as immigrant groups, often strive for integration28,29, whereas national minorities engaged in ongoing political or existential conflict with the majority may tend toward separation and distancing30,31. In addition, power relations between groups can shape helping behaviors32. Groups with higher social power may increase their helping behavior toward less privileged groups in ways that sustain existing power dynamics – affirming their relationship as helper and helped. When inequality is stable and perceived as legitimate, disadvantaged groups are expected to feel grateful; however, when inequality is seen as illegitimate, helping behaviors may be resented33. Thus, intergroup bias in prosocial behavior may emerge from the dynamic interplay between social identity motives, perceived conflict and power structures, and the contextual salience of group boundaries.

The present study

The present study examined the separate and combined influences of cognitive and social factors on social decision-making. We tested two groups: native Arabic-speaking Israeli citizens, who constitute a national minority and often identify as Palestinians, and native Hebrew-speaking Israeli citizens, who represent the dominant national majority. Language is a strong marker of group affiliation in Israel, for example, grade-school education system is conducted in Hebrew in predominantly Jewish population areas and in Arabic in predominantly Arabic population areas, as are most daily interactions. However, higher education is mostly conducted in Hebrew. As such, all Arabic university students are multilingual, with Arabic as their native language and Hebrew as a second language. In addition, all university students are required to demonstrate a minimum English language proficiency level at enrollment, which means that all participants, Hebrew and Arabic native speakers, had English as an additional language. Importantly, as all students learn together at the University of Haifa, where about a third of the student body are native Arabic speakers, Hebrew and Arabic native speakers are in daily contact and interaction in classes and on campus. We measured participants’ language proficiency in all languages (see below).

To examine cognitive factors, participants completed the SVO task in different languages of presentation (Arabic, Hebrew, or English) to measure prosociality. This means that some participants completed the task in their native language (L1), while others completed it in a second language (L2) which was either associated with a neutral group (English, Neutral L2), or with the language of a group they are currently in conflict with (Hebrew, Majority-group L2). To examine contextual social factors, data were collected from the Israel majority group of native Hebrew speaking and an Israel-minority group of Arabic-speaking citizens. We initially planned a single data collection session in the summer of 2023. On October 7th 2023 Hamas attack led to the Hamas-Israel war, which completely changed the intensity of intergroup conflict in Israel. We decided to collect new cohort of participants, native Hebrew and Arabic speaker students at the University of Haifa. This allowed us to capture the impact of an acute escalation in intergroup conflict.

Given the distinct sociopolitical contexts of Arabic and Hebrew speakers, we first asked whether prosocial decisions in the SVO task were predicted by presentation language, recipient identity and conflict context across the full sample, and then tested whether these effects differed between groups. When examining effects within Arabic and Hebrew speakers separately, we also examined how social identity rankings, specific to each group, interacted with the factors above.

Methods

Participants

We recruited participants using the participant recruitment and messaging platform (SONA) of the Psychology Department at the University of Haifa. All participants were Israeli citizens and students at the University of Haifa at the time of the experiment. All participants were paid ILS 20 (about USD 5.33) for participating in the task, which took about 30 min to perform. In addition, participants could earn a bonus payment based on their and other players’ decisions in the task. The average bonus payment was ILS 8 (about USD 2.13). Before beginning the experiment, participants received information about the study, and could talk to an experimenter (in their native language), and they gave their informed consent to participating. The study was approved by the University of Haifa ethics committee (project 481/22). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

We verified our experimental design in a small pilot study (see Table S1a in the supplementary materials). We used R Software and the effectsize packages34 and PWR packages35 to estimate the sample size needed to detect an effect size of 0.1 Eta-square with power of 0.9 with an alpha of 0.05 (see Table S1b). This resulted in an estimation of 40 participants overall. As we had a relatively small sample size in the pilot study, and based on previous works using SVO, we decided to increase the sample size to 30 participants per group, i.e., 90 overall among Arabic speakers and 60 overall Hebrew speakers.

Table 1 includes details on the number of participants in each experimental group, and table S5 in the supplementary materials encompasses demographic information on the participants in these groups. We started recruiting participants for the native Arabic and Hebrew speaker conditions before the outbreak of the war on October 7th 2023, but did not meet our sample-size criteria. As we assumed that the war might affect participants’ behavior, we recruited new independent cohorts of participants during the war. Note that our participants are not a representative sample of the Israeli population, as we did not aim to generalize claims about the Israeli society. We aimed to keep our groups similar in terms of gender and age (and used these as control variables) to examine the effects of language, group identity and conflict between groups.

Experimental design and SVO task

Native-Arabic and native-Hebrew speakers performed an online SVO task14,36 presented in different languages, using Qualtrics software. The experimental blocks included 6 scenarios in which participants were asked to choose between pairs of monetary rewards for themselves and for another player (Fig. 1). Each scenario had 9 options for allocation of the money amount (in ILS). In each option, an amount of money appeared for the participants and another amount for another recipient participant (see Appendices 1a-1c). Both the acting participants and the recipient participants were given a monetary bonus of up to ILS 10, according to their decisions (in addition to the ILS 20 for completing the task). The average amount of money across all decisions was divided by 10 to determine the bonuses awarded to the acting participants and to the recipient participants.

Experimental design (see Appendices 1a-1c). (a) Example of a money allocation scenario used in our experiments. The scenarios were presented in different languages for Arabic and Hebrew speakers. Native Arabic speakers viewed the task either in Arabic (L1), Hebrew (L2 affiliated with the majority group), or English (Neutral L2). Native Hebrew speakers viewed the task in either Hebrew (L1) or English (Neutral L2). (b) Participants performed two blocks of money allocation scenarios with two different recipients.

The money allocation scenarios were given in two blocks of 6 questions each. In the in-group block, participants were told that the other player speaks the same language as them (Native-Arabic or Native-Hebrew). In the out-group block, the participants were told that the other payer’s native language is different from theirs (Native-Hebrew/Native-Arabic). The order of blocks 1–2 was randomized across participants (see Appendices 1a-1c and Fig. 1). An additional condition with an unknown receiver was also included in the design but is not analyzed in the present study and will be presented in a separate paper.

The experiment was presented to the participants in one of three languages, randomly assigned by the experimental platform, one of which was their native language (L1) and the others were non-native (L2) (see Appendices 1a-1c and Fig. 1). Each participant received only one language version of the task. Hebrew speakers completed the task either in Hebrew or in English, and native-Arabic speakers completed the task in either Arabic, Hebrew or English (Table 1) ( Hebrew speakers were not assigned to an Arabic version because they are typically bilingual in Hebrew (L1) and English (L2). Only a small minority of Hebrew speakers have any knowledge of Arabic, and literacy in Arabic (reading and writing) is even rarer.).

The overall design incorporated three main manipulations: (a) language (L1 vs. L2), to examine the potential cognitive effect of language use; (b) recipient identity (in-group vs. out-group), to examine intergroup bias; and (c) conflict context (before vs. during the Israel–Hamas war), to examine how active conflict shapes social decisions. This design allowed us to test both the independent and the combined influences of cognitive and social-identity factors on prosocial behavior.

After completion of the main task, participants filled in several questionnaires to evaluate their language proficiency levels and their social identity. Language proficiency levels were measured with the Multilingual Language Background Questionnaire37, adapted from the LeapQ38. To obtain a composite measure of participants’ language proficiency, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) on the questionnaire items related to language skills, usage, and exposure among Arabic and Hebrew speakers. The resulting factor scores were used to create the language proficiency variable. Detailed results of the PCA and component loadings are provided in table S2 in Appendices 3b.

In addition, we measured the ethnic identity of the participants – the extent to which they belong to different national and religious ethnic groups in Israeli society (see Appendix 2a). Similar to the language proficiency measure, a PCA was conducted on the social-identity items among Arabic and Hebrew speakers, to derive a composite index representing participants’ overall social-identity orientation (see tables S3-S4 in Appendices 3b). Demographic information (age, gender, education, religion, country of birth, year of immigration) was collected from each participant (see Appendix 2b).

Statistical analysis

We used mixed-effect linear regressions to examine the effect of presentation-language, native-language (Arabic/Hebrew), recipient identity and conflict context, on prosociality, measured the SVO task. Anova tables are reported to clearly present main effect as well as effect sizes, as well as standard coefficients. All tables are available in the supplementary materials.

First, general models across the full sample were estimated. We tested whether prosocial decisions in the SVO task were predicted by recipient identity (same vs. different identity), group membership (Arabic vs. Hebrew speakers), presentation language, and sample time (before vs. during the war). Demographic covariates (age, gender) and individual identity variables (Israeli identity, language proficiency) were included as controls. Both main-effect model and interaction model were tested, the latter including the interaction between sample time and recipient identity to capture potential changes in intergroup bias across conflict contexts.

Second, separate models for Arabic and Hebrew speakers were conducted. These models included the same predictors as above, with group-specific identity measures added: for Arabic speakers - Palestinian identity, Israeli identity, religion, and religiosity; for Hebrew speakers -Israeli identity, and religiosity. For each group, we tested both main-effect models and interaction models including the interaction between sample time and recipient identity. Follow-up post-hoc analyses were performed to examine simple effects of the sample time × recipient identity interaction within each group.

Finally, three-way interaction models were tested separately for Arabic and Hebrew speakers. These models examined whether the effect of recipient identity on prosociality was moderated simultaneously by national identity (Palestinian identity for Arabic speakers; Israeli identity for Hebrew speakers) and by sample time (before vs. during the war). For Arabic speakers, this model revealed a significant three-way interaction, prompting follow-up simple effects analyses to clarify how Palestinian identity related to prosociality under different conditions. For Hebrew speakers, the corresponding three-way interaction was not significant, and recipient identity remained the only consistent predictor.

Pre-registration

The study was originally pre-registered based on the full experimental design prior to October 7th, 2023. However, following the outbreak of the war on October 7th and its substantial impact on intergroup dynamics in Israel, we diverged from the original pre-registration. Specifically, we decided to adapt our analytic approach to focus on the effect of active conflict on intergroup bias among national minority and majority groups in Israel. The analyses reported here therefore follow the steps described above, which better capture the unique context and research questions that emerged in light of these events.

Results

Intergroup bias in the full sample

First, we tested a general model across the full sample, examining whether recipient identity, group membership, presentation language, sample time, predicted prosocial decisions (measured by SVO scores). In addition, individual-level control parameters (age, gender, Israeli identity, and language proficiency) were included, and individual intercepts as random effects (see Table 2, and Tables S6a – S6b in Appendix 3d). In this analysis, recipient identity reflected whether the recipient shared the participant’s native language (in-group) or not (out-group). We found that recipient identity significantly predicted levels of prosociality (F(1,335.83) = 11.55, p = < 0.001, partial η² = 0.03). Standard coefficients showed that participants in the in-group condition were more prosocial compared to those in the out-group condition (β = 0.14, 95% CI [0.06, 0.22]).

We also tested an additional model that incorporated the interaction between recipient identity and sample time to assess whether the effect of identity type differed before and during the war (Table 2 and Tables S7a – S7b in Appendix 3d). A marginally significant interaction emerged between sample time and recipient identity (F(1,334.83) = 3.74, p = .054, partial η² = 0.01). Presentation language and other control variables were not significant in the two models described above.

Separate models for Arabic and Hebrew speakers: active conflict effect on intergroup bias

Second, we estimated separate models for Arabic and Hebrew speakers to test whether the predictors operated differently across the two groups. For both groups, interaction models included recipient identity (in-group/out-group) and sample time to assess whether the effect of the manipulation of recipient identity changed during the war.

In the main model among Arabic speakers, significant effects were observed for recipient identity (F(1,193) = 5.97, p = .015, partial η² = 0.03), and sample time (F(1,181) = 4.29, p = .039, partial η² = 0.02) (Table 3 and Table S8a and S8b). Standard coefficients indicated that participants were more prosocial in the in-group condition than in the out-group condition (β = 0.14, CI [0.03, 0.25]). Regarding sample time, prosociality was higher during the war compared to before (β = 0.32, CI [0.02, 0.63]).

National identity effects on intergroup bias

Finally, we tested three-way interaction models separately for Arabic and Hebrew speakers to capture whether the impact of recipient identity was moderated by national identity (Palestinian identity among Arabic speakers; Israeli identity among Hebrew speakers) and by sample time (see Methods and Tables 3 and 4, S13a – S15b).

Among Arabic speakers, a significant three-way interaction was found between sample time, recipient identity, and Palestinian identity (F(1,190) = 4.39, p = .037, partial η² = 0.02) (Table 3, S13a). This was reflected in the effect of the three-way interaction (β = 0.25, CI [0.02, 0.49]), the two-way interaction between sample time and Palestinian identity (β = -0.33, CI [-0.65, -0.01]), and the two-way interaction between recipient identity and Palestinian identity (β = -0.33, CI [-0.65, -0.01]) (Table S13b).

The simple effects analysis of the three-way interaction between sample time, recipient identity, and Palestinian identity revealed distinct patterns before and during the war (see Tables S14a – S14b). Before the war, Palestinian identity was positively related to prosociality in both the in-group (M = 0.082, SE = 0.039) and out-group (M = 0.080, SE = 0.039) conditions, with no significant difference between them (B = -0.002, SE = 0.028, t(190) = -0.078, p = .9998). During the war, however, a divergence emerged: while the positive association between Palestinian identity and prosociality persisted in the in-group condition (M = 0.060, SE = 0.026), it became negative in the out-group condition (M = -0.012, SE = 0.026) (B = -0.072, SE = 0.018, t(190) = -4.013, p = .0005). No other pairwise contrasts were significant.

These findings indicate that Palestinian identity predicted greater prosociality consistently before the war, but during the war, this effect was contingent on recipient identity: it remained positive toward in-group members yet reversed toward out-group members (see Fig. 3).

Associations between Palestinian identity and prosociality among Arabic speakers, expressed as the difference between in-group and out-group allocations, as a function of sample time. Before the war, Palestinian identity was not related to intergroup bias. During the war, Palestinian identity was related to intergroup bias, as it was positively associated with prosociality toward in-group members, and negative (though non-significant) association toward out-group members.

For Hebrew speakers, the three-way interaction between recipient identity, sample time, and Israeli identity was not significant (F(1,137.91) = 1.48, p = .225, partial η² = 0.01) (Table 4, S15a, S15b and Fig. 4). The only consistent predictor was recipient identity (F(1, 137.91) = 4.48, p = .035, partial η² = 0.03), with higher prosociality in the in-group condition (β = 0.10, CI [-0.07, 0.27]).

Associations between Israeli identity and prosociality (SVO) among Hebrew speakers, expressed as the difference between in-group and out-group allocations, as a function of sample time. Israeli identity was not significantly related to prosociality. Correlations were weak and non-significant both before and during the war, in both the out-group and in-group conditions, consistent with the null three-way interaction.

Discussion

We investigated how cognitive factors, social-identity and intergroup conflict context shape prosocial decision-making among minority and majority groups in Israel, before and during the 2023 Hamas-Israel war. Using a Social Value Orientation task, we manipulated both the language of presentation (L1, majority-group L2, and neutral L2) and the recipient identity. Contrary to our initial expectations, language condition and language proficiency did not significantly affect prosocial decisions. Our findings highlight the dominant role of social identity and conflict context. Across the full sample, participants favored their in-group over the out-group member, and this intergroup bias intensified during the war. Among Arabic speakers, Palestinian identification differentially predicted prosociality: during conflict, stronger Palestinian identity promoted generosity toward in-group members but reduced generosity toward out-group members. Hebrew speakers showed a consistent in-group bias that did not vary with conflict or language. Overall, these results indicate that social identity and conflict context, rather than cognitive language factors, are central in shaping intergroup decisions.

We observed clear intergroup bias in participants’ treatment of in-group and out-group receivers. While previous studies have demonstrated such biases even under minimal group conditions5,6,10–12,21, in the present study the effect was most pronounced during the active Israel-Hamas war. Before the conflict, intergroup bias was evident but relatively moderate, particularly among Arabic-speaking participants. Presumably, during this period the national minority group of native Arabic speakers was influenced by the operation of competing motivations, namely the motivation to integrate versus the motivation to maintain separation within the majority society. Interestingly, during the war, the escalation of violence magnified in-group favoritism and out-group derogation, especially among those identifying strongly as Palestinian. Thus, heightened group-based threat26,30,39 and reduced perceived opportunities for integration may have shifted motivations away from fit-in toward stand-out, resulting in stronger in-group bias. Hebrew-speaking participants, by contrast, exhibited a more stable in-group preference across contexts, likely reflecting their majority-group position, which affords greater consistency and less identity conflict29,31, and more general expectation of helping behavior due to their perceived higher status32. These results underscore that intergroup relations are dynamic rather than fixed, fluctuating according to contextual salience, real-world events, and power and identity positions of minority versus majority groups.

These findings highlight how world events affect the behavior we observe in lab-settings, and how experimental tools such as SVO can capture shifts in attitudes following current events. This ability to track changes across different real-life circumstances allows us to expand our observations and understanding beyond peaceful times and lab manipulations, capturing responses to intense conflicts that cannot be induced in a laboratory setting. Furthermore, while lab experiments are aimed at isolating the contributions of different factors on decisions40, these decisions are multifaceted, and the attention and weight given to different factors may shift over time, as demonstrated here. Intergroup bias, and its manifestation in participants’ behavior, may therefore be more dynamic than previously assumed.

This dynamic view may have important implications for tracking the state of intergroup conflict and conflict resolution. As demonstrated here, intense conflict intensified intergroup bias. However, our results also demonstrate the ways in which intergroup conflict can be attenuated. Our participants were native Hebrew and Arabic speakers, learning as students together at the same university and interacting daily. The strength of interactions may vary, but even weak ties can have a substantial effect41. Thus, before the Israel-Hamas war, intergroup bias was relatively low and was not related to group identity. It increased during the war, though the effect could have been even larger given the intensity of the conflict. This dynamic result provides an encouraging basis for interventions that can shape perceptions of the other group. For example, making visible helping behaviors and prosocial decisions made by ingroup members toward outgroup members – and vice versa –can reduce intergroup tension42. Other interventions are aimed at evaluating information and misinformation about outgroups and minorities43, with the goal of preparing media consumers to recognize such practices and inspiring more critical evaluation of such information44,45. Recent work has demonstrated how such pre-bunking treatments can reduce intergroup bias46. Our work suggests that intergroup bias is a dynamic process, and future research can test how interventions interact with real-life events in shaping this process.

While we expected to find a language effect, by which prosocial decisions in L1 would differ from those made in L2, we did not find support for such effects. Previous research on the foreign language effect has shown that using a non-native language can influence decision-making by reducing emotional resonance, increasing cognitive load, and promoting more deliberative reasoning8,9,16–18. However, these studies typically examined individual-level cognitive mechanisms in relatively neutral or decontextualized settings. In contrast, our study introduced additional social and contextual factors – including intergroup identity and real-world conflict, that likely interact with or even overshadow the cognitive effects of language. When strong social identity cues are present, particularly in the context of active intergroup conflict, the salience of group belonging may dominate cognitive influences such as emotional distance or reduced affective processing in L2. Thus, while language proficiency and the language of presentation were hypothesized to affect prosocial behavior, our findings suggest that these factors may not be as influential as social identity markers, such as national or ethnic affiliation. This interpretation aligns with evidence that social identity can override other cognitive influences in shaping intergroup behavior47,48 and supports the idea that in conflict-laden environments, group-based motivations may take precedence over cognitive processing modes in determining prosocial decisions.

The study has several limitations that could have an impact on the conclusions drawn from our findings. A problem with our experimental design is that it does not cover the exact root of differences in prosociality between groups. For example, it is not clear if what we see is heightened in-group prosociality, increased out-group discrimination, or a combination of both31,32. This is an important distinction, which relates to the power structure between groups, and should be more rigorously examined in future works. Another limitation is the relatively small number of participants before the war, which reduced the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Additionally, the study’s design could not accommodate a within-subjects experiment to assess the effects of presentation language. Importantly, as we did not return to the same participants before and during the war, and recruited a new cohort of students, it is not possible to examine shifts in prosociality within subjects. However, recruitment was similar across groups and participants’ profile was similar, isolating the active-conflict as the predominant difference between the pre- and during the war groups. Finally, our task relied mostly on non-verbal information, mostly the numeric amounts of money allocated to the players. It could be that more verbose tasks, for example relying on textual vignettes describing social dilemmas, might lead to more pronounced presentation language effects. Nevertheless, our main conclusions remain robust: intergroup bias was consistently observed across groups and conditions, and identity-based effects were replicated even under varied linguistic and contextual manipulations. These patterns highlight that social identity processes play a salient role in shaping prosocial decisions, even when cognitive and contextual factors vary. Future studies should further explore group power relations and the sense of belonging to a shared identity.

In conclusion, while intergroup bias in prosocial decisions is well documented, here we tested its susceptibility to cognitive and social factors. We found that cognitive factors, in the shape of presentation language, did not contribute much to prosocial decisions. Social identity factors made the strongest effect on these decisions, especially in the context of active intergroup conflict. This indicates that social decisions are dynamic and can change in light of intergroup relations, such as minority-majority, active conflicts, and social motivations, that make one dimension more salient than others. These results have implications on the way we understand intergroup bias as a dynamic and multifaceted process, and the importance of understanding it in context.
