Application of machine learning in osteoporosis screening: a narrative review
Authors: Xueting Shen, Jin Xiong, Shu Wang, Guohui Hu, Huanbing Liu, Siying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning has the potential to address limitations of traditional osteoporosis screening through advanced data processing and pattern recognition capabilities. This review provides a critical analysis of ML applications in opportunistic screening, precision risk assessment, and quantitative bone quality analysis, while addressing current implementation challenges and future research directions to enhance early diagnosis, reduce fracture risk, and improve patient outcomes. As a narrative synthesis, this review highlights strategic directions and translational challenges rather than providing a systematic appraisal of evidence. Introduction

Epidemiology and public health burden of osteoporosis

Osteoporosis, a systemic skeletal disease characterized by reduced bone mass and microstructural deterioration, leads to increased bone fragility and a higher risk of fractures from even minor trauma1. Against the backdrop of global population aging, it has become an increasingly severe public health challenge, with its high incidence and disability rates imposing a significant socioeconomic burden2. Globally, approximately 200 million people are affected by this condition, and among individuals over 50, one-third of women and one-fifth of men are likely to experience an osteoporotic fracture3. In China, the rapid aging of the population makes this issue even more urgent, with the number of osteoporotic fractures expected to more than double by 2050 compared to 20204. These fractures, especially hip fractures, often lead to severe pain, long-term disability, and even death, with a one-year mortality rate exceeding 20%5. Consequently, combating this “silent killer” through efficient and accurate early screening has become a critical global health priority6.

Current status and challenges of osteoporosis screening

Despite the clear public health risks, early screening for osteoporosis remains suboptimal, creating a significant “screening gap” where many high-risk individuals fail to receive timely diagnosis7. The root of this issue lies in the multiple challenges faced by existing screening strategies. The current gold standard, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), measures bone mineral density (BMD) but is hindered by expensive equipment and the need for specialized personnel, limiting its accessibility, particularly in primary care and resource-limited regions8,9. Furthermore, there is significant variability in screening guidelines across countries and regions. Some guidelines recommend routine assessments for men in specific age groups, while others do not, creating inconsistencies that hinder global coordination and efficiency10,11. More fundamentally, a deeper challenge lies in the diagnostic limitations of BMD itself. It is well-established that BMD only explains 60–70% of the variation in bone strength, failing to account for crucial factors of bone quality such as microstructure and mineralization12. This explains why nearly half of fragility fractures occur in individuals whose BMD does not meet the diagnostic threshold for osteoporosis, highlighting the insufficiency of relying solely on BMD for risk assessment13. While technologies like quantitative CT (QCT) offer more comprehensive data, their high radiation dose and cost preclude routine screening14,15. This confluence of accessibility barriers and diagnostic limitations creates a clear and urgent research gap: the need for a new screening paradigm that is accurate, accessible, and cost-effective.

Machine learning (ML): a promising yet unproven frontier

In recent years, ML has emerged as a powerful tool with the potential to address key challenges in osteoporosis screening. By identifying hidden patterns in vast, complex datasets, ML offers novel solutions, most notably through “opportunistic screening”16. This strategy leverages routine radiographic images (e.g., CT scans, X-rays) taken for other medical purposes, applying ML algorithms to assess bone health, which could, in principle, improve screening accessibility without additional dedicated scans or radiation exposure16,17. Furthermore, ML models can integrate multimodal data—from imaging and clinical indicators to genomics—to construct precise, individualized risk stratification tools that far exceed the capacity of single-metric assessments.

However, despite this immense technical potential, a significant gap persists between algorithmic performance in research settings and its tangible impact in clinical practice. While numerous studies report high diagnostic accuracy, the translation of these models into reliable, equitable, and integrated clinical tools remains a formidable challenge. The initial optimism fueled by high-performance metrics is now tempered by critical questions regarding model generalizability across diverse populations, the “black-box” nature of many algorithms, and the practicalities of workflow integration. This sets up the central tension that this review aims to address: how do we bridge the chasm between what ML can do in theory and what it must do to be effective in the real world?

Purpose, scope, and innovations of this review

Therefore, this review aims to move beyond a simple survey of technical achievements and provide a critical and forward-looking analysis of the state of machine learning in osteoporosis screening. The primary objective of this narrative review is to address three specific, answerable questions: (1) What are the technical approaches, performance, and applicable scenarios of ML-driven opportunistic screening based on different imaging modalities (CT, conventional X-ray, dental panoramic radiographs)? (2) What advancements have been made in using ML for precise risk stratification through multimodal data fusion and for quantitative bone quality assessment? (3) What are the key data-related, model-related, and ethical challenges hindering the translation of these research models into clinical practice?

This narrative review distinguishes itself through a distinct clinical translation perspective. Unlike systematic reviews, it does not employ a pre-registered search or formal quality appraisal but is based on a critical analysis of literature identified through broad, targeted searches. Its central thesis moves beyond diagnostic accuracy to critically examine the translational gap between algorithmic performance and clinical utility. By deconstructing implementation barriers and proposing a strategic framework, this review advocates for a paradigm shift towards measurable clinical impact, to provide a strategic framework for stakeholders to navigate the complexities of AI implementation in osteoporosis care.

Skeletal anatomy, physiology, and pathophysiology of osteoporosis

Skeletal anatomy and physiological characteristics

The skeleton is a dynamic metabolic system, not a static structure. It continuously renews and remodels itself through the coordinated activity of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes18. Understanding the normal anatomical and physiological features of the skeleton, along with the pathological changes seen in osteoporosis, is essential for developing and applying advanced ML-based screening techniques.

Macroscopic structure and functional characteristics of bone

Bone tissue consists primarily of cortical and trabecular (cancellous) bone, which differ in structure, distribution, and function19. Cortical bone forms the solid outer shell of the skeleton, making up about 80% of its total volume. It is dense and provides rigidity and strength, particularly under bending and torsional loads20–22. In contrast, trabecular bone, found inside the skeleton, has a porous, sponge-like structure composed of interconnected trabeculae. Its surface area is much greater than that of cortical bone, and it is metabolically more active. Trabecular bone primarily absorbs and dissipates impact forces, with a higher concentration in areas that bear compressive loads, such as the vertebral bodies, where approximately 75% is trabecular bone23. Variations in the proportion of cortical and trabecular bone across skeletal sites determine their mechanical properties and primary load-bearing patterns. These differences provide critical prior knowledge for ML models. For example, in vertebral CT analysis, models can be trained to emphasize texture and density variations in trabecular bone, whereas in long bones of the extremities, greater focus is placed on cortical thickness and porosity. This anatomical heterogeneity underpins the development of site-specific AI diagnostic models.

Multidimensional determinants of bone strength

Bone strength is a complex, multifactorial trait, primarily determined by BMD and bone quality24. BMD, which measures bone mineral content per unit volume or area, is the primary clinical marker for diagnosing osteoporosis and assessing fracture risk, typically measured via DXA25,26. However, BMD only accounts for 60–70% of the variation in bone strength and fracture risk19. The concept of bone quality was introduced to encompass other structural and material factors affecting bone strength beyond BMD20,27. These include the bone’s microstructure (such as cortical porosity, trabecular number, thickness, connectivity, and anisotropy), material properties (e.g., collagen cross-linking, mineral crystal size and alignment), accumulation and repair of microdamage, and bone metabolic turnover rates and their spatial distribution27,28. ML, particularly radiomics-based imaging analysis, enables the quantitative extraction of these intricate bone quality features from medical images, offering a more comprehensive evaluation of bone strength29,30. Importantly, BMD and bone quality are not independent but interact in complex ways20,24. For instance, increased bone metabolic turnover can simultaneously affect both BMD and bone microstructure, while changes in BMD may trigger microstructural remodeling through mechanical adaptation31. The complexity and multidimensional nature of “bone quality” pose challenges for traditional statistical models but offer significant opportunities for ML. Radiomics-based approaches, in particular, can quantitatively extract hundreds to thousands of high-dimensional features from routine medical images such as CT and X-rays, capturing details of bone microarchitecture, texture, morphology, and grayscale distribution. These features provide a digital representation of “bone quality,” delivering information beyond BMD and enabling the development of more accurate bone strength prediction models. The interaction between BMD and bone quality further highlights the importance of multimodal fusion models that integrate BMD, radiomics features, and clinical data to achieve a more comprehensive assessment of bone strength.

Osteocyte types and their functional network

Bone tissue consists of three primary cell types that interact through a complex signaling network to maintain bone homeostasis and integrity32,33. Osteoblasts, derived from mesenchymal stem cells, are responsible for synthesizing and mineralizing the bone matrix32. Osteoclasts mediate bone resorption by dissolving mineralized bone and degrading the matrix33,34. Osteocytes, derived from osteoblasts, are embedded deep within the bone matrix and account for 90–95% of all bone cells35. These osteocytes form an intricate three-dimensional network through their extensive cellular processes, acting not only as primary mechanosensors that detect mechanical stress and microdamage but also as key regulators of bone remodeling35,36. By secreting signaling molecules such as sclerostin, RANKL, and OPG, osteocytes precisely control the activity of both osteoblasts and osteoclasts36,37. Studies suggest that osteocyte dysfunction is a common pathological factor across various forms of osteoporosis, including glucocorticoid-induced, age-related, and postmenopausal types37,38. Changes in osteocyte function are strongly linked to the development of these conditions, and related alterations in the bone microenvironment could potentially be captured through advanced imaging techniques, offering valuable analytical targets for ML models.

Pathophysiological changes in osteoporosis

Bone remodeling imbalance and the mechanisms of osteoporosis

Bone tissue undergoes continuous “bone remodeling,” a dynamic process where bone resorption and formation are tightly coupled39. This process is regulated by the bone remodeling unit (BMU) and key signaling pathways, such as RANKL-RANK-OPG and Wnt/β-catenin40–42. The core pathophysiological mechanism of osteoporosis is bone remodeling imbalance, where bone resorption exceeds bone formation, leading to net bone loss and deterioration of bone microstructure20,43. Factors such as hormonal changes (e.g., a sharp decline in estrogen after menopause), decreased mechanical loading, chronic inflammation, and age-related cellular dysfunction contribute to this imbalance21,27–29,44–46. These factors influence bone remodeling through various mechanisms, leading to similar osteoporosis phenotypes. Notably, the bone remodeling imbalance differs between types of osteoporosis, such as postmenopausal and age-related osteoporosis47. For example, postmenopausal osteoporosis typically presents as a high-turnover type with significantly increased bone resorption, whereas age-related osteoporosis may manifest as a low-turnover type with significantly reduced bone formation47,48. The heterogeneity of bone remodeling indicates that a “one-size-fits-all” diagnostic model may be suboptimal. ML models designed for individualized risk prediction should integrate features that reflect diverse pathophysiological states. Beyond imaging features, incorporating biochemical markers of bone turnover can help distinguish between high- and low-turnover risk groups. This mechanism-based subgroup analysis offers the potential for more precise risk stratification and improved guidance for targeted treatment.

Differential changes in cortical and trabecular bone

In osteoporosis, both cortical and trabecular bones undergo pathological changes, but their loss patterns, timing, and impact on fracture risk differ49,50. Trabecular bone, due to its higher metabolic activity and faster remodeling rate (approximately every 200 days)51, experiences more rapid loss in the early stages of osteoporosis, particularly near menopause in women52. Pathological changes in trabecular bone primarily include thinning, reduced quantity, disrupted connectivity, and increased anisotropy, which lead to significant deterioration of its three-dimensional network and a sharp decline in compressive strength50,53. As the disease progresses, especially in the elderly, cortical bone loss becomes more pronounced52,54. Key changes in cortical bone include reduced thickness, a substantial increase in cortical porosity (from the normal 5–10% to over 40%), and enhanced pore connectivity54–56. The increase in porosity negatively affects bone strength, with each 1% increase in porosity leading to a 2–5% reduction in strength33,57. These differential changes in cortical and trabecular bone help explain the timing of fractures at different sites (e.g., early vertebral fractures and late femoral neck fractures) and suggest that ML models should prioritize different features when analyzing various imaging techniques (such as CT, which distinguishes cortical from trabecular bone) or skeletal sites58,59.

The mechanisms linking osteoporosis and fracture risk

The mechanisms by which osteoporosis increases fracture risk are far more complex than merely reduced bone mass20. First, the deterioration of bone microstructure, such as the loss of trabecular bone connectivity and increased cortical bone porosity, leads to stress concentration, making the bone more prone to fractures under lower external forces30,60. Second, changes in bone material properties, including abnormal collagen cross-linking and altered mineral crystal characteristics, diminish the toughness and energy absorption capacity of bone, increasing brittleness60,61. Additionally, a reduced ability to repair microdamage leads to the accumulation of microcracks, further weakening bone strength61,62. Crucially, fracture risk assessment in osteoporosis requires integrating intrinsic bone factors (e.g., BMD and bone quality) with extrinsic factors (e.g., fall risk and external force magnitude)63. Notably, about 50% of hip fracture patients do not meet the diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis based on BMD alone, underscoring the importance of bone quality and non-BMD factors13,63. The complex interplay between intrinsic and extrinsic factors presents an ideal setting for the application of ML. This intricate interplay between intrinsic and extrinsic factors highlights the limitations of conventional tools such as FRAX®, which, despite incorporating clinical risk factors, rely on linear models incapable of capturing nonlinear relationships and higher-order interactions13,64. By contrast, ML approaches—particularly deep learning and ensemble methods—excel in automatically detecting and modeling these complex patterns. Through the integration of imaging data, clinical risk factors, gait analysis (to assess fall risk), and even genetic information, AI can construct a high-dimensional, nonlinear, and individualized fracture risk prediction engine that markedly outperforms traditional methods. This represents the central focus of the subsequent sections of this review.

ML-driven innovations in osteoporosis screening methods

Early screening for osteoporosis is crucial for reducing fracture risk and improving patient prognosis. However, traditional screening methods face significant challenges in terms of accuracy, accessibility, portability, and cost-effectiveness. ML, as a powerful data analysis tool, is driving innovative research into new osteoporosis screening strategies with transformative potential. It is driving the shift from “passive detection” to “active prediction and opportunistic screening,” and holds the potential to move from “single-parameter diagnosis” to “multimodal data-driven precise risk stratification”65,66.

Opportunistic screening: shifting from “dedicated testing” to “incidental discovery”

Opportunistic screening leverages data from routine medical exams conducted for other conditions, which are then analyzed using ML models. This method offers anatomical visualization and quantitative data with zero added cost or radiation exposure, enabling automatic screening of high-risk osteoporosis populations. The key advantage lies in its use of existing exam data, eliminating the need for separate bone density tests and additional radiation. This approach addresses the core issues in traditional screening methods (e.g., DXA), such as dependence on specialized equipment, low coverage, and poor patient compliance67. CT-based screening

CT scans taken during examinations for abdominal, chest, or heart conditions provide a valuable data resource for osteoporosis opportunistic screening. This transition from traditional ML to end-to-end deep learning highlights a significant advancement in the field. Early methods relied on “feature engineering,” where experts manually or semi-automatically extracted texture features, grayscale histograms, and other radiomics features from regions of interest (ROIs), such as the vertebral body. These features were then analyzed using traditional ML models like support vector machines (SVM) and random forests for osteoporosis classification or BMD prediction68. While these methods demonstrated the potential of CT scans for providing bone density information, they were cumbersome, heavily reliant on manual input, and lacked consistent, reproducible feature extraction.

Modern techniques are marked by the rise of deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Researchers have developed end-to-end models that automatically locate the vertebral body, segment the region of interest, and directly learn and extract discriminative features from raw CT images. Numerous retrospective studies have demonstrated the promising performance of these models, particularly in lumbar spine analysis, with some achieving an area under the curve (AUC) as high as 0.994, indicating outstanding diagnostic accuracy69. However, it is crucial to note that such high metrics often come from optimized, single-center datasets, and their generalizability to broader clinical practice requires further validation. For example, Yasaka et al. employed CNN to analyze abdominal CT scans of the L1 vertebra, achieving an AUC of 0.965–0.970 for osteoporosis prediction70. The application of transfer learning has further accelerated this process, allowing models pre-trained on large natural image datasets (e.g., ImageNet) to be fine-tuned for medical imaging tasks. This approach significantly reduces the dependency on large annotated datasets while improving model performance and generalization. Kim et al.‘s deep learning-based BMD tool, for instance, showed a higher success rate in accurately positioning and adjusting ROIs compared to older feature-based automated algorithms71. Additionally, in some cases, Quantitative CT (QCT) may outperform DXA, particularly when degenerative changes such as vertebral osteophytes or endplate sclerosis are present, which can lead to BMD overestimation by DXA72. Consequently, CT-based opportunistic screening offers a potential advantage in identifying osteoporosis patients who might be misdiagnosed as normal by DXA due to degenerative changes. However, CT-based opportunistic screening faces several challenges, primarily the heterogeneity of data resulting from variations in scanning devices, reconstruction algorithms, and scanning parameters (such as tube voltage and slice thickness). This necessitates the development of highly robust models or stringent device-specific calibration before deployment.

Screening based on conventional X-ray radiographs

Conventional X-ray radiographs, particularly chest X-rays, are among the most frequently performed imaging tests worldwide, offering exceptional potential for osteoporosis screening. This method leverages deep learning models to identify and decode hidden features related to three-dimensional bone structure and bone density from two-dimensional X-ray images, such as those of the chest or hip. A study by Sato et al. showed that deep learning models based on chest radiographs, combined with two simple clinical variables—age and gender—could effectively predict BMD and T-scores. The correlation coefficient for predicting hip BMD reached 0.75, and the area under the curve (AUC) for T-score prediction in osteoporosis diagnosis was 0.8467. The primary advantage of X-ray-based screening lies in its high accessibility, allowing coverage of resource-limited primary care areas. However, the main challenge stems from the limitations of two-dimensional projection images, which are prone to tissue overlap and variable projection angles, requiring enhanced model robustness.

Screening based on dental panoramic radiographs (PRs)

Integrating osteoporosis screening into routine dental care is an innovative approach. Dental panoramic radiographs (PRs) are widely used in dentistry. This method utilizes advanced AI models to analyze visible anatomical structures in PRs, such as the morphology of the mandibular cortex, trabecular texture, and occasionally parts of the cervical spine, to indirectly assess the patient’s overall skeletal health. The potential of this approach is vast, extending screening access to the large population undergoing regular dental check-ups, offering an innovative pathway toward “universal screening”. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that AI-based screening using dental panoramic radiographs achieved a pooled sensitivity of 87.9% and specificity of 81.9%, underscoring its significant potential as an effective initial screening tool66. However, the primary challenge lies in the indirect relationship between the bone information provided by PRs and the bone density of axial bones (such as the lumbar spine and hip), as well as the limited anatomical coverage. Consequently, the model must be carefully designed to extract reliable predictive features.

Precise risk stratification: the deep evolution from “population standards” to “individual prediction”

To achieve truly individualized and precise risk assessment, it is essential to go beyond the single parameter of BMD and integrate multidimensional information. ML models, especially ensemble learning algorithms (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM) and deep neural networks (DNN), with their efficient ability to process high-dimensional, heterogeneous data, can show promise in constructing complex predictive models that have, in some studies, outperformed traditional statistical tools (e.g., FRAX model) on retrospective data. This suggests a potential pathway towards more individualized risk assessment, facilitating the transition from “population standards” to “individual prediction”73.

The intrinsic logic of multimodal data fusion

The risk of osteoporosis-related fractures results from the interaction of multiple factors. It depends not only on the bone’s biomechanical properties (e.g., BMD, bone microstructure, bone strength) but also on clinical history (e.g., age, gender, prior fractures, fall history), lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity), medication use (e.g., corticosteroid use), and even genetic factors. These variables often interact in complex, nonlinear ways, which traditional linear statistical models struggle to capture. The strength of ML lies in its ability to automatically detect and model these intricate relationships from large, heterogeneous datasets. By automating feature engineering (particularly in deep learning) and leveraging powerful nonlinear mapping, ML models can generate risk prediction models that more accurately reflect an individual’s unique health profile. To effectively integrate these diverse data modalities, a sophisticated architectural framework is required. Integrating medical imaging with clinical risk factors

Integrating quantitative features extracted from medical imaging (i.e., radiomics) with traditional clinical risk factors (such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), fracture history, medication history, and comorbidities) enhances the performance of osteoporosis and fracture risk prediction models. Radiomic features can capture intricate details of image textures, shapes, and intensities that are difficult to discern visually, potentially reflecting bone microstructure and heterogeneity. Numerous studies have demonstrated that fused models significantly outperform those based on single data modalities. For example, Fang et al. showed that combining hip CT radiomic features with two basic clinical variables—age and gender—improved the AUC for osteoporosis screening from 0.882 (image-only model) to 0.88674. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled C-index (concordance index) for ML models predicting osteoporotic fracture risk could reach 0.75, with the best-performing CNN model achieving a C-index of 0.98, far surpassing traditional clinical risk assessment tools75. These fusion models not only enhance predictive accuracy but also provide a more comprehensive individual risk profile, aiding clinicians in developing more personalized intervention strategies. However, variations in data integration methods (such as feature selection and weight distribution across modalities) may affect model generalizability. Additionally, the size, diversity (e.g., gender, age, ethnicity), and fracture type definitions in the training dataset significantly influence model performance, necessitating larger, multicenter prospective studies for further validation.

Fusion of radiomics and clinical risk factors

In primary care settings or resource-limited areas without access to advanced imaging equipment, developing “non-imaging” osteoporosis risk prediction models holds significant practical value. The core idea behind this innovative approach is to leverage easily accessible routine clinical data for risk prediction. This approach greatly enhances the accessibility and universality of screening. A prospective study using only routine clinical data, including age, arm circumference, weight, blood calcium, and folic acid, developed ML models (such as random forests and gradient boosting machines) that achieved 93% accuracy in predicting osteoporosis risk, with an AUC of 0.9476. This provides a feasible solution for implementing low-cost, efficient initial osteoporosis screening in primary and resource-limited settings, helping to identify high-risk individuals early and guiding them for further DXA examination.

Radiogenomics

Radiogenomics represents one of the cutting-edge exploration directions in the field of precise risk assessment for osteoporosis. Its core objective is to integrate imaging phenotypes (macroscopic or microscopic quantitative features extracted from medical images by AI, such as CT-based bone density, bone structure texture, trabecular pattern, cortical bone porosity, etc.) with genomic data (such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), gene expression profiles), proteomics, metabolomics, and other microscopic molecular biological data for correlation analysis and integrated modeling77. Although the application of radiogenomics in osteoporosis is still in its early stages, its successful cases in oncology (e.g., predicting tumor invasiveness, treatment response, and patient prognosis) provide important insights for future research. By revealing the deep connections between specific genetic variations, molecular pathways, and the skeletal phenotypic features observed in imaging, radiogenomics is expected to elucidate more intricate genetic and molecular mechanisms behind the pathogenesis of osteoporosis and further identify novel biomarkers and disease subtypes. A study summarized GWAS findings from the past 12 years, identifying hundreds of loci associated with BMD and fracture risk, providing a genomic foundation for radiogenomics78. In the future, by analyzing the correlations between specific genetic variations and AI-extracted imaging bone microstructure features, it is expected to identify subgroups of individuals who are highly susceptible to osteoporosis, who exhibit different responses to specific treatments, or who differ in fracture healing ability. Currently, the relatively high cost of obtaining genetic data and the complexity of integrating and analyzing multi-omics data limit the extensive research of radiogenomics in the field of osteoporosis. Future work should focus on developing standardized workflows for multi-omics data collection and analysis, building large-scale, high-quality radiogenomics databases, to achieve deeper levels of precise risk stratification, provide early warning for high-risk populations, and potentially offer key insights for developing personalized drug targets and precision treatment strategies, thereby advancing osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment into the era of “precision medicine”.

Bone quality quantification and frontier exploration

Bone strength is not only dependent on bone mass but is also profoundly influenced by bone quality. ML technologies are advancing the analysis of bone imaging from macroscopic density measurements to more refined assessments of bone microstructure. At the same time, more advanced artificial intelligence models and interpretability technologies designed to enhance model transparency are continuously emerging, injecting new vitality into this field.

The application of ML in bone microstructure quantification

Currently, the evaluation of bone quality mainly covers two areas: laboratory analysis and clinical testing79. Laboratory analysis primarily involves high-resolution imaging (such as Micro-CT) and mechanical testing of bone biopsy samples or animal model bones. ML can be used to process these high-resolution imaging data, automatically segment and quantify microstructural parameters of trabeculae, such as thickness, number, spacing, connectivity density, and anisotropy, and establish predictive models linking these parameters to skeletal mechanical properties (e.g., elastic modulus, ultimate strength). Clinical non-invasive testing is generally divided into the following categories:

CT/HR-pQCT-based: ML algorithms, especially deep learning models, can extract more detailed bone microstructural information from clinical CT or HR-pQCT images. For instance, by analyzing CT images of the vertebrae or proximal femur, AI models can quantify cortical bone thickness, cortical porosity, and trabecular bone texture features, all of which have been shown to correlate with bone strength and fracture risk.

CT/HR-pQCT-based: ML algorithms, especially deep learning models, can extract more detailed bone microstructural information from clinical CT or HR-pQCT images. For instance, by analyzing CT images of the vertebrae or proximal femur, AI models can quantify cortical bone thickness, cortical porosity, and trabecular bone texture features, all of which have been shown to correlate with bone strength and fracture risk.

Raman Spectroscopy: This non-invasive optical technique detects scattered light caused by molecular vibrations in bone tissue, providing chemical composition information about the bone’s organic matrix (such as collagen cross-linking and conformation) and inorganic minerals (such as carbonate substitution and crystallinity)79. A random forest model constructed from Raman spectroscopy data of 82 human bone samples achieved an accuracy of 89.8% and an AUC of 0.96 in differentiating normal bone, osteopenia, and osteoporosis, significantly outperforming traditional statistical methods80. This demonstrates the potential of combining this technology with ML for non-invasive bone quality assessment.

Through multi-component analysis, parameters such as the bound water/pore water (BW/PW) ratio and magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) can be obtained. Studies have shown that these UTE-MRI parameters are significantly correlated with BMD, bone porosity, and mechanical properties of the skeleton, providing new imaging tools for non-invasive evaluation of bone diseases such as osteoporosis. ML models can be used to integrate these multi-parameter UTE-MRI data to improve the accuracy of bone quality and strength predictions81.

Emerging AI models and interpretability exploration

As AI technologies advance, more efficient and specialized models are being applied to bone imaging analysis. For instance, HarDNet (Harmonic Densely Connected Network) is a lightweight, efficient CNN architecture that reduces computational load and memory usage by optimizing inter-layer connections and feature reuse, while maintaining high accuracy. It shows potential for osteoporosis screening using hand X-rays, a resource-limited scenario82. In a development and validation study, the DeepDXA-Hand model, which employs HarDNet to analyze hand X-rays for predicting BMD, demonstrated a correlation coefficient of 0.745 with DXA-measured BMD, achieving a sensitivity of 0.73, specificity of 0.83, and accuracy of 0.80 in binary osteoporosis classification within that study cohort82.

However, many high-performance deep learning models are essentially “black boxes,” lacking transparency in their decision-making processes, which limits their credibility and acceptance in clinical practice. Thus, the research and application of Explainable AI (XAI) are becoming increasingly crucial. XAI aims to provide insights into AI model predictions, offering interpretability. Common XAI techniques include SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which quantifies each feature’s contribution to a prediction based on Shapley values from game theory; Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), which creates a simple, interpretable surrogate model in the vicinity of a prediction instance to explain complex models’ behavior; Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), which generates heatmaps by analyzing gradients to highlight image regions most influential to model decisions, commonly used for CNN visualization; and Permutation Feature Importance, which evaluates feature importance by randomly shuffling feature values and observing performance degradation. These methods have been applied to osteoporosis risk prediction models, identifying the clinical features or image regions most influential in model decisions, which enhances clinicians’ trust in model results and may uncover new biological insights83,84. For example, Grad-CAM analysis of the DeepDXA-Hand model reveals that the model primarily focuses on carpal bones (such as the scaphoid, lunate, hook, and triquetrum) and the head of the second metacarpal to predict BMD82. Additionally, new XAI frameworks, such as the Shadow Learner System, investigate hidden patterns and valuable insights by analyzing misclassified samples (false positives and false negatives), further enhancing the clinical interpretability and applicability of the model85. Carvalho et al. applied a SHAP-based approach to interpret the decision logic of a one-year postoperative mortality risk prediction model in orthopedic inpatients and to quantify feature contributions, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the model’s risk stratification outcomes86. Their framework—combining ML prediction with explainable AI tools—offers a valuable reference for applying ML in geriatric skeletal health, particularly in postoperative risk assessment. This methodology shares an analytical rationale similar to that used in osteoporosis fracture prevention.

However, the interpretation methods used in these models have inherent limitations, and their explanatory outcomes require further validation for fidelity and consistency. To address this gap, future studies could adopt model-agnostic statistical approaches—such as Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau correlation analyses, as proposed by Carvalho and Gavaia—to independently evaluate associations between clinical features (e.g., BMI) and disease risk87. Such methods would provide complementary validation for XAI-derived interpretations and strengthen the reliability of model conclusions.

Key challenges and ethical considerations

A critical appraisal of machine learning in osteoporosis screening must balance its significant potential against the predominantly retrospective, proof-of-concept nature of much of the current evidence and the substantial hurdles to real-world deployment. The path from research to broad clinical application is fraught with such challenges, alongside complex ethical considerations, across multiple dimensions, including data, models, clinical translation, and social equity.

Data-level challenges

High-quality data are foundational to the success of ML models. However, existing datasets in osteoporosis research present notable limitations. Public repositories such as the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) and the Canadian Multicentre Osteoporosis Study (CaMos)88 offer considerable scale and data diversity, but their volunteer cohorts may not be representative of the general population. Additionally, gold-standard annotations—such as DXA-measured BMD or confirmed fragility fractures—are often sparse, limiting the effectiveness of supervised learning approaches. Many studies also rely on proprietary datasets, raising concerns about reproducibility and generalizability. As such, transparency in reporting is essential; studies that fail to document baseline characteristics, data preprocessing methods, or ethical oversight should be interpreted with caution.

Beyond dataset limitations, heterogeneity in data sources remains a major technical barrier to model generalization. Differences in imaging equipment, scanning protocols, and reconstruction algorithms across institutions significantly reduce model portability89. To address this, standardized data acquisition and preprocessing protocols are urgently needed, along with the development of robust models that can accommodate such variability. Moreover, the high cost, time intensity, and subjectivity of annotating large volumes of medical imaging data further hinder model development.

A deeper issue involves data bias and its implications for fairness. The risk of osteoporosis varies significantly by sex, age, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status90–92, yet training datasets often focus on Caucasian subpopulations93,94. Leveraging the NHANES database, Carvalho and Gavaia’s study underscored the importance of developing ensemble learning models that integrate ethnicity-specific biomarkers and clinical data to improve predictive equity76. This lack of diversity can result in models that systematically underperform for underrepresented groups, exacerbating existing health disparities. Ensuring demographic diversity in data collection and developing tools for detecting and mitigating bias are critical steps toward algorithmic fairness. Finally, data security and privacy are paramount. Given the sensitivity of medical information, data collection, storage, and usage must comply with regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). Implementing advanced anonymization techniques and exploring privacy-preserving frameworks—such as federated learning—are essential to balancing patient privacy with the need for collaborative research.

Model-level challenges

A key challenge at the model level lies in the inherent opacity of “black-box” algorithms. Many high-performing deep learning models rely on internal decision-making processes that are not easily interpretable by humans, making it difficult for clinicians to fully trust their outputs—particularly in high-stakes clinical scenarios. To address this, explainable AI (XAI) techniques such as SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM have been developed to improve model transparency. However, these methods still face limitations in fidelity and consistency. Integrating them effectively into clinical workflows, so they function as truly reliable and interpretable decision-support tools, remains a critical area of research. Equally important are model robustness and generalizability. A model may achieve excellent performance on training data but deteriorate significantly when exposed to unfamiliar inputs, different clinical environments, or even minor data perturbations. Ensuring stable and reliable performance in real-world clinical settings is essential for practical deployment.

Current validation and evaluation practices also present significant shortcomings. It is important to distinguish between internal validation and true external validation. Internal methods, such as k-fold cross-validation, assess model stability but do not accurately reflect performance on independent, unseen data. Many studies lack rigorous external validation using datasets that differ temporally, geographically, or institutionally from the training data. This gap limits our ability to assess true generalizability and hinders fair comparisons across models. Consequently, there is an urgent need for the research community to establish standardized, publicly available benchmark datasets to promote fair evaluation and support the field’s sustainable development.

Challenges in clinical translation and application

Integrating AI screening tools into clinical practice is a complex, multifaceted process that extends beyond technical development to include workflow alignment, economic viability, and regulatory compliance. Technically, AI systems must be seamlessly integrated into existing Hospital Information Systems (HIS) and Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS), while aligning with clinical workflows to avoid adding burden to healthcare providers.

Clinician trust and acceptance are critical for successful implementation. This requires targeted training to ensure physicians understand the AI model’s mechanisms, limitations, and appropriate use. Importantly, AI tools should be positioned as decision-support aids—enhancing clinical efficiency without undermining the physician’s authority or clinical judgment.

Economically, the development, deployment, and maintenance of AI tools involve substantial investment. Therefore, robust health economic evaluations are essential to demonstrate cost-effectiveness compared to conventional methods. Such evidence is key to securing reimbursement support and driving large-scale adoption. Regulatory and legal issues present additional challenges. As Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), AI screening tools must comply with rigorous approval processes, such as those set by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European CE Marking. Moreover, legal accountability in cases where AI errors result in patient harm—whether it lies with developers, healthcare institutions, or clinicians—remains ambiguous. Establishing a clear legal and ethical framework is urgently needed to delineate responsibilities and support safe, accountable AI deployment.

Ethical considerations

The use of AI in osteoporosis screening must be firmly rooted in sound ethical principles. Patient autonomy and informed consent are foundational—individuals have the right to know whether their medical data are used for AI training or clinical decision support, and should be empowered to make informed choices after understanding the associated benefits and risks. Equity and accessibility are equally critical ethical dimensions. AI screening tools should be developed and implemented to reduce, rather than exacerbate, existing health disparities, ensuring fair access for underserved populations and communities from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds. In addition, the high sensitivity of AI systems may increase the risk of false positives95, potentially leading to unnecessary anxiety, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment. Therefore, developers and clinicians must carefully weigh the benefits of early detection against the potential harms. Ultimately, ensuring the safety, efficacy, and fairness of AI technologies is essential to promoting patient well-being and supporting ethical clinical innovation.

Future perspectives and research directions

It must be acknowledged that even the gold-standard DXA-measured BMD only accounts for 60–70% of the variation in bone strength12, implying that significant biological and environmental influences remain unaccounted for. This fundamental limitation underscores the urgent need for more diversified and powerful approaches, positioning machine learning as a critical tool for future innovation. Despite the significant promise of machine learning in osteoporosis screening, its widespread clinical adoption faces substantial challenges. Future research should focus on advancing technological maturity and real-world integration to realize its potential in enhancing early diagnosis and patient care. A central priority is the development of models that are reliable, transparent, and equitable. Cutting-edge approaches—such as federated learning, transfer learning, and self-supervised learning—offer viable solutions to data scarcity, high labeling costs, and data silos, while maintaining strict patient privacy and improving generalizability.

Next-generation models must go beyond current capabilities by integrating multi-omics data and deploying advanced data fusion algorithms to capture nonlinear relationships and synergistic patterns across diverse sources. Leveraging longitudinal follow-up data will further enable dynamic prediction of disease progression and treatment response, transforming risk assessment from a static process into a continuous, personalized decision-making tool. For ML to transition from research to routine clinical use, rigorous validation is essential. Large-scale, multicenter, prospective clinical trials are urgently needed to assess the efficacy, safety, and real-world impact of AI-based screening tools, particularly regarding hard outcomes such as fracture incidence. Retrospective studies and internal validation alone are insufficient; robust external validation on independent datasets that reflect diverse populations and healthcare settings is crucial. Emerging, technology-enabled screening paradigms also hold great promise. AI can analyze data from ubiquitous smartphones and wearable sensors—such as physical activity levels and gait characteristics—to assess associations with bone health and fall risk. These tools could enable convenient, preliminary risk screening and home-based monitoring. Integrating AI with telemedicine platforms may further expand access to high-quality screening in primary care and resource-limited regions, where local healthcare workers can operate AI tools with remote expert support—or tools may be used directly by patients—substantially broadening screening reach.

Beyond screening, the ultimate goal is to leverage AI for proactive and personalized intervention. The potential to guide individualized bone health strategies, including tailored exercise programs and nutritional recommendations, represents a significant step towards holistic patient care. This forward-looking concept is already taking shape in early research. For instance, one study demonstrated the feasibility of using AI not only to diagnose osteoporosis from clinical risk factors but also to subsequently propose personalized sports protocols based on the model’s output96. Another study specifically employed the K-NN algorithm to predict osteoporosis in elderly women and then used these predictions to prescribe suitable physical activity regimens97. These pioneering efforts illustrate a critical evolution from passive risk identification to active, data-driven health management, empowering both patients and clinicians with actionable strategies to prevent fractures before they occur.

Ultimately, realizing the full potential of ML in osteoporosis screening requires robust interdisciplinary collaboration. Engagement from clinicians, radiologists, AI researchers, biomedical engineers, data scientists, epidemiologists, health economists, ethicists, legal professionals, and patient representatives is essential to creating an end-to-end ecosystem encompassing development, validation, and deployment. Government agencies and professional societies must lead in establishing ethical guidelines, technical standards, data-sharing policies, and regulatory frameworks to ensure responsible and sustainable innovation. Encouraging the development of open, high-quality osteoporosis imaging and clinical databases will further accelerate model advancement and support the field’s continued progress.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that machine learning is reshaping the paradigm of osteoporosis screening, charting a clear trajectory from opportunistic detection using routine imaging to personalized risk assessment through multimodal data integration. However, to translate this technological potential into widespread clinical benefit, it is essential to acknowledge and bridge the multiple gaps between research and real-world practice.

Positioning this review and its core contributions

Compared with existing systematic reviews and narrative reviews on the application of AI/ML in osteoporosis, this review exhibits distinct characteristics in research perspective, technical depth, and clinical orientation, which directly inform the paradigm shift advocated herein. First, regarding research scope, most existing systematic reviews75,98 tend to aggregate the overall performance of AI models in a single technical direction, focusing on pooled metrics such as AUC or sensitivity without in-depth analysis of how model efficacy varies across specific populations (e.g., postmenopausal women vs. elderly men), imaging modalities (e.g., low-dose CT vs. conventional X-ray), or clinical settings (e.g., tertiary hospitals vs. primary care). In contrast, this review takes “clinical translation” as the core thread, systematically dissects the technical paths and applicable boundaries of ML in opportunistic screening (covering CT, conventional X-ray, and dental panoramic radiographs), precise risk stratification, and bone quality quantification, and clarifies differentiated selection strategies for diverse scenarios—all of which highlight the limitations of a one-size-fits-all “algorithm-centric” approach that dominates many prior reviews. Second, in terms of technical exploration, existing narrative reviews83,99 often elaborate on the advantages of AI (e.g., high accuracy, opportunistic screening potential) and list general challenges (e.g., data heterogeneity, interpretability), but rarely propose targeted, actionable solutions to bridge the “translational gap.” This review goes further by constructing a multimodal data fusion framework integrating radiomics, clinical data, and genomic information, and providing a practical technical package for key bottlenecks: for data heterogeneity, it proposes equipment-specific calibration and federated learning schemes; for interpretability, it integrates SHAP, Grad-CAM, and Shadow Learner System—laying the foundation for shifting from “technical feasibility” to “clinical utility.” Finally, in clinical connection, existing reviews and guidelines do not explicitly separate AI from clinical workflows but lack in-depth integration: clinical guidelines100,101 focus on treatment indications and traditional screening tools (e.g., DXA) without specifying how AI can be embedded into existing pathways; systematic reviews75,102 verify AI’s diagnostic accuracy but do not clarify how AI results should guide subsequent clinical actions (e.g., who to refer for DXA, how to adjust intervention strategies). In contrast, this review emphasizes the integration of AI into real-world clinical practice, establishing a multidimensional evaluation system covering clinical net benefit, workflow adaptability, and health economic efficiency, and delineates a clear practical pathway consisting of three sequential steps: AI-based preliminary screening to identify high-risk populations, followed by DXA testing for diagnostic validation, and culminating in personalized intervention tailored to individual risk stratification—thereby directly linking AI technology to the treatment processes recommended in clinical guidelines.

These differences from prior reviews underscore why the field is at a critical inflection point. The singular pursuit of higher AUC scores on retrospective datasets, which has dominated many existing reviews, is showing signs of “diminishing returns” and often misaligns with practical clinical needs. Building on the differentiated analysis and solution-oriented framework presented in this review, future progress necessitates a fundamental paradigm shift from a “model-centric” to a “patient-centric” focus. The standard of evaluation must evolve from a single accuracy metric to a multidimensional definition of “value,” encompassing clinical net benefit, fairness across patient subgroups, workflow integration, and health economic efficiency. The central question is no longer “Can we make the model more accurate?” but rather “Can it reliably, equitably, and efficiently improve patient outcomes in real, diverse clinical settings?”

Actionable recommendations for future work

To navigate this transition and translate algorithmic potential into clinical reality, we propose three strategic priorities for the research community:

Move Beyond Internal Metrics to Robust, Real-World Validation: The obsession with near-perfect accuracy on homogenous datasets must be replaced by a commitment to demonstrating robustness in heterogeneous, real-world settings. Future research funding and high-impact publications should prioritize studies that conduct rigorous external validation across multiple centers and diverse demographic populations. The creation of standardized, open-access benchmark datasets is an essential prerequisite for enabling fair model comparison and fostering genuine innovation over incremental metric-chasing.

Limitations of this review

The conclusions of this review should be interpreted within its methodological context. As a narrative review, it did not employ a pre-registered systematic literature search strategy, which may have led to the omission of some relevant studies. The selection and synthesis of literature were inevitably influenced by the authors’ perspective, introducing potential selection bias. Furthermore, no formal quality assessment or risk-of-bias evaluation was performed on the included studies. Therefore, this work aims to provide a strategic, critical framework and forward-looking perspective based on a synthesis of key evidence; its conclusions should be considered as comprehensive insights into the current field dynamics, offering direction for subsequent systematic reviews and primary research.

In summary, the future of machine learning in osteoporosis screening lies not in the pursuit of algorithmic perfection but in building robust, trustworthy, and valuable tools that function effectively in the complex reality of clinical care. By embracing this new clinical-value-oriented paradigm, focusing on robust validation, multidimensional assessment, and deeply integrated collaboration, we can finally harness the full potential of AI to alleviate the global burden of osteoporotic fractures.
