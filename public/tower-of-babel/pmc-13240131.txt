Fibromyalgia in the Setting of Prior Epstein–Barr Virus Infection: A Case Report
Authors: Saachi Bhattessa, Tirth Dave, Priti Modi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccurate diagnosis of fibromyalgia requires careful consideration of overlapping conditions. This case underscores the role of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) serology in patients with persistent fatigue and pain, while illustrating the challenges of diagnosing fibromyalgia amid concurrent health problems.Keywords: general medicine, infectious disease, rheumatology, virology

Introduction

Fibromyalgia is a chronic musculoskeletal pain disorder often coupled with fatigue, cognitive disturbances, psychiatric, and somatic symptoms [1]. According to the American College of Rheumatology, diagnosis of fibromyalgia is based on at least 3 months of widespread pain affecting seven or more of the nineteen designated regions, and a symptom severity score indicating that fatigue, sleep, and cognition are affected by a score of nine or more [2]. Vitally, the pain exhibited must not be explained by any other underlying cause, such as inflammation, damage, or autoimmunity. The aberrant pain seen in fibromyalgia is similar to that of other disorders, such as temporomandibular disorder and irritable bowel syndrome, establishing their comorbidity in many patients [3]. Additionally, higher levels of anxiety and depression have been noted in women, leading to a greater incidence of fibromyalgia in women [3]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, nearly 4 million adults in the United States suffer from fibromyalgia [4].

In spite of 2% of the US population having fibromyalgia, a controversy over the disease's existence persists [4, 5]. This is primarily due to the lack of objective findings demonstrated when diagnosing fibromyalgia [5]. Studies have found that individuals with fibromyalgia often have elevated IgG antibodies against Epstein–Barr virus, reflecting past EBV infection. This suggests that immune responses following EBV exposure may be associated with fibromyalgia‐related symptoms [6]. While EBV serology is not diagnostic, its detection may provide contextual support in understanding the clinical complexity of fibromyalgia and help reduce diagnostic uncertainty.

Epstein–Barr virus is a member of the herpes virus family, HHV‐4 specifically [7]. It is known to infect and remain latent in B lymphocytes for life [7]. EBV is proven to have tropism for NK, T, and epithelial cells as well [7]. Cross antigen mimicry and reactivation of EBV in times of stress have been associated in several diseases' pathogenetic mechanisms, including those of cancerous, autoimmune, or unknown etiology [7, 8]. Little is known, however, about how EBV transitions between cell types and affects uninfected cells [7]. Regardless, viral persistence has been suggested to be involved as an element in the pathogenesis of many conditions thought to be idiopathic [9]. The role of EBV infection in those with aberrant pain is not completely understood, but it may be associated with the development of fibromyalgia‐related symptoms [10].

Case History

We are reporting a 32‐year‐old female patient established at the internal medicine clinic with obesity, generalized anxiety disorder, managed with buspirone, and mixed irritable bowel syndrome. She presented with persistent fatigue and unspecified myalgia over the span of 3 months. Alongside these complaints, the patient experienced general feelings of being unwell, coughing with expectoration, and hemoptysis for the past couple of weeks. These symptoms presenting simultaneously contributed to the challenge of diagnosing fibromyalgia. Due to her respiratory symptoms, she was prescribed levofloxacin 500 mg tablet, to which she experienced the side effect of anxiety. After discontinuation of the drug, treatment was continued with bactrim DS 800 mg‐160 mg tablets.

To address the pain which did not align with the respiratory symptoms, x‐rays and MRIs of the bilateral shoulders, bilateral knees, and right foot were conducted. All imaging impressions were negative, with no significant osseous, articular, or soft tissue abnormalities identified (Figures 1 and 2). Ordered labs revealed that antinuclear antibody profiles, rheumatoid factor, and anti‐CCP antibody were all negative. C‐reactive protein was 2.1 mg/L (reference range 0–8 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 6 mm/H (reference range 0–20 mm/H). Serum interleukin 6 was 1.98 pg/mL (reference range less than 5.00 pg/mL).

After initiation of treatment with bactrim, it was reported that the patient developed drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms syndrome. This was managed sufficiently with prednisone; however, the fatigue and pain experienced by the patient were persistent and unexplained. She expressed emotional and mental distress in regard to her inconclusive understanding of her overall medical state.

The patient's evolving respiratory symptoms and eventual pneumonia confounded the clinical picture, as fatigue, myalgia, and elevated inflammatory markers are nonspecific findings. The persistence of her emphasized pain and fatigue prompted further investigation which later revealed that the patient had a serum creatine kinase of 40 U/L (reference range 29–143 U/L), WBC count of 13.1 thousand/μL (reference range 3.8–10.8 thousand/μL), and a neutrophil count of 10,231 cells/μL (reference range 1500–7800 cells/μL). The hematologic findings suggested a current infection. Serum EBV VCA IgM was less than 36.00 U/mL (less than 36.00 U/mL is negative), EBV VCA IgG was 220.00 U/mL (greater than 21.99 U/mL is positive), and EBV NA IgG was greater than 600.00 U/mL (greater than 21.99 U/mL is positive). This serologic pattern (positive EBV VCA IgG and EBV nuclear antigen IgG with negative IgM) is consistent with prior Epstein–Barr virus infection and does not indicate active or reactivated disease.

Based on clinical assessment, the patient met the 2016 American College of Rheumatology diagnostic criteria for fibromyalgia, with widespread pain involving multiple body regions for longer than 3 months, significant fatigue, nonrestorative sleep, and cognitive symptoms. The calculated Widespread Pain Index (WPI) was ≥ 7, and the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) score was ≥ 9, with no alternative diagnosis identified to explain the presentation. In light of these findings and clinical history, fibromyalgia diagnosis was made.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient's presentation of fatigue, diffuse myalgia, and respiratory symptoms prompted consideration of various infections, autoimmune diseases, and chronic conditions. Acute Epstein–Barr virus infection was excluded by negative IgM, while pneumonia explained respiratory complaints but not chronic pain. Autoimmune causes including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and polymyositis were ruled out by negative serologies, normal inflammatory markers, and unremarkable imaging. Chronic fatigue syndrome and medication side effects were considered but did not account for the persistent widespread pain. After exclusion of these conditions, fibromyalgia was determined to be the most likely diagnosis.

Conclusion and Results

Following fibromyalgia diagnosis, treatment with meloxicam 15 mg tablets, as needed, was initiated. Patient education provided sufficient reassurance. Concurrently, the patient's pneumonia progressed but was eventually managed with augmentin and azithromycin.

Follow‐up for comorbidities continued. Three months later, physical therapy was prompted to improve mobility, strength, and reduce fear of instability. Additionally, low dose sertraline improved the patient's condition. Ongoing treatment with a better understanding of the patient's overall health proved to be helpful.

Discussion

Fibromyalgia is a prevalent disorder marked by continual and widespread pain linked to significant fatigue, disrupted sleep, diminished cognitive and physical abilities, and emotional distress [11]. The exact cause of fibromyalgia remains unclear or unknown, and it frequently coexists with other rheumatic conditions like connective tissue disorders, ankylosing spondylitis, and spondyloarthritis [12, 13]. Fibromyalgia is associated with infections like hepatitis C, Lyme disease, Coxsackievirus, Parvovirus B19, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV). EBV, due to symptom similarities, has been considered a potential trigger for fibromyalgia, but as of now, a direct link has not been substantiated [6, 14]. Reshkova et al.'s [6] study revealed a correlation between elevated EBV IgG levels in the blood of fibromyalgia patients and the severity of pain and related clinical symptoms. This finding aligns with the understanding that fibromyalgia may be linked to the immune reaction against specific infectious agents such as EBV and CMV [6]. Importantly, this case does not demonstrate active Epstein–Barr virus infection; rather, it highlights the association between prior EBV exposure and fibromyalgia symptoms, without implying causation. Physical activity, psychological therapy, various approaches involving multiple disciplines, balneotherapy, massage, and pharmacotherapy including duloxetine, pregabalin, and amitriptyline are the treatment options for fibromyalgia [13].

Chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) is a persistent multisystem illness characterized by fatigue lasting at least 6 months, accompanied by several symptoms such as headaches, aching throat, muscle and joint pain, weakness, post‐exertional malaise, disrupted sleep patterns, and secondary anxiety and depression. A viral infection might trigger the onset, and psychological stress could reactivate dormant viruses like the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV). Recent reports indicate that in a subset of CFS patients, there was an increase in the expression of EBV‐induced gene 2 (EBI2) in their blood [15]. CFS often shares symptoms with fibromyalgia as both these disorders are characterized by significant fatigue, muscle pain, and disruptions in sleep patterns [16, 17]. This association, though not solely, has been linked to chronic Epstein–Barr virus infection [17]. Cognitive behavior therapy, graded exercise therapy, and pharmacotherapy including NSAIDs, duloxetine, corticosteroids, and tricyclic antidepressants are the treatment options for CFS [18].

In a study conducted by Buchwald, fifty patients who had been treated by an academic rheumatology practice and had been diagnosed with primary fibromyalgia often had symptoms more usually linked with “Chronic Epstein‐Barr Virus Infection” than with conventional signs of fibromyalgia. Recurring adenopathy (33%), persistent cough (40%), recurring rash (47%), recurrent sore throat (54%), and repeated low‐grade fevers (28%) were among these symptoms. Remarkably, a viral syndrome‐like abrupt onset of disease occurred in 55% of the patients [19]. When addressing shared symptoms in patients with a previous EBV history, it is important to consider CFS and fibromyalgia.
