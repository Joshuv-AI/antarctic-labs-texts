Trends in prevalence of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages among US young adults, 2011–2020
Authors: Rui Tang, Jaejin An, Brandon K Bellows, Andrew E Moran, Yiyi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a multistage, multisystem disorder defined by the American Heart Association (AHA). We examined recent trends in CKM stages among US young adults aged 18–39 years and assessed disparities by age, sex, and race/ethnicity.MethodsWe analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2020. Individuals were classified into CKM stages 0–4 based on metabolic, cardiovascular, and kidney risk factors.ResultsOf the 7331 participants, 2202 (30.0%) were young adults. Among young adults, 48.2% were female and 56.9% non-Hispanic White. No significant temporal trends in CKM stage prevalence from 2011–2012 to 2017–2020 were observed among young adults and adults aged ≥40 years. In 2017–2020, among young adults, 20.4% (15.4% to 26.5%) had stage 0, 34.0% (28.5% to 40.0%) had stage 1, 44.4% (39.1% to 49.8%) had stage 2, and 1.2% had stage 3 or 4 CKM. Male and non-Hispanic Black young adults were more likely to have stage 2 or higher CKM compared to female and non-Hispanic White young adults. Among young adults with stage 2 CKM, 20.8% had stage 2 hypertension, 53.5% prediabetes, 3.4% diabetes, and 47.8% hypertriglyceridemia, and 8.9% high LDL-cholesterol ≥160 mg/dL; however, only 11.1% were on anti-hypertensive medications and 2.0% were on lipid-lowering medications.ConclusionOver 45% of US young adults had stage 2 or higher CKM, with notable disparities by sex and race/ethnicity. Identification of young adults with CKM allows opportunities for early screening and tailored interventions to prevent progression to clinically significant cardiovascular or kidney disease.Keywords: CKM, prevalence, young adults, NHANES

Introduction

Cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic (CKM) diseases are closely interconnected [1], sharing overlapping pathophysiologic pathways and common risk factors such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, and obesity [2], [3]. They collectively affect over a quarter of US adults [4], and lead to substantial morbidity, mortality, and health care expenditures [5], [6]. Recognizing these shared risks, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced a novel CKM syndrome framework in 2023, categorizing individuals into five distinct CKM stages (stages 0–4), with higher stages indicating more advanced disease [6]. Previous research has characterized the prevalence and temporal trends of CKM stages in the overall US adult population, but young adults have typically been included within broader age groups rather than examined as the primary population of interest [7]. More recently, a study of 1283 young adults (mean [SD] age, 22.9 [0.7] years) from the Future of Families-Cardiovascular Health Among Young Adults demonstrated a substantial burden of CKM among young adults and found that more advanced CKM stages were associated with worse cardiovascular health and greater early arterial injury [8]. However, that study was not nationally representative and included a narrower and younger age range. Therefore, nationally representative data characterizing the prevalence, temporal trends, and potentially modifiable clinical and behavioral factors associated with CKM stages specifically among US young adults aged 18–39 years remain limited. Given the rising incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among US young adults in recent years [9], [10], it is crucial to evaluate the prevalence and trends of CKM stages within this population to inform early prevention and intervention efforts.

This study aimed to evaluate recent trends in CKM stages among US young adults and to examine potential disparities by age, sex and race/ethnicity. To achieve these objectives, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), collected from 2011–2012 through 2017–2020, were analyzed.

Methods

Study design and population

The NHANES is a series of cross-sectional surveys of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) within the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [11]. NHANES has been carried out in two-year cycles since 1999–2000. For the current study, we analyzed data from 2011-2012 through 2017–2020. NHANES 2021–2023 cycle was not included because the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) was not administered during this survey period. Data collection for the 2019–2020 cycle was paused in March 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. To maintain national representation, NCHS combined the 2019–2020 data with the 2017–2018 cycle [12].

The current analysis included adults aged 18 years or older who completed both the interview and examination components (Fig. S1). We excluded individuals who were pregnant, did not fast at least 8 to 24 h, or were missing covariates to define CKM stages and health behaviors included in the AHA's Life's Essential 8 (LE8) [13]. The NCHS Institutional Review Board approved the study protocols, and all participants gave written informed consent.

Assessments of covariates

Information on age, sex, race/ethnicity, health insurance status, family poverty index, educational status, medical history, and medication usage (anti-hypertension, anti-diabetic, and lipid-lowering medications) was obtained by standardized questionnaires. Race/ethnicity was recorded using predefined categories (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, Hispanic, and other). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with a score of 10 or higher used to identify depressive symptoms [14]. Weight, height, blood pressure, and blood glucose were measured under standardized protocols in mobile examination centers. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2). Non-high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol was obtained by subtracting HDL cholesterol from total cholesterol. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation [15].

The four health behaviors included in the LE8 (diet, physical activity, tobacco/nicotine exposure, and sleep) were analyzed [13]. Diet was evaluated using the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet score, calculated from the mean values of two nonconsecutive 24-h dietary recalls. Information on physical activity (minutes of moderate or vigorous recreational activity per week), tobacco/nicotine exposure (combustible tobacco use and secondhand smoke exposure), and sleep duration were obtained by standardized questionnaires. Each component was scored from 0 to 100 according to the AHA algorithm, and scores below 50 were considered as indicative of poor health behaviors [13]. Detailed scoring methods are provided in supplementary materials (Table S1) and previous studies [13], [16].

Assessments of CKM syndrome

Individuals were classified into five CKM stages [6] (Tables S2 and S3): stage 0, no CKM risk factors; stage 1, overweight/obesity, abdominal obesity, or prediabetes without the presence of other metabolic risk factors or CKD; stage 2, at least one of the other metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, and metabolic syndrome) or CKD; stage 3, subclinical CVD with metabolic disorders or CKD; stage 4, clinical CVD with metabolic disorders or CKD. Subclinical CVD was defined as having an estimated 10-year CVD risk ≥20% based on the Predicting Risk of CVD EVENTs (PREVENT) equations [17], or very high-risk CKD based on the kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification [6]. The PREVENT equations were developed and validated for adults aged 30–79 years; thus, adults <30 years were assigned an age of 30 years, and adults >79 years were assigned an age of 79 years when determining the 10-year CVD risk [17]. History of CVD was defined based on self-reported history of coronary heart disease, heart attack, stroke, or heart failure.

Statistical analyses

We first summarized participants' demographic, clinical, and CKM-related variables by survey cycle and by age (18–39 vs. ≥40 years). Baseline characteristics of participants included in the final analytic sample and those excluded due to missing information on CKM and LE8 were also compared using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD ≥0.10 considered a potentially meaningful difference [18]. We described continuous variables using weighted mean (SE) for normally distributed data, median (25th, 75th percentile) for skewed data, and percentage (95% CI) for categorical variables.

To examine temporal trends across survey cycles, we estimated age-standardized prevalences for each CKM stage stratified by age. Age standardization was performed using direct standardization, with each survey cycle standardized to a common reference distribution defined by the survey weighted age distribution of the combined NHANES 2011–2012 through 2017–2020 cycles; the age categories used for standardization were 18–39 (34.7%), 40–59 (37.7%), and ≥60 years (27.7%). Poisson regression models were used to assess temporal trends in the prevalence of each CKM stage, with the midpoint year of each survey cycle modeled as a continuous variable, and to assess differences in CKM stage prevalence between young adults aged 18–39 years vs. adults aged ≥40 years.

We performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the analyses of CKM stage prevalence and temporal trends by restricting the sample to young adults aged 30 to 39 years, the age range for which the PREVENT equations were derived and validated. Second, to account for potential selection bias due to complete case exclusion, we estimated each participant's probability of inclusion in the analytic sample using a survey-weighted logistic regression model adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, fasting glucose, health insurance status, family poverty index, BMI, SBP, HDL cholesterol, and survey cycle. Stabilized inverse probability weights derived from these probabilities were multiplied by the corresponding NHANES fasting subsample weights and applied to complete case participants in the sensitivity analyses.

Additionally, among young adults aged 18–39 years, we compared clinical characteristics across the five CKM stages. We also estimated the prevalence of CKM stages by sex and race/ethnicity. Young adults with stages 3 or 4 CKM were combined into one category of advanced CKM due to small numbers [7]. All estimates and models incorporated NHANES fasting subsample weights, strata, and primary sampling units to account for the complex survey design and to produce estimates representative of the US population. Relative standard error (RSE) was calculated for all estimates, and those with an RSE of ≥30% were considered as potentially unreliable and should be interpreted with caution [19]. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value <0.05.

Results

Of the 7331 participants included, 2202 (30.0%) were young adults (Table S4). Among young adults, 48.2% were female, 56.9% self-reported as non-Hispanic White (Table S5). Detailed characteristics of the study participants by survey cycle and by age were shown in Tables S4 and S5. Compared with participants included in the final analysis, those excluded because of missing CKM or LE8 information were younger and less likely to be non-Hispanic White (Table S6). They also had a more favorable profile for most cardiometabolic measures but a higher prevalence of diabetes and poorer scores for diet, nicotine exposure, and sleep health.

Between 2011–2012 and 2017–2020, no significant temporal trends in CKM stage prevalence were observed among adults aged 18–39 years and those aged ≥40 years (all P for trend >0.05; Fig. 1 and Table S7). In 2017–2020, the age-standardized prevalence among young adults aged 18–39 years was 20.4% (15.4% to 26.5%) for stage 0, 34.0% (28.5% to 40.0%) for stage 1, 44.4% (39.1% to 49.8%) for stage 2, and 1.2% (0.6% to 2.6%) for stage 3 or 4 CKM. Compared to young adults, adults aged ≥40 years were less likely to have CKM stage 0 or 1, and more likely to have CKM stage 2 or higher (all P for age difference < 0.05). In sensitivity analyses restricted to young adults aged 30 to 39 years, stage 1 CKM decreased from 40.6% in 2011–2012 to 32.1% in 2017–2020 (P for trend = 0.03), whereas stage 2 CKM increased from 41.5% to 51.4% (P for trend = 0.04). Stage 3 or 4 CKM remained uncommon across survey cycles (Table S8). In the sensitivity analysis using inverse probability weighting, age-standardized CKM stage prevalence estimates were similar to those in the primary analysis across years (Table S9).

Age-standardized prevalence of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages, by age.

Among young adults with stage 2 CKM (the category representing the majority of young adult participants), 50.5% had stage 1 or 2 hypertension, 53.5% had prediabetes, 3.4% had diabetes, 47.8% had hypertriglyceridemia, and 8.9% had high LDL-cholesterol ≥160 mg/dL (Table 1). However, only 11.1% were taking anti-hypertensive medications, 1.6% were taking antidiabetic medications, and 2.0% were on lipid-lowering medications. The prevalence of poor LE8 health behaviors (score < 50) was 63.6% for diet, 45.6% for physical activity, 29.8% for nicotine exposure, and 14.1% for sleep health. Compared with young adult females, young adult males had a higher prevalence of stage 2 CKM (45.4% vs 38.0%; P < 0.05), and a lower prevalence of stage 0 CKM (15.7% vs 25.2%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2 and Table S10). Non-Hispanic Black young adults were more likely to have stage 2 CKM or higher, whereas non-Hispanic Asians were more likely to have stage 0 CKM.

Discussion

No significant temporal trends in CKM stage prevalence from 2011–2012 to 2017–2020 were observed among both young adults aged 18–39 years and those aged ≥40 years. Among young adults, stage 2 CKM was the most prevalent (44.4%), whereas stages 3 and 4 were relatively uncommon (<2%). Among young adults with stage 2 CKM, over half had hypertension, prediabetes or diabetes, and dyslipidemia; however, only a small proportion were receiving antihypertensive, antidiabetic, or lipid-lowering therapy. Additionally, young adult males and non-Hispanic Black young adults were more likely to have stage 2 or higher CKM compared to females and non-Hispanic White individuals. These findings underscore the importance of early screening and tailored interventions, particularly among young adult males and non-Hispanic Black individuals, to prevent progression to clinically significant cardiovascular or kidney disease later in life.

The CKM staging framework reflects the progression from excess or dysfunctional adiposity and metabolic risk factors to subclinical and clinical CVD. The 2026 AHA/ACC/ADA/ASN CKM guideline recommends CKM staging in both youth and adults to facilitate early identification of CKM risk factors and prevent progression to more advanced stages [20]. This framework is particularly relevant in young adults, among whom clinical CVD is uncommon, but excess or dysfunctional adiposity and metabolic risk factors are often already present. Identifying individuals at stages 1 or 2 CKM therefore provides an opportunity to address the upstream drivers of CKM health earlier in the life course to prevent progression to subclinical and clinical CVD.

The current study found that about 80% of US young adults met the criteria for CKM syndrome (stage 1 or higher). However, the prevalence of CKM syndrome did not trend downward between 2011 and 2020, consistent with the stagnation observed in previous studies [7], [21]. This persistent burden may reflect opposing trends among individual risk factors, where modest improvement in smoking reduction and lipid control appear to be offset by rising rates of overweight/obesity and prediabetes. Notably, our study found that approximately 45% of young adults had stage 2 CKM, and among these individuals, more than half had hypertension, prediabetes or diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Additionally, the prevalence of poor LE8 health behaviors increased with higher CKM stages, which is consistent with a recent binational study of US and South Korean adults showing that higher LE8 scores, including health behavior scores, were associated with lower CKM prevalence, with the protective association between overall LE8 and CKM particularly pronounced among adults aged 30–44 years [22].

The observed disparities in CKM prevalence by sex and race/ethnicity highlight important targets for clinical and public health interventions. The higher burden of more advanced CKM stages among young adult males may reflect sex-specific biological pathways (e.g., differences in adiposity distribution and hormonal influences compared with young adult females), as well as variations in health behaviors (e.g., comparatively unhealthful dietary patterns, fewer healthcare services interactions) [23], [24], [25]. Similarly, the higher prevalence of more advanced CKM stages among non-Hispanic Black young adults may be rooted in structural inequities (e.g., disparities in healthcare access, socioeconomic resources, and psychosocial stress) [26]. Clinically, these findings emphasize the need for sex- and race/ethnicity-conscious screening protocols and tailored interventions of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and prediabetes [27], [28]. Public health efforts should prioritize culturally tailored interventions, equitable access to preventive care, and policies addressing upstream social determinants to reduce disparities and prevent CKM syndrome progression in high-risk populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, history of CVD and lifestyle factors such as diet and sleep data were self-reported, which may lead to misclassification and recall bias. Second, due to limited sample sizes, estimates for stages 3 or 4 CKM among young adults may not be reliable and need to be interpreted with caution. Third, key diagnostic data necessary to accurately identify advanced CKM stages, such as coronary artery calcification and atrial fibrillation, were not available in NHANES, which may lead to an underestimation of stages 3 and 4 CKM prevalence. Fourth, exclusions because of missing CKM or LE8 information could introduce selection bias. However, sensitivity analysis applying inverse probability of selection weights yielded CKM stage prevalence estimates and temporal patterns similar to those from the primary analysis. Finally, temporal trend analyses were based on only four NHANES survey periods, including the combined 2017 to 2020 cycle, which limited statistical power to detect changes in CKM stage prevalence over time.

Conclusion

No significant temporal trends in CKM stage prevalence from 2011–2012 to 2017–2020 were observed among both young adults and those aged ≥40 years. Among young adults, about 80% met criteria for CKM stage 1 or higher, with stage 2 CKM being the most prevalent. Notable disparities were observed among young adult men and non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic individuals. These findings underscore the need for early screening and targeted interventions to prevent CKM progression and reduce health disparities among young adults.
