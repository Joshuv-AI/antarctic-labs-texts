ANaGRAM: A Natural Gradient Relative to Adapted Model for efficient PINNs learning
Nilo Schwencke, Cyril Furtlehner

Abstract
In the recent years, Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have received strong interest as a method to solve PDE driven systems, in particular for data assimilation purpose. This method is still in its infancy, with many shortcomings and failures that remain not properly understood. In this paper we propose a natural gradient approach to PINNs which contributes to speed-up and improve the accuracy of the training. Based on an in depth analysis of the differential geometric structures of the problem, we come up with two distinct contributions: (i) a new natural gradient algorithm that scales as min ⁡ ( P 2 ​ S , S 2 ​ P ) \min(P^{2}S,S^{2}P) , where P P is the number of parameters, and S S the batch size; (ii) a mathematically principled reformulation of the PINNs problem that allows the extension of natural gradient to it, with proved connections to Green’s function theory.

Contributions:

Müller and Zeinhofer 2024

(i)

Position of the problem

2.1
Neural Networks and parametric model

Our starting point is the following functional definition of parametric models, of which neural networks are a non-linear special case:

Definition 1

(Parametric model)
.

Given a domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
and a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
consisting of functions
\Omega\to\mathbb{K}^{m}
, a parametric model is a differentiable functional:

u:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{P}&\to&\mathcal{H}\\
{\bm{\theta}}&\mapsto&\big({\bm{x}}\in\Omega\mapsto u({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\big)\end{array}\right..

(1)

To prevent confusion, we will write
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}})
instead of
u\big({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}\big)
,
\textrm{for all }{\bm{x}}\in\Omega
.

Since a parametric model is differentiable by definition, we can define its differential:

Definition 2

(Differential of a parametric model)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathcal{H}
be a parametric model and
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
. Then the differential of the parametric model
u
in the parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
is:

\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{P}&\to&\mathcal{H}\\
{\bm{h}}&\mapsto&\sum_{p=1}^{P}{\bm{h}}_{p}\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{p}}\end{array}\right..

(2)

To simplify notations, we will write
\textrm{for all }1\leq p\leq P
and
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
,
\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
, instead of
\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{p}}
.

Given a parametric model
u
, we can define the following two objects of interest:

The image set of
u

: this is the set of functions reached by
u
,
i.e.
:

\mathcal{M}:=\Ima u:=\left\{u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,:\,{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}.

(3)

Although not strictly rigorous
1
1

1

In particular, because
u
may not be injective.
,
\mathcal{M}
is often considered in deep-learning as a differential submanifold of
\mathcal{H}
, so we will keep this analogy in mind for pedagogical purposes.

The tangent space of
u
at
{\bm{\theta}}

: this is the image set of the differential of
u
at
{\bm{\theta}}
,
i.e.
the linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of the functions reached by
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
,
i.e.
:

T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}:=\Ima\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}=\Span\big(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,:1\leq p\leq P\big)\subset\mathcal{H}.

(4)

Once again, this definition is made with reference to differential geometry.

We give several examples of Parametric models in

B
. We now introduce PINNs.

2.2
Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

As in
1
, let us consider a domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with a probability measure
\mu
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
,
\partial\Omega
its boundary endowed with a probability measure
\sigma
, and
\mathcal{H}
a Hilbert space composed of functions
\Omega\to\mathbb{K}^{m}
. Then let us consider two functional (
inter alia
differential) operators:

\displaystyle D:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{H}&\to&\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)\\
u&\mapsto&D[u]\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle B:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{H}&\to&\textrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\sigma)\\
u&\mapsto&B[u]\end{array}\right.,

that we will assume to be differentiable
2
2

2

It can be shown that, if
D
and
B
are defined and differentiable on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega\to\mathbb{K}^{m})
then such a
\mathcal{H}
always exists;
cf.
chapter 12 of
Berezansky et al. 1996
.
. We can then consider the functional equation (
inter alia

\begin{cases}D(u)=f\in\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)&\mathrm{in}\,\Omega\\
B(u)=g\in\textrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\sigma)&\mathrm{on}\,\partial\Omega\end{cases}.

(9)

The PINNs framework, as introduced by
Raissi et al. 2019a
consists then in approximating a solution to the PDE by making the ansatz
u=u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
, with
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
a neural network, sampling points
(x^{D}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq S_{D}}
in
\Omega
according to
\mu
,
(x^{B}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq S_{B}}
in
\partial\Omega
according to
\sigma
and then to optimize the loss:

\ell({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{2S_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{S_{D}}\left(D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{D}_{i})-f(x^{D}_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2S_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{S_{B}}\left(B[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{B}_{i})-g(x^{B}_{i})\right)^{2},

(10)

by classical gradient descent techniques, used in the context of deep learning, such as Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
, or L-BFGS
(
Liu and Nocedal 1989
)
. One of the cornerstones of
Raissi et al. 2019a
is also to use automatic differentiation
(
Baydin et al. 2018
)
to compute the operators
D
and
B
, thus obtaining quasi-exact calculations, whereas most classic techniques require either approximating operators with Finite Differences, or carrying out the calculations manually with Finite Elements.

Although appealing due to its simplicity and relative ease of implementation, this approach suffers from several well-documented empirical pathologies
(
Krishnapriyan et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Grossmann et al. 2024
)
, which can be understood as an ill conditioned problem
(
De Ryck et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
and for which several
ad hoc

(
Karnakov et al. 2022
;
Zeng et al. 2022
;
McClenny and
Braga-Neto 2022
)
.
Following
Müller and Zeinhofer 2024
, we argue in this work that the key point is rather to theoretically understand the geometry of the problem and adapt PINNs training accordingly.

2.3
Natural Gradient

Natural gradient has been introduced, in the context of Information Geometry by
Amari and Douglas 1998
. Given a loss:
\ell:{\bm{\theta}}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the gradient descent:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\,\nabla\ell,\vskip-2.84544pt

(11)

is replaced by the update:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\,F_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\nabla\ell,\vskip-2.84544pt

(12)

with
F_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
being the Gram-Matrix associated to a Fisher-Rao information metric
(
Amari 2016
)
or equivalently, the Hessian of some Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Kullback and Leibler 1951
)
, and
\dagger
the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse.
This notion has been later further extended to the more abstract setting of Riemannian metrics in the context of neural-networks by
Ollivier 2015
. In this case, given a Riemannian-(pseudo) metric
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}
, the gradient-descent update is replaced by:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta G_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\nabla\ell,\vskip-2.84544pt

(13)

where
{G_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}_{p,q}:=\mathcal{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}},\partial_{q}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}})
is the Gram matrix of partial derivatives relative to
\mathcal{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
.
Despite its mathematically principled advantage, natural gradient suffers from its computational cost, which makes it prohibitive, if not untractable for real world applications. Indeed:

•

Computation of the Gram matrix
G_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
is at least quadratic in the number of parameters.

•

Inversion of
G_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
is cubic in the number of parameters.

Different approaches have been proposed to circumvent this limitations. The most prominent one is K-FAC introduced by
Heskes 2000
and further extended by
Martens and Grosse 2015
;
Grosse and
Martens 2016
, which approximates the Gram matrix by block-diagonal matrices. This approximation can be understood as making the ansatz that the partial derivatives of weights belonging to different layers are orthogonal. A refinement of this method has been proposed by
George et al. 2018
, in which the eigen-structure of the block-diagonal matrices are carefully taken into account in order to provide a better approximation of the diagonal rescaling induced by the inversion of the Gram matrix. This method has been extented to PINNs by
Dangel et al. 2025
. In a completely different vein,
Ollivier 2017
has proposed a statistical approach that has been proved to converge to the natural gradient update in the
0
learning rate limit.

To conclude this section, let us give a more geometric interpretation of natural gradient. To this end, let us consider the standard quadratic regression problem :

\ell({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{2S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\left(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right)^{2},

(14)

with
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
a parametric model, for instance a neural-network,
(x_{i})
sampled from some probability measure
\mu
on some domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
In the limit
S\to\infty
(population limit), this loss can be reinterpreted as the evaluation at
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
of the functional loss:

\mathcal{L}:v\in\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mu)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|v-f\right\|_{\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mu)}^{2}.

(15)

Taking the Fréchet derivative, one gets:
\textrm{for all }v,h\in\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mu)

\textrm{d}\mathcal{L}|_{v}(h)=\left\langle v-f\,,\,h\right\rangle_{\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mu)},

(16)

i.e.
the functional gradient of
\mathcal{L}
is
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|v}:=v-f
. As noted for instance in
Verbockhaven et al. 2024
, Natural gradient has then to be interpreted from the functional point of view as the projection of
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}}
onto the tangent space
T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}
with respect to the
\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
metric.
However, this functional update must be converted into a parameter space update. Since the parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{P}
is somehow identified with
T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}

via
the differential application
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
, it would be sufficient to take the inverse of this application to obtain the parametric update. In general
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
is not invertible but at least it admits a pseudo-inverse
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}^{\dagger}
. Moreover, since
T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}=\Ima\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
by definition,
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}^{\dagger}
is defined on all
T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}
. Thus,
we have that the natural gradient in the population limits corresponds to the update:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\,\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\left(\Pi^{\bot}_{T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}\right)\right).

(17)

Note that the use of the pseudo-inverse implies that the update in the parameter space happens in the subspace
(\Ker\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}})^{\bot}\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
.

3
Empirical Natural Gradient and ANaGRAM

In practice, one cannot reach the population limit and thus
Equation

17
is only an asymptotic update. Nevertheless, we can derive a more accurate update, when we can rely only on a finite set of points
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{S}
that is usually called a batch. Following
Jacot et al. 2018
, we know that standard quadratic gradient descent update with respect to a batch in the vanishing learning rate limit
\eta\to 0
, rewrites in the functional space as:

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}{\textrm{d}t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{S}{NTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})\left(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})-y_{i}\right),

\displaystyle{NTK}_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y):=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\right)\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y)\right)^{t}.

(18)

Furthermore,
Rudner et al. 2019
;
Bai et al. 2022
show that under natural gradient descent, the
Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
should be replaced in
Equation

18
by the
Natural NTK (NNTK)
:

\displaystyle{NNTK}_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\right){G_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}}_{pq}\left(\partial_{q}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y)\right)^{t},

\displaystyle{G_{{\bm{\theta}}}}_{p,q}

\displaystyle:=\left\langle\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,,\,\partial_{q}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(19)

As a consequence, one may see that the update under natural gradient descent with respect to a batch
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{S}
happens in a subspace of the tangent space, namely the
empirical Tangent Space
:

\widehat{T}^{NNTK}_{{\bm{\theta}},(x_{i})}\mathcal{M}:=\Span({NNTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x_{i})\,:\,(x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq S})\subset T_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{M}.

(20)

Subsequently,
Equation

17
can then be adapted to define the
empirical Natural Gradient update
:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\,\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\left(\Pi^{\bot}_{\widehat{T}^{NNTK}_{{\bm{\theta}},(x_{i})}\mathcal{M}}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}\right)\right).

(21)

Note that this update can be understood from the functional perspective as the standard Nyström method
(
Sun et al. 2015
)
, bridging the gap between our work and the many methods developed in this field. Nevertheless, the
{NNTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}}
kernel cannot be computed explicitly in our case, since it requires
a priori
inverting the Gram matrix, which adds further challenge. With this in mind, we present a first result, encapsulated in the following theorem, which is one of our main contributions:

Theorem 1

(ANaGRAM)
.

Let us define
\textrm{for all }1\leq i\leq S
and
\textrm{for all }1\leq p\leq P
:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}$}_{i,p}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x_{i})\,;

\displaystyle\mbox{$\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}}$}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}}(x_{i})=u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x_{i})-f(x_{i}).

Then:

\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}^{\dagger}\left(\Pi^{\bot}_{\widehat{T}^{NNTK}_{{\bm{\theta}},(x_{i})}\mathcal{M}}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}}\right)=\left(\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}+E_{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{metric}}\right)\left(\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}}+E^{\bot}_{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(22)

where
E_{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{metric}}
and
E^{\bot}_{\bm{\theta}}
are correction terms specified in

61

3

3

orthogonal to the whole tangent space
T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}
.
of the functional gradient.

4
ANaGRAM for PINNs

Generalizing ANaGRAM to PINNs only requires to change the problem perspective.

4.1
PINNs as a least-square regression problem

(D,B)\circ u:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllll}\mathbb{R}^{P}&\to&\mathcal{H}&\to&\mathbf{\textrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega,\partial\Omega\right)}:=\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)\times\textrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\sigma)\\

4.2
PINNs Natural Gradient is a Green’s Function

Knowing the Green’s function of a linear operator is one of the most optimal ways of solving the associated PDE, since it then suffices to estimate an integral to approximate a solution
(
Duffy 2015
)
. However, this requires prior knowledge of the Green’s function, which is not always possible. Here, we show that using the natural gradient for PINNs implicitly uses the operator’s Green’s function. In
Appendix

D
, we briefly recall the main definitions required to state and prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

Let
D:\mathcal{H}\to\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)
be a linear differential operator and
u:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathcal{H}
a parametric model. Then
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
, the generalized Green’s function of
D
on
T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}=\Ima\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
is given by:
\textrm{for all }x,y\in\Omega

g_{T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}}(x,y):=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\,G^{\dagger}_{p,q}\partial_{q}D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](y),

(25)

with:
\textrm{for all }1\leq p,q\leq P

G_{pq}:=\left\langle\partial_{p}D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}]\,,\,\partial_{q}D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}]\right\rangle_{\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)}.

(26)

In particular, the natural gradient of PINNs
4
4

4

cf.
last line of
Table

17
in
Section

C.4
.
can be rewritten:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\,\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\left(x\in\Omega\mapsto\int_{\Omega}g_{T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}}(x,y)\nabla\mathcal{L}_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(y)\mu(\textrm{d}y)\right),

(27)

A few comments should be made about
Equation

27
. First, if
\eta=1
, then the natural gradient can be understood as the least-square’s solution of
D[u]=f
at order 1,
i.e.
in the affine space
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}+T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}
. However, it does not hold
a priori
that:

•

D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}+T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}]
correctly approximates
f\in\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)
.

•

u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}+T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}
correctly approximates the image space
\mathcal{M}=\left\{u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,:\,{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}\right\}
.

Multiplying by a learning rate
\eta\ll 1
is then essential. In this way, natural gradient can be understood as moving in the direction of the solution of
D[u]=f
in the affine space
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}+T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}
, and thus getting closer to the solution, while expecting that the change induced by this update will improve the approximation space
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}}+T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}}\mathcal{M}
5
5

5

To our best knowledge, rigorous proof of this phenomenon has not yet been provided. We can therefore only rely on empirical evidence, which is illustrated in
Section

5
.
. On the other hand, when we approach the end of the optimization,
i.e.
when the space
D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}+T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}]
approximates
f
“well enough”,
while
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
approximates “well enough”
\mathcal{M}
, then it is in our best interest to solve the equation completely,
i.e.
to take learning rates
\eta
close to 1. This is why the use of line search in ANaGRAM (
cf.
line
2
in Algorithm
2
) is essential. We should then conclude that the quality of the solution found by the parametric model
u

depends only
on:

•

How well
\Gamma=\{D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}]\,:\,{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}\}
can approximate the source
f\in\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)
.

•

The curvature of
\Gamma
. More precisely, if its non-linear structure induces convergence to a
D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}]
such that
f-D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}]
is non-negligible, while being orthogonal to the tangent space
D[T_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\mathcal{M}]
.

If we assume now that
D
is also nonlinear, then all the above analysis also holds for the linear operators
\textrm{d}D_{|u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}
, the difference being that the operator changes at each step. This means that in the case of non-linear operators, we have to deal with both the non-linearity of
D
and
u
, but that does not change the overall dynamic.

Finally, assuming that both
D
and
u
are linear (this is for instance the case when we assume
u
to be a linear combination of basis functions, like in Finite Elements, or Fourier Series). Then “learning”
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
with natural gradient (and learning rate
1
) corresponds to solve the equation in the least-squares sense with a generalized Green’s function.

Results summary

:
We demonstrated that our approach can achieve comparable accuracy to
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
on linear problems, consistent with the equivalence established in
Appendix

E
, while maintaining a per-step computational cost at most reasonably higher than that of Adam. Excluding Adam and GD, which consistently get stuck at high error levels, the bottom line is that ANaGRAM consistently outperforms both E-NGD and L-BFGS—often by a significant margin—on at least one or even both criteria: precision and computation time. The cases where the computation times of E-NGD and ANaGRAM are similar occur when small-sized architectures are sufficient for the problem.

6
Conclusion and Perspectives

We introduce empirical Natural Gradient, a new kind of natural gradient that scales linearly with respect to the number of parameters and extend it to PINNs framework through a mathematically principled reformulation. We show that this update implicitly corresponds to the use of the Green’s function of the operator.
We give empirical evidences that this optimization in its simplest form (vanilla ANaGRAM) already achieves highly accurate solutions, comparable to
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
for linear PDEs at a fraction of the computational cost, and with significant improvements for non-linear equations, for which equivalence of the two algorithms does not hold anymore.

Still, the present formulation of the algorithm has two limitations: one concerns the chosing procedure of the batch points, which is so far limited to simple heuristics; the second is the hyperparameter tunning, more specifically the cutoff factor, which is so far chosen by hand, while it may probably be automatically chosen based on the spectrum of
\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}
.

Important perspectives include
exploring approximations schemes for terms
E_{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{metric}}
(
e.g.
using Nyström’s methods,
cf.

Sun et al. 2015
) and
E^{\bot}_{\bm{\theta}}
(
e.g.
using
Cohen and Migliorati 2017
), introduced in
1
,
the design of an optimal collocation points procedure, coupled with SVD cut-off factor adaptation strategy for ANaGRAM, as well as incorporation of common optimization techniques, such as momentum. From a theoretical point of view, it seems particularly important to us to include data assimilation in this theoretical setting, and understand its regularizing effect, while establishing connections to classical solvers such as FEMs.

B.1
Partial Fourier’s series

Let us fix a dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We then define the
N
-partial Fourier’s Serie in
[0,1]^{d}
as:

S_{N}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}&\to&\textrm{L}^{2}([0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{C})\\
\left(\alpha_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}}\right)&\mapsto&\left(x\in[0,1]^{d}\mapsto\sum\limits_{k_{1}=-N}^{N}\cdots\sum\limits_{k_{d}=-N}^{N}\alpha_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}}e^{2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}x_{l}\right)}\right)\end{array}\right..

(33)

We see that
\textrm{for all }k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}
and
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}
,
\partial_{k}{S_{N}}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}=\left(x\in\Omega\mapsto e^{2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}x_{l}\right)}\right)
.
As a consequence:
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}

\textrm{d}{S_{N}}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}=S_{N},

an thus:
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}

\mathcal{M}=T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}=\Span\left(x\in[0,1]^{d}\mapsto e^{2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}x_{l}\right)}\,:\,k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}\right)

(34)

This precisely means that
S_{N}
is a linear parametric model.

B.2
Multilayer perceptron

Historically, Multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) were the first neural network models to be proposed
[
Rosenblatt 1958
]
. Without going into an unnecessarily formal description, we will define MLPs of depth
L\in\mathbb{N}
as a function
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by induction:

Initialization (Input Layer)

:
n_{0}=n
, and

\mathbf{a}^{(0)}:=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}

Inductive Step (Hidden Layers)

:
\textrm{for all }1\leq l\leq L-1,\,n_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma^{(l)}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{(l)}

\displaystyle:=\underbracket{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}_{\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l},n_{l-1}}}\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}+\underbracket{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}_{\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}}\,;\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{(l)}

\displaystyle:=\underbracket{\sigma^{(l)}}_{\textrm{componentwise}}(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}),

Final Step (Output Layer)

:

f_{\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)},\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)_{l=1}^{L}}(x):=\underbracket{\mathbf{W}^{(L)}}_{\in\mathbb{R}^{m,n_{L-1}}}\mathbf{a}^{(L-1)}+\underbracket{\mathbf{b}^{(L)}}_{\in\mathbb{R}^{m}},

(35)

Equipped with this definition, we define a parametric model
u
associated to
f_{\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)},\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)_{l=1}^{L}}
by considering any differentiable parametrization
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\Pi_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{R}^{w_{l-1}\times w_{l}}\times\mathbb{R}^{w_{l}}
, and then defining:

u:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{P}&\to&\mathcal{H}\\
{\bm{\theta}}&\mapsto&f_{\varphi({\bm{\theta}})}\end{array}\right.,

(36)

i.e.
using
\varphi
to encode the coefficients of the weights
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}
and biases
\mathbf{b}^{(l)}
in the coordinates of a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{P}
. Note that if
\varphi
is bijective, then
P=\sum_{l=1}^{L}(w_{l-1}+1)w_{l}
.

Remark 2
.

A parametric model
u
associated to an MLP, as defined in
Equation

36
, is not linear,
if activations
(\sigma^{(l)})_{1\leq l\leq L}
are not, and
L\geq 2
(yielding the qualifier “deep” in “deep learning”). Nevertheless,
if
\varphi
is linear, we may note that
u
is still linear with respect to the parameters associated to weight
\mathbf{W}^{(L)}
and bias
\mathbf{b}^{(L)}
. In particular,
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
,
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\in\Ima\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}
.

,

To this end, we need to review the notions of Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) in

C.1
Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)

Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
has been introduced by
Jacot et al. 2018
as a fundamental tool connecting neural networks to kernel methods, another very popular tool in Machine-learning
[
Schölkopf et al. 2002
]
. More precisely, it shows that for an empirical quadratic loss:

\ell({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{2S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\left(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right)^{2},

(
14
)

the gradient descent:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\,\nabla\ell,

(
11
)

can be reinterpreted in the functional space, in the limit
\eta\to 0
, as the
the differential equation in
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\textrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\right)
, with the initial data
u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}=u_{0}\in\textrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\right)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}{\textrm{d}t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{S}{NTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})\left(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})-y_{i}\right),

\displaystyle{NTK}_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y):=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\right)\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y)\right)^{t}.

(
18
)

By the same observations as for
Equation

17
, we observe that
Equation

18
induces the following differential equation in
{\bm{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{P}
, with the initial data
{\bm{\theta}}(0)={\bm{\theta}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
:

\frac{\textrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}}{\textrm{d}t}=\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{NTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})-y_{i})\right)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\Big({NTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})\Big)(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})-y_{i}),

(37)

Using Euler’s approximation method, this can of course be rewritten as the discrete upate:

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\Big({NTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})\Big)(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})-y_{i}),

(38)

Note that if we assume
\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
to be inversible, we can
implicitly
compute the inverse, yielding:

\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{\dagger}\Big({NTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})\Big)=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})\,\mbox{${\bm{e}}^{(p)}$},

(39)

making
Equation

38
effectively correspond to the usual gradient descent in
Equation

11
.

Rudner et al. 2019
further extended this framework to the case of natural gradient descent in the context of information geometry. They demonstrate that the learning dynamic in this scenario is governed by a new kernel, named the
Natural Neural Tangent Kernel (NNTK)
, which is defined as:
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}

{NNTK}_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y):=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\right){F_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}}_{pq}(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y))^{t},

(40)

where
F_{\bm{\theta}}
is the Fisher information matrix. In the more general context of Riemannian Geometry,
Bai et al. 2022
show that the NNTK is given by:
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}

{NNTK}_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y):=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\right){G_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}}_{pq}(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y))^{t},

(41)

with
G_{\bm{\theta}}
being the Gram matrix relative to a Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}
as introduced in
Section

2.3
:
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
,
\textrm{for all }1\leq p,q\leq P

{G_{{\bm{\theta}}}}_{p,q}:=\mathcal{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}},\partial_{q}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}).

(42)

In particular, when
\mathcal{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}
is given by the metric of an ambient Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, this yields:
\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
,
\textrm{for all }1\leq p,q\leq P
,
\textrm{for all }x,y\in\Omega

\displaystyle{NNTK}_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\left(\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x)\right){G_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}}_{pq}\left(\partial_{q}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y)\right)^{t},

\displaystyle{G_{{\bm{\theta}}}}_{p,q}

\displaystyle:=\left\langle\partial_{p}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,,\,\partial_{q}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(
19
)

For the quadratic problem of
Equation

14
,
natural gradient then yields the functional dynamics:

\frac{\textrm{d}u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}{\textrm{d}t}(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{NNTK}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x,x_{i})(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}(x_{i})-y_{i}).

(43)

In the following, we will further explore the (N)NTK and its connection to the natural gradient in the context of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) theory.

C.2
A perspective on Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS)

In this subsection we will carefully review the intimate link between neural tangent kernels, projections and reproducing kernels.
To begin, let us define what a kernel function is, following
Paulsen and
Raghupathi 2016
:

Definition 3
.

A function
k:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{K}
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
is called a
kernel function
provided that:
\textrm{for all }N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\textrm{for all }(x_{i})\in\Omega^{N}
,
\textrm{for all }\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{N}

\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\alpha_{i}k(x_{i},x_{j})\overline{\alpha}_{j}\geq 0

(44)

Equipped with
3
, we can state the following theorem, which binds different perspectives on RKHS:

Theorem 3
.

An Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
consisting of functions
\Omega\to\mathbb{K}
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, is a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space if and only if one of the following equivalent conditions are met:

1.

There is a kernel
k:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{H}=\overline{\Span\left(k(x,\cdot):x\in\Omega\right)}
and:
\textrm{for all }x,y\in\Omega

\left\langle k(x,\cdot)\,,\,k(y,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=k(x,y).

(45)

In particular, we have the reproducing property:
\textrm{for all }v\in\mathcal{H}
,
\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega

v(x)=\left\langle k(x,\cdot)\,,\,v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(46)

2.

For all
x\in\Omega
, the evaluation form
e_{x}:f\in\mathcal{H}\mapsto f(x)
is continuous.

A proof of this theorem can be found in
Paulsen and
Raghupathi 2016
.
We now draw some easy but essential consequences from
3
:

Corollary 1
.

Any finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a RKHS

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional, all norms are equivalent. In particular
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}:f\in\mathcal{H}\mapsto\sup_{x\in\Omega}|f(x)|
is equivalent to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
. Then by point 2 in
3
,
\mathcal{H}
is a RKHS, since
\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega
,
e_{x}
is continuous for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
.
∎

In order to set out an important theorem that highlights the link between RKHS and projections, we need the following definition:

Definition 4
.

A linear operator
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is an integral operator given that there is
k:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{K}
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, such that:
\textrm{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}
,
\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega

A\big(f\big)(x)=\left\langle k(x,\cdot)\,,\,f\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(47)

We can now state:

Theorem 4
.

If
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subset\mathcal{H}
is a RKHS, then the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}
is an integral operator whose kernel is the reproducing kernel
k
of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, i.e. :
\textrm{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}
,
\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega

\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\big(f\big)(x)=\left\langle k(x,\cdot)\,,\,f\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(48)

In addition,
k
is given by:
\textrm{for all }x,y\in\Omega

k(x,y)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}L_{i}(x)L_{i}(y)

(49)

where
(L_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is any orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Proof.

The second claim is essentially equivalent to

Paulsen and
Raghupathi 2016
. To prove the first claim, let us notice that the reproducing property:
\textrm{for all }v\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega

v(x)=\left\langle k(x,\cdot)\,,\,v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

(50)

exactly means that the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is an integral operator whose kernel is the reproducing kernel
k
of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Since the restriction of
{\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}}
to
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, it is sufficient to conclude the proof to show that:
\textrm{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\bot}
,
\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega

\left\langle k(x,\cdot)\,,\,f\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0.

(51)

But this is an immediate consequence of the second claim.
∎

Remark 3
.

4
encapsulates finite dimensional case, since one may take
L_{i}=0
for
i
greater than
D\in\mathbb{N}
, yielding
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{0})\leq D
, which implies in particular that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is an RKHS by
1
.

Remark 4
.

The assumption
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is an RKHS is essential, since there is no guaranty that such a space (finite dimension aside) is indeed a RKHS. One may think for instance to the case where the
(L_{i})
are the Fourier’s polynomials defined in
Section

B.1
. In this case the associated kernel is the Dirichlet kernel, which is well known to be non convergent, neither pointwise, nor in
\textrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
.

4
prompts the question of how to construct such an orthogonal basis
(L_{i})
. Assume that we already have a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\overline{\Span(u_{p}:p\in\mathbb{N})}\subset\mathcal{H}
.
While a Gram-Schmidt procedure could be used, there is another approach that, in a certain sense, is far more optimal. For the sake of simplicity, let us use suppose that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is finite dimensional
6
6

6

The infinite-dimensional case is more technical, as we have to be careful with the continuity of linear applications. As we are only considering a finitely-parameterized model, this is beyond the scope of our present work.
. Then:

Lemma 1
.

Let us be
\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\Span(u_{p}:1\leq p\leq P)\subset\mathcal{H}
and consider the Gram matrix
G_{pq}:=\left\langle u_{p}\,,\,u_{q}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
of
(u_{p})
and its eigen-decomposition
G=U\Delta^{2}U^{t}
. Then:

L_{p}:=\sum_{1\leq q\leq P}u_{q}U_{q,p}\Delta^{\dagger}_{p},

(52)

is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
In particular,
\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
is an integral operator whose kernel is:

k(x,y)=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}u_{p}(x)G^{\dagger}_{p,q}u_{q}(y).

(53)

Furthermore
L_{p}
are the left-singular vector of the so-called
synthesis
operator
7
7

7

Name and notation are taken from
Adcock and Huybrechs 2019
.
:

\mathcal{T}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{P}&\to&\mathcal{H}_{0}\\
\alpha&\mapsto&\sum\limits_{1\leq p\leq P}\alpha_{p}u_{p}\end{array}\right..

(54)

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is generated by the finite frame
(u_{p})_{1\leq p\leq P}
, it is an RKHS by
1
and there exist (for instance by the Gram-Schmidt procedure), an orthonormal basis
(V_{p})_{1\leq p\leq P}
of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Then by
4
, the operator
\Pi
defined by:
\textrm{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}

\Pi(f):=\sum_{1\leq p\leq P}V_{p}\left\langle f\,,\,V_{p}\right\rangle

(55)

is the orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. But the basis
(L_{p})_{\leq p\leq P}
of
Equation

52
is precisely orthonormal. Indeed:

\displaystyle\left\langle L_{p}\,,\,L_{q}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{1\leq k\leq P}u_{k}U_{k,p}\Delta_{p}^{\dagger}\,,\,\sum_{1\leq l\leq P}u_{l}U_{l,q}\Delta_{q}^{\dagger}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq P}\sum_{1\leq l\leq P}\Delta_{p}^{\dagger}U_{p,k}\left\langle u_{k}\,,\,u_{l}\right\rangle U_{l,q}\Delta_{q}^{\dagger}=\mbox{${\bm{e}}^{(p)}$}^{t}\Delta^{\dagger}U^{t}GU\Delta^{\dagger}\mbox{${\bm{e}}^{(q)}$}

\displaystyle=\mbox{${\bm{e}}^{(p)}$}^{t}\Delta^{\dagger}U^{t}U\Delta^{2}U^{t}U\Delta^{\dagger}\mbox{${\bm{e}}^{(q)}$}=\delta_{pq},

where
\delta_{pq}
is the Kronecker symbol such that
\delta_{pq}=1
if and only if
p=q
.
Now building
\Pi
upon this basis yields:
\textrm{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\Pi(f)

Natural Neural Tangent Kernel and empirical Tangent Space of PINNs

Seeing PINNs as a quadratic regression problem with respect to the coumpound model of

, as established in

, we see that the “natural” defintion of NNTK that arises from

\textrm{for all }{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
,
\textrm{for all }x,y\in(\Omega\times\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle NNTK_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y)=\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\partial_{p}\left((D,B)\circ u\right)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x){G_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\dagger}_{p,q}\partial_{q}\left((D,B)\circ u\right)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y)^{t}.

with:

G_{\bm{\theta}}:=\left\langle\partial_{p}\left((D,B)\circ u\right)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,,\,\partial_{q}\left((D,B)\circ u\right)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\right\rangle_{\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)}

(95)

The problem lies in the fact, that in order to define the empirical Tangent Space, we would like to be able to separate
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
contributions. To do this, we have to remark that the coumpound model defined in
Equation

24
outputs functions that have a two-dimensional output,
i.e.
the function is vector-valuated and not scalar-valuated anymore. More precisely, we have that
\textrm{for all }f\in\Ima((D,B)\circ u)=\Gamma\subset\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)=\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R})\times\textrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R})

and

such that

\textrm{for all }x=(x_{\Omega},x_{\partial\Omega})\in\Omega\times\partial\Omega

(96)

Hence, the associated reproducing kernel should be a bit revisited. In particular the reproducing property rewrites
[
Alvarez et al. 2012
, Section 3.2]
:
\textrm{for all }f=(f_{\Omega},f_{\partial\Omega})\in T_{\bm{\theta}}\Gamma\subset\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)
,
\textrm{for all }x=(x_{\Omega},x_{\partial\Omega})\in\Omega\times\partial\Omega

\textrm{for all }c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\left\langle f\,,\,NNTK_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,x)c\right\rangle=f(x)^{T}c=f_{\Omega}(x_{\Omega})c_{1}+f_{\partial\Omega}(x_{\partial\Omega})c_{2}.

(97)

In particular, we have:

\Omega
and

are linearly independent and can therefore be separated. More precisely:
defining the partial

•

\textrm{for all }y\in\Omega\times\partial\Omega
,
\textrm{for all }x_{\Omega}\in\Omega
,
\textrm{for all }x_{\partial\Omega}\in\partial\Omega

\displaystyle NNTK^{\Omega}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y,x_{\Omega})

\displaystyle:=NNTK_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y,(x_{\Omega},x_{\partial\Omega})){\bm{e}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\partial_{p}((D,B)\circ u)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y){G_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\dagger}_{p,q}\partial_{q}(D\circ u)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x_{\Omega}),

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{1\leq p,q\leq P}\partial_{p}((D,B)\circ u)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(y){G_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\dagger}_{p,q}\partial_{q}(B\circ u)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(x_{\partial\Omega}),

\textrm{for all }x=(x_{\Omega},x_{\partial\Omega})\in\Omega\times\partial\Omega

\textrm{for all }f\in T_{\bm{\theta}}\Gamma

•

f_{\Omega}(x_{\Omega})=\left\langle f\,,\,NNTK^{\Omega}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x_{\Omega})\right\rangle=\left\langle f\,,\,NNTK_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x){\bm{e}}^{(1)}\right\rangle
.

•

f_{\partial\Omega}(x_{\partial\Omega})=\left\langle f\,,\,NNTK^{\partial\Omega}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x_{\partial\Omega})\right\rangle=\left\langle f\,,\,NNTK_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x){\bm{e}}^{(2)}\right\rangle

This allows us to define an empirical tangent space for PINNs in the same way as in
Equation

20

and
(x_{i}^{\partial\Omega})\in\partial\Omega^{S_{\partial\Omega}}

\widehat{T}_{{\bm{\theta}},(x_{i}^{\Omega}),(x_{j}^{\partial\Omega})}^{NNTK}:=\Span\left(NNTK^{\Omega}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x^{\Omega}_{i}),NNTK^{\partial\Omega}_{|{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot,x^{\partial\Omega}_{j})\,:\,1\leq i\leq S_{\Omega},1\leq j\leq S_{\partial\Omega}\right)

(102)

Empirical Natural Gradient and associated ANaGRAM derivation poses then no particular difficulty and are derived in a similar way to
Section

C.3
.

To make this more concrete, we plot below some NTK and NNTK for the heat equation and the 2D Laplace equation. What we observe is that the NNTK yields a much more specialized kernel that NTK, in the sense that it is much more localized
and also perfectly centered on the reference points. This localization property is expected to be more pronounced as the complexity of the model is increased. The drastic discrepancy observed between NTK and NNTK explain why PINNs fail to train under classical descent while empirical natural gradient solves this issue. The excellent locality of the NNTK kernel leads indeed to a much better optimization schema because the residues are indeed arbitrarily shrunk in small regions around each sample batch point by independent modifications of the function which is obviously impossible with the NTK. When increasing the complexity of the model the spatial range of the NNTK is expected to decrease accordingly and then more points will be needed to “percolate” the optimization over the domain. In such case if the batch size increases too much various principled strategies can be considered to control the complexity of the empirical natural gradient, precisely because the NNTK defines a natural distance between points which can be leverage to define an approximate block Gram matrix. In our point of view this is one of the great advantages of the empirical tangent space over the “parameters” tangent space, because no such good metric is given for free in parameters space.

In this section, we will establish the relationship between Natural Gradient and Green’s function. To set the stage, let us first introduce some definitions.

D.1
Preliminary definitions

Definition 7

(Green’s function)
.

Let
D:\mathcal{H}\to\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)
be a linear differential operator. A Green’s function of
D
is then any kernel function
g:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that the operator:

R:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}D[\mathcal{H}]&\to&\mathcal{H}\\

(103)

is a right-inverse to
D
,
i.e.
such that
D\circ R={I_{\mathcal{H}}}
.

Remark 7
.

We can rephrase
7
, by saying that
g
is a Green’s function if:
\textrm{for all }x,s\in\Omega

where

Remark 8
.

7

is solved by
u(x):=\int_{\Omega}g(x,s)f(s)\mu(\textrm{d}s)

Definition 8

(Solution in the least-squares sense)
.

Let

If

[0,1]^{d}
:

\Delta:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\textrm{H}^{2}([0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{C})&\to&\textrm{L}^{2}([0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{C})\\
v&\mapsto&\sum_{l=1}^{d}\partial_{ll}v\end{array}\right.

For this purpose, let us then fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the associated partial Fourier’s Serie
S_{N}
as defined in
Equation

33
:

S_{N}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}&\to&\textrm{L}^{2}([0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{C})\\
\left(\alpha_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}}\right)&\mapsto&\left(x\in[0,1]^{d}\mapsto\sum\limits_{k_{1}=-N}^{N}\cdots\sum\limits_{k_{d}=-N}^{N}\alpha_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}}e^{2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}x_{l}\right)}\right)\end{array}\right..

We will then derive according to
2
the generalized Green’s function of
\Delta
on the tangent space of
S_{N}
defined in
Equation

34
, namely:

\mathcal{M}=T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}=\Span\left(x\in[0,1]^{d}\mapsto e^{2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}x_{l}\right)}\,:\,k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}\right)

To this end, let define:
\textrm{for all }k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}

e_{k}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}[0,1]^{d}&\to&\mathbb{C}\\
x&\mapsto&e^{2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}x_{l}\right)}\end{array}\right.

(115)

and compute:
\textrm{for all }k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}
,
\textrm{for all }1\leq m\leq d

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{m}^{2}}~e_{k}=-(2\pi)^{2}k_{m}^{2}e_{k},

then:
\textrm{for all }k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}

\displaystyle\Delta~e_{k}=-(2\pi)^{2}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}k_{m}^{2}\right)e_{k},

and thus:
\textrm{for all }k_{1},k_{2}\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}

\displaystyle G_{k_{1},k_{2}}:=\left\langle\Delta~e_{k_{1}}\,,\,\Delta~e_{k_{2}}\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{4}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}{k_{1}}_{m}^{2}\right)^{2}\delta_{k_{1},k_{2}},

where
\delta_{k_{1},k_{2}}
is the Kronecker symbol such that
\delta_{k_{1},k_{2}}=1
if and only if
k_{1}=k_{2}
.
This implies that:

\displaystyle G_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{\dagger}=(2\pi)^{-4}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}{k_{1}}_{m}^{2}\right)^{-2}\left(1-\delta_{k_{1},0}\right)\delta_{k_{1},k_{2}},

yielding:

\displaystyle g_{T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}e_{k_{1}}(x)G_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{\dagger}\overline{\Delta\,e_{k_{2}}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}}e_{k_{1}}(x)(2\pi)^{-4}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}{k_{1}}_{m}^{2}\right)^{-2}\left(1-\delta_{k_{1},0}\right)\delta_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(-(2\pi)^{2}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}{k_{2}}_{m}^{2}\right)\overline{e_{k_{2}}}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{e_{k}(x)\,\overline{e_{k}}(y)}{\sum_{m=1}^{d}k_{m}^{2}}=\frac{-1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{k\in[\![-N,N]\!]^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\exp\left(2i\pi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}(x_{l}-y_{l})\right)\right)}{\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}^{2}}

Finally, in order to add some consistency to our illustration, let us show for
d=1
that in the limit
N\to\infty
, we indeed find the classical Green’s function of the Laplacian operator. In this case let us first remark, that we have:

\displaystyle g_{T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{k\in[\![-N,N]\!]\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\exp\left(2i\pi k(x-y)\right)}{k^{2}}=\frac{-1}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\cos(2\pi k(x-y))}{k^{2}}.

Since
|\cos(2\pi k(x-y))|\leq 1
, the serie is absolutly convergent and thus in the limit
N\to\infty
, we have:

\displaystyle g_{\infty}(x,y):=\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}g_{T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2\pi^{2}}C_{2}(2\pi(x-y)),\textrm{ with }C_{2}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\cos(kz)}{k^{2}}

From
Abramowitz and
Stegun 1968
, we know that
\textrm{for all }z\in(0,2\pi)
:

\displaystyle C_{2}(z)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\frac{\pi z}{2}+\frac{z^{2}}{4},

Thus, thanks to the parity of
C_{2}
we have
\textrm{for all }(x-y)\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle g_{\infty}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\frac{\pi 2\pi|x-y|}{2}+\frac{\left(2\pi(x-y)\right)^{2}}{4}\right)=-\frac{1}{12}+\frac{|x-y|}{2}-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}.

which is indeed a Green function for the Laplacian in
1
d.

Appendix E
Mathematical equivalence of ANaGRAM and Energy Natural Gradient implementation
[
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
]
for linear operators

Let us begin by briefly recalling the definition of Energy Natural Gradient, as introduced in
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
. Given an Energy (Equation 2 in
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
):

E(u)=\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{D}[u]-f)^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\tau\int_{\partial\Omega}(B[u]-g)^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

(116)

associated to operators
D:\mathcal{H}\to\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\mu)
,
B:\mathcal{H}\to\textrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\sigma)
, and a parametric model
u:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathcal{H}
, we have the following definition (Definition 1 in
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
):

Definition 10

(Energy Natural Gradient)
.

Consider the problem
\min_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}L({\bm{\theta}})
, where

L({\bm{\theta}}):=E(u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}).

(117)

Denote the Euclidean gradient by
\nabla L({\bm{\theta}})
. Then we call

\nabla^{E}L({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq G_{E}^{\dagger}({\bm{\theta}})\nabla L({\bm{\theta}}),

(118)

the
energy natural gradient (E-NG)
, where
G_{E}
is a Gram matrix defined by:
\textrm{for all }1\leq p,q\leq P

G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})_{pq}\coloneqq D^{2}E(u_{\bm{\theta}})(\partial_{{\bm{\theta}}_{p}}u_{\bm{\theta}},\partial_{{\bm{\theta}}_{q}}u_{\bm{\theta}}),

(119)

with
D^{2}E
being the second derivative of
E
in the Fréchet sense.

As noted in Equation (9) of
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
, in the case where
D
and
B
are linear,
Equation

119
reduces to:
\textrm{for all }1\leq p,q\leq P

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})_{pq}=\int_{\Omega}D[\partial_{{\bm{\theta}}_{p}}u_{\bm{\theta}}](x)D[\partial_{{\bm{\theta}}_{q}}u_{\bm{\theta}}](x)\mathrm{d}x+\tau\int_{\partial\Omega}B[\partial_{{\bm{\theta}}_{p}}u_{\bm{\theta}}](s)B[\partial_{{\bm{\theta}}_{q}}u_{\bm{\theta}}](s)\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

(120)

Note that in this case, by setting
\tau=1
and taking
\mu
and
\sigma
uniform, this corresponds exactly to the Gram matrix:
\textrm{for all }1\leq p,q\leq P

\displaystyle{G_{\bm{\theta}}}_{pq}:=\left\langle\partial_{p}\big((D,B)\circ u\big)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\,,\,\partial_{q}\big((D,B)\circ u\big)_{|{\bm{\theta}}}\right\rangle_{\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)},

(121)

where the compound model
(D,B)\circ u
and the space
\textrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\partial\Omega)
are those introduced in
Equation

24
.
Now the key element lies in the sentence quoted from page 6, section 4.1 of
Müller and Zeinhofer 2023
(which is confirmed in practice by the code):

“The integrals in (15) are computed using the same collocation points as in the definition of the PINN loss function
L
in (14).”

This means that the same set of collocation points is used to evaluate the integrals defining
L({\bm{\theta}})
in
Equation

117
and the integrals defining
G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})
in
Equation

120
.

Let us fix some collocations points
(x^{D}_{i})_{i=1}^{S_{D}}
in
\Omega
, and
(x^{B}_{i})_{i=1}^{S_{B}}
in
\partial\Omega
. Then
Equation

117
discretization exactly corresponds up to factor
\frac{1}{2}
, to the scalar loss of PINNs defined in
Equation

10
:

\ell({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{2S_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{S_{D}}\left(D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{D}_{i})-f(x^{D}_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2S_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{S_{B}}\left(B[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{B}_{i})-g(x^{B}_{i})\right)^{2},.

The Euclidean gradient
\nabla L({\bm{\theta}})
is then approximated by the Euclidean gradient
\nabla\ell({\bm{\theta}})
given by:
\textrm{for all }1\leq p\leq P

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\nabla\ell({\bm{\theta}})\right)_{p}=&\frac{1}{S_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{S_{D}}\partial_{p}D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{D}_{i})\left(D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{D}_{i})-f(x^{D}_{i})\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{S_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{S_{B}}\partial_{p}B[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{B}_{i})\left(B[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{B}_{i})-g(x^{B}_{i})\right)\end{split}

(122)

Let us define:

•

\textrm{for all }1\leq p\leq P
,
\textrm{for all }1\leq i\leq S_{D}
, and
\textrm{for all }1\leq j\leq S_{B}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\widehat{\phi}^{D}_{\bm{\theta}}$}_{p,i}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{p}D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{D}_{i});

\displaystyle\mbox{$\widehat{\phi}^{B}_{\bm{\theta}}$}_{p,j}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{p}B[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{B}_{j}),

(123)

•

\textrm{for all }1\leq i\leq S_{D}
and
\textrm{for all }1\leq j\leq S_{B}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}^{D}_{\bm{\theta}}$}_{i}

\displaystyle:=D[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{D}_{i})-f(x^{D}_{i});

\displaystyle\mbox{$\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}^{B}_{\bm{\theta}}$}_{j}

\displaystyle:=B[u_{|{\bm{\theta}}}](x^{B}_{j})-g(x^{B}_{j}),

(124)

and finally:

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\phi}^{D}_{\bm{\theta}},&\widehat{\phi}^{B}_{\bm{\theta}}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}^{D}_{\bm{\theta}}\\
\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}^{B}_{\bm{\theta}}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{S_{D}}{\bm{I}}_{S_{D}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{S_{B}}{\bm{I}}_{S_{B}}\end{pmatrix}.

(125)

Thus
Equation

122
can be rewritten as:

\nabla\ell({\bm{\theta}})=\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\theta}}.

(126)

But since the same set of collocation points is used to evaluate the integrals defining
G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})
, we also have that
Equation

119
is discretized by:

G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})\simeq\widetilde{G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})}:=\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}^{t}.

(127)

This implies that
Equation

118
is approximated by:

\nabla^{E}L({\bm{\theta}})\simeq\widetilde{\nabla^{E}L({\bm{\theta}})}\coloneqq\widetilde{G_{E}({\bm{\theta}})}^{\dagger}\nabla\ell({\bm{\theta}})=\widehat{\phi}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\theta}},

(128)

which corresponds indeed to the update direction in line
2
in ANaGRAM algorithm
2
.

To conclude, it should be noted that when
D
or
B
are not linear, then the equivalence between
Equation

120
and
Equation

121
not longer holds, with the result that E-NGD and ANaGRAM are no longer equivalent either.
