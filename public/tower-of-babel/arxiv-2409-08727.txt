Run supports and initial algebra supports of weighted automata
Manfred Droste, Heiko Vogler

Abstract
We consider weighted automata over words and over trees where the weight algebras are strong bimonoids, i.e., semirings which may lack distributivity. It is well known that, for each such weighted automaton, its run semantics and its initial algebra semantics can be different, due to the absence of distributivity. Here we investigate the question under which conditions on a zero-sum-free strong bimonoid the support of the run semantics equals the support of the initial algebra semantics. We prove a characterization of this equality both for weighted automata over words and for weighted automata over trees in terms of two weakened distributivity laws for the strong bimonoids which are required to hold only for expressions evaluating to zero. This provides a natural extension of two classical results on the coincidence of the run semantics and the initial algebra semantics. We also consider shortly the images of the two semantics functions.

For a weighted automaton
\mathcal{A}
we can distinguish (at least) two computation methods: the run semantics and the initial algebra semantics. In the run semantics (
[

, VI.6]
for the case of words, and
[

for the case of trees), to each position of an input structure an arbitrary state of

is assigned. Taking the assignment to the direct predecessors into account, this assignment (called

) determines for each position a transition. The weight of a run is computed by multiplying inital weights, the weights of these transitions and final weights. Finally, the weights of all the runs on the input structure are summed up. Summation
\oplus
and multiplication
\otimes
are provided by the underlying strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
. In this way, the run semantics is a mapping
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}}
of type
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}}:\Gamma^{*}\to B
(for the word case;
\Gamma^{*}
is the set of words over an alphabet
\Gamma
) and
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}}:\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}\to B
(for the tree case;
\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}
is the set of trees over a ranked alphabet
\Sigma
).

The notion of initial algebra semantics is due to the seminal paper
[
33
]
.
In the initial algebra semantics of a weighted automaton
\mathcal{A}
with state set
Q
, one uses state vectors
u\in B^{Q}
to describe
the dynamically evolving weighted behavior of
\mathcal{A}
. For the word case, this evolvement starts with the state vector of initial weights. Each input symbol

respectively
k

\sigma\in\Sigma
(including

k
-ary operation on

\sigma
, respectively, which end up in

After executing all symbols from the given word respectively tree, the weight vector obtained is multiplied with the final weight vector of

. This results in the initial algebra semantics
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}}:\Gamma^{*}\to B
respectively
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}}:\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}\to B
. It can be shown that this yields an initial algebra semantics precisely as developed in
[
33
]
and thereby enables the application of fruitful algebraic methods for this setting. Moreover, the initial algebra semantics has the advantage that, given a strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}
with computable operations, the value
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}}(w)
for
w\in\Gamma^{*}
respectively
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}}(\xi)
for

\xi\in\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}
can be computed with a number of additions or multiplications which is linear in the length of

respectively the size of

(in contrast to the algorithm following the definition of the run semantics), cf.
[

, Thm. 6.1.1]

For a weighted automaton over a strong bimonoid

We have

over

if and only if, in the word case, the strong bimonoid

In this paper, we deal with the supports of the two semantics of weighted automata and, briefly, also with the images of these functions.
The support of the run semantics of a weighted automaton
\mathcal{A}
is the set
\mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}})
of all input structures which are not mapped to the neutral element
\mathbbold{0}
of
\mathsf{B}
; similarly the support of the initial algebra semantics is defined.
Supports of the semantics of weighted automata have been investigated since the beginnings of weighted automata theory, due to their connections to formal language theory. It is known, for instance, that the run support of a weighted automaton might be not recognizable and even not context-free, cf.
[
7
, chapter III]
.
On the other hand, for each weighted automaton over a zero-sum free commutative semiring, the run support is recognizable
[
37
, Thm. 3.1]
. This even holds if the assumption on distributivity is dropped
[
13
,
30
,
24
]
.

We will prove a characterization of the property:

\text{for each weighted automaton $\mathcal{A}$ over $\mathsf{B}$ we have }\ \mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}})=\mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}})

(
\mathrm{Supp}
)

in terms of properties of the underlying strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}
, assuming that
\mathsf{B}
is zero-sum-free. Up to now, only sufficient conditions for Property (

Supp

) are known, which we recall here and in theorems below (the references to the literature are discussed there):

•

for the word case:
\mathsf{B}
is positive or right-distributive (cf. Theorem
4.2
)

•

for the tree case:
\mathsf{B}
is positive or distributive (cf. Theorem
5.5
).

The proofs in these cases are rather straightforward, which shows that the assumptions of positivity or distributivity are very strong. We wish to weaken these assumptions.
We will show that restricting the distributivity law to expressions evaluating to
\mathbbold{0}
, together with the usual zero-sum-freeness of addition, will serve our purposes.
In particular, we use the concept of zero-right-distributivity, already shortly introduced in
[
34
]
, and we introduce the new concept of zero-distributivity.
Then we obtain characterization results for zero-sum-free strong bimonoids as follows:

Theorem
4.3
:
Let
\Gamma
be an alphabet and
\mathsf{B}
be a zero-sum-free strong bimonoid. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

\mathsf{B}
is zero-right-distributive.

(2)

For each weighted automaton
\mathcal{A}
over
\Gamma
and
\mathsf{B}
, we have
\mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}})=\mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}})
.

Theorem
5.7
:
Let
\Sigma
be a branching ranked alphabet and
\mathsf{B}
be a zero-sum-free strong bimonoid.
Then the following two statements are equivalent.

(1)

\mathsf{B}
is zero-distributive.

(2)

For each weighted tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathsf{B}
, we have
\mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}})=\mathrm{supp}([\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}})
.

As mentioned above, the equality of the two semantics for any weighted automaton (over words resp. over trees as inputs) has been characterized by the strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}
being right-distributive resp. distributive. In both conditions (2) above, we weaken the equality of the semantics
to equality of the supports, i.e., intuitively, equality of the semantics values up to being zero or non-zero.
Our results show that for zero-sum-free strong bimonoids
\mathsf{B}
, this corresponds precisely to weakening (right-) distributivity of
\mathsf{B}
to (right-) distributivity at zero.

The following implications hold for each zero-sum-free strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}
:

\mathsf{B}
positive
\Rightarrow

\mathsf{B}
zero-distributive
\Rightarrow

\mathsf{B}
zero-right-distributive.

In Section
3
we show natural examples for each of these classes, and we show that each of these implications is strict.
In particular, natural classes of bounded lattices provide zero-distributive strong bimonoids which are neither positive nor right-distributive; hence to these lattices the above results apply (but not the known results for semirings or for positive or right-distributive strong bimonoids).

Our proofs for Theorems
4.3
and
5.7

proceed through a careful comparison of computations of the run semantics and the initial algebra semantics of a weighted automaton
\mathcal{A}
; in particular for trees, this also involves an analysis of the structure of the trees involved.
The new concepts of zero-(right-)distributivity for zero-sum-free strong bimonoids allow us, in a product,

•

to extend a factor by a sum with the factor as one of its summands and

•

to reduce a sum occurring as a factor in the product into one of its summands

without changing the property of the product of being
\not=\mathbbold{0}
.
Note that for trees, in comparison to words, we need a stronger assumption on the underlying strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}
in order to achieve the equality condition for the run and initial algebra supports for all weighted tree automata
\mathcal{A}
.

As a consequence, we obtain for zero-right-distributive and zero-sum-free computable strong bimonoids and any weighted automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
that it can be decided for any given word
w
in linear time (in the length of
w
) whether its run semantics assigned by
{\mathcal{A}}
is non-zero; the same complexity holds for zero-distributive and zero-sum-free computable strong bimonoids and weighted tree automata
(cf. Corollary
5.11
).

We note that lattice-valued automata have been investigated in the area of model-checking, cf., e.g.,

[
41
]
; hence the above computability results could be applied, for natural classes of bounded lattices, to these automata.

Finally, we show that the characterization theorems mentioned above of the equality of the two semantics for weighted automata can be strengthened as follows: if for all weighted automata
\mathcal{A}
the images of the two functions
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}}
and
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}}
coincide, then for all weighted automata
\mathcal{A}
, the two functions
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}}
and
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{init}}
coincide; see Theorems
6.1
and
6.3
. This, at first sight surprizing, result follows easily as known examples can be exploited to show that the assumption on the images implies that the strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}
is right-distributive in the word case respectively distributive in the tree case.

3
Zero-sum-free strong bimonoids

In this section we will define our new notion of zero-distributive strong bimonoids, and we compare them with positive strong bimonoids.

Let
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
be a strong bimonoid.
We say that
B
is

•

zero-sum-free
, if for all
a,b\in B
we have:

a\oplus b=\mathbbold{0}
iff
a=b=\mathbbold{0}
.

•

zero-divisor-free
, if for all
a,b\in B
we have:

a\otimes b=\mathbbold{0}
iff
a=\mathbbold{0}
or
b=\mathbbold{0}
.

•

positive
, if it is zero-sum-free and zero-divisor-free.

•

zero-right-distributive
(cf.
[
34
, Lm. 4.3]
), if for all
a,b,c\in B
we have:

(a\oplus b)\otimes c=\mathbbold{0}
iff
a\otimes c\oplus b\otimes c=\mathbbold{0}
.

•

zero-distributive
, if for all
a,b,b^{\prime},c\in B
we have:

a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\otimes c=0
iff

(a\otimes b\otimes c)\oplus(a\otimes b^{\prime}\otimes c)=\mathbbold{0}
.

We note that zero-distributivity implies the conjunction of zero-right-distributivity and the analogously defined zero-left-distributivity, but the converse does not hold, due to absence of distributivity.

Clearly, we have the implications

\mathsf{B}
positive
\Rightarrow

\mathsf{B}
zero-distributive
\Rightarrow

\mathsf{B}
zero-right-distributive.

Next, we will give examples for these classes of strong bimonoids and show that in general the above implications cannot be reversed. A reader interested in the automata-theoretic results may continue directly with Section
4
or
5
.

There are many well-known examples of positive semirings. We give only a few examples of positive strong bimonoids some of which are not semirings.

Example 3.1
.

1.

The Boolean semiring
\mathsf{Boole}=(\{0,1\},\vee,\wedge,0,1)
,
the strong bimonoid
\mathsf{Nat}_{+,\min}=(\mathbb{N}_{\infty},+,\min,0,\infty)
, and
the plus-plus strong bimonoid
\mathsf{Nat}_{+,+}=(\mathbb{N}{0},\oplus,+,\mathbbold{0},0)
from Example
2.1
are positive.

2.

Every bounded lattice
\mathsf{L}=(L,\vee,\wedge,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
is zero-sum-free. If
\mathsf{L}
contains an element
a\in L
such that

\mathbbold{0}<a\leq x
for each
x\in L
with
x>\mathbbold{0}
, then
\mathsf{L}
is a positive strong bimonoid.

3.

Near-rings and related structures provide further typical examples, cf.
[

.
E.g., the polynomials
(\mathbb{N}[x],\oplus,\circ,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})

The following easy characterizations of when a strong bimonoid is zero-right-distributive and zero-sum-free, respectively zero-distributive and zero-sum-free, will be very useful subsequently. The proof is straightforward by checking the definitions.

Observation 3.3
.

Let
\mathsf{B}
be a strong bimonoid.

(1)

\mathsf{B}
is zero-right-distributive and zero-sum-free if and only if
for all
a,b,c\in B
we have:

(a\oplus b)\otimes c=0
iff
a\otimes c=b\otimes c=\mathbbold{0}
.

(2)

\mathsf{B}
is zero-distributive and zero-sum-free if and only if
for all
a,b,b^{\prime},c\in B
we have:

a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\otimes c=0
iff
a\otimes b\otimes c=a\otimes b^{\prime}\otimes c=\mathbbold{0}
.

We also note that the notion of zero-distributivity is well-known and has been much investigated in lattice theory.
A lattice
\mathsf{L}=(L,\vee,\wedge,\mathbbold{0})
with smallest element
\mathbbold{0}
is called

meet-semidistributive at
\mathbbold{0}
, if whenever
a,b,c\in L
with
a\wedge c=b\wedge c=\mathbbold{0}
,
then also
(a\vee b)\wedge c=\mathbbold{0}
(cf., e.g.,
[
1
]
, p.112). (This condition can be verbally expressed by saying: ”If two elements are disjoint to a third element, then their join is also disjoint to this third element.”)
The lattice
\mathsf{L}
is said to be
pseudo-complemented

if for each
c\in L
there is a greatest element

such that
c\wedge c^{*}=\mathbbold{0}
.
Clearly, each pseudo-complemented lattice is meet-semidistributive at
0
, and if
L
is finite, the converse is also true. Then we have:

Observation 3.4
.

Let
\mathsf{L}=(L,\vee,\wedge,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
be a bounded lattice.
Then
\mathsf{L}
is a zero-distributive strong bimonoid if and only if
\mathsf{L}
is meet-semidistributive at
0
.
In particular, each pseudo-complemented lattice is a zero-distributive zero-sum-free strong bimonoid.

Proof.

Note that since
a\leq a\vee b
, clearly

(a\vee b)\wedge c=\mathbbold{0}
implies
a\wedge c=b\wedge c=\mathbbold{0}
, for any
a,b,c\in L
.
Then the defining properties easily translate into each other.
∎

Observation 3.6

(1)

A zero-right-distributive strong bimonoid is zero-distributive if it is commutative or left-distributive.

Now we show that there are zero-sum-free and right-distributive strong bimonoids which are not zero-distributive.

Example 3.8
.

There is a zero-sum-free right-distributive strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})

which is not zero-distributive.

Proof.

We use a standard example from the theory of near-rings and near-semirings (cf., e.g.,
[
3
]
; also cf.
[
28
, Ex. 2.7.15(5)]
) Let
(C,+,0)
be a commutative monoid. We consider the set
[C\to C]
of all mappings

f:C\to C
such that
f(0)=0
, together with pointwise addition and with composition of mappings; that is,

(f\oplus g)(c)=f(c)+g(c)
and
f\circ g(c)=f(g(c))
for all
f,g\in[C\to C]
and
c\in C
.
Also
\mathbbold{0}(c)=0
and
\mathbbold{1}(c)=c
for all
c\in C
. Then
\mathsf{B}=([C\to C],\oplus,\circ,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
is a right-distributive strong bimonoid, but in general not left-distributive.

Now if
(C,+,0)
is a zero-sum-free commutative monoid, that is,
a+b=0
implies
a=b=0
for all
a,b\in C
,
then the above strong bimonoid
[C\to C]
is also zero-sum-free, as is easy to check, but often not zero-distributive. For instance, let
(C,+,0)=(\mathbb{N},+,0)
and define
g:C\to C
by letting

g(2n)=0
and
g(2n+1)=2n+1
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
g\circ(g+g)=\mathbbold{0}
but
g\circ g\neq\mathbbold{0}
.
∎

Next we show that there are zero-sum-free and right-distributive strong bimonoids which are not zero-distributive but, nevertheless, satisfy one of the two implications in the definition of zero-distributivity.

Example 3.9
.

There is a zero-sum-free right-distributive strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
satisfying

a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}\ \text{ implies }\ a\otimes b\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}\text{ or }a\otimes b^{\prime}\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0},\ \text{ for all }a,b,b^{\prime},c\in B,

but
\mathsf{B}
is not zero-distributive. In particular,
\mathsf{B}
is zero-right-distributive but not zero-distributive.

Proof.

Let
B=\mathbb{N}\langle x\rangle
be the set of all polynomials over
\mathbb{N}
. We let
\oplus
be the usual addition of polynomials. We define the multiplication
p\otimes q
of two polynomials
p,q\in B
as follows:

•

p\otimes q=p\cdot q
, the usual product, if
q
is a monome; thus, if
p=\sum_{i=0,...,n}a_{i}x^{i}
and
q=bx^{j}
for some
b\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\geq 0
, then
p\otimes q=\sum_{i=0,...,n}a_{i}bx^{i+j}
;

•

p\otimes q=p(0)\cdot q
if
q
is not a monome, that is,
q=\sum_{k=0,...,n}b_{k}x^{k}
with
n\geq 1
and
b_{i},b_{j}\neq 0
for some
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
; in particular, note that if
p=\sum_{i=1,...,n}a_{i}x^{i}
, then
p\otimes q=\mathbbold{0}
.

Clearly, this multiplication
\otimes
satisfies the strong bimonoid axioms for
\mathbbold{0}
and
\mathbbold{1}
. We show that
\otimes
is associative. Let
p,q,r\in B
. We claim that
(p\otimes q)\otimes r=p\otimes(q\otimes r)
, and we may assume that
p,q,r
are different from
\mathbbold{0}
and
\mathbbold{1}
.

First, if
q
and
r
are monomes, our claim is clear. Secondly, let
q
be a monome, but
r
not be a monome. Then
(p\otimes q)\otimes r=(p\cdot q)(0)\cdot r=p(0)\cdot q(0)\cdot r
and
p\otimes(q\otimes r)=p\otimes(q(0)\cdot r)
. Since
q
is a monome with
q\neq\mathbbold{0}
, we have
q(0)\neq\mathbbold{0}
and
q(0)\cdot r
is not a monome. Hence
p\otimes(q(0)\cdot r)=p(0)\cdot(q(0)\cdot r)
showing our claim.
Thirdly, let
q
not be a monome, but let
r
be a monome. Then
(p\otimes q)\otimes r=p(0)\cdot q\cdot r
. Since
r\neq\mathbbold{0}
, the product
q\cdot r
is not a monome. Hence we obtain
p\otimes(q\otimes r)=p\otimes(q\cdot r)=p(0)\cdot q\cdot r
as needed. Finally, assume that
q
and
r
are not monomes. Then
(p\otimes q)\otimes r=(p(0)\cdot q)(0)\cdot r=p(0)\cdot q(0)\cdot r
and
p\otimes(q\otimes r)=p\otimes(q(0)\cdot r)
. If
q(0)=\mathbbold{0}
, both required expressions are
\mathbbold{0}
. Therefore we may assume that
q(0)\neq\mathbbold{0}
. Then
q(0)\cdot r
is not a monome. Thus
p\otimes(q(0)\cdot r)=p(0)\cdot(q(0)\cdot r)
as needed. Hence
\otimes
is associative.

Clearly,
\otimes
is right-distributive over
\oplus
, so
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
is a zero-sum-free right-distributive strong bimonoid.

First, assume that
a,b,b^{\prime}\in B
with
a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\neq\mathbbold{0}
. We claim that then
a\otimes b\neq\mathbbold{0}
or
a\otimes b^{\prime}\neq\mathbbold{0}
. We have
a\neq\mathbbold{0}
and
b\oplus b^{\prime}\neq\mathbbold{0}
. We may assume that
b\neq\mathbbold{0}
.
Clearly, if
b
is a monome, then
a\otimes b
is the usual product and we have
a\otimes b\neq\mathbbold{0}
. Next, assume that
b
is not a monome. Clearly, also
b\oplus b^{\prime}
is not a monome.
Then
a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})=a(0)\cdot(b\oplus b^{\prime})
, thus
a(0)\neq\mathbbold{0}
. Hence we have
a\otimes b=a(0)\cdot b\neq\mathbbold{0}
, showing our claim.

Now let
a,b,b^{\prime},c\in B
with
a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}
. We claim that
a\otimes b\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}
or
a\otimes b^{\prime}\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}
. We have

a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\otimes c=a\otimes(b\otimes c\oplus b^{\prime}\otimes c)
. By what we showed above, we directly obtain our claim.

We have
x\otimes(1\oplus x)=\mathbbold{0}
, showing that
\mathsf{B}
is not zero-distributive.
∎

Example 3.10
.

There is a zero-sum-free right-distributive strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
satisfying

a\otimes b\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}\ \text{ implies }\ a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}\ \text{ for all }a,b,b^{\prime},c\in B,

but
\mathsf{B}
is not zero-distributive.

Proof.

(Sketch)
Let
(B(X),\oplus^{\prime},\otimes^{\prime},\mathbbold{0}^{\prime},\mathbbold{1}^{\prime})
be the free right-distributive strong bimonoid having a non-empty
X
as its set of free generators. This can be viewed as the set of terms using the elements of
X
and
\mathbbold{0}^{\prime},\mathbbold{1}^{\prime}
and the operations
\oplus^{\prime},\otimes^{\prime}
such that the axioms of right-distributive strong bimonoids are satisfied, cf.
[
11
,
9
]
. Let
\sim
be the congruence defined by putting
x\otimes^{\prime}y\sim\mathbbold{0}^{\prime}
for all
x,y\in X
, and let
\mathsf{B}=B(X)/_{\sim}
be the factor algebra of
B(X)
modulo
\sim
. We write
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
.
Then
\mathsf{B}
is a zero-sum-free right-distributive strong bimonoid.

Since
\mathsf{B}
is right-distributive, to show the required implication it suffices to consider the case
c=\mathbbold{1}
.
Now if
a,b,b^{\prime}\in B
with
a\otimes(b\oplus b^{\prime})=\mathbbold{0}
, we claim that
a\otimes b=a\otimes b^{\prime}=\mathbbold{0}
. We may assume that
a\neq\mathbbold{0}
and, since
\mathsf{B}
is zero-sum-free, that
b\oplus b^{\prime}\neq\mathbbold{0}
. The argument proceeds through an analysis of the term structure of

and

, employing right-distributivity and the definition of
\sim
. As illustration, assume that
a=\bigoplus_{i\in I}[x_{i}]_{\sim}
for some finite collection

and
b\oplus b^{\prime}=[y]_{\sim}
for some

. Then
b=[y]_{\sim},b^{\prime}=\mathbbold{0}
or viceversa. In the first case, by right-distributivity,
a\otimes b=\bigoplus_{i\in I}[x_{i}\otimes^{\prime}y]_{\sim}=\mathbbold{0}
and trivially
a\otimes b^{\prime}=\mathbbold{0}
(and similarly in the other case), showing our claim in this case. The general case can be reduced to this subcase.

For
x\in X
we have
[x]_{\sim}\otimes[x]_{\sim}=\mathbbold{0}
,
but
[x]_{\sim}\otimes([x]_{\sim}\oplus[x]_{\sim})=[x\otimes^{\prime}(x\oplus^{\prime}x)]_{\sim}\neq\mathbbold{0}
, showing that
\mathsf{B}
is not zero-distributive.

Finally, we show that there are commutative zero-sum-free strong bimonoids which satisfy either one of the implications of the definition of zero-right-distributivity, but not the other one.

Example 3.11
.

There are commutative zero-sum-free strong bimonoids
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})
and
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}=(B^{\prime},\oplus^{\prime},\otimes^{\prime},\mathbbold{0}^{\prime},\mathbbold{1}^{\prime})
satisfying, respectively,

(1)

a\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0}\ \text{ implies }\ (a\oplus b)\otimes c\neq\mathbbold{0},\ \text{ for all }a,b,c\in B,

(2)

(a\oplus^{\prime}b)\otimes^{\prime}c\neq\mathbbold{0}\ \text{ implies }\ a\otimes^{\prime}c\neq\mathbbold{0}\ \text{or}\ b\otimes^{\prime}c\neq\mathbbold{0},\ \text{for all }a,b,c\in B^{\prime},

but both of them are not zero-right-distributive.

Proof.

Let
B=\{\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1},2,3\}
. We define the operations
\oplus
and
\otimes
of the commutative strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})

such that

\mathbbold{1}\oplus\mathbbold{1}=\mathbbold{1},\ \ 2\oplus j=3\oplus j=3
for each
j\in\{\mathbbold{1},2,3\}
and
2\otimes 2=\mathbbold{0},\ 2\otimes 3=2,\ 3\otimes 3=3
.

Then
\mathsf{B}
is commutative and zero-sum-free and satisfies (1) but not the corresponding condition of (2), since
(2\oplus 2)\otimes 2=3\otimes 2=2\neq\mathbbold{0}
, but
2\otimes 2=\mathbbold{0}
.

Secondly, let
B^{\prime}=\{\mathbbold{0}^{\prime},\mathbbold{1}^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}
. We define the operations
\oplus^{\prime}
and
\otimes^{\prime}
of the commutative strong bimonoid
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}=(B^{\prime},\oplus^{\prime},\otimes^{\prime},\mathbbold{0}^{\prime},\mathbbold{1}^{\prime})

such that

\mathbbold{1}^{\prime}\oplus^{\prime}\mathbbold{1}^{\prime}=\mathbbold{1}^{\prime}\oplus^{\prime}2^{\prime}=2^{\prime}\oplus^{\prime}2=2^{\prime}
and
2^{\prime}\otimes 2^{\prime}=\mathbbold{0}^{\prime}
.

Then
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
is commutative and zero-sum-free and satisfies (2) but not the corresponding condition of (1), since
(\mathbbold{1}^{\prime}\oplus^{\prime}\mathbbold{1}^{\prime})\otimes^{\prime}2^{\prime}=\mathbbold{0}^{\prime}
.
∎

\mathsf{B}=(B,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbbold{0},\mathbbold{1})

\mathcal{A}=(Q,I,\mu,F)

\mu(a)_{p,q}=\mu(p,a,q)

(p,q\in Q)

Run semantics.

The

\mathcal{A}

[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]^{\mathrm{run}}:\Gamma^{*}\to B
defined for each
w=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
in
\Gamma^{*}
(with

and

A strong bimonoid

over
\Sigma

(2)

over
\Sigma
and

have state set
