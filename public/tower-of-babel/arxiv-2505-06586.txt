To what extent does the consideration of positive total flux influence the dynamics of Keller–Segel-type models?
† Khadijeh Baghaei, ♯ Silvia Frassu, ♭ Yuya Tanaka, and ♮ Giuseppe Viglialoro ⋆

Abstract
. Since the introduction of the Keller-Segel model in 1970 to describe chemotaxis (the interactions between cell distributions u u and chemical distributions v v ), there has been a significant proliferation of research articles exploring various extensions and modifications of this model within the scientific community. From a technical standpoint, the totality of results concerning these variants are characterized by the assumption that the total flux, involving both distributions, of the model under consideration is zero . This research aims to present a novel perspective by focusing on models with a positive total flux. Specifically, by employing Robin-type boundary conditions for u u and v v , we seek to gain insights into the interactions between cells and their environment, uncovering important dynamics such as how variations in boundary conditions influence chemotactic behavior. In particular, the choice of the boundary conditions is motivated by real-world phenomena and by the fact that the related analysis reveals some interesting properties of the system. 1.
Chemotaxis phenomena. The homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions

1.1.
The landmarking Keller–Segel model

The term
chemotaxis
indicates the movement in a certain environment of living organisms (e.g. cells) occupying habitats under the influence of a chemical stimulus, therein distributed and changing over time.
The pioneering mathematical model for the description of the mentioned phenomenon was proposed by physicist Keller and mathematician Segel in 1970 (
[
30
,
31
]
), by means of two coupled Partial Differential Equations. If
(x,t)
identifies a position
x
in a region (a bounded domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\geq 1
) at some time
t
(with
t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}})
, and
T_{\textup{max}}\in(0,\infty]
), the classical Keller–Segel model reads:

(1)

\begin{cases}u_{t}=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v),\qquad v_{t}=\Delta v-v+u&\textrm{ in }\Omega\times\left(0,T_{\textup{max}}\right),\\
u_{\nu}=v_{\nu}=0\;\textrm{ on }\;\partial\Omega\times\left(0,T_{\textup{max}}\right),\;u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\;v(x,0)=v_{0}(x)&x\in\bar{\Omega}.\end{cases}

This system describes the self-organized aggregation of a starving slime mould colony. In this context, individual cells (with density
u=u(x,t)\geq 0
and initial configuration
u_{0}(x)=u(x,0)\geq 0
) direct their otherwise random movement toward higher concentrations of an attractive chemical signal substance, which they produce (with concentration
v=v(x,t)\geq 0
and initial distribution
v_{0}(x)=v(x,0)\geq 0
).
Here,
\partial\Omega
represents the boundary of
\Omega
, and
\nu
is its outward normal vector. The homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions (also referred to as “h.N.b.c.”)
u_{\nu}=v_{\nu}=0
on
\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}})
idealize an insulated and impenetrable domain.

The cell density equation in system (
1
) reflects the balance between diffusion (
\Delta u
) and the amplifying effect of taxis-driven chemosensitivity (
-\chi\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)
), which intensifies with larger
\chi
, due to the absence of external sources and the isolation of the domain, any net growth in cell mass over time is excluded. This follows from integrating over
\Omega
and applying the h.N.b.c., yielding
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u=0
, so
\int_{\Omega}u
stays equal to its initial value
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)dx
.

Mathematically, model (
1
) admits both global bounded solutions (with
T_{\textup{max}}=\infty
and
\lVert u(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
finite for all
t>0
) and unbounded solutions that blow up in finite time (
T_{\textup{max}}
finite, with
\limsup_{t\to T_{\textup{max}}}\lVert u(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\infty
). For
n=1
, all solutions remain bounded over time (diffusion dominates self-attraction), while for
n\geq 2
, self-attraction can overpower diffusion, leading to finite-time blow-up depending on the initial data. Classical results and surveys,
all derived by relying on the mentioned boundedness of the mass
, are available, for instance, in
[
7
,
28
,
43
,
65
,
36
]
.

1.2.
The Keller–Segel system with logistic and strong logistic sources

Especially in a setting involved with population dynamics, the combination of (
1
) with sources distributed
internally
in the domain describing population growth or decay is very natural; we are specially referring to logistic terms (see
[
2
,
45
]
).

In this context, species distribution is also affected by reproduction and mortality, with death rates increasing at higher population densities. The general
logistic source
in the equation

u_{t}=\nabla u-\chi\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)+f(u)

takes the form

f(u)=au^{\alpha}-bu^{\beta}
, where
a,b\geq 0
and
1\leq\alpha<\beta
; the positive term models births, while the negative term models deaths. We now examine various scenarios:

\triangleright

First, for
b=0
and
a>0
, a spatial integration of the equation for the cells implies, due to the h.N.b.c (and using the Hölder inequality)

(2)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u=a\int_{\Omega}u^{\alpha}\geq a|\Omega|^{1-\alpha}\left(\int_{\Omega}u\right)^{\alpha}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

which leads to explosion of
\int_{\Omega}u
(and henceforth of
\lVert u(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
) at

T_{\textup{max}}\leq\frac{|\Omega|^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)dx\right)^{1-\alpha}}{a(\alpha-1)}.

\triangleright

Secondly, for
a=0
and
b>0
, similarly, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u=-b\int_{\Omega}u^{\beta}\leq 0\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

thus, the mass decreases;
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\leq\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)dx
for all
t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}})
.

\triangleright

For
a,b>0
and
\beta>\alpha\geq 1
the uniform-in-time finiteness of
\sup_{t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}})}\int_{\Omega}u
can be as well achieved with some rearrangements, entailing

\int_{\Omega}u\leq\max\left\{\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)dx,\left(\frac{a}{b}|\Omega|^{\beta-\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}}\right\}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

More precisely, analogously to the previous cases, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u=a\int_{\Omega}u^{\alpha}-b\int_{\Omega}u^{\beta}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

and, thanks to the Hölder inequality, we have

-\int_{\Omega}u^{\beta}\leq-\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha}}\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad-\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\alpha}}\leq-\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert^{\frac{(1-\alpha)(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha}}\left(\int_{\Omega}u\right)^{\beta-\alpha}\quad\text{in }(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

By combining the above expressions, we arrive at

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u\leq a\int_{\Omega}u^{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{b}{a}\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert^{\alpha-\beta}\left(\int_{\Omega}u\right)^{\beta-\alpha}\right)\quad\textrm{on }(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

so concluding by invoking the ODE comparison principle in
[
14
, Lemma 3.3]
.

A natural question is whether the inclusion of logistic terms can ensure boundedness and prevent finite-time blow-up detected in (
1
). This has been confirmed only for sufficiently large
b
(e.g.,
\beta=2
, see
[
60
,
66
]
), while blow-up may still occur for certain
\beta>1
close to 1 (see
[
68
,
72
]
). However, blow-up can be avoided by strengthening the logistic source. Specifically, if
f(u)
is replaced by
h(u,|\nabla u|)=f(u)-c|\nabla u|^{\gamma}
, then any solution to

u_{t}=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)+h(u,|\nabla u|)

remains globally bounded for all
c>0
,
\beta>\alpha\geq 1
, provided
\frac{2n}{n+1}<\gamma\leq 2
(see
[
29
,
37
]
for theoretical and applied results on gradient-dependent sources in chemotaxis).

Remark 1

(On the logistic terms with strong/gradient dissipative effects)
.

The mention of the logistic term
h=h(u,|\nabla u|)
with gradient nonlinearities at this stage of the discussion is intentional and not without purpose. Indeed, these terms will prove to be of crucial importance in our forthcoming discussion, as will become apparent in

4
below.

1.3.
Nonlinear diffusion and sensitivity and further models

Over recent decades, numerous extensions of model (
1
) have been proposed to describe taxis-driven phenomena, often formulated as

(3)

u_{t}=\nabla\cdot(S(u,v)\nabla u-T(u,v)\nabla v)+f(u),\qquad v_{t}=\Delta v+g(u,v)\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions and given initial data. Here,
S(u,v)
models diffusion,
T(u,v)
captures chemotactic sensitivity (positive for attraction, negative for repulsion), and
f(u)
accounts for source effects. The chemical
v
may be produced (
g(u,v)=-v+u
) or consumed (
g(u,v)=-uv
) by the cells.

Depending on
S,T,f,g
, solutions may exhibit either global boundedness or finite-time blow-up. For parameters
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\rho,\gamma>0
and
\beta>\alpha\geq 1
, we briefly review key studies, the associated qualitative behaviors of the aforementioned terms:

2.
Modification in the boundary conditions. Requiring the total flux to vanish

Recently, research on chemotactic systems has expanded to explore various types of boundary conditions, moving beyond the Neumann-type conditions analyzed in previous sections.
Our project is situated within this evolving framework
; in particular, the foundations of our investigation are based on the the following considerations.

3.
Positive total flux in chemotaxis. Real applications and mathematical formulation

3.1.
The Robin boundary conditions

In the literature, boundary conditions of the form
r\psi+s\psi_{\nu}=g
on
\partial\Omega
, where
\psi
and
g
are functions and
r,s
are constants, are known as Robin (or mixed) boundary conditions (possibly abbreviated as “R.b.c.”). For specific values of
(r,s)=(1,0)
or
(r,s)=(0,1)
, these reduce to Dirichlet or Neumann conditions, respectively. In their more general form, Robin boundary conditions provide important advantages for modeling complex phenomena. For instance, as described by Souplet in
[
52
]
, for a single (biological) species density
u
in a bounded domain, a Robin boundary condition models a scenario in which the species can cross the boundary of its environment. (Further examples and applications of Robin boundary conditions in parabolic problems can be found in
[
4
,
54
,
51
,
48
,
49
]
.)

In the frame of taxis-driven processes where the response of cells to chemical gradients near boundaries is a crucial aspect, Robin boundary conditions may offer a more nuanced representation of boundary interactions compared to Dirichlet or Neumann conditions. As illustrated in Table
1
, R.b.c. have already been employed in the study of chemotaxis mechanisms, specifically in modeling the behavior of chemical signals under zero-flux conditions.

3.2.
The Bacillus subtilis, Eukaryotic T-Cells, and Microbial Communities

This study investigates how chemical concentrations influence total flux across boundaries, with particular attention to the role of boundary conditions in shaping chemotactic responses. In the below biological transport mechanisms, where total flux is governed by both chemical gradients and cellular distribution, a net positive flux often emerges—capturing the hallmark of active cellular adaptation to environmental stimuli.

In line with
[
61
]
, Bacillus subtilis often thrives in thin fluid layers near oxygen contact lines, where oxygen uptake rates are singular and controlled by boundary concentrations.

Chemotaxis is commonly measured by tracking the number of cells crossing a boundary per unit time, such as cells migrating through a chemoattractant-infused filter. In
[
56
]
, eukaryotic cell migration is used as an indicator of chemotactic responsiveness.

In tumor biology, models couple tumor cell dynamics with chemokine concentration, driving T-cell migration toward the tumor via blood vessels, with the movement rate proportional to chemoattractant levels (see
[
3
]
).

Swarms and biofilms, microbial communities forming at the interface of solid substrates and strong fluid flows, are also influenced by boundary dynamics. These communities interact with flow-induced signal gradients, underscoring the importance of boundary conditions in microbial behavior (see
[
64
]
).

3.3.
The role of the chemoattractant and positive total flux. Mathematical interpretation and formulation

We now present a consistent mathematical formulation of the phenomena discussed above. Since in chemotaxis mechanisms the cells direct their movement in the exact opposite direction to that of the gradient of the chemical signal, if the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions
u_{\nu}=v_{\nu}=0
on
\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}})
are replaced by

u_{\nu}=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{\nu}=-hv\quad\textrm{on}\quad\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

indicating an outflow of the chemoattractant and the null flux of the cells’ distribution, we derive that the total flux
u_{\nu}-uv_{\nu}=huv
is
nonnegative
on
\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}})
. This effectively models how the outward flux of the chemoattractant
v
triggers cellular migration across the boundary into the domain, resulting in a
inward total flux.

But the relevance of this transport mechanism can be more importantly intuited by this observation, which will make meaningful the definition of our boundary conditions. Indeed, even for appropriate outward flux of the cells’ configuration, the taxis-driven effect of the outward flow of the chemoattractant can yet keep producing a positive total flux. Formally, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
the following boundary conditions on
u
and
v

(5)

u_{\nu}=(\alpha-1)\chi huv\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{\nu}=-hv\quad\textrm{on}\quad\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

both modeling outward flux for
u
and
v
, turn to an essentially positive total flux:

(6)

u_{\nu}-\chi uv_{\nu}=\alpha\chi huv\quad\textrm{on}\quad\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

Consequently, the boundedness of the mass discussed in
\lx@sectionsign
2.1
is no more ensured. Indeed, by integrating the first equation in (
1
) and taking into consideration (
6
) it is only possible to conclude that

(7)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u=\alpha\chi h\int_{\partial\Omega}uv\geq 0\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

This relation merely indicates that the total mass
\int_{\Omega}u
exhibits a nondecreasing behavior over the interval
(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
, but it does not establish a direct connection between the evolution of the mass and a functional dependence on the mass itself. In particular, if one were able to derive from (
7
) an ordinary differential inequality analogous to that in (
2
), then (by the same reasoning) finite-time blow-up would necessarily follow. However, such an inference is not supported by the available data, and it remains entirely plausible that the total mass may instead converge to a finite value as
t\to T_{\mathrm{max}}
.

Remark 2

(On the connection between conditions (
5
) and (
6

.

The parameter
\alpha
effectively regulates the balance between the fluxes of
u
and
v
, as defined in equation (
5
), and their contribution to the total flux in equation (

, the boundary permits substantial outflow of

\alpha=1

. Undoubtedly, the chosen boundary conditions reflect real-world phenomena and reveal valuable and interesting insights into the system’s behavior.

4.
Boundedness of the mass by means of strong logistics in models with positive flux

As previously discussed, in chemotaxis models with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, logistic terms of the form
au-bu^{2}
are commonly introduced to ensure the boundedness of
u
on the entire time interval
(0,\infty)
. In contrast, such terms are not even sufficient to guarantee the boundedness of the total mass on
(0,T_{\max})
when positive total flux boundary conditions are imposed. Indeed, in this case, relation (
7
) takes the form

(8)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u=\alpha\chi h\int_{\partial\Omega}uv+a\int_{\Omega}u-b\int_{\Omega}u^{2},\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T_{\max}),

where the boundary integral
\int_{\partial\Omega}uv
remains difficult to control and may dominate the dynamics.

Given the critical role of uniform-in-time mass boundedness in the theoretical study of taxis-driven systems, it becomes essential to identify mechanisms that ensure this property under the boundary conditions introduced in (
5
). As we shall see, more robust and efficient damping terms are required to effectively counteract the potential growth of
\int_{\Omega}u
on
(0,T_{\max})
.

4.1.
Logistics with damping gradient terms; applications and their role through the boundedness of the mass

As it will be shown, the mathematical estimates developed to control the evolution of the mass naturally inherit a structure that includes two key positive contributions: one involving the square of the cell density, and another involving the quadratic norm of its gradient. Consequently, to effectively manage the growth of
\int_{\Omega}u
described in (
8
), the introduction of suitably designed damping terms with gradient nonlinearities (as discussed in Remark
1
) becomes not merely a modeling choice, but a necessary component to ensure the qualitative stability and biological plausibility of the system under consideration.

Specifically, we will consider the modified source term
h(u,|\nabla u|)=au-bu^{2}-c|\nabla u|^{2}
in the Keller–Segel model (
1
). Then, the cell density evolves according to the following equation:

u_{t}=\Delta u-\chi\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)+h(u,|\nabla u|)\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T_{\max}).

At this stage, to ensure the boundedness of the mass, in this research we will operate in a twofold manner. The first approach relies on the use of trace inequalities, by means of which it will be possible to rewrite the undesired boundary integral as the sum of integrals over the entire domain. The second approach, which does not rely on the trace theory, involves the introduction of appropriate functionals and ad hoc manipulations, aimed at rendering any boundary terms negligible.

As will be rigorously established in the sequel, both strategies provide a mechanism for controlling the boundary integral corresponding to the positive part of the total flux. In essence, this quantity can be estimated in terms of a suitable norm of
u
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, at least morally:

\alpha\chi h\int_{\partial\Omega}uv\leq c\textsubscript{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}+c\textsubscript{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}})\textrm{ and some }c\textsubscript{1},c\textsubscript{2}>0.

Remark 4

(Quadratic-gradient-driven phenomena and their connection to positive fluxes)
.

While gradient nonlinearities arising from external sources, interpreted in biological contexts as mechanisms of “accidental deaths”, have been previously studied within single-equation models (see, e.g.,
[
52
,
53
,
51
,
13
,
20
]
and references therein), their impact within chemotactic systems has only recently begun to attract attention. Notably, recent works such as
[
29
,
37
]
have started to explore their relevance in biologically and ecologically meaningful settings. In light of the preceding considerations (both mathematical and biological) it should no longer be surprising that, in models characterized by a positive total flux, there exists an intrinsic connection between the growth of the total mass and the presence of external agents exerting strong damping effects.

5.

Motivated by both the biological insights and the mathematical considerations discussed above, and taking into account the apparent lack of comprehensive studies on chemotaxis models featuring
positive total flux

\lozenge

Lemma 6.1

that can be also written, since
(u,v)
is radial, as
f=-r^{1-n}(r^{n-1}v_{r})_{r}+v-u

and
g=\frac{u_{r}}{\sqrt{u}}-\chi\sqrt{u}v_{r}
.
Now, we dedicate ourselves to estimate the functional
\mathcal{F}_{h}
in terms of the dissipation rate
\mathcal{D}
on the set
\mathcal{S}
. As the first step, we estimate
the integral
\int_{\Omega}uv
in terms of
\mathcal{D}
: this is the goal of the next three lemmas.

Lemma A.7
.

There exists
C(M,h)>0
such that for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}(m,M,B,\kappa,h)
we have

(80)

\int_{\Omega}uv\leq 2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+C(M,h)\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2n+4}{n+4}}+1\right).

Proof.

By multiplying the expression of
f
in (
79
) for
v
and by integrating over
\Omega
, we have

(81)

\int_{\Omega}uv=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+h\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}+\int_{\Omega}v^{2}-\int_{\Omega}fv.

Since
\Omega
is a ball, we can invoke
[
47
, Lemma A.1]
to control the integral term on the boundary, yielding after an application of the Young inequality

h\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\leq\frac{hn}{R}\int_{\Omega}v^{2}+2h\int_{\Omega}v|\nabla v|\leq\left(\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)\int_{\Omega}v^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2},

which, inserted in equality (
81
), gives

(82)

\int_{\Omega}uv\leq\frac{4}{3}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)\int_{\Omega}v^{2}-\int_{\Omega}fv.

At this point we manage the terms involving
\int_{\Omega}v^{2}
and
-\int_{\Omega}fv
as in the proof of
[
70
, Lemma 4.2]
; this leads for some

c\textsubscript{9}=c\textsubscript{9}(M)
,
c\textsubscript{10}=c\textsubscript{10}(M)
and

c\textsubscript{11}=c\textsubscript{11}(M)
to

\left(1+\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\leq\frac{1}{3}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+c\textsubscript{9}\left(1+\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)^{\frac{n+2}{2}}

and

-\int_{\Omega}fv\leq\frac{1}{3}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+c\textsubscript{10}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2n+4}{n+4}}+c\textsubscript{11}.

By plugging these two estimates into bound (
82
), we find

(83)

\int_{\Omega}uv\leq 2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+\max\left\{c\textsubscript{10},c\textsubscript{11}+c\textsubscript{9}\left(1+\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)^{\frac{n+2}{2}}\right\}(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2n+4}{n+4}}+1),

so concluding.
∎

Remark 6

(Comparison with the Neumann boundary conditions)
.

We point out that the presence of the positive constant
h
in (
83
) defining
C(M,h)
in Lemma
A.7
is manifestly connected to the boundary condition under investigation. In particular, if
h\to 0
we have that
c\textsubscript{9}\left(1+\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)^{\frac{n+2}{2}}\to c\textsubscript{9}
, the constant
C(M,h)\to C(M)
, so recovering
C(M)
of
[
70
, Lemma 4.2]
.

In the next lemma, we will control the term
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}
.

Lemma A.8
.

There exists
C(m,M,B,\kappa,\chi)>0
such that each
(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}(m,M,B,\kappa,h)
satisfies

(84)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}uv+C(m,M,B,\kappa,\chi)\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2\theta}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+1\right),

where

(85)

\theta:=\frac{1}{1+\frac{n}{(2n+4)\kappa}}\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right).

Proof.

For
n\geq 3
, and accordingly to
[
70
, Lemma 4.3 and Lemma 4.4]
, by splitting the domain
\Omega
as
\Omega=B_{r_{0}}\cup(\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}})
, where
B_{r_{0}}
is a small inner ball, the estimate on
\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}}|\nabla v|^{2}
is obtained by replacing
[
70
, (4.14)]
with

\alpha\int_{\Omega}v^{\alpha-1}|\nabla v|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}fv^{\alpha}+\int_{\Omega}uv^{\alpha}-\int_{\Omega}v^{\alpha+1}-h\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{\alpha+1}\leq\int_{\Omega}fv^{\alpha}+\int_{\Omega}uv^{\alpha}.

(For
n=2
we refer to
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
.)

On the other hand,
\int_{B_{r_{0}}}|\nabla v|^{2}
is similarly controlled as in
[
70
, Lemma 4.4]
, for
n\geq 3
, and in
[
16
, Lemma 2.4]
for
n=2
.

From
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}=\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}}|\nabla v|^{2}+\int_{B_{r_{0}}}|\nabla v|^{2}
and following
[
70
, Lemma 4.5 (ArXiv version)]
, we definitively can conclude.
∎

Now we are in the position to obtain the desired estimate of the term
\int_{\Omega}uv
.

Lemma A.9
.

There exists
C(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)>0
such that for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}(m,M,B,\kappa,h)
we have

(86)

\int_{\Omega}uv\leq C(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2\theta}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+1\right)

with
\theta\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
given in (
85
).

Proof.

By exploiting bound (
84
) in estimate (
80
), we obtain the claim, after an application of the Young inequality, since
\frac{2n+4}{n+4}\leq 2\theta
, exactly as in
[
70
, Lemma 4.1]
.
∎

Finally, the previous lemma allows us to estimate the Lyapunov functional
\mathcal{F}_{h}
in terms of the dissipation rate
\mathcal{D}
on the set
\mathcal{S}
, which is the other main tool to prove blow-up.

Lemma A.10
.

There exists
C(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)>0
such that for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}(m,M,B,\kappa,h)
we have

\mathcal{F}_{h}(u,v)\geq-C(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)\,(\mathcal{D}^{\theta}(u,v)+1)

with
\theta\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
given in (
85
).

Proof.

By exploiting that
\xi\ln\xi\geq-\frac{1}{e}
for all
\xi>0
and bound (
86
), we derive for some
c\textsubscript{12}=c\textsubscript{12}(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{h}(u,v)&=\frac{\chi}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{\chi}{2}\int_{\Omega}v^{2}-\chi\int_{\Omega}uv+\int_{\Omega}u\,\ln u+\frac{\chi h}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\\
&\geq-\chi\int_{\Omega}uv-\frac{|\Omega|}{e}\\
&\geq-c\textsubscript{12}\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2\theta}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+1\right).\end{split}

Since
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
, an application of the Young inequality yields for some

c\textsubscript{13}=c\textsubscript{13}(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)

\mathcal{F}_{h}(u,v)\geq-c\textsubscript{13}\left((\chi\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2})^{\theta}+1\right)=-c\textsubscript{13}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\theta}(u,v)+1\right),

where, by virtue of the definition of
f,g
and
\mathcal{D}(u,v)
given in (
79
) and (
77
), respectively, we have exploited that
\mathcal{D}(u,v)=\chi\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
.
∎

Now, by applying Lemma
A.6
and Lemma
A.10
we derive a differential inequality for
-\mathcal{F}_{h}(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))
with superlinearly growing nonlinearity. This implies that
(u,v)
cannot exist globally for initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})
with large negative energy
\mathcal{F}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})
.

Lemma A.11
.

Let
m,A,h,\chi>0
,
\kappa>n-2
if
n\geq 3
, and
\kappa>2
if
n=2
. Then there exist
K=K(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)>0
and
C=C(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)>0
such that for each
(u_{0},v_{0})
from the set

\begin{split}\mathcal{\tilde{B}}(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi):=&\left\{(u_{0},v_{0})\textrm{ complying with }\eqref{AssumpAnnex},\textrm{ and with }\int_{\Omega}u_{0}=m,\|v_{0}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\leq A\textrm{ and }\mathcal{F}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})\leq-K(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)\right\},\end{split}

the corresponding solution
(u,v)
of (
67
) has the property

(87)

\mathcal{F}_{h}(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\leq\frac{\mathcal{F}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})}{(1-Ct)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

where
\theta\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
is as given by (
85
).
In particular, for any such solution we have
T_{\textup{max}}<\infty
, i.e.,
(u,v)
blows up in finite time.

Proof.

By reasoning as in
[
70
, Lemma 5.2]
, exploiting Lemma
A.5
and bounds (
70
) and (
71
), we have that the solution
(u,v)
of problem (
67
) emanating from
(u_{0},v_{0})
belongs to the set
\mathcal{S}(m,M,B,\kappa,h)
for all
t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}})
. This implies that Lemma
A.10
guarantees the existence of
c\textsubscript{14}=c\textsubscript{14}(m,M,B,\kappa,h,\chi)
such that the solution
(u,v)
fulfills

(88)

\mathcal{F}_{h}(u,v)\geq-c\textsubscript{14}\,(\mathcal{D}^{\theta}(u,v)+1)\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

To show that (
87
) holds for all
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{\tilde{B}}(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)
, let us define

(89)

K=K(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi):=2c\textsubscript{14}\quad\textrm{and}\quad C=C(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi):=\frac{1-\theta}{2c\textsubscript{14}\theta}.

From the regularity properties of
(u,v)

we derive that

y_{h}(t):=-\mathcal{F}_{h}(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\in C([0,T_{\textup{max}}))\cap C^{1}((0,T_{\textup{max}})).

Moreover
y_{h}(t)
is positive; indeed from
relation (
78
) we have that
y_{h}
is nondecreasing and since
y_{h}(0)\geq K=2c\textsubscript{14}
(recall (
89
)), this leads to

(90)

y_{h}(t)\geq y_{h}(0)\geq 2c\textsubscript{14}>0\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

By manipulating inequality (
88
) and taking into account bound (
90
), we obtain

\mathcal{D}^{\theta}(u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t))\geq\frac{y_{h}(t)}{c\textsubscript{14}}-1\geq\frac{y_{h}(t)}{2c\textsubscript{14}}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

that, inserted in relation (
78
), implies

y^{\prime}_{h}(t)\geq\left(\frac{y_{h}(t)}{2c\textsubscript{14}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}).

After some computations we have

y_{h}(t)\geq y_{h}(0)\left(1-\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}(2c\textsubscript{14})^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}y_{h}^{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}(0)t\right)^{\frac{-\theta}{1-\theta}}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\textup{max}}),

which gives inequality (
87
) after have noted from relation (
90
) (and recalling definition (
89
)) that

\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}(2c\textsubscript{14})^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}y_{h}^{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}(0)\geq\frac{1-\theta}{2c\textsubscript{14}\theta}=C,

so we can conclude.
∎

Finally, we can state the other main result of this Appendix:

Theorem A.12

(Finite-time blow-up
\tau=1
)
.

Let
\Omega=B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\geq 2
and
R>0
, and let
m,A,h,\chi>0
. Then there exist
T(m,A,h,\chi)>0
and
K(m,A,h,\chi)>0
with the property that given any
(u_{0},v_{0})
from the set

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}(m,A,h,\chi)=&\left\{(u_{0},v_{0})\textrm{ complying with }\eqref{AssumpAnnex},\textrm{ and with }\int_{\Omega}u_{0}=m,\|v_{0}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\leq A\textrm{ and }\mathcal{F}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})\leq-K(m,A,h,\chi)\right\},\end{split}

for the corresponding solution
(u,v)
of (
67
) we have
T_{\textup{max}}\leq T(m,A,h,\chi)<\infty
, i.e.,
(u,v)
blows up before or at time
T(m,A,h,\chi)
.

Proof.

In order to remove the auxiliary parameter
\kappa
, that appears in Lemma
A.11
, we have to fix an arbitrary
\kappa>n-2
if
n\geq 3
and a
\kappa>2
if
n=2
. Therefore, we apply Lemma
A.11
to derive the statement for
K(m,A,h,\chi):=K(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)
and
T(m,A,h,\chi):=\frac{1}{C(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)}
with
K(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)
and
C(m,A,\kappa,h,\chi)
as given by Lemma
A.11
.
∎

Remark 7

(Comparison of the time
T
of blow-up: Neumann vs Robin)
.

Let us assume for simplicity
\chi=1
and denote by
T_{N}
and
T_{R}
the time of blow-up in the Neumann and in the Robin case, respectively. By
[
70
, Theorem 1.1]
we know that

T_{N}=\frac{1}{C(m,A,\kappa)}=\frac{2c_{N}\theta}{1-\theta},

where
\theta
is given in (
85
) and
c_{N}:=\max\{\overline{C}(m,A,\kappa),\underline{C}(m,A,\kappa)\}
.
By retracing the previous lemmas from Lemma
A.7
and
paying attention to the dependence of the constants on
h
(see in particular estimate (
83
), we derive that

T_{R}=\frac{1}{C(m,A,\kappa,h)}=\frac{2c_{R}\theta}{1-\theta},

where
c_{R}:=\max\{\overline{C}(m,A,\kappa),\underline{C}(m,A,\kappa)\rho(h)\}
with
\rho(h):=\left(1+\frac{hn}{R}+3h^{2}\right)^{\frac{n+2}{2}}
.
By comparing the values
T_{N}
and
T_{R}
we obtain that

c_{N}:=\max\{\overline{C}(m,A,\kappa),\underline{C}(m,A,\kappa)\}<c_{R}:=\max\{\overline{C}(m,A,\kappa),\underline{C}(m,A,\kappa)\rho(h)\},

this implies that

T_{N}<T_{R},

and they coincide when
h\to 0
since
\rho(h)\to 1
.
