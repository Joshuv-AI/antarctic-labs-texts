Transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair in frail older adults: a cardiogeriatric framework for integrated care
Authors: Rémi Esser, Marc Harboun, Marlène Esteban, Marine Larbaneix, Christine Farges, Guillaume Akuda, Abdelkrim Hamdi, Alejandro Mondragon, Sophie Nisse Durgeat, Olivier Maurou, Vincenzo Palermo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is increasingly recognised as a major cause of morbidity in older adults with heart failure (HF). Conventional surgical repair or replacement carries prohibitive risks in frail patients, leading to therapeutic inertia and progressive loss of function. The development of transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) offers a less invasive, guideline-supported option for selected individuals previously considered inoperable. This narrative review summarises contemporary evidence and explores the specific challenges of T-TEER in frail older adults through a cardiogeriatric lens. It integrates epidemiology, diagnostic complexity, frailty and cognitive assessment, procedural considerations, and multidisciplinary coordination. Randomised trials and real-world registries consistently demonstrate symptomatic and functional improvements with acceptable procedural risk in octogenarians. Drawing on the experience of the Cardiogeriatric Department, Hôpital La Porte Verte (Versailles, France), this review outlines an integrated model embedding geriatrics, advanced nursing, and telemonitoring (Satelia® Cardio) within HF care. This approach aligns with the Age-Friendly Health Systems framework and the ESC 2021 Heart Failure Guidelines, emphasising functional preservation and patient-centred priorities.

Introduction

Right-sided valvular disease, particularly tricuspid regurgitation (TR), has long been regarded as the “forgotten valve disease” [1]. Traditionally viewed as a secondary consequence of elevated pulmonary pressures due to left-sided heart failure (HF) or pulmonary hypertension, TR is now recognised as a distinct entity and an independent predictor of mortality and rehospitalisation in older adults [2, 3]. The prevalence of moderate-to-severe TR rises exponentially with age, affecting approximately 8–10% of individuals over 75 years [4]. In this population, right-sided dysfunction often coexists with atrial fibrillation, renal impairment, and frailty, amplifying both clinical and functional burden.

A neglected burden in ageing populations

Older patients with HF often present with right-sided congestion—oedema, ascites, and fatigue—yet remain undertreated. These symptoms are frequently misattributed to physiological ageing or non-cardiac comorbidities such as venous insufficiency, malnutrition or deconditioning [5]. Consequently, diagnosis is delayed, functional status deteriorates, and repeated admissions ensue. Prognostic analyses confirm that severe untreated TR confers an annual mortality exceeding 10%, independent of left-ventricular ejection fraction [3].

Despite such outcomes, fewer than 1% of elderly patients with significant TR undergo surgical correction, largely due to the prohibitive peri-operative risk (10–15% in octogenarians) and the presence of frailty, sarcopenia, and cognitive impairment [6]. This therapeutic gap has fostered a pervasive sense of nihilism, inconsistent with contemporary principles of value-based, person-centred medicine [7].

The paradigm shift toward percutaneous therapy

The evolution of transcatheter valve therapy has redefined the management of valvular heart disease, first with transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and subsequently mitral edge-to-edge repair. The same philosophy now extends to the tricuspid position with transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER), which achieves leaflet coaptation via a minimally invasive approach [8, 9]. Devices such as TriClip™ and PASCAL™ enable effective regurgitation reduction under conscious sedation, substantially lowering procedural trauma [10].

Early clinical series demonstrated significant improvements in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, six-minute walk test, and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) scores [9, 10]. These outcomes are particularly meaningful in frail older adults whose treatment goals prioritise comfort, mobility, and independence over longevity alone.

The cardiogeriatric perspective

Traditional cardiology frameworks focus predominantly on anatomical correction and haemodynamic improvement. In contrast, the cardiogeriatric approach acknowledges that outcomes in older adults are strongly modulated by frailty, cognition, nutrition, and social context [11, 12]. Without integrating these parameters, the therapeutic balance may tilt toward overtreatment of robust elderly individuals and undertreatment of frail yet motivated patients.

At the Cardiogeriatric Department, Hôpital La Porte Verte (Versailles, France), a structured model has been developed to embed geriatric assessment within cardiovascular decision-making. The programme integrates acute cardiology, geriatrics, day-hospital follow-up, and digital telemonitoring via Satelia® Cardio. This structure supports early identification of functional decline, collaborative treatment planning, and post-procedural continuity of care—principles entirely consistent with the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework [13]. The term “cardiogeriatric model” refers here to an integrated framework combining cardiovascular expertise with systematic geriatric assessment, functional evaluation and multidisciplinary follow-up.

Rationale for this review

Recent landmark trials—TRILUMINATE Pivotal and CLASP TR Early Feasibility—have consolidated the clinical value of T-TEER in high-risk populations [9, 10]. However, evidence addressing geriatric domains such as frailty, cognition and quality of life remains limited. Current literature focuses primarily on procedural endpoints rather than person-centred outcomes [14].

Accordingly, this narrative review pursues three objectives:

To synthesise the latest evidence supporting T-TEER in elderly and frail patients;To delineate the interaction between frailty, cognitive function and clinical decision-making;To describe a cardiogeriatric framework integrating T-TEER into multidisciplinary heart-failure care pathways.

Epidemiology and diagnostic challenges in frail older adults

Epidemiological burden

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is increasingly recognised as a major public-health issue, especially among older adults with multimorbidity. Epidemiological data estimate a moderate-to-severe TR prevalence of approximately 0.8% in the general population, rising to > 5% in individuals over 75 years [15]. Unlike left-sided valvular disease, which often presents with earlier symptoms or murmurs, TR evolves silently and is typically under-diagnosed until advanced right-heart failure develops [16].

This age-related rise is largely driven by the accumulation of atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and structural remodelling linked to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)—all conditions highly prevalent in geriatric cohorts. The interplay between frailty, volume overload, and diuretic resistance further amplifies the haemodynamic burden, leading to a progressive decline in functional status and quality of life.

Diagnostic under-recognition

Despite its prognostic impact, TR remains frequently overlooked in both community and hospital practice. Auscultation is unreliable in older adults due to chest wall changes and concomitant murmurs. Echocardiography remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, yet the right-sided chambers are often under-evaluated in standard reports [17].

The complexity of assessing right-ventricular function, which is highly load-dependent, and the lack of widely adopted severity staging for right-heart failure contribute to substantial diagnostic heterogeneity. Furthermore, the overlapping symptoms with other geriatric syndromes — such as fatigue, oedema, and dyspnoea on exertion — frequently delay appropriate cardiology referral.

Impact of frailty and comorbidity

Frailty compounds diagnostic uncertainty. In many older adults, the functional decline associated with right-heart failure is attributed to “normal ageing” rather than disease progression. Cognitive impairment or communication difficulties often limit self-reporting of symptoms, while atypical presentations (e.g., anorexia, falls, or confusion) may precede overt congestion.

The coexistence of renal dysfunction, anaemia, and polypharmacy makes interpreting natriuretic peptide levels or weight fluctuations more difficult, further blunting clinical suspicion. These challenges underscore the need for integrated cardiogeriatric assessment, combining echocardiographic, biological, and functional markers into a holistic diagnostic framework.

Under-representation in clinical trials

Older adults with frailty or multimorbidity are markedly under-represented in major TR and heart-failure trials [18]. Consequently, the evidence base guiding intervention timing, medical management, or post-procedural outcomes is disproportionately derived from younger and fitter populations.

The result is a widening “evidence–practice gap”, where older patients are less likely to receive referral for specialised evaluation or advanced therapies such as T-TEER. Recognising this imbalance, current ESC and AHA recommendations advocate a multidisciplinary heart-team approach, integrating geriatric principles into clinical decision-making.

Frailty, cognition and therapeutic options

Frailty as a determinant of outcomes

Frailty represents a central determinant of outcome in older adults with tricuspid regurgitation (TR). It reflects decreased physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors, beyond chronological age alone [19, 20]. The overlap between frailty and right-sided heart failure symptoms—fatigue, anorexia, oedema, and exercise intolerance—makes diagnosis and follow-up complex. Several studies have demonstrated that frail cardiac patients experience longer hospital stays, higher rates of readmission, and poorer survival after intervention [21].

In the context of advanced TR, frailty directly influences decision-making regarding the appropriateness and intensity of care. The Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) provide reproducible assessments that can be integrated into heart-team discussions to balance procedural benefit against functional decline [22]. Importantly, frailty is dynamic and may improve after volume optimisation, rehabilitation, or correction of anaemia, emphasising the need for reassessment rather than a one-time label of “inoperability”.

Cognitive vulnerability and decision-making

Cognitive impairment is prevalent among patients with severe TR and chronic heart failure, affecting nearly 40–50% of elderly individuals [23]. Executive dysfunction and attention deficits hinder adherence to therapy and complicate shared decision-making. Cognitive assessment should therefore be standard in cardiogeriatric evaluation, ideally through brief yet validated tools such as the Mini-Cog or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).

These assessments not only inform capacity for consent but also help tailor communication with caregivers, ensuring that therapeutic decisions respect autonomy while maintaining safety. Integrating cognitive and frailty data into multidisciplinary meetings has been shown to improve alignment between treatment intensity and patient-centred goals.

Medical management and optimisation

Pharmacological treatment remains the cornerstone of symptomatic management in TR, though evidence-based options are limited. Loop diuretics remain the first-line therapy for volume overload; however, older adults often exhibit diuretic resistance due to renal impairment or neurohormonal activation. Combination therapy with thiazide-like agents or the use of sequential nephron blockade can enhance efficacy.

Emerging data support the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in both reduced and preserved ejection-fraction heart failure, including those with right-sided congestion, with favourable renal and diuretic-sparing effects [24].

Close monitoring for hypotension, electrolyte imbalance, and renal decline is essential. Optimisation of blood pressure, rate control for atrial fibrillation, and correction of iron deficiency should accompany diuretic therapy. Yet, pharmacological management alone rarely halts the progression of TR-related right-heart dysfunction in the elderly.

Percutaneous therapies and patient selection

For patients deemed unsuitable for conventional surgery, transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) has emerged as a viable minimally invasive alternative. The pivotal TRILUMINATE Pivotal Trial demonstrated that T-TEER reduces TR severity, improves quality of life, and lowers rates of heart-failure hospitalisation compared with medical therapy alone [9].

In frail older patients, the cardiogeriatric approach enables nuanced patient selection—considering not only anatomical suitability but also frailty trajectory, cognitive status, nutritional condition, and social support.

Procedural tolerance is generally excellent, with low periprocedural mortality (< 2%), though long-term benefit depends on comprehensive post-intervention rehabilitation and optimisation of comorbidities. The inclusion of physiotherapists, dietitians, and advanced practice nurses ensures that recovery extends beyond hemodynamic correction to functional restoration.

Rehabilitation and functional trajectory

Post-procedural recovery must address both cardiovascular and geriatric dimensions. Cardiac rehabilitation programmes adapted to older adults, integrating resistance training and nutritional support, have been shown to enhance functional capacity and autonomy. Early mobilisation prevents deconditioning, while cognitive stimulation and caregiver education promote sustained benefit.

Frailty trajectories should be monitored longitudinally through telemonitored weight, symptom reporting, and physical-activity metrics. Digital health platforms such as Satelia® Cardio can assist in early detection of congestion relapse and facilitate medication adjustment in the community setting.

Evidence, procedural Care, and the cardiogeriatric model

Evidence base for transcatheter tricuspid repair

The evidence supporting transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) has grown rapidly in recent years, shifting the perception of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) from a neglected comorbidity to a treatable disease entity. Early single-arm feasibility studies, such as TriValve and TRILUMINATE, first established the safety of the approach, demonstrating procedural success rates exceeding 85% and low in-hospital mortality (< 2%) [14].

More recently, the TRILUMINATE Pivotal Trial, a randomised controlled study comparing T-TEER with medical therapy, confirmed significant reductions in TR severity and improvements in quality of life and functional capacity at 12 months [9]. Importantly, symptom improvement was observed even in patients with persistent moderate TR, suggesting that the benefit extends beyond complete anatomical correction to include haemodynamic stabilisation and neurohormonal relief.

Despite these encouraging results, older adults remain markedly under-represented: in most registries, mean age does not exceed 78 years, and patients with cognitive impairment, dependency, or advanced frailty are typically excluded. This gap reinforces the need for real-world, cardiogeriatric data to guide implementation in high-risk, elderly populations.

A key limitation of current evidence is that major T-TEER trials, including the TRILUMINATE Pivotal study, excluded patients with pacemaker- or ICD lead–associated tricuspid regurgitation. As device-related TR is common in older adults, these exclusions restrict the generalizability of trial findings to real-world geriatric populations.

Procedural optimisation and peri-operative strategy

T-TEER in frail older patients requires meticulous peri-procedural planning. Pre-intervention optimisation includes correction of anaemia, management of volume overload, and assessment of frailty and nutritional status. In La Porte Verte’s cardiogeriatric practice, patients undergo a structured pre-procedural day-hospital evaluation, where cardiologists, geriatricians, anaesthetists, and nurses jointly review cognitive and functional scores before heart-team validation.

Procedures are typically performed under general anaesthesia or deep sedation, with real-time transoesophageal echocardiographic guidance. The presence of a geriatrician in the peri-operative care team helps anticipate delirium, prevent immobilisation, and coordinate early rehabilitation. Length of stay after T-TEER averages 3 to 4 days in our model, substantially shorter than the national average for cardiac surgery in octogenarians (> 10 days) [25].

Early mobilisation, electrolyte monitoring, and rapid adjustment of diuretic regimens are prioritised to prevent decompensation. This multidisciplinary vigilance reduces functional decline and ensures that haemodynamic success translates into tangible patient benefit.

Post-procedural follow-up and telemonitoring integration

Post-intervention recovery extends well beyond discharge. The cardiogeriatric framework integrates structured day-hospital re-assessment within 4–6 weeks, focusing on medication titration, iron optimisation, and physical reconditioning. Thereafter, telemonitoring via the Satelia® Cardio platform enables daily symptom reporting and early detection of congestion relapse.

Alerts are triaged by advanced practice nurses, who coordinate with the attending cardiologist to adjust oral diuretics or trigger a day-hospital infusion when necessary. This hybrid system combines digital surveillance with human continuity, aligning perfectly with ESC 2021 recommendations for integrated, multidisciplinary heart-failure management.

Beyond safety, telemonitoring promotes empowerment: patients and caregivers better understand symptom fluctuations and engage actively in self-management. Among our institutional cohort, this proactive strategy has markedly reduced emergency-department visits and facilitated timely diuretic interventions, illustrating how digital cardiogeriatrics can operationalise prevention in real-world practice.

Education, rehabilitation, and transitional care

Educational reinforcement represents a cornerstone of the cardiogeriatric model. Nurse-led sessions address medication adherence, nutrition, and recognition of early congestion signs. Physiotherapists design personalised rehabilitation programmes adapted to functional capacity, balancing endurance and strength training to counteract sarcopenia.

In parallel, social workers liaise with community health networks to secure continuity of care, including home-hospitalisation (HAD) and nursing-home partnerships. This ecosystem ensures that vulnerable patients remain supported within their living environment while maintaining direct access to cardiogeriatric expertise for acute interventions.

Future directions, conclusion and full reference list

Future directions and research needs

The management of severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) in frail older adults stands at a crossroads between interventional innovation and geriatric realism. The evidence now supports transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) as a safe and effective option for carefully selected high-risk patients, yet major knowledge gaps remain concerning long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and quality-of-life trajectories in those over 85 years [26].

Future research must adopt a multidimensional lens encompassing frailty, cognition, nutrition, and social vulnerability. Prospective registries and pragmatic trials should systematically collect geriatric variables—ADL, IADL, CFS, MoCA, nutritional scores—to characterise heterogeneity and guide personalised therapeutic goals. Integration of digital health tools (telemonitoring, remote assessment, electronic frailty indices) may facilitate continuous phenotyping and early detection of decline, allowing proactive adaptation of management strategies.

A further research frontier concerns the implementation science of cardiogeriatric models: identifying which organisational configurations most effectively reduce functional decline, rehospitalisation, and institutionalisation. Comparative analyses between day-hospital, home-hospitalisation, and telehealth-first strategies are crucial to determine scalability and resource optimisation across health systems.

Ethical and person-centred considerations

The expansion of interventional options for elderly and frail patients raises pressing ethical questions. Shared decision-making must reconcile technological feasibility with individual goals of care, quality of life, and autonomy. Over-medicalisation near the end of life risks displacing comfort and dignity.

Embedding structured geriatric and palliative evaluation within the cardiology pathway ensures that procedures such as T-TEER remain aligned with patient-centred priorities, rather than procedural enthusiasm. The involvement of family caregivers, social services, and ethics committees is essential to sustain transparency and trust.

Translational potential and international scalability

The cardiogeriatric pathway implemented at Hôpital La Porte Verte (Versailles, France) exemplifies how integrated, multidisciplinary frameworks can be operationalised in real-world European contexts. Its modular structure—acute care, day-hospital, telemonitoring, and home-based support—makes it adaptable to various national health systems.

Such models are not limited to TR or heart failure management: they offer a blueprint for managing complex multimorbidity in ageing populations, uniting hospital expertise with community-based continuity.

International collaboration through registries and exchange networks could accelerate adoption, refine protocols, and strengthen the evidence base for cardiogeriatrics as a subspecialty bridging internal medicine, cardiology, and gerontology.

Conclusion

The emergence of transcatheter therapies for tricuspid regurgitation has opened a new frontier in structural cardiology—one that must now evolve through a geriatric lens.

Frailty, cognitive impairment, and social vulnerability are not contraindications but parameters for individualised care planning. Embedding these dimensions within procedural decision-making ensures that interventional success translates into meaningful outcomes: preserved function, autonomy, and quality of life. While ESC guidelines provide procedural recommendations, they often overlook frailty and cognitive impairment, which are crucial determinants of decision-making in this population.

The cardiogeriatric model—combining multidisciplinary evaluation, minimally invasive intervention, and structured telemonitored follow-up—offers a pragmatic, reproducible, and ethically sound approach to caring for the oldest and frailest patients. By bridging geriatric and cardiovascular medicine, this model exemplifies the transformation towards value-based, person-centred, and age-friendly healthcare in the 21 st century. While based on a single French cardiogeriatric centre, the modular structure of this pathway—combining acute, day-hospital and telemonitoring components—is adaptable to various health systems.
