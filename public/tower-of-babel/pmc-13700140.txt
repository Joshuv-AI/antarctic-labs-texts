Discipline-associated locomotor coordination and asymmetry patterns in trained Quarter Horses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Renata Farinelli de Siqueira, Lavínia Andressa Brito, Isabela Marques Figueiredo, Larissa Santos dos Anjos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLocomotor asymmetry in horses is traditionally interpreted as a potential indicator of pain-related lameness. However, objective gait analysis has shown that measurable asymmetries may also occur in horses without overt clinical lameness. Whether trained horses from different athletic disciplines show discipline-associated asymmetry patterns under standardized straight-line conditions remains unclear. This study examined locomotor asymmetry and vertical displacement asymmetry in Quarter Horses competing in three Western disciplines: barrel racing, reining, and ranch sorting.MethodsNinety adult Quarter Horses were initially recruited, and 57 horses were retained after signal quality control and stride validation, with 19 horses per discipline. Horses were evaluated in-hand at walk and trot on a straight 100 m sandy track. Locomotor data were recorded using ProMove-mini inertial measurement units from the Equi-Pro system at 200 Hz, positioned on the head, withers, sacrum, and limbs. Vertical displacement asymmetry was assessed using Mindiff and Maxdiff of the head, withers, and sacrum. Interlimb asymmetry was evaluated using the Symmetry Index, Asymmetry Ratio, and absolute side-to-side difference. Discipline effects were tested using horse-level averaged values, with discipline as the main fixed factor and gait velocity included as a covariate when appropriate. Static morphometric traits were analyzed using principal component analysis, and exploratory morpho-functional associations were assessed using cross-validated Elastic Net models.ResultsDiscipline-associated differences were observed in selected variables of vertical displacement asymmetry, spatiotemporal, angular, and vertical acceleration. Reining horses showed greater head and withers vertical displacement asymmetry than barrel racing horses, whereas ranch sorting horses showed greater hindlimb absolute side-to-side differences in acceleration and angular range-of-motion variables. Several effects were gait- and segment-dependent, indicating that asymmetry patterns were not uniform across locomotor domains. Morphometric principal components did not clearly separate horses by discipline, and morpho-functional models exhibited limited global explanatory power. Elastic Net models identified moderate associations only for selected outcomes, with cross-validated R² values ranging from 0.35 to 0.42.ConclusionsTrained Quarter Horses from different Western disciplines exhibited discipline-associated patterns of locomotor and vertical displacement asymmetry when evaluated under standardized in-hand conditions. Static morphometric variation showed limited and outcome-specific associations with locomotor variables, suggesting that conformation alone did not explain the observed locomotor differences. These findings support the interpretation of locomotor asymmetry as a multidimensional biomechanical phenomenon that should be considered in relation to discipline context, clinical assessment, and individual locomotor history.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05766-4.

Introduction

Locomotor symmetry has traditionally been considered a key indicator of soundness in horses, particularly during symmetrical gaits such as the walk and trot. Clinical gait assessment has long been based on the premise that symmetrical vertical movement of the head and pelvis reflects balanced limb loading and coordinated musculoskeletal function [1]. In this context, deviations from symmetry are often viewed as signs of pain-related lameness and are commonly used as diagnostic criteria in clinical locomotor exams.

However, objective gait analysis studies have shown that measurable locomotor asymmetries are common in clinically sound horses, indicating that asymmetry alone is not definitive evidence of lameness [2–4]. Locomotor asymmetry may stem from inherent neuromechanical coordination patterns or motor laterality rather than pathological load redistribution, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of objective gait asymmetry metrics [5, 6]. Studies using inertial measurement units (IMUs) and other quantitative motion analysis systems have found low-level asymmetries in head and pelvic movement in horses without clinical signs of lameness [7–9]. Interpreting locomotor asymmetry is further complicated by the biomechanical context of locomotion. Factors such as surface characteristics, speed, rider influence, and circular motion can alter patterns of vertical displacement and interlimb forces, thereby changing how asymmetry is expressed [10]. Additionally, the interaction between axial motion and limb mechanics contributes to whole-body locomotor coordination, suggesting that asymmetry should be understood within an integrated axial–appendicular biomechanical framework rather than through isolated limb-based measures [11]. Establishing thresholds for clinically meaningful asymmetry remains challenging, as horses considered sound may still exceed commonly used objective symmetry thresholds, emphasizing the importance of contextual biomechanical interpretation [4, 12]. Exercise conditions, such as circular motion, can introduce systematic biomechanical asymmetries even in sound horses due to centripetal forces and body lean, complicating the interpretation of objective symmetry metrics [13, 14].

Beyond environmental and mechanical influences, training history and discipline-specific demands can also shape locomotor organization in athletic horses [15]. Western performance disciplines expose horses to distinct mechanical contexts that may influence neuromechanical coordination strategies. Barrel racing emphasizes rapid acceleration and tight turns; reining requires controlled deceleration and highly collected movements; and ranch sorting involves repeated directional changes during cattle handling. Over time, repeated exposure to these discipline-specific demands may be associated with distinct locomotor patterns, although such adaptations cannot be inferred directly from cross-sectional data.

Historically, conformation has often been considered a factor in locomotor mechanics in horses. Relationships between morphological traits and specific kinematic variables have been documented in various breed-specific studies, suggesting that anatomical structure may influence certain aspects of movement [16]. However, the extent to which static morphological characteristics predict dynamic locomotor patterns remains uncertain. Increasingly, locomotion is viewed as an emergent property arising from interactions among skeletal conformation, neuromuscular control, and adaptive responses to training and mechanical loading [17].

Experimental studies have shown that higher orthopedic pain is associated with greater movement asymmetry, supporting the clinical relevance of asymmetry metrics and underscoring their multifactorial nature [18].

Despite advances in objective locomotor analysis, a significant knowledge gap remains. Most studies investigating locomotor asymmetry in horses have focused on technology validation, lameness-detection thresholds, or comparisons among measurement systems. Relatively few studies have examined whether training discipline is associated with systematic differences in asymmetry patterns when horses are evaluated under standardized straight-line locomotor conditions.

Differences in vertical displacement asymmetry and limb kinematics may be expected across Western performance disciplines, exposing horses to distinct mechanical contexts that may be linked to variations in locomotor organization. Barrel racing features rapid acceleration and barrel turns; reining requires controlled deceleration, collection, spins, and sliding stops; and ranch sorting involves frequent short-radius redirections and lateral adjustments during cattle handling. These tasks may influence how load is distributed between the forelimbs and hindlimbs and how the upper body responds to limb support. Although these maneuvers were not performed during the present assessment, repeated exposure to discipline-specific mechanical demands may be associated with measurable differences in standardized straight-line locomotion.

Therefore, the present study examined locomotor and vertical displacement asymmetry in trained Quarter Horses competing in three Western disciplines: barrel racing, reining, and ranch sorting. High-resolution inertial sensor data were combined with morphometric characterization and multivariate statistical modeling to assess discipline-associated differences in interlimb asymmetry indices, limb kinematics, vertical acceleration variables, and upper-body vertical displacement asymmetry during standardized in-hand walk and trot.

We hypothesized that trained Quarter Horses from barrel racing, reining, and ranch sorting would differ in selected spatiotemporal, angular, vertical acceleration, and vertical displacement asymmetry variables during standardized in-hand walk and trot. We further hypothesized that static morphometric variables would have limited ability to explain these locomotor differences.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional observational study compared locomotor asymmetry, vertical displacement asymmetry, and morpho-functional relationships among Quarter Horses trained in three Western disciplines under standardized in-hand conditions.

Study population

A total of 90 adult Quarter Horses actively competing in barrel racing, reining, or ranch sorting were initially recruited. All horses were privately owned and trained at facilities in São Paulo State, Brazil. Recruitment relied on convenience sampling through contact with owners, trainers, and competition yards participating in official Brazilian Quarter Horse Association events. Inclusion criteria required active training and competition in one of the three target disciplines for at least one uninterrupted year. Accordingly, the study population should be interpreted as a field-based convenience sample of trained Western performance Quarter Horses rather than a randomly selected population.

Horses were excluded if they showed overt lameness, defined as AAEP grade ≥ 2/5 on visual in-hand assessment at walk or trot on a straight line, or if pain, swelling, joint effusion, or musculoskeletal abnormalities were detected on palpation.

After signal quality control, velocity filtering, and stride validation procedures (described below), 57 horses (19 per discipline) provided valid datasets for both walk and trot and were included in the final asymmetry analysis.

Horses remained in their usual management and feeding systems at their home facilities, and no experimental interventions beyond in-hand locomotor assessment and sensor placement were performed. No adverse events were observed.

No randomization was performed because horses were allocated to groups based on their pre-existing training discipline. Blinding was not feasible during data collection because discipline and horse identity were evident to the investigators; however, signal processing and statistical analyses were conducted using standardized computational pipelines. A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed because this was an observational field study based on the availability of eligible, actively competing horses within the target disciplines.

Experimental protocol

Horses were evaluated in-hand on a straight 100 m sandy track (out-and-back 50 m). Trials were conducted at controlled speeds corresponding to walk (1.2–1.8 m·s⁻¹) and trot (3.0–4.0 m·s⁻¹). Velocity was continuously monitored using the GNSS sensor integrated with the withers-mounted IMU to ensure that locomotion remained within the predefined speed ranges.

Each horse was evaluated once during the same assessment session. For each gait, horses were led in hand along a 50-m straight track using an out-and-back protocol, yielding approximately 100 m of recorded locomotion per gait. At least 20 consecutive valid strides were required for inclusion. Stride-level data were first extracted from each valid stride and then averaged within each horse-gait combination, yielding one representative value per horse, gait, limb, or segment for the statistical analyses.

Instrumentation

Inertial measurement units from the Equi-Pro system, using ProMove-mini sensors (Inertia Technology, The Netherlands; sampling frequency: 200 Hz), were attached to the poll, withers, sacrum, and proximal lateral regions of the metacarpals and metatarsals of all limbs, following the manufacturer’s recommended placement protocol. Sensors recorded triaxial acceleration and angular velocity. Raw data were exported for offline processing.

Signal processing

Signal processing was performed offline in Python using the NumPy, SciPy, and Statsmodels libraries. Acceleration signals were low-pass filtered with a zero-lag fourth-order Butterworth filter to remove high-frequency sensor noise while preserving locomotion-related components. Cutoff frequencies were set to 20 Hz for limb-mounted sensors, and 15 Hz for axial sensors, consistent with the frequency content typically reported for equine locomotion signals from inertial measurement units. Angular velocity signals were filtered with a 25 Hz cutoff.

Sensor orientation was estimated using the Madgwick orientation filter (β = 0.1), a quaternion-based sensor fusion algorithm that combines accelerometer and gyroscope signals to estimate orientation while reducing gyroscope integration drift [19]. The resulting orientation estimates were used to rotate all signals into an earth-fixed coordinate system, with the vertical axis aligned with gravity.

Vertical displacement of axial segments was obtained by double integrating the gravity-corrected vertical acceleration signal. Because integrating acceleration signals can cause low-frequency drift, several complementary drift-control procedures were applied. These included high-pass filtering at 0.10 Hz to remove slow baseline drift, stride-wise zero-mean displacement constraints to stabilize stride-level trajectories, and cubic spline detrending with knots placed every 3 s to correct longer-term signal drift. This combined approach has been widely adopted in inertial sensor–based biomechanical analyses to obtain stable displacement estimates from accelerometer data. The vertical axis of the earth-fixed reference frame was defined using the estimated gravity vector from sensor fusion, thereby separating gravitational and dynamic acceleration components before displacement integration.

Stride segmentation and data quality control

Stride segmentation was performed independently for each limb using an algorithm-based detection approach applied to vertical acceleration and pitch angular velocity signals from the inertial sensors. The stride detection approach was based on previously validated inertial sensor algorithms for equine gait segmentation. Foot-on and foot-off events were identified from characteristic extrema in the vertical acceleration signal, combined with changes in the pitch angular velocity profile, following previously described approaches for IMU-based stride detection in horses [20].

To ensure the physiological plausibility of detected strides, duration constraints were applied to the stance phase. Acceptable stance durations were set to 0.40–1.10 s for walk and 0.18–0.45 s for trot, consistent with values reported in prior inertial sensor studies of equine locomotion [10, 20]. Strides recorded outside the predefined velocity range or containing transient acceleration artifacts exceeding 0.5 m·s⁻² were excluded from further analysis.

For each horse-gait combination, limb-specific kinematic variables were averaged across all valid strides to obtain a single representative value per limb for statistical analysis.

During data collection, trials were repeated when the Equi-Pro application indicated abnormal signal behavior or possible displacement of the sacral sensor. This was particularly common in horses with pronounced gluteal musculature or greater croup height relative to withers height, where precise alignment and stabilization of the sacral sensor were more challenging. Recordings with persistent sacral sensor instability or poor signal quality were excluded during quality control.

Interlimb asymmetry was calculated separately for the forelimbs (LF vs. RF) and hindlimbs (LH vs. RH). For each horse and gait, asymmetry indices were computed from limb-specific summary values of the stride-averaged kinematic variables.

For statistical analyses, a single asymmetry value per horse, gait, and variable was computed by averaging across all valid strides. These indices were then used to evaluate discipline-associated differences in locomotor asymmetry and to characterize multidimensional patterns of asymmetry across locomotor domains.

Morphometric assessment

Linear morphometric measurements were obtained with horses standing square on a level, non-slip surface, with all four limbs fully weight-bearing and the head in a neutral position. Measurements were taken with a flexible measuring tape by the same trained evaluator. Angular morphometrics were obtained from standardized right-lateral photographs. Before imaging, anatomical landmarks were identified by palpation and marked with colored adhesive markers. Photographs were taken with the camera positioned perpendicular to the horse’s sagittal plane at a fixed distance, with the lens approximately aligned with the mid-thoracic region. Joint and segmental angles were measured in ImageJ using predefined vertex and arm landmarks. The angular variables included scapular inclination, scapulohumeral, humeroradial, metacarpophalangeal, coxofemoral, femorotibial, tibiometatarsal, and metatarsophalangeal angles. The measurements followed the standardized scheme depicted in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed separately for walk and trot to avoid gait-related confounding. For each horse, stride-level variables obtained after quality control were averaged within gait, limb pair, or axial segment, yielding one representative value per horse for each outcome. Therefore, the horse was treated as the experimental unit for the primary discipline comparisons.

Effects of discipline on locomotor variables were evaluated using linear models or analysis of covariance, with discipline as the primary fixed factor. Mean gait velocity was included as a covariate when appropriate to control for speed-related effects on locomotor parameters. When model assumptions were violated, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were used. Pairwise comparisons between disciplines were conducted using Tukey-adjusted post hoc tests for parametric models or Dunn-type post hoc comparisons for non-parametric analyses.

To account for multiple testing across the set of locomotor variables, the false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with the significance threshold set at q < 0.05. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²p) for parametric models. For non-parametric analyses, effect magnitude was interpreted using rank-based group differences and post hoc contrasts.

Interlimb asymmetry was evaluated using three complementary metrics: Symmetry Index (SI), Asymmetry Ratio (AR), and absolute side-to-side difference (Δ). Because ratio-based metrics can be sensitive to denominator instability, particularly when the denominator is near zero or negative, interpretation focused primarily on Δ as the most stable measure of asymmetry magnitude. SI and AR were retained as complementary descriptors of directional and proportional asymmetry.

Morpho-functional relationships between conformational traits and locomotor variables were examined with Elastic Net regression models. Morphometric variables were standardized before modeling. Models were fit with 10-fold cross-validation to balance model stability and variable selection while reducing the risk of overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using cross-validated coefficients of determination (R²_cv). These analyses were treated as exploratory and used to assess whether static morphology explained selected locomotor outcomes.

Model assumptions were verified by visually inspecting residual and quantile–quantile plots to assess normality and homoscedasticity. Influential observations were evaluated using Cook’s distance. All signal processing and statistical analyses were performed in Python (version 3.11.2) with the NumPy, SciPy, Statsmodels, and scikit-learn libraries.

Full outputs of the statistical models are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

Study population

Of the 90 initially recruited horses, 57 met all predefined criteria for signal quality, velocity, and stride validation and were included in the final analysis. During data collection, trials were repeated whenever the Equi-Pro application indicated abnormal signal behavior or possible sensor displacement. Only recordings that met the predefined quality, velocity, and stride-validation criteria were retained. Exclusions were mainly due to insufficient valid strides within the required velocity range, transient signal artifacts, incomplete recordings, or persistent sensor instability.

Discipline was modeled as a fixed factor, with the horse as the experimental unit. Mean velocity did not differ significantly across disciplines at walk (p > 0.10) or trot (p > 0.10), indicating comparable locomotor conditions across groups.

Demographic and morphometric characteristics of the horses retained after quality control are presented in Table 1. After QC, 19 horses per discipline were included in the final analysis. Age was similar across groups, whereas body mass and height at the withers differed. Sex distribution included mares, geldings, and stallions in all groups, except for Ranch Sorting, which had no geldings after QC.

Interlimb asymmetry

Interlimb asymmetry was assessed using three complementary metrics: Symmetry Index (SI), Asymmetry Ratio (AR), and absolute side-to-side difference (Δ). Because ratio-based metrics may be sensitive to denominator instability when variables are near zero or negative, the primary interpretation focused on Δ as the most stable descriptor of the magnitude of asymmetry. SI and AR were retained as complementary descriptors of directional and proportional asymmetry and are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Discipline-associated differences in Δ were observed for selected forelimb and hindlimb variables (Table 3). The most consistent effects were observed in the hindlimbs, particularly for vertical acceleration and angular range-of-motion variables. At walk, Ranch Sorting horses showed greater hindlimb Δ values for az_range, az_min, rom_yaw, and rom_roll than the other disciplines. At trot, a similar pattern was observed, with Ranch Sorting horses showing greater hindlimb Δ values for az_range, az_min, rom_roll, rom_yaw, and adduction_deg.

Principal Component Analysis of angular and linear morphometric variables revealed broad overlap across disciplines. Discussion

The present study provides objective data showing that trained Quarter Horses competing in different Western disciplines display discipline-associated patterns of locomotor asymmetry and vertical displacement asymmetry when assessed on a straight track. Rather than a uniform locomotor profile, horses from different disciplines exhibited distinct locomotor patterns despite sharing the same breed and broadly similar morphometric features. These results suggest that locomotor organization in athletic horses may be associated with both structural traits and discipline-related movement demands, although causal mechanisms cannot be established from the present cross-sectional design.

Locomotor asymmetry as functional variability

Previous research has shown that subtle asymmetries often appear in healthy horses and may simply reflect natural biological variation rather than signs of problems [7, 9, 21]. Objective gait analysis using inertial sensors has demonstrated that vertical displacement asymmetries exceeding the previously established lameness thresholds can occur in sound horses during straight-line movement [22]. This highlights the importance of interpreting asymmetry patterns in context. Our findings build on these results, indicating that locomotor asymmetry can differ among groups of horses competing in different disciplines. Notably, these asymmetries were identified in horses without clinical signs of lameness and under controlled conditions. Therefore, differences between disciplines should not be automatically interpreted as signs of dysfunction but rather as potential indicators of discipline-associated locomotor organization. These differences may reflect, at least in part, training history, discipline-related mechanical demands, individual laterality, and other management-related factors. Western disciplines such as barrel racing, reining, and ranch sorting involve directional loads, rapid acceleration and deceleration, and asymmetric maneuvers during performance. These mechanical demands provide a plausible context for the discipline-associated differences observed in both upper-body and limb variables. Overall, locomotion in trained horses should be interpreted as the result of multiple interacting factors, including conformation, laterality, clinical status, management, and training history. From this perspective, grouping horses by discipline may reveal repeatable locomotor profiles associated with different performance contexts, without proving long-term training adaptation.

Vertical displacement asymmetry

Variations in vertical displacement asymmetry variables, especially head Mindiff and sacral Maxdiff, suggest that discipline groups may differ in upper-body movement patterns. Upper-body motion is closely related to equine locomotor coordination, reflecting the interaction among limb support, trunk movement, and dynamic balance during locomotion [1, 11].

In this study, differences in vertical displacement asymmetry across disciplines may reflect variations in trunk–limb coordination associated with discipline, individual movement patterns, and repeated exposure to different mechanical contexts. For instance, reining horses showed greater vertical head asymmetry than barrel racing horses, which may align with the collected movements and controlled deceleration patterns typical of reining. By contrast, the increased sacral displacement asymmetry observed in ranch sorting horses may align with the frequent directional changes and active hindlimb engagement typical of cattle-handling tasks.

Importantly, these interpretations should be regarded as biomechanical hypotheses rather than direct causal explanations because the present study was observational and did not assess locomotion during discipline-specific tasks.

Distal limb mechanics and discipline-associated locomotor patterns

Differences in distal-limb kinematics further suggest that locomotor coordination varies across disciplines. Barrel-racing horses showed shorter stance durations and higher acceleration peaks than other groups. These features align with locomotor patterns used for impulsive propulsion and rapid directional changes. Such patterns may be biomechanically suited to rapid force generation and redirection, although elastic energy storage was not directly measured [21]. By contrast, reining horses exhibited longer stance phases and moderate acceleration amplitudes. Increased ground contact time may support controlled deceleration and postural adjustments during maneuvers, emphasizing balance and accuracy. Ranch sorting horses displayed the widest ranges of hindlimb acceleration and angular motion. These patterns align with the locomotor demands of agility and rapid redirection, enabling quick responses to unpredictable cattle movements.

This interpretation applies specifically to horses assessed in-hand on a straight line at walk and trot and should not be directly extrapolated to ridden or discipline-associated maneuvers. Overall, these findings suggest that discipline-specific locomotor differences are expressed through distinct combinations of stance duration, acceleration amplitude, and limb angular excursion.

Multidimensional asymmetry

An important finding of this study is that asymmetry patterns are multidimensional, involving both axial and appendicular components. Differences were observed across asymmetry measures, including symmetry indices, asymmetry ratios, and displacement differences. This supports the idea that locomotor asymmetry cannot be fully characterized by a single parameter. In equine biomechanics, understanding asymmetry within a whole-body coordination framework—considering limb movements and upper-body dynamics—is important [1, 11]. Our results are consistent with this view, showing that discipline-associated locomotor patterns involved both upper-body displacement and limb kinematic variables. Previous gait analysis studies have revealed that subtle asymmetries often occur in clinically sound horses, reflecting natural variation rather than disease [2, 8, 9, 23]. Data from inertial measurement units indicate that vertical displacement asymmetries can occur in clinically sound horses during straight-line trot, suggesting that asymmetry magnitude alone cannot reliably distinguish functional variations from lameness [10, 24]. Additionally, training discipline may be one of several factors that influence how these asymmetries are expressed in athletic horses.

Interaction between morphology and locomotor organization

Morphometric analysis indicated that structural features accounted for only a small share of the variation in locomotor behavior. Principal component analysis identified morphological axes related to body proportions and limb alignment; however, the predictive models showed only limited to moderate associations with selected locomotor variables. These findings suggest that morphology may shape the structural context in which locomotion is expressed, while locomotor patterns within this framework may be linked to training history, individual laterality, management, and other discipline-related factors. In this view, locomotor organization may reflect interactions between structural traits and functional movement patterns, rather than conformation alone. Consequently, observed asymmetries and stance adjustments in trained horses may reflect repeatable, discipline-associated locomotor patterns expressed within a morphologically constrained yet variable locomotor system. This is consistent with the selective performance of the Elastic Net models, which indicated a limited and outcome-specific connection between morphology and function.

Interpretation of asymmetry in clinical contexts

Understanding locomotor asymmetry remains a central challenge in equine gait analysis. In clinical practice, asymmetry in the vertical displacement of the head or pelvis is commonly used as an indicator of pain-related lameness and to support locomotor examinations. Experimental studies have shown that increased orthopedic pain is associated with greater movement asymmetry, supporting the clinical relevance of objective asymmetry metrics for lameness assessment [17, 24].

However, advances in objective gait analysis have also shown that measurable locomotor asymmetries can occur in horses that appear clinically sound, particularly when assessed with high-resolution inertial sensor systems. Subtle asymmetries in head and pelvic movement have been reported in sound horses during straight-line locomotion, indicating that asymmetry magnitude alone should not be taken as direct evidence of lameness [2, 8, 9]. Therefore, objective asymmetry measurements should be interpreted within a broader biomechanical and clinical context.

The current findings support this perspective by showing that horses trained in different Western disciplines exhibited distinct profiles of locomotor asymmetry, even when evaluated under standardized in-hand conditions and without overt clinical lameness. Nevertheless, these discipline-associated patterns should not be taken as direct evidence of long-term training adaptation. In the present study, asymmetry refers to measurable left–right differences in temporal, angular, acceleration-related, and vertical displacement variables. These differences may align with the mechanical demands of each discipline, such as acceleration, deceleration, and directional changes, but they may also reflect natural laterality, individual movement variability, subclinical asymmetry, shoeing, previous musculoskeletal history, or management factors. Therefore, the observed asymmetry profiles should be interpreted as discipline-associated locomotor patterns rather than as confirmed adaptive traits or evidence of pathology.

Importantly, these observations do not suggest that asymmetry lacks clinical significance. Instead, they underscore that locomotor asymmetry is a complex biomechanical phenomenon shaped by multiple interacting factors, including orthopedic pain, individual laterality, mechanical loading history, management, and training background. Prior studies have also shown that the magnitude of asymmetry alone cannot reliably distinguish functional variation from clinically relevant lameness [4, 23].

From a clinical perspective, these findings support integrating objective gait metrics with contextual information, including training discipline, locomotor history, shoeing, prior musculoskeletal events, and individual baseline movement patterns. Interpreting asymmetry within a whole-body axial–appendicular coordination framework may offer a more informative assessment of locomotor function than relying on isolated limb-based metrics alone. Longitudinal monitoring may be particularly useful for distinguishing repeatable individual movement patterns from progressive changes associated with developing musculoskeletal disorders.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the current findings. First, locomotor data were collected during standardized in-hand walk and trot rather than during discipline-specific ridden tasks. This approach improved comparability among horses and reduced external sources of variation, but it also means that the asymmetry patterns identified here may not fully reflect the locomotor patterns observed during actual performance.

Second, the observational, cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference about the relationship between training discipline and locomotor coordination. Therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted as discipline-associated locomotor patterns rather than as confirmed discipline-associated patterns.

Third, although 90 horses were initially recruited, the final number retained after data quality control varied slightly across disciplines and gaits. Most analyses included 19 horses per discipline, which is comparable to or larger than many field-based equine biomechanical studies but remains limited for detecting small effects or exploring within-discipline variability. In addition, horses were recruited via convenience sampling from a restricted geographic area and a limited number of owners or trainers. Therefore, the results should be generalized to broader populations of Quarter Horses or Western performance horses with caution.

Finally, locomotor measurements were obtained during a single assessment session, so intra-individual repeatability of asymmetry patterns across multiple days could not be evaluated. Future studies that integrate objective locomotor analysis, longitudinal monitoring, clinical follow-up, and discipline-associated performance tasks may help clarify the stability, clinical relevance, and functional significance of the asymmetry patterns observed in athletic Quarter Horses.

Conclusion

Trained Quarter Horses from different Western disciplines showed discipline-associated differences in locomotor asymmetry and in selected spatiotemporal, angular, vertical acceleration, and vertical displacement asymmetry variables when evaluated under standardized in-hand walk and trot conditions. Although measurable asymmetries were detected across all groups, their distribution varied by discipline, indicating that objective gait metrics should be interpreted in the context of the horse’s athletic use, locomotor history, and clinical presentation.

Static morphometric variation showed limited, outcome-specific associations with locomotor variables, suggesting that conformation alone did not account for the observed locomotor differences. These findings support the interpretation of locomotor asymmetry as a multidimensional biomechanical phenomenon rather than a single indicator of dysfunction or discipline-related adaptation. Integrating objective gait analysis with clinical assessment, discipline context, and longitudinal monitoring may improve interpretation of asymmetry patterns in athletic horses.
