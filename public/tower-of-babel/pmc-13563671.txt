DLRNA-BERTa: a transformer approach for predicting molecule-RNA binding affinities
Authors: Pasquale Lobascio, Khalid Saeed, Asifullah Khan, Ziaurrehman Tanoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapies targeting RNA are rapidly expanding, with 24 FDA-approved RNA therapeutics and over 130 currently in clinical trials, highlighting RNA’s growing role in drug discovery. In this context, transformer-based language models provide a scalable and cost-effective approach to accelerate RNA-targeted drug discovery by enabling the prediction of molecule–RNA binding affinities directly from sequence information. This study introduces DLRNA-BERTa, a RoBERTa-based framework that integrates RNA-BERTa and ChemBERTa-v2 to model molecule–RNA binding affinities. The framework includes six RNA class-specific models, aptamers, repeats, ribosomal RNAs, riboswitches, microRNAs, and viral RNAs, along with a general model for other RNA classes. DLRNA-BERTa outperformed existing approaches across different RNA classes on a randomly split validation dataset. On an independent and relatively diverse test set, the model achieved AUROC values of 0.57–0.59, demonstrating performance comparable to the other evaluated methods.Application of DLRNA-BERTa to a library of 3492 approved drugs identified 2859 compounds with predicted binding affinities (pKd ≥ 6) across 294 RNA targets, suggesting its potential utility for RNA-based drug repurposing and prioritization of candidate molecules for further investigation. To facilitate broader use and reproducibility, we provide a publicly accessible web application and API are available at https://huggingface.co/spaces/IlPakoZ/DLRNA-BERTa, enabling users to predict binding affinities between custom compounds and RNA sequences.Keywords: drug-target interaction, RNA interactions, drug-RNA interactions, drug target binding affinity, drug discovery

Introduction

Over the past three decades, therapeutics targeting RNAs have emerged as a transformative modality in modern medicine, enabling precise modulation of gene expression for the treatment of rare genetic disorders and the development of advanced vaccines, including those against SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) [1, 2]. Because of their molecular specificity, drugs targeting RNAs are widely regarded as offering significant advantages over traditional protein-targeting therapeutics [3]. These advantages arise from their ability to directly engage nucleic acid targets through complementary base pairing, enabling mechanisms such as gene silencing via antisense oligonucleotides or RNA interference (RNAi), as well as therapeutic protein expression through the delivery of synthetic messenger RNA (mRNA) [4, 5]. As a result, RNA targeting therapeutics are increasingly recognized for their capacity to address a broad range of intracellular and nucleic acid–associated targets, including many that were previously considered “undruggable” [3]. The growing maturity of this therapeutic class is reflected in regulatory approvals. By 2023, 22 RNA-based drugs are approved by the FDA and EMA [6], with two additional approvals reported in 2024. These approvals span diverse RNA modalities and disease indications, highlighting both the clinical relevance and expanding scope of RNA-targeted interventions. Among these, olezarsen is approved as an antisense oligonucleotide targeting APOC3 mRNA to suppress apolipoprotein C-III expression for the treatment of familial chylomicronemia syndrome (FCS) [7]. In parallel, imetelstat, a chemically modified antisense oligonucleotide, binds the RNA template region of the telomerase RNA component (TERC) and demonstrated therapeutic benefit in lower-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) with transfusion-dependent anemia [8]. By inhibiting telomerase activity, imetelstat limits the replicative potential of malignant hematopoietic cells, underscoring the ability of RNA therapeutics to modulate non-coding RNA targets in addition to protein-coding transcripts.

As RNA therapeutics continue to diversify, there is a growing need for computational strategies capable of systematically characterizing molecule-RNA interactions at scale. In parallel, advances in artificial intelligence (AI) are reshaping biomedical data analysis, driven by the increasing availability of high-throughput datasets in genomics, transcriptomics, and drug screening. AI-based methods have become integral to modern drug discovery pipelines [9], with applications ranging from drug repurposing [10–13] to de novo molecular design [14, 15], potentially improving discovery efficiency and reducing development timelines.

Within this context, drug–target interaction (DTI) prediction has emerged as a central computational task, aimed at estimating binding affinity between drug-like molecules and biological targets. Accurate DTI models enable high-throughput virtual screening and facilitate both novel compound discovery and drug repurposing [16]. For instance, Aron et al. [17] employed an attention-based regression model to screen hundreds of thousands of compounds across 1251 human proteins. Similarly, other transformer-based methods such as MolTrans [18] and DLM-DTI [19] frame DTI prediction as a classification problem, leveraging sequence embeddings to capture complex drug–target relationships. Despite these advances, the majority of DTI research has focused on protein targets, while RNA targets remain comparatively underexplored. This imbalance is largely attributable to the historical prioritization of proteins in drug discovery and the limited availability of high-quality molecule-RNA interaction datasets [20]. Nevertheless, recent studies have started to bridge this gap, for instance, DeepRSMA [21] introduced a cross-fusion transformer to integrate RNA and compound features, while Xia et al. [22] utilized a geometric deep learning approach to model 3D molecule-RNA complexes, achieving a Pearson correlation of 0.67. Similarly, fingeRNAt [23] uses structural interaction fingerprints (SIFts) combined with machine learning to predict small-molecule binding to RNA targets. A comprehensive review by Zhuo et al. [24] summarizes recent progress and highlights methodological directions in molecule-RNA modeling. Among early quantitative benchmarks, Krishnan et al. [25] proposed a regression-based model using engineered features derived from RNA sequences and small molecules, achieving a Pearson correlation of ~0.83 on a validation set. Using a binding affinity threshold (pKd ≥ 4.0), they further reported an F1-score of ~0.97 on an external classification dataset, demonstrating strong generalization performance. More recently, DeepRNA-DTI [26] introduced a sequence-based dual language model that jointly predicts compound-RNA interactions and RNA binding sites, reflecting growing interest in representation-learning approaches for RNA-targeted drug discovery.

In this work, we introduce DLRNA-BERTa, a large language model (LLM) framework designed to predict molecule-RNA binding affinities from primary molecular representations, namely RNA nucleotide sequences and molecule SMILES strings. The architecture integrates ChemBERTa-v2 [27] with RNA-BERTa, a transformer pretrained on 9.76 million RNA sequences using masked language modeling (MLM). RNA and molecule embeddings are combined through a cross-attention mechanism, followed by affinity prediction using a linear output layer. Inspired by DLM-DTI [19], we incorporate cross-attention and μParametrization (μP) [28], while excluding Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [29] and LoRA-Fine-Tuning-aware Quantization (LoFTQ) [30]. DLRNA-BERTa demonstrated improved performance over the benchmark model of Krishnan et al. [25], across six RNA classes and the general model. We further applied the trained model to virtually screen 3492 FDA-approved drugs, identifying molecules with predicted binding affinities to RNA targets. Several top-ranked molecules, including bleomycin, kanamycin, streptomycin, netilmicin, and tobramycin, are supported by published literature as interacting with miRNAs or repeat RNA elements, providing evidence for the biological relevance of the predictions. To support accessibility, we provide a graphical user interface (GUI) and an API, available at https://huggingface.co/spaces/IlPakoZ/DLRNA-BERTa. The source code and datasets are also publicly available at https://github.com/IlPakoZ/rnaberta-dti-prediction in accordance with FAIR principles. As the model is hosted on a public Hugging Face Space, temporary downtime may occur; in such cases, users can restore functionality by selecting the “Restart this Space” option. Material and methods

Pre-training datasets

The pretraining dataset comprises 10 841 246 RNA sequences, partitioned into 9 757 119 sequences for training and 1 084 127 sequences for validation. Collectively, these sequences represent ~1.22 billion tokens, of which ~1.07 billion are assigned to the training set. RNA sequences are obtained from RNAcentral [31] and NCBI [32], with individual sequence lengths capped at 2000 nucleotides. To ensure consistency with downstream applications, only RNA classes present in the fine-tuning dataset, aptamers, repeats, ribosomal RNAs, riboswitches, ribozymes, and viral RNAs, are retained. The mean RNA sequence length across the dataset is 901 nucleotides. To reduce the risk of data leakage between pretraining and fine-tuning, BLASTn [34] is used with an e-value cutoff of 1e−2 to identify and remove sequences in the pretraining set that were highly similar to fine-tuning sequences. A total of 1252 sequences are removed. Although this filtering criterion is conservative, it was adopted to preserve the robustness and validity of downstream model evaluation. Full details of the RNAcentral query procedures and BLAST command-line parameters are provided in Section S2 of the Supplementary Information.

Pre-training methods

RNA-BERTA consists of 12 encoder blocks, context length of 512 tokens, and an embedding size of 512 (reduced from the standard 768), totaling 55.56 M parameters. This architecture is selected to align with the compute–optimal parameter-to-token ratio of 1:20 proposed by Hoffmann et al. [35]. Tokenization is performed using a character-level Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) algorithm, rather than a byte-level implementation, to provide a more natural representation of nucleotide tokens. Following the guidelines of Tao et al. [36], the vocabulary size is set to 9700 tokens. Based on this approach, the number of vocabulary parameters is obtained from the compute, which was estimated to be ~4.05e+17 FLOPS by inverting the formula for the number of non-vocabulary parameters of the model. This estimate closely matches the FLOPS predicted by the Chinchilla framework [35].

Pretraining is performed in three stages (Fig. 1). Because exhaustive hyperparameter optimization (HO) is computationally prohibitive for large transformer models, the μP framework [28] is adopted to enable scalable hyperparameter transferability. In this scheme, the learning rate and number of warm-up steps are optimized on a reduced RNA-BERTa model and then generalized to the full architecture. This strategy reduced the computational cost of HO by ~4.5-fold compared with direct optimization on the complete model. HO experiments are executed on four NVIDIA V100 GPUs (150 GB RAM) and completed in ~48 h. The complete implementation details are provided in Section 2.6. Across all pretraining phases, cosine learning-rate scheduling with warm-up and a 10-fold decay factor over one epoch is employed, consistent with best practices outlined by Rae et al. [37] and Hoffmann et al. [35]. The μP-compatible implementations of AdamW and RoBERTa [28, 38] are used, and the attention multiplier is set to to ensure consistent parameter transfer during downstream fine-tuning.

Fine-tuning datasets

The fine-tuning dataset comprises 1439 molecule-RNA interaction pairs, each consisting of an RNA sequence, a molecule SMILES representation, the dissociation constant (pKd), and the corresponding RNA class. The pKd values represent the negative logarithm of the dissociation constant (Kd values expressed in nanomolar units) between each molecule and its corresponding RNA target. In total, the dataset includes 759 unique molecules and 294 unique RNA targets. Of the 1439 interaction pairs, 37 exceeded the model’s maximum context length of 512 tokens and were therefore truncated. RNA sequence lengths range from 4 to 615 tokens (average: 26.48 tokens), while SMILES strings range from 17 to 333 tokens (average: 70.24 tokens).

This dataset was originally published by Krishnan et al. [25] and is publicly available in PDF format on the RSAPred website. A minor discrepancy exists between the number of interactions reported in the publication (1480) and the number provided online (1439). After acquisition, the dataset is parsed and curated to remove extraneous columns and ensure compatibility with the model input format. The processed dataset is publicly available at: https://huggingface.co/spaces/IlPakoZ/DLRNA-BERTa. The distribution of RNA classes in the fine-tuning dataset is summarized in Table 1.

Fine tuning methods

The pretrained RNA model is integrated with ChemBERTa-v2 [27] to fine-tune seven distinct models. One model is trained on the full set of molecule-RNA interactions (the combined model), while the remaining six are trained exclusively on individual RNA classes. For each model type, 25 HO trials are performed using Optuna with the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) [39] sampler. The optimized hyperparameters include the learning rate, weight decay, dropout, and scheduler decay ratio. All models are subsequently trained and evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation (CV). For the combined model, CV is repeated using three independently optimized hyperparameter configurations to assess model robustness. In each fold, training and validation splits are generated using stratified random split by RNA class, preserving class distributions such that ~90% of interactions associated with each class are allocated to training and 10% to validation. Although random stratified splitting has known limitations, particularly with respect to potential data leakage, it is adopted to ensure direct comparability with Krishnan et al., who employed the same 10-fold CV strategy. More stringent splitting approaches (e.g. scaffold-based or similarity-based splits) were not feasible due to the limited availability of quantitative molecule–RNA binding affinity data, as such methods would substantially reduce the effective training set size and compromise statistical reliability. Furthermore, despite checking recent releases (up to 2026) of major databases, including ChEMBL and BindingDB, we could not find other datasets with quantitative RNA-target binding data beyond that reported by Krishnan et al. Consequently, we adopted their evaluation protocol to enable direct comparisons.

Prior to fine-tuning, dissociation constant (pKd) values are standardized using z-score normalization to promote faster and more stable convergence. After CV, the best-performing configuration for each RNA class specific model is selected, while three high-performing configurations are retained for the combined model. All models are then retrained on the full available data for their respective RNA classes, and their final performance is reported relative to the baseline established by Krishnan et al. [25]. Detailed evaluation results are presented in Section 3.3.3.

External evaluation

All selected models are also evaluated against the external ROBIN classification datasets provided by the Krishnan et al. [25]. Again, these datasets are used to enable comparison with Krishnan et al. and follow the same splitting scenario as adapted in Krishnan et al.

The ROBIN datasets contain a total of 5534 molecule-RNA pairs, including 2538 negative and 2991 positive interactions. In contrast to the training and validation dataset, which is generated using stratified random splitting, the ROBIN test set is designed to minimize data leakage, sharing only 3 of 18 RNA sequences (two aptamers and one miRNA) and none of the 3941 molecular SMILES strings with the training and validation set. This stringent separation provide a more rigorous assessment of the model’s generalization performance on previously unseen RNA targets and chemical compounds, although it may still present challenges for highly dissimilar RNA sequences or chemical structures. ChemBERTa-v2 (77M-MTR) [27] produces embeddings of size 384, whereas RNA-BERTa produces embeddings of size 512. To maintain consistent embedding dimensions, RNA-BERTa outputs are projected to 384 through a linear layer. Both RNA and molecule embeddings are then passed through linear layers before being fed into a cross-attention module that serves as the interaction head. This module employs a single attention head to promote interpretable.

Within the cross-attention mechanism, the molecule SMILES representation provides the key and value matrices, while the RNA sequence provides the query matrix. The molecule is designated as the key following a lock-and-key analogy, where the RNA sequence represents the lock and the molecule’s SMILES represents the key. The context-updated RNA token embeddings are subsequently passed through a linear integration layer, followed by a second linear layer that reduces the embedding size from 384 to 1. Because linear layers operate independently across embedding dimensions, token identity is preserved. Finally, the token representations are passed through a final linear layer, where vector–vector multiplication is decomposed into two steps: (i) element-wise multiplication between input and weight vector components, and (ii) summation of the resulting values as shown in the following equation.

Here, represents the contribution of token i to the prediction. Prior to summation, a learned padding value replaces contributions for all padding tokens, ensuring equal contribution across them. The summation yields the predicted binding affinity (pKd) between the molecule and RNA target. These token-level contributions are further analyzed during interpretation. Both unnormalized and normalized visualization plots are generated, where contributions from padding tokens are redistributed among non-padding tokens. For normalized plots, negative contributions are iteratively reassigned to positive tokens until all values are non-negative. Special tokens such as “<s>” and “</s>” are retained during fine-tuning and interpretation to ensure consistency with the pretraining process. The cross-attention mechanism also facilitates ablation and perturbation studies, an example of which is presented in Section 3.1.2.

Finally, we trained two additional model variants to assess the impact of key architectural design choices: one in which the cross-attention mechanism was replaced with simple feature concatenation, and another in which the token-independent interpretable pooling layer was replaced with mean pooling. Substituting cross-attention with concatenation resulted in reduced performance, whereas replacing interpretable pooling with mean pooling led to an improvement in AUROC. Despite this, we focus our downstream analyses on the interpretable pooling model to preserve interpretability (see Supplementary Section S10 for a comprehensive performance comparison).

Hyperparameter optimization

HO is performed for both pretraining and fine-tuning. For pretraining, 32 Optuna trials are executed using the TPE sampler. Because μP supports transfer of only non-regularization parameters [28], weight decay is fixed to the standard value of 0.01. Sixteen-bit floating point precision is not used during HO, as it has been reported to cause unstable training under μP [40]. For post–layer normalization transformers such as the RoBERTa [38] implementation, Yang et al. [28] demonstrated that optimal hyperparameters transfer reliably across different training step counts. Leveraging this, each HO model is trained for 5000 steps rather than the full 38 000 steps available for training, thereby reducing computational cost while preserving the transferability of the selected hyperparameters. To account for the slight left-shift in the optimal learning rate reported by Yang et al., three additional models are trained with learning rates equal to the HO-derived optimal value multiplied by 1.0, 0.875, and 0.75, respectively.

Fine-tuning HO is conducted using 10-fold CV to account for variability arising from different training set partitions. To accelerate optimization, each of the 25 Optuna trials is evaluated on a subset of five folds out of the 10 total, resulting in 125 models trained per model type, each for 100 epochs. Optimization is performed using the Weighted Mean Squared Error (WMSE) objective function, a variant of mean squared error where updates are weighted by a factor proportional to the abundance of the corresponding RNA target class in the dataset. Evaluation metrics

During pretraining, model quality is evaluated using the bits-per-character (BPC) metric across all phases. BPC is a standard pretraining metric that measures the average number of bits required to encode each character in the text, thereby the model’s ability to predict masked characters during pretraining.

For fine-tuning HO, the validation set is assessed using three metrics: R2 score, mean absolute error (MAE) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R). The R2 metric quantifies the proportion of variance in the dependent variable (pKd) explained by the independent variables (RNA sequences and molecule SMILES strings). MAE measures the average absolute difference between predicted and actual pKd values. The final model performance is evaluated using R2, MAE, and R.

Web application for proposed DLRNA-BERTa

The general model is deployed on a dedicated Hugging Face Space (https://huggingface.co/spaces/IlPakoZ/DLRNA-BERTa) to provide free and user-friendly access. As the model is hosted on a public Hugging Face Space, it may occasionally become temporarily unavailable. In such cases, users can restore functionality by clicking the “Restart this Space” button. Only the general model is deployed, as the limited training data available for the six class-specific models may restrict their ability to generalize effectively to previously unseen RNA targets submitted by end users.

The GUI is organized into four tabs: Prediction & Analysis, Visualizations, Model Settings, Dataset and About. By default, users are directed to the Prediction & Analysis tab, which provides input fields for an RNA sequence and up to 2000 compound SMILES strings, with one SMILES string per line, along with two action buttons: Predict Interaction and Generate Visualizations. Results and discussion

Training and validation performance

Pretraining

HO over 5000 training steps yields an optimal learning rate of 0.00063 and 765 warm-up steps. The optimization landscape is sensitive around the optimum, with even small increases in the learning rate causing convergence toward what appears, based on the observed reduction in gradient norms, to be a more stable but suboptimal local solution at ~11.4 evaluation BPC. A contour plot of the optimization space illustrating this phenomenon is presented in Fig. S3, while the slice plot in Fig. S4 further demonstrates the sharp sensitivity of the optimization space to minor learning rate increases.

Following HO, three model variants are trained with learning rates of 0.00063, 0.000552, and 0.000443, each using 765 warm-up steps. Among these, the model with a learning rate of 0.000552 achieves the best performance, with an evaluation BPC of 3.20, compared with 3.32 for the initially identified HO-identified optimum. The final model is therefore trained with a learning rate of 0.000552, achieving a final evaluation BPC of 1.78. The corresponding training and evaluation loss curves are shown in Fig. S5.

Examples of fold-level validation predictions are shown in Fig. S6. As 10-fold CV is employed, some models have relatively few validation samples per fold, making individual fold-level performance estimates susceptible to sampling variability. Accordingly, averaging across the 10 folds provides a more stable estimate of true model performance. Notably, Krishnan et al. [25] also adopt 10-fold CV for their baseline models. The final models are retrained on the combined training and validation sets, after which interpretation analyses are performed. From the final linear layer, values and weights are extracted, and both normalized and unnormalized visualizations are generated for all samples with predicted binding affinity pKd > 8 and MAE < 0.5. As an illustrative case, the interaction between the Bacillus subtilis flavin mononucleotide (FMN) riboswitch aptamer and its ligand FMN is analyzed (Fig. S7). In the unnormalized visualization, tokens contribute both positively and negatively to affinity prediction. The strongest positive contributions arise from “<s>” and “UUCGGGGC,” whereas “UAGUAA” contributes most negatively. These results identify sequence regions that contribute most strongly to the predicted binding affinity for this compound, although they should not be interpreted as evidence of direct physical interactions. The normalized visualization (Fig. S8) yields similar patterns because negative contributions are relatively small and are redistributed during normalization.

A second example involves the bacterial 50S ribosomal RNA subunit and the antibiotic azithromycin (Figs S9–S10). Here, differences between normalized and unnormalized visualizations are more pronounced, revealing alternating blocks of positive and negative contributions that resemble sequence conservation patterns. One possible interpretation is that these patterns reflect the functional importance of specific sequence regions involved in RNA folding or activity. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation. The observation is additionally supported by the consistency of token-importance profiles across different compounds for the same RNA sequence (Fig. S11), although the limited compound diversity available for each RNA sequence in the training dataset may also influence this pattern.

Ablation studies are further performed to probe interpretability. In one case, the FMN riboswitch aptamer with FMN is examined. Figure S12 shows pre-ablation cross-attention weights, where the ChemBERTa “[CLS]” token exhibits the strongest attention, particularly toward the subsequence “AUUCAG” (weight ≈ 0.32). When this weight is set to zero, the corresponding cell in the heatmap is removed (Fig. S13), leading to a slight increase in attention weight for “AUUCAG” but a decrease in predicted pKd, resulting in a larger prediction error. This observation suggests that the attention mechanism downweights this subsequence, and its removal redistributes attention through the softmax operation, thereby altering the predicted binding affinity.

Another ablation example examines the interaction between 2-amino-N6-hydroxyadenine and the B. subtilis guanine riboswitch (Figs S14–S15). Here, the highest weight (≈ 0.18) corresponds to the interaction between subsequence “UGUCAG” and a defined carbon atom of the compound. Setting this weight to zero increases the final token contribution and predicted pKd, again increasing the prediction error. As in the FMN example, cross-attention appears to downweight specific subsequences, with their removal leading to compensatory attention redistribution and changes in the predicted binding affinity.

Comparison with other methods

Training and validation performance is first compared with the baseline models of Krishnan et al. (RSAPred) [25]. Across all RNA classes, the proposed models achieved higher cross-validation performance than RSAPred. This may reflect the ability of transformer-based architectures to capture more complex relationships between molecule–RNA pairs than the linear regression framework employed by RSAPred. In addition, the present cross-validation approach does not include stratification by pKd, which may provide a less constrained evaluation setting. Although random splitting with RNA class stratification is not ideal, since RNA targets or molecules may still appear in both training and validation sets, the results indicate that the proposed models achieve higher performance than RSAPred, particularly when evaluated under this experimental setting. The relatively low specificity observed for both DLRNA-BERTa and RSAPred should be interpreted in the context of the evaluation protocol. Following Krishnan et al. [25], interactions were classified using a permissive threshold of pKd ≥ 4 to enable direct comparison. Such a threshold favors sensitivity at the expense of specificity by increasing the number of false-positive predictions. To assess the influence of the decision threshold, we repeated the analysis using a more stringent cutoff (pKd ≥ 6). As expected, specificity increased substantially (aptamers: 0.952, riboswitches: 0.922, miRNAs: 0.952, and viral RNAs: 0.993), although sensitivity decreased accordingly (0.029, 0.139, 0.065, and 0.016, respectively). These results indicate that specificity is strongly influenced by the selected operating threshold and should therefore be interpreted together with threshold-independent metrics such as AUROC. For practical virtual screening, a higher pKd threshold may be preferred when prioritizing a smaller set of high-confidence candidates.

Virtual screening of approved drugs against RNA targets

Following evaluation using the internal cross-validation and external benchmark datasets, the final models were used to predict binding affinities for each of the six RNA target classes. These models are applied to a dataset of 3492 approved drugs sourced from the ChEMBL database (v33). There are 2859 drugs predicted to exhibit relatively strong binding affinities (pKd ≥ 6) across 294 distinct RNA targets (Supplementary File 1). When the affinity threshold is increased to pKd > 8, 1613 drugs retain high predicted binding across 55 RNA targets (Supplementary File 2). The large number of positive predictions may reflect not only useful model behavior but also the prediction distribution, threshold selection, or a tendency toward false-positive predictions. As shown in Fig. 6, the five drugs with the broadest predicted activity are bivalirudin, nintedanib, vancomycin, ceruletide, and cosyntropin, while the most frequently engaged RNA targets are pre-miR-24-2, petracycline riboswitch A08G mutant, petracycline in vitro aptamer, tetracycline riboswitch, FMN riboswitch, and Streptomyces davawensis.

Because these predictions are generated from a regression model with moderate external generalization performance (AUROC ≈ 0.57–0.60), they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of direct RNA binding. Furthermore, the relatively permissive threshold (pKd ≥ 6) was selected to prioritize candidate interactions for downstream analysis rather than to maximize specificity. As discussed in Section 3.2, increasing the threshold substantially improves specificity while reducing sensitivity, illustrating the trade-off between false-positive and false-negative predictions. Consequently, the predicted interactions should be regarded as candidates for experimental validation rather than confirmed RNA-binding events.

These large-scale predictions demonstrate the applicability of DLRNA-BERTa for prioritizing candidate molecule–RNA interactions in RNA-targeted drug repurposing. The resulting hit lists may serve as a resource for subsequent experimental follow-up and biochemical validation. As a proof of concept, literature evidence is compiled for bleomycin, kanamycin, streptomycin, netilmicin, and tobramycin, which are among the predicted high-affinity compounds (pKd > 8), as discussed in Section 3.4.

Literature-based validation of approved drugs binding to RNA targets

Among the predicted high-affinity interactions, several molecules are previously reported to interact directly or indirectly with RNA targets. Although these examples do not constitute independent experimental validation of DLRNA-BERTa, they provide qualitative support for the biological plausibility of selected predictions and illustrate the potential utility of the framework for prioritizing candidate RNA-binding compounds.

Small molecules have been reported to interact predominantly with aptamers, viral RNA sequences, repeat elements, and miRNAs, consistent with recent findings reported in multiple studies [42–47]. miRNAs regulate post-transcriptional gene expression and are implicated in cancer, cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurodegenerative diseases [48, 49]. Small molecules capable of binding miRNAs therefore represent promising therapeutic candidates in several disease settings [50]. miRNAs are selected as model RNA targets for small-molecule binding validation in computational pipelines, as they provide several advantages: biological relevance, tractable secondary structures, robust datasets, and experimental feasibility [51]. Accordingly, they provide an appropriate context for illustrating the predictions generated by DLRNA-BERTa.

Bleomycin, a glycopeptide-derived anticancer agent, was predicted by DLRNA-BERTa to interact with multiple miRNA and primary transcript targets (Supplementary File 1 contains 122 interactions). Previous biochemical studies have shown that bleomycin preferentially cleaves purine-rich RNA regions, particularly contiguous AU pairs and RNA hairpins [52], in addition to its well-characterized DNA-binding and cleavage activity [53, 54]. Moreover, bleomycin has been explored in conjugation with RNA-targeting ligands, enabling dual substrate recognition [55, 56]. This design allows the distinct substrate preferences of the RNA-binding ligand and bleomycin to be leveraged for selective degradation of different RNA targets.

Among the predicted interactions, pri-miR-96 (Pri-miR-96RNAC, Pri-miR-96RNA4, Pri-miR-96RNA1, Pri-miR-96RNA5, and Pri-miR-96RNA2), an oncogenic miRNA family, emerged as one of the higher-ranked predicted targets, with predicted pKd values ranging from 6.01 to 6.47 (Supplementary File 1). These predictions are consistent with previous studies showing that Targaprimir-96, a bleomycin conjugate, selectively cleaves pri-miR-96 through sequence-specific recognition [57]. Since multiple individual targets can originate from the same gene family, e.g. the pri-miR-96 subfamily includes Pri-miR-96RNAC, Pri-miR-96RNA4, Pri-miR-96RNA1, and Pri-miR-96RNA5; therefore, we report a range of predicted pKd values rather than a single value in Table 7.

Additional predicted interactions include miR-21 (predicted pKd: 6.79) and pre-miR-17/18a/20a (predicted pKd: 7.17), components of the oncogenic miR-17-92 cluster. These predictions are compatible with previous reports suggesting that bleomycin or its derivatives may influence multiple miRNA pathways through RNA backbone scission or conformational stabilization [54, 58] (Table 7). Structured repeat elements, including CCUG (predicted pKd: 6.42) and CUG (predicted pKd: 6.0–7.1), were also predicted as potential RNA-binding targets of bleomycin. Previous studies have implicated these repeat RNAs in therapeutic strategies for myotonic dystrophy types 1 and 2 (DM1 and DM2), where expanded CCUG/CUG repeats play pathogenic roles [59, 60] (Table 7; Supplementary File 1).

Our analysis further predicted that several aminoglycosides, including kanamycin, streptomycin, netilmicin, and tobramycin, bind pre-miR-21, consistent with previous biochemical and biophysical studies (Supplementary Table S2) [46, 61, 62]. Beyond these direct RNA binders, several molecules predicted as potential RNA interactors (Supplementary File 1) are known to act as indirect modulators of miRNA pathways. These molecules influence miRNA function by targeting associated protein cofactors or by altering miRNA expression levels, despite the absence of confirmed direct small-molecule–RNA binding. A representative example is enoxacin, a fluoroquinolone antibiotic identified as a global miRNA enhancer in a screen of ~2000 FDA-approved drugs [63]. Enoxacin has been reported to interact with components of the miRNA-processing machinery and with specific pre-miRNA structures, thereby facilitating miRNA maturation [64]. Notably, it modulates miR-18a levels through interactions with protein cofactors involved in miRNA biogenesis [64]. In addition, doxorubicin, an anthracycline chemotherapeutic agent, has been shown to significantly upregulate miR-21 expression in both mouse cardiac tissue and H9C2 cardiomyocytes [65].

Taken together, these examples demonstrate that several high-confidence predictions generated by DLRNA-BERTa are consistent with previously reported RNA-related activities. Although such literature-based evidence does not constitute independent experimental validation and may be subject to confirmation bias, it supports the biological plausibility of selected predictions and illustrates the potential utility of the framework for prioritizing candidate molecule–RNA interactions for future experimental investigation.

Conclusion

This study introduces DLRNA-BERTa, a transformer-based deep learning framework designed to predict binding affinities (pKd) between molecules and diverse RNA targets using only primary representations, namely RNA sequences and SMILES strings. By leveraging a cross-attention mechanism, the model captures contextual interactions between molecules and RNA tokens, enabling end-to-end, structure-free prediction of molecule–RNA interactions.

Cross-validation results indicate that DLRNA-BERTa achieves improved performance compared with previously reported regression-based approaches. On the general RNA interaction dataset, the model achieves a Pearson correlation coefficient (R) of 0.92, an R2 of 0.84, and a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.33 under 10-fold cross-validation, exceeding the performance reported for the reference model by Krishnan et al. (R ≈ 0.82). DLRNA-BERTa also demonstrates better cross-validation performance across individual RNA subclasses, with Pearson R values ranging from 0.93 to 0.98, including RNA classes with relatively limited training data, such as repeats and riboswitches. However, because the validation protocol relies on random interaction-level splitting with overlap between training and validation data, these results should be interpreted as measures of performance under the adopted evaluation protocol rather than as estimates of generalization to unseen RNA targets or compounds.

Evaluation on the external ROBIN benchmark provides a more stringent assessment of model generalization. Under this setting, DLRNA-BERTa achieved AUROC values between 0.57 and 0.60 and performed competitively with existing approaches while outperforming other regression-based methods. These results suggest that the framework may capture a limited degree of molecule–RNA interaction signal, while also highlighting the substantial challenges associated with generalization.

Despite these advances, several limitations warrant consideration. The fine-tuning dataset remains relatively small, with overlapping RNA sequences between training and validation sets. Because random interaction-level splitting and sequence overlap reduce the independence between these datasets, the resulting cross-validation performance should not be interpreted as a reliable estimate of generalization to previously unseen RNA targets or molecules. Consequently, the present study cannot conclusively determine whether the strong cross-validation performance reflects the learning of genuinely generalizable molecule–RNA interaction patterns or is partly attributable to the reduced independence between the training and validation sets. In contrast, the external ROBIN benchmark, which exhibits substantially lower overlap with the training data, provides a more realistic assessment of model generalization, although additional experimental validation remains essential to establish its practical predictive utility. Furthermore, the limited availability of quantitative molecule–RNA binding affinity datasets currently precludes comprehensive evaluation using more rigorous protocols, such as RNA target-level or similarity-aware splitting, representing an important direction for future work. In addition, while attention-based visualizations provide intuitive insights, limited data and design choices, such as the inclusion of special tokens, constrain interpretability. The available training data also exhibits a bias toward intermediate-affinity interactions, which influences predictive behavior. Finally, although the model can represent sequences beyond the 512-token context window, predictions for longer RNAs should be interpreted cautiously.

Looking ahead, future work may enhance DLRNA-BERTa by incorporating geometric deep learning to better capture structural determinants of molecule–RNA interactions and by expanding training datasets to reduce bias and improve generalization. The framework can also be extended to additional ligand classes, including peptides and oligonucleotides, as suitable data become available. The virtual screening results presented here should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating resource for prioritizing candidate molecule–RNA interactions for experimental investigation rather than as evidence of confirmed RNA binding. Similarly, the literature-based case studies provide qualitative support for the biological plausibility of selected predictions but should not be interpreted as independent validation of model performance. To support reproducibility and community-driven development, the DLRNA-BERTa source code is released through GitHub under FAIR principles, enabling further methodological development and benchmarking in RNA-targeted drug discovery.
