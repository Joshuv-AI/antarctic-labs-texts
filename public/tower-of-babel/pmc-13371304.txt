The relationship between spiritual well-being, state anxiety, and psychological resilience among cross-matched liver transplant donors in Turkey
Authors: Bilsev Demi̇r, Kübra Kayaoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aims to examine the relationship between spiritual well-being, anxiety, and psychological resilience in cross-matched liver transplant donors. It contributes to the international literature on anxiety, spiritual well-being, and psychological resilience in individuals who donate their organs by participating in the cross-matched system as donors.Materials and methodsThis descriptive and cross-sectional study was conducted with 116 donors participating in the cross-matching system at a liver transplant institute in a university hospital in Turkey. Data were collected using the FACIT-Sp Spiritual Well-Being Scale, the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I), and the Brief Psychological Resilience Scale (BPRS). Data were analyzed using mean, standard deviation, Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U test, and Spearman’s correlation and regression analysis.ResultsDonors demonstrated high levels of spiritual well-being, moderate levels of psychological resilience, and high levels of anxiety. A statistically significant positive association was found between spiritual well-being and psychological resilience (r = 0.381, p < 0.05). Significant positive associations were also observed between psychological resilience and the meaning, peace, and faith subdimensions of spiritual well-being (p < 0.05). No significant association was found between spiritual well-being and anxiety. Despite high spiritual well-being, donors reported elevated anxiety levels, which may reflect the inherently stressful and uncertain nature of the cross-matched transplantation process rather than the influence of individual spiritual resources alone.ConclusionCross-matched liver transplant donors exhibited high spiritual well-being alongside elevated anxiety and moderate psychological resilience. Spiritual well-being was statistically associated with psychological resilience but not with anxiety. These findings suggest that spiritual well-being may support adaptive psychological functioning without necessarily reducing situational anxiety in this high- risk medical context.

Introduction

Liver transplantation is one of the most common solid organ transplants worldwide and ranks second in overall frequency after kidney transplantation. The type of donor varies by region, with donors being either living or cadaveric. Despite significant innovations in graft optimization and organ allocation processes over the past twenty years, the current shortage of liver donor organs remains a major problem. This situation has led to an increase in the average waiting time for transplantation. Data from the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) shows that approximately 1,200 patients die each year while waiting for a liver transplant. This situation highlights the importance of living donor liver grafts [1]. According to the latest global transplant activity reports, approximately 42,497 liver transplants were performed worldwide in 2024, with living donor transplants accounting for approximately 23% of this number [2]. Living donor liver transplants (LDLT) account for more than 80% of all liver transplants in Asian countries, excluding China. South Korea has the highest LDLT rate at 21.6 per million population, while the rate in the US is 1.2 per million population [2, 3]. The Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation reported that 29,031 living donor liver transplants were performed worldwide and 4,605 in Turkey over the past three years [2]. However, finding a suitable donor for transplantation poses a serious medical and ethical problem [4]. Despite advances in living donor liver transplantation, many potential living liver transplant donors cannot donate their organs to their relatives due to blood incompatibility and unsuitable anatomy. The cross-matched liver transplantation procedure is being explored as a solution to this problem [5]. Cross-matched liver transplantation enables incompatible donor and recipient pairs to donate organs to each other, providing a suitable match for organ transplantation. Cross-matched liver transplantation offers a vital solution for more patients [6, 7]. However, it also involves a complex ethical and psychological process [8].

The increase in living donors, allocation policies, and improvements in pre- and post-operative care have led to an increase in transplants. Despite this increase, liver transplant rates vary significantly between regions and countries due to differences in donor availability, healthcare infrastructure, and organ donation policies. Consequently, while global liver transplant numbers continue to rise, the gap between the number of patients awaiting transplantation and the number of procedures performed remains substantial, highlighting persistent disparities in access to this life-saving intervention [9, 10]. Liver transplantation is a complex, resource-intensive medical procedure that places a significant burden on healthcare systems due to its surgical complexity, the need for long-term follow-up and lifelong immunosuppressive therapy. Despite global increases in transplantation rates, disparities in access to resources remain a major challenge [3, 11–14].

Liver transplantation places a significant burden on healthcare systems due to the complexity and long-term nature of post-transplant management, beyond the surgical and financial aspects. Transplant care extends far beyond the surgical process and requires continuous, multidisciplinary involvement over the long term. Long-term inpatient care, repeated outpatient follow-up visits, laboratory and imaging monitoring, immunosuppressive treatment management, and treatment requirements for transplant complications place a significant burden on healthcare infrastructure, workload capacity, and institutional resources [15, 16]. This also requires coordinated contributions from surgeons, nurses, and relevant healthcare professionals. It must be addressed not only in terms of medical outcomes but also in terms of psychosocial and supportive dimensions.

The cross-matched liver transplant process directly affects not only the recipients but also the donors [17]. Donors’ voluntary participation in organ donation can also bring a significant psychological burden. Alongside the donor’s physical recovery process, their emotional and psychological recovery process is also important. Stress, anxiety, and depression experienced by donors are among the observed psychological impressions [18, 19]. Transplantation is not only a medical procedure, but also a situation that tests individuals’ psychological resilience. Psychological resilience defines individuals’ capacity to cope with situations such as state anxiety [20]. This study can also be framed within the context of coping theory, which views psychological adaptation as a process involving the appraisal of stress and the mobilisation of internal resources. In this context, liver transplantation is a significant stressor, and state anxiety reflects the individual’s immediate emotional response. Spiritual well-being and psychological resilience can be understood as relatively stable internal resources that support coping with such stressors. Individuals with high psychological resilience have been found to respond more positively to stressful processes and to recover more quickly [21, 22].

Recent studies have increasingly drawn attention to the psychosocial difficulties experienced by liver transplant patients, particularly donors. Qualitative and quantitative studies have reported that living and matched liver transplant donors frequently experience increased anxiety, emotional distress, and anxiety related to uncertainty before and after transplantation. A recent meta-analysis of qualitative studies highlighted that donors generally face psychological stress related to surgical risk, fear of adverse outcomes, disruptions to daily life, and long-term emotional adjustment, underscoring the complex psychosocial burden associated with liver donation [1, 23, 24]. Previous research has demonstrated that spirituality and religiosity are closely associated with various psychological outcomes throughout life and in different clinical populations. There is evidence to suggest that higher levels of spirituality and religiosity are generally linked to lower levels of anxiety and distress, as well as improved emotional well-being and quality of life [25]. Furthermore, studies conducted with patients suffering from chronic or life-threatening conditions, such as cancer or neurological disorders, indicate that spiritual well-being is positively associated with quality of life and adaptive psychological functioning [26, 27]. These associations appear consistent across different age groups and diagnostic categories, supporting the idea that spirituality is an important psychosocial resource for coping with illness and stress. Although the relationships between spiritual well-being, anxiety, and psychological resilience are well documented in the general population and various clinical groups, their applicability to cross-matched liver transplant donors has not been sufficiently examined [1, 23, 28]. Cross-matched donors constitute a highly specific subgroup who are exposed to unique psychological conditions. These include donating an organ to an unknown recipient, heightened ethical responsibility, uncertainty regarding transplant outcomes and the absence of direct medical benefit. These contextual characteristics differentiate cross-matched donors from conventional living donors and may result in alterations to the operation of established psychosocial relationships.

Spiritual well-being is a concept linked to individuals’ search for meaning in life, existential values, and personal beliefs [29, 30]. Spiritual well-being can be considered a factor that enhances psychological resilience by improving individuals’ ability to cope with stress, guiding their decisions, and helping them establish balance in their relationships with their environment [23, 31]. In this context, cross-matched living liver transplant donors likely experience satisfaction with both the existential and faith dimensions of spiritual well-being.

Cross-matched liver transplant donors are individuals who struggle to save the lives of their loved ones by putting their own health at risk. When donating an organ to someone they have never met, they are told that not every liver transplant recipient will recover, and that the recipient may die. Similarly, they may encounter significant complications and adverse effects in the post-transplant period. Spiritual well-being may guide the decisions of individuals who agree to become donors despite all these risks. This may increase psychological resilience. In theoretical terms, psychological resilience is defined as the capacity to adapt and maintain, or regain, psychological functioning in stressful situations. In contrast, state anxiety is defined as an acute emotional response to situational uncertainty and perceived threat [32, 33]. Spiritual well-being, which encompasses meaning, peace, and faith, is theorised as an internal psychosocial resource that may support adaptive functioning by facilitating the creation of meaning and emotional regulation in high-stress contexts [24, 34]. In this model, stress-related anxiety and psychological resilience are conceptually related psychological constructs that may be associated within the same clinical context. Recent studies have generally analyzed spiritual well-being or psychological resilience in LDLT donors in a limited and separate manner [23, 24]. The focus has been on patients who have undergone liver transplantation [20, 35]. Furthermore, studies on donors’ anxiety levels are also limited [36–39]. Nevertheless, the interplay among spiritual well-being, psychological resilience, and anxiety levels remains significantly under-researched within the context of liver transplant donors. Several studies have been conducted on spiritual well-being and psychological resilience in living liver transplant donors [26, 27, 31, 40]. However, no study has yet been found that links spiritual well-being to anxiety and psychological resilience in cross-matched liver transplant donors. In conclusion, this study aims to contribute to the literature as one of the first investigations examining the relationship between three variables: spiritual well-being, psychological resilience, and anxiety levels among cross-matched liver transplant donors in the Turkish context. In our study, we aimed to analyze the relationship between spiritual well-being, state anxiety, and psychological resilience in cross-matched liver transplant donors. In addition, an exploratory linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between spiritual well-being and psychological resilience. In this model, psychological resilience was treated as the dependent variable and spiritual well-being as the independent variable.

This cross-sectional, correlational study examined the associations between spiritual well-being and state anxiety and psychological resilience in liver transplant donors. Rather than testing causal pathways or mediation mechanisms, the study focuses on identifying the strength and direction of the relationships between these psychological constructs. Previous literature suggests that spiritual well-being may be associated with lower anxiety levels and greater resilience through shared psychosocial resources, such as meaning-making, emotional regulation, and adaptive coping. However, the examination of these relationships within transplant donor populations has been inconsistent. Accordingly, the current study adopts an associative analytical framework to explore these relationships, without implying indirect or causal effects. The conceptual model was constructed to reflect the hypothesised associations consistent with the correlational statistical approach employed.

In this context, this study aims to examine the relationship between the anxiety levels experienced by cross-matched liver transplant donors and their psychological resilience during the transplant process and their spiritual well-being. These variables can also be interpreted within the framework of coping theory. In this context, liver transplantation is a significant stressor, and state anxiety reflects an individual’s immediate emotional response to it. Spiritual well-being and psychological resilience can be conceptualised as relatively stable internal resources that support adaptation to stress. Rather than assuming direct causal relationships, the present study examines the associations between these coping-related resources and stress-related outcomes. These assumptions form the basis of our study. To test the hypotheses of this study and visualize the conceptual relationships, the following structural model (Fig. 1) was developed, showing the measurement tools and the predicted relationships. Figure 1 illustrates the study’s expanded conceptual framework is grounded in the principles of meaning-based coping theory and contemporary psychological resilience models. Spiritual well-being is conceptualised as a core internal psychosocial resource that is associated with how individuals appraise state anxiety and their adaptive psychological functioning. State anxiety is a primary psychological response to the transplantation process, while psychological resilience is the capacity to adapt positively in the face of adversity. In addition to the direct associative pathways between spiritual well-being and psychological resilience, the model considers state anxiety as a related psychological outcome within the same stress context. It is important to note that all relationships depicted are of an associative nature and do not imply causality. This is in accordance with the study’s cross-sectional and correlational design. The study aims to extend existing psychosocial models to an underrepresented donor population by situating these constructs within a clearly defined theoretical and contextual framework, rather than proposing novel causal mechanisms.

Method

Design and setting

This study was conducted using a descriptive and cross-sectional design. This study was conducted in organ transplant clinics at a university hospital in eastern Turkey. Data were collected between April 2024 and August 2025. This hospital, where cross-matched living liver transplants were performed for the first time in our country, is the largest center in the region and the country.

Participants and sample

The study population consisted of cross-matched living liver transplant donors in organ transplant clinics. Sample size calculation was performed using the ‘G*Power Version 3.0.10’ (Franz Paul, Universität Kiel, Germany) power analysis program and calculated at a 95% confidence level. As a result of the analysis, when the standardized effect size at the = 0.05 level for simple regression analysis was taken as a medium effect size (Cohen, 2013), the minimum sample size was calculated as 94 with 0.95 theoretical power. In the study, it was acknowledged that data forms might be incomplete or incorrectly completed in parallel with the increase in statistical power. However, 30 individuals could not be contacted, 8 individuals completed the data form incompletely, and as a result, the study was completed with a total of 116 cross-matched living liver transplant donors. Thirty eligible participants could not be reached and were therefore not included in the study. No comparative data was available for these individuals.

Sampling followed a consecutive census approach. All cross-matched living liver transplant donors who were eligible and presented to the transplant centre during the data collection period were approached sequentially. This sampling strategy was chosen because of the limited and highly specific nature of the cross-matched donor population. Losses in the sample occurred due to non-response rather than dropout over time. As the final sample size (n = 116) exceeded the minimum required by the power analysis (n = 94), no additional recruitment was necessary. Furthermore, cross-matched liver transplant donors constitute a time-limited, clinically defined population, which restricts the feasibility of replacing participants outside the same clinical pathway. Participants were consecutively recruited from organ transplant clinics during routine clinical visits within the data collection period. All cross-matched liver transplant donors who met the inclusion criteria were approached by researchers and invited to participate. Due to the highly specific and limited nature of the cross-matched donor population, this sample is broadly representative of individuals undergoing this procedure at the study centre. However, as recruitment was conducted at a single centre and participation was voluntary, the generalisability of the findings to other settings may be limited.

The inclusion criteria for the study were being over 18 years of age, having no problems that would prevent communication, having no diagnosis of psychiatric illness, and being a cross-matched liver transplant donor. Individuals with other chronic diseases (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, etc.) were excluded from the study.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were established to ensure reliable self-reporting and minimise the influence of psychological and clinical factors that could affect anxiety, spiritual well-being, or psychological resilience independently. Chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic respiratory disorders were excluded, as these conditions are associated with increased anxiety and reduced psychological resilience. Excluding these comorbidities enhanced clinical homogeneity and enabled clearer interpretation of associations specifically related to the transplantation process.

Data collection tools

The following instruments were used to collect data: the “Personal Information Form”, the “FACIT-Sp Spiritual Well-Being Scale”, the “Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I)”, and the “Short Psychological Resilience Scale (BPRS)”.

The Personal Information Form was prepared by the researchers to determine certain descriptive and medical characteristics of the patients. The form consisted of two sections: the first section contained questions about the sociodemographic characteristics of the individuals, and the second section contained questions about their medical characteristics.

FACIT-Sp Spiritual Well-Being Scale, developed by Peterman and colleagues in 2002, consists of 12 items and 3 subscales scored between 0 and 4. These subscales are meaning, peace, and faith. The score obtained from the scale ranges from 0 to 48 points, and a higher score indicates a higher level of spiritual well-being in the individual [40]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Aktürk et al. in 2017. The Turkish reliability coefficient of the scale was determined as follows: the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Faith sub-dimension was 0.93, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Meaning sub-dimension was 0.78, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Peace sub-dimension was 0.81, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale as a whole was 0.87 [41]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this study was 0.82; the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the Faith, Meaning, and Peace subscales were determined to be 0.81, 0.79, and 0.70, respectively.

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I) was developed by Spielberger and colleagues in 1966. The scale adapted for the Turkish population consists of 20 items. Each item on the scale is scored from 1 to 4. Ten items on the scale are reverse-scored. The state anxiety score is calculated by adding 50 points to the difference between the total weighted scores of the direct statements and the reverse statements. The scale ranges from 20 to 80 points. A score below 36 indicates no anxiety, a score between 37 and 42 indicates mild anxiety, and a score above 42 indicates high anxiety. A higher scale score indicates a higher level of anxiety in the individual. In this study, anxiety was defined exclusively as state anxiety and measured using the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I). No separate instrument to assess perceived stress was administered. Therefore, all references to anxiety-related outcomes in the analyses specifically correspond to state anxiety. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were established by Öner and colleagues in 1983, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was reported to be 0.91 [42]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was determined to be 0.77.

The Short Psychological Resilience Scale (BPRS) was developed by Smith and colleagues in 2008 and consists of 6 items [43]. A high score indicates a high level of psychological resilience. The scale ranges from 6 to 30 points. A score of “6–11” indicates low resilience, “12–22” indicates moderate resilience, and “23–30” indicates high resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale, whose validity and reliability in Turkish were established by Doğan in 2015, was reported as 0.83 [44]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was determined to be 0.84.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics Version 22.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The data were analysed according to the variables’ types. Demographic data were expressed as percentages, numbers, and means. Continuous variables (FACIT-Sp, STAI-I, BPRS) were summarised with mean ± standard deviation (SD) to distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which indicated significant deviations from normality (FACIT-Sp: D = 0.114, p<0.001; STAI-I: D = 0.098, p = 0.012, and BPRS: D = 0.121, p < 0.001), justifying the use of non-parametric analyses. Consequently, the decision to utilise non-parametric analyses was supported by an examination of the distribution characteristics and the consistency of these with the ordinal nature of the scale scores. Related values are given in Table 2. The Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to measure the relationship between spiritual well-being, anxiety, and psychological resilience. Before interpretation, regression diagnostics were examined. Linearity was assessed using scatterplots and was deemed acceptable. The independence of the residuals was confirmed by Durbin–Watson statistics. Homoscedasticity was evaluated using residual plots, which showed no major violations. As only one predictor variable was included in the model, multicollinearity was not a concern. Due to the cross-sectional design, the regression analysis was interpreted as associative rather than causal. An exploratory linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between spiritual well-being (FACIT-Sp total score) and psychological resilience (BPRS total score). Based on the study’s theoretical framework, which conceptualises spiritual well-being as an internal psychosocial resource supporting adaptive functioning, psychological resilience was specified as the dependent variable. Assumptions of linearity, independence of errors, and homoscedasticity were assessed and met. The regression assumptions were assessed visually using scatter plots and standardised residual plots. Linearity and homoscedasticity were evaluated using residual-versus-fitted plots, with no significant deviations observed. The normality of the residuals was assessed by inspecting a histogram and a Q–Q plot. As the analyses were exploratory, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. However, this increases the risk of a Type I error, so the findings should be interpreted with caution. Standardized beta coefficients and 95% confidence intervals were reported. All results were evaluated at a significance level of p<0.05.

Structural equation modelling (SEM) could not be applied to the present study because the research design was descriptive and cross-sectional, and the variables were analysed as observed scale scores rather than latent constructs. Additionally, the normality assumptions required for covariance-based SEM were not met, and the sample size was insufficient to ensure stable estimation of a structural model with acceptable fit indices. Therefore, non-parametric correlation analysis and exploratory regression analysis were considered the most appropriate methods for addressing the study objectives.

Ethical considerations

Prior to data collection, the necessary permission was obtained from the Liver Transplantation Institute of Inonu University and ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Malatya Turgut Ozal University (Decision No.: 2024/41, Date: 29.03.2024). Furthermore, all cross-matched living liver transplant donors were informed about the study, and those who voluntarily agreed to participate were included. They were also informed that their information would be protected and that they could withdraw from the study upon request. The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As the study was non-interventional and observational, with no procedures beyond routine clinical assessment and questionnaire administration, written informed consent was not obtained from the participants.

Due to the small and highly identifiable nature of the cross-matched liver donor population, additional measures were implemented to ensure confidentiality. No personal identifiers (such as names, national identification numbers, or medical record numbers) were recorded. Data collection forms were coded numerically, and only the principal investigators had access to the code list. All electronic data were stored on password-protected devices, and only aggregated data were reported, in order to prevent the indirect identification of participants. These measures were implemented to minimise the risk of deductive disclosure and ensure that individual participants could not be identified in any publications or presentations arising from this study.

Discussion

The findings of the study conducted to determine the relationship between spiritual well-being state anxiety, and psychological resilience levels in cross-matched liver transplant donors, were discussed in light of the literature. However, no study was found that examined the relationship between spiritual well-being and state anxiety and psychological resilience levels in cross-matched liver transplant donors.

In this study, the mean spiritual well-being score of donors who underwent cross-matched liver transplantation was found to be 39.94 ± 5.19, indicating a high level. Our results were almost identical to those of similar studies [23]. Research examples examining the effect of spiritual well-being on organ transplant donors generally consisted of individuals who were not organ donors [24, 45, 46]. On the other hand, our study consisted of individuals who donated their organs to strangers for their relatives by participating in the cross-matched system as donors. Consequently, the findings of our study constitute a significant source of information and are unique.

The limited number of studies focusing specifically on cross-matched liver transplant donors means that direct comparisons with previous research are difficult to make. Therefore, the findings were primarily interpreted in relation to studies involving living liver transplant donors and transplant recipients with comparable psychosocial characteristics.

In this study, the mean state anxiety level score of cross-matched liver transplant donors was found to be 63.44 ± 4.11, indicating a high level. This finding indicates that cross-matched liver transplant donors experience elevated levels of state anxiety, reflecting the acute and situational psychological demands of the transplantation process. A study on liver transplant donors found that the mean health anxiety level score was high [47]. A meta-analysis examining 39 articles on the prevalence of depression and anxiety in living liver transplant donors showed that donors should be considered high-risk groups in terms of anxiety [37]. It was found that living liver transplant donors experienced moderate anxiety in the postoperative period [48]. Being a liver donor is as important as being a recipient. Because liver regeneration occurs within a short period of one year, becoming a liver donor may be easier than becoming a donor of other organs [49]. However, while this may encourage donation, the possibility of post-transplant complications and the necessity of coping with the burden of care in the postoperative period may cause the donor to experience a difficult period [23]. In addition to these situations, many other factors, such as the health status of the recipient and the donor’s own relatives after the transplant, can be sources of stress. Therefore, effective anxiety management must be continuously ensured during the postoperative period.

In this study, the total score on the psychological resilience scale for cross-matched liver transplant donors was found to be 21.31 ± 5.40, indicating that patients have a moderate level of psychological resilience. There are studies on psychological resilience in recipients after liver and organ transplantation, but no studies on donors have been found. Therefore, the findings of our study are an important and unique source of information. The concept of psychological resilience aligns with the principles of positive psychology, a field that emphasises optimal functioning and the promotion of well-being. Within positive psychology, positive emotion, psychological resilience, and health are considered important tools for promotion and development [50].

Our study has demonstrated a modest but statistically significant relationship between the spiritual well-being and psychological resilience of cross-matched liver transplant donors. A similar correlation was found between meaning, peace, and faith. As the spiritual well-being of donors increased, so did their psychological resilience. Spiritual care and treatment represent a significant component of psychological care and treatment in transplant patients [31]. A study by Karayurt et al., transplant patients’ compliant behavior and spiritual well-being may be related to their sense of responsibility towards their relatives who donated their organs [51]. Therefore, it can be assumed that donors also feel a similar sense of responsibility and that feeling spiritual well-being appears to contribute modestly to psychological resilience within this sample. On the other hand, saving a life is also an important psychological gain.

Spiritual well-being may act as an internal psychosocial resource, facilitating the creation of meaning, inner peace and faith-based coping strategies. These mechanisms can strengthen an individual’s ability to adapt to adversity, thereby enhancing their psychological resilience, even when they are experiencing persistent stress.

A further finding from this study is that the spiritual well-being accounted for approximately 12% of the variance in psychological resilience. This finding indicates a modest but statistically significant associative contribution. Although the observed associations were statistically significant, they were of moderate magnitude, and the regression model accounted for only a limited proportion of the variance in psychological resilience. These findings suggest that spiritual well-being is one of several psychosocial factors associated with resilience, rather than being the main explanatory factor. The clinical implications of this study should be interpreted with caution. Although spiritual well-being accounted for a proportion of the variance in psychological resilience, the explained variance was limited, and no significant association between spiritual well-being and state anxiety was observed. These findings suggest that spiritual well-being alone is not a significant factor in anxiety-related outcomes in cross-matched liver transplant donors. Instead, spiritual well-being may be one of several psychosocial resources that promote adaptive functioning in situations of sustained stress. Alternative model specifications, such as treating state anxiety as an outcome variable, are theoretically feasible within coping frameworks. However, the present study took an exploratory associative approach rather than testing directional or causal models.

The lack of a significant relationship with state anxiety suggests that acute anxiety in this population may primarily be driven by situational and procedural factors related to the donation process, which may not readily be attenuated by internal psychosocial resources. The findings support a nuanced clinical interpretation in which spiritual well-being is considered a complementary factor, rather than a primary factor, in the psychosocial assessment of donors. Further research incorporating a broader range of psychosocial and clinical variables is required to clarify the contribution of spiritual well-being to resilience and anxiety outcomes. The relevant literature emphasizes that the psychiatric status of liver transplant donors does not deteriorate and that their spiritual well-being positively affects their life satisfaction [23, 52]. A study involving liver transplant donors showed that donors had high levels of spiritual well-being after organ donation [23]. Similarly, another study in Turkey examining the effects of spiritual well-being on attitudes toward organ donation reported that participants had high levels of spiritual well-being [46]. Donating a portion of their liver by participating in a cross-matched system to help a patient with liver failure survive and cling to life may have increased the donor’s inner peace. Therefore, having a high level of spiritual well-being may result in enhanced psychological resilience.

The absence of a significant association between spiritual well-being and state anxiety, as measured by the STAI-I, may be explained by the situational nature of state anxiety. State anxiety is highly sensitive to acute uncertainty and perceived risk. In the context of cross-matched liver transplantation, for example, donors are exposed to intense situational stressors, including surgical risk, uncertainty regarding recipient outcomes, and the ethical complexity of donating to an unknown individual. In such high-risk medical situations, spiritual well-being may promote resilience and adaptive functioning rather than reducing acute anxiety levels directly. The direction of the regression model was determined a priori based on theoretical considerations and the conceptual framework of this study. Spiritual well-being was conceptualised as a relatively stable internal resource reflecting meaning, peace, and faith, while psychological resilience was considered to represent adaptive capacity in response to adversity. Consequently, spiritual well-being was treated as the predictor variable and psychological resilience as the outcome variable.

The present study did not directly examine the association between stress-related anxiety and psychological resilience. Instead, the analyses focused on the associations of spiritual well-being with state anxiety and psychological resilience seperately.In line with contemporary resilience theories, resilience is understood as the capacity to adapt in the presence of stress, rather than being a direct consequence of reduced anxiety. In cross-matched transplantation, donors may experience sustained situational anxiety due to surgical risk and uncertainty while maintaining adaptive functioning, which is supported by spiritual and existential resources. Therefore, the absence of a significant association between spiritual well-being and state anxiety does not contradict theoretical expectations; rather, it reflects the distinction between stress exposure and adaptive capacity. By grounding the conceptual framework in established stress–adaptation and resilience theories, and by maintaining alignment with the correlatonal analytical approach, the present study clarifies how well-known psychosocial relationships manifest themselves in a new clinical and ethical context. The findings should therefore be interpreted as providing a theoretical and empirical basis for future longitudinal and mechanistic research in cross-matched transplant donor populations, and as hypothesis-generating.

The findings of the present study should be interpreted in the context of its correlational design. Spiritual well-being was found to be significantly associated with psychological resilience, but not with state anxiety. The observed relationships should be interpreted as associative rather than casual. In line with the revised conceptual framework, the results suggest that individuals who report higher levels of spiritual well-being tend to exhibit greater psychological resilience; however, no significant association was observed with state anxiety. However, the analytical approach was not designed to test mediation or predictive models. The present study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that spiritual well-being is positively associated with psychological resilience, but not significantly related to state anxiety, in cross-matched liver transplant donors. This contribution is made by maintaining consistency between the conceptual framework and the statistical procedures. Although the sample size exceeded the minimum requirement indicated by the power analysis, statistical adequacy does not necessarily imply broad generalisability, particularly in highly specific clinical and cultural contexts, such as cross-matched liver transplantation. The findings should be interpreted as context-specific and hypothesis-generating, rather than as evidence that can be applied more widely. This highlights the need for multicentre and cross-cultural studies.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the single-centre design limits the generalisability of the results. Participants were recruited from a single, university-affiliated transplant centre, which has specific clinical practices, organisational structures, and patient selection criteria. Consequently, the findings may not apply to other transplant centres with different institutional protocols or healthcare delivery models. Participants were enrolled in the cross-matching program, which was conducted for the first time in our country. Secondly, the study was conducted within a specific cultural and healthcare context in Turkey. Cultural norms, religious beliefs, family dynamics and societal attitudes towards organ donation may influence spiritual well-being, anxiety and psychological resilience. Therefore, the observed associations may not be directly transferable to populations in different cultural or socioeconomic settings. Thirdly, while excluding individuals with chronic medical conditions strengthens internal validity by reducing potential confounding factors, it may also reduce ecological validity. In real-world clinical practice, many potential living liver transplant donors have comorbid conditions that can affect psychological resilience and anxiety responses. Consequently, the present findings likely reflect a subgroup of cross-matched donors who are both medically and psychologically healthier. Fourth, the absence of a control or comparison group, such as non-donors, conventional living liver transplant donors, or donors evaluated outside the cross-matched system, limits the ability to determine whether the observed psychological profiles are specific to cross-matched liver transplant donors or representative of living donors in general. In addition, the cross-sectional design prevents any causal inferences regarding the directionality of the observed associations. Although the sample size exceeded the minimum requirement indicated by the power analysis, statistical adequacy does not necessarily imply broad generalisability, particularly in highly specific clinical and cultural contexts. Furthermore, the use of self-report instruments may introduce response bias, including social desirability effects. Taken together, these limitations suggest that the findings should be considered context-specific and exploratory. Another limitation of this study is the scope of the statistical analyses. The analyses were limited to correlation and simple regression methods, rather than including multiple regression models with adjustments for potential confounding variables, such as age, sex, educational level and donation-related clinical factors. Furthermore, no advanced modelling approaches were applied. Consequently, the relationships involving state anxiety should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive. Future research would benefit from multicentre, cross-cultural and longitudinal designs; the inclusion of donors with comorbid conditions; and the use of appropriate comparison groups, in order to enhance external validity and deepen our understanding of the psychosocial processes involved in cross-matched liver transplantation. In addition, a comparison between respondents and non-respondents could not be made as no data were available for individuals who could not be contacted. This could introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings. In addition, while the study was interpreted within a coping theory framework, specific spiritual coping strategies were not measured directly. Future studies that incorporate validated spiritual coping instruments may provide a clearer picture of how individuals utilise spiritual resources in response to stress related to transplantation.

Conclusion

Cross-matched liver transplant donors exhibited high spiritual well-being, moderate psychological resilience, and elevated state anxiety levels. Spiritual well-being was statistically associated with psychological resilience but not with state anxiety. The absence of a significant association between spiritual well-being and state anxiety can be explained by the transient and situational nature of state anxiety. In high-intensity medical contexts, such as liver transplantation, anxiety levels may primarily reflect exposure to immediate stress rather than the availability of internal coping mechanisms. Within a coping theory framework, psychological resilience and spiritual well-being can be conceptualised as internal resources. However, the presence of these resources does not necessarily lead to a reduction in perceived stress or anxiety. The effectiveness of coping strategies depends not only on the availability of resources, but also on how these resources are utilised in response to stress. Previous research suggests that not all forms of religious or spiritual coping are beneficial. In particular, negative religious/spiritual coping (e.g. feelings of spiritual struggle or abandonment) has been shown to be more strongly associated with increased stress and anxiety than positive coping with stress. As the present study assessed general spiritual well-being rather than specific coping strategies, it is possible that both adaptive and maladaptive spiritual processes were present but not differentiated. Therefore, the absence of a direct link between spiritual well-being and state anxiety in this study may be indicative of the intricacy of coping mechanisms, where the mere existence of internal resources is insufficient to impact immediate emotional responses unless these resources are effectively utilised. These findings highlight the importance of considering spiritual dimensions within a broader psychosocial framework, while emphasizing the need for longitudinal and comparative research designs.

In line with these results, it is recommended that nurses responsible for cross-matched liver transplant donors, who have high state anxiety levels due to the transplant process, actively participate in developing spiritual well-being to increase individuals’ psychological resilience. ensuring the active participation of nurses specializing in situations that affect both state anxiety levels and psychological resilience, and organizing training and activities aimed at raising awareness about cross-matched liver transplantation. Living liver transplant donors play an important role in alleviating donor shortages. It is also crucial to encourage potential donors in the new system of cross-matched liver transplantation, provide the necessary psychosocial support, and increase access to these support services. Furthermore, integrating faith-based coping strategies into state anxiety management programs can support donors’ spiritual well-being and enhance their psychological resilience.
