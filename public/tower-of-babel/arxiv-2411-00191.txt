Sharp Bounds on the Variance of General Regression Adjustment in Randomized Experiments
Jonas M. Mikhaeil, Donald P. Green

Abstract
A growing statistical literature focuses on causal inference in the context of experiments where the target of inference is the average treatment effect in a finite population and random assignment determines which subjects are allocated to one of the experimental conditions. In this framework, variances of average treatment effect estimators remain unidentified because they depend on the covariance between treated and untreated potential outcomes, which are never jointly observed. Conventional variance estimators are upwardly biased. Recent work provides an estimator for the variance of the difference-in-means estimator that is asymptotically sharp. The practical relevance of this result, however, remains limited, as researchers often rely on some form of covariate adjustment, such as linear regression, when estimating the average treatment effect. Adapting propositions from empirical process theory, we provide asymptotically sharp variance estimators for general regression adjustment. We apply these results to linear regression adjustment and show benefits both in a simulation and in three empirical applications drawn from different disciplines.



Keywords—
Causal Inference, Finite Populations, General Regression Adjustment, Randomized Experiments, Variance Estimation

1
Introduction

Although a century has passed since the publication of
Neyman (1923)
, the finite population framework for analyzing randomized experiments remains a vibrant topic, perhaps because it cleanly differentiates between the analysis of an experiment on a given set of subjects and extrapolations to other populations. Recent scholarship has made important contributions to the understanding of the finite population setting (cf. central limit theorems in
(
Li and Ding, 2017
;
Schochet et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2024
)
, yet variance estimation remains a lingering concern. Neyman’s original result noted the fact that the variance of the difference-in-means estimator is not point-identified except under special conditions, such as homogeneous treatment effects among all subjects. The widely-used classical variance estimator, described below, is unbiased under homogeneous treatment effects but positively biased under heterogeneous treatment effects. The classical variance estimator is recommended in textbook treatments
(
Ding, 2024
;
Imbens and Rubin, 2015
;
Gerber and Green, 2012
)
on the grounds that it offers a “conservative” assessment of variance, which in turn implies that confidence intervals will have nominal coverage or greater.

Well aware of this problem of the unidentified variance of the average treatment effect estimator,
Neyman (1923)
derived an alternative variance estimator based on the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that provides a less conservative upper bound on the variance.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, however, may be unnecessarily loose.

Aronow et al. (2014)
, generalizing a result in
(
Robins, 1988
)
, derive a variance estimator based on sharp bounds on the estimator’s variance.
Aronow et al. show that this estimator renders a less conservative variance estimate than the classic formula as well as the bounds proposed by
Neyman (1923)
.

The practical utility of these sharp bounds, however, remains limited. As proposed by
Aronow et al. (2014)
, they are only available for the difference-in-means estimator.
This drastically limits their applicability, as randomized controlled trials
often use some form of covariate adjustment, such as weighting
(
Schochet, 2024
;
Zubizarreta et al., 2023
)
, linear regression
(
Lin, 2013
)
or machine learning methods
(
Bloniarz et al., 2016
;
Wu and Gagnon-Bartsch, 2018
;
Su et al., 2023
)
in order to improve precision.
To fill this gap, the present paper derives a sharp variance bound and an accompanying variance estimator for general regression adjustment.
To illustrate our results, we explicitly apply it to the variance of linear regression adjustment
(
Lin, 2013
)
, see Section
4.2
. In Appendix
D
, we apply our results in the context of a decorrelation method for general regression adjustment
(
Su et al., 2023
)
.
We provide an R package
to make our sharp variance estimators accessible to practitioners.

The sharp variance estimators proposed here are guaranteed to be no larger than conventionally used variance estimators, and they potentially offer improvements under heterogeneous treatment effects.
As such, they are well-suited to randomized trials in which treatment effects are suspected to be heterogeneous ex ante, e.g., when pre-analysis plans propose to investigate heterogeneous effects across subgroups. Given the many literature reviews in domains such as biomedical research
(
Kent et al., 2018
)
,
policy analysis
(
Ferraro and Miranda, 2013
)
, political science
(
Kertzer, 2022
)
, evaluation research
(
Smith, 2022
)
, psychology
(
Hickin et al., 2021
)
, and behavioral science more generally
(
Bryan et al., 2021
)

that offer theoretical and empirical grounds for expecting heterogeneous effects, it appears that many studies would benefit from sharp variance estimation. Even if the gains in precision from the sharp bounds estimator are often modest – we report benefits of up to 15 percent compared to conventional variance estimates – the cumulative gains are sizable given the vast number of studies that could potentially benefit from more accurate variance estimation.

The operating characteristics of our sharp variance estimator for linear regression adjustment are demonstrated via simulations. We also show the potential practical benefits of this approach by reanalyzing three experiments from a range of different fields. The first tests the “anchoring effect” that has long figured prominently in behavioral economics
(
Lee and Morewedge, 2022
)
; the second assesses the effects of exposure to the TV sitcom
Little Mosque on the Prairie
on attitudes toward Arabs
(
Murrar and Brauer, 2018
)
; the third revisits a biomedical trial that evaluates a treatment for acute lung injury
(
The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) Clinical Trials Network, 2006
)
.
We conclude by discussing the advantages and limitations of sharp bounds for variance estimation in the context of regression-adjusted estimation of average treatment effects.

Related work:

The design-based, or finite population, perspective on causal inference that our paper takes is widely adopted in the literature
(
Rosenbaum, 2002
;
Imbens and Rubin, 2015
;
Li and Ding, 2017
;
Lin, 2013
)
.
Abadie et al. (2020)
discuss the benefits of design-based inference in the context of regression analysis.

As noted above, the history of design-based inference for randomized experiments goes back to
Neyman (1923)
, who derives variance bounds based on the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to allow for conservative inference despite the unidentified nature of the variance of treatment effect estimators. Generalizing
(
Robins, 1988
)
,
Aronow et al. (2014)
derive sharp variance bounds for the difference-in-means estimator for a randomized experiment with two arms.

Drawing on the sharp bounds result in
Aronow et al. (2014)
,
Imbens and Menzel (2021)
consider the least-favorable coupling of treated and untreated potential outcomes given the marginals of these potential outcomes to design a “causal bootstrap” procedure.

Yu et al. (2025)
extend sharp variance estimators to stratified experiments.
Moving beyond Fréchet-Hoeffding bounds, which offer a solution to the optimization problem defining sharp variance bounds for two-armed experiments, multi-marginal optimal transport can be used to define sharp variance bounds when there are more than two potential outcomes per unit
(
Gao et al., 2025
)
, as in the case of multi-arm trials or factorial designs.

Improving variance bounds for complicated designs and under interference is an open problem. In this context,
Harshaw et al. (2026)
investigate optimized variance bounds for linear treatment effect estimators in the class of bounds that are quadratic forms in the potential outcome vectors.

2
Setup

Consider a finite population
\mathcal{U}_{N}=\{(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Invoking the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption, we denote with
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
the potential outcomes
(
Neyman, 1923
;
Rubin, 1974
)
under treatment and control. The vector
X_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{ik})^{\top}
comprises pre-treatment covariates (for convenience, we will assume
\bar{X}=0
throughout). Taking a design-based perspective
(
Abadie et al., 2020
;
Li and Ding, 2017
;
Lin, 2013
)
, we treat the finite population
\mathcal{U}_{N}
as fixed, considering only the randomness due to the treatment assignment
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})^{\top}
, which is defined such that
Z_{i}=1
if the unit is treated and
Z_{i}=0
otherwise.
We are interested in determining the average treatment effect

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)\big)=\bar{Y}(1)-\bar{Y}(0)

(1)

of the finite population.
We consider the case of a completely randomized experiment (CRE) in which
n_{1}
of the
N
units are randomly assigned into the treatment group and the remaining
n_{0}
units receive the control. In the Appendix, we show that all our results also apply to Bernoulli randomized experiments (BRE) in which units are assigned into the treatment arm with probability
\pi_{1}
. The
regression-adjusted estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}(Y_{i}(1)-f_{1}(X_{i}))-\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})(Y_{i}(0)-f_{0}(X_{i}))

(2)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(f_{1}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})),

with fixed outcome models
f_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
f_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is unbiased for the average treatment effect
\tau
. For
f_{0}=f_{1}\equiv 0
, this estimator reduces to the difference-in-means estimator. In Section
4.2
, we apply our results explicitly to linear regression adjustment.
The goal of regression adjustment is to find outcome models such that the
population-adjusted
potential outcomes

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}(q):=Y_{i}(q)-f_{q}(X_{i})\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }q\in\{0,1\}

are as close to zero as possible.

The variance of this fixed outcome model estimator is

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0}))=\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}S^{2}\big(\varepsilon(1)\big)+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}S^{2}\big(\varepsilon(0)\big)+2S\big(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0)\big)\bigg),

(3)

where
S^{2}(\varepsilon(q))=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varepsilon_{i}(q)-\bar{\varepsilon}(q))^{2}
for
q=0,1
and
S(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varepsilon_{i}(1)-\bar{\varepsilon}(1))(\varepsilon_{i}(0)-\bar{\varepsilon}(0))
with
\bar{\varepsilon}(q)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(q)
. We refer to the last term
S(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))
including both adjusted potential outcomes as the cross-term between adjusted potential outcomes.

Although the average treatment effect is point-identified, the variance of the effect estimator is not. This lack of identification stems from the fact that the cross-term between the adjusted potential outcomes
S(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))
remains unidentified because
\varepsilon_{i}(1)
and
\varepsilon_{i}(0)
are never observed jointly.

Due to the unidentified nature of the variance of the effect estimator, inference is only possible by resorting to identified upper bounds of the variance, which we will simply refer to as variance bounds.
Following
Neyman (1923)
, a variance bound based on the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the arithmetic mean-geometric mean (AM-GM) inequality
2S(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))\leq 2\sqrt{S^{2}(\varepsilon(1))S^{2}(\varepsilon(0))}\leq S^{2}(\varepsilon(1))+S^{2}(\varepsilon(0))
can be derived

\displaystyle\text{VB}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0}))^{\text{Conv}}=\bigg(\frac{1}{n_{1}}S^{2}(\varepsilon(1))+\frac{1}{n_{0}}S^{2}(\varepsilon(0))\bigg).

(4)

In the case of difference-in-means and linear regression adjustment, this variance bound leads to the conventionally used variance estimators (see Equation
14
).

The outcome models
f_{1}
and
f_{0}
need to be determined based on data.
If all potential outcomes were observable, we could minimize the above variance bound by choosing outcome models given a function class
\mathcal{F}
via least-squares

\displaystyle f_{q}\in\text{arg}\min_{f_{q}\in\mathcal{F}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}(q)-f_{q}(X_{i}))^{2}\bigg\}

on the control and treated potential outcomes separately.

In practice, we face the difficulty that only the potential outcomes associated with the allocated treatment are observed. Due to this inherent problem of missingness,
we need to work with an estimator of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{N}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(0)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{f}_{1}(X_{i})-\hat{f}_{0}(X_{i})),

where
\hat{f}_{1}
is an estimate of
f_{1}
based on the treated units and
\hat{f}_{0}
is an estimate of
f_{0}
based on the control units. We refer to
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(q)=Y_{i}(q)-\hat{f}_{q}(X_{i})
as the
sample-adjusted
potential outcomes.

In the cases considered in this paper, the additional variation introduced by the estimates
\hat{f}_{1}
and
\hat{f}_{0}
is asymptotically negligible, which allows us to work with plug-in estimators for the variance bound of the fixed outcome model estimator.

Following
Neyman (1923)
, one can use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to arrive at the following variance estimator,

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\tau}_{N})^{\text{CS}}=\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}S^{2}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\big)+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}S^{2}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big)+2\sqrt{S^{2}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\big)S^{2}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big)}\bigg),

(5)

which we call the Cauchy-Schwarz variance estimator. Here
S^{2}(\hat{\varepsilon}(1))=\frac{1}{n_{1}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(1)-\hat{\bar{\varepsilon}}(1))^{2}
and
S^{2}(\hat{\varepsilon}(0))=\frac{1}{n_{0}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(0)-\hat{\bar{\varepsilon}}(0))^{2}
with
\hat{\bar{\varepsilon}}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(1)
and
\hat{\bar{\varepsilon}}(0)=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(0)
.
In practice, the more convenient (but often more conservative) estimator based on the further application of the AM-GM inequality is used

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\tau}_{N})^{\text{Conv}}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}S^{2}(\hat{\varepsilon}(1))+\frac{1}{n_{0}}S^{2}(\hat{\varepsilon}(0)),

(6)

which we call the conventional variance estimator.
While linear regression adjustment will serve as our main illustration, our results are more general. Our results on sharp variance bound estimators (Proposition
1
) apply to general regression adjustment as discussed above.
In Appendix
D
, we give an additional example and apply our Proposition to a decorrelation estimator for general regression adjustment developed in
(
Su et al., 2023
)
. While the asymptotics of general regression adjustment have received much attention recently
(
Bloniarz et al., 2016
;
Wu and Gagnon-Bartsch, 2018
;
Guo and Basse, 2023
)
, we choose their method as an example because their asymptotic theory only requires
o_{p}(1)
consistency for the outcome estimates, matching the mild conditions of our theory.

We stress, however, that linear regression adjustment and the decorrelation method for general regression adjustment only serve as examples of our sharp bounds and their estimators. Our results on variance estimation apply broadly to general regression adjustment.

3
Sharp Variance Bounds

In the previous section, we have seen that the variance of effect estimators in the design-based setting remains unidentified. This necessitates the use of identified variance bounds. Conventional variance bounds rely on the application of the Cauchy-Schwarz and AM-GM inequalities. This naturally raises the question of whether tighter – or even sharp – variance bounds exist. This section answers this question in the affirmative.

3.1
Population CDFs and Sharp Variance Bounds

While we remain in the design-based setting and treat the (population-adjusted) potential outcomes as fixed, it is helpful to introduce the
notation
of population CDFs to utilize concepts from probability theory. For example, the use of these CDFs opens the door to results from empirical process theory.
We provide a P-Glivenko-Cantelli result for empirical distributions in the finite population setting in Appendix
B.1
. While these results may be useful more broadly in the finite population setting, here they will be crucial to prove consistency of sharp variance bound estimators for general regression adjustment, see Section
4
.

We begin by defining the population joint CDF of the population-adjusted potential outcomes

\Gamma_{N}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{\varepsilon_{i}(1)\leq\xi_{1},\varepsilon_{i}(0)\leq\xi_{0}\}.

This joint CDF is unidentified because
\varepsilon_{i}(1)
and
\varepsilon_{i}(0)
are never observed jointly. The marginal CDFs, however,

\displaystyle G_{N}(\xi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{\varepsilon_{i}(1)\leq\xi\}\kern 4.62497pt\text{and}\kern 4.62497ptF_{N}(\xi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{\varepsilon_{i}(0)\leq\xi\}

(7)

are identified. These finite population distributions are useful as their moments match the finite population quantities that appear in the variance expression, see Equation
3
.
Throughout, we will use the following notation
\mathbb{E}_{G}[\varepsilon]\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim G}[\varepsilon]
. Then, for example,
\mathbb{E}_{G_{N}}[\varepsilon(1)]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(1)=\bar{\varepsilon}(1)
and

\frac{N}{N-1}\text{Var}_{G_{N}}(\varepsilon(1))=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\varepsilon_{i}(1)-\bar{\varepsilon}(1)\big)^{2}=S^{2}(\varepsilon(1))
.

This notation allows us to re-express the variance of the fixed outcome model estimator

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\text{Var}_{G_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(1)\big)+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\text{Var}_{F_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(0)\big)+2\text{Cov}_{\Gamma_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0)\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=:\text{Var}_{\Gamma_{N}}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0})),

where
\text{Cov}_{\Gamma_{N}}(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))\equiv\mathbb{E}_{(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))\sim\Gamma_{N}}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)]-\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon(1)\sim G_{N}}[\varepsilon(1)]\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon(0)\sim F_{N}}[\varepsilon(0)]
.

Note that the variance is completely determined by the unidentified joint CDF
\Gamma_{N}
. Any joint CDF
\gamma
on the population-adjusted potential outcomes thus immediately implies a variance of the effect estimator
\text{Var}_{\gamma}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0}))
.

Holding the identified marginal CDFs
G_{N}
and
F_{N}
fixed, we ask what the largest possible variance of the effect estimator is over all unidentified joint CDFs compatible with the identified marginals. That is,

\displaystyle\text{SVB}_{N}:=\max_{\gamma\in\Pi(G_{N},F_{N})}\text{Var}_{\gamma}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0})),

(8)

where
\Pi(G_{N},F_{N})
is the set of all joint CDFs with marginal CDFs
G_{N}
and
F_{N}
. We call such variance bounds
sharp
.
Similarly, a sharp lower variance bound can be defined, see Appendix
F
.

3.2
Fréchet-Hoeffding copula bounds

We follow the literature
(
Robins, 1988
;
Aronow et al., 2014
)
in using Fréchet-Hoeffding copula bounds to determine the sharp variance bound in Equation
8
.

The works of
Fréchet (1960)
and
Hoeffding (1940)
provide bounds on copulas:

Theorem 1

(Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds, see
(
Jaworski et al., 2010
)
)
.

For any
d
-dimensional copula
C
and any
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, the following bounds hold

\displaystyle W(u)\leq C(u)\leq M(u),

where
W(u):=\max(1-d+\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i},0)
and
M(u):=\min(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
are the Fréchet–Hoeffding lower and upper bounds, respectively.

The upper bound is always a copula and hence sharp.
In two dimensions, the upper copula bound leads to the following Lemma
(
Puccetti and Wang, 2015
)
:

Lemma 1
.

For two random variables
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
with marginal CDFs
G
and
F
, we have

\max_{\gamma\in\Pi(G,F)}\mathbb{E}_{(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\gamma}[X_{1}X_{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\Gamma^{U}}[X_{1}X_{2}]

with
\Gamma^{U}(x_{1},x_{2})=\min\{G(x_{1}),F(x_{2})\}
.
The upper bound is attained when
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are comonotonic.

The application to randomized experiments is immediate.
We define the joint CDF of extremal dependence
\Gamma^{U}_{N}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\min\{G_{N}(\xi_{1}),F_{N}(\xi_{0})\}
based on the marginal CDFs of the population-adjusted potential outcomes in Equation
7
.
With Lemma
1
, we have that the sharp variance bound is given by

\displaystyle\text{SVB}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\text{Var}_{G_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(1)\big)+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\text{Var}_{F_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(0)\big)+2\text{Cov}_{\Gamma^{U}_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0)\big)\bigg).

(9)

4
Estimation of Sharp Variance Bounds

In the context of a randomized experiment, the marginal CDFs
G_{N}
and
F_{N}
, while identified, remain unobserved. Instead, we estimate the marginals with the empirical distributions
\hat{G}_{N}(\xi)=1/n_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(1)\leq\xi\}
and
\hat{F}_{N}(\xi)=1/n_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(0)\leq\xi\}
.

These yield the plug-in estimators for the sharp variance bound

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{SVB}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N-1}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\text{Var}_{\hat{G}_{N}}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\big)+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\text{Var}_{\hat{F}_{N}}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big)+2\text{Cov}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(1),\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big)\bigg),

(10)

where we use the plug-in estimator of the joint of extremal dependence
\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\min\{\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}),\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0})\}
.
To study the asymptotic regime of these estimators of the sharp bounds, we make the following regularity condition:

Assumption 1

(Convergence of the joint distribution of the population-adjusted potential outcomes)
.

The joint distribution of the population-adjusted potential outcomes
H_{N}
converges weakly to a distribution
H
with marginals
H(\cdot,\infty)=G(\cdot)
and
H(\infty,\cdot)=F(\cdot)
.

Compared to
Aronow et al. (2014)
, we face the additional difficulty that the arguments of the indicator functions in
\hat{G}_{N}
and
\hat{F}_{N}
now depend on the treatment assignment
Z
as well. This difficulty arises because we do not observe the population-adjusted potential outcomes
\varepsilon_{i}(q)
based on oracle models
f_{1}
and
f_{0}
but only the sample-adjusted potential outcomes
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(q)
that depend on the (random) estimates of the outcome models
\hat{f}_{1}
and
\hat{f}_{0}
fitted to the observed data.
The following proposition shows that this difficulty can be overcome without additional assumptions (beyond classical regularity conditions). We show that the plug-in estimator for the sharp variance bound (Equation
10
)
is consistent.

Proposition 1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Further, assume:

(a)

The proportion of treated units converges
\frac{n_{1}}{N}\rightarrow\rho\in(0,1)
.

(b)

The estimates of the outcome models are
o_{p}(1)
consistent, that is,

\displaystyle\big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(f_{q,N}(X_{i})-\hat{f}_{q,N}(X_{i}))^{2}\big)^{1/2}=o_{p}(1)\hskip 8.5359pt\text{for }q\in\{0,1\}.

(c)

The population-adjusted potential outcomes
\varepsilon_{i,N}(q)=Y_{i}(q)-f_{q,N}(X_{i})
are uniformly square-integrable, that is

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i,N}(q)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\varepsilon_{i,N}(q)^{2}\geq\beta]\rightarrow 0

as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
for
q\in\{0,1\}
.

Then

\displaystyle N\cdot\emph{\text{SVB}}_{N}\rightarrow\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\emph{\text{Var}}_{G}[\varepsilon(1)]+\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\emph{\text{Var}}_{F}[\varepsilon(0)]+2\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(G,F)}\emph{\text{Cov}}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0))

and

\widehat{\emph{\text{SVB}}}_{N}-\emph{SVB}_{N}=o_{p}(1/N).

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
1
can be found in Appendix
C
. Notice that the proof strategy provided by
Aronow et al. (2014)
does not generalize to covariate adjustment because it requires uniform convergence of the empirical distributions. Our proof proceeds by using weaker metrics – the Lévy distance and the bounded-Lipschitz distance – that metrize weak convergence and thus allow us to establish consistency without requiring convergence of CDFs on discontinuity points. This turns out to be crucial when extending to covariate adjustment because of the additional challenge of the indicator functions in the empirical distributions being random. Our proof builds on a P-Glivenko-Cantelli result for the empirical distributions
\hat{G}_{N}
and
\hat{F}_{N}
that we derive in Appendix
B.1
.
∎

Remark
.

By using weaker metrics than total variation that metrize weak convergence, the proof strategy developed here does not require the marginals
G_{N}
and
F_{N}
to converge pointwise to
G
and
F
at their respective discontinuity points. Following the proof developed here, this assumption in
(
Aronow et al., 2014
)
Proposition 1 can thus be relaxed. Our work also extends their results to BREs.

4.1
Calculation of the Estimators for the Sharp Variance Bounds

In this section, we will show how to compute the cross-term between adjusted potential outcomes under the joint distribution of extremal dependence. These, in turn, allow for the computation of our sharp variance bound estimator.
The sharp bound on the second moment in Lemma
1
corresponds to random variables that are comonotonic. Defining the left-continuous inverse of CDFs as
F^{-1}(x)=\inf\{y:F(y)\geq x\}
, we can express comonotonicity of the sample-adjusted potential outcomes as
(\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1),\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0))\sim(\hat{G}_{N}^{-1}(U),\hat{F}_{N}^{-1}(U))
, where
U\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]
is uniformly random
(
Puccetti and Wang, 2015
)
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}[\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}(0)]=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}_{N}^{-1}(u)\hat{F}_{N}^{-1}(u)du.

Let
\hat{\varepsilon}_{(1)1}^{obs},\dots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{(n_{1})1}^{obs}
and
\hat{\varepsilon}_{(1)0}^{obs},\dots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{(n_{0})0}^{obs}
be the ordered sample-adjusted outcomes in the treatment and control group, respectively, and let
\{p_{0},\dots p_{P}\}
be the ordered and distinct elements of
\{i/n_{1}\}_{i=0}^{n_{1}}\bigcup\{j/n_{0}\}_{j=0}^{n_{0}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}[\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}(0)]=\sum_{i=1}^{P}(p_{i}-p_{i-1})\hat{\varepsilon}_{(\lceil\tilde{n}_{1}p_{i}\rceil)1}^{obs}\hat{\varepsilon}_{(\lceil\tilde{n}_{0}p_{i}\rceil)0}^{obs}.

(11)

We provide code to calculate these sharp bounds in the form of an R package.

4.2
Application to Linear Regression Adjustment

One common way of adjusting for covariates is choosing
\mathcal{F}
to be the class of all linear outcome models (i.e.,
f_{q}(X_{i})=\alpha_{q}+\beta_{q}^{\top}X_{i}
). While this adjustment comes with finite-sample bias
(
Freedman, 2008
)
,
Lin (2013)
showed that with large samples, linear covariate adjustment (with full treatment-covariate interactions) is at least as efficient as a difference-in-means estimator, which makes no use of the covariates.
For linear outcome models, the treatment effect estimator in Equation
2
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{N}(\beta_{1},\beta_{0})=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\beta_{1}^{\top}X_{i}\big)-\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\big(Y_{i}(0)-\beta_{0}^{\top}X_{i}\big)

(12)

and is often referred to as Lin’s estimator.

Li and Ding (2017)
show that
the variance of
\hat{\tau}(\beta_{1},\beta_{0})
is minimized by choosing
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{0}
as the OLS coefficients of
X_{i}
in the linear projection of the potential outcomes
Y(1)
and
Y(0)
onto
(1,X_{i})
:

\displaystyle(\beta_{q},\alpha_{q})=\underset{\beta_{q},\alpha_{q}}{\text{argmin}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(Y_{i}(q)-\alpha_{q}-\beta_{q}^{\top}X_{i}\bigg)^{2}\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,q=0,1.

These optimal coefficients remain unknown, and we have to make do with the estimated least-squares coefficients

\displaystyle(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\alpha}_{1})\,

\displaystyle=\,\underset{\beta_{1},\alpha_{1}}{\text{argmin}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\bigg(Y_{i}(1)-\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}^{\top}X_{i}\bigg)^{2}\,\,\,\text{and}

\displaystyle(\hat{\beta}_{0},\hat{\alpha}_{0})\,

\displaystyle=\,\underset{\beta_{0},\alpha_{0}}{\text{argmin}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\bigg(Y_{i}(0)-\alpha_{0}-\beta_{0}^{\top}X_{i}\bigg)^{2}.

While using the estimates
\hat{\beta}_{1}
and
\hat{\beta}_{0}
introduces finite-sample bias of order
o_{p}(1/\sqrt{N})

(
Lin, 2013
)
,
Li and Ding (2017)
show that
\hat{\tau}_{N}(\beta_{1},\beta_{0})
and
\hat{\tau}_{N}(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{0})
have the same asymptotic distribution. Here the population-adjusted potential outcomes are
\varepsilon_{i}(q)=Y_{i}(q)-(\alpha_{q}+\beta_{q}^{\top}X_{i})
, and the variance of
\hat{\tau}(\beta_{1},\beta_{0})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\tau}(\beta_{1},\beta_{0}))\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{N-1}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(1)^{2}+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(0)^{2}+2\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(1)\varepsilon_{i}(0)\bigg),

(13)

where we have relied on the fact that for linear regression the population-adjusted potential outcomes are mean zero.
Typically, the (asymptotically) conservative estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\tau}(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{0}))=\frac{1}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(1)^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{0}(n_{0}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(0)^{2},

(14)

where
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(q)=Y_{i}(q)-(\hat{\alpha}_{q}+\hat{\beta}_{q}^{\top}X_{i})
are the sample-adjusted outcomes, is used. This variance estimator is equivalent to the conventional estimator (Equation
6
) in Section
2
.

Lin (2013)
showed that this conservative variance estimator can be conveniently approximated by Huber-White (EHW) robust standard errors.

Additionally, it can be shown that post-stratification
(
Miratrix et al., 2013
)
based on discrete covariates
C_{i}
with
K
categories is equivalent to linear regression adjustment with covariates
X_{i}=\big(\mathbbm{1}\{C_{i}=1\}-\pi_{1},\dots,\mathbbm{1}\{C_{i}=K-1\}-\pi_{K-1}\big)
, where
\pi_{k}
is the proportion of units within stratum
k

(
Ding, 2024
)
.

In practice, Lin’s estimator can be calculated by linearly regressing
Y^{obs}
on
(1,Z,X,Z\times X)
after mean-centering
X
. Lin’s estimator
\hat{\tau}(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{0})
of the average treatment effect is then given by the OLS coefficient of
Z
.

The following Corollary applies Proposition
1
to the case of linear regression adjustment.

Corollary 1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Further, assume the following classical regularity conditions:

(a)

The proportion of treated units converges
\frac{n_{1}}{N}\rightarrow\rho\in(0,1)
.

(b)

Finite population variances and covariances among potential outcomes and covariates have limiting values.

(c)

Finite population covariance of covariates and its limits are nonsingular.

(d)

The potential outcomes and covariates have bounded fourth moments

Then the following holds

\displaystyle\frac{(\hat{\tau}_{N}(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{0})-\tau)}{\sqrt{\kappa\widehat{\emph{\text{SVB}}}_{N}}}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

for the asymptotic distribution of Lin’s estimator, where

Under these assumptions,

5
Simulation Results

While our sharp variance estimator is never larger than the conventional variance estimator, this section offers a simulation study showcasing that our estimator is less conservative under heterogeneous treatment effects.
Additionally, simulations allow us to compare the finite-sample properties of the available variance estimators.

We know that the conventional variance estimator is consistent in the case of constant treatment effects. By extension, when the sample-adjusted potential outcomes are linearly dependent, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is exact.
In both cases, sharp variance bounds cannot provide any benefits.
We thus set up a simulation that interpolates between these two extremes by introducing heterogeneity into the treatment effects. As such, the simulation serves as a simple illustration of a case in which sharp variance bounds outperform other variance estimators.

Let
p_{i}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(\theta)
,
e_{i}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
x_{i}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\text{Unif}([-1,1])
. We then define the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)=x_{i}+e_{i}
and

\displaystyle Y_{i}(1)=\begin{cases}Y_{i}(0)\hskip 42.67912pt&\text{if }p_{i}=0\\
10+0.5e_{i}\,\,\,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

The parameter
\theta
can be thought of as a heterogeneity parameter. In keeping with the finite-population framework, we only draw the potential outcomes
Y(0)
and
Y(1)
once. The randomness in the subsequent simulations stems purely from randomly drawing the treatment indicator
Z
, i.e.,
Y_{i}^{obs}=Z_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-Z_{i})Y_{i}(0)
inherits its randomness only from
Z_{i}
. The parameter
\theta
moves the simulation between the extremes of the sharp null (
\theta=0
) and linear dependence between the adjusted potential outcomes (
\theta=1
).
For intermediate values of
\theta
, this data-generating process yields heterogeneous effects with a non-linear relationship between the adjusted potential outcomes
\varepsilon(1)
and
\varepsilon(0)
.

In this section, we apply our approach to experiments from an array of different fields. In all three examples, prognostic covariates improve the precision with which the ATE is estimated. Using our sharp variance bounds for the regression-adjusted treatment effect estimator reduces the estimated variance in all three examples, highlighting the potential benefits of applying sharp bounds to covariate-adjusted estimators.

6.2
Behavioral Economics Example: Anchoring Bias

A longstanding debate in behavioral economics concerns the extent to which people have stable and well-ordered preferences over consumer products, policy outcomes, risk, and other inputs to personal or collective utility. One challenge to traditional economic models that presuppose stable and well-ordered preferences is the phenomenon called “anchoring bias”: decisions tend to change systematically depending on the information that people are presented with prior to making a choice. For example, marketing scholars have noticed that presenting people with initial examples of very expensive consumer goods raises subjects’ subsequent willingness to pay for other consumer goods in the same class. The anchoring phenomenon has been shown not only for consumer preferences, but also for guesses about objective quantities, such as the height of the tallest redwood
(
Green et al., 1998
)
.

Lee and Morewedge (2022)
present a series of experiments that demonstrate the anchoring effect on consumer preferences concerning Miami hotels. In experiment 3a, 400 subjects completing an online survey were randomly assigned to two conditions, high price or low price. In each condition, subjects were instructed to imagine “purchasing a hotel
room for one night during an upcoming trip to Miami” whereupon they
were shown the name, a photograph, and a star rating for one of
two hotels. In the low-anchor condition, “participants first
saw this information and the price of a room for one night
in a one-star Miami Beach hotel…In the high-anchor
condition, participants saw this information and the price
of a room for one night in a five-star Miami Beach hotel.” (p.67) Subjects in both conditions were later shown analogous information for “either a two-star or a four-star Miami Beach hotel…without prices”; the outcome measure is the maximum amount each subject reports being willing to pay for a room. The anchoring hypothesis is that the higher anchor will generate a higher expressed willingness to pay. A regression of willingness to pay for the specified hotel on treatment assignment (a description of either a one-star or a five-star hotel) and covariates (whether the target hotel is described as two-star or four-star, as well as subjects’ gender and age) yields a large anchoring effect of $101.3 with a (conventional) standard error of $11.1. Their data are publicly available
(
Lee, 2021
)

7
Conclusion

This paper derives sharp variance bounds for general regression adjustment for two-arm randomized experiments. We provide a consistent estimator for these sharp variance bounds. For illustration, we apply our sharp variance estimators to linear regression adjustment
(
Lin, 2013
)
and a decorrelation method for general regression adjustment
(
Su et al., 2023
)
. Moreover, we provide software (in the form of an R package that enables experimental researchers to make use of this method.

Our sharp variance estimator yields less conservative variance estimates for randomized experiments than conventional variance estimators. This improved method of variance estimation has practical implications for power calculations, confidence intervals, and significance testing in randomized experiments.

That said, sharp variance bounds may provide negligible improvements when the distributions of the adjusted potential outcomes in treatment and control are similar. In many experimental applications, treatment effects appear to be small and homogeneous, in which case sharp bounds will generate variance estimates that are similar to (but not larger than) conventional estimates.
By the same token, the benefits of sharp variance estimates may be marginal in applications where almost all of the variation in outcomes is predicted by the observed covariates, as this reduces the heterogeneity in the adjusted outcomes that drives the improvement beyond Neyman’s Cauchy-Schwarz estimator.

Less conservative variance estimators, and sharp estimators in particular, have the potential for large cumulative benefits even if the gains in precision may be modest for individual studies.
The potential advantages of sharp variance bounds are especially relevant for biomedical trials, which often involve small numbers of subjects but evaluate treatments that are expected ex ante to produce heterogeneous effects. Such trials frequently use regression adjustment to wring additional precision from limited data, and the method presented here offers an opportunity to estimate the variance in a less conservative manner.

Despite their advantages, these sharp variance bounds remain limited to simple experimental designs. It is unclear how to extend the notion of sharp variance bounds to more complicated designs in which the variance of the treatment effect estimator may not necessarily be expressible purely in terms of a joint distribution.

Appendix A
Sharp Variance Bounds for Bernoulli Random Experiments

Similar to the discussion in the main text, in a Bernoulli Random Experiment (BRE), the variance of the effect estimator remains unidentified. The variance under this design is given by

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{N}(f_{1},f_{0}))=\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{G_{N}}\big[\varepsilon(1)^{2}\big]+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{F_{N}}\big[\varepsilon(0)^{2}\big]+2\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{N}}\big[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)\big]\bigg).

(15)

We can immediately see that the discussion of the main paper applies similarly to BREs.
In this case, the sharp variance bound takes the form

\displaystyle\text{SVB}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{G_{N}}\big[\varepsilon(1)^{2}\big]+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{F_{N}}\big[\varepsilon(0)^{2}\big]+2\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma^{U}_{N}}\big[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)\big]\bigg),

(16)

with the joint of extremal dependence
\Gamma^{U}_{N}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\min\{G_{N}(\xi_{1}),F_{N}(\xi_{0})\}
.

To estimate the sharp variance bound, we work with the empirical CDFs
\hat{G}_{N}(\xi)=1/{\tilde{n}_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(1)\leq\xi\}
and
\hat{F}_{N}(\xi)=1/({N-\tilde{n}_{1}})\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}(0)\leq\xi\}
, where
\tilde{n}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}
. In case that either
\tilde{n}_{1}
or
N-\tilde{n}_{1}
are 0, we define the respective empirical measure to be
\delta(0)
. The probability of this happening vanishes as
N
tends to infinity so that this choice does not affect our asymptotic results. For details, see the proof of Proposition
1*
in Appendix
C
. These yield the plug-in estimators for the sharp variance bound

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{SVB}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}(1)^{2}\big]+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}(0)^{2}\big]+2\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big]\bigg)

(17)

with
\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\min\{\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}),\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0})\}
.

Appendix B
Theoretical Results and Proof of Proposition
1

To make our discussion of our theoretical results more general, we will consider the general regression-adjusted estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{N}=\frac{1}{n_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}(Y_{i}(1)-f_{1}(X_{i}))-\frac{1}{n_{\bar{T}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{T}_{i}(Y_{i}(0)-f_{0}(X_{i}))+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(f_{1}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})),

(T_{i},\bar{T}_{i})=(T_{i},1-T_{i})

B.1
Some Results on Empirical Process Theory for Finite Populations

In this section, we will extend some results of empirical process theory to the finite-population setting.
Let

3

Lemma 2

(19)

Lemma 3

These lemmata are useful because they allow us to bound the Orlicz norm

The following Lemma

Lemma 4

(22)

Bounds on the Orlicz norm, in turn, provide maximal inequalities. We will require the following lemma
(

Lemma 5

For any random variables

(iii) Convergence of the extremal joint distributions

We define the extremal joint distributions
\Gamma^{U}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\min\{G(\xi_{1}),F(\xi_{0})\}
and
\Gamma^{L}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\max\{0,G(\xi_{1})+F(\xi_{0})-1\}
and want to show that
if the marginals converge weakly, so do

\hat{\Gamma}_{N}^{U}(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})=\min\{\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}),\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0})\}
and
\hat{\Gamma}_{N}^{L}=\max\{0,\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1})+\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0})-1\}
. The Lévy metric

\displaystyle d_{L}(F_{1},F_{2})=\inf\{\epsilon>0:F_{1}(x-\epsilon\boldsymbol{1}_{d})-\epsilon\leq F_{2}(x)\leq F_{1}(x+\epsilon\boldsymbol{1}_{d})+\epsilon\,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

metrizes weak convergence for multivariate CDFs. We have

\displaystyle d_{L}(\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N},\Gamma^{U})\,

\displaystyle=\,\inf\{\epsilon>0:\min\{\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}-\epsilon),\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0}-\epsilon)\}-\epsilon\leq\min\{G(\xi_{1}),F(\xi_{0})\}

(38)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt\,\leq\min\{\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}+\epsilon),\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0}+\epsilon)\}+\epsilon\,\forall(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}

\displaystyle\,\leq 2(d_{L}(\hat{G}_{N},G)+d_{L}(\hat{F}_{N},F))

and

\displaystyle d_{L}(\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N},\Gamma^{L})\,

\displaystyle=\,\inf\{\epsilon>0:\max\{0,\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}-\epsilon)+\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0}-\epsilon)-1\}-\epsilon

(39)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt\,\leq\max\{0,G(\xi_{1})+F(\xi_{0})-1\}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt\,\leq\max\{0,\hat{G}_{N}(\xi_{1}+\epsilon)+\hat{F}_{N}(\xi_{0}+\epsilon)-1\}+\epsilon\,\forall(\xi_{1},\xi_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}

\displaystyle\,\leq 2(d_{L}(\hat{G}_{N},G)+d_{L}(\hat{F}_{N},F)).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle d_{L}(\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N_{k}},\Gamma^{U})\rightarrow 0\,\,\,\text{almost surely and }

(40)

\displaystyle d_{L}(\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N_{k}},\Gamma^{L})\rightarrow 0\,\,\,\text{almost surely.}

\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}\overset{w}{\rightarrow}\Gamma^{U}
and
\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N}\overset{w}{\rightarrow}\Gamma^{L}
in probability.

With
\hat{\varepsilon}_{N_{k}}(q)
being uniformly square-integrable with respect to
\{\hat{G}_{N_{k}}\}
and
\{\hat{F}_{N_{k}}\}
almost surely, we have that

and
\{\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N_{k}}\}
almost surely.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0)]\overset{P}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma^{U}}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)]=\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(G,F)}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)]\,\,\,\text{and}

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N}}[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0)]\overset{P}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma^{L}}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)]=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(G,F)}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)].

The proposition now follows immediately.

Appendix E
Practical Considerations regarding Benefits of Sharp Bounds

Figure 4:
Q-Q plot of the sample-adjusted potential outcomes in treatment and control for our simulation in Section
5
for
\theta=0.5
.

In this section, we compare the sharp variance estimator for regression adjustment with the conventional and Cauchy-Schwarz variance estimators. For ease of exposition, we focus on linear regression adjustment for which the sample-adjusted potential outcomes are mean-centered. This allows us to switch between second moments and covariances.

Comparing our sharp variance estimator (Equation
10
) with the Cauchy-Schwarz variance estimator (Equation
5
), we see that they only differ in how they treat the unidentified cross-term.
For the sharp variance estimator, the term is estimated based on the cross-moment of the sample-adjusted potential outcomes under the extremal joint distribution (i.e., assuming the sample-adjusted outcomes to be comonotonic). This cross-moment will match the estimate in the Cauchy-Schwarz estimator if and only if the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields an equality, that is, when the sample-adjusted potential outcomes are linearly dependent. Deviations from linear dependence can be detected by inspection of Q-Q plots. Figure
4
shows the Q-Q plot for the sample-adjusted potential outcomes from the simulation performed in Section
5
for
\theta=0.5
, and Figure
2
presents the Q-Q plots for the experiments in
(
Murrar and Brauer, 2018
)
and
(
Lee and Morewedge, 2022
)
, which we discuss in Section
6
. Both plots clearly show deviations from the diagonal, apropos of the efficiency gains we found numerically in Figure
1
and Tables
1
and
2
.

In the case of linear regression, the sample-adjusted potential outcomes have zero mean. If we further assume a balanced design, the cross-moment under the extremal joint (Equation
11
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0))=\sum_{i=1}^{P}(p_{i}-p_{i-1})\hat{\varepsilon}_{(\lceil\tilde{n}_{T}p_{i}\rceil)1}^{obs}\hat{\varepsilon}_{(\lceil\tilde{n}_{\bar{T}}p_{i}\rceil)0}^{obs}=\text{Cov}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}(\hat{\varepsilon}^{obs}_{1},\hat{\varepsilon}^{obs}_{0})

reduces to the covariance between the sorted sample-adjusted potential outcomes. Defining
\rho
to be the correlation between the sorted sample-adjusted potential outcomes in treatment and control, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{U}_{N}}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0))}{(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i,N}(1)^{2})^{1/2}(\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i,N}(0)^{2})^{1/2}}=\rho.

The ratio of the cross-term estimate of the sharp variance estimator and the Cauchy-Schwarz estimator is given by the correlation
\rho
of the sorted sample-adjusted potential outcomes.
In balanced designs, the correlation between sorted sample-adjusted outcomes provides an additional diagnostic tool beyond Q-Q plots to gauge the benefits of the sharp variance estimator compared to the Cauchy-Schwarz variance estimator.

The conventional variance estimator (Equation
6
) is looser than the Cauchy-Schwarz variance estimator when the AM-GM inequality is loose. That is the case when the variances in treatment and control differ.
It is straightforward to see, however, that very uneven variances in treatment and control lead the variance of the general regression estimator (Equation
3
) to be dominated by the identified part. Let
r=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{0}}
be the ratio of the standard deviation of the sample-adjusted potential outcomes in treatment and control. We then have

\displaystyle\frac{N\widehat{\text{SVB}}_{N}}{\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)^{2}\big]+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0)^{2}\big]}=\frac{\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}r^{2}+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}+2r\rho}{\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}r^{2}+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}}\rightarrow 1

as
r\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, with significantly different variances in treatment and control, the differences between the sharp and Cauchy-Schwarz variance estimators become negligible.

Similarly, the differences among these estimators become less relevant for extremely unbalanced designs. We have

\displaystyle\frac{N\widehat{\text{SVB}}_{N}}{\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)^{2}\big]+\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{F}_{N}}\big[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0)^{2}\big]}\rightarrow 1

if either
\frac{n_{0}}{n_{1}}\rightarrow\infty
or
\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}\rightarrow\infty
. Biomedical randomized controlled trials tend to use balanced designs, highlighting one key domain that may benefit from sharp variance bounds.

Appendix F
Sharp Lower Bounds

By the same logic by which we derived sharp upper bounds on the variance of treatment effect estimators, we may also derive sharp lower variance bounds. Sharp lower bounds have been derived for the difference-in-means estimator by
Aronow et al. (2014)
.

Using the Fréchet-Hoeffding copula bounds from Theorem
1
, we immediately have the following lemma characterizing a lower bound on the expectation of a product of two random variables:

Lemma 7
.

For two random variables
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
with marginal CDFs
G
and
F
, we have

\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(G,F)}\mathbb{E}_{(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\gamma}[X_{1}X_{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\Gamma^{L}}[X_{1}X_{2}]

with
\Gamma^{L}(x_{1},x_{2})=\max\{-1+G(x_{1})+F(x_{2}),0\}
.
The lower bound is attained when
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are counter-monotonic.

The sharpness of the preceding result is a consequence of the lower Fréchet-Hoeffding bound being a copula in two dimensions
(
Joe, 2014
)
.

Based on this result, we can then derive a sharp lower bound on the variance (LSVB) and a related estimator. Under a CRE, we have

\displaystyle\text{LSVB}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\bigg(\frac{N-n_{T}}{n_{T}}\text{Var}_{G_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(1)\big)+\frac{N-n_{\bar{T}}}{n_{\bar{T}}}\text{Var}_{F_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(0)\big)+2\text{Cov}_{\Gamma^{L}_{N}}\big(\varepsilon(1),\varepsilon(0)\big)\bigg)\kern 4.62497pt\text{and}

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{LSVB}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\bigg(\frac{N-n_{T}}{n_{T}}\text{Var}_{\hat{G}_{N}}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(1)\big)+\frac{N-n_{\bar{T}}}{n_{\bar{T}}}\text{Var}_{\hat{F}_{N}}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big)+2\text{Cov}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N}}\big(\hat{\varepsilon}(1),\hat{\varepsilon}(0)\big)\bigg).

The consistency of the estimator for the sharp lower bound on the variance
\widehat{\text{LSVB}}_{N}
follows analogously to the proof of the consistency of
\widehat{\text{SVB}}_{N}
, see Appendix
C
.

The key ingredient
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{N}}[\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(1)\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(0)]\overset{P}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma^{L}}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)]=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(G,F)}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[\varepsilon(1)\varepsilon(0)]

is proved in Appendix
