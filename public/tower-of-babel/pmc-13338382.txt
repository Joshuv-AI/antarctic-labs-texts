Joint risk factor within target range and hazard of premature mortality in people with obesity
Authors: Hongxuan Chen, Lu Wang, Xukun Luo, Jiayin Li, Shunhong Luo, Juan Duan, Hulalai Hader, Xiankui Tan, Jian Zhou, Tang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Individuals with obesity are at higher risk for premature mortality compared to the general population. However, whether achieving multiple modifiable risk factors within target ranges is associated with attenuated excess mortality risk in obesity remains unclear. We included 83,505 participants with obesity and 143,198 individuals without obesity from the UK Biobank, all free of baseline cancer and cardiovascular disease (CVD). The main exposure was the number of risk factors within target range, including glycated hemoglobin < 48 mmol/mol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) < 2.5 mmol/L, absence of albuminuria, non-current smoker, and systolic blood pressure < 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg. Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, education, socioeconomic status, alcohol intake, healthy diet score, and physical activity were used to evaluate the association between the degree of risk factor within target range and risks of all-cause, cancer, CVD, and other-cause premature mortality. Fine-Gray competing risk models were performed as sensitivity analyses. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Among individuals with obesity, each additional risk factor within target range was associated with 17%, 11%, 28%, and 16% lower risks of all-cause, cancer, CVD, and other-cause premature mortality, respectively. Participants with obesity who achieved all five risk factors had 47%, 38%, 63%, and 47% lower risks compared to those meeting ≤ 2 factors. However, as the number of risk factors within target range increased, the excess mortality risks progressively diminished. Individuals with obesity meeting all 5 factors showed risks approaching those of individuals without obesity, particularly for CVD and other-cause mortality. Introduction

Over the past five decades, global obesity rates have surged nearly threefold since 1975, underscoring its rise as a major public health challenge worldwide1,2. Obesity is widely acknowledged as a significant health concern, strongly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, as well as mortality from CVD and cancer across diverse populations3,4

Although the benefits of managing individual risk factors like blood pressure in reducing mortality among people with obesity are well established5, whether the joint achievement of multiple risk factors within target ranges would further attenuate excess mortality remains uncertain.

In a recent study of participants with diabetes, those with multiple risk factors within target ranges showed substantially attenuated excess mortality risk compared with those with few risk factors within target ranges6. However, whether varying degrees of joint risk factor management further attenuate obesity-related excess mortality remains uncertain.

This study aimed to examine the association between the number of risk factors within target ranges and all-cause and cause-specific premature mortality in people with obesity. Additionally, we evaluated whether individuals with obesity who achieved multiple risk factors within target ranges had excess mortality risks approaching those of individuals without obesity. We also tested the relative importance of the risk factors among the obese participants.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort study that enrolled 502,366 participants aged 40–69 years between 2006 and 2010 across 22 assessment centers in England, Scotland,and Wales7. Data collection involved detailed questionnaires, face-to-face interviews, and physical assessments, which have provided an extensive dataset for researching the genetic, lifestyle, and environmental determinants of a wide range of diseases. The UK Biobank study was approved by the National Health and Social Care Information Management Board and the North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382).

Selection of participants

We computed the BMI by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. The obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, in accordance with the European guidelines for managing obesity in adults8. A total of 502,366 participants were recruited from UK Biobank. First, we excluded 200 participants who withdrew from the study, 42,410 with a diagnosis of cancer at baseline, and 29,003 with existing cardiovascular disease. Additionally, 184,318 participants with underweight or overweight status and 2,501 without BMI data were removed. This left 243,934 participants: 100,736 with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) and 143,198 without obesity (BMI 18.5–29.9 kg/m2). From the obesity group (n = 100,736), we excluded 17,231 with missing risk factor data, leaving 83,505 with obesity. All 143,198 participants without obesity were retained. This resulted in a final sample of 226,703 participants (83,505 with obesity and 143,198 without obesity) for direct comparison analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Definition of risk factors within target ranges at baseline

In line with previous guidelines and research on obesity management, our study identified participants with varying levels of over five modifiable risk factors within target range: glycated hemoglobin, LDL-C, albuminuria, smoking and blood pressure. Baseline plasma glycated hemoglobin levels were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography, employing the Bio-Rad VARIANT II Turbo analyzer. Glycemic status was considered within target range if HbA1c was < 48 mmol/mol (6.5%). LDL-C levels were assessed using the enzymatic selective protection method (Beckman Coulter UK, Ltd). Lipid status was considered within target range if LDL-C < 2.5 mmol/L in the 2019 ESC/EAS guidelines. Baseline urine samples were analyzed for albumin using immunoturbidimetric assays. Albuminuria status was considered within target range if the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was < 3 mg/mmol, or if microalbumin was undetectable (indicating absence of albuminuria). Smoking status was self-reported via a touchscreen questionnaire, with participants categorized as never, former, or current smokers. It was considered within target range if participants reported never or former smoking status. Blood pressure was measured twice at UK Biobank assessment centers using an Omron 705 IT electronic monitor, with manual measurements used when automated readings were unavailable. Blood pressure status was considered within target range if both systolic blood pressure was < 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure was < 90 mmHg.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality during follow-up, classified using ICD-10 codes: all-cause (all codes), cancer (C00-C97), cardiovascular disease (I00-I99), and other causes. Premature mortality was defined as death occurring before the age of 80 years following previous study24. Data on date and cause of death were obtained through linkage with national death registries, including the NHS Information Centre Death Registry for England and Wales, and the NHS Central Register for Scotland. Further details on these registries are available from NHS Digital. Follow-up was defined as the period from the baseline assessment to the date of death or the censoring date (December 19, 2022), whichever occurred first. Causes of death were classified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). The analysis focused on deaths from all causes, cancer (ICD-10 codes C00-C97), cardiovascular disease (ICD-10 codes I00-I99), and other causes.

Other variables

Age, sex, ethnic background, TDI, education years, and frequency of alcohol consumption were self-reported. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). The healthy diet score was derived based on the intake of vegetables, fruits, fish, processed meats, and unprocessed red meats, as detailed in previous studies9,10. We classified participants into two groups based on their total moderate physical activity minutes per week, following global recommendations for physical activity and health7. One minute of vigorous physical activity was considered equivalent to two minutes of moderate physical activity. The two groups were defined as follows: < 150 or ≥ 150 min/week of physical activity.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used as the primary analysis to examine associations between the degree of risk factor within target range and premature mortality risks among individuals with obesity. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals (Supplemental Table 11). Fine-Gray competing risk models were performed as sensitivity analyses to account for competing risks of death (Supplemental Table 12). Participants with ≤ 2 risk factors within target range served as the reference group in all analyses. This reference group is heterogeneous, including individuals meeting 0, 1, or 2 targets. We chose this categorization to ensure adequate sample size for stable hazard ratio estimates. The basic model was adjusted for age (years) and sex (male or female). The basic model was adjusted for age and sex. The multivariable model was adjusted for age, sex, ethnic background, Townsend deprivation index, education years, alcohol intake, healthy diet score, and physical activity. Missing data and categorical variables with missing values were both handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). The number and proportion of participants with missing data are presented in the Supplementary Table.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness, four sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we excluded participants who died within two years of follow-up. Second, we restricted analyses to participants with complete covariate data. Third, we examined overweight participants separately and compared them with normal-weight individuals without obesity, and stratified normal-weight participants by number of risk factors within target range. Fourth, we performed Fine-Gray competing risk models for cause-specific mortality to account for competing risks of death.

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 and R version 4.1.2. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05. Results remained consistent across all sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Degree of joint risk factor within target range and obesity-related excess risk of premature mortality among participants with obesity compared with participants without obesity

A total of 7,508 deaths were recorded among participants without obesity. When compared to individuals without obesity (reference group), individuals with obesity meeting < 2 risk factors had substantially elevated mortality risks: HR 1.97 (95% CI 1.83–2.12) for all-cause, HR 1.74 (95% CI 1.56–1.95) for cancer, HR 2.96 (95% CI 2.55–3.43) for CVD, and HR 1.78 (95% CI 1.57–2.05) for other causes (Fig. 2). As the number of risk factors within target range increased, the excess mortality risks progressively diminished. Among those meeting all 5 factors, the hazard ratios were HR 1.07 (95% CI 1.00–1.15) for all-cause, HR 1.10 (95% CI 1.00–1.22) for cancer, HR 1.14 (95% CI 0.97–1.35) for CVD, and HR 1.00 (95% CI 0.88–1.13) for other-cause mortality. These risks approached those observed in individuals without obesity, particularly for CVD and other-cause mortality (Fig. 2). Notably, individuals with obesity meeting 4 or 5 risk factors showed no significant excess risk for other-cause premature mortality, and those meeting all 5 factors showed risk comparable to non-obese individuals for CVD and other-cause mortality.

Other analysis

The associations between the degree of risk factor within target range and premature mortality remained largely unchanged after excluding participants who died within the first two years of follow-up (Supplement Tables 4–5). Similarly, consistent results were observed when participants with missing covariate data were excluded from the analysis (Supplement Tables 6–7). Risk factor stratification in overweight and normal-weight populations demonstrated similar inverse associations with mortality (Supplement Tables 8–10).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study, we observed graded inverse associations between the number of risk factors within target ranges and premature mortality among individuals with obesity. Each additional risk factor within target ranges was associated with 17%, 11%, 28%, and 16% lower risks of all-cause, cancer, CVD, and other-cause premature mortality, respectively (Table 2). Furthermore, having all five risk factors within target ranges was associated with 47%, 38%, 63%, and 47% lower risks of all-cause, cancer, CVD, and other-cause premature mortality, respectively, compared to those meeting ≤ 2 factors (Table 2). Moreover, the excess mortality risks associated with obesity, relative to individuals without obesity, were progressively lower with increasing numbers of risk factors within target ranges (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that meeting multiple risk factors within target ranges is associated with substantially lower obesity-related mortality burden.

To our knowledge, few studies have examined the association between the degree of risk factor achievement and premature mortality in people with obesity. We observed that individuals with obesity who met multiple risk factor targets had substantially lower excess risk of premature mortality compared to those meeting ≤ 2 targets. Our results are consistent with previous studies in diabetes populations, which demonstrated similar inverse associations between the number of risk factors within target ranges and mortality risk. Similarly, in diabetes populations, having all risk factors within optimal ranges was associated with lower excess mortality risk.

The biological mechanisms underlying these associations remain to be clarified. CVD and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are principal complications in obesity that contribute to elevated premature mortality risk11,12. Previous intervention studies have shown that achieving multiple metabolic targets (glycated hemoglobin, blood pressure, LDL-C) is associated with lower incidence of coronary artery disease and heart failure13,14. Additionally, combined achievement of blood pressure and albuminuria targets has been associated with reduced progression to kidney failure15,16. Cardiorenal interactions play a key role in the progression of numerous diseases, and cardiorenal syndrome has been linked to poor clinical outcomes, highlighting the benefits of integrated management of CVD and CKD risk factors17,18.

The magnitude of association was greater for achieving multiple versus single risk factors (Fig. 2), suggesting potential synergistic associations among risk factors, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Notably, only 17.0% of individuals with obesity met all 5 risk factor targets (Table 1), indicating substantial room for improvement in risk factor profiles within this population.

Exploratory analysis of relative importance revealed that smoking was the strongest predictor of all-cause and cancer premature mortality (Fig. 3), consistent with previous research demonstrating substantially elevated mortality rates among individuals with obesity who smoke19. For CVD mortality, smoking, albuminuria, and LDL cholesterol emerged as important predictors20. Albuminuria21 has been consistently associated with adverse CVD outcomes and increased mortality in obesity populations22.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several notable strengths, including its prospective design involving a large cohort of obese participants and the comprehensive assessment of covariates. Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, risk factors were measured only at baseline. Changes in risk factor status during follow-up (due to lifestyle modification, treatment initiation, or disease progression) could not be assessed, likely resulting in regression dilution bias and underestimation of associations. Repeat measurements were unavailable for meaningful time-updated analysis. Second, we cannot determine whether weight change during follow-up mediated the observed associations. Third, the reference group (≤ 2 risk factors within target range) is heterogeneous, combining individuals with 0, 1, or 2 targets achieved. This categorization was necessary for statistical power but may obscure differences within this group. Fourth, The cohort is predominantly White (90.6%), limiting generalizability to South Asian, African, or East Asian populations. UK Biobank participants are healthier, more educated, and of higher socioeconomic status than the general UK population, which may limit applicability to more disadvantaged populations facing greater barriers to achieving risk factor targets. Participants were aged 40–69 years at baseline; findings may not apply to younger adults or elderly individuals. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort study, achieving multiple modifiable risk factors within target ranges was associated with substantially attenuated premature mortality risk among individuals with obesity. The excess mortality burden associated with obesity progressively diminished with increasing numbers of risk factors within target ranges.
