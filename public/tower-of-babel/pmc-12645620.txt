Identifying racial inequalities in long-term outcomes among survivors of critical illness with sepsis in a US cohort: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hiam Naiditch, Victor B Talisa, Jared W Magnani, Seyed Mehdi Nouraie, Sachin Yende, Florian B Mayr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesRacial disparities in critical illness outcomes are well-described, with social determinants of health as likely contributors. We sought to identify inequalities in readmissions and mortality between black and white patients among survivors of critical illness with sepsis and assess whether these disparities were explained by neighbourhood characteristics, health insurance and hospital quality.DesignRetrospective cohort study examining 90-day and 9-month readmissions and survival as coprimary outcomes. Models included age, sex, race and area deprivation index (ADI), Medicaid status or hospital Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Star rating. Accelerated failure time and Cox proportional hazards models with subgroup analyses by age and surgical status were employed.Setting14 community and tertiary hospitals in Western Pennsylvania.Participants48 027 survivors of sepsis with critical illness; 20 952 (50.4%) male; 6489 (13.5%) identified as black.ResultsBlack patients were younger (mean age 59.0 years vs 65.8 years), more likely to have higher ADI, Medicaid insurance and receive care at lower-quality hospitals. Black patients had higher readmission risk: (90-day subdistribution HR (SDHR) 1.13 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.23); p=0.003); 9-month SDHR: 1.11 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.20); p=0.005). Adjusting for age and sex, we found no difference in 90-day and 9-month mortality (90-day acceleration factor (AF): 1.04 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.19); p=0.556; 9-month: 1.08 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.22); p=0.196), which remained consistent when including ADI, Medicaid status or hospital quality. Mortality among black patients was increased relative to white patients among patients ≥60 years (9-month AF 1.23 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.42; p=0.004)) and among surgical patients (90-day AF: 1.23 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.50; p=0.04); 9-month AF: 1.28 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.53; p=0.006)). Medicaid status, but not ADI or hospital quality, attenuated racial differences in subgroup mortality.ConclusionsIn a retrospective analysis of intensive care unit (ICU) survivors with sepsis, black patients had higher readmission rates but comparable mortality to white patients, except among older and surgical subgroups. Medicaid status influenced racial inequalities in mortality, highlighting a need for targeted post-ICU interventions.Keywords: Sepsis, Health Equity, Adult intensive & critical care, Aging

Introduction

Despite significant advances in critical care, approximately one in eight intensive care unit (ICU) patients do not survive to 1 year, and one-third are readmitted within 90 days.1,3 For some, increased healthcare utilisation and care complexity can persist for up to 2 years postdischarge.4,8 ICU survivors often face significant long-term challenges including physical, psychological and social effects, which contribute to care complexity.5 6 9 However, outcomes for ICU survivors are not uniformly distributed. Social determinants of health (SDOH), including race and ethnicity, continue to drive disparities in postdischarge care and outcomes.8 10 Disability, unemployment and social isolation may further compound inequities.11 Identifying inequities and factors that may contribute to them is essential to ensuring equitable health outcomes and interventions.12

Sepsis is a confirmed or suspected infection accompanied by new or worsening organ dysfunction in the context of a dysregulated host response to infection.13 Previous work has found that black patients face a 44% higher risk of severe sepsis compared with white patients in the acute setting.14 On discharge, black patients in the USA experience higher rates of both sepsis-related15 and non-sepsis-related hospital readmission.16 17 Prior work has shown that black patients in the USA are more likely to be uninsured,18 live in economically deprived areas19 and are more likely to undergo surgery at low-quality hospitals in segregated regions.20 While racial disparities in the ICU have been well documented, few studies have extended their focus to long-term outcomes among ICU survivors, particularly in minority populations.21,24 Moreover—in spite of increasing evidence pointing to the association of systemic factors with outcome differences by race in other health conditions25,27—fewer studies still explore potential mediators of these inequities among ICU survivors, such as residence-level deprivation, health insurance status and hospital quality.

Here, we aim to address these gaps by exploring the association of race with 90-day and 9-month hospital readmission and mortality among sepsis survivors. We hypothesise that the inclusion of area deprivation index (ADI), Medicaid health insurance status and hospital quality will attenuate racial differences in mortality and readmissions. By examining these outcomes in a contemporary cohort, we provide novel insights into racial disparities beyond the acute phase of critical illness.

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective cohort study among adults (≥18) who presented to an emergency department from 2010 to June 2018, met Sepsis-3 criteria during the first 7 days of hospitalisation,28 and survived a sepsis-related hospitalisation that included an ICU stay. Data were obtained using a previously described multicentre electronic health record (EHR) database29 spanning 14 academic and community hospitals across Western Pennsylvania affiliated with a multihospital regional healthcare system. Patients who did not experience an ICU stay were excluded. Given a small number of patients of other races (n=2415 (4.72%); unknown n=760 (1.48%)), we restricted our original survivor cohort (N=51 202) to black and white patients. Mortality data among survivors were determined using inpatient EHR data as well as Social Security Death Indexes linked to the outpatient EHR, as previously described.29 30

Derivation of the ADI and insurance subcohort

To further evaluate SDOH, we incorporated data from a linkage subcohort containing available deidentified state-level ADI—a ranking system for socioeconomic status that includes factors related to housing quality, education, employment and income31—and Medicaid insurance status. Available data included n=15 604 for ADI and n=14 923 including insurance stats (online supplemental e-tables 1 and 2). These linkage sources were combined with our primary dataset by patient identifier after harmonising variables (n=14 923 comprising the ‘ADI and insurance subcohort’) (online supplemental e-table 3). We compared patients between the linkage cohort and the main cohort using standardised mean differences (SMDs) comparing age, sex, race, comorbidity, body mass index (BMI) and 24-hour Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), treating |SMD|≤0.10 as negligible imbalance. We restricted our analyses for ADI and Medicaid insurance status to this cohort.

Outcomes

We defined our primary outcomes as 90-day or 9-month hospital readmission or time-to-survival following hospital discharge, controlling for age and sex, with and without the inclusion of ADI, hospital quality and Medicaid insurance status by race.

Statistical analysis

Choice of accelerated failure time for survival and Fine-Gray competing risk model for readmissions

We used accelerated failure time (AFT) models as the proportional hazards (PH) assumption was violated for the Cox PH (CPH) mortality model (online supplemental e-figure 1). Akaike information criterion (AIC) confirmed the log-normal distribution provided the best fit (online supplemental file 1), outperforming other distributions and the CPH model. The acceleration factor (AF; inverse time ratio or 1/exp(β))—a measure of how much a covariate accelerates the occurrence of an outcome—was used to describe associations.32

90-day and 9-month readmissions were evaluated using a Fine-Gray model (via R package ‘cmprsk’) accounting for the mortality as a competing event. The PH assumption was not violated for our readmissions model (online supplemental e-figure 3).

Survival curves were modelled by both unadjusted non-parametric Kaplan-Meier curves and age-adjusted and sex-adjusted survival curves based on AFT or CPH models where appropriate.

Covariates

We controlled for age and sex as baseline confounders. Race was self-reported. To evaluate the interaction of hospital location and quality, Centers for Medicare & Medicaid (CMS) Overall Star ratings were obtained for each hospital (npatients=48 027) from 2016 to 2018, when CMS began recording this metric.33 Low hospital quality was defined as ≤2. We incorporated deidentified state-level ADI—a ranking system for socioeconomic status that includes factors related to housing quality, education, employment and income31—and Medicaid insurance status in analyses restricted to patients with available data as previously described. ADI data were obtained through a publicly available resource from the University of Wisconsin School of Medicine and Public Health and linked by geographic location.31 A higher ADI indicates more deprivation in the socioeconomic factors measured by ADI. Comorbidities were defined using a validated weighted composite index (the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)) binned at 0 (no comorbidity burden), 1–2 (mild-moderate comorbidity burden) and ≥3 (severe comorbidity burden).34,37 Similarly, the 24-hour SOFA score reflects the highest SOFA measured within the first 24 hours of critical illness and is a validated metric of overall burden of organ dysfunction among patients with sepsis.38 Both CCI and 24-hour SOFA were obtained through available data in the EHR and included in sensitivity analyses. Additional patient-level data including BMI, a widely used measure of obesity defined as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. P values for interaction were obtained for race, age and sex; we identified age as an effect modifier.

Subgroup analysis

We found a significant interaction between age and race (online supplemental e-table 4), prompting visual inspection of our data and stratification by age subgroups: <60 and ≥60 (online supplemental e-figure 4). Given previously reported healthcare disparities among surgical patients,39,41 we examined mortality among patients who underwent surgery, comparing them to non-surgical patients using an AFT model adjusted for age and sex.

Racial differences in hospital quality, ADI and Medicaid insurance

Given the non-normal distribution of hospital quality and ADI, medians and IQR were compared between groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to confirm significance. Logistic regression was used to calculate the odds of receiving care at a low-quality hospital, having a higher ADI or having Medicaid insurance, adjusting for age and sex. Each SDOH covariate was then incorporated into AFT models to evaluate whether the association of Black race and mortality or readmissions remained significant.

Handling of missing data

Mortality data were available to at least 9 months (270 days), prompting administrative censoring beyond this period. Complete case analysis was used to evaluate differences between black and white patients due to a small number of unknown patients in the original cohort (n=760; 1.48%). For patients listed as having more than one race (n=97; 0.19%), the first race was chosen. Patients presumed transferred (n=1097) were excluded from the readmissions analysis; a sensitivity analysis was performed to ensure veracity of our findings. Measures of health insurance and state-level ADI were available for a linkage subcohort comprising approximately one-third of ICU survivors (n=15 604 for ADI; n=14 923 for insurance). The final ADI and insurance subcohort comprised a total n=14 923, as described above. All ADI/insurance analyses were confined to this subcohort. Within the ADI and insurance subcohort, insurance data were missing for 1813 patients (12.2%) (online supplemental e-table 3). We used multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) (R package ‘mice’, m=20, 10 iterations, seed=123) to impute insurance, specifying a multinomial logistic model for insurance (method=‘polyreg’). The imputation model included black race, age, hospital and ADI state rank as predictors. Variables were typed prior to imputation (insurance and hospital as factors; age and ADI rank as numeric). Convergence was checked via the loggedEvents output (no issues observed). Models were fit in each imputed dataset and combined using Rubin’s rules (R ‘mice:with()’ and ‘pool()’). For readmissions, 232 patients (3.90%) remained without insurance or ADI data postimputation (table 1); complete case analysis was used to avoid double imputation. Patients geocoded to Census ‘Group Quarters’ (GQ; eg, nursing facilities, correctional institutions, dormitories) were excluded (n=16, 0.27% of the patients with available readmissions data) because ADI is not defined/meaningful for institutional living arrangements as well as due to small numbers.

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, we fit flexible parametric survival models (Royston-Parmar/generalised survival models) using restricted cubic splines for baseline hazard. We evaluate PH and PO link functions and allowed time-varying effects for race when supported by fit (df chosen by AIC and visual calibration). Model-based absolute risks at 90 and 270 days and corresponding risk differences (RD, in percentage points) and risk ratios were obtained from these models. 95% CIs were obtained using the delta method (R package ‘restpm2’) and confirmed in a patient-level bootstrap (fixed model form; B=200).

Further, while we sought to model baseline risk by age and sex, we sought to evaluate the role of acute illness and comorbidities as confounding the association between race and outcomes as in typical modelling approaches. As such, we included 24-hour SOFA score and comorbidities in our baseline AFT models to assess the sensitivity of our results. Furthermore, given varying impact on outcomes seen with individual comorbidities,42 43 we calculated the impact of adjusting individually for congestive heart failure (CHF), lung disease, stroke, CKD, liver disease, malignancy and diabetes identified using International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th editions (ICD-9/10) codes among our readmissions database.

Analyses were performed using R V.4.4.1 (2024-06-14 ucrt).

Patient and public involvement

This study was based on a retrospective analysis of EHR database data; therefore, patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Primary outcomes

Black patients were more likely to be readmitted within 90 days and 9 months

Among 7047 patients with available readmission data, 1097 were excluded due to transfer, leaving a final cohort of 5950 patients (864 black, 5086 white) (). Patients who were not readmitted were included in the analysis. When accounting for the competing risk of mortality, black patients had a higher likelihood of readmission at both 90 days (650/864 (75.2% (95% CI 72.3% to 78.1%)); subdistribution HR (SDHR) 1.13 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.23); p=0.003) and 9 months (832/864 (96.3% (95% 95.0% to 97.6%)); SDHR 1.11 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.20); p=0.005). Similar results were seen on inclusion of transferred patients as reported below in our sensitivity analysis. Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier and cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves for Fine-Gray competing risks are shown in figure 1. Comprehensive CIF outcomes by race are reported in online supplemental e-table 6.

When incorporating ADI into our model, a significant difference in readmissions persisted (90-day SDHR 1.12 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.22; p=0.006); 9-month SDHR 1.10 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.19; p=0.010) (online supplemental e-table 7). When incorporating Medicaid insurance into our model, readmissions at 90 days (SDHR 1.13 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.23; p=0.005)) and 9 months (SDHR 1.11 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.20); p=0.006) remained significant (online supplemental e-table 7). When incorporating hospital quality into our model, readmissions at 90 days (SDHR 1.11 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.21; p=0.013)) and 9 months (SDHR 1.10 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.18); p=0.014) remained significant (online supplemental e-table 7).

Black patients had comparable post-ICU mortality to white patients up to 9 months

An AFT model with log-normal distribution was fitted to examine the effect of race, age and sex on 90-day and 9-month mortality after discharge from a hospitalisation that included an ICU stay. The AF for black patients compared with white patients was 1.04 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.19; p=0.556) for 90-day mortality and 1.08 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.22; p=0.196) for 9-month mortality, suggesting no significant difference in the primary outcome of 90-day or 9-month mortality. In our models for both 90-day and 9-month mortality, both age (AF for 90 days: 1.08 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.08; p<0.001); 9 months: 1.08 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.09; p<0.001)) and male sex (AF for 90 days: 1.28 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.40; p<0.001); 9 months: 1.32 (95% CI 1.22 to 1.43; p<0.001)) were associated with lower survival. 90-day unadjusted mortality was 1013/6489 (15.6%) for black patients and 7568/41 538 (18.2%) for white patients. 9-month unadjusted mortality was 1532/6489 (23.6%) for black patients and 11 168/41 538 (26.9%) for white patients. Kaplan-Meier curves and survival curves based on AFT models, adjusted for age and sex, are shown in figure 2. On adjusting for ADI within the ADI and insurance subcohort, there was no significant difference in mortality (90-day AF 1.13 (95% CI 0.89 to 1.43; p=0.321); 9-month AF 1.08 (95% CI 0.87 to 1.33; p=0.492) (online supplemental e-table 7). Similarly, no significant difference in mortality was seen when adjusting for Medicaid insurance status. Within the primary cohort, no difference in mortality was seen on incorporation of CMS Star hospital quality rating (online supplemental e-table 9 and 10).

Black patients were more likely to be subject to adverse SDOH

Among a subgroup with available state-level ADI data (n=15 604; median 7 (IQR 5–9), black patients were more likely to have higher ADI compared with white patients (median for black patients: 9 (7–10); for white patients: 7 (4–9); p<0.0001). Black patients were more likely to have Medicaid insurance (aOR 1.94 (95% CI 1.67 to 2.25; p<0.001)). Black patients were more likely to be treated in a lower quality hospital (median Star rating for black patients: 2 (2–3); for white patients: 2 (2–4); p<0.001). Black patients had a significantly higher odds of being treated at a low-quality hospital (aOR 1.81 (95% CI 1.71 to 1.93; p<0.001)).

Secondary outcomes

Subgroup analysis by age group

Given a significant interaction between age and black race, we conducted a subgroup analysis among patients <60 and ≥60. Among black patients ≥60, time-to-9-month mortality was accelerated compared with white patients (AF 1.23 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.42; p=0.004) but not 90 days (1.13 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.32; p=0.11)). In contrast, no difference was seen among black patients <60 (AF for 90 days: 0.81 (95% CI 0.56 to 1.10) p=0.17; for 9 months: 0.78 (95% CI 0.60 to 1.01); p=0.058). A forest plot comparison by subgroup is shown in figure 3. Kaplan-Meier and adjusted survival curves are shown in online supplemental e-figure 5. Similarly, when incorporating ADI, no significant association between black race and mortality was seen among older black patients (90-day AF 1.24 (95% CI 0.94 to 1.64; p=0.128); 9-month AF 1.25 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.60; p=0.080), although baseline non-ADI-adjusted differences were insignificant for this population in the smaller cohort (90-day AF 1.21 (95% CI 0.92 to 1.60; p=0.168); 9-month AF 1.23 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.57; p=0.092) (online supplemental e-table 8). When Medicaid insurance was included, the association between older black patients and 9-month mortality was rendered non-significant (AF 1.18 (95% CI 0.92 to 1.51; p=0.272)) (online supplemental e-table 9). In contrast, when adjusting for CMS Star Hospital Quality Rating, the association between older black patients and 9-month mortality remained significant (AF 1.26 (95% CI 1.09 to 1.453; p=0.002)) (online supplemental e-table 10).

Subgroup analysis by surgical status

We evaluated whether mortality differences were explained by surgical status. We found that black patients who underwent surgery had significantly accelerated 90-day and 9-month mortality (AF for 90 days: 1.23 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.50; p=0.04); for 9 months: 1.28 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.53; p=0.006). Among non-surgical patients, no significant difference was seen for 90-day mortality (AF 0.91 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.09; p=0.30) or 9-month mortality (0.95 (95% CI 0.80 to 1.11), p=0.67). Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier curves and AFT-based adjusted survival curves for both surgical and non-surgical patients are shown in online supplemental e-figure 6. On adjusting for ADI within our surgical cohort, the association with 90-day and 9-month mortality remained significant (90-day AF 1.77 (95% CI 1.23 to 2.54; p=0.002); 9-month AF 1.58 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.16; p=0.004) (online supplemental e-table 8). On adjusting for Medicaid among black patients who underwent surgery, 90-day mortality remained significant (AF 1.48 (95% CI 1.02 to 2.15; p=0.039) but was attenuated at 9 months (AF 1.34 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.86; p=0.075) (online supplemental e-table 9). When adjusting for CMS Star Hospital Quality Rating, patients who underwent surgery continued to show higher mortality at both time points (90-day AF 1.25 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.52; p=−0.024); 9-month AF: 1.30 (95% CI 1.09 to 1.56; p=0.004)) (online supplemental e-table 10).

Sensitivity analysis

Inclusion of patients presumed transferred into our competing risk model led to similar findings of a higher likelihood of readmission for black patients at both 90 days (SDHR 1.14 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.24); p=0.001) and 9 months (SDHR 1.22 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.32); p<0.001) (online supplemental e-table 11). Using flexible parametric survival models selected by AIC, we found that differences were statistically significant but small in absolute magnitude and directionally consistent with our AFT results (online supplemental e-table 12). In our primary model, the non-significant association between race and 90-day or 9-month mortality remained after adjusting for comorbidities and 24-hour SOFA score (online supplemental e-table 13). Among patients ≥60 and surgical subgroups, inclusion of these covariates led to a non-significant association between race and mortality. Conversely, in both the age <60 and non-surgical subgroups, black patients experienced improved survival at 90 days and 9 months (online supplemental e-table 14 and 15). As a further sensitivity analysis, we evaluated adjustment for individual comorbidities including CHF, pulmonary disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease, diabetes and malignancy and found that the significant increase in readmissions for black patients at 90 days or 9 months was retained (online supplemental e-table 16).

Discussion

We evaluated racial inequities in hospital readmission and mortality among survivors of critical sepsis, incorporating area deprivation, insurance status and hospital quality. No difference in survival was found across all age groups, but black patients had higher rates of 90-day and 9-month readmission rates, even after adjusting for ADI, Medicaid status and hospital quality. These findings align with prior studies on 7-day and 30-day readmissions among black patients in other healthcare settings.16 17 Our findings build on previous studies of ICU outcomes summarised by McGowan et al, including an updated time frame (2010–2018) and a focus on racial disparities in ICU survivors.24 We further incorporate socioeconomic and hospital quality variables, which are critical to understanding social and structural determinants of health.

In our study, we sought to model the complex relationship of race and outcomes as mediated by structural factors by integrating SDOH, including area deprivation, health insurance status and hospital quality. We found that black patients tend to have higher ADI, are more likely to be insured by Medicaid and are more likely to be treated at a low-quality hospital, consistent with other reports.18,20 However, adjusting for these factors did not alter the association with readmissions, reinforcing previous findings that inequities may persist even when ADI or hospital quality are accounted for.44,46 Our findings suggest a role for unmeasured confounders such as healthcare access and healthcare utilisation,47 which may be particularly impactful for ICU survivors with complex care needs. Moreover, as with other criteria,48 CMS Star ratings may be an imperfect measure of hospital quality for minorities. Finally, while Medicaid insurance has been shown to influence post-ICU outcomes such as transfer to a long-term acute care hospital,49 inclusion of Medicaid did not significantly affect readmissions, possibly due to a smaller sample size.

Among patients ≥60, we found shortened time to 9-month mortality. This subgroup analysis was informed by a significant interaction of age with race, suggesting effect modification. While these results align with the reported literature,50 our primary mortality findings contrast with reports in other disease populations,51 including septic shock survivors.52 Further, when examining a subgroup of patients who underwent surgery, we found that black patients had shortened time to 90-day and 9-month mortality, consistent with the reported literature for common surgical procedures.40 53 Importantly, for both subgroups, the mortality difference was retained when incorporating ADI and hospital quality, but dissipated when including Medicaid status. Our findings may reflect that Medicaid captures elements of socioeconomic vulnerability and access to care not fully accounted for by neighbourhood deprivation or hospital quality. Prior work has shown that Medicaid-insured patients often experience worse outcomes, which may relate to barriers in care continuity and healthcare access.54 At the same time, expanding Medicaid eligibility has been shown to reduce mortality,55,57 improve access to care and increase preventative service utilisation.58 Taken together, these findings suggest a complex, potentially bidirectional relationship: Medicaid status may attenuate apparent racial inequities in some models because it acts as a proxy for structural disadvantage, yet improving Medicaid coverage has the potential to mitigate these same inequities by expanding access to care. In contrast to the attenuation seen on adjusting for Medicaid, the lack of attenuation on adjusting for hospital quality suggests that what happens after a patient leaves the hospital is at least as important as what happens during the hospital stay,59 60 particularly for elderly and postoperative patients. Moreover, ADI—although a validated measure of socioeconomic disadvantage—may not fully capture postdischarge barriers to care. For instance, a previous study showed higher rates of admission and inpatient mortality for sepsis among different age groups in medically underserved areas, which proved more significant than other indicators accounted for by the ADI such as high school education, median income and proportion of the population below the poverty level.61 Similarly, differences in access to care between rural and urban areas are also not fully captured using ADI.62 On discharge, differences seen in medically underserved areas may be exacerbated, particularly when care burden is complex, as is often the case with ICU survivors. Future studies would likely benefit from the inclusion of complementary indices that more adequately capture healthcare access and medical utilisation.

We conducted sensitivity analyses incorporating the CCI and SOFA scores. In the age and surgical subgroups, adjusting for acute and chronic illness diminished the association between race and mortality (online supplemental e-table 9–10). This suggests that structural inequalities in health status may contribute to healthcare disparities, and addressing identified comorbidities will likely alleviate the inequalities seen among Black patients with sepsis who survive an ICU stay. Interestingly, the inclusion of individual comorbidities such as CHF and CKD did not alter the rate of readmissions in spite of their recently recognised association with adverse outcomes,42 43 which may be due to the limitations of our dataset.

Our baseline model included age and sex as covariates, which may show intersectionality with race.63 64 This approach allowed us to identify high-risk groups, particularly older black ICU survivors, where disparities are pronounced, emphasising that such characteristics rarely function in isolation. Further, the intersection of sex and race may change—and inequalities may accumulate—with increasing age, which may in turn influence access to care and care processes.65 66

Early follow-up has been shown to reduce unplanned readmissions for patients with CHF, COPD67 and ischaemic stroke68 69 as well as mortality following admissions for CHF.67 However, its impact for ICU survivors remains less well-studied.70,72 Among the various interventions under study, post-ICU clinics may help alleviate the complex care burden of ICU survivors,73 74 particularly among those who might suffer compounded care complexity related to compounded social and structural inequities. While the impact of post-ICU care on racial inequities has not been robustly studied, examples from other populations including stroke survivors point to transitional care models that incorporate culturally tailored, community-based programmes.75 76 Further studies may explore optimal interventions for ICU survivors across the healthcare spectrum.

Our findings also highlight Medicaid status as one key factor in the association of race and mortality. Policy-level interventions can include hospital-based strategies to reduce readmissions and enhanced community-based follow-up care for ICU survivors to address inequities in comorbidities and geographical location. Targeted interventions may prioritise older ICU survivors, surgical patients and Medicaid-insured individuals. Potential strategies include early or more frequent77 primary care or subspecialist follow-up67 and expansion of dedicated post-ICU interventions to mitigate care complexity.

Limitations

This study examines disparities between black and white patients, limiting the generalisability of our findings. More diverse datasets are needed. Our cohort is likewise restricted to hospitals in Western Pennsylvania, and further, our readmissions data included only those readmissions within one healthcare system, a recognisable limitation. SDOH data were only available for a subset of the cohort (approximately one-third of the patients), further limiting the generalisability of our findings. Future studies should include other racial and ethnic groups and broader geographic regions to improve the external validity of our findings.24 78 79 Our dataset includes only patients presenting to UPMC emergency department, excluding patients who were transferred from an outside hospital who may have experienced delays in sepsis care such as antibiotic initiation and resuscitation which may in turn contribute to worse outcomes.80 81 We additionally note that our focus on ICU survivors limits the generalisability of our findings to patients with less severe sepsis. We further recognise that not differentiating between in-hospital versus community-acquired sepsis is a limitation within our study given differing severity and outcomes between the two categories.82 Data availability was limited to 9 months postdischarge rather than a longer follow-up period, such as 1 year. We recognise that race serves as a proxy for social constructs shaped by sociopolitical, environmental and cultural factors influencing care processes and health. While we incorporated ADI, health insurance status and hospital quality as measures of socioeconomic status and SDOH, a larger dataset with additional variables would better capture the impact of social and SDOH. Furthermore, CMS Quality Star ratings were not available prior to 2016 and may have changed over time. Additionally, while the use of CMS Quality Star ratings has been previously reported in the literature.83 84 Star ratings have been shown to be higher for small hospitals with fewer Medicaid patients, limiting their interpretability.85 Finally, Star ratings include readmissions, raising the risk of overadjustment by embedding the outcome in the model, further limiting model interpretability.

Our subgroup analyses did not account for specific types of surgery, which may be an important confounder. Future analyses should examine how comorbidities and other covariates mediate the association between race, mortality and readmissions. Finally, we acknowledge that subgroup analyses are hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-testing, warranting further validation.

Conclusions

Black patients had comparable survival to white patients but were more likely to be readmitted at 90 days and 9 months. Black patients were more likely to have higher ADI, be insured by Medicaid and receive care at lower-quality hospitals; however, these differences did not impact the likelihood of readmission. Subgroups of black patients who were ≥60 or who underwent surgery were less likely to survive to 9 months; only Medicaid status attenuated the effect of race and mortality, but not readmissions. Further research is needed identifying effective interventions that may reduce healthcare disparities among ICU survivors is warranted.
