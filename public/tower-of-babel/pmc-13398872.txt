Machine Learning Methods to Predict the Length of Stay for Acute Stroke: A Scoping Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Zhenran Xu, Monique F. Kilkenny, Tzu‐Yung Kuo, Jia Rong, Lachlan L. Dalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReliable hospital length of stay (LOS) predictions improve patient outcomes and optimize clinical pathways in stroke care. There is limited evidence systematically investigating benefits and limitations of existing machine learning (ML) models for determining factors associated with LOS. We conducted a scoping review to: (1) investigate factors affecting LOS in stroke to identify key predictors for enhancing ML models; and (2) assess the performance of predictive models (ML and traditional models) to generate insights for improving LOS predictions. This review was undertaken in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, and Scopus, searching English‐language records from 2014 to 2024 using the search terms “stroke” and “length of stay”. We included studies investigating associated factors or assessing modeling approaches for predicting LOS in stroke. A meta‐analysis using Bayesian methods was conducted to compare predictive performance (e.g., pooled C‐statistics) of ML and logistic regression. Of 38 included studies, we identified 38 factors consistently extending LOS (patient characteristics [n = 20], health outcomes [n = 9], social circumstances [n = 2], and clinical care processes [n = 7]). Nine studies (24%) reported predictive models for predicting LOS (continuous or binary outcomes), with stroke severity (particularly the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale at admission) being the most important predictor across seven studies. For binary outcomes, pooled C‐statistic with 95% credible intervals was 0.764 (0.715–0.812) for best‐performing ML models and 0.743 (0.692–0.795) for logistic regression across studies (no statistical difference; Bayes factor = 0.783). ML models did not demonstrate superior predictive performance, clinical reliability, and clinical utility over traditional models. Future ML models should prioritize a data‐driven selection and transformation of informative inputs, enhance model generalizability and calibration, improve clinical interpretability, and implement a comprehensive model evaluation (including discrimination, calibration, and reclassification metrics). These methodological improvements enable more clinically meaningful LOS predictions to optimize personalized clinical pathways and support precise clinical decision‐making at an early stage of stroke care.

Background

The economic and clinical impact of stroke is increasing. In 2019, the global value of lost welfare for stroke (estimated by combining gross domestic product and disability‐adjusted life years) was nearly $2060 billion [1]. Beyond direct medical costs, healthcare costs could be reduced by optimizing hospital length of stay (LOS). In Victoria (Australia), optimizing LOS for stroke could save over AUS$125 million annually [2]. Longer LOS increases the risk of developing complications or poststroke depression [3, 4]. Predictive modeling of LOS, especially clinically explainable models, enables clinicians to deliver personalized interventions by highlighting patient‐specific drivers of prolonged hospitalization [5, 6].

Despite its potential, predictive modeling of LOS should be distinguished from explanatory modeling to understand their respective applications for stroke care. Explanatory modeling assesses associations between inputs and outcomes using statistical metrics (e.g., odds ratios) [7]. This approach identifies factors affecting LOS to select clinically relevant inputs for models. However, LOS is shaped by diverse factors, such as disease severity [8]. There is a need to synthesize factors associated with LOS in stroke. In contrast, predictive modeling focuses on optimizing performance through improving metrics (e.g., accuracy) [7]. Predictive modeling of LOS (e.g., logistic regression) has been used to enhance healthcare resource allocation and inpatient care [9]. While both modeling types have their respective advantages, most published studies have primarily implemented explanatory modeling to assess associations between factors and LOS [10]. There is limited evidence assessing the performance of predictive models, especially machine learning (ML) methods, for determining the clinical reliability of LOS predictions in stroke [10].

The aims of this review were to (1) investigate factors influencing LOS for acute stroke, in order to identify key inputs that enhance ML models and support precise clinical decision‐making; and (2) assess the performance of models (e.g., ML and traditional methods) for predicting LOS, generating insights to improve ML models and optimize clinical pathways in stroke care.

Methods

This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guideline [11]. We registered a protocol on the Open Science Framework on 08 August 2024 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/EB3HG).

Scoping Review Framework

The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome framework was adopted to develop the review question. Population of interest was adult patients with acute stroke. Intervention was the use of ML methods. Comparison was with traditional modeling methods (e.g., logistic regression). Outcome focused on the performance in predicting LOS.

Given the diversity of factors influencing LOS across patient, clinical, and system levels, as well as substantial methodological variations in predictive modeling approaches, this review needed a comprehensive mapping of heterogeneous evidence rather than a narrowly focused evaluation. A scoping review enables a broader exploration of available evidence to identify characteristics related to the topic [12]. We also conducted a meta‐analysis to move beyond descriptive comparison, enhancing analytical depth.

Search Strategy

Our search strategy comprised two keywords: “stroke” and “length of stay”, including possible synonyms (Table S1). The search strategy was undertaken in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, and Scopus databases on August 13, 2024 (Table S2).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included publications in English between January 1, 2014, and August 13, 2024, to capture contemporary modeling methods. Reviews, conference abstracts, editorials, grey literature, and studies in rehabilitation settings were excluded. Original articles were included if they used explanatory modeling for identifying associated factors or predictive modeling for prediction.

Article Screening

One author (Zhenran Xu) implemented the search strategy and extracted results. Two authors (Zhenran Xu and Tzu‐Yung Kuo) independently assessed titles, abstracts and full texts for eligibility. Disagreements were resolved with a third author (Lachlan L. Dalli). Inter‐rater reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa (κ). κ = 0–0.20 was interpreted as no agreement, 0.21–0.39 as minimal, 0.40–0.59 as weak, 0.60–0.79 as moderate, 0.80–0.90 as strong, and > 0.90 as almost perfect agreement [13].

Data Extraction

One author (Zhenran Xu) extracted data from included articles in consultation with all authors, focusing on article characteristics (e.g., year, region, and sample size), factors affecting LOS, and details of predictive modeling for LOS (e.g., stroke type, data source, outcome, predictors, and performance metrics). A second author (Tzu‐Yung Kuo) independently validated the extracted data.

Factors were grouped into five categories: patient characteristics, health outcomes, social circumstances, clinical care processes, and health system. Categories were adapted from a framework that comprehensively organizes stroke‐related factors across patient, clinical, and system levels [14]. Important predictors were identified if they were explicitly reported as statistically important in predictive models (e.g., through explainable artificial intelligence [AI] such as SHapley Additive exPlanations [SHAP]).

Risk of Bias Assessment

Quality and applicability of included studies were assessed using an updated Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool [15]. Assessment criteria were adjusted to align with the aims of this scoping review (Table S3). Two authors (Zhenran Xu and Tzu‐Yung Kuo) independently conducted the assessment, and disagreements were resolved with a third author (Lachlan L. Dalli).

Statistical Analyses

We conducted a random‐effects Bayesian meta‐analysis to pool C‐statistics (i.e., receiver operating characteristic area under the curve [AUC]) across studies predicting binary LOS. Bayesian methods synthesize data from a small number of studies with potential heterogeneity and provide a probabilistic interpretation of pooled estimates with 95% credible intervals (CIs) [16]. Studies with missing data on C‐statistics were excluded from this meta‐analysis. Where multiple C‐statistics were reported in a single study, the best‐performing C‐statistic per modeling approach (ML or logistic regression) was selected. Standard errors of C‐statistic were estimated using C‐statistic, sample size, and event size [17].

We applied a prior using an empirical range of C‐statistics observed across studies. Specifically, the mean C‐statistic for baseline logistic regression was assigned as an intercept with N (μ = 0.75, σ = 0.05). Change in C‐statistic by ML (β) was assigned a N (μ = 0, σ = 0.05), reflecting prior uncertainty about performance improvement. Between‐study heterogeneity (τ) was modeled with a half‐Student‐t distribution (ν = 3, σ = 0.1). A truncated Gaussian likelihood ensured pooled C‐statistics remained within 0–1. Posterior summaries of pooled C‐statistics and between‐study heterogeneity were estimated. Posterior probability and Bayes factor (BF) assessed the strength of evidence that ML achieved higher pooled C‐statistics. BF 0.3–3 was defined as weak evidence, > 3–10 as moderate evidence, and > 10 as strong evidence [18]. Meta‐analysis was analyzed using R programming (v4.4.1) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with “metamisc,” “bridgesampling,” and “brms” packages.

Sensitivity Analysis

Prior assumptions in Bayesian methods influence posterior distributions. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using alternative priors to evaluate the robustness of results. Alternative priors included variations in intercept mean (µ = 0.65, 0.85), representing different baseline C‐statistics of logistic regression. Variations in the standard deviation of intercept (σ = 0.025, 0.10) reflected narrower or wider ranges of C‐statistics of logistic regression. Variations in the standard deviation of β (σ = 0.025, 0.10) represented smaller or larger expected changes in C‐statistics by ML. Variations in the heterogeneity scale (σ = 0.05, 0.20) represented restrictive or flexible between‐study heterogeneity.

Results

This review finally included 38 unique studies (Figure 1). Moderate agreement was observed during the titles/abstracts (κ = 0.66) and full‐text screening stage (κ = 0.60; Table S4). Of the 38 studies, 71% were retrospective; 53% were in Asia; 87% included ≤ 10,000 patients (Table 1). Most data sources were electronic health records (55%) or stroke registries (32%). Nine studies used predictive modeling for LOS in stroke (Table S5). Seven of these nine studies focused on models specifically for ischemic stroke, while others included both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. LOS outcomes were either continuous (i.e., regression task) or classification outcomes (i.e., classification task). For the classification task, LOS was generally dichotomized into prolonged and short LOS groups. The median LOS ranged from 4 to 10 days in these nine studies. Thresholds of dichotomizing LOS varied across studies (7, 9, 11, and 14 days).

Quality and Applicability Concerns

Quality and applicability concerns of included studies were summarized in Table 2. For quality concerns, 21 studies (55%) were rated as high risk and 14 studies (37%) as unclear risk. In the participants' domain, 11 studies (29%) were rated as high risk for quality primarily due to inappropriate inclusion and exclusion criteria of participants. The greatest quality concern was observed in the analysis domain, where 13 studies (34%) were rated as high risk and 21 studies (55%) as unclear risk, largely due to inappropriate reporting on how missing data were handled. For applicability concerns, two studies (5%) were rated as high risk due to the inclusion of insurance status as a predictor. Insurance status is closely related to healthcare financing systems that may differ substantially across countries.

Sixty‐four unique factors were identified as associated with LOS in five categories (Table 3): patient characteristics (n = 35), health outcomes (n = 12), social circumstances (n = 4), clinical care processes (n = 9), and health system (n = 4). Thirty‐eight factors were consistently associated with prolonged LOS (patient characteristics [n = 20], health outcomes [n = 9], social circumstances [n = 2], and clinical care processes [n = 7]).

Predictive Models for LOS

Nine studies utilized predictive modeling for LOS (Table S5). Most ML models for continuous LOS reported R 2 below 0.200 [46, 50]. Best‐performing ML models for the classification task achieved AUC values from 0.701 to 0.857 across studies [22, 46, 50, 51, 52]. Seven studies utilized single‐center data, while two studies used multicenter data sets. Three studies used data‐driven feature selection (e.g., recursive feature elimination) [46, 51, 52]. Other studies either relied on previous clinical experiences and literature or had no explicit selection. ML models using SHAP interpretation were reported in two studies [10, 51]. Three studies evaluated the model calibration [22, 46, 51]. National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) at admission was consistently reported as an important predictor in predictive models (Table 4) [4, 22, 44, 49, 50, 51, 52].

Discussion

In this review, five categories of factors affecting LOS for stroke were identified: patient characteristics, health outcomes (e.g., pneumonia), social circumstances (e.g., marital status), clinical care processes, and health system. This diversity reflects the inherent complexity of predicting LOS for stroke. ML and traditional models demonstrated similar discrimination performance, suggesting that algorithmic advances do not necessarily improve predictive performance. Future ML models should prioritize a data‐driven selection and transformation of model inputs, enhance generalizability, and emphasize interpretability to improve clinical reliability and utility. Comprehensive evaluation of model discrimination, calibration, and reclassification performance can ensure LOS predictions are clinically meaningful. These improvements enable more granular LOS predictions to optimize clinical decision‐making in stroke care.

Risk of bias assessment revealed several limitations affecting the reliability of current LOS models. Inappropriate inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in an unrepresentative study population and reduced model generalizability across broader populations of patients with acute stroke. Handling of missing data emerged as a recurrent source of bias. Most studies provided limited justification for how missing data were handled. These shortcomings undermine the robustness of reported models and underscore the need for improved analytical rigor in LOS models. Future studies should follow standardized reporting guidelines (e.g., TRIPOD‐AI) to ensure appropriate population selection and handling of missing data in model development to improve generalizability and reliability [55].

The heterogeneity of factors influencing LOS in stroke presents challenges for improving clinical interpretability. Incorporating all relevant variables increases model complexity and risk of multicollinearity, which undermines model performance and interpretability [49]. Data‐driven feature selection can effectively identify informative inputs from candidate variables by directly learning data patterns, enabling the development of simpler models without compromising predictive power. However, feature selection approaches remain underutilized in existing ML models (only in three studies) [46, 51, 52]. The engineering of new features from raw data (i.e., feature engineering) can also improve clinical interpretability. Feature engineering enables the transformation of raw data into clinically aligned representations. However, clinically informed transformations (e.g., categorizing clinical data into risk strata or aggregating multiple comorbidities into clinically meaningful burden scores) remain underutilized in existing models. Such transformations can align model inputs with established clinical reasoning to make models more interpretable. For example, continuous NIHSS scores are often categorized into clinically relevant categories of stroke severity (e.g., mild, moderate, or severe). This transformation allows clinicians to better understand how stroke severity contributes to LOS predictions, thereby enhancing the model's interpretability.

Stroke severity (e.g., NIHSS at admission) consistently ranked as the most influential predictor in LOS models [49, 50, 51, 52]. Greater NIHSS scores were associated with increased LOS for stroke [22, 28, 37, 41]. Patients with severe stroke need specialized care and early multidisciplinary involvement. They frequently experienced complications (e.g., dysphagia and pneumonia) that prolonged hospitalization [37, 39, 51]. Early assessment of stroke severity at admission supports precise decisions on resource allocation and therapy intensity. It also facilitates communication with patients or families regarding discharge planning and recovery timelines. Incorporating stroke severity into ML models enhances their clinical utility in stroke care.

The type of LOS prediction output affects the utility of ML models within clinical workflows. Continuous LOS prediction (i.e., regression task) is commonly evaluated using R 2, which reflects the proportion of variance in LOS explained by models [56]. In this review, most reported R 2 values were below 0.200 (i.e., explaining < 20% of variance), highlighting the substantial methodological challenge to accurately predicting continuous LOS using ML models. Further research is needed to improve the validity of continuous LOS models to enable more reliable and granular LOS predictions in stroke. In contrast, ML models predicting binary LOS outcomes have generally reported AUC around 0.800 (i.e., acceptable discrimination performance) [57]. Moreover, integration of binary prediction outputs (i.e., prolonged or short LOS) into clinical workflows could support early‐stage planning of the patient journey (e.g., within 24–48 h after admission). Such ML models could incorporate routinely available admission predictors (e.g., stroke severity or demographics) to better identify patients at risk of prolonged hospitalization early in their stay. This early risk stratification may enhance clinical decision‐making, care coordination, discharge planning, and communication with patients or families. Implementation studies or clinician feedback (e.g., evaluating changes in hospital workflow efficiency and resource utilization before and after model deployment) are needed to validate whether ML models predicting binary LOS can meaningfully support early‐stage decision‐making in clinical practice.

ML and traditional models showed similar discrimination performance in predicting binary LOS in the meta‐analysis. This finding is consistent with evidence from broader clinical settings (not specific to stroke) where ML showed no consistent AUC advantage over logistic regression in many clinical prediction tasks [58]. However, other performance, such as calibration (e.g., Brier score) and risk reclassification (e.g., Net Reclassification Improvement), is also essential to assess the clinical reliability of ML models. Without proper calibration and reclassification performance, clinicians may draw misleading clinical insights from model outputs even when models show excellent AUC values. Future studies should implement comprehensive evaluation frameworks to ensure the reliability of LOS predictions in stroke care.

While the meta‐analysis provided an overall comparison of ML and logistic regression in binary LOS prediction across studies, robust comparisons between specific ML algorithm types (e.g., tree‐based ensemble) and logistic regression were not feasible within this review. The algorithms of best‐performing ML models varied across studies, and the included models were developed using heterogeneous data sets and modeling strategies (e.g., different preprocessing methods and hyperparameters). Consequently, comparisons between specific ML algorithms and logistic regression could not be reliably conducted, precluding meaningful summarization of performance trends by algorithm type. Future studies should perform head‐to‐head comparisons of specific ML algorithms and traditional models under the same computational environment, ideally across large and diverse data sets, to evaluate their predictive performance.

Limited generalizability is a major concern regarding current ML models. Models developed using single‐center data often show reduced AUC when externally validated [59]. Utilizing multicenter data improves model generalizability. For example, a federated learning framework using data from 20 institutions worldwide outperformed single‐center models when predicting oxygen requirements in patients with COVID‑19 [60]. However, simply utilizing multicenter data does not always guarantee better generalizability [61]. Between‐center heterogeneity (e.g., differences in referral mechanisms) and population selection can affect robustness. Future modeling should appropriately quantify between‐center heterogeneity to adjust for performance variations [61]. Using data from centers with broader patient case‐mix, rather than highly specialized centers, can capture real‐world variability and mitigate selection bias [61]. These strategies enhance the robustness of ML models across healthcare systems.

Calibration remains underreported in ML models of LOS in stroke, despite its importance in generating reliable predictions. Calibration ensures that predicted probabilities closely reflect observed outcomes [62]. Reliable clinical predictions are influenced by the size and quality of data for model development [63, 64, 65]. ML models developed with low sample size or low events‐per‐variable (EPV) ratio may lead to good AUC while having poor calibration [66]. Maintaining an adequate EPV ratio (EPV threshold varies across algorithms) improves the stability of prediction and mitigates calibration optimism [66]. Post‐hoc calibration techniques, such as Platt scaling, which applies a sigmoid function, also recalibrate model outputs to improve calibration performance [62].

ML models for predicting binary LOS in stroke offer limited interpretability, hindering their clinical adoption when predicted outcomes cannot be explained by clinical reasoning [8]. Model‐agnostic post‐hoc explainable AI (e.g., SHAP) should be adopted to enhance interpretability. SHAP method decomposes a predicted outcome into specific numerical contributions of each predictor [67], enabling clinicians to understand how each predictor influences the predicted outcome (e.g., a longer or shorter hospital stay). Clinicians might be able to examine whether model outputs align with clinical reasoning. Thus, ML models should strengthen their interpretability using explainable AI methods to facilitate their clinical adoption.

There are some limitations of this review. The inability to distinguish stroke types affected the performance comparison between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. Given their distinct pathophysiology and clinical pathways, analyzing them independently may provide additional insights. LOS may have varied between countries due to differences in healthcare systems (e.g., diverse discharge policies for stroke). We could not examine the potential impact of contextual factors on prediction, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Finally, thresholds of dichotomizing LOS varied across studies (from 7 to 14 days) [22, 44, 46, 50, 51, 52]. This variation potentially introduces bias when pooling C‐statistics across studies.

Conclusions

This review synthesized current evidence on factors influencing LOS for stroke and assessed the predictive performance of existing ML models. The heterogeneity of identified factors highlights the multifactorial nature of LOS. ML models show no clear performance advantage over traditional approaches. Future ML models should prioritize data‐driven feature selection and transformation, enhance model generalizability, emphasize model interpretability, and adopt comprehensive evaluation frameworks (including discrimination, calibration, and risk reclassification). These methodological improvements enable meaningful LOS predictions to optimize care pathways and decision‐making in stroke care.
