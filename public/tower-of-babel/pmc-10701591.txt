Disease burden and predictors associated with non-response to antihistamine-based therapy in chronic spontaneous urticaria
Authors: Weily Soong, Dhaval Patil, Irina Pivneva, James Signorovitch, Michael A. Wells, Maria-Magdalena Balp, Merin Kuruvilla
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundH1-antihistamines (H1AH) are the first-line treatment for chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), but 50% of patients have inadequate disease control at standard doses.ObjectiveTo assess the comorbidity burden and healthcare resource utilization (HRU) associated with non-response to H1AH-based treatments; to identify predictors of non-response.MethodsOptum® de-identified Electronic Health Record dataset (2007–2020) was used to identify adult patients with CSU who initiated a H1AH, alone or in combination with other oral non-biologics (index treatment). Based on twelve-month treatment patterns observed after index treatment initiation, patients were categorized as responders (continued index treatment or had only 1 next H1AH treatment without corticosteroids) or non-responders (continued corticosteroids or had 2 or more treatment switches). Patient characteristics and HRU were assessed in the 12 months before (baseline) and ≥12 months after (follow-up) index treatment initiation. Baseline predictors associated with non-response were identified using machine learning.ResultsThere were 17 062 patients who met inclusion criteria, and 14824 (86.9%) were classified as non-responders. A higher proportion of non-responders had records of CSU-related symptoms, comorbidities, polypharmacy, and certain laboratory tests than responders at baseline. A higher proportion of non-responders than responders visited an allergist or dermatologist during follow-up (59.5% vs 53.0%). Non-responders had a larger increase in hospitalizations (15.7% vs −2.4%) than responders during follow-up vs baseline. Predictors of non-response included index and baseline treatment classes, types of specialists seen, chronic pulmonary disease, depression, and female sex.ConclusionA large proportion of CSU patients treated with H1AH-based therapies had uncontrolled disease, contributing to increased HRU and patient burden. Non-responders had more comorbidities and HRU at baseline and follow-up, with steep increases in follow-up hospitalizations relative to baseline, highlighting an urgent need for early disease control.

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a disabling condition characterized by the appearance of wheals and angioedema that persist for longer than 6 weeks.1 Some patients with CSU may present with comorbid conditions, including allergic rhinitis, asthma, autoimmune diseases (eg, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes), and depression.2, 3, 4, 5 Accordingly, patients with CSU often incur considerable healthcare resource utilization (HRU), with 1 US study reporting 1.7, 2.4, and 2.1 times higher rates of inpatient (IP), emergency room (ER), and outpatient visits (OP), respectively, relative to controls.6

Given the substantial clinical and economic burden of CSU, early initiation of effective treatment is instrumental to achieving symptom control. In the United States (US), management of CSU involves a step-wise approach as outlined by the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI) and American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (ACAAI) guidelines.7 First-line treatment consists of monotherapy with a second-generation H1-antihistamine (sgH1AH) at US Food and Drug Administration–approved doses (Step One). If symptoms are not sufficiently controlled on this regimen, guidelines recommend increasing the dose of the sgH1AH and/or adding another sgH1AH, an H2-antihistamine (H2AH), a leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA), or a first-generation H1-antihistamine (fgH1AH) at bedtime (due to sedating effects; Step Two). At Step Three, dose advancement with the fgH1AH hydroxyzine or antidepressant doxepin may be considered if tolerated by the patient. Finally, if CSU continues to be refractory to treatment, US guidelines recommend the use of cyclosporine or other anti-inflammatory agents, immunosuppressants, or biologics, such as omalizumab (Step Four).7

Although treatment with H1AH is effective for some patients with CSU, on average up to 50% of patients remain non-responsive to sgH1AH.8 Therefore, it is important to identify patient and disease factors that may predict non-response to treatment to guide clinical decision-making. A recent systematic review by Fok et al found that high disease activity, concomitant chronic inducible urticaria (CIndU), and elevated D-dimer or C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were strong predictors of non-response to sgH1AH.9 Although prior studies have based the definition of non-response on the lack of disease control achieved following specific doses of H1AH, dose increases still fail to control symptoms for every third to fourth patient.8,10, 11, 12 Due to the chronic and recurring nature of CSU, regular long-term treatment (compared to as-needed treatment) is recommended by US guidelines to achieve optimal overall disease control and patients with a high risk of non-response may use additional healthcare resources, especially if they have additional comorbidities.13 To this end, the current study assessed the additional comorbidity burden and HRU of non-response to H1AH alone or in combination with other non-biologic treatments for CSU. Moreover, this study sought to identify predictors associated with non-response to H1AH-based treatment among patients with CSU, which may facilitate earlier escalation to advanced therapies.

Methods

Data source

Data from the Optum® de-identified Electronic Health Record (EHR) dataset (Q1 2007–Q2 2020) were used. The database houses longitudinal clinical data from multiple care delivery sites, covering more than 102 million lives receiving care from more than 700 hospitals and 7000 clinics across the United States. The database comprises data from IP and ambulatory EHRs, including clinical data (eg, demographics, medications prescribed and administered, immunizations, laboratory test results) and detailed information from provider notes (eg, symptoms). Over-the-counter pharmacy information was available for a portion of the patients and was included in the analysis. However, dose information was poorly populated in the data source and could not be included in the analyses. Data are de-identified and comply with the patient requirements of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996. Therefore, no reviews by an institutional review board were required.

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective cohort study design was used. In initial data investigations, over 45 000 treatment sequences were observed during the twelve-month period following the first H1AH-based treatment on or after the date of the first CSU diagnosis (ie, based on idiopathic, other, or unspecified urticaria codes: International Classification of Diseases, ninth revision [ICD-9] 708.1, 708.8, 708.9, 995.1; International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision L50.1, L50.8, L50.9, T78.3; Supplemental Table 1),14 including those treatments not explicitly stated in the guidelines (eg, treatment with corticosteroids alone as the first treatment after the first CSU diagnosis; Supplemental Table 2). To that end, it was determined that the study would focus on H1AH alone or in combination with H2AH, LTRAs, doxepin, or corticosteroids, which were operationally defined as oral non-biologic treatments.

Specifically, the first treatment on or after the first CSU diagnosis was defined as the index treatment and included 1 of the following: (1) fgH1AH alone or sgH1AH alone, (2) fgH1AH and sgH1AH in combination, (3) H1AH in combination with non-corticosteroids, or (4) H1AH in combination with corticosteroids (Supplemental Figure 1). The index date was defined as the date of initiation of the index treatment after the first CSU diagnosis. The baseline period comprised the twelve-month period prior to the index date, while the follow-up period spanned the ≥12 months from the index date until the end of clinical activity, medical insurance coverage, data availability, or death, whichever occurred first.

Patients were included in the study if they met the following criteria: (1) had at least 1 primary diagnosis of idiopathic urticaria and a second primary or secondary diagnosis of idiopathic urticaria at least 6 weeks but no more than 12 months after the first diagnosis (Supplemental Table 1);14 (2) initiated an index treatment after the first CSU diagnosis (patients were excluded if the index treatment was initiated beyond 6 months after the first CSU diagnosis); (3) were aged ≥18 years at the index date; (4) had ≥12 months of data before and after the index date; (5) had at least 2 visits or days with a record of a prescription related to CSU (ie, H1AH, H2AH, LTRA, doxepin, corticosteroids, or omalizumab; Supplemental Table 2); (6) had either a CSU diagnosis in the last 3 months or a record of prescription in the last 6 months of the twelve-month period after the index date (Supplemental Figure 1).

Study outcomes

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized for both the non-responder and responder cohorts during the baseline period. Specialist visits (ie, allergy and immunology, dermatology) and all-cause and CSU-related HRU were assessed during both the baseline and follow-up periods for the study-defined cohorts and overall. HRU comprised IP admissions (ie, hospitalizations) and IP length of stay, ER visits, and OP visits and was reported per-patient-per-year (PPPY). CSU-related HRU was defined as visits on the date of medical records with a diagnosis of CSU or prescription of H1AH.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R version 3.6.2, STATA version 16.1 and SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1. Continuous variables were summarized using means, SD and medians, while categorical variables were summarized using frequency counts and percentages.

Baseline clinical characteristics were compared between the non-responder and responder cohorts using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables. HRU was compared between the 2 cohorts using generalized linear regression models with a log link and a negative binomial distribution; duration of follow-up period was used as the offset variable. Results were reported as unadjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values.

Identifying predictors of non-response

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to evaluate which patient characteristics (independent variables) may be associated with non-response (dependent variable). Independent variables considered in the model included patient demographic and clinical characteristics measured at the date of the first H1AH-based treatment prescription and during the baseline period, such as age, sex, race, region, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), body mass index (BMI), timing of first diagnosis of CSU, CSU-related symptoms, comorbidities, treatments, and laboratory tests. A penalization parameter to remove irrelevant variables was optimized through cross-validation (ie, the selected value of the penalization parameter was that for which the resulting model had the best performance on unseen folds of data). Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding p-values were reported for each predictor included in the model.

To supplement the LASSO regression, random forest was used to identify additional predictors of non-response. Random forest allowed for the use of a broad and agnostic set of candidate predictors while avoiding numerical issues like overfitting the data; it permitted the discovery of novel and possibly unexpected predictors of non-response. It allowed for a nonlinear relationship between the predictors and the outcome and was able to account for complex interactions between variables when building a prediction. Random forest consists of an ensemble of classification trees generated using bootstrap samples and randomly selected features; therefore, the individual decision trees do not have access to the entire dataset, thus mitigating the risk of overfitting. Patients are classified as non-responders or responders based on the majority vote of predictions across trees. Random forest hyperparameters, including tree depth and minimum node size, were optimized using cross-validation to prevent model overfitting. Predictive accuracy was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC). All diagnoses, treatments, procedures, laboratory tests, and symptoms available in the EHR were considered as the initial candidate predictors; no limit was imposed on the number of predictors included in the initial model since various aspects of a patient's medical journey could be related to CSU, comorbidities, general health, and thus, response/non-response to treatment. A threshold level of variable importance was optimized to remove variables that performed worse than random noise, thus reducing the risk of identifying predictors by chance.

In both LASSO and random forest models, the predictive strength of a variable was determined based on its combined effect with other variables. Of note, a variable can be an important predictor of non-response without there necessarily being significant differences between responders and non-responders.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the initial sample of 993 203 patients, 17 062 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study (Fig. 2). A total of 14 824 (86.9%) patients were classified as non-responders and 2238 (13.1%) patients as responders (Fig. 3). Overall, the mean age of the sample was 47.0 years at index and 77.0% were female, with the majority Caucasian (74.8%) and located in the Midwest (60.0%; Table 1). On average, the first diagnosis of CSU occurred 10.6 days before the index date; it co-occurred with the index date for 78.7% of patients (Table 1). Notably, 15 098 (88.5%) patients initiated their index treatment within 30 days of their first CSU diagnosis. Among the 6190 (36.3%) patients with a CSU-related symptom recorded in their patient chart during the baseline period and/or on the index date, the prevalence of certain CSU-related symptoms was higher among non-responders than responders, including rash, swelling, and itch (Table 1). In addition, the average CCI score was higher among non-responders compared to responders (Table 1). Accordingly, the prevalence of certain comorbidities was higher among non-responders than responders, including respiratory diseases (eg, chronic pulmonary diseases), atopic diseases (e.g., allergic rhinitis), potential indicators of systemic disease (e.g., mast cell activation disorder [MCAS] and abdominal pain), and depression (Table 1).

Polypharmacy was observed to a greater extent among non-responders, with a higher proportion among them using oral corticosteroids, H2AH, and LTRAs compared to responders during the baseline period (Table 1).

Overall, patients in the study had numerous laboratory tests (Table 1). Among non-responders, there was a higher proportion with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)-Schwartz/eGFR-Mayo records, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) tests, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) tests, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) tests than responders during the baseline period. In contrast, a higher proportion of patients among responders than non-responders had immunoglobulin-E (IgE) tests (Table 1).

Discussion

In this retrospective, EHR-based study, non-responders and responders to H1AH-based treatment for CSU were identified based on the frequency and types of treatment changes observed over a twelve-month period. Of note, patients who remained on corticosteroids during the study period were classified as non-responders. Non-responders were found to have a higher proportion of patients with more CSU-related symptoms, a larger comorbidity burden, a higher rate of polypharmacy, and a higher proportion of patients with laboratory tests done during the baseline period than responders. Non-responders also generally incurred higher HRU than responders; furthermore, non-responders also had a larger increase in all-cause and CSU-related HRU (except for CSU-related OP visits) from baseline to follow-up in relation to responders. With regards to predictors of non-response, several factors were identified by LASSO and/or the random forest models, including female sex, index and baseline treatments, and the following recorded during the baseline: type of specialists seen, the presence of chronic pulmonary diseases and other indicators of respiratory involvement, depression, and indicators of pain.

The present study found that 86.9% of patients with CSU were classified as non-responders to H1AH-based treatment based on treatment patterns observed over a twelve-month period. This novel definition of non-response represents the chronic nature of CSU that is challenging to control for some patients and the need for long-term, regular treatment to achieve overall disease control, as recommended by treatment guidelines.13 Non-response and response to H1AH have been evaluated in other studies using different definitions. Although the non-response rate to sgH1AH has previously been reported as 50%,6 this rate may actually be higher than previously thought. Indeed, in studies measuring response to H1AH (from European countries), 78%–91% of patients treated with licensed doses of H1AH were non-responders (based on persistent symptoms or move to a new guideline-defined treatment line within 3 months), and among those who subsequently received up to four-fold doses of H1AH, 70%–95% remained non-responsive.10,12 Although these findings are not directly comparable to the current study because of the use of different non-response definitions, they highlight the important proportion of patients in which H1AH treatment fails to control symptoms. A more recent study based in Turkey identified a non-response rate of 28.2% after treatment with sgH1AH and updosed sgH1AH alone or in combination with fgH1AH, H2AH, or LTRA, but patients treated with corticosteroids were not included and the time period used to measure response was not specified, thereby limiting its comparability with the present study.15

There is a paucity of literature comparing HRU between non-responders and responders. Therefore, the present study contributes valuable insight by describing the higher HRU incurred by non-responders compared to responders and the increase in HRU following initiation of the first H1AH-based treatment. Notably, the increases observed in CSU-related OP visits overall likely indicate active disease management for both cohorts following initiation of treatment. The substantial use of healthcare services among non-responders is corroborated by findings from a real-world, multinational study of patients with CSU who remained symptomatic despite H1AH treatment.3 Maurer et al found that 14.4% of included patients had at least 1 CSU-related ER visit over a twelve-month period, while 8.2% had a hospitalization for CSU, suggesting a large clinical burden among these high-risk patients.3

In addition, the current study identified several predictors of non-response to H1AH-based treatments, including a higher proportion of patients with different CSU-related symptoms, a larger comorbidity burden, and a higher rate of polypharmacy, which, together, may indicate more severe disease. Relatedly, presence of an emergency medicine visit during the baseline period or on the index date was also a predictor of non-response, as it may proxy a potential CSU-related flare-up or generally worse general health that could make CSU management more challenging. In a systematic review of response predictors by Fok et al strong evidence was found for hive and itch severity, D-dimer levels, and CRP levels to be predictive of non-response to sgH1AH.9 While different from the predictors identified in the present study, there is alignment in that those reported by Fok et al are also linked to more severe disease and symptoms.9 With regards to specific predictors, 1 retrospective study found that antihistamine (AH)-refractory CSU was associated with asthma, rhinitis, and rhinosinusitis,16 which is consistent with the respiratory-related predictors (eg, respiratory symptoms, chronic pulmonary diseases) identified in the current study and the observation that baseline asthma was more common in non-responders than responders. Similarly, depression was identified as a predictor in this analysis and has been linked to H1AH refractoriness and higher CSU activity in the literature.2,4 Of note, the extra-cutaneous nature of several key predictors (eg, asthma, miscellaneous pain, digestive system symptoms) suggest that CSU may have systemic effects, as well. In addition, a high proportion of patients had concomitant diagnosis of MCAS, which, in the absence of associated tryptase levels, may have represented diagnosis of CSU as a secondary MCAS.17,18 Nevertheless, all patients included in the study had an ICD-confirmed diagnosis of urticaria. Indeed, in an exploratory literature search study conducted by Kocatürk et al the authors found evidence of systemic effects in CU, including cardiac, respiratory (eg, asthma), gastrointestinal (eg, abdominal pain, diarrhea), central nervous system, and musculoskeletal symptoms.19 Furthermore, CSU has been known to be associated with chronic gastrointestinal symptoms and arthralgia/articular angioedema, and misdiagnosis of the latter as arthritis can delay the appropriate treatment of angioedema.20,21 However, further research is warranted to determine the significance of these effects and their relationship to CSU treatment response.

In the current study, almost half of the patients (40.6%) initiated H1AH and corticosteroids as their first H1AH-based treatment prescription. US guidelines do not recommend the use of corticosteroids unless it is a short course to control symptoms until other therapies can achieve disease control.7 As such, the large proportion of patients initiating corticosteroids may reflect an inappropriate choice of first treatment or that many patients present with uncontrolled CSU. While the rationale for choosing corticosteroid treatment is not available in the data, its frequent use, all while being agnostic to the reason(s), represents an unmet treatment need, since long-term use of corticosteroids has been shown to lead to several serious adverse events, including lipid disorder, hypertension, diabetes, neuropsychiatric conditions (eg, depression), cataracts, and obesity.22 Additionally, in this study and elsewhere,23 use of corticosteroids has been associated with non-response to H1AH. Accordingly, the high use of corticosteroids in this study points to the need for more effective and tolerable long-term treatment options for patients with CSU.

Together with the large number of patients initiating H1AH and corticosteroids as their first H1AH-based treatment prescription, in initial data investigations, some patients were observed to have received corticosteroids alone as the first treatment after CSU diagnosis. Prior studies have demonstrated that patients with CSU are undertreated or not treated according to guideline recommendations.3,24, 25, 26 The heterogeneous treatment patterns observed in the current study also appear to be inconsistent with existing guidelines, including those from the United States and Europe.7,27 These findings suggest that the current approach to CSU therapy may be more focused on acute symptom control (eg, with corticosteroids) rather than long-term disease management. Instead, patients may benefit from individualized treatment of CSU based on clinical and disease characteristics that predict non-response, response, and time to clinical remission, thus avoiding treatments that may not be as effective.9,28 More importantly, patients at risk of non-response may be prioritized for closer follow up and earlier initiation of advanced therapies. Further research is warranted to determine if the predictors identified in this study may also guide disease stratification in CSU, manifestations of which are currently very heterogeneous. Additionally, while this study did not include patients experiencing clinical remission, future work may use different models to incorporate both non-response and clinical remission states as a single outcome.

Limitations

This study is subject to a few limitations. Due to the absence of an ICD code specific to CSU, idiopathic urticaria codes were used as a proxy, so it is possible that some patients may have been misclassified. Additionally, since patients were only observed during their period of insurance coverage, the first CSU diagnosis and prescription captured may not have been the actual first. Moreover, guideline-specified treatments (eg, antihistamines, corticosteroids) are not specific to CSU and could have been used for other conditions (eg, allergic rhinitis), further adding to the uncertainty around observing the first treatment for CSU. Some treatments may have been obtained over the counter; although this data was not available for all patients, 25% of the study sample received their first H1AH-based treatment over the counter, which was included in the analyses. In addition, changes to H1AH dosing could provide information on treatment response, but dose information was poorly populated in this database and so was not reported. Similarly, laboratory test results were not available for the majority of patients and thus could not be included in the analyses. The choice to switch/not switch therapies may have been due to factors other than treatment efficacy (eg, cost, patient preference or tolerance). Relatedly, the reasons for prescribing different therapies were not available in the data. Additionally, prescriptions were not stratified by physician specialty; future work can assess whether there is a relationship between non-response/response rate and physician specialty. Although CSU is a heterogeneous disease with many subtypes, this study focused on a high-level assessment of non-response to treatment in CSU. Furthermore, the LASSO and random forest models did not identify causal effects between predictors and non-response. Relatedly, there was a risk of identifying predictors by chance and of overfitting the random forest model; however, several measures were employed to mitigate these risks, as previously described in the Methods section. Lastly, the study is subject to limitations inherent to the use of EHR data, including limited generalizability of the findings to the overall CSU population, the possibility of coding errors and omissions in the data, and restrictions to sample selection that may introduce selection biases (e.g., the requirement for ≥12 months of data before and after the index date).

Conclusion

In this real-world comparison of responders and non-responders to H1AH-based treatment, non-responders had larger comorbidity and symptom burdens, more frequent polypharmacy, and more laboratory tests conducted at baseline. Non-responders had increased HRU at baseline and follow-up, and steep increases in follow-up hospitalizations and ER visits compared to baseline, suggesting that CSU may not be well-managed by the currently observed treatments. A large portion of AH-treated patients with CSU may have uncontrolled disease, contributing to increased HRU and patient burden, thus highlighting a key unmet need in current CSU management.

The findings of this study suggest that screening for certain symptoms and key historical and treatment elements may present a strategy to rapidly identify CSU patients at risk of treatment non-response and increased HRU. Moreover, this study also highlights the importance of optimizing comorbid conditions in CSU. Future studies should be undertaken to validate these associations and provide insight into their pathophysiology to develop more effective treatment strategies.
