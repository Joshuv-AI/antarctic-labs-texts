An open access trauma registry for developing countries: a tested selected minimal dataset with its free database program
Authors: Sami Shaban, Hani O Eid, Fikri M Abu-Zidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInjury is a global health problem, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Sustainable trauma registries in these settings require concise, locally relevant, and low-cost solutions. This methodology paper shares a minimal trauma registry dataset developed and tested over 7 years, together with its accompanying MS Access database, which is made freely available to help LMIC institutions establish locally owned trauma registries.MethodsTwo prospectively tested registries were sequentially designed, implemented, analyzed, and refined in Al-Ain City, United Arab Emirates: a 200-variable single-center hospital registry and a 50-variable multicenter road traffic collision registry. Finally, an 80-variable Trauma and Emergency Research Group registry was developed. Variables were retained or removed according to feasibility, data completeness, clinical usefulness, prevention value, and demonstrated research utility.ResultsThe first registry enrolled 2573 patients over 3 years and the second enrolled 1008 patients over 18 months. Experience from these registries generated 21 publications from the first registry, 13 from the second, 3 combined analyses, and 5 follow-up studies, which informed the final open-access registry. The resulting tool is a five-page, 80-variable minimal dataset organized into seven sections: personal details, trauma details, road trauma details, emergency department assessment, discharge summary, death details, and injuries and scores. It balances prevention variables such as crash mechanics, safety equipment, education level, and injury location with core outcome variables needed for benchmarking and system evaluation.ConclusionsA carefully selected minimal dataset can support clinically useful, prevention-oriented, and affordable trauma surveillance in resource-limited settings. Making the form and accompanying MS Access database freely available may help LMIC institutions establish locally owned trauma registries that are scalable, sustainable, and adaptable to national systems, provided that minimum resources for implementation, maintenance, governance, data quality, and reporting are planned.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13017-026-00707-w.Keywords: Trauma registry, Minimal dataset, Developing countries

Introduction

Injury is a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide, accounting for nearly 8% of all deaths and contributing substantially to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), particularly among young and economically productive populations [1, 2]. Developing countries are projected to experience increasing road traffic injuries, which will further burden already stretched health systems [3]. When this burden occurs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) without robust injury-surveillance systems, the result is limited local evidence to guide trauma care and prevention.

Lack of accurate, relevant data hinders the identification of injury patterns, the evaluation of care delivery, and the rational allocation of resources. Trauma registries address this gap by providing standardized longitudinal data on mechanisms of injury, management, and outcomes [4]. They are central to trauma-system development and performance monitoring, yet they are often absent or underdeveloped in LMICs, where fragmented care contributes to avoidable mortality [5]. Even in resource-constrained settings, registries can be effective if they combine prehospital, hospital, and outcome data in a cost-conscious and sustainable manner [4]. Trauma systems that lack registry-based data cannot be evaluated objectively.

Trauma registries in developing countries should therefore focus on the minimum dataset required to remain feasible and useful. The proposed dataset was developed over 7 years through a three-stage quality-assurance cycle in which a registry was built, tested as a research and surveillance tool, refined, and retested. This process produced a final 80-variable minimal dataset tailored to local needs in developing countries while preserving information relevant to both clinical management and injury prevention. The aim of this paper is to share this minimal trauma registry dataset and its accompanying MS Access database freely, to help LMIC institutions establish locally owned trauma registries.

Methods

Local experience with trauma registries

The Trauma Group at United Arab Emirates University was established over 2 years (2001–2002). During this period, the group defined its mission and vision, recruited multidisciplinary members, raised funds, developed the first trauma registry form which was adapted from the Perth, Western Australia Trauma Registry through a multidisciplinary approach, developed the computer program, and trained fellows in data collection [6]. The first registry, the Al Ain Hospital Trauma Registry, included 200 variables in seven pages and registered 2573 patients over a 3 years period starting in 2003. The second registry, the Al-Ain City Road Traffic Collision Registry, included 50 variables in four pages, and prospectively registered 1008 patients over 18 months, from April 2006 to October 2007. The third and final registry, the Trauma and Emergency Research Group Registry, includes 80 variables in five pages.

Quality assurance cycle

Variable selection was based on continuous publication experience and practical data-collection experience derived from the first two prospectively tested registries. Variables were retained when they were feasible to collect, had good data completeness, supported clinical evaluation, had prevention value, or had demonstrated usefulness in prior research outputs; redundant, incomplete, or low-yield variables were removed. Because this is a methodology and experience-based paper, the aim was to describe the development of a practical minimal dataset rather than to perform a specific study statistical analysis among registry variables. This refinement reduced registry costs by more than 50% compared with traditional models while preserving data quality and utility. The dataset had already been refined and validated through numerous peer-reviewed publications on wide range of specific and injury prevention studies that demonstrated its ability to capture important outcomes and injury patterns. The gradual, locally driven process emphasized feasibility, local priorities, prevention value, and economic considerations, thereby improving data completeness and enabling injury surveillance and trauma-system evaluation in a resource-limited setting. Over 7 years (2001–2007), and through three single-center and multicenter registries, a practical minimum dataset suited to developing countries was achieved.

Free access trauma and emergency research group trauma registry

The final registry form (the trauma and emergency research group trauma registry) (online Appendix 1) was developed for a nationwide, multicenter trauma registry initiative, initially piloted at Tawam Hospital and planned to expand to other UAE hospitals. Its design was based on lessons learned from the first two phases to develop a concise, standardized data-entry form optimized for use across multiple centers and focused on critical trauma information for benchmarking and prevention. Although this form incorporated the most valuable elements of the earlier versions, it added important fields, especially those relevant to public health and multicenter applicability, and removed redundant or overly detailed items. This minimization was deliberate; the form was reduced to five pages with only 80 well-selected variables covering both clinical management and injury prevention, thereby enhancing usability, data completeness, and compliance among data collectors.

Results

Three registries were developed and used for different purposes. Table 1 summarizes their characteristics. Publications arising from the first two registries helped formulate the minimal dataset for the latest registry (Online Appendix 2): 21 publications from the first registry, 13 from the second, 3 combined analyses, and 5 follow-up studies using the National Trauma Bank/ACS-based registry (a total of 42 published studies). These outputs informed the development of the third registry, the trauma and emergency research group trauma registry, which is made freely available for research use as both a data collection form and an MS Access database. The Registry Minimal Dataset Form is available in Appendix 1. It contains seven sections: Personal details, Trauma details, Road Trauma details, Assessment at Emergency Department, Discharge summary, Death details, and Injuries and Score.

The free MS Access registry program is provided as Online Appendix 3. This refined and balanced form is intended as a practical starting platform for a local single-center registry in developing countries. The registry is concise enough to avoid overburdening data collectors, yet comprehensive enough to generate meaningful injury statistics, temporal trends, and research outputs. It covers all hospitalized trauma patients rather than one specific mechanism or anatomical injury type, while including some common mechanism-specific variables which are important for injury prevention, like those of road traffic collisions.

For multicenter implementation, data from local MS Access copies can be merged periodically. When real-time multicenter entry, secure data transfer, role-based access, or centralized governance are required, migration to a secure MS SQL/server-based database with a web-based interface or an equivalent platform would be more appropriate.

The form intentionally places strong emphasis on preventive data and epidemiological context, including education level, crash mechanics, safety equipment, and exact injury location. These variables support analysis of modifiable risk factors and patterns, such as urban-rural and temporal variation, for injury prevention. At the same time, clinical outcome data are limited to the core elements required to assess trauma system performance, including mortality, length of stay, and disability. Nevertheless, detailed coding of all injuries (Online Appendix 1, page 5) can be used to define all patients with specific anatomical injuries to retrospectively perform clinical diagnostic and management studies using a specific designed protocol to review the case records and collect detailed radiological and clinical data needed to answer a specific research question.

Single-center experience showed that a single full-time, trained, and monitored data collector could collect data on more than 1000 patients annually using this selected minimal dataset, reducing costs by at least 50%. This observation refers to single-center implementation and should be interpreted in relation to annual trauma volume, availability of source data, local workflow, and data-governance requirements; hospitals with higher volumes or more complex information systems may require additional data-collection support. Completeness for difficult variables, such as safety-device compliance (for example, helmet use), was very high, at more than 98%, compared with about 70% in classical registries.

Discussion

Trauma registries are essential tools for clinical audit, key performance indicator monitoring, and continuous quality improvement [4]. They enable institutions to compare performance over time, identify gaps in care, and assess the effects of interventions. They also provide epidemiological information that can guide injury-prevention policies. Data on mechanism of injury, environmental circumstances, alcohol use, safety equipment, and crash dynamics can help identify modifiable risk factors [7, 8]. Registries can therefore support evidence-based interventions such as road safety legislation, occupational safety measures, and public health promotion, while also allowing longitudinal evaluation of whether these interventions reduce injury burden and severity.

A high-functioning trauma registry should include both management-related and prevention-related variables. Focusing only on clinical outcomes overlooks upstream determinants of injury, whereas purely epidemiological data lack the clinical context needed to evaluate system performance [9]. By linking prevention, acute care, and outcomes, trauma registries create a feedback loop that supports locally relevant evidence generation, policy decisions, and improved patient outcomes in developing settings [10]. Registry-based analyses from this program previously identified actionable prevention priorities including seatbelt use, work-related injuries, helmet use, and child restraint use in vehicles [11–14].

Trauma registry models designed for high-income countries are not always directly transferable to developing-country health systems. Systems such as the American College of Surgeons National Trauma Data Standard are comprehensive and require standardized abstraction, robust infrastructure, and sustained personnel support [5, 15]. These requirements may be achievable in well-resourced settings, but they can be difficult to sustain in LMICs, where health systems are already strained and informatics capacity may be limited [16]. Excessively complex datasets can increase workload, reduce completeness, and compromise the long-term continuity of data collection [17]. A registry that cannot be maintained has limited practical value regardless of its level of detail.

Developing countries may be tempted to adopt high-income-country registries primarily for benchmarking or accreditation, but this should not be the first priority. The priority should be to improve trauma care through tailored, low-cost, locally developed registries that can be sustained over time. Common barriers in LMICs include limited digital infrastructure, shortages of trained health-informatics personnel, and challenges in data standardization and security across centers [18, 19]. In addition, many variables used in high-income-country registries are not directly aligned with the epidemiological and clinical priorities of developing settings. A carefully chosen minimal dataset is therefore more efficient, scalable, and sustainable because it focuses on the variables most relevant to care improvement and injury prevention while preserving data quality and long-term availability [20].

By pairing the form with a free MS Access database, this work provides a practical starting point for hospitals and health systems in resource-limited settings that wish to establish or scale trauma surveillance. In operational terms, low-cost means avoiding commercial registry software and using a concise dataset and simple interface that can run on locally available hardware. The database program is free of charge, but implementation still requires resources, including a laptop or desktop computer with Microsoft Office/MS Access, trained personnel, supervision, data-quality checks, secure storage and backup, and regular reporting.

Initial implementation costs should be distinguished from recurrent maintenance costs. Initial setup includes local adaptation of the form, installation of the database, assignment of a registry lead, training of data collectors, and pilot testing. Maintenance requires ongoing data collection, data cleaning, quality assurance, secure backup, reporting, technical support, periodic updates, and governance. MS Access is best viewed as a local single-center starting platform. In multicenter or national systems, additional resources are needed for coordination across hospitals, secure data transfer, role-based access, standardization of definitions, and central oversight. Conclusion

Experience from developing three trauma registries has produced a carefully selected minimal dataset that can support clinically useful, prevention-oriented, and affordable trauma surveillance in resource-limited settings. An initially lengthy registry was refined into an open-access five-page form with 80 carefully selected variables that retain both clinical management and injury-prevention value. Making the form and accompanying MS Access database freely available may help LMIC institutions establish locally owned trauma registries that are scalable, sustainable, and adaptable to national systems, while recognizing the need for local training, governance, quality assurance, and maintenance.
