Protocol for the generation of three-dimensional micropatterned neuroepithelial tissues using hPSCs, bioprinting, and matrix scaffolds
Authors: Kenneth Kin Lam Wong, Negin Imani Farahani, George Allen, Abhimanyu Minhas, Lisa M Julian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicropatterning technology that spatially guides the self-assembly of human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) into neural tissues offers enhanced fidelity to investigate early neurodevelopment and disease mechanisms. Here, we present a protocol to produce micropatterned and scaffolded neuroepithelial tissues (scNETs) from hPSCs, leveraging three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting to deposit extracellular matrix (ECM) droplets with defined geometries. We describe steps for bioprinting of ECM micropatterns, hPSC culture and seeding, and the subsequent neural induction process to form scNETs within 5 days.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Imani Farahani et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Developmental biology, Model Organisms, Neuroscience, Stem Cells, Organoids, Tissue Engineering

Before you begin

This protocol produces hPSC-derived 3D tissues that recapitulate the cell types and architecture of the human neural tube at Carnegie stage 13–14 (weeks 4–5 post-fertilization), with reproducible size and structure and at mid-high throughput scale.1 It uses a bioprinter to deposit droplets of ECM substrates that mediate cell growth restriction, necessary to initiate the formation of single-lumen neural tube-like tissues. Before starting, expand hPSC cultures and prepare plates with bioprinted matrix droplets as instructed, aliquot reagents, and prepare neural induction media using the recipe provided. hPSC lines of interest must also be acquired or produced and validated for pluripotency, lack of bacterial, viral or mycoplasma infection and genomic integrity. Comprehensive guidelines for hPSC validation are widely available, including from STEMCELL Technologies (https://www.stemcell.com/psc-cell-quality).

Innovation

hPSC-derived brain organoids have become valuable tools to decipher the impacts of genetic mutations on human brain development.2,3,4,5,6,7 However, technical difficulties associated with organoid processing during the first 1–2 weeks of development, and the inaccessibility of native human brain tissues, means our understanding of the mechanisms regulating human neural tube development is limited.5,8 To address this knowledge gap we have developed a robust protocol to produce human hPSC-derived neural tube tissues of reproducible size and architecture using a bioprinter, which facilitates the formation of micropatterned brain tissues in high throughput. Our method substantially increases the accessibility and scalability of this technology compared to other micropatterning approaches.9,10,11,12,13

Previous studies produced micropatterned neural tube-like tissues from hPSCs, but these used specialized tools like microfluidic chips, photolithography or polymer-based stamps, which exploit differential adhesive properties to facilitate spatial control of cell adhesion.9,10,11,12,13 Our approach is accessible to a wider range of research environments, given the increasing popularity of bioprinters, and greatly improves the throughput at which these tissues can be produced. Given the high reproducibility of the cellular composition and structural arrangement of these tissues, our protocol offers a one-of-a-kind strategy to study the mechanisms that drive neural tube development and closure and that underlie disease-associated phenotypes.1 These include cell overgrowth, lumen abnormalities, aberrant differentiation and structural defects of the early developing brain. This protocol marks an advance in the neural cell micropatterning and organoid fields by providing an accessible, scalable and reproducible approach to produce human neural tube tissues.

Step-by-step method details

Prepare Matrigel-coated plates for hPSC culture

Timing: overnight (16 h) thawing and 30 min of handling

This section describes the procedures to properly thaw Matrigel and prepare coated plates for plating and expansion of hPSCs.

For convenience, we typically do so overnight (or for 16 h) while also thawing the Matrigel stock.CRITICAL: Matrigel will quickly polymerize at 22°C. Store undiluted Matrigel at −20°C. Avoid multiple freeze-thaw cycles to maintain matrix integrity.c.Prepare aliquots by pipetting a set volume of concentrated Matrigel, known as the dilution factor, indicated by the manufacturer instructions for the specific Matrigel lot.CRITICAL: Keep Matrigel aliquots on ice all times while handling to prevent polymerization.Note: The dilution factor of Matrigel is lot-specific and found in the Certificate of Analysis.Note: We find that Matrigel aliquots are typically ∼135 μL in volume of the concentrated stock. Each aliquot will be sufficient to subsequently prepare 15 mL of diluted Matrigel in DMEM/F12 at 100 μg/mL.d.Store the aliquots immediately upon preparation at −20°C. These should be thawed and used as soon as possible but can be stored at −20°C for up to 3 months or at −80°C for up to 1 year, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

4.hPSC passaging with ReLeSR.a.Before beginning, remove the diluted Matrigel from the wells of the 6-well plate and add 2 mL of mTeSR Plus per well.b.Aspirate media from the culture plate and add 1 mL of HBSS (or PBS −/−).c.Aspirate the HBSS from the wells (a long incubation is not needed) and add 1 mL of ReLeSR per well of a 6-well plate. Let this sit for about 30 s before aspirating.d.Incubate the plate in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for 4 min.Note: The ReLeSR dry incubation time may need to be optimized to the cell line. If cells do not sufficiently detach as outlined below, gradually increase this incubation time.e.Remove the plate from the incubator and slowly add 1 mL of mTeSR Plus to the cells. To detach the colonies, firmly tap the side of the plate 3–5 times.CRITICAL: Avoid over-pipetting as this will break down aggregates into single cells and decrease viability.f.Tilt the plate and collect the cell aggregates using a 5 mL serological pipette and add this to a labeled 15 mL conical tube.g.Flick the conical tube to break up the aggregates into smaller sizes (until they appear ∼50–200 μm in diameter under a phase contrast microscope).h.At this point perform a split ratio passage, typically plating the cells at 1:10 (the contents of one well into 10 new wells of a 6-well dish) or count the number of cell clumps.i.Add your cell suspension volume to the prepared plate from step 2a. Place the plate in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator, ensuring to rock the plate to distribute the cell aggregates, and leave undisturbed for 24 h.CRITICAL: An uneven distribution of cell aggregates may cause unwanted clumping of hPSC colonies on the dish, which increases regional cell density and thus the chance of spontaneous differentiation.j.Perform a media change with mTeSR Plus every other day, assessing the culture to monitor growth rate and spontaneous differentiation.CRITICAL: In cases where spontaneous differentiation is present, mark the bottom of the plate with a felt-tip pen to later scrape these areas off with a pipette tip.

Expected outcomes

This bioprinting approach to generate micropatterned scNETs permits the development of neuroepithelial tissues that display apicobasal polarity, pseudo stratification, interkinetic nuclear migration and early neurogenic divisions1. scNETs closely resemble the human neural tube as observed in vivo at Carnegie stages 13–14 (Figure 3A).16,17 The scNET tissues generated from the outlined 5-day differentiation protocol have been validated in our laboratory using multiple hPSC lines including iPSCs and hESCs. We find that producing scNETs on ECM droplets of 800 μm diameter or less consistently leads to single-lumen 3D neuroepithelial tissues with an > 80% success rate. However, ECM droplet diameters of 1000 μm produce both single- and multi-lumen tissues depending on the hPSC line used. Thus, this protocol outlined will consistently yield single lumen scNETs that contain mostly proliferating neural precursors and a few differentiating neurons (∼1–4),1 making it highly amenable to identifying quantifiable neurodevelopmental alterations induced by various genetic mutations or environmental factors. This protocol offers an accessible and efficient approach to generate early-stage human brain tissues of reproducible size and structure, from multiple hPSC lines, that can be used as a versatile tool for disease modeling and drug screening. Our protocol also substantially advances the throughout at which hPSC-derived neural tube tissues can be produced with up the development of up to 96 separate tissues achievable per cell line per plate using the steps that we outline here.

Limitations

This protocol generates neuroepithelial tissues from hPSCs over the span of 5 days, reflecting cellular mechanisms that occur early in brain development during neural tube formation. Although our use of a bioprinter to produce the ECM droplets that facilitate tissue micropatterning offer a more accessible and high-throughput tool than previously used approaches, not all laboratory environments will have direct access to an extrusion-based bioprinter. Additionally, though we developed this protocol using Cultrex and Matrigel, due to their extensive use in the brain organoid field, defined alternative matrices exist, such as laminin-521. We have not directly tested additional matrices, but we expect they can be used in place of Cultrex and Matrigel, thus further increasing the reproducibility of our protocol. Finally, this current model does not capture events that occur in later stages of neurodevelopment such as highly active stages of neurogenesis, gliogenesis and neuron maturation.

Problem 3

scNETs form multiple lumens instead of one central ventricle (related to step 10).

Potential solution

Reduce the diameter of the printed ECM droplets, as ECM islands that are too large (>1 mm in diameter) will often give rise to scNETs with multiple lumen-like structures. Additionally, though we have optimized these parameters for 168 id2 iPSCs and H1 ESCs, other hPSC lines may require larger or smaller ECM droplets to produce optimum single-lumen scNETs.
