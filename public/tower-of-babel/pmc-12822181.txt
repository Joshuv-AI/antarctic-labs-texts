Changes in the risk of depression in older adults in Poland from 2010 to 2019: results of two nationwide surveys
Authors: Paweł Zagożdżon, Przemysław Waszak, Katarzyna Broczek, Hanna Kujawska-Danecka, Małgorzata Mossakowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined sociodemographic differences in late-life depression over time using nationally representative data from the PolSenior (PS) and PolSenior2 (PS2) surveys—two cross-sectional multidisciplinary studies on the aging of the Polish population.MethodsThe surveys were carried out in 2008-2010 (PS) and 2018-2019 (PS2), which included 4979 (PS) and 5987 (PS2) participants aged 65 years and older. Depressive symptoms were measured using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS). GDS was analyzed according to level of education, income, activities of daily living, and cognitive function. Multiple logistic regression was used to assess the association between depression and other individual characteristics.ResultsThe proportion of older adults with clinically relevant depression (GDS >5) was 30.7% in PS and 25.3% in PS2, with a higher prevalence among women than men (35.1% vs. 23.8% in PS; 28.6% vs. 20.5% in PS2). However, after adjusting for confounders, the risk of clinically relevant depression was higher in the PS2 survey compared with the PS survey (OR 1.28, 95% CI: 1.15–1.44). The greatest difference in GDS scores between the two surveys was observed in relation to economic status: individuals in poor economic conditions showed a higher prevalence of clinically relevant depression in PS2 (60.2%) than in PS (48.8%). Notably, both surveys demonstrated a clear trend of increasing GDS scores with advancing age.ConclusionsHigher prevalence of clinically relevant depression in certain groups and increased risk possibly reflect unfavorable socioeconomic changes that occurred for the elderly population in Poland in the analyzed period.Keywords: Late-life depression, Repeated cross-sectional data, Socioeconomic determinants

Introduction

The prevalence of depression among older adults is influenced by multiple factors, including geographic region, economic conditions, and the time period in which the research was conducted [1]. In Europe, most countries report a gradual decline in the prevalence of depression with increasing age [2, 3]. A similar tendency was noticed in the US population [4]. A meta-analysis of studies assessing the prevalence of depression in older adults (aged 75 and above) demonstrated substantial variability in reported estimates. The pooled prevalence was 7.2% (95% CI: 4.4%–10.6%) for major depressive disorder and 17.1% (95% CI: 9.7%–26.1%) for depressive disorders overall [5]. Depression is influenced by many social determinants — including economic, psychological, and physical health factors — which may change over time as societal conditions evolve. Historically, the poverty rate among older adults peaked at 18.2% in December 2020, and fell to a record low of 11.7% in December 2014 [6]. These changes may influence older Poles’ participation in healthcare services as well as their overall health-related quality of life { [7]. However, there is limited information on how the prevalence of depression among older adults in Central and Eastern Europe has changed over time, or on the factors driving these changes. The aim of the present study is therefore to compare the prevalence of clinical depression and to estimate the risk of depression using data from two cross-sectional surveys conducted among older adults in Poland: the PolSenior (PS) study carried out in 2008–2010, and the PolSenior2 (PS2) study conducted in 2018–2019 [8, 9].

From 2010 to 2019, Poland underwent a significant demographic shift marked by rapid population aging, with a steady rise in both the proportion and absolute number of older adults (aged 60 and above). By late 2019 and early 2020, the number of older persons surpassed 9.8 million, representing approximately 25.6% of the population. Projections suggest that this share will continue to grow, reaching nearly 40% by 2050 [10]. Between 2010 and 2019, Poland’s rapid population aging placed increasing pressure on public services and generated growing socio-economic challenges for older adults. These strains likely undermined subjective well-being and contributed to a rise in depressive symptoms. The authors’ hypothesis—that such demographic and social changes adversely affected mental health outcomes among older respondents—is consistent with documented trends in healthcare access, social support, and economic hardship during this period. Similarly, global data indicate that in many ageing societies experiencing rapid growth in the older population, the age-standardized incidence rate of depressive disorders has increased among older adults [11].

Methods

Sampling method

This study used a repeated cross-sectional, population-based design conducted in Poland at two time points: the PS survey (2008–2010) and the PS2 survey (2018–2019). At each wave, independent samples were drawn from the same nationwide target population, comprising 5,695 respondents in PS and 5,987 respondents in PS2. Response rates were 49% and 56%, respectively [8, 9]. Cross-sectional studies often face higher non-response or partial-response rates among older age groups.

In both surveys, a three-stage sampling scheme was employed, with participants organized into clusters. Local administrative units (urban, rural, and urban-rural municipalities) were selected across all 16 voivodships of Poland. Towns and cities were categorized into five size groups, resulting in 78 territorial strata. The number of participants per stratum was proportional to its population size. A total of 137 municipalities were then selected with probability proportional to the population aged 60 and above. Within these municipalities, clusters were formed by randomly selecting villages (in rural areas) or cities (in urban areas) using the national registry of localities. Individual participants were randomly drawn from these clusters via the national PESEL registry (Universal Electronic System for Registration of the Population).

For the current analysis, study subjects included 4,979 respondents from PS and 5,987 from PS2, divided into 5-year age cohorts (60–64, 65–69, …, 90+). Nurses visited selected households up to three times at different times of the day. If a respondent was unavailable but located elsewhere in the same municipality, the nurse attempted to locate and invite them, excluding institutions such as hospitals or prisons. The response rate was calculated as the proportion of successfully contacted and participating individuals among all randomly selected and reachable respondents [8, 9].

Because the study analyzed equal-sized age strata, the sample did not reflect the actual age structure of the population aged 65 and older. The design effect was calculated as the reciprocal of the dwelling sampling fraction within each stratum. Sampling weights were adjusted using non-response rates estimated separately for each stratum. Strata were defined by age, gender, and the size of the municipality of residence, based on the structure of the Polish population in 2016.

Questionnaire and selected geriatric scales and tests

In the PS and PS2 surveys, the 15-item version of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) was used to assess the prevalence of depressive symptoms. The GDS is a widely used screening tool for depression in older adults, with well-established psychometric properties [12, 13].

Before evaluating cognitive function, participants underwent brief screening for hearing and vision. Cognitive abilities were assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clock Drawing Test to detect potential cognitive impairment. Functional capacity was evaluated with the Katz Index of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Lawton’s Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) Scale.

The ADL scale evaluates independence (1 point) or dependence (0 points) in six basic activities of daily living [14]. Total scores classify respondents as able-bodied (5–6 points), moderately disabled (3–4 points), or severely disabled (0–2 points). The IADL scale assesses the ability to perform and organize eight complex daily activities [15], scored as: independently (3 points), with assistance or aids (2 points), or not performed (1 point). Using a modified classification, respondents were categorized as fully functional (24 points), moderately disabled (19–23 points), or severely disabled (≤ 18 points).

The cognitive screening of respondents was conducted by nursing staff trained by geriatric specialists. The MMSE was administered in the participants’ homes. Scores between 27.0 and 30.0 points were considered within the normal range. Scores indicating suspected cognitive impairment were classified as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) for 24.0–26.99 points and severe cognitive impairment for 19.0–23.99 points [16, 17]. MMSE scores obtained in the survey were subsequently adjusted according to the Mungas method, which compensates for differences in age and educational level among participants. The adjustment formula is as follows: MMSE (adjusted, MMSE_Mungas) = MMSE – [0.471 × (years of education – 12)] + [0.131 × (age – 70)] [18].

The GDS was administered by nurses based on respondents’ answers to oral questions. Only participants scoring at least 19 points on the MMSE were included in the analysis. Individuals were classified as having depressive symptoms if they scored 6–15 points, including those with missing responses that did not affect their classification. Depression severity was categorized as follows: no depressive symptoms (0–5 points), moderate depression (6–10 points), and severe depression (11–15 points) [13]. GDS > 5 was defined as clinically relevant depression for the purposes of this study.

The prevalence of clinically relevant depression was analyzed according to participants’ age, sex, marital status, place of residence, self-assessed economic situation, morbidity, and functional capacity, assessed using ADL and IADL scales. The second part of the questionnaire collected medical information on comorbidities, medications used, and socioeconomic status.

The medical section included both general and detailed questions about participants’ current health status, medical history, and previous hospitalizations. It also involved compiling a comprehensive list of all currently used prescription medications, over-the-counter (OTC) drugs, and dietary supplements.

Socio-economic questionnaire

Socioeconomic assessment included questions on personal and family circumstances, household composition, social networks, and the need for and provision of care. It also addressed access to, use of, and satisfaction with healthcare and social services, including experiences of age-related discrimination. Another section focused on financial status, including the ability to manage finances and meet financial needs.

To ensure high-quality data collection, most questions were closed-ended and covered both factual information and personal opinions. When a respondent’s health condition prevented active participation, factual information could be provided by family members or caregivers. However, questions regarding opinions and specific psychometric scales were directed solely to the respondents themselves.

Statistical methods

A complex sampling design and post-stratification were accounted for in the calculation of means, percentages, and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Logistic regression was used to assess the association between clinically relevant depression and key characteristics. All statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 3.6.3.

Results

The sociodemographic characteristics of participants in the PS and PS2 surveys are presented in Table 1. The PS survey included 4,979 respondents, while PS2 included 5,987. The smaller number of older participants in PS2 reflects lower response rates among the oldest age groups in the more recent survey. Differences in education level and marital status between PS and PS2 also reflect significant sociodemographic changes that occurred during the study period.

Sociodemographic characteristics of PolSenior1 and PolSenior2 study groups

Geriatric depression scale

Among participants aged 65 and older, the mean GDS score was higher in PS (4.24) than in PS2 (4.07), with women showing a decrease (4.66 to 4.37) and men a slight increase (3.59 to 3.64). Scores increased with age, ranging from approximately 3.4–3.9 in the 65–69 age group to over 5 in participants aged 90 and above, and were consistently higher in women. Higher GDS scores were associated with lower education, rural residence, and disadvantaged socioeconomic conditions, while higher education corresponded to the lowest scores. Greater dependence in ADL/IADL nearly doubled mean GDS compared to those without impairments, and elevated scores were also observed in participants with a history of depression, stroke, or cognitive impairment (Table 2).

Mean geriatric depression scale scores in sociodemographic groups

The prevalence of clinically relevant depression

The overall prevalence of clinically relevant depression (GDS score > 5) among older adults was 30.7% (95% CI: 28.4–33.1) in the PS survey and 25.3% (95% CI: 23.5–27.1) in PS2. Clinically relevant depression was more common in women than in men (35.1% PS and 28.6% PS2 vs. 23.8% PS and 20.5% PS2, respectively) (Table 3). Prevalence varied by age group, ranging from 15% in the youngest men (65–69 years) to 47% in women aged 85–89 (Table 3).

Percentage prevalence of clinically relevant depression (Geriatric depression Scale > 5 points) among sociodemographic groups

Clinically relevant depression was nearly three times more likely in participants with only elementary education compared to those with higher education. Among women with at most elementary education, about four in ten had clinically relevant depression. Place of residence did not appear to influence depression prevalence. In contrast, economic status was strongly associated with depression: participants who rated their economic situation as poor were three times more likely to have clinically relevant depression than those in good economic circumstances (48.8% PS and 60.2% PS2 vs. 24.9% PS and 20.5% PS2, respectively).

Higher prevalence of clinically relevant depression was observed in more disabled groups, depending on IADL category and the need for assistance (Table 3). The proportion of respondents with both clinically relevant depression and cognitive impairment was approximately twice as high as in those without cognitive deficits (47.4% PS and 43.7% PS2 vs. 23.7% PS and 17.7% PS2, respectively). Feelings of loneliness were strongly associated with depression: clinically relevant depression was present in about one in eight individuals who did not feel lonely, compared with nearly three-quarters of those who regularly experienced loneliness. Married participants were less likely to have clinically relevant depression than widowed or divorced individuals (Table 3).

Time trends

Although overall GDS scores and the prevalence of clinically relevant depression were higher in PS than in PS2 (30.7% vs. 25.3%), certain subgroups showed the opposite trend, with an increase in the severity of depressive symptoms. This pattern was mainly observed in sociodemographic subgroups that already had the highest mean GDS scores in PS, particularly among participants aged 90 and above. In this age group, mean GDS increased by 14.6% for women (from 4.73 to 5.42) and 18.9% for men (from 4.40 to 5.23).

Mean GDS also increased in participants with poor economic status (+ 22.4% for women, + 32.6% for men) and those with moderate disability (ADL 3–4 points; +23.6% for women, + 18.0% for men). Among women with higher education, GDS increased by 16.3%, while it decreased by 4.4% in men. In the elementary education group, men’s scores increased by 19.1%, whereas women’s increased by 6.9%.

Conversely, the greatest decreases in GDS scores were observed in the 65–69 age group (−13.3% for women, −12.2% for men), divorced individuals (−16.0% for women, −30.7% for men), and the most disabled participants (ADL 0–2 points; −31.5% for women, −48.4% for men). Other groups showing a decline in depressive symptoms included those living alone (−9.6% for women, −5.1% for men) and respondents without a permanent partner (−5.4% for women, −16.3% for men).

Multiple logistic regression

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population were statistically significant in univariate analyses. For multivariable analyses, the two samples were pooled, and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were calculated (Table 4). Logistic regression showed a higher risk of clinically relevant depression in PS2 compared with PS, with an aOR of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.15–1.44).

After adjustment, statistically significant factors included male gender, education level, economic situation, feelings of loneliness, need for assistance, cognitive function, and IADL score. Higher education and better economic status were most strongly associated with lower odds of clinically relevant depression. Specifically, participants with higher education had an aOR of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.52–0.80), and those in a good economic situation had an aOR of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.47–0.67).

Conversely, greater feelings of loneliness, need for assistance, and cognitive decline were associated with higher odds of depression. Participants reporting feelings of loneliness “often/always” had an aOR of 8.76 (95% CI: 7.15–10.74), while severe cognitive impairment was associated with an aOR of 1.89 (95% CI: 1.62–2.21) (Table 4). Interaction analyses showed no statistically significant interaction between survey period (PS vs. PS2) and other covariates in the model.

Discussion

This study examines changes in the occurrence of depressive symptoms in two groups of Polish older adults assessed at different time points. Although both study samples were representative of the Polish population, the same individuals did not participate in both surveys. Therefore, the main focus was on analyzing sociodemographic variables that may influence the likelihood of depression.

The key finding is a significant increase in the risk of clinically relevant depression in 2018–2019 compared with 2008–2010, after adjusting for multiple confounding factors. This increase was particularly evident among participants already in disadvantaged situations—those with low income, lower education, dependence on others, disability, or cognitive impairment. Comparing PS and PS2, these inequalities appear to have widened over time. Interestingly, this increased risk was not accompanied by a corresponding rise in prevalence; overall prevalence of depression decreased in PS2 compared with PS. Nevertheless, mean GDS scores increased in PS2, particularly among men and in the oldest age groups of women.

Our results indicate that the need for assistance from others is associated with a significant increase in the risk of depression. Moreover, feelings of loneliness emerged as the most important factor linked to clinically relevant depression.

There were no significant differences in mean GDS scores between the PS and PS2 surveys. The proportion of women with clinically relevant depression was significantly higher in PS compared to PS2, while the prevalence among men was similar in both surveys. Overall, the prevalence of clinically relevant depression in Poland is relatively high, exceeding that reported in most European countries [2]. Both surveys also demonstrate a clear trend of increasing depression prevalence with age: the older the age group, the higher the prevalence.

From a public health perspective, monitoring the prevalence of clinically relevant depression in older adults in Poland is increasingly important. According to 2022 statistics, individuals aged 60–64 make up 7% of the total population, approximately 2.6 million people. Overall, nearly 10 million Poles are aged 60 and above, and this number is expected to rise as life expectancy increases [7, 19, 20]. In addition, due to low birth rates and migration trends, older adults will constitute an ever-larger proportion of the population [20]. Poland is currently the second fastest-aging country in the European Union [7].

The observed trend of increasing depression prevalence with age in Poland contrasts with patterns seen in Western European countries. In much of Western Europe, a U-shaped relationship is often reported, with the lowest depression prevalence in the 65–74 age groups compared to both younger and older adults, as observed in Germany [3]. In contrast, a US study found a ‘paradoxical’ decrease in depression frequency with age [21] The differences between Western data and the Polish population are likely due to strong variations in depression risk factors among older adults, particularly socioeconomic determinants.

The birth cohort effect, largely shaped by generational experiences, was not supported by the PS and PS2 data. For example, the 70–74 age group in PS had the lowest depression severity, but almost a decade later, the corresponding age group (80–84) no longer had the lowest risk [9]. Given a relatively stable economic situation in Poland between the two surveys, the determinants of depression in older adults likely remained unchanged, reinforcing the observed trend of worsening depression with age [22, 23].

Another important factor contributing to increased depression in older age is the extension of life expectancy in Poland and the decreasing mortality associated with depression [24]. This is reflected in a reduction in depression severity among individuals with comorbidities, such as women with stroke and men with myocardial infarction [25]. However, life expectancy at age 60 declined in Poland during 2017–2019 compared with 2016 [10], which may reflect the same factors that contributed to the increased risk of depression observed in our study.

Lower susceptibility to depression in older age appears to be influenced more by psychological and socioeconomic factors than by biological ones [26]. The greatest increases in mean GDS were observed among the oldest age group, participants with poor economic status, and those with moderate disability. These findings highlight growing health inequalities related to depression in older adults in Poland and suggest a significant impact of depression on the aging process, as reported in other studies [27].

Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of depression in the elderly remain essential. Depression, dependence on others, and feelings of loneliness are important risk factors for suicide making preventive measures particularly important in this population [28]. Additionally, evidence suggests that depression may independently increase the risk of non-suicide mortality in older adults [29, 30], with effects on mortality potentially independent of vascular events [29].

Evidence suggests that policies targeting social and economic determinants could reduce the burden of depression in vulnerable groups. Social support may be an effective intervention to mitigate the negative impact of lower socioeconomic status on late-life depression [31]. Additionally, experimental studies indicate that psychoeducational and psychological interventions aimed at enhancing protective factors can reduce the risk of depression in older adults by 20–25% over 1–2 years [32]. Prevention strategies should focus on breaking the link between depression, comorbidities, disability, and lack of social support, thereby helping to maintain independence in older adults.

Limitations

In both PS and PS2, the GDS was used to assess the presence and severity of depression. The utility of the GDS for diagnosing depression has been extensively validated, with multiple studies confirming its reliability and consistency compared to other assessment methods [33, 34]. In the present study, a score above 5 on the 15-item GDS was used as the cut-off for diagnosing clinically relevnt depression. Some researchers suggest that using a less conservative threshold, such as a score above 4, may increase the detected prevalence of depression compared with criteria based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) [34].

The main limitation of this study, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships, is its cross-sectional design. Additionally, the study was conducted in different populations at two time points. A key limitation is the potential for attrition bias, particularly given the focus on older and very old adults who are at higher risk of mortality, illness, or cognitive decline. Although reserve participants were used to replace non-respondents, this may not fully account for systematic differences between participants and non-participants. Individuals in poorer health or with lower functional status may have been less likely to participate or be reachable, potentially leading to underrepresentation of the most vulnerable groups.

Nonetheless, the relatively large and representative samples in both surveys allow for meaningful comparisons and provide insights into changes in the prevalence of depressive symptoms in Poland over nearly a decade.

Another limitation relates to the methods used to assess cognitive function. Participants with suspected cognitive impairment were identified based on MMSE scores. While the MMSE is a useful screening tool, it is not a diagnostic instrument. Future studies should include additional assessments, such as informant interviews, clinical evaluations, or dementia-specific diagnostic tools, to improve the accuracy of identifying cognitive impairment.

Conclusions

The risk of depressive symptoms increased among older adults in Poland between 2010 and 2019 after adjusting for relevant confounding factors. At the same time, the overall prevalence of clinical depression did not decrease significantly. Unlike in other developed countries, in Poland the prevalence of depression rises with age. This paradoxical pattern—an increased risk alongside stable prevalence—likely reflects widening within-group disparities in mean GDS scores over the study period, which were strongly associated with differences in economic status, education level, dependence on others, degree of disability, and feelings of loneliness. These findings highlight the need for policy interventions targeting the economic and social determinants of depression in older adults in Poland.
