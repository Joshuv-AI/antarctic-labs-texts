Internal desynchrony of the hepatic circadian system with dissociated systemic rhythms in middle-aged mice under social jet lag-like conditions
Authors: Saki Fukushima, Dan Yang, Tatsumi Morita, Kenshiro Aragane, Sae Yamakawa, Kaito Kurogi, Ryohei Matsuo, Tiantian Ma, Keisuke Ikegami, Mitsuhiro Furuse, Shinobu Yasuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial jet lag (SJL) leads to a misalignment between the circadian clock and social time. In this study, we investigated the effects of weekly shifts in light-dark (LD) conditions: two days per week with 6-h delayed LD cycles (simulating Saturday and Sunday). Core body temperature rhythms rapidly entrained to the delayed LD cycles on weekends, and these delayed rhythms persisted even after the LD cycle was advanced on Monday. In contrast, plasma corticosterone rhythms on Mondays did not show marked delays. In the livers of SJL mice on Monday, the expression rhythms of Per1, Per2, and Hsp70 were delayed compared to that in controls, whereas Rev-erbα expression rhythms remained comparable to those of the controls. The expression of lipid and glucose metabolism-related genes showed either delayed rhythms or no significant changes. In ex vivo experiments using liver slices, high-temperature stimulation induced Per2 and Hsp70 expression, while dexamethasone induced Per1 expression. High temperature and dexamethasone affected distinct sets of genes, and these responses were altered by weekly shifts in LD cycles. Overall, our findings indicate that weekly shifts in LD cycles induce internal desynchronization within the hepatic clock and metabolic pathways, possibly through uncoupling systemic circadian signals and gene-specific responses to stimuli.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53698-8.Keywords: Body temperature, Circadian clock, Clock genes, Jet lag, Liver, MetabolismSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

The circadian clock regulates approximately 24-h rhythms of behavioral and physiological processes. In mammals, the master clock is located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which coordinates circadian oscillations in both the central nervous system and peripheral organs. One of the key mediators of peripheral clock resetting is core body temperature, whose circadian rhythms are regulated by the SCN1,2. The entrainment of peripheral clocks to body temperature involves heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) and HSF1-driven heat shock proteins (HSP)1,3,4. Rhythms of hepatic HSPs are observed even in the absence of a functional clock4, indicating that they are primarily regulated by systemic body temperature rhythms, independently of the local clock. Circulating glucocorticoids also contribute to the resetting of peripheral clocks. These hormones are rhythmically secreted from the adrenal gland in an SCN-dependent manner via the sympathetic nervous system and help synchronize peripheral clocks2. In addition to these resetting factors, food intake rhythms influence peripheral clocks through nutrients or metabolic hormones, such as insulin5. At the molecular level, circadian rhythms are generated by transcriptional-translational feedback loops composed of clock genes such as Per1, Per2, Bmal1, Clock, and Rev-erbα6. The molecular clock is tightly coupled to physiological functions through the transcriptional regulation of clock-controlled genes. For instance, the cell cycle is linked to the circadian clock, as CLOCK and BMAL1 directly regulate the expression of cell cycle genes c-Myc and Wee17,8. Peripheral clocks generate rhythmicity in glucose and lipid metabolism9,10, while hippocampal clocks play critical roles in neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and cognition11,12.

Aberrant lighting conditions, such as exposure to bright light at night or frequent shifts in light–dark (LD) cycles, lead to circadian desynchronization or disruption. Chronic circadian desynchronization is widely recognized to be closely associated with an increased risk of diseases, including obesity, diabetes, psychiatric disorders, and neurological diseases13,14. In animal studies, circadian disruption has been induced by exposure to constant light, chronic jet lag, or forced desynchronization protocols15. These treatments cause clock desynchronization within cells and organs; consequently, clock-controlled physiological functions become misaligned or are lost altogether. These effects may be exacerbated by aging, as the circadian clock in aged mice exhibits a blunted response to shifts in LD cycles16,17. Repeated advances in LD cycles every several days have been reported to increase mortality in mice and to accelerate immune senescence18,19.

Social jet lag (SJL) refers to a discrepancy in sleep patterns between weekdays and weekends in humans, resulting in a misalignment between the internal circadian clock and social time20,21. SJL has been associated with increased body mass index and a higher risk of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes20,22,23. In animal studies, the weekly pattern of circadian delay and advance observed in SJL was replicated by repeated shifts of LD cycles over a seven-day schedule: a 6-h delay for two days (i.e., experimental Saturday and Sunday), followed by a return to the standard cycle with 6-h advances over five days (i.e., experimental Monday to Friday)24,25. In SJL model mice, activity rhythms and Per2 protein rhythms in both the central and peripheral clocks were delayed throughout the week, accompanied by impaired cognitive function24. Considering that the re-entrainment speeds of peripheral clocks to acute LD advances are highly tissue- and gene-specific26,27, the SJL-like model may exhibit clock desynchronization among molecular clocks, metabolic rhythms, and systemic rhythm mediators. However, few studies have demonstrated the dissociation of expression rhythms in clock- and metabolism-associated genes within a single organ or the dissociation of systemic cues such as core body temperature and hormones.

This study aimed to investigate how SJL-like conditions involving weekly shifts in the LD cycle induce desynchronization of the circadian clock system, as well as alterations in behavioral and metabolic parameters. We used middle-aged male mice (aged > 8 months) as aging is associated with slower re-entrainment of shifted circadian rhythms to environmental cycles17. We expected to detect exacerbated circadian desynchronization in these mice. The basic function of the hepatic clock and its controlling physiological functions at these ages are likely intact, as circadian oscillations in the liver under normal light-dark cycles in aged mice (> 18 months old) are comparable to those in young mice (3–6 months old)28. Because circadian clock disruption leads to metabolic impairments, primarily related to dysfunction in the liver9, we analyzed the diurnal patterns of the expression of clock, clock-controlled, and metabolism-associated genes in the liver to evaluate the desynchronized patterns of rhythmic transcription within an organ.

Results

SJL-like conditions impact body weight, behavior, and hepatic clock

For SJL-like conditions, the lights-on and lights-off times were advanced by 6 h on day 1, delayed by 6 h on day 6, and returned to the original cycle by 6 h on day 1 of the following cycle (SJL-like LD treatment, SLD; Fig. 1A). These patterns induce phase delays and advances in the circadian clock24, thereby mimicking working days (days 1–5, Monday–Friday) and weekends (days 6 and 7, Saturday and Sunday) in people. SLD treatment induced higher body weight (p < 0.0001) over experimental periods, compared to control group (CTL) (Fig. 1B). At the final point, SLD treatment increased body weight (p = 0.0034) and epididymal fat weight (p = 0.0145) (Fig. 1C). In the OFT performed on simulated Mondays, Fridays, and Sundays, the total distance traveled and the distance traveled in the center were significantly lower with SLD mice throughout the week (main effect of treatment, p = 0.0116 and p = 0.0202, respectively); profound reduction was observed on Mondays (p = 0.0310 and 0.0455, respectively) (Fig. 1D and E). Immobility in the TST was not significantly altered by SLD (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that SLD increased anxiety-like behavior, but had minor effect on depression-like behavior.

Next, we examined the expression of clock genes (Per1 and Rev-erbα), the clock-controlled cell cycle regulatory gene (Wee1), and Hsp70 in the liver on Mondays, Fridays, and Sundays at two time points per day (Fig. 1G). In SLD mice, day–night variations in the expression of Per1, Rev-erbα, and Wee1 were similar to those in CTL mice on Mondays and Fridays, but the overall Per1 and Wee1 expression levels decreased on Mondays. The expression patterns of these genes were markedly altered in SLD mice on Sundays compared with those in CTL mice. Regarding Hsp70, the expression patterns in SLD mice were similar to those in CTL mice on Sundays, whereas lower expression levels were observed on Mondays and Fridays. These results suggest that day–night variations in the expression of the hepatic clock, clock-control genes, and Hsp70 responded to SLD treatment in a gene-specific manner.

SJL-like conditions induce dissociation between plasma corticosterone, body temperature, and feeding activity rhythms

To confirm the gene-specific expression responses to SLD treatments, we analyzed six time points on a simulated Monday (Fig. 2A). Body weight significantly increased in SLD mice (p = 0.0062) (Fig. 2B). To examine the diurnal patterns of systemic circadian regulators, we analyzed plasma corticosterone levels, core body temperature rhythms, and feeding activity rhythms. Plasma corticosterone levels in CTL group showed significant diurnal variations, with a peak at 10:00 (p = 0.0039), which were blunted in the SLD group with non-significant variations (Fig. 2C). Marked changes in peak phases were not observed in the SLD group.

Heatmaps and temporal plots of core body temperature showed stable rhythmicity throughout the week in CTL mice (Fig. 2D and E). In contrast, SLD mice exhibited weekly delays in daily peak body temperature, particularly on Mondays and Tuesdays (Fig. 2F and G). The average body temperature peaks across the three weeks remained stable in CTL mice, whereas daily variation was evident in SLD mice (main effect of treatment, p = 0.0023; interaction between treatment and days; p = 0.0004, Fig. 2H and I). In SLD mice, body temperature peaks on Mondays (p < 0.0001), Tuesdays (p = 0.0006), and Thursdays (p = 0.0397), and Sundays (p < 0.0001) were significantly delayed compared to those on Fridays. Body temperature amplitude also fluctuated within a week in SLD mice (interaction between treatment and day; p = 0.0019), being significantly reduced on Mondays (p = 0.0249), Saturdays (p = 0.0059), and Sundays (p < 0.0001) compared with Fridays (Fig. 2J). A reduced amplitude on Mondays was also observed in the CTL mice (p < 0.0001).

The feeding activity rhythms in CTL mice were stable within weeks, whereas delayed peaks for the rhythms were noted in SLD mice (main effect of treatment, p = 0.0003; interaction between treatment and days; p = 0.0336, Fig. 2K and L). In SLD mice, feeding activity peaks on Mondays (p = 0.0004), Tuesdays (p = 0.0023), Wednesdays (p = 0.0227), Saturdays (p = 0.0043), and Sundays (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2K and L) were significantly delayed compared to those on Fridays. Food intake levels were not significantly altered by treatment (CTL: 3.08 ± 0.19 g; SLD: 3.226 ± 0.15 g). These findings indicate that plasma corticosterone, body temperature, and feeding activity rhythms became dissociated on Mondays under the SLD schedule.

SJL-like conditions with weekly LD shifts induce internal desynchronization in the hepatic clock and metabolism

We examined the expression rhythms of clock and clock-controlled genes related to the cell cycle, lipid metabolism, and glucose metabolism, alongside Hsp70, in the liver on Monday following SLD treatment. Among the clock genes examined, acrophases of Per1 and Per2 expression were delayed by 4.637 and 4.136 h in SLD mice, whereas delays of Bmal1 and Rev-erbα expression were limited compared to them (Fig. 3A, Table S1). Hsp70 and Wee1 expression exhibited 3.729 and 2.796 h delay, whereas other clock-controlled cell cycle–associated genes (p53 and c-Myc) were largely unaffected (Fig. 3B, Table S1). Similarly, several clock-controlled metabolic genes, including Cyp7a1 showed delayed expression rhythms, whereas other genes showed little to no change (Fig. 3C–E).

To analyze the protein-metabolizing conditions, we analyzed diurnal variations in plasma free amino acid and dipeptide levels. Among twenty-four amino acids or dipeptides examined, seven exhibited significant rhythmicity in the CTL mice (Table S2). Among them, the acrophases of taurine, ornithine, carnosine, and citrulline were delayed by 3.829, 2.663, 2.183, and 2.082 h, respectively, in SLD mice, whereas the phase delays of the arginine, lysine, and valine levels were less than 1.2 h (Fig. 3F, Table S2). Other amino acids and dipeptides exhibited non-significant diurnal variations (Table S2, Fig. S1). These findings suggest that hepatic gene expression and metabolic processes undergo internal desynchronization on Mondays under SJL-like conditions, characterized by a dissociation between delayed and retained components.

High temperature, dexamethasone, and insulin induce gene-specific responses in liver slices

The experiments described above suggest a dissociation between systemic signals—such as body temperature, hormonal secretion, and feeding activity rhythms—and the expression rhythms of hepatic clock and metabolic genes. These findings suggest that weekly shifts in the LD cycle induce internal desynchronization within the hepatic molecular clock and metabolic pathways through the misalignment of core body temperature rhythms, hormonal rhythms, and gene-specific responses to these stimuli. To analyze gene-specific responses, we performed ex vivo cultures using liver slices from middle-aged mice following 3- and 6-h treatment with high temperature, dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid, and insulin (Fig. 4A). Slice preparation was performed at 5:00 to evaluate the responses during the latter half of the subjective day.

High temperatures induced the expression of Per2 (6 h), Hsp70 (3 and 6 h), and Pck1 (6 h) (Fig. 4B), all of which exhibited delayed peak rhythms in SLD mice on Mondays (Fig. 3). Dexamethasone (6 h) induced the expression of Per1, Pparα, and G6pc (Fig. 4B), which showed either blunted rhythms or non-significant alterations in SLD mice (Fig. 3). In contrast, insulin induced minimal changes in the expression of all examined genes (Fig. 4B). We also examined the effects of high temperature, dexamethasone, and insulin on the liver clock using liver slices derived from period 2:: luciferase fusion protein (Per2::Luc) knock-in mice. A stimulation-specific response of Per2::Luc bioluminescence was observed, with acute induction by high temperature and delayed rhythms by dexamethasone but not insulin (Fig. S2). Taken together, these results indicate that high temperature, dexamethasone, and insulin differentially affect the liver clock and gene expression. They suggest that gene-specific responses to systemic signals may be related to the dissociation of gene expression rhythms on Mondays in SLD mice.

We further investigated whether aging and SLD treatment affect gene expression responses. Liver slices were prepared from young and middle-aged mice maintained under 12L:12D or subjected to SLD treatment for 16 weeks, and then treated with high temperature, dexamethasone, and insulin (Fig. S3 and 4). Aging had minor or attenuated effects on gene responses to 3 and 6 h stimulation with high temperature, whereas the Per1 response to 6 h stimulation with dexamethasone was enhanced in aged mice (Fig. 5, 6 h stimulation; Fig. S5, 3 h stimulation). Aging enhanced the responses of several clock and metabolic genes (Per2, Cyp7a1, and Pck1) to 3 h of insulin treatment in SLD mice, whereas it blunted the induction of Hsp70 expression by insulin (Fig. S5). SLD treatment markedly reduced the induction of Hsp70 expression by high temperature in both young and middle-aged mice (Fig. 5 and Fig. S5). The suppressive effect of SLD exposure was also observed in response to 3 h of insulin treatment, but not after 3 h of dexamethasone (Fig. S5). Among the metabolic genes examined, G6pc was the most responsive to 6 h of dexamethasone and insulin treatment, with a marked enhancement in young SLC mice (Fig. 5). Collectively, these findings suggest that gene-specific responses to systemic signals such as high temperature, dexamethasone, and insulin are strongly influenced by both aging and SLD treatment.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that SJL-like conditions, involving weekly shifts in the LD cycle, induced desynchronization between core body temperature, feeding activity, and glucocorticoid rhythms in middle-aged male mice. On simulated Mondays, core body temperature and feeding activity rhythms were delayed, resembling the delay observed in locomotor activity rhythms24, whereas corticosterone rhythms did not exhibit marked delays. These findings led us to hypothesize that systemic desynchronization promotes dissociation between hepatic clock components and their downstream pathways. We further identified dissociation in the expression rhythms of clock-, cell cycle-, and lipid/glucose metabolism–associated genes. One group of genes exhibited delayed rhythms, whereas another group remained mostly synchronized with the LD cycle. A similar dissociation was observed in plasma free amino acid and dipeptide rhythms, which reflect protein metabolism. Furthermore, liver slice culture experiments demonstrated gene-specific responses to either elevated temperatures or hormonal stimuli. These responses were significantly influenced by both age and prior exposure to SLD conditions. Taken together, weekly shifts in the LD cycle cause internal desynchronization within the liver molecular clock and clock-controlled pathways, possibly through the misalignment of systemic signals. Indeed, the re-entrainment speed of peripheral clocks to abrupt LD cycle advances is highly tissue- and gene-specific26 and is closely associated with core body temperature rhythms27.

Our study demonstrated largely delayed Per1 and Per2 expression rhythms in the liver on Mondays under SJL-like conditions, along with delayed expression of Hsp70. High-temperature stimulation induced Per2 and Hsp70 expression in liver slices, suggesting that body temperature regulates the oscillations in their expression. Previous studies have shown that circadian oscillation of hepatic Per2 production is modulated by both the molecular clock and HSF1-mediated temperature signaling1,4,29,30. Notably, Per2 and Hsp70 expression rhythms persist even in the absence of functional clock circuitry4. Per2 expression in fibroblasts responds to heat-shock pulses in an HSF1-dependent manner31. Exposure of mice to cold temperatures also induces Per2 expression in brown adipose tissue via HSF1 binding to the Per2 promoter for subsequent thermogenesis by uncoupling protein 130. However, a recent study reported that physiological temperature stimulation enhances Per2 translation without affecting Per2 transcription32. This discrepancy may be explained by the age-dependent increase in Per2 sensitivity observed in this study. We also demonstrated that aging amplifies the transcriptional response of Per1 to high temperature, insulin, and dexamethasone stimulation. These modulations likely reflect changes in the temporal profiles of gene expression. For example, Per1 expression was significantly induced in liver slices from SLD and middle-aged mice after 3 h of stimulation, whereas it was not detected at 3 h and 6 h in those from young CTL mice. This was likely due to a delayed response in young animals, as observed in primary hepatocytes33. Aging reshapes the expression patterns of hepatic clock genes34, accompanied by changes in circadian clock oscillation, locomotor activity, and hormonal rhythms35. Age-dependent sensitization of clock genes to systemic signals may therefore contribute to the functional characteristics of the aged circadian clock.

Among the metabolic genes analyzed, the peak expression rhythms of Cyp7a1 were significantly delayed on Mondays. Expression rhythms of Pparα, and Pck1 also showed delayed tendency. Their expression is regulated by both the molecular clock36–38 and systemic signals, as evidenced by their gene-specific responses to elevated temperature and hormonal stimulation in this study. Other genes exhibited distinct regulatory patterns, such as the overall upregulation of G6pc or the minimal effect on Srebf1 expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that internal desynchronization occurs within lipid and glucose metabolic pathways, which may in turn contribute to increased body weight. This study exhibited delayed feeding activity rhythms on most days of the week in the SLD mice. As the responses of several clock and metabolic genes to insulin stimulation were amplified by aging and SLD conditions, the delayed feeding activity rhythms may contribute to this desynchronization. In support of this, weekly shifts in food intake by restricted feeding in mice have been shown to induce phase shifts in peripheral clocks24.

This study showed desynchronization between delayed rhythms of core body temperature and feeding activity, and modest changes in plasma corticosterone rhythms on Mondays in SLD mice. Core body temperature and feeding activity rhythms were also desynchronized on other days; feeding activity rhythms were delayed on all days except for Thursdays and Fridays, but body temperature rhythms remained aligned with LD cycles on Wednesdays, Fridays, and Saturdays. Body temperature amplitude also varied across weeks in the SLD mice, such that the amplitude increased on Saturdays and Sundays. A reduced amplitude was observed on Mondays in both the CTL and SLC mice, which may have been due to weekly body weight measurements during the light phase on Mondays. Although we did not measure plasma corticosterone rhythms on other days, a previous study demonstrated that corticosterone rhythms in mice do not entrain an 8 h delay in the LD cycle within 3 days39. Considering these gradual shifts, the corticosterone rhythms on Saturdays and Sundays in our study may have remained in the pre-shift phase on Fridays. As SLD mice returned to their original LD cycles on Mondays, corticosterone rhythms may remain in a stable phase from Tuesday to Fridays. Systemic signal-specific variations in entrainment to weekly LD shifts may lead to constant desynchronization of the circadian system throughout the weeks in the SLD condition.

Our analysis primarily focused on liver tissue, as the hepatic clock and its controlling metabolic functions are highly sensitive to circadian clock disruption9. SLD likely influences the circadian clock in other tissues, such as white adipose tissue, and the brain, leading to dysregulation, as we observed increased fat weight and anxiety-like behavior in SLD mice in the present study. This is consistent with previous findings in male mice subjected to chronic jet lag treatment40,41. However, the effect of SLD may interact with the effects of aging in a tissue-specific manner. For instance, hippocampal clock activity is markedly damped with aging, according to a proteomic study that showed that only 1.6% of proteins exhibited circadian rhythmicity in middle-aged mice, compared with 15% in young mice42. In contrast, the hepatic clock in aged mice (> 18 months old) maintained oscillatory amplitudes comparable to those in young mice under 12 L:12D conditions28. The adverse effects of SLD may be less pronounced in tissues that are vulnerable to aging in older animals.

In conclusion, weekly shifts in the LD cycle lead to internal desynchronization of the hepatic molecular clock and associated metabolic pathways, possibly through altered core body temperature rhythms, hormonal fluctuations, feeding activity rhythms, and gene-specific responses to these systemic cues. The extent of gene-specific responses is strongly influenced by both age and light exposure. Moreover, weekly alterations in housing conditions affect age-related physiological parameters and behavioral outcomes. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for a comprehensive understanding of health disturbances under jet lag–like conditions, accounting for both biological rhythms and environmental factors that fluctuate weekly.

Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan) and housed in plastic cages (175 × 245 × 125 mm) in groups of three or four for all experiments. Per2::Luc knock-in mice43 were provided by Dr. Joseph Takahashi (Southwestern Medical Center, Texas University, TX). Water and a standard laboratory rodent diet (MF; Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) were provided ad libitum and replenished at least once per week. Mice were maintained in a light-tight box placed in a temperature-controlled room (24 ± 1°C) under 12 h light (70 lx):12 h dark (12L:12D) conditions for at least two weeks prior to experimentation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of the Faculty of Agriculture at Kyushu University, Law No. 105, and Notification No. 6 of the Japanese Government, as well as ARRIVE guidelines. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyushu University (A19-212-0, A19-365-0, A20-236-0, and A25-227-0).

SJL-like experiments

Three cohorts of male mice were used for the experiments. In the first cohort, mice were divided into CTL and SLD groups under a 12L:12D cycle (n = 25 per group). At 35 weeks of age, weekly cycles of LD conditions were initiated (Fig. 1A). The SLD group was exposed to a weekly cycle (days 1–7) of light–dark shifts: on day 1 lights-on and lights-off times were advanced by 6 h by shortening the dark phase; this new cycle was maintained from days 2 to 5. On day 6, lights-on and lights-off times were delayed by 6 h by extending the dark phase, and this continued on day 7. The CTL group remained under standard housing with 12L:12D conditions throughout the experiment. The body weights of a subset of animals (n = 9 per group) were measured weekly. The OFT and TST were performed at weeks 14 and 15, respectively. Behavioral tests were conducted on days 1, 5, and 7 using separate cohorts of animals. A priori power analysis indicated that a minimum of eight animals per group was required to achieve 80% power to detect a 40% difference (p < 0.05) in the TST. Thus, we used 8–9 animals per day and groups for the behavioral tests. Mice were euthanized at week 19 using isoflurane gas at 6:00 and 12:00 (n = 4–5 per time point). The final body and epididymal fat weights of all animals were measured. Liver samples were harvested and stored for qPCR analysis.

The second cohort was used to analyze six time points on a simulated Monday. Thirty-five-week-old mice was divided into CTL and SLD groups (n = 24 per group), with the SLD group exposed to weekly shifts in the light–dark cycle, as described above. The body weights of all the animals were measured weekly on simulated Mondays. Six weeks after the start of the experiment, a temperature logger (diameter: 17 mm, height: 6 mm; KN Laboratories, Osaka, Japan) was implanted into the abdominal cavity of a subset of animals (n = 4 per group) under isoflurane anesthesia administered using an inhalation device (Natsume Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Following surgery, cephalexin (15 mg/kg) was dissolved in saline and injected subcutaneously. Core body temperature was recorded every 15 min until the end of the experiment. After implantation, the weight of the logger was subtracted from the weekly measured body weight. In week 10, mice were euthanized using isoflurane gas at 2:00, 6:00, 10:00, 14:00, or 18:00 (n = 4 per time point) on simulated Monday. After decapitation, trunk blood was collected into heparinized tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain plasma samples for corticosterone and amino acid analyses. Liver samples were harvested and stored for qPCR analysis.

The third cohort was used for the feeding activity measurements. Thirty-five-week-old mice were divided into CTL and SLD groups as described above (n = 6 per group). We established a wireless real-time method for measuring the mouse feeding activity rhythm (Fig. S6). A food container (5 cm in diameter, 3.5 cm in height) was placed at the bottom of each cage, and an infrared area sensor (MESH-100MD; Sony, Tokyo, Japan) was attached to the cage lid. A plastic mesh was placed over the container to prevent spillage. To restrict detection exclusively to the feeding area, the sensor was covered with a sticker containing a 1-mm-diameter aperture positioned directly above the food container. This allowed the sensor to count the number of mice entering the feeding area, while excluding movements in other regions of the cage. The sensor signals were recorded as timestamped events using the MESH Creative DIY Toolkit (Sony). This system enabled the automatic and continuous recording of feeding-related behaviors as event counts. Feeding-related behaviors were monitored continuously for a week.

Liver ex vivo cultures

In the first experiment, male mice of 9 months-old maintained under 12L:12D for two weeks were used (n = 3). In the second experiment, young (8-week-old) and middle-aged (40 weeks-old) mice were maintained under either 12L:12D or subjected to weekly shifts in the LD cycle, as described above, for 16 weeks. Thus, four groups were tested in total (young-CTL, young-SLD, middle-aged-CTL, middle-aged-SLD) (n = 3).

In both experiments, mice were euthanized using isoflurane gas at 05:00, and their livers were immersed in Hank’s balanced salt solution on ice. The mice at slice preparation were approximately 40 weeks old in the first experiment, and 24 and 56 weeks old in the second experiment. Liver tissue was sliced into 250 µm sections using a tissue chopper (Cavey Laboratory Engineering Co Ltd, Surrey, UK) The slices were preincubated on a culture membrane (Millicell-CM PICM0RG50, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) in a 35-mm petri dish and cultured in serum free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 1% penicillin / streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 incubator at 36°C for 1 h, then stimulated with either high temperature (39 °C), 100 nM dexamethasone (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), a synthetic glucocorticoid, or 100 nM insulin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation).

In the first experiments, ten slices were prepared from each animal. The slices were then separated into two batches. In the first batch (four slices), the effect of high temperature was examined using non-stimulated controls (two slices per group). In the second batch (six slices), the effects of dexamethasone and insulin were analyzed using vehicle (0.0001% EtOH)-treated controls (two slices per group). The second experiment used eight slices from one animal; two slices were stimulated with either high temperature, dexamethasone, or insulin, with non-stimulated controls. The slices were collected for qPCR analysis after 3 and 6 h of stimulation (one slice at each time point).

For real-time bioluminescence recordings using liver slices from Per2::Luc knock-in mice, adult homozygous mice were euthanized using isoflurane gas, and their liver slices were prepared as described above. Bioluminescence rhythms were monitored using a dish-type luminometer (CL-19, Churitsu Electric Corp, Nagoya, Japan) with a recording medium consisting of DMEM, D-luciferin potassium salt (200 μM, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Recordings were performed at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The bioluminescence signals were monitored for 3 s every 2 min and presented as counts per second. For high-temperature stimulation, four slices were prepared from two animals. The slices were transferred to a luminometer placed in a preheated CO₂ incubator at 39 °C, and the temperature was switched to 36 °C after 6 h of stimulation. For dexamethasone and insulin stimulation, 12 slices were prepared from two animals. The slices were stimulated for 6 h with either dexamethasone or insulin in vehicle (0.0001% EtOH)-treated controls (four slices per group).

Behavioral tests

The OFT was conducted in a white square box (40 × 40 cm) with 40 cm-high walls under 40 lx illumination. Each mouse was placed at the center of the apparatus and recorded for 5 min using a digital video camera. Open-field behavior was analyzed using the ANY-maze software (Stoelting Co., IL, USA), with the field divided into 25 squares (a 5 × 5 grid). The total distance moved, distance moved within the nine central grids, and time spent in the central grids (in 30-s bins) were automatically recorded by the software.

In the TST, a tape was placed on the mouse’s tail and suspended from a hook (40 cm above the floor) attached to a stand. The illuminance near the head of the mouse was adjusted to 40 lx. Behavior was recorded for 6 min, and the total immobility time was measured for five minutes, excluding the first minute after the mouse was suspended.

Plasma corticosterone assay

Total plasma corticosterone levels were analyzed in duplicate using a corticosterone EIA kit (Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, MI) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with the exception of using 2.5% Steroid Displacement Reagent (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) during the plasma dilution step. The detection limit of the assay was 30 pg/ml. The intra- and interassay coefficients of variation were 2.8% and 8.9%, respectively.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Shiga, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). qPCR was performed on a Stratagene Mx3000P system (Agilent Technologies; Santa Clara, CA) with an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification at 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3. mRNA levels were quantified based on threshold cycle values and compared with a standard curve. The calculated levels were normalized to the mRNA levels of Hprt1 and Rplp0. A melting curve analysis was performed for each gene to confirm the specificity of the PCR conditions.

Amino acid and dipeptide analyses

Amino acid and dipeptide levels in plasma were measured using fluorescence-based ultra-performance liquid chromatography. Plasma samples were centrifuged with ultrafiltration tubes (Amicon Ultra-0.5, Millipore, Bedford, USA) to collect the supernatants for analysis. The samples were analyzed using an ACQUITY UPLC system, consisting of a Waters Binary Solvent Manager, Sample Manager, FLR Detector, ACCQTAGTM ULTRA C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm), and Empower 3 software (Waters, Milford, MA). The excitation and emission wavelengths for fluorescence detection were 350 nm and 450 nm, respectively. Data were normalized by the median signal intensity to adjust for scale differences.

Statistical analysis

Temporal changes in body weight were analyzed using repeated measures two-way ANOVA. Final body weight and epididymal fat weight was analyzed using t-tests. Behavioral data and gene expression at two time points per day on Mondays, Fridays, and Sundays were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc analyses using Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test. Gene expression levels were analyzed daily. Diurnal variations in plasma corticosterone levels were analyzed using one-way ANOVA in each group.

For core body temperature, fluctuations each day across three weeks were averaged and smoothed (GraphPad Prism, San Diego, CA, USA). The peak time and amplitude of the smoothed rhythms were then used for statistical analyses. Regarding feeding activity rhythms, hourly event counts were calculated for each day for each animal, and the peak time was used for further analysis. Repeated measures two-way ANOVA was used to compare the peaks and amplitudes of core body temperature rhythms as well as the peaks of food intake activity rhythms. For comparisons across days in each group, post hoc analyses were performed using Dunnett’s multiple comparison test and compared with the values on Fridays.

To evaluation the 24 h rhythmicity of hepatic gene expression and amino acid or dipeptide levels, cosinor analysis was performed based on linear harmonic regression using CircWave software (Roelof Hut, University of Groningen, version 1.4) with 0.05 for an assumed period of 24 h. This analysis allowed us to estimate the significance of circadian variations, acrophases as centers of gravity, and the amplitude from the peak to the middle of the fitted cosinor wave.

For ex vivo data, expression values for each stimulation were compared with control values using two-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc analyses using Bonferroni’s (for high-temperature stimulation) or Dunnett’s (for dexamethasone and insulin stimulation) multiple comparison tests. The results from the second experiment are shown as fold changes relative to the unstimulated control tissues at each time point. Fold changes across the four groups (young-12L:12D, young-LD, old-12L:12D, and old-LD) were further compared using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test.

For all statistics, differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
