The Anomalously High Boiling Point of Water Revisited
Authors: Lucas de Azevedo Santos, Siebe Lekanne Deprez, F Matthias Bickelhaupt, Célia Fonseca Guerra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTypically, boiling points increase with molecular weight because dispersion forces strengthen. However, H2O deviates from this trend, exhibiting a higher boiling point than its heavier group‐16 analogs. This anomaly is traditionally attributed to water’s larger dipole moment, which is thought to promote stronger electrostatic intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Yet, this textbook explanation lacks quantitative support. Here, alongside thermodynamic and structural effects, we identify a key microscopic, quantum chemical factor that explains trends in interaction strength, which in turn are associated with water’s abnormally high boiling point. Within the series of binary hydrides studied, Kohn–Sham molecular orbital theory reveals that the Pauli exclusion principle is an important factor behind water’s high boiling point, as it modulates intermolecular distances and, thereby, influences the strength of all attractive interactions, including Coulombic attraction. The compact H2O molecule experiences significantly less intermolecular Pauli repulsion, allowing for shorter intermolecular distances, enhancing all attractive forces, and resulting in elevated boiling points. These findings not only provide a rigorous, quantitative framework for understanding a fundamental property of water but also call for a critical re‐evaluation of conventional teaching and interpretation.Keywords: boiling point, bond theory, density functional calculations, hydrogen bonds, Kohn–Sham molecular orbital theory

Introduction

In general chemistry books, the physical and chemical properties of small molecules are often described by classical mechanics. For example, the boiling point of substances generally increases with the molecular mass [1, 2, 3]. This trend is largely due to the fact that larger, heavier molecules contain more electrons, which facilitates the formation of temporary dipoles, enhancing the dispersion forces that hold molecules together [1, 2, 3]. Additionally, larger molecules have a greater surface area, allowing for more intermolecular interactions [1, 2, 3]. As a result, stronger dispersion forces acting over a larger area lead to more stabilizing interactions between molecules, increasing the energy required to separate them and, thus, raising their boiling points.

An increase in the boiling point is observed as the molecular mass of the binary hydrides of group‐16 elements increases as one moves down the group in the periodic table. For instance, at 1 atm, the boiling point rises from approximately −60 °C for H2S to around −41 °C for H2Se (Figure 1) [1, 2, 3]. However, water has a boiling point of 100 °C, the highest in the series, despite being the lightest group‐16 hydride [4]. A similar trend is seen for group‐17 hydrides, where the boiling points show a V‐shaped dependence on the molecular weight (Figure 1) [4]. The commonly accepted textbook explanation for this anomaly is the presence of exceptionally strong intermolecular interactions, specifically hydrogen bonding, in second‐row hydrides like H2O [1, 2, 3, 5]. Classically, hydrogen bonding in water is attributed to the large permanent dipole of the H2O molecule and the small size of the hydrogen atom, which enables strong, short‐range Coulomb attractions between partially charged atoms (H–Oδ–•••δ+H–O), thereby increasing its boiling point [1, 2, 3, 5]. While this explanation is intuitive, its quantitative validity remains unclear and warrants closer examination.

Boiling points versus the molecular weight for representative binary hydrides of elements of groups 16 and 17 of the periodic table [4].

In this work, we quantum chemically investigate the origin of the abnormally high boiling point of water and other small molecules compared to those of their heavier analogs. On the one hand, we confirm that trends in boiling points across binary hydrides of group‐16 elements are related to the strength of two‐body interactions. On the other hand, we show that this strength is not determined by the Coulomb attraction between individual monomers. In fact, the relatively strong O•••H–O hydrogen bond in water arises primarily from the shorter intermolecular distance between compact H2O molecules rather than from stronger electrostatic forces compared to those of the S•••H–S and Se•••H–Se analogs. This shorter distance, as will become clear throughout the article, is due to significantly reduced steric repulsion, a consequence of the Pauli exclusion principle. The same phenomenon is observed in the binary hydrides of group‐17 elements.

Results and Discussion

Boiling Points and Intermolecular Interactions

The textbook explanation for trends in the boiling points, T b, of binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements invokes a simple picture based on two‐body dipole–dipole or van der Waals interactions [1, 2, 3]. In this section, we confirm that this simplified picture is partially correct. Specifically, T b trends within a group in the periodic table as a function of molecular weight are dominated by the strength of two‐body interactions.

T b and enthalpies of vaporization at T b, ΔH vap(T b), are related through Trouton’s rule, a well‐known empirical relationship stating that the entropy of vaporization at T b, ΔS vap(T b), is approximately constant for many liquids [6, 7, 8].

For the series of binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements, variations in ΔS vap(T b) are smaller than 0.01 kcal K–1 mol–1 (see Table S1). Consequently, ΔH vap(T b) is expected to scale linearly with T b through a constant C, which corresponds approximately to 1/ΔS vap(T b):

We graphically illustrate the expected linear relationship between T b and the experimental ΔH vap(T b) for a series of binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements in Figure 2. Here, we show that T b ∝ ΔH vap(T b) for this series of compounds, which are commonly used to demonstrate the increase in T b with respect to the molecular weight, as well as the anomalies associated with second‐period elements. For example, T b increases from −67 and −41 °C for the heavier HBr and H2Se to 20 and 100 °C for the lighter HF and H2O, while ΔH vap(T b) varies from 4.2 and 4.7 to 7.3 and 9.7 kcal mol–1, along the same series [9, 10, 11, 12]. Importantly, this relationship also shows that the elevated T b for lighter binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements is not caused by entropic effects.

In other words, ΔH vap(T b) is directly related to –ΔE liq, which, in principle, contains pairwise interactions and many‐body effects, and, therefore, T b ∝ ΔH vap(T b) ∝ –ΔE liq. Here, we demonstrate that the relationship can be further simplified to two‐body, hydrogen‐bonded dimer dissociation energies, –ΔE dim, in order to explain the elevated T b of HF and H2O, provided that the series of compounds shares a similar three‐dimensional structure.

To demonstrate the linear relationship between ΔH vap(T b) and –ΔE liq, we calculate the dissociation energies for n‐body [H2Ch] n and [H2X] n model clusters (n = 3, 4; Ch = O, S, Se; X = F, Cl, and Br), namely, the trimer (–ΔE trim) and tetramer (–ΔE tetram) dissociation energies. Although the liquid phase consists of a dynamic ensemble of structures, we employ a systematic set of model clusters that enables investigation of hydrogen bond formation and assessment of the textbook explanation for the observed T b trends. To this end, we selected structures that resemble the most common arrangements in the liquid phase while maintaining similar bonding motifs across each [H2Ch] n or [HX] n series. Binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements are known to adopt different three‐dimensional structures in the liquid phase. For instance, H2O and H2S form branched, cyclic, and three‐dimensional networks [13, 14, 15], whereas HF and HCl are known to form zigzag linear chain structures, as shown by Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics simulations [16, 17, 18]. Our model clusters are constructed to reflect these structural motifs; as such, [H2Ch] n correspond to true local minima, whereas C s constraints were imposed on [HX] n to enforce representative structural motifs consistent with known liquid‐phase arrangements (see Figure 3). The ability of these clusters to capture liquid‐phase interactions is supported by the strong correlation between their calculated stabilities and the corresponding experimental thermodynamic data, as discussed below.

We find that there is a strong linear relationship between the experimental ΔH vap(T b) and both –ΔE trim and –ΔE tetram, showing that our model clusters are representative of the liquid‐phase structures to reproduce the experimental trends of thermodynamic data (Figure 4a). Accordingly, T b scales with –ΔE trim and –ΔE tetram and, thus, T b ∝ –ΔE trim and T b ∝ –ΔE tetram (Figure 4b). This leads to our second key conclusion: variations in T b across binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements, and thus the elevated T b for HF and H2O, are dominated by electronic effects, with thermal and enthalpic contributions playing a minor role.

To understand the trends in –ΔE 2b, we analyze the dissociation energy of simple dimers. In contrast to –ΔE 2b, –ΔE dim shows a poorer linear relationship with the experimental ΔH vap(T b) (R 2 = 0.8888) and, consequently, T b (R 2 = 0.8517; see Figure S1). This is because –ΔE dim cannot accurately reproduce trends in T b for systems with similar molecular weights, such as when comparing group‐16 and group‐17 hydrides containing elements from the same period. For example, –ΔE dim increases from 2.2 to 2.4 kcal mol–1 from H2S to HCl, whereas T b decreases from −60 to −85 °C (Figure S1 and Table S2). Nonetheless, –ΔE dim already shows stronger interactions for HF and H2O, −4.8 and −5.2 kcal mol–1, respectively, leading to their elevated T b.

In fact, H2Ch compounds exhibit higher T b than HX due to their more branched three‐dimensional structures, which enable the formation of more hydrogen bonds than the zigzag linear chains found in HX (see Figure 3) [20]. This structural difference between H2Ch and HX has a non‐negligible impact already at the level of the second‐order term in the many‐body expansion, leading to more destabilizing –ΔE 2b for [H2Ch] n compared to the [HX] n series (n = 3, 4). Notably, this structural effect is not captured by –ΔE dim alone at the second‐order level.

On the other hand, there is a strong linear relationship between T b and the experimental ΔH vap(T b) with the quantum chemically calculated –ΔE dim for a series of compounds with the same three‐dimensional structure, e.g., within the series of H2O, H2S, and H2Se or the series HF, HCl, and HBr (Figure 5). These correspond to the standard textbook series in which the abnormally high T b of the second‐period elements is observed [1, 2, 3]. Consequently, trends in –ΔE dim as a function of molecular weight for H2Ch and HX compounds closely mirror those in ΔH vap(T b) and, thus, T b. For example, –ΔE dim increases from 2.2 to 2.4 to 5.2 kcal mol–1 along [H2S]2, [H2Se]2, and [H2O]2, while T b increases from −60 to −41 to 100 °C along the same series (Figure 5b; see also Table S2).

Therefore, within the series of H2Ch and HX, we find that variations in T b are largely governed by two‐body interactions that can be approximately reduced to pairwise hydrogen‐bond interactions, i.e., T b ∝ ΔH vap(T b) ∝ –ΔE dim. Consistent with this interpretation, a recent work by Hoffman and Xantheas [20] shows that two‐body interactions account for more than 80% of the bond energy in H2Ch tetramers. This also explains why pairwise potentials can predict vapor pressures and boiling points of water with surprisingly good accuracy [21, 22, 23, 24].

We emphasize that boiling points are macroscopic properties that depend on a balance of many‐body interactions and entropic effects in the liquid phase. The present cluster‐based analysis does not aim to provide a complete quantitative description of the liquid state or predict the T b value of the studied compounds. Rather, it is used to isolate and rationalize the trends in intermolecular interaction strengths within chemically similar series, that is, the stronger cohesive forces for the smaller second‐period hydride compounds that lead to their larger ΔH vap(T b) and T b compared to those of their heavier analogs. The observed correlations between cluster interaction energies and experimental thermodynamic quantities should not be used to predict general trends in T b. Instead, they suggest that the underlying two‐body hydrogen bond interactions provide a meaningful descriptor of these trends, while entropic, higher‐order many‐body terms and quantum nuclear effects primarily modulate their absolute magnitude.

In summary, we demonstrate that the anomalously high boiling points of second‐period binary hydrides relative to their heavier analogs originate mainly from the bonding mechanism governing their dimerization. This conclusion holds because, within the H2Ch or HX series of compounds, the following conditions are satisfied: (i) ΔS vap(T b) does not vary significantly [6, 7, 8]; (ii) variations in thermal corrections to the dissociation energies are negligible; and (iii) a roughly similar three‐dimensional structural configuration is adopted in the liquid phase. In the following sections, we investigate the stability of the studied dimers to elucidate the origin of the trends in T b.

Classical Interpretation of Hydrogen Bonding

Within the series of binary hydrides of group‐16 elements, the H2O dimer is significantly more stable than its heavier analogs. In textbooks, this is commonly attributed to water’s well‐known ability to form hydrogen bonds, whereas H2S and H2Se are generally, although incorrectly, considered incapable of doing so [1, 2, 3]. The classical interpretation of hydrogen bonding suggests that these interactions are primarily Coulombic in nature. According to this view, the larger partial charges on oxygen and hydrogen atoms would lead to stronger electrostatic stabilization in H2O compared to that in its heavier analogs [1, 2, 3, 5]. This expectation is supported by an analysis of the molecular electrostatic potentials (MEPs) of the H2Ch fragments, which reveal much greater charge separation in H2O. For instance, the MEP of H2O ranges from −31 to +43 kcal mol–1, whereas the range is only −16 to +24 kcal mol–1 for H2S and −14 to +19 kcal mol–1 for H2Se (Figure 6b). Similar observations can be made for binary hydrides of group‐17 elements, as reported in Figure S2.

However, is the stronger Coulombic attraction between H2O fragments truly responsible for the high stability of the H2O dimer and, consequently, the elevated boiling point of water?

The Exact Coulombic Interactions

We begin by investigating the formation of H2Ch•••H2Ch (Ch = O, S, Se), starting from the individual H2Ch fragments, and plotting their ΔE as a function of r Ch•••H (Figure 7a). At longer r Ch•••H values, all H2Ch•••H2Ch exhibit comparable stability. However, the energy minima are reached at larger distances for the heavier H2S•••H2S and H2Se•••H2Se. In contrast, the formation of H2O•••H2O proceeds further at shorter r Ch•••H values, where its ΔE becomes significantly more stabilizing than that of its heavier analogs.

Next, we apply the activation strain model (ASM), which divides ΔE dim into two components [25]. The first term is the strain energy (ΔE strain) associated with the geometrical deformation of the individual fragments as dimerization occurs. For the analyzed H2O, H2S, and H2Se dimers, ΔE strain is negligible, amounting to less than 0.1 kcal mol–1 (Table 2). The second component is the interaction energy (ΔE int) between the deformed fragments, which is primarily responsible for the formation of H2Ch•••H2Ch. In the framework of Kohn–Sham molecular orbital (KS‐MO) theory, ΔE int can be further dissected into physically meaningful steps. These steps begin with the wavefunctions of the deformed H2Ch fragments and culminate in the final wavefunction of the corresponding H2Ch dimer. The energy associated with each step is quantified using our energy decomposition analysis (EDA) method, which has been explained in detail elsewhere [25]. The contribution of weak dispersion forces to the ΔE int profile and, thus, to the trends in boiling points is negligible, as the dispersion energy curves (ΔE disp) are nearly constant and, therefore, are not discussed here (see Figures S3 and S4).

In the first step, we quantify the electrostatic interactions (ΔV elstat) between the unperturbed charge distributions of the H2Ch fragments during dimer formation (Figure 7b). Our quantum chemical calculations reveal that the H2O dimer experiences the least stabilizing electrostatic attraction within the H2Ch dimer series. Specifically, the ΔV elstat curves become progressively more stabilizing along the series H2O•••H2O, H2S•••H2S, and H2Se•••H2Se (Figure 7b). Thus, the ΔV elstat curve for H2O•••H2O is the least stabilizing in the case that two H2O molecules approach each other from an infinite separation.

This occurs despite H2O being the most polar fragment with the largest charge separation. This apparent contradiction arises because atoms and molecules do not behave as point charges but rather as dispersed charge distributions[30]. In reality, the larger positively charged nuclei and more diffuse negatively charged electron densities of the heavier H2S and H2Se fragments allow for a greater overlap of their charge distributions, resulting in more stabilizing electrostatic attraction in their corresponding dimers compared to H2O. Therefore, Coulombic interactions are not directly responsible for the unexpectedly high boiling point of water. Instead, as will become clear in the next sections, they would predict a monotonic increase in T b along H2O•••H2O, H2S•••H2S, and H2Se•••H2Se.

The misleading assumption that more polar H2O should exhibit more stabilizing ΔV elstat than its heavier analogs arises from differences in the intermolecular Ch•••H distances (r Ch•••H) rather than from the dipole moments of the H2Ch monomers. As noted above, Coulombic interactions arise from the distance‐dependent overlap of charge distributions. At longer r Ch•••H, ΔV elstat is more stabilizing for H2S•••H2S and H2Se•••H2Se due to their more diffuse charged distributions (Figure 7b). For example, at 2.80 Å, ΔV elstat varies from −2.2 to −2.4 to −2.8 kcal mol–1 across H2O•••H2O, H2S•••H2S, and H2Se•••H2Se (Table 2).

However, H2S•••H2S and H2Se•••H2Se reach their equilibrium distances earlier along r Ch•••H, at 2.71 Å and 2.78 Å, respectively, whereas the equilibrium geometry of H2O•••H2O occurs at a significantly shorter r Ch•••H of 1.95 Å. This shorter equilibrium distance allows a greater overlap of charge distributions in H2O•••H2O, resulting in a more stabilizing ΔV elstat compared with H2S•••H2S and H2Se•••H2Se. For example, at the equilibrium geometries, ΔV elstat varies from −8.0 to −2.8 to −3.0 kcal mol–1 across H2O•••H2O, H2S•••H2S, and H2Se•••H2Se (Table 2).

In summary, water experiences more attractive Coulomb interactions because of the shorter intermolecular O•••H distance, not because of its larger dipole moment. The same conclusion applies to the HX•••HX series (X = F, Cl, and Br; see Table S2). This raises the question: why can H2O form shorter Ch•••H bonds than its heavier analogs?

The Origin of the Abnormally High Boiling Point of Water

In the next step of our analysis, a fundamental quantum mechanical condition must be satisfied: two electrons in the same system cannot occupy the same spin orbital, i.e., they cannot have identical sets of quantum numbers [31]. In other words, electrons in two different spin orbitals cannot be simultaneously at the same point in space. This is the Pauli exclusion principle, which is manifested when there is overlap between same‐spin‐occupied orbitals. The Pauli principle is satisfied by antisymmetrizing the product wavefunction of the two H2Ch fragments, which ensures the correct fermionic nature of the total wavefunction. The associated energy increase due to the required orthogonalization of the occupied orbitals is referred to as the steric Pauli repulsion (ΔE Pauli).

Our analysis shows that the unexpectedly high boiling point of water arises from the more compact lone‐pair (LP) orbitals on the oxygen atom of the H2O fragment, which introduce significantly less ΔE Pauli compared to the more diffuse LP orbitals of H2S and H2Se during dimer formation. As shown in Figure 7c, the ΔE Pauli curves become progressively less destabilizing along the series H2Se•••H2Se, H2S•••H2S, and H2O•••H2O (see numerical examples at consistent geometries in Table 2). This trend occurs because ΔE Pauli is proportional to the overlap squared of same‐spin‐occupied orbitals [25]. As the LPCh orbitals become more compact from H2Se to H2S to H2O, the destabilizing occupied–occupied orbital overlap squared between the LPCh and the σH ─ Ch bonding orbital (σH–Ch) on the opposite H2Ch fragment (S 2 LP–σ) decreases, leading to a reduction in ΔE Pauli across the series (Figure 8).

Crucially, ΔE Pauli is the only term acting as a repulsive wall, preventing the two H2Ch fragments from approaching each other until they reach the equilibrium geometry of their respective H2Ch dimers (Figure 7c). ΔV elstat and ΔE oi remain essential stabilizing components. However, they do not determine the trends in ΔE dim as their magnitude is strongly dependent on the intermolecular distance set by ΔE Pauli. In other words, ΔE Pauli emerges as the dominant factor controlling the equilibrium distance and, thereby, indirectly governs the strength of all stabilizing interactions. Due to the reduced ΔE Pauli, H2O•••H2O can achieve a shorter r Ch•••H, where all stabilizing interactions, including ΔV elstat and ΔE oi, become more favorable. The latter is discussed in the following section. The same conclusions are drawn for dimers of binary hydrides of group‐17 elements, as can be seen in Figure S4, which shows that the ΔE Pauli curve is significantly less destabilizing for HF•••HF, whereas ΔV elstat and ΔE oi become more favorable for HCl•••HCl and HBr•••HBr.

To demonstrate this, we calculate ΔE dim(r Ch•••H) for fictitious H2Ch dimers, fict‐[H2Ch•••H2Ch], which are forced to experience the same ΔE Pauli(r Ch•••H) as H2O•••H2O (Figure 9a). In this scenario, steric repulsion is enforced to have no impact on the trends in ΔE dim, which are, then, fully governed by Coulomb attractions and donor–acceptor interactions. Notably, fict‐[H2O•••H2O] ≡ H2O•••H2O retains the same ΔE dim curve as shown in Figure 7a, albeit on a different scale in Figure 9a.

In contrast, the reduced ΔE Pauli in fict‐[H2S•••H2S] and fict‐[H2Se•••H2Se] leads to significantly shorter equilibrium distances and more strongly bound dimers (Figure 9a). As shown previously, ΔV elstat(r Ch•••H) and ΔE oi(r Ch•••H) become increasingly favorable as Ch increases in size (Figure 7b,d). Consequently, ΔE dim(r Ch•••H) becomes progressively more stabilizing along the series H2O•••H2O, fict‐[H2S•••H2S], and fict‐[H2Se•••H2Se]. In addition, ΔE Pauli(r Ch•••H) is not enough to prevent the close approach of the heavier H2Ch fragments, and the ΔE dim minima shift from approximately 2.75 Å in [H2S•••H2S] and [H2Se•••H2Se] to approximately 1.10 Å in fict‐[H2S•••H2S] and fict‐[H2Se•••H2Se].

Without the increased ΔE Pauli as Ch increases in size along the H2Ch dimers, the anomaly in T b trends across binary hydrides of group‐16 elements would not exist. We emphasize that the linear relationship between T b and –ΔE dim in Figure 5b cannot be used to predict general trends in T b. However, as a mere thought experiment, we used it to qualitatively estimate the T b for fict‐H2S and fict‐H2Se. Accordingly, T b is expected to significantly increase along with H2O, fict‐H2S, and fict‐H2Se (Figure 9b). In reality, such extremely high T b values are not observed for H2S and H2Se because they cannot form short‐range pairwise interactions in the liquid phase. See Figure S6 for similar analyses of fictitious dimers of binary hydrides of group‐17 elements.

In summary, the anomalous high boiling points for the second‐period binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 arise from their compact nature, which enables shorter and more strongly bound pairwise interactions. Although these compact molecules also happen to possess larger dipole moments, the dipole moment itself is not the physical origin of their stronger pairwise interactions.

The Quantum Chemical Interpretation of Hydrogen Bonding

In the final step, mixing occurs between the occupied and unoccupied orbitals of the two H2Ch fragments. This leads to orbital relaxation in the antisymmetric wavefunction of H2Ch•••H2Ch, i.e., H2Ch dimers are stabilized, yielding the final wavefunction of H2Ch•••H2Ch. The energy change associated with this process is referred to as the orbital interaction energy (ΔE oi) [25].

Hydrogen bonds are not purely electrostatic in nature; they also exhibit an important covalent component arising from donor–acceptor interactions [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. In H2Ch dimers, these donor–acceptor interactions occur when the LPCh orbital on one fragment donates charge into the empty σ∗ H–Ch antibonding orbital (σ∗ H–Ch) on the opposite fragment (Figure 8).

This stabilizing interaction is not unique to water. In fact, the covalent contribution to the H2Ch•••H2Ch hydrogen bond becomes increasingly stabilizing as the group‐16 element becomes heavier. As shown in Figure 7d, the ΔE oi curves become more stabilizing from H2O•••H2O to H2S•••H2S to H2Se•••H2Se (see numerical examples at consistent geometries in Table 2). This trend arises because ΔE oi is proportional to the orbital overlap squared between LPCh and σ∗ H–Ch (S 2 LP–σ*) and inversely proportional to their orbital energy gap (Δε). Heavier group‐16 elements exhibit more diffuse LPCh orbitals and lower‐energy σ∗ H–Ch orbitals, both of which contribute to more stabilizing ΔE oi by increasing S 2 LP–σ* and decreasing Δε, respectively (Figure 8). Consequently, the bonding components of the hydrogen bonding (ΔV elstat and ΔE oi) strengthen for heavier group‐16 elements, potentially leading to more stable dimers and higher boiling points for H2S•••H2S and H2Se•••H2Se. The same conclusion is found for many(n)‐body systems of groups 16 and 17, that is, the electrostatic and covalent components are more stabilizing and would favor the formation of more compact clusters for heavier (H2Ch) n and (HX) n systems. For example, at an average hydrogen bond length of 2.00 Å, ΔV elstat varies from −30.9 to −76.3 kcal mol–1, and ΔE oi varies from −18.8 to −109.1 kcal mol–1 along the [H2O]4, [H2S]4, and [H2Se]4 series (Table S4). However, this potential for forming stronger hydrogen bonds is counteracted by the increased Pauli repulsion, which imposes a large repulsive wall. Along the same series, ΔE Pauli varies from 25.8 to 185.6 kcal mol–1 at an average hydrogen bond length of 2.00 Å. Consequently, heavier binary hydrides are forced to adopt longer and weaker intermolecular interactions.

Conclusion

We provide a quantum‐chemical explanation based on quantitative Kohn–Sham molecular orbital theory for the origin of water’s anomalously high boiling point relative to its heavier analogs in the binary hydrides of group‐16 elements, as described in general chemistry textbooks [1, 2, 3]. Alongside thermodynamic and structural effects, the key lies in the stronger intermolecular interactions experienced by H2O molecules in contrast to those of their heavier analogs, such as H2S and H2Se. However, contrary to the classical explanation often found in textbooks, this is not due to water’s larger dipole moment. In reality, quantum mechanics reveals that molecules do not behave as simple point charges. Despite being less polar, heavier molecules can exhibit stronger, not weaker, Coulombic attractions due to a larger overlap between their more diffuse charge distributions.

Water and other small molecules experience significantly less steric Pauli repulsion. This quantum mechanical effect arises from the Pauli exclusion principle, which prevents fermions, e.g., electrons, from occupying the same quantum state. Consequently, when electron‐rich regions of different molecules approach each other, their overlapping spin orbitals force electrons into higher‐energy, unstable states. Small molecules, which contain fewer electrons and more compact orbitals, are less affected by Pauli repulsion. This mechanism contributes to the formation of shorter and stronger intermolecular interactions. In the case of water, the compactness of the H2O molecule ultimately contributes to its stronger intermolecular interactions and an unusually high boiling point. While the present analysis is restricted to binary hydrides of groups 16 and 17 elements, the underlying mechanism, namely, the interplay between Pauli repulsion and distance‐dependent stabilization, may be expected to operate more broadly in other hydrogen‐bonded systems, although further investigation is required to assess its generality.

The hydrogen bond is not unique to water, but it is also present in H2S and H2Se. Contrary to the classical mechanical view, hydrogen bonding has a significant covalent characteristic. Like the Coulombic and electrostatic component, the covalent contribution to hydrogen bonding strengthens as the group‐16 element increases in size. Together, these Coulombic and covalent components could, in principle, lead to elevated boiling points for heavier molecules. However, larger molecules are unable to form shorter and stronger intermolecular interactions due to the increased steric repulsion arising from the quantum mechanical Pauli principle.
