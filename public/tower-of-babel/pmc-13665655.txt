A Comprehensive Toolkit to Support Self‐Report Questionnaires for Autistic Adults With Cognitive Disability
Authors: Vicky Caron, Isabelle Courcy, Isabelle Soulières
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAutistic individuals with cognitive disability are underrepresented in research using self‐report measures. This study aimed to develop a toolkit of customizable accommodations to facilitate self‐report and to explore its usability through implementation with quality of life (QoL) questionnaires.MethodsA consultation process in three rounds was conducted with 10 experts in autism and/or cognitive disability to identify accommodations. The resulting accommodations were implemented with QoL questionnaires (WHOQoL‐BREF‐ID, WHOQoL‐DIS‐ID, ASQoL). Twenty‐two autistic participants with cognitive disability, accompanied by a parent, completed the questionnaires. Feedback interviews explored participants' experiences with the accommodations.ResultsSixty‐four accommodations and a cross‐cutting concern: the need for a flexible protocol were retained in the toolkit. Feedback supported the usefulness of several accommodations while highlighting areas for improvement.ConclusionsThis toolkit facilitates self‐report among autistic individuals with cognitive disabilities, which is crucial for valid QoL assessments and development of relevant services.Keywords: accommodation, adults, autism, intellectual disability, quality of life

Autistic individuals are highly heterogeneous in terms of their personal characteristics and level of support required. Even if around 30% of autistic people have a diagnosis of intellectual disabilities, most research on autism focuses on the ones without cognitive disability (Gibbs et al. 2024; Simpson et al. 2024). Underrepresentation of autistic individuals with cognitive disability is even more pronounced when considering only studies that use self‐report methods (Gibbs et al. 2024). Indeed, Gibbs et al. (2024) found only 14 studies on any topic conducted between 2013 and 2023 that collected self‐reports in a sample that was predominantly composed of autistic adults with a diagnosis of intellectual disabilities. Quality of life (QoL) research makes no exception. Indeed, according to a systematic review of 256 studies on the QoL and/or well‐being of autistic people across all ages, only 11% of the studies using a self‐report measure focused on autistic individuals with a diagnosis of intellectual disabilities (Simpson et al. 2024). This is particularly concerning given that self‐report is essential for meaningful participation in research, especially when assessing highly subjective constructs such as QoL (Hartley and MacLean 2006; Shogren et al. 2021). Moreover, QoL is frequently used as an outcome measure in intervention research. As such, accurate measurement of QoL is essential for identifying interventions that meaningfully improve it.

Several factors contribute to the limited use of self‐report measures within this population. Among these factors are cognitive and language difficulties that would prevent adequate comprehension of the items and the ability to provide a valid response (McGillivray et al. 2009; Stancliffe 2000). However, we may wonder whether the ‘inability’ to complete a self‐report measure is rather due to the lack of adaptations and accommodations rather than to the individual's characteristics (Shaddock et al. 2000). Indeed, research has demonstrated that with certain adaptations and accommodations, it is possible to obtain a valid measure of QoL or well‐being by self‐report from people with cognitive disability (Balboni et al. 2013; Davison et al. 2022; Fellinger et al. 2021; Havercamp et al. 2022; Koch et al. 2015; Shogren et al. 2021) and autistic individuals (Beck et al. 2024; Courchesne et al. 2021; Hong et al. 2016). Kooijmans et al. (2022) also demonstrated that adapting self‐reported measures of adaptive functioning led to more reliable and valid results among adults diagnosed with ‘mild intellectual disability’ or ‘borderline intellectual functioning’. Indeed, after the adaptations were implemented, participants reported finding the task easier to complete and a higher proportion of items were understood. Furthermore, although this does not, in itself, imply an improvement in validity, the agreement between self‐reported and proxy‐reported measures improved. Specifically, the accommodations implemented ranged from reducing the number of questions and simplifying the language to modifying the layout and more.

Other authors have reported on accommodations employed for autistic people with cognitive or communication difficulties to overcome obstacles during interviews or questionnaire completion (Courchesne et al. 2021; Davison et al. 2022; Nicolaidis et al. 2020; Simpson et al. 2024; Stalker 1998). Strategies include simplifying language, substituting complex terms, simplifying Likert scales, incorporating visuals, adding further explanation or examples for clarification, presenting one item at a time, alongside offering diverse response modalities. These approaches aim to enhance communication effectiveness and participant comfort within research settings. Although not specific to autistic individuals, Kooijmans et al. (2022) identified 161 suggestions and recommendations to develop inclusive self‐report measures to meet the needs of individuals with cognitive disability. However, they found that studies typically excluded individuals diagnosed with intellectual or adaptive functioning below the moderate disability level from the outset. Additionally, flexibility in methodological approach that involves collaboration with the participants and their relatives, which can help us understand and adapt to participants' communication preferences, contributes to a positive experience and is preferable (Gibbs et al. 2024).

Altogether, numerous accommodations have been proposed and implemented for interviewing or administering questionnaires to autistic individuals with cognitive disability. Unfortunately, authors that implement accommodations for self‐reported measures sometimes lack transparency on the accommodations made, rarely mention the reasons for specific accommodations and possible challenges encountered in their implementation (Davison et al. 2022; Gibbs et al. 2024; Kooijmans et al. 2022). A better understanding of accommodations to support self‐report with autistic individuals with cognitive disability, from their selection to their relevance and appreciation, is needed.

The aim of the current study was to develop a comprehensive toolkit of customizable accommodations for administering questionnaires to autistic individuals with cognitive disability to enhance comprehension and enable self‐report. In this study, the term ‘cognitive disability’ is used as a broader term to encompass the range of cognitive and communication profiles represented in our sample, which included autistic individuals with an intellectual disability diagnosis, as well as individuals without an intellectual disability diagnosis who had a standard intellectual functioning evaluation below average or were minimally speaking. We chose this broader term to avoid repeatedly describing these different profiles throughout the manuscript and to facilitate readability while still accurately reflecting the diversity of participants included in our study.

This paper describes the development process of the toolkit followed by its implementation for three QoL measures (WHOQoL‐BREF‐ID, WHOQoL‐DIS‐ID, ASQoL) and its appreciation by participants, their relatives and the research team. QoL measures were selected for implementation because QoL is an important research priority for the autistic community and because the lack of self‐report from autistic individuals with cognitive disability has been clearly identified in this field (Simpson et al. 2024).

Methods

Development of a Comprehensive Toolkit of Customizable Accommodations

This research project is part of a broader study on social support. Ethical validation from the Centre Intégré Universitaire de Santé et de Services Sociaux du Nord‐de‐l'Île‐de‐Montréal (CIUSSS‐NIM) was received. We conducted a consultation process of three rounds inspired by the Delphi method to adapt questionnaires' administration to enable autistic adults with cognitive disability to communicate their responses themselves.

Community Involvement

Three autistic adults without intellectual disability were part of the research team. They were paid for their time and recognized as expert collaborators by the funding institution (Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council [SSHRC]). At the start of the project, we held a meeting during which team members were able to share their expectations (e.g., roles, tasks, everyone's involvement), preferences and needs to support everyone's participation. Expert collaborators participated in research team meetings and contributed to decisions related to research design, selection of questionnaires and scales, recruitment and analysis of results. They were invited to provide feedback on knowledge dissemination materials and some publications arising from the project (e.g., scientific conference posters). Expert collaborators did not participate in the writing of the present paper.

Panel Members

Ten adults with expertise in autism and/or cognitive disability were recruited to form the expert panel. We opted for a diversified panel in order to obtain a broader perspective of the research question (Nasa et al. 2021). The panel included two autistic individuals (one man and one woman) who were also members of the research team as expert collaborators, two special education teachers (both women), two mothers of autistic adolescents with cognitive or communication difficulties, three researchers (two women and one man/one in neuropsychology, one in sociology and one in psychiatry) and a speech therapist (one woman). Experts were selected through incidental sampling based on either their professional training or personal experience with autism and/or cognitive disability. Professionals and researchers were required to have over 1 year of experience with this population. Experts were recruited via email, which detailed the nature of their participation and the project. Experts received monetary compensation for their contributions to the project.

First Round

An initial qualitative round, consisting of individual semi‐structured interviews, was conducted by Caron. The interviews were conducted in French on the Zoom platform, except for one expert who participated by phone. No specific accommodations were made to facilitate the participation of autistic and community members in the expert panel. At the beginning of each interview, the study's purpose was explained to the expert. Subsequently, they responded to an open‐ended question: ‘In your opinion, what is important to put in place to facilitate the completion of questionnaires in the context of an interview with an autistic person with cognitive or communication difficulties (e.g., minimally speaking or with intellectual disability), so that they can communicate their answers’. After the expert proposed a few suggestions, to elicit further ideas, the researcher presented the example item from the original version of the WHOQoL‐Bref disability module (Figure 1).

Example item from the original version of the WHOQoL‐Bref disability module presented to experts during the first round of the consultation process.

Individual interviews lasted between 24 and 56 min (M = 37 min). The interviews were subsequently transcribed in their entirety. A thematic qualitative analysis following the steps outlined by Braun and Clarke (2008) was performed with NVivo software. An a priori coding framework was developed based on 26 categories identified through a narrative review of the literature. This framework served as the starting point for coding the expert interviews. An additional 69 categories were identified in the expert interviews. Categories were organized and consolidated into broader themes according to the aspect or stage of questionnaire administration they addressed, following a semantic approach. These categories and themes, along with examples, were presented to the research team for discussion to ensure coherence and consistency with the coded data. This process resulted in 95 primary categories consolidated into five main themes. The ‘interviewer's skills’ theme covers recommendations concerning the way interviewers should conduct the questionnaires. The ‘prior knowledge of the participant’ theme covers recommendations concerning the information it would be useful to have about the participant before the questionnaires are administered, in order to make them more adapted for them. The ‘administration modalities’ theme covers recommendations concerning the presentation of questions and instructions of questionnaires. The ‘modalities of response’ theme covers recommendations on how to provide answers to the questionnaires. The ‘context’ theme covers recommendations concerning the environment in which the questionnaires will be administered, in both physical and temporal space. We also identified, in both the narrative review of the literature and the expert interviews, the need for an individualized, flexible protocol as a cross‐cutting concern. This stipulates that none of the accommodations should be used systematically without thought. Thus, all the recommendations set out above are options that need to be carefully chosen according to the needs and particularities of a given participant.

We then counted the frequency of experts mentioning each code. For this first round, the consensus criterion was predefined as 50% or more agreement among experts. If 5 out of 10 experts mentioned the same accommodation, consensus was achieved and the accommodation was immediately retained. We opted for a lower consensus threshold as the goal was to develop a comprehensive toolkit of diverse accommodations rather than precisely identifying the most common or ‘important’ ones. All other accommodations were carried forward to the second round. In the first round, out of 95 different accommodations, 19 made consensus and were retained directly. The remaining 76 accommodations moved on to the second round (see Figure 2).

Summary of the consultation process.

Second Round

Experts were e‐mailed a secured link to an online survey presenting the 76 accommodations identified during the first round that had not made consensus. They were asked to indicate their level of agreement, on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, as to the importance of each accommodation proposed for the development of the toolkit. Survey answers were compiled in SPSS version 27, and frequency analyses were performed. Items with 60% agreement or strong agreement, and no disagreement (the remaining 40% being neutral), were considered consensual. However, accommodations with 60% agreement but with at least one expert disagreement, as well as items with 50% agreement with no disagreement (meaning the 50% remaining in the middle/neutral option), were considered ambiguous and would be up for discussion. The other items were excluded. After the second round, 55 of the 76 accommodations obtained consensus, seven were up for discussion (ambiguous ratings) and 14 were rejected.

Third Round

The third round took the form of a virtual group discussion on the platform Zoom to stimulate debate and refine suggested items. Six experts were present: both autistic individuals, one special education teacher, one mother and two researchers. One expert withdrew from the project after Round 2 because she went on maternity leave. Firstly, the accommodations retained thus far were presented to the group. Ambiguous accommodations from the second round were then presented. The group was invited to comment on these accommodations and discuss their relevance. Finally, experts took an anonymous survey in order to assess their level of agreement on the importance of each ambiguous item on a dichotomous scale (agree or disagree). The consensus criterion was 50% or more agreement. Of the seven ambiguous accommodations up for discussion, four were retained. The presentation and results from this third round were shared with the experts who were absent, and they were invited to provide their comments in writing; however, only one general positive comment was received. In sum, at the end of the three round Delphi method, 78 accommodations were retained to be part of the toolkit. Since some of these recommendations covered similar topics, some were grouped into sub‐recommendations, resulting in 64 different accommodations (see Table 1).

Toolkit Implementation for QoL Measures

Implementation

Accommodations from the toolkit were implemented for the QoL measures. We employed the simplified version (WHQoL‐BREF‐ID) of the World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Scale (WHOQOL‐BREF) which is a 26‐item subjective QoL questionnaire divided into four sub‐domains: Physical health, Psychological health, Social relationships and Environment. We also employed the World Health Organization Disabilities Module (WHOQOL‐DIS), which is a complementary module for adults with physical or intellectual disabilities. Finally, the Autism Quality of Life Measure (ASQoL) was employed to capture aspects of QoL specific to autism (McConachie et al. 2018).

To our knowledge, the simplified versions of the WHOQoL‐BREF and the WHOQOL‐DIS were not currently available in French; therefore, a translation of the questionnaires was conducted for this study. The scales were first translated from English into French by Caron and back‐translated into English by a research assistant who was unfamiliar with both instruments. The back‐translated versions were subsequently reviewed by Caron and Courcy. Discrepancies between the original English versions and the back‐translations were discussed and revisions were made to the French translations to improve conceptual and linguistic equivalence with the original instruments.

All implementations were reviewed by the research team. Instructions and each question were revised and reworded in the simplest possible way that is, using simple, concrete vocabulary and short sentences, phrased in the affirmative (accommodations 21, 23, 57). To illustrate and concretize each question, alternative phrasings, sub‐questions and visual aids (pictograms were selected and transformed from Pictogramas y recursos para la Comunicación Aumentativa y Alternativa) 1 were developed and compiled into a supplementary binder for interviewers to use when participants did not understand a question or required additional support (accommodations 22, 24.b, 29, 34).

A three‐point Likert scale was adopted, and the response options were simplified by replacing the original terminology with ‘No’ for ‘Not at all’, ‘A little bit’ for ‘Moderately’ and ‘Yes’ for ‘Totally’ (accommodations 21, 37). To support the response scale, we customized three sets of smiley face pictograms (feminine, masculine and neutral) (accommodations 42). Unlike the original version of the WHOQoL‐BREF‐ID, we separated the smiley pictograms from the response scale (accommodations 41, 43). Using a practice item, participants were presented with the response scale and the available pictogram options and were asked whether they wished to use pictograms as a visual support when answering the questions (accommodations 38, 64). Participants were offered the opportunity to respond using their preferred communication modality. Depending on individual needs, additional tailored instructions were provided, such as responding by pointing or using a response token (accommodations 36, 47). An ‘I don't understand/I need help’ and a ‘Stop’ option were added to allow participants to indicate when they required additional explanations or a break, and corresponding pictograms were also proposed (accommodation 40). The use of pictograms to illustrate questions, support response options and the ‘I don't understand/I need help’ and a ‘Stop’ options was based on participants' preferences and the interviewer's jugement.

If the relative was present, they were provided with specific instructions: they were permitted to encourage the participant, ask guiding questions to facilitate the formation of ideas and offer illustrative examples to enhance comprehension of the questions but were explicitly instructed not to provide direct answers to the participant (accommodation 52). Various sensory stimulation objects were also made available to participants such as weighted stuffed lizard, fidget toys and stress balls (accommodation 62). Finally, before each appointment, interviewers had to consult an ‘Interviewer skills reminder sheet’ to remind themselves of the good practices to adopt (i.e., talk to both members of the dyad) (accommodations 3–9 and 20, 24, 26, 39, 48, 60, 61, 63).

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Autism database at Hôpital en santé mentale Rivière‐des‐Prairies and social media (associations and community organizations). All participants officially took part in the study with one relative. The information and consent form was presented verbally in a simplified manner, and both verbal and written consent were obtained from participants and, when applicable, from their legal guardians.

Twenty‐two dyads took part in the study. Autistic participants were aged between 18 and 47 years old (M = 28.91 years, SD = 7.18) and all had an official diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. Eighteen participants were males and four were females. Of the 22 participants, 10 had a diagnosis of intellectual disability (seven mild, one mild/moderate, one moderate and one not specified), 10 were not diagnosed with intellectual disability but had a standard intellectual functioning evaluation below average (full scale intellectual quotient [FSIQ] below 80) in their file (FSIQ range between 63 and 78) and two were minimally speaking. With regard to adaptive functioning, 18 had an Adaptive Behaviour Composite score in the low range (standard score below 70), three in the moderately low range (71–85) and one in the adequate range (86–114) (M = 51.00, SD = 21.44; range = 20–86).

Of the 22 relatives, 20 were the mother and two were the father of the participant. They were aged between 39 and 74 years (M = 59.73 years, SD = 8.26). For one participant, both parents took part in the feedback discussion.

Data Collection

Accommodations from the toolkit were also implemented trough the data collection process. This study is part of a larger research project on social support and data collection took place over two appointments. During the initial recruitment contact, information was gathered regarding participants' communication preferences, general level of comprehension, existing environmental accommodations and any characteristic behaviours that should be considered during the assessment process (e.g., echolalia) (accommodations 10–15). During the recruitment process, optimal conditions for participation were identified and meeting locations were determined in collaboration with participants (accommodations 49, 50, 51, 54, 58). A photo of the interviewers was sent to the participant and the relative in advance and other requests were addressed (e.g., received the questionnaires in advance) (accommodations 1,2,17, 51.b, 55, 56).

The first appointment (semi‐structured interviews for the larger project) allowed us to get to know the participant and become familiar with their communication style. The QoL measures were completed during the second appointment in the form of a directed interview. This appointment lasted approximately 1 h and 15 min.

During the appointment, prior to administering the QoL questionnaires, autistic participant was asked about their preference regarding the presence of their relative during the completion of the questionnaires and how the previous 2 weeks had been (accommodation 16, 53). Most of them (n = 19) completed the questionnaires in the presence of their relative. The questionnaires were administered in the form of an interview, where questions were visually presented one by one to the participant (accommodation 18, 30, 31). One researcher read the questions aloud, while a second researcher assisted with the pictograms and recorded the participant's responses and relevant information (i.e., comments from the relative) (accommodation 19, 32).

The appointment ended with a feedback session to gather insights on their experience with the questionnaires and throughout the research process to identify any suggestions for additional accommodations that could enhance the toolkit. The feedback session consisted in a semi‐structured verbal interview. Participants were asked to share their overall impressions of the questionnaire, including whether it was easy or difficult to complete and whether the provided accommodations (e.g., pictograms) were helpful. They were also invited to suggest any potential improvements. These questions were first addressed to participants and then to their relative. The questions were adapted to the participants' language and cognitive abilities. Depending on their comprehension, either general open‐ended questions or more specific prompts were used. The interviews were recorded and subsequently transcribed in their entirety. A thematic qualitative analysis following the steps outlined by Braun and Clarke (2008) was performed with NVivo software. Feedback sessions were analysed using an inductive, data‐driven approach, informed by the 64 accommodations of the toolkit. Codes were generated from the feedback sessions and subsequently grouped into three broad categories: facilitators, challenges and potential improvements. The coding was cross‐checked by Courcy.

Parents also completed the Vineland‐III (VABS‐III), a standardized scale in the form of a semi‐structured interview assessing the adaptive functioning of their autistic relative.

Results

General Impression and Experience

All participants completed the three questionnaires. Some completed them in a single session, whereas others required one or more breaks. Missing data were minimal, with a total of six unanswered items across three participants (two items per participant). The three participants had a diagnosis of intellectual disability (two mild and one unspecified). Five missing responses were from the ASQoL, and one was from the WHOQOL‐DIS disability module.

Most autistic individuals reported that their experience in completing the questionnaires and their participation in this research were enjoyable. They reported varying degrees of ease in completing the questionnaires. A majority indicated some questions as easy and others as more difficult, whereas some participants described the questionnaires as generally easy and others as generally difficult to complete.

Relatives also generally reported a positive experience and reported that the process was generally well‐conducted. A few parents expressed surprise at their relative's understanding of the questions or ability for self‐reflection, which revealed higher than they had anticipated. One mother said: ‘There are questions he understood earlier that I never would have thought he'd understand the meaning of, then, with what he answered you after, I said yes, he understood what he had to answer’. 2 On the other hand, some parents expressed concerns about whether certain questions were understood. Finally, one mother pointed out that the research allowed her to see her daughter interacting with strangers, something she rarely had the opportunity to do.

Facilitators

Autistic participants often mentioned that pictograms supporting the response scale and the additional pictograms illustrating certain questions were helpful (see Figure 3). Several participants mentioned that the possibility to ask for help, the question reformulations and the examples provided when necessary were helpful. The examples provided by their relative were also noted as helpful. One participant with intense sound sensitivity reported no excessive sensory overload during the questionnaires, which were presented mainly visually with minimal speaking. The completion time was considered adequate. One participant also indicated that he found the second appointment, during which the QoL questionnaires were completed, less stressful due to reduced stress associated with meeting new people.

Visual aid for Question 2 of WHOQOL‐BREF. The original question is: ‘How satisfied are you with your health?’.

Parents who witnessed the administration of the QoL questionnaires also found the pictograms for both the response scale and additional support, examples, question reformulations and the possibility to ask for help to be helpful. The short questions and the brevity of the process in general were also highlighted as positive aspects of the protocol. Additionally, one mother noted that the questionnaire administration did not seem too boring for her son, which allowed us to maintain his interest. Conducting interviews at participants' homes was helpful as it allowed autistic individuals to maintain their routines and made it easier to take breaks. Some relatives also mentioned that they would not have been able to participate in the study without this opportunity. Finally, one mother mentioned that words of encouragement and the highlighting of completed and remaining items in the question binder facilitated engagement.

Challenges

Autistic individuals themselves reported that some questions were more difficult than others due to their wording or subject matter. Indeed, questions on autistic identity, intimate relationships, social activities and access to psychosocial services were mentioned as being more complex. One participant sometimes did not know how to answer due to the lack of nuance in the question. One participant reported that he did not see the relevance of some questions. Finally, one participant felt that the questions or the process itself took too long.

Parents also reported challenges. First, the vocabulary was sometimes deemed too abstract or unfamiliar, making comprehension difficult. The phrasing of some questions was also challenging. A few parents noted that certain questions, such as those related to financial security, did not apply to their child's reality, as these are not matters their child manages or worries about. Questions that were subject to misunderstandings were: access to psychosocial services, money, dreams about the future and autistic identity.

The general and non‐specific nature of the questions may lead to a lack of nuance and responses that do not fully reflect participants' reality. For example, regarding the item on energy (Do you have enough energy to do what you want to do during the day?), one participant answered ‘yes’. However, during the feedback session, his mother indicated that, in her opinion, his response did not accurately capture his reality: ‘You say you have energy, yes, but your energy is very limited. It has to be nearby’. She referred to tasks such as shopping that require travelling further from home and using public transportation, which are particularly demanding for his son. A mother questioned whether the three‐level response scale could adequately capture the richness of information. One parent mentioned that some questions seemed repetitive; even though they were phrased differently, they essentially addressed the same point.

It was also mentioned that patience could be a challenge for some participants. In fact, one mother noted that her daughter tended to want to get the task over with and therefore would answer randomly. Another mother mentioned that her son tended to be less patient, given that we met with him after his school day. Finally, rephrasing the question after an initial answer to confirm the participant's understanding could sometimes lead to frustration, as it made the person feel as though they were incorrect or did not understand.

Potential Improvements

When asked if anything else could be implemented to facilitate the process, the majority of autistic participants responded that everything was satisfactory as it was, although one participant suggested further simplifying the questions. Some participants mentioned that they would have liked more help but had no specific suggestions to improve the situation.

As for the parents, some responded that everything was satisfactory as it was. They also suggested further simplifying the questions and using simpler, more concrete and familiar vocabulary (ex. Doctor or dentist instead of health professional), as well as shorter questions. It was suggested to use pictograms for all questions (supplementary pictograms were not available for all questions). One mother suggested using more complex and complete illustrations to convey concepts such as friendship. Changes to the response scale have been suggested, such as offering more nuanced options (e.g., ‘often’ instead of ‘yes’) and adjusting the wording depending on whether frequency or intensity is being discussed (e.g., changing ‘a little bit’ to ‘sometimes’ when referring to frequency). Finally, it was suggested that a visual timer be used to allow participants to see how much time has elapsed and how much time remains. Interviewers' Additional Observations

Regarding the toolkit and the accommodations implemented, accommodation 25 was not applied. Although assessing comprehension of each concept is theoretically relevant, implementing this procedure would have made the assessment process excessively burdensome. Indeed, QoL questionnaires encompass a multitude of different concepts. Therefore, we believe that this practice may be more appropriate when evaluating a more specific concept. In addition, photographs were not used, and none of the participants chose to use their own pictograms.

Regarding facilitators, sensory materials provided (e.g., heavy lizard, tangle) were frequently used by participants and contributed to a positive experience. Having a prior appointment before using the toolkit allowed the interviewers to build a bond with the participant, to become accustomed with their communication style and to learn more about their life. This allowed for the formulation of more individualized examples during completion of the QoL questionnaires at the second appointment.

As for the presence of the parent during the administration of the questionnaires, both advantages and disadvantages were noted by the interviewers. Firstly, parents can serve as communicative intermediaries, helping the interviewers to adapt to the communication style and language level of the participant. In the context of echolalia, the involvement of a relative helped distinguish genuine responses from stereotyped or echolalic ones. Additionally, the presence of a parent can sometimes make participants feel more comfortable and less anxious. The interviewers also observed that some participants tended to seek their parent's approval or perception when faced with more complex questions, which can indicate that a question is challenging and that additional support may be needed. Conversely, the presence of parents can also result in differences of opinion and influence the autistic participant's answers.

Although semi‐structured interviews generally allow for more in‐depth responses, this was not consistently the case with minimally speaking participants. During the first appointment (semi‐structured interview), the participation of some autistic persons was notably limited. In contrast, questionnaires completion, facilitated by the toolkit, encouraged participation. It seemed that structured questionnaires provided a clearer framework with well‐defined expectations. Moreover, selecting from a set of predefined options may have been more accessible and appropriate for these participants than generating responses to open‐ended questions.

Discussion

The current study aimed at developing a comprehensive toolkit of customizable accommodations for administering questionnaires to autistic individuals with cognitive disabilities. The consultation process inspired by the Delphi method with a diverse panel of experts resulted in the identification of a wide array of accommodations. In the end, 64 accommodations grouped into five main themes were selected for inclusion in the toolkit. A cross‐cutting concern, that is individualized flexible protocol, was also identified. The toolkit was then implemented in the context of QoL questionnaires with 22 autistic adults diagnosed with intellectual disabilities, intellectual functioning assessment below average or who are minimally speaking, each accompanied by a parent.

This study represents an initial step toward promoting the inclusion of autistic individuals with cognitive disabilities in research involving self‐report measures, extending beyond QoL assessments to other areas where individuals' own perspectives are essential. Indeed, as shown in previous research, this study further supports the feasibility of using self‐report measures, with certain accommodations, among autistic individuals with cognitive disabilities (Beck et al. 2024; Courchesne et al. 2021; Havercamp et al. 2022; Hong et al. 2016). We found, as did Kooijmans et al. (2022) and Davison et al. (2022), that implementing adaptations improved the accessibility of questionnaires for people with cognitive disability. Indeed, although this was not formally evaluated through a first‐round administration of the original un‐adapted questionnaires, it is likely that many participants in our sample would have experienced significant difficulty completing the original questionnaires, and that some may not have been able to complete them at all. Indeed, like Gibbs et al. (2024), our findings suggest that rigid standardization of a research protocol is not suited to the needs of autistic participants with cognitive disability, and that collaboration with the participants and their relatives is essential. Therefore, although scales are usually designed for standardized administration to ensure validity and comparability, these requirements may exclude many individuals, thereby raising important ethical concerns. This underscores the need to adapt administration procedures, at a minimum, to improve inclusivity. We believe that flexibility does not necessarily preclude methodological rigour and reliable and meaningful data, as long as accommodations are systematically documented and transparently reported. We could even argue that a lack of accessibility may compromise validity. Drawing on the work of Beddow et al. (2013) on the accessibility of standardized academic assessments, access is defined as the ‘degree to which a test and its constituent item set permit the test taker to demonstrate his or her knowledge of the target construct’. Therefore, when a test contains barriers that limit access for certain individuals, the results obtained by these individuals may not accurately reflect their abilities, thereby compromising the validity of the results. Although the assessment of QoL and other subjective constructs differs from academic testing in that there are no right or wrong answers, the same principle may apply. If QoL measures do not allow individuals to adequately express their own experiences and perceptions, the validity of the resulting scores may also be compromised. From this perspective, providing accommodations to improve accessibility may support, rather than undermine, the validity of QoL assessment (Beddow et al. 2013; Kramer and Schwartz 2017). For instance, Hartley and MacLean (2006), in their review on the use of Likert‐type scales among adolescents and adults diagnosed with intellectual disabilities, reported that the use of pictograms to illustrate response scales, allowing interviewers to paraphrase or expand upon questionnaire items, and asking clarifying questions (e.g., ‘Tell me more about it?’) to ensure participants understood the questions were associated with improved reliability and validity. Nevertheless, a balance must be maintained between flexibility and maintaining standardized administrations (Hartley and MacLean 2006).

Many of the accommodations selected for the toolkit correspond to the ones identified in previous research (Courchesne et al. 2021; Kooijmans et al. 2022; Nicolaidis et al. 2020; Simpson et al. 2024). Results from the feedback sessions confirmed the effectiveness of several accommodations and identified areas needing further improvement to optimize the toolkit. Indeed, as observed by Beck et al. (2024), some items remain inaccessible despite accommodations. As reported in their study, the question on romantic relationships was confusing for our participants, requiring reformulation by asking if the participant was satisfied with their current romantic situation. In contrast to Beck et al. (2024), some additional items in our study elicited confusion, including those related to financial security, challenges in formal contexts, perceived control over one's life, autistic identity, social support from friends and work capacity. These discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in sample characteristics between the two studies.

As mentioned by parents, certain items may have been perceived as confusing because they did not reflect participants' lived experiences. For instance, in our sample, some participants did not manage their finances, making questions on this topic more difficult to answer. However, it remains crucial to ask these questions, as the absence of concerns in these areas can contribute to a better QoL. Indeed, ‘QoL is defined as individuals' perceptions of their position in life in the context of culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns’ (The WHOQoL Group 1998, 551). Furthermore, as mentioned during the feedback sessions, autistic participants occasionally demonstrated a greater level of understanding than initially expected by their parents. It is therefore preferable to ask these questions rather than risk neglecting important areas of concern based on assumptions of limited understanding or interest.

More creative approaches that extend beyond traditional language‐based questionnaires may enhance accessibility by providing alternative ways for individuals to express their experiences. Hopewell‐Kelly et al. (2026) found that nonverbal mental health assessment methods for children and individuals with additional communication needs, such as the use of imagery, storytelling and drawing, can support the assessment process by reducing reliance on verbal language. Although these approaches have been explored in mental health assessment, they may also hold promise for improving the accessibility of QoL assessments for autistic individuals with cognitive disabilities. However, further research is needed to examine whether these approaches can be implemented with sufficient methodological rigour to be used in research settings.

Finally, discrepancies in opinion between the participants and their relatives do not necessarily indicate that participants misunderstood the questions. Rather, they may reflect genuine differences in perspectives between respondents, with both viewpoints providing meaningful information (Kramer and Schwartz 2017). Therefore, both informants can provide complementary sources of information that may capture different aspects of an individual's experience.

A significant strength of our study is its inclusion of participants who are frequently excluded from research either due to cognitive or communication challenges or because of the logistical barriers associated with travelling to research centres. Indeed, Kooijmans et al. (2022) found that individuals diagnosed with intellectual or adaptive functioning below the moderate disability level were usually excluded from studies. Although our study did not include participants diagnosed with severe intellectual disabilities, the assessment of adaptive functioning revealed that the majority of our participants had overall adaptive behavioural functioning in the ‘low range’. Another limitation of our study is the absence of people with cognitive disability in the group of experts involved in designing the toolkit.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature by providing a clearer understanding of the accommodations themselves and remaining challenges to improve accessibility of self‐report questionnaires for autistic individuals with cognitive disability. This toolkit facilitates more accurate self‐reporting among autistic individuals with cognitive or communication difficulties, which is crucial for valid assessments of QoL and, in turn, for the effective development of services (Schalock et al. 2002). We hope that other researchers will utilize or draw inspiration from the proposed toolkit to facilitate the participation of autistic individuals with cognitive or communication challenges in their studies, not only in QoL but on any subject for which self‐report is preferable.
