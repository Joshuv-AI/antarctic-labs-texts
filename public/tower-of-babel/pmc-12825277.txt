New electrocardiographic changes as predictors of acute coronary interventions in the emergency department: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Katherine Holmes, Emily Drone, Kaitlyn Lizardo, Garrett Frazier, Liana Milis, Daniel Phillips, Jeffy Jose, Kassidy Fretz, Charles Huggins, Hao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElectrocardiograms (EKGs) are integral to the early evaluation of chest pain in the emergency department (ED), and the HEART score is frequently used for risk stratification of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). However, the HEART score typically relies on static EKG assessments, overlooking recent EKG changes that may reveal evolving ischemia. This study investigates the association between serial EKG changes and the need for urgent cardiac interventions, assessing whether integrating serial EKG data can enhance the predictive accuracy of the HEART score.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective observational study of ED patients who presented at least twice with chest pain or equivalent symptoms from 2019 to 2023. Eligible patients had serial EKGs and documented HEART scores. New EKG changes were categorized into three groups: (1) new common ischemic patterns (e.g., T-wave inversions, ST-T segment depressions); (2) pseudo-normalization (e.g., resolution of prior abnormalities); and (3) new uncommon ischemic patterns (e.g., QT prolongation, U waves, LBBB, etc.). The primary outcome was the requirement for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Propensity score matching was used to control confounding factors. Inter-rater agreement for EKG classification was assessed using Cohen’s kappa.ResultsAmong 1,862 patients, 149 (8.0%) underwent PCI/CABG interventions. New EKG changes were observed in nearly 80% of patients who required intervention, compared with less than 30% of those who did not (p < 0.001). The most frequent patterns were T-wave inversions and ST-T depressions. Despite higher HEART scores in the intervention group, 10.7% were misclassified as low risk under traditional scoring.ConclusionsNew EKG changes may be strongly associated with the need for PCI/CABG and provide prognostic value beyond static EKG interpretation. Relying solely on traditional HEART scoring may underestimate risk in a significant subset of patients.

Introduction

Chest pain is one of the most common chief complaints leading patients to seek care in the emergency department (ED) [1]. Among this population, acute coronary syndrome (ACS) represents a critical subset of diagnoses that demand timely recognition and intervention. Missed or delayed diagnoses of ACS have been linked to significant adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, long-term disability, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality [2–4]. Therefore, early identification of patients at risk for ACS is a clinical priority in emergency medicine.

The electrocardiogram (EKG) remains a cornerstone in the initial evaluation of chest pain in the ED. It plays a vital role in screening for potential cardiac ischemia and in guiding immediate management decisions. Among several risk stratification tools, the HEART score, which includes History, EKG findings, Age, Risk factors, and Troponin, has been widely adopted to assess the likelihood of ACS, particularly for effectively stratifying low-risk patients [5]. Within the HEART score, EKG findings are a key component (i.e., E-score). E-score is typically categorized as normal (score of 0), nonspecific repolarization abnormalities (score of 1), or significant ST-T wave changes suggestive of ischemia (score of 2) [6]. However, the E-score is calculated based on a static, single EKG and does not consider serial EKG comparisons, despite the fact that many ED patients undergo multiple EKGs either during their current visit or in prior encounters.

The literature has increasingly recognized that new changes in EKG patterns, such as evolving ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities, may provide important clues about underlying myocardial ischemia [3, 7]. Nevertheless, many previous studies have been limited by a narrow focus on select EKG patterns, restricted observation windows, or variability in outcome measures [7, 8]. Some investigations have examined short-term changes or correlated EKG evolution with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), while others have assessed hospitalization or broader composite outcomes [9, 10]. These limitations constrain our understanding of how the timing and type of EKG changes relate to clinically significant endpoints, particularly those requiring invasive cardiac interventions.

To address these gaps, this study aims to evaluate the association between specific EKG change patterns and the need for acute cardiac interventions, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), among ED patients presenting with chest pain. By analyzing serial EKG data and correlating it with procedural outcomes, this study seeks to enhance clinical decision-making regarding risk stratification and timely intervention in patients at risk for ACS.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a single urban tertiary referral and chest pain center. The study hospital’s ED has an annual patient volume of approximately 120,000 to 130,000 visits and hosts an Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME)-accredited Emergency Medicine residency program. Institutional review board approval was obtained with a waiver of informed consent (IRB No. 1541042-4).

Study participants

Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) presented to the ED at least twice between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, with chest pain or equivalent symptoms (e.g., jaw pain, facial pain, upper arm pain, or shortness of breath) suggestive of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), as assessed by clinicians; (2) had HEART (History, Electrocardiogram, Age, Risk factors, and Troponin) scores calculated; and (3) had at least one EKG performed during each ED visit.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) only one ED visit related to chest pain or equivalent symptoms with only one EKG; (2) ED visits without a documented EKG; (3) index ED visits in which an initial EKG was diagnostic of ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) resulting in immediate transfer to the cardiac catheterization lab; and (4) patients under 18 years of age, incarcerated individuals, pregnant patients, or those with chest pain clearly attributed to non-cardiac causes (e.g., trauma, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism).

For patients with multiple ED visits during the study period, two selection pathways were used: (1) If the patient did not undergo any cardiac intervention (e.g., percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI] or coronary artery bypass grafting [CABG]) during the study period, the last two ED visits were included; and (2) If a cardiac intervention was performed, the indexed ED visit and the immediately preceding ED visit were selected for analysis.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome was the pattern of EKG changes. EKG change was defined as a variation in EKG characteristics either during the ED stay or between the index and preceding ED visits. For each patient, all EKGs obtained during the index ED visit were compared with those from the preceding ED visit. If multiple EKGs were performed during either visit, all available EKGs from the index encounter were reviewed against those from the prior encounter. Any new or evolving abnormalities observed between the two visits were documented as EKG changes. Based on literature review, changes were categorized into three groups: (1) New common ischemic patterns: including new T-wave inversions and new ST-T segment depressions; [3] (2) Pseudo-normalization: including apparent resolution of previously inverted T-waves or ST-T changes and disappearance of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) patterns; [9, 11, 12] and (3) New uncommon ischemic patterns: including QT prolongation [13, 14], U-waves [15, 16], Wellens’ sign [16, 17], de Winter T-waves [18, 19], ST-elevation in lead aVR [20, 21], new left bundle branch block (LBBB) [3, 22], and hyperacute T-waves [9, 23].

Each EKG characteristic change pattern was independently reviewed by at least two trained reviewers who were blinded to clinical outcomes. In cases of discrepancy, a third reviewer adjudicated the findings and made the final determination. All reviewers received standardized training in identifying and classifying EKG change patterns prior to the review process to ensure consistency and accuracy.

All these EKG characteristic change patterns have previously been associated with cardiac ischemia. To assess clinical relevance, patients were stratified into two groups: those who received urgent/emergent cardiac interventions (PCI or CABG) and those who did not.

Sociodemographic variables

Demographic variables included age, sex, race and ethnicity, marital status, preferred language, mode of ED arrival, and insurance status.

Propensity score matching

Given a preliminary imbalance in group sizes with more patients in the non-intervention group, propensity score matching was employed. The match was based on HEART scores and sociodemographic characteristics to ensure a balanced comparison between intervention and non-intervention groups. To assess the balance of baseline characteristics between the propensity score-matched cohort and the PCI/CABG group, we calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each covariate. SMD is a commonly used metric in observational studies to evaluate covariate balance between treatment groups, as it is independent of sample size. An SMD value close to 0 indicates better balance; values less than 0.1 are generally considered negligible. In this study, we calculated SMDs using the stdiff function in STATA and reported the differences both before and after matching. Negative values indicate that the mean or proportion is lower in the treatment group compared to the reference group, while positive values indicate higher values.

Data analysis

Patients were categorized based on receipt of PCI/CABG. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups. After propensity score matching, the primary outcome (i.e., EKG change patterns) was analyzed, and SMD was reported. Inter-rater reliability for the classification of EKG change patterns was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistics, with a kappa value greater than 0.6 indicating substantial agreement between independent reviewers. Pearson’s Chi-square test was used for categorical comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 17.0 (College Station, TX, USA), with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, the study included a total of 1,862 unique patients who presented to the emergency department. Of these, 149 patients underwent PCI/CABG, while 1,713 did not. The patients who received the interventions were generally older, more likely to be male, and more often arrived by ambulance than those in the non-intervention group. Traditional HEART score comparisons among patients with or without PCI/CABG interventions

To address baseline imbalances between groups, propensity score matching was performed based on demographics and HEART scores. Post-matching assessment demonstrated good covariate balance, with all SMDs falling below the conventional threshold of 0.1, indicating negligible imbalance across matched variables. All EKGs from these patients were reviewed by at least two independent reviewers with a high degree of agreement (κ = 0.6020, p < 0.001). Post-matching analysis of EKG change patterns showed that nearly 80% of patients who underwent PCI/CABG exhibited new EKG changes, compared to fewer than 30% in the non-intervention group (p < 0.001). The most observed EKG alterations were new T-wave inversions and ST-T segment depressions. Additionally, the median time interval between the two EKGs was significantly shorter in the intervention group than in the control group (p = 0.013) (Table 3).

Discussion

In contrast to static EKG characteristics, new changes in EKG features, particularly those following specific ischemic patterns such as T-wave inversion and ST-T segment depression, have been consistently associated with acute cardiac ischemia [3]. In this study, we categorized new EKG changes into three distinct patterns and observed that the most prevalent among them was the combination of T-wave inversion and ST-T segment depression. Patients exhibiting these changes demonstrated a significantly higher likelihood of undergoing PCI with stent(s) placement or CABG. While these findings align with previous studies, our analysis also highlighted the presence of other dynamic patterns, such as pseudo-normalization and various less commonly reported ischemic EKG abnormalities [3, 12]. These lesser-known EKG changes remain underexplored in the literature. Furthermore, our investigation found that while the HEART score is primarily designed to stratify risk among low-risk ACS patients, its predictive performance diminishes when applied to forecasting the need for PCI/CABG, rather than its original endpoint of major adverse cardiac events (MACE). This suggests that clinicians should exercise caution when relying solely on the traditional HEART score for decision-making regarding ACS interventions. Our study adds to the growing body of evidence emphasizing the critical role of comparing serial EKGs to identify new ischemic changes rather than relying solely on static EKG interpretations.

Several ischemic EKG patterns have been well-documented in the literature, including T-wave inversion, ST-T segment depression, and pseudo-normalization, the latter including reversion of previously inverted T-waves, normalization of previously depressed ST segments, and attenuation of LVH changes [12, 15]. These patterns are frequently associated with myocardial ischemia and demand prompt recognition in emergency clinical settings [12, 15]. Less commonly emphasized in the literature are dynamic changes involving QT interval prolongation, prominent U waves, left bundle branch block (LBBB), and hyperacute T waves, each of which may also signal evolving myocardial injury [14, 15, 22, 23]. Recognition of such EKG alterations has been shown to enhance the diagnostic accuracy for ACS, especially in patients presenting with recurrent or atypical chest pain. Importantly, serial EKG comparisons allow for the capture of transient or evolving abnormalities that may otherwise be missed in isolated assessments. As a result, integrating serial EKG analysis into routine clinical workflows may significantly improve early ACS detection and treatment initiation, particularly in high-risk populations.

Regarding EKG interpretation, all EKGs were independently reviewed by at least 2 reviewers, yielding moderate inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.602). While this does not reflect near-perfect concordance, it is consistent with prior literature and is generally considered acceptable in the context of EK assessment, where interpretation variability is common [24, 25]. Several published studies have reported kappa values ranging from 0.6 to 0.7 among experienced cardiologists, describing these levels as “reasonably high” or indicative of “good” agreement [24, 25]. For instance, one study found a mean kappa of 0.67 among board-certified cardiologists interpreting a large EKG dataset [24]. Another study comparing human and automated interpretations reported average kappa scores of 0.67 ± 0.09 for cardiologists, classified as substantial agreement [25]. Given this, we believe that κ > 0.6 demonstrates adequate reproducibility and reflects the clinical variability present in practice.

The HEART score remains one of the most widely used risk stratification tools in emergency medicine due to its simplicity and evidence-based approach [5]. However, one notable limitation is its reliance on static EKG interpretations for the E-component of the score, without accounting for new EKG evolution. In our study, applying the traditional HEART score would have resulted in the misidentification of over 10% of patients requiring urgent/emergent PCI/CABG. This underlines a critical limitation in using the HEART score unmodified for PCI/CABG prediction. When the E-score was redefined, we observed a marked improvement in predictive accuracy for patients requiring PCI/CABG. This suggests that a modified HEART score, particularly in patients with serial EKGs across multiple ED visits, may provide superior stratification for PCI/CABG compared to the traditional model. While the HEART score was originally developed to predict short-term MACE outcomes, it may not be well-suited to predict the need for cardiac interventions. However, incorporating new EKG changes into the traditional HEART scoring system appears to enhance its utility in guiding appropriate management decisions for such patients.

Our study possesses several notable strengths that enhance the clinical relevance of its findings. First, the primary focus on new EKG changes rather than static EKG characteristics offers a more temporally sensitive approach to identifying myocardial ischemia. Second, we conducted an extensive literature review and systematically categorized new EKG changes into three major patterns, thereby contributing a comprehensive framework for future studies. Third, we employed propensity score matching to mitigate confounding variables, reducing bias in the estimation of treatment effects when randomization is not feasible, thus allowing a clearer interpretation of the association between EKG changes and the need for PCI/CABG [26]. Fourth, by choosing PCI/CABG as the primary outcome instead of MACE, our study more accurately reflects real-world clinical decision-making. For instance, in practice, patients with recurrent chest pain and borderline EKG findings may undergo coronary angiography as a definitive diagnostic measure, even if the results are negative. Using PCI/CABG as an outcome metric helps reduce misclassification bias related to non-diagnostic interventions. Together, these strengths highlight the clinical significance of our findings and emphasize the utility of new EKG analysis in guiding intervention strategies for ACS.

Despite its strengths, our study is subject to several limitations. First, it is a single-center, retrospective analysis, which may limit generalizability. Variations in institutional protocols, local patient demographics, and clinical practice patterns may also influence the results and their applicability to other healthcare settings. Second, the sample size is relatively small, and despite the propensity score matching, the balance between comparison groups remains imperfect, raising concerns about residual confounding and the robustness of the subgroup analyses. Additionally, while propensity score matching is a robust statistical method for reducing observed confounding, it does not fully substitute for the rigor of prospective validation. Our matched-cohort analysis was designed to approximate balance across comparison groups and improve internal validity. However, these findings still require prospective studies to further validate them across diverse populations and multiple clinical settings. Third, our analysis focused exclusively on the HEART score, excluding other potential predictive models and clinical variables that may influence cardiac intervention decisions. Future studies comparing the predictive performance of various scoring systems, including TIMI, GRACE, and EDACS, in the context of procedural interventions would offer a more comprehensive understanding and enhance generalizability. Fourth, important social determinants of health, such as socioeconomic status, psychosocial stressors, or healthcare access, were not accounted for, potentially affecting intervention decisions. Fifth, while PCI/CABG serves as a robust indicator of clinically significant ischemia, it may not fully represent the broader range of management pathways or outcomes following ED evaluation. Taken together, future large-scale, prospective studies are necessary to externally validate our findings and to evaluate the clinical utility of integrating new EKG changes into risk stratification models.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the importance of new EKG changes, particularly patterns such as T-wave inversion, ST-T segment depression, and pseudo-normalization, in predicting PCI/CABG. Traditional HEART scoring may not adequately capture this dynamic information, potentially resulting in underestimation of patient risk. Modifying the E-score component to reflect EKG changes may significantly improve the prediction of the need for PCI/CABG. These findings advocate greater emphasis on serial EKG evaluations in emergency care and suggest that refining existing risk stratification tools could improve the accuracy of ACS diagnosis and management. However, given that these findings are based on a retrospective, single-center analysis, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding their generalizability.
