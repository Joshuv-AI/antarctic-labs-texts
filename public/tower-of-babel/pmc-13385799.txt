Reentrant Landau levels in a Dirac topological insulator

ABSTRACT
Pentatellurides have emerged as an ideal platform for exploring topological phase transitions and their electronic properties. Positioned at the boundary between a strong and weak topological insulator phase, their low carrier density and linear dispersion enable a three-dimensional Dirac Hamiltonian description. A complete understanding of these materials remains elusive due to unexplained sample variability. In particular, these materials have been shown to host anomalous magnetoresistance including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (B)$$\end{document}log(B)-periodic signals, non-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (B)$$\end{document}log(B) and non-1/B oscillations, in addition to the conventional 1/B quantum oscillations. Differing interpretations invoke many-body effects and there is currently no unified understanding of these oscillations. Here we report non-1/B oscillations in ZrTe5 down to 700 mK and up to 60 T, with a temperature and magnetic field dependence that violates the Lifshitz–Kosevich framework. We show that nonlinear Landau-level back-bending, arising from the interplay of cyclotron energy and strong spin-orbit coupling in a non-interacting Dirac system, captures all observed regimes and provides a foundation for understanding the electron dynamics of Dirac topological insulators beyond the quantum limit.

Introduction

The ability to control topological phases in solids has become central to modern condensed-matter physics, offering routes to engineer robust quasiparticles1, manipulate dissipationless boundary states, and design material platforms relevant for quantum technologies2. Pentatellurides have attracted intense attention in recent years due to their highly tunable topological properties, which enable controlled access to different topological regimes and their associated electronic responses. In ZrTe5 and HfTe5, the non-trivial topology originates from the nonsymmorphic crystal symmetry (Cmcm, D2h), which drives the band inversions that place these compounds near the phase boundary separating weak and strong topological insulating states3,4. This proximity allows external parameters to tune the topological phase: temperature can close and invert the band gap, driving a transition between weak and strong topological regimes5; strain can similarly induce gap closure with distinct topological signatures across the transition6–8; and magnetic field can modify the topological character by driving the crossing of the lowest Landau levels (LLs), producing a field-induced band inversion and a transition from Dirac to Weyl quasiparticles9,10.

The ultralow carrier density and nearly linear energy dispersion at the Fermi energy enable pentatellurides to be accurately described by an effective low-energy 3D Dirac Hamiltonian11–14. These characteristics make this class of materials a particularly clean platform in which the topological band structure plays a dominant role in shaping the electronic response. Within this Dirac framework, the materials host relativistic topological Dirac and Weyl quasiparticles, giving rise to a diverse range of emergent phenomena, including negative longitudinal magnetoresistance associated with the chiral anomaly15, a temperature-driven metal–insulator transition linked to a Lifshitz reconstruction of the Fermi surface16–19, and large magnetoresistance across broad temperature and field ranges11,20,21.

Beyond their tunable topological properties, pentatellurides have also become the focus of an active debate regarding the role of many-body interactions and whether they substantially influence the dynamics of their topological quasiparticles. Several experimental studies have reported signatures that have been interpreted as interaction-driven instabilities: high-field transport suggests that the lowest LLs may be susceptible to interaction-enhanced Dirac mass generation20; magneto-optical and transport measurements have revealed a magnetic-field-induced gap opening that has been associated with excitonic pairing in the lowest LL22,23; and putative charge density-wave formation has been reported12. These observations have led to proposals of correlation-driven phases emerging at high magnetic fields. However, the extent to which many-body interactions are necessary to explain the electronic response of pentatellurides remains an open question.

Among the most intriguing manifestations of the Dirac/Weyl quasiparticles in pentatellurides is the emergence of unconventional magnetoresistance (MR) oscillations in ZrTe5 that depart from the standard 1/B periodicity. These oscillations persist well above the quantum limit (QL) and extend to unusually high magnetic fields. The quantum limit is achieved when electrons are confined to the lowest LL. In ungapped, 3D systems, the system is not strictly confined, as electrons may have finite momentum parallel to the magnetic-field direction. In this scenario, the quantum limit has conventionally13,17,20,21,24,25 been used to describe the condition when the electrons are confined to the lowest Landau band with dispersion along the magnetic field direction. We adopt this nomenclature in this work, consistent with the established pentatelluride literature26,27. The MR oscillations above the QL defy the semiclassical Landau-quantization picture in which quantum oscillations (QO) arise from successive LL crossings of the Fermi energy and are strictly 1/B periodic28. Early reports identified a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (B)$$\end{document}log(B)-periodic component in the ultra-quantum regime, attributed to discrete-scale-invariant bound states stabilized by Coulomb interactions among relativistic Dirac quasiparticles26. Later experiments revealed a different class of anomalous oscillations that are neither \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (B)$$\end{document}log(B)-periodic nor 1/B-periodic and were instead interpreted as arising from Rashba-split flat bands, generating a distinct interference mechanism27.

Remarkably, these unconventional responses do not appear universally: while some samples display such nonstandard oscillations, others exhibit entirely conventional QO fully consistent with the Lifshitz–Kosevich framework11,14,25. This coexistence of anomalous and conventional behaviors, together with the competing interpretations proposed to explain them, raises fundamental questions about pentatellurides and the nature of their ground state. Understanding the origin of these MR oscillations is therefore essential, as they represent a powerful probe of the underlying electronic state. Yet a unified framework capable of reconciling the distinct oscillatory behaviors—\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (B)$$\end{document}log(B), non-1/B, and conventional 1/B periodicity—remains elusive. Establishing such a consistent description would allow these oscillations to be used unambiguously to determine the electronic properties and Fermi-surface structure of this relevant class of tunable topological materials.

In this work, we investigated the high-field regime of ZrTe5, well beyond the QL. Our MR experiments, performed at temperatures as low as 700 mK and in magnetic fields up to 60 T, revealed non-1/B periodic oscillations that persist to the highest available fields. We also observed non-monotonic temperature dependence in the oscillatory amplitudes, indicating a departure from the conventional LK framework. Furthermore, our data suggests that the electronic effective mass increases with the magnetic field. Finally, angular magnetoresistance (AMR) measurements tracking the MR oscillations indicate a 3D ellipsoidal Fermi surface at low magnetic fields. To explain the data, we employ a well-established low-energy k⋅p model that captures the nonlinear LL dispersion arising from the competition between cyclotron and Zeeman energies in the presence of strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC)11,12,21,29. This interplay causes LL “back-bending”, allowing low-index levels to re-cross the Fermi energy above the QL. This has previously been theoretically predicted for pseudospin Dirac systems30. This model successfully accounts for the anomalous observations in our experiments, including the non-1/B periodic oscillations, non-LK temperature dependence, and AMR. Importantly, we show that this framework naturally reconciles both the conventional 1/B-periodic and all anomalous non-1/B-periodic oscillations reported in ZrTe5, providing a unified description. This offers an important step toward understanding the role of sample dependence in topological Dirac insulators and semimetals.

Results and discussion

Semiconductor-like behavior

Figure 1b shows the temperature dependence of the electrical resistance, R(T), of the flux-grown single-crystal ZrTe5 sample studied in this work. The resistance exhibits semiconducting behavior from 200 K down to 0.4 K, with a clear saturation below 10 K (see Supplementary Materials). This behavior is consistent with previous reports on flux-grown ZrTe5 crystals7,18,26,27. However, it is important to note that even among flux-grown samples, significant variability exists. The energy gap and the position of the Fermi energy can differ markedly from sample to sample, with reported gap values ranging from as low as 10 meV to as high as 60 meV7,12,31–33. Role of Zeeman energy in LLs in Dirac systems

We now examine how the relativistic nature of Dirac fermions in ZrTe5 influences the LL spectrum under high magnetic fields. In particular, we analyze the competition between Zeeman and cyclotron energies and how their interplay affects the behavior of oscillations in a narrow-gap Dirac semimetal.

In systems with large SOC, such as ZrTe5, Zeeman splitting can no longer be simply treated as an additive term to a spin-independent band structure. The SOC entangles spin and momentum, so the Hilbert space cannot be decomposed into spin and orbital sectors. This leads to a more complex field dependence of the LL energies. In such systems, the effective g-factor can be extremely large, and the Zeeman contribution must be explicitly included when describing the LL structure.

To quantify these effects, we adopt a low-energy k⋅p Hamiltonian for a 3D Dirac system with time-reversal, inversion, and mirror symmetries. We emphasize that r denotes the oscillation index (not the LLs index), so the absolute value of θ cannot be uniquely determined. Since the index of the first oscillation is not experimentally accessible, we reference all oscillations relative to an arbitrary index r0. We also highlight that two important considerations are made: (1) the peak in the density of states occurs when a LL crosses the Fermi energy, i.e., we assume a fixed chemical potential, which to first order is a good approximation; and (2) the magnetization of ZrTe5 is small enough that μ0H ≈ B.

We set M and g using experimentally motivated values, with M = 5 meV based on Arrhenius fits to thermally activated transport (see Fig. 1 and Supplementary Material) and g = 27, consistent with prior reports for flux-grown ZrTe5 samples exhibiting similar resistivity trends39. We can fit the oscillation extrema using only two free parameters, v and EF. Fitting the oscillation extrema yields v ≈ 1.01(6) × 105 m/s and EF ≈ 6(1) meV, in line with earlier studies7,13,14,20,26.

Using these parameters, we compute the LL spectrum (Fig. 3b, c). While we cannot definitively determine whether EF lies in the conduction or valence band, the behavior of the LLs with field remains qualitatively the same in both cases. For illustrative purposes, we assume that EF intersects the valence band to be consistent with experimental work on samples with similar electronic properties40. Despite the Fermi energy intersecting one of the electronic bands, the sample exhibits semiconducting behavior at higher temperatures due to the low carrier concentration. This results from EF residing close to the band edge, where thermal excitation across the gap remains the dominant transport mechanism. A supporting calculation included in the Supplementary Materials confirms that this scenario is consistent with the observed thermally activated resistance.

The evolution of the LLs with field provides further insight into the origin of the MR oscillations. At low magnetic fields (B < 3 T), the oscillations arise from spin-up and spin-down LLs within the valence band. At higher fields, however, the dominant contributions originate from spin-up LLs of the conduction band and spin-down LLs from the valence band. Consequently, in both low- and high-field regimes, spin-resolved LLs-offset in phase-govern the oscillatory behavior. Notably, the phase difference between these levels increases with field, consistent with the emergence of field-tunable interference discussed next.

This behavior supports a scenario in which LL back-bending and spin-selective crossings drive the anomalous features observed in the quantum oscillation frequency.

The temperature dependence of the oscillation amplitude, ΔRpeak/valley/Rbackground, directly reflects the temperature dependence of A(B, T) for fixed field temperature cuts. Here, ΔRpeak/valley denotes the deviation of the resistance from the smooth background at each oscillation extremum (peak or valley)38,41. According to the LK model, the oscillation amplitude decreases monotonically with increasing temperature. In contrast, our data deviate from this behavior: a local minimum in oscillation amplitude as a function of temperature consistently appears below 15 K at all magnetic fields (see Fig. 4c).

To explain this anomaly, we consider an interference model in which two sets of LLs, originating from Zeeman-split spin-up and spin-down states, cross the Fermi energy with the same frequency F but with a phase offset28,42. This explains the non-monotonic evolution of the oscillation amplitude versus temperature. It arises from the interference between two spin-resolved contributions with different effective masses, each thermally damped at different rates and with a finite phase offset. At a characteristic temperature, their amplitudes become comparable and out of phase, leading to destructive interference and a dip. With an increasing magnetic field, the growing mass asymmetry and phase shift move this dip to a higher temperature.

Importantly, this enables the extraction of the parameters of the two spin-resolved contributions within the interference model. While we denote them as spin-up and spin-down for convenience, the fitting alone does not allow us to assign the heavier or lighter mass to a specific spin orientation. For this reason, we label the extracted effective masses as m1 and m2, corresponding to the lighter and heavier branches, respectively. The extracted masses m1 and m2 are distinct and both increase with magnetic field (Fig. 4d), indicating field-induced mass enhancement. The phase offset ϕ also increases with field (Fig. 4e), consistent with the LL calculations (Fig. 3b, c). Since the spin-up and spin-down LLs cross the Fermi energy at different magnetic fields due to Zeeman splitting, this separation is small at low field, when both contributions originate from the same band, and grows at higher field when the dominant crossings come from different bands. This leads to an increasing phase shift with increasing magnetic field.

The presence of two distinct effective masses arises from spin splitting in a Dirac system. In parabolic bands, LLs depend linearly on the magnetic field, so the spacing between adjacent LLs near the Fermi energy is field independent, and the Zeeman interaction only rigidly shifts the spin-up and spin-down branches, resulting in a single cyclotron mass. In Dirac systems, the LLs are intrinsically nonlinear functions of magnetic field, leading to a field-dependent LL spacing near the Fermi energy and, consequently, to a field-dependent effective mass, as observed in materials such as EuO and KTaO343. The Zeeman interaction shifts the spin-resolved LLs relative to the Fermi energy, so that spin-up and spin-down branches cross the Fermi energy at different magnetic fields. Because these crossings occur at different points along a nonlinear LL spectrum, they probe different local LL spacings, giving rise to two distinct effective masses. A semiclassical picture capturing this effect is discussed in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

In this work, we have shown, through a combination of experimental data and theoretical modeling, that the origin of the non-1/B-periodic oscillations in ZrTe5 lies in the reentrant LLs crossing the Fermi energy as a consequence of Zeeman-induced back-bending. The field dependence of the oscillation frequency is well fit by Eq. (5), directly reflecting the nonlinear evolution of the LLs in the magnetic field.

From the 3D Dirac Hamiltonian used in this work, distinct quantum-oscillation regimes emerge, depending on the initial sample conditions (such as carrier density and Fermi-surface size) and on the applied magnetic field. For high-carrier-density samples (EF ≫ M), the term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({E}_{F}^{2}-{M}^{2})/(2e\hslash {v}^{2})$$\end{document}(EF2−M2)/(2eℏv2) dominates over the Zeeman contribution, so that for fields up to B ~ 60 T the 1/B term controls the spectrum and conventional quantum oscillations are observed. For typical high-density samples, the field scale at which the Zeeman-dominated regime would appear lies beyond experimentally accessible fields. In contrast, for low-carrier-density samples, as studied here, the Zeeman and nonlinear Dirac terms become comparable at accessible fields, leading to deviations from conventional 1/B periodicity and the emergence of the anomalous oscillation regime. Interestingly, when the same data are plotted on a logarithmic field scale, the oscillations appear nearly log(B)-periodic. This apparent scaling behavior emerges naturally from the analytic form of Eq. (5). Expanding this expression around a characteristic field B0 yields \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r(B)\simeq \phi+A\log (B)+A^{\prime} {[\log (B)]}^{2}+\cdots$$\end{document}r(B)≃ϕ+Alog(B)+A′[log(B)]2+⋯, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A=-\frac{{E}_{F}^{2}-{M}^{2}}{2e\hslash {v}^{2}{B}_{0}}-\frac{{\mu }_{B}^{2}{g}^{2}{B}_{0}}{8e\hslash {v}^{2}}$$\end{document}A=−EF2−M22eℏv2B0−μB2g2B08eℏv2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A^{\prime}=A/2$$\end{document}A′=A/2. The oscillations observed in this work show a similar field dependence to those reported in ref. 27, with contributions from both a linear-in-B term and a 1/B term. However, owing to our extended temperature and field range (down to 0.7 K and up to 60 T), we are able to resolve clear deviations from the LK temperature dependence, indicating the presence of two distinct effective masses contributing to the oscillations. This behavior cannot be explained by an LK description with band flattening due to Rashba effects27, but is naturally accounted for by spin-up and spin-down states arising from the 3D Dirac Hamiltonian. Furthermore, measurements up to 60 T and over a wide range of field orientations allow us to fit the angular dependence of the oscillations, map the low-field contribution to an ellipsoidal Fermi surface, and determine its size, supporting our interpretation of a common physical origin for the different oscillatory regimes.

In modeling the oscillations, we have adopted a fixed Fermi energy, a choice that is well justified for ZrTe5 given the extremely low carrier density of its Dirac cones. In this limit, even a small electronic reservoir—arising from nearby parabolic bands or impurity states—can efficiently pin the Fermi level, preventing large field-induced shifts. Moreover, even in the clean limit where the chemical potential could, in principle, evolve with magnetic field, the back-bending of the LLs ensures that states continuously cross near the Fermi energy. This intrinsic reentrant behavior will stabilize the Fermi energy around a nearly constant value, making the fixed-Fermi-energy approximation used in Eq. (5) physically consistent with the experimental behavior. Consistent with this picture, the oscillation extrema occur at nearly identical magnetic fields across all measured temperatures, while the background magnetoresistance shows only a weak temperature dependence. These observations confirm that variations in carrier mobility do not substantially influence the oscillation frequency or phase, and that temperature primarily affects the oscillation amplitude through the expected thermal damping resulting from the Fermi-Dirac statistics and described by the LK formalism and interference effects.

This work establishes that ZrTe5 electronic properties are accurately captured by a low-energy three-dimensional Dirac Hamiltonian. These results reveal a unified ground state consistently described by this minimal Dirac framework, in which the competition between Zeeman and cyclotron energies drives the LLs to backbend and re-cross the Fermi energy, explaining the anomalous temperature and field dependence of the oscillations.

Importantly, the different magnetic-field dependences observed in pentatelluride samples,conventional 1/B and non-1/B periodic oscillations, including log(B)-like behavior-do not reflect distinct electronic mechanisms. They arise from Landau quantization of the same underlying Dirac band and the same precursor Fermi surface. The relevant difference is the Fermi surface size: in the samples studied here, it is smaller than in samples that predominantly exhibit conventional 1/B oscillations, which enhances the visibility of the non-1/B anomalous contribution.

By identifying a robust single-particle ground state whose observable response depends sensitively on the underlying carrier density, this work provides a foundation for disentangling Dirac physics from possible interaction-driven phenomena at high fields.

Methods

Sample preparation

Single crystals of ZrTe5 were synthesized using a Te-rich flux technique7. High-purity zirconium (99.9%, Alfa Aesar) and tellurium (99.9999%, Alfa Aesar) were sealed in an evacuated quartz ampule with a molar ratio of Zr:Te = 1:100. The ampule was heated to 900 ∘C over a period of 9 h and maintained at this temperature for 72 h. It was subsequently cooled to 505 ∘C over 48 hours. To enhance crystal size, the temperature was cycled multiple times between 440 ∘C and 505 ∘C. Finally, the system was cooled to 460 ∘C, at which point the excess flux was removed by centrifugation.

To improve the electrical contacts on the sample, gold contact pads were deposited by sputtering. Electrical contacts were made using 25 μm gold wire, which was fixed to the sample using highly conductive silver epoxy (EPO-TEK H20E).

PPMS

The zero-field temperature dependence of the electrical resistance (Fig. 1) was measured using a four-point probe configuration using a 372-resistance bridge from Lakeshore with an excitation current of 31.6 μA. The sample was measured down to 400 mK in a PPMS.

Pulsed field experiments

MR measurements of our sample were conducted at the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory Pulsed Field Facility at Los Alamos National Laboratory. The sample was immersed in liquid helium (4He) for experiments conducted between 1.5 and 4 K, and in liquid (3He) for experiments below 1.5 K. For temperatures above 4 K, 4He gas was used. The electrical resistance was measured using a four-point probe configuration.

All measurements were performed using a lock-in technique with a frequency of 220 kHz and a current of 100 μA. A self-heating test was performed in zero field at 4 K, and ohmic behavior was observed for an excitation current range of 10–400 μA. For the pulsed magnetic field measurements, the field profile in time is asymmetric, with a rapid initial magnetic field rise to peak field and a comparatively slow magnetic field decay from peak field to zero. Eddy current heating was observed during the initial magnetic field rise, but the sample thermalizes with negligible eddy current heating during the magnetic field decay. Additional details are available in the Supplemental Information. All pulsed field data presented in this manuscript were taken from the falling magnetic field region of the magnetic field pulse.

The sample was mounted on a 3D-printed cryogenic goniometer suitable for pulsed-field environments. In-situ rotation was performed with a precision of 0. 2∘, and the angular position was monitored using a mechanical counter and verified via pick-up coils mounted on the rotating stage45.
