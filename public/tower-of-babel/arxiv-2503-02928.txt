Finite-temperature quantum topological order in three dimensions
Shu-Tong Zhou, Meng Cheng, Tibor Rakovszky, Curt von Keyserlingk, Tyler D. Ellison

Abstract
We identify a three-dimensional system that exhibits long-range entanglement at sufficiently small but nonzero temperature—it therefore constitutes a quantum topological order at finite temperature. The model of interest is known as the fermionic toric code, a variant of the usual 3D toric code, which admits emergent fermionic point-like excitations. The fermionic toric code, importantly, possesses an anomalous 2-form symmetry, associated with the space-like Wilson loops of the fermionic excitations. We argue that it is this symmetry that imbues low-temperature thermal states with a novel topological order and long-range entanglement. Based on the current classification of three-dimensional topological orders, we expect that the low-temperature thermal states of the fermionic toric code belong to an equilibrium phase of matter that only exists at nonzero temperatures. We conjecture that further examples of topological orders at nonzero temperatures are given by discrete gauge theories with anomalous 2-form symmetries. Our work therefore opens the door to studying quantum topological order at nonzero temperature in physically realistic dimensions.



In recent decades, one of the central goals
of condensed matter physics has been the classification and characterization of quantum phases of matter at zero temperature. This has led, most notably, to the concept of topological order, where long-range entanglement manifests in exotic properties, such as anyonic excitations. The investigation of zero-temperature topological orders has culminated in conjectured classifications in low dimensions based on sophisticated mathematics
Kitaev 2006
;
Wen 2015
;
Johnson-Freyd 2022
;
Wen 2017
;
Lan et al. 2018
;
Lan and Wen 2019
.

Despite the theoretical progress, physical experiments cannot be performed in complete isolation from their environment.
Consequently, systems will inevitably equilibrate to thermal states at nonzero temperature---possibly destroying the zero-temperature order

Topological orders in low dimensions can be stabilized far from thermal equilibrium, but this requires highly engineered dynamics (e.g. active error correction). This may be feasible in quantum devices but is less so in conventional solid state systems.
.
A question of fundamental importance is thus: what topological phases of matter can be realized by thermal states above zero temperature?

It is known that certain topological orders can survive at nonzero temperature; however, to date, all known examples require four spatial dimensions or greater
Dennis et al. 2002
;
Alicki et al. 2010
;
Hastings 2011
;
Bombin et al. 2013
;
Hsin et al. 2024
. The prospect of finite-temperature topological order in fewer dimensions has been bleak, with no-go theorems in Refs.
Nussinov and Ortiz 2008
;
Bravyi and Terhal 2009
;
Alicki et al. 2009
;
Yoshida 2011
;
Roberts et al. 2017
;
Kato and Brandão 2019
;
Kochanowski et al. 2024
;
Bakshi et al. 2025
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
. This is usually attributed to the fact that these phases possess point-like excitations, which proliferate above zero temperature and destroy the topological order
Peierls 1936
;
Lan et al. 2018
;
Lan and Wen 2019
;
Castelnovo and Chamon 2008
.

Three-dimensional topological orders, such as the 3D toric code, however, exhibit both point-like excitations and loop-like excitations. The latter of which only proliferate at some finite critical temperature
Peierls 1936
;
Hastings et al. 2014
, implying a finite-temperature phase transition. It is tempting to conclude that the topological order persists at nonzero temperature
Castelnovo and Chamon 2008
. However, this is not guaranteed to be
quantum
topological order at finite temperature, which is characterized by long-range entangled (LRE) thermal states, according to the definitions in Refs.
Hastings 2011
;
Sang et al. 2024
.

Indeed, for the 3D toric code, it was shown in Ref.
Hastings 2011
that its finite-temperature thermal states are short-range entangled (SRE), i.e., they are classical mixtures of SRE pure states. Therefore, its finite-temperature phase transition is between classical phases
Lu et al. 2020
. It was suggested in Ref.
Hastings 2011
that the same conclusion holds for all finite group gauge theories in 3D. Given that these exhaust a large class of topological orders in 3D
Lan et al. 2018
;
Lan and Wen 2019
, it is important to settle: is it possible to have quantum topological order at nonzero temperature in 3D?

In this letter, we answer this question in the affirmative. We identify a Hamiltonian in 3D with local interactions such that, below a finite-temperature, its thermal states are topologically ordered,
and moreover cannot be approximated by SRE states.
It therefore constitutes the first example of a finite-temperature topological order in fewer than four spatial dimensions.

Our example is based on the so-called fermionic toric code (
fTC
), whose zero-temperature topological order is explored in Refs.
Levin and Wen 2003
;
von Keyserlingk and Burnell 2015
;
Chen and Kapustin 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021
. Similar to the 3D toric code, referred to henceforth as the bosonic toric code (bTC), the
fTC
is defined on a system of qubits. The key difference from the bTC is that its elementary point-like excitations have fermionic exchange statistics – i.e., swapping the positions of two of the excitations produces a sign in the pure state wave function. The model furthermore possesses an anomalous 2-form symmetry—simply saying that it is invariant under moving a fermionic excitation along a closed path
Bhardwaj et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Chen and Kapustin 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Ellison et al. 2023
. The symmetry is considered to be ‘anomalous’, because the resulting string operator inherits the fermionic self-statistics. We show that this is
directly responsible for the long-range entanglement of the low-temperature thermal states.

We emphasize that, although the
fTC
can be used as a quantum memory at zero temperature, it strictly behaves as a classical memory for nonzero temperatures below a finite-temperature phase transition. This is in contrast to the 4D toric code, which serves as a quantum memory at low temperatures
Alicki et al. 2010
.

fTC
before proving that its low-temperature thermal states are LRE, as made precise in Theorem

Fermionic toric code –

The
fTC
is defined on a cubic lattice with a single qubit on each edge, and for simplicity, we assume that the system has periodic boundary conditions. The Hamiltonian can be written as:

\displaystyle H=-\sum_{v}A_{v}-\sum_{p}B_{p},

(1)

where the first sum is over vertices
v
and the second is over plaquettes
p
.
We refer to the
A_{v}
and
B_{p}
as the vertex and plaquette terms, respectively. They are represented graphically in Fig.
1
.

The Hamiltonian terms are mutually commuting and thus define a Pauli stabilizer code. They further satisfy the following relations on a torus:

\displaystyle\prod_{v}A_{v}=1,\quad\prod_{p\in c}B_{p}=1,

(2)

where the second product is over the six plaquettes of an arbitrary cube
c
. These relations are identical to those of the bTC, and as such, the models share the same finite-temperature phase transition
Weinstein et al. 2020
.

Excitations–
The
fTC
exhibits two types of excitations, which correspond to violations of the vertex and plaquette terms. The violations of the
A_{v}
terms are point-like and live at the vertices of the lattice. These excitations can be created by operators supported along a path in the lattice, i.e., they are created at the endpoints of string operators. The string operators can be built from the short string operators
W_{e}
shown in Fig.
1
.
Notably, in contrast to the bTC, the point-like excitations of the
fTC
have fermionic exchange statistics, as shown in Ref.
Chen and Kapustin 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021
.

It is important to note that the Hamiltonian commutes with the string operators defined on closed paths. This defines a 2-form symmetry of the Hamiltonian, i.e., where the symmetry operators are supported on codimension-2 submanifolds. Due to the point-like excitations having fermionic exchange statistics, the 2-form symmetry is anomalous. In other words, there is an obstruction to gauging the symmetry, as this would correspond to condensing the emergent fermions. Note, however, that the symmetry is anomaly free in the presence of physical fermions as these can be bound to the emergent fermions and condensed
Chen et al. 2021
.

The second type of excitation in the
fTC
corresponds to violations of the
B_{p}
terms. Because of the local relations in Eq. (
2
), every cube must have an even number of violated
B_{p}
terms. This means that the violated
B_{p}
terms form loops in the dual lattice. Accordingly, these excitations are loop-like excitations.

The loop excitations can be created at the boundaries of membrane operators supported on surfaces in the dual lattice. One choice of membrane operator for a surface
\Sigma
takes the form
M_{\Sigma}=\prod_{e\in\Sigma}X_{e}
.
This operator commutes with all of the
A_{v}
terms and only fails to commute with the
B_{p}
terms at the boundary of
\Sigma
.

In analogy to the bTC, the point-like excitations and the loop-like excitations have nontrivial mutual braiding statistics, i.e., moving a fermion around a loop excitation incurs a sign. In other words, string operators that move a fermion along a closed path detect the number of loop excitations enclosed modulo 2.

Next, we leverage the fact that the loop-less state

\rho_{\varnothing}

Finally, we use that the QLC that cleans up the loop excitations of

maps SRE states to SRE states. If
\rho_{{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}}

\rho_{\varnothing}

Step one:
The first step is to define a QLC that approximately maps the thermal state
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
to the loop-less state
\rho_{\varnothing}
.
We accomplish this with a QLC

\ln(L)^{2}
. After applying all five layers, the only loop excitations that are left behind must have lengths larger than
\ln(L)^{2}
. These large loop excitations are exponentially suppressed in the thermal state at low temperatures. Thus, the QLC approximately maps
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
to
\rho_{\varnothing}
, in terms of the fidelity of mixed states. We note that, alternatively, a QLC based on the sweep decoder of Ref.
Kubica and Preskill 2019
can be used to remove the small loop excitations, as elaborated on in the Supplemental Materials (
SM

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{b}(\cdot)=\sum_{\mathcal{L}|_{b}}U_{\mathcal{L}_{b}}P_{\mathcal{L}|_{b}}\cdot P_{\mathcal{L}|_{b}}U_{\mathcal{L}_{b}},

where

is a loop configuration restricted to

is thus
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\bigotimes_{b}\mathcal{C}_{b}

Notice that loop excitations that cross between blocks are not removed by
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, regardless of their size. Therefore, in the subsequent layers, we clean up loop excitations along the seams of the first blocking, starting with those perpendicular to the
x

Step two:
The second step is to argue that

cannot be approximated by a SRE state. To make this explicit, we let

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{SRE}}=\sum_{i}p_{i}|\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}|.

We assume here that each

The fidelity can be immediately bounded using a simple generalization of Corollary 2 in Ref.

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\varnothing},\sigma_{\text{SRE}})<e^{\frac{-cL^{3}}{(d+1)^{3}}},

(9)

for some positive constant

If

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\varnothing},\sigma_{\text{SRE}})=O(L^{-\infty}).

(10)

The absence of loop excitations in

means that it obeys a strong 2-form symmetry.
Thus, while the thermal state
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
itself is not exactly symmetric, by Eq. (

Step three:
We now upper bound the fidelity between a thermal state
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}

T_{0}
and an arbitrary SRE state

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}},\sigma_{\text{SRE}})\leq F\left(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}),\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{\text{SRE}})\right).

Since

In particular, there exists a SRE state of the form
\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}=\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{\text{SRE}})\otimes I

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}},\sigma_{\text{SRE}})\leq F\left(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}),\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}\right).

(12)

This then implies that

\displaystyle F(\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})\leq 1-\left[1-\sqrt{F(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3})}-\sqrt{1-F(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}))}\right]^{2}.

Conclusions –
We have demonstrated the existence of finite-temperature quantum topological order in three dimensions, exemplified by the
fTC
below a temperature
{T_{0}=2\ln(5)^{-1}}
. The crucial property of the
fTC
, which enforces long-range entanglement at nonzero temperature, is that it posses an anomalous 2-form symmetry. This is in sharp contrast to the bTC, which has been the focus of previous investigations into finite-temperature topological order in three dimensions
Castelnovo and Chamon 2008
;
Hastings 2011
;
Lu et al. 2020
.

Our argument used that
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
at low temperatures can be related to the loop-less state
\rho_{\varnothing}
by a QLC. We conjecture that the opposite is also true, and there exists a QLC mapping
\rho_{\varnothing}
back to
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
. This would imply that the two belong to the same mixed-state phase of matter, by the definitions of Refs.
Sang et al. 2024
;
Coser and Pérez-García 2019
. Furthermore, due to arguments similar to Refs.
Wang et al. 2025
;
Sohal and Prem 2025
;
Ellison and Cheng 2025
, we expect
\rho_{\varnothing}
to be inequivalent to any pure state topological order. This would then suggest that the finite-temperature
fTC
order that we have identified is a quantum phase of matter that exists
only
at nonzero temperature, or in open systems, as a mixed-state topological order
Fan et al. 2024
;
Bao et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Sohal and Prem 2025
;
Ellison and Cheng 2025
;
Coser and Pérez-García 2019
;
Li and Mong 2024
;
Sang and Hsieh 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Ogata 2025
.

We expect that further examples of finite-temperature topological orders can be found by generalizing the
fTC
to other models with anomalous 2-form symmetries. A large class of examples are given by Walker-Wang models based on premodular categories with a transparent fermion
Walker and Wang 2011
;
Deligne 2002
;
Ellison and Cheng 2025
.
At an intuitive level, these are discrete
G
gauge theories obtained by gauging the symmetry of a system with physical fermions.

The existence of finite-temperature topological order in 3D opens up a number of interesting questions. First, is the long-range entanglement exhibited by the
fTC
thermal states, and thus the topological order, stable to perturbations of the Hamiltonian? Our arguments used the fact that the
fTC
Hamiltonian is 2-form symmetric. For a perturbed Hamiltonian, however, the 2-form symmetry is in general emergent, i.e., the system is only guaranteed to have an anomalous 2-form symmetry in its ground-state subspace. Can QLCs be developed to clean up the loop excitations in this case, to (approximately) restore the anomalous 2-form symmetry of the ground state?

Another pressing question is: what diagnostics can be used to detect the topological order of the
fTC
at nonzero temperature? In principle, one could use a QLC to clean up the loop excitations, then measure the anomalous 2-form symmetry operators in the loop-less state.
Alternatively, it is natural to expect that the long-range entanglement can be detected by computing the topological entanglement negativity, analogous to the 4D toric code in Ref.
Lu et al. 2020
. We leave the search for efficiently computable diagnostics of the finite-temperature topological order to future explorations.

Furthermore, it would be interesting to identify physical systems able to realize the finite-temperature topological order of the
fTC
. The Kitaev model in Ref.
Mandal and Surendran 2009
may be particularly amenable to this pursuit, due to it natively requiring only 2-body interactions. Similar Kitaev-like 2-body interactions have been proposed for neutral atom systems through Floquet engineering in Ref.
Kalinowski et al. 2023
.

Sweep decoder

In the main text, we argued that loop excitations of length
\ln(L)^{2}
can be removed with the QLC
\mathcal{C}
. This channel is comprised of unitaries
U_{\mathcal{L}_{b}}
whose supports have linear size
\ln(L)^{2}
, as written in Eq. (
5
). It may be convenient to instead clean up the loop excitations using a sequence of constant-depth channels. This would only require strictly local operations. However, cleaning up all loops of length
\ln(L)^{2}
will necessitate a circuit of depth
O(\ln(L)^{2})
.

Such a strictly local channel is given by the sweep decoder of Ref.
Kubica and Preskill 2019
. Broadly speaking, the sweep decoder cleans up loop excitations by incrementally sweeping them in a predetermined direction. We take this direction to be the
(1,1,1)
-direction in the cubic lattice, for simplicity. This is referred to as the causal direction, following Ref.
Kubica and Preskill 2019
.

To specify how the loop excitations are swept in the causal direction, it is convenient to work on the dual lattice. The loop excitations then live on the edges of the dual lattice. For a given loop excitation, we define
\mathsf{L}
to be the set of edges and vertices (in the dual lattice) along the path of the excitation.
For any vertex
{\bar{v}}
in the dual lattice, we further define
\mathsf{L}|_{\bar{v}}
to be the edges in
\mathsf{L}
that are connected to
{\bar{v}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}|_{\bar{v}}=\{{\bar{e}}\in\mathsf{L}\,:\,{\bar{v}}\in{\bar{e}}\}.

(1)

We say that a vertex
{\bar{v}}
is trailing if it belongs to some loop excitation
\mathsf{L}
and all of the edges
{\bar{e}}\in\mathsf{L}|_{\bar{v}}
are in the positive causal direction from
{\bar{v}}
, i.e., in the positive
x
,
y
, or
z
directions from
{\bar{v}}
. Importantly, any of the vertices of a loop excitation
\mathsf{L}
that are furthest back in the causal direction are trailing vertices. There may of course be other vertices in
\mathsf{L}
that are trailing yet are not the vertices that are furthest back in the causal direction.

The way the sweep decoder works is that it identifies the trailing vertices and applies local operators to push those vertices in the causal direction. For example, for the
fTC
, suppose that the vertex
{\bar{v}}
in some loop excitation
\mathsf{L}
is trailing. The two edges in
\mathsf{L}|_{\bar{v}}
share a neighboring plaquette
{\bar{p}}
in the dual lattice. Thus, the sweep decoder applies a Pauli
X
operator on the edge
e
dual to the plaquette
{\bar{p}}
. This moves the excitation in the causal direction across the plaquette
{\bar{p}}
.

A single round of the sweep decoder can be implemented by a constant-depth channel. Explicitly, for every
B_{p}
term, we introduce an ancilla in the
|0\rangle
state. We then apply local controlled gates to flip the ancilla depending on the eigenvalue of their associated
B_{p}
term. In effect, this copies the configurations of the loop excitations onto the ancilla. Next, we apply controlled gates that act with a Pauli
X
operator on an edge
e
to move the loop excitation only if the vertex
{\bar{v}}
is a trailing vertex. Note that, whether a vertex is trailing or not can be determined locally – we do not need information about the global loop configuration. Finally, we trace out the ancilla.

A loop excitation of length
\ell
can be removed with
O(\ell)
applications of the sweep decoder. To see this, we first note that any loop of length
\ell
can definitely be contained within a box of dimensions
\ell\times\ell\times\ell
. Given a loop excitation
\mathsf{L}
of length
\ell
, suppose we have chosen an
\ell\times\ell\times\ell
box that contains
\mathsf{L}
. We then denote the vertex of the box that is furthest back in the causal direction by
{\bar{v}}_{0}
. Every other vertex in the box is in a positive causal direction from
{\bar{v}}_{0}
.

After each application of the sweep decoder, the trailing vertices are moved further from
{\bar{v}}_{0}
. More precisely, letting
\bar{\mathsf{V}}^{(t)}
be the set of trailing vertices of the loop excitation after
t
applications of the sweep decoder, we have:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bar{v}}\in\bar{\mathsf{V}}^{(t+1)}}\left\{\text{dist}({\bar{v}}_{0},{\bar{v}})\right\}-\min_{{\bar{v}}\in\bar{\mathsf{V}}^{(t)}}\{\text{dist}({\bar{v}}_{0},{\bar{v}})\}\geq 1,

(2)

where
\text{dist}({\bar{v}}_{0},{\bar{v}})
is the taxicab distance between
{\bar{v}}_{0}
and
{\bar{v}}
.
This tells us that, in particular, the trailing vertices closest to
{\bar{v}}_{0}
are moved away from
{\bar{v}}_{0}
by at least one unit. Furthermore, the taxicab distance between
{\bar{v}}_{0}
and the furthest forward vertex in the box is
3\ell
. Therefore, it takes at most
3\ell
applications of the sweep decoder to clean up the loop of length
\ell
. Consequently, loops of length
\ln(L)^{2}
can be removed with
3\ln(L)^{2}
applications of the sweep decoder, implying that the sweep decoder provides another means of cleaning up loops less than length
\ln(L)^{2}
with a QLC.

.2
Approximating the loop-less state
\rho_{\varnothing}

To remove the small loop excitations from
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
, we applied the QLC
\mathcal{C}
. Here, we show that, at low temperatures, the state
\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}})
is a good approximation to the loop-less state
\rho_{\varnothing}
. Specifically, we show that

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}),\rho_{\varnothing})\geq 1-L^{3}e^{-(2\beta-\ln(5))\ln(L)^{2}},

(3)

as claimed in Eq. (
6
).

We start by writing
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
and
\rho_{\varnothing}
explicitly in the
fTC
eigenbasis. We label the eigenstates as
|\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}\rangle
, where
\mathcal{L}
denotes a configuration of loop excitations, and
\mathcal{V}
labels both a configuration of the fermionic excitations and the logical states. In this basis, the thermal state of the
fTC
is:

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{V}}}|\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}|.

(4)

Here, the exponents
E_{\mathcal{L}}
and
E_{\mathcal{V}}
denote the energy of the loop excitations and point-like excitations, respectively, in the configuration
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{V}
. We have also introduced the notation
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{\mathcal{L}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}},\quad\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}=\sum_{\mathcal{V}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{V}}}.

(5)

The loop-less state is then

\displaystyle\rho_{\varnothing}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{\mathcal{V}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{V}}}|\varnothing,\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\varnothing,\mathcal{V}|,

(6)

with
\varnothing
denoting the configuration without any loop excitations.

We next apply the loop-cleaning channel
\mathcal{C}
to
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
. We let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
label the configuration obtained from
\mathcal{L}
after acting with
\mathcal{C}
. The loop-cleaning channel then acts on the eigenstates of the
fTC
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(|\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}|)=\begin{cases}|\varnothing,\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\varnothing,\mathcal{V}|,&\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}\\
|\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}|,&\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing},\end{cases}

(7)

where
\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}
denotes the set of loop configurations that are fully cleaned up by
\mathcal{C}
. Applying
\mathcal{C}
to
\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}
produces

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}\sum_{\mathcal{V}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{V}}}|\varnothing,\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\varnothing,\mathcal{V}|+\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}\sum_{\mathcal{V}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{V}}}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}|.\end{split}

(8)

Notice that the states in the first sum do not depend on
\mathcal{L}
, therefore we can perform the sum over
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}
. This gives us

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}})=\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing})\rho_{\varnothing}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}\sum_{\mathcal{V}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{V}}}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}\rangle\langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}|.\end{split}

(9)

Here,
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing})
is the probability that a loop configuration is cleaned up by
\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}.

(10)

We are now prepared to compute the fidelity between
\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}})
and
\rho_{\varnothing}
explicitly. Since both states are diagonal in the
fTC
eigenbasis, the fidelity simplifies to

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}),\rho_{\varnothing})=\Tr\left[\left(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}})\rho_{\varnothing}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}.

(11)

Using that the states
|\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{V}\rangle
, for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}
, are orthogonal to states of the form
|\varnothing,\mathcal{V}\rangle
in
\rho_{\varnothing}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}),\rho_{\varnothing})=\Tr\left[\left(\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing})\rho_{\varnothing}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}=\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing})\Tr[\rho_{\varnothing}]^{2}=\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}).

(12)

The probability that a loop configuration can be cleaned up by
\mathcal{C}
can alternatively be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing})=1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}.

(13)

Therefore, the fidelity is

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\text{f\penalty\hskip 0.63779ptTC}}),\rho_{\varnothing})=1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}.

(14)

To evaluate the bound on the fidelity further, we define
\epsilon
to be the deviation from unity in Eq. (
14
):

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{L}}}.

(15)

We note that the calculation of
\epsilon
that follows, is similar to Lemma 4 in Ref.
Roberts et al. 2017
.
The first step is to replace the sum over configurations with a sum over energies of loop configurations. This gives us

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{E}\Omega_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}(E)e^{-\beta E},

(16)

where
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}(E)
is the multiplicity of configurations
\mathcal{L}
with energy
E_{\mathcal{L}}=E
such that, after applying
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
has at least one loop excitation.

The multiplicity
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}(E)
is challenging to compute in general. However, we can upper bound it using the fact that all loop excitations of length
\ln(L)^{2}
are cleaned up by
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, the configurations counted by
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}(E)
must have at least one loop whose length is larger than
\ln(L)^{2}
.
This observation allows us to conclude:

\displaystyle\Omega_{d}\Omega(E-2d)\geq\Omega_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}(E).

(17)

Here,
\Omega_{d}
is the number of configurations for a single loop of length
d=\ln(L)^{2}
and
\Omega(E-2d)
is the multiplicity of loop configurations of energy
E-2d
. We have subtracted by
2d
, since a loop of length
d
has energy penalty
2d
.

Plugging the inequality in Eq. (
17
) into the definition of
\epsilon
, we find:

\begin{split}\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{E\geq 2d}\Omega_{d}\Omega(E-2d)e^{-\beta E}.\end{split}

(18)

Note that we have changed the sum to be over energies
E\geq 2d
. This is because
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{S}_{\varnothing}}(E)=0
for energies that are less than
2d
.

Next, we shift the summand by
2d
and allow the sum to run over all energies of loop configurations. This gives us:

\displaystyle\epsilon<\Omega_{d}e^{-\beta 2d}\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{E}\Omega(E)e^{-\beta E}.

(19)

The sum is precisely
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}}
, so we find:

\displaystyle\epsilon<\Omega_{d}e^{-\beta 2d}.

(20)

The number of configurations
\Omega_{d}
for a loop of length
d
is upper-bounded by the number of random walks of length
d
. For a fixed starting point in the cubic lattice, the number of random walks is
5^{d}
, since at each step, there are 5 possible (non-backtracking) directions to choose. We can further choose any of the
L^{3}
sites to start the random walk, so
\Omega_{d}
is bounded as:

\displaystyle\Omega_{d}<L^{3}5^{d}.

(21)

Therefore, replacing
d
with
\ln(L)^{2}
, the bound on
\epsilon
becomes:

\displaystyle\epsilon<L^{3}e^{-(2\beta-\ln(5))\ln(L)^{2}},

(22)

which, along with Eq. (
14
), implies Eq. (
3
).

.3
Mapping SRE states to SRE states

Here, we argue that QLCs map SRE states to SRE states, up to tensoring with the identity on ancillary degrees of freedom
4
4

4

We thank Shengqi Sang for this observation.
. To see this, we first note that any QLC can be implemented through the following three operations: (i) tensoring with ancilla in a product state, (ii) applying a quasi-local circuit, and (iii) tracing out a subset of the ancilla. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a QLC and
\sigma_{\text{SRE}}
be an arbitrary SRE state, as in Eq. (
8
). Then, the action of
\mathcal{C}
on
\sigma_{\text{SRE}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{\text{SRE}})=\Tr_{A}\left[\sum_{i}p_{i}V|\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}|V^{\dagger}\right],

(23)

where a product state has been absorbed into
|\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}\rangle
,
V
is a quasi-local circuit, and the trace is over the subset of the ancilla
A
.

Suppose that, prior to tracing out the ancilla, we add depolarizing noise. As a reminder, for a single qubit, the depolarizing noise channel
\mathcal{N}
acts on an arbitrary mixed state
\rho
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\frac{1}{4}(\rho+X\rho X+Y\rho Y+Z\rho Z).

We define
\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}
as the state obtained after applying depolarizing noise:

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}=\frac{1}{4^{|A|}}\sum_{P_{A}}\sum_{i}p_{i}P_{A}V|\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}|V^{\dagger}P_{A}.

(24)

Here, the sum is over all Pauli operators on the ancilla, and
|A|
is the number of qubits in
A
.
As the notation suggests,
\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}
is a SRE mixed state, since each of the pure states
P_{A}V|\psi_{\text{SRE}}^{i}\rangle
is SRE.

Note that, due to the depolarizing noise,
\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}
commutes with every Pauli operator on
A
. Therefore, it must be the identity on
A
and take the form:
\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}=\rho_{\bar{A}}\otimes I_{A}
, for some mixed state
\rho_{\bar{A}}
on the complement of
A
. By tracing over
A
, we find:

\displaystyle\rho_{\bar{A}}=\Tr_{A}\left[\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}\right]=\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{\text{SRE}}).

(25)

In the second equality, we used that the depolarizing noise on
A
, prior to tracing out
A
, has no effect on the resulting state. Thus, tensoring
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{\text{SRE}})
with the identity on
A
gives
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{\text{SRE}})\otimes I_{A}=\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{SRE}}
, which is SRE. This shows that, as claimed, QLCs map SRE states to SRE states, up to tensoring with the identity.
