Prevalence and associated factors of intestinal parasitic infections in the school children of Sarlahi, Nepal
Authors: Merina Lama, Janak Raj Subedi, Rajendra Prasad Parajuli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA large proportion of the global population is affected by intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) caused by protozoa and helminths. These infections lead to intestinal inflammation, malabsorption, and nutritional deficiencies, ultimately impairing growth and child health. This study aims to fill the knowledge gap regarding the link between nutritional status and IPIs in children over the age of five. It investigates this relationship among schoolchildren aged 6 to 17 years attending public and private schools in the Sarlahi district of Nepal. Data collection included child and adolescent interviews, anthropometric measurements, and stool sample analysis. The microscopic examination was performed using direct wet mount and concentration techniques. The overall parasitic prevalence was 30.8% (74/239), among which more females (35.3%) were infected than male children (25.2%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06). The prevalence of parasites was found more in public school (35.8%) than private school (24.5%). Ascaris lumbricoides was the most prevalent with a prevalence of 25.0% followed by Giardia lamblia (5%), Entamoeba histolytica and E. coli (i.e., 1.7 to 1.3%, respectively). Helminth parasites were more frequent (i.e., 25.4%) than protozoan (i.e., 8.3%) parasites. Students from public schools had a significantly higher prevalence of G. lamblia infection and co-infections compared to those from private schools (p < 0.05). Barefooted Participants had significantly higher unadjusted odds of infection (cOR = 9.37; 95% CI: 1.03–85.35). Likewise, Janajati and Dalit participants showed increased odds of any IPIs (cOR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.01–4.34) compared to their reference groups. There was no significant association between IPIs and nutritional status. Despite ongoing mass deworming programs, the relatively high prevalence of IPIs among school-aged children remains concerning. Poor personal hygiene, inadequate sanitation, cohabitation with livestock, and the use of unboiled drinking water may contribute to persistent infections, particularly among females and children from Janajati and Dalit communities with lower socioeconomic status. Targeted school-based health education and hygiene promotion programs are warranted to reduce the infection burden and sustain control efforts.Keywords: Intestinal parasites, School children, Prevalence, Associated factors, Sarlahi, Nepal

Introduction

Approximately 24% of the global population (1.5 billion people) is affected by intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs), primarily caused by hookworms, Ascaris lumbricoides, and Trichuris trichiura. The highest prevalence is observed in sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas, China, and East Asia, with tropical and subtropical regions being the most commonly affected (Fong and Chan, 2022; Torgerson et al., 2015). Symptoms are observed in approximately 450 million individuals, with over 200,000 deaths reported each year (Hajare et al., 2021). IPIs are widespread globally, affecting 40.17% of school-age children (Bertoncello et al., 2017).

Schoolchildren are a vulnerable population due to their heightened physical activity levels and increased likelihood of coming into contact with surfaces contaminated by intestinal parasites (Richert et al., 2024). They are in a critical growth phase, yet remain highly susceptible to infections and immune deficiencies (Cunningham-Rundles and Lin, 2007). Intestinal parasites contribute to inflammation of the intestinal wall, which can lead to issues such as indigestion, malabsorption, diarrhea, and nutritional deficiencies, ultimately hindering growth and negatively impacting children's nutritional health (Erwin, 2023; Kesete et al., 2020; Twahirwa et al., 2024). Malnutrition, in turn, increases vulnerability to infections like diarrhea, intestinal worms, and weakened immune function (Kesete et al., 2020; Twahirwa et al., 2024). Children aged 5 to 19 years make up approximately 1.8 billion of the global population, with 90% residing in low- and middle-income countries. Despite representing 27% of the population in these regions, there is a limited number of studies focusing on the prevalence of IPIs and malnutrition (Galloway, 2017). Children in this age group also face a high burden of parasitic infections, which are closely linked to inadequate sanitation and poor hygiene practices (Gebretsadik et al., 2020; Gebru et al., 2023).

A high prevalence of IPIs is widely reported in Nepal, particularly among school-aged children (Dahal et al., 2022; Raut et al., 2021). IPIs prevalence has been decreasing since the adoption of the national deworming program initiated in 2004 (Kunwar et al., 2016a, Kunwar et al., 2016b). Yet, some earlier studies among ethnic groups still reported a high prevalence (Adhikari et al., 2021; Gautam et al., 2024, Gautam et al., 2025; Subedi et al., 2021). Undernutrition is also widespread in Nepal; one in three adolescents are stunted (height-for-age Z-score < −2 SD), one in four boys is undernourished (i.e., body mass index (BMI)-for-age Z-score < − 2 SD), and one in five adolescent girls has anemia (Ministry of Health and Population, 2018). Many earlier studies reported an inconsistent association between IPIs and different nutritional profiles/statuses (Loglo et al., 2024; Parajuli et al., 2024b, Parajuli et al., 2024a).

Ample of earlier studies in different populations and communities have suggested that a wide range of socioeconomic, demographic, behavioral, and lifestyle factors are linked to different types of IPIs in different geographic locations. For example, personal unhygienic behavior (Parajuli et al., 2024a; Parajuli et al., 2014; Parajuli et al., 2009; Parajuli et al., 2024b), including poor sanitation practices, (Chongbang et al., 2016; Choy et al., 2014; Fuhrimann et al., 2016; Khadka et al., 2021; Okeke and Ubachukwu, 2015), being a farmer (Okeke and Ubachukwu, 2015), source of drinking water (Khadka et al., 2021), specific occupational factors, such as being a laborer, and living in mud-constructed houses (Gyawali and Khanal, 2013), river bathing, and barefoot walking (Choy et al., 2014; Gyawali and Khanal, 2013; Rajoo et al., 2017), not undergoing deworming in the past 12 months (Fuhrimann et al., 2016), practices such as eating raw meat and inadequate handwashing after using the toilet or touching dirty materials (Solomon et al., 2018), having open pig farms (Ruang-areerate et al., 2021), lack of safe drinking water (Chin et al., 2016; Choy et al., 2014), low hemoglobin levels and body mass index (BMI) in adults (Wiria et al., 2013) were reported to be associated with IPIs. These findings highlight the complex nature of IPIs and emphasize the need to address multiple risk factors for effective prevention and control.

IPIs not only contribute to nutritional deficiencies, illness, and stunted growth (Ross et al., 2017), but also contribute to increased school absenteeism or reduced intelligence quotient (IQ) (Parajuli et al., 2024a), and heightened vulnerability to diseases such as tuberculosis, ultimately impacting productivity and the socioeconomic development of a country (Feleke, 2016). This study investigates the prevalence of IPIs and the association between risk factors for IPIs including the nutritional status (i.e., BMI as a proxy of nutritional indicators) of schoolchildren, in both private and public schools. The results aim to inform policymakers, NGOs, and health professionals in developing targeted interventions to enhance children's health, enabling them to achieve their full cognitive potential and better prepare for a competitive future and better quality of life.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted among school children studying in classes 1–10 in community school and private school in Sarlahi district, Lalbandi Municipality, Ward number 5 and 7 Nepal (Fig. 1). Sarlahi is located in the southeastern part of Nepal. According to the 2021 AD census, the Lalbandi municipality has a total area of 238.50 km2, is situated at an elevation ranging from 105 to 697 masl and is geographically located at 27°4′14″N 85°39′12″E with a population of 66,419 people [i.e., 3562 people in ward number 5 and 4465 people in ward number 7 (Central bureau of statistics (CBS), 2021)]. The region has a subtropical monsoon climate. Based on data from the Janakpur Climatic Station, the average maximum temperature is 31 °C, peaking at 42 °C, while the average minimum temperature is 20 °C, with a low of 4 °C. Around 80% of the annual 1699.6 mm rainfall occurs during the monsoon season (July–September).

Sample collection

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) (Ref. No. 143/080/081) of the Institute of Science and Technology (IOST). The study was conducted in the first week of August 2023 using a purposive selection process to identify representative communities and schools within the chosen wards. At the time of data collection in August 2023, the most recent national mass deworming campaign was conducted in April 2023 (Baisakh 2080), approximately four months earlier. These campaigns, organized biannually by the Ministry of Health and Population, are typically held in April and October across Nepal, including Province 2, with reported coverage rates exceeding 85% among children. No formal sample size calculation was undertaken, as this was an exploratory cross-sectional study intended to generate preliminary estimates of IPI prevalence and associated risk factors among school-aged children in the region. The sample size of 239 was determined based on logistical feasibility, expected school enrollment, and the time and resources available during the study period. Wards 5 and 7 of Lalbandi Municipality were purposively selected due to their demographic heterogeneity, inclusion of both public and private schools, and the cooperation of local school authorities. Although these wards may not fully capture the diversity of the entire Sarlahi district, they represent a relevant cross-section of its urban–rural continuum and socioeconomic variability. As such, they provide an informative contextual backdrop for assessing IPI patterns and for informing the design of larger-scale, district-wide studies in the future. Selection criteria included the number of enrolled students and the willingness of school authorities to participate. Using school records, each student was assigned a unique code, and 239 participants were randomly selected through a lottery system, comprising 133 from public schools and 106 from private schools. Selected students and their parents were informed about the study objectives and procedures. Consent was obtained through signed forms, including parental consent and participant assent for those under 18 years of age. Participants were provided with dry, screw-capped containers and instructed to collect approximately 5 g of stool from the beginning, middle, and end portions of the stool, ensuring no contamination with urine, water, or soil. The stool samples, collected the next morning, were mixed with 2.5% potassium dichromate solution, stored in sterile zip-lock bags with proper labeling, and transported to the Parasitology Laboratory at the Central Department of Zoology. The samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

Laboratory examination

Wet mount technique

Approximately 2 g of each fecal sample was thoroughly mixed with a few drops of normal saline or Gram's iodine. A drop of the mixture was placed on a glass slide and examined under a light microscope at 400× magnification, following the protocol outlined in earlier studies (Adhikari et al., 2021).

Saturated salt flotation technique

Approximately 1 mL of the filtrate was combined with 13 mL of sodium chloride (NaCl) in a 15-mL centrifuge tube. The mixture was centrifuged for 5 min, after which the tubes were placed in test tube stands. The concentrated NaCl solution was carefully added until a convex meniscus formed at the top of the tube, and a coverslip was positioned to contact the solution. After 15–20 min, the coverslip was gently removed, placed on a glass slide, and examined under a microscope for parasite diagnostic stages following standard protocols (Zajac and Conboy, 2012).

Formal ether sedimentation technique

A mixture of 1 mL of filtrate and 10 mL of 10% formal saline was prepared in a 15 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 3 mL of ether, which was thoroughly combined. The solution was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. Two drops of the sediment were then transferred onto a glass slide containing Lugol's iodine, and the parasitic stages were observed under a microscope (Zajac and Conboy, 2012). For quality assurance, duplicate slides were prepared and independently examined by two trained microscopists for each diagnostic technique. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by consultation with a senior parasitologist to ensure consistency and reproducibility of the microscopic findings. Due to the morphological similarity between Entamoeba histolytica and E. dispar, identification was based on conventional parasitological criteria following published guidelines (Zajac and Conboy, 2012). However, we acknowledge that definitive differentiation was not possible without molecular confirmation.

Questionnaire

A concise set of structured questionnaires was developed to collect comprehensive data on socioeconomic status (SES), sociodemographic variables (age, gender, ethnicity, family size, and parents' occupation), and associated behavioral risk factors. These factors included sanitation behaviors (e.g., hand-washing habits, nail trimming, and wearing shoes), types of drinking water consumed, and practices such as walking barefoot outdoors. The questionnaires were designed to capture essential information aligned with the study objectives. Data collection involved administering the questionnaires to students capable of answering independently, while younger children were assisted through interviews with their parents or guardians.

Anthropometric measurement

To assess the weight and height of the children, WHO-recommended methods were followed (De Onis and Habicht, 1996). Height was measured using a measuring tape, while weight was recorded with a weighing scale. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula: BMI = weight (kg) / (height in meters) 2. The calculated BMI below 18.5 values were defined as underweight among schoolchildren (World Health Organization, 2007).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel for analysis and preparing tables. The normality of the data was evaluated prior to descriptive or inferential statistical analysis. Parametric independent t-tests were used for continuous data (i.e., for normally distributed data) and nonparametric Chi-square tests were used for categorical data (i.e., for skewed data) for group differences. The chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was used as inferential statistics wherever appropriate. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression was conducted to obtain the crude odds ratio (cOR), and adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of IPIs prevalence. Multivariate logistic regression was conducted in two steps. In Model 2, only variables that showed significant associations in univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were included. In Model 3, two key demographic/contextual variables-gender and type of school-were included alongside the significant variables from Model 1, to allow for broader adjustment and to assess potential confounding. In all cases, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software package.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants, who were predominantly early adolescents with a mean age of 12.39 years. Among students in the same grade, those attending public schools were significantly older than their private school counterparts (p = 0.015). Adolescents enrolled in private schools were more likely to come from educated families with non-farming occupations, whereas most public-school students had parents engaged in agriculture. Private school students were taller and heavier than those from public schools; however, body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly between the groups. A greater proportion of public-school students reported living in mud-constructed houses compared to private school students. Overall, more than three-quarters of the participants lived in mud-built homes and belonged to Janajati or Dalit ethnic groups, while over two-thirds reported living in nuclear families.

Table 2 presents the behavioral and lifestyle characteristics of the study participants. There were no significant differences between public and private school students in most behavioral and lifestyle variables, including recent deworming, abdominal discomfort, raw meat consumption, morbidity status, source of drinking water, nail-biting, use of soap for handwashing, and dietary type (vegetarian or non-vegetarian). However, more participants from public schools compared to private schools reported suboptimal hygiene practices, such as walking barefoot, eating fruits and vegetables without washing, trimming nails less frequently, drinking unboiled water, and co-living with livestock (p < 0.05). Overall, most participants reported consuming a non-vegetarian diet and practicing generally good hygiene-washing fruits and vegetables before eating, avoiding barefoot walking, using soap for handwashing before meals, using tap water as the main source of drinking water, and avoiding raw or undercooked meat. More than two-thirds of participants trimmed their nails regularly, refrained from nail-biting, and did not play in soil. Yet, a large proportion did not boil their drinking water and had not taken anthelmintic medication in the past six months. Approximately half of the participants reported living in households with livestock, and about one-quarter did not trim their nails regularly and reported nail-biting habits.

Table 3 shows the prevalence of IPIs among the study participants. About one third of the samples (i.e., 74; 30.8%) found shedding one or more species of IPIs (Table 3). Of the five species of parasites identified, three were protozoa (i.e., Entamoeba histolytica, Giardia lamblia and Entamoeba coli) and two were helminths (i.e., Ascaris lumbricoids and Hymenolepis nana) (Fig. 2). Overall, A. lumbricoids indicated highest prevalence (25.0%) followed by G. lamblia (5%), and E. histolytica and E. coli (i.e., 1.7 to 1.3%, respectively). Helminth parasites were more frequent (i.e., 25.4%) than protozoan (i.e., 8.3%) parasites. Although female participants showed a higher overall prevalence of all types of IPIs, none of the individual parasite types or categories demonstrated a statistically significant difference between males and females. However, students from public schools had a significantly higher prevalence of G. lamblia infection and co-infections compared to those from private schools (p < 0.05). Overall, the prevalence of most IPIs categories-including any IPIs, any helminth, and any protozoan infection-was higher among public school students than among private school students, although these differences were only marginally significant.

Table 4 presents the associations between any IPIs and contextual behavioral, nutritional and lifestyle factors. The prevalence of any parasitic infection was higher among barefooted participants compared to those who consistently used footwear, and among individuals belonging to Janajati and Dalit ethnic groups (p < 0.01). Bivariate analysis showed that barefooted participants had significantly greater odds of infection, as reflected by the crude odds ratio (cOR = 9.37; 95% CI: 1.03 to 85.35). Similarly, higher infection rates (p < 0.05) and odds of any IPIs (cOR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.01 to 4.34) were observed among Janajati and Dalit participants compared to their reference groups. However, these associations that were significant in the bivariate analysis were no longer significant after adjustment for covariates in the multivariable models. In both, the partially adjusted (Model 2) and fully adjusted (Model 3) logistic regression analyses, the odds of IPIs remained higher-but statistically non-significant-among those who walked barefoot and among Janajati and Dalit participants compared with their counterparts. Female students and those attending public schools also exhibited consistently higher odds of infection, although these associations did not reach statistical significance in either the bivariate or multivariable analyses.

Discussion

This study revealed a moderately high prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections among school-aged children in Sarlahi, Nepal, despite nationwide deworming efforts. Helminth infections, particularly A. lumbricoides, were predominant. The burden was disproportionately higher among females, public-school students, and children from the Janajati and Dalit ethnic groups. Behavioral and environmental factors such as walking barefoot and suboptimal sanitation appeared to drive infection risk, whereas no significant association was observed between parasitic infection and nutritional status.

The overall prevalence of IPIs among schoolchildren in this study was 30.8%, which is lower than the 51.1% reported among schoolchildren in the Surkhet, Achham, and Dailekh districts of Nepal (Shrestha et al., 2020), 39.7% reported among schoolchildren in the Dolakha and Ramechhap districts, of Nepal (Shrestha et al., 2018), and 61.7% reported among schoolchildren from Bahir Dar City of Ethiopia (Feleke, 2016). However, it was higher than the 18.7% observed among schoolchildren in Devdaha Municipality, Rupandehi (Subedi et al., 2020), 19.9% among schoolchildren in Kathmandu (Shrestha et al., 2019), and 23.6% among schoolchildren in Buner District, Pakistan (Khan et al., 2022). The prevalence in our study was comparable to the prevalence (i.e., 31.7%) reported among schoolchildren in Kalaiya, Bara District (Regmi et al., 2014), 27.7% prevalence among schoolchildren in Bhaktapur district (Shrestha and Maharjan, 2013) and 33% prevalence observed among school children of Saptari district, Nepal (Gupta et al., 2020). Recent studies across three Nepalese communities-western hilly region (22%), the Butwal squatter settlement (28.8%), and the Badi community in western Nepal (27%)-report overall IPI prevalence ranging from 22% to 28.8%, indicating a consistently high burden across diverse settings (Bhandari et al., 2025; Gautam et al., 2024, Gautam et al., 2025). Differences in prevalence across studies may reflect variations in geographic settings, sanitation conditions, and diagnostic methodologies. In our study, a considerable proportion of children did not boil drinking water, had not taken anthelmintic medication in the past six months, and lived in households with livestock. About one-quarter did not trim their nails regularly and reported nail-biting habits. Moreover, a greater proportion of public-school students lived in mud-constructed houses, and over three-quarters of participants belonged to Janajati or Dalit ethnic groups, indicating lower socioeconomic status. These disparities in hygiene practices and living conditions likely contributed to the relatively high prevalence observed in specific underprivileged cluster of populations. Because data were collected in August during Nepal's summer monsoon season, the elevated prevalence observed may reflect seasonal patterns driven by high rainfall, poor drainage, and increased environmental contamination. Seasonal variation should be considered when interpreting these results. Further studies are needed to confirm these associations and clarify the underlying socioeconomic determinants of infection.

The present study found a markedly higher prevalence of G. lamblia and co-infections among children attending government schools compared to those in private schools. This finding is consistent with earlier studies conducted in Nepal. Mishra et al. (2020) reported infection rates of 71% among government school students and 29% among private school students in Lalitpur, Nepal (Mishra et al., 2020). Similarly, Shrestha et al. (2019) observed higher infection rates in public school children (26.1%) than in private school children (12.1%) in Kathmandu, Nepal (Shrestha et al., 2019), while Subedi et al. (2020) also documented a greater prevalence in public schools (22.7%) compared to private schools (14.7%) among schoolchildren in the Devdaha Municipality of Rupandehi district (Subedi et al., 2020). The persistently higher infection rates in public schools-including overall IPIs, helminthic, and protozoal infections-may reflect poorer living conditions linked to lower socioeconomic status, inadequate sanitation, poor hygiene practices, and limited health awareness among public school students, many of whom reported residing in mud-constructed houses compared to those in private schools.

The present study showed a borderline higher prevalence of infections among schoolgirls compared to boys. Similar observations were made in the Dadeldhura district of Nepal, where Rijal (2013) reported slightly higher infection rates among girls (32.2%) than boys (30.4%) (Rijal, 2013). In contrast, Tandukar et al. (2013) in Lalitpur found a higher prevalence among males (17.8%) than females (15.5%) (Tandukar et al., 2013). Likewise, Mishra et al. (2020) noted that males (57%) were more frequently infected than females (43%) (Mishra et al., 2020). Globally, the findings vary. For instance, Alli et al. (2013) in Ota, Ogun State, Nigeria, observed nearly equal infection rates between females (41.9%) and males (41.2%) (Alli et al., 2013), whereas Bakarman et al. (2019) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, reported a marked male predominance (87.1% versus 12.9% in females) (Bakarman et al., 2019). Similarly, Khan et al. (2022) found higher infection rates among males (54.5%) than females (45.5%) (Khan et al., 2022). The relatively higher prevalence among females in the current study may be linked to their greater participation in household chores and agricultural work specifically among Janajati and Dalits of the lower SES cluster, which increases their exposure to contaminated soil, water, and food sources.

The study found the highest prevalence of A. lumbricoides followed by G. lamblia, E. histolytica, and H. nana. Similar findings of high A. lumbricoides prevalence were reported in previous studies (Chukwu et al., 2023; Dahal et al., 2022; Jaiswal et al., 2014; Shrestha and Maharjan, 2013; Subedi et al., 2020). The dominance of A. lumbricoides may result from its eggs' ability to survive on land for over a decade, coupled with high egg-laying capacity (200,000–250,000 per worm) and poor hygiene and sanitation (Subedi et al., 2020). The prevalence of G. lamblia could be attributed to water contamination by cysts (Sharma et al., 2020). Although Nepal conducts biannual deworming campaigns, the self-reported coverage among our participants was only 20.8% within the past six months. This discrepancy may reflect recall bias, age-related gaps in coverage (as most official reports focus on children under five), or limitations in access for older children. Nonetheless, the persistence of IPIs in a setting with routine deworming underscores the need to integrate hygiene education and sanitation improvements alongside chemoprevention efforts.

In the present study, a comprehensive range of potential behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic risk factors were evaluated for their association with IPIs. Among these, walking barefoot and belonging to Janajati or Dalit ethnic groups were found to increase the odds of overall IPIs in the crude models. However, these associations lost statistical significance after adjustment for confounders, although the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) remained elevated. This attenuation of associations may be explained by the relatively small sample size, low overall prevalence of IPIs, or the widespread distribution of risky lifestyle and behavioral factors across participants, which could have diluted potential group-level differences. The very wide confidence intervals for some associations (e.g., barefoot walking) also reflect sparse data within specific subgroups, limiting the statistical precision of those estimates. Interestingly, a higher proportion of students residing in mud-constructed houses-a proxy indicator of lower socioeconomic status-were enrolled in public schools. Female students, those attending government schools, and schoolchildren from Janajati and Dalit communities appeared to have a greater burden of infections compared to their counterparts. These observations reinforce the influence of social and structural inequalities on infection risk, where poverty, inadequate sanitation, and limited access to hygiene facilities collectively contribute to higher disease transmission. Furthermore, several previously recognized determinants of IPIs were identified in our analysis, including having farmer parents, consuming unwashed raw vegetables, walking barefoot, not trimming or frequently biting nails, playing in contaminated soil, drinking unboiled or unsafe water, and not receiving anthelminthic medication in the past six months. Additional risk factors included cohabiting with livestock and residing in mud houses, all of which were associated with elevated infection odds both before and after adjustment(Bhandari et al., 2025; Gautam et al., 2024, Gautam et al., 2025; Parajuli et al., 2024a; Parajuli et al., 2014; Parajuli et al., 2009; Parajuli et al., 2024b). Children studying in public schools and those with BMI <18.5 (underweight) also showed greater susceptibility to IPIs, highlighting the interplay between nutritional status and infection risk. Our previous studies have similarly identified walking barefoot as a significant risk factor for IPIs, supporting the notion that direct skin contact with contaminated soil serves as a key transmission pathway (Parajuli et al., 2009). This aligns with observations from Pakistan (Khan et al., 2022) and observations among children from five schools in Bhairahawa, Nepal (Raut et al., 2021), who also documented the detrimental impact of inadequate footwear and poor sanitation on the persistence of parasitic infections among school-aged children. Overall, the results emphasize that interventions addressing hygiene behavior, sanitation infrastructure, and socioeconomic disparities remain critical to reducing the burden of IPIs in endemic communities. Some behavioral data (e.g., deworming history, hygiene practices) relied on self-reports and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias, particularly among younger children. This limitation should be considered when interpreting associations with behavioral risk factors.

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between potential risk factors and intestinal parasitic infections, as both exposure and outcome were assessed at a single point in time. Second, the study represents a point prevalence estimate, although it considered a broad range of behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic factors to provide contextual understanding of infection dynamics. Third, the absence of molecular or advanced diagnostic methods may have led to underestimation of mixed or low-intensity infections, particularly for morphologically similar protozoa. Another limitation is the potential misidentification of E. histolytica due to the inability to distinguish it reliably from E. dispar in microscopy-based analysis, which may have led to overestimation of true pathogenic infections. While BMI-for-age Z-scores are recommended for assessing nutritional status in children, the adult cutoff of BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was used in this study due to its simplicity, established relevance in adolescent populations in South Asia, and practical challenges in standardizing Z-scores across a broad age range in field settings. Due to logistical and resource constraints, we did not quantify the intensity of parasitic infections. Future studies incorporating egg count methods (e.g., Kato-Katz) are warranted to assess the burden of infection more precisely. Finally, the relatively modest sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect some associations; however, efforts were made to ensure random selection of participants to enhance representativeness of the target population.

Conclusions

This study revealed that intestinal parasitic infections remain a notable concern among school-aged children in Sarlahi, Nepal, despite long-standing deworming programes. The 30.8% prevalence, dominated by Ascaris lumbricoides, points to persistent transmission linked to socioeconomic disparities and hygiene behaviors. Higher infection rates among females, Janajati and Dalit groups, and public-school students indicate that infection risk is shaped by inequities in living conditions, sanitation, and health awareness. Although barefoot walking and poor water and hygiene practices were strong predictors of infection, no significant association was found between IPIs and nutritional status. Strengthening school-based health education, safe water access, and hygiene promotion alongside routine deworming could substantially reduce the infection burden. Future longitudinal studies and policy efforts should focus on addressing environmental and social determinants to achieve sustained control and eventual elimination of intestinal parasitic infections in Nepal.
