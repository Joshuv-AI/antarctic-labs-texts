Serum cytokine analysis in cavitary and non-cavitary pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex disease: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Keita Takeda, Maho Suzukawa, Yuko Matsumoto, Yume Nakamura, Eri Nakano, Masato Watanabe, Yasuhiro Morimoto, Takafumi Kato, Osamu Narumoto, Masahiro Kawashima, Junko Suzuki, Atsuhisa Tamura, Hideaki Nagai, Yoshiteru Morio, Hirotoshi Matsui, Yuka Sasaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) disease, serum cytokine profiles may offer insights into the immune responses underlying cavitary lesion development. We aimed to compare serum cytokine profiles between patients with cavitary and those with non-cavitary nodular bronchiectasis (NB) and to identify cytokines potentially associated with cavitary lung lesions.MethodsThis single-center, cross-sectional study included patients with pulmonary MAC disease presenting with NB, categorized into the cavitary and non-cavitary groups. The serum levels of 34 exploratory cytokines were measured using the MAGPIX system and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. Intergroup comparisons were subsequently performed.ResultsNine and 45 patients with cavitary and non-cavitary NB MAC, respectively, were included. Significantly higher levels of interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-8/C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (inflammatory markers), matrix metalloproteinase-8 and chitinase-3-like protein 1 (tissue remodeling factors), and IL-17E/IL-25 and suppression of tumorigenicity 2/IL-33R (T-helper type 2-related cytokines) were observed in the cavitary group. No significant differences were identified for the remaining cytokines.ConclusionsCavitary lung lesions in pulmonary MAC disease are associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers, tissue remodeling factors, and T-helper type 2 related cytokines. Future research should investigate whether these cytokine differences contribute to cavity formation or occur as a consequence, and explore their potential as therapeutic targets.Keywords: Cytokine profile, Inflammation, Lung cavity, Mycobacterium intracellulare, Non-tuberculous mycobacteria

Background

Non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) lung disease has been increasingly recognized as a significant respiratory condition due to its rising incidence and substantial clinical impact [1, 2]. The Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) represents the primary causative agent of pulmonary NTM disease [3]. Clinically, pulmonary NTM disease is categorized into two major types: fibrocavitary (FC) and nodular bronchiectasis (NB). The FC type is generally associated with poor treatment outcomes and an unfavorable prognosis [4, 5]. Within the NB type, the disease is further classified as cavitary or non-cavitary, with the cavitary form typically associated with a poorer prognosis compared with the non-cavitary form [4, 5]. Accordingly, the presence or absence of lung cavitary lesions plays a crucial role in the clinical management of pulmonary NTM disease [3].

The differences in clinical presentation between these subtypes are presumed to result from variations in host immune responses [6, 7]. The elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in FC, along with the potential involvement of soluble programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) in cavitary NB [6, 7], suggest a link between cavitary lung lesions and immune system activity. Other cytokines, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and T-helper type 2 (Th2) cytokines, have also been reported to contribute to the pathogenesis of MAC disease and the destruction of pulmonary structures [8–13]. Multiple cytokines are likely involved in the formation of cavitary lesions. However, only a few studies have comprehensively evaluated immune cytokines, leaving this relationship largely unexplored.

Cytokine profiles may serve as biomarkers for differentiating clinical subtypes of pulmonary NTM disease and as key indicators of underlying disease mechanisms associated with cavitary lung lesions. In this exploratory study, serum cytokine profiles were compared between the cavitary and non-cavitary NB groups to identify cytokines potentially associated with cavitary lung lesions in patients with pulmonary NTM disease.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, cross-sectional study included patients with NB-type pulmonary MAC disease who presented to the National Hospital Organization, Tokyo National Hospital between June 2023 and September 2024. Inclusion criteria were (1) diagnosis of pulmonary MAC disease according to the American Thoracic Society, European Respiratory Society, European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases, and Infectious Diseases Society of America diagnostic criteria [3]; (2) no receipt of multidrug combination therapy for pulmonary mycobacterial disease within the previous year; and (3) age ≥ 18 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) human immunodeficiency virus infection; (2) autoimmune diseases; (3) use of steroids, immunosuppressants, or biologics; (4) malignancies; or (5) pregnancy, possible pregnancy, or breastfeeding.

Patient characteristics, such as age, sex, comorbidities, smoking history, and laboratory data, were extracted from electronic medical records. Based on high-resolution chest tomography findings obtained within 1 month of blood collection, patients were classified into two groups: (1) those with pulmonary cavitary lesions (cavitary NB MAC group) and (2) those without pulmonary cavitary lesions (non-cavitary NB MAC group).

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Hospital Organization Tokyo National Hospital (approval no. 230002; approval date: April 26, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cytokine multiplex assay

Blood samples were collected at the time of enrollment, and serum samples were stored at − 80 °C until cytokine analysis. The cytokine levels were measured using the MAGPIX System (Luminex, Austin, TX, USA) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples for the cytokine measurement were processed and analyzed in a single batch to minimize inter-batch variability. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for subsequent analysis.

The cytokines analyzed in this study were selected based on the previous literature [6, 7] and an exploratory multiplex panel. The following cytokines were included: interleukin (IL)-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra)/IL-1F3, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8/C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) 8, IL-12/p70, IL-13, IL-17E/IL-25, IL-18/IL-1F4, and IL-33; suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2)/IL-33 receptor (IL-33R); tumor necrosis factor-α; interferon-gamma (IFN)-γ; CXCL10/IFN-γ-induced protein 10/cytokine responsive gene-2; matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP)-1, MMP-2, MMP-3, MMP-8, MMP-12, chemokine (C-C motif) ligands (CCLs) CCL2/JE/monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, CCL3/macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α), CCL4/MIP-1β, CCL5/regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted, CCL11/eotaxin, and CCL17/thymus- and activation-regulated chemokine; leptin/OB; platelet-derived growth factor-BB; periostin/osteoblast-specific factor-2; chitinase-3-like protein 1; tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1; transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1); and PD-1. A summary of these cytokines is presented in Table 1. IL-12/p40, TGF-β1, and PD-1 levels were measured using ELISA, while the remaining cytokines were assessed using the MAGPIX system.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, whereas continuous variables were compared between the cavitary and non-cavitary NB MAC groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 13 patients with cavitary NB MAC and 45 patients with non-cavitary NB MAC were included in this study. Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) was significantly more prevalent in the cavitary NB MAC group than in the non-cavitary group (30.8% vs. 0%, p = 0.0017). As CPA were reported to affect pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [14], four patients with CPA were excluded from further analysis. Table 2 presents a summary of the baseline characteristics of both groups. No significant differences were observed in age, sex, or smoking history between the two groups. Laboratory findings revealed that the cavitary NB MAC group had higher C-reactive protein levels and neutrophil counts and lower lymphocyte counts compared with the non-cavitary group.

Discussion

In this study, cytokine production profiles were comprehensively examined in patients with cavitary and non-cavitary NB MAC. Significant differences were identified in the levels of several cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8/CXCL8, IL-17E/IL-25, MMP-8, ST2/IL-33R, and chitinase-3-like protein 1.

In the cavitary NB MAC group, elevated levels of IL-6 and IL-8/CXCL8 suggest increased inflammatory activity. IL-6 is known to be upregulated during mycobacterial infections [15, 16]. It facilitates the recruitment of immune cells by stimulating endothelial cells and leukocytes to secrete chemokines (e.g., IL-8/CXCL8) and growth factors such as granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, thereby sustaining neutrophil-driven inflammation [17]. IL-8, a potent neutrophil chemoattractant, has been associated with the progression of pulmonary infections [18]. Its elevated concentration may reflect an exaggerated neutrophilic response within the lung parenchyma, potentially contributing to tissue destruction and cavity formation. By contrast, no significant differences were observed in the levels of other inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and CXCL10, between the two groups. Notably, previous studies have identified CXCL10 as an inflammatory marker in pulmonary MAC disease [6]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in disease classification, as previous studies have compared FC and NB types, whereas the present study focused on comparisons between cavitary and non-cavitary subtypes within the NB classification. Additionally, differences in study populations or experimental conditions may have contributed to this discrepancy. Overall, the findings suggest that inflammation is associated with cavity lung lesions in patients with pulmonary MAC disease; however, variations in disease phenotype may result in variability in the profile or magnitude of inflammatory marker expression.

Levels of cytokines involved in tissue remodeling, such as MMP-8 and chitinase-3-like protein 1, were elevated in the cavitary NB MAC group compared with those in the non-cavitary NB MAC group. MMP-8 has been suggested to be released through neutrophil extracellular trap formation during MAC infection, thereby amplifying inflammation and promoting tissue destruction [8]. No differences were observed in the levels of MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-3, or MMP-12, despite previous reports of their association with mycobacterial infections [9–12]. Elevated levels of chitinase-3-like protein 1 may indicate ongoing chronic inflammation and impaired tissue repair [18], which could contribute to the progression of cavitary lesions. The observed variability in tissue remodeling cytokines may reflect underlying differences in the mechanisms of cavity formation in pulmonary MAC disease.

The levels of IL-17E/IL-25 and ST2/IL-33R, but not of other Th2 cytokines (such as IL-4, IL-5, or IL-13), were elevated in the cavitary NB MAC group. The extent to which IL-17E/IL-25 levels are increased in NTM disease remains controversial [19, 20]. Although Th2-type inflammation has been associated with pulmonary MAC infection [13], the specific role of individual Th2 cytokines in MAC infection remains unexplored. The IL-33/ST2 signaling pathway promotes Th2-type immune responses [21]. Hence, further studies are needed to clarify the potential involvement of these cytokines in MAC infections.

Contrary to previous findings that reported lower PD-1 levels in cavitary NB NTM disease [7], no significant differences were observed between the groups in the present study. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the NTM species examined or the definitions applied to cavitary lesions. The present study focused exclusively on MAC, whereas previous studies included a broader range of NTM species. In addition, variations in the criteria used to define cavitary lesions—such as differences in wall thickness thresholds—may have influenced the results. Differences in patient characteristics, including a trend toward older age and lower body mass index (BMI) in the present study population, may have also contributed to the divergent findings.

This study has some limitations. First, the small sample size—particularly in the cavitary NB MAC group—limited the statistical power and precluded the use of multivariate analyses, potentially leading to underestimation or overestimation of cytokine differences. As this was an exploratory study, further analyses of cytokines potentially involved in cavity formation will require studies with adequate sample sizes. Additionally, the limited sample size restricted the ability to perform meaningful subgroup analyses. Second, the observed differences in cytokine levels between the two groups were relatively modest, with substantial overlap in individual cytokine values despite significant differences. The clinical relevance of these differences remains uncertain and warrants further investigation. Third, whether the cytokine differences are causative of or secondary to cavity formation remains unclear. Elevated cytokine levels may reflect an immune response to tissue damage rather than serving as a causal factor. Further in vitro and in vivo studies—such as longitudinal analyses of cytokine levels before and after cavity formation in the same patients—are needed to clarify cytokine dynamics across different stages of disease progression. Fourth, although no significant differences were observed in patient characteristics such as BMI, comorbidities (including diabetes mellitus), or smoking history between the two groups, these factors may still have influenced the cytokine levels. Finally, sample collection was not standardized. Diurnal variation in cytokine levels (e.g., IL-6, IL-8/CXCL8, IL-17E/IL-25, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) has been reported [22], and the lack of time-controlled sampling may have contributed to variability in the results.

Conclusions

This exploratory study demonstrated a potential association between differences in cytokine levels and the presence of lung cavities in patients with pulmonary MAC infection. The findings suggest that inflammatory cytokines, tissue remodeling–associated factors, and Th2 cytokines might be involved in the pathogenesis of cavitary NB MAC. Therapeutic strategies aimed at inhibiting MMP activity or modulating inflammation and immune responses might represent promising therapeutic strategies to mitigate lung cavity formation and tissue damage, warranting further longitudinal and mechanistic investigation.
