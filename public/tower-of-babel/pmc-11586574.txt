Dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses with benign appearance in dogs: A retrospective medical review of 95 cases among 198 dogs (2019‐2022)
Authors: Arthur Petitpre, Pauline Deprez, Mario Cervone, Mathieu Magnin, Anaïs Lamoureux
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses with benign macroscopic appearance are frequently observed during retrograde nasopharyngoscopy, particularly in brachycephalic breeds, but are not well described.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo characterize these masses, assess their frequency, and identify the potential factors associated with their presence.AnimalsDogs that underwent retrograde nasopharyngoscopy at a private hospital.MethodsMedical records between November 2019 and October 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Dogs were included if suitable nasopharynx images were available for review. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the factors associated with these masses.ResultsOne‐hundred ninety‐eight dogs met the inclusion criteria of which 47.9% (95/198) had a dorsal nasopharyngeal mass. The masses measured <10%, 10%‐30%, and >30% of the nasopharyngeal height in 64.2% (61/95), 28.4% (27/95), and 7.4% (7/95) of cases, respectively. Univariable analysis identified associations between the presence of a nasopharyngeal mass and several factors: brachycephalic conformation (P < .001), sleep disturbances (P = .04), presence of laryngeal collapse (P = .01), and aberrant caudal turbinates (P = .04). However, according to the multivariable analysis, only the association between the presence of a mass and brachycephalic conformation was significant (odds ratio = 2.3 [1.1; 5.0], P = .03).Conclusion and Clinical ImportanceDorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses were common in the studied dog population. These masses are mostly small and have the same appearance across breeds. Brachycephalic conformation appears to be associated with the presence of a mass.

INTRODUCTION

In dogs, nasopharyngeal masses have numerous causes. These masses can originate from various conditions, including neoplastic processes (osteosarcoma, 1 , 2 fibrosarcoma, 1 , 2 adenocarcinoma, 1 , 2 neuroendocrine carcinoma, 3 angioleiomyoma, 4 mast cell tumor, 5 extracranial meningioma 6 ), sialoceles, 7 nasopharyngeal polyps, 8 granulomas caused by Cryptococcus spp. infection, 1 , 9 vascular anomalies such as hamartomas, 10 or congenital conditions such as Rathke's cleft cyst, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Tornwaldt's cyst, 2 , 15 and nasopharyngeal branchial cleft cyst. 16 Clinical signs associated with nasopharyngeal masses include respiratory, digestive, auricular or neurological signs, but the signs can vary depending on the size and location of the mass. 1 , 2 Diagnosis typically relies on imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or retrograde flexible endoscopic evaluation of the nasopharynx, in addition to histopathology or cytopathology. 1 , 2

In our clinical experience, dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses with a benign appearance are observed in various dog breeds undergoing retrograde nasopharyngoscopy. These masses appear to be predominantly located in the dorsal midline of the nasopharynx and exhibit similar macroscopic features. Subjectively, they seem more often to be observed in brachycephalic breeds.

Numerous brachycephalic breeds are affected by brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS), a condition characterized by primary lesions involving the bone and soft tissues. 17 These primary lesions are categorized as static anomalies, such as abnormal skull conformation, aberrant conchae and nasopharyngeal turbinates, stenotic nares, soft palate elongation, macroglossia, tracheal hypoplasia, and narrowing of the glottis and cricoid cartilage. 17 These lesions can cause an increase in intraluminal pressure, resulting in secondary lesions, including nasopharyngeal obstruction and collapse; laryngeal, tracheal, and bronchial collapse; and tonsillar eversion. 17 A recent case series reported nasopharyngeal sialoceles in 11 brachycephalic dogs; the authors hypothesized that they could represent an undescribed secondary lesion of BOAS. 7 These sialoceles were characterized by a cyst‐like appearance and were covered with well‐vascularized pink mucosa 7 ; they had a similar appearance to the masses we frequently observed.

To date, veterinary descriptions of dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses with benign appearance have been limited predominantly to anecdotal case reports or small case series. Based on the veterinary literature search, no comprehensive studies have investigated the description, frequency, clinical features, or factors associated with the presence of these dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses in brachycephalic and nonbrachycephalic dog breeds.

Based on our clinical experience and previous veterinary literature indicating a possible association between BOAS and nasopharyngeal sialocoeles, we hypothesized that dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses are common, particularly in brachycephalic breeds, and could be correlated with indicators of BOAS severity. Therefore, our aims were to characterize these masses, evaluate whether brachycephalic conformation is associated with their presence, and investigate potential associations with historical and clinical data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and case selection

Our retrospective review of medical records was conducted at a single private veterinary hospital center (Centre Hospitalier Vétérinaire Anicura Nordvet). Medical records for all dogs undergoing retrograde nasopharyngoscopy performed by a single internal medicine specialist (AL) between November 2019 and October 2022 were reviewed.

Dogs were included if the endoscopic images of the nasopharynx obtained using retrograde nasopharyngoscopy were available for review.

Dogs that had received corticosteroids within 24 hours of presentation or those that were undergoing chemotherapy protocols or treatment with long‐acting corticosteroids were not included because these treatments potentially could interfere with the presence or size of nasopharyngeal masses. Dogs were excluded if endoscopic images of the nasopharynx were of poor quality or if visualization was not complete enough to determine if a mass was present or could have been missed.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of VetAgro Sup under No. 2294.

Data collection

All of the data were retrospectively collected from medical records and tabulated (Microsoft Excel 2020, Microsoft Corp.).

Demographic data

The demographic data collected from the medical records included age, sex, breed, and body condition score (BCS).

Clinical and imaging data

Clinical data collected included reason for presentation, duration of clinical signs (categorized as days, weeks, months, or years), exercise intolerance, heat intolerance, sneezing, reverse sneezing, nasal discharge, sleep disturbances, cyanosis, collapse, digestive disorders (regurgitation, vomiting, or diarrhea), stenotic nares, stridor, stertor, inspiratory effort, expiratory effort, abnormal pulmonary sounds, and final diagnosis.

Sleep disturbances were defined based on the owners' responses to various questions regarding the quality of their dogs' sleep. Specifically, owners were asked if they had observed their dogs waking up abruptly during sleep and then falling back asleep, if they noticed breathing interruptions during sleep, or if the dogs were seeking specific sleeping positions during the day. We classified the brachycephalic status of each dog as “yes” or “no” according to its breed using the classification parameters set by the BOAS Research Group at the University of Cambridge and the American Kennel Club. 18 , 19

Endoscopic data collected from the records included the presence or absence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass, nasopharyngeal hyperemia, laryngeal collapse, laryngeal granuloma, nasopharyngeal stenosis and aberrant caudal turbinates.

Head CT data collected from the records, if available, included the presence or absence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass. Head CT was performed on dogs in sternal recumbency; the mouth was either closed or open depending on the case. All images were acquired using a Toshiba Aquilion CXL scanner. In all cases, images were obtained both before and after IV administration of iodinated contrast media (Omnipaque 300 mgI/mL; 600 mgI/kg).

Retrospective review of endoscopic and CT images

Endoscopic images of the nasopharynx were extracted from the final reports and blindly reviewed by 2 board‐certified internal medicine specialists (AL, MC). Each reviewer independently analyzed a single fixed endoscopic image capture for each animal, without access to the clinical data or the other's initial assessment. Every image was examined for the presence or absence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass. A grading scheme was created by 1 of the authors (AL) based on her clinical experience with these masses. If no mass was observed, a grade of 0 was assigned. If a mass was present, the maximum height of the mass and the maximum height of the nasopharynx were measured on fixed images. A ratio was calculated by dividing the maximum height of the nasopharyngeal mass by the maximum height of the nasopharyngeal lumen. The masses then were graded according to the calculated ratio as masses that had a height <10%, masses with a height between 10% and 30%, and masses with a height >30% of the nasopharyngeal height corresponding to grade 1, 2, or 3 masses, respectively (Table 1).

After blind review of the images by each observer, the results were compared. In cases in which there was disagreement about the inclusion criteria, these cases were excluded. If the disagreement was about the presence or absence of a mass, the case was recorded as the absence of a mass. For disagreements related to grading (1‐3), each observer was asked to provide a second measurement to see if a consensus could be reached; if not, the case was excluded. When available, head CT images were blindly reviewed by a board‐certified radiologist (PD) to determine whether a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass was visible on sagittal images. The radiologist was blinded to the clinical data except for the owner, animal name and patient skull conformation.

Data analysis

After blind review of the images, the agreement between the 2 observers was assessed using Cohen's kappa (κ) coefficient, with κ ≤ 0 indicating no agreement; κ = 0.01‐0.20, slight agreement; κ = 0.21‐0.40, fair agreement; κ = 0.41‐0.60, moderate agreement; κ = 0.61‐0.80, substantial agreement; and κ > 0.81, almost perfect agreement.

First, descriptive statistics were performed. Categorical variables are reported as proportions. Quantitative variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro‐Wilk test and graphical representations (histograms and quantile‐quantile plots). Because the studied variables were not normally distributed, the results are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR; first quartile to third quartile).

The dogs were categorized based on the presence or absence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass (Figure 1). Univariable analysis was performed to detect associations between the presence of a mass and historical or clinical variables that could reflect more severe clinical signs, especially respiratory signs: age (in months), brachycephalic status (yes or no), duration of clinical signs before presentation (days, weeks, months, or years), BCS (on a 5‐point scale), presence of sleep disturbances (yes or no), cyanosis (yes or no), digestive disorders (vomiting, diarrhea or regurgitation; yes or no), laryngeal collapse (yes or no), aberrant caudal turbinates (yes or no), laryngeal granuloma (yes or no), and nasopharyngeal hyperemia (yes or no). Univariable analysis was performed using logistic regression with presence of a mass as the dependent variable and the other parameters previously described as explanatory variables. Then, a multivariable logistic regression model was built to identify independent factors associated with the presence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass. This model included the presence of a mass as the dependent variable and the parameters for which the P‐value was <.2 in the univariable analysis as explanatory variables. Odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were subsequently calculated. A binomial family function was used for all the models. A P‐value <.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio (version: 2023.09.1).

RESULTS

During the study period, 317 dogs underwent retrograde nasopharyngoscopy for the evaluation of respiratory, oropharyngeal or digestive signs (Figure 1). Twenty dogs were not included because of the administration of corticosteroids within the prior 24 hours or the recent administration of long‐acting corticosteroids. No dog had received chemotherapy at the time of presentation. Ninety‐nine dogs were excluded because of poor or incomplete visualization of the nasopharynx during the endoscopic image review. Overall, 198 dogs met the inclusion criteria.

Descriptive findings

Demographic data

The study population consisted of 61 different breeds, with the most common being French Bulldog (n = 38), Cavalier King Charles Spaniel (n = 12), Labrador Retriever (n = 10), Golden Retriever (n = 9), Belgian Shepherd (n = 7), Pugs (n = 7), Jack Russell‐terrier (n = 6), and Chihuahua (n = 5). There were 114 males (57.6%, 114/198), 32 of which were neutered (28.1%, 32/114) and 84 females (42.4%, 84/198), 48 of which were spayed (57.1%, 48/84). Median age was 63 months (range, 31‐110), median weight was 14.3 kg (range, 10‐30), and median BCS was 3 (range, 3‐3.5).

Eighty‐two dogs were classified as brachycephalic (41.4%, 82/198) based on their breed. 16 , 17 Among these, French Bulldog was the most common breed, accounting for 38 of the 82 brachycephalic dogs (46.4%, 38/82). Other brachycephalic breeds included Cavalier King Charles Spaniels (14.6%, 12/82), Pugs (8.5%, 7/82), Chihuahuas (6.1%, 5/82), English Bulldogs (6.1%, 5/82), Boxers (3.7%, 3/82), Shih Tzus (3.7%, 3/82), American Bully (3.7%, 3/82), Boston Terriers (2.4%, 2/82), American Staffordshire Terriers (2.4%, 2/82), Chows (1.2%, 1/82), and Brabant Griffons (1.2%, 1/82). There were 51 males (62.2%, 51/82), 12 of which were neutered (23.5%, 12/51), and 31 females (37.8%, 31/82), 18 of which were spayed (58.1%, 18/31). Median age was 43 months (range, 23‐73), median weight was 11.6 kg (range, 9.4‐14.4), and median BCS was 3 (range, 3‐3.5).

Retrospective review of endoscopic and CT images

Cohen's κ agreement coefficient revealed identified almost perfect agreement between the 2 observers who blindly reviewed the captured images, with κ = 0.90 (95% CI, 0.86‐0.94). Agreement was reached for 295 cases. Disagreement occurred in 22 cases, of which 16 were excluded because of disagreements about inclusion criteria. In 2 cases, the disagreement was about the grading, but a consensus was reached after each observer provided a second blinded measurement. Finally, in 4 cases, the disagreement was about the presence or absence of a mass, and all were classified as the absence of a mass.

Among the study population, 47.9% (95/198) had a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass visualized on fixed, blindly‐reviewed, endoscopic images (Table 3). In these dogs, endoscopic evaluation consistently identified a well‐circumscribed and smooth mass that was covered with pale, slightly vascularized, pinkish mucosa, and the masses were located dorsally in the midline of the nasopharynx. Among the masses, 64.2% (61/95) were grade 1, 28.4% (27/95) were grade 2, and 7.4% (7/95) were grade 3 (Figure 2). Interestingly, only 12.6% (12/95) of these masses were described in the medical records. Of these, 3 were grade 1, 6 were grade 2, and 3 were grade 3 (Table 3).

Fifty dogs underwent both a head CT and endoscopy. Twelve masses were visible on the CT image reviews, yet none of them were mentioned in the CT report. In all of these dogs, CT identified a nonenhancing, smoothly marginated, broad‐based, soft tissue attenuated nodule or mass on the dorsal nasopharyngeal wall. These lesions were variable in size and protruded into the nasopharynx lumen, thereby decreasing the lumen diameter. The lesions were located on the midline of the nasopharynx lumen at the level of the suture between the basiphenoid and presphenoid bones (Figure 3). Out of these 50 dogs, only 14% (7/50) had masses that were visible on both endoscopy and head CT reviews. Five of the 12 masses (41.7%) identified on CT image review were not visualized during endoscopy, whereas 15 of 22 (68.2%) identified on endoscopy were not visible on CT review (Table 5).

Among the 22 dogs with a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass observed during endoscopy and that underwent head CT, the masses were graded based on endoscopic appearance as follows: grade 1 in 68.2% (15/22), grade 2 in 18.2% (4/22), and grade 3 in 13.6% (3/22). Of these masses, only 31.8% (7/22) were visible on head CT. Computed tomography identified 26.7% (4/15) of grade 1, 25% (1/4) of grade 2, and 66% (2/3) of grade 3 masses, as graded on endoscopic appearance.

None of the dogs included in the study underwent histopathological or cytological examination of the nasopharyngeal mass.

Final diagnosis

Among the 198 dogs that underwent retrograde nasopharyngoscopy, 66.2% (131/198) were diagnosed with upper respiratory tract diseases, including 54 dogs having BOAS. Additionally, 16.2% (32/198) had lower respiratory tract diseases; 9.6% (19/198) had diseases affecting the oropharyngeal area; 3% (6/198) had gastrointestinal diseases; and 5% (10/198) were categorized under other conditions (Table 6).

Factors associated with the presence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass

According to univariable analysis, 4 parameters were significantly associated with the presence of a dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass (Table 7): brachycephalic conformation (OR = 2.7 [1.5; 4.9], P < .001), the presence of sleep disturbances (OR = 4.1 [1.2; 18.7], P = .04), laryngeal collapse (OR = 2.8 [1.4; 6], P = .01) and the presence of aberrant caudal turbinates (OR = 2.3 [1.1; 5.3], P = .04).

DISCUSSION

We determined that dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses are commonly found in dogs undergoing retrograde nasopharyngoscopy, with 47.9% of the dogs being affected. Based on the proposed grading scheme, these masses are mostly small (64.2% grade 1), and are consistently well‐circumscribed, smooth, and covered with pale, slightly vascularized, pink mucosa. Dogs with a brachycephalic conformation were commonly affected, with 62.2% of brachycephalic dogs having a mass versus only 38% of nonbrachycephalic dogs.

Dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses with benign macroscopic appearance have not been well described in the veterinary literature. However, they frequently were observed in our population of dogs, because nearly half (47.9%) of the dogs undergoing retrograde nasopharyngoscopy were affected. These masses can be recognized by their distinct appearance: well‐circumscribed and smooth mass or nodule with slightly vascularized, pale to pinkish mucosa. An important finding is that brachycephalic conformation was commonly associated with the presence of these masses in our study population of dogs according to multivariable analysis. Nevertheless, these results cannot be extrapolated to healthy brachycephalic dogs, because most were evaluated for respiratory signs.

The others factors identified in the univariable analysis, such as the presence of sleep disturbances, laryngeal collapse, or aberrant caudal turbinates, were confounding factors for brachycephalic conformation and did not reach statistical significance in multivariable analyses. Table 2 illustrates that these parameters were predominantly found in brachycephalic dogs. Specifically, among the 41 dogs presenting with laryngeal collapse, 37 were brachycephalic (90.2%; 37/41); of the 13 dogs presenting with sleep disturbances, 9 were brachycephalic (69.2%; 9/13); and among the 32 dogs with aberrant caudal turbinates, 30 were brachycephalic (93.8%; 30/32).

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the etiology of dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses, including Rathke's cleft cysts, Tornwaldt's cysts, and nasopharyngeal sialocoeles.

Rathke's cleft is an embryological precursor of the adenohypophyseal region of the pituitary gland. It originates as a pouch from the oropharyngeal ectoderm, migrates upward through the future sphenoid bone and differentiates into the adenohypophysis. Along its migration path, remnants of Rathke's cleft can form cysts. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 In human medicine, these cysts may lead to neurological symptoms if they occur intracranially, and they may cause nasopharyngeal obstruction if they develop outside of the cranium 20 , 21 ; however, they are often asymptomatic. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Descriptions of Rathke's cleft cysts in the veterinary literature are scarce. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 In dogs, they are primarily reported in association with dwarfism resulting from hypopituitarism or with other neurological signs. However, cases involving nasopharyngeal obstruction also have been documented. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 No reports of endoscopic or CT examinations of nasopharyngeal Rathke's cleft cysts have been found in the veterinary literature. Only descriptions of excised tissue are available. These lesions are large, firm, and cystic, and originate from the dorsal midline of the nasopharynx with a bony defect in the sphenoid bone. 11 , 12 In human medicine, CT can identify a lesion with a smooth contour and homogeneous variable density (iso‐, hypo‐, or hyperdense signal) without calcification. 24 This finding partially aligns with our observations, because the lesions were located dorsally on the midline of the nasopharynx, 20 but the previously described bony defect was not visible on CT.

In human medicine, Tornwaldt's cysts are rare benign lesions that arise as a result of defective embryological development within the midline of the nasopharynx. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 These cysts often go undiagnosed because of their small size and benign nature, but larger cysts can lead to nasal obstruction and discharge, headache, neck stiffness, and eustachian tube dysfunction. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 To our knowledge, only 1 highly suspected case of a Tornwaldt's cyst has been reported in a 9‐year‐old Yorkshire Terrier with respiratory signs and a sleeping disorder. 15 Suspicion of a Tornwaldt's cyst was based on respiratory clinical signs, with CT identifying a rostral obstructive nasopharyngeal cystic mass, and endoscopy showing a smooth, vascularized mass with mucinous content. Histopathology confirmed a cyst lined with respiratory epithelium, with other potential causes excluded. These findings align with the characteristics of Tornwaldt's cysts described in humans. 15 This description has some similarities with the masses described here, which have a similar endoscopic appearance, a small size, midline localization, and lack of clinical signs. However, some dissimilarities can be noted, as the masses described in our study seemed more caudal, and their CT characteristics differed from the typical cystic appearance described in humans.

In the veterinary literature, a retrospective case series also described nasopharyngeal sialoceles in 11 brachycephalic dogs. 7 The endoscopic appearance of these sialoceles, which are characterized as cyst‐like masses covered with well‐vascularized pinkish mucosa, 7 strongly aligns with the masses described in our study. Despite this similar appearance, some discrepancies should be noted between the 2 studies. First, in the previous study, masses had a predominantly ventral distribution (8 of 11 dogs), 7 whereas we included only dorsal masses because of our previous clinical experience with this dorsal location. Second, the masses described were highly obstructive (>70% of the nasopharyngeal opening) in 8/11 of the dogs, 7 but in our population of brachycephalic dogs with masses, grade 3 masses accounted for only 4 of 51 cases. This difference may be explained by recruitment bias, as the authors retrospectively included animals diagnosed with nasopharyngeal sialoceles, and smaller masses with a benign appearance might not have been considered, potentially explaining the higher prevalence of high‐grade masses in their study. Furthermore, low‐grade masses can gradually progress into high‐grade masses over time and are more likely to be sampled to obtain a diagnosis. Moreover, histological findings regarding nasopharyngeal sialoceles identified in the previous study were consistent with extravasation sialoceles. Extravasation sialoceles are commonly found in minor salivary gland ducts and are caused by fluid leakage from surrounding tissue ducts or acini, usually resulting from trauma and inflammation associated with stagnant mucus. 29 The collapse of the nasopharynx and the increased intraluminal pressure associated with BOAS 2 , 17 may lead to repeated trauma and barotrauma, predisposing brachycephalic dogs to nasopharyngeal extravasation sialoceles. One study identified the presence of dilated glandular ducts, glandular hyperplasia, and sialoceles in the soft palate of adult dogs with grade 1 BOAS, 30 whereas another study conducted by the same authors concluded that neonatal brachycephalic dogs do not have these lesions. 31 These observations imply that these abnormalities are acquired rather than congenital in origin.

Considering these findings and previous publications, certain features of the masses described here, such as the appearance, location and association with brachycephalic conformation, bear similarity with nasopharyngeal sialoceles, Tornwaldt's or Rathke's cleft cyst, suggesting 1 of these diseases as a possible underlying etiology. Nonetheless, the cause and pathogenesis of these masses is unknown and other differential diagnoses are therefore also possible. Further investigations and histopathological examinations may provide additional insight into the true origin of these masses in dogs.

The role of endoscopy in the evaluation of the upper respiratory tract cannot be overlooked. In our study, out of 22 masses visualized by endoscopy and 12 visualized by head CT, only 7 dogs had a confirmed dorsomedian nasopharyngeal mass detected by both modalities. One potential explanation for this observation could be the size of the masses. Although statistical analysis was not performed because of small sample size, identification of masses became more straightforward as mass grade increased, which is understandable. However, 1 grade 3 mass and 3 grade 2 masses were not visible on CT images, and these findings were unexpected. In our study, mass grading was established using 1 fixed endoscopic image capture of the nasopharynx, and some masses potentially could have been misgraded.

Conversely, 5 masses identified by CT image review were not visualized during endoscopy. These observed differences may have been caused by visualization of nasopharyngeal secretions, which can mimic the presence of a nasopharyngeal mass to some extent.

Finally, none of the 12 masses visible on the CT images reviewed was mentioned in the CT report, and only 12 of the 95 masses visible on the endoscopy imaging review were mentioned in the endoscopy report. These findings could be explained by the fact that the masses were mostly small and probably were considered incidental findings (ie, not contributing to the clinical signs) by most clinicians. This possibility also could explain the absence of removal and histopathological analysis of the masses.

Our study had some limitations. First, because of its retrospective design, some data were missing and some investigations were not performed. Thus, an important limitation of our study is the lack of histopathological or cytopathological diagnostic data for nasopharyngeal masses. Second, the high frequency of dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses found in our study should be interpreted only in this population of dogs undergoing retrograde nasopharyngoscopy for respiratory, oropharyngeal, or digestive diseases, and these results may not be generalizable to healthy dogs. Furthermore, as a referral center, a substantial number of brachycephalic breeds were presented for surgical correction of their BOAS, potentially leading to overrepresentation of these breeds, which could have caused overestimation of the frequency of dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses in the study population. Additionally, the criteria for classifying some breeds as brachycephalic is a subject of debate. For example, the Chihuahua breed lacks a clear brachycephalic status, but Chihuahuas can be affected by BOAS. The BOAS Research Group from the University of Cambridge has included Chihuahuas as a brachycephalic dog breed, and we decided to use this classification in our study. 18 , 19 Moreover, because of the retrospective nature of our study and the lack of available data, we were unable to incorporate the BOAS grading system 32 into our analysis. However, adding this system would have been interesting to better define the severity of BOAS. Third, the grading of the dorsal nasopharyngeal masses was not entirely reliable, despite the use of a grading scheme to assess their height ratio. This situation arises from the limited perspective provided by fixed endoscopic image capture. Identifying the degree of nasopharyngeal obstruction using image capture is challenging and may have led to the misgrading of some masses. To limit this bias, the images were independently reviewed by 2 blinded observers, who reached almost perfect agreement. Assessing nasopharyngeal obstruction by measuring height is only an approximation. A more accurate assessment would involve comparing the area of the masses to the area of the nasopharynx at the same level. However, using fixed endoscopic photographs makes doing so difficult and would lead to excessive approximation. This situation emphasizes the necessity of using CT and standardized patient positioning for accurate measurement. Therefore, we did not employ grade as a parameter for statistical analysis but only for descriptive data.

In conclusion, our study represents the first large report of dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses in various breeds of dogs with various medical issues. In this population, dorsomedian nasopharyngeal masses frequently are observed during retrograde nasopharyngoscopy, and brachycephalic conformation seems to be commonly associated with the presence of these masses. They are mostly of small size and have the same appearance across breeds, but their origin remains unknown. A prospective study involving histopathological examination of these masses could determine their exact nature and potential association with severe cases of BOAS.
