Acoustic black hole immersed sonoreactor for high-efficiency cavitation treatment
Authors: Cheng Chen, Yang Liu, Chenghui Wang, Jianzhong Guo, Shuyu Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Developing innovative sonoreactors to enhance acoustic processing efficiency holds immense importance in the field of sonochemistry. Traditional immersed sonoreactors (TISs) mainly produce cavitation at the probe tip, with a relatively weak cavitation around the probe, resulting in posing challenges for high-efficiency cavitation treatment. Here we propose an acoustic black hole immersed sonoreactor (ABHIS) in longitudinal-flexural coupled vibration, enabling high-efficiency cavitation treatment by unleashing the cavitation potential of the probe. The symmetrical structure of the probe is altered to introduce a coupling of flexural vibration mode, and an acoustic black hole (ABH) profile is integrated to further enhance both flexural wave number and amplitude. In this paper, we present a systematic theoretical design method for ABHIS and compare its performance with TIS using finite element method (FEM). An ABHIS prototype is fabricated and subjected to experimental tests and cavitation observation. The results demonstrate that our theoretical analysis model accurately predicts the frequency characteristics of ABHIS. The proposed ABHIS exhibits satisfactory dynamic characteristics, with significantly increased vibration displacement and acoustic radiation ability compared to TIS. Importantly, the ABH design significantly expands ultrasonic cavitation regions and enhances acoustic radiation intensity of ABHIS, resulting in a substantial improvement in acoustic processing efficiency.

Introduction

Immersed sonoreactors (ISs), which harness ultrasonic energy to accelerate chemical reactions, are utilized in various domains such as industrial clean, sewage treatment, food processing, organic synthesis, and biotechnology [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. The traditional immersed sonoreactor (TIS) typically consists of an ultrasonic transducer and an amplitude amplifying probe, operating at a frequency range of 20 to 40 kHz [7], [8]. The amplitude of the probe tip can be controlled by adjusting the electrical power input to the transducer. Generally, the power density of up to several hundred W/cm2 can be achieved on the radiation surface of the probe. However, with rapid advancements in sonochemistry, TIS faces significant challenges in meeting diverse requirements across various scenarios, particularly in terms of acoustic processing range [9], [10]. The limited design structure and single vibration mode result in cavitation primarily occurring at the probe tip, while cavitation around the probe is relatively weak, as shown in Fig. 1. (a). This seriously restricts its acoustic processing range, efficiency, and broader application in various sonochemistry fields. Therefore, developing innovative ISs to enhance acoustic processing efficiency has become an urgent task with substantial significance and challenges.Fig. 1Ultrasonic cavitation distribution diagram: (a) TIS, (b) ABHIS, (c) structure design diagram of ABHIS, (d) ABHIS prototype.

Ultrasonic cavitation distribution diagram: (a) TIS, (b) ABHIS, (c) structure design diagram of ABHIS, (d) ABHIS prototype.

Moreover, acoustic black hole (ABH) structures have gained widespread attention in the field of acoustic engineering due to their unique ability to focus and capture waves, demonstrating significant advantages and potential for development in various areas such as vibration reduction [11], [12], pipeline noise reduction [13], [14], energy recovery [15], [16], wave regulation [17], [18], and particle manipulation [19], [20]. The fundamental mechanism involves adjusting the thickness of the structure at specific positions to reduce the propagation speed of flexural waves and minimize boundary reflections, thereby creating regions with high energy density [21], [22]. However, our literature review has revealed that current applications of ABH primarily focus on vibration and noise reduction, with relatively less research on the development of more powerful ultrasonic devices. Therefore, in our previous work, we successfully integrated the ABH structure into the design of an ultrasonic scalpel and got a significant progress in improving surgical cutting efficiency and hemostasis [23]. Regarding enhancing the performance of ISs, we believe that incorporating the ABH design will also be an effective and brilliant approach.

Based on this, we propose a novel acoustic black hole immersed sonoreactor (ABHIS) in longitudinal-flexural coupled vibration. The aim is to address the limited acoustic processing range and efficiency issues of TIS by leveraging modal coupling and ABH design, as shown in Fig. 1. (b). The main work in section 2 includes introducing the design principle and formulaic model of ABHIS, establishing finite element analysis models of the vibration and sound field of ABHIS and TIS, as well as introducing specific experimental settings. Section 3 analyzes in detail the results obtained from theoretical calculations, finite element analysis, and experiments, with a focus on performance improvements brought by the ABH design for ABHIS. Finally, section 4 summarizes the conclusions and prospects of our research.

Methods and characterization

Design principle and formulation

Fig. 1. (c) shows the structure design diagram of ABHIS, which comprises an ultrasonic transducer and an ABH probe. The ultrasonic transducer is composed of a back mass, a piezoelectric ceramic stack, a flange and a front mass. Among them, r7, r6, L11, L10 are the radii and lengths of the two circular section rods of the back mass. r6, L9 are the radius and thickness of a single piezoelectric ceramic piece. r4, r5, r6, L7, L8 are the radii and lengths of the three circular section rods of the flange. The front mass is a composite ultrasonic transformer with a circular transition section. r4, L6 are the radius and length of the big end circular rod of the front mass, r3, L5 are the radius and length of the middle end circular rod of the front mass, r2, L4 are the radius and length of the small end circular rod of the front mass. The ABH probe consists of a circular rod with equal cross-section and a circular rod with variable cross-section. r1, L2 represent the radius and length of the circular rod with equal cross-section, r0 is the tip radius of the probe, and L1 is the length of the circular rod with variable cross-section, m is the radius change coefficient.

According to the design principles of the resonance system, the ultrasonic transducer and the ABH probe are designed as two 25 kHz half-wave longitudinal oscillators. Subsequently, a semi-ring groove is incorporated into the equal cross-section circular rod of the ABH probe to induce coupling of the flexural vibration mode. The groove is located at the anti-node of the flexural vibration, serving to enhance the flexural vibration by reducing the structural stiffness. The size parameters can be determined through theoretical calculation and parameterized scanning optimization by FEM, with the performance indicators being the maximum flexural and longitudinal amplitude values of the probe. The width of the semi-ring groove obtained after optimization is L3, and the radius thickness is 0.2r1. Furthermore, the ABH profile is embedded in the variable cross-section circular rod of the ABH probe, its radius function (0 ≤ x ≤ L1) can be expressed as:(1)rx=r1-r0L1mxm+r0According to the premise of the theoretical design of ABH beam [21], the radius change coefficient m must be larger than or equal to 2. Ideally, as the tip radius r0 of the probe approaches 0, the local flexural wave propagation speed will gradually decrease to 0, while the wave number and amplitude will tend towards infinity. However, achieving a zero tip radius for the ABH probe is unattainable in practical applications. Nevertheless, even with a non-zero tip radius, the characteristic of accumulating wave number and amplitude still persists, which will enable the generation of multiple high-energy acoustic radiation regions around the probe to enhance acoustic processing range and efficiency.

Finally, to achieve the same-frequency coupling between the high-order flexural vibration mode and the first-order longitudinal vibration mode of the ABH probe, we keep the other design dimensions constant and adjust the values of m and L1 to make the two vibration frequencies approach at 25 kHz.

Fabrication and experiments

Prototype fabrication

Based on theoretical model calculations and FEM-based modeling, an ABHIS prototype is fabricated, as depicted in Fig. 1. (d). The transducer comprises a steel back mass, two rings of lead-zirconate-titanate piezoelectric ceramic (PZT-4), copper electrodes, an aluminum flange, and an aluminum front mass. Assembly of the transducer is achieved using a high-strength steel prestressed bolt. The ABH probe is made of a titanium alloy, with the semi-ring groove completed using EDM (Electric Discharge Machining) technology. A high-strength steel bolt is utilized to connect the transducer and ABH probe.

Electrical impedance analysis experiment (EXP1)

Precision impedance analyzer (WK6500B, Wayne Kerr Electronics) is used to measure the frequency response characteristics of equivalent electrical parameters of ABHIS as shown in Fig. 5. ABHIS is tested under a 1 V AC excitation. The frequency scanning range is 22 kHz-28 kHz, with 800 scan points, and the impedance amplitude range is selected adaptively. The internal fifth-type equivalent lumped parameter circuit is used, where RE1 represents the equivalent resistance, LE1 represents the equivalent inductance, CE1 represents the equivalent capacitance, and CE0 represents the equivalent static capacitance. The formula for calculating the mechanical quality factor is QF = 2πfrLE1/RE1, and the formula for calculating the effective electromechanical coupling coefficient is keff = [(fa2-fr2)/fa2]1/2, where fr represents the resonance frequency (series resonance frequency) and fa represents the anti-resonance frequency (parallel resonance frequency).Fig. 5The experiment of using WK6500B precision impedance analyzer to measure the frequency response characteristics of equivalent electrical parameters of ABHIS.

The experiment of using WK6500B precision impedance analyzer to measure the frequency response characteristics of equivalent electrical parameters of ABHIS.

Laser measurement vibration experiment (EXP2)

Full-field laser scanning vibration measurement system (Polytec PSV-400), including the laser scanning head, junction box, vibration controller (OFV-5000), data management system, and operating center, is used to measure the vibration modes and displacement frequency response characteristics of ABHIS as shown in Fig. 6. The outline flexural displacement and the tip longitudinal displacement of the probe are measured by sweeping frequency measurement in range of 22 kHz-28 kHz, using a 1 V excitation voltage and adaptively setting the scanning range. 150 points are selected on a circular area at the tip of the probe for longitudinal vibration measurement, and 175 points are selected on the ABH profile line for flexural displacement measurement.Fig. 6The experiment of using full-field laser scanning vibration measurement system (Polytec PSV-400) to measure the vibration characteristics of ABHIS.

The experiment of using full-field laser scanning vibration measurement system (Polytec PSV-400) to measure the vibration characteristics of ABHIS.

High-speed photographic cavitation experiment (EXP3)

ABHIS is installed on a fixed device, with the fixed position being the flange to avoid affecting vibration. The ABH probe is immersed in a transparent water tank (100 × 100 × 300 mm3, water depth 230 mm) filled with large quantities of deaerated tap water. The length of the ABH probe immersed in water is L1. The cavitation field around the probe is observed using a high-speed camera (i-SPEED 726, iX Cameras Ltd. UK) as shown in Fig. 7. The bubble structure is illuminated by the lighting system (DannyU high-power LED) during the experiment.Fig.7The experiment of using high-speed camera (i-SPEED 726) to observe the cavitation field of ABHIS.

The experiment of using high-speed camera (i-SPEED 726) to observe the cavitation field of ABHIS.

A power excitation system is set up to achieve high-power excitation of ABHIS, consisting of a signal generator (RIGOL DG1022U), an impedance matching box (T&C MODEL SUT 2 K LF-7), an inductance matching box (T&C AI7-1), and a power amplifier (T&C AG1012). The specific incentive process is as follows: First, the resonance frequency of ABHIS after immersing in water tank is measured using the WK6500B precision impedance analyzer, with the result being 25,402 Hz. Then, the signal generator outputs a continuous sine wave signal at 25,402 Hz and with a voltage of 500 mVpp. After being amplified by the power amplifier, the signal is transmitted to ABHIS through an impedance and an inductance matching box. The power amplifier display allows for real-time monitoring of the signal's amplification level. The choose of the impedance and an inductance should be determined based on the optimal ABHIS's effective input electric power.

Results and discussion

Frequency characteristic analysis

Fig. 8 shows the frequency response curves of electrical impedance of ABHIS obtained by the EECM, FEM, and EXP1. The trends of the three curves are basically consistent, and the resonance frequencies of ABHIS obtained are fr-EECM = 25,987 Hz, fr-FEM = 25,508 Hz, and fr-EXP1 = 25,424 Hz, while the anti-resonance frequencies are fa-EECM = 26,068 Hz, fa-FEM = 25,567 Hz, and fa-EXP1 = 25,476 Hz. The errors are calculated as follows: e1=(fr-EECM-fr-FEM)/fr-EECM, e2=(fr-EECM-fr-EXP1)/fr-EECM, e3=(fa-EECM-fa-FEM)/fa-EECM, and e4=(fa-EECM-fa-EXP1)/fa-EECM. As shown in TABLE 3, the frequency errors from all three methods are less than 2.3 %, indicating the accuracy of the theoretical model. The main source of error stems from partial approximation in the theoretical model. While the impact of the connecting bolt is considered in longitudinal vibration design, it is disregarded in flexural vibration design. Incorporating the influence of the bolt into flexural vibration design would result in a more intricate theoretical model, leading to heightened demand for computational resources and reduced calculation speed. Therefore, we have implemented appropriate approximations. Despite this, the ultimate frequency error remains within an acceptable range (≤5%) for engineering purposes. The effective electromechanical coupling coefficient keff of ABHIS is calculated from measured resonance/anti-resonance frequencies to be 0.11. Mode analysis and vibration performance comparison

Fig. 9 shows the vibration modes obtained by FEM of ABHIS and TIS. TIS operates in a pure second-order longitudinal vibration mode without any noticeable flexural displacement at 22,637 Hz, while ABHIS operates in a longitudinal-flexural coupled vibration mode at 25,508 Hz with obvious flexural displacement of the probe. The principal axis displacements of the two ISs are then extracted and analyzed, as depicted in Fig. 10. The results from Fig. 10. These distribution patterns are attributed to three characteristics of the ABH structure: (1) an increase in wave number leads to multiple cavitation regions; (2) a decrease in wave speed results in smaller spacing between cavitation regions; and (3) amplitude accumulation amplification leads to dense concentration of cavitation bubbles at the probe tip. Conclusions

In this study, we introduce a novel acoustic black hole immersed sonoreactor (ABHIS) and perform comprehensive theoretical analysis, experimental testing, and cavitation observation. The following are the pivotal discoveries:(1)The developed theoretical model accurately predicts the frequency characteristics of ABHIS in longitudinal-flexural coupled vibration, facilitating rapid design.(2)Simulation results of vibration and sound field demonstrate that the longitudinal and flexural vibration displacements of ABHIS are significantly amplified compared to TIS. Notably, the ABH design enhances local flexural wave number, leading to multiple high-intensity acoustic radiation regions around the probe. Vibration energy accumulates at the probe tip, resulting in increased acoustic radiation intensity.(3)Equivalent electrical parameters and vibration measurement experiments validate the feasibility of the model design and accuracy of theoretical calculations, confirming satisfactory dynamic characteristics and electromechanical conversion ability of proposed ABHIS.(4)High-speed photography cavitation results reveal that ABH design substantially expands ultrasonic cavitation regions of ABHIS, greatly improving acoustic processing efficiency.(5)The ABHIS has advantages such as compact size, easy electrical matching, low power input with strong performance output, which is anticipated to be applied in small-scale, high-intensity ultrasonic cavitation processing areas, including but not limited to biological cell disruption, protein extraction and purification, accelerated synthesis of chemical materials such as nanomaterials and functional ceramics, as well as food processing.
