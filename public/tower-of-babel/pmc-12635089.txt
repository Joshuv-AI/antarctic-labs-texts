Epidemiological, morphological, and molecular characterization of Thelazia spp. in European bison (Bison bonasus) in Poland
Authors: Katarzyna Filip-Hutsch, Marcos Antonio Bezerra-Santos, Alessia Ricci, Michał Czopowicz, Aleksander W. Demiaszkiewicz, Paulina Balińska, Krzysztof Anusz, Wanda Olech, Domenico Otranto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nematodes of the genus Thelazia (also referred to as eyeworms) pose a growing concern for wild and domestic ruminants. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence, species composition, and associated ocular pathology of Thelazia infection in several European bison populations in Poland. The eyes of 223 European bison were examined over five years (2020–2024). Nematodes of the genus Thelazia were identified based on morphometrical and molecular analysis of the cox1 sequences. Epidemiological analysis of Thelazia infection in relation to age, sex, genetic line of European bison, as well as severity of ocular changes, habitat, and season, was performed. Morphometrical and molecular analysis revealed the presence of two species – Thelazia gulosa and T. skrjabini. Nematodes were detected in 61.9% of examined animals, with T. skrjabini being significantly more prevalent than T. gulosa (p < 0.001). Infection with T. skrjabini was significantly associated with the severity of ocular lesions, which might suggest higher vulnerability of European bison to this eyeworm species. In the study period, prevalence of Thelazia infection significantly decreased in the Lowland line of European bison (p = 0.022) while it remained unchanged in the Lowland-Caucasian bison (p = 0.328). The study shows that Thelazia spp. is a common parasite of the European bison, equally spread across different studied populations, irrespective of habitat, geographical region, season or individual factors. Introduction

Nematodes of the genus Thelazia (Spirurida, Thelazidae) infect the eyes and associated tissues (e.g., conjunctival sac, lacrimal ducts, the surface of the cornea and the space under the nictitating membrane) of various mammalian hosts, including humans1–3. Depending on the nematode burden and location, they cause disease of various severity, with conjunctivitis, lacrimation, corneal opacity, and even corneal ulceration being the most frequently reported clinical signs. While most reports on eyeworms focus on Thelazia callipaeda in domestic and wild carnivores4–8, there is a growing concern regarding the occurrence of thelaziosis caused by T. skrjabini, T. gulosa, and T. rhodesi in ruminants9,10. These Thelazia spp. are vectored by secretophagous flies of the genus Musca11, which transmit infective larvae to the conjunctival sac of the definitive host during feeding on eye secretions12. Therefore, the distribution and prevalence of eyeworms are closely related to the occurrence of eye-frequenting flies as well as environmental and ecological factors. Bovine thelaziosis is increasingly reported in cattle from Europe9,13,14, as well as in European bison (Bison bonasus)10,15,16, with numerous cases of infectious keratoconjunctivitis associated to eyeworm infection in Poland17. Although multiple infectious agents, both viruses and bacteria, are responsible for the development of ocular changes in these animals in Poland, the role of Thelazia spp. should not be neglected17. European bison is a protected species and after near-extinction in the past century its population is now vigorously increasing18. In these circumstances, the knowledge of the role of various pathogens in this species has become increasingly important19, and thelaziosis undoubtedly poses a growing threat for their health and welfare.

In this study, we aimed to describe epidemiological, morphological, molecular, and characteristics of Thelazia spp. infecting European bison population in Poland as well as pathological changes associated with their infection.

Results

Ocular lesions were observed in 192/223 European bison (86.1%; CI 95%: 80.9%, 90.0%). Lesions were bilateral in 128/192 animals (66.7%) and unilateral in 64/192 (33.3%). In 15 individuals (6.7%), only one eye was examined due to the absence or damage of the second eye during the carcass examination. The ocular lesions were moderate in 132/192 bison (65.1%), severe in 54/192 bison (28.1%), and mild in only 13/192 bison (6.8%). The severity of ocular changes did not differ significantly between the right and left eye (p = 0.315).

Nematodes of the genus Thelazia were found in 138/223 European bison (overall prevalence of 61.9%; CI 95%: 55.4%, 68.0%). The prevalence of T. skrjabini (56.5%; CI 95%: 49.9%, 62.8%; 126/223) was significantly higher than prevalence of T. gulosa (14.8%; CI 95%: 10.7%, 20.1%; 33/123; p < 0.001). Co-infections with both T. gulosa and T. skrjabini were observed in 21/223 European bison (9.4%; CI 95%: 6.2%, 14.0%) including 16 animals with both parasite species in the same eye and five animals with T. gulosa and T. skrjabini in opposite eyes. Co-infections accounted for 15.2% of 138 Thelazia-infected bison.

Infection with Thelazia spp. was significantly positively associated with the presence of ocular lesions (OR 4.20; CI 95%: 1.87, 9.45; p < 0.001) (Table 1). However, when analysed separately for Thelazia species this association was significant only in the case of infection with T. skrjabini (OR 3.81; CI 95%: 1.67, 8.73; p = 0.001). For T. gulosa, this association was insignificant (p = 0.124) although also positive (OR 2.79; CI 95%: 0.63, 12.3). Severity of ocular signs did not differ significantly between animals infected with one or both Thelazia species (p = 0.419).

At morphological identification, T. skrjabini was characterized by a whitish, cylindrical body and visible cuticular cross-striations. In the anterior edge, the buccal capsule was very small, with a trapezoid-like shape, tapering gradually toward the base (Fig. 2a). Thelazia gulosa presented a pale cylindrical body, and cuticle with delicate cross-striations. In the anterior edge, a medium-sized cup-shaped buccal capsule was present (Fig. 2b). In both species, the oesophagus was nearly cylindrical, gradually dilated posteriorly, without clear division into muscular and glandular sections. In T. skrjabini, the posterior edge was bluntly rounded in both females and males and twisted in the latter, while in T. gulosa it was slightly curved ventrally in females, and ventrally curved and twisted in males. The caudal end of T. skrjabini males presented two small and nearly equal spicules (Fig. 2c), whereas males of T. gulosa presented two asymmetric spicules (i.e., the left was longer and flagelliform whereas the right was shorter and stout; Fig. 2d).

Both Thelazia spp. were of the same length (p = 0.308), however T. gulosa was significantly wider (p < 0.001) and had significantly longer and wider buccal capsule (p = 0.001) than T. skrjabini. Molecular analysis based on sequencing of cox1 gene unequivocally confirmed species identity, revealing a high degree of sequence conservation. The obtained sequences (GenBank accession nos: PX452899, PX452900) exhibited 99.53% nucleotide identity with Thelazia skrjabini (GenBank accession numbers OL362009.1 and OQ988148.1) and two other obtained sequences (GenBank accession nos: PX452901, PX452902) exhibited 99.69% identity with Thelazia gulosa (GenBank accession numbers OQ988094.1 and OQ988097.1), thereby supporting the accuracy of morphological identification and providing robust molecular evidence for species assignment. The phylogenetic analysis of cox1 gene showed that the Thelazia spp. sequences from the current study clustered in species-specific clades of the same species (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Data herein assessed demonstrated a high prevalence of Thelazia spp. (62%) in European bison, consistent with earlier reports15,16. Although current data on Thelazia nematodes in ruminants from central Europe remain scarce, these parasites have been increasingly reported in cattle in southern Europe13,20, albeit with a markedly lower prevalence (not exceeding 20%) than observed in the present study. This five-year survey indicates that Thelazia spp. infection is widespread in European bison, likely as a consequence of conservation-related increases in animal density and the absence of anthelmintic treatments in this wild population19,21. However, since most examined animals were culled due to health issues, including ocular lesions, the reported prevalence is biased toward symptomatic individuals and cannot be directly extrapolated to the overall Polish population of approximately 3,060 European bison.

Two species (i.e., T. skrjabini and T. gulosa) were identified through molecular analysis, confirming earlier morphological observations. The morphological findings generally agreed with other research9 and highlighted distinct differences between the nematode species. Both T. skrjabini and T. gulosa are typical eyeworms of bovids and have also been detected in wild and domestic ruminants worldwide9,22–24. However, in southern Europe, a dominant species infecting ruminant was T. rhodesi, while T. skrjabini and T. gulosa occurred more sporadically9,13. Although T. rhodesi is generally regarded as a rare species outside southern latitudes9,11,15, it used to be reported in several central and eastern European countries25,26. The apparent absence of this species in Poland might be caused by different factors, including the vulnerability of the host, competitive dynamics between nematodes9, or deworming protocols for livestock in the studied area22.

The strong association between T. skrjabini infection and ocular lesions confirms its clinical relevance, consistent with prior reports in wild and domestic ruminants2,9. Conversely, the presence of T. gulosa was not significantly linked to ocular lesions, despite its higher individual worm burden, suggesting a less pathogenic role for this species. Additionally, T. gulosa consistently outnumbered T. skrjabini, also in co-infections, which may reflect competitive advantage or niche differentiation between the species; however, no clear differences in the location of nematodes were observed during the study.

The rate of co-infections with both Thelazia species was higher than previously described9 probably as an effect of exposure to many species of muscid fly vectors11,27,28. Infection intensity and prevalence over the years showed no significant seasonal variation probably because European bison are not subjected to seasonal deworming, like domestic ruminants22,29. In the latter, distinct patterns for higher Thelazia parameters in late summer and autumn were recorded, possibly connected with the activity of Muscidae flies and of cattle deworming during spring9,11,30,31. In addition, global warming might favour the activity of vectors throughout the year32, thus it might be considered one of the reasons for the high prevalence of Thelazia nematodes in European bison and their uniform seasonal occurrence.

No relationship between Thelazia infection and altitude or latitude has been observed, despite other authors reporting that higher altitude limits the presence of eye-frequenting flies and thus the prevalence of eyeworms9,31. One reason might be the similar ecological features of European bison populations examined or similar distribution of vectors, which favour homogeneous occurrence of parasites across studied habitats. Individual factors, like age and sex of animals also did not affect Thelazia infection in our study.

Other authors reported Thelazia nematodes more often in adult animals which might be related to re-infections and absence of immunological response of the host11,27,32. Previous studies of European bison in Poland revealed higher parasitic loads in females, which live in herds in comparison with males leaving solitary life16. It is possible that severe ocular lesions in most examined European bison in the present study, together with probable bacterial or viral infections33–35 might modulate disease outcome and affect nematode lifespan and survival, thus hindering impact of individual or environmental factors on Thelazia parameters.

This study presents the most extensive epidemiological assessment of Thelazia spp. infections in European bison to date, highlighting trends in prevalence, species composition, and associated ocular pathology. The obtained results revealed that Thelazia spp. is a common parasite of European bison, equally spread across different studied populations, irrespective of habitat, geographical region, season or individual factors, which might suggest endemicity of thelaziosis in this animal species. Moreover, European bison seems to be more vulnerable to infection with T. skrjabini, and a significant association between this eyeworm species and severe ocular lesions indicates its pathogenicity. However, other bacterial and viral factors that remain unrecognized may influence the development of ocular lesions in European bison, thereby affecting the estimated pathogenic contribution of T. skrjabini. Since the examined animals were predominantly culled due to health problems, including ocular lesions, the reported data should be interpreted with caution and regarded primarily as evidence of the clinical significance of eyeworm infection, rather than an accurate estimate of its true prevalence in the population, which is likely lower. Under the above circumstances, it is crucial to perform further studies, considering the presence, biology and seasonality of Thelazia vectors, parasite-host interactions and the impact of climate change on high nematode prevalence. In addition, examination of Thelazia infection in domestic ruminants in the studied areas is necessary to assess livestock exposure to eyeworms and parasite transmission patterns.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

Between 2020 and 2024, 223 European bison were culled according to the permission of the General Directorate for Environmental Protection in Poland implemented by the Regional Head Offices of National Forests. Culling was a part of a population-control program officially carried out in Poland and the decision to cull a particular bison was made based on apparent health problems, including injuries, infectious diseases, and ocular signs. Animals were shot by professional hunters with respect to the animal’s welfare and safety rules in the presence and assistance of qualified veterinarians. Data on sex, age, geographic origin, genetic line along with the sampling date were collected and recorded for each animal.

The study included 129 males (57.9%) and 94 females, aged from 3 months to 24 years, with a median age of 12 years (IQR: 7 − 15 years). Lowland-Caucasian genetic line (LC) of European bison was represented by 157 animals (70.4%) from the Bieszczady Mountains in Southern Poland, while 66 individuals (29.6%) belonged to Lowland genetic line (LB) of European bison, including 56 animals from Białowieża Forest, Knyszyńska Forest, Augustowska Forest, and Borecka Forest in Northeastern Poland and 10 individuals from Pszczyna Breeding Center in Southern Poland (Fig. 4).

European bison were examined almost half by half in the grazing season (May to October) (n = 116; 52.0%) and the winter season (November to April) (n = 107; 48.0%).

Examination of eyes

Anatomopathological changes in the eyes were assessed by a qualified veterinarian during the post-mortem examination of European bison as described in Filip-Hutsch et al.17. Severity of ocular lesions was scored from 0 to 3 according to Filip-Hutsch et al.17, with 0 indicating clinically normal eyes, 1 – increased lacrimation and/or mild conjunctivitis (mild lesions), 2 – keratitis and conjunctivitis, corneal ulceration, purulent ocular inflammation, fibrin deposits (moderate lesions), and 3 – deformation of the eyeball (severe lesions).

Both eyeballs and adjacent tissues were collected from each animal, preserved in 70% ethanol, and transported at 4℃ for further analysis. Under laboratory conditions, eyes were subjected to parasitological examination by rinsing the conjunctival sac, tear ducts, and surface of the cornea in tap water. Nematodes of the genus Thelazia were isolated from the decantated eye sediment under the stereoscopic microscope (Delta Optical SZ-450 T, Delta Optical, Poland) and preserved in 70% ethanol.

Morphological and molecular analysis of thelazia nematodes

Nematodes were cleared in lactophenol solution and observed for morphometric examination by optical microscopy (Zeiss Axioscope 5) with Zen Pro version 3.1 software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmH, Germany). Specimens were morphologically and morphometrically identified according to Skrjabin12 and Anderson1.

Molecular analysis was performed to confirm the morphological identification. For this purpose, genomic DNA was extracted from individual worms using Dneasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Thereafter PCR analysis was performed using the primers NTF (5′-TGATTGGTGGTTTTGGTAA-3′) and NTR (5′-ATAAGTACGAGTATCAATATC-3′) that amplify a portion (689 bp) of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene as previously described36. Amplicons were purified directly from the from the reaction product and sequenced in both directions using the Big Dye Terminator v.3.1 chemistry in a 3130 Genetic analyzer (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) in an automated sequencer (ABI-PRISM 377). Sequences were analyzed using FinchTV Sequence Alignment Software and AliView program and compared with sequences available in GenBank through the BLAST search tool.

For phylogenetic analysis, cox1 gene sequences of Thelazia spp. herein obtained were assessed other sequences available in the GenBank database. The evolutionary history was inferred by using the Maximum Likelihood method and Tamura-Nei model37. The initial tree for the heuristic search was obtained automatically by applying Neighbor-Join and BioNJ algorithms to a matrix of pairwise distances estimated using the Tamura-Nei model and then selecting the topology with superior log likelihood value. A discrete Gamma distribution was used to model evolutionary rate differences among sites (5 categories (+ G, parameter = 0.2674)). Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA X38–40.

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables were examined for normality of distribution using the normal probability Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the assumption of normality was violated for European bison age (p = 0.001), body weight (p < 0.001), and Thelazia count (p < 0.001). Therefore, the numerical variables were summarised as the median, interquartile range (IQR), and range and they were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon signed rank test (2 paired groups), Mann-Whitney U test (2 unpaired groups), or Kruskal-Wallis H test (> 2 unpaired groups). Correlations between numerical variables were examined using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (Rs), and the correlation was classified as weak (Rs >−0.3 or < 0.3), moderate (Rs = −0.7 to −0.3 or 0.3 to 0.7), and strong (Rs <−0.7 or > 0.7). Categorical variables were summarised as the numbers (n) and proportions (%), and 95% confidence intervals (CI 95%) were calculated using the Wilson score method41. Categorical variables were compared between groups using the maximum likelihood G test, and the trend was examined using the χ2 test for trends42. Strength of association was measured as the odds ratio (OR). All tests were two-sided, and the significance level (α) was set at 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed in TIBCO Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA).
