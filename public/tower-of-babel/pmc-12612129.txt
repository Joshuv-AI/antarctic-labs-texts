Enhanced stabilisation of soil carbon via arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and biochar
Authors: Alexander R G Mason, Bahareh Bicharanloo, Matthias Salomon, Andrew Lowe, Feike A Dijkstra, Timothy Cavagnaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fate of carbon (C) in soil is governed by a complex interplay of chemical, physical, and biological processes that determine whether C inputs are rapidly respired or stabilised within more permanent pools. The latter is critical for climate change mitigation, as soil can act as both a source and sink of atmospheric CO₂. Optimising stabilisation pathways is therefore essential to increasing soil C persistence and ensuring meaningful sequestration outcomes. Despite growing recognition of the role of fungi in soil C cycling, the biological mechanisms governing long-term soil C storage remain poorly understood. Here, we introduce the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory, which proposes that fungal hyphae can transport plant-derived C from the plant sphere to more stable soil environments. We test this theory by examining the effects of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) inoculation on C partitioning across plant and soil pools using stable isotope ¹³C pulse labelling. We also evaluated biochar as a potential facilitator of this process, given its role in enhancing carbon retention through physical protection, adsorption capacity, and modulation of microbial activity. Biochar did not alter AMF colonisation; however, elevated ¹³C concentrations in both the stable soil fraction and biochar-associated extracts under AMF colonisation indicate that AMF facilitate C transfer away from the highly respiratory root zone toward physically protected microsites. These results point to a previously underappreciated pathway of soil C stabilisation, in which fungal-mediated transport and biochar interactions contribute to C accrual through physical protection and sorption mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23219-0.Keywords: Soil carbon sequestration, Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), Stable isotope (13C) tracing, Mineral-associated organic matter (MAOM), Hyphal Carbon Transfer TheorySubject terms: Carbon cycle, Fungal systems biology

Introduction

Soils are the largest terrestrial reservoir of organic carbon (C), containing more than twice the amount present in the atmosphere1,2. The cycling and stabilisation of soil C is therefore central to regulating atmospheric CO₂ concentrations and mitigating climate change. However, climate change itself threatens soil C stocks, as warming and drying are expected to accelerate microbial decomposition and increase CO₂ release. Indeed, some soil systems are projected to become net sources of atmospheric CO₂ over the coming decades3. A key challenge is therefore ensuring the permanence of sequestered C, as the climate benefit of soil C accrual depends on the longevity of C retained in stable pools. These pools are often described as recalcitrant, a term historically used to denote C that persists due to chemical resistance but increasingly also applied to physically protected fractions. Here we use the term stable, as we focus on and measure soil C retained through physical stabilisation, rather than chemical resistance or size and density fractionation methods. This growing focus on stability has intensified interest in the biological and physicochemical processes that govern C turnover, including the role of fungi in decomposition, nutrient cycling, and C re-processing. As dominant agents of organic matter decomposition and nutrient cycling, fungi influence the flux of CO₂ from soils to the atmosphere as well as contributing to broader ecosystem processes4–6. Several fungal functional groups contribute to soil C cycling through mechanisms including enhanced plant growth, stress tolerance, and necromass production. Among these groups, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) have been highlighted as a promising contributor to soil C sequestration, with AMF inoculation associated with positive impacts on soil C dynamics7. AMF exchange phosphorus and nitrogen for C compounds derived from their host8,9, with evidence suggesting that up to 20% of plantderived C may be transferred to fungal partners. However, the mechanisms underlying AMF-mediated carbon formation, redistribution, and stabilisation remain little understood10–13. The ability to harness AMF as an agent of soil C sequestration requires elucidation and clear understanding of these mechanisms, which may ultimately contribute to the accumulation of more stable soil C pools. While labile C pools have turnover times of days to months, stable pools, such as those occluded from microbial action via aggregation and mineral association, can persist for decades to centuries, depending on environmental conditions and protection mechanisms14,15.

Directly, AMF can enhance soil C stocks by incorporating plant-derived C into their biomass, including hyphae and mycelia, which contributes to microbial necromass—a vital and stable component of soil organic matter13,16–19. AMF also facilitate the transfer of plant-derived C into more stable soil C pools through two key processes: (1) the translocation of plant exudates away from the high respiratory activity around the root into deeper soil horizons and soil aggregates promoting the formation of aggregated C (Agg-C) which can be a highly stable form of soil C20–22; and (2) the production of more stable C compounds, such as extracellular polysaccharides and polyaromatic compounds like melanin which contribute to long-term C storage23,24. Indirectly, AMF promote increased C inputs into soil by enhancing plant growth and biomass production10. Through improved nutrient acquisition, particularly phosphorus, and increased water uptake facilitated by hyphal networks, AMF boost the photosynthetic efficiency of host plants, leading to greater C allocation to roots and soil8,25. This enhanced plant fitness can result in increased deposition of root exudates and organic matter into the soil, further augmenting soil C stocks. A large body of work also highlights rhizodeposition (the release of root exudates, mucilage, and sloughed cells) as a dominant pathway of C transfer into soils26, with direct implications on microbial activity and soil C cycling27. AMF interact with these rhizodeposits, altering their fate and stability in soil, further augmenting soil C stocks28,29. The extent to which AMF can direct these inputs along a continuum from labile toward increasingly stable soil C fractions remains poorly understood and seldom tested. Of these largely theorized yet scarcely examined mechanisms, it is the utility of AMF to promote the stable soil C fraction that is of greatest consequence in the effort to address rising atmospheric CO₂ levels. One mechanism yet explored is the ability of AMF hyphal networks to translocate photosynthetic C to mineral surfaces, supporting the generation of mineral associated organic matter C (MAOM-C), hereon referred to as the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory. The Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory proposes that AMF hyphae act as conduits for the movement of plant-derived C into the soil matrix, where it is subsequently stabilised through multiple pathways. Hyphae translocate photosynthate into more stable soil microenvironments (such as mineral surfaces) as well as re-process these inputs into fungal necromass and other microbial transformation products, both of which contribute to longer-lived C pools. In addition, AMF release extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), including glomalin-related soil proteins, which can bind organic matter to mineral surfaces and enhance soil Agg-C formation, physically protecting it from microbial decomposition. Through these combined mechanisms (organic matter translocation and re-processing, microbial byproduct production, and EPS-mediated sorption and aggregation) hyphal activity may accelerate the incorporation of plant C into MAOM-C and other physically protected fractions, representing a C stabilisation pathway that could be further exploited through the manipulation of the soil environment.

The application of targeted soil amendments is recognised as a C sequestration strategy30. Amendments such as dolomite, gypsum, and compost are used to physically and chemically modify the soil environment to enhance plant productivity and nutrient use efficiency, processes that are closely tied to C sequestration31,32. Other mineral and organic inputs have also been investigated for their potential to influence soil structure, microbial activity, and the stability of organic matter, highlighting the broader potential of amendment-based approaches33. Yet to be explored is the ability of specific amendments to support fungal-mediated pathways of C sequestration. Biochar appears a particularly promising product in this regard, possessing a highly porous structure that enhances soil aeration and water retention while providing a stable habitat for microbial and fungal communities34. Additionally, biochar’s unique surface structure and chemistry may facilitate a series of processes capable of stabilising plant- and microbial-derived C inputs. For instance, biochar adsorbs root-derived carbon (rhizodeposits) onto its surface, shielding these inputs from microbial action35. Rhizodeposits interact with biochar to form organic interfaces consisting of fine mineral coatings that accumulate on biochar surfaces, creating organo-mineral interfaces. These protective layers not only retain rhizodeposits but can also incorporate microbial necromass, further enhancing C retention. Successive cycles of adsorption and protection lead to the development of new organic and organo-mineral coatings on the biochar surface. We propose that in this way biochar can promote the formation of stable soil C phases that function analogously to MAOM-C, thereby contributing to long-term C storage35.

The physisorption capacity of biochar largely depends on its surface area and porosity, which is critical for retaining organic C molecules such as rhizodeposits and microbial byproducts36. The effect of pyrolysis temperature on pore development is well established, with higher temperatures (>700 °C) generally producing biochars of higher porosity, carbon content, and pH, but lower volatile matter and cation exchange capacity37. Biochar produced at very high temperatures (>800 °C) has been shown to develop disordered aromatic domains, sometimes described as turbostratic crystallites, which can increase microporosity (< 1 nm) and surface area38,39.Such structures may provide microenvironments favourable to the stabilisation of organic compounds and contribute to the long-term persistence of carbon in soils40. The physical inaccessibility of micropores to microbial enzymes, combined with the chemical recalcitrance of aromatic C, may play an important role in protecting organic matter from decomposition19,20. The potential for AMF hyphal networks to actively transport C to biochar surfaces, thereby facilitating its stabilisation, remains an underexplored but promising pathway for soil C sequestration research.

We hypothesised that AMF inoculation, particularly in combination with biochar, would enhance the accumulation of newly assimilated C in the stable soil C fraction. To test this, we used stable isotope ¹³C pulse labelling to trace the fate of recent photosynthate and examine how plant-derived C is partitioned across plant and soil fractions. This approach provides a powerful means to disentangle complex C transfer pathways and quantify the contribution of AMF to belowground C flows. Specifically, we tested two mechanisms:

M1. The Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory: AMF facilitate the transfer of plant-derived C away from the rhizosphere and into the biochar and bulk soil complex, enhancing the stable C pool.

M2. Biochar Adsorption: Biochar particles adsorb plant and microbial derived C inputs, protecting them from microbial decomposition.

Methods

Experimental design

A 13C pulse labelling experiment was established to explore the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory and the partitioning of C in response to mycorrhizal association and biochar application. Wheat (Triticum aestivum Stealth) was grown in a greenhouse at The University of Sydney (Camden, NSW) for 14 weeks in unamended (control) and biochar-amended soil with and without AMF, resulting in four treatment levels (control – AMF, control + AMF, biochar – AMF, biochar + AMF). Experimental soil was collected from the 0–20 cm layer of a cropping field located in central west New South Wales (Australia), sieved to < 2 mm, and autoclaved at 121 °C for 1 h. The soil was classified as a red chromosol loam with a nutrient profile consistent with that of a typical rotational cropping system in the region; total C (0.81%), total N (0.094%), K (1.03%), and P (Colwell) at 34 mg kg−1. Treatments with AMF were established using pot-culture inoculum of Rhizophagus irregularris (DAOM 181602), whilst for those without AMF we used an AMF-free ‘mock’ inoculum prepared as follows. Processed field soil was homogenised and split into two portions, one fortified with AMF inoculated soil and the other with non-inoculated (mock) soil which contained the same bacterial community without AMF. Inoculated and non-inoculated soil was self-produced in 2020 by growing marigold (Tagetes sp.) with and without AMF inside a closed bag41. Both inoculants were grown in a sandy substrate composed of nine parts autoclaved sand and one part autoclaved soil. Biochar was then added to a portion of the + and - AMF soils at a rate of ~ 1.7 t− 1 ha− 1 (1.4 g pot− 1) and batched ready for potting. Biochar was produced at The University of Adelaide using wheat straw feedstock which was pyrolyzed using a laboratory scale cylindrical reactor for 150 min at a maximum temperature of 900 °C (>800 °C/54 min).

In order to obtain background 13C levels we included an additional un-labelled set of plants for each of the four treatment groups, resulting in a total of 32 pots. A second inoculation event was undertaken after seedling emergence via the addition of 0.6 g pot− 1 of a commercially produced Rhizophagus irregularis inoculant (equating to ~ 1800 propagules pot− 1). Labelling was undertaken six weeks after planting. Plants were placed in a sealed transparent plexiglass chamber (39.5 cm width × 54.5 cm length × 60 cm height) and exposed to 13C labelled CO2 (13CO2 − 99.0 atom %, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc., Andover MA, USA). Briefly, 120 mL of 13CO2 using a 30 mL syringe (four continuous injections) was injected to raise the CO2 concentration to ~ 1000 ppm inside each transparent chamber, and reinjected once the concentration dropped to 450 ppm. The labelling was conducted over a 6-hour period, with the chamber vented twice during this period to reduce heat and stimulate greater uptake of labelled C. The CO2 concentration inside chambers was monitored using a sensor (MG811 CO2 gas module, DFRobot, Shanghai, China). Two icepacks were placed inside the chamber to prevent overheating.

Data collection

To explore the partition of C in our plant and soil pot system, we analysed several C pools for the presence of 13C, namely; aboveground plant biomass (shoot), belowground plant biomass (root), labile soil C and stable soil C. Two harvests were undertaken, one (H1) at the tillering or peak growth stage (6 weeks) and another (H2) following plant senescence or final harvest (14 weeks). 24 h following labelling the first set of plants were harvested (H1) as follows. Plants were removed from pots with roots rinsed clean in tap water. Fresh shoot and root weights were taken with material dried for 48 h at 60 °C and weighed for dry weight (DW). A separate root sub-sample (~ 0.3 g) was collected and stored in 50% ethanol for further AMF colonisation quantification via the grid intersect method42. Dried plant material was ground and analysed for 13C and total C.

We designed and employed a C water extraction assay to determine the physical stability of soil C. We diluted 3 g of fresh bulk soil with reverse osmosis water at a 10:1 dilution and shook using a reciprocating shaker for 1 h at 100 RPM. Samples were then centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm from which a supernatant was collected. The supernatant was acidified with 150 µL 1 M H3PO4 to remove inorganic C. We then added K2SO4 to a concentration of 0.05 M and solutions were dried at 40 °C, with the remaining salts ground and analysed for 13C on Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometry (IRMS) and considered as the easily extractible/labile soil fraction. The remaining soil pellet was dried at 40 °C, ground and analysed for 13C as the stable soil fraction. This method was chosen as traditional fractionation methods, such as density and size fractionation or chemical oxidation, can introduce classification challenges, particularly in biochar-amended soils where biochar particles may be misclassified as particulate organic matter C (POM-C) despite their stability43. Our water-extraction and centrifugation method was designed to differentiate labile and stable soil C pools based on solubility and physical stability, rather than relying on MAOM-C and POM-C fractionation. The method is based on the principle that easily extractable organic C (EOC) represents the unbound, labile pool of C, while the remaining C in the soil pellet consists of more physically or chemically stabilised forms, including mineral bound C and biochar associated C44–46.

To explore the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory (that AMF can facilitate the deposition of photosynthetic C to mineral surfaces) and to test the physisorption capacity of biochar, we extracted a biochar sample from the soil at H2. Briefly, remaining soil (following the abovementioned sample collections) was submerged in water, mixed, and left to settle for 30 min. Floating material (biochar) was collected using tweezers and placed in a 50 ml falcon tube and left to settle for another 24 h. The remaining material (~ 75% biochar) was then collected from tubes and dried at 60 °C for 24 h, ground and analysed for 13C.

All plant and soil samples were analysed for 13C and total C on an Elementar Vario EL Cube elemental analyzer (Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Hanau, Germany) interfaced to a PDZ Europa 20–20 isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Sercon Ltd., Cheshire, UK) at the University of California Davis.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.3.1)48. For initial visualisation, root colonisation and plant biomass variables (plant head, shoot and root) were plotted using the ggplot2 package, with global statistical tests conducted and presented in the figures to assess overall differences among treatment groups for each response variable. To assess the partitioning of C in response to AMF inoculation and biochar application we calculated the concentration of excess 13C in each of our designated C pools (shoots, roots, biochar, and soil labile and stable C). Ambient 13C levels were obtained from our un-labelled samples, deducted from the labelled samples, and multiplied by the total C content of the sample to derive the concentration of excess 13C in each pool. We also assessed the proportion of excess 13C allocated to each of our C pool as a percentage of the total excess 13C pool. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using the aov() function in the base R package. ANOVA assumptions were tested using the shapiro_test() function (for normality; base R package) and the leveneTest() function (for homogeneity of variance; car package)49. Additionally, residuals were assessed through histograms and Q-Q plots for visual inspection. When multiple models were considered (e.g. interactions of ‘Excess ¹³C’, ‘Mycorrhizal status’, ‘Soil treatment’), the best-fitting model was identified using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)50. Pairwise comparisons between treatments were conducted using the TukeyHSD() function (base R package)51. We analysed the dataset both separately for each harvest and as a pooled dataset. Select results from each analysis are presented where appropriate.

Results

Root colonisation

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi colonisation was successful, with an average root length colonised (RLC) of 36.8% across the two soil treatments. Conversely, none of the non-mycorrhizal treatments achieved an RLC above 1%. Root length colonisation in mycorrhizal treatments was not significantly influenced by soil treatment but did significantly increase at H2 compared to H1 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1/Supplementary A).

Plant biomass

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi inoculation led to a trend towards higher shoot biomass across regular/unamended treatments; however, statistical significance was not observed in either H1 or H2. The same trend was observed for root biomass in H2. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi inoculation significantly increased head (p = 0.031) and grain DW (p = 0.031) in un-amended control soil, and showed an increase with biochar addition (Fig. 2/Supplementary B &C).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the role of AMF and biochar in the partitioning of newly derived C within a plant-soil system. We employed a 13C pulse-labelling approach to quantify the fate of photosynthetically derived C across plant and soil pools, testing two core hypotheses; that AMF inoculation enhances the stable soil C pool, and that biochar promotes C stabilisation via adsorption of newly derived C products. These hypotheses map onto two proposed mechanisms: M1 the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory, whereby AMF translocate C to mineral or biochar surfaces, and M2 the physisorption of microbial and plant exudates onto biochar, forming a protected soil C pool, proposed here as biochar associated stable C (BAS-C) (Fig. 6).

A wheat seedling was grown under a ¹³CO₂ pulse-labelling chamber to trace C flow through plant and soil pools across four treatments: Control, +AMF, +Biochar, and + AMF + Biochar. We propose that AMF hyphae facilitate the transfer of recently assimilated ¹³C from the rhizosphere to surrounding soil and biochar particles. The inset shows a magnified view of the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory, highlighting microbial interactions and mineral binding. Excess ¹³C was measured in the unbound labile and mineral-bound stable soil C pools, with the + AMF + Biochar treatment resulting in greatest C stabilisation.

Biochar had no effect on mycorrhizal colonisation

Both regular and biochar-amended soils supported similarly high levels of colonisation, with no significant differences between treatments. The lack of a significant impact suggests that biochar did not hinder AMF colonisation, which is important for its application in production systems. Previous studies that have reported variable effects of biochar on AMF colonisation. For instance, while some research indicates that biochar can enhance AMF colonisation by providing favourable microhabitats and improving soil properties52, other studies have found that biochar addition may reduce mycorrhizal colonisation, potentially due to changes in soil nutrient dynamics53. This variability underscores the complexity of biochar-AMF interactions and highlights the need for context-specific investigations to optimise their combined use in agricultural systems. Further research should explore how biochar properties (e.g., production temperature, particle size, and application rate) interact with AMF colonisation and whether certain biochar formulations can enhance AMF establishment and function in soil ecosystems (e.g. nutrient lacing).

Mycorrhizal colonisation improved plant productivity

Overall, plant biomass results suggest that AMF inoculation has a positive influence on plant growth and productivity, specifically head and grain DW under regular soil conditions. However, the effects appear to be less pronounced or variable when biochar is introduced, potentially indicating interactive effects between biochar and AMF that require further investigation. This result is in line with the growing body of evidence suggesting that AMF play a crucial role in enhancing plant productivity within agricultural and natural systems by improving nutrient uptake, drought resilience, and soil structure54. Studies, including our previous work10, have demonstrated that AMF symbiosis enhances plant biomass accumulation under controlled conditions, which has potential to indirectly positively influence soil C accrual. Although this correlation was not found here, the increase in plant productivity (as evidenced by significant increases in head and grain DW) does support the case for greater utilisation of AMF products in production systems, or to manage soils to bolster natural occurring mycorrhizal communities55.

Mycorrhizal inoculation enhanced the stable soil C pool

Stable soil C was the only C pool to return a significant difference in excess 13C levels, with AMF inoculation increasing both the absolute and proportional level of 13C in this pool (Figs. 3 and 4). This result is significant and validates our hypothesis; that AMF inoculation will lead to greater stable soil C storage. In addition to direct hyphal C transfer, several other mechanisms may also explain the buildup of C in the stable soil fraction under AMF inoculation. Hyphal turnover contributes necromass rich in chitin and other stable compounds, which can persist in soils and add to stable microbial-derived pools56. AMF also promote aggregate formation by physically enmeshing soil particles and through the production of extracellular polysaccharides and glomalin-related proteins, thereby providing physical protection to organic matter within microaggregates57. AMF exudates can stimulate microbial processing of labile plant C into transformation products that are more readily adsorbed to biochar surfaces or occluded within soil aggregates, effectively accelerating their transition into less bioavailable forms. However, determining stable vs. labile soil C fractions is not a straightforward process, as soil organic C exists along a continuum of stability, influenced by physical protection, chemical composition, and microbial accessibility58,59. One limitation of our C water extraction method is that both large and small C fractions are pooled together in the soil pellet (the stable fraction), meaning some labile POM-C components (e.g., plant and root material) could be included. However, in the context of our results, this issue is mitigated by the fact that AMF status had no significant influence on the amount of C allocated to the shoot or root pools (Supplementary B & C). This suggests that differences detected in the soil pellet should be attributed to C associated with the soil or biochar fraction within the pellet, rather than any potentially incorporated particulate plant matter, as there was no difference in either 13C concentration or biomass between shoot and root pool treatments. Given the exclusion of shoot and root material pools, the excess ¹³C signal in the soil pellet likely originates from microbial transformation products, fungal necromass, root exudates, or hyphal C translocated into soil microaggregates or adsorbed to biochar surfaces. Long term, this would result in increased soil C sequestration.

Traditional soil C stabilisation models suggest that the formation of MAOM-C is a slow process, occurring over decades as organic inputs are progressively decomposed, transformed, and adsorbed onto mineral surfaces60,61. Our findings suggest that biological and physicochemical interactions, specifically those mediated by AMF and biochar amendments can accelerate the incorporation of newly introduced C into less bioavailable soil fractions, via mechanisms of physical stabilisation and biochar adsorption rather than direct mineral association. As well as providing strong support of our hypothesis, results suggest that the two key mechanisms proposed (M1 and M2) may play a central role in stable soil C storage. AMF inoculation combined with biochar increased the proportion of excess ¹³C allocated to the non-extractable/stable soil C fraction and biochar extracts (Figs. 3, 4, 5), providing direct evidence for the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory (M1). Beyond facilitating the transfer of plant-derived C, AMF hyphae have also been shown to release extracellular polysaccharides, which may contribute to the structural stabilisation of soil aggregates and indirectly enhance C persistence in the bulk soil fraction (soil pellet)62.

Biochar associated stable carbon as a distinct soil carbon pool

The second mechanism proposed here (M2); that biochar particles adsorb plant and microbial derived C inputs, protecting them from microbial decomposition is partly supported in the findings. While biochar extracts associated with AMF inoculation were found to have a higher mean proportion of excess 13C, the effect was not statistically significant. Biochar provides highly adsorptive surfaces that can bind low-molecular-weight C compounds derived from microbial and root exudates, effectively creating a physically protected C pool35. The high surface area and cation exchange capacity of biochar may further enhance C retention within soil aggregates, reducing microbial accessibility. While conventional wisdom holds that biochar is primarily a passive C sink, our results suggest that in biologically active systems, biochar may act as a facilitator of C stabilisation, protecting C from decomposition through adsorption and occlusion mechanisms. The standard deviation in the biochar extract + AMF group was 14.8 mg g− 1, compared to 3.37 mg g− 1 in the non-inoculated group, resulting in a coefficient of variation of 100% and 60%, respectively. This high variability in the AMF treatment may have masked statistical significance despite the observed mean increase in excess ¹³C of 163% (Fig. 5). Although falling short of fully validating our concept, our findings offer positive preliminary insights that strongly warrant further investigation using a more targeted experimental approach. We propose that biochar-bound C, while not traditionally classified as MAOM-C, may function as a stabilised C pool through adsorption, microbial necromass retention, and physical occlusion. This leads us to introduce the concept of BAS-C as a distinct category of persistent soil C. BAS-C represents a pool that is less bioavailable than POM-C but does not strictly conform to MAOM-C, likely contributing to medium to long-term C stabilisation.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that AMF-driven soil C sequestration mechanisms, specifically hyphal C transport and biochar-mediated retention, may have potential to significantly enhance the persistence of soil C inputs. We provide evidence that AMF inoculation, in combination with biochar, enhances the stabilisation of newly introduced C in soil systems. Our findings challenge traditional models of soil C stabilisation, which assume slow MAOM-C generation over decades. Instead, results suggest that AMF and biochar amendments can accelerate C stabilisation processes via the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory. This has important implications for C sequestration strategies, particularly in agricultural systems where soil amendments are routinely applied to enhance productivity. The introduction of the BAS-C concept highlights a potentially distinct category of soil C that has, until now, been unquantified. We propose that the Hyphal Carbon Transfer Theory and its associated mechanisms contribute to a previously overlooked pathway of C stabilisation, wherein fungal-mediated transport and biochar interactions facilitate stable soil C accrual. While our study provides initial experimental evidence supporting these processes, further research is needed to determine the long-term stability of BAS-C and its role in broader soil C cycling models. These results align with previous findings demonstrating positive AMF + biochar effects on wheat growth and soil C dynamics, while extending this knowledge by providing direct isotopic evidence of the mechanisms underpinning C stabilisation. Future studies should assess the interactions between AMF, biochar, and MAOM-C across different soil types, amendments and environmental conditions to refine our understanding of these potential stabilisation pathways.
