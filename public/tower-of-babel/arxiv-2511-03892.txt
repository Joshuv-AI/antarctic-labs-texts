A general technique for approximating high-dimensional empirical kernel matrices
Chiraag Kaushik, Justin Romberg, Vidya Muthukumar

Abstract
We present simple, user-friendly bounds for the expected operator norm of a random kernel matrix under general conditions on the kernel function k ⁡ ( ⋅ , ⋅ ) k(\cdot,\cdot) . Our approach uses decoupling results for U-statistics and the non-commutative Khintchine inequality to obtain upper and lower bounds depending only on scalar statistics of the kernel function and a “correlation kernel” matrix corresponding to k ⁡ ( ⋅ , ⋅ ) k(\cdot,\cdot) . We then apply our method to provide new, tighter approximations for inner-product kernel matrices on general high-dimensional data, where the sample size and data dimension are polynomially related. Our method obtains simplified proofs of existing results that rely on the moment method and combinatorial arguments while also providing novel approximation results for the case of anisotropic Gaussian data. Finally, using similar techniques to our approximation result, we show a tighter lower bound on the bias of kernel regression with anisotropic Gaussian data.

Introduction

Kernel methods are commonly employed to solve a range of problems in engineering, science, statistics, and machine learning
[
49
]
. Traditionally, much of the power of these methods has been derived from the fact that, in classical statistical settings with fixed data dimension, the eigenvalues of the empirical kernel matrix derived from samples behave akin to those of the original kernel integral operator
[
30
]
. Together with the universal approximation properties of several common kernels
[
50

[

,

[
28
]
showed that in the proportional regime where the number of samples is proportional to the data dimension (

inner-product kernel
(i.e., a kernel of the form

curse of dimensionality
for kernel methods, where the nonlinear approximation power becomes negligible when the dimension of the data is proportional to the sample size.

More recently, equivalences between certain types of wide neural networks and kernel methods

11

[

,
24
,
36

polynomial scaling regime

, where the number of samples scales as a polynomial power

) and/or specialized assumptions on the data (uniform on the sphere/Boolean hypercube) due to the intricate dependencies between entries of the empirical kernel matrix. Indeed, even the proofs of the results in these specialized settings, e.g.,

\lfloor q\rfloor

The central goal of this work is to provide a sharp and general-purpose technique for kernel matrix approximation, applicable in high-dimensional regimes like the above. Specifically, we aim to provide a general technique that can be used to show tight bounds on

Contributions:

In this paper, we first provide a general-purpose bound on the the expected operator norm of an empirical kernel matrix under minimal distributional assumptions and mild integrability conditions on the kernel function
k(\cdot,\cdot)

We then focus especially on applying our result to inner-product kernels that include those derived as the asymptotic limit of random-feature models/wide neural networks when the number of features/width tends to infinity. For such kernels, our technique recovers the specialized kernel matrix approximation results of

While we apply our bound primarily to these types of approximation problems, we have not seen our general kernel matrix bounds stated in this form in the existing literature, and we believe they may find broader use in the analysis of kernel methods on high-dimensional data. Partial progress:

Our results leave open the question of whether the “polynomial approximation barrier” can be tightened further from
\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor
to the conjectured
\lfloor q\rfloor
under general anisotropic data.
However, Theorem
1
, being an upper bound that is matched in our applications by a lower bound (up to logarithmic factors in
n
), provides valuable insight.
In particular, the lower bound in Corollary
2
shows that the approximation barrier cannot be improved beyond
\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor
,
even for isotropic Gaussian data
, if univariate Hermite polynomials are used for the approximation.
This highlights a subtle and fundamental distinction between the utility of using different orthogonal decompositions in kernel matrix approximation and mirrors the key intuition of the recent work
[
27

, which also argues that the spherical harmonics (rather than Hermite polynomials) are a more natural univariate basis for analyzing a certain family of single-index models.
For the isotropic Gaussian case, it is possible to achieve the correct approximation barrier of
\lfloor q\rfloor

\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}_{d})
into independent norm and unit vector terms — this allows us to approximate the kernel matrix by a degree
\lfloor q\rfloor

that are uniformly distributed on the sphere by appealing to the Gegenbauer polynomial expansion (with random coefficients depending on the norms of the data points).
We formalize this result in Proposition
1

As we discuss briefly in Section
4
, this polar decomposition trick can also be applied to anisotropic Gaussian data, but the rescaled vector terms now become anisotropically scaled versions of a uniform distribution on the sphere and are far more complex to deal with.
Finding the right orthogonal basis and decomposition for this case is an important question that we leave open.
However, our results already rule out the Hermite polynomial basis for approximation and provide a flexible testbed for alternatives.

Related work

Decoupling and bounds on random matrices with dependent entries

Decoupling inequalities, which aim to reduce stochastic dependencies between random variables, have been developed and applied extensively in the study of U-statistics
[
14
,
13
]

[

3

[

[

[
3
]
. In this paper, we explore the use of decoupling inequalities and the NCK inequality to bound the expected norm of random empirical kernel matrices, a domain we have not seen previously explored in the literature. Similar to the recent work
[
53
]
, which studies norms of matrix-valued polynomial chaoses, we find that decoupling leads to much simpler and more generalizable proofs than the popular moment/trace method, which aims to bound
\E\lVert\bm{K}\rVert^{2p}\leq\E[\operatorname{tr}(\bm{K})^{2p}]

. This type of bound typically requires intricate combinatorial arguments and counting the number of occurrences of different dependency subclasses
[
1

53
]
. Moreover, the techniques used are often tailored to a specific problem’s structure. By contrast, the decoupling approach we use allows for bounds that depend only on simple scalars related to the kernel function and a correlation kernel matrix which we show has a simple form in many applications of interest.

On high-dimensional data, we expect the bias to be a significant factor due to non-trivial approximation error

We provide one such partial characterization in the form of a tighter lower bound on the bias of inner-product kernels on general anisotropic Gaussian data (Theorem

An alternative approach, that is powerful when we have explicit access to the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the kernel integral operator, is to appeal to linear model analysis by showing equivalence to

Main result

In this section, we develop a general bound for the expected operator norm of random kernel matrices. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be independent variables in a probability space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})
, and let
k\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive semi-definite kernel function satisfying
\E\lvert k(x_{1},x_{2})\rvert<\infty

\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\bm{K}_{ij}=k(x_{i},x_{j})

\bm{K}

Theorem 1

(General kernel matrix upper bound)
.

Let
z,x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}~\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}~\mathcal{P}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\E\lVert\bm{K}\rVert

\displaystyle\lesssim\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|k(x_{i},x_{i})|+n\sqrt{\log{n}\E[\E_{z}[k(x_{1},z)]^{2}]}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n\log{n}\E\lVert\bm{G}\rVert}+\log{n}\sqrt{n\E\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(k(z,x_{i})-\E_{x_{i}}k(z,x_{i}))^{2}\right]},

where
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the correlation matrix with entries given by
G_{ij}=\E_{z}[k(x_{i},z)k(z,x_{j})]
.

Before proceeding with the proof, we note that this result obtains an upper bound in terms of relatively simple scalar quantities related to the statistics of
k

\bm{G}

First, by separating the diagonal and off-diagonal parts of

The first step is to relate the operator norm of

In particular, note that we can write

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{k(x_{i},x_{j})}{2}(\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}).

Here, the range space of each

.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\|\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}\|\Big|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\right]\lesssim\left\lVert\E\left[\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}\big|(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\right]\right\rVert+\sqrt{\log(n)\cdot V}+\log(n)\cdot L,

(2)

where we define

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\E\left[(\bm{Z}_{j}-\E[\bm{Z}_{j}\big|(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})])^{2}\Big|(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\right]\right\rVert\text{ and }

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle=\E\left[\max_{j}\left\lVert\bm{Z}_{j}-\E[\bm{Z}_{j}\big|(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})]\right\rVert^{2}\Big|(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\right].

Consequently, to upper bound the original quantity of interest,
\E\left[\|\bm{\Delta}\|\right]
, it now suffices to upper bound the expectation of the RHS of Equation (
2
) over the original data
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. To this end, we individually characterize each term appearing in Equation (
2
). In the remainder of the proof, we will use the shorthand
\E_{\tilde{x}}[\cdot]\coloneqq\E_{(\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n})}[\cdot]
for brevity.

Bounding the norm of the expected matrix:

For convenience, define the function
h(x)=\E[k(x,\tilde{x})\>|\>x]
. Then, this term can be written as

\displaystyle\E\left\lVert\E_{\tilde{x}}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\E[\lVert\bm{H}+\bm{H}^{\top}\rVert],

where
H_{ij}=h(x_{i})
. By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\E\left\lVert\E_{\tilde{x}}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\E[\lVert\bm{H}\rVert]

\displaystyle=\E\left\lVert(h(x_{1}),\dots,h(x_{n}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\E h(x_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=n\sqrt{\E h(x_{1})^{2}}=n\sqrt{\E[\E_{z}[k(x_{1},z)]^{2}]},

where the second-to-last line follows from Jensen’s inequality.

Bounding
L
:

Note that for any
j
, the matrix
\bm{Z}_{j}-\E\bm{Z}_{j}
is symmetric and consists of a single non-zero row and column. Hence, we can upper bound the operator norm by the Frobenius norm to obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{Z}_{j}-\E_{\tilde{x}}\bm{Z}_{j}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\bm{Z}_{j}-\E_{\tilde{x}}\bm{Z}_{j}\right\rVert^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{4}(k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})-\E_{\tilde{x}}[k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})])^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})-\E_{\tilde{x}}[k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})])^{2}.

Substituting into the expression for
L
, we arrive at

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\tilde{x}}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})-\E_{\tilde{x}}[k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})])^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E_{\tilde{x}}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}(k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})-\E_{\tilde{x}}[k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})])^{2}.

So, taking the expectation over
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and applying Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\E L

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\E L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\max_{j}\left[k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})-\E_{\tilde{x}}[k(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})]\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\E_{(z,x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(k(z,x_{i})-\E[k(z,x_{i})\>|\>z])^{2}}.

Bounding
V
:

For simplicity of notation, let
\bar{k}(x_{1},x_{2}):=k(x_{1},x_{2})-\E_{x_{2}}k(x_{1},x_{2})
be the centered kernel (with respect to the second input
x_{2}
). We also define the matrix
\bar{\bm{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to have entries given by
\bar{\bm{G}}_{i,i^{\prime}}=\E_{z}[\bar{k}(x_{i},z)\bar{k}(x_{i^{\prime}},z)]
. Note that
\bar{\bm{G}}
is itself a PSD matrix, since it is a Gram matrix with entries given by inner products in
L^{2}(\mathcal{P})
. We will also write
\bar{\bm{G}}_{\setminus j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to denote the “leave-one-out” versions of
\bar{\bm{G}}
, where the
j
-th row and column are set to
0
.

With this notation in hand, we can compute

\displaystyle\E_{\tilde{x}}[(\bm{Z}_{j}-\E_{\tilde{x}}\bm{Z}_{j})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E_{\tilde{x}}\left(\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{2}\bar{k}(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})(\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{i})^{\top}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\neq j}\E_{\tilde{x}_{j}}[\bar{k}(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{j})\bar{k}(x_{i^{\prime}},\tilde{x}_{j})](\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}+\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\bar{\bm{G}}_{\setminus j}+\sum_{i\neq j}\bar{\bm{G}}_{ii}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\right).

Therefore, we have

V=\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bar{\bm{G}}_{\setminus j}+\sum_{i\neq j}\bar{\bm{G}}_{ii}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\right)\right\rVert.

(3)

Applying the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{\bm{G}}_{\setminus j}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\neq j}\bar{\bm{G}}_{ii}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\bar{\bm{G}}\odot((n-2)\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}+\bm{I}_{n})\right\rVert+\max_{j}\sum_{i\neq j}\bar{\bm{G}}_{ii}

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{\leq}n\lVert\bar{\bm{G}}\rVert+\operatorname{tr}{\bar{\bm{G}}}\lesssim n\lVert\bar{\bm{G}}\rVert,

where inequality (1) uses the triangle inequality. Finally, taking the expectation over
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and using Jensen’s inequality, we arrive at

\displaystyle\E_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\sqrt{V}\leq\sqrt{n\E\lVert\bar{\bm{G}}\rVert}.

To convert this into a bound in terms of the uncentered correlation kernel matrix
\bm{G}
(corresponding to the original kernel
k
), note that
\bar{\bm{G}}=\bm{G}-\bm{G}^{\prime}
, where
G^{\prime}_{i,j}=\E_{z}[k(x_{i},z)]\E_{z}[k(x_{j},z)]
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\E\lVert\bar{\bm{G}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\E\lVert\bm{G}\rVert+\E\lVert\bm{G}^{\prime}\rVert.

We bound the second term using a Frobenius norm upper bound as below:

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{G}^{\prime}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\E_{z}\left[k(x_{i},z)\right]^{2}\E_{z}\left[k(x_{j},z)\right]^{2}}.

Taking the expectation over
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and again using Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\E_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\lVert\bm{G}^{\prime}\rVert\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\E_{x_{i}}(\E_{z}\left[k(x_{i},z)\right])^{2})^{2}}\leq n\E_{x_{1}}(\E_{z}k(x_{1},z))^{2}.

Combining the above, we obtain the final bound on
V
:

\E_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\sqrt{V}\leq\sqrt{n\E\lVert\bm{G}\rVert}+n\sqrt{\E_{x_{1}}(\E_{z}k(x_{1},z))^{2}}.

Substituting each of these 3 bounds into Equation (
2
) completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

2.1
Lower bound

We next note that the key steps of the above proof (namely, decoupling and the application of the non-commutative Khintchine inequality) also have matching lower bounds, so we can also obtain a similar lower bound on
\E\lVert\bm{K}\rVert
. The lower bound we state holds for general kernel functions with conditionally zero mean, i.e., where the expectation when conditioning on one input is zero. We note that we expect the dominant term in the lower bound to be
\E\sqrt{n\lVert\bm{G}\rVert}
, which matches the expression obtained in the proof of Theorem
1
(in the proof of Theorem
1
we further upper bound this using Jensen’s inequality, yielding the term
\sqrt{n\E\lVert\bm{G}\rVert}
— we did this for convenient usage in applications to follow).

Theorem 2

(General kernel matrix lower bound)
.

Assume the kernel function
k
additionally satisfies
\E[k(x,z)\>|\>z]=0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\E\lVert\operatorname{diag}^{\perp}(\bm{K})\rVert

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max\left\{\E\sqrt{n\lVert\bm{G}\rVert},\E\sqrt{\sum_{i>1}G_{ii}}\right\},

where
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the correlation kernel matrix defined in Theorem
1
.

Proof.

Again, directly applying the decoupling inequality (Theorem 1 in
[
14
]
) — after noting that the corresponding
f_{ij}
’s are symmetric — gives us

\displaystyle\E\left[\|\bm{\Delta}\|\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\E\left[\|\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}\|\right],

(4)

with
\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}
defined as in the proof of Theorem
1
. Again conditioning on
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and applying Theorem 1 of
[
51
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\E\left[\|\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}\|\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\E\left[\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{Z}_{j}\right\rVert\Big|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\right]\right]\gtrsim\E\sqrt{V}.

Substituting the expression for
V
computed in Equation (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\E\sqrt{V}

\displaystyle=\E\sqrt{\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{G}_{\setminus j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\neq j}G_{ii}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\E\sqrt{\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{G}_{\setminus j}\right\rVert},\E\sqrt{\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\neq j}G_{ii}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\right\rVert}\right\},

where we use the fact that
\lVert\bm{A}+\bm{B}\rVert\geq\max\{\lVert\bm{A}\rVert,\lVert\bm{B}\rVert\}
for two PSD matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
. The first term can be written as

\displaystyle\E\sqrt{\left\lVert\bm{G}\odot((n-2)\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}+\bm{I}_{n})\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\E\sqrt{\left\lVert(n-2)\bm{G}+\operatorname{diag}(\bm{G})\right\rVert}\gtrsim\E\sqrt{n\lVert\bm{G}\rVert},

and the second term can be bounded below by

\displaystyle\E\sqrt{\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i\neq j}G_{ii}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\geq\E\sqrt{\sum_{i>1}G_{ii}}.

This concludes the proof of the lower bound.
∎

3
Applications of Theorem
1

In this section, we apply our general theorem to a few situations of interest that arise in the study of high-dimensional kernel regression equipped with inner-product kernels.
As mentioned in the introduction, in all of the applications we will use the theorem to bound the error of the original empirical kernel matrix with respect to a suitable low-degree approximation. Before proceeding, we note that although we state these corollaries as bounds on the expected norm, a simple application of Markov’s inequality can be used to convert our results to high-probability bounds. For example, we can conclude that the same upper bounds with an additional multiplicative factor of
\log{n}
hold with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{\log{n}}
.

3.1
Gegenbauer polynomial kernels

We first consider
Gegenbauer polynomial kernels
which arise naturally in the analysis of inner-product kernels on data that are uniformly distributed on the sphere
[
23
,
38
]
.
In this case, we will show that Theorem
1
directly gives the approximation-theoretic bounds proved in
[
23
,
38
]
as a simple corollary.

Let
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}
be independent and uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
, and let
k(\bm{x},\bm{y})=Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle)
, where
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}\colon[-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}]\to\mathbb{R}
is the
\ell
-th Gegenbauer polynomial. These polynomials form an orthogonal basis for the space
L^{2}([-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}],\tilde{\tau}_{d-1})
where
\tilde{\tau}_{d-1}
is the distribution of
\sqrt{d}\langle\bm{x},\bm{e}_{1}\rangle
when
\bm{x}\sim\text{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
. We follow the normalization convention in
[
23
]
, which is restated below:

\langle Q_{k}^{(d)},Q_{\ell}^{(d)}\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\tau}_{d-1})}=\frac{1}{B(d,\ell)}\delta_{k\ell},

(5)

where
B(d,\ell)\asymp d^{\ell}
denotes the number of spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
in
d
dimensions. Under this scaling, we also have
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(d)=1
and
the crucial property that for
\bm{x},\bm{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
and
\bm{y}\sim\text{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
,

\displaystyle\E_{\bm{y}}\left[Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle)Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(\langle\bm{y},\bm{z}\rangle)\right]=\frac{1}{B(d,\ell)}Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{z}\rangle)\delta_{k\ell}.

(6)

Note that Equation (
6
) essentially implies that the correlation matrix of a Gegenbauer polynomial kernel is equal to the original Gegenbauer kernel matrix scaled down by the factor
B(d,\ell)
. We refer the reader to
[
23
]
for further background on these polynomials.

Consider the off-diagonal component of the Gegenbauer polynomial kernel matrix, denoted by
\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}
, with entries

\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}_{ij}=Q_{k}(\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}\rangle)\mathbf{1}\{i\neq j\}.

Applying Theorem
1
, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 1
.

For the Gegenbauer polynomial matrix described above and
\ell>0
,

\sqrt{nd^{-\ell}}\lesssim\E[\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert]\lesssim_{\log}nd^{-\ell}+\sqrt{nd^{-\ell}}.

In particular, if
n\asymp d^{q}
for
q<\ell
, then
\E[\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert]=o_{d}(1)
.

Before proceeding to the proof, we note that this corollary recovers the main result of
[
23
, Proposition 3]
and
[
41
, Proposition 13]
via a much simpler argument that does not rely on the moment method and involved combinatorial calculations. This result is important in the analysis of kernel regression with uniform spherical data and can be used to show that, in the polynomial scaling regime
n\asymp d^{q}
, kernel regression estimates for a wide class of inner product kernels behave like low-degree polynomial kernels up to degree
exactly equal to

\lfloor q\rfloor
.
This, in turn, facilitates a sharp analysis of kernel ridge/ridgeless regression — see
[
23
]
for such a full analysis, and also the subsequent works
[
38
,
41
]
.
We additionally note that Corollary
1
recovers the optimal rate of
[
41
, Proposition 13]
up to a poly-logarithmic factor in
n
.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

Applying Theorem
1
and the property of Gegenbauer polynomials in Equation (
6
), we obtain (suppressing log factors):

\E[\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert]\lesssim_{\log}\sqrt{nd^{-\ell}(\E\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert+1)}+\sqrt{n\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}}.

For the latter term, note that

\displaystyle\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\E\max_{i}\left[Q_{\ell}\left(\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}\rangle\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{c}(n)\cdot\E_{\bm{z}}\left\lVert Q_{\ell}(\langle\cdot,\bm{z}\rangle)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we apply Lemma
6
conditionally on
\bm{z}
. We can bound the
L^{2}
norm using Equation (
6
) by noting that (with the expectation conditional on
\bm{z}
)

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\ell}(\langle\cdot,\bm{z}\rangle)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\E_{\bm{x}}\left[Q_{\ell}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{z}\rangle)^{2}\right]\asymp d^{-\ell}.

Substituting this into the bound above we obtain

\E[\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert]\lesssim_{\log}\sqrt{nd^{-\ell}(\E\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert+1)}+\sqrt{nd^{-\ell}},

which implies the stated upper bound. For the lower bound, we apply Theorem
2
and only take the second term in the maximum to conclude that

\E[\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\rVert]\gtrsim\E\sqrt{\sum_{i>1}d^{-\ell}}\asymp\sqrt{nd^{-\ell}}.

∎

A similar result also holds for the “hypercubic Gegenbauer” polynomials studied in
[
38
]
and uniform data on the binary hypercube; the argument is identical, so we do not include it here.

3.2
Hermite polynomial kernels

In this subsection and the next section, we turn to the more difficult problem of approximating an empirical kernel matrix whose entries consist of inner-product kernel evaluations on high-dimensional, anisotropic Gaussian data.
Recall that the only tight approximations known in this case were obtained for the linear regime
d\propto n
(corresponding to
q=1
)
[
28
]
and the quadratic regime
d\propto n^{2}
(corresponding to
q=2
)
[
45
]
.
Ultimately, we will present improved approximation results for the general polynomial regime
d\propto n^{q}
, where
q
may or may not be an integer.
A natural candidate for polynomial approximation, as put forward by
[
45
]
, would be the univariate Hermite polynomials.
Formally, let
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
. Denote
\tau_{k}\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{k})
and
R=\frac{\tau_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{4}}
. The quantities
\tau_{k}
and
R
can be considered notions of
effective dimension
that all reduce to
d
when
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}_{d}
; we will see that the bounds we obtain depend on these quantities in a nuanced way. Consider the off-diagonal component of the Hermite polynomial kernel matrix
\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}
given by

\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}_{ij}\coloneqq\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)\mathbf{1}\left\{i\neq j\right\}.

Accounting for differences in scaling between the Gegenbauer polynomials considered in the previous section, a natural conjecture (in the isotropic/well-conditioned case) would be that
d^{-\ell/2}\E\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\right\rVert\to 0
as
n,d\to\infty
provided that
\ell>q
, which would prove the desired polynomial approximation barrier of degree-
\lfloor q\rfloor
(that matches the spherical/hypercubic cases).
Showing this would be extremely challenging via the standard moment method.
This is because the combinatorial “skeletonization” process of
[
23
]
involves repeatedly computing correlation-matrix entries, but the elegant identity of Equation (
6
) no longer holds — instead, only an
approximate
form of this identity can be shown to hold (see our Lemma
5
).
On the other hand, because the bound of Theorem
1
only requires calculating the correlation matrix once, it is much simpler to work with.
In particular, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2

(Operator norm of Hermite matrices)
.

Let
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
. For any
\ell\geq 0
, the matrix
\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}
satisfies

\E\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\right\rVert\lesssim_{\log}\sqrt{n}+nR^{-\ell/4}.

Furthermore, if
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}_{d}
and
\ell\geq 4
is even, we have the lower bound

\E\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\right\rVert\gtrsim nd^{\lfloor\frac{\ell}{4}\rfloor-\frac{\ell}{2}}.

Corollary
2
directly implies a better approximation barrier of
\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor
, as shown in the next section.
We note that in the limit as the effective dimension
R\to\infty
with fixed
n
, we expect entries of this matrix to be close to Hermite polynomials of independent Gaussian variables (by the CLT), and the operator norm to scale like
\sqrt{n}
(as in Wigner-type ensembles); this behavior is captured by our bound. In the general polynomial scaling regime where both
n
and
R
are growing, this bound provides a novel approximation of the empirical kernel matrix, as we will see in the next section.

Proof.

We need to compute each of the terms that appear in the bound given by Theorem
1
, applied to the kernel
k(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)
.

•

Applying Lemma
4
, we have

\displaystyle n\sqrt{\E[\E_{z}[k(x_{1},z)]^{2}]}

\displaystyle\lesssim n\sqrt{\E\left\lvert\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right\rvert^{\ell}}\lesssim n\sqrt{R^{-\ell/2}}=nR^{-\ell/4},

where the second-to-last inequality is a consequence of Whittle’s inequality (Lemma
1
).

•

For the next term, we again apply Lemmas
4
and
1
to obtain

\displaystyle n\sqrt{\E_{\bm{x}_{1}}(\E_{\bm{z}}k(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{z}))^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim n\sqrt{\E_{\bm{x}_{1}}\left\lvert\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right\rvert^{\ell}}\lesssim nR^{-\ell/4}.

•

We bound the expected operator norm of the off-diagonal part of
\bm{G}
by the expected Frobenius norm. In particular, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

\E\lVert\operatorname{diag}^{\perp}\bm{G}\rVert\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\neq j}\E(\E_{\bm{z}}k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z})k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{j}))^{2}}\leq n\sqrt{\E_{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}}(\E_{\bm{z}}k(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{z})k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{2}))^{2}}.

Using Lemmas
5
and
1
, along with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have, for some constants
c_{m,\ell}
(depending only on
m
and
\ell
),

\displaystyle\E_{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}}(\E_{\bm{z}}k(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{z})k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{2}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}c_{m,\ell}\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{m}\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{m}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}_{i^{\prime}}\right)^{\ell-2m}\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\E\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{2m}\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{2m}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}_{i^{\prime}}\right)^{2\ell-4m}\tau_{2}^{4m-2\ell}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}R^{-2m}\tau_{4}^{\ell-2m}\tau_{2}^{4m-2\ell}

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{-\ell}.

For the diagonal part of
\bm{G}
, we have

\displaystyle\E\lVert\operatorname{diag}\bm{G}\rVert

\displaystyle=\E\max_{i}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}c_{m,\ell}\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{2m}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}_{i}\right)^{\ell-2m}\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}\E\max_{i}\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{2m}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}_{i}\right)^{\ell-2m}

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{\lesssim}_{\log}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}\tau_{4}^{m}\tau_{2}^{-2m}\tau_{2}^{\ell-2m}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

where inequality (1) uses Lemma
6
and bounds on moments of Gaussian quadratic forms
[
35
]
. Combining the above bounds on the norms of the off-diagonal and diagonal parts of
\bm{G}
, we can conclude that
\E\lVert\bm{G}\rVert\lesssim_{\log}nR^{-\ell/2}
.

•

Lastly, we consider

\displaystyle\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{i})-\E_{\bm{x}_{i}}k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\E\max_{i}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)-\E_{\bm{x}_{i}}\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\E_{\bm{z}}\log^{c}(n)\cdot\left\lVert P_{\ell}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we use Lemma
6
and define
P_{\ell}(\cdot)=\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\cdot,\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)-\E_{\bm{x}}\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)
, which is a standard Gaussian polynomial conditional on
\bm{z}
.
We can bound the
L^{2}
norm using Lemma
5
by noting that

\displaystyle\left\lVert P_{\ell}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left(\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)\Big|\bm{z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\bm{x}_{i}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}c_{m,\ell}\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{z}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{2m}\left(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{z}\right)^{\ell-2m}\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}.

Substituting this into the bound above and taking the expectation with respect to
\bm{z}
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Lemma
1
, and bounds on moments of quadratic forms
[
35
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{i})-\E_{\bm{z}}k(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{c}(n)\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\E\left[\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{z}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{2}}-1\right)^{2m}\left(\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{z}\right)^{\ell-2m}\right]\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{c}(n)\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}R^{-m}\tau_{2}^{\ell-2m}\tau_{2}^{2m-\ell}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{c}(n).

Combining these bounds in each of the terms of Theorem
1
yields the desired upper bound. For the lower bound in the case where
\ell
is even and
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}_{d}
, we use Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\E\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\E\bm{\Delta}^{(\ell)}\right\rVert=n\left\lvert\E\text{He}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right\rvert\asymp n\left\lvert\E\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{x}_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{d}-1\right)^{\ell/2}\right\rvert\asymp nd^{-\ell/2}d^{\lfloor\ell/4\rfloor},

where the second line uses Lemma
4
and the last line uses the fact that the
k
-th central moment of a
\chi^{2}(d)
random variable scales like
d^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
for a positive integer
k\geq 2
(see, e.g., Lemma G.1 in
[
44
]
).
∎

4
Kernel matrix approximation in the polynomial regime

In this section, we show how Corollary
2
can be used to provide new results for approximating general inner product kernel matrices with anisotropic Gaussian data. Recall that we consider
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
and denote
\tau_{k}=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{k})
. We will consider the high-dimensional polynomial scaling regime where
c\leq\frac{n}{\tau_{1}^{q}}\leq C
, for some
q>0
and constants
c,C>0
. Let
k\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an inner product kernel of the form

\displaystyle k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=f\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\rangle}{\operatorname{tr}{\bm{\Sigma}}}\right),

(7)

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be a
C^{\lfloor 2q\rfloor+1}
function in a neighborhood of
0
and is
L
-Lipschitz in a neighborhood
[1-\delta,1+\delta]
, for some
\delta>0
.

We are interested in studying the behavior of the empirical kernel matrix
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
\bm{K}_{ij}=k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
. In this section, we assume without loss of generality that
\lVert\bm{\Sigma}\rVert=1
.
For conciseness, we will occasionally write
\tau\coloneqq\tau_{1}
.

Next, we define the matrix

\bar{\bm{K}}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor}\frac{f^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!\tau_{1}^{\ell}}(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top})^{\odot\ell}+\sum_{\ell=\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor 2q\rfloor}\frac{f^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!\tau_{1}^{\ell}}\tau_{2}^{\ell/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor}c_{k\ell}\bm{H}^{(k)}+\left(f(1)-\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor}\frac{f^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\right)\bm{I},

where
c_{k\ell}=\frac{1}{k!}\E_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[z^{\ell}\text{He}_{k}(z)]
and

H^{(k)}_{ij}\coloneqq\text{He}_{k}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}\rangle}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\right).

Note that
\bar{\bm{K}}
is the sum of a multiple of the identity matrix and a polynomial kernel matrix of degree at most
\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor
. Our main result in this section is the following theorem, proven in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 3
.

In the setting described above,

\lVert\bm{K}-\bar{\bm{K}}\rVert\lesssim_{\log}\tau^{q-\frac{\lfloor 2q\rfloor}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}+\tau^{q-\frac{3}{4}\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor-\frac{3}{4}}+\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}

with probability at least
1-\frac{c}{\log{n}}
. Hence,

\lVert\bm{K}-\bar{\bm{K}}\rVert\to 0
in probability as
n,\tau\to\infty
.

To our knowledge, this is the sharpest known kernel approximation result with anisotropic Gaussian data in the general polynomial scaling regime. We recover the bounds developed for the linear
[
28
]
and quadratic scaling regimes
[
45
]
, while tightening the approximation result in the general polynomial scaling regime from a degree
\lfloor 2q\rfloor
polynomial to a degree
\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor
polynomial. Due to our use of the sharp bounds from Corollary
2
, we are able to obtain a faster convergence guarantee than
[
45
]
in the quadratic case (on the order of
d^{-1/2}
instead of
d^{-1/12}
). It is an interesting open question to study whether Theorem
3
can be improved further to the conjectured degree-
\lfloor q\rfloor
polynomial approximation, to match known results for the uniform spherical and hypercubic distributions. However, the lower bound we prove in Corollary
2
implies that a decomposition of
f
in terms of univariate Hermite polynomials will not lead to the conjectured approximation result.

In some sense, this tells us that the univariate Hermite basis is the wrong orthogonal decomposition to use to approximate general inner product kernels with Gaussian data, even when the covariance is isotropic!
In the special case of isotropy, we can form an alternative approximation by leveraging the sharp result for uniform data on the sphere (cf. Corollary
1
). Formally, the polar decomposition of a vector
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}_{d})
into independent norm and unit vector terms allows us to approximate the kernel matrix by a degree-
\lfloor q\rfloor
polynomial of
unit vectors
— with random norm-based coefficients — via a Gegenbauer polynomial expansion.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I}_{d})
. For each
i
, denote
r_{i}\coloneqq\lVert\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}
and
\bm{u}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\bm{x}_{i}}{\lVert\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}}
. Then, under the same assumptions as in Theorem
3
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{K}-\bar{\bm{K}}\rVert\to 0

in probability as
n,d\to\infty
, where we define the approximating matrix as

\bar{\bm{K}}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor 2q\rfloor}\frac{f^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!d^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}\left(\frac{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\top}}{d}\right)^{\odot\ell}\odot\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor q\rfloor}c_{j\ell}^{(d)}\bm{Q}^{(j)}\right)+\left(f(1)-\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor q\rfloor}\frac{f^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\right)\bm{I}_{n},

c_{j\ell}^{(d)}\coloneqq B(d,j)\E_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})}[\langle\bm{x},\bm{e}_{1}\rangle^{\ell}Q_{j}^{(d)}(\sqrt{d}\langle\bm{x},\bm{e}_{1}\rangle)].

Above,
\bm{r}
is the vector with entries
r_{i}
, and
\bm{Q}^{(j)}
is the matrix with entries
Q_{j}^{(d)}(\langle\sqrt{d}\bm{u}_{i},\sqrt{d}\bm{u}_{j}\rangle)
.

While Proposition
1
is relatively simple to prove given the tools we developed in the previous section (see Appendix
C
), we have not seen it stated in this general form in the literature.
An intriguing question is whether this polar decomposition trick can be leveraged for anisotropic Gaussian data of the form
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
.
Indeed, it is possible to decompose
\bm{x}=r\bm{v}
where
r:=\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{x}\rVert
and
\bm{v}=\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{u}
(where
\bm{u}
is uniformly distributed on the sphere) and
r
is independent of
\bm{v}
.
However, identifying the correct basis for inner products of the form
\langle\bm{v}_{i},\bm{v}_{j}\rangle
is challenging since it will likely need to involve calculations with generalized ellipsoidal harmonics, and we do not believe an elegant identity of the form of Equation (
6
) holds in general.
We leave this as an important direction for future work.

4.1
Lower bound on the bias of KRR

In Theorem
3
, we showed that general inner product kernel matrices with anisotropic Gaussian data are well-approximated by polynomial kernel matrices of degree
\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor
under the high-dimensional scaling
n\asymp\tau^{q}
. In this section, we use similar Hermite decompositions to show that the generalization error of kernel ridge regression (KRR) is lower bounded by the bias of the best degree-
\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor
polynomial approximation to the target function. The overall strategy we use is similar to the generalization analysis in
[
45
]
, but we will consider the target function
g^{*}
belonging to a family of generalized additive models.
Specifically, we consider i.i.d. samples drawn from the model

\displaystyle y_{i}=g^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},

where
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
, and
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}\overset{\mathclap{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. We will consider the target
g^{*}
to take the form

\displaystyle g^{*}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_{k}g_{k}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{v}_{k}\rangle),

(8)

for some constant
K
, fixed unit vectors
\bm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, and univariate functions
g_{k}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N}(0,1))
. Intuitively, this condition requires that
g^{*}
depends only on
K
scalar projections of the whitened input.

Given these
n
samples, the standard KRR estimator is constructed as

\hat{g}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bm{k}_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x}),

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the ridge regularization parameter,
\bm{k}_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector with entries
k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x})
, and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector with entries
y_{i}
.

The main result of this subsection is stated below and proven in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 4

(Lower bound on the bias of KRR)
.

Consider the kernel regression estimate corresponding to the inner product kernel in Equation (
7
) with ridge regularization parameter
\lambda\geq 0
. Assume that
g^{*}
is of the form in Equation (
8
) and that there exists some integer
L>4q-2
such that
f^{(L+1)}
is uniformly bounded by a constant. Then, in the scaling regime
n\asymp\tau^{q}
,

\mathsf{Bias}(\hat{g},g^{*})\geq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor}}\lVert p-g^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-o_{\tau}(1),

where
\mathcal{P}_{\leq k}
is the space of multivariate polynomials of degree less than or equal to
k
in
d
dimensions.

Our theorem shows that even for this relatively simple class of target functions, KRR suffers from a polynomial approximation barrier and is unable to learn functions of degree greater than
\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor
. It is an interesting direction for future work to strengthen this bound to the conjectured
\lfloor q\rfloor
lower bound (which would match the uniform spherical and binary hypercube case, as analyzed in
[
23
,
38
]
).
Based on the bias and variance calculations in
[
23
]
, we believe that an optimal degree-
\lfloor q\rfloor
approximation result would be instrumental for strengthening the bias lower bound (as well as providing a matching upper bound and characterizing the variance of KRR).

Lemma 4

.

(9)

where the last line follows from Lemma

Lemma 5

(Conditional correlation of Hermite matrix entries)

(10)

Lemma 6

(Expected maximum of polynomials under hypercontractivity)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a probability measure satisfying the following hypercontractivity property:

\lVert Q\rVert_{L_{q}}\leq(q-1)^{k/2}\lVert Q\rVert_{L_{2}},

for any polynomial
Q
of degree at most
k
in
d
variables and any integer
q\geq 2
. In particular, this property is satisfied for the standard normal distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, and the uniform distribution on the
d
-dimensional binary hypercube.

Let
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables from
\mathcal{P}
and let
Q
be a polynomial of degree
k\geq 1
. Then, for
s\geq\frac{2}{k}
,

\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Q(\bm{z}_{i})|^{s}\lesssim(\log{n})^{ks/2}\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s},

where
\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\coloneqq(\E[Q(z)^{2}])^{1/2}
denotes the
L^{2}
norm of
Q
with respect to the distribution
\mathcal{P}
, and the suppressed universal constant depends only on
k
and
s
.

Proof.

We first obtain a tail bound for the maximum, following the approach in the proof of Proposition 5.48 in
[
2
]
. Let
q\geq 2
be a constant we will fix later in the proof. By a union bound and Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Q(\bm{z}_{i})|^{s}>t\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq n\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left\{|Q(\bm{z}_{1})|^{s}>t\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq nt^{-q}\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{-qs}\E\left[|Q(\bm{z}_{1})|^{qs}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{\leq}nt^{-q}(qs)^{kqs/2}

where inequality (1) follows from hypercontractivity. Choosing
q=\frac{t^{2/ks}}{es}
, which satisfies
q\geq 2
for
t\geq(2es)^{ks/2}
, we obtain

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Q(\bm{z}_{i})|^{s}>t\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\right\}\leq n\text{exp}\left(-\frac{k}{2e}t^{\frac{2}{ks}}\right).\\

Letting
C>0
be a constant and integrating the tail bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Q(\bm{z}_{i})|^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\left[(C\log{n})^{ks/2}+\int_{(C\log{n})^{ks/2}}^{\infty}n\text{exp}\left(-\frac{k}{2e}t^{\frac{2}{ks}}\right)dt\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\left[(C\log{n})^{ks/2}+n\int_{C^{\prime}\log{n}}^{\infty}\text{exp}\left(-u\right)u^{\frac{ks}{2}-1}du\right]

\displaystyle=\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\left[(C\log{n})^{ks/2}+n\Gamma\left(\frac{ks}{2},C^{\prime}\log{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{2}}^{s}\left[(C\log{n})^{ks/2}+n\exp\left(-C^{\prime}\log{n}\right)(C^{\prime}\log{n})^{\frac{ks}{2}-1}\right],

where the second line uses the substitution
u=\frac{k}{2e}t^{2/ks}
, the third line uses the definition of the incomplete Gamma function, and the last line uses the upper bound
\Gamma(a,x)\leq ae^{-x}x^{a-1}
, which holds for
a\geq 1
and
x>a

[
19
, Proposition 4.4.3]

Lemma 7

(Operator norm of Hadamard product with outer product)
.

Let
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,

\lVert\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\top}\odot\bm{P}\rVert\leq\lVert\bm{a}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert\bm{P}\rVert.

Proof.

We directly have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\top}\odot\bm{P}\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}=1}\lVert(\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\top}\odot\bm{P})\bm{u}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}=1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}a_{i}a_{j}u_{j}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{a}\rVert_{\infty}\max_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}=1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}a_{j}u_{j}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\bm{a}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\bm{P}(\bm{a}\odot\bm{u})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{a}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\bm{P}\rVert\lVert\bm{a}\odot\bm{u}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\bm{a}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\bm{P}\rVert\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}u_{i}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{a}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\lVert\bm{P}\rVert.

∎

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem

We consider i.i.d. samples drawn from the model

y_{i}=g^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},

where
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\text{ i.i.d.}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})

\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}\text{ i.i.d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

is uniformly bounded by a constant. Moreover, we will consider

of the form

\displaystyle g^{*}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_{k}g_{k}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{v}_{k}\rangle),

(12)

for some constant
K
, fixed unit vectors
\bm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}

We set up the following basic notation:

•

As is standard, we denote
\bm{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}&\ldots&\bm{x}_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

\bm{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}&\ldots&y_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

\bm{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{1}&\epsilon_{2}&\ldots&\epsilon_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

•

We write the function evaluation vector as

\bm{g}=\begin{bmatrix}g^{*}(\bm{x}_{1})&g^{*}(\bm{x}_{2})&\ldots&g^{*}(\bm{x}_{n})\end{bmatrix}

•

We denote the empirical kernel matrix by
\bm{K}
where
K_{ij}=k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
. Further, we denote the vector
\bm{V}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&V_{2}&\ldots&V_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

\bm{M}

With this notation, the expression for the test bias of KRR can be written in closed form as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Bias}(\hat{g},g^{*}):=\|\E_{y}\hat{g}-g^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert g^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-2\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bm{V}+\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bm{M}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bm{g}.

(13)

We begin by approximating the matrix
\bm{M}
and the vector

separately. We will condition on the event

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{X}\colon\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\left\lvert\frac{\langle\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{j}\rangle}{\tau_{2}}-\delta_{ij}\right\rvert\lesssim\tau_{2}^{-1}\tau_{4}^{1/2}\log{n}\right\}

(14)

Furthermore, by assumption on
g^{*}
, we can also condition on the event that

\lVert\bm{g}\rVert_{2}^{2}\lesssim n\log{n},

which holds with probability tending to
1
by Markov’s inequality and because
g^{*}\in L^{2}
.

D.1
Approximation of the
\bm{M}
matrix

First, consider a fixed
\bm{x}
and perform a Taylor expansion of the kernel function to get

\displaystyle k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\frac{f^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!\tau^{\ell}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}_{i}\rangle^{\ell}+\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{i})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1},

where
\zeta_{i}
is between
0
and
\frac{1}{\tau}\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}\rangle
. Defining
\bm{z}\coloneqq\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{x}
,
r_{i}\coloneqq\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}
and
\bm{u}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}}{\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}}
, we can write

\displaystyle k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\frac{f^{(\ell)}(0)r_{i}^{\ell}}{\ell!\tau^{\ell}}\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle^{\ell}+\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{i})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1}.

We rewrite the first term in terms of a univariate Hermite expansion to obtain

\displaystyle k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}b_{\ell,i}\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle)+\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{i})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1},

(15)

where

b_{\ell,i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\ell!}\sum_{m=\ell}^{L}\frac{f^{(m)}(0)r_{i}^{m}}{m!\tau^{m}}\E_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[z^{m}\text{He}_{\ell}(z)].

It is easy to verify that, on the event
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
, these coefficients are bounded as
|b_{\ell,i}|\lesssim_{\log}\tau_{1}^{-\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell/2}
.
Using the decomposition in Equation (
15
) and Lemma
3
, we can write

\displaystyle M_{ij}=\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\ell!b_{\ell,i}b_{\ell,j}\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j}\rangle^{\ell}}_{M_{ij}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}b_{\ell,i}\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle)\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{j})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1}\right]}_{M_{ij}^{(2)}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}b_{\ell,j}\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{j}\rangle)\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{i})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1}\right]}_{M_{ij}^{(3)}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{i})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1}\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{j})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}\rangle^{L+1}\right]}_{M_{ij}^{(4)}}

For the latter three terms, we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and event
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
to obtain the following bounds (using the cruder bound
|b_{\ell,i}|\lesssim_{\log}\tau_{1}^{-\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell/2}\lesssim`\tau_{1}^{-\ell/2}
):

\displaystyle\left\lvert M^{(2)}_{ij}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\tau^{-\ell/2}\tau^{-L-1}\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{L+1}\lesssim_{\log}\tau^{\frac{-L-1}{2}},

\displaystyle\left\lvert M^{(3)}_{ij}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\tau^{-\ell/2}\tau^{-L-1}\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{L+1}\lesssim_{\log}\tau^{\frac{-L-1}{2}},

\displaystyle\left\lvert M^{(4)}_{ij}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{-L-1}\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{L+1}\tau^{-L-1}\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{L+1}\lesssim_{\log}\tau^{-L-1},

where we use the fact that we have conditioned on
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
and
\tau_{2}\leq\tau
.
Hence, the corresponding matrices have operator norm bounded up to log factors by
n\tau^{\frac{-L-1}{2}}=\tau^{q-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}
.
Next, consider the term

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\ell!b_{\ell,i}b_{\ell,j}\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j}\rangle^{\ell}=\colon\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}M_{ij}^{(1,\ell)}.

Bounding each term in the summation separately, we obtain for
i\neq j
(again, using the event
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
), we have

\left\lvert M^{(1,\ell)}_{ij}\right\rvert\lesssim\tau_{1}^{-2\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell}|\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j}\rangle|^{\ell}\lesssim_{\log}\tau_{1}^{-2\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell}\tau_{2}^{-\ell}\tau_{4}^{\ell/2}=\tau_{1}^{-2\ell}\tau_{4}^{\ell/2}\lesssim\tau_{1}^{-3\ell/2}.

Now, for any
\ell>\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor
, we can use the triangle inequality to upper bound the operator norm of
\left\lVert\bm{M}^{(1,\ell)}\right\rVert
by the sum of the norm of the diagonal part (i.e., the maximum absolute diagonal entry) and the norm of the off-diagonal part (for which we use a simple Frobenius norm bound).
In more detail, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{M}^{(1,\ell)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\log}n\tau^{-3\ell/2}+\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert M_{ii}^{(1,\ell)}\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim n\tau^{-3\ell/2}+\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}|b_{\ell,i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{q-3\ell/2}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\tau_{1}^{-2\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{q-3\ell/2}.

Finally, defining

\bar{M}_{ij}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor}\ell!b_{\ell,i}b_{\ell,j}\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j}\rangle^{\ell},

we can conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}(\bm{M}-\bar{\bm{M}})(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bm{g}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\bm{g}\rVert_{2}^{2}\lVert(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\rVert^{2}\lVert\bm{M}-\bar{\bm{M}}\rVert

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\log}n(\tau^{q-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}+\tau^{q-\frac{3}{2}(\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor+1)})

\displaystyle=\tau^{2q-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}+\tau^{2q-\frac{3}{2}(\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor+1)}

\displaystyle=o_{\tau}(1).

The second line above uses the fact that
\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}
has eigenvalues larger than a constant. In the case
\lambda=0
, this is guaranteed with probability tending to
1
by the approximation result for
\bm{K}
in the main paper (note this only requires the more crude bound obtained by
[
16
]
. More precisely, by Weyl’s inequality and Theorem
3
, we have

\mu_{n}(\bm{K})\geq\mu_{n}(\bar{\bm{K}})-o_{\tau}(1)\geq\left(f(1)-\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor}\frac{f^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\right)-o_{\tau}(1)\geq c,

for some
c>0
and sufficiently large
\tau
. We note that we use the approximation from Theorem 3, but for this result one could also use the more crude approximation in
[
16
]
.

D.2
Approximation of the
\bm{V}
vector

We approximate this term in a similar manner. We can write the
i
-th entry of
\bm{V}
as

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}b_{\ell,i}\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle)g^{*}(\bm{x})\right]}_{V_{i}^{(1)}}+\underbrace{\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\frac{f^{(L+1)}(\zeta_{i})}{(L+1)!\tau^{L+1}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}_{i}\rangle^{L+1}g^{*}(\bm{x})\right]}_{V_{i}^{(2)}}.

We use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and event
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
to obtain the bound

\left\lvert V_{i}^{(2)}\right\rvert\lesssim_{\log}\tau^{-L-1}\tau_{2}^{(L+1)/2}\lesssim\tau^{\frac{-L-1}{2}},

from which we can conclude
\lVert\bm{V}^{(2)}\rVert_{2}\lesssim_{\log}\sqrt{n}\tau^{\frac{-L-1}{2}}=\tau^{\frac{q-L-1}{2}}
.
For
\bm{V}^{(1)}
, consider each term separately.

\left\lvert V_{i}^{(1,\ell)}\right\rvert\coloneqq b_{\ell,i}\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle g^{*}(\bm{x})\right]\lesssim\tau^{-\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell/2}\left\lvert\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle g^{*}(\bm{x})\right]\right\rvert.

Using the assumed form of
g^{*}
in Equation (
8
), we can compute this expectation as

\displaystyle\lvert\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle g^{*}(\bm{x})\right]\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_{k}\E_{\bm{z}}[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle)g_{k}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{v}_{k}\rangle]\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{jk}\E_{\bm{z}}[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle)\text{He}_{j}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{v}_{k}\rangle)]\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{k=0}^{K}\ell!c_{k}\alpha_{\ell,k}\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{v}_{k}\rangle^{\ell}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{K}\lvert\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{v}_{k}\rangle\rvert^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}r_{i}^{-\ell}\lvert\langle\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{x}_{i},\bm{v}_{k}\rangle\rvert^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}r_{i}^{-\ell}\lvert\langle\bm{z}_{i},\bm{\Sigma}\bm{v}_{k}\rangle\rvert^{\ell},

where
\alpha_{jk}
are the Hermite coefficients of
g_{k}
. Recall also the
\bm{v}_{k}
are fixed unit vectors. Note that for any
k
, we have

\lvert\langle\bm{z}_{i},\bm{\Sigma}\bm{v}_{k}\rangle\rvert\overset{d}{=}\lVert\bm{\Sigma}\bm{v}_{k}\rVert\lvert z_{k}\rvert\leq\lvert z_{k}\rvert,

for a standard normal variable
z_{k}
, so via a standard Gaussian tail bound and a union bound over all
K
variables, we have
\lvert z_{k}\rvert\leq\sqrt{2\log{n}}
for all
k
, with probability at least
1-\frac{K}{n}
. So, we can conclude that

\lvert\E_{\bm{x}}\left[\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle g^{*}(\bm{x})\right]\rvert\lesssim_{\log}r_{i}^{-\ell}\lesssim_{\log}\tau_{2}^{-\ell/2},

with probability tending to
1
. From this, we have

\left\lVert V_{i}^{(1,\ell)}\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim_{\log}\sqrt{n}\tau^{-\ell}\tau_{2}^{\ell/2}\tau_{2}^{-\ell/2}\asymp\tau^{\frac{q}{2}-\ell}

Combining the above, we can define
\bar{\bm{V}}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor}\bm{V}^{(1,\ell)}
. So, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}(\bm{V}-\bar{\bm{V}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\log}\sqrt{n}\tau^{\frac{q}{2}-\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor-1}=\tau^{q-\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor-1}=o_{\tau}(1).

(Note that we could have actually used the sharper approximation
\bar{\bm{V}}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q\rfloor}\bm{V}^{(1,\ell)}
for this part of the proof.
However, we pick the degree-
\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor
approximation to match the approximation of the
\bm{M}
term for convenience.)

D.3
Concluding the argument

Motivated by the results from the previous two sections, we can define the following function (which depends on
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}
):

\bar{g}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bar{k}(\bm{X},\bm{x}),

where
\bar{k}(\bm{X},\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector with
i
-th entry given by

\bar{k}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x})\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor}b_{\ell,i}\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{z},\bm{u}_{i}\rangle)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor 4q/3\rfloor}b_{\ell,i}\text{He}_{\ell}(\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{u}_{i}\rangle).

Note that
\bar{g}
is a polynomial of
\bm{x}
of degree at most
\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor
, so its bias is lower bounded by the bias of the best
\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor
approximation to
g^{*}
. Moreover, we have

\mathsf{Bias}(\bar{g},g^{*})=\lVert g^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-2\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bar{\bm{V}}+\bm{g}^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bar{\bm{M}}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I}_{n})^{-1}\bm{g}

The results of the previous two sections imply that

\left\lvert\mathsf{Bias}(\hat{g},g^{*})-\mathsf{Bias}(\bar{g},g^{*})\right\rvert=o_{\tau}(1).

Therefore, we can conclude that

\mathsf{Bias}(\hat{g},g^{*})\geq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq\lfloor\frac{4q}{3}\rfloor}}\lVert p-g^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-o_{\tau}(1).

∎
