Protocol for generating highly enriched sex-specific primary cortical astrocytes from individual mouse pups
Authors: Sanarya Al-Jaf, En-Hung Ai, Julia A Wilson, Khaled S Abd-Elrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for generating highly enriched primary cortical astrocytes from individual neonatal mouse pups. We describe steps for mechanically dissociating cortical tissue, culturing in coated flasks, and removing oligodendrocyte progenitor cells through mechanical shaking and selective pharmacological depletion of microglia. We then detail procedures for PCR-based analysis of tail-biopsy DNA to determine sex and genotype to genetically define astrocyte cultures. This protocol enables the investigation of sex- and genotype-specific mechanisms while avoiding the confounding effects of pooled tissue.Subject areas: Cell Biology, Molecular Biology, Neuroscience

Before you begin

Astrocytes are multifunctional glial cells that are indispensable for central nervous system (CNS) development and homeostasis. Dysregulation of astrocyte signaling pathways is increasingly recognized as a critical driver of neurological disease pathology.1 Consequently, in vitro astrocyte models remain essential for dissecting astrocyte-intrinsic mechanisms that govern CNS physiology and disease progression.

Primary astrocyte cultures derived from neonatal rodent cortices provide a high-yield, physiologically relevant system enriched in proliferative glial progenitors capable of differentiating into mature astrocytes in vitro. While established protocols achieve significant depletion of neuronal population, traditional mixed glial preparations inevitably contain heterogeneous populations of microglia and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs). Thus, rigorous removal or depletion of non-astroglial cell types is critical for ensuring experimental specificity.

Several enrichment strategies have been developed, with mechanical shaking being a long-standing method to detach OPCs based on their weaker adherence to poly-D-lysine (PDL)-coated flasks. While effective for OPCs reduction, mechanical dissociation alone does not adequately remove microglia, which remain adherent and functionally active within the culture.2 Advances in microglial biology have identified the colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) as an essential regulator of microglial survival, enabling selective microglial depletion with small-molecule CSF1R inhibitors such as PLX5622 in both, in vitro and in vivo models.3,4 PLX5622 treatment therefore provides a robust and reproducible method for eliminating microglia from mixed glial cultures without disrupting astrocyte viability.

In our optimized protocol, we integrated CSF1R inhibition into primary astrocyte workflows to markedly increase astrocyte purity and reduce confounding effects from microglia-derived factors. Our rationale was that combining PDL-based adhesion with pipette-mediated mechanical dissociation during the initial tissue-processing step facilitates astrocyte attachment and culture establishment. Sequential removing of OPCs through differential adhesion-based techniques, including tapping and shaking further enriches the astrocyte population, generating stable cultures suitable for molecular, imaging-based and biochemical assays. By exposing the cultures to PLX5622 for 2 days to selectively deplete microglia, this method reliably leads to highly purified astrocyte cultures with minimal contamination, enhancing consistency and reliability for downstream studies.

A further advantage of this protocol is its compatibility with sex/genotype-specific analyses. Unlike many traditional astrocyte preparation methods, which require pooling tissue from multiple (often ≥4) neonatal brains to achieve adequate cell yield,5,6 our approach generates sufficient astrocyte numbers from a single mouse brain. This eliminates the need to pool sexes and enables researchers to derive independent male and female cultures without compromising cell yield or purity.

Innovation

Most primary astrocyte protocols pool cortical tissue from multiple neonatal pups to reach adequate yield, a practice that obscures individual sex and genotype and precludes studies of sex-specific astrocyte biology. This protocol overcomes that limitation by generating a high-purity, high-yield astrocyte culture from a single mouse pup brain, preserving sex- and genotype-specific information throughout. This protocol integrates two complementary astrocyte enrichment strategies into a single streamlined workflow by combining mechanical removal of OPCs through differential adhesion with selective pharmacological depletion of microglia using the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622. Systematic optimization of PLX5622 treatment identified a 48-hour exposure as the optimal condition, achieving complete microglial depletion, while preserving astrocyte viability and avoiding the cytotoxicity associated with prolonged treatment. In addition, each culture is derived from an individual animal through PCR-based sex determination from the corresponding tail biopsy, eliminating the need for tissue pooling. Together, these innovations generate highly purified, sex- and genotype-defined astrocyte cultures from single mouse brains, enabling the use of transgenic mouse lines requiring individual genotyping and facilitating studies of sex-dependent astrocyte biology using downstream molecular, biochemical, and imaging approaches.

Expected outcomes

Previous enrichment protocols achieved robust depletion of neurons and OPCs from mixed cortical cultures5,6; however, effective microglial depletion remained a challenge. PLX5622 is a selective inhibitor of CSF1R widely used to achieve efficient and reversible microglial depletion in the CNS. By blocking CSF1R signaling, PLX5622 enables precise experimental investigation of microglial contributions to neural homeostasis and disease.3,4 We propose a novel application of PLX5622, used in combination with established methodologies, to generate highly pure, enriched primary astrocyte cultures with defined sex specificity. To optimize this process, we performed varying durations of PLX5622 treatment followed by immunocytochemistry to determine the treatment duration that achieved complete microglial depletion while minimizing potential effects on astrocyte viability and morphology. Conditions ranged from 24 to 96 h of treatment with 0.01 mg/mL PLX5622 in astrocyte media with each culture being entirely comprised of cells of a specific sex.7 Efficacy of the enrichment process was evaluated with astrocyte and microglia markers, GFAP and Iba1, respectively, in immunostaining experiments whilst PCR and gel electrophoresis is used to determine sex of origin animal and derived culture. The respective purity of cultures and presence of astrocytes and microglia is visualized via immunofluorescence imaging and subsequently quantified through cell counting and analysis (Figure 1).

Immunocytochemical examination of the cultures indicated that 24 h of PLX5622 treatment was not sufficient to fully eliminate microglia from the cultures as we detected some microglial contamination (immunostained using Iba1 marker; Figure 1A). At the 48-h mark of PLX5622 treatment, we can observe an effective depletion of microglia in culture with astrocyte count unaffected (Figure 1B). In the 72-h and 96-h conditions, we similarly observe total depletion of microglia, however, astrocyte count does also appear to show a decline correlated with treatment duration (Figures 1C and 1D). This may suggest potential off-target effects of prolonged PLX5622 exposure, including reduced astrocyte viability. Although the underlying mechanism was not directly investigated, these findings may reflect some degree of cytotoxicity or cell loss driven directly by PLX5622 or through downstream effect. We believe that 48 h represents the optimal treatment duration, as it achieves robust astrocyte enrichment while minimizing unnecessary extension of the protocol. Our continued use of this protocol at 48 hr PLX5622 exposure paradigm also demonstrates that the cells maintain their viability and function throughout the culture period.

These findings are congruent with immunoblots (Figure 2), also displaying the presence of Iba1 in the no-PLX5622 condition and lack thereof in PLX5622 treated conditions. Although Iba1 was not readily detected by immunoblotting following 24 h of PLX5622 treatment, Iba-1+ cells remained clearly detectable by immunocytochemistry at this time point. This likely reflects the greater sensitivity of immunocytochemistry for detecting Iba1 at the individual-cell level, as immunocytochemistry provides spatially resolved visualization of even relatively small populations of residual Iba1-positive cells, whereas immunoblotting measures Iba1 abundance across the entire protein lysate and is therefore influenced by the overall abundance of Iba1, sample composition, and assay detection threshold.8 Together, these findings demonstrate that PLX5622 induces a time-dependent depletion of microglia in primary astrocyte cultures, as supported by both immunofluorescence and immunoblot analyses. When considered collectively, the qualitative and quantitative findings from both approaches indicate that 48 h of PLX5622 treatment provides the optimal balance between effective microglial depletion and preservation of astrocyte viability and morphology.

GFAP and Iba1 immunoblots under various PLX5622 treatment conditions

Representative immunoblots showing the protein expression of GFAP (using anti-GFAP; 1:1000) and Iba1 (using anti-Iba1; 1:1000) in the absence and the presence of various PLX5622 treatments. In PLX5622-treated samples, Iba-1+ cells are absent at 48, 72 and 96 h, indicating its efficacy in depleting microglia compared with untreated controls. In contrast, GFAP+ astrocytes are consistently present across all conditions, regardless of PLX5622 exposure. This demonstrates selective depletion of microglia without significant loss of astrocytes.

Alongside immunoblotting and immunocytochemical analysis, in a process known as sex typing, a simplex PCR using primers for the X-linked Rbm31x gene and Y-linked gametolog Rbm31y is conducted on genetic material (tails) derived from the source animals that are respectively used for sex-specific astrocyte cultures.9,10 This process of sex-typing is a crucial part of the protocol. The strength of the protocol also lies in its ability to produce cultures of cells comprised entirely of one sex. As such, it is imperative that sex typing is performed to identify and capitalize on the protocol’s ability to give rise to sex-specific in vitro research models.

The DNA amplification process by the PCR is followed by gel electrophoresis and imaging for sex determination to identify the specific sex of isolated and purified cell cultures (Figure 3). We observe an expected and consistent result whereby each animal and thus, each culture, gives rise to clear, clean bands, with 2 bands indicating a male animal and subsequent culture and 1 band indicating a female animal and subsequent culture.

PCR Gel electrophoresis for sex determination

PCR gel electrophoresis was performed on genomic DNA extracted from tail biopsies of the same neonatal mice used to generate primary astrocyte cultures. Amplification of the X-linked Rbm31x gene and Y-linked Rbm31y gene produced distinct banding patterns, with one band indicating female (XX) samples and two bands indicating male (XY) samples. A molecular weight ladder was included to verify expected amplicon sizes. Females will exhibit one band at roughly 269Bp whilst males will have two bands, one at 353Bp and the other at 269Bp. While no densitometric or statistical analyses were performed, as sex identification represents a binary outcome, the banding patterns were consistent across samples and allowed for reliable sex assignment that was confirmed by anatomical validation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0. Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Limitations

Though this protocol offers a reproducible means of generating enriched, sex-specific astrocyte cultures, it has limitations. As it relies on postnatal day 0–2 mouse cortices, it is inherently time-sensitive; tissue must be harvested within this narrow developmental window, which restricts flexibility in experimental scheduling and limits scalability for larger cohorts.

OPCs are removed by exploiting their weaker adhesion relative to astrocytes and microglia,11 but this adhesion difference is not absolute; the manual shaking required to dislodge OPCs therefore carries a small, difficult-to-quantify risk of concurrent astrocyte detachment and loss, or conversely, incomplete OPC depletion dependent on individual force applied. Moreover, pharmacological depletion of microglia with the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622 similarly requires precise timing, as exposures shorter than 48 h can leave residual microglia, whereas exposures approaching 96 h begin to exhibit off-target cytotoxic effects on astrocytes themselves, so the enrichment window must be tightly controlled to balance depletion efficacy against astrocyte viability.

PDL coating quality is another point of fragility in the protocol; inadequate coverage, improper storage, or insufficient drying of coated flasks can result in poor cell attachment or uneven monolayers, independent of the biological steps that follow. Finally, as with any purified primary culture, astrocytes isolated by this protocol exist outside their native cellular environment and are not fully representative of in vivo conditions; findings derived from these cultures should therefore be interpreted with this caveat and, where possible, corroborated using complementary in vivo or ex vivo approaches.

Problem 1

Incomplete microglial depletion following PLX5622 treatment could be due to incomplete dissolution of PLX5622, insufficient exposure time, or high initial microglial density (related to Step 3 in preparation).

•Ensure PLX5622 is fully dissolved prior to addition to culture media, with no visible particulates.

Problem 5

Mixed primary glial culture may exhibit signs of over-confluency or under-confluency at the suggested 10–15-days mark for enrichment, leading to potential contamination or drastically lower astrocyte yield post-enrichment. This may be due to a myriad of reasons, including natural variability of size and yield of neonatal mouse cortices, non-standardized dissection techniques and/or variability of mechanical dissociation (related to Step 15).

Potential solution

•Monitor confluency of mixed glial culture daily and proceed with enrichment once confluency is reached. While the 10–15-days window is largely representative of mixed glial cultures derived by this protocol, there may still be instances where enrichment falls outside of the 10–15-days window due to the aforementioned factors.
