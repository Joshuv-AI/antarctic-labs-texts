Øyvind S. Auestad

Abstract
. We introduce a framework for studying pathwise time regularity and numerical approximation of L 0 L^{0} -valued stochastic evolution equations. At the core of our framework are two Burkholder–Davis–Gundy type inequalities accommodating Itô integrals with respect to only stochastically integrable processes. The first of these inequalities is formulated in suitable metrics which metrize convergence in probability on the space of integrands and integrals. The second is a modified version, tailored for deriving pathwise properties of the integral. By combining it with a refined version of the Kolmogorov continuity test, we obtain a powerful method for deriving Hölder regularity of Itô integrals in their most general form. Moreover, it provides a simple and powerful way of deriving rates of pathwise convergence of numerical approximations of stochastic evolution equations. Both applications are illustrated for a class of linear parabolic stochastic evolution equations with generalized Whittle–Matérn type noise, and our findings are verified by numerical experiments from this setting.

1.
Introduction

We study
L^{0}
-valued Itô integrals and stochastic evolution equations. The main result of this paper are the inequalities of Theorem
3.1
and
3.2
, which are Burkholder–Davis–Gundy type inequalities for Itô integrals with respect to only stochastically integrable processes. The first of these inequalities is formulated in appropriate metrics which metrize convergence in probability on the space of integrands and integrals, and provides a simple and direct alternative way of extending the definition of the Itô integral from elementary integrands to any stochastically integrable process. In literature, this extension is done by the localization procedure (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
). The second inequality is a modified version of the first, tailored for deriving pathwise properties of the Itô integral. By combining this inequality with Lemma
3.3
and
3.5
, we get simple and powerful methods for deriving pathwise Hölder regularity and rates of convergence of numerical approximations of only
L^{0}
-valued stochastic evolution equations. In literature, time regularity and convergence rates of numerical approximations of stochastic evolution equations are usually derived using the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality, which requires solutions in
L^{2}
.

As an illustrating example, we consider the following parabolic stochastic evolution equation with generalized Whittle–Matérn type noise,

(1.1)

\displaystyle du(t)=-A_{1}u(t)\,dt+b(t)A_{2}^{-\gamma}\,dW(t),\quad u(0)=\xi,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Here,
W
is a cylindrical Wiener process on
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
,
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\leq 3
, a bounded polygonal domain,
\xi
a random variable,
\gamma>d/4-1/2
a parameter,
b
a scalar stochastic process, and
A_{j}
,
j=1,2
second order elliptic operators (see Assumption
4.1
for details). The model (
1.1
) is a generalization of a popular model in applied statistics, studied in
[
2
]
, in the sense that the noise,
b(t)A_{2}^{-\gamma}\,dW(t)
, is scaled by the scalar process
b
. While the parameter
\gamma
controls the spatial regularity of the noise, and hence the time and space regularity of the solution, the process
b
provides a way to control the statistical regularity, i.e. to which
L^{p}
space the solution belongs. Only assuming that
b
is a predictable process with bounded trajectories, one cannot expect
u(t)
to be in
L^{2}
, and so the classical
L^{2}
-dependent methods for studying pathwise properties of
u
and its numerical approximation no longer apply. This warrants the development of more powerful methods also accommodating
L^{0}
-valued stochastic evolution equations, and Theorem
4.6
and
4.12
describe pathwise Hölder regularity and convergence rates of a fully discrete finite element approximation of (
1.1
).

The paper is structured as follows: in Section 2 we cover preliminaries on stochastic integration. In Section 3 we develop the inequalities of Theorem
3.1
and
3.2
, and outline an alternative and simple way of extending the definition of the Itô integral to any stochastically integrable process, which is described in Theorem
3.1
. We then state Lemma
3.3
and
3.5
, which are key for deriving pathwise properties of the Itô integral. In Section 4 we apply the framework of Section 3 to study time regularity and numerical approximation of the model problem (
1.1
). Precise conditions on (
1.1
) and its numerical approximation are formulated in Assumption
4.1
and
4.7
, and Theorem
4.6
and
4.12
describe time regularity and convergence rates under these conditions. Finally, we verify the convergence rates obtained in Theorem
4.12
by numerical experiments.

2.1.
Stochastic integration

For a Banach space
X
and
p\geq 0
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{W}^{p}(0,T;X):=\{\Phi\in L^{p}(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T;X)),\ \Phi:(\Omega_{T},\mathcal{P}_{T})\to(X,\mathcal{B}(X))\ \text{is measurable}\},

and use the notation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T):=\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T;L_{2}(H)),\qquad\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T):=\mathcal{N}_{W}^{0}(0,T;L_{2}(H)).

An elementary integrand
\Phi\in\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T)
, is of the form,

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Phi_{n}\chi_{(t_{n},t_{n+1}]},\qquad\Phi_{n}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}},P;L_{2}(H)),

where
N>0
is an integer,
\chi_{D}(\cdot)
is the indicator function on the set
D
, and the Itô integral with respect to
\Phi
is defined as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW:=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Phi_{n}(W(t_{n+1}\wedge t)-W(t_{n}\wedge t)).

It may be seen that for
\Phi
elementary:

(a)

Itô’s isometry holds

(2.2)

\displaystyle E[\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{2}]=\int_{0}^{t}E[\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}]\,ds,

(b)

for any
p>0
, there is
C_{p}>0
such that the following Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality holds,

(2.3)

\displaystyle E[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{p}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}E[\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}]\,ds\bigg)^{p/2},

(see Theorem 4.36 in
[
4
]
), and

(c)

\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi\,dW
has continuous trajectories
P
-a.s.

An immediate consequence of (a)–(c) above is that the Itô integral extends (by continuity) to
\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T)
, and (a)–(c) also holds for integrals with respect to integrands in
\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T)
. The procedure of extending the integral to
\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)
, which in literature is referred to as “stochastically integrable processes”, is called the localization procedure (see, e.g., Chapter 4 in
[
4
]
). In Theorem
3.1
we provide an alternative way of performing this extension, in the setting of Lemma
2.2
.

3.
Framework

The following two theorems are the main results of this paper, and contain Burkholder–Davis–Gundy type inequalities for Itô integrals with values in
L^{0}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Itô integral,

\displaystyle\Phi\mapsto\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi\,dW,

with
\Phi
elementary, extends by continuity to a continuous linear mapping,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)\to L^{0}(\Omega;C([0,T];H))\cap\mathcal{N}_{W}^{0}(0,T;H),

where the topologies on
\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)
and
L^{0}(\Omega;C([0,T];H))\cap\mathcal{N}_{W}^{0}(0,T;H)
are those given by the metric (
2.1
), i.e. convergence in probability of random variables in
L^{2}(0,T;L_{2}(H))
and
C([0,T];H)
, respectively. For any
\Phi\in\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)
and
p>0
, there is
C_{p}>0
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{p}E[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt]^{p/2}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the inequality of the theorem holds for
\Phi
elementary, since elementary integrands are dense in
\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)
,
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi\,dW\in L^{0}(\Omega;C([0,T];H))\cap\mathcal{N}_{W}^{0}(0,T;H)
for
\Phi
elementary, and the metrics,

\displaystyle d(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=E[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds]^{1/2},\quad\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T),

and

\displaystyle d(x,y)=E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|x(t)-y(t)\|_{H}^{2}]^{1/2},\quad x,y\in L^{0}(\Omega;C([0,T];H))\cap\mathcal{N}_{W}^{0}(0,T;H),

define complete metrics on
\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)
and
L^{0}(\Omega;C([0,T];H))\cap\mathcal{N}_{W}^{0}(0,T;H)
, respectively, due to Lemma
2.2
. Note in particular that convergence in the metrics above imply convergence in
P_{T}
-measure of random variables with values in
L_{2}(H)
and
H
, respectively, and so any limit will be
\mathcal{P}_{T}
-measurable. Thus, the extension by continuity of the integral follows by the inequality of the theorem with
p=2
.

We now show the inequality for
\Phi
elementary, starting with the case
p\in(0,2]
. To that end, define

\displaystyle Z(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds,

and the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau:=\inf\{t\in[0,T]\ :\ Z(t)\geq 1\},

with convention
\inf\emptyset:=T
. We have

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle=E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}(\chi_{\{Z(T)<1\}}+\chi_{\{Z(T)\geq 1\}})]

\displaystyle\leq E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}\chi_{\{Z(T)<1\}}]+E[\chi_{\{Z(T)\geq 1\}}]

\displaystyle=:(i)+(ii).

For
(i)
, we get using that
\tau=T
when restricted to the set
\{Z(T)<1\}
,
\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi\in\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T)
and the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality (
2.3
)

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}\chi_{\{Z(T)<1\}}]

\displaystyle\leq E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq E[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}E[\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt]^{p/2}

\displaystyle=C_{p}E[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt]^{p/2}.

For
(ii)
, we note that

\displaystyle E[\chi_{\{Z(T)>1\}}]\leq E[1\wedge Z(T)]=E[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt]\leq E[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt]^{p/2}.

Combining these terms in the case of
p\in(0,2]
, we get the estimate for these powers.

For
p>2
, define

\displaystyle X(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\,dW,

the stopping time (with convention
\inf\emptyset:=T
) and stopped process

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}:=\inf\{t\in[0,T]\ :\ \|X(t)\|_{H}\geq 1\},\quad X^{\prime}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{[0,\tau^{\prime}]}\Phi\,dW,

and note in particular that
1\wedge\|X(t)\|_{H}\leq\|X^{\prime}(t)\|_{H}
and
1\wedge\|X^{\prime}(t)\|_{H}=\|X^{\prime}(t)\|_{H}
. By the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X(t)\|_{H}^{p}]\leq E[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X^{\prime}(t)\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{p}E[\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi_{[0,\tau^{\prime}]}\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds]^{p/2}.

Owing to Itô’s isometry and the case
p=2
shown above, we get

\displaystyle E[\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\chi_{[0,\tau^{\prime}]}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds]

\displaystyle=E[\|X^{\prime}(T)\|_{H}^{2}]

\displaystyle=E[1\wedge\|X^{\prime}(T)\|_{H}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq CE[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\chi_{[0,\tau^{\prime}]}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds]

\displaystyle\leq CE[1\wedge\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds],

which when inserted into the expression above gives the inequality for
p>2
.
∎

The following theorem is a modified version of the inequality of Theorem
3.1
above, and will be key for deriving pathwise properties of the Itô integral.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any sequence
\{\Phi_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{W}(0,T)
and
p\geq 2
, there is
C_{p}>0
, such that

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{p}E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt\bigg)^{p/2}].

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
3.1
. Assume for the moment that
\Phi_{n}
are elementary, as general
\Phi_{n}
follows by density. Define the process and stopping time

\displaystyle Z(t):=\sum_{n}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,ds\bigg)^{p/2},\quad\tau:=\inf\{t\in[0,T]\ :\ Z(t)\geq 1\},

with convention
\inf\emptyset:=T
. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle=E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}(\chi_{\{Z(T)<1\}}+\chi_{\{Z(T)\geq 1\}})]

\displaystyle\leq E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}\chi_{\{Z(T)<1\}}]+E[\chi_{\{Z(T)\geq 1\}}]

\displaystyle=:(i)+(ii).

For
(i)
we have, using that
\tau=T
when restricted to the set
\{Z(T)<1\}
,
\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\in\mathcal{N}_{W}^{2}(0,T)
, the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality (
2.3
), and Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}\chi_{\{Z(T)<1\}}]

\displaystyle\leq E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq E[\sum_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}E[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\,dW\|_{H}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\sum_{n}E[\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt]^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\sum_{n}E[\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt\bigg)^{p/2}]

\displaystyle=C_{p}E[\sum_{n}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi_{[0,\tau]}\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt\bigg)^{p/2}]

\displaystyle=C_{p}E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt\bigg)^{p/2}].

For
(ii)
, we get

\displaystyle E[\chi_{\{Z(T)\geq 1\}}]\leq E[1\wedge Z(T)]=E[1\wedge\sum_{n}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(H)}^{2}\,dt\bigg)^{p/2}].

∎

The following is a version of the Kolmogorov continuity test which accommodates stochastic processes that are only in
L^{0}
, and is tailored for the inequality of Theorem
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space, and
x:[0,T]\times\Omega\to X
a stochastic process. If for some
\delta,\beta>0
,

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sum_{m\geq n}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}\frac{d(x(jT2^{-m}),x((j+1)T2^{-m}))^{\delta}}{(T2^{-m})^{\beta\delta}}]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,

the trajectories of
x
are
\beta
-Hölder continuous
P
-a.s.

Proof.

The proof is similar to, e.g., the proof of Theorem 3.3 in
[
4
]
, but with a few modifications. See Appendix
A
for the details.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

From Lemma
3.3
we can deduce the following (weaker) continuity test, which is more analogous to the usual one (see, e.g., Theorem 3.3 in
[
4
]
): if for some
\delta,\epsilon,C>0
, and
t,s\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle E[1\wedge d(x(t),x(s))^{\delta}]\leq C|t-s|^{1+\epsilon},

then for any
\beta<\epsilon/\delta
,
x
has
\beta
-Hölder continuous trajectories
P
-a.s. However, this test will not be able to detect any Hölder continuity in the example we consider in the next section.

The next lemma is an extension of the technique usually used in order to derive rates of pathwise convergence from convergence in
L^{p}
,
p\geq 1
, and accommodates random variables in
L^{0}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x_{h}:\Omega\to[0,\infty)
,
h\in(0,1)
, be a collection of random variables, and suppose that for some
p>0
and sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n}

\displaystyle E[1\wedge\sum_{m\geq n}\frac{x_{h_{m}}^{p}}{h_{m}^{p}}]\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Then there exists a random variable
M:\Omega\to[0,\infty)
, independent of
h_{n}
, ensuring that
x_{h_{n}}\leq Mh_{n}

P
-a.s.

Proof.

Note that for any integer
n\geq 0

\displaystyle P(\limsup_{m}\{\frac{x_{h_{m}}}{h_{m}}>1\})

\displaystyle\leq P(\{\sup_{m\geq n}\frac{x_{h_{m}}^{p}}{h_{m}^{p}}>1\})

\displaystyle\leq P(\{\sum_{m\geq n}\frac{x_{h_{m}}^{p}}{h_{m}^{p}}>1\})

\displaystyle\leq E[1\wedge\sum_{m\geq n}\frac{x_{h_{m}}^{p}}{h_{m}^{p}}]\to 0,

and so
P(\limsup_{n}\{\frac{x_{h_{n}}}{h_{n}}>1\})=0
. Therefore
\sup_{n}\frac{x_{h_{n}}}{h_{n}}<\infty

P
-a.s., and setting

\displaystyle M(\omega):=\begin{cases}\sup_{n}\frac{x_{h_{n}}}{h_{n}},\quad&\text{when }\sup_{n}\frac{x_{h_{n}}}{h_{n}}<\infty,\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

we find
x_{h_{n}}\leq Mh_{n}

P
-a.s.
∎

With Lemma
3.3
and
3.5
at hand, we are ready to study Hölder regularity and convergence rates of numerical approximations of stochastic evolution equations with values in
L^{0}
. The idea is to use Theorem
3.2
combined with Lemma
3.3
and Lemma

, respectively.

4.
Example: a linear parabolic SPDE with generalized Whittle–Matérn noise

In this section we apply the framework of the previous section to the linear parabolic stochastic evolution equation (
1.1
), where the noise is scaled by a scalar process, ensuring that it is statistically irregular enough for the
L^{2}
-dependent approaches to no longer apply.

Let
H:=L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. The operators
A_{j}:D(A_{j})\to H
in (
1.1
) are related to sesquilinear forms
a_{j}:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
, by
(A_{j}u,v)_{H}=a_{j}(u,v)
for any
u\in D(A_{j})\subseteq V,v\in V
, with
V\subseteq H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
some closed subspace. Here,
H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
is defined in the usual way as the completion of
C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
using the
H^{1}
-norm, given by
\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}:=\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. The sesquilinear forms are given by

\displaystyle a_{j}(u,v):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{A}_{j}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}+(b_{j}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{v}+\alpha_{j}u\overline{v}\,dx,\quad j=1,2,

with coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
b_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\alpha_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\overline{v}
is the complex conjugate of
v
.

We make the following assumption on (
1.1
).

Assumption 4.1
.

(M1):

There is

Proof of lemma
2.2
.

That
d_{p}
is a metric follows from the Minkowski inequality for integrals.

First we show that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{P}x\implies d_{p}(x_{n},x)\to 0
. Since
x_{n}
converges to
x
in probability, there exists a subsequence,
\{x_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
converging to
x
almost surely. Hence,
1\wedge d(x_{n_{k}},x)^{p}
converges to
0
in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
by dominated convergence theorem. Note that for any subsequence
\{x_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
we can find such a subsequence. Therefore, since convergence in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
is in a topology, the original sequence
1\wedge d(x_{n},x)^{p}
has to converge to
0
in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, and therefore also in our metric.

For the other direction,
d_{p}(x_{n},x)\to 0\implies x_{n}\xrightarrow{P}x
, we note that since
1\wedge d(x_{n},x)^{p}\to 0
in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
, we must have that it also converges in probability. Therefore also
d(x_{n},x)
converges to
0
in probability, and the implication follows.

It remains to show completeness. For this it suffices to show that any metric that gives the topology of convergence in probability is complete. Thus, it suffices to show it only for the metric
d_{1}(x,y)
. To do so, we use the Cauchy summability characterization of completeness: that is,
(L^{0}(\Omega;X),d_{1})
is complete if and only if it has the property that for every sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq L^{0}(\Omega;X)
, where,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{1}(x_{n},x_{n+1})<\infty,

we must have
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\in L^{0}(\Omega;X)
. Note that if,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{1}(x_{n},x_{n+1})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}E[1\wedge d(x_{n},x_{n+1})]=E[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1\wedge d(x_{n},x_{n+1})]<\infty,

we must have that
\sum_{n}d(x_{n},x_{n+1})<\infty
for almost every
\omega
. Therefore, for almost every
\omega
we must have that
x_{n}(\omega)\to x(\omega)
, by the completeness of
X
, and so
x_{n}\to x
almost surely, and therefore also converges in probability. It follows that
x\in L^{0}(\Omega;X)
, since it is an almost sure limit of measurable functions, and completeness follows.
∎

t=t^{\prime}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{\infty}\epsilon_{j}2^{-j}
,
\epsilon_{j}\in\{0,1\}
, with
j_{0}:=-\lceil-\log_{2}(t-s)\rceil
. Therefore, using (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we get

\displaystyle d(x(t),x(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq d(x(t^{\prime}+\epsilon_{j_{0}}2^{-j_{0}}),x(t^{\prime}))+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{\infty}d(x(t^{\prime}+\sum_{n=j_{0}}^{j+1}\epsilon_{n}2^{-n}),x(t^{\prime}+\sum_{n=j_{0}}^{j}\epsilon_{n}2^{-n}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{\infty}2^{N(\omega)}2^{-\beta j}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{N(\omega)}(1-2^{-\beta})(t-s)^{\beta},

and similarly for
d(x(t^{\prime}),x(s))
. This gives,

\displaystyle d(x(t),x(s))\leq 2^{N(\omega)+1}(1-2^{-\beta})|t-s|^{\beta}.

∎
