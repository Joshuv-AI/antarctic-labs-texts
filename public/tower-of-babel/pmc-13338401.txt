Randomized, open-label study of the short-term pharmacokinetics of oral magnesium oxide in healthy volunteers
Authors: R K Swetha, Balasubramaniam Gayathri, Vijaya Kumar, K Sananthya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnesium oxide, though poorly soluble with low bioavailability, is widely used for magnesium supplementation due to its high elemental content and affordability. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and short-term, absorption-related pharmacokinetics of oral magnesium oxide at two dosage levels in healthy male volunteers. A randomized, open-label pharmacokinetic study was conducted in 24 healthy male volunteers aged 18–45 years, who were equally assigned to receive a single oral dose of either 750 mg or 2000 mg of magnesium oxide. Serum magnesium concentrations were measured at baseline and multiple time points up to 12 h post-dose using a colorimetric assay. Pharmacokinetic parameters related to systemic exposure and absorption, including Cmax, Tmax, and AUC0–12, were calculated using non-compartmental analysis. Parameters dependent on terminal elimination (t1/2, AUC0–∞, CL/F, MRT) were not estimated because a clear terminal log-linear phase was not identifiable within the 12-hour sampling window. Safety was assessed by monitoring vital signs, laboratory parameters, ECG, and adverse events. The study showed non-dose-proportional systemic exposure, with no proportional increase in Cmax or AUC at the higher dose. Incremental exposure parameters were also comparable between doses, further suggesting non-dose-proportional absorption within the studied dose range. Tmax was significantly shorter in the 2000 mg group (p = 0.025). Both doses were well tolerated without serious adverse events or gastrointestinal side effects. In summary, this study characterizes the short-term absorption pharmacokinetics of oral magnesium oxide, demonstrating non-proportional systemic exposure across the 750–2000 mg dose range within the 12-hour observation period. As only two dose levels were evaluated, further studies with multiple doses and formal dose-proportionality analyses are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms. These findings provide exploratory pharmacokinetic insights and should not be extrapolated to clinical efficacy or perioperative dosing. Introduction

Magnesium is the second most abundant intracellular cation and serves as an essential cofactor for over 300 enzymatic reactions regulating diverse physiological processes1. Maintaining adequate magnesium levels is crucial for normal cellular and systemic function2. Hypomagnesemia is highly prevalent, affecting 10–30% of the general population and up to 60% of critically ill patients3. This deficiency is significantly associated with increased postoperative pain, cardiac arrhythmias, and prolonged recovery, highlighting magnesium’s physiological importance in surgical populations4. Consequently, magnesium administration is valued for its analgesic, neuroprotective, and antiarrhythmic properties through mechanisms like antagonism of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors5.

Magnesium oxide (MgO) is widely used for magnesium supplementation due to its high elemental content and affordability. However, MgO exhibits inherently low aqueous solubility, resulting in a low systemic bioavailability, typically estimated between 4 and 10%. Despite its clinical importance, comprehensive data characterizing the pharmacokinetics (PK) and dose proportionality of oral MgO are sparse and inconsistent6. The poor solubility requires gastric acid for conversion into absorbable magnesium ions, which complicates absorption kinetics, and previous studies have lacked the detailed characterization needed to better understand absorption characteristics7. Previous systematic reviews have also highlighted the clinical benefits of magnesium administration in perioperative settings, including improved analgesia and reduced opioid requirements8. Although magnesium oxide has been widely used clinically, detailed pharmacokinetic data describing the short-term systemic exposure following commonly used oral doses remain limited. In particular, comparative pharmacokinetic data evaluating different single oral dose levels under controlled conditions are scarce.

This study was designed to rigorously evaluate the safety and single-dose pharmacokinetic profiles of oral magnesium oxide at two distinct dosage levels: 750 mg and 2000 mg in healthy male volunteers. The primary objective was to characterize the early absorption pharmacokinetics of oral magnesium oxide following single-dose administration and to evaluate whether systemic exposure increased proportionally across two dose levels. We hypothesized that the intestinal absorption of MgO might exhibit non-linear behavior within the studied dose range, potentially reflecting capacity-limited intestinal transport mechanisms described in previous studies9.

Methodology

Study design

This was a randomized, open-label pharmacokinetic study conducted at the Clinical Trial and Research Unit, SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Center. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP)10. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. ECR/8958/INST/TN/2013/RR-19; dated 23/12/2024), and the study was registered prospectively with the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2025/02/081449; dated 28/02/2025). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either 750 mg or 2000 mg of oral magnesium oxide using a computer-generated randomization sequence prepared prior to study initiation. Allocation was performed sequentially according to the randomization list. Because the objective of this study was to characterize the pharmacokinetic profile of orally administered magnesium oxide following single-dose administration, a placebo control group was not included. Instead, baseline serum magnesium concentrations were measured, and baseline-corrected analyses were performed to account for the endogenous nature of magnesium.

Study population

Twenty-four healthy male volunteers aged 18–45 years, weighing at least 50 kg, were recruited. Eligibility criteria included normal clinical evaluation and laboratory tests, no history of smoking or alcohol intake, and no use of medication or supplements that could influence magnesium pharmacokinetics for at least 14 days prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria encompassed systemic disorders (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, tuberculosis); clinically significant abnormalities in hepatic, renal, cardiac, gastrointestinal, neurological, endocrine, respiratory, metabolic, psychiatric, or hematological function; recent surgery; substance abuse; known hypersensitivity to magnesium compounds; participation in other clinical trials within 3 months; and significant blood donation or loss within the preceding three months. As a phase I exploratory pharmacokinetic study, the population was intentionally restricted to healthy young male volunteers to minimize biological variability. The findings are therefore not generalizable to females, elderly individuals, or patients with comorbidities or perioperative stress.

Randomization

Participants were randomized using a simple randomization method into two equal groups (n = 12 each) to receive a single oral dose of either 750 mg or 2000 mg of magnesium oxide.

Allocation concealment

Sealed opaque envelopes containing group allocation were prepared by an independent staff member who was not involved in participant recruitment or assessment. The envelopes were sequentially numbered and were opened only after participant enrollment and completion of baseline assessments.

Blinding

This was an open-label study; neither participants nor investigators were blinded to treatment allocation. However, laboratory personnel performing magnesium assays were blinded to group allocation to minimize bias in analytical procedures.

Procedure

Volunteers fasted overnight for at least 8 h before dosing to minimize dietary confounding on magnesium absorption. On the study day, participants received the assigned magnesium oxide dose orally with 200 mL of water in a seated position under continuous medical supervision. Standard meals were provided one-hour post-dose. (Participant recruitment and flow through the study are shown in Fig. 1)

Sample collection and processing

Venous blood samples (2 mL) were collected at baseline (pre-dose, T = 0) and at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4-, 6-, 8-, and 12-hours post-dose via an indwelling catheter. Blood was collected into heparinized tubes and centrifuged within 30 min to separate plasma, which was stored at 2–8 °C and analyzed on the same day. Hemolyzed or lipemic samples were excluded from analysis.

Assay method

Serum magnesium concentrations were assessed using a validated colorimetric assay based on complex formation with xylidyl blue in an alkaline medium, measured by Beckman AU480/AU680 analyzers. Calcium interference was blocked by adding GEDTA. The assay exhibited linearity between 0.5 and 8 mg/dL, with between-day variation under 5% and accuracy better than 10%. Detection and quantification limits were 0.1 and 0.5 mg/dL, respectively. Calibration was performed weekly or with each lot change. Serum magnesium concentrations were determined using a validated colorimetric assay, which is widely used in clinical laboratories for routine quantification of magnesium due to its reliability, reproducibility, and suitability within physiological concentration ranges. Colorimetric methods provide adequate analytical sensitivity and specificity for serum magnesium measurements and have been commonly employed in clinical pharmacokinetic and metabolic studies11,12. Although more advanced techniques such as atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) offer higher analytical sensitivity, these approaches are typically used for trace element analysis rather than routine clinical magnesium determination.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using non-compartmental analysis with PK Solutions 2.0 (Summit Research Services, Montrose, Colorado, USA). Primary absorption-related parameters included peak plasma concentration (Cmax) and time to peak concentration (Tmax). The area under the plasma concentration- time curve from time zero to 12 h (AUC0–12) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal rule. Because a clearly defined terminal log-linear elimination phase was not identifiable within the 12-hour sampling window, the linear-up/log-linear-down approach was not applied, and Ke-dependent parameters such as the elimination rate constant (kel) were not estimated.

Estimation of terminal elimination parameters requires identification of a clear log-linear elimination phase. Because a clear terminal log-linear elimination phase was not reliably identifiable within the 12-hour sampling window, Ke-dependent parameters such as elimination half-life (t1/2), AUC0–∞, mean residence time (MRT), and apparent oral clearance (CL/F) were not estimated. Because the study focused on early systemic exposure within a 12-hour observation period, extrapolation of AUC beyond the sampling window was not performed to avoid unreliable estimates.

Statistical analysis

The data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and confirmed for normality. The data, which are normally distributed, are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and for not normally distributed variables are summarized as median with interquartile range accordingly. Group comparisons for non-normally distributed data utilized the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons of each time point with baseline values were performed using a paired t-test.

A sample size of 24 was calculated for an 80% power to detect, at a 5% significance level, a 1.15 as the effect size (Cohen’s d)13. Data analysis was performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Safety monitoring

Vital signs were recorded throughout the study period. Laboratory safety evaluations included electrocardiography (ECG), renal and hepatic function tests, complete blood count, and serum electrolytes. Gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal discomfort were closely monitored. Any adverse events were documented. Participants received compensation consistent with ethical committee guidelines.

All participants completed the study and provided evaluable pharmacokinetic data. Pharmacokinetic parameters

Non-compartmental analysis over the 0–12 h demonstrated non-dose-proportional systemic exposure of magnesium following oral magnesium oxide, suggesting non-linear absorption that may reflect saturable processes. Because magnesium is an endogenous analyte, both observed and baseline-corrected (incremental) analyses were performed using each subject’s pre-dose concentration at 0 h (C0).

Non-proportionality in systemic exposure

Despite a 2.67-fold increase in the administered dose, the systemic delivery of magnesium did not increase proportionally. The key exposure parameters were similar across groups.

Parameters not reliably estimated

Because the sampling period focused on early absorption, only exposure metrics within the 12-hour observation window were considered reliable for pharmacokinetic interpretation.

A robust terminal log-linear elimination phase could not be confidently identified within the 0–12 h sampling window. Given the endogenous nature of magnesium and the absence of a distinct post-peak terminal decline Ke-dependent parameters (t1/2, AUC0–∞, CL/F, MRT) were not considered reliable and were not estimated. (Although such parameters can be derived from short studies when a clear, stable terminal decline with adequate quantifiable points is present, curves that continue to rise, plateau, or are dominated by baseline physiology yield unreliable estimates).

Safety and tolerability

Both magnesium oxide doses were well tolerated. No serious adverse events or clinically significant changes in vital signs, laboratory tests, or electrocardiography were observed during the study. Importantly, gastrointestinal adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, or abdominal pain were not reported. These findings support a favorable safety profile for single oral doses of magnesium oxide at both 750 mg and 2000 mg in healthy adult males.

Discussion

Although magnesium oxide has been widely used for many years, published pharmacokinetic data describing the early absorption characteristics of commonly used oral doses under controlled clinical conditions remain limited. The present study was designed to characterize the early absorption profile and short-term systemic exposure of two commonly used oral doses of magnesium oxide in healthy volunteers.

This study provides a rigorous, single-dose pharmacokinetic (PK) and safety evaluation of oral magnesium oxide (MgO) in healthy volunteers, yielding critical data on its absorption profile relevant to perioperative supplementation.

Because only two dose levels were evaluated, the present study does not allow formal assessment of dose proportionality. Future studies including additional dose levels and dedicated dose-proportionality analysis would be required to confirm saturable absorption mechanisms.

Our primary findings suggest non-dose-proportional systemic exposure of MgO across the studied dose range. Despite a 2.67-fold dose increase (2000 mg vs. 750 mg), systemic exposure did not increase proportionally, as reflected by the absence of statistically significant differences in Cmax and AUC0–12. This pattern was maintained after accounting for the endogenous nature of magnesium: incremental Cmax and baseline-corrected iAUC0–12 also remained similar between dose groups, with substantial inter-individual variability. Together, these results suggest a possible ceiling effect on MgO-related systemic magnesium delivery within this dose range14.

Magnesium oxide is characterized by slow dissolution and limited bioavailability, which likely contributes to the gradual rise in serum magnesium across the 12-hour observation period. Importantly, while total exposure was not dose-proportional, the absorption rate appeared dose-dependent. The significantly shorter Tmax in the 2000 mg group (median 2 h) compared with the 750 mg group (median 6 h) suggests that a higher luminal magnesium load may accelerate early uptake without increasing overall absorbed fraction. A possible explanation may involve previously described intestinal magnesium transport mechanisms, including regulated transcellular pathways and passive paracellular diffusion across intestinal tight junctions. Maier et al17. previous physiological reviews have described that magnesium absorption occurs through both active transcellular transport and passive paracellular pathways in the small intestine15, resulting in faster initial appearance of magnesium in the circulation but limited net gain in total systemic exposure. However, the present study was not designed to directly evaluate these molecular transport mechanisms, and therefore these interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating. Mori H et al16. reported that approximately 15% of orally administered MgO is absorbed, with peak plasma concentrations occurring around 3 h16.

Baseline correction proved especially informative in this context. Because serum magnesium is endogenous and homeostatically regulated, observed concentration–time curves inherently incorporate both physiological background and dose-related increments. The larger coefficient of variation (CV) for baseline-corrected metrics (incremental Cmax and iAUC0–12) underscores the heterogeneous absorption of MgO.

From a clinical standpoint, both doses demonstrated excellent tolerability, with no reported gastrointestinal adverse effects or clinically meaningful safety signals. This is noteworthy given the common association of higher-dose MgO regimens with gastrointestinal intolerance in routine use. The absence of symptoms in this trial may reflect the single-dose design and controlled study conditions. Although the incremental increases in circulating magnesium were modest, such changes may still be relevant in perioperative settings where magnesium’s pharmacodynamic effects, particularly the neuromodulatory and analgesic mechanisms, are not necessarily dependent on large absolute serum elevations.

The significantly shorter Tmax in the 2000 mg group (median 2 vs. 5 h, p = 0.009) suggests that the higher luminal concentration facilitated quicker initial absorption. This is likely to involve capacity-limited intestinal transport mechanisms. Experimental studies have suggested that magnesium absorption can occur through regulated transcellular transport pathways such as TRPM6/7, together with passive paracellular diffusion across intestinal tight junctions17. However, the present pharmacokinetic study was not designed to directly evaluate these molecular transport mechanisms, and therefore this interpretation should be considered exploratory. Previous investigations, including the work of Pardo et al20., have reported that magnesium absorption may demonstrate dose-dependent but non-linear characteristics, which may contribute to the non-dose-proportional Cmax and AUC observed in the present study18.

Both MgO doses were well tolerated with no reported gastrointestinal adverse effects. This is supported by Ivanovic et al., who showed that short-term MgO supplementation increased total and ionized serum magnesium without major electrolyte disturbances, suggesting non-dose-proportional absorption within the studied dose range19.

The modest increases in serum magnesium observed in this study may still be relevant for perioperative supplementation. Magnesium has established roles in pain modulation, including attenuation of central sensitization through NMDA receptor antagonism. Evidence from perioperative studies of magnesium (including intravenous formulations) suggests that even modest changes in circulating magnesium can be associated with improved analgesic outcomes20,21 Similarly, prior oral supplementation studies have reported good tolerability with measurable increases in serum magnesium over comparable sampling windows. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary pharmacokinetic information that may be relevant for future investigations of oral magnesium supplementation in perioperative settings. However, the present study did not evaluate clinical efficacy or analgesic outcomes, and therefore any clinical implications should be interpreted cautiously.

Although systemic absorption of MgO is limited, unabsorbed luminal magnesium may interact with the gut environment and microbiota. This gut-brain axis hypothesis could represent a complementary pathway through which oral MgO might influence perioperative recovery but requires dedicated mechanistic and clinical studies22,23.

Serum magnesium concentrations increased gradually over the 0–12 h period in both dose groups, consistent with slow dissolution and absorption of magnesium oxide24. Because a robust terminal log-linear decline was not identifiable within this window K-dependent parameters were not estimated. This gradual absorption profile may be relevant in perioperative contexts, although the present study was not designed to assess clinical outcomes. where sustained rather than abrupt changes in circulating magnesium are desirable25,26. Accordingly, the mechanistic explanations and potential clinical implications discussed above should be considered exploratory interpretations intended to guide future research rather than definitive conclusions derived from the present pharmacokinetic dataset.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the pharmacokinetic sampling window was limited to 12 h, and a robust terminal log-linear phase could not be clearly identified. Consequently, elimination-dependent parameters such as the elimination rate constant (kel), elimination half-life (t½), AUC₀–∞, apparent oral clearance (CL/F), and mean residence time (MRT) were not estimated. The present analysis therefore reflects short-term systemic exposure rather than complete pharmacokinetic characterization. Future studies with extended sampling beyond 24 h may allow clearer characterization of elimination kinetics.

Second, the study evaluated only two dose levels of magnesium Oxide. As a result, formal assessment of dose proportionality was not performed, and the findings should be interpreted as indicating a lack of dose proportionality within the studied range rather than definitive evidence of saturable absorption.

Finally, because magnesium is an endogenous analyte subject to tight physiological homeostatic regulation, pharmacokinetic interpretation may be influenced by baseline variability. Although baseline-corrected analyses were applied, the use of simple paired statistical tests may not fully account for repeated measurements and inter-individual variability. These findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. More advanced longitudinal analytical approaches may provide additional insight in future studies. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Conclusion

In summary, this study characterizes the short-term absorption pharmacokinetics of oral magnesium oxide, demonstrating non-proportional systemic exposure across the 750–2000 mg dose range within the 12-hour observation period. As only two dose levels were evaluated, further studies with multiple doses and formal dose-proportionality analyses are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms. These findings provide exploratory pharmacokinetic insights and should not be extrapolated to clinical efficacy or perioperative settings.
