Metal-Free Photoinduced Hydrogen Atom Transfer for Direct Thiolation of Hydridic α‑C(sp3)–H of Ethers
Authors: Alexander Karr, Elias Picazo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
C–S bond formation via hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) remains underdeveloped despite the importance of thioethers in organic synthesis, materials science, and medicinal chemistry. Herein, we report a mild, rapid, and operationally simple method for the direct α-C­(sp3)–S bond formation in ethers using alkyl or aryl thiosulfonates as sulfur sources and (4-methoxyphenyl)­(4-(trifluoromethyl)­phenyl)­methanone as the diaryl ketone catalyst. The reaction proceeds at room temperature under a 390 nm irradiation, delivering a broad range of thioethers in the absence of metals or stoichiometric oxidants. The method exhibits excellent functional group tolerance, predictable regioselectivity favoring the most hydridic C–H bonds, and compatibility with various cyclic and acyclic ethers and amides. Preliminary mechanistic studies support a radical pathway consistent with a well-established photoexcited ketone-mediated HAT followed by radical addition to the thiosulfonate. This approach expands the toolkit for redox-neutral C­(sp3)–S bond construction.

Introduction

Functionalizing C–H bonds has enabled site-specific transformations, where site-selectivity is governed by a complex interplay of kinetic and thermodynamic factors. − Hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) has gained popularity as a C–H functionalization strategy for its ability to take part in late-stage functionalization of organic scaffolds, improve atom economy, and activate select bond types. Polar effects can influence C–H bond activation in HAT mechanisms through strong charge transfer stabilization in the transition state (Figure A), enabling the predictable formation of valuable C–C or C–heteroatom bonds. Despite tremendous success in developing HAT strategies for C–N and C–O bond formation, methodologies for C–S bond formation remain limited but important in material, agrochemical, and pharmaceutical discoveries.

(E) This strategy involves a diaryl ketone that acts as a potent HAT reagent and photocatalyst for rapid synthesis of thioethers from hydridic α-C­(sp3)–H bonds using thiosulfonates.

Due to the significance of C–S bonds, several approaches for their construction have been developed. Although primary and secondary alkyl halides are most commonly reacted with thiols through bimolecular nucleophilic substitutions, there has been a rise in the development of systems leveraging alternative strategies (Figure B). − Reactions between alkyl halides and thiols can result in overalkylation or thiol oxidative coupling, forming undesired sulfonium salts or disulfides, respectively. Strategies employing transition-metal catalysis have been developed with Pd, Cu, and Ni under elevated temperatures, harsh alkaline conditions, or using directing groups to achieve targeted reactivity that primarily addresses C­(sp2)–S bond formation. Single-electron transformations have gained attention as a means to expand the scope of methodologies. Our group has contributed to this strategy by forming thioether products in the C­(sp3)–S space through iron catalysis. −

Photochemistry serves as an interesting paradigm to widen the single-electron approach. Ir/Ni metallaphotoredox procedures, redox active esters paired with an organo-photocatalyst (4CzIPN), and (super)­stoichiometric use of peroxides with heat, light, or metals have found success. − Tetra-n-butylammonium decatungstate (TBADT) is a popular inorganic catalyst used to provide a highly electrophilic oxygen radical when excited with 365 nm light (Figure C). , Although TBADT can typically be used in lower molar loadings than organic photocatalysts, its high molecular weight results in high mass loadings. In an effort to improve atom economy, reaction efficiency, and utility, − compounds with sufficiently high triplet energies and that feature an n → π transition have been implemented as dual-role reagents that operate as both a photocatalyst and HAT initiator. Recently, Lahiri and Maiti demonstrated that an organic dual role reagent, thioxanthone, can be leveraged to address the dependence on metallic- or peroxide-based strategies (Figure D). The search for efficient and inexpensive organic dual catalysts remains imperative. Although thiosulfonates have been previously explored in TBADT catalysis, , merging them with a diaryl ketone photocatalyst known from distinct photocatalysis applications , provides a rapid route to α-C­(sp3) thiolation of ethers. This approach expands direct, redox-neutral strategies for hydridic C–H bond activation (Figure E).

Results and Discussion

Reaction Design and Optimization

Due to the polarization across the S–S bond, thiosulfonates can participate in electrophilic, nucleophilic, and radical reactions with higher reactivity than N–S thiolating reagents. ,, The reactivity along with ease of preparation and stability led us to react tetrahydrofuran (THF, 1) with thiosulfonate 2 in the presence of known diaryl ketone A1 , and base to yield the desired thioether product 3 (Figure ). Initial optimization revealed that cosolvents used with solvent–substrate THF (1) lead to diminished yields when compared to the reaction using excess THF as the solvent (see Supporting Information Tables S1 and S2). The need for liquid substrates is a clear drawback, limiting the substrate scope of this system to common organic solvents and related molecules. Various carbonate, phosphate, and organic bases were examined (see Supporting Information Table S3), and it was determined that the inclusion of tripotassium phosphate (K3PO4) is optimal with a great degree of reproducibility within reaction duplicates (see Supporting Information Table S3 Note). Lithium, sodium, and potassium tribasic result in 62, 51, and 67% yields, respectively, while potassium dibasic provides a 52% product yield. Further optimization showed that 3.0 equiv of K3PO4 increases the yield to 76%. Omitting the base additive occasionally yielded the product in a 92% yield (Figure , entry 4), but these results were highly inconsistent.

Several catalysts known to undergo HAT in their excited state were evaluated (see Supporting Information Table S4). Thioxanthone and fluorenone yield 27% and 3% of product, respectively, while Michler’s ketone and DDQ give no reaction. Benzophenone provides the product in a 67% yield, consistent with efficient HAT arising from diaryl ketone scaffolds. The inclusion of a push–pull system in (4-methoxyphenyl)­(4-(trifluoromethyl)­phenyl)­methanone (A1) red-shifts molecular absorbance to better align with the 390 nm light source, providing greater photochemical reactivity. Although the reaction using 10 mol % of A1 catalyst yields the product in 76%, increasing catalytic loading to 30 mol % provides the product in an 81% yield. We opted to continue the study using 30 mol % of A1 though systems can evidently be independently optimized for lower catalyst loadings. Using degassed, HPLC grade THF further increases the reaction yield to 88% (see Supporting Information Table S5). The presence of oxygen hinders the reaction through presumed catalyst quenching, evidenced by the lower yield of 59% when running the reaction open to air. Increasing the intensity of the 390 nm PR160L Kessel lamp to 100% increases product formation by 5%, and the final reaction conditions at 0.2 M provide the desired sulfur-containing product in a 96% yield (see Supporting Information Tables S6 and S7). Control experiments demonstrate that the light and catalyst are critical for reactivity, while the base and cooling system are necessary for consistent levels of substrate conversion (Figure ). The strategic use of thiosulfonates is further highlighted by the lack of reactivity when using a disulfide or thiol as the sulfur source (Figure , entry 6).

Reaction Generality

With the optimized conditions in hand, a variety of substituted benzenesulfonothioates were evaluated in the thiolation of THF (1) (Figure ). In addition to an unsubstituted phenyl group, which provides product 6 in a 69% yield, electronic variations are well supported by the reaction. Examples of electron-donating groups include 4-OMe (4, 71%) and alkyl substituents such as 4-methyl group and 4-tert-butyl groups (3 in 88 and 5 in 82% yield, respectively). Electron-withdrawing 4-F, 4-Cl, 4-Br, and 4-CF3 groups provided the respective products 7–10 in good yield (63–74%). 3-Substituted benzenesulfonothioates were investigated to better evaluate inductive effects without resonance contributions. The 3-F, 3-Cl, 3-Br, 3-Me, and 3-OMe series 11–15 smoothly underwent the reaction in 73–81% yields, demonstrating robust reactivity independent of changes in the electronic character of the benzenesulfonothioate. Disubstituted 3,4-dichlorophenyl benzenesulfonothioate generates 16 in an 88% yield. We investigated 2-OMe, 2,4-dichlorophenyl, and 2,4-dimethylphenyl benzenesulfonothioates, which all provide their respective products 17–19 in 71, 80, and 58% yields, respectively. These results emphasize resilience to substrates with increased 2-substituted steric demands, which is of synthetic interest. Heterocycle-containing thiosulfonates also reliably form the desired C­(sp3)–S bond, as exemplified by pyrimidine- (73%) and furan (31%)-derived products 20 and 21. The low yield of 21 could be attributed to the additional sterics provided by the C2 methyl group at or its additional electron density.

Substrate scope of various aryl and alkyl thiosulfonates. Reactions were run on a 0.3 mmol scale with respect to the thiosulfonate.

We next tested our methodology with alkyl-thiosulfonates. The respective benzyl-thiosulfonate is compatible, producing the desired product 22 in a 69% yield. Further, single and double homologation of the benzyl-thiosulfonate substrate also provide products 23 and 24 in 66% and 75% yields, respectively. Reactivity with additional functionality was also demonstrated in the presence of an aryl chloride (25, 57%), a furan (26, 62%), an unprotected alcohol (27, 36%), and nonbenzylic, benzylic, and bis-benzylic secondary alkyl groups (28–30 in 47–86%). The diastereomeric ratio (d.r.) for product 29 was determined to be 2.5:1. It should be noted that tert-butylthiosulfonate fails to react, which is presumably due to the steric demand of the tert-butyl group (see Supporting Information Figure S5).

With this success, other commonly used ethereal molecules were examined as substrates (Figure ). To our delight, direct thiolation at α-C­(sp3)–H positions of 1,4-dioxane, tetrahydropyran, and diethyl ether was achieved to afford the respective sulfur-containing products 31–33 in good yields (51–81%) using (p-tolyl)-4-methylbenzenesulfonothioate 2 as the sulfur source. The following reactivity trend was identified: THF, tetrahydropyran, 1,4-dioxane, and diethyl ether. While tetrahydrofuran and tetrahydropyran differ only in ring size, we attribute the poor performance of diethyl ether to the entropic penalty associated with its freely rotating acyclic structure. Furthermore, the reduced reactivity of 1,4-dioxane relative to tetrahydropyran can be rationalized by the presence of the second electronegative oxygen atom at the β-position relative to the site of reactivity. Unable to contribute electron density through resonance, this atom exerts a purely inductive electron-withdrawing effect that decreases the hydridic nature of the targeted C–H bond. Acyclic amides are supported, exemplified by the formation of product 34 in a 30% yield from dimethylacetamide (DMA). The decrease in yield of 34 compared to that of 31–33 can be attributed to the electronic differences of the nucleophiles. The neighboring carbonyl group withdraws electron density from the nitrogen atom, causing the α-C­(sp3) hydrogen atom to be less hydridic. Next, hydridic species that display inequivalent hydrogen atoms were examined. 1,3-Dioxolane presents an interesting case of regioselectivity between the two distinct hydridic α-C­(sp3)–H bonds, providing a 4.5:1 ratio of 35a and b favoring C2–H bond activation over C4–H bond activation. N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), a lactam with both an endocyclic and exocyclic α-C­(sp3)–H bond, forms the desired C–S bond in 62% in a ratio of 16:1 favoring the endocyclic (36a) C–H bond. Both 1,3-dioxolane and NMP favor the hydrogen bearing the lowest bond dissociation energy (BDE), which also coincides with the formation of a more stable radical. , TBADT is reported to be selective for the C4–H site for 1,3 dioxolane, while matching our NMP endocyclic regioselectivity, albeit with lower selectivity (10:1). Our observed ratios are also higher than those produced when leveraging peroxide and heat (5:1). 2-Methyltetrahydrofuran, an asymmetric cyclic ether, was investigated to probe additional α-C­(sp3)–H substitution patterns, with one pathway traversing through a tertiary carbon radical and the other forming a secondary radical intermediate. It was found that isomers 37a and 37b were formed in a 3.4:1 ratio favoring the product forming through the tertiary carbon radical. This result demonstrates that HAT selectivity in our system still prefers hydrogen atoms with the lowest BDE, despite the additional steric effects imparted by the methyl group. , The minor product was formed as a 1:1 diastereomeric mixture. The 3.4:1 ratio is slightly higher than what is observed through using electrochemical methods or peroxides. 1,2-Dimethoxyethane, an acyclic diether with a primary and secondary site, was also explored. The desired C–S bond was made in a 44% yield and a selectivity of 1.5:1 for 38a:38b. This regioselectivity further demonstrates that HAT under our conditions happens with the hydrogen bearing the lowest BDE. Together, the expansion into alkyl substrates and enhanced selectivity highlight the complementary nature of our strategy to alternative TBADT, peroxides and heat, electrochemical, or photochemical approaches.

Mechanistic Insights

To gain preliminary mechanistic insight, (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-yl)­oxyl (TEMPO) was added under standard reaction conditions (see Supporting Information Figure S3). The reaction is completely inhibited in the presence of the spin trap, consistent with a radical pathway. Quadrupole-time-of-flight high-resolution mass spectrometry (QTOF–HRMS) analysis of the reaction crude mixture shows an m/z of 228.1953, corresponding to the formation of the intermediate I proton adduct [M + H]+ (Figure A). These data are consistent with a mechanism involving a HAT event on tetrahydrofuran 1, followed by addition to the sulfur transfer agent 2 via a bimolecular homolytic substitution (SH2) pathway. The absence of intermediates II, III, and adducts thereof, further aligns with the lack of direct, light-induced homolytic cleavage of 2 under a 390 nm photoirradiation. A competitive kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiment resulted in a significant 4:1 (K H/K D) ratio, indicating that C­(sp3)–H bond cleavage may be the rate-determining step (Figure B). Given the large excess of substrate-solvent 1,4-dioxane (8.80 mmol deuterated and 8.80 mmol nondeuterated for a total of 17.6 mmol), the reaction was allowed to run to complete consumption of the benzenesulfonothioate substrate (see Supporting Information Figure S4 for NMR). This large excess, even at a 100% conversion of the limiting coupling partner, causes the H/D solvent pool to operate under pseudozeroth-order conditions. Consequently, substrate depletion effects are inherently prevented, and the final product ratio directly reflects the intrinsic intermolecular competitive KIE.

(A) TEMPO trapping experiment, forming intermediate I per QTOF-HRMS analysis. (B) Competitive KIE experiment using 1,4 dioxane. (C) Proposed mechanism.

These mechanistic data, in combination with the previously reported literature, enable the construction of the plausible mechanism shown in Figure C. Catalyst A1 is excited into its triplet radical state, A1*, under irradiation by a Kessil PR-160 390 nm lamp (see Supporting Information Figures S1 and S2 for UV–vis). The newly formed A1* diradical possesses a potent electrophilic oxygen radical that can readily accept hydridic hydrogen atoms. A HAT event can thus occur between the α-C­(sp3)–H of THF and the oxygen radical in A1*. This forms a radical at C2 of THF and transforms A1* to diaryl ketone radical A1′, which is known to have a half-wave reduction potential of E 1/2 red (A1) = – 2.05 V vs Ag/AgNO3 in MeCN. A SOMOphile in the form of thiosulfonates can then act as a radical acceptor, extruding a sulfinyl radical while forming the new C­(sp3)–S bond through THF radical capture. A1′ can then go through a single electron transfer with the sulfinyl radical to produce a sulfinate anion. The sulfinate anion or exogenous base can recycle A1′ into A1 and reset the catalytic cycle.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a mild, metal-free, and operationally simple method for the direct thiolation of hydridic α-C­(sp3)–H bonds in ethers and related substrates. Using (4-methoxyphenyl)­(4-(trifluoromethyl)­phenyl)­methanone as a photocatalyst and HAT reagent along with thiosulfonates as versatile sulfur sources, the transformation proceeds efficiently at room temperature under a 390 nm irradiation. Although the method is limited to liquid solvent–substrates, the protocol delivers a wide range of thioethers in good to excellent yields, exhibiting broad functional group tolerance and predictable regioselectivity that consistently favors the most hydridic C–H bonds. Preliminary mechanistic studies support a classical photoexcited ketone-mediated HAT followed by radical addition to the thiosulfonate via an SH2 pathway, with C–H cleavage contributing to the rate-determining step. This work expands on redox-neutral, atom-economical C­(sp3)–S bond construction.
