Triple M Overlap Syndrome After Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors
Authors: Maria F Osorio, Alekhya Mitta, Fabricio Malaguez-Webber, Chinmay T Jani, Marijo Bilusic, Jennifer Maning, Omar Wever-Pinzon, Yiannis S Chatzizisis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitor–associated Triple M overlap syndrome (TMOS), defined by concurrent myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis, is a rare but life-threatening immune-related adverse event. We report a single-center case series of 8 consecutive patients who developed TMOS during immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy for solid malignancies between 2023 and 2025. Median age was 76 years and median time to symptom onset was 14.5 days after exposure. All patients had myositis symptoms; 5 required mechanical ventilation and 1 subsequently required tracheostomy. Cardiac involvement was characterized by troponin elevation and frequent electrical abnormalities, including complete atrioventricular block, ventricular tachyarrhythmias, and bundle branch block, despite preserved left ventricular systolic function on imaging. Corticosteroid monotherapy was insufficient in practice, and all patients required additional immunomodulatory therapy. TMOS requires early recognition, close monitoring, and rapid multidisciplinary escalation of care.Key Words: bulbar weakness, cardio-oncology, complete atrioventricular block, conduction disease, immunotherapy toxicity, steroid-refractory disease

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer therapy, but they can also cause clinically significant immune-related adverse events involving multiple organ systems. In the broader oncology population, the development of mild immune-related adverse events has been associated with improved tumor response and better survival outcomes among patients treated with ICIs.1 Nevertheless, a subset of these events may be severe and life-threatening. Among the most serious is ICI-associated myocarditis, a rare complication with an estimated incidence of 0.5% to 2% that carries a high risk of death, with reported case-fatality rates exceeding 40%.2 This concern is particularly relevant given the rapid expansion in the use of these agents since their first regulatory approval in 2011, with the proportion of patients eligible for ICI therapy rising from 1.5% in 2011 to more than 56% by 2023.3

Outcomes appear to be particularly poor when myocarditis occurs together with myositis and myasthenia gravis (MG). Triple M overlap syndrome (TMOS) can progress rapidly, with malignant arrhythmias, respiratory failure due to neuromuscular involvement, and severe clinical deterioration despite preserved left ventricular systolic function,4 with reported mortality as high as 37% to 60%.5,6 Importantly, organ-specific guidelines are available for ICI-associated myocarditis and for the management of neurologic immune-related toxicities,7,8 yet there is no unified, phenotype-specific diagnostic or therapeutic algorithm for this triad, and the optimal timing, sequencing, and escalation of adjunctive immunomodulatory therapy remain poorly defined. We describe an 8-patient single-center case series of TMOS to highlight common diagnostic pitfalls and the frequent need for early multimodal immunosuppression.

Case Series

This retrospective case series was approved by the institutional review board at the University of Miami (Protocol #20250860). We identified 8 consecutive patients who developed TMOS after ICI therapy between 2023 and 2025. Inclusion required active ICI exposure for solid malignancy, clinical evidence of myocarditis, myositis, and MG, and exclusion of alternative etiologies when clinically indicated. All patients underwent continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), and serial troponin and creatine kinase assessment.

Median age was 76 years (range 57-80 years), and all patients were male. Underlying malignancies included melanoma (n = 3), renal cell carcinoma (n = 2), non–small-cell lung cancer (n = 1), gastric cancer (n = 1), and prostate cancer (n = 1). ICIs included nivolumab (n = 4), pembrolizumab (n = 3), and ipilimumab plus nivolumab (n = 1). Symptom onset occurred early, at a median of 14.5 days after ICI exposure. Cardiac involvement was characterized predominantly by troponin elevation (Figure 1) and electrical abnormalities, including complete atrioventricular block (n = 3), ventricular tachyarrhythmias (n = 2), right bundle branch block (n = 3), trifascicular block (n = 1), and atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (n = 1), despite preserved or only mildly reduced left ventricular systolic function on imaging. All 8 patients had evidence of myositis, characterized predominantly by proximal weakness involving the upper and/or lower extremities; 4 also reported myalgias. Creatine kinase ranged from 1,200 to 8,500 U/L. Bulbar and/or ocular neuromuscular manifestations were common. Five patients required mechanical ventilation, and 1 required tracheostomy during the acute course. Notably, 5 of 8 patients were seronegative for MG antibodies, underscoring reliance on clinical phenotype rather than serology to trigger urgent therapy.

Patient 1

A 79-year-old man with gastric cancer receiving nivolumab developed TMOS 7 days after ICI initiation. TTE showed preserved systolic function. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) showed linear mid-myocardial late gadolinium enhancement in the basal anteroseptal wall and a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of approximately 50%. No T2-based marker of edema was present; therefore, the modified Lake Louise criteria were not fully met. His course was marked by progressive electrical instability, including right bundle branch block, trifascicular block, and a high burden of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. He later developed bulbar and respiratory weakness, consistent with seronegative MG. Immunomodulatory therapy was escalated from pulse-dose intravenous (IV) methylprednisolone to prolonged high-dose prednisone, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) with dose escalation, abatacept, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and later plasma exchange (PLEX) and ruxolitinib (Table 1). Despite these interventions, he progressed to treatment-refractory respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation and died.

Patient 2

A 57-year-old man with melanoma developed dyspnea, ptosis, and generalized weakness 11 days after nivolumab initiation. At an outside hospital he was treated with pulse-dose corticosteroids and IVIG, and developed complete heart block requiring temporary pacing followed by permanent pacemaker implantation before transfer. On arrival to our institution, troponin remained markedly elevated and TTE showed preserved LVEF. MG serologies were positive for acetylcholine receptor modulating and blocking antibodies. His course was complicated by progressive bulbar and respiratory weakness requiring intubation, as well as hospital-acquired pneumonia. Despite corticosteroids, MMF, pyridostigmine, IVIG, and PLEX, he deteriorated and died.

Patient 3

A 77-year-old man with melanoma receiving pembrolizumab presented with ptosis and generalized myalgias 18 days after treatment initiation. He developed complete heart block requiring dual-chamber permanent pacemaker implantation before transfer. On arrival to our institution, troponin remained elevated and TTE showed preserved LVEF. Although CMR was not feasible, myocarditis was supported clinically by marked biomarker elevation and subsequent arrhythmias, including an episode of sustained ventricular tachycardia and high ectopy burden. He later developed aspiration and worsening bulbar weakness with respiratory failure requiring intubation, followed by cardiogenic shock. Despite corticosteroids, pyridostigmine, IVIG, PLEX, MMF, and abatacept, he had no meaningful recovery and was transitioned to comfort-focused care and died the next day.

Patient 4

An 80-year-old man with melanoma receiving nivolumab presented with ptosis, gait instability, and generalized weakness 21 days after ICI exposure. Electrocardiogram showed a new-onset right bundle branch block, and TTE showed preserved LVEF. Neuromuscular symptoms improved with pulse-dose corticosteroids and IVIG. Despite persistent troponin elevation, he had no overt cardiac symptoms or malignant arrhythmias. He was seronegative for MG antibodies and received pyridostigmine and IVIG. He improved without the need for advanced cardiac or respiratory support and was discharged home on a 6-week corticosteroid taper and pyridostigmine, which was discontinued 5 months after initial presentation. He continued physical therapy and returned to his baseline level of activity by 6 months and at 22-months follow-up reported no recurrent symptoms.

Patient 5

A 75-year-old man with renal cell carcinoma receiving a third dose of nivolumab and cabozantinib presented 6 days after treatment with acute dysphagia. TTE demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function with basal inferoseptal and inferior akinesis. His principal electrical manifestation was new-onset atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. MG was supported by positive acetylcholine receptor antibodies. He was treated with IV corticosteroids, IVIG, and repeated PLEX, with gradual improvement. He was discharged on a prolonged corticosteroid taper. Two weeks later, he was readmitted with bilateral lower-extremity deep venous thromboses. Fourteen months after the acute presentation, he underwent tracheostomy after prolonged intubation and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy placement following elective C4 to C7 spinal surgery for progression of metastatic disease. At his last follow-up, 20 months after initial presentation, he continued to have residual bulbar symptoms and generalized weakness, largely attributed to deconditioning. He is currently receiving home hospice care.

Patient 6

A 66-year-old man with renal cell carcinoma treated with pembrolizumab developed bilateral ptosis and progressive bulbar weakness 8 days after exposure. During his hospital stay, he received pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, and pyridostigmine. He had progressive respiratory failure requiring intubation. TTE showed preserved LVEF, with septal motion abnormalities attributed to conduction disturbance. His cardiac presentation included a new-onset right bundle branch block and prolonged corrected QT interval. MG evaluation revealed positive striated muscle antibodies. He received PLEX but ultimately required tracheostomy and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy placement. He was ultimately discharged to a long-term acute care facility. Over the following 7 months, he completed intensive physical therapy and achieved near-baseline functional recovery without assistive devices. During that interval, his tracheostomy was decannulated as his respiratory status improved substantially. At 1 year after presentation, he remained on pyridostigmine and low-dose prednisone.

Patient 7

A 79-year-old man with prostate cancer receiving pembrolizumab developed gait instability, ptosis, diplopia, and limb weakness 24 days after treatment initiation. CMR was incomplete and did not allow definitive myocarditis assessment, although both CMR and TTE showed preserved left ventricular systolic function. No major electrical abnormality was documented. He was seronegative for MG antibodies and treated with pyridostigmine and IVIG. He improved clinically and was discharged with a 12-month corticosteroid taper. At 1-year follow-up, he had returned to baseline and continued pyridostigmine. He has remained off oncologic therapy, with surveillance imaging demonstrating stable disease throughout the past year.

Patient 8

A 57-year-old man with metastatic nonsmall cell lung cancer receiving ipilimumab plus nivolumab and lorlatinib developed syncope and severe dyspnea 31 days after treatment initiation. He presented with complete heart block with escape rhythm and cardiogenic shock requiring vasopressors. TTE demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function with septal flattening consistent with right ventricular volume overload. He was seronegative for MG antibodies and was treated with pyridostigmine, pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone, and PLEX. His course was complicated by temporary pacing on hospital day 5 and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation on hospital day 6. Rituximab was initiated with subsequent hemodynamic improvement, and dual-chamber permanent pacemaker implantation was ultimately required. He improved sufficiently for discharge. Lorlatinib was rechallenged 1 month later, with subsequent imaging showing an excellent response and stable disease thereafter.

Discussion

This case series highlights 3 recurring features of TMOS. First, severe cardiac electrical instability was common, especially high-grade atrioventricular block requiring pacing support. Second, echocardiography was frequently nondiagnostic, with preserved LVEF despite marked biomarker elevation and clinically severe myocarditis. Third, corticosteroid monotherapy was insufficient in practice, and all patients required rapid escalation to adjunctive immunomodulatory therapy.

A major diagnostic pitfall was the mismatch between electrical and biomarker severity on one hand and relatively mild imaging findings on the other.5 In our cohort, most patients had preserved LVEF on initial echocardiography despite substantial biomarker elevation and major electrical complications. Only 1 patient underwent CMR with systolic assessment, which showed mildly reduced LVEF (∼50%). Diastolic findings were likewise subtle, with only 1 case of grade I dysfunction and the remainder reported as normal or indeterminate. These relatively mild imaging findings may have contributed to underrecognition of myocarditis severity early in the course. In TMOS, preserved LVEF may be misleading when marked troponin elevation, conduction disease, and bulbar/respiratory symptoms are present.

High-dose corticosteroids are first-line for ICI-associated myocarditis, yet corticosteroids can transiently worsen MG symptoms.9 This creates a management dilemma: delaying steroids risks myocarditis progression, while steroids may aggravate bulbar weakness and precipitate respiratory failure in MG-dominant overlap. In prior literature, 57.4% of patients with TMOS experienced worsening of myasthenic symptoms after steroid treatment, with 66.6% of those requiring ventilatory support.6 In practice, this tension favors: 1) early intensive care unit–level monitoring for airway/ventilatory decline; 2) low threshold for nonsteroidal adjuncts that treat MG physiology concurrently rather than sequentially; and 3) tight, phenotype-driven reassessment rather than stepwise delays.

In our cohort, corticosteroid monotherapy was not sufficient, and all patients required adjunctive immunomodulatory therapy. Escalation beyond corticosteroids was heterogeneous in both timing and regimen. IVIG was generally introduced early, whereas PLEX and steroid-sparing agents were deployed later and according to dominant phenotype and severity. Second-line agents in our cohort included mycophenolate mofetil, abatacept, rituximab, and ruxolitinib. Despite aggressive treatment, outcomes remained poor, with 3 of 8 patients dying and survivors often experiencing substantial residual morbidity, including tracheostomy dependence, permanent pacing, and discharge to long-term acute care.

Although no comparative studies have established that Triple-M overlap is intrinsically more steroid-refractory than isolated ICI-myocarditis, published overlap case series and reviews report high morbidity and mortality with frequent need for early adjunctive immunomodulation beyond corticosteroids.5,9 Prospective multicenter data are needed to establish standardized diagnostic criteria, identify predictors of steroid nonresponse, and evaluate the comparative effectiveness of upfront combination strategies vs stepwise escalation. Until such evidence is available, TMOS should be approached as a cardio-oncology emergency requiring rapid multidisciplinary coordination and escalation guided by clinical phenotype and electrical risk rather than imaging alone.
