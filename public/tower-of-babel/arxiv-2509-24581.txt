Spontaneous π \pi flux trapping in granular rings of unconventional superconductors
Junyi Zhang, Yi Li

Abstract
We study Josephson couplings in unconventional superconductors from a symmetry-based perspective, extending the Sigrist–Rice formula to broader classes of pairing symmetries and in the presence of junction interface disorder effects. Applying this formalism to granular superconducting rings, we show that single-band chiral superconductors cannot spontaneously trap magnetic flux, which rules out chiral p p -wave pairing as a candidate for β \beta - Bi 2 ​ Pd {}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd} superconductor, given the observation of half quantum flux in recent Little-Parks experiments. Taking into account the full crystalline symmetry and time-reversal symmetry of the superconducting state of β \beta - Bi 2 ​ Pd {}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd} , we propose that the effective pairing is a triplet helical equal-spin state, enforced by spin-orbit couplings arising from local inversion symmetry breaking. We further demonstrate that granular rings of such helical superconductors can support spontaneous flux trapping, even in the presence of strong junction interface orientation disorder. Our proposal reconciles apparent inconsistencies among different experimental observations and highlights the crucial role of local inversion symmetry breaking in understanding the pairing symmetry in β \beta - Bi 2 ​ Pd {}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd} .



Josephson junctions are powerful phase-sensitive probes for investigating the superconducting pairing order.
For example, in the superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID),
supercurrent flows in
a ring across two Josephson junctions and exhibits
magnetic-field-dependent superconducting phase
interference, analogous to optical double-slit interference
Wollman et al. 1993
;
Brawner and Ott 1994
.
Furthermore, superconducting rings composed of Josephson junctions can spontaneously trap a magnetic flux
when the Josephson couplings are frustrated.
Frustration can arise, for instance, when one of the Josephson couplings changes sign, thereby favoring a
\pi
phase difference between adjacent superconducting grains.
As a result, even in the absence of an applied magnetic field, a half superconducting flux quantum,
\phi_{0}/2
with
\phi_{0}=h/(2e)
,
can be spontaneously trapped in the ring.
In Josephson junctions made of conventional superconductors,

\pi
phase difference can arise when the junction barrier contains magnetic impurities
Shiba and Soda 1969
;
Bulaevskii et al. 1977
,
or non-magnetic impurities in the presence of strong electron-electron correlations
Spivak and Kivelson 1991
.
In unconventional superconductors,
where the pairing order transforms nontrivially under the crystal symmetry group,
the Josephson coupling becomes sensitive to the orientation of supercurrent relative to crystallographic axes.
As a result, intrinsically frustrated Josephson rings can be engineered
by carefully controlling the crystallographic alignment of the constituent superconducting grains
and tuning the orientations of the junction interfaces.

The use of Josephson junctions to probe superconducting pairing symmetry was first proposed theoretically by Geshkenbein, Larkin, and Barone
Geshkenbein and Larkin 1986
;
Geshkenbein et al. 1987

and later extended to cuprates, by Sigrist and Rice

Each grain supports a uniform pairing order of the form
\hat{\Delta}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{n}}

where
\hat{\Delta}_{n}
is defined relative to the crystallographic orientation grain,

U(1)

When the two superconducting grains are weakly coupled, a Josephson supercurrent can tunnel through the interface at zero voltage bias.
The first-order Josephson coupling is described by a free energy of the form

\begin{split}F_{J}=\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{L},\hat{\Delta}_{R}]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\phi_{L}-\phi_{R})}+\text{c.c.},\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the Josephson coupling amplitude,
which acts as a sesquilinear functional on pairing functions,
i.e., linear in
\hat{\Delta}_{L}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{L}}

and antilinear in
\hat{\Delta}_{R}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{R}}
.
For a given microscopic model, the Josephson coupling can be derived using linear response theory,
generalizing the approach of Ambegaokar and Baratoff
Ambegaokar and Baratoff 1963a
;
Ambegaokar and Baratoff 1963b

to multiband unconventional superconductors,
as detailed in Supplementary Material (S.M.)
Sec.
S1

In Ginzburg-Landau theory,

1

Importantly, the Josephson free energy
F_{J}
depends only on the gauge-invariant phase difference
(\phi_{L}-\phi_{R})
,
and a single Josephson junction can always adjust the relative
U(1)
phases to minimize the free energy.

The symmetries of the system further constrain the Josephson coupling amplitude functional
\mathcal{I}
.
To analyze these constraints, we first expand the pairing functions
\hat{\Delta}_{n}

in a suitable basis

\mathcal{B}_{n}=\{\hat{\Delta}^{n}_{b}\}

that spans the allowed pairing channels for each grain,

\hat{\Delta}_{n}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{n}}d^{n}_{b}\hat{\Delta}^{n}_{b},

where the coefficients
d^{n}_{b}
are complex amplitudes in the corresponding basis channels.
By the sesquilinearity of
\mathcal{I}
, the Josephson coupling amplitude takes the form

\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{L},\hat{\Delta}_{R}]=\sum_{b_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{L},b_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}}d^{L}_{b_{1}}(d_{b_{2}}^{R})^{*}I_{b_{1},b_{2}},

where
I_{b_{1},b_{2}}=\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{L}_{b_{1}},\hat{\Delta}^{R}_{b_{2}}]

are the matrix elements of
\mathcal{I}
in the chosen basis.
It is often more convenient to impose symmetry constraints directly on the matrix elements
I_{b_{1},b_{2}}
.
Consider, for example, a lateral Josephson junction with the
x
-axis normal to the interface, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a).
The system exhibits a macroscopic mirror reflection symmetry

\mathcal{M}_{y}:(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)
,
under which the basis functions can be classified according to their mirror parity.
Due to this symmetry, couplings between channels of opposite mirror parity are forbidden,
as
\mathcal{I}[\mathcal{M}_{y}\hat{\Delta}^{L}_{\pm},\mathcal{M}_{y}\hat{\Delta}^{R}_{\mp}]=-\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{L}_{\pm},\hat{\Delta}^{R}_{\mp}]
.
The dependence of the Josephson coupling amplitude on the relative crystallographic orientations of the grains
is determined by the rotational symmetry representations of the pairing functions.
Under a rotation
R(\alpha)
, each grain rotates relative to its bulk frame, and the pairing function transforms as

\hat{\Delta}_{n}^{\alpha}=\sum_{b^{\prime},b\in\mathcal{B}_{n}}d^{n}_{b^{\prime}}R^{n}_{b^{\prime},b}({\alpha})\hat{\Delta}_{b}^{n},

where
R^{n}_{b^{\prime},b}(\alpha)
is the matrix representation of the rotation in the basis
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
Using the sesquilinearity of
\mathcal{I}

the Josephson coupling amplitude between two rotated grains takes the form

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{L}^{\alpha_{L}},\hat{\Delta}_{R}^{\alpha_{R}}]=&\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1},b_{1},b^{\prime}_{2},b_{2}}d_{b^{\prime}_{1}}^{L}R^{L}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b_{1}}({\alpha_{L}})\\
&\times(d_{b^{\prime}_{2}}^{R})^{*}[R^{R}_{b^{\prime}_{2},b_{2}}({\alpha_{R}})]^{*}I_{b_{1},b_{2}}.\end{split}

(2)

Next, we discuss spontaneous flux trapping in granular superconducting rings.
Consider a ring of
N_{g}
grains,
as shown in Fig.
1
(c),
where neighboring grains form Josephson junctions at grain interfaces,
and a magnetic flux
\Phi_{B}
threads through the central hole.
The total free energy consists of three contributions

F=F_{S}+F_{J}+F_{L}
,
where

F_{S}=\sum_{n}F_{S}^{(n)}
is the sum of bulk free energies of individual grains,

F_{J}=\sum_{n}F_{J}^{(n,n+1)}
accounts for the Josephson coupling energies at grain interfaces,
and
F_{L}
is the self-inductance energy due to circulating supercurrent.
To simplify the analysis while preserving the essential physics, we make two assumptions.
First, for the mesoscopic devices studied in Refs.
22
and
23
,
we assume the self-inductance is negligible, allowing us to omit
F_{L}
.
Second, we consider grain sizes larger than the superconducting coherence length,
so that the bulk condensation energy dominates.
This enables independent minimization of each
F_{S}^{(n)}
fixing the pairing function within each grain
and leaving only the
U(1)
phase degrees of freedom to adjust via the Josephson coupling
F_{J}
.

In the presence of a magnetic flux,
the
U(1)
phases in Eq. (
1
)
are modified as

\phi_{n}=\tilde{\phi}_{n}+\Phi_{n}
,
where
\tilde{\phi}_{n}
is the uniform phase within grain

and

\Phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})=(e^{*}/\hbar)\int^{\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

is the accumulated phase from the vector potential.
The Josephson coupling free energy then reads

\begin{split}F_{J}=&\sum_{n=1}^{N_{g}}-I_{n,n+1}\cos\Big[(\tilde{\phi}_{n}-\tilde{\phi}_{n+1})\\
&+\delta\varphi_{n,n+1}+(\Phi_{n,+}-\Phi_{n+1,-})\Big],\end{split}

(3)

where
the intrinsic phase shift
\delta\varphi_{n,n+1}

and the coupling amplitude
-I_{n,n+1}
are determined according to Eq. (
2
),
and

denotes the magnetic phase shift due to vector potential

Without loss of generality,

If

\begin{split}(\tilde{\phi}_{n}-\tilde{\phi}_{n+1})+\delta\varphi_{n,n+1}+(\Phi_{n,+}-\Phi_{n+1,-})=2\pi k_{n},\end{split}

The total magnetic phase shift is related to the magnetic flux as

Summing over all

We first demonstrate this for chiral

-wave superconductors with pairing function

\hat{\Delta}_{d^{m}}(\mathbf{k})=\Delta_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}),

is the azimuthal angle of momentum

and

\begin{split}F_{J}=&\sum_{n}-I_{n,n+1}\cos\Big[(\tilde{\phi}_{n}-\tilde{\phi}_{n+1})\\

This proportionality

\hat{\Delta}_{p^{\pm}}(\mathbf{k})

The resulting intrinsic phase shift is

\sum_{n}\delta\varphi_{n,n+1}=0

Therefore, we conclude that granular rings of single-band chiral superconductors with rotational symmetry cannot support spontaneous flux trapping,
regardless of the grain orientations.

layers.
The band Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{b}=&\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big\{\sum_{l,s}[\epsilon_{0}(\mathbf{k})-\mu]c_{\mathbf{k},l,s}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l,s}\\
&+\sum_{l}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}_{l}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{s,s^{\prime}}c_{\mathbf{k},l,s}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l,s^{\prime}}\\
&+\sum_{s}(t_{\perp})c_{\mathbf{k},l=1,s}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l=2,s}+\text{h.c.}\Big\},\end{split}

(7)

where
l=\pm
is the layer index and
s,s^{\prime}
are spin indices.
The first and last terms describe in-plane dispersion and interlayer hopping, respectively,
while the second term encodes layer-dependent SOC arising from local inversion symmetry breaking in the
{}\mathrm{Bi}
layers.
Moreover,
\mathbf{g}_{+}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{g}_{-}(-\mathbf{k})
ensures global inversion symmetry.
The detailed forms of
\mathbf{g}_{\pm}(\mathbf{k})
depend on the crystal structure (see Supplemental Material Sec.
S6
).
A schematic of the
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
structure highlighting the local inversion symmetry breaking is shown in the Supplemental Material Fig.
S3
, illustrating the origin of this layer-dependent SOC in the model.

In
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
, as in other centrosymmetric materials with local inversion-symmetry breaking,
the Fermi surfaces remain doubly degenerate due to global inversion and time-reversal symmetries,
while SOC associated with the local inversion asymmetry induces a “hidden spin polarization” on the Fermi surfaces
Fischer et al. 2023b
.
Assuming a short-range phonon-mediated attraction
Zheng and Margine 2017
;
Saib et al. 2017
,
we find that the effective pairing on the Fermi surfaces,
expressed in the polarized pseudospin basis,
reduces to a helical equal-spin pairing (HESP) state (see Supplemental Material Sec.
S6
),

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}_{ES}(\mathbf{k})=\Delta_{0}\begin{pmatrix}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}&0\\
0&-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(8)

This structure reflects the spin-momentum locking of the Fermi surfaces
and realizes a time-reversal invariant equal-spin triplet pairing.

The Josephson free energy receives contributions from both spin components, each forming a chiral
p
-wave pairing as in Eq. (
6
). Time-reversal symmetry enforces
I_{n,n+1}^{\uparrow}=I_{n,n+1}^{\downarrow}=I_{n,n+1}
, leading to a total free energy

\begin{split}F_{J}=&\sum_{n=1}^{N_{g}}-2I_{n,n+1}\cos(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{n+1})\\
&\times\cos\Big[(\tilde{\phi}_{n}-\tilde{\phi}_{n+1})+(\Phi_{n,+}-\Phi_{n+1,-})\Big].\end{split}

(9)

Unlike Eq. (
6
), here the angular dependence appears as a separate multiplicative factor, resembling single-band
d
-wave superconductors in the dirty junction limit (See details in
S. M. Sec.
S2.1
).
Therefore, granular rings of such superconductors can spontaneously trap a
\pi
flux,
a feature that remains robust even with interface orientation disorder driving the Josephson couplings into the dirty limit.

To conclude, we have developed a general symmetry-based framework to determine Josephson couplings in both clean and dirty junctions.
Benchmarking this method against
d
-wave cuprates, we recover the generalized Sigrist–Rice formula
and provide a clearer physical understanding of how interface disorder affects Josephson couplings.
Our analysis sheds new light on flux trapping in granular rings of unconventional superconductors.
In particular,
we show that granular rings of a single-band chiral superconductor do not intrinsically support spontaneous flux trapping,
which rules out chiral
p
-wave pairing as a candidate for unconventional superconductivity in
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
,
given the observed half-quantum flux trapping in Little-Parks oscillations of its granular rings.
Moreover, we propose a helical equal-spin triplet pairing,
enforced by spin-orbit coupling
arising from local inversion symmetry breaking.
Although this is illustrated using a minimal two-band model,
capturing the full complexity of
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
will require first-principles calculations,
which remains for future research.
We also suggest that experiments on superconducting rings with controlled grain orientations, akin to those in cuprates
Tsuei et al. 1994
;
Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
, would provide a direct test of our proposal.

S1
Microscopic Derivation of the Josephson Coupling Free Energy

We derive the first-order Josephson coupling using a microscopic model,
extending the approach of Ambegaokar and Baratoff
Ambegaokar and Baratoff 1963a
;
Ambegaokar and Baratoff 1963b
to multiband superconductors with more complex pairing functions.
This generalization demonstrates that,
for weakly coupled superconductors,
the Josephson free energy takes the form of a sesquilinear functional of the pairing functions, as expressed in Eq. (
1
) of the main text.

Consider a Josephson junction formed by two superconductors in contact, allowing electrons to tunnel between them.
The system is described by the Hamiltonian

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}=&\mathcal{H}_{\text{SC}1}+\mathcal{H}_{\text{SC}2}+\mathcal{H}_{T},\\
\mathcal{H}_{\text{SC}n}=&\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\left[c_{n,\mathbf{k},\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{H}^{a}_{\mu,\nu}(\mathbf{k})c_{n,\mathbf{k},\nu}+\left(c_{n,\mathbf{k},\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{\Delta}^{n}_{\mu,\nu}(\mathbf{k})c_{n,-\mathbf{k},\nu}^{\dagger}+\text{h.c.}\right)\right],\\
\mathcal{H}_{T}=&\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\sum_{m,n}c_{2,\mathbf{k}_{2},m}^{\dagger}\hat{T}_{m,n}(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{1})c_{1,\mathbf{k}_{1},n}+\text{h.c.},\end{split}

(S1)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\text{SC}n}
(
n=1,2
) describes the two superconductors on either side of the junction
[see Fig.
1
(a) of the main text],
and
\mathcal{H}_{T}
encodes the tunneling of electrons across the interface.
The indices
\mu
and
\nu
label internal degrees of freedom,
including spin, orbital, and sublattice components, extending beyond the spin only case considered in Refs.
39
;
40
.
For convenience, we refer to them as “band indices”,
although the single-particle Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{a}{\mu,\nu}
need not be diagonal in this basis.

Since the two superconductors are weakly coupled, we treat the tunneling Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{T}
as a perturbation.
Using linear response theory, the expectation value of the tunneling current to first order in
\mathcal{H}_{T}
is given by

\begin{split}I^{(1)}(t)=&-\mathrm{i}\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[I_{T}(t),\mathcal{H}_{T}(t^{\prime})]\rangle,\end{split}

(S2)

where
I_{T}=-\mathrm{i}e(\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{T}^{\dagger})
is the tunneling current operator,
and
\theta(t-t^{\prime})
is the Heaviside function ensuring causality.
Substituting
\mathcal{H}_{T}=\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
into Eq. (
S2
), the commutator expands into contributions from different tunneling processes

\begin{split}&I^{(1)}(t)=I_{N}+I_{J},\\
&I_{N}=-e\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\Big\{\langle[\mathcal{T}(t),\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})]\rangle-\langle[\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(t),\mathcal{T}(t^{\prime})]\rangle\Big\}\\
&I_{J}=-e\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\Big\{\langle[\mathcal{T}(t),\mathcal{T}(t^{\prime})]\rangle-\langle[\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(t),\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})]\rangle\Big\},\end{split}

(S3)

where
I_{N}
corresponds to normal tunneling,
while
I_{J}
describes anomalous pair tunneling and gives rise to the Josephson current.
Defining the retarded anomalous response function

\begin{split}X^{R}(t)=-\mathrm{i}e\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[\mathcal{T}(t),\mathcal{T}(t^{\prime})]\rangle,\end{split}

(S4)

we can calculate the Josephson current as

I_{J}=2\text{Im}X^{R}
.

It is more convenient to evaluate the associated Matsubara Green’s function
\mathcal{X}(\tau)

using Wick’s theorem
in terms of the fermionic Green’s functions
as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{X}(\tau)=-e\int\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\langle\mathscr{T}_{\tau}\{\mathcal{T}(\tau)\mathcal{T}(\tau^{\prime})\}\rangle\\
=&-e\int\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}\\
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu,\nu\\
\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\langle\mathscr{T}_{\tau}\{c_{2,\mathbf{k}_{2},\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{T}_{\mu,\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{1})c_{1,\mathbf{k}_{1},\nu}(\tau)c_{2,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{2},\mu^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{T}_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{2},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{1})c_{1,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{1},\nu^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime})\}\rangle\\
=&-e\int\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu,\nu\\
\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left[\hat{T}_{\mu,\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{1})\mathcal{F}_{1,\mathbf{k}_{1};\nu^{\prime},\nu}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\hat{T}_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k}_{2},-\mathbf{k}_{1})\mathcal{F}_{2,\mathbf{k}_{2};\mu^{\prime},\mu}^{*}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\right],\end{split}

(S5)

where
\mathcal{F}_{n,\mathbf{k};\mu,\nu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\langle\mathscr{T}_{\tau}\{c_{n,-\mathbf{k},\mu}(\tau)c_{n,\mathbf{k},\nu}(\tau^{\prime})\}\rangle
,
n=1,2

are Gor’kov’s anomalous Green’s functions.
For the first-order Josephson coupling,
we expand the anomalous Green’s function

\mathcal{F}_{n}

to leading order in the pairing function
as

\mathcal{F}_{n}\approx g_{n}^{p}\hat{\Delta}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{n}}g_{n}^{h}
,
where
g_{n}^{p,h}

are the normal-state propagators for the particle and hole sectors of the Bogoliubov–de Gennes Hamiltonian.
It follows directly that the anomalous response function

\mathcal{X}
is
linear in
\hat{\Delta}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}}

and antilinear in
\hat{\Delta}_{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{2}}
,
making it a sesquilinear functional of the pairing functions.
Factorizing out the overall
U(1)
phase dependence,
we express the response function and the resulting Josephson current as

\begin{split}X^{R}=&-2e\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathbf{k}),\hat{\Delta}_{2}(\mathbf{k})]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})},\\
I_{J}=&2\mathrm{i}e\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathbf{k}),\hat{\Delta}_{2}(\mathbf{k})]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})}+\text{h.c.},\end{split}

(S6)

where the prefactor of
2
is introduced for normalization convenience.
This expression clearly shows that the Josephson current depends on the
gauge invariant phase difference

\Delta\phi=(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})
across the junction.

In Ginzburg–Landau theory, the free energy associated with the first-order Josephson coupling takes the form

\begin{split}F_{J}(\Delta\phi)=&\mathcal{J}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Delta\phi}+\text{h.c.}\end{split}

(S7)

The corresponding Josephson current follows from the derivative of the free energy

\begin{split}I_{J}=&2e\frac{\partial F_{J}}{\partial\Delta\phi},=2\mathrm{i}e\mathcal{J}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Delta\phi}+\text{h.c.}.\end{split}

(S8)

Comparing Eqs.(
S6
)
and (
S8
),
we identify the Josephson coupling coefficient as
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathbf{k}),\hat{\Delta}_{2}(\mathbf{k})]
.
This provides a microscopic foundation for the Ginzburg–Landau form of the Josephson free energy given in Eq. (
1
) of the main text.

S2
Angular Dependence of Josephson Couplings for
d
- and
p
-Wave Superconductors

The angular dependence of the Josephson coupling,
dictated by Eq. (
2
) of the main text,
follows from symmetry considerations.
In this section, we derive this dependence explicitly for
d
- and
p
-wave superconductors.
In particular,
when applied to
d
-wave superconductors such as the cuprates,
it reproduces the generalized Sigrist–Rice formula,
along with a symmetry-allowed correction term to the original Sigrist–Rice formula
Sigrist and M. Rice 1992
that has been previously identified by Walker and Luettmer-Strathmann using a series expansion method
Walker and Luettmer-Strathmann 1996
.

S2.1
Single-Band
d
-Wave Superconductors

For
d
-wave superconductors with in-plane rotational symmetries, such as the cuprates, it is convenient to choose a basis
\mathcal{B}_{d}=\{\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}\}
that spans the space of a two-dimensional representation.
Notably, these two basis functions have opposite mirror parities under the mirror reflection
\mathcal{M}_{y}
,
i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{y}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}=+\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}

and
\mathcal{M}_{y}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}=-\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}
.
Using the sesquilinearity of the functional

\mathcal{I}
we find

\begin{split}&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]=\mathcal{I}[\mathcal{M}_{y}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\mathcal{M}_{y}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]=-\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}].\end{split}

(S9)

As a result, the inter-channel Josephson coupling

\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]=0
.
By the same argument,

\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]

also vanishes.
In contrast, the intra-channel couplings are allowed.
Therefore,
we summarize the values of
\mathcal{I}

evaluated on the basis
\mathcal{B}_{d}
as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]=-I_{v},\\
&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]=0,\\
&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]=0,\\
&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]=-I_{d},\end{split}

(S10)

where
I_{v}
and
I_{d}
are positive constants
The minus signs are fixed such that the free energy is minimized
when the two grains are aligned and the relative phase difference vanishes.

We now derive the angular dependence of the Josephson coupling by examining
how the pairing functions transform under in-plane rotations.
Consider a rotation by an angle
\alpha
,
denoted
R_{\alpha}
.
Under this transformation,
the pairing functions rotate within the basis
\mathcal{B}_{d}
as

\begin{split}&\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}=\cos(2\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}+\sin(2\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\\
&\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{d_{xy}}=-\sin(2\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}+\cos(2\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}.\end{split}

(S11)

Substituting these rotated pairing functions into the sesquilinear functional
\mathcal{I}
,
we obtain the angular dependence of the Josephson coupling amplitudes between two
d
-wave grains with orientations
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]=&\cos(2\alpha_{1})\cos(2\alpha_{2})\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]\\
&+\cos(2\alpha_{1})\sin(2\alpha_{2})\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]\\
&+\sin(2\alpha_{1})\cos(2\alpha_{2})\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]\\
&+\sin(2\alpha_{1})\sin(2\alpha_{2})\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}}]\\
=&-\cos(2\alpha_{1})\cos(2\alpha_{2})I_{v}-\sin(2\alpha_{1})\sin(2\alpha_{2})I_{d},\\
\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{d_{xy}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{d_{xy}}]=&-\sin(2\alpha_{1})\sin(2\alpha_{2})I_{v}-\cos(2\alpha_{1})\cos(2\alpha_{2})I_{d}.\end{split}

(S12)

Therefore, the Josephson coupling free energy between two
d
-wave grains,
such as those found in cuprates,
takes the form

\begin{split}F_{J}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}]=&-2\left[\cos(2\alpha_{1})\cos(2\alpha_{2})I_{v}+\sin(2\alpha_{1})\sin(2\alpha_{2})I_{d}\right]\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}).\end{split}

(S13)

When
I_{d}=0
,
Eq. (
S13
) reduces to

F_{J}=-2\cos(2\alpha_{1})\cos(2\alpha_{2})I_{v}\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})
,
which is also known as the Sigrist–Rice formula
Sigrist and M. Rice 1992
.
This expression has been used in the design of tricrystal experiments for probing unconventional pairing in clean Josephson junctions
Tsuei et al. 1994
;
Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
.
The second term proportional to
I_{d}
is also symmetry-allowed,
but it was not included in the original discussion by Sigrist and Rice.
It was later identified by Walker and Luettmer-Strathmann using a series expansion approach
Walker and Luettmer-Strathmann 1996
.
Our symmetry-based derivation naturally recovers this full expression,
providing a generalized form of the Sigrist–Rice formula given in Eq. (
S13
).

For chiral
d
-wave superconductors,
the pairing functions can be expressed as

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}}=&\hat{\Delta}_{d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{xy}},\end{split}

(S14)

and the corresponding Josephson coupling amplitudes are given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}}]=&-(I_{v}+I_{d}),\\
\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\mp 2}}]=&-(I_{v}-I_{d}),\end{split}

(S15)

where
I_{v}
and
I_{d}
are the same constants introduced earlier.
Under an in-plane rotation
R_{\alpha}
,
the pairing functions transform as

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{d_{\pm 2}}=&\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}\alpha}\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}},\end{split}

(S16)

leading to the following angular dependence of the Josephson couplings

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{d_{\pm 2}}]=&\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}\alpha_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}\alpha_{2}}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}}]=-\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})}(I_{v}+I_{d}),\\
\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{d_{\mp 2}}]=&\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}\alpha_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}\alpha_{2}}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}_{d_{\mp 2}}]=-\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}(I_{v}-I_{d}),\end{split}

(S17)

where we have used the sesquilinearity of the functional
\mathcal{I}
.
The Josephson coupling free energy between two chiral
d
-wave grains of the same chirality then takes the form

\begin{split}F_{J}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{d_{\pm 2}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{d_{\pm 2}}]=&-2(I_{v}+I_{d})\cos\left[\mp 2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})+(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\right].\end{split}

(S18)

S2.2
Single-Band
p
-Wave Superconductors

For
p
-wave superconductors, we analyze the Josephson couplings in parallel with the
d
-wave case.
we analyze the Josephson couplings for
p
-wave superconductors.
For simplicity, we first neglect spin-orbit coupling
and assume that the spin part of the Cooper pairs transforms trivially under in-plane rotational symmetries.
It is then convenient to adopt the mirror eigenbasis

\mathcal{B}_{p}=\{\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}}=p_{x}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y})\sigma_{z},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}}=p_{y}(\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y})\sigma_{z}\}

which diagonalizes the mirror reflection
\mathcal{M}_{y}
.
In this basis, the Josephson coupling functional evaluates to

\begin{split}&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}}]=-I_{x},\\
&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}}]=0,\\
&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}}]=0,\\
&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}}]=-I_{y}.\end{split}

(S19)

The inter-channel Josephson couplings vanish due to opposite mirror parities under
\mathcal{M}_{y}
,
while the intra-channel couplings

-I_{x}
and
-I_{y}

are symmetry allowed.
Under an in-plane rotation
R_{\alpha}
,
the pairing functions transform as

\begin{split}&\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{p_{x}}=\cos(\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}}+\sin(\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}},\\
&\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{p_{y}}=-\sin(\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}}+\cos(\alpha)\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}}.\end{split}

(S20)

Substituting these into the sesquilinear functional
\mathcal{I}
,
we obtain the angular dependence of the Josephson coupling amplitudes

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{x}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{x}}]=&-\cos(\alpha_{1})\cos(\alpha_{2})I_{x}-\sin(\alpha_{1})\sin(\alpha_{2})I_{y},\\
\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{y}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{y}}]=&-\sin(\alpha_{1})\sin(\alpha_{2})I_{x}-\cos(\alpha_{1})\cos(\alpha_{2})I_{y},\end{split}

(S21)

Moreover, the Josephson coupling free energy between two
p
-wave grains with the same orbital channel follows as

\begin{split}F_{J}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{x}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{x}}]=&-2\left[\cos(\alpha_{1})\cos(\alpha_{2})I_{x}+\sin(\alpha_{1})\sin(\alpha_{2})I_{y}\right]\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}),\\
F_{J}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{y}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{y}}]=&-2\left[\sin(\alpha_{1})\sin(\alpha_{2})I_{x}+\cos(\alpha_{1})\cos(\alpha_{2})I_{y}\right]\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}).\end{split}

(S22)

For chiral
p
-wave superconductors,
the pairing functions can be expressed as

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\pm}}=&\hat{\Delta}_{p_{x}}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{\Delta}_{p_{y}}.\end{split}

(S23)

and the corresponding Josephson coupling amplitudes are given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\pm}},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\pm}}]=&-(I_{x}+I_{y}),\\
\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\pm}},\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\mp}}]=&-(I_{x}-I_{y}).\end{split}

(S24)

Under an in-plane rotation
R_{\alpha}
,
the pairing functions transform as

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{p_{\pm}}=&\mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathrm{i}\alpha}\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\pm}},\end{split}

(S25)

leading to the following angular dependence of the Josephson couplings

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{\pm}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{\pm}}]=&-\mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathrm{i}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})}(I_{x}+I_{y}),\\
\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{\pm}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{\mp}}]=&-\mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathrm{i}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}(I_{x}-I_{y}).\end{split}

(S26)

The Josephson coupling free energy between two chiral
p
-wave grains of the same chirality then takes the form

\begin{split}F_{J}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{\pm}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{\pm}}]=&-2(I_{x}+I_{y})\cos\left[\mp(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})+(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\right].\end{split}

(S27)

In the presence of spin-orbit coupling,
it is more natural to work in the chiral basis

\mathcal{B}_{p_{\pm j_{z}}}
,
where the Cooper pairs carry definite total angular momentum due to in-plane rotation symmetries.
Although the explicit form of the chiral pairing functions can be complicated,
it is their transformation under in-plane rotation
R_{\alpha}

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha}_{p_{\pm j_{z}}}=&\mathrm{e}^{\mp j_{z}\mathrm{i}\alpha}\hat{\Delta}_{p_{\pm j_{z}}},\end{split}

(S28)

that governs the angular dependence of the Josephson couplings.
Using this symmetry property,
the Josephson coupling free energy between two chiral
p
-wave grains of the same chirality is given by

\begin{split}F_{J}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{\pm j_{z}}},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{p_{\pm j_{z}}}]=&-2I_{p_{\pm j_{z}}}\cos\left[\mp j_{z}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})+(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\right].\end{split}

(S29)

S3
Effects of Interface Orientation Disorder

Interface disorder is unavoidable in realistic experimental settings
and can substantially alter the angular dependence of Josephson couplings,
as noted by Tsuei and collaborators
Tsuei et al. 1994
.
In the context of
d
-wave superconductors such as the cuprates,
they proposed a modified version of the Sigrist–Rice formula for junctions in the “dirty limit”,
where the disorder effect at the junction interface is strong.
To avoid confusion with the conventional use of “dirty” for bulk disorders,
we adopt the term dirty (Josephson) junction to describe junctions with significant interface disorders,
and dirty junction limit for strongly disordered cases.
Interestingly, the modified Sigrist–Rice formula proposed for the dirty junction limit in cuprates
coincides formally with the clean-junction result derived by Walker and Luettmer-Strathmann
Walker and Luettmer-Strathmann 1996
;
Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
when one sets
I_{v}=-I_{d}
in Eq. (
S12
). However, this agreement appears to be purely coincidental from a mathematical standpoint and lacks a physical justification grounded in symmetry principles.

Here, we examine the effects of interface disorder arising from fluctuations in the interface orientation,
following the physical mechanism identified by Tsuei and collaborators
Tsuei et al. 1994
.
We model the disorder as a random distribution of local interface orientations, enabling a more systematic and quantitative analysis.
For
d
-wave superconductors,
we find that the modification proposed by Tsuei et al.
Tsuei et al. 1994
remains accurate,
while that for
p
-wave superconductors contains correction terms of order
30\%
,
which may qualitatively alter the conclusions.
Interestingly, we also find that Josephson couplings in chiral superconductors are comparatively robust against such interface orientation disorder.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
of the main text,
interface orientation disorder can be modeled as local random deviations
\beta
from the average junction orientation.
The effect of these deviations on the Josephson couplings can be incorporated by modifying the grain orientations as
\alpha_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{\alpha}_{n}=\alpha_{n}-\beta
.
The total Josephson coupling is then obtained by averaging over the disorder variable
\beta
.
Assuming
\beta
follows a probability distribution
P(\beta)
, the disorder-averaged Josephson coupling is given by

\begin{split}\bar{\mathcal{I}}=&\overline{\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\tilde{\alpha}_{1}}_{L},\hat{\Delta}^{\tilde{\alpha}_{2}}_{R}]}_{\beta}=\int\mathrm{d}\beta P(\beta)\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}-\beta}_{L},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}-\beta}_{R}].\end{split}

(S30)

We now show that the disorder-averaged weighting factors are governed by the generating function
\Pi(l)
associated with the probability distribution
P(\beta)
.
For simplicity and without loss of generality, we assume that the orientation fluctuation
\beta
follows a normal distribution,
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\beta}^{2})
.
The corresponding generating function takes the form

\begin{split}\Pi(l)=&\int\mathrm{d}\beta P(\beta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}l\beta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}}\int\mathrm{d}\beta\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}l\beta}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}_{\beta}l^{2}}.\end{split}

(S31)

Applying this framework to
d
-wave superconductors,
whose Josephson coupling is governed by the generalized Sigrist–Rice formula [Eq. (
S13
)],
and using the generating function in Eq. (
S31
),
we find that the angular dependent factors in the square brackets are averaged to

\begin{split}\overline{\cos(2\tilde{\alpha}_{1})\cos(2\tilde{\alpha}_{2})}_{\beta}=&\frac{1}{2}\cos[2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})]w^{d}_{-}+\frac{1}{2}\cos[2(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]w^{d}_{+},\\
\overline{\sin(2\tilde{\alpha}_{1})\sin(2\tilde{\alpha}_{2})}_{\beta}=&\frac{1}{2}\cos[2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})]w^{d}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\cos[2(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]w^{d}_{+},\end{split}

(S32)

where the weighting factors are

w^{d}_{-}=\Pi(0)=1

and
w^{d}_{+}=\Pi(4)=\mathrm{e}^{-8\sigma^{2}_{\beta}}
.
In the dirty junction limit with large
\sigma_{\beta}
,
Tsuei et al. estimated a maximal disorder
\sigma_{\beta}\approx\pi/4
for tetragonal lattices
Tsuei et al. 1994
,
leading to

w_{+}^{d}\approx\exp(-\pi^{2}/2)\approx 7.2\times 10^{-3}
.
which implies that the
\cos[2(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]
term is exponentially suppressed by over two orders of magnitude.
As a result, both disorder-averaged prefactors reduce to a form proportional to
\cos[2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})]
.
Thus, in the dirty junction limit, the Josephson coupling simplifies to

\begin{split}\bar{\mathcal{I}}\approx&-\frac{1}{2}(I_{v}+I_{d})\cos[2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})],\\
\bar{F}_{J}\approx&-(I_{v}+I_{d})\cos[2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})]\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}).\end{split}

(S33)

which coincides with the form proposed by Tsuei and collaborators
Tsuei et al. 1994
;
Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
.

We emphasize, however, the distinction between this result and the clean-junction expression [Eq. (
S13
)].
While the same
\cos[2(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})]
dependence can be recovered in the clean limit by setting
I_{v}=-I_{d}
,
as noted in Refs.
38
;
21
,
such a choice lacks physical justification: no symmetry enforces equal magnitudes and opposite signs for these two couplings.
The agreement is therefore a mathematical coincidence rather than a symmetry-enforced equivalence.
In contrast, Eq. (
S33
) arises robustly from the disorder averaging procedure and remains valid regardless of the specific values of
I_{v}
and
I_{d}
,
as long as
w_{+}^{d}
is sufficiently suppressed,
which provides a more rigorous and physically grounded justification for the angular dependence of Josephson couplings in the presence of interface disorder.

In cuprates with tetragonal lattice symmetry,
the relative magnitudes of
I_{v}
and
I_{d}
can be qualitatively estimated as follows.
When
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
,
the Josephson coupling is entirely governed by
I_{v}
,
corresponding to tunneling along the crystallographic axes.
In the thin-wall limit, electron tunneling occurs predominantly through nearest-neighbor bonds,
and from Eq. (
S5
),
we estimate
I_{v}\sim|t|^{2}
,
where
t
denotes the nearest-neighbor hopping amplitude.
In contrast, when
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\pi/4
,
the coupling is governed solely by
I_{d}
, which arises from tunneling along the diagonal direction.
In this configuration,
the tunneling is mediated by next-nearest-neighbor hopping processes,
yielding
I_{d}\sim|t^{\prime}|^{2}
,
where
t^{\prime}
is the next-nearest-neighbor hopping amplitude.
Since typically
|t^{\prime}/t|\sim 0.1
in cuprates,
we estimate the ratio
I_{d}/I_{v}\approx|t^{\prime}|^{2}/|t|^{2}\sim 10^{-2}
, indicating that
I_{d}\ll I_{v}
.

We now turn to the Josephson couplings in a dirty Josephson junction composed of
p
-wave superconductors. In parallel with the
d
-wave case, the angular prefactors in Eq. (
S22
) are modified by the interface disorder and become

\begin{split}&\overline{\cos(\tilde{\alpha}_{1})\cos(\tilde{\alpha}_{2})}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})w^{p}_{-}+\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})w^{p}_{+},\\
&\overline{\sin(\tilde{\alpha}_{1})\sin(\tilde{\alpha}_{2})}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})w^{p}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})w^{p}_{+},\end{split}

(S34)

where the weight factors are given by
w^{p}_{-}=\Pi(0)=1
and
w^{p}_{+}=\Pi(2)=\exp(-2\sigma_{\beta}^{2})
.
Substituting these into Eq. (
S22
), the disorder-averaged Josephson coupling becomes

\begin{split}&\bar{\mathcal{I}}=-\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})(I_{v}+I_{d})w_{-}^{p}-\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(I_{v}-I_{d})w_{+}^{p},\\
&\bar{F}_{J}=-\left[\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})(I_{v}+I_{d})w_{-}^{p}+\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(I_{v}-I_{d})w_{+}^{p}\right]\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}).\end{split}

(S35)

For a tetragonal lattice in the dirty junction limit,
we estimate
\sigma_{\beta}\approx\pi/4
,
which yields
\exp(-2\sigma_{\beta}^{2})\approx 0.29
.
In contrast to the
d
-wave case,
the
\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})
term remains appreciable in
p
-wave superconductors.
Despite being exponentially suppressed, its weight factor still retains roughly
30\%
of that of the
\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})
term,
highlighting a key distinction between the two cases.
As a result, applying the approximated form proposed by Tsuei and collaborators to junctions composed of
p
-wave superconductors can lead to substantial quantitative deviations and potentially incorrect conclusions.

For chiral superconductors, the effects of interface disorder manifest differently. The angular dependence of the disorder-averaged Josephson couplings for chiral
d
- and
p
-wave superconductors, given in Eqs. (
S17
) and (
S26
), can be evaluated as

\begin{split}\overline{\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\tilde{\alpha}_{1}}_{\pm m},\hat{\Delta}^{\tilde{\alpha}_{2}}_{\pm m}]}_{\beta}=&\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{\pm m},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{\pm m}],\\
\overline{\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\tilde{\alpha}_{1}}_{\pm m},\hat{\Delta}^{\tilde{\alpha}_{2}}_{\mp m}]}_{\beta}=&w^{\prime}_{c}\mathcal{I}[\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{1}}_{\pm m},\hat{\Delta}^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mp m}],\end{split}

(S36)

where
w^{\prime}_{c}=\Pi(2m)=\mathrm{e}^{-2m^{2}\sigma^{2}{\beta}}
.
This result shows that Josephson couplings between chiral superconductors of the same chirality are unaffected by interface orientation disorder,
whereas couplings between opposite chiralities are exponentially suppressed.

S5
\pi
-Ring Probability

Although Tsuei and collaborators
Tsuei et al. 1994
demonstrated
\pi
-flux trapping in a tricrystal experiment using YBCO,
fabricating granular superconducting rings with precise control over both grain and interface orientations remains experimentally challenging.
In practice, it is more feasible to pattern polycrystalline thin films into ring geometries, where the number of grains
N_{g}
can be much larger than three.
As
N_{g}
increases, the grain configurations that support
\pi
-ring formation form a complex subset within a high-dimensional parameter space.
Given the lack of precise control over these configurations,
it is more heuristic to examine statistically the probability of obtaining a
\pi
-ring in systems with
N_{g}>3
.

The general criterion for realizing a
\pi
-ring with arbitrary grain number
N_{g}
is given in Eq. (
5
) of the main text.
In this section, we first compute the probability of obtaining a
\pi
-ring in three- and four-grain rings composed of helical equal-spin pairing superconductors.
We then use Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the probability of
\pi
-ring formation in rings with
N_{g}\geq 3
for both helical equal-spin pairing and
d
-wave superconductors, in both clean and dirty junction limits.
Our results show that as
N_{g}
increases, the probability
P
of realizing a
\pi
-ring grows and asymptotically approaches
P=1/2
in the large-
N_{g}
limit.
This suggests that fabricating granular superconducting rings by patterning polycrystalline thin films can be a viable approach, provided the underlying unconventional superconductor supports such states.

The free energy of Josephson coupling for an
N_{g}
-grain ring composed of helical equal spin pairing superconductor is given by Eq. (
9
) of the main text
that we quote here again as

\begin{split}F_{J}=&\sum_{n=1}^{N_{g}}-2I_{n,n+1}\cos(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{n+1})\cos\Big[(\tilde{\phi}_{n}-\tilde{\phi}_{n+1})+(\Phi_{n,+}-\Phi_{n+1,-})\Big].\end{split}

(S42)

Similar to the case of three-grain

-wave superconducting ring as discussed in Sec.

we observe that

&-I^{31}\cos(\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1})\cos(\tilde{\phi}_{3}-\tilde{\phi}_{1}+\Phi).\end{split}

(S43)

\begin{split}\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}=&\frac{\pi}{2}+k_{1,2}\pi,\\

(S45)

When

\begin{split}F_{J}=&-I^{12}\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})\cos(\tilde{\phi}_{1}-\tilde{\phi}_{2})\\

\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{4}=&\frac{\pi}{2}+k_{3,4}\pi,\\

(S48)

(S49)

\beta

\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
crystallizes in the tetragonal space group
I4/mmm
.
A conventional unit cell is shown in Fig.
S3
.
The
{}\mathrm{Bi}
atoms (red) occupy the
4e
Wyckoff positions,
and the
{}\mathrm{Pd}
atoms (blue) reside at the
2a
Wyckoff position that coincides with an inversion center of the lattice.
The
{}\mathrm{Pd}
and
{}\mathrm{Bi}
atoms form
{}\mathrm{Bi}
–
{}\mathrm{Pd}

{}\mathrm{Bi}

Despite its structural simplicity, the material exhibits rather intricate Fermi surfaces as a result of multiple bands crossing the Fermi level.
The layered structure leads to predominantly quasi-two-dimensional (2D) Fermi surface geometry.
These bands originate mainly from
{}\mathrm{Bi}

p
-orbitals,
while the
{}\mathrm{Pd}

d
-orbitals are fully occupied.
As a result,
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}

In the presence of both time-reversal and global inversion symmetries,
each Fermi surface in
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
remains doubly degenerate.
However, despite this degeneracy,
the Fermi surfaces exhibit hidden spin polarization with nontrivial spin textures,
as a consequence of spin-orbit coupling (SOC) arising from
local

Specifically, although the crystal as a whole possesses inversion centers at the
{}\mathrm{Pd}

individual
{}\mathrm{Bi}

as indicated by the symmetry-allowed polar vectors (blue arrows) in Fig.
S3

Under inversion, a given
{}\mathrm{Bi}
layer is mapped to its counterpart on the opposite side of the
{}\mathrm{Pd}
layer, rather than onto itself, and the associated polar vectors reverse their orientation.
As a result, electrons moving within the
{}\mathrm{Bi}
planes experience a locally noncentrosymmetric environment,
giving rise to a non-vanishing local SOC field.

To model the effects of SOC and local inversion symmetry breaking on superconducting pairing,
we consider a minimal two-band model that incorporates all the essential ingredients.
Specifically, we consider two square-lattice layers arranged in an
AA
stacking configuration.
For simplicity, we assume that each site hosts a single orbital that transforms as a
{}\mathrm{Bi}

p_{z}
-orbital under the space group symmetries.
The band Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{b}=&\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big\{\sum_{l,s}[\epsilon_{0}(\mathbf{k})-\mu]c_{\mathbf{k},l,s}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l,s}+\sum_{s}[(t_{\perp})c_{\mathbf{k},l=1,s}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l=2,s}+\text{h.c.}]\\
&+\sum_{l}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}_{l}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{s,s^{\prime}}c_{\mathbf{k},l,s}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l,s^{\prime}}\Big\},\end{split}

(S50)

where
\epsilon_{0}(\mathbf{k})
describes the in-plane dispersion,

t_{\perp}
denotes the interlayer hopping amplitude,
and
\mathbf{g}_{l}(\mathbf{k})
encodes the SOC arising from local inversion asymmetry within each layer.
Physically, the absence of mirror symmetry
\mathcal{M}_{z}
in each
{}\mathrm{Bi}
layer
(red planes in Fig.
S3
)
allows a local polar field perpendicular to the plane,
indicated by the blue arrows along
c
-axis in Fig.
S3
.
Therefore, each
{}\mathrm{Bi}
layer supports a Rashba-type SOC with
\mathbf{g}_{l}(\mathbf{k})\propto\mathbf{e}_{z}\times\mathbf{k}
.
Nevertheless,
since the polar vectors reverse their orientation for adjacent layers connected by the global inversion symmetry of the crystal,
the assign SOC amplitudes
should also change the sign as

\alpha_{l}=(-)^{l}\alpha_{R}
,
leading to the explicit form

\mathbf{g}_{l}(\mathbf{k})=(-1)^{l}\alpha_{R}\mathbf{e}_{z}\times\mathbf{k}
,
where
\alpha_{R}
is the strength of the SOC.

The band eigenstates are obtained by diagonalizing the band Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{b}
.
The resulting energy dispersions are given by

\epsilon_{\pm}(\mathbf{k})=\epsilon_{0}(\mathbf{k})-\mu\pm\sqrt{\alpha_{R}^{2}k^{2}+(t_{\perp})^{2}}
,
where
k=|\mathbf{k}|
,
The corresponding eigenstates can be represented in terms of tensor products of spin- and layer-spinors as

\begin{split}\epsilon_{+}(\mathbf{k}):\chi_{1}=\eta^{l}_{+,+}\otimes\xi^{s}_{+},\\
\epsilon_{-}(\mathbf{k}):\chi_{2}=\eta^{l}_{-,+}\otimes\xi^{s}_{+},\\
\epsilon_{-}(\mathbf{k}):\chi_{3}=\eta^{l}_{-,-}\otimes\xi^{s}_{-},\\
\epsilon_{+}(\mathbf{k}):\chi_{4}=\eta^{l}_{+,-}\otimes\xi^{s}_{-},\end{split}

(S51)

where the spin- and layer-spinors are explicitly defined as follows

\begin{split}&\xi^{s}_{+}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\\
\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix},\xi^{s}_{-}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\end{pmatrix},\\
&\eta^{l}_{+,+}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}+\cos(\gamma/2)\\
+\sin(\gamma/2)\end{pmatrix},\eta^{l}_{-,+}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin(\gamma/2)\\
+\cos(\gamma/2)\end{pmatrix},\\
&\eta^{l}_{+,-}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}+\sin(\gamma/2)\\
+\cos(\gamma/2)\end{pmatrix},\eta^{l}_{-,-}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}-\cos(\gamma/2)\\
+\sin(\gamma/2)\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(S52)

Here, the angle
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
denotes the argument of
k_{x}+\mathrm{i}k_{y}
,
and the angle
\gamma
is defined by
\tan\gamma=t_{\perp}/(\alpha_{R}k)

characterizing the layer hybridization.
The superscripts
s
and
l
indicate that the corresponding spinors are defined in the spin and layer subspaces, respectively.

In this model,
the time-reversal symmetry is represented by

\hat{\mathcal{T}}:\psi(\mathbf{k})\mapsto\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}\psi^{*}(-\mathbf{k})
,
and the inversion symmetry is represented by

\hat{\mathcal{I}}:\psi(\mathbf{k})\mapsto\lambda_{1}\psi(-\mathbf{k})
,
where
\sigma_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}
are Pauli matrices acting on the spin and layer degrees of freedom, respectively.
It is straightforward to verify that the degenerate band eigenstates defined in Eq. (
S51
)
are connected by the combined

\hat{\mathcal{I}}\circ\hat{\mathcal{T}}
operation,
i.e.,

\hat{\mathcal{I}}\circ\hat{\mathcal{T}}[\chi_{1}(\mathbf{k})]=-\chi_{4}(\mathbf{k})

and

\hat{\mathcal{I}}\circ\hat{\mathcal{T}}[\chi_{2}(\mathbf{k})]=+\chi_{3}(\mathbf{k})
.

Experimental evidence indicates that superconductivity in
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
is nodeless and preserves time-reversal symmetry. In addition, theoretical studies suggest that the pairing mechanism is likely phonon-mediated. Based on these observations, we assume that the leading superconducting instability is driven by an isotropic, short-range attractive interaction.
At the mean-field level, this leads to an
s
-wave–like pairing in momentum space, described by the following pairing Hamiltonian

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}=&\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{l}\Delta_{0}c_{-\mathbf{k},l,\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},l,\uparrow}^{\dagger}+\text{h.c.},\end{split}

(S53)

where
\Delta_{0}
denotes the uniform pairing amplitude.

In the weak pairing limit, where
|\Delta_{0}|\ll\alpha_{R}k_{F}
,
inter-band pairing can be neglected.
The effective superconducting pairing on the Fermi surfaces is then obtained by projecting the full pairing Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
onto the low-energy subspace near the Fermi level.
Focusing on the
\epsilon_{+}(\mathbf{k})
band,
whose Fermi surface is spanned by the eigenstates
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{4}
,
the projected gap function in this subspace takes the form

\begin{split}\hat{\Delta}_{\text{eff}}=\Delta_{0}\begin{pmatrix}+\mathrm{i}\exp(-\mathrm{i}\theta_{\mathbf{k}})&0\\
0&-\mathrm{i}\exp(+\mathrm{i}\theta_{\mathbf{k}})\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(S54)

which corresponds to a helical equal-spin pairing state.
It is straightforward to verify that the effective pairing described by Eq. (
S54
) is fully gapped and preserves time-reversal symmetry,
in full agreement with experimental observations.
Despite the spin splitting induced by spin-orbit coupling (SOC),
the superconducting state remains robust and is not suppressed by SOC or inter-layer hybridization.
This stability arises because Cooper pairs still form between time-reversal conjugate states,
consistent with the Anderson theorem.

This minimal model captures the essential features of systems with spin-orbit coupling induced by local inversion symmetry breaking,
and it explains how effective helical equal-spin pairing can naturally arise in such systems.
However, the Fermi surfaces in
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
exhibit a more complex topology, as all three
p
-orbitals of Bi contribute to the electronic structure near the Fermi level.
A more detailed comparison with experiments therefore requires more sophisticated models informed by first-principles calculations.
Furthermore, due to the presence of both time-reversal and inversion symmetries,
the superconducting state of
\beta
-
{}{}\mathrm{Bi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{Pd}
belongs to the symmetry class DIII, which supports topological phases characterized by a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant in quasi-
2
D systems and a
\mathbb{Z}
invariant in
3
D systems.
Experimental evidence suggests the presence of topologically nontrivial states in the vortex cores
Lv et al. 2017
.
Nevertheless, the precise nature of the topology is obscured by the complexity of the Fermi surfaces,
and further investigation is needed to clarify the topological character of the superconducting state.
