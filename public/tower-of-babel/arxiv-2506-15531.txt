Relaxation for massive particles: transport and causality
Matej Bajec, Alexander Soloviev

Abstract
Correlators at finite density and temperature encode important information about a physical system, such as transport and causality. For a massive gas of particles in the relaxation time approximation of kinetic theory, we provide complete analytic results for all first order transport coefficients, including thermoelectric coefficients. We demonstrate the first complete picture of the complex structure of the correlators as a function of mass, providing an interpretation in terms of the causal structure of lightcones. We provide a simple criterion to extract the effective lightcone velocity from the cut of the retarded correlator.



I
Introduction

The emergence of collective phenomena from microscopic dynamics has been a key research frontier, leading to robust characterization of near- and non-equilibrium physics. To that end, an often utilized
microscopic description is
kinetic theory
Pitaevskii and Lifshitz 2012
;
de Groot et al. 1980
, which describes the ballistic trajectories of (quasi-)particles under the influence of external fields and scattering against other microscopic degrees of freedom.

Over the years, a crucial theoretical laboratory in kinetic theory has been the relaxation time approximation (RTA)
Anderson and Witting 1974
. RTA kinetic theory dramatically simplifies the collision kernel by focusing on a single relaxation time for which the system relaxes to equilibrium. As such, it captures the near equilibrium behavior of a gas, while remaining analytically tractable. This is a valid approximation when there is a well-defined lowest eigenvalue of the collision kernel
Liboff 2003
;
Grozdanov et al. 2016
. There have been numerous recent results including: the determination of retarded correlators due to charge and momentum transport
Romatschke 2016
, the exact analytic solution in
0+1
dimensions for transversely homogeneous boost-invariant systems
Florkowski et al. 2013a
;
Florkowski et al. 2013b
(and additionally the massive case
Florkowski et al. 2014
), extensions to energy dependent relaxation time
Kurkela and Wiedemann 2019
;
Kurkela et al. 2019a
;
Dash et al. 2022
;
Ambrus et al. 2023
;
Brants 2024
;
Hu 2024
;
Singh et al. 2025
;
Mukherjee et al. 2025
, analytic analysis of the Bjorken attractor
Blaizot and Yan 2021
;
Aniceto et al. 2025
, determining the dissipative fluid dynamics from the massive RTA gas

Ambrus et al. 2022
;
Jaiswal et al. 2022
;
Ambruş et al. 2024
, serving as a useful benchmark for numerical methods
Ochsenfeld and Schlichting 2023
, the extraction of thermoelectric coefficients from analytic retarded correlators in systems with momentum relaxation
Bajec et al. 2024
, analytic determination of the three point function in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism
Abbasi and Rischke 2025
and developing a multi-species RTA
Rocha and Denicol 2025
. Phenomenological applications vary from e.g. the hadron resonance gas
Dusling and Schäfer 2012
;
Das et al. 2020

to the Nernst effect in band structures of semiconductors
Zebarjadi et al. 2021
.

In the present work, we provide a complete understanding of the analytic structure of correlators in the RTA for a massive gas, both in terms of transport as well as providing a link between the complex structure of the correlator and causality. Previous works, e.g.
Romatschke 2012
, have computed the thermodynamic quantities and some transport coefficients in the massive gas. We add to these results by providing closed form expressions for transport coefficients (in all spatial dimensions
d>1
, see
Appendix

A
), as well as computing the thermoelectric coefficients for a massive gas.
We would like to point out some inaccuracies in
Hataei et al. 2025
, which among other claims, indicated that the diffusive mode in RTA kinetic theory becomes unstable for a certain value of mass. We show that this is not the case for any
d>1
. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the correlators in the massive case possess a cut in the complex
\omega
plane along which the correlator has a discontinuity. We find this cut to be undeformable and therefore refrain from using the term ‘branch cut’, opting to refer to it as simply the ‘cut’, unlike the proposed recent findings of
Lin et al. 2025
, where they propose an undeformable branch cut arises as a ‘condensation’ of continuously many branch points. We furthermore find that the discontinuity cut ends in what were branch points in the massless case
\omega=\pm k-i/\tau_{R}
and provide a physical interpretation of the endpoints as well as the discontinuity profile along the cut. The essential picture is:

–

the location of the end points of the cut in complex frequency space corresponds to the fastest a signal can travel, i.e. the speed of light,

–

the profile of the discontinuity along the cut provides a measure of the effective velocity of the particles,

–

and the poles correspond to collective, i.e. hydrodynamic,

II
Set-up

In this section we provide the reader with a brief overview of RTA kinetic theory with a constant relaxation time. We will follow the discussion and conventions from
Bajec et al. 2024
.

We begin by considering the RTA Boltzmann equation for the one particle distribution function
f=f(t,\textbf{x},\textbf{p})

\displaystyle p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f+\mathcal{F}^{i}\partial_{p^{i}}f=\frac{p_{\mu}u^{\mu}}{\tau_{R}}\left(f-f_{eq}\right),

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}^{i}
is the external force, e.g. the electric and gravitational force, can be encoded via

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=F^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}-\Gamma^{\mu}_{{\alpha\beta}}p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}.

(2)

Here
F^{{\mu\nu}}[A_{\mu}]
is the field strength tensor and
\Gamma^{\mu}_{{\alpha\beta}}[g_{\mu\nu}]
is the Christoffel symbol. Note that the four velocity,
u^{\mu}
, is timelike normalized
u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-1
and
\tau_{R}
is the relaxation time which we take to be constant.
In writing the above, we have assumed the mass-shell condition
p_{0}^{2}=p^{2}+m^{2}.

At present, we will work with an equilibrium distribution function given by the Maxwell-Jüttner distribution,
f_{\rm eq}=e^{(p^{\mu}u_{\mu}+\mu_{0})/T_{0}}
.
Moments of the one-particle distribution function correspond to macroscopic quantities, such as the number current and energy momentum tensor

\displaystyle J^{\mu}=\int_{\textbf{p}}\frac{p^{\mu}}{p^{0}}f,\quad\quad T^{\mu\nu}=\int_{\textbf{p}}\frac{p^{\mu}p^{\nu}}{p^{0}}f,

(3)

where
\int_{\textbf{p}}=\int{d^{3}p}\,(2\pi)^{-3}.

We are interested in extracting the linear response of the RTA kinetic theory in the case of massive particles. To do this, we turn on an external gauge potential,
\delta A_{\mu}
, and/or an external metric,
\delta g_{\mu\nu}
. The presence of external fields induces a change in the macroscopic quantities via

\displaystyle\mu(t,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}+\delta\mu(t,\textbf{x}),

(4)

\displaystyle T(t,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=T_{0}+\delta T(t,\textbf{x}),

(5)

\displaystyle u^{\mu}(t,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=u^{\mu}_{0}+\delta u^{\mu}(t,\textbf{x}),

(6)

where
u^{\mu}_{0}=(1,0,0,0).

This in turn shifts both distribution functions

\displaystyle f(t,\textbf{x},\textbf{p})

\displaystyle=f_{0}(\textbf{p})+\delta f(t,\textbf{x},\textbf{p}),

(7)

\displaystyle f_{\rm eq}(t,\textbf{x},\textbf{p})

\displaystyle=f_{0}(\textbf{p})+\delta f_{\rm eq}(t,\textbf{x},\textbf{p}),

(8)

where
f_{0}=e^{-\frac{p^{0}-\mu_{0}}{T_{0}}}
and
the change to the equilibrium distribution function is given by

\displaystyle\delta f_{\rm eq}=\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}\left[\delta\mu+\frac{\delta T}{T_{0}}(p^{0}-\mu_{0})+p_{i}\delta u^{i}\right].

(9)

We parameterize the four momentum via
\tilde{v}^{\mu}=p^{\mu}/p^{0}=(1,\gamma v^{i})
, where

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{\absolutevalue{\textbf{p}}}{p^{0}}

(10)

and
v^{i}v_{i}=1
. The linearized Boltzmann equation around the flat background
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\text{diag}(-1,\textbf{1})
and at finite density
A^{(0)}_{\mu}=(\mu_{0},\textbf{0})
is then given by:

\displaystyle p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\delta f+\mathcal{F}^{i}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial p^{i}}=\frac{p\cdot u}{\tau_{R}}\left(\delta f-\delta f_{\mathrm{eq}}\right).

(11)

Note that the zeroth order solution is given by the equilibrium distribution
f_{0}=f_{\text{eq}}
.
The solution of (
11
) in Fourier space
2
2

2

Our convention for the Fourier transform is
f(\omega,\textbf{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int d^{3}x\,e^{i\omega t-i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{x}}f(t,\textbf{k})
.
is

\displaystyle\delta f=\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}\frac{\gamma E^{i}v_{i}-\Gamma^{0}_{\mu\nu}p^{0}\tilde{v}^{\mu}\tilde{v}^{\nu}+\left(\delta\mu+p^{i}\delta u_{i}+\frac{\delta T}{T_{0}}(p^{0}-\mu_{0})\right)/\tau_{R}}{-i\omega+i\gamma\,\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{v}+1/\tau_{R}},

(12)

where
E^{i}=F^{0i}=ik^{i}A_{0}+i\omega A^{i}
is the electric field. In the following computations, we will without loss of generality assume that perturbations are aligned with the
z
direction, i.e. that
k
points along the
z
direction.
It is then straightforward to compute changes to the conserved operators, namely the number current and the energy momentum tensor from
eq.

3
.
Additionally, requiring the conservation of the respective currents leads to the RTA matching conditions

\displaystyle\int_{\textbf{p}}\frac{1}{p^{0}}\left(f-f_{\rm eq}\right)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\int_{\textbf{p}}\frac{p^{\mu}}{p^{0}}\left(f-f_{\rm eq}\right)=0.

(13)

To compute correlators, we will use the variational approach (see e.g.
Romatschke 2016
;
Bajec et al. 2024
), where we expand the conserved operators in powers of the external perturbations. Explicitly,

\displaystyle J^{\mu}

\displaystyle=J^{\mu}_{0}-G^{\mu,\nu}_{JJ}\delta A_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}G^{\mu,{\alpha\beta}}_{JT}\delta g_{{\alpha\beta}}+\ldots

(14)

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=T^{\mu\nu}_{0}-\frac{1}{2}G^{{\mu\nu},{\alpha\beta}}_{TT}\delta g_{{\alpha\beta}}-G^{{\mu\nu},\alpha}_{TJ}\delta A_{\alpha}+\ldots

(15)

which leads to the definition of the correlators

\displaystyle G_{JJ}^{\mu,\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta J^{\mu}}{\delta A_{\nu}},\quad G_{TJ}^{{\mu\nu},\alpha}=-\frac{\delta T^{\mu\nu}}{\delta A_{\alpha}},\quad G_{JT}^{\mu,\alpha\beta}=-2\frac{\delta J^{\mu}}{\delta g_{{\alpha\beta}}},\quad G_{TT}^{{\mu\nu},{\alpha\beta}}=-2\frac{\delta T^{\mu\nu}}{\delta g_{\alpha\beta}}.

(16)

II.1
Thermodynamics at finite mass

Here, we present the thermodynamics of a massive kinetic theory, restating known results in the literature, see e.g.
Romatschke 2012
. We note that the static susceptibility is a function of the mass

\displaystyle\chi(m)=\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}f_{\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=T_{0}^{2}\frac{e^{\mu_{0}/T_{0}}}{2\pi^{2}}\mathfrak{m}^{2}K_{2}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right),

(17)

where
K_{2}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind and
\mathfrak{m}\equiv m/T_{0}
.
From
eq.

3
, the equilibrium number density
J^{0}=n_{0}
is

\displaystyle n_{0}=\chi T_{0}.

(18)

Similarly we can determine the equilibrium energy density,
T^{00}=\varepsilon,
and pressure,
P_{0}=T^{ii}
(no sum), from
eq.

3
to be

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}

\displaystyle=T_{0}^{4}\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\left(\mathfrak{m}K_{1}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right)+3K_{2}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right)\right),

(19)

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=T_{0}^{4}\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}K_{2}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right),

(20)

in the absence of chemical potential.
In the limit
m\rightarrow 0
we recover the conformal equation of state,
P_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}/3
and
\varepsilon_{0}\propto T_{0}^{4}
.
From these expressions we can immediately determine the speed of sound squared at zero chemical potential,
c_{s}^{2}=\partial P/\partial\varepsilon
, evaluated at local equilibrium

\displaystyle c_{s}^{2}=\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{2}K_{3}(\mathfrak{m})}{\left(\mathfrak{m}^{3}+12\mathfrak{m}\right)K_{0}(\mathfrak{m})+\left(5\mathfrak{m}^{2}+24\right)K_{1}(\mathfrak{m})},

(21)

which agrees identically with the result from
Romatschke 2012
. For clarity, the small/large asymptotic behavior of the speed of sound is given by

\displaystyle c_{s}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(1-\frac{1}{24}\mathfrak{m}^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathfrak{m}^{4}\right),&\mathfrak{m}\ll 1,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{4\mathfrak{m}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({\mathfrak{m}^{-5/2}}\right),&\mathfrak{m}\gg 1.\end{cases}

(22)

III
Transport properties at finite mass

In the present section, we provide the necessary steps to extract transport coefficients as a function of mass from the retarded correlators.

III.1
Charge transport

Here, we study the response in presence of an external electric field only, which induces a change in the chemical potential. In other words we turn off the external metric perturbations
\delta g_{\mu\nu}=0
, which in turn sets
\delta T=0
and
\delta u^{i}=0
. In this case, (
12
) reduces to

\displaystyle\delta f=\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}\frac{\delta\mu/\tau_{R}+\gamma E^{i}v_{i}}{-i\omega+i\gamma\,\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{v}+1/\tau_{R}}.

(23)

Recalling that the change in the number density is related to the change in the chemical potential via the susceptibility,
\delta J^{0}=\delta n=\chi\delta\mu
,
a simple calculation leads to the density-density correlator:

\displaystyle G^{0,0}_{JJ}=\frac{ik\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,p^{2}\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}I^{03}}{\chi-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,p^{2}\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}I^{00}},

(24)

where the static susceptibility
\chi
is given in (
17
) and

\displaystyle I^{ab}(p)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{\tilde{v}^{a}\tilde{v}^{b}}{1+\tau_{R}\left(-i\omega+ik\gamma\cos\theta\right)},

(25)

where
\tilde{v}_{a}=(1,\gamma v^{i})
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle I^{00}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i\gamma\,k\tau_{R}}L_{\gamma},\quad I^{03}=\frac{1-i\omega\tau_{R}}{2\gamma\,k^{2}\tau_{R}^{2}}L_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{i\,k\tau_{R}},

(26)

where we defined the shorthand for the mass dependent logarithm

\displaystyle L_{\gamma}=\log\left(\frac{\omega-\gamma\,k+i/\tau_{R}}{\omega+\gamma\,k+i/\tau_{R}}\right).

(27)

Therefore the pole of the correlator
eq.

24
is given implicitly by:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{2}\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}\left(1-I^{00}\right)=0.

(28)

In the massless case,
I^{00}
does not depend on
p
. Therefore the above integral equation is satisfied when

\displaystyle I^{00}\big|_{m=0}=1\Leftrightarrow\omega=\frac{i}{\tau_{R}}\left(k\tau_{R}\cot(k\tau_R)-1\right),

(29)

where we used
\gamma\big|_{m=0}=1
. Expanding for small
k
to quadratic order leads to the value of the charge diffusion constant
D=\tau_{R}/3
. This agrees with the massless result
Romatschke 2016
.

Returning to the massive case, we can expand (
28
) in small
\omega
and
k
up to quadratic order, which leads to

\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{2}f_{0}(p)\left(\gamma^{2}k^{2}\tau_{R}+3\omega(\tau_{R}\omega-i)\right)=0.

(30)

The above equation can be solved analytically after integration to obtain:

\displaystyle\omega(k)=-iD(m)k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{4}),

(31)

where

\displaystyle\frac{D(m)}{\tau_{R}}=\frac{1}{3}+\mathfrak{m}\frac{G_{1,3}^{2,1}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{2}}{4}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0\\
\end{array}\right)}{12K_{2}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right)}+\mathfrak{m}\frac{\pi}{6K_{2}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right)},

where
G^{2,1}_{1,3}
is the Meijer
G
–function (see
Gradshteyn et al. 2015
for definitions). In the limit
\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow 0
the above equation reduces to the known result
\omega(k)=-i\tau_{R}k^{2}/3

Romatschke 2016
. One can obtain the analytic expressions for the two limits
\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow 0
and
\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow\infty
, namely

\displaystyle\frac{D}{\tau_{R}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{6}\mathfrak{m}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{3}),&\mathfrak{m}\ll 1,\\
\frac{1}{\mathfrak{m}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathfrak{m}^{-2}\right),&\mathfrak{m}\gg 1.\end{cases}

(34)

We would like to stress that the diffusion coefficient remains positive for all values of the mass. There is no instability arising from a negative value of mass, as claimed by
Hataei et al. 2025
. We believe that this claim came from an erroneous extrapolation of the small mass result. Furthermore, we can determine the diffusion coefficient in arbitrary dimension, the details of which we present in
Appendix

A
, where we also comment on the absence of instability in arbitrary spatial dimensions
d>1
.

III.2
Momentum transport

In the present section, we turn to momentum transport, which is mediated via a background metric perturbation. The background metric perturbation induces a change in the temperature and the four velocity. We turn off the background electric field and consequently,
\delta\mu=0.
Then
eq.

12
reads

\displaystyle\delta f=\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}\frac{\left(p^{i}\delta u_{i}+p^{0}\frac{\delta T}{T_{0}}\right)/\tau_{R}-\Gamma^{0}_{\mu\nu}p^{0}\tilde{v}^{\mu}\tilde{v}^{\nu}}{-i\omega+i\gamma\,\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{v}+1/\tau_{R}}.

(35)

III.2.1
Shear channel

We perform a similar derivation of the diffusion constant equation as in the charge diffusion case. The simplest case is the shear diffusion channel, schematically denoted with
\delta T^{0\perp}
, where
\perp
is one of the transverse components to our chosen axis aligned with the wave vector
k
. For concreteness, we fix the transverse direction to
\perp=y
; other choices are physically equivalent. Because of the decoupled nature of the spin
1
channel, we turn off sources that couple to
\delta T
as well as
\delta u^{j}=0
with
j\neq y

. In this case, the self–consistency condition,
\delta T^{0y}=(\varepsilon_{0}+P_{0})\delta u^{y}
, is trivially solved. A short calculation then shows:

\displaystyle\delta T^{0y}=\frac{-\tau_{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}p^{2}(p^{0})^{2}\gamma\,J_{\text{sh}}}{1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}+P_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}p^{2}(p^{0})^{2}\,I^{11}}

(36)

where
I^{ab}
was defined in (
25
) and

\displaystyle J_{\text{sh}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{v^{y}\Gamma^{0}_{\mu\nu}\tilde{v}^{\mu}\tilde{v}^{\nu}}{1+\tau_{R}\left(-i\omega+ik\gamma\cos\theta\right)}.

(37)

The denominator structure encodes all the information about the collective modes in the spin
1
channel. Using
\varepsilon_{0}+P_{0}=\frac{\partial T^{0y}}{\partial u^{y}}=\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}p^{2}(p^{0})^{2}\gamma^{2}
, we obtain the integral equation describing shear diffusion:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{f_{0}}{T_{0}}p^{2}(p^{0})^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{3}-I^{11}\right)=0.

(38)

The above equation can be generalized to arbitrary spatial dimensions, see
Appendix

A
.
The integral can be solved analytically in the hydrodynamic limit by expanding the LHS up to cubic terms with respect to
k
and
\omega
in order to obtain the diffusive dispersion relation:

\displaystyle\omega(k)=-iD_{\text{sh}}(m)k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{4}),

(39)

where

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\text{sh}}}{\tau_{R}}=\frac{1}{5}+\mathfrak{m}^{2}\frac{G_{1,3}^{2,1}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{2}}{4}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2},0\\
\end{array}\right)}{40K_{3}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right)}-\mathfrak{m}^{2}\frac{\pi}{30K_{3}\left(\mathfrak{m}\right)}.

The small and large mass limits of (
III.2.1
) are given by

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\text{sh}}}{\tau_{R}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{5}\left(1-\frac{1}{60}\mathfrak{m}^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathfrak{m}^{4}\right)&\mathfrak{m}\ll 1,\\
\frac{1}{\mathfrak{m}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathfrak{m}^{-2}\right)&\mathfrak{m}\gg 1.\end{cases}

(42)

We show this result in the left panel of
Figure

2
, where we note that shear diffusion is positive for all values of the mass for any number of spatial dimensions
d>1
(see
Appendix

A
). This is in decided contrast to the results of
Hataei et al. 2025
, who predicted that the shear diffusion should become negative for a certain value of
m/T_{0}.
Comparing with results obtained from hydrodynamics
Kovtun 2012
, we can identify
D_{\text{sh}}=\eta/(\varepsilon_{0}+P_{0})
. An independent calculation will cross check and confirm this result in
Section

III.2.2
Sound channel

We now consider the longitudinal channel, i.e. the sound. Here we only turn on sources that induce
\delta T
and
\delta u^{z}
, since
k^{i}\propto\delta^{i}_{z}
. In order to solve the self–consistency condition and obtain the analytic structure of the correlators in this channel, one must solve two coupled linear equations for the perturbations
\delta T
and
\delta u^{z}
or equivalently for
\delta T^{00}
and
\delta T^{03}
using
\delta T^{00}=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{0}}{\partial T_{0}}\delta T
and
\delta T^{0z}=(\varepsilon_{0}+P_{0})\delta u^{z}
:

\displaystyle\delta T^{00}=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{0}}{\partial T_{0}}\delta T=\mathcal{I}_{00}\frac{\delta T}{T_{0}}+\mathcal{I}_{03}\delta u^{z}+J_{\text{s}}^{0},

\displaystyle\delta T^{0z}=(\varepsilon_{0}+P_{0})\delta u^{z}=\mathcal{I}_{03}\frac{\delta T}{T_{0}}+\mathcal{I}_{33}\delta u^{z}+J_{\text{s}}^{z},

IV.1
Physical interpretation of analytic structure in the massive case

Here, we interpret the behavior of the cut in the previous section for the massive gas.
To build intuition, we begin with a non-interacting massless gas, restating the arguments in
Kurkela and Wiedemann 2019
. Particles are travelling in ballistic trajectories at the speed of light. Now, consider a plane wave perturbation in the sound channel. The response at some spacetime location
(t,\textbf{x})
depends on the contribution over the sphere of radius
ct.
Maximal signal,
\omega=k
, will correspond to particles moving along the direction of the perturbation. Any other direction will have a reduced signal,
\omega=\textbf{v}\cdot\textbf{k}.
This corresponds to a pole in the complex frequency plane. Integrating over all angles, the poles assemble into a logarithmic branch cut

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{1}{-i\omega+i\textbf{v}\cdot\textbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2ik}\log{\frac{\omega-k}{\omega+k}}.

(91)

Including interactions via relaxation to equilibrium in the RTA leads to damping, which pushes the branch cut deeper into the lower half plane. Practically, this amounts to shifting
\omega\rightarrow\omega+i/\tau_{R}.

In the massive non-interacting case, particles move at a distribution of speeds slower than the speed of light. As such, in addition to the angular integration, the situation in the complex plane is further complicated by needing to integrate over all momenta

\displaystyle\int dp\,p^{2}f_{0}(p)\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{1}{-i\omega+i\gamma(p)\textbf{v}\cdot\textbf{k}}=\int dp\,p^{2}f_{0}(p)\frac{1}{2i\gamma(p)k}\log{\frac{\omega-\gamma(p)k}{\omega+\gamma(p)k}}.

(92)

We see that the branch cut structure arises both from the angular and momentum integration, which should be contrasted to the massless case where the continuous spectrum assembles from the continuous set of poles from solely the angular integration.
The continuous string of signals coming from the momentum integration is a consequence of finite mass
m>0
since now the speed of propagation of the individual particles between collisions is energy dependent (and not fixed to the speed of light as was the case in the massless theory), c.f.
Equation

90
.

While the explicit expression of the integral in
Equation

92
is unattainable in closed form, one can again follow the discussion from the previous sections as well as
Section

B.1
to extract the discontinuity profile.
We see generically from
Figure

v_{\rm cut}
.
Taking
eq.

85

\displaystyle\mathrm{disc}\,G^{2,2}_{JJ}(\omega_{*},k)=\epsilon,

(93)

from which we find
\omega_{*}=v_{\rm cut}k+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
. We extract the effective cut velocity via

For a simpler, explicit and analytic example in the case of a logarithm, see
eq.

137

Interestingly, it seems that the structure of the discontinuity along the cut seems to encode causal information from the propagator. In the massless case, the branch points of the logarithm

\omega=\pm k

It would seem natural that in the massive case a similar causal structure holds. Indeed, we find that the end points are the same as in the massless case. The major difference is that in the massive case, the argument of the logarithm is momentum dependent. As there is a distribution of particles of all different velocities, particles from different directions angular directions contribute with varying weight. The contribution of velocities
\gtrsim v_{\rm cut}
is exponentially suppressed due to the distribution function. For velocities below
v_{\rm cut},
more signal reaches the spacetime point.

IV.2
Indeformability of the cut at finite mass

In this subsection, we discuss the freedom one has in usually choosing the branch cut.
We begin by considering the branch cut in the correlators at zero mass. Such a structure stems from integrals over complex variables of the form

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dz\frac{1}{1-i\omega\tau_{R}+ik\tau_{R}z}.

(96)

Since the contour of the above can be freely deformed, this results in the deformation of the branch cut of the correlator in the complex
\omega
plane, which is given by solving for the zero of the denominator of the integrand, see
Section

B.2
(and also Appendix A of
Bajec et al. 2024
).

At finite mass
m>0
the situation changes substantially, since we now have an integral schematically of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}du\int_{-1}^{1}dz\,e^{-\frac{m}{T_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}}\frac{1}{1-i\omega\tau_{R}+ik\tau_{R}\,zu}.

(97)

For simplicity, we take
u\in[0,1]
and deform the angular integration contour
\int_{-1}^{1}dz\mapsto\int_{\gamma_{c}}dz
with a general (smooth) path
\gamma_{c}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. We see that the integral is not well-defined for an entire two dimensional area in the complex
\omega
plane. Indeed, not only must
\omega
not lie on the contour
\gamma_{c}
, it must also not lie within the region swept by the ray from the origin to the contour, courtesy of
u\in[0,1]
. Deforming the integration contour therefore cuts out a whole region of the complex
\omega
plane where the correlator is not defined. Different choices of integration contour cut out different regions of the complex plane, which can be made arbitrarily large (see also
Brants 2024
for the discussion on two dimensional areas of non-analyticity in the complex
\omega
plane). This may be inconsistent with the general properties of the causal correlators.

However, keeping both integration variables
z
and
u
on the real axis bypasses the aforementioned problems, since this choice cuts out a finite
curve
in the complex plane, where the correlator is not defined, namely

\displaystyle\omega=k\,zu-i/\tau_{R},

(98)

where
zu\in[-1,1]
. The fact that the angular and momentum/energy integration are non-trivially coupled in the
m>0
case implies a unique choice of the cut – the straight line between
\pm k-i/\tau_{R}
. This is in partial agreement with
Lin et al. 2025
, however we disagree with their interpretation of a ‘branch point condensate’ forming a branch cut.
In our view, the unique (or preferred) choice of the cut is inconsistent with the characteristic property of a branch cut, namely the ability to deform its shape. We also note that this non-analytic structure stems solely from the numerator, as the denominator is trivial and analytic in the case of
G^{2,2}_{JJ}
. The change in the analytic structure when
m>0
is, at least in this case, indeed abrupt and not regulated by the numerator/denominator cancelling effects.

This unique choice for the cut at
m>0
has deep consequences that were first noticed in the context of massless particles
Romatschke 2016
, where it was proposed, that the hydrodynamic (charge diffusion) pole ceases to exist at certain values of
k\tau_{R}
as it merges with the branch cut. However, as we discussed above, in the massless case the cut can be freely deformed, in particular in such way, that the pole never reaches the branch cut. On the other hand, in the massive case, the straight cut provides a cutoff on
k
, since the (diffusive) poles of the retarded correlators can reach it at a finite value of the (modulus of the) wavevector,
k_{*}
, which is analogous to the statement of
Romatschke 2016
. Conservatively, the behavior of the pole and cut interacting in the massive case is not clear as it lays outside the domain of the usual theory and crashing into the cut. We find that the cutoff wavelength
k_{*}
, at which the poles merge with the cut, i.e. when
\imaginary\omega=-i/\tau_{R}
, increases both with mass
m
and with the number of spatial dimensions
d\geq 2
, although the large mass asymptotics seem to agree for arbitrary
d
.

We interpret this marked contrast between
m=0
and
m>0
as a consequence of conformal symmetry breaking in the equation of state.
4
4

4

We thank Sašo Grozdanov for this comment.
It seems natural for a conformal theory (
m=0
) to be in principle valid for arbitrary large
k
.
The branch cut can in this case be deformed freely, allowing the poles of the correlators to be valid for arbitrary large
k
, as they do not hit the cut for any finite
k
. On the other hand, any finite
m>0
breaks the conformal symmetry and introduces a cut-off in our theory. This is manifested in the inability to deform the cut. We note again that this inability itself stems from the analytic properties of the retarded correlators.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we computed retarded correlators at finite temperature and chemical potential in the case of a massive gas in the RTA. We demonstrated via analytic expressions that the transport coefficients are monotonically decreasing positive functions of mass, which corrects the results of
Hataei et al. 2025
, where they predicted e.g. the shear diffusion mode becoming negative for some value of mass. Furthermore, as we had the complete expression for the retarded correlators, we were able to extract analytically the profile of the discontinuity of the cut, which we showed corresponded to physical information from the cut, leading to an interpretation of the profile of the discontinuity as additional causal structure of the correlator.

The overall picture in the massive case is: the hydrodynamic poles correspond to collective phenomena. The non-hydrodynamic behavior due to the ballistic motion of the particles is captured in the correlator by the cut, which spans between the end points situated at
\omega=\pm k-i/\tau_{R}
that indicate the maximal speed of causal propagation. The cut has a particular profile due to the mass, which we show has most of its support between
\omega=\pm v_{\rm cut}k-i/\tau_{R}
. From a technical perspective, an important take home message is that, as we demonstrated in
Section

IV
, multivalued functions stemming from an integral equation should be studied on the level of the integrand in order to avoid confusion about the analytic structure of the result.

It will be interesting to explore what hidden structures can be uncovered from the perspective developed here. It seems clear that a natural first step is to consider RTA with an energy dependent relaxation time
Kurkela and Wiedemann 2019
;
Kurkela et al. 2019b
;
Kurkela et al. 2019a
;
Brants 2024
;
Hu 2024
. The integration over momenta in this case is more nuanced, since the branch cut of the integrand is being driven deeper in the complex plane for typically studied relaxation times of the form
\tau_{R}\propto(p^{0})^{\alpha}.
Moreover, the causal structure of the correlators are largely unexplored in extensions to higher levels in the BBGKY hierarchy
Grozdanov and Soloviev 2024
and in the kinetic theory of scalar field theory, including in the expanding FLRW background
Bazow et al. 2016
. It would be curious to further explore the complex structure in models where it is explicitly known that interactions lead to dynamical lightcones, e.g. in two fluid model with dynamical effective metrics
Kurkela et al. 2018
.

In this appendix we showcase the general procedure to describe the analytic structure of an integral of a function with branch points via several examples of increasing complexity.

Example 1.
We start with the simplest example

6

6

(122)

\log z
to lie along the negative real axis
(-\infty,0]

to the whole complex plane. As

Example 1.
The prototypical example of an integral sharing key features with computations encountered in the physical of kinetic theory in RTA approximation is

\displaystyle F(\omega)\equiv\int_{-1}^{1}dz\frac{1}{z-\omega}.

(139)

Treating the integration variable as complex, we may deform the above integration contour arbitrarily into the complex plane. The above integral is then defined for any
\omega
, which does not lie on the integration contour, as per the usual complex analysis arguments, cf.
Ahlfors
. This results in the domain of
F:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
to be a cut complex plane, with the cut given by the integration contour
\gamma_{c}
, i.e.
\Omega=\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{im}\,\gamma_{c}
. This is the basis of the argument why the logarithmic branch cut can be arbitrarily deformed in the massless case. The discontinuity of the function
F(\omega)
is deduced from the residue theorem as follows – we position ourselves with our parameter
\omega
just above and below a point on the contour
\omega_{*}
to define
\omega_{\pm}=\omega_{*}\pm i\varepsilon
for some infinitesimal
\varepsilon>0
.

\omega_{*}
is not parallel to the imaginary axis. Otherwise we infinitesimally displace (also) the real parts.
As we send
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we see that the difference between the two values is precisely
2\pi i
times the residue of the integrand at the point

Example 2.
It is then clear that the generalization of the above example to

\displaystyle F(\omega)\equiv\int_{\gamma_{c}}dz\frac{f(z,\omega)}{z-\omega},

(141)

with
f(z,\omega)
holomorphic on the integration contour (or rather in its neighborhood), results in the discontinuity profile of
f(z=\omega_{*},\,\omega=\omega_{*})
, where
\omega_{*}
runs along the integration contour;
\omega_{*}\in\mathrm{im}\,\gamma_{c}
.

Example 3.
We conclude with an example applicable to the discussion in the main text, where we discuss the discontinuity profile at the branch cut resulting from energy integration at a fixed angle:

\displaystyle F(\omega;k,m)\equiv\int_{m}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-\frac{x}{T_{0}}}\frac{1}{1+\tau_{R}\left(-i\omega+ik\sqrt{1-\frac{m^{2}}{x^{2}}}\cos\theta\right)}.

(142)

The derivation is clearer if we introduce a new variable
u=\sqrt{1-\frac{m^{2}}{x^{2}}}
:

\displaystyle F(\omega;k,m)=\frac{1}{ik\tau_{R}\cos\theta}\int_{0}^{1}du\frac{mu}{(1-u^{2})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{m}{T_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\right)\frac{1}{u-\frac{i\omega\tau_{R}-1}{ik\tau_{R}\cos\theta}}.

(143)

Taking the integration contour along the real axis (
u\in[0,1]
) results in the cut along

\displaystyle\frac{i\omega\tau_{R}-1}{ik\tau_{R}\cos\theta}\in[0,1]\Longleftrightarrow\omega=sk\cos\theta-\frac{i}{\tau_{R}},\quad s\in[0,1].

(144)

Then the discontinuity at this cut, for fixed
k
and
\theta
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{disc}\,F(\omega;k,m)\bigg|_{\omega=k\cos\theta\cdot s-\frac{i}{\tau_{R}}}=\frac{2\pi i}{ik\tau_{R}\cos\theta}\frac{m\,s}{(1-s^{2})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{m}{T_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\right).

(145)
