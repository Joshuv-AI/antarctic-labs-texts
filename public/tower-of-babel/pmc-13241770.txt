Evolutionary divergence of nocturnin led to redox regulation in mammalian orthologs
Authors: Crystal A. Olivas-Rasmussen, Peng Gao, Emil S. Rasmussen, Carla B. Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
NADP(H) and NAD(H) are essential cofactors involved in nearly 500 metabolic reactions, making them fundamental to cellular function and redox homeostasis. Loss of redox homeostasis through alterations in NADP(H) regulation has been implicated in various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders and cardiovascular diseases. The levels of these metabolites are tightly regulated and highly compartmentalized. Nocturnin (NOCT), a circadian clock-controlled phosphatase, hydrolyzes both oxidized and reduced forms of NADP(H) and exists in cytosolic and mitochondrial forms. Here, we compare the activity of Xenopus and mammalian NOCT, which reveals a novel redox-dependent regulatory mechanism in mammals. Mammalian NOCT undergoes cysteine-mediated disulfide bond formation, leading to enzyme oligomerization and inactivation, while reduction of these cysteines restores NOCT to its monomeric, active form. In contrast, Xenopus laevis NOCT lacks these regulatory cysteines, does not oligomerize, and remains constitutively active. We show that oligomerization of mammalian NOCT occurs physiologically in the mitochondria while the comparatively highly reduced environment of the cytosol keeps NOCT in its monomeric conformation. Furthermore, sequence analysis reveals that mitochondrial NOCT emerges predominantly in mammals and coincides with the acquisition of redox-sensitive cysteines, highlighting a possible adaptation to oxidative metabolic environments. These findings suggest that mammalian NOCT functions as a redox sensor, dynamically modulating NADP(H) availability in response to oxidative stress. Understanding NOCT's evolution and regulation offers new insights into NADP(H) metabolism and its broader implications for oxidative stress.

Introduction

The pyridine dinucleotides NADP(H) and NAD(H) are among the most utilized cofactors/substrates in human biochemistry, playing roles in nearly 500 metabolic reactions [1]. The pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis has been linked to NADPH oxidase generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) [2,3]. NADPH is specifically essential for many reductive biosynthesis reactions producing fatty acids, nucleotides, and amino acids [4]. It also plays a crucial role in maintaining redox homeostasis by contributing its reducing power to antioxidants such as glutathione and thioredoxin [4]. NAD+ is the coenzyme for oxidoreductases and a substrate for the SIRT enzyme family and PARP family [[5], [6], [7]]. The levels of NADP(H) and NAD(H) are highly compartmentalized in the cell, and these subcellular pools are regulated independently, indicating strict enzymatic control over these metabolites [1,8].

One key regulator of NADP(H) metabolism is the phosphatase, Nocturnin (NOCT). NOCT catalyzes the removal of the 2’ phosphate from NADP(H) which produces NAD(H) [9,10]. NOCT is acutely induced by multiple signaling pathways [[11], [12], [13], [14]] and is also regulated by the circadian clock [15], suggesting a tightly controlled functional role in cellular metabolism. In mammals, NOCT is expressed widely throughout the body and is present in two forms, a cytosolic form and a mitochondrial form [9,16]. These two forms are expressed from a single gene (Noct) and are the result of two in-frame translation start sites. The mitochondrial form arises from the use of an upstream translation start site encoding a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), while the cytosolic form is generated by an alternative downstream start site [9,17]. Mitochondrial NOCT levels are rhythmic, peaking at the onset of the active phase in mice. Cytosolic NOCT is present throughout the day with weak, low amplitude rhythms [9].

Because NOCT activity contributes to NADP(H) pool size, it is not surprising that NOCT has been implicated in the oxidative stress response. Increased NOCT expression in mammalian cells exacerbates reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced cell death, whereas NOCT knockout cells were protected against this loss in cell viability under oxidative stress conditions [9]. This increased expression of NOCT resulted in damped NADP(H) levels, impairing the cells’ abilities to combat ROS. Since NADP(H) pools are discretely maintained in the cytosol and mitochondria [8], and NOCT is the only NADP(H) phosphatase in the latter, its regulation is particularly important for redox homeostasis.

The NOCT gene was first discovered in Xenopus laevis retina [18] and exhibits high amplitude rhythmic expression peaking at the onset of darkness. Unlike its mammalian counterpart, Xenopus NOCT (xNOCT) has been reported to be expressed exclusively in the eye, and there is no reported mitochondrial-targeting form [19]. This raises intriguing questions about the evolutionary divergence between Xenopus and mammalian NOCT, particularly regarding its subcellular localization and regulatory adaptations. It is unclear whether mitochondrial NOCT evolved in mammals to meet specific requirements for NADP(H) regulation in oxidative environments.

Here, we investigate the evolutionary divergence of NOCT between Xenopus and mammals. We uncover the emergence of novel regulation of NOCT in mammals and its potential role in redox regulation. We propose that mammalian NOCT has evolved additional regulatory mechanisms to control NADP(H) homeostasis in response to compartmentalized oxidative stress, offering new insights into its broader metabolic function.

Results

Xenopus NOCT displays amplified activity compared to mammalian NOCT in cell culture

Initially, we wanted to discriminate whether there were any differences between the human NOCT (hNOCT), mouse NOCT (musNOCT), and xNOCT structures and catalytic domains. musNOCT and hNOCT structures have been reported extensively [9,20,21] and are practically identical with an RMSD of 1.2 Å and a sequence identity of 93.4%. The AlphaFold-predicted model of xNOCT is highly conserved with an RMSD of 0.38 Å and a sequence identity of 81% compared to hNOCT (Fig. 1A). Because of their nearly identical nature, musNOCT and hNOCT will both be referred to as mammalian NOCT (mNOCT) throughout, and all amino acid numbering will reflect the position in the human protein if not specified differently. We first examined the effect of NOCT overexpression in U-2 OS cells, a human osteosarcoma cell line with an epithelial morphology, which aided in observations of mitochondrial and nuclear alteration. After cells were transfected with mNOCT and xNOCT, we observed that the xNOCT-expressing cells displayed abnormal morphology (Fig. 1C). The contacts between individual cells appeared as a procession, and mitochondria were distributed along the elongated connections. However, the cells contained discrete nuclei. This morphology was exacerbated upon treating the cells with 500 μM H2O2 for 30 min. While the mNOCT-expressing cells appeared to have some condensation of mitochondria, the xNOCT-expressing cells had mitochondrial and nuclear staining colocalization. The nuclei were no longer self-contained, and nuclear contents were dispersed throughout the cells. We also observed that transfection of xNOCT caused rapid cell death (∼<24 h) in both U-2 OS and HEK293 cells, while the mNOCT-expressing cells appeared healthy. Because the cells expressing xNOCT were visibly unhealthy, we performed a cell viability assay to quantify overall cell health compared to wild-type (WT) and mNOCT expressing cells. We performed these assays using HEK293 cells primarily due to ease of transfection to ensure the majority of cells were transfected. The expression constructs contained an mCherry-tag in-frame at the C-terminus of mNOCT and xNOCT so that protein levels could be assessed via fluorescence and Western blot (Fig. S1). The expression of xNOCT was equivalent to that of mNOCT, indicating that differences in these cells were solely due to functional changes in NOCT. Another observation is the presence of a larger molecular weight band in the mNOCT-expressing cells but not in the xNOCT cells. This band is the size of the precursor isoform of NOCT, which contains the mitochondrial targeting sequence and has been observed before [9]. Notably, since this band is not observed in the xNOCT-expressing cells, it is possibly indicative of the lack of a mitochondrial isoform of xNOCT. The dose-dependent treatment of these cells with H2O2 revealed that while overexpression of mNOCT reduces cell viability (as reported previously [9]), the overexpression of xNOCT dramatically increased ROS-mediated cell death at much lower concentrations of H2O2 (Fig. 1D). The EC50 for H2O2-mediated cell death decreased in xNOCT-expressing cells by 5.8-fold over those expressing mNOCT and 11.7-fold compared to WT cells. To determine whether this correlated with the NADP(H) phosphatase activity of NOCT, we measured NADP(H) levels in these cells and found it was significantly reduced in the xNOCT-expressing cells compared to mNOCT (Fig. 1E). This suggests that xNOCT is more active than mNOCT in cells and that reduction in NADP(H) pool size is detrimental to cellular health. This difference is not due to increased expression levels of xNOCT, as the quantified fluorescence intensity per cell for xNOCT and analysis of protein levels by Western blot, showed there were no significant differences (Fig. 1F–S1). This finding is intriguing considering xNOCT and mNOCT share structural and sequence homology and suggests that their regulation has diverged.

mNOCT but not xNOCT activity is regulated by redox

Despite having identical active sites, xNOCT and mNOCT displayed differing activity in cell culture. We sought to unravel this difference by measuring their catalysis in vitro and determine whether the oxidation state of NOCT affected their activity by introducing dithiotreitol (DTT), a reductant, to the reaction. Steady-state kinetics of mNOCT revealed robust activity and substrate specificity upon the addition DTT in the reaction buffer (Fig. 2A). Upon addition of DTT, the specificity constant, kcat/Km, dramatically increased from 3.8 mM−1 s−1 to 86 mM−1 s−1. Conversely, xNOCT activity was unaffected by the addition of reductant (Fig. 2B), reflected in the unchanged kcat/Km, so we hypothesized that mNOCT activity is regulated by reduction. DTT is a powerful reductant that is commonly used to protect thiols from oxidation and prevent intra- and intermolecular disulfide formation between cysteine residues. Therefore, we speculated that mNOCT forms inhibitory disulfide bonds. We have recently shown that NOCT exists as a stable monomer with a molecular weight of 42 kDa using analytical ultracentrifugation, although these experiments included 1 mM DTT [22]. Based on proximity in the folded protein, the most likely intramolecular pair to make a disulfide bond is C192 and C322 in the catalytic domain, which would stabilize the beta-sheet core (Fig. S2). However, generation of a C322S NOCT mutant did not result in an increase in activity, demonstrating that this cysteine pair is not responsible for the DTT effects on activity (Fig. 2C). This result suggests that DTT may be affecting intermolecular bonds rather than intramolecular disulfides. Intermolecular bonds can be assessed by an increase in the apparent molecular weight in the absence of reductant. Using purified recombinant protein, we performed SDS-PAGE with native samples and samples containing the canonical protein gel electrophoresis reductant, β-mercaptoethanol (BME). mNOCT formed higher order oligomers with a predominant band at 100 kDa in the absence of reductant (Fig. 2D). These oligomers disappeared, with only the monomer size observed, when BME was added to the buffer, suggesting they are indeed disulfide-mediated. Conversely, xNOCT was monomeric in both conditions. We then used an orthogonal approach to confirm these differences in oligomerization using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). To ensure a homogenous sample, NOCT was dialyzed in buffer with or without reductant prior to the experiment and centrifuged at high speed to remove protein aggregates. In the presence of 1 mM DTT, mNOCT elutes with a sharp peak, indicating a homogenous singular molecular weight consistent with its migration on SDS-PAGE (Fig. 2E, dashed line) and with our previous analytical ultracentrifugation results. However, when mNOCT is eluted with SEC buffer in the absence of reductant, the elution profile broadens and mNOCT elutes earlier, indicating a heterogenous mixture of higher molecular weight species (solid line). The apparent molecular weights of these species were calculated using a calibration curve, and the higher order oligomers elute well after the void volume, suggesting these multimers are not aggregated protein. While oligomerization was prominent for mNOCT in vitro, we sought to confirm this phenomenon in cell culture. Since the cell is a mostly reducing environment, we stressed the cells with exogenous H2O2 to produce a more oxidizing environment and expected mNOCT to oligomerize in the presence of ROS. As hypothesized, in cells that were not treated with H2O2, mNOCT was detected at the monomer molecular weight, while in H2O2 treated cells, mNOCT was present in additional higher molecular weight bands (Fig. 2G). The most prominent bands are estimated to be 43, 88, 250, and above 250 kDa. Because this experiment utilized non-reducing SDS-PAGE, this suggests these oligomers are disulfide-mediated. When these same samples were reduced with BME-containing sample buffer (which breaks disulfide bonds), the single monomer band was observed. This observation suggests that mammalian NOCT is regulated by disulfide formation in oxidating conditions in the cell.

NOCT oligomerization is isolated to the mitochondria under non-stress conditions

Because inhibition of mNOCT activity by oligomerization was prominent in oxidizing conditions in cells and in vitro, we considered whether compartmentalization of NOCT played a role in this regulation physiologically. It was imperative to determine whether this oligomerization occurred without exogenous stress since H2O2 is likely to cause unspecific aggregation of proteins. To examine this, we expressed C-terminally GFP-tagged mNOCT and xNOCT in U-2 OS cells. Because the expression of the exogenous constructs was robust, both mNOCT and xNOCT were visualized ubiquitiously throughout the cell and colocalized with the mitochondria (Fig. 3A). While mNOCT contains an endogenous N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence, xNOCT does not; therefore, this served as a model for the effect of xNOCT in the mitochondria. After fractionating the cells to obtain the cytosolic and mitochondrial pools, NADPH and NADP+ levels were quantified using the Promega bioluminescent assay kit. While mNOCT was more active in the cytosol with reduced levels of both NADPH and NADP+, xNOCT was significantly more active in the mitochondria (Fig. 3B and C). NADP+ levels in the mitochondria were equal for mNOCT and xNOCT (Fig. 3C). To determine whether the differing mNOCT activity levels were due to oligomeric regulation, non-reducing and reducing SDS-PAGE were performed to assess cytosolic and mitochondrial mNOCT. Indeed, mitochondrial mNOCT formed higher order oligomers while the cytosolic fraction remained as a monomer (Fig. 3D). With the addition of reductant in the form of BME, the samples were identical. For clarity, the multiple bands visualized on the blot correspond to mNOCT-GFP and MTS-mNOCT-GFP (which is in the process of being cleaved). HSP60 was used as the fractionation quality control. Quantification of the band volume above the monomeric mNOCT-GFP expected size (69 kDa) shows a robust increase in higher MW species (Fig. 3E). This data supports our hypothesis that mNOCT is physiologically regulated by environmental differences in the mitochondria which, in turn, inhibit mNOCT to retain higher NADPH pool sizes. Cysteines 136, 302, and 372 are involved in disulfide-mediated regulation of mammalian NOCT

To further understand the differences between xNOCT and mNOCT, we looked beyond the active-site for cysteines that could be involved in disulfide formation. There are 4 cysteines which are not conserved between xNOCT and mNOCT: C177, C136, C302, and C372 (Fig. 4A). These cysteines are replaced by serine in xNOCT except for C177, which is a tyrosine (Fig. 4B). While C372 in xNOCT is replaced by serine, the subsequent residue is a cysteine in xNOCT and an arginine in mNOCT, effectively shifting this cysteine one residue. The mNOCT sequence preceding and proceeding C177, C136, C302, and C372 are highly conserved in xNOCT (Fig. 4B). We performed site-directed mutagenesis to mutate each of the four cysteines in mNOCT and assayed their phosphatase activity in the absence of reductant. Compared to WT mNOCT, C322S (present in the core of the catalytic domain) and C177S did not produce a difference in activity. However, C136S, C302S, and C372S all exhibited significantly increased activity compared to WT mNOCT even when reductant was not present (Fig. 4C). Additionally, we repeated the cell viability assays to ascertain if these mutations would also result in decreased cell viability compared to mNOCT overexpression. Overexpression of the single cysteine mutant proteins resulted in similar susceptibility to H2O2 -mediated cell death to the WT mNOCT overexpression (Fig. 4D). Each cysteine mutant is still able to be activated further by DTT (Fig. S3), suggesting the increases in activity must work in concert to be observed physiologically. We hypothesized that this is due to a possible synergistic effect of these cysteines. However, when testing triple and quadruple mNOCT mutants, we found that this protein was highly unstable and could not be detected in cells. This was a surprise since the xNOCT protein lacking these same cysteines expressed well. This reinforced our use of xNOCT as our model for cysteine disruption, as evolution has optimized the stability of the protein concurrent with preservation of identical phosphatase activity as mNOCT. These data support that cysteines C136, C302, and C372 in mNOCT are redox-reactive and responsible for the decrease in activity in the absence of reductant and are therefore forming disulfides. Activation of mNOCT via glutathionylation of C136, C302, and C372

Proteins containing redox-reactive cysteines are prone to additional post-translational modifications such as glutathionylation, a physiological reductant within the cell [23]. S-glutathionylation is regulated by the ratio of reduced glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione disulfide (GSSG). Glutathionylation of redox-sensitive cysteines has been shown to affect transcription, signal transduction, enzymatic activity, and retroviral replication [24,25]. Because mNOCT activity is affected by the redox environment, both in cells and in vitro, we hypothesized that mNOCT may be glutathionylated and that this will affect its activity. Indeed, addition of 1 mM GSSG to mNOCT resulted in an increase of the kcat by 6.7-fold and a significant decrease in the Km as compared to no reductant (Fig. 5A). Because addition of GSSG to the reaction mixture caused an activation of NOCT, we sought to confirm whether the addition of GSSG was resulting in direct glutathionylation of the protein using intact mass spectrometry. The conjugation of one glutathione molecule can be detected by a mass increase of 305 Da. Intact mass spectrometry showed a peak at the correct molecular weight of the unmodified mNOCT at 41,209 Da. Three additional peaks corresponding to +305, +610, and +915 Da were detected, indicating there are 3 glutathionylated residues when purified mNOCT is incubated with 1 mM GSSG for 24 h (Fig. 5B). We further validated that glutathionylation of NOCT also occurs in cell culture. A vector containing mNOCT-3XFLAG was transfected into HEK293 cells after which the protein was pulled down using anti-FLAG beads. The Western blot was probed using anti-NOCT for confirmation followed by an anti-glutathione antibody. We subsequently determined whether the redox-sensitive residues C136, C302, and C372 were glutathionylated in mNOCT. The mutant proteins were subjected to intact mass spectrometry, and all had a peak at the correct molecular weight for unmodified NOCT of 41,194 Da (Cys to Ser mutation caused a reduction in 15 Da). The Cys to Ser mutants were incubated with 1 mM GSSG, and their intact mass spectra were obtained. We confirmed that C136, C302, and C372 are the three glutathionylated cysteines by a loss of 1 peak at 42,126 Da when each was mutated to serine (Fig. 5D–F). Other cysteine mutations such as C322S, which is conserved in xNOCT, showed no change in its intact mass spectrum when mixed with GSSG, demonstrating the specificity of this modification (Fig. S4).

We hypothesized that glutathionylation of these three cysteine residues is responsible for activation of mNOCT by GSSG (Fig. 5G). While WT mNOCT is activated by 1 mM GSSG, the three mutated proteins have lost this activation (Fig. 5G) and have basal activity that represent a partially active enzyme. These data indicate that C136, C302, and C327 are glutathionylated and this modification results in an increase in activity. We then examined whether GSSG affected the oligomerization of NOCT using a GSSG protection assay by non-reducing SDS-PAGE as utilized previously [26]. Recombinantly purified mNOCT was pretreated with GSSG prior to treating with 500 μM H2O2. The protein treated with H2O2 resulted in non-specific oligomerization while pretreatment with GSSG provided protection against this oligomerization (Fig. 5H). Higher than 1 mM GSSG did not cause additional protection suggesting 1 mM was sufficient for complete protection. Therefore, GSSG-induced glutathionylation protects against oligomerization when mNOCT is exposed to H2O2. Taken together, these data demonstrate that mNOCT activity can be regulated by the redox environment through glutathione-conjugated cysteines.

Evolutionary push for mitochondrial NOCT in mammals correlates with cysteine-mediated regulation

We then asked why mammalian NOCT evolved to be regulated by the redox environment. While the mitochondrial matrix is generally a reducing environment, the cytosol is significantly more reductive [27]. We have shown that mNOCT is in its monomeric, high activity state and that in the mitochondria, NOCT oligomerizes to form an inhibited conformation. It has been reported that mNOCT contains a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) and the expression of this localization is under circadian control [9,17]. Since NAD(P)(H) pools are highly compartmentalized, we examined whether non-mammalian NOCT also contains an MTS and if this coincided with the emergence of the redox-reactive cysteines. Using two independent MTS prediction engines, TargetP 2.0 [28] and MitoFates [29], we found Xenopus, Drosophila [30], and Danio rario (zebrafish) do not have a predicted MTS (Fig. 6A). It is important to note that the full cDNA sequence has been reported for zebrafish and Carassius auratus (goldfish) where NOCT splice variants were detected [31,32]. As in xNOCT, sequence alignment of Drosophila and zebrafish NOCT homologs reveal they also do not contain cysteines 136, 302, and 372, while other cysteines are conserved (Fig. 6B). To further understand this divergence amongst species, we performed a BLAST search for mNOCT using the NCBI database, resulting in 1000 NOCT homologs (including splice variants). We then analyzed the sequences using principal component analysis (PCA) and identified four predominant clusters of mNOCT homologs corresponding to the classes Mammalia, Aves, Amphibia, and Reptilia (Fig. 6C). The NOCT homologs in the Aves, Amphibia and Reptilia clusters did not contain all three of the redox-sensitive cysteines and did not show a significant number of MTS-containing sequences. In contrast, the mammalian cluster is comprised of NOCT homologs that include an MTS and the redox-sensitive cysteines. Discussion

Herein, we have shown that mammalian NOCT is regulated by disulfide-mediated oligomerization which decreases its enzymatic activity. In contrast, Xenopus NOCT is not subject to this process due to the absence of key cysteines (C136, C302, and C372), resulting in consistently higher phosphatase activity and lower NADPH levels. This suggests that the introduction of these thiols in mNOCT renders the enzyme sensitive to the redox environment, adding an additional layer of regulation. We showed that this inhibitory oligomerization occurs primarily in the mitochondria to keep NADPH pools elevated. NOCT is a member of the exonuclease/endonuclease/phosphatase (EEP) family which share a common catalytic mechanism with a large variety of substrates. One such member, APE1, has been recently reported to undergo redox-mediated regulation on sensitive cysteines [33] which suggests that this regulatory mechanism may also be common within the EEP family.

We have shown that mNOCT exists in a monomeric, high activity state in the cytosol, which is likely due to its highly reductive environment. We previously reported that NOCT also resides in the ER membrane through N-terminal myristoylation [9], suggesting a potential role in ER-associated regulation. NOCT does not contain an ER-translocation sequence, so it is likely tethered to the outer membrane of the ER, however additional experiments would need to be completed to confirm this hypothesis. The NADPH oxidase family (NOX) is a major source of ROS and have been found to associate with a variety of internal membranes such as the ER and mitochondria [2]. Therefore, future studies into NOCT in the ER fraction would be informative to the NADPH pools at the ER outer membrane. In the mitochondria, oxidative metabolism generates ROS creating a distinct redox environment that necessitates tight control over NADP(H) availability. Because NADPH levels are discrete to either the mitochondria or the cytosol [1,8], compartmentalized NOCT regulation may have evolved to fine-tune mitochondrial NADP(H) homeostasis. Based on our data, NOCT activity directly affects the NADP(H) pool size, and depletion of the pool is detrimental to cell viability. Our findings suggest that in mammals, NOCT has acquired an additional layer of redox-mediated regulation to meet these compartment-specific metabolic demands. However, it is important to note that this evolution is not restricted to mammals, as a few NOCT homologs in other classes are predicted to have a mitochondrial targeting sequence. For example, it was recently reported that in the migratory locust, L. migratoria, NOCT is localized to the mitochondria [34] and this localization may have evolved due to the high energy demands of insect development. While mammals are the primary class to contain mitochondrial NOCT, it is not exclusive to mammals, and deeper evolutionary analysis is required to track the emergence of the individual redox-sensitive cysteines of NOCT in different species.

Another curious result is the poor expression of the triple and quadruple cysteine mNOCT mutant, suggesting an apparent decreased stability of these constructs. Despite these mutations existing in xNOCT and producing a stable protein, we were unable to recapitulate that in mNOCT, presumably due to additional amino acid substitutions or nucleotide sequence preference. Additional mutational studies between the two NOCT homologs would need to be completed to determine the stability-inducing residues in xNOCT which could reveal additional insights into evolutionary aspects of NOCT.

Disulfide-mediated oligomerization has been reported to be reversible by disulfide reductases, such as thioredoxins (TXN) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and protein disulfide isomerases in the ER [35], and by the physiological reductant glutathione, suggesting that the redox environment can toggle this equilibrium. Glutathionylation has also been shown to prevent further oxidation of cysteine thiols to protect oxidized proteins from degradation [36,37]. We found that mNOCT is glutathionylated on the three redox sensitive cysteines: C136, C302, and C372 and that glutathione conjugation produces a rise in activity. In parallel, we found that glutathionylation of mNOCT provides protection against H2O2-mediated oligomerization, which is consistent with the reduction of mNOCT oligomers resulting in increased activity. Therefore, glutathionylation could be acting in two roles to activate NOCT and/or protect the protein from permanent oxidation. Since Glutaredoxin2 (GLRX2)-catalyzed glutathionylation is prevalent in some cell types when the mitochondrial matrix GSH/GSSG is decreased, this redox equilibrium is likely essential to maintaining NADPH levels by directly toggling NOCT activity. In the cases where GSH/GSSG does not decrease, the TXN2/TXNRD2 redox couple [38] may decrease leading to disulfide bond formation in NOCT. Many emerging reports on redox regulation of cysteines, as well as this study, support the idea that redox-based oligomerization of proteins is in fact physiological and there are evolutionary pushes for increased thiol-containing proteins to support complex cellular demands.

Regulation of NOCT is an important nodal point for control of the NADP(H) pool size, and NOCT's rhythmic expression indicates that NAD(P)(H) levels may also be rhythmically regulated. Previous work has shown that NOCT's levels and intracellular localization change throughout the day under control of the circadian clock and in response to other acute signals [8,39]. The management of NADP(H) and NAD(H) pool sizes (as opposed to redox ratios) is not well studied but there are suggestions that they are under circadian control. It has been shown that NAD(P)(H) and ROS, specifically H2O2, may influence the core CLOCK/BMAL1 transcription/translation feedback loop [[39], [40], [41]]. Additionally, there are reports that the enzymatic complex peroxiredoxin/thioredoxin/NADPH oscillates while metabolizing H2O2 in various tissues [42]. Others have suggested that NOCT modulates temporal and compartmental NADP(H) levels via its CLOCK-controlled expression [10,39]. As a result, NADP(H) levels could be affected synergistically by NOCT and ROS rhythmicity. Future studies on the effect of NOCT's rhythmic expression and oligomerization in the mitochondria would be the crux of understanding rhythmic NADP(H) levels in this organelle. While the emergence of mitochondrial NOCT shows robust rhythmicity in mammals specifically in the liver, the cytosolic Xenopus NOCT also has high amplitude rhythms in the retina. We speculate this could be a tissue-specific phenomena, but more tissue-specific circadian studies would need to be completed.

In summary, we propose a novel mechanism where mammalian NOCT has evolved to function as a redox sensor that dynamically regulates NADP(H) levels in response to oxidative stress. Under oxidative conditions, NOCT forms disulfide-linked oligomers, rendering the enzyme inactive and leading to increased NADP(H) availability, particularly in the mitochondria. As the cell oxidizes, mitochondrial matrix GSH/GSSG becomes oxidized, GLRX2 may catalyze the glutathionylation of NOCT cysteines 136, 302, and 372 and protect them from forming intermolecular disulfides leading to NOCT inactivation. Because of NOCT's function in the exacerbation of oxidative stress [9] and in diet-induced obesity [43], its tight redox regulation likely ensures NADPH homeostasis under fluctuating cellular demands. Our data support the idea that mammalian NADP(H) levels are directly regulated via NOCT redox sensing, solidifying NOCT's essential role in NADP(H) availability.

Methods

Cell culture

The U-2 OS cell line (ATCC) was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Six-well plates containing glass cover slips were treated with 100 μg/mL poly-d-lysine (Gibco A3890401) prior to seeding 100,000 cells. The cells were transfected 1 μg of the appropriate DNA (pCMV-NOCT-mCherry or pCMV-NOCT-GFP) using the lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (ThermoFisher #L3000001). The cells were allowed to transfect over 24 h and the appropriate wells were treated with 500 μM H2O2 for 30 min. The media was then removed and replaced with media containing 1X Mitotracker Red CMXRos (Cell Signalling #9082). The wells were then washed with room temperature Dulbecco's phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) (Gibco# 14190-144) and fixed using pre-warmed 4% paraformaldehyde (ChemCruz #SC-281692) for 30 min at room temperature (RT). The cells were then washed 3x with PBS and the coverslips were mounted onto slides using Vectashield Vibrance Antifade Mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories #H1800). The slides were allowed to dry overnight.

Human embryonic kidney epithelial cells (HEK293T) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were seeded in 6-well tissue culture plates (1.0 × 105) 24 h before transfection. The cells were transfected with 1 μg of a plasmid expressing mouse NOCT under control of the CMV promoter (pCMV-musNOC) using lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent for 48 h. At which time, the cells were lysed using 200 μL of RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris HCL pH 7.5, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% Na-Deoxycholate, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 50 mM NaF) containing protease inhibitors (Roche complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, Sigma Aldrich #11836170001) per well.

Microscopy

Slides were visualized using an Agilent BioTek Cytation 10 confocal imaging reader. Images were collected at 20× magnification. Fluorescence was detected using TRITC and DAPI confocal filters. The images were deconvolved with BioTek Gen 5 software.

For transfection efficiency determination, 200,000 cells per 35 mm well were transfected with the appropriate construct (2 wells per construct). After 24 h, the total and mCherry positive (using a Texas Red widefield filter) HEK293 cells were counted using an Agilent BioTek Cytation 10 widefield imaging reader. Brightfield and Texas Red signal images were deconvolved and quantified using the BioTek Gen 5 software. The fluorescence intensity is reported as mean intensity which is calculated by the Gen 5 software and defined as the average fluorescence intensity per pixel across all pixels in the image. All images were similar in confluency and equal in size. In each well, 2 images were taken and quantified to ensure counts were unbiased. The data was plotted using GraphPad Prism and a T-test was used to determine significance.

Western blots

Cell lysates were nutated at 4 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm. The samples (10 μg of protein) were run on BioRad TGX SDS-free gels (Bio-Rad #4568096) and transferred to a PVDF membrane. For those samples indicated as non-reducing SDS-PAGE, sample buffer was free of reductant. The membrane was blocked with 5% milk in Tris-Buffered Saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at RT. For detection of NOCT, an anti-rabbit polyclonal Nocturnin primary antibody (made in house) was diluted 1:10,000 in 3% sterile filtered BSA in TBST or for mCherry2 containing constructs, anti-RFP was diluted 1:2000 (Rockland #600-401-379). The secondary antibody was anti-rabbit HRP (Cell Signaling Technologies #7074S) diluted 1:2000 in 5% milk in TBST. Clarity max western ECL substrate (BioRad #1705062) was incubated on the blot for 5 min at RT before measurement of the chemiluminescent signal on a ChemiDoc MP imager. Quantification of molecular weight and volume was determined using BioRad Image Lab.

For detection of glutathionylated NOCT, HEK293T cells were transfected with pCMV-3X-Flag-mouseNOCT using lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent for 48 h. The cells were lysed as described above with lysis buffer including 0.1% SDS. Prior to pull-down using Anti-Flag Dynabeads M2 Magnetic beads (Millipore Sigma #M8823), 10 μL of input was saved. The captured protein was eluted by competitive binding with 100 μg/mL Flag-Peptide (Sigma #F4799). The samples were analyzed using Western blot (SDS-PAGE) described above with anti-NOCT rabbit antibody first then the blot was inactivated using 0.5% sodium azide. ECL reagent was used to confirm inactivation of the blot before proceeding to the next step and was washed with TBST 4 × 10 min. The inactivated blot was then probed with an anti-glutathione antibody (Virogen #101-A) to detect glutathionylation.

Expression and purification of NOCT

Recombinant NOCT was expressed and purified using a modified protocol similar to that described previously [22]. Briefly, full-length human NOCT is cloned in frame with an N-terminal His-tagged NusA protein separated by a TEV cleavage site in the pET22B vector. Two liters of LB + ampicillin were inoculated with 1.5 mL of an overnight starter culture and incubated at 37 °C while shaking at 250 rpm until the OD at A600 reached 0.7, at which point the flasks were removed from the incubator and cooled down to 25 °C. The flasks were then returned to a 20 °C incubator and induced with 100 μg/mL IPTG followed by an additional 18 h of growth, after which the cultures were harvested at 5000 rpm in a JLA 8.1 rotor for 15 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were lysed and homogenized using 40 mL lysis buffer per liter of culture containing 50 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1% tween-20 and 1x Roche complete protease inhibitor (Sigma #11697498001). The cells were homogenized using a 15 mL glass homogenizer until smooth then the lysate was sonicated for 4 × 30s with 30s breaks. The sonicated lysate was then ultracentrifuged for 50 min at 48 K rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was then applied to an equilibrated 1 mL HisTrap column (Cytiva #17524701) with 50 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT and 20 mM imidazole (Buffer A). After application, the column was washed with Buffer An until the A280 was below 0.1. The protein was eluted with Buffer A containing 300 mM imidazole. The peak A280 fractions were pooled and desalted using Cytiva PD 10 columns (Sigma #GE17-0851-01) after which the A280 was measured and TEV protease was added to the pool at a ratio of 50:1 (mg of NOCT: mg of TEV) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The digested sample was applied to the HisTrap column using the same buffers as above. The high A280 fractions during the wash step were collected for NOCT as the NusA chaperone contains the N-terminal his tag and remains bound. These wash fractions were concentrated to 500 μL and applied to a Superdex S200 10/300 GL column that was equilibrated with 50 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol and 1 mM DTT (SEC buffer). The resulting fractions containing the predominant peak were collected and pooled. The protein concentration was determined by its extinction coefficient at A280 (ε280 = 41.7 mM−1 cm−1) and size confirmed by SDS-PAGE.

Site-directed mutagenesis and cloning

The cysteine to serine mutations were introduced using the pET22B vector containing the NusA-human NOCT gene using the QuikChange II (Agilent#200521) site-directed mutagenesis kit with oligonucleotides from Sigma. BL21(DE3) competent cells were transformed with the resulting constructs (C136S NOCT, C302S NOCT or C372S NOCT). The coding regions of all expression plasmids were Sanger sequenced at Eurofins Genomics to verify the mutation and to ensure no other mutations were present.

musNOCT was replaced by xNOCT in the pCMV-mCherry vector using NEBuilder Hifi DNA Assembly. PCR was used to linearize the vector and also amplify the xNOCT insert. The fragments were assembled using the standard protocol using the NEBuilder Master Mix (NEB #E2621). The coding regions of all expression plasmids were Sanger sequenced at Eurofins Genomics to verify the insertion and to ensure no other mutations were present.

Measurement of NOCT phosphatase activity

NOCT activity was determined using the Malachite Green Phosphate Assay kit (Sigma #MAK307). The reaction buffer contained 100 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2 and 0.005 % triton-x. In steady state experiments, 1 mM DTT or GSSG was added to the reaction buffer. For samples without DTT, the protein was dialyzed with SEC buffer (not containing DTT) overnight at 4 °C with 2 buffer changes using SpectraPor membrane dialysis tubing with a MWCO of 14,000 Da (Fisher Sci # 08-670-3BB). NOCT (0.2 μM) was mixed with 0-4 mM NADPH for a final concentration of 0.1 μM and 0-2 mM, respectively. For steady state experiments, the reaction time was 0, 30, 60 s after which the reaction was quenched with 4 μL of 0.5 M EDTA. After a 30-min incubation of the assays with the working reagent, the reactions were read using a plate reader at 620 nm. The phosphate concentrations were quantified using a standard curve for 0-40 μM phosphate. The data were fit with the Michaelis-Menten equation to determine the steady-state kinetic parameters using GraphPad Prism. All activity determinations were completed in triplicate.

Activity of cysteine NOCT mutants were determined using 1 μM enzyme, 100 μM NADPH and the reaction proceeded for 5 min prior to quenching with 0.5 M EDTA.

Measurement of cellular NAD(P)(H) levels

Two 15-cm plates were plated with U-2 OS cells and allowed to grow to 80% confluency at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The plates were transfected with either pCMV-mNOCT-mCherry or pCMV-xNOCT-mCherry as described above. After 24 h, the cells were collected and processed using the Abcam mitochondrial isolation kit (Abcam #ab288084) and mitochondria were isolated using the reagent-based method. For verification of purified mitochondria, Western blot measurement of HSP60 (Santa Cruz #sc-59567) was used as the fractionation quality control. Finally, NADP/NADPH levels were quantified using the Promega bioluminescent assay kit (#G9071).

Size exclusion chromatography

A 10/300 Superdex S200 column was equilibrated with SEC buffer prior to the injection of 500 μL of 20 μM hNOCT in the absence and presence of 1 mM DTT. Samples without DTT was dialyzed with SEC buffer (not containing DTT) overnight at 4 °C with 2 buffer changes using SpectraPor membrane dialysis tubing with a MWCO of 14,000 Da (Fisher Sci # 08-670-3BB). Calibration was performed using Blue Dextran 2000 (Sigma #D4772), Lactate dehydrogenase (Roche #10127876001), Bovine Serum Albumin (Sigma #A9647), and RNase A (Invitrogen #12091-021). 5 mg/mL of each protein was loaded onto a 500 μL loop and applied using 500 mM Tris pH 8.0 and 100 mM KCl at 0.250 mL/min. The retention time for the peaks at A280 as a function of the log(MW) were used to generate the plot and apparent molecular weights were calculated using a linear regression in GraphPad Prism. The R2 for this fitting was 0.98.

Intact mass spectrometry for detection of glutathionylation

hNOCT and hNOCT cysteine mutants in storage buffer containing 1 mM DTT were incubated with 1 mM GSSG (instead of GSH to promote conjugation in the absence of oxidation as described previously [23,44]) for 24 h at 4 °C prior to sample submission. NOCT samples were analyzed by LC/MS, using a Sciex X500B QTOF mass spectrometer coupled to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II HPLC. Samples were injected onto a POROS R1 reverse-phase column (2.1 × 30 mm, 20 μm particle size, 4000 Å pore size) and desalted. Buffer A contained 0.1% formic acid in water and buffer B contained 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The mobile phase flow rate was 300 μL/min and the gradient was as follows: 0-3 min: 0% B, 3-4 min: 0-15% B, 4-16 min: 15-55% B, 16-16.1 min: 55-80% B, 16.1-18 min: 80% B. The column was then re-equilibrated at initial conditions prior to the subsequent injection.

The mass spectrometer was controlled by Sciex OS v.3.0 using the following settings: Ion source gas 1 30 psi, ion source gas 2 30 psi, curtain gas 35, CAD gas 7, temperature 300 °C, spray voltage 5500 V, declustering potential 135 V, collision energy 10 V. Data was acquired from 400 to 2000 Da with a 0.5 s accumulation time and 4 time bins summed. The acquired mass spectra for the proteins of interest were deconvoluted using BioPharmaView v. 3.0.1 software (Sciex) in order to obtain the molecular weights. The peak threshold was set to ≥5%, reconstruction processing was set to 20 iterations with a signal-to-noise threshold of ≥20 and a resolution of 2500.

Glutathione protection assay

1 ng of purified hNOCT was incubated with 0-10 mM reduced glutathione (GSH) for 10 min at room temperature prior to the addition of 500 μM H2O2. GSH was utilized in this assay to act as a physiological reductant since the protein is being exposed to ROS and this will form the thiyl intermediate. After an additional 10 min, non-reducing sample buffer was added to the samples and the samples were applied to a BioRad 4-20% TGX gel. Electrophoresis was carried out using standard SDS containing running buffer. After which, the gel was stained using Coomassie blue. Oligomers were quantified using BioRad Image lab.

Cell viability of NOCT mutants

HEK293 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 50,000 cells/well in complete media (DMEM, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin). After 24 h, the cells were transfected using 1 μg DNA and 1 μL lipofectamine 3000. The next day, with cells were trypsinized and plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 10,000 cells in 100 μL fresh media. After 24 h, the cells were treated with H2O2, which was diluted in complete media, and allowed to incubate for 24 h. The viability was determined using the Aquabluer Cell Viability Assay (Multitarget Pharm #6015). After 4 h of incubation at 37 °C, the fluorescence intensity (RFU) was read at 540ex/590em using a plate reader. The % viability was calculated using the formula: %viability = (RFUsample/RFUmedia) x 10 where RFUmedia was the fluorescence intensity of media alone. The data was fit with a non-linear regression (dose-dependent inhibition) using GraphPad Prism.

Principle component analysis of NOCT proteins from multiple species

The full-length human NOCT amino acid sequence was used as the input for a BLAST search using the NCBI protein blast server [45] with default parameters, except for “Max target sequences” which was set to 1000. The resulting 1000 sequences were saved in FASTA format and sequences that were not labeled as NOCT, sequences that were labeled “hypothetical”, “predicted” or “partial” were filtered out. Additionally amphibian NOCT sequences were added from uniport [46], as they were not found in the blast search, resulting in a total of 365 sequences. Next, the sequences were aligned using the MAFFT algorithm [47] with the –auto flag, and the aligned sequences were used to identify if the 3 cysteine amino acids were conserved, using an in-house python script comparing the amino acids at location 136,302 and 372 in hNOCT. The Target-2P webserver was used to predict if the protein included the mitochondrial transit peptide (MTP). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed by computing K-mer frequency vectors for each sequence using a sliding window approach to generate 3-mers (k = 3). A dictionary of all possible 3-mers was created, and each sequence was analyzed for the occurrence of these 3-mers. The counts were normalized by the total number of k-mers in each sequence. PCA was conducted using the PCA function from scikit-learn [48].
