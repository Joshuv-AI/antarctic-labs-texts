Introduction

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic is a classical nonparametric test statistic widely used for comparing an empirical distribution function with a reference distribution or for comparing two empirical distributions
[
2
]
. Since its inception, the KS statistic has played a crucial role in fields ranging from statistical hypothesis testing to model validation, particularly due to its simplicity and robustness under minimal assumptions about the underlying data distributions. The two primary forms of the KS test, the one-sample and two-sample versions, offer versatile tools for evaluating goodness-of-fit and detecting differences between distributions, respectively.

The one-sample KS statistic evaluates the supremum of the absolute differences between an empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) and a theoretical CDF. It provides a nonparametric method to assess whether a sample follows a specific distribution. The two-sample KS statistic, on the other hand, compares the empirical CDFs of two independent samples, allowing for the detection of distributional differences without requiring parametric assumptions. The statistic’s distribution under the null hypothesis is pivotal for deriving p-values and making statistical inferences.

While the KS statistic and its limiting distributions are well-established, there remain opportunities to refine its practical applicability and understanding, particularly under finite sample conditions. Existing results, such as the Smirnov-Birnbaum-Tingey theorem and the Gnedenko-Rvateva-Feller theorem, provide exact distributions for the one-sided KS statistics under specific conditions. However, these results are not as widely known or utilized, despite their potential utility in modern statistical analysis.

In this paper, we revisit the KS statistic with a focus on both one-sample and two-sample cases, considering both one-sided and two-sided variants. We derive exact probabilities for the supremum of the empirical process and provide a unified treatment of the KS statistic under various settings. Additionally, we explore the discrete nature of the hitting times of the normalized empirical process, offering insights into the practical computation of KS test p-values. Our work also includes a discussion of the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz-Massart (DKWM) inequality and its implications for constructing confidence bands for distribution functions
[
6
,
10
]
. Finally, empirical process theory is used to show the limit distribution of KS statistic when the true distribution has unknown parameters.

2
One-sided One Sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov Statistic

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be a random sample with continuous distribution function
F
and let
F_{n}
be its empirical counterpart, i.e.,

F_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\leq t).

Let

Z_{n}(t)=\sqrt{n}\left(F_{n}-F\right)(t)

be the normalized empirical process and
D_{n}^{+},D_{n}^{-}
be the one-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, i.e.,

D_{n}^{+}=\sup_{t}\frac{Z_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}=\sup_{t}(F_{n}(t)-F(t)),

D_{n}^{-}=\sup_{t}\frac{-Z_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}=\sup_{t}(F(t)-F_{n}(t)).

The following result seems to be very useful but not well-known.

Theorem 2.1
(Smirnov-Birnbaum-Tingey).

Let
\lambda\in[0,\sqrt{n}]
and
\epsilon=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}
. Set

\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\inf_{t}\{t:Z_{n}(t)\leq-\lambda\}

to be the first time that
Z_{n}(t)
hits
-\lambda
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon)^{n}+\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n(1-\epsilon)\rfloor}\binom{n}{j}\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)^{j-1}\left(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n}\right)^{n-j}.

(1)

If we further assume that
F(t)=t
, i.e.,
X
is uniform on
[0,1]
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\binom{n}{j}\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)^{j-1}\left(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n}\right)^{n-j}

(2)

for all
j=0,1,\cdots,\lfloor n-\lambda\sqrt{n}\rfloor.

Remark 1
: The assumption that
F(t)=t
can always be true in practice since the distributions of
Z_{n}(t)
and
D_{n}^{+}
do not depend on
F
. If we do not wish to make such an assumption, then we simply make an inverse transformation, i.e., set
\tau_{n}(\lambda)=F^{-1}\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)
.

Remark 2
: It is worth noting that
\tau_{n}(\lambda)
is discrete despite of a positive probability that
\tau_{n}(\lambda)=+\infty
. The process
Z_{n}(t)
is always decreasing if
t
is not a jump point (Figure
1
). At
t=X_{i}
, it has jump size
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. Hence, if
Z_{n}(t)
does not cross below
-\lambda
at
X_{(i)}
, the
i
-th order statistic, then it has to wait another
\frac{1}{n}
time to see whether it is below
-\lambda
or not. But the first possible hitting time is
\epsilon=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}
given that no
X_{i}
is less than
\epsilon
. In other words, the equation

F_{n}(t)-t=-\epsilon

has at most only finite number of solutions and they are of form
\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}
.

Fig 1:
Normalized Empirical Process
Z_{n}
with
\lambda=0.8

By simple observations, the event
\{\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\}
can happen only if there are
n-j

X_{i}
’s are greater or equal to
\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}
. The rest of
j

X_{i}
’s need to be placed carefully since at time
\epsilon+\frac{k}{n},k\leq j
we must have at least
k

X_{i}
’s being observed, otherwise the process
Z_{n}(t)
will reach
-\lambda
at some point. After some simple algebra, the problem of finding the distribution of
\tau_{n}(\lambda)
boils down to compute the following integral:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\int_{X_{1}}^{\epsilon+\frac{1}{n}}\cdots\int_{X_{j-1}}^{\epsilon+\frac{j-1}{n}}\int_{\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}}^{1}\int_{X_{j+1}}^{1}\cdots\int_{X_{n-1}}^{1}dX_{n}\cdots dX_{j+2}dX_{j+1}dX_{j}\cdots dX_{2}dX_{1}.

The multiple integral can be written as a product of two parts:

\left(\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\int_{X_{1}}^{\epsilon+\frac{1}{n}}\cdots\int_{X_{j-1}}^{\epsilon+\frac{j-1}{n}}dX_{j}\cdots dX_{2}dX_{1}\right)\left(\int_{\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}}^{1}\int_{X_{j+1}}^{1}\cdots\int_{X_{n-1}}^{1}dX_{n}\cdots dX_{j+2}dX_{j+1}\right).

The second part, by direct calculation, is

\frac{(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n})^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}

while the first part is more complicated. We need the following formula due to
Birnbaum and Tingey 1951
.

\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\int_{X_{1}}^{\epsilon+\frac{1}{n}}\cdots\int_{X_{j-1}}^{\epsilon+\frac{j-1}{n}}dX_{j}\cdots dX_{2}dX_{1}=\epsilon\frac{(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n})^{j-1}}{j!}.

Hence, the integral is equal to

\frac{\epsilon\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)^{j-1}\left(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n}\right)^{n-j}}{j!(n-j)!}.

But for each random sample of size
n
, there are
n!
equally-likely orderings, so we multiple the integral by
n!
and this is the probability that
\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}
:

\epsilon\binom{n}{j}\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)^{j-1}\left(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n}\right)^{n-j}.

Finally, the event
\{D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon\}
is the union of

\bigcup_{j=0}^{\lfloor n(1-\epsilon)\rfloor}\{\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\}

so that

\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon)=\epsilon\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n(1-\epsilon)\rfloor}\binom{n}{j}\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)^{j-1}\left(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n}\right)^{n-j}.

∎

One-sided Two Sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov Statistic

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}
be a random sample with continuous distribution function
F
and
Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{m},m\leq n
be another random sample with continuous distribution function
G
. Let
F_{n}
and
G_{m}
be the empirical counterparts of
F
and
G
, i.e.,

F_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\leq t),\ G_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(Y_{j}\leq t).

Theorem 3.1
(Gnedenko-Rvateva-Feller).

Let
D_{n,m}^{+}
be the one-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, i.e.,

D_{n,m}^{+}=\sup_{t}\left(F_{n}(t)-G_{m}(t)\right).

If
F=G
, i.e.,
X
and
Y
have the identical distribution, then the distribution of
D_{n,m}^{+}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n,m}^{+}\geq\delta\right)=\frac{\binom{n+m}{n-k+l}}{\binom{n+m}{n}}

(3)

where
\delta
is of form
\frac{k}{n}-\frac{l}{m}>0
for any
k=1,\cdots,n,\ l=0,1,\cdots,m
.

Consider a symmetric random walk starting at 0 that takes
n+m
steps, where each step is either
+1
or
-1
with equal probability.
After
n+m
steps, the walk arrives at position
n-m
, as there are
n
steps in the positive direction and
m
steps in the negative direction.
The total number of such paths is

\binom{n+m}{n}.

The event
\{D_{n,m}^{+}\geq\delta\}
is equivalent to the event where the path hits or exceeds the level
k-l
at some step,
where
\delta=\frac{k}{n}-\frac{l}{m}>0
for integers
k=1,\ldots,n
and
l=0,1,\ldots,m
.

By the
Reflection Principle

[
7
]
, for each path that hits or exceeds the level
k-l
and then returns to the final position
n-m
,
there exists a corresponding reflected path that instead arrives at position

2(k-l)+m-n.

This reflection is achieved by reflecting the path at the first point where it hits the level
k-l
. The number of such reflected paths, which end at
2(k-l)+m-n
, is given by

\binom{n+m}{n-k+l}.

Since each path is equally likely, the probability of the event
\{D_{n,m}^{+}\geq\delta\}
is the ratio of the favorable paths
to the total number of paths:

\mathbb{P}(D_{n,m}^{+}\geq\delta)=\frac{\binom{n+m}{n-k+l}}{\binom{n+m}{n}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1
(Feller
[
8
]
).

If
n=m
and
r=k-l
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n,n}^{+}\geq\frac{r}{n}\right)=\frac{\binom{2n}{n-r}}{\binom{2n}{n}}.

(4)

Corollary 2
.

If
n=m\rightarrow\infty
, we have asymptotically

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n,n}^{+}\geq\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\rightarrow e^{-r^{2}}.

(5)

Let
k=\lfloor\sqrt{n}r\rfloor
and
k+1=\lceil\sqrt{n}r\rceil
. The probability is lower and upper bounded by

\frac{n!n!}{(n-k-1)!(n+k+1)!},\ \frac{n!n!}{(n-k)!(n+k)!},

respectively. Since
k\rightarrow\infty
and
k/n\rightarrow 0
, the limit of the second term is

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n!n!}{(n-k)!(n+k)!}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac{1}{\frac{n}{k}-\frac{i-1}{k}}\right)^{-1}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(1+\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{-\sqrt{n}r}=e^{-r^{2}}

and the same holds for the first term.
∎
Remark 3
: An alternative proof uses the Stirling’s formula
[
7
]
:

k!\approx\sqrt{2\pi k}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}.

Two-sided One Sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov Statistic

The two-sided one sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic is defined as

D_{n}=\sup_{t}|F_{n}(t)-F(t)|

and we can always take
F(t)=t
to be the uniform distribution by Remark 1. The celebrated Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz-Massart (DKWM) inequality states that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}>\epsilon\right)\leq 2e^{-2\epsilon^{2}}

and it can be used to construct confidence bands for any continuous distribution functions.
Kosorok 2008
shows that the inequality also holds for distribution functions with jumps. Here is a sketch of the proof idea of the DKWM inequality
[
10
]
.

•

The key step is to provide a one-sided bound for
D_{n}^{-}
in section
2
since

\mathbb{P}(D_{n}>\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{+}>\epsilon)+\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon).

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor n(1-\epsilon)\rfloor}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\epsilon+\frac{i}{n})

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n(1-\epsilon)\rfloor}\binom{n}{j}\left(\epsilon+\frac{j}{n}\right)^{j-1}\left(1-\epsilon-\frac{j}{n}\right)^{n-j}.

where
\epsilon=\lambda/\sqrt{n}
. Hence, we have shown

•

For the other term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{+}>\epsilon)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}^{+}\leq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\left\{F_{n}(X_{(i)})-X_{(i)}\leq\epsilon\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{X_{(i)}\geq\frac{i}{n}-\epsilon\}\right)

where
X_{(i)}
is the
i
-th order statistic. The probability can be evaluated using a transformation
t_{i}=1-X_{(i)}
and by
Birnbaum and Tingey 1951
, it is the same as

1-\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon).

Hence, we have shown
\mathbb{P}(D_{n}>\epsilon)\leq 2\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon)
.

•

The local comparison is between the probability mass function of
\tau_{n}(\lambda)
and the density of first hitting time of a standard Brownian Bridge. That is,

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{n}(\lambda)=\epsilon+\frac{i}{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}f_{\lambda}\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{3}+\frac{i}{n}\right)

where

f_{\lambda}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}}s^{-3/2}(1-s)e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2s(1-s)}}

is the density of the first hitting time of a standard Brownian Bridge. The proof is based on concentration bounds for Bernoulli variables and laborious calculus.

•

The one-sided DKWM is proven by bounding a Riemann summation using mean value interpolation. That is,

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}^{-}>\epsilon\right)\leq e^{-2\epsilon^{2}}.

•

Put two parts together,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-2\epsilon^{2}}.

(6)

Two-sided Two Sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov Statistic

The two-sided two sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic is defined as

D_{n,m}=\sup_{t}|F_{n}(t)-G_{m}(t)|

and its limiting distribution is

\displaystyle\lim_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{nm}{n+m}}D_{n,m}>\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k-1}e^{-2k^{2}\lambda^{2}}.

(7)

This can be shown via Donsker’s theorem or empirical process theory
[
9
]
and more generally, we can define

D_{m,n}=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}\lVert F_{n}-G_{m}\lVert_{\mathcal{F}}

where
\lVert\cdot\lVert_{\mathcal{F}}
is supreme over the function class
\mathcal{F}
but the limiting distribution may depend on
F=G
the common distribution function.

6
When the Target Distribution Depends on Unknown Parameters

In some scenarios, the i.i.d. sample
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
may come from
F_{\theta}
where
\theta
is a vector of unknown parameters. For example,
F_{\theta}
can be the normal distribution and
\theta=(\mu,\sigma^{2})^{T}
contains mean and variance. We have to first estimate
\theta
via, say, maximum likelihood estimation (or M-estimation in general). Define

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left(F_{n}(t)-F_{\theta}(t)\right)

(8)

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t,\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left(F_{n}(t)-F_{\widehat{\theta}}(t)\right)

(9)

and the problem is to find the limiting distribution of
Z_{n}(t,\widehat{\theta})
. Taking measurability for granted (M-estimation is in general not measruable so we have to use the concept of outer measurability here), we have the following heuristic argument due to
Babu and Rao 2004
, see also
[
5
,
11
,
4
]
.

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t,\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=Z_{n}(t,\theta)+\sqrt{n}\left(F_{\theta}(t)-F_{\widehat{\theta}}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=Z_{n}(t,\theta)+\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(t)}{\partial\theta}\sqrt{n}(\theta-\widehat{\theta})+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}(1)

\displaystyle=Z_{n}(t,\theta)+\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(t)}{\partial\theta}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\theta}(X_{i})+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}(1)

where we have assumed that

\sqrt{n}(\theta-\widehat{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\theta}(X_{i})+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}(1)

and
\psi_{\theta}
is called the “influence function" and
\mathbb{E}\psi_{\theta}(X_{i})=0
. Then the weak limit can be derived from the joint limit of
(Z_{n}(\cdot,\theta),\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\theta}(X_{i}))
. The Donsker theorem states that
Z_{n}(\cdot,\theta)\Rightarrow B(F(\cdot))=B\circ F
where
B
is a standard Brownian bridge. The usual central limit theorem states that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\theta}(X_{i})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma(\theta))
where
\Sigma(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\psi_{\theta}(X_{1})^{\otimes 2}
. The covariance can be computed from

Cov(\mathbb{I}(X_{1}\leq t),\psi_{\theta}(X_{1}))=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\psi_{\theta}(x)F_{\theta}(dx):=h(t,\theta).

Hence, we have the finite-dimensional weak convergence. The weak convergence of the stochastic process
Z_{n}(t,\widehat{\theta})
is shown after a tightness argument
Babu and Rao 2004
and the limit is a mean-zero Gaussian process
Z(\cdot,\theta)
with covariance

\displaystyle Cov\left(Z(s,\theta),Z(t,\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=F_{\theta}(s\wedge t)-F_{\theta}(s)F_{\theta}(t)

(10)

\displaystyle-h(t,\theta)^{T}\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(s)}{\partial\theta}-h(s,\theta)^{T}\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(t)}{\partial\theta}+\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(s)}{\partial\theta}^{T}\Sigma(\theta)\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(t)}{\partial\theta}.

In a somewhat fancier notation, we write

\displaystyle Z_{n}(\cdot,\widehat{\theta})\Rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{\theta}(\cdot)-\mathbb{G}_{\theta}\left[\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(\cdot)}{\partial\theta}^{T}\psi_{\theta}(X)\right]

(11)

where
\mathbb{G}_{\theta}(\cdot)=B\circ F_{\theta}(\cdot)
is the composition of standard Brownian bridge process and underlying true distribution function
F_{\theta}
, and

\mathbb{G}_{\theta}\left[\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(t)}{\partial\theta}^{T}\psi_{\theta}(X)\right]=\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(t)}{\partial\theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{\theta}(x)B(F(dx)).

To sum up, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
(Durbin-Babu-Rao).

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be a random sample with common distribution function
F_{\theta}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Let
\widehat{\theta}
be an asymptotically linear estimator of
\theta
with influence function
\psi_{\theta}
. Define the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic as

D_{n}(\widehat{\theta})=\sup_{t}|F_{n}(t)-F_{\widehat{\theta}}(t)|.

Then asymptotically, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}D_{n}(\widehat{\theta})>\epsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t}|Z(t,\theta)|>\epsilon)

where
Z(\cdot,\theta)
is a mean zero Gaussian process with covariance specified in
10
. Such probability can be approximated by bootrstrapping or Monte Carlo simulation.
