Epidemiology of appendicitis in Ecuador, 2004–2024: a national time-series analysis of incidence, trends, geographic variation, and case-fatality rates
Authors: Esteban Ortiz-Prado, Micaela Vergara, María Gabriela Dávila Rosero, Jorge Vasconez-Gonzalez, Paul Astudillo Neira, Juan S Izquierdo-Condoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAppendicitis is a common surgical emergency and a leading cause of gastrointestinal hospital admission in Ecuador. However, its long-term national patterns have not been comprehensively characterized. We aimed to describe appendicitis-related hospital discharge rates, temporal and geographic variation, and in-hospital case-fatality in Ecuador during 2004–2024.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide descriptive ecological study using publicly available, anonymized hospital discharge records from the Ecuadorian National Institute of Statistics and Censuses. The unit of temporal and geographic analysis was the canton-year. Discharges with a primary ICD-10 diagnosis of K35–K37 were included. Annual population denominators were used to calculate crude and age- and sex-specific hospital discharge rates per 100,000 population. In-hospital case-fatality was calculated as deaths among appendicitis-related discharges. Comparisons were descriptive and unadjusted.FindingsWe identified 717,639 appendicitis-related discharges, including 378,080 (52.7%) among males and 339,559 (47.3%) among females, and 1179 in-hospital deaths (case-fatality 0.164%). Recorded ethnicity was Mestizo in 602,817 (84.0%), Indigenous in 12,918 (1.8%), other in 9329 (1.3%), White in 3588 (0.5%), and Afro-Ecuadorian in 2153 (0.3%); 23,682 (3.3%) were recorded in additional categories, including Montubio, and 63,152 (8.8%) were unknown or unrecorded. Discharge rates increased until 2018–19, declined in 2020, and only partially recovered by 2024. Rates were highest mainly among adolescents and young adults, and crude case-fatality varied across health-system characteristics.InterpretationAppendicitis represents a substantial surgical burden in Ecuador. The observed temporal, geographic, and health-system variation supports strengthening timely diagnosis, referral pathways, and equitable surgical access. Findings are ecological and should not be interpreted causally.FundingNo funding.Keywords: Appendicitis, Epidemiology, Ecuador, Incidence, Case-fatality, Geographic variation, Fatality

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar for studies published between database inception and April 2026, using the terms “appendicitis”, “epidemiology”, “incidence”, “case fatality”, “hospital discharge”, “Ecuador”, and “Latin America”, without language restrictions. Globally, the Global Burden of Disease studies have documented a rising incidence of appendicitis between 1990 and 2021, with the steepest increases in newly industrialized and middle-income regions and the highest age-standardized incidence and disability-adjusted life year rates in Andean Latin America. Most national and regional analyses, however, come from high-income settings, where case-fatality is low and relatively uniform. For Ecuador, the available evidence was limited to a single undergraduate thesis reporting basic national counts and crude rates for a shorter period (2004–2019), together with small, single-center or cross sectional reports. No prior study had characterized long-term national trends, age- and sex specific rates, provincial and cantonal variation, health-system and insurance-related differences in case-fatality and length of stay, or the burden of disease for appendicitis in Ecuador.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive national characterization of appendicitis in Ecuador to date, based on 717,639 hospital discharge records and 1179 in-hospital deaths over 21 years (2004–2024). It extends previous work by adding age- and sex-specific hospital discharge rates, provincial and cantonal geographic variation, differences across healthcare sectors, managing institutions, insurance categories, and discharge specialties, and a burden-of disease estimate (DALYs). Hospital discharge rates more than doubled before the COVID-19 pandemic, declined sharply in 2020, and had not returned to pre-pandemic levels by 2024. Although overall in-hospital case-fatality was low (0.164%), it was unevenly distributed, with higher unadjusted case-fatality in the public sector, among uninsured, foreign, and Indigenous patients, and in selected non-surgical specialties—patterns that resemble those reported in other low- and middle-income countries and contrast with the lower, more uniform case-fatality of high-income settings.

Implications of all the available evidence

Even where overall appendicitis outcomes are favorable by international standards, substantial within-country disparities can persist and represent actionable targets for health systems. In Ecuador, the findings support prioritizing timely surgical access and efficient referral in high burden provinces and underserved populations, expanding insurance coverage, and strengthening diagnostic and emergency–care pathways. Because all analyses were descriptive, ecological, and unadjusted, the reported associations are hypothesis-generating; they should be confirmed in future studies that link hospital discharge records with clinical, surgical, and mortality certification data to distinguish disease severity from health-system delays and to identify avoidable deaths.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis is the most common abdominal surgical emergency worldwide.1 It is typically initiated by obstruction of the appendiceal lumen, which leads to increased intraluminal pressure, impaired venous outflow, ischemic injury, bacterial overgrowth, and subsequent neutrophilic inflammation of the appendiceal wall.2 Its etiology varies by age: in children, lymphoid hyperplasia is the most common underlying cause, whereas in adults it is more often associated with fecaliths, infection, or, less commonly, tumors.3, 4, 5 Established risk factors for acute appendicitis therefore include luminal obstruction, young age (peak at 10–30 years), male sex, and dietary patterns such as low fiber intake.6 According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2019, there were 17,698,765 incident cases and 672,203 prevalent cases of appendicitis worldwide in 2019.1 Over the period 1990–2019, the global burden of appendicitis increased substantially, with marked rises in both absolute hospital discharge rates and age-specific hospital discharge rates, although disability-adjusted life-years declined during the same period.1,7 Acute appendicitis also shows a slight male predominance, with an estimated lifetime risk of 8.6% in males compared with 6.7% among females.8 The highest hospital discharge rates and prevalence occur in the 15–19-year age group, after which rates decline progressively with age.9,10

At the global level, appendicitis rates have increased rapidly in newly industrialized countries across Asia, the Middle East, and South America.1,11 Latin America and the Caribbean have reported the highest incidence of acute appendicitis among world regions, and within this context, Andean Latin America showed the highest age-specific incidence and disability-adjusted life-year rates worldwide in 2019.7

Only one undergraduate thesis conducted by our group has previously examined this topic at the national level, reporting basic national counts and crude rates of acute appendicitis for a shorter period (2004–2019).12 That work was limited to overall national figures and did not address geographic variation at the provincial and cantonal levels, age- and sex-specific rates, health-system and insurance-related differences in case-fatality, length of stay, or burden-of-disease estimates. The present study substantially extends that initial description by covering a longer period (2004–2024) and by characterizing these additional temporal, geographic, demographic, health-system, and burden dimensions at the national scale. In addition, a small number of local reports suggest that appendicitis has consistently ranked among the leading causes of hospital morbidity, in some years exceeding 200 hospital discharges per 100,000 inhabitants and disproportionately affecting school-age children and adolescents among pediatric surgical patients.13 This pattern is consistent with hospital-based evidence from Ecuador: in one pediatric surgical series, acute abdomen accounted for 32.7% of admissions, with acute appendicitis predominating, particularly among adolescents.14

Despite its frequency, comprehensive national analyses of long-term trends, age- and sex-specific patterns, geographic variation across Ecuadorian regions, healthcare sector differences, and ethnic disparities remain scarce.15 The available reports are limited by small sample size, restricted geographic scope, cross-sectional design, or outdated periods of observation, and therefore do not capture the national distribution and hospital burden of appendicitis over the past two decades.15

Ecuador has experienced substantial socioeconomic development over recent decades. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, the country's Sociodemographic Index (SDI) increased from 0.518 in 1990 to 0.66 in 2021, reflecting improvements in income, education, and fertility patterns.16 Despite this progress, important subnational disparities in healthcare access and outcomes persist, particularly between urban and rural areas and across different regions of the country.17 These contextual factors provide an important framework for interpreting the distribution and hospital burden of surgical conditions such as appendicitis.

To address these gaps, we conducted a nationwide ecological study using appendicitis-related hospital discharge and mortality data from the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INEC) and the Ministry of Public Health of Ecuador from 2004 to 2024. The primary objective was to describe national, temporal, geographic, and demographic patterns of appendicitis-related hospital discharge rates and in-hospital case-fatality, and to estimate the associated disease burden (DALYs), over a 21-year period. As a secondary objective, we examined how these indicators varied across health-system characteristics (healthcare sector, managing institution, insurance status, and discharge specialty) to identify high-risk groups and potential disparities in access to timely surgical care that may inform prevention, diagnosis, and management strategies in Ecuador.

Methods

Study design

This was a nationwide ecological study based on annual aggregated hospital discharge and mortality data for appendicitis in Ecuador from 2004 to 2024. The primary unit of analysis for temporal and geographic comparisons was the canton-year. Additional stratified analyses were performed by aggregating discharge records according to demographic and health-system characteristics. The analysis was conducted at an aggregate level: although the source registry is organized at the episode level (one de-identified record per hospitalization), records lack personal identifiers and could not be linked across admissions, and key clinical covariates (disease severity, perforation status, comorbidity) were not captured. Records were therefore aggregated by demographic, geographic, and health-system categories; rates and burden were estimated at the population level, and comparisons were unadjusted because the data did not contain the clinical variables required for confounder adjustment.

Setting

Ecuador is located in northwestern South America and is administratively divided into 24 provinces and 221 cantons. The country comprises four major natural regions: the Coastal region, the Andean region, the Amazon region, and the Insular region represented by the Galápagos Islands.

Population, case definition and eligibility criteria

According to Ecuador's 2022 Population and Housing Census, the country has a population of approximately 18 million inhabitants. In terms of self-identified ethnicity, 77.5% of the population is Mestizo, 7.7% Montubio, 7.7% Indigenous, 4.8% Afro-Ecuadorian, and 2.2% White. We included all hospital discharge records with a primary ICD-10 diagnosis code of K35 (acute appendicitis), K36 (other appendicitis), or K37 (unspecified appendicitis). The INEC hospital discharge registry is an administrative census of all inpatient discharges from public and private hospitals nationwide; outpatient and non-hospitalized cases are not captured. In routine clinical practice in Ecuador, acute appendicitis is generally diagnosed through clinical assessment supported by laboratory testing and imaging when clinically indicated, followed by surgical evaluation. When appendectomy is performed, the resected appendix is generally submitted for histopathological examination, although institutional practices and access to pathology services may vary. However, the INEC hospital discharge registry contains neither operative findings nor histopathological results. Case ascertainment in this study was therefore based exclusively on the primary ICD-10 discharge diagnosis (K35–K37), and we could not determine which episodes were histologically confirmed or identify possible negative appendectomies separately. Age was categorized into 5-year bands (<1, 1–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, and ≥80 years). Deaths were defined as in-hospital deaths occurring among the included appendicitis-related hospital discharge records. Because the database is episode-based, the analysis reflects appendicitis-related hospital discharges rather than unique individuals.

Variables and outcomes

We assessed four primary outcome metrics. First, the appendicitis-related hospital discharge rate per 100,000 population, used as a proxy for incidence and reported as crude and age- and sex-specific rates. Sex was recorded in the INEC administrative registry as a binary variable, male or female. The database did not include information on gender identity; therefore, all sex-disaggregated analyses refer to recorded sex rather than gender. Ethnicity was based on the self-identified category recorded in the INEC administrative registry. Categories included Mestizo, Montubio, Indigenous, Afro-Ecuadorian, White, other, and unknown or not recorded, according to the categories available in the source datasets. Ethnicity was treated as a sociocultural construct rather than a biological characteristic. Analyses by ethnicity were restricted to records with available information, and missing or unknown values were reported separately.

Second, the in-hospital case-fatality proportion, defined as the number of in-hospital deaths divided by the total number of appendicitis-related discharges in the same stratum, expressed as a percentage. Third, the mean length of hospital stay, in days. Fourth, the burden of disease, summarized as disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). DALYs represent the total healthy life years lost and are the sum of two components: years of life lost to premature mortality (YLLs), defined as the number of deaths multiplied by the standard remaining life expectancy at the age of death; and years lived with disability (YLDs), defined as the number of cases multiplied by the average disease duration and the corresponding disability weight. Secondary, stratified analyses examined variation by age group, sex, self-identified ethnicity, insurance status, healthcare sector, managing institution, discharge specialty, geographic location (province and canton), and temporal factors (month and day of admission). Analyses involving a specific variable were restricted to records with non-missing information for that variable, and the corresponding denominators are reported for each stratified analysis.

Data sources

Two data sources were used. Appendicitis-related hospital discharge and general national mortality records (2004–2024), both obtained from the national Hospital Discharge and Vital Statistics registries of the Ecuadorian National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INEC) (https://www.ecuadorencifras.gob.ec/defunciones-generales). Annual population denominators, by age and sex, were derived from official INEC national population projections; crude rates were expressed per 100,000 population. In-hospital deaths were ascertained directly from the vital-status field (alive or deceased at discharge) recorded within each episode of the hospital discharge registry; the two registries were therefore not individually linked, and in-hospital case-fatality was derived entirely from the discharge records rather than from record linkage with the general mortality registry. National SDI values for 1990–2021 were obtained from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Geographic analyses were based on the patient's place of residence at the province and canton levels, and cantons were additionally interpreted within Ecuador's four major natural regions. Altitude was available for descriptive contextualization only and was not modeled as an explanatory variable.

Statistical analysis

Rate estimation

Crude and age- and sex-specific hospital discharge rates were calculated per 100,000 population. Annual crude hospital discharge rates were calculated by dividing the number of appendicitis-related discharges recorded in each calendar year by the corresponding annual INEC population estimate and multiplying by 100,000. Annual age- and sex-specific rates were calculated using the corresponding age-, sex-, and year-specific INEC population denominators. For analyses summarized across the entire 2004–2024 period, numerators and denominators were aggregated across the corresponding calendar years; no single average population denominator was applied to the full study period.

Comparative analyses

Two distinct comparisons were undertaken. First, to compare male and female age-specific hospital discharge rates, we used a paired t-test in which the 18 age bands served as the matched units: each band contributed one male and one female rate, and pairing by age stratum accounts for the dependence between male and female rates within the same age group. Second, to compare the in-hospital case-fatality proportion between independent groups such as public vs private sector, insured vs uninsured, and non-national vs Ecuadorian patients, we used the χ2 test of independence, or Fisher's exact test when any expected cell count was below five, and estimated crude risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (normal-approximation [Katz] method on the log scale). Categorical comparisons across more than two independent groups (e.g., ethnicity, managing institution) were also evaluated with the χ2 test. These two approaches were applied to different data structures—paired age-stratified rates vs independent group proportions—and were not used interchangeably. All comparisons were descriptive or bivariate; no multivariable adjustment was performed, and all reported risk ratios and group comparisons are unadjusted.

Temporal association with SDI

To examine whether temporal changes in appendicitis paralleled national development, the annual national discharge rate was correlated with the annual SDI (2004–2021) using Pearson and Spearman coefficients, with a sensitivity analysis on first differences to remove the shared secular trend. Comparisons between the pre-pandemic period and the years affected by COVID-19 were descriptive. No interrupted time-series model, counterfactual projection, or formal causal effect estimate was used to quantify the pandemic-related disruption.

Burden of disease

DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs were estimated using a GBD-based framework adapted to national administrative data rather than the full GBD modeling platform. YLLs were calculated by multiplying appendicitis deaths in each age–sex group by the standard remaining life expectancy at the age of death from the GBD reference life table. For YLDs, we applied a disability weight of 0.20 for the acute phase of appendicitis, consistent with Global Burden of Disease Study estimates for moderate abdominal pain,1,18 together with a mean disability duration of 0.038 years (approximately 14 days), based on the average length of hospital stay among survivors plus a short post-discharge recovery period; YLDs were obtained as the number of cases multiplied by the disability weight and the disability duration. DALYs were obtained as the sum of YLLs and YLDs and were aggregated by year, sex, age group, and geographic unit.

Choropleth maps were produced in ArcGIS at the cantonal level, with provincial boundaries overlaid. Cantonal rates were displayed using predefined, manually set class intervals (hospital discharge rates in 50-per-100,000 increments, from 0–50 to >400; mortality rates in 0.25-per-100,000 increments, from 0–0.25 to >1), with darker shades representing higher rates. No spatial interpolation (e.g., kriging) was applied; rates were mapped directly to administrative cantonal units. No minimum case threshold was imposed, but cantons with very small populations and few cases were interpreted with caution because their rate estimates are unstable.

All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 28, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Figures were finalized in PRISMA chart, Adobe Illustrator, and BioRender.

Ethics approval

The study used anonymized, publicly available secondary administrative data and involved no direct contact with participants. The protocol was reviewed by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universidad de Las Américas (CEISH-UDLA; approval code 2023-EXC-008), which determined that the study was exempt from full ethical review. Individual informed consent was therefore not required.

The burden-of-disease estimates are reported in accordance with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER), and the observational analyses follow the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidance. The completed STROBE checklist is provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Role of the funding source

This study received no specific funding. Therefore, no funder had any role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, writing of the report, or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

Between 2004 and 2024, a total of 717,639 appendicitis-related hospital discharge records were identified in Ecuador. Of these, 716,460 ended in live discharge and 1179 in in-hospital death, yielding an overall in-hospital case-fatality proportion of 0.164%. Males accounted for 378,080 discharge records (52.7%), whereas females accounted for 339,559 (47.3%).

Annual hospital discharge rates increased substantially over the study period in both sexes. In males, rates rose from 128.8 per 100,000 population in 2004 to a peak of 289.6 per 100,000 in 2019. In females, rates increased from 136.3 per 100,000 in 2004 to a peak of 246.7 per 100,000 in 2018. This represented more than a twofold increase over the pre-pandemic period (p < 0.001). In 2020, hospital discharge rates declined to 239.6 per 100,000 in males and 205.4 per 100,000 among females, corresponding to reductions of 17.3% and 20.7%, respectively (Fig. 1).

Bars show the corresponding annual in-hospital case-fatality proportions on the right axis.

When the pre-pandemic period (2004–2019) is considered separately, rates rose almost monotonically to their 2019 peak. Relative to this pre-pandemic trajectory, rates fell sharply in 2020 and only partially recovered thereafter, remaining below the 2019 peak through 2024 (221.3 per 100,000 among males and 200.5 among females). This descriptive pattern was consistent with a pandemic-period reduction in appendicitis-related hospital discharges, although the study was not designed to estimate a counterfactual trend or causal pandemic effect.

Burden of disease of appendicitis in Ecuador

Between 2004 and 2024, appendicitis generated an estimated 46,530 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in Ecuador, equivalent to approximately 2216 DALYs per year. Most of this burden was attributable to premature mortality, with 37,728 years of life lost (YLLs), representing 81.1% of total DALYs, compared with 8802 years lived with disability (YLDs; 18.9%). Overall, the burden was slightly higher in males (25,359 [54.5%]) than in females (21,171 [45.5%]). The burden was also shaped by the large number of hospitalizations, the concentration of deaths in younger age groups, and persistent differences across seasons, insurance categories, and health-care settings.

Age and sex differences

Age-specific hospital discharge rates differed between males and females across the age groups examined (exploratory paired t test, p < 0.001). Rates were higher among males in 16 of the 18 age groups, with an overall male-to-female rate ratio of 1.66. The largest absolute differences were observed in the 30–34-year age group (+53.3 per 100,000) and the 10–14-year age group (+52.9 per 100,000). The male-to-female rate ratio ranged from 1.33 in the 5–9-year group to 0.90 in the 15–19-year group. The only age group in which the hospital discharge rate was higher among females was 15–19 years (343.4 vs 308.9 per 100,000; p = 0.012; Fig. 2).

Rates remained elevated between 10 and 34 years of age (>240 per 100,000). Hospital discharge rates were highest in males aged 25–29 years (313.7 per 100,000) and in females aged 15–19 years (343.4 per 100,000).

Sociodemographic factors

Among records with available urban–rural information, 693,957 (96.7%) appendicitis-related hospital discharges occurred in urban facilities and 23,682 (3.3%) occurred in rural facilities. The in-hospital case-fatality proportion was higher in urban than in rural settings (0.138% vs 0.055%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.148).

Of the 717,639 appendicitis-related hospital discharges, 602,817 (84.0%) were recorded as Mestizo, 12,918 (1.8%) as Indigenous, 9329 (1.3%) as other, 3588 (0.5%) as White, and 2153 (0.3%) as Afro-Ecuadorian. An additional 23,682 records (3.3%) corresponded to other registry categories, including Montubio, whereas ethnicity was unknown or not recorded in 63,152 records (8.8%). The crude in-hospital case-fatality proportion was 0.153% in the Mestizo category and 0.275% in the Indigenous category. Within the Mestizo category, case-fatality was similar among males and females (0.150% vs 0.157%). Within the Indigenous category, crude case-fatality was 0.320% among females and 0.228% among males; a similar pattern was observed among records with unknown ethnicity (0.398% vs 0.329%).

Health-system factors associated with length of stay and in-hospital case-fatality

The public sector managed most appendicitis-related hospital discharges (482,971 [67.3%]). In public hospitals, the mean length of stay among survivors was 4 days in males and 3 days in females, increasing to 9 and 8 days, respectively, among those who died, and public hospitals showed the highest in-hospital case-fatality proportions (0.18% in males and 0.19% in females). Private for-profit facilities had shorter stays among survivors (2 days in both sexes) and lower case-fatality proportions (0.117% in males and 0.118% in females), whereas private non-profit institutions handled a smaller share of discharges and showed the longest stays among fatal cases (up to 17 days in males) despite comparatively low case-fatality. Overall, treatment in a public hospital was associated with a higher unadjusted risk of in-hospital death than private care (0.192% vs 0.115%; unadjusted RR = 1.67, 95% CI 1.42–1.96; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Case-fatality also varied by facility type and managing institution. Specialized hospitals had a higher case-fatality proportion than the reference group of pediatric, day-care, and other facilities (0.200% vs 0.116%; RR = 1.72; p < 0.001). Compared with the Ministry of Public Health, the Junta de Beneficencia de Guayaquil showed the highest case-fatality proportion (0.519%; RR = 3.35), followed by the Ministry of Justice (0.268%; RR = 1.73), whereas private non-profit institutions showed lower case-fatality (0.083%; RR = 0.54) (p < 0.001).

Differences were also observed by insurance status. Compared with uninsured patients, those affiliated with IESS (Ecuadorian Institute of Social Security) general insurance had the lowest case-fatality proportion (0.054%; RR = 0.34), whereas patients covered by ISFFA (Armed Forces Social Security Institute) or grouped in an unspecified insurance category showed slightly higher risks; the overall association between insurance type and case-fatality was highly significant (p < 0.001).

Finally, case-fatality varied across discharge specialties. Information on discharge specialty was available for 166,506 records (23.2% of all discharges), among which 226 in-hospital deaths occurred, yielding an overall in-hospital case-fatality proportion of 0.136% in this subgroup. This subgroup captured 19.2% of all in-hospital deaths recorded during the study period. Most discharges occurred from General Surgery (111,892 [67.2%]), which accounted for the largest absolute number of deaths (138) while maintaining a low case-fatality proportion (0.123%). Using General Surgery as the reference, Internal Medicine (0.402%; 24 deaths among 5973 discharges; RR = 3.27) and Obstetrics (0.396%; RR = 3.22) showed the highest case-fatality proportions, whereas Pediatrics had the lowest (0.030%; RR = 0.24); intermediate values were observed for Gastroenterology (0.151%), Pediatric Surgery (0.092%), and Traumatology (0.365%). Some low-volume services showed higher case-fatality proportions, but these estimates were based on very small numbers and are unstable. Overall, most specialties had case-fatality proportions of 0.2% or lower (Fig. 4).

Bivariate associations with in-hospital case-fatality

In unadjusted bivariate analyses, the in-hospital case-fatality proportion varied significantly according to patient, temporal, and healthcare system characteristics. Non-national patients had a higher in-hospital case-fatality proportion than Ecuadorian patients (0.219% vs 0.149%), corresponding to a 47% higher unadjusted risk of in-hospital death (RR = 1.47; p = 0.003). The in-hospital case-fatality proportion also varied significantly by month of admission (global χ2 p < 0.001) and by day of the month (global χ2 p = 0.002); the highest proportions were observed in June and on days 29–31, respectively. Because these specific peak categories were identified post hoc, only the global test results are reported, and the individual category values are shown descriptively in Fig. 3.

Geographic distribution of appendicitis across provinces and cantons

Marked geographic variation was observed across Ecuador. In absolute numbers, Pichincha contributed the largest volume of appendicitis-related hospital discharges in both sexes (87,944 in males and 57,032 in females), followed by Guayas (72,398 and 43,668), Manabí (30,187 and 18,709), Azuay (20,715 and 14,376), and Tungurahua (17,909 and 12,129).

At the provincial level, the highest hospital discharge rates in males were recorded in Santo Domingo (258.10 per 100,000 population), Santa Elena (254.78), Galápagos (242.47), and Tungurahua (240.31), whereas in females the highest rates were observed in Tungurahua (217.06), Santa Elena (212.66), Santo Domingo (207.79), and Imbabura (193.70). At the cantonal level, the highest hospital discharge rates in males were observed in Ibarra (443.51 per 100,000), Latacunga (398.90), Ambato (387.14), Riobamba (360.44), and Tulcán (348.38). In females, the highest rates were recorded in Ibarra (437.32), Azogues (382.75), Ambato (375.32), Latacunga (362.45), and Riobamba (330.82).

In absolute terms, the greatest cantonal burden was concentrated in Quito, Guayaquil, Cuenca, Ambato, and Ibarra, which also accounted for most deaths. By contrast, the highest cantonal mortality rates were observed in smaller cantons. In males, the highest values were found in Mocha (4.12), Chaguarpamba (3.59), El Pan (3.20), San Juan Bosco (2.51), and Quilanga (2.38), whereas in females the highest values were observed in Chambo (2.23), San Juan Bosco (2.09), Deleg (1.73), Atahualpa (1.56), and Pallatanga (1.48) (Fig. 5).

SDI and temporal trends in appendicitis

Over the study period, Ecuador's national SDI rose steadily from 0.566 in 2004 to 0.661 in 2021, paralleling the increase in appendicitis-related hospital discharge rates. For the years with available SDI data (2004–2021; n = 18), the annual national discharge rate was strongly and positively correlated with SDI (total: Pearson r = 0.87, p < 0.001; males: r = 0.89; females: r = 0.83; Spearman ρ = 0.82–0.85). The association was stronger after excluding 2020, the year affected by the COVID-19 pandemic (r = 0.89–0.92). In a linear model, each 0.01-point increase in SDI corresponded to an additional 11.2 discharges per 100,000 population (R2 = 0.75). However, when the series were analyzed in first differences to remove the shared temporal trend, the association was null (r = 0.04, p = 0.874), indicating that the correlation reflected a common secular trend rather than a year-to-year relationship. The two indicators also diverged in 2020, when the discharge rate fell by 19% relative to 2019 while SDI continued to rise, and the discharge rate increased roughly six times faster in relative terms than SDI over the period (+102% vs +16.7%) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This nationwide ecological analysis of annual trends provides, to our knowledge, the most comprehensive epidemiological characterization of appendicitis-related hospital burden in Ecuador to date, based on 717,639 hospital discharge records and 1179 in-hospital deaths over a 21-year period. The overall in-hospital case-fatality proportion of 0.164% was low and broadly comparable to figures reported in high-income settings.1,19,20 Nevertheless, the cumulative burden reflected by more than 717,000 hospitalizations and nearly 1200 in-hospital deaths remain substantial. The observed pattern was characterized by male predominance, the highest hospital discharge rates in adolescents and young adults, substantial geographic variation across provinces and cantons, including high rates in several Sierra provinces, and important differences across healthcare sectors, insurance categories, discharge specialties, and temporal strata. Together, these findings extend previous local reports and identify priority areas for strengthening surgical systems in a middle-income Andean country.21, 22, 23, 24 Ecuador's increasing SDI provides useful context for interpreting the rise in appendicitis-related hospital discharge rates over the study period. The strong positive correlation between SDI and discharge rates likely reflects a shared long-term secular trend rather than a direct year-to-year relationship, as shown by the null association in first-difference analyses. Therefore, socioeconomic development may have contributed to the observed increase by improving access to hospital diagnosis, emergency surgery, and referral pathways, but it does not fully explain the magnitude or timing of the trend.19 Other factors, including expanded diagnostic capacity, changes in healthcare-seeking behavior, temporal changes in coding practices, and possible variation in the proportion of uncomplicated or negative appendectomy-related admissions, may also have influenced the increasing hospitalization burden.25 Furthermore, exploratory forecasting models based on these historical trajectories (Supplementary File 1) project a continuous escalation of hospital discharge rates through 2030 and 2050.

A noteworthy temporal pattern was the combination of relatively low hospital discharge rates and higher in-hospital case-fatality at the beginning of the study period, followed by rising discharge rates and declining case-fatality in later years (Fig. 1). This inverse pattern is consistent with improvements in case ascertainment and access to care over time: in the earlier years, lower hospitalization rates may have reflected underdiagnosis and the preferential admission of more advanced or complicated cases, which would be associated with higher case-fatality, whereas the subsequent increase in discharges likely captured a broader spectrum of milder, uncomplicated cases, diluting the overall case-fatality. Expanded diagnostic capacity, earlier presentation, and improved surgical and referral systems may also have contributed to lower case-fatality as hospitalization rates rose. Because these are ecological, unadjusted observations, this interpretation is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Our findings can be placed in international context. The overall in-hospital case-fatality proportion in Ecuador (0.164%) is broadly comparable to the low case-fatality reported in high-income settings, where appendicitis mortality is generally well below 0.3% and has declined over recent decades.10,20 The rising hospital discharge rates we observed are consistent with global trends: the Global Burden of Disease studies have documented increasing appendicitis incidence worldwide between 1990 and 2021, with the steepest rises in newly industrialized and middle-income regions, and the highest age-standardized incidence and DALY rates in Andean Latin America.1,7,19 The marked male predominance and the concentration of cases in adolescents and young adults are also in line with international data.8 However, the geographic and health-system disparities we describe higher case-fatality in the public sector, among uninsured patients, and in lower-resourced settings resemble patterns reported in other low- and middle-income countries, where delayed presentation, limited surgical capacity, and access barriers contribute to worse outcomes26, 27, 28 and contrast with high-income settings where case-fatality is lower and more uniform.19,20 These comparisons suggest that, although Ecuador's overall outcomes are favorable by international standards, the within-country inequities remain a distinctive and actionable feature of the national burden.

This projected growth should be interpreted cautiously, but it reinforces the need for short- and medium-term planning to expand surgical capacity, strengthen timely diagnostic pathways, emergency referral systems, and equitable access to surgical care. In particular, the decline in appendectomy volume during the COVID-19 pandemic, without a clear parallel increase in in-hospital case-fatality, suggests that more selective diagnostic and therapeutic approaches may be feasible in some settings, provided that patient safety and timely management of complicated cases are ensured.29 From a policy perspective, these trends suggest that reducing the future burden of appendicitis in Ecuador will depend not only on timely access to emergency surgical care, but also on better prevention-oriented health promotion, more efficient diagnostic pathways, and avoidance of unnecessary surgical overuse. Interventions already implemented or expanded in many health systems including standardized emergency triage, wider availability of ultrasound and computed tomography, early surgical consultation pathways, laparoscopic capacity, antibiotic protocols for selected uncomplicated cases, and improved referral systems may contribute to more accurate diagnosis, more appropriate selection of surgical candidates, fewer complicated presentations, shorter length of stay, and lower postoperative morbidity.30,31

Hospital management practices for appendicitis are not standardized nationally and may vary substantially across provinces and healthcare facilities. For example, high hospital discharge rates were observed in several provinces across Ecuador, including multiple Sierra provinces such as Imbabura, Cotopaxi, Tungurahua, Chimborazo, and Azuay, which is broadly consistent with earlier Ecuadorian data and regional Latin American patterns.12 These geographic comparisons are descriptive; because no formal spatial statistical analysis was performed, the observed clustering is hypothesis-generating and was not tested for statistical significance. Also, since altitude was not formally modeled as an exposure variable in this study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal altitude effect. Rather, the observed clustering in highland provinces may reflect a combination of contextual factors, including regional dietary patterns, differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, urban concentration, referral dynamics, and other unmeasured geographic or health-system characteristics.32, 33, 34 Conversely, the lower hospital discharge rates observed in coastal and Amazonian provinces may reflect lower treated burden, underdiagnosis, barriers to access, or differences in referral and hospitalization patterns, rather than true differences in disease occurrence.

A higher unadjusted in-hospital case-fatality proportion was observed in the public sector than in private facilities (0.192% vs 0.115%; RR = 1.67), a pattern that has been described in other low- and middle-income settings. In Ecuador, public hospitals managed most appendicitis-related hospital discharges (67.3%) and may also receive a greater proportion of patients with delayed presentation, more advanced disease, or limited prehospital access. In addition, public-sector facilities often operate under structural constraints, including limited operating-room availability, fewer specialists, and higher patient-to-surgeon ratios.26,28 By contrast, private facilities may treat a different case mix and may benefit from faster access to diagnostic and surgical resources.27,35,36 These interpretations remain plausible rather than definitive, because the administrative database did not capture clinical severity at presentation. This unadjusted association should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of differential system performance.

A notable finding was the higher in-hospital case-fatality proportion observed in Obstetrics (0.396%; RR = 3.22) and Internal Medicine (0.402%; RR = 3.27) compared with General Surgery. In obstetric settings, appendicitis may occur during pregnancy, when physiological and anatomical changes, diagnostic uncertainty, and the need to balance maternal and fetal considerations can complicate timely diagnosis and management.37, 38, 39, 40 In Internal Medicine, appendicitis may occur in older adults or in patients with multiple comorbidities, and it may initially be interpreted within a broader differential diagnosis, potentially contributing to delayed surgical evaluation.41, 42, 43 Although these explanations cannot be directly tested with the available data, the findings support the importance of prompt surgical assessment when appendicitis is suspected in non-surgical services.

Patients without health insurance had a higher in-hospital case-fatality proportion than those affiliated with IESS general insurance. This pattern is consistent with evidence showing that lack of insurance can delay access to emergency evaluation and surgery through financial barriers, reduced continuity of care, and broader social determinants of health, including poverty, rural residence, and limited health literacy.44,45 Conversely, the lower case-fatality observed among patients with formal insurance is consistent with literature suggesting that coverage facilitates earlier access to surgical care and may reduce the risk of advanced disease at presentation.46,47

Specialized hospitals also showed a higher in-hospital case-fatality proportion (0.200%; RR = 1.72) than other facilities. This pattern may reflect referral bias, as these centers are more likely to receive severe, perforated, transferred, or medically complex cases from lower-level institutions.48 Accordingly, the excess case-fatality observed in specialized hospitals should not be interpreted as evidence of lower quality of care, but rather as a likely reflection of underlying differences in case mix.

In-hospital case-fatality was also higher in urban than in rural facilities (0.138% vs 0.055%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.148) and should be interpreted with caution. Two non-mutually exclusive explanations are likely. First, urban hospitals where higher-complexity and referral centers are located probably receive a disproportionate share of severe, perforated, or transferred cases, so the higher urban case-fatality may again reflect referral bias and case-mix differences rather than poorer care. Second, because cases were classified by the patient's place of residence, rural patients who reach hospital care particularly those able to access urban referral centers may represent a more selected subgroup, while the smaller number of rural deaths yields unstable estimates sensitive to chance. Given the non-significant difference and these potential biases, this finding should be regarded as exploratory.

Temporal analyses showed significant variation in in-hospital case-fatality by month of admission (global χ2 p < 0.001) and by day of the month (global χ2 p = 0.002), with the highest proportions in June and on days 29–31, respectively. These findings suggest that appendicitis outcomes may vary over time, although the mechanisms remain uncertain. The June peak may coincide with seasonal disruptions associated with the rainy season in several regions of Ecuador, including impaired road access, changes in healthcare utilization, and competing healthcare demands.49 The higher case-fatality observed at the end of the month may reflect operational or organizational factors, but this interpretation should be made cautiously, as the study was not designed to identify staffing, workflow, or hospital-capacity mechanisms directly. These unadjusted, ecological temporal associations are hypothesis-generating, and the proposed rainfall-related mechanism cannot be tested with the present data.

Non-national patients had a 47% higher unadjusted risk of in-hospital death than Ecuadorian patients (RR = 1.47). This difference is consistent with previous evidence indicating that migrants and other non-national populations may experience delayed care because of administrative barriers, lack of insurance, fragmented health–care pathways, and social vulnerability.50, 51, 52 Discharge records classified as Indigenous showed a higher crude in-hospital case-fatality proportion than those classified as Mestizo. This finding should not be interpreted as reflecting biological susceptibility or an independent effect of ethnicity. Ethnicity is a sociocultural construct, and the observed difference might reflect structural and geographic inequities, differences in access to timely surgical care, referral pathways, socioeconomic conditions, administrative recording, or unmeasured case-mix factors. Because ethnicity was incompletely recorded and the analyses were unadjusted, this result is exploratory and requires confirmation using data specifically designed to evaluate inequities in surgical access and outcomes.53, 54, 55 This association is therefore hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted cautiously, but it is consistent with broader evidence on social disparities in health service access and outcomes.

Although individual-level risk factors for the onset of appendicitis could not be assessed in this administrative dataset, several modifiable determinants of poor outcomes are directly relevant to health policy. These include delayed presentation, delayed imaging or surgical assessment, limited availability of emergency operating rooms, fragmented referral pathways, financial barriers among uninsured patients, and incomplete coding of disease severity or complications. Addressing these factors may be more feasible than primary prevention of appendicitis itself and could reduce perforation-related complications, length of stay, and preventable in-hospital mortality. The observed differences by insurance status, facility type, discharge specialty, and geographic area therefore support a surgical-systems agenda focused on timely diagnosis, equitable access, and quality improvement rather than attributing outcomes to patient or institutional factors alone.

The burden-of-disease analysis estimated 46,530 DALYs attributable to appendicitis over the study period (approximately 2216 DALYs per year), with 81.1% of the total burden explained by premature mortality (YLLs). This predominance of YLLs indicates that, despite its low in-hospital case-fatality proportion, appendicitis may still account for a meaningful loss of healthy life years, particularly when deaths occur at younger ages. The greater burden observed among males, uninsured individuals, and public-sector patients further suggests that appendicitis burden in Ecuador is shaped not only by disease frequency, but also by differences in access, timing of care, and health-system organization.

Limitations

This study provides a nationwide assessment of appendicitis-related hospital burden in Ecuador over 21 years, using a census of inpatient discharge records and annual population denominators. Its principal strengths include the long observation period, national coverage, disaggregation by age and sex, analysis at provincial and cantonal levels, and evaluation of temporal, geographic, and health-system variation. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered.

First, the INEC registry records hospital discharge episodes rather than unique individuals. Consequently, repeated admissions and inter-hospital transfers could not be identified and may have contributed more than one record, whereas cases managed exclusively in outpatient settings or cases that did not reach hospital care were not captured. Hospital discharge rates should therefore be interpreted as indicators of treated inpatient burden rather than direct estimates of person-level incidence. Second, the database lacked detailed clinical and operative information, including symptom duration, comorbidities, perforation status, diagnostic imaging, disease severity, surgical approach, postoperative complications, transfer status, and cause-specific information on in-hospital deaths. The specific clinical circumstances leading to death and individual-level risk factors for appendicitis or adverse outcomes could therefore not be evaluated.

Third, case ascertainment relied exclusively on the primary administrative ICD-10 diagnosis codes K35–K37. Although appendectomy specimens are generally submitted for histopathological assessment in routine surgical practice, the INEC registry does not contain operative or pathology findings. The coded diagnoses could not therefore be validated against surgical or histopathological confirmation, and administrative miscoding, diagnostic misclassification, clinically suspected cases, and possible negative appendectomies cannot be excluded. Changes in diagnostic capacity, coding practices, and reporting completeness over the study period might also explain part of the observed increase in hospital discharge rates, independently of any change in underlying disease occurrence.

Fourth, all comparisons were descriptive or bivariate. No multivariable adjustment was possible for potential confounders such as age, sex, comorbidity, disease severity, delayed presentation, perforation, or referral patterns. Accordingly, all reported risk ratios are crude and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as independent or causal effects. Facility-level comparisons are particularly susceptible to referral and case-mix bias because public, tertiary, and specialized hospitals may receive a disproportionate number of severe, perforated, medically complex, or transferred cases. The higher crude case-fatality observed in these settings should not therefore be interpreted as evidence of poorer quality of care or an adverse institutional effect.

Fifth, geographic assignment was based on the patient's recorded place of residence rather than the location of the treating hospital. This approach supports population-based rate estimation but does not capture inter-cantonal referral pathways or the geographic distribution of healthcare delivery. No cantons were excluded or grouped because of small population size or low event counts, and no spatial smoothing or formal clustering analysis was performed. Rates from sparsely populated cantons may therefore be unstable and should not be interpreted as evidence of persistent geographic excess risk. Altitude was not modeled as an explanatory exposure, and geographic differences might reflect variation in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, coding, urban concentration, or referral patterns rather than differences in underlying disease occurrence.

Sixth, we did not apply segmented regression, interrupted time-series methods, or a formal counterfactual model to assess the COVID-19 period. The decline in hospital discharge rates observed in 2020 and the subsequent partial recovery are therefore descriptive and cannot be attributed causally to the pandemic. Pandemic-related changes in healthcare-seeking behavior, hospital utilization, surgical activity, referral, and data capture may also have altered both discharge rates and the clinical profile of admitted cases. Finally, the ecological design precludes individual-level inference; observed temporal, geographic, demographic, and health-system differences should therefore be interpreted as population-level patterns that require confirmation through linked administrative, operative, pathology, and mortality data or prospective clinical studies.

Conclusions

Appendicitis remains a major surgical burden in Ecuador, as reflected by more than 717,000 appendicitis-related hospital discharges recorded between 2004 and 2024. The burden was concentrated in adolescents and young adults, was higher in males, and showed substantial geographic heterogeneity, with several Sierra provinces and cantons accounting for the highest hospital discharge rates. Although overall in-hospital case-fatality was low, it was unevenly distributed: higher unadjusted case-fatality was observed among uninsured and non-national patients, among Indigenous patients, in the public sector, and in selected non-surgical specialties. Because individual-level clinical data were not available, these associations are descriptive and cannot be interpreted as causal; nonetheless, they point to persistent inequities in insurance coverage, timely emergency diagnosis, referral efficiency, and surgical capacity across regions and healthcare sectors, particularly in high-burden Sierra provinces and underserved populations. The administrative database captured only vital status at discharge, without cause-of-death certification, disease severity, perforation status, operative findings, postoperative complications, or quality-of-care indicators; the specific causes of in-hospital death therefore could not be determined. Future studies linking hospital discharge records with clinical, surgical, and mortality-certification data are needed to identify avoidable deaths, distinguish disease severity from health-system delays, and evaluate quality-of-care gaps. Overall, these findings support prioritizing timely surgical access and referral capacity in high-burden settings, while underscoring the need for improved clinical data systems, routine surgical audits, and adjusted analytical models.
