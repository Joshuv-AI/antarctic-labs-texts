Fractional domatic number and minimum degree
Quentin Chuet, Hugo Demaret, Hoang La, François Pirot

Abstract
The domatic number of a graph G G is the maximum number of pairwise disjoint dominating sets of G G . We are interested in the LP-relaxation of this parameter, which is called the fractional domatic number of G G . We study its extremal value in the class of graphs of minimum degree d d . The fractional domatic number of a graph of minimum degree d d is always at most d + 1 d+1 , and at least ( 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ d / ln ⁡ d (1-o(1))\,d/\ln d as d → ∞ d\to\infty . This is asymptotically tight even within the class of split graphs. Our main result concerns the case d = 2 d=2 ; we show that, excluding 8 8 exceptional graphs, the fractional domatic number of every connected graph of minimum degree (at least) 2 2 is at least 5 / 2 5/2 . We also show that this bound cannot be improved if only finitely many graphs are excluded, even when restricting to bipartite graphs of girth at least 6 6 . This proves in a stronger sense a conjecture by Gadouleau, Harms, Mertzios, and Zamaraev (2024). This also extends and generalises results from McCuaig and Shepherd (1989), from Fujita, Kameda, and Yamashita (2000), and from Abbas, Egerstedt, Liu, Thomas, and Whalen (2016). Finally, we show that planar graphs of minimum degree at least 2 2 and girth at least g g have fractional domatic number at least 3 − O ⁡ ( 1 / g ) 3-O(1/g) as g → ∞ g\to\infty .

Introduction

Given a graph
G
, a
dominating set
of
G
is a set
X\subseteq V(G)
such that
N[X]=V(G)
. Dominating sets are often used to model monitoring problems in networks. A classical problem in Graph Theory is to find the minimum size
\gamma(G)
of a dominating set of
G
, called the
domination number
of
G

[
17
,
16
]
. A possible approach to this problem is to study a stronger parameter, the
domatic number
of
G
, denoted
\dom(G)
, which is the
maximum number of pairwise disjoint dominating sets of
G
. Since dominating sets are stable through vertex addition,
\dom(G)
can equivalently be defined as the maximum size of a partition of
V(G)
into dominating sets.
Such a partition is usually called a
domatic partition of
G
, and can also be captured by the notion of
dominating
k
-colouring
of
G
, that is a mapping
\varphi\colon V(G)\to[k]
such that
\varphi(N[v])=[k]
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
— in other words, every closed neighbourhood spans all the colours in
\varphi
. Then
\dom(G)
is the maximum
k
such that
G
has a dominating
k
-colouring.
A straightforward application of the Pigeonhole Principle yields that the minimum colour class in a dominating
k
-colouring of
G
has size at most
|V(G)|/k
, hence
\gamma(G)\leq\lvert V(G)\rvert/\dom(G)
. As a consequence, any lower bound on
\dom(G)
yields an upper bound on
\gamma(G)

For instance, Matheson and Tarjan
[
16
]
proved that every
n

2
-connected planar graph, all internal faces of which are triangles) has domination number at most
n/3

3

A parallel can be made with independent sets and the chromatic number of graphs. Remarkably, there is no known proof for the fact that every
n
-vertex planar graph contains an independent set of size at least
n/4
that does not rely on the
4
-Colour Theorem
.

We are interested in the LP-relaxation of the domatic number, called the

, which provides a tighter bound on the domination number. This parameter was first formally introduced in
[
22

[
9

Domatic number and chromatic number

The study of the domatic number is analogous to that of the chromatic number.
The chromatic number of a graph
G
, denoted
\chi(G)
, is the minimum size of a partition of
V(G)
into independent sets. The fractional chromatic number
\chi_{f}(G)
corresponds to the LP-relaxation of the chromatic number. As for
\fdom(G)
, there are many equivalent definitions of
\chi_{f}(G)
; let us mention that related to colourings. Given integers
p\geq q
, a

(p:q)
-colouring
of

is a mapping
\varphi\colon V(G)\to\binom{[p]}{q}
such that
\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)=\emptyset
for every edge

. When

and
q
are not explicit, we say that
\varphi
is a
fractional proper colouring
of
G
.
Then
\chi_{f}(G)
is the minimum
p/q
such that there is a proper
(p:q)
-colouring of
G
.
Said otherwise, each vertex is assigned a set of
q
colours among
p
different ones in total, and two adjacent vertices must have no colour in common. For example,

1(b)
is also a fractional proper colouring of

, since each colour class forms an independent set — this is not the case in

There are many similarities between the chromatic number and the domatic number, as well as between their fractional counterparts. Typically, we have the following well-known (and relatively easy to derive) sequence of inequalities for the chromatic parameters of a graph
G
on
n

\max\left\{\omega(G),\frac{n}{\alpha(G)}\right\}\leq\chi_{f}(G)\leq\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G)+1

(5)

where
\omega(G)
,
\alpha(G)
, and
\Delta(G)
denote the clique number, the independence number, and the maximum degree of
G

Interestingly, there is a sequence of inequalities for the domatic parameters of a graph
G
on
n
vertices mirroring (
5
).

(1-o(1))\frac{\delta(G)}{\ln\Delta(G)}\leq\dom(G)\leq\fdom(G)\leq\min\left\{\delta(G)+1,\frac{n}{\gamma(G)}\right\}.

(6)

The left-most lower bound is from
[

]
, and the right-most upper bounds can be found in
[

]
.
Let us provide some insight on the similarities between (
5
) and (
6
).

The bound
\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G)+1
comes from a simple algorithm that extends a partial proper colouring of a graph greedily, introducing a new colour each time it is needed. This is possible because proper colourings are hereditary: a proper colouring of a graph induces a proper colouring of any of its subgraphs. This is not the case for dominating colourings, so there is no similar bound in the sequence of inequalities associated with
\dom(G)
. Instead, lower bounds on
\dom(G)
that depend on
\delta(G)
and
\Delta(G)
can be derived with the probabilistic method
[
8
]
.

A proper colouring of
G
is a partition of
V(G)
into independent sets, all of which have size at most
\alpha(G)
. From that observation, we infer the bound
\chi(G)\geq n/\alpha(G)
. Similarly, a dominating colouring of
G
is a partition of
V(G)
into dominating sets, all of which have size at least
\gamma(G)
. Hence,
\gamma(G)
is to
\dom(G)
what
\alpha(G)
is to
\chi(G)
, and we derive the bound
\dom(G)\leq n/\gamma(G)
.

We now delve into the relationship between
\delta(G)
and
\dom(G)
that has similarities and differences with that between
\chi(G)
and
\omega(G)
.
In a proper colouring, every colour must appear at most once in any clique. Similarly, in a dominating colouring, every colour must appear at least once in any closed neighbourhood. This explains why the relation between
\delta(G)+1
and
\dom(G)
shares similarities with that between
\omega(G)
and
\chi(G)
.
The latter has been extensively studied after it has been observed that there exist triangle-free graphs
G
(with
\omega(G)=2
) with arbitrarily large chromatic number (and even arbitrarily small independence ratio, see
[
4
]
).
This motivated the study of classes of graphs where, on the contrary, the chromatic number is bounded as a function of the clique number. For a given class of graph
\mathcal{G}
, we say that
\mathcal{G}
is
\chi
-bounded — respectively
\chi_{f}
-bounded — if there exists a function
f
such that
\chi(G)\leq f(\omega(G))
— respectively
\chi_{f}(G)\leq f(\omega(G))
— for every graph
G\in\mathcal{G}
.
As previously discussed, the class of all graphs is neither
\chi
-bounded, nor
\chi_{f}
-bounded.
The studies of simple sufficient conditions for and obstructions to
\chi
-boundedness have been an extensive research area of Chromatic Graph Theory; we refer the reader to a survey by Scott and Seymour
[
20
]
to learn more about this.

The same notion can be defined for
\dom
and
\fdom
thanks to their relation to minimum degree. More formally, we say that a class
\mathcal{G}
of graphs is
\dom
-bounded — respectively
\fdom
-bounded — if and only if there exists a function

such that
\delta(G)\leq f(\dom(G))
— respectively
\delta(G)\leq f(\fdom(G))
— for every graph

While the class of all graphs is not
\dom
-bounded due to
[
24
]

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}w(v),

\displaystyle\mbox{Subject to}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle w(D)\geq 1&\mbox{for all $D\in\mathcal{D}(G)$;}\\
w(v)\geq 0&\mbox{for all $v\in V(G)$.}\end{array}\right.

By the Strong Duality Theorem, one has
\fdom(G)=\tau^{*}(G)

Fractional bottlenecks are a convenient tool for certifying upper bounds on the fractional domatic number. As an introductory demonstration, we use duality to prove that the trivial upper bound
\dom(G)\leq\delta(G)+1
also holds for the fractional domatic number.

Proposition 4
.

Let
G
be a graph of minimum degree
d
. Then
\fdom(G)\leq d+1
.

Proof.

Let
v
be a vertex of degree
d
, and assign the weight
1
to every vertex of
N[v]
. Every dominating set
D
must intersect
N[v]
, and thus has weight
w(D)\geq 1
. Therefore, we have a fractional bottleneck of weight
d+1
, which certifies that
\fdom(G)=\tau^{*}(G)\leq d+1
.
∎

It follows directly from the Pigeonhole Principle that, if
G
has
n
vertices and domination number
\gamma
, then
\dom(G)\leq n/\gamma
. Using fractional bottlenecks, it is straightforward to show that the same upper bound holds for
\fdom(G)
; this was already shown in
[
11
]
using a simple counting argument applied to
Equation

3
, but we include our alternative proof for the sake of completeness and as another simple illustration of fractional bottlenecks.

Proposition 5
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices and domination number
\gamma
. Then
\fdom(G)\leq\frac{n}{\gamma}
, with equality if
G
is vertex-transitive.

Proof.

We assign the weight
1/\gamma
to every vertex of
G
. Every dominating set
D
contains at least
\gamma
vertices, and thus has weight
w(D)\geq 1
. Therefore, we have a fractional bottleneck of weight
n/\gamma
, which certifies that
\fdom(G)=\tau^{*}(G)\leq\frac{n}{\gamma}
.

If
G
is vertex-transitive, let
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
be the set of dominating sets of
G
of size
\gamma
, and observe that any two vertices
u
and
v
are contained in the same number of dominating sets of
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
. Indeed, by considering an automorphism of
G
which maps
u
onto
v
, we obtain a bijection between
\{D\in\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}:u\in D\}
and
\{D\in\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}:v\in D\}
. Consequently, the uniform distribution
\mathbf{D}\sim\mathcal{U(D_{\gamma})}
verifies
\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}\right]=\frac{\gamma}{n}
for all
u\in V(G)
, since all vertices are equally likely to belong to
\mathbf{D}
and
\sum_{v\in V(G)}\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]=\mathbb{E}\big[|\mathbf{D}|\big]=\gamma
. This shows that
\fdom(G)\geq\frac{n}{\gamma}
if
G
is vertex-transitive, and equality follows from the previous point.
∎

To illustrate how duality helps us compute the fractional domatic number of certain graph families, we apply it to the class of complete bipartite graphs.

Proposition 6
.

For every integers
m\geq n\geq 2
, one has

\fdom(K_{m,n})=1+n\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right).

Proof.

Let

, and let

be the two parts of

, with

and
|B|=n
.

We first exhibit a fractional dominating colouring of

of weight

, under the form of a solution to (

and

is a dominating set of

. We assign to the dominating set

. It is straightforward to check that this satisfies the constraints of (

We now exhibit a fractional bottleneck of

of weight

Proposition 8

\displaystyle nw_{A}+\binom{n}{d}w_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{d(q+1)}{(\ln d-2\ln\ln d)q}+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{n-d+i}{n-m-d+i}

\displaystyle=0+\frac{n+1}{5n-2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}+\frac{4n-4}{5n-2}\cdot\frac{3}{8}=\frac{2n-1}{5n-2}.

Otherwise, if
n
is odd, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{3n-1}{(n+1)(5n-2)}\,\mathbb{P}\left[v\in D_{0}\right]+\frac{n-1}{5n-2}\,\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}_{1}\right]+\frac{4n^{2}}{(n+1)(5n-2)}\,\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=0+\frac{n-1}{5n-2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}+\frac{4n^{2}}{(n+1)(5n-2)}\left(\frac{(n-1)(n+1)}{4n^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{4n^{2}}\right)=\frac{2n-1}{5n-2}.

This covers all possible cases, so
\mathbf{D}
is a fractional dominating colouring of
G_{n}
of weight
\frac{5n-2}{2n-1}
.

\lozenge

We conclude that
\fdom(G_{n})=\frac{5n-2}{2n-1}
.
∎

3
Lower bound on
\fdom
of graphs with minimum degree 2

3.1
Preliminary definitions and notations

Let
f\colon V(G)\to[0,1]
be a
demand function
on
G
.
For every real number
r
such that
0<r\leq 1
, an
f
-dominating
r
-colouring
of
G
is a random subset
\mathbf{D}\subseteq V(G)
such that for every vertex
v\in V(G)
,

\mathbb{P}\left[N[v]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq f(v)\mbox{ and }\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]=r.

When
f(v)=1
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, we simply refer to it as a
dominating
r
-colouring
.
Let
p,q
be integers such that
0<q\leq p
. An
f
-dominating
(p:q)
-colouring
of
G
is a mapping
\varphi\colon V(G)\to\binom{[p]}{q}
such that
|\varphi(N[v])|\geq f(v)p
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
.
We will also refer to them as
partially dominating colourings
as they can be understood as generalisations of dominating colourings where each vertex
v
is “partially dominated”, i.e.
v
only has to see a proportion
f(v)
of the total number of colours in its closed neighbourhood.

3.2
Technical tools

The two notions of partially dominating colourings defined above are closely related.

Lemma 13
.

Let
G
be a graph,
f
a demand function on
G
, and
q,p
integers such that
0<q\leq p
.
If there exists an
f
-dominating
(p:q)
-colouring
\varphi
of
G
, then there exists an
f
-dominating
q/p
-colouring of
G
.

Proof.

Let
i
be drawn uniformly at random from
[p]
, and let
\mathbf{D}\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}(\{i\})
. For every vertex
v
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]=|\varphi(v)|/p=q/p
, and
\mathbb{P}\left[N[v]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]=\frac{|\varphi(N[v])|}{p}\geq f(v)
.
We conclude that
\mathbf{D}
is the desired
f
-fractional
q/p
-colouring of
G
.
∎

In the definition of a partially dominating
r
-colouring, we require equality for the probability
r
that a vertex is in the random set. This equality helps us inductively build partially dominating
r
-colourings but can be annoying to ensure.
The following observation shows that, when building a partially dominating
r
-colouring, we may replace that equality constraint with an inequality.
Intuitively, domination constraints cannot be violated if we add additional colours to vertices with fewer colours so that every vertex is assigned a fraction
r=q/p
of the total number of colours.

Lemma 14
.

Let
G
be a graph,
f
a demand function on
G
, and
r
a real number such that
0<r\leq 1
.
If there exists
\mathbf{D}
such that
\mathbb{P}\left[N[v]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq f(v)
and
\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]\leq r
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
,
then there exists an
f
-dominating
r
-colouring
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
of
G
.

Proof.

We construct a random set
\mathbf{X}
by taking each vertex
v\notin\mathbf{D}
independently with probability
\frac{r-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]}{1-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]}
.
Let
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
be the union of
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{X}
.
By construction,
\mathbf{D}\subseteq\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
, so we have
\mathbb{P}\left[N[v]\cap\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq f(v)
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
.
Moreover,

\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{X}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]+\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]\right)\frac{r-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]}{1-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]}=r.\qed

Consider a graph with two blocks, i.e. maximal
2
-connected subgraphs, glued on a common cut-vertex. If each block has a colouring with the same number of colours on each vertex, then we can combine the two colourings by permuting the colours to match them on the cut-vertex and get a colouring of the whole graph.
Moreover, we can choose a permutation that also maximises the proportion of colours appearing in the neighbourhood of the cut-vertex.
As a consequence, two partially dominating colourings, one for each block, where the only vertex that is partially dominated is the cut-vertex, can combine to form a dominating colouring of the whole graph.
Formally, we prove the following more general statement.

Lemma 15
.

Let
G
be a graph,
(G_{0},G_{1})
a separation of order
1
of
G
, and
v\in V(G)
such that
V(G_{0}\cap G_{1})=\{v\}
.
Let
f_{0},f_{1}
be demand functions on
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
respectively.
Let
r
be a real number such that
0<r\leq 1
.
If every
G_{i}
has an
f_{i}
-dominating
r
-colouring
\mathbf{D}_{i}
, then
G
has an
f
-dominating
r
-colouring
\mathbf{D}
, where

\begin{cases}f(v)\coloneqq\min\left\{1,f_{0}(v)+f_{1}(v)-r\right\}&\mbox{and}\\
f(w)\coloneqq f_{i}(w)&\mbox{if $w\in V(G_{i})\setminus\{v\}$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

For every
i\in\{0,1\}
, let
A_{i}
be the event
v\in\mathbf{D}_{i}
,
B_{i}
the event
v\notin\mathbf{D}_{i}
and
N_{G_{i}}(v)\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}\neq\emptyset
, and
C_{i}
the event
N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}=\emptyset
— observe that the events
A_{i},B_{i},C_{i}
form a partition of the probability space.
Moreover,
(\mathbb{P}\left[A_{0}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right])+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{0}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}\neq\emptyset\right]+(\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{1}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}\neq\emptyset\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}_{1}\right])\geq f_{0}(v)+f_{1}(v)-r
.

For every
X\in\{A,B,C\}
and every
i\in\{0,1\}
, we let
\mathbf{D}_{i}^{X}
be
\mathbf{D}_{i}
conditioned on the event
X_{i}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}^{A}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}\neq\emptyset\mid v\in\mathbf{D}_{i}\right]=1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}^{B}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}\neq\emptyset\mid v\notin\mathbf{D}_{i}\et N_{G_{i}}(v)\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}\neq\emptyset\right]=1,\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}^{C}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}\neq\emptyset\mid N_{G_{i}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{i}=\emptyset\right]=0.

Case 1.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]\geq 1-r
.
Observe that
r+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right]=r+(1-\mathbb{P}\left[A_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right])+(1-\mathbb{P}\left[A_{1}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right])=2-r-\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]\leq 1
.
Let

\mathbf{D}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{D}_{0}^{A}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{A}&\mbox{with probability $r$,}\\
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{C}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B}&\mbox{with probability $\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]$,}\\
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{C}&\mbox{with probability $\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right]$, and}\\
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B}&\mbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

By construction,
\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]=r
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle=r\,\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap(\mathbf{D}_{0}^{A}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{A})\neq\emptyset\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap(\mathbf{D}_{0}^{C}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B})\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap(\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{C})\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle~~~+(1-r-\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right])\,\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap(\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B})\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq r\,\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{0}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{A}\neq\emptyset\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{1}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{0}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle~~~+(1-r-\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right])\,\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{0}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq r+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right]+1-r-\mathbb{P}\left[C_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[C_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1\geq f(v).

Observe that, for each
i\in\{0,1\}
, by construction,
\mathbf{D}\cap V(G_{i})
follows the same probability distribution as
\mathbf{D}_{i}
. Hence
\mathbf{D}
induces in particular an
f_{i}
-dominating
r
-colouring of
G_{i}
. This ends the proof of Case 1.

Case 2.

Suppose on the contrary that
\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]<1-r
.
Let

\mathbf{D}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{D}_{0}^{A}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{A}&\mbox{with probability $r$,}\\
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{C}&\mbox{with probability $\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]$,}\\
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{C}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B}&\mbox{with probability $\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]$, and}\\
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{C}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{C}&\mbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

By construction,
\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]=r
. Moreover, a computation similar to that performed in Case 1 yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq r\,\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{0}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{A}\neq\emptyset\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{0}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{B}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G_{1}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}^{B}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle~~~+(1-r-\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right])\,\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap(\mathbf{D}_{0}^{C}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{C})\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq r+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{0}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[B_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq f_{0}(v)+f_{1}(v)-r=f(v).

As in Case 1, for each
i\in\{0,1\}
,
\mathbf{D}\cap V(G_{i})
follows the same probability distribution as
\mathbf{D}_{i}
. Hence
\mathbf{D}
induces in particular an
f_{i}
-dominating
r
-colouring of
G_{i}
. This ends the proof of Case 2.
∎

Consider a separation
(G^{\prime},H)
of
G
of order 2 with
V(G^{\prime}\cap H)=\{u,v\}
. Let
\varphi^{\prime}
be a fractional dominating colouring of
G^{\prime}
. Like the previous case, if
H
is colourable, then we look for a way to combine the colourings of
G^{\prime}
and
H
to get one fractional dominating colouring of the whole graph. However, this combination requires that the number of colours in common between
u
and
v
is the same for both colourings. Let us assume that
|\varphi^{\prime}(u)\cap\varphi^{\prime}(v)|=\alpha|\varphi^{\prime}(u)|
, for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
If
H
has two colourings
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{1}
such that
\varphi_{0}(u)\cap\varphi_{0}(v)=\emptyset
and
\varphi_{1}(u)=\varphi_{1}(v)
, then we can produce a colouring
\varphi
of
H
such that
|\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)|=\alpha|\varphi(u)|
; this is obtained by letting a proportion
\alpha
of the colours of
\varphi
be copies of colours of
\varphi_{1}
, and a proportion
(1-\alpha)
be copies of colours of
\varphi_{0}
.
Then, one can obtain
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}
from
\varphi^{\prime}
by taking
|\varphi(u)|
copies of each colour class, and similarly one obtains
\widetilde{\varphi}
from
\varphi
by taking
|\varphi^{\prime}(u)|
copies of each colour class.
Up to colour permutation, we now have
\widetilde{\varphi}(u)=\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(u)
and
\widetilde{\varphi}(v)=\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(v)
, so
\widetilde{\varphi}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}
can be combined together. We formalise this argument in the following lemma.

Lemma 16
.

Let
G
be a graph,
(G^{\prime},H)
a separation of order
2
of
G
, and
u,v\in V(G)
such that
V(G^{\prime}\cap H)=\{u,v\}
.
Let
f^{\prime},h
be demand functions on
G^{\prime}
and
H
respectively.
Let
r
be a real number such that
0<r<1/2
.
If
G^{\prime}
has an
f^{\prime}
-dominating
r
-colouring
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
and
H
has two
h
-dominating
r
-colourings
\mathbf{D}_{0},\mathbf{D}_{1}
such that

u
and
v
belong to disjoint subsets of realisations of
\mathbf{D}_{0}
and to the exact same realisations of
\mathbf{D}_{1}
,
then
G
has an
f
-dominating
r
-colouring
\mathbf{D}
where
f(w)=f^{\prime}(w)
if
w\in V(G^{\prime})
and
f(w)=h(w)
otherwise.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mid v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]
and
\beta=\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mid v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mid v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha r,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)r,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mid v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]\right)\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)r,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=1-(2-\alpha)r,

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left[v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-(2-\alpha)r}{1-r},\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\beta-\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1-(2-\alpha)r-\alpha(1-r)}{1-r}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\frac{1-2r}{1-r}.

Since
r<1/2
,
\beta-\alpha\geq 0
and
0\leq\alpha/\beta\leq 1
.
Let
{\bm{\iota}}\leftarrow Ber(\alpha/\beta)
be a random
(0,1)
-valued variable such that
\mathbb{P}\left[{\bm{\iota}}=1\right]=\alpha/\beta
.

For every
i\in\{0,1\}
, let
\mathbf{D}_{i}^{u},\mathbf{D}_{i}^{v}
be
\mathbf{D}_{i}
conditioned on the events
u\in\mathbf{D}_{i}
and
v\in\mathbf{D}_{i}
respectively,
\mathbf{D}_{i}^{\neg u},\mathbf{D}_{i}^{\neg v}
be
\mathbf{D}_{i}
conditioned on the events
u\notin\mathbf{D}_{i}
and
v\notin\mathbf{D}_{i}
respectively, and
\mathbf{D}_{i}^{\neg u,v}
be
\mathbf{D}_{i}
conditioned on the event (
u\notin\mathbf{D}_{i}
and
v\notin\mathbf{D}_{i}
).
By hypothesis,

\mathbf{D}_{1}^{u}=\mathbf{D}_{1}^{v}
and
\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\neg u}=\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\neg v}
.
Now, let

\mathbf{D}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{u}&\mbox{if $u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and $v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$,}\\
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\cup\mathbf{D}_{0}^{u}&\mbox{if $u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and $v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$,}\\
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\cup\mathbf{D}_{0}^{v}&\mbox{if $u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and $v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$,}\\
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\cup\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\neg u}&\mbox{if $u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and $v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and ${\bm{\iota}}=1$, and}\\
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\cup\mathbf{D}_{0}^{\neg u,v}&\mbox{if $u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and $v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}$ and ${\bm{\iota}}=0$.}\\
\end{cases}

By definition of
\mathbf{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]=r;

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]=r;

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[u]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G^{\prime}}[u]\cap\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq f^{\prime}(u);

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G^{\prime}}[v]\cap\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq f^{\prime}(v).

For every vertex
w\in V(G^{\prime})\setminus\{u,v\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[w\in\mathbf{D}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[w\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]=r,\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G}[w]\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left[N_{G^{\prime}}[w]\cap\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right]\geq f^{\prime}(w).

By hypothesis,
the events
u\in\mathbf{D}_{0}
,
v\in\mathbf{D}_{0}
, and (
u\notin\mathbf{D}_{0}
and
v\notin\mathbf{D}_{0}
) form a partition of the probability space.
Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}_{0}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}_{0}\right]=1-\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}_{0}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\mathbf{D}_{0}\right]=1-2r.

For every subset of vertices
W\in V(H)\setminus\{u,v\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}^{u}\neq\emptyset\right]\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{u}\neq\emptyset\right]\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{v}\neq\emptyset\right]\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\in\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\neg u}\neq\emptyset\right]\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et{\bm{\iota}}=1\right]

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{\neg u,v}\neq\emptyset\right]\mathbb{P}\left[u\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et v\notin\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\et{\bm{\iota}}=0\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}^{u}\neq\emptyset\right]\alpha r+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{u}\neq\emptyset\right](1-\alpha)r+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{v}\neq\emptyset\right](1-\alpha)r

\displaystyle~~~+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\neg u}\neq\emptyset\right]\beta(1-r)\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\mathbb{P}\left[W\cap\mathbf{D}_{0}^{\neg u,v}\neq\emptyset\right]\beta(1-r)\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)

colours are dominating sets of
G
, so we obtain a dominating
(a-bt:b)
-colouring of
G
. Thus,
\fdom(G)\geq\fdom(G+K_{t})-t
. With both inequalities proven, we have shown that
\fdom(G+K_{t})=\fdom(G)+t
.
∎

Observe that if
G
is a split graph, then
G+K_{t}
is also a split graph. Using
25
and
26
together with the NP-hardness of deciding
\chi_{f}\geq 3
, we deduce the following.

Corollary 27
.

Let
k\geq 3
be a fixed integer. Given a split graph
G
, it is NP-hard to decide whether
\fdom(G)\geq k
.

Domatically full regular graphs

We recall that a given graph
G
is
domatically full
if
\dom(G)=\delta(G)+1
, and we say that it is
fdom-full
if
\fdom(G)=\delta(G)+1
.
We begin with a simple observation concerning regular graphs.

Proposition 28
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular graph. Then
G
is domatically full if and only if
\chi(G^{2})=d+1
. It is fdom-full if and only if
\chi_{f}(G^{2})=d+1
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be a dominating
(p:q)
-colouring of
G
with
p=q(d+1)
. If there is a vertex
v\in V(G)
and two vertices
u,u^{\prime}\in N[v]
such that
N(u)\cap N(u^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
, then
|\varphi(N[v])|\leq(d+1)q-1<p
.
Conversely, if
|\varphi(N[v])|<p=q(d+1)
for some integer
v
, then by the Pigeonhole Principle there are two vertices
u,u^{\prime}\in N[v]
with
\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)\neq\emptyset
.
We conclude that a
(p:q)
-colouring of
G
with
p=q(d+1)
is dominating if and only if it is a proper colouring of
G^{2}
.
By fixing
q=1
, this implies that
\dom(G)\geq d+1\iff\chi(G^{2})\leq d+1
, and by considering all possible values of
q
, this implies that
\fdom(G)\geq d+1\iff\chi_{f}(G^{2})\leq d+1
.
The conclusion follows since
\dom(G)\leq\fdom(G)\leq d+1
by
4
and
\chi(G^{2})\geq\chi_{f}(G^{2})\geq\omega(G^{2})\geq d+1
.
∎

Given two graph
G
and
H
, an
H
-cover of
G
is a mapping
f\colon V(G)\to V(H)
such that
f
induces a bijection from
N_{G}(v)
to
N_{H}(f(v))
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
.
Observe that, if
H
is
d
-regular and there exists an
H
-cover of
G
, then
G
must also be
d
-regular.
Note that a proper
(d+1)
-colouring of
G^{2}
when
G
is a
d
-regular graph corresponds exactly to a
K_{d+1}
-cover of
G
. Deciding whether a
K_{d+1}
-cover of
G
exists is NP-hard (see
[
14
, Theorem 8]
), so deciding whether a
d
-regular graph
G
is domatically full is NP-hard.

In
[
9
, Proposition 3.1]
, the authors claim that, for every
d
-regular graph
G
,

\dom(G)=d+1\iff\fdom(G)=d+1.

(8)

This would directly imply that determining whether a
d
-regular graph is fdom-full is NP-hard for every integer
d\geq 3
.
Unfortunately, that statement (which also appears in
[
11
, Theorem 4]
) turns out to be false.

Proposition 29
.

The Coxeter graph is cubic and fdom-full, but not domatically full.

Proof.

Let
G
be the Coxeter graph, depicted in
Figure

11(a)
, which is known to be vertex-transitive.
G
is cubic, has
n=28
vertices, and has domination number
\gamma=7
(a dominating set of size
7
is shown in
Figure

11(a)
, and we must have
\gamma\geq n/4=7
since each vertex dominates at most
4
vertices). Since
G
is vertex transitive, we may apply
5
and conclude that
\fdom(G)=n/\gamma=4=\delta(G)+1
.

One can computationally verify that
\dom(G)=3
(or simply ask “domatic number of the Coxeter graph” as a query on the website wolframalpha.com). Therefore, we have a cubic graph which verifies
\fdom(G)=4
and
\dom(G)=3
.
∎

Proposition 30
.

The odd graph
KG(7,3)

Proof.

It is well known that the odd graph

Open problems

We conclude with a list of open problems that arise naturally from our work.

Problem 1
.

Given an integer
n\geq 5
, what is the smallest
q(n)
such that every
n
-vertex graph
G
with
\fdom(G)\geq 5/2
has a dominating
(5q(n):2q(n))
-colouring?

We can infer from the proof of
9
that
q(n)=\Omega(\log n)
, and we have claimed in the previous subsection that
q(n)=\exp(O(n))
. One can show that a fractional dominating colouring of the graph obtained from
K_{n}
by subdividing each edge into a hammock needs
\Omega(n)
colours, and so
q(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.

Proposition 32
.

Let
n\geq 2
, and let
G
be the graph obtained from
K_{n}
by subdividing each edge into a hammock. Then
\fdom(G)=5/2
, and if
\varphi
is a dominating
(5k:2k)
-colouring of
G
, for some integer
k\geq 1
, then
k\geq n/5
.

Proof.

Since
G
contains a hammock, by
10
one has
\fdom(G)\leq 5/2
. Since
G
is connected, has minimum degree
2
, and
G\notin\mathcal{B}
, by
2
one has
\fdom(G)\geq 5/2
.
We conclude that
\fdom(G)=5/2
.

Let
\varphi
be a dominating
(5k:2k)
-colouring of
G
.
Let
u,v
be a pair of vertices from the graph
K_{n}
before subdivision.
Let
u\text{--}x\text{--}y\text{--}v
and
u\text{--}z\text{--}v
be the two suspended paths of the hammock between
u
and
v
in
G
.
We have
\varphi(x)\cup\varphi(y)\cup\big(\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)\big)=[5k]
, so
|\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)|\geq 5k-|\varphi(x)|-|\varphi(y)|=k
.
We have
\varphi(z)\cup\big(\varphi(u)\cup\varphi(v)\big)=[5k]
, so
|\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)|\leq 4k-|\varphi(u)\cup\varphi(v)|\leq 4k-\big(5k-|\varphi(z)|\big)=k
.
We conclude that
|\varphi(u)\cap\varphi(v)|=k
.

Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{5k}
be the colour classes of
\varphi
; we claim that we must have
5k\geq n
. To see this, let
M
be the
n\times 5k
incidence matrix of the vertices and colours of
\varphi
. Then
MM^{T}=(b_{i,j})_{i,j\in[n]}
where
b_{i,i}=2k
and
b_{i,j}=k
if
i\neq j
. So
n=rank(B)\leq rank(M)\leq 5k
.
∎

2
indirectly provides a complete characterisation of graphs
G
such that
\fdom(G)<5/2
: those are all graphs with minimum degree
\delta(G)<2
, or which contain some
B\in\mathcal{B}
as an isolated subgraph. In particular, one can observe that
\fdom(G)\in\{1,2,7/3\}
in that case.
In
[
17
, Section 3]
, the authors characterise the class
\Gamma
of all graphs
G
such that
|V(G)|/\gamma(G)=5/2
. Extending this result to the fractional domatic number would be of interest.

Problem 2
.

Characterise the class
\mathscr{F}
of all graphs
G
such that
\fdom(G)=5/2
.

We note that these classes are incomparable. For instance,
K_{2,3}\in\Gamma\setminus\mathscr{F}
and
K_{2,4}\in\mathscr{F}\setminus\Gamma
.

Problem 3
.

Provide a full description of the set of possible values for
\fdom(G)
.

We note that the graphs
G_{n}
constructed in the proof of
12
have
\fdom(G_{n})=\frac{5n-2}{2n-1}
, so the aforementioned set contains infinitely many values in the interval
[5/2,5/2+\varepsilon]
for every
\varepsilon>0
.
On the other hand, there is no graph which has fractional domatic number in the range
(1,2)\cup(2,7/3)\cup(7/3,5/2)
.
In contrast, the possible range of values for the fractional chromatic number
\chi_{f}
is known to be
\big(\{1\}\cup[2,\infty)\big)\cap\mathbb{Q}
. Indeed, for every rational number
a/b\in\mathbb{Q}
with
a/b\geq 2
, one has
\chi_{f}(KG(a,b))=a/b
, where
KG(a,b)
is the Kneser Graph on the
b
-subsets of
[a]
. Moreover
\chi_{f}(G)=1
if
G
has no edge, and
\chi_{f}(G)\geq\omega(G)\geq 2
if
G
has at least one edge.

Problem 4
.

Does there exist a sharp complexity threshold for the
Fractional Dominating Colouring Problem
, i.e. is there some
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, given any graph
G
, one can decide in polynomial time whether
\fdom(G)\geq x-\varepsilon
, while it is NP-hard to decide whether
\fdom(G)\geq x+\varepsilon
?

One can infer from
2
and from
27
that
5/2\leq x\leq 3
, if such a threshold
x
exists.
We note that the
Fractional Proper Colouring Problem
has such a threshold, whose value is
2
: for all
\varepsilon>0
, given a graph
G
, it is NP-hard to decide whether
\chi_{f}(G)\leq 2+\varepsilon

[
19
, Theorem 3.9.2]
, while
\chi_{f}(G)\leq 2
if and only if
G
contains no odd cycle.

In what follows, given a class of graphs
\mathscr{G}
, we denote
\fdom(\mathscr{G})\coloneqq\inf\{\fdom(G):G\in\mathscr{G}\}
and
\dom(\mathscr{G})\coloneqq\min\{\dom(G):G\in\mathscr{G}\}
.

Problem 5
.

Is it possible to remove the planar hypothesis in
3
? If not, what is the value of

\lim_{g\to\infty}\fdom(\{G:\delta(G)=2,{\rm girth}(G)\geq g\})?

We have proved
3
by relying on the existence of long suspended paths in planar graphs of large girth with minimum degree
2
. This is not guaranteed in non-planar graphs of large girth: for instance, the
1
-subdivision of a cubic graph of large girth contains only suspended paths of length
2
. So a positive answer to this question would require new ideas.

For every integer
g\geq 3
, we denote
\textsc{Planar}[\delta=2,{\rm girth}\geq g]
the class of planar graphs of minimum degree
2
and girth at least
g
.

Problem 6
.

For every integer
g\geq 3
, compute the extremal value

\fdom(\textsc{Planar}[\delta=2,{\rm girth}\geq g]).

We have already established that this extremal value is
2
if
g\leq 4
,
7/3
if
5\leq g\leq 7
, and
5/2
if
8\leq g\leq 10
. We suspect that this extremal value is attained when
G
is a cycle, which yields the following conjecture.

Conjecture 33
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
, if
3k-1\leq g\leq 3k+1
, then

\fdom(\textsc{Planar}[\delta=2,{\rm girth}\geq g])=\frac{3k+1}{k+1}.

Given a graph class
\mathscr{G}
and an integer
d\geq 1
, we denote
\mathscr{G}[\delta\geq d]
the family of graphs in
\mathscr{G}
of minimum degree at least
d
.
When
\mathscr{G}[\delta\geq d]\neq\emptyset
for every
d\geq 1
, we say that
\mathscr{G}
is
\dom
-bounded
if

\dom(\mathscr{G}[\delta\geq d])\underset{d\to\infty}{\to}\infty,

and we say that
\mathscr{G}
is
linearly
\dom
-bounded
if

\dom(\mathscr{G}[\delta\geq d])=\Theta(d).

A similar definition can be made for a
(linearly)
\fdom
-bounded class
. We note that, by
1
, the class
\mathscr{G}
of all graphs is
\fdom
-bounded, since we have

\fdom(\mathscr{G}[\delta\geq d])\underset{d\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{d}{\ln d}.

Problem 7
.

Explicit some non-trivial classes of graphs that are (linearly)
\dom
/
\fdom
-bounded.

We observe that if
\mathscr{G}
contains the class of bipartite graphs, then
\mathscr{G}
is neither
\dom
-bounded (because of
9
with
q_{0}\coloneqq 1
) nor linearly
\fdom
-bounded (because of
8
). The same holds if
\mathscr{G}
contains the class of split graphs (because of Remarks
1
and
2
).
