Evaluation of some anions in groundwater in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and human health risk assessment of nitrate and fluoride
Authors: Gamal A. E. Mostafa, Abdulaziz S. Alhusain, Maha F. El-Tohamy, Haitham AlRabiah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Groundwater is a vital source of water for human and agricultural use in many parts of the world. The purpose of this research was to establish the quality of groundwater in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, as well as the human health concerns associated with it. We collected and examined groundwater samples for pH, EC, TDS, CaCO3, fluoride (F−), chloride (Cl−), sulfate (SO42−), and nitrate (NO3−). The ion chromatography conductometric detection method was constructed to determine fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate in groundwater. The suggested method worked well for the anions that were being studied; it had a high coefficient of determination (r2 > 0.998) and average recoveries for all analytes that were between 97.5% and 99.0%, with a range of error of 0.77 to 2.37%. Fluoride concentrations were detected between 0.001 and 0.14 mg/L, which are within the acceptable limit by several organizations. Chloride was measured in the range of 17.1 to 966.5 mg/L, with some samples above the limits. The influence on sulfate ranged from 2.0 to 1136.0 mg/L, with several samples exceeding the limits. In contrast, with nitrate levels ranging from 1.4 to 5.0 mg/L, the majority of the samples fall within the acceptable range. The overall intake of fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate is 0.00605, 138.911, 65.515, and 1.19, respectively, which is lower than the recommended daily consumption except for chloride. The groundwater sample contains fluoride and nitrate with HQ values less than one: 0.000064–0.0641 and 0.033654–0.120192. Humans in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, do not pose a health risk when digesting or absorbing groundwater fluoride or nitrate.

Introduction

Freshwater resources are critical for the survival of all living organisms in a variety of habitat systems on a global scale.1,2Consequently, these resources act as the primary source of drinking water for sustainable development, especially in semi-arid and arid areas that experience limited or no rainfall. As a result, the availability of fresh water resources, particularly groundwater resources, decreases. Groundwater is an essential element of Saudi Arabia’s water supply, particularly in a nation with little surface water resources. It constitutes more than 80% of the potable water utilized in urban regions.3,4Groundwater resources are essential to multiple industries, such as agriculture and manufacturing. Groundwater is a vital resource that influences both the lives and livelihoods of humans and the expansion of the world economy5,6. However, geogenic activities such as chemical weathering of rocks and ion exchanges, as well as anthropogenic processes such as residential wastewater release, man-induced saltwater interference, and fertilizer and pesticide use, all have a substantial impact on groundwater geochemistry7–9. Human interaction in the environment frequently causes the accumulation of chemical compounds in groundwater, such as nitrate, fluoride, sulfate, and chloride, at levels that are hazardous to human health10,11. Groundwater with an excess of these anions can have a number of negative consequences, both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic.

Consequently, assessing the present condition of groundwater resources regarding availability, quality, and quantity is essential. Groundwater’s environmental impact is critical for assessing its water quality and suitability for human use, as well as for analyzing potential health concerns for humans.

Cyanosis and methemoglobinemia, for instance, can result from excessive nitrate10,11 whereas fluoride in water beyond the maximum allowable content can cause bone fractures and dental fluorosis12. Increased sulfate consumption may be detrimental since it irritates the lungs and damages lung health, causing catharsis while also leading to dehydration13. Chloride toxicity increases with increased ingestion of high chloridconcentrations, resulting in hypertension and, in severe circumstances, heart failure13. As a result, major inorganic anions such as fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate can alter the appropriateness of groundwater for human consumption and have an impact on consumer health. There have been reports of several analytical procedures for identifying inorganic anions, including spectrophotometry14,15, HPLC16,17, and capillary electrophoresis18,19.The main responsibility of analytical chemists is the development of new techniques and the enhancement of current ones for water analysis. Therefore, the assessment of inorganic anions and cations in water is necessary. Ion chromatography (IC) has largely supplanted most wet chemical techniques used in water analysis20–22. Several regulatory agencies, including the British Pharmacopeia (BP), the United States Pharmacopeia (USP), the Association of Official Agricultural Chemists (AOAC), and others, have approved and widely employed IC, leading in numerous developments in this technique throughout the years23,24.

Rizwan et al. (2016)25 measured bromate, nitrate, and nitrite using ultra-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry. The investigation found that nitrate levels are at the permitted range, but bromate concentrations in some samples are greater than the recommended limit. Sahal et al. (2008)26 investigated the detection of cations and anions in water from various stations as well as groundwater. This investigation reveals that cation and anion detection levels are within the WHO and SASO recommendations. Al-Otaibi and his team carried out another study in 201227. This study focused on documenting the physical and chemical properties of water, including several cations and anions (chloride, sulfate, ammonia, and nitrate)27. Al-Barakah et al. (2017)28 conducted a study to determine the quality of Zamzm water in Mecca, Saudi Arabia. This research found that the majority of the metals and anions in the water sample are at the recommended levels, and the water quality is class I.

IC is more reliable for routine limit testing and regular monitoring of many inorganic analytes because it has high sensitivity, specificity, and rapid processing time, is reusable, fully automated, and can be used in multiple areas.29,30 Several factors influence the dependability of IC approaches, including sample preparation, detectors, stationary phase mode, and even analyte type, all of which influence method development31. As a result, IC may be easily modulated to increase selectivity and specificity. Despite the wide range of samples being analyzed by IC, environmental analysis remains the most common application area in terms of instrument used and total number of samples evaluated. Inorganic anions are the most important in environmental IC applications. The fundamental reason for this is a lack of alternative methods for anion analysis, as opposed to cations, which have a wide range of instrumental techniques accessible. The current investigation involved the simultaneous determination of trace anions (sulfate, chloride, floride, and nitrate). The following is the established basis for achieving the subsequent goals: (a) The first step is to examine the chemical characteristics of groundwater (b) and compare it to national and international standards. (c) Determine the possible noncarcinogenic health risk caused by the groundwater channel to the adult human population. The results of this study may open up new avenues for groundwater research and water quality, perhaps benefiting public health in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

Apparatus

We used the DIONEX ICS-5000® system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dionex ICS series AS-AP, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), which included two Dionex ICS-5000 gradient pumps and a Dionex ICS-5000+® conductivity detector from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

A guard column, Dionex® IonPac CG16® (5 × 50 mm), was linked to the Dionex® IonPac® AS19 (4 × 50 mm) from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Chromeleon 6.8 software was used to control the equipment and acquire data. The mobile phase was 20 mM KOH with a flow rate of 1 mL/min, and the separation was done at 30 °C. The pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were measured using a HANNA pH meter (pH 211) and a thermo-orinon conductometr (Orion 145 A+). The Mille-Q integrated water purification system (Millipore, Michigan, USA) produced deionized water. Microsoft Office (Microsoft Office\root\Office16\EXCEL.EXE) was used for calculation.

Chemicals and reagents

Analytical-grade KOH (Milford, USA) and a standard solution of 1000 mg/L of fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate (Thermoscientific, USA). Working solutions were prepared from the stock solution by diluting it with deionized water. The solvents and other chemicals that were used were all analytical grade. All other chemicals and solvents used were of analytical grade. All eluents were degassed using an ultrasonic bath and filtered using a Millipore 0.45 μm filter. We used a calibration curve to estimate the concentration of each anion in the samples and build a link between analyte concentration and peak area, also known as peak high. Each sample was examined in triplicate.

Study area and samples collection

The study was conducted in Riyadh, covering an area of approximately 1,973 square kilometers (about 762 square miles). It is situated in the middle area of the country, namely within Najd. It is located at around 24.7136° N latitude and 46.6753° E longitude (Fig. 1). The desert environment affects its climate, characterized by exceedingly hot summers and temperate winters. Water samples were collected from various stations and artesian wells located in Ryidah City (Table 1). Thirty-three groundwater samples from various stations and artesian well sites across the city of Riyadh in all directions (north, south, east, west, and downtown) were collected, referred to as plants (S1-S11), as shown in Table 1. The groundwater samples were collected in 1-L polypropylene bottles, soaked overnight in diluted nitric acid (10%), washed several times with deionized water, and then dried at room temperature. Then, wash the bottles with water samples several times before taking the sample to ensure that there are no foreign particles. The samples were collected randomly from each location, covering the area under test. The samples were kept in a refrigerator at 4 °C before being transported for analysis, as per the specified procedure32. Validation of the IC method

The recovery test and precision were used to determine the correctness of the proposed method. We made a pure solution of the tested anion in deionized water from the prepared stock solution, then estimated concentrations using calibration curves built with the studied anions, fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate (five points for each anon) standards33. We validated the analytical procedure for the quantitative analysis of the investigated elements (fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate) in ground water by looking at the following parameters: working and linear ranges, limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), accuracy, and precision. We validated the analytical method using ISO standards34,35, designating results less than the limit of detection as undiscovered (ND). Values that exceed the detection limit but fall short of the quantification limit are deemed below the limit of quantification (LOQ). Health risk assessment

The US Environmental Protection Agency36,37 (USEPA, 1989, 1993) proposed a quantitative methodology for health risk assessment. Fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate are the major pollutants, and we consider them non-carcinogenic health risks to the populace. Therefore, the following equation computes the exposure dose of fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate through direct water consumption in terms of estimated daily intake (EDI).

Where EDI is the estimated daily intake dose (mg/kg/day), Cfn is the concentration of a particular contaminant (anions), and IRw represents the groundwater ingestion rate in L/day (2.5 L/day for adults). EFr is the exposure frequency in days/years (365 days); ED represents exposure duration in years (64 years); and BW is body weight in kg (65 kg). AT indicates the average exposure time (23360 days)36,37. Where EDI is the estimated daily intake dose (mg/kg/day), RfD represents the reference dose for fluoride and nitrate, which are 0.06 mg/kg/day and 1.6 mg/kg/day, respectively37,38. If HQ < 1, there is no potential health risk to the exposed population or no negative effects, and if HQ > 1, there may be a potential health danger39. The hazard index (HI) was designed to calculate the risk to human health from exposure to different anions36. The hazard index is determined using Eq. (2), which is the sum of the hazard quotients fluoride and nitrate40. A total (HI) of less than or equal to 1 implies that there are no adverse health risks to humans, whereas a value above 1 is considered a higher health risk or unsafe for humans36,37.

Chromatographic conditions

As shown in Table 1, the chromatographic conditions were optimized. The stationary phase was the Dionex® IonPac® AS19 column (50 × 4 mm), and the mobile phase generally consists of 20% KOH, using conductivity detection, which has a low background interference and great selectivity. The conductometric detection was performed by inserting the conductivity cell at a temperature of 30° C and a current of 50 mA. The mobile phase contained 20% KOH at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. As a result, IC is regarded as a more environmentally friendly chromatography process because it does not use any harmful or hazardous organic modifiers. The complete set of condition parameters has been included in Table 1. The results showed that using 20 mM of KOH as a mobile phase improved resolution for fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate within 14 min.

Method of validaton

The validation of the approach was guided by ICH standards34,35. The developed method involves the following: system suitability and specificity, linearity, recovery, accuracy, and precision.

Optimization of chromatographic conditions

Ion chromatography is one of the most popular analytical methods for determining inorganic anions. Separation and determination are based on the separation phase (column), mobile phase, and flow rate. We screened these three variables during the optimization of chromatographic conditions, using mixed anion standard solutions. We used the IonPac® AS19 analytical columns for anion separation, respectively. The mobile phase flow rate was fixed to 1.0 mL per minute. The best way to separate fluoride, chloride, nitrate, and sulfate was with a mobile phase of 20 mM KOH. The separation of the studied anions is admirable, with symmetrical peaks and a suitable time for separation. The results indicated that a 20 mM mobile phase could improve resolution for fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate within 14 min.

Calibration range

The separation of the examined anions at optimal conditions reveals excellent linearity between the analyte concentration and the peak area, or peak height33. Five linearity standard solutions, with concentrations ranging from 0.25 to 40 mg/L for fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate, were created. The calibration graphs were created by graphing the concentrations of the examined anions against their peak area or peak height responses. The calibration curves had appropriate regression coefficients (R2 = 0.998–0.999). Recovery

The recoveries of spiking mixture of fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate of 1, 10, 20, and 5 mg/ were measured three times in a pure deionized water sample33. Table 2 shows that the mean recoveries for all analytes ranged from 97.0 to 99%, with a standard deviation of 0.77 -2.37%. All analytes performed satisfactorily in water samples, and the results were within the usually accepted range.

Accuracy and precision

The accuracy of the suggested approach was determined by comparing the calculated and true concentrations33. We attained accuracy by injecting a predetermined concentration of the analyte standard and measuring the recovery of the results. Table 2 shows the percentage recovery of the proposed method. The results showed that the values met the acceptance requirements. The precision of an analytical procedure is determined by the degree of conformity between individual test findings. Table 2 provides the RSD values for the investigated approach. The RSD value ranged from 0.77 to 2.37%. The results show that the approach has excellent reproducibility for both recovery and RSD values.

Non-carcinogenic risk

The Hazard Quotient (HQ), which is the ratio of the pollutants determined dose to a reference dose level, is used to quantify the health concerns associated with people using groundwater. The HI value expresses the combined non-carcinogenic effects of multiple components. If HQ > 1, the exposed population will most certainly suffer a negative consequence44. The HQ values for fluoride and nitrate in the groundwater sample are 0.000064–0.0641 and 0.033654–0.120192, respectively. The results show that the HQ of fluoride and nitrate in groundwater samples is less than one, implying that humans in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, do not pose a significant health risk from digesting or absorbing fluoride or nitrate in groundwater. Conclusions

The current project entails determining the presence of several anions (fluoride, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate) in groundwater in Riyadh City. The investigation revealed levels of nitrate, sulfate, chloride, and fluoride that were below the hazard standards set by the USEPA, WHO, and SASO. On the other hand, we measured and confirmed the acceptable physical properties of groundwater, such as pH, EC, TDS, and carbonate, in accordance with USEPA regulations. The study recommends continuously monitoring specific anions in diverse groundwater sources. Organizations responsible for groundwater safety must make extensive efforts to assure the safety of the groundwater under study. However, we discovered no anions that could be harmful to human health. The estimated dealy intak EDI and hazardus quation HQ values indicate that the concentration of fluoride and nitrate in groundwater has no effect on human health in the study area. The suggested method determines the studied anions simultaneously and with greater precision and selectivity than existing analytical techniques for routine anion analysis. We recommend the ion chromatographic method as a standard method for analyzing water quality.
