Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation for energy eigenstates of SU(2)-symmetric quantum many-body systems
Jae Dong Noh, Aleksander Lasek, Jade LeSchack, Nicole Yunger Halpern

Abstract
The fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT) is a fundamental result in statistical mechanics. It stipulates that, if perturbed out of equilibrium, a system responds at a rate proportional to a thermal-equilibrium property. Applications range from particle diffusion to electrical-circuit noise. To prove the FDT, one must prove that common thermal states obey a symmetry property, the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) relation. Energy eigenstates of certain quantum many-body systems were recently proved to obey a KMS relation. The proof relies on the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH), which explains how such systems thermalize internally. This KMS relation contains a finite-size correction that scales as the inverse system size. Non-Abelian symmetries conflict with the ETH, so a non-Abelian ETH was proposed recently. Using it, we derive a KMS relation for SU(2)-symmetric quantum many-body systems’ energy eigenstates. The finite-size correction scales as usual under certain circumstances but can be polynomially larger in others, we argue. We support the ordinary-scaling result numerically, simulating a Heisenberg chain of 16–24 qubits. The numerics, limited by computational capacity, indirectly support the larger correction. This work helps extend into nonequilibrium physics the effort, recently of interest across quantum physics, to identify how non-Abelian symmetries may alter conventional thermodynamics.



The fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT) forms a pillar of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics
Kubo et al. 1985
;
Mazenko 2006
. The theorem arises in linear-response theory, describing how quickly a system responds to a perturbation. One quantifies the responsiveness with a second derivative of a free energy. This derivative is proportional to a two-time thermal correlation function, according to the FDT. Knowing an equilibrium property, therefore, one can infer about a nonequilibrium response. Famous instances of the FDT include the Einstein–Smoluchowski relation, which relates a particle’s mobility to its diffusion constant and temperature. Classical and quantum systems obey the FDT. Yet the quantum FDT governs a system that can be in a thermal quantum state. A symmetry property of this state, called the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) relation, underlies the FDT.

Thermal states are mixed; in contrast, closed, isolated quantum systems are in pure states. How to extend the FDT to such systems is therefore unclear. Yet generic such many-body systems thermalize internally: a small subsystem will likely approach a thermal state, the rest of the system serving as an effective environment. This thermalization has drawn attention across theory and experiment recently
Luca D’Alessio and Rigol 2016
;
Gogolin and Eisert 2016

We introduce a
fine-grained KMS relation
obeyed by quantum many-body systems subject to SU(2) symmetry and the non-Abelian ETH. The relation depends not only on a temperature, as usual, but also on analogous parameters associated with spin (or, more generally, angular-momentum) quantum numbers.
The proof requires a certain parameter regime, as well as approximations of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients introduced by the non-Abelian symmetry. Under certain conditions, we argue, the FDT’s finite-size correction is

, as in the absence of any non-Abelian symmetry. Under other conditions, we contend, the finite-size correction can be polynomially larger. Hence non-Abelian symmetry appears able to alter a standard thermodynamic result by an amount polynomial in the system size.

To support our analytical arguments, we numerically simulate a one-dimensional (1D) chain of 16–24 qubits. We directly observe evidence for the
O(N^{-1})
scaling. Limited by computational capacity, we observe indirect evidence for the larger correction. This work shows how non-Abelian symmetry may sometimes preserve, and sometimes alter, a result crucial for nonequilibrium statistical mechanics.

VI
Outlook

We have derived and numerically supported a fine-grained KMS relation obeyed by energy eigenstates of quantum many-body systems subject to non-Abelian symmetries. The proof relies on the recently introduced non-Abelian ETH. Under certain conditions, we have argued, the KMS relation’s finite-size correction scales as
O(N^{-1})
, as usual (as in the absence of any non-Abelian symmetry). Under other conditions, the correction may be polynomially larger. Hence charges’ noncommutation may enable an anomalously large deviation from a result applied in nonequilibrium statistical physics. This result exemplifies quantum thermodynamics, given noncommutation’s role in quantum phenomena such as uncertainty relations. Our result helps extend into nonequilibrium thermodynamics the rapidly growing program of identifying how charges’ noncommutation influences dynamics
Li et al. 2023
;
Majidy et al. 2023c
;
Marvian 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Varikuti and Bandyopadhyay 2024
;
Dabholkar and Alet 2024
;
Saraidaris et al. 2024
;
Zadnik et al. 2024
;
Bai and Ren 2024
;
Cataldi et al. 2025
.

Our results differ from two related results. First, Manzano
et al.
proved that charges’ noncommutation lowers linear–response entropy production
Manzano et al. 2022
. Yet their result concerns the Onsager relations, not the FDT. Their setup differs, featuring two thermal systems at different effective temperatures. Nor does many-body physics feature in their discovery, which involves no finite-size correction.

Second, Murthy
et al.
proved that, if a Hamiltonian
H
and a
T^{(k)}_{q}
obey the non-Abelian ETH, the operator’s time-averaged expectation value can differ from its thermal expectation value by an anomalously large correction
Murthy et al. 2023
. The result derives from the non-Abelian ETH’s first term. In contrast, our result derives from the second term, which encodes how
\left\langle T^{(k)}_{q}\right\rangle
fluctuates over time. Also,
Murthy et al. 2023
contains no numerical evidence and invokes an assumption that we do not.

Our work establishes five opportunities for future research. First, we argued analytically that the fine-grained KMS relation contains an anomalously large correction under certain conditions, including
s_{\alpha}=O(N^{1/2})
.
Other parameter regimes might admit of anomalously large corrections, too. So might non-Abelian symmetry groups other than SU(2).

Second, one might observe our KMS relation’s anomalous correction by simulating larger systems. The simulations may be classical. However, third, experiments can test our KMS relation. The correlator equals an average over a Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobability distribution, as all correlators do
Yunger Halpern et al. 2018
. Quasiprobabilities resemble probabilities but can violate certain axioms of probability theory. Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobabilities can assume negative and nonreal values. Yet one can measure Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobabilities in many ways
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2024
;
Wagner et al. 2024
. Furthermore, noncommuting-charge thermodynamics was recently tested in a trapped-ion experiment
Kranzl et al. 2023
. Other feasible platforms include superconducting qubits and ultracold atoms
Yunger Halpern et al. 2020
;
Yunger Halpern and Majidy 2022
.

Third, our work leverages the non-Abelian ETH’s off-diagonal term, expected to illuminate thermalization dynamics
Murthy et al. 2023
. By extending this work, one may elucidate dynamical questions—for example, does charges’ noncommutation slow thermalization? Does it enlarge the fluctuations, undergone over time, by a local operator’s expectation value?

Fourth, Appendix
B
shows that SU(2) symmetry supports a nonzero static correlator across a relatively wide parameter regime. A static correlator never decays and so evidences memory. We will quantify this memory’s capabilities in future work
Yunger Halpern and Noh prep
.

Appendix A
Review of the conventional KMS relation and FDT for quantum systems

This appendix contains a pedagogical three-step review of the quantum KMS relation and FDT. In App.
A 1
, we derive the Kubo formula for a quantum system’s response function. In App.
A 2
, we derive the conventional KMS relation. Combining these two ingredients, we prove the conventional FDT in App.
A 3
. The overall derivation appears in many references; we follow Sections 3.3.2–3.3.6 of
Cugliandolo 2023
.

Before embarking upon the derivation, we review the reason for the name
fluctuation–dissipation theorem.
Kubo explains eloquently in his review
Kubo 1966
. For simplicity, consider a classical statistical mechanical system, such as an electrically charged particle in a fluid. In equilibrium, the particle undergoes Brownian motion: fluid particles bump into the charged particle, which random-walks around the fluid. That is, the charged particle’s position fluctuates. Now, consider applying an electric field. It forces the charged particle along a particular direction. To move there, the charged particle knocks aside fluid particles, which slow it down. That is, the fluid particles exert a drag force, which dissipates energy from the charged particle. This dissipation shares its origin with the equilibrium fluctuations—collisions between the charged particle and fluid particles—so the two phenomena must be related. The FDT quantifies this relationship.

This appendix concerns a quantum system of the following type. The system has observables
A
and
B
. From time 0 to time
t^{\prime}
, and from time
t^{\prime}+\epsilon
to time
t
, the system evolves under an unperturbed Hamiltonian
H
.
\epsilon
is infinitesimally small. During the infinitesimal interval, the system evolves under the perturbed Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}\coloneqq H-hB
. Later, we will consider the zero-field limit (
h=0
). At time 0, the system begins in a state
\rho
. In App.
A 1
,
\rho
is arbitrary; afterward, it is thermal, as described in App.
A 3
. We set Planck’s reduced constant to
\hbar=1

A 1
Derivation of Kubo formula

This section addresses the following question about the time-
t

A
. Consider the expectation value’s rate of change with respect to
h\epsilon
in the zero-field limit. This rate quantifies the system’s response to the perturbation
-hB
. What is that response?

To answer this question, we introduce the expectation value. First, we define the unitary that encodes the full time evolution:

\displaystyle U_{B}(t,0)\coloneqq e^{-iH(t-t^{\prime}-\epsilon)}\,e^{-i(H-hB)\epsilon}\,e^{-iHt^{\prime}}\,.

(A1)

This unitary evolves
A
in the Heisenberg picture to

A(t)\coloneqq U_{B}^{\dagger}(t,0)\,A\,U_{B}(t,0)
.
The time-evolved operator has the expectation value

\displaystyle\left\langle A(t)\right\rangle_{B}\coloneqq{\rm Tr}\left(U_{B}^{\dagger}(t,0)\,A\,U_{B}(t,0)\,\rho\right).

(A2)

In terms of this expectation value, we define the response function:

\displaystyle R_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{\partial\left\langle A(t)\right\rangle_{B}}{\partial(h\epsilon)}\Bigg\lvert_{h=0}\Theta(t-t^{\prime}).

(A3)

The Heaviside function encodes causality: the system can respond to perturbations applied in the past, not the future.

Let us calculate the response function. By the product rule,

\displaystyle R_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})=\left[{\rm Tr}\left(\frac{\partial U_{B}^{\dagger}(t,0)}{\partial(h\epsilon)}\,A\,U_{B}(t,0)\right)\Bigg\lvert_{h=0}+{\rm Tr}\left(U_{B}^{\dagger}(t,0)\,A\,\frac{\partial U_{B}(t,0)}{\partial(h\epsilon)}\right)\Bigg\lvert_{h=0}\,\right]\Theta(t-t^{\prime}).

(A4)

We approximate the derivatives to first order in
h\epsilon
. To do so, we approximate the central factor on the right-hand side of Eq. (
A1
). This exponential of a sum would equal a product of exponentials, if
H
commuted with
B
. The two operators might not commute, however. According to the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula,

\displaystyle e^{-iH\epsilon}\,e^{ih\epsilon B}=e^{-i(H-hB)\epsilon+\frac{ih\epsilon^{2}}{2}\,[H,B]+\frac{ih\epsilon^{3}}{12}\,[H,[H,B]]+\ldots}

(A5)

The largest correction is of
O(h\epsilon^{2}[H,B])
, which we neglect.
Next, we Taylor-approximate the
e^{ih\epsilon B}\,.
The unitary formula (
A1
) becomes

\displaystyle U_{B}(t,0)=e^{-iH(t-t^{\prime})}\left\{\mathbbm{1}+ih\epsilon B+O\left([h\epsilon]^{2}\right)\right\}e^{-iHt^{\prime}}\,.

(A6)

This result implies the unitary’s rate of change with respect to
h\epsilon
in the zero-field limit:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial(h\epsilon)}\,U_{B}(t,0)\bigg\lvert_{h=0}=i\,e^{-iH(t-t^{\prime})}\,B\,e^{-iHt^{\prime}}\,.

(A7)

Calculating the Hermitian conjugate, too, is worthwhile:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial(h\epsilon)}\,U_{B}^{\dagger}(t,0)\bigg\lvert_{h=0}=-i\,e^{iHt^{\prime}}\,B\,e^{iH(t-t^{\prime})}\,.

(A8)

Let us substitute into Eq. (
A4
):

\displaystyle R_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left[-i{\rm Tr}\left(e^{iHt^{\prime}}\,B\,e^{iH(t-t^{\prime})}\,A\,e^{-iHt}\,\rho\right)+i{\rm Tr}\left(e^{iHt}\,A\,e^{-iH(t-t^{\prime})}\,B\,e^{-iHt^{\prime}}\,\rho\right)\right]\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})

(A9)

\displaystyle=i\left[{\rm Tr}\bm{(}A(t)\,B(t^{\prime})\,\rho\bm{)}-{\rm Tr}\bm{(}B(t^{\prime})\,A(t)\,\rho\right)\bm{)}\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})

(A10)

\displaystyle=i{\rm Tr}\left(\left[A(t),B(t^{\prime})\right]\rho\right)\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})

(A11)

\displaystyle\equiv i\left\langle\left[A(t),B(t^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle_{\rho}\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(A12)

We have defined
\left\langle X\right\rangle_{\rho}\coloneqq{\rm Tr}(X\rho)
. The final equality gives the Kubo formula for the response function.

A 2
Derivation of conventional KMS relation

The following considerations motivate the KMS relation’s derivation. The Kubo formula (
A12
) expresses the time-domain response function
R_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})
in terms of a time-domain correlator
\left\langle\left[A(t),B(t^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle_{\rho}
. The FDT will express a frequency-domain response function in terms of a frequency-domain correlator. The latter correlator obeys a symmetry property used to derive the FDT. That symmetry property is the KMS relation.

First, we introduce an assumption and notation. From now on, we assume that
\rho
is the canonical thermal state,

\rho_{\rm th}\coloneqq e^{-\beta H}/Z
.
The partition function is defined as
Z\coloneqq{\rm Tr}(e^{-\beta H})
.
The two-time thermal correlator has the form

\displaystyle C_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})\coloneqq\left\langle A(t)\,B(t^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\rho_{\rm th}}\,.

(A13)

Now, we prove a time-domain version of the KMS relation,

\displaystyle C_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})=C_{BA}(t^{\prime},t+i\beta).

(A14)

We substitute into the left-hand side from the definition (
A13
). Then, we substitute in the forms of
A(t)
and
\rho_{\rm th}
:

\displaystyle C_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{Z}\,{\rm Tr}\left(e^{iHt}\,A\,e^{-iHt}\,B(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta H}\right).

(A15)

Let us cycle the
B(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta H}
to the trace’s lefthand side. The thermal exponential combines with the
e^{iHt}
into
e^{iH(t+i\beta)}\,.
Then, we insert

\mathbbm{1}=e^{-\beta H}\,e^{\beta H}
rightward of
A
. The
e^{\beta H}
combines with the
e^{-iHt}
. The
e^{-\beta H}
commutes through them, to form a new thermal exponential on the right-hand side of the trace’s argument:

\displaystyle C_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})={\rm Tr}\left(B(t^{\prime})\,e^{iH(t+i\beta)}\,A\,e^{-iH(t+i\beta)}\,e^{-\beta H}\right)={\rm Tr}\bm{(}B(t^{\prime})\,A(t+i\beta)\,\rho_{\rm th}\bm{)}=C_{BA}(t^{\prime},t+i\beta).

(A16)

We have proved Eq. (
A14
).

We now take advantage of a property of the thermal state.
\rho_{\rm th}
is stationary, or invariant under time evolution.
C_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})
therefore is stationary, depending on
t
and
t^{\prime}
only through the difference
t-t^{\prime}
. Abusing notation, we redefine
C_{AB}(t,t^{\prime})
as
C_{AB}(t-t^{\prime})
. The time-domain KMS relation becomes

C_{AB}(t-t^{\prime})=C_{BA}(t^{\prime}-t-i\beta)
. Let us relabel
t-t^{\prime}
as
t
:

\displaystyle C_{AB}(t)=C_{BA}(-t-i\beta).

(A17)

From this relation, we derive the frequency-domain KMS relation. We define the Fourier transform and inverse Fourier transform as in solid-state physics and in
Luca D’Alessio and Rigol 2016
;
Noh et al. 2020
:

\displaystyle\bar{f}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,f(x)\,e^{ikx}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\,\bar{f}(k)\,e^{-ikx}\,.

(A18)

Fourier-transforming each side of Eq. (
A17
) yields

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{AB}(\Omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,C_{BA}(-t-i\beta)\,e^{i\Omega t}\,.

(A19)

We multiply the right-hand side by

1=e^{\beta\Omega}\,e^{-\beta\Omega}=e^{\beta\Omega}\,e^{i\omega(i\beta)}\,,

incorporating the final factor into the
e^{i\Omega t}
:

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{AB}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Omega}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,C_{BA}(-t-i\beta)\,e^{i\Omega(t+i\beta)}

(A20)

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Omega}\,\bar{C}_{BA}(-\Omega).

(A21)

We have derived the (frequency-domain) KMS relation.

A 3
Derivation of conventional FDT

We derive the FDT using the Kubo formula (
A12
) and the KMS relation (
A21
). First, we introduce notation for antisymmetrized and symmetrized thermal correlators:

\displaystyle C_{[A,B]}(t,t^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\,\left\langle\left[A(t),B(t^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle_{\rho_{\rm th}}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\{A,B\}}(t,t^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\,\left\langle\left\{A(t),B(t^{\prime})\right\}\right\rangle_{\rho_{\rm th}}\,.

(A22)

The notation
\{X,Y\}\equiv XY+YX
represents the anticommutator. Why have we introduced the correlators (
A22
)? The Kubo relation expresses the response function in terms of
C_{[A,B]}
. We aim to replace this correlator with
C_{\{A,B\}}
for the following reason. Consider reinstating
\hbar\neq 1
. In the classical limit (as
\beta\hbar\omega\to 0
), we should recover the classical FDT from the quantum FDT.

We interrelate the correlators as follows. Both are stationary; so, abusing notation again, we redefine

C_{[A,B]}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv C_{[A,B]}(t-t^{\prime})
and

C_{\{A,B\}}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv C_{\{A,B\}}(t-t^{\prime})
.
We then Fourier-transform the equations (
A22
), beginning with the commutator equation. Since

C_{[A,B]}(t-t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}[\left\langle A(t)\,B(t^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\rho_{\rm th}}-\left\langle B(t^{\prime})\,A(t)\right\rangle_{\rho_{\rm th}}]
,

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{[A,B]}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\bar{C}_{AB}(\Omega)-\bar{C}_{BA}(-\Omega)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{-\beta\Omega}\right)\,\bar{C}_{AB}(\Omega).

(A23)

The final equality follows from the KMS relation, Eq. (
A21
).
Similarly, the second equation in (
A22
) Fourier-transforms to

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+e^{-\beta\Omega}\right)\,\bar{C}_{AB}(\Omega).

(A24)

Let us divide Eq. (
A23
) by Eq. (
A24
) and rearrange factors:

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{[A,B]}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\tanh\left(\beta\Omega/2\right)\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega).

(A25)

To combine this equality with the Kubo formula (
A12
), we must Fourier-transform the latter. We do so in multiple steps. First, we represent the Kubo formula’s correlator as an inverse Fourier transform. The dummy
\Omega^{\prime}
serves as the variable conjugate to
t-t^{\prime}
. Then, we substitute in from Eq. (
A25
):

\displaystyle R_{AB}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=i2C_{[A,B]}(t-t^{\prime})\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})=2i\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\times\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega^{\prime}\,\bar{C}_{[A,B]}(\Omega^{\prime})\,e^{-i\Omega^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})}

(A26)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\Theta(t-t^{\prime})}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega^{\prime}\,\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\Omega^{\prime}}{2}\right)\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega^{\prime})\,e^{-i\Omega^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})}\,.

(A27)

We now Fourier-transform each side, using the dummy
\Omega
as the variable conjugate to
t-t^{\prime}
. The left-hand side Fourier-transforms to
\tilde{R}_{AB}(\Omega)
. The right-hand side requires detailed evaluation:

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{AB}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\Omega(t-t^{\prime})}\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega^{\prime}\,\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\Omega^{\prime}}{2}\right)\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega^{\prime})\,e^{-i\Omega^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})}\,

(A28)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega^{\prime}\,\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\Omega^{\prime}}{2}\right)\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\,e^{i(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})(t-t^{\prime})}\,.

(A29)

We have interchanged the integrals. The Heaviside function cuts off the final integral from below at
t-t^{\prime}=0
. That integral becomes, via complex-analysis identities,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})(t-t^{\prime})}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{i}{(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})+i\varepsilon}=\pi\,\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})+i\frac{P}{\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}}\,.

(A30)

P
denotes the principal part. We substitute the final expression into Eq. (
A29
) and evaluate the remaining integral. Then, we split up the response function as

\bar{R}_{AB}(\Omega)=\bar{R}^{(1)}_{AB}(\Omega)+i\bar{R}^{(2)}_{AB}(\Omega)
, wherein

\displaystyle\bar{R}^{(1)}_{AB}(\Omega)=\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\Omega}{2}\right)\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}

(A31)

\displaystyle\bar{R}^{(2)}_{AB}(\Omega)=-\frac{2P}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}}\,\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\Omega^{\prime}}{2}\right)\bar{C}_{\{A,B\}}(\Omega^{\prime}).

(A32)

Equation (
A31
) forms the conventional quantum FDT.

B 1
Modified NATS

The multiqubit system’s NATS is defined as follows

Lostaglio et al. 2017

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\rm NATS}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\left(-\beta H-\sum_{a=x,y,z}\mu_{a}S_{a}\right)/\tilde{Z}=\exp\left(-\beta H-\mu S_{z}\right)/\tilde{Z}.

(B1)

The final equality follows from pointing the

\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{x},\mu_{y},\mu_{z})
[equivalently, parallel to
{\rm Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{\rm NATS}\vec{S})

. The partition function

\tilde{Z}\coloneqq{\rm Tr}\bm{(}\exp\left(-\beta H-\mu S_{z}\right)\bm{)}

normalizes the state.

Having reviewed the NATS, we define the modified NATS. First, we introduce the observable

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha,m}s_{\alpha}\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha,m\rvert.

(B2)

Its eigenvalues—the spin quantum numbers
s_{\alpha}

s_{\alpha}(s_{\alpha}+1)
of
\vec{S}^{2}

the thermodynamic force conjugate to

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm NATS}\coloneqq\exp\left(-\beta\left[H-\mu S_{z}-\gamma S\right]\right)/Z.

(B3)

The partition function

. Justifying the use of
\rho_{\rm NATS}
is subtle:
\rho_{\rm NATS}
does not follow from a common derivation of the thermal state’s form
Landau and Lifshitz 1980

The total entropy depends straightforwardly on the total energy and on other total extensive charges. The system-of-interest charges contribute additively to these total charges. One can therefore trace out the environment from the microcanonical state, leaving the system-of-interest state expressed in terms of its extensive charges. However,
S
is not extensive (additive).
The thermal state therefore appears not to depend on
S
, according to the microcanonical derivation.
5
5

5

Hence, in much prior literature, the NATS has not depended on

(e.g.,
Guryanova et al. 2016

Lostaglio et al. 2017

Halpern et al. 2016

Yunger Halpern et al. 2020

Kranzl et al. 2023

Murthy et al. 2023
;
Majidy et al. 2023a
).

This independence resembles the thermal state’s independence of

,

, etc. Yet
S
is an observable whose expectation value we can control and have information about. Introducing
S
into the system-of-interest density operator is therefore reasonable

, though.
Having introduced

1.

\bm{Z}

We compute the partition function’s trace using

\displaystyle Z\coloneqq{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H-\mu S_{z}-\gamma S}\right)=\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{m=-s_{\alpha}}^{s_{\alpha}}e^{-\beta(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha})}=\sum_{\alpha}e^{-\beta(E_{\alpha}-\gamma s_{\alpha})}\sum_{m=-s_{\alpha}}^{s_{\alpha}}e^{\beta\mu m}\,.

\displaystyle\left\langle S_{z}\right\rangle\approx G^{\prime}_{\left\langle S\right\rangle}(\beta\mu)/G_{\left\langle S\right\rangle}(\beta\mu).

(B17)

Calculating the right-hand side from Eq. (

Kittel 2004

-\mathcal{B}\sum_{j}S_{z}^{(j)}
. The magnetization depends on the

\displaystyle B_{s}(x)\coloneqq\frac{2s+1}{2s}\,\coth\left(\frac{x}{2s}\,[2s+1]\right)-\frac{1}{2s}\,\coth\left(\frac{x}{2s}\right).

(B18)

At the inverse temperature
\beta

\langle S_{z}^{(j)}\rangle=s\,B_{s}(\beta s)

Our system has a similar magnetization:

\displaystyle\left\langle S_{z}\right\rangle\approx\left\langle S\right\rangle\,B_{\left\langle S\right\rangle}\left(\beta\mu\left\langle S\right\rangle\right).

(B19)

Two useful limiting behaviors of

derive from limiting behaviors of the hyperbolic tangent. First, suppose each function’s argument is small:

\displaystyle\coth(x\approx 0)=\frac{1}{x}+\frac{x}{3}+O\left(x^{3}\right)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad B_{s}(x\approx 0)=\frac{x(s+1)}{3s}+O\left(x^{3}\right).

(B20)

Now, suppose each function’s argument is large:

\displaystyle\coth(x\gg 1)=1+O\left(e^{-2x}\right)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\lim_{x\to\infty}B_{s}(x)=1.

(B21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{\alpha}}\tilde{S}_{\rm th}(E,s_{\alpha})\Big\lvert_{\left\langle H\right\rangle,\left\langle S\right\rangle}\approx-\beta\gamma.

(B22)

Next, we calculate

B 2 iii
Proof that, at the peak of
\mathscr{F}(E,s_{\alpha})
,

\left\langle H\right\rangle\approx E_{*}
and
\left\langle S\right\rangle\approx s_{*}

Let us prove that
\left\langle H\right\rangle\approx E_{*}
and
\left\langle S\right\rangle\approx s_{*}
where

\mathscr{F}(E,s_{\alpha})
[Eq. (
B10

To do so, we calculate
\left\langle S\right\rangle

\left\langle H\right\rangle

\displaystyle\left\langle S\right\rangle={\rm Tr}\left(S\rho_{\rm NATS}\right)=\frac{1}{Z}{\rm Tr}\left(S\,e^{-\beta(H-\mu S_{z}-\gamma S)}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\gamma}\,.

(B26)

Applying Laplace’s method to

B9

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{\alpha}}\int dE\,\exp\bm{(}\mathscr{F}(E,s_{\alpha})\bm{)}\approx\sum_{s_{\alpha}}\int dE\,\exp\bm{(}\mathscr{F}(E\approx E_{*},s_{\alpha}\approx s_{*})\bm{)}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{s_{\alpha}\approx s_{*}}\int dE\,\exp\Big(\mathscr{F}(E_{*},s_{*})+\vec{\nabla}\mathscr{F}(E,s_{\alpha})\Big\lvert_{E_{*},s_{*}}\cdot(E-E_{*},s_{\alpha}-s_{*})

B30

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\sum_{m=-s_{*}}^{s_{*}}(2s_{*}+1)\,\exp\left(\tilde{S}_{\rm th}(E_{*},s_{*})-\beta[E_{*}-\mu m-\gamma s_{*}]\right)

\displaystyle\approx E_{*}-\mu\left\langle S_{z}\right\rangle-\gamma\left\langle S\right\rangle.

(B36)

Again, we have approximated

B 3 i
Correlators defined in terms of the modified NATS

This subsubsection bridges the conventional KMS relation (App.
A 2
) to the fine-grained KMS relation.
Define the time-domain connected two-point correlator

\displaystyle C_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle A(t)\,B\right\rangle-\left\langle A(t)\right\rangle\left\langle B\right\rangle.

(B38)

Section
III
concerned a disconnected correlator, for consistency with the conventional KMS relation (App.
A
). Here, we analyze a connected correlator because it more reliably encodes correlations between variables, by definition. However, the proof below (of the fine-grained KMS relation) extends directly to disconnected correlators; one simply eliminates certain terms.

To evaluate
C_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(t)
, we replace the Heisenberg-picture operator
A(t)
with its definition,
e^{iHt}\,A\,e^{-iHt}\,.
We also calculate the traces using the energy eigenbasis. Finally, we insert an

\mathbbm{1}=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert
rightward of the first
A
:

\displaystyle C_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(t)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left(e^{iHt}\,A\,e^{-iHt}\,B\,\frac{e^{-\beta(H-\mu S_{z}-\gamma S)}}{Z}\right)-{\rm Tr}\left(e^{iHt}\,A\,e^{-iHt}\,\frac{e^{-\beta(H-\mu S_{z}-\gamma S)}}{Z}\right){\rm Tr}\left(B\,\frac{e^{-\beta(H-\mu S_{z}-\gamma S)}}{Z}\right)

(B39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,m,\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\Bigg(\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,e^{-i\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}\right)t}

(B40)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m^{\prime}-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}\Bigg).

Fourier-transforming the time-domain correlator yields the frequency-domain correlator. We follow the Fourier-transform convention in App.
A
:

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\>C_{AB}(\Omega)\,e^{i\Omega t}

(B41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,m,\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\Bigg(\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\>e^{i\left[\Omega-\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}\right)t\right]}

(B42)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m^{\prime}-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\>e^{i\Omega t}\Bigg)

The Dirac delta function has the integral representation

\delta(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,e^{ikx}\,.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{\alpha,m,\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\Bigg\{\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta\left(\Omega-\left[E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}\right]\right)

(B43)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta(\Omega)\Bigg\}.

Whether
\bar{C}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega)
obeys a KMS relation is unclear, so we fine-grain the correlator.

B 3 ii
Fine-grained correlator and KMS relation for the modified NATS

To motivate the fine-grained correlator, we highlight the Dirac delta function in Eq. (
B43
). That delta function singles out an energy difference,
E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}
. Energy plays a role analogous to the magnetic spin quantum number and to the spin quantum number throughout the rest of Eq. (
B43
). Hence we put the latter two quantities on completely equal footing with energy: we introduce a
\Delta m
and a
\Delta s
analogous to
\Omega
. We do so using Kronecker delta functions
\delta_{m^{\prime}\,(m+\Delta m)}
and

\delta_{s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,(s_{\alpha}+\Delta s)}
, rather than Dirac delta functions, because the spacings between consecutive
m
values remains constant in the thermodynamic limit, as do the spacings between consecutive
s_{\alpha}
values. We define the
fine-grained (frequency-domain connected) correlator for the modified NATS
as

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{C}}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega,\Delta m,\Delta s)\coloneqq 2\pi\sum_{\alpha,m,\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\Bigg\{\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta\left(\Omega-\left[E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}\right]\right)\,

(B44)

\displaystyle\times\delta_{m^{\prime}\,(m+\Delta m)}\,\delta_{s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,(s_{\alpha}+\Delta s)}-\langle\alpha,m\rvert A\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert B\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m^{\prime}-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta(\Omega)\,\delta_{(\Delta m)\,0}\,\delta_{(\Delta s)\,0}\,.

Summing the fine-grained correlator over
\Delta m
and
\Delta s
yields the conventional correlator for the modified NATS [Eq. (
B43
)]:

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Delta m,\Delta s}\hat{\bar{C}}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega,\Delta m,\Delta s).

(B45)

The first sum must run over
\Delta m=q
, and the second sum must run over

\Delta s=-\min\{k,k^{\prime}\},-\min\{k,k^{\prime}\}+1,\ldots,\min\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Otherwise, the sums’ limits are arbitrary.

One can measure
\hat{\bar{C}}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega,\Delta m,\Delta s)
experimentally by, for example, eigendecomposing the
A
and
B
. The correlator reveals itself as an average with respect to an extended Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobability
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2024
. One can infer such a quasiprobability from each of multiple experimental protocols
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Gherardini and De Chiara 2024
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2024
. Granted, such protocols involve weak or strong measurements of the energy eigenbasis. Such measurements are impractical if the system is large. Three points weigh against this criticism, however: (i) The same criticism applies to direct experimental tests of the ETH, which is used widely nonetheless.
(ii)
\hat{\bar{C}}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega,\Delta m,\Delta s)
is measurable in principle and therefore is well-defined physically. (iii) Experimentalists’ control over quantum many-body systems has been advancing rapidly
Fauseweh 2024
.

The fine-grained correlator obeys the
fine-grained KMS relation for the modified NATS
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{C}}_{AB}^{\rm NATS}(\Omega,\Delta m,\Delta s)=e^{\beta\left(\Omega-\mu\Delta m-\gamma\Delta s\right)}\,\hat{\bar{C}}_{BA}^{\rm NATS}(-\Omega,-\Delta m,-\Delta s).

(B46)

To prove this equation, we substitute into the right-hand side from the definition (
B44
):

\displaystyle e^{\beta\left(\Omega-\mu\Delta m-\gamma\Delta s\right)}\,\hat{\bar{C}}_{BA}^{\rm NATS}(-\Omega,-\Delta m,-\Delta s)=2\pi\sum_{\alpha,m,\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\bigg\{\langle\alpha,m\rvert B\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert A\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\,e^{\beta(\Omega-\mu\Delta m-\gamma\Delta s)}\,

(B47)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\;\times\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta\left(-\Omega-\left[E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}\right]\right)\,\delta_{m^{\prime}\,(m-\Delta m)}\,\delta_{s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,(s_{\alpha}-\Delta s)}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\langle\alpha,m\rvert B\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert A\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\,e^{\beta(\Omega-\mu\Delta m-\gamma\Delta s)}\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m^{\prime}-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta(-\Omega)\,\delta_{(-\Delta m)\,0}\,\delta_{(-\Delta s)\,0}\bigg\}.

The first Dirac delta function simplifies as

\delta(-\Omega-[E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}])=\delta(\Omega+[E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-E_{\alpha}])=\delta(\Omega-[E_{\alpha}-E_{\alpha^{\prime}}])
.
It is nonzero if and only if
\Omega=E_{\alpha}-E_{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Therefore, we replace the first thermal exponential’s
\Omega
with
E_{\alpha}-E_{\alpha^{\prime}}
while keeping the Dirac delta function present. Similarly, first term’s Kronecker deltas are nonzero if and only if

and
\Delta s=s_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,.

In the final term of Eq. (

\displaystyle e^{\beta\left(\Omega-\mu\Delta m-\gamma\Delta s\right)}\,\hat{\bar{C}}_{BA}^{\rm NATS}(-\Omega,-\Delta m,-\Delta s)=2\pi\sum_{\alpha,m,\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\bigg\{\langle\alpha,m\rvert B\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert A\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m^{\prime}-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\delta\left(\Omega-\left[E_{\alpha}-E_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right]\right)\,\delta_{m^{\prime}\,(m-\Delta m)}\,\delta_{s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,(s_{\alpha}-\Delta s)}

(B48)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\langle\alpha,m\rvert B\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\!\langle\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rvert A\lvert\alpha^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle\,\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-\mu m-\gamma s_{\alpha}\right)}}{Z}\frac{e^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\mu m^{\prime}-\gamma s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)}}{Z}\,\delta(\Omega)\,\delta_{(\Delta m)\,0}\,\delta_{(\Delta s)\,0}\,.

Does the anomalous correction extend beyond

which does not depend on temperature. Third, the high-temperature (

Appendix C
Proof of property of Clebsch–Gordan coefficient

In App.
B 6
, we invoked a property of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients:

\displaystyle\text{If}\;s=O\left(N^{\zeta}\right),\;\zeta\in(0,1],\;\text{and}\;k,q,m=O(1),\;\text{then}

(C1)

\displaystyle\langle s+\nu,m+q\lvert s,m;k,q\rangle=\bar{c}(\nu;k,q)\,\left[1+O\left(s^{-1}\right)\right],\;\text{wherein}\;

(C2)

\displaystyle\bar{c}(\nu;k,q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sqrt{\frac{(k+\nu)!\,(k-\nu)!}{(k+q)!\,(k-q)!}}\sum_{\ell=\max\{0,q-\nu\}}^{\min\{k+q,k-\nu\}}(-1)^{\ell}{k+q\choose\ell}{k-q\choose\nu-q+\ell}.

(C3)

The beginning of our argument echoes Eqs. (B29) and (B30) in
Lasek et al. 2024
. There, the general formula for a Clebsch–Gordan coefficient is copied from Eq. (2.41) of
Bohm 1986
:

\displaystyle\langle s+\nu,m+q\lvert s,m;k,q\rangle

(C4)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{[2(s+\nu)+1]\,(2s+\nu-k)!\,(k+\nu)!\,(k-\nu)!\,(s+m+\nu+q)!\,(s-m+\nu-q)!\,(s+m)!\,(s-m)!\,(k+q)!\,(k-q)!}{(2s+\nu+k+1)!}}

\displaystyle\quad\;\times\sum_{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!\,(k-\nu-\ell)!\,(s-m-\ell)!\,(k+q-\ell)!\,(s+m+\nu-k+\ell)!\,(\nu-q+\ell)!}\,.

The sum runs over the integers
\ell
that render every factorial’s argument non-negative.

Let us begin to approximate the Clebsch–Gordan coefficient. First, we approximate a fairly general factorial. If
x\gg\Delta
, then

\displaystyle(x+\Delta)!=[(x+\Delta)(x+\Delta-1)\ldots(x+1)]x!=x^{\Delta}\,x!\left[1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right].

(C5)

By Eq. (
C1
),
s\gg k,q,m
. Because
s\gg k
and by the selection rule obeyed by the Clebsch–Gordan coefficient in (
C2
),
s\gg\nu
. We apply these inequalities and the approximation (
C5
) to Eq. (
C4
). Many factors cancel exactly:

\displaystyle\langle s+\nu,m+q\lvert s,m;k,q\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sqrt{\frac{[2(s+\nu)+1]\,(k+\nu)!\,(k-\nu)!\,(k+q)!\,(k-q)!\,[1+O(s^{-1})]}{(2s)[1+O(s^{-1})]}}

\displaystyle\quad\;\times\sum_{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!\,(k-\nu-\ell)!\,(k+q-\ell)!\,(\nu-q+\ell)!\,[1+O(s^{-1})]}\,.

(C6)

The corrections simplify as

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{[1+O(s^{-1})]}{[1+O(s^{-1})]}}\,\frac{1}{[1+O(s^{-1})]}

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{1}{2}\,O\left(s^{-1}\right)\right]\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\,O\left(s^{-1}\right)\right]\left[1-O\left(s^{-1}\right)\right]=1+O\left(s^{-1}\right).

(C7)

Also, the square-root in Eq. (
C
) contains two factors that approximately cancel:

\displaystyle\frac{2(s+\nu)+1}{2s}=1+O\left(s^{-1}\right).

(C8)

Hence Eq. (
C
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\langle s+\nu,m+q\lvert s,m;k,q\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\,\sqrt{(k+\nu)!\,(k-\nu)!\,(k+q)!\,(k-q)!}\,

(C9)

\displaystyle\quad\;\times\sum_{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!\,(k-\nu-\ell)!\,(k+q-\ell)!\,(\nu-q+\ell)!}\,\left[1+O\left(s^{-1}\right)\right].

The proof’s final steps center on the sum. The summand, with the sentence below Eq. (
C4
), implies that
\ell\geq 0
,
k-\nu\geq\ell
,
k+q\geq\ell
, and
\nu-q\geq-\ell
. In summary,

\max\{0,q-\nu\}\leq\ell\leq\min\{k-\nu,k+q\}
.
Consider inserting these limits in Eq. (
C9
). The result is equivalent to Eqs. (
C2
)–(
C3
), one can check by evaluating the binomial coefficients: if
a,b\geq 0
, then

{a\choose b}=\frac{a!}{b!(a-b)!}\,.

Appendix D
Transformation property of fine-grained correlator evaluated in energy eigenstate

This appendix introduces the transformation property invoked in the numerical analysis (Sec.
V
). By invoking the property, one can easily deduce general results from the examples analyzed. The property stems from the Wigner–Eckart theorem as follows. In Sec.
V
, we analyze correlators of spherical tensor operators
A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q}
and
B^{(k)}_{q}\,.
The correlators depend on elements of the matrices that represent the operators relative to the energy eigenbasis,
\{\lvert\alpha,m\rangle\}
. By the Wigner–Eckart theorem, Clebsch–Gordan coefficients encapsulate all the matrix elements’
m
- and
q
-dependences. For example,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha^{\prime},m+q|B^{(k)}_{q}|\alpha,m\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle s_{\alpha^{\prime}},m+q|s_{\alpha},m;k,q\rangle\langle\alpha^{\prime}\|B^{(k)}\|\alpha\rangle=\frac{\langle s_{\alpha}^{\prime},m+q|s_{\alpha},m;k,q\rangle}{\langle s_{\alpha}^{\prime},0|s_{\alpha},0;k,0\rangle}\langle\alpha^{\prime},0|B^{(k)}_{0}|\alpha,0\rangle

(D1)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{M}_{1}(s_{\alpha},s_{\alpha^{\prime}},m,k,q)\langle\alpha^{\prime},0|B^{(k)}_{0}|\alpha,0\rangle

(D2)

We have defined the Clebsch–Gordan ratio

\mathsf{M}_{1}(s,s^{\prime},m,k,q)\coloneqq\frac{\langle s^{\prime},m+q|s,m;k,q\rangle}{\langle s^{\prime},0|s,0;k,0\rangle}.

(D3)

In Eq. (
D2
), the prefactor depends on
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
only through
s_{\alpha}
and
s_{\alpha^{\prime}}
. This property allows us to transform one fine-grained correlator into another easily.

The matrix-element symmetry (
D2
) extends to the fine-grained dynamical correlator,

\hat{\bar{C}}^{\rm dyn}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q},B^{(k)}_{q}}(\Omega,\Delta m{=}q;\Delta s;\alpha,m)
[Eq. (
14
)]. If

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{2}(s,\Delta s,m,k,k^{\prime},q)\coloneqq\mathsf{M}_{1}(s+\Delta s,s,m+q,k^{\prime},-q)\mathsf{M}_{1}(s,s+\Delta s,m,k,q),\quad\text{then}

(D4)

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{C}}^{\rm dyn}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q},B^{(k)}_{q}}(\Omega,\Delta m{=}q,\Delta s;\alpha,m)=\mathsf{M}_{2}(s_{\alpha},\Delta s,m,k,k^{\prime},q)\,\hat{\bar{C}}^{\rm dyn}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{0},B^{(k)}_{0}}(\Omega,\Delta m{=}0,\Delta s;\alpha,0).

(D5)

Therefore, we can deduce about general dynamical fine-grained correlators upon evaluating a correlator between spherical tensor operators’
q{=}0
components on
\lvert\alpha,m{=}0\rangle
.

To test the fine-grained KMS relation, we define the logarithmic ratio

\tilde{\mathcal{{L}}}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q}B^{(k)}_{q}}(\Omega,q,\Delta s;\alpha,m)\coloneqq\ln\left(\frac{\hat{\bar{C}}^{\rm dyn}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q}B^{(k)}_{q}}(\Omega,\Delta m{=}q,\Delta s;\alpha,m)}{\hat{\bar{C}}^{\rm dyn}_{B^{(k)}_{q}A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q}}(-\Omega,\Delta m{=}{-q},-\Delta s;\alpha,m)}\right).

(D6)

By the transformation rule (
D5
), the log-ratio transforms as

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{-q}B^{(k)}_{q}}(\Omega,q,\Delta s;\alpha,m)=\mathsf{M}_{3}(s_{\alpha},\Delta s,m;k,k^{\prime},q)+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{A^{(k^{\prime})}_{0}B^{(k)}_{0}}(\Omega,0,\Delta s;\alpha,0).

(D7)

We have defined the function

\mathsf{M}_{3}(s,\Delta s,m;k,k^{\prime},q)=\ln\left(\frac{\mathsf{M}_{2}(s,\Delta s,m,k,k^{\prime},q)}{\mathsf{M}_{2}(s,-\Delta s,m,k^{\prime},k,-q)}\right)

(D8)

of 8 Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.
