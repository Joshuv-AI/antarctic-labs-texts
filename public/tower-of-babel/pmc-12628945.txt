Effects of physiological oxygen tension on human cumulus-oocyte-complex metabolism during in vitro maturation: an exploratory study
Authors: Ellen Anckaert, Gamze Ates, Arjen Liveyns, Heidi Van Ranst, Linde Mostinckx, Wilfried Cools, Anneleen Mortier, Ann Massie, Michel De Vos, Nazli Akin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In vitro oocyte maturation (IVM) is an internationally recognized, patient-friendly assisted reproductive technology, particularly valuable for women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). Biphasic IVM protocols, incorporating a pre-IVM step to promote cytoplasmic maturation under meiotic arrest, outperform standard IVM yet remain inferior to conventional methods involving controlled ovarian stimulation. Considering suboptimal culture conditions, such as supraphysiological oxygen, might be responsible for lower IVM oocyte competence, in this study, we investigated energy metabolism in human sibling cumulus-oocyte-complexes (COCs) through culturing them under different oxygen concentrations during pre-IVM. COCs (n = 239) from follicles < 10 mm, collected in non-PCOS hyper-responder research oocyte donors (n = 21) with minimal (or no) stimulation prior to oocyte retrieval, were allocated for the study. COCs per donor were randomly cultured in both 20% (atmospheric) and 5% (physiological) oxygen. After 24-hours pre-IVM culture, the samples were processed to (i) assess COC metabolic function with Seahorse ATP Production Rate Assay and MitoStress Test, (ii) measure COC ATP concentrations, (iii) analyze spent medium (glucose, lactate, amino acids), and (iv) evaluate oocyte mitochondrial and antioxidative functions (CellROX, JC-1, and mBCl stainings). COCs cultured under 5% oxygen contained higher ATP despite lower mitochondrial respiration, accompanied by steady glucose uptake and increased lactate production, while mitochondrial potential and antioxidative markers remained unchanged. These findings suggest reliance on an alternative energy substrate, such as fatty acids, under low oxygen conditions. By demonstrating improved metabolic efficiency in human COCs under physiological oxygen, this study highlights the need to re-evaluate IVM culture conditions to better mimic the in vivo environment.

Introduction

In vitro maturation (IVM) is a mild-approach assisted reproductive technology (ART) designed to obtain mature oocytes following the culture of immature cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs), collected from small/mid- antral follicles (2–10 mm). In IVM, exogenous gonadotropin administration is minimum or absent, and the ovulation trigger is abandoned, compared to several days long conventional ovarian stimulation (COS). Once considered experimental, IVM has gained recognition as of 2021 [1] and is now recommended for the management of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) related infertility [2]. Women with PCOS have the ideal follicle cohort for IVM due to a high proportion of small antral follicles, with nearly 80% measuring under 6 mm [3]. In this follicle cohort, minimal gonadotropin administration and the absence of an ovulatory trigger allow for the collection of a homogeneous population of immature COCs during oocyte pick-up (OPU), facilitating the development of robust culture systems. In fact, a recent study by Vuong et al. [4] showed that abolishing the gonadotropin treatment prior to IVM yields comparable embryological outcomes, which paves the way for approving the use of IVM beyond PCOS, particularly in fertility preservation for patients requiring urgent cancer therapy or those with estrogen-sensitive tumors.

The emergence of biphasic IVM systems has been among the most important developments in IVM technology in recent years. This two-step culture system leverages an initial pre-IVM phase, in which meiotic arrest is maintained using C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP), to enhance oocyte cytoplasmic maturation before nuclear maturation is triggered during the subsequent IVM phase. By synchronizing cytoplasmic and nuclear maturation, the biphasic IVM system has achieved higher oocyte maturation rates, increased numbers of good-quality embryos, and enhanced developmental capacity compared to standard one-step IVM systems [5–8]. Yet, cumulative live birth rates remain lower than COS [9], suggesting that biphasic IVM still requires optimization.

One of the key challenges in IVM optimization is mimicking the in vivo environment of maturing COCs, particularly with respect to oxygen tension which has been insufficiently investigated. Although COCs are exposed to 2–9% O₂ in vivo [10, 11], IVM is typically conducted under atmospheric oxygen, which may disrupt energy metabolism and elevate oxidative stress. Focusing on energy metabolism in mouse biphasic IVM, we demonstrated that the altered COC bioenergetic profiles observed during the pre-IVM step [12] were restored by culturing under 5% oxygen, resulting in a metabolic state comparable to in vivo COCs without affecting oocyte developmental competence [13]. Building on these findings, the present study extends this knowledge to human COCs by investigating the metabolic response to physiological (5%) versus atmospheric (20%) oxygen specifically during pre-IVM, assessed through several endpoints. Given that previous data from mouse biphasic IVM identified pre-IVM as the culture phase most sensitive to oxygen tension, here we focused exclusively on elucidating the metabolic changes occurring during this step in response to physiological oxygen tension, aiming to determine whether similar oxygen-dependent alterations also take place in human COCs.

Materials and methods

Patient inclusion and characteristics

This study was approved by Universitair Ziekenhuis Brussel Ethics Committee for human clinical studies (approval no: 2016 − 411). A total of 21 donors participated in the study between January 2023 and July 2024. The following inclusion criteria is used: age < 37 years, > 30 antral follicles on the baseline ultrasound scan, anti-mullerian hormone > 2 ng/ml. In total 23 OPU were performed, with 2 donors undergoing more than one IVM cycle, with the COCs from the different cycles being allocated for different endpoints. All participants donated all of their oocytes from the same cycle for research purposes. Donor demographics and reproductive outcomes are indicated in Table 1.

Cumulus-oocyte-complex collection and culture

In this study, highly purified human menopausal gonadotropin (HP-hMG) was used for minimal stimulation. The majority of the OPUs (16/23) were performed after 2 days of stimulation, while five were performed after one day, and two were performed without any stimulation. Two donors received 75 IU HP-hMG for two days, one donor received 225 IU HP-hMG only for one day, and the remaining donors received 150 IU HP-hMG either for one (n = 4) or two (n = 14) days. The stimulation duration and dosage were adjusted based on the donors’ clinical characteristics and supported by evidence showing no significant outcome differences between stimulated and unstimulated IVM cycles [4].

In case of stimulation is used, OPU was performed 42–46 h after the last injection. OPU and IVM culture were performed as explained previously [14]. A 17-gauge single-lumen needle was used at the pressure of −70 mmHg to aspirate the follicles < 10 mm. A collection media for follicular aspirates was prepared by supplementing Ferticult Flushing media (FertiPro, Belgium) with 50 nM CNP (Peptide Institute) and 20 nM estradiol (Sigma, Germany). Collected aspirates were filtered using a 70 μm mesh size strainer (BD Biosciences Falcon). COCs were identified and collected after washing the strainer using a search media, prepared by supplementing Ferticult Flushing with 25 nM CNP and 10 nM estradiol. Collected COCs were morphologically assessed considering the cumulus cell (CC) layer surrounding the oocytes and divided into three categories as (i) fully surrounded, if there was a full layer of tightly compacted CC around the oocytes, (ii) partially denuded, with some CC patches were missing around the corona radiata, and (iii) fully denuded, when the oocytes were completely naked. Only fully surrounded and partially denuded COCs (n = 239) were used for the culture, while fully denuded oocytes were discarded.

COCs were transferred into one well of 4-well culture dish (Nunc) containing 500 µl pre-IVM culture medium under 350 µl Ovoil (Vitrolife), cultured in groups of max 10 COCs/well. For the pre-IVM medium, Medicult IVM medium (Origio) was used as base and was supplemented with 1 mIU/ml recombinant FSH (Puregon), 5 ng/ml insulin (Roche), 10 nM estradiol, 10 mg/ml human serum albumin (HSA; Vitrolife), and 25 nM CNP. Pre-IVM culture was performed for 24 h, at 37 °C, 6% CO2 and in (i) 20% atmospheric O2 and (ii) 5% physiological O2 conditions.

Real-time metabolic analysis with Seahorse Analyzer

The ATP production kinetics and mitochondrial function of the COCs cultured under different O2 concentrations were analyzed using ATP Rate Assay and MitoStress Test (both from Agilent Technologies), respectively. The assays were prepared as previously described [13]. Briefly, the XFe96 sensor cartridge was hydrated overnight with sterile water in a 37 °C CO₂-free incubator. The following day, the water was replaced with pre-warmed calibrant solution, and the cartridge was incubated for an additional hour under the same conditions. Seahorse assay plates were coated with Cell-Tak (Corning) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay medium was composed of XF DMEM supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 5 mM glucose [15], and 175 µL of this medium was added to each assay well. At the end of the culture period, COCs were washed three times in assay medium and transferred into individual wells in 5 µL of assay medium. Before each assay, the plate was de-gassed in the CO2-free incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. The following final concentrations were used for the reagents: oligomycin 1 µM, FCCP (carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoro-methoxyphenyl hydrazone) 2.5 µM and Rot/AA (rotenone/antimycin A) 2.5 µM. Sample protein concentration, obtained using a QIAcube as explained in [13], was used to normalize the data post run.

In total, 107 COCs (53 from 20% O2, and 54 from 20% O2) from 11 research donors were used for all Seahorse Analyzer experiments, with 3–7 COCs analyzed per condition/well.

CellROX, JC-1 and monochlorobimane staining, imaging and image analysis

CellROX Deep Red, MitoProbe JC-1 assay kit and monochlorobimane (mBCl) (all from Thermo Fisher) were used to assess reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial membrane potential and intracellular glutathione, respectively. OR2 blastocyst media (Origio) and Ovoil were preincubated overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO2, 5% O2 and 100% humidity prior to use. A staining solution was prepared with 5 µM CellROX, 2 µM JC-1 and 12.5 µM mBCl in OR2 media. Mechanically denuded GV oocytes were washed three times in OR2 before being transferred into 25 µl staining solution, to be incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, 5% O2 and 100% humidity. After the incubation period, the stained oocytes were transferred into 2 µl drops of OR2 medium (per condition), prepared in 35 cm glass bottom confocal dishes (MatTek), under 3 ml oil.

Live imaging was performed on a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope using live imaging chamber (37 °C, 5% CO2, 5% O2 and humidity) as described previously [13], taking three images with a Z-step of 10 μm on a 20x magnification. With ooplasm being the region of interest, mean intensity values were measured per channel, on all z-stacks, using ZenBlue analysis wizard (Zeiss). Averages were obtained per oocyte and values per patient were normalized using oocytes cultured under 20% O2 as reference.

In total, 77 COCs were denuded after culture to perform the aforementioned staining. 58 GV oocytes (n = 29 per condition) from 8 research donors were analyzed, with 1–9 oocytes per research donor.

ATP measurement

A semiquantitative bioluminescent kit (ab65313, Abcam) was used to measure the total COC ATP. A standard curve ranging from 0 to 250 nM ATP (FLAAS, Sigma) was prepared to quantify the ATP concentrations. Cell lysis was performed using 45 µl nucleoside releasing buffer (provided with the kit). Briefly, samples with the buffer were incubated on ice for 5 min, followed by three snap freeze – thaw cycles to release the cell content. For each measurement, 20 µl of lysate was transferred to the wells of the appropriate plates and measured in duplicate using a GloMax (Promega) following manufacturer’s protocol (for ATP measurement). Protein quantification per sample was completed using Qubit Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher) and sample protein concentration was used to normalize the data.

In total, 12 samples, collected from 6 research donors, were analyzed for ATP measurements. Each sample contained a pool of 3–8 COCs, totaling 55 COC (27 from 20% O2, and 28 from 20% O2).

Spent media analysis

Glucose and lactate concentrations were measured using a Cobas 8000 c702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics). Glucose was quantified using an enzymatic UV spectrophotometric method based on hexokinase. Lactate was measured using an enzymatic colorimetric method involving lactate oxidase and peroxidase. Before the downstream analysis concentrations were normalized to the number of the COCs/well after subtracting blank values. In total, 18 sibling samples (from 9 patients) were assessed.

Amino acids were measured using a Biochrom 30 + amino acid analyzer (Biochrom Ltd, Cambridge Science Park, England) according to a validated clinical protocol. 200 µL of sample was mixed with 200 µL of a protein precipitation solution containing sulfosalicylic acid (393.4 mmol/L) and an internal standard, 2,4-diaminobutyric acid (250 µmol/L). The mixture was incubated on ice for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 1800 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size filter and adjusted to pH 2.2 using 1 M lithium hydroxide. 50 µL was injected onto a cation-exchange analytical column (Ultropac resin, 200 × 4.6 mm, Biochrom). Post-column derivatization was performed using ninhydrin reagent, and amino acids were detected by absorbance at 440 nm (proline and hydroxyproline) and 570 nm (all other amino acids). Data processing (peak integration, calibration, quantification) was performed using Agilent OpenLab EZChrom software. In total, 20 sibling samples (from 10 patients) were assessed alongside blanks (n = 4).

Statistical analysis

For all test, significance was considered when p < 0.05 and 95% confidence interval was used. Statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA), latter was also used for the visualization.

For the Mito Stress Test, linear mixed model was used to analyze the data accommodating the correlation following the clustered data collected from independent experiments. For the rest of the analysis where comparisons between two groups (ATP concentration, spent medium glucose, lactate, and stainings) were completed, appropriate paired t-test was used. Normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. When the data did not follow normal distribution, the values were log transformed, and parametric test was completed. For the amino acid concentrations, the observed differences between the spent medium versus blank did not exceed the assay’s minimal detectable differences (3.6–6.8%). Consequently, no further statistical analysis was conducted.

Results

ATP Rate Assay results showed that COCs cultured under atmospheric oxygen (20% O₂) exhibited a significantly greater reliance on mitochondrial respiration compared with those cultured under physiological oxygen (5% O₂). Specifically, the proportion of ATP generated through mitochondrial pathways was higher in the 20% O₂ group (43 ± 5%) compared to the 5% O₂ group (27 ± 10%, p = 0.021), consequently glycolytic ATP contribution was correspondingly lower (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this finding, the MitoStress Test demonstrated significantly higher maximal respiration in COCs cultured under 20% O₂ (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, despite this apparent mitochondrial dominance, intracellular ATP content was lower in the 20% O₂ group compared with the 5% O₂ group (50.3 ± 17.9 nmol/µg vs. 75.2 ± 32.7 nmol/µg, p = 0.019) (Fig. 1C).

Assessing oxidative stress and mitochondrial health, we next measured intracellular ROS, mitochondrial membrane potential and gluthathione content. CellROX staining demonstrated similar ROS levels between the groups, although a trend toward higher accumulation was observed under 20% O₂ (p = 0.059) (Fig. 1D). Mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio) and intracellular glutathione levels (mBCl) did not differ between conditions (Fig. 1E and F).

Analysis of substrate utilization in the spent medium revealed comparable glucose consumption between the two conditions (Fig. 1G). However, lactate production per COC was significantly higher in the 5% O₂ group (0.06 ± 0.03 mmol/L/COC) than in the 20% O₂ group (0.04 ± 0.03 mmol/L/COC, p = 0.014) (Fig. 1H). On the other hand, targeted profiling of amino acid concentrations in the culture medium showed subtle variations between experimental and blank samples. However, these changes did not exceed the minimal detectable differences of the assay (3.6–6.8%), and therefore no further statistical analyses were performed.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that culture oxygen tension during pre-IVM, the cytoplasmic maturation period, markedly affects COC metabolism. Mitochondrial respiration, assessed through real-time metabolic assays, was higher in the COCs from 20% O₂ condition compared to 5% O₂ group, a finding that is comparable to the previous mouse biphasic IVM study [13]. Although COCs cultured at 20% O₂ exhibited greater mitochondrial respiration, those exposed to physiological (5%) oxygen generated higher overall ATP levels, suggesting a shift in metabolic pathway utilization. Considering the relative ATP efficiency per substrate, stimulation of anaerobic glycolysis alone cannot account for these changes despite increased lactate production. Together with steady glucose uptake, these findings indicate a reliance on an alternative energy source under physiological oxygen conditions. Notably, these differences occurred without alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential or antioxidative capacity, suggesting that oxygen-sensitive changes in energy balance arise independently of mitochondrial dysfunction.

Oocyte’s energy demands are met by mitochondrial oxidation of several substrates, including fatty acids that are indispensable for nuclear oocyte maturation during IVM in several species including mouse, bovine and porcine [16]. A shift toward increased fatty acid oxidation would potentially explain the higher ATP concentrations despite lower metabolic activity under 5% O₂, given that ATP yield of this reaction is approximately three times higher than that of metabolizing glucose. This metabolic state also comes in alignment with the Quiet Embryo Hypothesis, which is identified as the Goldilocks principle of metabolism and links lower but efficient metabolic turnover to higher developmental competence [17, 18]. Therefore, physiological oxygen may be crucial for IVM oocyte competence by promoting a more quiescent metabolic profile producing sufficient ATP, in contrast to atmospheric oxygen, which induces higher metabolic activity without substantially improving energy yield.

On the other hand, it is important to recognize that promoting fatty acid oxidation without adequate substrate availability may inadvertently jeopardize oocyte developmental competence. Unlike other substrates, fatty acids, available from follicular fluid in vivo or supplied by culture medium components such as serum in vitro, can be stored within oocytes as lipid droplets [19, 20]. Consequently, in serum-free biphasic IVM systems oocytes are forced to rely on their inherent and limited lipid stores for fatty acid oxidation. In this case, any additional stimulation of the pathway could deplete the endogenous lipid reserves and ultimately compromise IVM oocyte competence. Therefore, establishing physiologically relevant and robust IVM systems requires not only adjusting oxygen tension but also culture medium composition.

Although the sibling oocyte design and the use of multiple endpoints represent strengths of this study, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The oxygen consumption rate, identified through real-time metabolic assays, is indicative of overall metabolic activity but it fails to reflect the type of substrate being oxidized. Further research, including direct assessment of lipid utilization and its relationship to developmental potential, will be eventually essential to reveal and confirm the exact mechanisms affected by the culture oxygen conditions. Moreover, given that PCOS patients exhibit altered glucose and lipid metabolism and increased oxidative stress, their COCs may respond differently to low oxygen conditions as well. Therefore, future studies assessing culture modifications utilizing samples from PCOS cohorts would be critical in IVM optimization as well as for identifying potential confounders that may require tailoring for PCOS and non-PCOS patients.

In conclusion, this study provides the first evidence in human COCs that oxygen tension during pre-IVM critically shapes metabolic efficiency. Physiological oxygen (5% O₂) supports ATP production through a more quiescent metabolic profile, whereas atmospheric oxygen (20% O₂) increases respiratory activity without improving energy balance. Although this exploratory study does not provide clinical outcome data, it offers important clues for optimizing IVM culture that should be addressed in future studies. Such protocol refinements will be key to enabling IVM to achieve cumulative results comparable to COS and significantly enhance its clinical uptake as a mild-approach ART, thereby expanding fertility treatment options for a wider range of patients.
