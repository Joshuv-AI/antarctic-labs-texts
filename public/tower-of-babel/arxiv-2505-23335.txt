Abstract
. Consider a degree- d d polynomial f ⁡ ( ξ 1 , … , ξ n ) f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}) of independent Rademacher random variables ξ 1 , … , ξ n \xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n} . To what extent can f ⁡ ( ξ 1 , … , ξ n ) f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}) concentrate on a single point? This is the so-called polynomial Littlewood–Offord problem . A nearly optimal bound was proved by Meka, Nguyen and Vu: the point probabilities are always at most about 1 / n 1/\sqrt{n} , unless f f is “close to the zero polynomial” (having only o ⁡ ( n d ) o(n^{d}) nonzero coefficients). In this paper we prove several results supporting the general philosophy that the Meka–Nguyen–Vu bound can be significantly improved unless f f is “close to a polynomial with special algebraic structure”, drawing some comparisons to phenomena in analytic number theory. In particular, one of our results is a corrected version of a conjecture of Costello on multilinear forms (in an appendix with Ashwin Sah and Mehtaab Sawhney, we disprove Costello’s original conjecture).

Introduction

Anticoncentration
inequalities are an important class of probabilistic inequalities, giving upper bounds on the probability that a
random variable falls in a small interval or is equal to a particular
value (i.e., they provide limits on the extent to which a random variable
“concentrates”). Such inequalities are ubiquitous, playing an
essential behind-the-scenes role in a wide range of different types
of probabilistic arguments.

In particular, an important direction is the
(polynomial)
Littlewood–Offord problem
, which concerns anticoncentration
of low-degree polynomials of independent random variables (making
minimal assumptions about the structure of the polynomials in question).
A representative theorem, due to Meka, Nguyen and Vu
[

is the uniform distribution on

1.1.1.
Connections to analytic number theory

Proposition

1.4
(our counterexample to Costello’s conjecture) is really a special case of a general observation
that if
P\in\mathbb{F}[y_{1},\dots,y_{k}]
is any polynomial with
N_{P}(B)
zeroes in
\{-B,\dots,B\}^{k}
, then one can construct a polynomial
f_{P}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

of the form
f_{P}=P(L_{1},\dots,L_{k})
for linear forms
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
such that
\mathbb{P}[f_{P}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=0]
scales roughly like
N_{P}(\sqrt{n})n^{-k/2}
. So, in order to fully understand the algebraic aspects of the
polynomial Littlewood–Offord problem, it seems to be necessary to understand how the algebraic features of a polynomial
P
affect the rate of growth of
N_{P}(B)
(which can be interpreted as a certain “density of integral points” on the variety defined by
P
). To this end, one is forced to confront
deep questions in analytic number theory.

Specifically, if we restrict our attention to polynomials of the form
f_{P}
, then the bound of shape approximately
1/\sqrt{n}
in
Theorem

1.1
corresponds to the “trivial bound”
N_{P}(B)=O(B^{k-1})
, which holds for any nonzero polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}[y_{1},\dots,y_{k}]
of fixed degree (see for example
[
4
, Equation (2.3)]
). It seems that in order to prove
Conjecture

1.7
for polynomials of the form
f_{P}
, one would need a power-saving improvement over this “trivial bound” when
P
is irreducible.
Such estimates are indeed available: for an irreducible
d
-form
P
, Cohen
[
8
]
proved that
N_{P}(B)\leq B^{k-3/2+o(1)}
, and this was later improved by Pila
[
48
]
to

(see also
[
62
]
). It is conjectured that

for all

(which would be best-possible, if true; this is an affine version of the so-called

of Heath-Brown
[
24
]
for projective varieties). The affine dimension growth conjecture was very recently proved when

[

1.1.3.
Inverse theorems

From a broader point of view, all the theorems and conjectures in this paper can be interpreted as falling under the umbrella of the
inverse Littlewood–Offord problem
, which is concerned with understanding the structural properties of
f
that control the anticoncentration behaviour of
f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
. In this paper we have considered only the
algebraic
aspects of this problem; aspirationally it would be of interest to consider the interplay between algebraic and
arithmetic
aspects.

To give some context: in the linear (
d=1
) case, there is no relevant algebraic structure; the anticoncentration behaviour of
f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
only depends on the multiset
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
of (degree-1) coefficients of
f
. It is now understood that, in fact, the anticoncentration behaviour of
f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\xi_{n}
is essentially determined by “how much
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
looks like a generalised arithmetic progression”. A particular theorem along these lines, due to Nguyen and Vu
[
45
]
, appears as
Theorem

2.10
later in the paper; earlier theorems in this spirit were proved by Halász
[
23
]
, Tao and Vu
[
59
,
58
]
and Rudelson and Vershinyn
[
52
]
.

For higher degrees, the only result in the literature considering both arithmetic and algebraic aspects of the Littlewood–Offord problem is a theorem of Nguyen
[
46
]
in the quadratic case (
d=2
). This theorem had important consequences in random matrix theory, but unfortunately it is very crude, and we are still a long way from “optimal” inverse theorems for higher degrees. In particular, Nguyen’s theorem is only capable of distinguishing “polynomial” anticoncentration from “sub-polynomial” anticoncentration (i.e., distinguishing whether there is a point probability at least
n^{-C}
, for some constant
C

For bilinear forms (a subfamily of quadratic polynomials), Costello made a more refined conjecture
[
9
, Conjecture 1]
but we do not know of a general conjecture for quadratic polynomials.

Notation

We use standard asymptotic notation throughout, as follows. For functions

We also write

f(n)/g(n)\to 0
as

. Subscripts on asymptotic notation indicate quantities that should be treated as constants.

Slightly less standardly, for a matrix

In addition, for a matrix

We say that a partition

In this section, we outline the proofs of
Theorems

1.11

1.10

1.11

are quite closely related, but the proof strategy for

2.1.
d
-multilinear forms

First, we outline the proof of
Theorem

1.9
.
Recall that a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a
d
-multilinear form
if there is a partition
\{1,\dots,n\}=I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{d}
such that every term of
f
is of the form
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{d}}
, where
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\in\mathbb{F}
and
i_{j}\in I_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Such a
d
-multilinear form can be equivalently viewed as a function
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}\to\mathbb{F}
which is linear in each of its
d
arguments. It can be encoded by the
|I_{1}|\times\dots\times|I_{d}|
array of coefficients
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}
, and it can also be viewed as an element of the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
. This gives rise to three different ways to define the notion of an
|I_{1}|\times\dots\times|I_{d}|

tensor
:

•

as a
d
-multilinear form, which can be interpreted as a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
or a function
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}\to\mathbb{F}
,

•

as a function
I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
(i.e., a
I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}
array with entries in
\mathbb{F}
), or

•

as an element of the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
.

We will switch between the these three points of view as convenient. Indeed, viewing a tensor as a function
I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
makes it easier to talk about subtensors (i.e., tensors induced by subsets
I_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq I_{1},\dots,I_{d}\subseteq I_{d}^{\prime}
), while viewing a tensor as an element of
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
makes it easier to talk about factorisation.

Definition 2.1
.

A tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
is
reducible
or has
partition rank at most 1
if there is a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
, and tensors
T_{1}\in\bigotimes_{j\in J_{1}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}
and
T_{2}\in\bigotimes_{j\in J_{2}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}
, such that
T
can be factored as a tensor product
T=T_{1}\otimes T_{2}
.
Equivalently, this says that the corresponding
d
-multilinear form can be factorised into lower-degree factors.

The first ingredient we need is the following theorem, which states that for any tensor
T
, if most of the small subtensors of
T
are reducible, then
T
itself is close to being reducible. In our proof, we will use this as a sort of local-to-global principle: we can study small subtensors of
T
individually, and make conclusions about the whole tensor
T
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
d>1
and
\varepsilon>0
, and any field
\mathbb{F}
.
Let
\delta=(\varepsilon/2)^{2^{d-1}}
, and let
n_{0}
be sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
\varepsilon
.
Consider a
d
-dimensional tensor
T:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
, where
|I_{1}|,\dots,|I_{d}|\geq n_{0}
, and suppose that all but at most a
\delta
-fraction of the
2^{d-1}\times\dots\times 2^{d-1}
subtensors of
T
are reducible. Then one can make
T
reducible by changing up to an
\varepsilon
-fraction of its entries.

Remark 2.3
.

The proof of
Lemma

2.2
is also suitable for other notions of “tensor rank at most 1”, most notably the so-called
slice rank
(see
[
57
,
54
]
).

The (short) proof of
Lemma

2.2
appears in
Section

4
. We will prove
Theorem

1.9
by induction on
d
, using Costello’s result for
d=2
as the base case. Given
Lemma

2.2
, the main ingredient for the induction step is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
d\geq 3
and
r\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.
Let
n
be sufficiently large in terms of
d
,
r
and
\varepsilon
.
Consider a tensor
T:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
, where
|I_{1}|=\dots=|I_{d-1}|=r
and
I_{d}=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For a vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, define the
(d-1)
-dimensional tensor
T\vec{x}:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d-1}\to\mathbb{F}
by

T\vec{x}(i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}T(i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i)x_{i}.

Then at least one of the following holds.

(1)

All but an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the
r\times\dots\times r
subtensors of
T
are reducible, or

(2)

letting
\vec{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
be a vector of i.i.d. Rademacher
random variables, we have

\mathbb{P}[T\vec{\xi}\text{ is reducible}]\leq n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}.

Roughly speaking, we will use
Lemma

2.4
as follows. Given a
d
-multilinear form
f
(with variables partitioned into
d
parts), we wish to understand the behaviour of
f
when the variables take random values. In the induction step, we reveal all the randomness in only the last of the
d

f
as a
(d-1)
-multilinear form in the remaining unrevealed variables.
One way to visualise this, from the point of view that the tensor of coefficients is a
d
-dimensional array, is to view our
d
-dimensional tensor as a “stack” of
(d-1)
-dimensional tensors. We assign a random sign to each of the
(d-1)
-dimensional tensors in the stack, and add them together to obtain the “collapsed”
(d-1)
-dimensional tensor. For the induction to work, we need to know that if the original
d
-multilinear form was far from being reducible, then the “collapsed”
(d-1)
-multilinear form is likely to also be far from being reducible. By
Lemma

2.2

2^{d-1}
(except in the
d

Lemma

2.4
.

In the statement of
Lemma

2.4
, note that

can be viewed as a linear combination of Rademacher random variables, with coefficients in the space of
|I_{d-1}|\times\dots\times|I_{d-1}|
tensors
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d-1}}
.
In

2
we are interested in the event that this random linear combination falls in the variety of reducible tensors. So, to prove

2.4
we will use the following “geometric Littlewood–Offord” theorem (closely related to results of Fox, Spink and the second author

Theorem 2.5
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
and let
\mathcal{Z}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a (possibly reducible) affine variety. Let

be sufficiently large (in terms of
\mathcal{Z},\varepsilon
), and consider vectors
\vec{a}_{1},\dots,\vec{a}_{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}

Then, at least one of the following holds.

(1)

There is a linear subspace
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{d}
containing all but at most
\varepsilon n
of the vectors
\vec{a}_{i}
, such that
\vec{w}+\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}

(2)

letting

be i.i.d. Rademacher

\mathbb{P}[\xi_{1}\vec{a}_{1}+\dots+\xi_{n}\vec{a}_{n}\in\mathcal{Z}]\leq n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}.

This result follows from the same proof as in

]
(which considers a more general setting). For completeness, we include a proof of

In order to actually apply

Lemma 2.6

Fix disjoint sets

, fix
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}

(1)

(2)

The maximal linear subspaces of

are all sets of tensors of the following form: fix a proper subset

Quadratic polynomials

In this subsection we outline the proofs of
Theorems

and

. We start with some elements common to both proofs, and then split into separate subsections for each of
Theorems

1.11
and

.

First, recalling the role that
Lemma

2.2
played for the proof of
Theorem

1.9
, we need a “local-to-global” lemma for the rank of a symmetric matrix (while
d
-multilinear forms are naturally encoded by
d
-dimensional tensors, quadratic forms are naturally encoded by symmetric matrices). Recall from
Section

1.3
that
\|A\|_{0}
denotes the number of nonzero entries in a matrix
A
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
n\geq r\geq 1
, let
\alpha\in[0,1]
and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
Consider a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
.
If all but at most an
\alpha
-fraction of the
r\times r
submatrices of
A
are singular, then there exists a symmetric matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
of rank less than
r
such that
\|A-A^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq O(r^{4}\alpha^{1/r}n^{2})
.

We remark that
Lemma

2.7
is very easy to prove if we drop the requirement that the approximating matrix
A^{\prime}
is symmetric. The challenge is that we want to modify
A
to have rank less than
r

while maintaining symmetry
. The details of the proof of
Lemma

2.7
appear in
Section

7
, but to give a brief idea: first, we show that we can adjust a small number of entries of
A
to obtain a matrix
B
which has low rank (but is not necessarily symmetric). Then, we identify a principal submatrix of
B
which is symmetric and “robustly has full rank”, and use this as a “guide” to construct
A^{\prime}
.

Remark 2.8
.

Throughout the paper we do not pay much attention to quantitative aspects, but it seems worth noting that in
Lemma

2.7
the dependence on
\alpha
is best-possible. Indeed, for any fixed integer
r\geq 1
, let
\ell=\lfloor\alpha^{1/2r}n/2\rfloor
and consider a binary matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
defined by

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }j\geq n-\ell+i\text{ or }i\geq n-\ell+j\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(this can be informally described as an all-zero matrix, in which an upper-triangular chunk of the top-right corner, and a lower-triangular chunk of the bottom-left corner, have been changed to “1”).
It is not hard to see that all but at most an
\alpha
-fraction of the
r\times r
submatrices are singular, but to make the rank less than
r
it is necessary to change an
\Omega_{r}(\alpha^{1/r})
-fraction of the entries.

Next, one very important aspect of the proofs of
Theorems

1.11
and
1.10
, which has no counterpart in the proof of

1.9
, is

. Namely, in the last subsection (on the proof of

1.9
), we crucially used that

forms in disjoint sets of variables. For example, a bilinear form in the variables

and

can be viewed as a linear form in
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
whose coefficients are themselves linear forms in

. So, one can study anticoncentration of bilinear forms via tools for

anticoncentration.

2.2.2.
A power-saving improvement in the “robustly irreducible” case

Now, we outline the proof of
Theorem

1.10

First, the significance of

is that a quadratic polynomial is reducible over
\mathbb{C}
if and only if it has rank at most 2. (On the other hand, reducibility over
\mathbb{R}
cannot be expressed in terms of rank:

and
x^{2}-y^{2}
both have rank two, but the former is irreducible and the latter is reducible).

Given this connection between irreducibility and rank, our proof of
Theorem

1.10
follows the same general scheme as the proof of

1.11

Theorem

1.11
we could wastefully assume our coefficient matrix

Just as for

1.11

2.12

Definition 2.15
.

we say that a random variable

Lemma 2.16
.

Fix
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.

•

Let

for some symmetric matrix

•

Let

•

Consider a partition
\{1,\dots,n\}=X\cup Y\cup Z
.

Then,

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{P}[f(\vec{\xi})=z]\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\vec{\alpha}^{\,T}A[X,Y]\vec{\beta}=0,\quad\vec{\alpha}^{\,T}A[X,Z]\vec{\gamma}=\varphi(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta}),\quad\vec{\beta}^{\,T}A[Y,Z]\vec{\gamma}=\psi(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})\Bigr]^{1/4},

(2)

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p,\delta\in(0,1)
and
n\geq r\geq 1
.
Suppose
I
is a random subset of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathbb{P}[|I|\geq(1-\delta)n]\geq p
.
Then, for all but a
2r\delta
-fraction of the
r
-element subsets
S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have
\mathbb{P}[S\subset I]\geq p/2.

Proof.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
be the event that
i\in I

and let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that
|I|\geq(1-\delta)n
, so we
are assuming
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]\geq p
. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the collection
of sets
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
of
r
distinct indices such that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{i_{1}}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{E}_{i_{r}}]<p/2\leq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]/2
.
So, our goal is to prove that
|\mathcal{Q}|\leq 2r\delta\binom{n}{r}
.

On one hand, we have

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\binom{|I|}{r}}}\right]\geq\binom{\lceil(1-\delta)n\rceil}{r}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}],

but on the other hand we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\binom{|I|}{r}}}\right]=\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{i_{1}}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{E}_{i_{r}}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{i_{1}}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{E}_{i_{r}}]+\sum_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\notin\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]

(3.2)

\displaystyle<|\mathcal{Q}|\cdot(\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]/2)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\binom{n}{r}-|\mathcal{Q}|}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}].

Combining

yields the desired inequality

|\mathcal{Q}|<2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\binom{n}{r}-\binom{\lceil(1-\delta)n\rceil}{r}}}\right)\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta n\cdot\binom{n-1}{r-1}}}\right)=2r\delta\binom{n}{r}.

(The second inequality has a combinatorial interpretation: given a

n

r

4.
A local-to-global lemma for reducibility of tensors

In this section, we prove
Lemma

2.2
: if most of the small subtensors of
T
are reducible, then
T
itself is close to being reducible.
There are different types of reducibility with respect to particular partitions, as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

For a tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
and a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
, we say that
T
is
reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
if there are tensors
T_{1}\in\bigotimes_{j\in J_{1}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}
and
T_{2}\in\bigotimes_{j\in J_{2}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}
, such that
T
can be factored as a tensor product
T=T_{1}\otimes T_{2}
.

We note that reducibility of a tensor
T
with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
can equivalently be interpreted as the property that a certain matrix associated with
T
has rank at most
1
, as follows.

Fact 4.2
.

Fix disjoint sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{d}
, a field
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, and a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
. For
s\in\{1,2\}
, let
r_{s}=\prod_{j\in J_{s}}|I_{j}|
, so we have a vector space isomorphism
\bigotimes_{j\in J_{s}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}\cong\mathbb{F}^{r_{s}}
.
Then,

\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}=\Bigg(\bigotimes_{j\in J_{1}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}\Bigg)\otimes\Bigg(\bigotimes_{j\in J_{2}}\mathbb{F}^{I_{j}}\Bigg)\cong\mathbb{F}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}};

that is to say,
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
gives rise to a correspondence between
I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}
tensors and
r_{1}\times r_{2}
matrices.
A tensor in
\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
is reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
if and only if the corresponding
r_{1}\times r_{2}
matrix has rank at most 1.

We also adopt the following notation for subtensors, similar to that of matrices.

Definition 4.3
.

Consider a tensor
T:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
(represented as a
d
-dimensional array). For subsets
S_{1}\subseteq I_{1},\dots,S_{d}\subseteq I_{d}
, write
T[S_{1},\dots,S_{d}]
for the corresponding subtensor (formally, this is a restriction of the function
T
to the set
S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{d}
).

We break the proof of
Lemma

2.2
into three simpler lemmas. First, by a simple averaging argument, we can reduce our consideration to the subtensors containing a particular nonzero entry.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
d,r>1
and
\delta>0
and
c\in[0,1]
, and any field
\mathbb{F}
.
Consider a
d
-dimensional tensor
T:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
(represented as a
d
-dimensional array).
Suppose that at least a
\delta^{c}
-fraction of the entries of
T
are nonzero and that all but at most a
\delta
-fraction of the
r\times\dots\times r
subtensors are reducible.

Then, there is a nonzero entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},{i^{\star}_{2}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
such that all but at most a
\delta^{1-c}
-fraction of the
r\times\dots\times r
subtensors which contain the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},{i^{\star}_{2}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
are reducible.

Proof.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, let
i_{j}^{\star},i_{j}^{1},\dots,i_{j}^{r-1}\in I_{j}
be a uniformly random sequence of
r
distinct indices from
I_{j}
.
So, by assumption we have

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big[T\big[\{i_{1}^{\star},i_{1}^{1},\dots,i_{1}^{r-1}\},\dots,\{i_{d}^{\star},i_{d}^{1},\dots,i_{d}^{r-1}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{P}\Big[T\big[\{i_{1}^{\star},i_{1}^{1},\dots,i_{1}^{r-1}\},\dots,\{i_{d}^{\star},i_{d}^{1},\dots,i_{d}^{r-1}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible}\;\Big|\;i_{1}^{\star},\dots,i_{d}^{\star}\Big]\bigg].

By Markov’s inequality, we deduce that for more than a
(1-\delta^{c})
-fraction of choices of
(i_{1}^{\star},\dots,i_{d}^{\star})
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big[T\big[\{i_{1}^{\star},i_{1}^{1},\dots,i_{1}^{r-1}\},\dots,\{i_{d}^{\star},i_{d}^{1},\dots,i_{d}^{r-1}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible}\;\Big|\;i_{1}^{\star},\dots,i_{d}^{\star}\Big]\leq\delta^{1-c},

meaning that all but at most a
\delta^{1-c}
-fraction of the
r\times\dots\times r
subtensors which contain the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
are reducible.
Since at least a
\delta^{c}
-fraction of the entries of
T
are nonzero, for at least one of these choices of
(i_{1}^{\star},\dots,i_{d}^{\star})
, the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
is nonzero.
∎

The second lemma (arguably the most important) allows us to relate reducibility in general to reducibility with respect to a particular partition.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
d>1
and
\delta>0
, and let
\ell=2^{d-1}-1
. Consider a tensor
T:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
(represented as a
d
-dimensional array) and suppose
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})\neq 0
for some indices
{i^{\star}_{1}}\in I_{1},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}}\in I_{d}
.
Suppose that all but at most a
\delta
-fraction of the
2^{d-1}\times\dots\times 2^{d-1}
subtensors of
T
which contain the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
are reducible.

Then, there is a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
, such that all but at most a
\delta^{1/\ell}
-fraction of the
2\times\dots\times 2
subtensors of
T
which contain the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
are reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
.

Proof.

Note that
\ell=2^{d-1}-1
is the number of partitions
\mathcal{P}=\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
of
\{1,\dots,d\}
, such that neither
J_{1}
nor
J_{2}
is empty. Let
\mathcal{P}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
be an enumeration of all these partitions.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, let
i_{j}^{1},\dots,i_{j}^{\ell}
be uniformly random indices sampled independently from
I_{j}\setminus\{i_{j}^{\star}\}
. Note that there may be fewer than
\ell
different indices among
i_{j}^{1},\dots,i_{j}^{\ell}
, due to repetitions; let
Q_{j}
be a random set of exactly
\ell
indices in
I_{j}\setminus\{i_{j}^{\star}\}
, obtained by starting from
\{i_{j}^{1},\dots,i_{j}^{\ell}\}
and adding the appropriate number of additional random indices. Do this independently for each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big[T\big[Q_{1}\cup\{{i^{\star}_{1}}\},\dots,Q_{d}\cup\{{i^{\star}_{d}}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\bigcap_{t=1}^{\ell}\Big\{T\big[Q_{1}\cup\{{i^{\star}_{1}}\},\dots,Q_{d}\cup\{{i^{\star}_{d}}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible with respect to }\mathcal{P}_{t}\Big\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\bigcap_{t=1}^{\ell}\Big\{T\big[\{i_{1}^{t},{i^{\star}_{1}}\},\dots,\{i_{d}^{t},{i^{\star}_{d}}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible with respect to }\mathcal{P}_{t}\Big\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\Big[T\big[\{i_{1}^{t},{i^{\star}_{1}}\},\dots,\{i_{d}^{t},{i^{\star}_{d}}\}\big]\text{ is not reducible with respect to }\mathcal{P}_{t}\Big].

Indeed, the first line is by the assumption on
T
and the fact that each
Q_{j}
is an independent and uniformly random subset of
I_{j}\setminus\{i_{j}^{\star}\}
of size
\ell
,
the second line is by the definition of reducibility, the third line is due to the fact that
Q_{j}\cup\{{i^{\star}_{j}}\}\supseteq\{i_{j}^{t},{i^{\star}_{j}}\}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, and the last line is by the independence of
(i_{1}^{t},\dots,i_{d}^{t})
between different
t
.

We deduce that there is some
t\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
such that, with probability at most
\delta^{1/\ell}
, the random subtensor
T\big[\{i_{1}^{t},{i^{\star}_{1}}\},\dots,\{i_{d}^{t},{i^{\star}_{d}}\}\big]
is not reducible with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{t}
.
That is to say, all but at most a
\delta^{1/\ell}
-fraction of
2\times\dots\times 2
subtensors of
T
containing the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
are reducible with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, as desired.
∎

In our final lemma, we observe that if
T
has many
2\times\dots\times 2
subtensors involving a single nonzero entry, which are all reducible with respect to the same partition, then we can construct a reducible tensor that agrees with
T
on many entries.

Lemma 4.6
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and
\delta>0
. Let
n_{0}
be sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
\delta
.
Consider a tensor
T:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
(represented as a
d
-dimensional array) with
|I_{1}|,\dots,|I_{d}|\geq n_{0}
, and a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
, and suppose
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})\neq 0
for some indices
{i^{\star}_{1}}\in I_{1},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}}\in I_{d}
.
Suppose that all but at most a
\delta
-fraction of the
2\times\dots\times 2
subtensors of
T
which contain the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
are reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
.

Then one can make
T
reducible by changing up to a
2\delta
-fraction of its entries.

Proof.

Suppose without loss of generality that
J_{1}=\{1,\dots,h\}
and
J_{2}=\{h+1,\dots,d\}
.
Let
T^{\prime}:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
be the tensor defined by

T^{\prime}(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})=\frac{T(i_{1},\dots,i_{h},{i^{\star}_{h+1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})\,T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{h}},i_{h+1},\dots,i_{d})}{T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})}.

By definition,
T^{\prime}
is reducible. Indeed, we have
T^{\prime}=T_{1}\otimes T_{2}
for
T_{1}:I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{h}\to\mathbb{F}
given by
T_{1}(i_{1},\dots,i_{h})=T(i_{1},\dots,i_{h},{i^{\star}_{h+1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
and
T_{2}:I_{h+1}\times\dots\times I_{d}\to\mathbb{F}
given by
T_{1}(i_{h+1},\dots,i_{d})=T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{h}},i_{h+1},\dots,i_{d})/T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
. It remains to show that
T^{\prime}
differs from
T
in at most a
2\delta
-fraction of its entries.

To see this, note that for all
i_{1}\in I_{1}\setminus\{{i^{\star}_{1}}\},\dots,i_{d}\in I_{d}\setminus\{{i^{\star}_{d}}\}
such that
T\big[\{i_{1},i_{1}^{\star}\},\dots,\{i_{d},i_{d}^{\star}\}\big]
is reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
, we have

T(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\,T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})-T(i_{1},\dots,i_{h},{i^{\star}_{h+1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})\,T({i^{\star}_{1}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{h}},i_{h+1},\dots,i_{d})=0

(this can be interpreted as a determinant of a
2\times 2
matrix of rank at most
1
, recalling
Fact

4.2
).
Rearranging, we obtain
T(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})=T^{\prime}(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
. But, by assumption, the number of such choices of
i_{1},\dots,i_{d}
is at least
(1-\delta)\prod_{i=1}^{d}(|I_{i}|-1)\geq(1-2\delta)\prod_{i=1}^{d}|I_{i}|
. (Here we are using that
I_{1},\dots,I_{d}
are sufficiently large with respect to
d
and
\delta
).
∎

We now combine
Lemmas

4.4
,
4.5
, and
4.6
to prove
Lemma

2.2
.

Proof of
Lemma

2.2
.

As in
Lemma

4.5
, let
\ell:=2^{d-1}-1
, and note that then
\delta=(\varepsilon/2)^{2^{d-1}}=(\varepsilon/2)^{\ell+1}
.
If at most a
\delta^{1/(\ell+1)}
-fraction of the entries in
T
are nonzero, then
T
can be made zero by changing up to a
\varepsilon
-fraction of the entries; we are done in this case.
So, assume that
T
contains more than a
\delta^{1/(\ell+1)}
-fraction of nonzero entries. We apply
Lemma

4.4
with
r=2^{d-1}
and
c=1/(\ell+1)
to see that
T
has a nonzero entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},{i^{\star}_{2}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
such that all but at most a
\delta^{\ell/(\ell+1)}
-fraction of the
2^{d-1}\times\dots\times 2^{d-1}
subtensors which contain this entry are reducible.
By
Lemma

4.5
,
there is a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
such that all but at most a
\delta^{1/(\ell+1)}
-fraction of the
2\times\dots\times 2
subtensors of
T
containing the entry
T({i^{\star}_{1}},{i^{\star}_{2}},\dots,{i^{\star}_{d}})
, are reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
. Then,
Lemma

4.6

guarantees that
T
can be made reducible by changing a fraction of up to
2\delta^{1/(\ell+1)}=\varepsilon
of its entries, as desired.
∎

5.
Linear subspaces of the variety of reducible tensors

In this section, we prove
Lemma

2.6
, characterising the linear subspaces of the variety of reducible tensors.

First, note that we can break up the set of reducible tensors according to the partition with respect to which they are reducible.

Fact 5.1
.

Fix disjoint sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{d}
, fix
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
and let
r=\prod_{j=1}^{d}|I_{j}|
. Let
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{I_{d}}
be the set of reducible
I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}
tensors, and for a non-trivial partition
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
, let
\mathcal{Z}_{J_{1},J_{2}}
be the set of
I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{d}
tensors that are reducible with respect to
\{J_{1},J_{2}\}
. Then

\mathcal{Z}=\bigcup_{\{J_{1},J_{2}\}}\mathcal{Z}_{J_{1},J_{2}},

where the union is over all
2^{d-1}-1
unordered non-trivial partitions
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
.

The above fact, together with
Fact

4.2
, makes
Lemma

2.6
(1) nearly immediate (i.e., the observation that the reducible tensors form a variety).

Proof of
Lemma

2.6
(1).

By
Fact

5.1
, it suffices to prove that each
\mathcal{Z}_{J_{1},J_{2}}
is an affine variety, and by
Fact

4.2
, it suffices to show that the set of
r_{1}\times r_{2}
matrices with rank at most 1 can be interpreted as an affine variety in
\mathbb{F}^{r_{1}r_{2}}
(i.e., it is the zero locus of a system of polynomial equations). This is well-known to be true: a matrix has rank at most 1 if and only if every
2\times 2
submatrix has zero determinant.
∎

Next, we prove
Lemma

2.6
(2), classifying the maximal linear subspaces of the variety of reducible tensors. In this proof, we need the simple algebraic geometry fact that linear subspaces are irreducible as affine varieties. This fact also holds in the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, even though the underlying field is not algebraically closed (it holds over any infinite field).

Proof of
Lemma

2.6
(2).

Recall that all linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{r}
are irreducible as affine varieties.
So, recalling
Fact

5.1
, any linear subspace of
\mathcal{Z}=\bigcup_{\{J_{1},J_{2}\}}\mathcal{Z}_{J_{1},J_{2}}
must in fact be a linear subspace of some particular
\mathcal{Z}_{J_{1},J_{2}}
.
That is to say, it suffices to classify the maximal linear subspaces of
\mathcal{Z}_{J_{1},J_{2}}
.
By
Fact

4.2
, this is really the same as classifying the maximal linear subspaces of the variety of
r_{1}\times r_{2}
matrices with rank at most 1.
That is to say, it suffices to prove the lemma in the case
d=2
(recalling that matrices are 2-dimensional tensors).

So, fix disjoint sets
I_{1},I_{2}
, and let
\mathcal{V}
be a linear subspace of the variety
\mathcal{Z}
of
I_{1}\times I_{2}
tensors (matrices) which have rank at most 1.
Our objective is to prove that there is a “column vector”
\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}
such that

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\{\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{x}:\vec{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{2}}\}

or there is a “row vector”
\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{2}}
such that

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\{\vec{y}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}:\vec{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\}.

Indeed, this will show that we have
\mathcal{V}=\{\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{x}:\vec{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{2}}\}
or
\mathcal{V}=\{\vec{y}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}:\vec{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}\}
, whenever
\mathcal{V}
is a maximal linear subspace of
\mathcal{Z}
.

If
\mathcal{V}
contains only the zero matrix, the statement trivially holds.
So, we can assume that
\mathcal{V}
contains a nonzero matrix, i.e., a matrix of rank exactly
1
. This means that there are nonzero vectors
\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}
and
\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{2}}
such that
\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\in\mathcal{V}
.

Claim 5.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{V}
. Then,
T
is of the form
\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{x}
for some
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{2}}
, or
\vec{y}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}
for some
\vec{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}
.

Proof of claim.

T\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
has rank at most 1, so we can write
T=\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}
for some
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{1}}
and
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{F}^{I_{2}}
.
We need to prove that
\vec{u}
is a multiple of
\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}
or
\vec{v}
is a multiple of
\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}
.

We can interpret
\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}+u^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}
as a matrix product
PQ
, where
P
is the
|I_{1}|\times 2
matrix with columns
\vec{u},\vec{u}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}
and
Q
is the
2\times|I_{2}|
matrix with rows
\vec{v},\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}
. Since
\mathcal{V}
is a linear space, we know that
\vec{u}\otimes\vec{v}+u^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}=T+u^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\otimes\vec{v}^{\hskip 0.40974pt\star}\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
has rank at most 1, so at least one of
P
or
Q
has rank at most 1 (since the product of a
|I_{1}|\times 2
matrix of rank
2
with a
2\times|I_{2}|
matrix or rank 2 always has rank 2 as well). That is to say,
\vec{u}
is a multiple of

2.4
: noting that
T^{\prime}[Q_{1},\dots,Q_{d-1}]=T[Q_{1},\dots,Q_{d-1},I_{d}]\,\vec{\xi}[I_{d}]
in the notation of
Lemma

2.4
, we see that at least a
(1-\delta/2)
-fraction of the
2^{d-1}\times\dots\times 2^{d-1}
subtensors of
T[Q_{1},\dots,Q_{d-1},I_{d}]
are reducible.
Summing over all these
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{d-1}
, we see that the fraction of
2^{d-1}\times\dots\times 2^{d-1}
subtensors of
T
that are reducible is at least
(1-\delta/2)(1-\delta/2)>1-\delta
.
In the end,
Lemma

2.2
implies that
T
can be made reducible by changing at most
\varepsilon|I_{1}|\cdots|I_{d}|\leq\varepsilon n^{d}
of its entries. This proves
Item

1
, as desired.
∎

7.
A local-to-global lemma for low-rank symmetric matrices

In this section we prove
Lemma

2.7
:
if almost all
r\times r
submatrices of a symmetric matrix
A
are singular, then
A
can be made to have rank less than
r
by changing only a few entries,
while maintaining symmetry
. Recall that
\|A\|_{0}
denotes the number of nonzero entries in a matrix
A
.

We split the proof of
Lemma

2.7
into the following two lemmas.
Roughly speaking, we first approximate
A
by a matrix of rank less than
r
, which may not be symmetric.
However, this matrix is necessarily
almost
symmetric. In the second step we adjust it to be symmetric while maintaining the rank. Most of the difficulty lies in the second step.

Lemma 7.1
.

Consider integers
n,m\geq r\geq 1
, let
\alpha\in[0,1]
and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field. Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
be a matrix such that all but at most an
\alpha
-fraction of its
r\times r
submatrices are singular.
Then, there exists a matrix
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
of rank less than
r
such that
\|A-B\|_{0}\leq\alpha^{1/r}nm
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Consider integers
n\geq q\geq 1
, let
\rho\in[0,1]
and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
be a matrix of rank at most
q
such that
\|A-A^{T}\|_{0}\leq\rho n^{2}
.
Then, there exists a symmetric matrix
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
of rank at most
q
such that
\|A-B\|_{0}\leq O(q^{4}\rho n^{2})
.

Lemma

2.7
follows immediately from
Lemma

7.1
and
Lemma

7.2
, as follows.

Proof of
Lemma

2.7
.

By
Lemma

7.1
, we first obtain a matrix
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
with rank less than
r
and
\|A-B\|_{0}\leq\alpha^{1/r}n^{2}
.
Since
A
is symmetric, it holds that

\|B-B^{T}\|_{0}\leq\|B-A^{T}\|_{0}+\|A^{T}-B^{T}\|_{0}=\|B-A\|_{0}+\|A-B\|_{0}\leq 2\alpha^{1/r}n^{2}.

Then,
Lemma

7.2
(with
q=r-1
) guarantees a symmetric matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
of rank less than
r
such that
\|B-A^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq O(r^{4}\alpha^{1/r}n^{2})
.
Also,
\|A-A^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq\|A-B\|_{0}+\|B-A^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq O(r^{4}\alpha^{1/r}n^{2})
, as desired.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to proving
Lemma

7.1
and
Lemma

7.2
.
We first give a short proof of
Lemma

7.1
. For the rest of this section, we use the notation
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.1
.

First, find the minimum
k\geq 1
such that all but at most an
\alpha^{k/r}
-fraction of the
k\times k
submatrices of the matrix
A
are singular.
Clearly,
k\leq r
by assumption.
If
k=1
, then
\|A\|_{0}\leq\alpha^{1/r}nm
, and we are done (by taking
B=0
).
From now on, we assume that
2\leq k\leq r
.

Second, we claim that there exist sets
I\subseteq[n],J\subseteq[m]
, both of size
k-1
, such that
A[I,J]
is nonsingular and
A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
is nonsingular for at most
\alpha^{1/r}(n-k+1)(m-k+1)
choices of
(i,j)\in([n]\setminus I)\times([n]\setminus J)
.
Indeed, suppose not. Then, whenever
A[I,J]
is nonsingular, more than
\alpha^{1/r}(n-k+1)(m-k+1)
choices of
(i,j)\in([n]\setminus I)\times([n]\setminus J)
are such that
A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
is nonsingular.
In addition, the minimality of
k
implies there are more than
\alpha^{(k-1)/r}\binom{n}{k-1}\binom{m}{k-1}
choices of
I,J
such that
A[I,J]
is nonsingular.
In total, we acquire more than

\alpha^{(k-1)/r}\binom{n}{k-1}\binom{m}{k-1}\cdot\alpha^{1/r}(n-k+1)(m-k+1)=k^{2}\alpha^{k/r}\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{k}

choices of
I,J,i,j
such that
A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
is a nonsingular
k\times k
submatrix of
A
. Since each submatrix is counted at most
k^{2}
times, the number of nonsingular
k\times k
submatrices of
A
is larger than
\alpha^{k/r}\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{k}
, contradicting our definition of
k
.

We have established that there is some choice of
I
and
J
such that
A[I,J]
is nonsingular, and
A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
is nonsingular for at most
\alpha^{1/r}(n-k+1)(m-k+1)
choices of
(i,j)
.
Fix such
I,J
, and let
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
be the unique matrix with the same row space as
A[I,[m]]
, such that
B[[n],J]=A[[n],J]
.
(In a bit more detail: since
A[I,J]
is invertible, for each row
A[i,[m]]
of
A
there is a unique vector in the row space of
A[I,[m]]
which agrees with
A[i,[m]]
in the coordinates indexed by
J
; we take that to be the corresponding row of
B
.)
Note that
B[I,[m]]=A[I,[m]]
and
B[[n],J]=A[[n],J]
, and furthermore

\rank B=\rank(A[I,[m]])=|I|=k-1<r
.

Now, take any
(i,j)\in([n]\setminus I)\times([n]\setminus J)
such that
A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
is singular; there are at least
(1-\alpha^{1/r})(n-k+1)(m-k+1)
such
(i,j)
, thanks to the choice of
(I,J)
.
Consider the two matrices
A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
and
B[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}]
.
Their ranks are both less than
k
, i.e.
\det(A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}])=\det(B[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}])=0
.
Hence,
\det(A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}])-\det(B[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}])=0
.
As discussed above, these two matrices are identical except that, possibly,
A[i,j]\neq B[i,j]
.
This means that

0=\big|\det(A[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}])-\det(B[I\cup\{i\},J\cup\{j\}])\big|=\big|\det(A[I,J])(A[i,j]-B[i,j])\big|.

But
\det(A[I,J])\neq 0
because
A[I,J]
is nonsingular.
So,
A[i,j]=B[i,j]
.
Recall also that
A[i,j]=B[i,j]
whenever
i\in I
or
j\in J
.
Therefore,
\|A-B\|_{0}\leq\alpha^{1/r}(n-k+1)(m-k+1)<\alpha^{1/r}nm
.
∎

Lemma

7.2
is more complicated.
Our strategy is as follows. First, we pass from
A
to a large submatrix
A[I,I]
, whose rank is “robust” (i.e., the rank is maintained whenever we delete a few rows and columns). Then, we show how to use this robustness to approximate
A[I,I]
with a symmetric matrix with the same row space. Finally, we show how this approximation can be extended to all of
A
.

The first of these steps (passing to a large submatrix whose rank is robust) is rather simple, as follows.

Lemma 7.3
.

Consider integers
n\geq q\geq 1
, let
\gamma>0
, and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
be a matrix with rank at most
q
.
Then, there exists
k\in\{0,\dots,q\}
and
I\subseteq[n]
of size
|I|\geq(1-q\gamma)n
such that
\rank(A[I^{\prime},I^{\prime}])=\rank(A[I,I])=k
for all
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
of size
|I^{\prime}|\geq|I|-\gamma n
.

Proof.

Take the minimum
k\in\{0,\dots,q\}
such that
\rank(A[I,I])\leq k
holds for some
I\subseteq[n]
of size
|I|\geq(1-(q-k)\gamma)n
; to see that such
k
exists, consider
k=q
.
We claim this
k
and
I
satisfy the conclusion of the lemma.
Clearly,
|I|\geq(1-q\gamma)n
.
Fix an arbitrary
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
with
|I^{\prime}|\geq|I|-\gamma n
.
Then, since
|I^{\prime}|\geq(1-(q-k+1)\gamma)n
, the minimality of
k
implies that
\rank(A[I^{\prime},I^{\prime}])\geq k
. That is to say,
k\geq\rank(A[I,I])\geq\rank(A[I^{\prime},I^{\prime}])\geq k
, so
\rank(A[I^{\prime},I^{\prime}])=\rank(A[I,I])=k
.
∎

The next lemma shows that when a matrix “has its rank robustly”, we can approximate it by a symmetric matrix with the same row space. The proof approach is similar to the approach of the second and third authors in
[
35
, Section 5.7]
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Consider integers
n\geq 1
and
r\geq 0
, let
\rho\in[0,1]
and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
be a matrix with
\|A-A^{T}\|_{0}\leq\rho n^{2}
, such that
\rank(A[I,I])=r
for all
I\subseteq[n]
of size
|I|\geq(1-r\sqrt{\rho})n
.
Then, there exists a symmetric matrix
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that
\|A-B\|_{0}\leq(r^{2}+1)\rho n^{2}
, and such that the row spaces of
A
and
B
are the same.

Proof.

Note that in the case
r=0
we must have
A=0
, and so we can take
B=0
. So let us from now on assume
r\geq 1
.

For
i,j\in[n]
, say that the pair
(i,j)
is
bad
if
a_{ij}\neq a_{ji}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
, write
N(i):=\{1\leq j\leq n:a_{ij}\neq a_{ji}\}
.
Also, define
{I_{\text{bad}}}
to be the set of indices
i
such that
|N(i)|\geq\sqrt{\rho}n
; hence,

|{I_{\text{bad}}}|\leq\|A-A^{T}\|_{0}/\sqrt{\rho}n\leq\sqrt{\rho}n.

First, we claim that there exists
V\subseteq[n]\setminus{I_{\text{bad}}}
of size
r
such that
A[V,V]
is symmetric and
\rank(A[[n],V])=r
.
To this end, we iteratively find, for each
\ell\in[r]
, a sequence
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}\in[n]\setminus{I_{\text{bad}}}
such that
\rank(A[[n],\{v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}\}])=\ell
and such that no
(v_{i},v_{j})
is a bad pair.
Suppose
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,r-1\}
and we have found
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
with these properties.
For all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we have
|N(v_{i})|\leq\sqrt{\rho}n
because
v_{i}\notin{I_{\text{bad}}}
.
Thus,
\tilde{I}:=[n]\setminus({I_{\text{bad}}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}N(v_{i}))
has size
|\tilde{I}|\geq n-\sqrt{\rho}n-\ell\sqrt{\rho}n\geq(1-r\sqrt{\rho})n
.
By our assumption on
A
,
\rank(A[[n],\tilde{I}])\geq\rank(A[\tilde{I},\tilde{I}])=r>\ell
, so there exists
v_{\ell+1}\in\tilde{I}
such that
\rank(A[[n],\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\ell+1}\}])=\ell+1
.
In addition, for each
i\in[\ell]
, the pair
(v_{i},v_{\ell+1})
is not bad because
v_{\ell+1}\in\tilde{I}\subseteq[n]\setminus N(v_{i})
.
This completes the iteration step. Having found
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
at the end of the iterative procedure, we set
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
, and note that
A[V,V]
is symmetric since none of the pairs
(v_{i},v_{j})
is bad.

Fix
V
as above. We next claim that
\rank(A[V,V])=r
, i.e.,
A[V,V]
is invertible.
To see this, first note that since
\rank(A[[n],V])=r=\rank(A)
, the column vectors of
A[[n],V]
span those of
A=A[[n],[n]]
.
Let
I:=[n]\setminus\bigcup_{v\in V}N(v)
; it holds that

(7.1)

|I|\geq n-\sum_{i=1}^{r}|N(v_{i})|\geq(1-r\sqrt{\rho})n.

The above discussion indicates that the column vectors of
A[I,V]
span those of
A[I,I]
, so
\rank(A[I,V])\geq\rank(A[I,I])
.
Similarly, we have
\rank(A[V,V])\geq\rank(A[V,I])
.
In addition, the definition of
I
guarantees that
a_{vi}=a_{iv}
for all
v\in V
and all
i\in I
. In short,
A[V,I]=A[I,V]^{T}
.
So,

\rank(A[V,V])\geq\rank(A[V,I])=\rank(A[I,V])\geq\rank(A[I,I])=r,

where the last inequality follows as
|I|\geq(1-r\sqrt{\rho})n
. That is to say,
\rank(A[V,V])=r
, i.e.,
A[V,V]
is invertible.

Now, take
B:=A[V,[n]]^{T}A[V,V]^{-1}A[V,[n]]
. Note that
B
is symmetric, and
\rank(B)\leq|V|=r
.
By the preceding discussion,

B[V,V]=A[V,V]^{T}A[V,V]^{-1}A[V,V]=A[V,V]

has rank
r
, so
\rank(B)=r
.
Also, the row vectors of
B
are spanned by those of
A
.
This, plus the fact that
\rank(B)=r=\rank(A)
, demonstrates that the row space of
B
is the same as that of
A
.

To complete the proof, it remains to show that
\|A-B\|_{0}\leq(r^{2}+1)\rho n^{2}
.
Since
r=\rank(A)\geq\rank(A[V,[n]])\geq\rank(A[V,V])=r
, we see that
A[V,[n]]
has the same row space as
A
,
i.e., for each
i\in[n]
, we have
A[i,[n]]=\vec{x}_{i}A[V,[n]]
for some
\vec{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{V}
.
In particular,
A[i,V]=\vec{x}_{i}A[V,V]
, i.e.
\vec{x}_{i}=A[i,V]A[V,V]^{-1}
.
Hence,
A[i,[n]]=A[i,V]A[V,V]^{-1}A[V,[n]]
.
By considering all
i\in I
, we deduce

(7.2)

A[I,[n]]=A[I,V]A[V,V]^{-1}A[V,[n]]=A[V,I]^{T}A[V,V]^{-1}A[V,[n]]=B[I,[n]].

Writing
I^{\mathrm{c}}=[n]\setminus I
, note that for any
i\in I^{\mathrm{c}}
and
j\in I
, the above equality implies that
b_{ij}=b_{ji}=a_{ji}
. So, we have
b_{ij}\neq a_{ij}
if and only if
a_{ij}\neq a_{ji}
.
We deduce that

(7.3)

\big\|A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]-B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]\|_{0}\leq\|A-A^{T}\|_{0}\leq\rho n^{2}.

Considering separately the subsets of entries indexed by
I\times[n]
,
I^{\mathrm{c}}\times I
and
I^{\mathrm{c}}\times I^{\mathrm{c}}
, using
7.1
,
7.2
, and
7.3
, we conclude

\|A-B\|_{0}\leq\big\|A[I,[n]]-B[I,[n]]\big\|_{0}+\big\|A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]-B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]\big\|_{0}+|I^{\mathrm{c}}|^{2}\leq(r^{2}+1)\rho n^{2}.\qed

Before completing the proof of
Lemma

7.2
, we record a necessary inequality on matrix ranks.

Lemma 7.5
.

Consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
over any field
\mathbb{F}
(for any integer
n\geq 1
). Then, for any
I,J\subseteq[n]
, we have

\rank(A)\geq\rank(A[I,[n]])+\rank(A[[n],J])-\rank(A[I,J]).

Proof.

Write
\Delta:=\rank(A[[n],J])-\rank(A[I,J])\geq 0
.
There exist distinct
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{\Delta}\in[n]\setminus I
such that for each
k\in\{1,\dots,\Delta\}
, the row vector
A[i_{k},J]
is not spanned by the row vectors of
A[I,J]
along with
A[i_{1},J],\dots,A[i_{k-1},J]
.
Hence, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,\Delta\}
, the row vector
A[i_{k},[n]]
is not spanned by the row vectors of
A[I,[n]]
along with
A[i_{1},[n]],\dots,A[i_{k-1},[n]]
.
In other words,
\rank(A[I\cup\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\Delta}\},[n]])\geq\rank(A[I,[n]])+\Delta
.
This shows
\rank(A)\geq\rank(A[I,[n]])+\Delta
, as desired.
∎

We now complete the proof of
Lemma

7.2
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.2
.

We first apply
Lemma

7.3
with
\gamma=q\sqrt{\rho}
to obtain
k\in\{0,\dots,q\}
and
I\subseteq[n]
of size
|I|\geq(1-q^{2}\sqrt{\rho})n
such that
\rank(A[I^{\prime},I^{\prime}])=\rank(A[I,I])=k
for all
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
of size
|I^{\prime}|\geq|I|-q\sqrt{\rho}n
.
Write
\rank(A[[n],I])=k+\Delta_{1}
and
\rank(A[I,[n]])=k+\Delta_{2}
; clearly,
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\geq 0
.
According to
Lemma

7.5
,
q\geq\rank(A)\geq k+\Delta_{1}+k+\Delta_{2}-k=k+\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\Delta_{1}\leq\Delta_{2}
(otherwise replace
A
with
A^{T}
).
Hence,
k+2\Delta_{1}\leq k+\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\leq q
.

Now,
Lemma

7.4
applied to
A[I,I]
(with
r=k
) yields a symmetric matrix
C\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times I}
such that

(7.4)

\|A[I,I]-C\|_{0}\leq(q^{2}+1)\rho n^{2}

and such that the row space of
C
is the same as that of
A[I,I]
.
The latter property implies that
A[I,I]=P_{1}C
for some
P_{1}\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times I}
.

We have shown how to approximate
A[I,I]
with the symmetric matrix
C
; we now need to deal with the other parts of the matrix
A
.
Recall that
\rank(A[[n],I])=\rank(A[I,I])+\Delta_{1}
.
Letting
I^{\mathrm{c}}=[n]\setminus I
, this means we can write
A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]=P_{2}A[I,I]+Q
, for some
P_{2},Q\in\mathbb{F}^{I^{\mathrm{c}}\times I}
and
\rank(Q)=\Delta_{1}
.
Let
P:=P_{2}P_{1}\in\mathbb{F}^{I^{\mathrm{c}}\times I}
, so
A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]=PC+Q
.

Now, let
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
be the symmetric matrix defined by

B[I,I]=C,\quad B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]=B[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]^{T}=PC+Q,\quad B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I^{\mathrm{c}}]=PCP^{T}+PQ^{T}+QP^{T}.

By construction, and our assumption on
A
, we have

(7.5)

\displaystyle\|B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]-A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]\|_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.6)

\displaystyle\|B[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]-A[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]\|_{0}

\displaystyle=\|B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]^{T}-A[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]\|_{0}=\|A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]^{T}-A[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]\|_{0}\leq\rho n^{2}.

Combining
7.6
and
7.5
with
7.4
yields

\displaystyle\|A-B\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\|C-A[I,I]\|_{0}\;+\;\|B[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]-A[I^{\mathrm{c}},I]\|_{0}\;+\;\|B[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]-A[I,I^{\mathrm{c}}]\|_{0}\;+\;|I^{\mathrm{c}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(q^{2}+1)\rho n^{2}+\rho n^{2}+q^{4}\rho n^{2}=O(q^{4}\rho n^{2}).

It remains to show that
\rank(B)\leq q
. This follows from a short sequence of elementary row and column operations:

\displaystyle\rank(B)

\displaystyle=\rank\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{ c | c }C&CP^{T}+Q^{T}\\
\hline\cr PC+Q&PCP^{T}+PQ^{T}+QP^{T}\end{array}}}\right]=\rank\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{ c | c }C&Q^{T}\\
\hline\cr PC+Q&PQ^{T}\end{array}}}\right]=\rank\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{ c | c }C&Q^{T}\\
\hline\cr Q&0\end{array}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\rank(C)+2\rank(Q)=k+2\Delta_{1}\leq q\,.\qed

8.
Decoupling inequalities

In this section we prove
Lemmas

2.12
and
2.16
, which are the decoupling inequalities that will be used in the proofs of
Theorems

1.11
and
1.10
respectively.
Both lemmas use the following fact, which is an easy application of Jensen’s inequality.

Lemma 8.1

(Decoupling with multiple copies)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}(E,F)
be an event depending on independent random objects
E,F
.
Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}(E,F)]\leq\mathbb{P}\big[\mathcal{E}(E_{0},F)\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{E}(E_{k},F)\big]^{1/(k+1)},

where
E_{0},\dots,E_{k}
are independent copies of
E
.

Proof.

By the law of total probability and the independence of
E_{0},\dots,E_{k}
, we have

This means that we can find at least

distinct

, such that the coefficient of
\xi_{i(1)}\dotsm\xi_{i(q)}
in the polynomial
P(\xi_{1}\vec{a}\mkern 2.0mu\vphantom{a}^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+\xi_{m}\vec{a}\mkern 2.0mu\vphantom{a}^{\prime}_{m})
is nonzero. Thus, the polynomial

has indeed at least
\varepsilon^{\prime}m^{q}
nonzero coefficients.
This completes the proof.
∎

A counterexample to some conjectures of Costello

(By Matthew Kwan, Ashwin Sah and Mehtaab Sawhney)

Here we prove

\mathbb{P}[f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=0]\geq\mathbb{P}\big[L_{1}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=0\text{ and }L_{2d}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=0\big]=\Omega((1/\sqrt{n})^{2})\geq\varepsilon/n,

We also need a simple combinatorial fact about of sums of vectors.

Definition B.3

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\vec{s}_{1},\vec{s}_{2},\vec{t}_{1},\vec{t}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}

(\vec{s}_{1}+\vec{t}_{2})_{\downarrow}\succ\vec{v}_{\downarrow}\text{ or }(\vec{t}_{1}+\vec{s}_{2})_{\downarrow}\succ\vec{v}_{\downarrow}.

Without loss of generality, suppose that
\vec{v}_{\downarrow}=\vec{v}

Note that the condition
(\vec{s}_{1},\vec{s}_{2})\neq(\vec{t}_{1},\vec{t}_{2})

\vec{s}_{1}+\vec{s}_{2}=\vec{t}_{1}+\vec{t}_{2}
