Yet another report of endotracheal tube herniation through the Doughty blade of the Boyle-Davis Gag! Call for action
Authors: Merlin Shalini Ruth S, Raghuraman M Sethuraman
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We present two cases of adenotonsillectomy in which the endotracheal tube (ETT) herniated through the slot of the Doughty tongue-plate of the Boyle-Davis gag (BDG). In both cases, a 5.5 size ETT was used, whereas the surgeon used a 4-inch slotted Doughty blade in the BDG. The procedures were uneventful; however, at the end of the procedure, the surgeon could not dislodge the Doughty plate from the BDG because the ETT was impacted in the slot (Fig. 1). Extubation was performed in the deep plane, and the patient was placed in the right lateral position. One child recovered well with spontaneous respiration, whereas the other required bag and mask ventilation for approximately 10 min.

The consequences of this problem are as follows: First, it can lead to accidental extubation upon the removal of the tongue blade at the end of the procedure. Second, even if accidental extubation is avoided, the tongue blade, which remains in situ, can provoke premature extubation. This necessitates the management of complications such as laryngospasm, aspiration, obstruction, hypoventilation due to residual anesthetic effects, and delayed recovery. Third, it can cause tube kinking, resulting in either inadequate ventilation or the requirement for higher airway pressure to deliver the same tidal volume intraoperatively. Fourth, the ETT cuff remains inflated if the inflation tube is entrapped [1]. Although we were able to circumvent these problems, any informed anesthesiologist would agree that it is wise to avoid this predicament.

Upon searching the literature, we observed that this problem was reported in the mid-1980s, and two important solutions were suggested. First, Buckley and Bush analyzed this problem and reported that the root cause was the choice of an inappropriate Doughty plate size for the ETT. They specified that for ETT sizes up to 5.5 mm, a 3-inch blade should be used [2]. In our cases, a 4-inch blade was used, which resulted in the entrapment of the ETT. Second, modification by adding a metal bar in the middle of the Doughty tongue plate would further prevent this problem, as suggested by Sellick [3]. In an original invention in 1957, Doughty provided a metal bridge at the distal end of the plate to prevent wedging of the ETT. Interestingly, the use of an appropriately sized plate according to the size of the ETT (Magill's) was also suggested at that time itself [4]. The remarkable increase in the compressibility of ETTs at body temperature is an important consideration in the current scenario. Although these measures were suggested a long time ago, this problem continues to occur, and a few cases have been reported even in this century.

Recently, we discovered the feasibility of using a grooved blade (Fig. 2) instead of a slotted Doughty blade. It remains unclear whether this might prevent complications, but its availability and the surgeon's adaptation to this design need to be considered.

In summary, herniation of the ETT into the Doughty blade is a potential problem but is easily preventable if the points discussed here are strictly implemented with proper coordination and communication between the anesthesiologist and surgeon.
