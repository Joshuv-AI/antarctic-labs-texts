Parameter-free Dynamic Regret: Time-varying Movement Costs, Delayed Feedback, and Memory
Emmanuel Esposito, Andrew Jacobsen, Hao Qiu, Mengxiao Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we study dynamic regret in unconstrained online convex optimization (OCO) with movement costs. Specifically, we generalize the standard setting by allowing the movement cost coefficients λ t \lambda_{t} to vary arbitrarily over time. Our main contribution is a novel algorithm that establishes the first comparator-adaptive dynamic regret bound for this setting, guaranteeing 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ( 1 + P T ) ​ ( T + ∑ t λ t ) ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{(1+P_{T})(T+\sum_{t}\lambda_{t})}) regret, where P T P_{T} is the path length of the comparator sequence over T T rounds. This recovers the optimal guarantees for both static and dynamic regret in standard OCO as a special case where λ t = 0 \lambda_{t}=0 for all rounds. To demonstrate the versatility of our results, we consider two applications: OCO with delayed feedback and OCO with time-varying memory . We show that both problems can be translated into time-varying movement costs, establishing a novel reduction specifically for the delayed feedback setting that is of independent interest. A crucial observation is that the first-order dependence on movement costs in our regret bound plays a key role in enabling optimal comparator-adaptive dynamic regret guarantees in both settings.



1
Introduction

Online Convex Optimization (OCO)
(
6
;
19
;
41
)
provides a robust framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. In the standard paradigm, a learner iteratively selects a decision and incurs a convex loss, aiming to minimize regret—the difference between the learner’s cumulative loss and that of the best fixed decision in hindsight. However, in many practical systems, changing decisions is rarely cost-free. In domains such as optimal control
(
15
;
17
)
, video streaming
(
27
)
, and geographical load balancing
(
31
)
, rapid fluctuations in strategy incur significant operational overhead, commonly referred to as
switching
or
movement costs
. Motivated by these challenges, we study a variation of online optimization in which the learner incurs an additional penalty proportional to the distance between consecutive decisions. Beyond its direct relevance to the aforementioned applications, the analysis of OCO with movement costs is instrumental in solving broader fundamental problems. A prime example is online learning with memory
(
38
;
2
)
, a framework designed to capture temporal dependencies in learning tasks. Reductions involving OCO with movement costs have proven essential for deriving state-of-the-art results in this domain (e.g.,
2
;
1
;
14
;
58
).

On the other hand, modern online learning challenges are frequently characterized by unconstrained decision spaces and non-stationarity. A typical example is portfolio management with leverage and short-selling
(
35
)
, where an agent allocates unbounded capital across assets and must adapt to shifting market regimes. In such environments, static benchmarks prove overly conservative, requiring the use of
dynamic regret
to track a moving target. Crucially, rebalancing incurs costs, such as transaction fees and market impact, that are not constant, but rather fluctuate with market liquidity and volatility. These factors motivate a unified framework that evaluates performance against an evolving benchmark while explicitly accounting for time-varying movement penalties.

Despite this motivation, existing literature addresses these challenges only in isolation. While
56
;
57
study unconstrained OCO with movement costs and
53
investigates dynamic regret with movement costs, the intersection of these two settings remains unexplored. Furthermore, all prior works assume fixed movement penalties, a restriction that fails to capture the temporal fluctuations described above. This raises the question:

Can we design efficient algorithms that achieve near-optimal regret in unconstrained domains with time-varying movement costs?

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we answer this question affirmatively by developing the first parameter-free algorithm for unconstrained OCO that simultaneously handles dynamic regret and time-varying movement costs. Our primary result establishes a regret bound of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\sqrt{(M+P_{T})\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\|g_{t}\|^{2}+\lambda_{t}\|g_{t}\|)\|u_{t}\|}\Big)
, where
T
is the total horizon,
g_{t}
is the realized gradient at round
t

M
is the maximum norm of the comparator sequence,
\lambda_{t}
and
u_{t}
are the movement cost coefficient and the comparator at round
t
, and
P_{T}
represents the comparator sequence path length. This guarantee exhibits three key layers of adaptivity: it scales with the comparator complexity, exploits favorable geometry through realized first-order information, and adjusts to arbitrary fluctuations in movement penalties.

We further demonstrate the versatility of our framework via reductions to two applications, showing that OCO with movement costs can serve as a primitive for other tasks.
First, we show that OCO with delayed feedback reduces to time-varying movement costs by treating the number of missing gradients as the movement scale.
This reduction yields a parameter-free dynamic regret bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{\left(M^{2}+MP_{T}\right)\left(T+d_{\mathrm{tot}}\right)}\bigr)
, where
d_{\mathrm{tot}}
denotes the total delay.
Compared with
49
, who establish a bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{(1+P_{T})(T+d_{\mathrm{max}}T)}\bigr)
for
bounded
domains (where
d_{\mathrm{max}}
denotes the maximum delay) and only improve this to a
d_{\mathrm{tot}}
dependence under the restrictive assumption of in-order feedback, our result handles
unbounded
domains and achieves the tighter
d_{\mathrm{tot}}
dependence without assuming in-order arrival.

Second, under the assumption of coordinate-wise Lipschitz continuity, OCO with time-varying memory can be similarly reduced to our setting. This yields a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{(M^{2}+MP_{T})\left(H^{2}T+GH\sum_{t}b_{t}^{2}\right)}\bigr)
regret bound, where
G
is the coordinate-wise Lipschitz constant,
H
bounds the gradient norm of the unary losses, and
b_{t}
denotes the time-varying memory length. Compared with
58

B\geq 1
over bounded domains and establish a parameter-free bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(\sqrt{(1+P_{T})\bigl(\sqrt{G}H^{2}B+GHB^{2}\bigr)T}\Bigr)

Problem Setting

Throughout the paper, we use
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
to denote the set of positive integers and
[m]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\dots,m\}
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Denote
\mathbf{0}
to be an all-zero vector in an appropriate dimension and we also use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the Euclidean norm.
A major focus of this paper is the problem of online convex optimization with
movement costs
.
The interaction between the learner and the environment lasts for
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
rounds. Before the game starts, the learner is given an unconstrained
n
-dimensional space
{\mathcal{W}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the decision set.
At each round
t\in[T]
, the learner selects
w_{t}\in{\mathcal{W}}
while the environment simultaneously selects a convex loss function
f_{t}:{\mathcal{W}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The learner then incurs the loss of their decision
f_{t}(w_{t})
with an additional penalty given by the movement cost
\lambda_{t}\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|
, where we define
w_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
Finally, the learner observes the gradient
g_{t}\triangleq\nabla f_{t}(w_{t})
as well as the movement-cost coefficient
\lambda_{t+1}
for the next round.
In this definition of movement costs, we consider arbitrary time-varying scaling factors
\lambda_{t}\geq 0
, with the initial one set to
\lambda_{1}=0
.
Without loss of generality, the final movement-cost coefficient is
\lambda_{T+1}=0
.

The goal of the learner is to minimize the dynamic regret:

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}(u_{1:T},\lambda_{1:T})\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(w_{t})+\lambda_{t}\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|\bigr)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(u_{t})+\lambda_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|\bigr)\;,

where
(u_{1},\dots,u_{T})\in{\mathcal{W}}^{T}
is an arbitrary sequence of comparators and
u_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.

We now introduce relevant quantities for this problem.
Define
P_{T}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|
to be the path-length of comparators, which is the most standard measure of complexity for the comparator sequence in dynamic regret.
Also define
M\triangleq\max_{t\in[T]}\|u_{t}\|
to be the maximum norm over the comparator sequence and
\lambda_{\max}\triangleq\max_{t\in[T]}\lambda_{t}
as the largest movement-cost coefficient over the
T
rounds.

While the above definition of dynamic regret
{\mathcal{R}}_{t}
is “symmetric” in that both the learner’s and the comparator’s sequence suffer from movement costs, some prior works
(
53
;
58
)
on OCO with movement costs consider the following harder objective:

R_{T}(u_{1:T},\lambda_{1:T})\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(w_{t})-f_{t}(u_{t})+\lambda_{t}\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|\bigr)\;,

where only the learner incurs the additional penalty given by movements.
Compared to the previous notion of regret
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
, we can observe that
R_{T}
is always larger.
More precisely, they satisfy the identity
R_{T}(u_{1:t},\lambda_{1:T})={\mathcal{R}}_{T}(u_{1:t},\lambda_{1:T})+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|
showing that controlling
R_{T}
is strictly more demanding than controlling
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
, but is sufficient to provide an upper bound on the latter.
Correspondingly, our results follow this observation: we directly analyze
R_{T}
for the algorithm we propose, and this already implies a guarantee on
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
which may be additively worse by
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|\leq\lambda_{\max}P_{T}
only.
We stress that this does not pose an issue, as this additive term is of lower order compared to the dominating
\sqrt{\lambda(1+P_{T})T}
part of the minimax regret
(
58
, Theorem 5)
for constant movement coefficient
\lambda
.

Goal.

Our main goal is to design
parameter-free
algorithms for the above problem that guarantee dynamic regret bounds simultaneously adapting to
(i) the realized first-order information in the observed gradients
(g_{t})_{t\geq 1}
,
(ii) the (time-varying) movement-cost coefficients
(\lambda_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, and
(iii) the complexity of the comparator sequence
(u_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
Importantly, we do so in an
unconstrained
setting with
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which makes it substantially more challenging to obtain parameter-free guarantees that remain meaningful without a-priori bounds on comparator norms or path length.
Finally, we show that guarantees for OCO with time-varying movement costs yield clean reductions to other notable online learning problems: they imply parameter-free dynamic regret bounds for
OCO with time-varying memory
(via standard Lipschitzness assumptions) and, perhaps more surprisingly, for
OCO with delayed feedback
.

Other notations.

For a finite set
{\mathcal{S}}
, we use
|{\mathcal{S}}|
to denote the cardinality of
{\mathcal{S}}
.
The Bregman divergence with respect to a differentiable function
\psi:{\mathcal{W}}\to\mathbb{R}
is
D_{\psi}(x\mid y)\triangleq\psi(x)-\psi(y)-\langle\nabla\psi(y),x-y\rangle
.
Given
G\geq 0
, we say that a function
f:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
is
G
-Lipschitz if
\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|\leq G\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{W}
.

Composite Mirror Descent for Movement Costs

The intuition behind the update in
Equation

1
is to stabilize the mirror descent step. Specifically, while the regularizer
\psi
enables the aggressive growth necessary for unbounded domains, its lack of strong convexity can lead to instability. The correction term
\varphi_{t}
mitigates this by penalizing sharp increases in the iterate’s magnitude, a technique crucial for performance on unbounded domains as shown in
23

\beta_{t}^{2}=(\|g_{t}\|+\lambda_{t+1})^{2}
to explicitly account for the time-varying movement costs. Intuitively,
\beta_{t}

Following similar analysis to
23

4
Improved Adaptivity to Movement Costs

As noted previously, while
Algorithm

2
successfully adapts to gradient norms and comparator properties, its squared dependence on movement costs is suboptimal. To refine this adaptivity, we observe that in the presence of movement costs, not every gradient observation justifies an
immediate update
. Intuitively, when the movement penalty is high, the learner should only move if the first-order information has sufficient magnitude to compensate for the cost. Otherwise, it is beneficial to temporarily freeze the iterate and aggregate information until an update becomes worthwhile.

Building on this intuition, we introduce an adaptive batching meta-algorithm that yields refined dynamic regret guarantees, improving the movement cost dependence from
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}
to
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}\|g_{t}\|
.

5
Applications

To demonstrate the versatility of our results for OCO with movement cost, in this section, we consider two applications: OCO with delayed feedback and OCO with time-varying memory.
We show that both problems can be reduced to instances of OCO with movement costs, thus allowing us to directly apply
Algorithm

3
to obtain near-optimal regret bounds for both problems.

5.1
Unconstrained OCO with Delayed Feedback

We first address the problem of unconstrained OCO with delayed feedback.
The interaction proceeds as follows: at each round
t\in[T]
, the learner selects a decision
w_{t}\in{\mathcal{W}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
while the environment simultaneously chooses a convex loss function
f_{t}:{\mathcal{W}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The learner incurs the loss
f_{t}(w_{t})
, but unlike the standard OCO setting, the gradient
g_{t}=\nabla f_{t}(w_{t})
is only revealed at the end of round
t+d_{t}
, where
d_{t}\geq 0
is an arbitrary delay unknown to the learner.
Without loss of generality, we assume
t+d_{t}\leq T
for all
t
, ensuring all feedback is received by the end of the horizon.
The learner’s performance is measured by the standard dynamic regret:

R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T},\mathbf{0})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(w_{t})-f_{t}(u_{t})\bigr),

and we use
R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})\triangleq R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T},\mathbf{0})
as a shorthand; the superscript
\mathrm{del}
is there to clarify that the underlying problem setting considered is that of OCO with delayed feedback.

To facilitate our analysis, we introduce the following standard notations for OCO with delayed feedback.
Let
o_{t}\triangleq\{\tau\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:\tau+d_{\tau}<t\}\subseteq[t-1]
denote the set of rounds whose gradients have been observed prior to round
t
.
Accordingly, let
m_{t}\triangleq[t-1]\setminus o_{t}
denote the set of rounds whose gradients have not yet arrived prior to round
t
.
Using these definitions, the feedback protocol is equivalent to receiving the batch of gradients
\{g_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}
at the end of round
t
.
Finally, we define
\sigma_{\max}\triangleq\max_{t\in[T]}|m_{t}|
as the maximum number of outstanding gradients at any step, and
d_{\mathrm{tot}}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}
as the total delay.

To utilize
Algorithm
3
, which is designed for OCO with movement costs, we show a novel connection between delayed feedback and movement penalties. To our knowledge, this connection has not been previously explored in the literature. The following technical lemma bounds the dynamic regret
R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})
by a linearized loss constructed from the feedback observed at each round, plus a movement cost rescaled by the number of missing observations.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
f_{1},\dots,f_{T}
are
G
-Lipschitz convex functions.
Then

R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Biggl\langle\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau},w_{t}-u_{t}\Biggr\rangle+G\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|+GP_{T}\sigma_{\max}\,.

The proof of this lemma is deferred to
Section

D.1
.
Specifically,
Lemma

5.1
decomposes the dynamic regret
R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}
into three components: (i) a linearized regret term driven by the aggregated gradients
\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau}
received at the end of round
t
; (ii) a movement penalty
G\sum_{t}|m_{t}|\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|
, weighted by the count of missing gradients; and (iii) a constant additive term
GP_{T}\sigma_{\max}
that is independent of the learner’s decisions.
This structure suggests a direct reduction from OCO with delayed feedback to OCO with movement costs: we simply treat the aggregated gradients as the instantaneous feedback and set the movement-cost coefficient at round
t
to
\lambda_{t}=G|m_{t}|
.
This highlights the necessity of incorporating
time-varying
movement-cost coefficients since
|m_{t}|
is naturally fluctuating over time and only revealed at the beginning of round
t
.
From an intuitive perspective, a larger number of missing gradients increases the cost for updating the decision while lacking that information about past rounds, making the learner more conservative. Conversely, when we have less missing information, the effective movement cost decreases, encouraging faster adaptation as feedback becomes promptly available.

5.2
Unconstrained OCO with Time-varying Memory

We now turn to our second application: unconstrained OCO with time-varying memory. The protocol is defined as follows. The interaction proceeds in
T
rounds. At each round
t
, the learner selects a decision
w_{t}\in\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Simultaneously, the environment chooses a loss function
f_{t}:\mathcal{W}^{b_{t}+1}\to\mathbb{R}
that depends on the current decision and the
b_{t}
preceding ones, where
b_{t}\in\{0,\dots,t-1\}
is the memory length.
We assume the sequence of memory lengths
\{b_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is known to the learner in advance, and we define
B\triangleq\max_{t\in[T]}b_{t}
.
The learner incurs the loss
f_{t}(w_{t-b_{t}:t})
, where
w_{t-b_{t}:t}\triangleq(w_{t-b_{t}},\dots,w_{t})
, and subsequently observes the full loss function
f_{t}
.
Note that while observing the full function is standard in the OCO with memory literature
(
2
;
58
)
, it is a richer form of feedback compared to the gradient feedback considered so far. The learner’s goal is to minimize the dynamic regret with respect to any comparator sequence
(u_{1},\dots,u_{T})\in\mathcal{W}^{T}
, defined as follows:

R^{\mathrm{mem}}_{T}(u_{1:T})\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(w_{t-b_{t}:t})-f_{t}(u_{t-b_{t}:t})\bigr)\;.

Following
2
, we impose structural assumptions on the loss functions. First, we require that the losses are
convex in memory
, meaning that the induced unary function
\widehat{f}_{t}(w)\triangleq f_{t}(w,\dots,w)
is convex.
Second, we assume coordinate-wise Lipschitz continuity and bounded gradients for the unary functions
2
;
58
.

Assumption 5.4

(Coordinate-wise Lipschitzness)
.

The loss function
f_{t}
is coordinate-wise
G

, i.e., for all

and inputs

Assumption 5.5

The gradient norm of the unary losses is bounded by

6
Conclusion

In this paper we provide new dynamic regret guarantees for several natural extensions of the OCO problem.
We developed the first comparator-adaptive dynamic regret guarantees for OCO with time-varying movement costs, and we achieve a result which adapts to all problem-dependent quantities of interest.
This result also allows us to develop the first comparator-adaptive guarantees in settings with delayed feedback and
settings with time-varying memory.
All of our algorithms can be efficiently implemented in
{\mathcal{O}}(\log\left(T\right))
per-round computation, matching the best-known complexity for obtaining
\sqrt{P_{T}}
dependencies in the vanilla OCO setting.

Appendix A
Auxiliary Lemmas

In this section, we provide some auxiliary technical results that will be helpful throughout the paper.
The first lemma is a crucial component for the analysis of
Algorithm

3
, essentially showing that there exists a learning rate value in the exponential grid that achieves a close-to-optimal tuning.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
0<\eta_{\min}\leq\eta_{\max}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}=\left\{\eta_{i}=2^{i}\eta_{\min}\wedge\eta_{\max}:i=0,1,\dots\right\}
.
Then for any
P\geq 0
and
V\geq 0
, there exists a
\eta\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
such that

\frac{P}{\eta}+\eta V\leq 3\sqrt{PV}+\frac{P}{\eta_{\max}}+\eta_{\min}V\;.

Proof.

Let
\eta^{*}=\sqrt{P/V}
be the optimal tuning of
\eta
minimizing
\frac{P}{\eta}+\eta V
.
Suppose that there is
\eta_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
such that
\eta_{i}\leq\eta^{*}\leq\eta_{i+1}\leq 2\eta_{i}
.
Then choosing
\eta=\eta_{i}
yields

\frac{P}{\eta}+\eta V=\frac{P}{\eta_{i}}+\eta_{i}V\leq\frac{2P}{\eta^{*}}+\eta^{*}V=3\sqrt{PV}\;.

If
\eta^{*}\leq\eta_{\min}
, then choosing
\eta=\eta_{\min}=\eta_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
guarantees

\frac{P}{\eta}+\eta V\leq\frac{P}{\eta^{*}}+\eta_{\min}V=\sqrt{PV}+\eta_{\min}V\;,

and otherwise, if
\eta^{*}\geq\eta_{\max}
, then choosing
\eta=\eta_{\max}

yields

\frac{P}{\eta}+\eta V\leq\frac{P}{\eta_{\max}}+\eta^{*}V=\frac{P}{\eta_{\max}}+\sqrt{PV}\;.

Combining these three cases proves the result:

\min_{\eta\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}}\left\{\frac{P}{\eta}+\eta V\right\}\leq\sqrt{PV}+\max\left\{2\sqrt{PV},\,\frac{P}{\eta_{\max}},\,\eta_{\min}V\right\}\leq 3\sqrt{PV}+\frac{P}{\eta_{\max}}+\eta_{\min}V\;.\qed

The second technical result is a generalization of a similar inequality used in the proof of Proposition 1 in
23
for the analysis of their Centered Mirror Descent algorithm.
In our case, this inequality is adopted within the regret analysis of
Algorithm

1
.

Lemma A.2
.

Given any
a,b,c>0
and
d\geq 0
, it holds that
\sup_{x\geq 0}\bigl\{\frac{d}{b}\log(\frac{x}{a}+1)-cx\bigr\}\leq\frac{d}{b}\log\bigl(\frac{d}{abc}+1\bigr)
.

Proof.

Let
f(x)\triangleq\frac{d}{b}\log\left(\frac{x}{a}+1\right)-cx
. Direct calculation shows that
f^{\prime}(\frac{d}{bc}-a)=0
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{d}{b(x+a)^{2}}\leq 0
, we consider two cases:

Case 1:

\frac{d}{bc}-a\leq 0
. Then, due to concavity, the supremum over
x\geq 0
occurs at the boundary
x=0
. Thus,
\sup_{x\geq 0}f(x)=f(0)=0\leq\frac{d}{b}\log\left(\frac{d}{abc}+1\right)
holds trivially.

Case 2:

\frac{d}{bc}-a>0
. Then the maximum of
f(x)
is attained at
x=\frac{d}{bc}-a
. Substituting
x=\frac{d}{bc}-a
into
f(x)
leads to:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\geq 0}f(x)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}\bigl\langle g_{t},u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}-u_{t}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}\Bigl\langle g_{t},\sum_{s\in I_{\tau}:s>t}(u_{s}-u_{s-1})\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{s\in I_{\tau}:s>t_{\tau}}\Bigl\langle\sum_{t=t_{\tau}}^{s-1}g_{t},u_{s}-u_{s-1}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{s\in I_{\tau}:s>t_{\tau}}\left\|\sum_{t=t_{\tau}}^{s-1}g_{t}\right\|\|u_{s}-u_{s-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{s\in I_{\tau}:s>t_{\tau}}\lambda_{s}\|u_{s}-u_{s-1}\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\lambda_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|\;.

(14)

In the second inequality we used that, for any

with
s>t_{\tau}
, the epoch
\tau
has not
terminated before time
s-1
, and hence
\bigl\|\sum_{t=t_{\tau}}^{s-1}g_{t}\bigr\|\leq\lambda_{s}
because of how epochs are determined by
Algorithm

3
.
Now, recall the definition of
\widetilde{u}_{\tau}
for each
\tau\in[N]
from the statement.
Then, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}\langle g_{t},\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\left\langle\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}g_{t},\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\right\rangle+\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}\langle g_{t},u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}-u_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\left\langle\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}g_{t},\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\right\rangle+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\lambda_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\left\|\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}g_{t}\right\|\cdot\|\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\|+\lambda_{\max}P_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\|\widetilde{g}_{\tau}\|\cdot\|\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\|+\lambda_{\max}P_{T}\;.

(15)

Observe that, by the triangle inequality,

\|\widetilde{g}_{\tau}\|\leq\left\|\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}g_{t}-g_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\right\|+G\leq\max_{t\in I_{\tau}}\lambda_{t}+G\;,

where the last step follows from the same observation on how epoch
I_{\tau}
is defined by the execution of
Algorithm

3
.
Plugging the above inequality into
Equation

15
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\sum_{t\in I_{\tau}}\langle g_{t},\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\|\widetilde{g}_{\tau}\|\cdot\|\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\|+\lambda_{\max}P_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\Bigl(G+\max_{t\in I_{\tau}}\lambda_{t}\Bigr)\|\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\|+\lambda_{\max}P_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(G+\max_{t\in[T]}\lambda_{t}\Bigr)\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\|\widetilde{u}_{\tau}-u_{t_{\tau+1}-1}\|+\lambda_{\max}P_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(G+2\lambda_{\max}\Bigr)P_{T}\;,

which combined with
Equation

13
concludes the proof.
∎

.

In this section, we first introduce useful lemmas that will be crucial in the regret analysis of

The following lemma (initially stated as
Lemma

5.1
in

5.1
) provides a reduction from OCO with delayed feedback to OCO with movement costs as described in
Section

5.1
.
See
5.1

Proof.

We decompose the regret as follows:

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(w_{t})-f_{t}(u_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{g_{t},w_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{g_{t},w_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle-\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau},w_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle}_{\clubsuit}+\,\clubsuit\;.

(21)

Define
z_{t+1}=z_{t}+\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau}-g_{t}
for any
t\in[T]
and
z_{1}=\mathbf{0}
.
Since
o_{T+1}=[T]
, we know that
z_{T+1}=\sum_{\tau\in o_{T+1}}g_{\tau}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}=\mathbf{0}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{g_{t}-\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau},w_{t}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle z_{t}-z_{t+1},w_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\langle z_{t+1},w_{t+1}-w_{t}\rangle

(since
z_{T+1}=z_{1}=\mathbf{0}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\langle\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}}g_{\tau}-\sum_{\tau\in[t]}g_{\tau},w_{t+1}-w_{t}\right\rangle

(by definition of
z_{t+1}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\langle\sum_{\tau\in m_{t+1}}g_{\tau},w_{t}-w_{t+1}\right\rangle\;,

(22)

where the last inequality holds by definition of
m_{t+1}=[t]\setminus o_{t+1}
.
Similarly, we can similarly obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{g_{t}-\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau},u_{t}}\right\rangle=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\langle\sum_{\tau\in m_{t+1}}g_{\tau},u_{t}-u_{t+1}\right\rangle\;.

(23)

Then, we know that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{g_{t},w_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle-\,\clubsuit

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{g_{t}-\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau},w_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{\sum_{\tau\in m_{t+1}}g_{\tau},w_{t}-w_{t+1}}\right\rangle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{\sum_{\tau\in m_{t+1}}g_{\tau},u_{t+1}-u_{t}}\right\rangle

(by

22
and
23
)

\displaystyle\leq G\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t+1}|\|w_{t+1}-w_{t}\|+G\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t+1}|\|u_{t+1}-u_{t}\|

(Cauchy-Schwarz inequality)

\displaystyle\leq G\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t+1}|\|w_{t+1}-w_{t}\|+G\sigma_{\max}P_{T}\;.

Combining the above inequality and
Equation

21
, we finally obtain that

R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau},w_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle+G\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|\cdot\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|+GP_{T}\sigma_{\max}\;.\qed

The following lemma quantifies the relationship between
m_{t}
,
o_{t}
, and
d_{t}
.
This results is a property for the delay sequence and the respective delay-dependent parameters, regardless of other parts of the online learning problem.
Its proof is an extension of similar arguments from the proof of Theorem E.1 in
43
.

Lemma D.1
.

For any sequence of delays,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|\cdot|o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}|\leq 2d_{\mathrm{tot}}
.

Proof.

Direct calculations show that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|\cdot|o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|(1+|m_{t}|-|m_{t+1}|)

(
|o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}|=|m_{t}|+1-|m_{t+1}|
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|+|m_{1}|^{2}-|m_{T}|\cdot|m_{T+1}|+\sum_{t=2}^{T}(|m_{t}|-|m_{t-1}|)|m_{t}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|+\sum_{t=2}^{T}(|m_{t}|-|m_{t-1}|)|m_{t}|

(
|m_{1}|=|m_{T+1}|=0
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|

(
|m_{t}|\leq|m_{t-1}|+1
)

\displaystyle\leq 2d_{\mathrm{tot}}\;,

where
d_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}|m_{t}|
is the total delay.
∎

The two technical lemmas we proved in this section enable the proof of our unconstrained parameter-free dynamic regret bound for the setting of OCO with delayed feedback, achieved by our
Algorithm

4
.
This is one of the two main implications of our results for OCO with time-varying movement costs and is formally shown in

5.2
, which we restate here for completeness and provide its proof.
See
5.2

Proof.

Recall that
h_{t}=\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}g_{\tau}
and
\lambda_{t+1}=G|m_{t+1}|
for each
t\in[T]
, as defined by

Using

At each time step

, when we run

wit

, an instance

By the triangle inequality and the

(24)

while

directly follows by definition of

.
Hence,
L\geq G\left(1+3\sigma_{\max}\right)
satisfies the condition in

4.1
, which we can in turn use to obtain that

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\bigl(|{\mathcal{S}}|\epsilon+\widetilde{M}_{T}(\epsilon)+\widetilde{P}_{T}(\epsilon)\bigr)L+\sqrt{\bigl(\widetilde{M}_{T}(\epsilon)+\widetilde{P}_{T}(\epsilon)\bigr)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\|h_{t}\|^{2}+G|m_{t}|\cdot\|h_{t}\|\right)\|u_{t}\|}\right)\;.

Let us now focus on the sum within the square root.
Fix any

.
By definition of

, each term can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\|h_{t}\|^{2}+G|m_{t}|\cdot\|h_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl(\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|\Biggr)^{\!2}+G|m_{t}|\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|

(

24

\displaystyle=\Biggl(\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|\Biggr)^{\!2}+G\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\||m_{\tau+d_{\tau}}|\;,

where we used the fact that
\tau+d_{\tau}=t
for all
\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}
at the last inequality.
Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\Biggl(\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|\Biggr)^{\!2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|^{2}+\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}\\
s\neq\tau\end{subarray}}\|g_{s}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|^{2}+G\bigl(|o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}|-1\bigr)\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|^{2}+G|m_{t}|\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|^{2}+G\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\||m_{\tau+d_{\tau}}|\;,

where the first inequality holds by

Then, considering the sum over all rounds

yields

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\|h_{t}\|^{2}+G|m_{t}|\cdot\|h_{t}\|\bigr)\|u_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|u_{t}\|\left(\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\|^{2}+2G\sum_{\tau\in o_{t+1}\setminus o_{t}}\|g_{\tau}\||m_{\tau+d_{\tau}}|\right)

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{del}}_{T}(u_{1:T})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\!\bigl(|{\mathcal{S}}|\epsilon+\widetilde{M}_{T}(\epsilon)+\widetilde{P}_{T}(\epsilon)\bigr)L+\sqrt{\bigl(\widetilde{M}_{T}(\epsilon)+\widetilde{P}_{T}(\epsilon)\bigr)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\|g_{t}\|^{2}+G|m_{t+d_{t}}|\cdot\|g_{t}\|\right)\|u_{t+d_{t}}\|}\right).

Similarly to what done in existing work, it provides a reduction from OCO with time-varying memory to OCO with time-varying movement costs.
The main difference in what we demonstrate is that we can handle arbitrary sequences of memory lengths, which can fluctuate over time, rather than limiting the applicability of our results to a uniform memory length
B

See
5.6

Proof.

For any
t\in[T]
, since
f_{t}
satisfies
5.4
, we know that

\displaystyle\left|f_{t}\left(w_{t-b_{t}:t}\right)-\widehat{f}_{t}\left(w_{t}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq G\sum_{i=0}^{b_{t}}\left\|w_{t}-w_{t-i}\right\|\leq G\sum_{i=0}^{b_{t}}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\left\|w_{t-j}-w_{t-j-1}\right\|

\displaystyle=G\sum_{i=1}^{b_{t}}\left(b_{t}-i+1\right)\left\|w_{t-i+1}-w_{t-i}\right\|,

where the second inequality uses triangular inequality on each summand.
Taking a summation over
t\in[T]

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|f_{t}\left(w_{t-b_{t}:t}\right)-\widehat{f}_{t}\left(w_{t}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=G\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left(\sum_{s=t}^{T}\mathbf{1}\left\{t\geq s-b_{s}+1\right\}\left(t-(s-b_{s})\right)\right)\left\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\right\|

w_{1}=w_{0}=\mathbf{0}

\xi_{t}
from the statement.

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{mem}}_{T}(u_{1:T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\widehat{f}_{t}(w_{t})-\widehat{f}_{t}(u_{t})\bigr)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}\left(w_{t-b_{t}:t}\right)-\widehat{f}_{t}(w_{t})\bigr)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}\left(u_{t-b_{t}:t}\right)-\widehat{f}_{t}(u_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{w_{t},h_{t}-u_{t}}\right\rangle+G\sum_{t=2}^{T}\xi_{t}\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|+G\sum_{t=2}^{T}\xi_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|

, and the last inequality uses the uniform bound

.
In particular, this last inequality can be proved in the following way: by denoting as

the positive part of any given real number, we have

\displaystyle\xi_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{t\geq s-b_{s}+1\}\bigl(t-(s-b_{s})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t}^{T}\bigl[t-(s-b_{s})\bigr]_{+}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=t}^{T}\bigl[B-(s-t)\bigr]_{+}

(using
b_{S}\leq B

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\min\{B-1,\;T-t\}}(B-d)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d=0}^{B-1}(B-d)

\displaystyle=\frac{B(B+1)}{2}

\displaystyle\leq B^{2}\;,

(27)

where the last inequality holds for any non-negative integer
B\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
∎

For completeness, we restate

and

Proof.

Using
