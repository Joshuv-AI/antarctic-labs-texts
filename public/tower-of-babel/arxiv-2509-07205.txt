On Sesquilinear Forms for Lower Semibounded (Singular) Sturm–Liouville Operators
Jussi Behrndt, Fritz Gesztesy, Seppo Hassi, Roger Nichols, Henk de Snoo

Abstract
. Any self-adjoint extension of a (singular) Sturm–Liouville operator bounded from below uniquely leads to an associated sesquilinear form. This form is characterized in terms of principal and nonprincipal solutions of the Sturm–Liouville operator by using generalized boundary values. We provide these forms in detail in all possible cases (explicitly, when both endpoints are limit circle, when one endpoint is limit circle, and when both endpoints are limit point).

1.
Introduction

The traditional three-coefficient Sturm–Liouville (generalized eigenvalue) problem on an arbitrary open interval

(a,b)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is of the form

-(p(x)f^{\prime}(x))^{\prime}+q(x)f(x)=zr(x)f(x)\,\text{ for a.e.~$x\in(a,b)$},\;z\in{\mathbb{C}},

(1.1)

where the coefficients
p,q,r
are real-valued (Lebesgue) a.e. on
(a,b)
,
p,r>0
a.e. on
(a,b)
, and
1/p,q,r\in L^{1}_{loc}((a,b);dx)
. In addition,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
represents a (generally, complex-valued) spectral parameter, and
f
and
pf^{\prime}
are assumed to be locally absolutely continuous on
(a,b)
; see Section
2
for details. More precisely, the differential expression
\tau
underlying (
1.1
),

\tau=\frac{1}{r(x)}\bigg[-\frac{d}{dx}p(x)\frac{d}{dx}+q(x)\bigg]\,\text{ for a.e.~$x\in(a,b)$},

(1.2)

naturally leads to a minimal closed symmetric operator

T_{min}
in the Hilbert space
L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)
(cf. (
2.10
) and (
2.11
)) and its
deficiency indices are then given by
(0,0)
,
(1,1)
, or
(2,2)
.
From the outset, the operator
T_{min}
is in general not lower semibounded.
However, in this paper it will be assumed that equation (
1.1
)
has solutions which are nonoscillatory at the endpoints
a
and
b
for some

z\in{\mathbb{R}}
and in this case
T_{min}
turns out to be lower semibounded.
As a consequence, all self-adjoint extensions of
T_{min}
are then
lower semibounded, see Proposition
2.8
. For example, in the special case of a one-dimensional Schrödinger operator where
\tau
simplifies to

\tau=-(d^{2}/dx^{2})+q(x)
for a.e.
x\in(a,b)
,
quantum mechanical considerations typically lead to the requirement of lower semibounded self-adjoint extensions of
T_{min}

and the characterization of the underlying quadratic forms (representing the sum of kinetic and potential energy)
corresponding to them.

In this paper we consider the natural and nontrivial question of determining the form domains associated with general, that is, lower semibounded, self-adjoint, singular, three-coefficient
Sturm–Liouville operators associated with
L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)
-realizations of the differential expression
\tau
in (
1.2
).
The corresponding sesquilinear forms are then connected to integrals of the form

\int_{a}^{b}\,dx\,\big[p(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)}g^{\prime}(x)+q(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x)\big]

(1.3)

for “appropriate” elements
f,g\in L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)
.
However, if one of the functions
f
or
g
is not compactly supported in
(a,b)
,
there might well be a problem with the convergence of the integral in (
1.3
).
This problem will be avoided when rewriting the integral
by means of the nonoscillatory solutions of (
1.1
) mentioned above.
These solutions will also be used to introduce generalized
boundary values (see Proposition
2.10
) that are associated to the particular self-adjoint extension of
T_{min}
under consideration. The main results in this paper are formulated in terms of
proper interpretations of the integral (
1.3
) and, in particular,
in terms of generalized boundary values, see Proposition
2.10
.

2.
Sturm–Liouville Operators, Generalized Boundary Values, and Self-Adjoint Realizations

The following hypothesis will be assumed throughout this paper.

Hypothesis 2.1
.

Let
-\infty\leqslant a<b\leqslant\infty
. Suppose that
p
,
q
, and
r
are Lebesgue measurable on
(a,b)
with
p^{-1},q,r\in L^{1}_{loc}((a,b);dx)
and real-valued a.e. on
(a,b)
with
r>0
and
p>0
a.e. on
(a,b)
.

We recall the basic construction and properties of Sturm–Liouville differential expressions and their associated operators. For a full treatment with proofs of the assertions in this section, we refer to
[
11
, Chapter 5]
.

Assuming Hypothesis
2.1
, we introduce the set

\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b))=\big\{g\in AC_{loc}((a,b))\,\big|\,g^{[1]}=pg^{\prime}\in AC_{loc}((a,b))\big\}

(2.1)

and the differential expression
\tau
defined by

\tau f=\frac{1}{r}\Big[-\big(f^{[1]}\big)^{\prime}+qf\Big]\in L_{loc}^{1}((a,b);r\,dx),\quad f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b)),

(2.2)

where the expression

f^{[1]}=pf^{\prime},\quad f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b)),

(2.3)

is the
first quasi-derivative
of
f
. For each
f,g\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b))
, the (modified) Wronskian of
f
and
g
is defined by

W(f,g)(x)=f(x)g^{[1]}(x)-f^{[1]}(x)g(x),\quad x\in(a,b).

(2.4)

Hence,
W(f,g)
is locally absolutely continuous on
(a,b)
and its derivative is

W(f,g)^{\prime}(x)=\big[g(x)(\tau f)(x)-f(x)(\tau g)(x)\big]r(x)\,\text{ for a.e.~$x\in(a,b)$.}

(2.5)

In particular, if
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, then the Wronskian of two solutions
u_{j}(z,\,\cdot\,)\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b))
,
j\in\{1,2\}
, of
\tau u=zu
on
(a,b)
is constant. Moreover,
W(u_{1}(z,\,\cdot\,),u_{2}(z,\,\cdot\,))\neq 0
if and only if
u_{1}(z,\,\cdot\,)
and
u_{2}(z,\,\cdot\,)
are linearly independent.

Definition 2.2
.

The differential expression
\tau
is said to be regular on
(a,b)
if
-\infty<a<b<\infty

(
i.e.,
a
and
b
are finite
)
and
p^{-1},q,r\in L^{1}((a,b);dx)
; otherwise,
\tau
is said to be singular on
(a,b)
.

If
\tau
is regular on
(a,b)
, then for each
f\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b))
the following limits exist and are finite:

\begin{split}&f(a):=\lim_{x\downarrow a}f(x),\quad f^{[1]}(a):=\lim_{x\downarrow a}f^{[1]}(x),\\
&f(b):=\lim_{x\uparrow b}f(x),\quad f^{[1]}(b):=\lim_{x\uparrow b}f^{[1]}(x).\end{split}

(2.6)

The differential expression
\tau
gives rise to linear operators in the Hilbert space
{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}
equipped with the standard inner product

(f,g)_{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}=\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,dx\,\overline{f(x)}g(x),\quad f,g\in{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}.

(2.7)

The
maximal operator
associated to
\tau
is denoted by
T_{max}
and is defined by

\displaystyle T_{max}f=\tau f,

(2.8)

\displaystyle f\in\dom(T_{max})=\big\{g\in{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}\,\big|\,g\in\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}((a,b)),\,\tau g\in{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}\big\}.

Furthermore, the Wronskian of any two functions
f,g\in\dom(T_{max})
possesses finite boundary values at the endpoints of
(a,b)
; that is, the following limits exist and are finite:

W(f,g)(a):=\lim_{x\downarrow a}W(f,g)(x),\quad W(f,g)(b):=\lim_{x\uparrow b}W(f,g)(x).

(2.9)

The
pre-minimal operator
associated to
\tau
is denoted by
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
and is defined by

\displaystyle\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}f=\tau f,

(2.10)

\displaystyle f\in\dom\big(\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}\big)=\big\{g\in\dom(T_{max})\,|\,g\text{ has compact support in }(a,b)\big\}.

One can show that the operator
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
is densely defined and symmetric in the Hilbert space
{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}
and
\big(\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}\big)^{*}=T_{max}
. The
minimal operator
associated to
\tau
is denoted by
T_{min}
and is defined to be the closure of the pre-minimal operator:

T_{min}:=\overline{\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}}.

(2.11)

In addition,
T_{min}
and
T_{max}
are adjoint to one another:

T_{min}^{*}=T_{max}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{max}^{*}=T_{min}.

(2.12)

Definition 2.3
.

A measurable function
f:(a,b)\to{\mathbb{C}}
is in
{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}
near
a

(
resp.,
b
)
if
\chi_{(a,c)}f

(
resp.,
\chi_{(c,b)}f
)
belongs to
{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}
for some
c\in(a,b)
.

Appendix A
Approach via Boundary Triplets and Boundary Pairs

In this appendix we briefly provide the background of the results in Section
3
and Section
4

of this paper in terms of the boundary triplets and boundary pairs
following the extensive
treatment in
[
3
, Chs. 2, 5, 6]
.
By means of boundary pairs one can systematically treat the semibounded forms
that are associated with the lower semibounded self-adjoint extensions of lower semibounded
symmetric operators.
In this paper inner products and sesquilinear forms are linear
in the second entry and anti-linear in the first entry;
in the references to
[
3
]
one should be aware of the present convention.
Thus, when a sesquilinear form
{\mathfrak{t}}
in a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{H}}

is densely defined, closed, and lower semibounded, then there exists a unique
self-adjoint operator
H
in
{\mathfrak{H}}
, such that

{\mathfrak{t}}[f,g]=(f,g)_{\mathfrak{H}}=(f,Hg)_{\mathfrak{H}},\quad f\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}),\,\,g\in\dom(H)\subset\dom({\mathfrak{t}}),

by the First Representation Theorem. The notation
{\mathfrak{t}}={\mathfrak{t}}_{H}
is used to indicate the connection with
H
.

Boundary Triplets.

Let
S
be a closed densely defined symmetric operator in a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{H}}

and assume that the defect numbers of
S
are equal to
(n,n)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
A triplet
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is called
a
boundary triplet
for
S^{*}
if
the linear mappings
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}:\dom(S^{*})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

satisfy the abstract Green identity,

(f,S^{*}g)_{\mathfrak{H}}-(S^{*}f,g)_{\mathfrak{H}}=(\Gamma_{0}f,\Gamma_{1}g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}-(\Gamma_{1}f,\Gamma_{0}g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}},\quad f,g\in\dom(S^{*}),

and
(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1})^{\top}:\dom(S^{*})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
is onto,
see
[
3
, Definition 2.1.1]
.
If
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
is a boundary triplet for
S^{*}
, then
one has

\dom(S)=\{g\in\dom(S^{*})\,|\,\Gamma_{0}g=\Gamma_{1}g=0\}

and the mapping
(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1})^{\top}:\dom(S^{*})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}

is continuous if
\dom(S^{*})
is equipped with the graph norm.
The self-adjoint extensions
A_{\Theta}
of
S
are parametrized
over the self-adjoint relations (multi-valued operators)

in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
via

A_{\Theta}g=S^{*}g,\quad g\in\dom(A_{\Theta})=\big\{h\in\dom(S^{*})\,|\,\{\Gamma_{0}h,\Gamma_{1}h\}\in\Theta\big\},

see
[
3
, Theorem 2.1.3]

We note that if

\dom(\Theta)=(\mul(\Theta))^{\perp}

is a self-adjoint operator in
\dom(\Theta)

and the second summand in the right-hand side is a purely multi-valued self-adjoint relation in
\mul(\Theta)
.

S^{*}
and let
S_{1}
be a self-adjoint extension of
S

which satisfies (
A.3
). Let
\Lambda:\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

be a linear mapping which is bounded when

\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})
is provided with the inner product associated with
{\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}-c
,
where
c
is below the lower bound of
S_{1}
.
If
\Lambda
extends
\Gamma_{0}
, then the self-adjoint extension
S_{0}
,
\dom(S_{0})=\ker(\Gamma_{0})
,
coincides with the Friedrichs extension
S_{\rm F}
and the following equalities hold:

\ker(\Lambda)=\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{\rm F}})\,\text{ and }\,\ran(\Lambda)={\mathbb{C}}^{n}.

Proof.

Since
\Lambda
extends
\Gamma_{0}
, one concludes that

\ran(\Lambda)=\ran(\Gamma_{0})={\mathbb{C}}^{n}

and also
\dom(S_{0})=\ker(\Gamma_{0})\subseteq\ker(\Lambda)
.
In particular,
\dom(S)\subseteq\ker(\Lambda)
and hence by continuity of
\Lambda
and
the definition of the Friedrichs extension
S_{\rm F}

one concludes that
\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{\rm F}})\subseteq\ker(\Lambda)
.
On the other hand, since the sum in (
A.4
) is direct
and
\dim(\ker(S^{*}-cI_{{\mathfrak{H}}}))=n<\infty
it follows that
\ker(\Lambda)=\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{\rm F}})

and that
\Lambda
maps
\ker(S^{*}-cI_{{\mathfrak{H}}})
bijectively onto
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Combining this with the stated inclusion

\dom(S_{0})\subseteq\ker(\Lambda)
gives
\dom(S_{0})\subseteq\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{\rm F}})
.
This implies that
S_{0}=S_{\rm F}
by
[
3
, Theorem 5.3.3]
.
∎

The pair
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Lambda\}
, where
\Lambda:\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is bounded in the form topology
on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}
is called a
boundary pair
for
S
if
\ker(\Lambda)=\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{\rm F}})
,
see
[
3
, Definition 5.6.1]
.
If, in addition,
\dom(S_{1})=\ker(\Gamma_{1})
,
then
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
and
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Lambda\}

are
compatible
corresponding to
S_{1}
,
see
[
3
, Definition 5.6.4]
, and the identity

(f,S^{*}g)_{{\mathfrak{H}}}={\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}[f,g]+(\Lambda f,\Gamma_{1}g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}},\quad f\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}),\,\,g\in\dom(S^{*}),

holds, see
[
3
, Corollary 5.6.7]
.
Hence, Lemma
A.1
offers general sufficient conditions needed to construct
a compatible boundary pair
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Lambda\}
for
S
corresponding to
S_{1}
.
Boundary pairs offer a general tool to describe forms generated by semibounded self-adjoint extensions
of lower semibounded symmetric operators via boundary conditions.

Now let
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Lambda\}
be a compatible boundary pair corresponding to
S_{1}
.
Then the closed semibounded form
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}
associated with the self-adjoint extension
A_{\Theta}

can be expressed in terms of the form
{\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}
and the boundary pair
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Lambda\}
as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}[f,g]={\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}[f,g]+(\Lambda f,\Theta_{\rm op}\Lambda g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}},\\[2.84526pt]
&f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})\,|\,\Lambda h\in\dom(\Theta_{\rm op})\},\end{split}

(A.5)

see
[
3
, Corollary 5.6.14]
. Hence, if
\Theta
is a matrix, then (
A.5
) reads

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}[f,g]={\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}[f,g]+(\Lambda f,\Theta\Lambda g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}},\quad f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}).

(A.6)

Moreover, if
\mul(\Theta)={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, then

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}},\quad\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})\,|\,\Lambda h=0\},

which corresponds to the Friedrichs extension. In particular, for the case

n=1
one has
\Theta\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
One notes that for
\Theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
the decomposition reads

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}[f,g]={\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}[f,g]+(\Lambda f,\Theta\Lambda g)_{{\mathbb{C}}},\quad f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}),

while for
\Theta=\infty
one has

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}},\quad\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})\,|\,\Lambda h=0\}.

Self-adjoint Linear Relations in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

The structure of the self-adjoint extensions in (
A.1
) is clarified next.
It follows from
[
3
, Theorem 1.10.5, Corollary 1.10.8, Proposition 1.10.3]

that any self-adjoint relation
\Theta
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
can be expressed as

\Theta=\left\{\{{\bf u},{\bf v}\}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\,|\,{\mathcal{B}}\bf u={\mathcal{A}}\bf v\right\},

(A.7)

where the
n\times n
matrices
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
satisfy

{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*},\quad\rank({\mathcal{B}}\;\;{\mathcal{A}})=n,

(A.8)

and
({\mathcal{B}}\;\;{\mathcal{A}})
stands for the
n\times 2n
matrix of the columns of
{\mathcal{B}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}
.
The multi-valued part of
\Theta
is given by

\mul(\Theta)=\left\{{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\,|\,{\mathcal{A}}\bf v=0\right\}=\ker({\mathcal{A}}),

so that it follows from (
A.2
) and (
A.7
) that

{\mathcal{B}}{\bf u}={\mathcal{A}}\Theta_{\rm op}{\bf u},\quad{\bf u}\in\dom(\Theta)=(\mul(\Theta))^{\perp}=(\ker({\mathcal{A}}))^{\perp}=\ran({\mathcal{A}}^{*}).

(A.9)

Therefore,
\Theta_{\rm op}
can be expressed as

\Theta_{\rm op}={\mathcal{A}}^{[-1]}{\mathcal{B}}\upharpoonright\ran({\mathcal{A}}^{*}),

(A.10)

where
{\mathcal{A}}^{[-1]}
stands for the Moore–Penrose inverse of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Hence, if
\ker({\mathcal{A}})=\{0\}
, then
\dom(\Theta)={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
\Theta={\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathcal{B}}

is an
n\times n
self-adjoint matrix.
Moreover, if
\ker({\mathcal{A}})={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, then
\dom(\Theta)=\{0\}
and
\Theta

is a purely multi-valued self-adjoint relation in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
given by
\Theta=\{0\}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

In the case
n=2
and
\dim(\ker({\mathcal{A}}))=1
the selfadjoint operator
\Theta_{\rm op}
,
acting in the invariant one-dimensional subspace
\dom(\Theta)
,
is multiplication by the unique real number
c_{\Theta}
given by

{\mathcal{B}}{\bf u}=c_{\Theta}{\mathcal{A}}{\bf u},\quad{\bf u}\in\dom(\Theta)=(\ker({\mathcal{A}}))^{\perp},\quad{\bf u}\neq 0.

(A.11)

In the case
n=1
the self-adjoint relation
\Theta
can be expressed as

\Theta=\left\{\{\bf u,\bf v\}\in{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\,|\,\cos(\gamma)\bf u+\sin(\gamma)\bf v=0\right\},

(A.12)

with
\gamma\in[0,\pi)
.
If
\gamma=0
, then
\mul(\Theta)={\mathbb{C}}
and
\Theta=\{0\}\times{\mathbb{C}}
,
whereas if
\gamma\neq 0
, then
\mul(\Theta)=\{0\}
and
\Theta=\Theta_{\rm op}

is multiplication by
-\cot(\gamma)
.

Summarizing, for a pair of
n\times n
matrices
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
satisfying (
A.8
)
and
\Theta
given by (
A.7
), the self-adjoint extension
A_{\Theta}
of
S
in (
A.1
)
is given by

A_{\Theta}g=S^{*}g,\quad g\in\dom(A_{\Theta})=\big\{h\in\dom(S^{*})\,\big|\,{\mathcal{B}}\Gamma_{0}h={\mathcal{A}}\Gamma_{1}h\big\}.

(A.13)

In this case the formula (
A.5
)
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}[f,g]={\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}[f,g]+\big(\Lambda f,{\mathcal{A}}^{[-1]}{\mathcal{B}}\Lambda g\big)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}},\\[2.84526pt]
&f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\big\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}})\,\big|\,\Lambda h\in(\ker({\mathcal{A}}))^{\perp}\big\}.\end{split}

(A.14)

The expression (
A.14
) can be further simplified in the situations described in (
A.11
) and (
A.12
).

Two Limit Circle Endpoints.

Return to the situation of Proposition
2.12
. Then choose the boundary triplet

\{{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
, defined on
\dom(T_{max})
, by

\Gamma_{0}g=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{g}(a)\\
\widetilde{g}(b)\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\Gamma_{1}g=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{g}^{\,\prime}(a)\\
-\widetilde{g}^{\,\prime}(b)\end{pmatrix},\quad g\in\dom(T_{max}).

(A.15)

Furthermore, introduce the form
{\mathfrak{t}}[f,g]=\mathfrak{Q}_{c,d}(f,g),f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}})=\dom(\mathfrak{Q}_{c,d})
,
as in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
);
cf.
[
3
, Equation (6.11.2)]
.
Then it is easy to see that

(f,T_{min}g)_{{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}}={\mathfrak{t}}[f,g],\quad f\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}),\,\,g\in\dom(T_{min})\subseteq\dom({\mathfrak{t}}),

(A.16)

see
[
3
, Corollary 6.11.2]
, and

\dom(T_{max})\subseteq\dom({\mathfrak{t}}),

(A.17)

see
[
3
, Lemma 6.11.3]
. Define
\Lambda:\dom({\mathfrak{t}})\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
by

\Lambda g=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{g}(a)\\
\widetilde{g}(b)\end{pmatrix},\quad g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}).

(A.18)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\|\Lambda g\|^{2}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}\leqslant\varepsilon{\mathfrak{t}}[g,g]+C_{\varepsilon}\|g\|^{2}_{{L^{2}((a,b);r\,dx)}},\quad g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}),

(A.19)

see
[
3
, Lemma 6.11.4]
. It now follows
from (
A.16
)–(
A.19
)
that
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\Lambda\}
is a boundary pair
which is compatible with the boundary triplet in (
A.15
), see
[
3
, Lemma 6.11.5]
.
Thus we can apply (
A.5
), see
[
3
, Theorem 6.11.6]
.
Note that
\mathfrak{Q}_{c,d}={\mathfrak{t}}_{S_{1}}
,
where
\dom(S_{1})=\ker(\Gamma_{1})
; cf. (
A.15
).
The self-adjoint extensions of
T_{min}
are parametrized via (
A.13
),
given (
A.7
) and (
A.8
). As before, our treatment will distinguish between separated
and coupled boundary conditions.

First, consider the case of separated boundary conditions in Theorem
3.8
,
where
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are
2\times 2
matrices of the form

{\mathcal{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin(\alpha)&0\\
0&\sin(\beta)\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha)&0\\
0&\cos(\beta)\end{pmatrix}.

(A.20)

Note that (
A.8
) is satisfied.
There are three subcases to be discussed.
First consider the case
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 0
.
Then
{\mathcal{A}}
is invertible and it follows
(
A.20
)
that
\Theta
is given by

\Theta={\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\cot(\alpha)&0\\
0&\cot(\beta)\end{pmatrix}.

(A.21)

Substitution of (
A.18
) and (
A.21
) into (
A.14
) leads to (
3.16
) in Theorem
3.8
.
The second case is that either
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
(without equality simultaneously).
Assume
\alpha=0
. Then
\ker({\mathcal{A}})
is one-dimensional and, in fact,
it follows from
\mul(\Theta)=\ker({\mathcal{A}})
and
\dom(\Theta)=(\mul(\Theta))^{\perp}

that

\mul(\Theta)=\ls\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right),\quad\dom(\Theta)=\ls\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Therefore one sees from

{\mathcal{B}}{\bf u}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\cos(\beta)\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{A}}{\bf u}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\sin(\beta)\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf u}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

together with (
A.11
), that
c_{\Theta}=\cot(\beta)
and hence the operator

\Theta_{\rm op}
acting in
\dom(\Theta)=\ls({\bf u})
is given by

\Theta_{\rm op}=\cot(\beta),\quad\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\big\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}})\,\big|\,\widetilde{h}(a)=0\big\}.

This together with (
A.14
) and (
A.11
) leads to (
3.18
).
Likewise, when
\beta=0
, then
c_{\Theta}=-\cot(\alpha)
and hence

\Theta_{\rm op}=-\cot(\alpha),\quad\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}})\,|\,\widetilde{h}(b)=0\},

and this leads to (
3.20
).
The third case concerns
\alpha=\beta=0
.
Then
\mul(\Theta)=\ker({\mathcal{A}})={\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and
\dom(\Theta)=\{0\}
.
Thus
\Theta_{\rm op}
is trivial and

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta}\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}},\quad\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\big\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}})\,\big|\,\widetilde{h}(a)=0=\widetilde{h}(b)\big\},

see (
3.22
), which corresponds to the Friedrichs extension. This treats
all cases of Theorem
3.8
.

Secondly, consider the case of coupled boundary conditions in Theorem
3.9
,
where
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are
2\times 2
matrices of the form

{\mathcal{A}}=-\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\varphi}R_{1,2}&0\\
e^{i\varphi}R_{2,2}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\varphi}R_{1,1}&-1\\
e^{i\varphi}R_{2,1}&0\end{pmatrix},

(A.22)

and hence (
A.8
) is satisfied.
There are two subcases to be discussed.

The first subcase is when
R_{1,2}\neq 0
.
Then
{\mathcal{A}}
is invertible and it follows from
(
A.22
) and
\det_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}(R)=1
,
that
\Theta
is given by

\Theta={\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathcal{B}}=-\frac{1}{R_{1,2}}\begin{pmatrix}R_{1,1}&-e^{-i\varphi}\\
-e^{i\varphi}&R_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}.

(A.23)

It follows from (
A.5
) and the expression in (
A.23
) that

(\Lambda f,\Theta\Lambda g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{R_{1,2}}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{f}(a)\\
\widetilde{f}(b)\end{pmatrix}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}R_{1,1}&-e^{-i\varphi}\\
-e^{i\varphi}&R_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{g}(a)\\
\widetilde{g}(b)\end{pmatrix},\quad f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}).

Together with (
A.14
) this implies Theorem
3.9

(i)
.

The second subcase occurs when
R_{1,2}=0
,
which implies that
1=\det_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}(R)=R_{1,1}R_{2,2}
.
Then
\ker({\mathcal{A}})
is one-dimensional and, in fact, it follows from
\mul(\Theta)=\ker({\mathcal{A}})
and
\dom(\Theta)=(\mul(\Theta))^{\perp}

that

\mul(\Theta)=\ls\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-e^{i\varphi}R_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\right),\quad\dom(\Theta)=\ls\left(\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\varphi}R_{2,2}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Therefore one sees from

{\mathcal{B}}{\bf u}=\begin{pmatrix}R_{1,1}R_{2,2}-1\\
R_{2,1}R_{2,2}\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{A}}{\bf u}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-R_{2,2}^{2}-1\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf u}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\varphi}R_{2,2}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

together with (
A.11
), that

c_{\Theta}=-\frac{R_{2,1}}{R_{1,1}+R_{2,2}}.

(A.24)

Thus by (
A.5
) and the expression in (
A.24

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(\Lambda f,\Theta_{\rm op}\Lambda g)_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}=-R_{1,1}R_{2,1}\overline{\widetilde{f}(a)}\widetilde{g}(a),\\[2.84526pt]
&f,g\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}}_{\Theta})=\big\{h\in\dom({\mathfrak{t}})\,\big|\,\widetilde{h}(b)=e^{i\varphi}R_{1,1}\widetilde{h}(a)\big\}.\end{split}

Together with (
A.14
) this implies Theorem
3.9

(ii)
.
