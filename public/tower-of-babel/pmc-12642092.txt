MiR-503-5p mediates cell cycle arrest and fibrosis of peritoneal mesothelial cells with a high peritoneal solute transport status
Authors: Ouyang Ji, Yingxin Xie, Yunyao Lin, Ruolin Li, Yan Tong, Pu Li, Junyan Fang, Yingli Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-term peritoneal dialysis (PD) induces persistent inflammation and fibrosis of the peritoneal membrane (PM), altering the peritoneal solute transport rate (PSTR). We previously found elevated miR-503-5p levels in exosomes from PD effluent (PDE) in patients with high PSTR. As PSTR is strongly influenced by intraperitoneal inflammation, this study aimed to explore the relationship between miR-503-5p, inflammation, and peritoneal fibrosis and the underlying mechanisms.MethodsBioinformatics analyses were initially performed to identify the miR-503-5p target genes and determine the research subject. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), miRNA RT-PCR, and western blotting were used to detect the expression levels of miR-503-5p and its targets in human MeT-5A mesothelial cells. A cell counting kit-8 assay was used to ascertain the impact of miR-503-5p on cellular proliferation, and cell cycle was evaluated using a FACScan flow cytometer. The miR-503-5p target genes were identified using mimic/inhibitor transfection and dual-luciferase reporter assays. The PDE exosomes (PDE-exos) labeled with PKH67 were used to observe their interactions with MeT-5A cells.ResultsPatients with PD who exhibited an increasing PSTR had notably elevated dialysate interleukin (IL)-6 concentrations, which demonstrated a positive correlation with the PSTR. The induction of lipopolysaccharides remarkably enhanced the expression of miR-503-5p and IL-6 in peritoneal mesothelial cells (PMCs). Overexpression of miR-503-5p induced cell cycle arrest in the G1/S phase and notably upregulated the expression of fibrotic indicators (alpha-smooth muscle actin and type I collagen) in PMCs. Cyclin D1 was a direct target of miR-503-5p and expressed in PMCs. Additionally, PKH67-labeled PDE-exos were internalized by PMCs.ConclusionsPatients with PD who had a high PSTR exhibited an elevated inflammatory state, and the presence of inflammatory factors stimulated the upregulation of miR-503-5p expression, which led to cell cycle arrest in PMCs and promoted peritoneal fibrosis development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04587-1.

Introduction

As the global health and healthcare burden of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) continues to escalate, peritoneal dialysis (PD) has become one of the major treatments for ESRD owing to its lower cost, better quality of life outcomes, and better preservation of residual renal function [1, 2]. Peritoneal fibrosis (PF) is a severe complication in PD that significantly impacts the long-term success of this treatment [3]. Studies show that 50%-80% of PD patients have varying degrees of PF within one to two years of treatment [4]. The mechanisms underlying PF are complex, involving multiple pathological processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, hypoxia, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). PF represents the end stage of progressive peritoneal alterations, which is the primary contributor to high peritoneal transport status and ultrafiltration failure, ultimately impairing the efficacy of PD [5]. Therefore, early detection and intervention for PF have become a prominent focus in the field of PD.

The effectiveness of PD largely relies on the intactness of both the structure and function of the peritoneal membrane (PM), which can be evaluated using the peritoneal solute transport rate (PSTR) as assessed by the peritoneal equilibration test (PET) [6]. It has been discovered that a higher peritoneal transport status was associated with not only a greater likelihood of mortality but also a propensity for heightened technical failure [7]. As a unique specimen of patients undergoing PD, the PD effluent (PDE) contains various solutes, electrolytes, peptides, and proteins secreted by the PM, which are correlated with pathological changes in the PM. Indeed, several miRNAs, proteins, and other substances are incorporated into exosomes, wherein they are more stable and resist degradation, thereby enabling a more comprehensive reflection of changes in the PM [8]. In our previous work, we extracted, identified, and analyzed miRNAs derived from PDE exosomes (PDE-exos) obtained from patients with different PSTRs [9] and found that the expression level of miR-503-5p derived from PDE-exos was significantly higher in the high/high average group than in the low/low average group and was positively correlated with the PSTR [10]. However, the reasons for the increased PDE-exo-miR-503-5p levels, as well as its biological functions and underlying mechanisms affecting peritoneal mesothelial cell (PMC) function, remain elusive.

According to bioinformatics analysis, we discovered that miR-503-5p may be implicated in the modulation of both cell cycle and tissue fibrosis. Mechanistically, similar to miR-21, miR-589 [11], and miR-15a-5p [12], miR-503-5p might influence PF or correlate with PSTR by regulating various target gene expressions. Persistent intraperitoneal inflammation, characterized by a continuously high or increasing PSTR, might render PM more prone to progressive fibrosis [13]. In this study, we cultured peritoneal mesothelial cells (PMCs) to investigate the effects of inflammation on miR-503-5p expression. We further explored its role in PMC proliferation and cell cycle regulation, used bioinformatics to identify CCND1 as a target gene, and transfected PMCs with miR-503-5p mimic and inhibitor to study their effects on cell cycle regulation and fibrosis. Collectively, our findings suggest that miR-503-5p may be a promising biomarker or therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of peritoneal inflammation and PF.

Methods

Patients

In this study, we conducted a single-center follow-up of 53 patients who received continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) at the Peritoneal Dialysis Center within the Department of Nephrology of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital between March 1, 2010, and December 31, 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) receiving routine PD treatment (defined as 3–4 times/day), (2) duration of dialysis ≥ 12 months, and (3) clinically stable for at least 3 months before evaluation. The inclusion criteria were patients (age 18–80 years) with stable PSTR. Patients were excluded if they had peritonitis, malignancy, a history of abdominal surgery, autoimmune disease, hepatitis, tuberculosis, lung infection, or systemic inflammation during the 3 months prior to or during the study period. Participants underwent PET every six months and were categorized into two groups based on their 3.86% 4 h D/PCr, [14] labeled as either “high/high average” (H/HA, ≥ 0.65) or “low/low average” (L/LA, <0.65). The PSTR was deemed stable if the patient had the same H/HA or L/LA outcomes in three consecutive assessments.

All participants provided written informed consent for the use of their PDE and clinical data following a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and data privacy assurances. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2020-T23-2) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Overnight PDE was collected from participants prior to the performance of PET. Each sample was aliquoted (1 mL) and immediately stored at − 80 °C until analysis. For interleukin-6 (IL-6) quantification, aliquots were thawed and centrifuged at 2500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for analysis. IL-6 concentrations were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (BioLegend, USA), strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured with a multimode microplate reader (Biotek Synergy H1). The minimum detectable concentration for IL-6 was 7.8 pg/mL.

Cell culture and transfection

Human MeT-5A mesothelial cells, sourced from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA), were cultivated in M199 medium (Hyclone Laboratories, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Biological Industries, Israel). The culture environment was maintained at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. MiR-503-5p mimic, inhibitor, and their respective negative controls (NCs) were transfected using the RiboFECT CP transfection kit (RiboBio, China). The dosages and procedures followed the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

To extract miRNA from PDE, polyadenylation was performed using the miDETECT A Track miRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (RiboBio, China), followed by the conversion of the RNA into cDNA through reverse transcription. For miRNA extracted from cells, reverse transcription was accomplished using stem ring primers, whereas mRNAs were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Japan). The real-time (RT)-PCR assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was conducted utilizing the TB Green Premix Ex Taq kit (Takara Bio Inc., Japan) with SYBR Green PCR amplification. The quantification of exosomal miRNA expression levels was standardized against that of cell-miR-39. Cellular miRNA and mRNA levels were normalized to U6 and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) levels, respectively. Relative expression levels were determined using the following formula: 2 − ΔΔCt.

Western blot analysis

Lysates were prepared from MeT-5A cells that had been seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates. Proteins were extracted using sequential lysis with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) mixed with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio, China). The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kit (Solarbio, China). Subsequently, the protein samples were denatured in 5× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then electrophoretically transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with the following primary antibodies: CCND1 (1:1000; SBI Medical, USA), α-SMA (1:1000; SBI Medical, USA), Col1αI (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), GAPDH (1:5000; Proteintech Group Inc., USA), and β-actin (1:2000; Proteintech Group Inc., USA). Secondary antibodies were then applied (1:5000; Proteintech Group Inc., USA). Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To ascertain whether CCND1 serves as the target gene for miR-503-5p, co-transfection was conducted in HEK-293T cells employing CCND1 wild-type (WT), mutant (MUT), and NC PmirGLO vectors, coupled with miR-503-5p mimic or its corresponding NC. We utilized Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), adhering meticulously to the manufacturer’s instructions. The luciferase activity was measured utilizing a dual-luciferase reporting and analysis system (Promega, USA) 48 h post-transfection. The ratio of luciferase activity to firefly luciferase activity was subsequently calculated, and each test was repeated three times.

Flow cytometry

MeT-5A cells (1.5 × 106 cells) were digested using trypsin and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. A single-cell suspension was then created in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The suspension was diluted to a final concentration of 75% by adding 3 mL of pre-cooled anhydrous ethanol and left to stand overnight (18–24 h) at 4 °C. The cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS and discarded at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 200 µL PBS was added, and the solution was transferred to a 1.5 mL EP tube. Afterward, 20 µL of RNase was added to resuspend the cells, which were digested for 30 min at 37 °C. Next, we added 20 µL of PI to a final concentration of 50 µg/mL and wrapped the EP tube with tin foil, stained the cells for 30 min at 4 °C, protected them from light, and then performed the assay. Data were acquired using a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) with ≥ 1 × 10⁴ single-cell events collected per sample. Gating was performed using Flowjo 10.1 software and ModFit LT V5.0.9 (Verity Software House, USA).

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

MeT-5A cells were inoculated in 96-well plates and subjected to transfection with either an NC, mimic, or inhibitor for 48 h. In each group, cells were treated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Biosharp, China), and then cultivated at 37 °C for 2 h. Cell viability was assessed through the measurement of absorbance at 450 nm.

Fluorescent labeling of exosomes

To investigate the impact of PDE-exo-miRNA on MeT-5A cells, we utilized the PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Connection Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), adhering meticulously to the manufacturer’s instructions. Overnight incubation was performed with MeT-5A cells, either in the presence of fluorescently tagged exosomes or the control solution. Afterward, the cellular slides underwent a washing step with PBS, and the cells were then fixed with a 4% formaldehyde solution at room temperature for 10 min. For cytoskeletal visualization, TRITC-phalloidin (Solarbio, China) was employed, while the nuclei of MeT-5A cells were specifically stained using 4’,6-diamino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Fluorescence microscopy was utilized to examine the internalization and uptake of labeled exosomes into the cells.

PDE-exosome characterization

Exosomes from PDE were characterized using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), NanoSight Tracking Analysis (NTA), and Western blotting. For TEM, 20 µL of the exosome suspension was placed on a copper grid, stained with 1% uranyl acetate, and examined under a Tecnai F20 microscope. Particle size and concentration were measured using NanoSight Tracking Analysis (ZetaView), following dilution of the sample in PBS. Western blotting was used to identify exosomal markers, including CD63, CD9, and TSG101, in the extracted exosome proteins. These methods were described in our previous work [9] and provide detailed characterization of exosome properties in PDE.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graphical representation were accomplished utilizing SPSS Statistics 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Conforming to Gaussian distribution, measurement data are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were statistically analyzed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data not adhering to Gaussian distribution are presented as M (1/4, 3/4). The comparison between the two groups was carried out using the Mann–Whitney U test. Count data was statistically evaluated employing either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. One-way analysis (ANOVA) of variance was employed to compare variations among multiple groups receiving different interventions. Correlation analyses were conducted utilizing Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Linear regression analysis was performed with adjustment for confounding factors. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were visualized using RStudio software (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Expression levels of intraperitoneal IL-6 and the peritoneal transport rate of solutes

We collected nocturnal PDE from 53 patients undergoing PD with different PSTRs and performed an ELISA to compare the intraperitoneal inflammatory status between the groups. The demographic and clinical data of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Except for the 4-h D/Pcr, there were no significant differences in sex, age, dialysis duration, eGFR, Kt/V, or diabetes among the groups. ELISA revealed that the concentration of IL-6 in the PDE was significantly higher in the H/HA group than in the L/LA group (p = 0.004; Fig. 1A), and IL-6 levels were significantly correlated with 4-h D/Pcr (r = 0.354, p = 0.009; Fig. 1B). Given the observed difference in diabetes prevalence between the groups (40.7% vs. 26.9%), a supplementary linear regression analysis was performed with adjustment for diabetes status. This analysis confirmed that the elevated IL-6 expression in the H/HA group remained statistically significant after accounting for diabetes (p = 0.007).

LPS stimulation increases IL-6 expression in MeT-5A cells

To more rigorously explore how inflammation affects IL-6 expression in PMCs, we treated the cells with different concentrations of LPS (0, 5, 10, 50, 100 ng/mL) for 48 h, as well as with 100 ng/mL LPS at different time points (0, 12, 24, 48 h). qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of IL-6 expression in the 100 ng/mL LPS group compared with that in any other group (Fig. 2A). Notably, IL-6 expression peaked in the 48-hour 100 ng/mL LPS group (Fig. 2B), suggesting that inflammation significantly promotes IL-6 expression in PMCs.

LPS induces the expression of miR-503-5p in MeT-5A cells

To determine whether inflammation could affect the expression of miR-503-5p in PMCs, we used qRT-PCR after MeT-5A cells were stimulated with LPS at different concentrations (0, 5, 10, 50, 100 ng/mL) for 48 h, as well as with 100 ng/mL LPS at different time points (0, 12, 24, 48 h). The results showed that the expression of miR-503-5p in the 100 ng/mL LPS group was significantly upregulated compared with that in any other group (Fig. 3A). Notably, miR-503-5p expression peaked in the 48-hour 100 ng/mL LPS group (Fig. 3B), suggesting that inflammation promotes the expression of miR-503-5p in PMCs.

Prediction and bioinformatics analysis of target genes of miR-503-5p

To clarify the molecular mechanism of action of miR-503-5p in PMCs, we used a Venn diagram to identify the overlapping target genes of miR-503-5p predicted by Starbase, MiRDB, and MiRTarBase (Fig. 4A). We obtained 87 overlapping genes and subjected them to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources. We found that these potential targets were functionally enriched in several biological processes. In the biological process (BP) category, the most enriched clusters were identified, including negative regulation of transcription by the RNA polymerase II promoter, G1/S transition of the mitotic cell cycle, negative regulation of the transforming growth factor beta receptor signaling pathway, and positive regulation of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 4B). In the molecular function (MF) category, the candidate genes were related to cyclin-dependent protein serine/threonine kinase regulator activity, RNA polymerase II transcription factor activity, sequence-specific DNA binding, and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor binding (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the enriched KEGG pathways were involved in the cell cycle, apoptosis, and many classical signaling pathways (Wnt, p53, mTOR, MAPK, JAK-STAT, VEGF, and TGF-β) (Fig. 4C). Based on the miRNA profiling data and bioinformatics predictions, we investigated the role of miR-503-5p in cell cycle and fibrosis because of its close relationship with the pathological alterations in PM, as reported previously.

MiR-503-5p inhibits proliferation and arrests the cell cycle at the G1/S phase

In the next series of experiments, we determined whether miR-503-5p affects the cell cycle of PMCs. Cell proliferation and cell cycle distribution were analyzed using CCK8 and flow cytometry after transfection of the miR-503-5p mimic or inhibitor into MeT-5A cells. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by miRNA RT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 5A, the relative expression of miR-503-5p in the MeT-5A mimic and inhibitor groups was significantly higher and lower than that in the control group, respectively. The CCK8 results showed that upregulated miR-503-5p inhibited the proliferation of MeT-5A cells; however, downregulated miR-503-5p revealed no significant differences compared with the control group (Fig. 5B). Flow cytometry analysis following a specific gating regimen (Fig. 5C) demonstrated that miR-503-5p overexpression in MeT-5A cells significantly increased the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase and decreased the number of cells in the S phase (Fig. 5D). These findings suggest that miR-503-5p overexpression can induce cell cycle arrest in the G1/S phase and reduce the proportion of S-phase cells, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation.

CCND1 is a target gene of miR-503-5p in MeT-5A cells

CCND1 is a crucial master regulator of the cell cycle G1/S transition and was identified as a predicted target of miR-503-5p by miRDB. The seed sequence of miR-503-5p was complementary to and highly conserved in the 3’UTR of CCND1. We utilized a dual-fluorescence reporter system to confirm the correlation between miR-503-5p and CCND1 expression (Fig. 6A). We observed that transfection of the miR-503-5p mimic significantly decreased the luciferase activity of 3′UTR in the wild-type reporter of CCND1 (Fig. 6B). However, the activity was not significantly different between the mutant and negative control groups (Fig. 6B). These data indicate the identification of the binding sites.

To further confirm that CCND1 is a target gene of miR-503-5p, we co-transfected the miR-503-5p mimic or inhibitor into MeT-5A cells. As shown in Fig. 6C, the transfection efficiency of miR-503-5p was reconfirmed. qRT-PCR and western blot analyses revealed that the levels of CCND1 mRNA and protein were significantly decreased in the mimic group compared with those in the control group (Fig. 6D, E), whereas the opposite results were observed for miR-503-5p inhibition (Fig. 6D, F).

Overexpression of miR-503-5p promotes EMT and fibrogenesis in MeT-5A cells

The EMT of PMCs is a key process in PF during PD. Here, we investigated the role of miR-503-5p in regulating EMT by examining the reactivity of MeT-5A cells treated with the miR-503-5p mimic or inhibitor. As shown in Fig. 7A, the transfection efficiency of miR-503-5p was confirmed. qRT-PCR analysis showed that the miR-503-5p mimic significantly upregulated the mesenchymal markers α-SMA and collagen I mRNA, whereas repression of miR-503-5p by transfection of its inhibitor had the opposite effect (Fig. 7B). Examination of α-SMA and Col-I protein expression with western blotting demonstrated similar results after transfection of the miR-503-5p mimic or inhibitor (Fig. 7C, D). These results indicated that miR-503-5p may play an important role in the regulation of EMT and fibrogenesis in PMCs.

Discussion

In the present study, we found that intraperitoneal IL-6 levels were significantly elevated in patients with PD who exhibited high PSTRs and positively correlated with the PSTR, reaffirming the role of inflammation as a key driver of PSTR. Our previous work revealed that changes in PDE-exo-miR-503-5p expression and its association with PSTR were similar to those in IL-6 [9, 10]. We further observed that miR-503-5p expression was significantly upregulated in PMCs under inflammatory conditions. MiR-503-5p suppressed the proliferation of PMCs, which could be mediated by reduced G1/S cell cycling. We verified that CCND1 is a target gene of miR-503-5p. Subsequent investigations revealed that miR-503-5p overexpression induced cell cycle arrest in the G1/S phase in human mesothelial cells (Met-5A), which also facilitated the transdifferentiation of mesothelial cells and elevated the expression of fibrosis factors.

Factors such as age, diabetes, volume overload, residual renal function, high PSTR, and treatment-related complications of PD affect the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing PD [15–18]. Among these, increased PSTR is particularly notable, as it is not only associated with a decline in ultrafiltration volume but also with a higher risk of peritonitis [19]. Studies on PD-related bacterial peritonitis have demonstrated that there is a significant intraperitoneal cytokine reaction, characterized by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative mediators [20, 21]. These cytokine networks promote fibrotic processes, and affect the structural and functional alterations in the PM, such as increased PSTR and decreased dialysis efficiency, even resulting in PF [22–24]. IL-6 is currently regarded as a reliable independent indicator of intraperitoneal inflammatory status [25]. The experimental data further support that inflammatory stimuli can robustly activate IL-6 expression in mesothelial cells, consistent with its established role as a downstream effector of TLR4/NF-κB signaling [26]. Our findings align with previous reports indicating that increased PSTR in PD is associated with higher intraperitoneal IL-6 levels, further supporting the role of local inflammation in altered peritoneal membrane function [27, 28]. Additionally, we identified a 1.72-fold higher level of miR-503-5p in patients undergoing PD with higher PSTRs using miRNA array analysis, which also correlated with D/Pcr [9, 10]. These consistent findings suggest a potential link between intraperitoneal inflammation and miR-503-5p, indicating that miR-503-5p may play a role in mediating inflammation-related changes in PSTR.

To determine whether the overexpression of miR-503-5p is influenced by peritoneal inflammation, we treated PMCs with LPS and observed a significant, dose-dependent increase in miR-503-5p expression. Zhou et al. also showed that IL-1β treatment upregulated miR-503-5p in β-cells, promoting insulin resistance [29]. These results establish a link between inflammation and miR-503-5p overexpression. Building on our previous findings that miR-503-5p is enriched in PDE-exosomes of high transporters [9, 10], we further demonstrated that these exosomes can be taken up by PMCs, supporting their role as carriers mediating inflammation-associated fibrotic responses.

Abnormal miR-503-5p expression is closely associated with human diseases as it modulates diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, angiogenesis, and tissue fibrosis [30]. However, the effect of miR-503-5p dysregulation on PMCs in PD and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. To overcome the limitations of utilizing a single database, we employed three distinct databases to predict the target genes of miR-503-5p and identified 87 overlapping genes. According to GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, these overlapping genes were mainly enriched in fibrosis-related signaling pathways and cell cycle regulation, particularly the G1/S transition. Non-physiological PDFs substantially impair the expression of genes involved in cell cycle regulation and division, thereby impeding PMC proliferation [31, 32]. CCK-8 and cell cycle assays also revealed that miR-503-5p induced cell cycle arrest at the G1/S phase and inhibited cell proliferation. Among the overlapping genes, we focused on CCND1, a crucial regulator of cell cycle progression that promotes the transition from the G1 to S phase. Aberrant expression of CCND1 can lead to the dysregulation of the cell cycle and cellular proliferation, contributing to the development of various diseases, such as atherosclerosis, diabetic retinopathy, and tumors [33–35]. In our study, qRT-PCR and western blotting demonstrated that miR-503-5p overexpression suppressed the mRNA and protein expression of CCND1. Using a dual-luciferase reporter system, we confirmed that CCND1 was a target gene regulated by miR-503-5p. These findings indicated that miR-503-5p targets and negatively regulates CCND1, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation by arresting the cell cycle in the G1 phase of PMCs during PD. Similarly, in endometriotic cyst stromal cells, miR-503-5p exerts its effects by downregulating CCND1, leading to cell cycle arrest [36]. However, the transport mechanism of miR-503-5p into the nucleus remains unclear, as it is localized in exosomes, while its target CCND1 is predominantly nuclear. Further studies are needed to elucidate how miR-503-5p is transported from exosomes to the nucleus and how it regulates nuclear targets like CCND1.

Additionally, exosomal miRNAs are crucial in PF development [37, 38]. For instance, BMSC-derived exosomes regulate fibrosis by modulating the miR-27a-3p/TP53 pathway [38], while miR-21 and miR-30a influence fibrosis through the PDCD4 and Snai1 targets, respectively [37]. We also found that the transfection of miR-503-5p mimic into PMCs increased the expression level of fibrotic markers α-SMA and Col1, whereas these effects were abrogated by the miR-503-5p inhibitor, suggesting that miR-503-5p is pro-fibrotic in patients undergoing PD with higher PSTRs. However, the underlying molecular mechanism remains unclear. Some studies have demonstrated that the regulation of post-transcriptional expression of miR-503-5p is involved in tissue fibrosis [39, 40]. In hepatic stellate cells, treatment with transforming growth factor (TGF)-β significantly increased miR-503 expression levels, and its overexpression further aggravated CCl4-induced hepatic fibrotic changes [39]. Another study showed that miR-503-5p targets apelin-13 to participate in the pro-fibrotic effects of angiotensin II in a cardiac fibrosis model [40]. Although we identified CCND1 as a target gene for miR-503-5p, bioinformatics analysis revealed 87 potential targets, raising the possibility that other key genes may contribute to the observed phenotype. Regarding cell cycle regulation, dysregulation and arrest are closely associated with organ fibrosis. For example, severe acute kidney injury induced G1/S or G2/M phase arrest in proximal tubular epithelial cells, leading to maladaptive repair and fibrosis [41–43]. Combined with our current findings, we propose another possibility: inflammation-induced G1/S arrest in PMCs may contribute to PF, and the miR-503-5p-CCND1 pathway could be involved in this regulatory process. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms, we need to investigate whether the phenotype is completely reversed upon CCND1 knockdown or to screen other high-scoring target genes for their roles in cell cycle regulation and fibrosis.

This study highlights the critical role of miR-503-5p in peritoneal inflammation and fibrosis in PD patients. We found that elevated IL-6 levels in PD patients with high PSTRs positively correlated with PSTR, suggesting inflammation as a key driver of PF [44]. MiR-503-5p expression was upregulated in PMCs under inflammatory conditions, inducing G1/S cell cycle arrest and suppressing PMC proliferation by regulating CCND1. These findings suggest that targeting the miR-503-5p-CCND1 pathway could serve as a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent or manage PF, improving long-term treatment outcomes and reducing PD discontinuation. Potential therapeutic measures could include developing miR-503-5p inhibitors or gene therapy strategies to block miR-503-5p activity, as well as identifying small molecules or biologics that can modulate CCND1 expression. MiR-503-5p in PDE may also serve as an early biomarker for peritoneal membrane injury, guiding interventions such as adjusting PD regimen or adding anti-inflammatory agents to manage inflammation. Moreover, dynamic miR-503-5p monitoring could guide risk-stratified management, such as the use of biocompatible solutions.

However, there are several limitations to this study. The lack of in vivo validation restricts the generalizability of the findings, as the in vitro models may not fully replicate the complex in vivo environment of PD patients. The effects of miR-503-5p on PF and cell cycle regulation require confirmation in animal models to better mimic the in vivo inflammatory microenvironment. Additionally, the relatively small clinical sample size of 53 patients limits the statistical power and generalizability of our conclusions, as it may not be representative of the diverse patient population undergoing PD. In the future, a larger cohort across different centers and patient demographics is needed to verify the findings and strengthen their clinical relevance. While we identified PDE-exosomes as a confirmed source of miR-503-5p, we acknowledge that other extracellular vehicles or non-vesicular forms (e.g., protein-bound miRNAs) may also contribute. The relative contribution of each pathway requires further investigation.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that the aberrant overexpression of miR-503-5p in patients undergoing PD with higher PSTRs was regulated by intraperitoneal inflammation. Additionally, we found that miR-503-5p is an important regulator of cell cycle progression and fibrosis in PMCs. Therefore, our data suggest that PDE-exos, as extracellular mediators, play an important role in the pathophysiology of peritoneal injury during PD. The exo-cargo and upstream regulators identified here may provide new strategies for the prevention and treatment of PF.
