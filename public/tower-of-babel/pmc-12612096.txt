Understanding the impacts of extreme weather on biological control through traveling wave analysis of a prey-predator model
Authors: Most Shewly Aktar, Yuanxi Yue, Majid Bani-Yaghoub, Chunhua Ou, Md Afsar Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effects of extreme weather events on the efficacy of biological control of invasive species using a prey-predator reaction-diffusion model with the Allee effect. The model incorporates weather and environmental factors that affect species mortality, growth, and interaction rates. We first derive exact traveling wave solutions of the reduced model using the generalized exponential rational function method. The stability of the wave solutions is then numerically confirmed by demonstrating that the model solutions, perturbed by various levels of noise in the initial condition, converge to the traveling wave solutions. This suggests that extreme weather events that only impact the initial population sizes have only transient effects and do not change the fate of the species. Nevertheless, asymptotic analysis of the full model reveals conditions for the existence of bistable traveling wave solutions. This implies that extreme weather events may lead to predator extinction and the subsequent establishment of prey in a spatial domain. Hence, extreme weather events may lead to failure of biological control efforts and the persistence of invasive species. In addition, we establish conditions under which extreme weather events drive both prey and predator populations to extinction, which would be an ideal outcome for biological control of invasive species. This study highlights the importance of refining widely accepted ecological models to enhance the forecasting of ecological outcomes under extreme weather events.

Introduction

The study of prey-predator dynamics in ecological systems has greatly benefited from reaction-diffusion (RD) models, which integrate spatial heterogeneity with temporal population changes1,2. For decades, researchers have relied on these models to better understand local interactions and spatial dispersal patterns of prey and predator species3,4. RD models are crucial for understanding complex ecological phenomena such as spatial pattern formation, species invasion, and population persistence5–12. In the past decade, there has been a growing interest in understanding how spatial and temporal ecological dynamics are shaped by variations in model parameters and functional formulations7,9,21–23. For instance, using predator–prey reaction–diffusion systems with Allee effects, Wang et al. demonstrate that introducing an Allee threshold can fundamentally change spatial self-organization and generate Turing instabilities that manifest as various spatial patterns24. Ducrot, Giletti, and Matano analyze spreading phenomena and show that coupled predator–prey wavefronts can propagate25. Jiang et al. establish the existence of traveling waves in predator–prey systems with reproductive Allee effects and time delays using geometric singular perturbation techniques, together with asymptotic characterizations of the resulting profiles26.

Recent studies have begun to incorporate climate change explicitly into well-known population models. This includes allowing environmental forcing to modulate demographic and interaction parameters, introducing stochasticity and memory, and creating spatial heterogeneity due to climate change. The modeling efforts include Ordinary and Partial Differential Equations (ODE and PDE) and discrete-time formulations with simulation of climate-sensitive shifts in equilibria, bifurcation analysis, pattern formation, and wave propagation (see, for example27–31). These developments motivate our modeling choice to treat extreme weather both as transient shocks to initial conditions and as persistent space–time variability in key rates. By doing so, we can increase our understanding of the possible impact of climatic variability on population dynamics. In the present work, we extend a well-known prey-predator RD model and investigate how its traveling wave solutions respond to variations due to extreme weather in initial conditions and the parameter values. Specifically, this study focuses on three key questions: (1) Are the traveling wave solutions stable under stochastic perturbations such as Gaussian or Lévy noise initially introduced to the prey and predator populations? (2) How do perturbations in model parameters, induced by extreme weather events, influence the dynamics and stability of traveling wave solutions? (3) What insights does the asymptotic analysis of traveling waves offer into the mechanisms governing species persistence or extinction under extreme weather events? Addressing these questions is essential to assessing the efficacy of biological control of invasive species under increasing climatic variability. In a prey–predator system, the invasive species serves as the prey, while the predator represents the organism intentionally introduced by ecologists to suppress or eradicate the invasive population. By examining stability of traveling wave solutions under random and structured disturbances, we develop a framework for understanding how extreme weather variability may disrupt or enhance invasion dynamics. Moreover, asymptotic analysis of traveling waves can identify thresholds that separate long-term persistence of invasive species from collapse, and it can ultimately enhance guidance for designing robust biological control strategies in the presence of extreme weather events. To answer these questions, we employ a variety of numerical and analytical methods to study the extended model.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section "The proposed model" presents the extension of the reaction-diffusion prey-predator model, which incorporates extreme weather effects. Section "Exact wave solutions of the reduced model" derives the exact traveling wave solutions for the reduced model by applying the Generalized Exponential Rational Function (GERF) technique. Section "Asymptotic analysis of the full model" investigates the asymptotic behavior of the solutions and their traveling wave solutions to the proposed model. Section "Numerical explorations" explores numerical simulations of the model with different levels of noise and validates the theoretical results with a focus on the influence of extreme weather on population dynamics. Finally, Section "Discussion" elaborates on the main contributions of the present study.

The proposed model

The influence of the Allee effect on the spatiotemporal dynamics of diffusive predator–prey models has been extensively investigated. Recent work has highlighted phenomena such as bistability, the existence of separatrices, and the role of the Allee effect in diffusion-driven pattern formation, giving rise to spatial patterns such as hot spots, stripe–hot spot mixtures, stripes, labyrinthine structures, stripe–cold spot mixtures, and cold spot replication32. In addition, studies have examined Allee-effect-induced instabilities24, the movement of traveling fronts and their spreading properties25, and the existence of traveling waves under Allee effects with delays26, among others.

System (4) has been widely used in studies on autocatalytic chemical reactions, biological invasion of foreign species, extinction or spread of flame balls in combustion, mutant gene propagation, biological film growth, and epidemic spread10,34,35. Moreover, the inclusion of the Allee effect in the reduced model (4) reveals rich dynamical behaviors, including traveling waves, species invasions, and localized extinction36–38. A number of studies have explored traveling wave solutions of system (4) to investigate the dispersal behaviors of interacting species, with a focus on invasion dynamics and ecosystem stability39–43. Moreover, the spatial and temporal patterns arising from the interaction of diffusion and nonlinear reactions offer valuable insights into prey-predator dynamics44,45. By examining the stability and speed of propagation46, one can predict invasion outcomes and the resilience of ecosystems under environmental perturbations47,48. For instance, Petrovskii et al.33,38 highlighted how the interplay between predation pressure and the Allee effect can halt or reverse biological invasions. Kim and Choi36 derived exact traveling wave solutions for prey-predator systems, demonstrating the impact of parameter values on the stability and propagation of such waves. Recently, Xiao-jing Zhuo et al.49 investigated the dynamics of a general prey-predator system influenced by Lévy noise. This provided insights on how stochastic perturbations, particularly those driven by Lévy processes, impact the long-term behavior and extinction probabilities of the interacting species. To build on these important developments, we first derive the exact wave solutions of the reduced model through a novel application of the GERF method and then numerically examine how noise affects the stability of these wave solutions.

Theorem 1

(Homogeneous Balancing Principle) The Homogeneous Balance Principle determines the balance between the highest-order derivatives and the nonlinear terms in a differential equation.

The proof of this theorem can be found in57,58.

We solved the PDEs with MATLAB’s pdepe, which uses a method-of-lines discretization in a bounded one-dimensional spatial domain for a variety of initial and boundary conditions. We verified that enlarging the spatial domain does not impact the results to two significant digits. Boundary conditions were Dirichlet and matched the closed-form wave profile; we also repeated runs with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions to confirm boundary insensitivity. We first investigated the stability of the exact wave solutions of the reduced model (5) and then explored possible wave solutions of the full model (3).

Discussion

The present study develops a reaction–diffusion framework in which extreme weather events act directly on the initial prey and predator population sizes, while the model parameters are formulated as space- and time-dependent functions of these events. By allowing spatial and temporal changes to the prey Allee threshold, predator mortality, and predator self-limitation (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(a_E(t,x), \sigma _E(t,x), d_E(t,x))$$\end{document}), the full model (3) is investigated to study transient and persistent shocks to the population densities. Under the Petrovskii-type hypotheses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(H_1)$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(H_3)$$\end{document}, the model reduces to the cubic RD Eq. (5), for which we derive closed-form traveling waves using the GERF method and the HB principle. To our knowledge, this is the first application of GERF to model (5), which also incorporates the extreme weather events. The explicit families in Tables 2–3 provide analytic profiles of the exact wave solution and define the speed c of propagation as a function of ecological rates (e.g., the positive association with b in Cohorts 1, 3, and 4), and reference solutions for numerical validations.

The asymptotic analysis of the full model (3) establishes spreading versus extinction behaviors. When the weather forcing becomes less intense and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(a_E(t,x), \sigma _E(t,x), d_E(t,x))$$\end{document} converge to constant values, the dynamics relax to the reduced system (12), where Theorems 2–4 determine conditions in which the prey invades while predators go extinct or both species collapse when prey is initially subcritical or predators are initially too abundant. Theorem 5 further provides a sufficient condition for joint extinction when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b \ge 1+r-1/\sqrt{d}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_0 \ge u_0/\sqrt{d}$$\end{document}.

A key finding of the present study is a robustness–fragility dichotomy63 with respect to extreme weather events. In particular, the numerical simulations in subsection "Numerical explorations" show that large perturbations to the initial condition, including Gaussian and Lévy noise, are typically transient: error in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell ^1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell ^2$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell ^\infty$$\end{document} norms decay and the PDE solutions converge to the exact waveforms (Table 4, Fig. 1). In contrast, functional parameter shifts caused by extreme weather can redirect asymptotic behavior by moving the system across separatrices, changing wave speeds and selected equilibria. This mechanism explains predator extinction with prey establishment in Example 1 and joint extinction in Example 2 in subsection "Simulations of the full model". To measure short-term vulnerability, we introduced an extinction-probability diagnostic based on thresholds for u(x, t); the simulations reveal that heavy-tailed Lévy shocks produce disproportionately large and rapid local collapses compared with Gaussian and Uniform perturbations (Fig. 2c–d; Table 5).

Our framework applies directly to biological control of invasive species such as emerald ash borer (EAB, Agrilus planipennis) managed by parasitoids (Tetrastichus, Oobius, Spathius)64,65. Severe cold can raise predator mortality (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _E$$\end{document}), whereas drought can elevate the prey Allee threshold (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_E$$\end{document}) by depressing low-density reproduction, and parasitoid interference can increase self-limitation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_E$$\end{document}). Under these assumptions the simulations of the model proposed in this study can yield testable predictions as follows: (i) observed spread from trap grids should track the sign and magnitude of the wave speed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c^*$$\end{document}; dimensional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c=c^*K\sqrt{D\alpha }$$\end{document}); (ii) extreme weather events can only change fate of species if they push the prey population u below the Allee threshold over a wide region of the spatial domain; (iii) persistent rate shifts that raise \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _E$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_E$$\end{document} (or increase \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_E$$\end{document}) can flip outcomes from control to predator v loss with prey establishment, whereas (iv) maintaining the predator population proportional to the prey population (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v \approx u/\sqrt{d}$$\end{document}) promotes joint extinction of prey and the predator (Theorem 5).

An extreme weather event, such as a cold snap, briefly decreases the density of both species, but it does not necessarily change invasion outcomes. In contrast, an extreme weather event that directly increases mortality, conversion efficiency, or self-limitation (i.e., changes in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(a_E, \sigma _E, d_E)$$\end{document}) can alter the reaction terms and result in traveling wavefront to reselect one of the two stable equilibria. This is an important outcome which can potentially determine when the biological control fails or succeeds under these extreme weather events. Specifically, failure occurs when parameters select a bistable u–front of (16) with v tending to zero (i.e., predator loss and prey establishment). Whereas, success can be interpreted as joint extinction, which arises under the sufficient conditions of Theorem 5 or when initial prey is subcritical (Theorem 4). Coexistence with spatial spread appears in the regime \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0<r<b<1$$\end{document}; our conjectures in section "Asymptotic analysis of the full model" suggest bistable waves from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_3$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_0$$\end{document} and monostable waves from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_3$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_1$$\end{document}, which aligns with the simulations in subsection "Simulations of the full model". The numerical simulations presented in this study suggest that effective, climate-resilient biological control should emphasize maintaining vital rates of prey population during extreme weather rather than attempting to counteract transient abundance dips. Interventions that reduce spikes in effective predator mortality \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _E$$\end{document}, maintain predator-to-prey ratios above the joint-extinction threshold suggested by Theorem 5, or temporarily lower the prey Allee threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_E$$\end{document} during extreme conditions can select favorable traveling waves and lock in desired long-term outcomes. Monitoring programs should attend to heavy-tailed disturbance statistics, as tail risk, not variance alone, governs transient collapse probabilities and recovery times. Near bistable boundaries, small, well-timed actions can flip the selected wave and thereby determine the fate of the invasion.

The present study carries a number of limitations as follows. To keep the study focused, we did not provide a comparison with the Turing-pattern literature or with studies on differential (“two-speed”) predator–prey fronts, nor did we include sensitivity analysis or bifurcation diagrams mapping wave existence and stability. Our theoretical results are strongest in the monotone scalar reductions of Case I, whereas the fully coupled, non-monotone two-species dynamics in Case II remain analytically delicate (see section "Asymptotic analysis of the full model"). The conjectured existence and uniqueness of bistable and monostable waves in that regime remain as open problems. Future work should also relax \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(H_1)$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(H_3)$$\end{document} to quantify how deviations from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}u$$\end{document} perturb the exact wave solutions, their speed, and their stability. The modeling and simulation can be extended to two-dimensional bounded or unbounded spatial domain with delay and nonlocality50–53. Moreover incorporating additional climate pathways such as diffusion modulation and moving habitat edges can further enhance the modeling approach. Recent advances on qualitative dynamics, stability, and stochastic perturbations in nonlinear population models further motivate our framework for comparisons in bifurcation structure, noise–driven transitions, and numerical schemes66–68.

In conclusion, our theoretical analysis, supported by comprehensive numerical simulations, provides deeper insights into prey-predator dynamics under the influence of extreme weather events.
